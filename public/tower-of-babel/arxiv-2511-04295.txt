Amitsur–Small Extensions and a Skew Amitsur–Small Theorem
Masood Aryapoor

Abstract
We introduce the notion of Amitsur–Small extensions to generalize a key lemma underlying the Amitsur–Small Theorem to the skew setting. Building on this framework, we establish a skew version of the Amitsur–Small Theorem.



1
Introduction

In
[
1
]
, Amitsur and Small established the following result:

Theorem 1.1

(Amitsur–Small Theorem)
.

If
D
is a division ring, then simple
D[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
-modules are finite-dimensional as vector spaces over
D
.

Here, the notation
D[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
denotes for the polynomial ring over
D
in
n
central indeterminates. Their proof relies on three lemmas, one of which states:

Lemma 1.2

(Lemma C in
[
1
]
)
.

If
L
is a maximal left ideal of
D[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, then
L\cap D[X_{k}]\neq 0
for all
k
.

Our primary goal is to extend this lemma to the skew setting and thereby obtain a skew version of the Amitsur–Small Theorem. In doing so, we introduce a special class of ring extensions, which we term
Amitsur–Small extensions
(see Definition
3.1
). Our definition of Amitsur–Small extensions depends on the notion of
skew polynomial rings in several variables
, which serves as a suitable generalization of polynomial rings for our purposes (see Definition
3.1
). It is worth noting that for a maximal left ideal
L
in
D[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, the left ideal
L\cap D[X_{k}]
need not be maximal in
D[X_{k}]
. A concrete example illustrating this phenomenon was recently constructed by Chapman and Paran in
[
4
]
, where they introduced and studied the concept of an Amitsur–Small ring.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notion of skew polynomial rings in several variables and explores their relationship to iterated skew polynomial rings and
\sigma
-PBW extensions. In Section
3
, we define the concept of an Amitsur–Small extension and prove that if
R\subset S
is an Amitsur–Small extension, then for any maximal left ideal
L
of
S
, we have
L\cap R\neq 0
(see Proposition
3.1
). The final section presents some examples of Amitsur–Small extensions, and in particular, establishes the following skew version of the Amitsur–Small Theorem (see Theorem
4.4
):

Theorem 1.3

(Skew Amitsur–Small Theorem)
.

Let
D
be a division ring. Let
\sigma:D\to D
be an automorphism and
\delta:D\to D
be a
\sigma
-derivation such that
\sigma\delta=\delta\sigma
. Consider the ring
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
, generated over
D
by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, subject to the relations
x_{i}x_{j}=x_{j}x_{i}
,
x_{i}a=\sigma(a)x_{i}+\delta(a)
for all
i,j
and
a\in D
. If the center of the skew polynomial ring
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
contains a nonconstant polynomial, then any simple
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
-module is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
D
.

The final section also presents a generalization of Lemma
1.2
(see Proposition
4.2
):

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
R
be a principal (left and right) ideal ring with infinitely many distinct maximal ideals. Then for every maximal left ideal
L
of the polynomial ring
R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
over
R
in central indeterminates, we have
L\cap R\neq 0
.

Throughout this paper, all rings are assumed to be unital and associative, though not necessarily commutative.

2
Skew Polynomial Rings in Several Variables

To generalize the Amitsur–Small theorem, we must consider polynomial rings beyond those in central indeterminates. This section introduces the class of
skew polynomial rings in several variables
, which serves as a suitable framework for such generalizations.

Let
R
be a subring of a ring
S
. An (ordered) sequence of elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
in
S
is called
(left) algebraically independent over

R
if the power products
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
, where
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, are left
R
-linearly independent. We emphasize that the definition depends on the order of the elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in S
be algebraically independent over
R
. Recall that a
term order
is a well-order
<
on
\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that (1)
(0,\dots,0)<\mathbf{i}
for all
(0,\dots,0)\neq\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and (2) if
\mathbf{i}<\mathbf{j}
, then
\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{k}<\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}
for all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. We fix a term order
<
on
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. The notation
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}}<\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{j}}
is sometimes used in place of
{\mathbf{i}}<{\mathbf{j}}
.
Given a nonzero element

f=r_{1}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}_{1}}+\cdots+r_{m}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\in S,

where
0\neq r_{i}\in R
and
\mathbf{i}_{1}<...<\mathbf{i}_{m}
, we define:
\operatorname{lt}(f)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}r_{m}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}_{m}}
, called the
leading term
of
f
;
\operatorname{lp}(f)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}_{m}}
, called the
leading power product
of
f
;
\operatorname{le}(f)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbf{i}_{m}
, called the
leading exponent
of
f
. We use the convention
\operatorname{le}(0)=(-\infty,\dots,-\infty)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a ring and
R
be a subring of
S
. We say that
S
is a
skew polynomial ring over
R
in variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
if the following conditions hold:

1)
S
is a free left
R
-module with basis
{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}
, where
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

2) There exists a term order
<
on
\mathbb{N}^{n}
with respect to which

\operatorname{le}(fg)=\operatorname{le}(f)+\operatorname{le}(g)

for all
f,g\in S\setminus\{0\}
.

Under these conditions, we write
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
.

Before we present some elementary properties of skew polynomial rings in several variables, let us give some examples of such rings.

Example 2.1
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and

S=R[x_{1};\sigma_{1},\delta_{1}]\dots[x_{n};\sigma_{n},\delta_{n}]

be an iterated skew polynomial ring over
R
such that every
\sigma_{i}
is injective. Assume furthermore that
\sigma_{i}(R)\subseteq R
,
\delta_{i}(R)\subseteq R
, and
\sigma_{i}(x_{j})\in(R\setminus\{0\})x_{j}+R
for all
i>j
. Clearly,
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are (left) algebraically independent over
R
. Let
<_{l}
be the lexicographical order on
\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
x_{1}<...<x_{n}
, i.e.,

x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}<_{l}x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}\iff i_{n}=j_{n},\dots,i_{p+1}=j_{p+1},i_{p}<j_{p},\text{ for some }p.

Then
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<_{l}]
is a skew polynomial ring over
R
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. In the case where
n=1
, every skew polynomial ring
S=R[x;<_{l}]
over
R
in
x
is a skew polynomial ring in the usual sense, that is,
S=R[x;\sigma,\delta]
for a monomorphism
\sigma:R\to R
and a
\sigma
-derivation
\delta:R\to R
. This follows from the fact that the only term order on
\mathbb{N}
is the order

1<2<3<\cdots
.

The next example shows that every
\sigma
-PBW extension is a skew polynomial ring in several variables. For a detailed study of
\sigma
-PBW extensions, see
[
6
]
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and

S=\sigma(R)\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
be a
\sigma
-PBW extension of
R
.
Let
<_{d}
be the degree lexicographical order on the set of all power products
x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
with
x_{1}<...<x_{n}
, i.e.,

x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}<_{d}x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}
if and only if
i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}<j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}
, or
i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}=j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}
and
x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}<_{l}x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{j_{n}}
.
Then
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<_{d}]
is a skew polynomial ring over
R
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.

In the following proposition, we collect some properties of skew polynomial rings in several variables.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
be a skew polynomial ring over
R
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Then the following statements hold:

1)
S
is an integral domain.

2) For every
i=1,\dots,n
, there exists a unique endomorphism
\sigma_{i}:R\to R
such that
\operatorname{lt}(x_{i}r)=\sigma_{i}(r)x_{i}
for all
r\in R
.

Proof.

1) This is a simple consequence of the equality
\operatorname{le}(fg)=\operatorname{le}(f)+\operatorname{le}(g)
, where
f,g\in S\setminus\{0\}
.

2) For every nonzero
r\in R
, we have
\operatorname{lt}(x_{i}r)=r^{\prime}x_{i}
for a unique
r^{\prime}\in R\setminus\{0\}
because
\operatorname{le}(x_{i}r)=\operatorname{le}(x_{i})+\operatorname{le}(r)
and
\operatorname{le}(r)=(0,\dots,0)
. This gives a map
\sigma_{i}:R\to R
with
\sigma_{i}(0)=0
such that
\operatorname{lt}(x_{i}r)=\sigma_{i}(r)x_{i}
for all
r\in R
. It is clear that
\sigma_{i}
is additive. Comparing the leading terms of
x_{i}(r_{1}r_{2})
and
(x_{i}r_{1})r_{2}
shows that
\sigma_{i}
is an endomorphism.
∎

The endomorphisms
\sigma_{i}
’s whose existence is established in the proposition will be called the
structural endomorphisms
of
S
.

3
Amitsur–Small Extensions

This section introduces the concept of Amitsur–Small extensions and presents a class of such extensions.

3.1
Amitsur–Small Extensions

In this part, we introduce the class of Amitsur–Small extensions, which is a subclass of skew polynomial rings in several variables. Let us fix some notation. Let
R
be a ring and
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
be a skew polynomial ring over
R
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Let
\sigma_{i}:R\to R
be the endomorphism corresponding to
x_{i}
(see Proposition
2.1
). For
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we denote the map
\sigma_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{n}^{i_{n}}:R\to R
by
\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}
. For a subset
A
of
R
and
r\in R
, we set

(A:r)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{s\in R\,|\,sr\in A\}.

Definition 3.1
.

The skew polynomial ring
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
is said to be an
Amitsur–Small extension
of
R
if it satisfies the following property:
For any collection
\{I_{\mathbf{i}}\}_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
of left ideals of
R
satisfying
\sigma^{\mathbf{j}}({I}_{\mathbf{i}})\subseteq

I_{\mathbf{i+j}}
for all
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, there exists a nonzero element
r\in R\setminus\{0\}
that is not left invertible,

rS\subseteq Sr
, and

(I_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r))\subseteq(rI_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r))
for all
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

As an example of an Amitsur–Small extension, let
D
be a division ring and
x
be a central indeterminate. Then the polynomial ring
D[x][t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
over
D[x]
in
n
central indeterminates is an Amitsur–Small extension of
D[x]
. This result is a consequence of Lemma A and Lemma B in Amitsur and Small’s paper
[
1
]
. Our main objective is to give a generalization of this fact (see Theorem
3.3
). We note that an arbitrary Ore extension of
D[x]
may not be an Amitsur–Small extension of
D[x]
. As an example, the Weyl algebra
k[x][t;id,\frac{d}{dx}]
over a commutative field
k
of characteristic zero is not an Amitsur–Small extension of
k[x]
(see Example
3.1
).

The concept of Amitsur–Small extensions is justified by the following result, whose proof is similar to that of Lemma C in
[
1
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
be an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
. Then for every maximal left ideal
I
of
S
, we have
I\cap R\neq 0
.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we set

I_{\mathbf{i}}=\{0\}\cup\{r\in R\,|\,\exists f\in I\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{lt}(f)=r{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}\}.

It is easy to see that each
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is a left ideal of
R
, and moreover,

\sigma^{\mathbf{j}}(I_{\mathbf{i}})\subseteq I_{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}}
for all
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}
. Since
S
is an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
, there exists
r_{0}\in R\setminus\{0\}
such that
r_{0}
is not left invertible,
r_{0}S\subseteq Sr_{0}
, and

(I_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r_{0}))\subseteq(r_{0}I_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r_{0}))

for all
\mathbf{i}
. If
r_{0}\in I
, we are done. Assume that
r_{0}\notin I
. Since
I
is maximal as a left ideal, there exists

Q=a{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{m}}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}<\mathbf{m}}a_{\mathbf{i}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}\in S

with
\operatorname{le}(Q)=\mathbf{m}
as small as possible such that the element

Qr_{0}-1=a\sigma^{\mathbf{m}}(r_{0}){\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{m}}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}<\mathbf{m}}a^{\prime}_{\mathbf{i}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}

belongs to
I
. We claim that
\mathbf{m}=(0,\dots,0)
. Assume, on the contrary, that
\mathbf{m}\neq(0,\dots,0)
. Since
a\sigma^{\mathbf{m}}(r_{0})\in I_{\mathbf{m}}
, that is,
a\in(I_{\mathbf{m}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{m}}(r_{0}))=(r_{0}I_{\mathbf{m}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{m}}(r_{0}))
, we see that
a\sigma^{\mathbf{m}}(r_{0})=r_{0}b
for some
b\in I_{\mathbf{m}}
. There exists an element
P\in I
such that

P=b{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{m}}+\sum_{x^{\mathbf{i}}<x^{\mathbf{m}}}b_{\mathbf{i}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{i}}.

Since
r_{0}S\subseteq Sr_{0}
, we can find
P_{1}\in S
such that
r_{0}P=P_{1}r_{0}\in I
. It is easy to check that
\operatorname{lt}(P_{1})=a{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathbf{m}}
.
It follows that

(Q-P_{1})r_{0}-1\in I.

We have
\operatorname{le}(Q-P_{1})<\operatorname{le}(Q)
, contradicting the choice of
Q
. This completes the proof of the claim. Therefore,
Q=a\in R
. Since
r_{0}
is not left invertible in
R
, the element
ar_{0}-1
is a nonzero element in
I\cap R
, completing the proof.
∎

We now give an example of a skew polynomial ring which is not an Amitsur–Small extension.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the Weyl algebra
S=k[x][t;id,\frac{d}{dx}]
over a commutative field
k
of zero characteristic as a skew polynomial ring over
R=k[x]
. The left ideal
I
of
S
generated by
t
is a maximal left ideal. Since
I\cap R=0
, by Proposition
3.1
,
S
is not an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
.

3.2
A Class of Amitsur–Small Extensions

In this part, we present a family of Amitsur–Small extensions, which requires a technical lemma. We refer the reader to Cohn’s book
[
5
]
for the terminology used in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a principal (right and left) ideal domain (PID), and let
0\neq r_{0}\in R
. If

(Rr_{0}:r)\nsubseteq(rRr_{0}:r)

for some Inv-atom
r\in R
, then
r
is the right bound of some irreducible left factor of
r_{0}
. In particular, there exist at most finitely many such elements
r
up to right associates for any
0\neq r_{0}\in R
.

Proof.

Let an Inv-atom
r\in R
satisfy

(Rr_{0}:r)\nsubseteq(rRr_{0}:r).

Then we have

r_{1}r=r_{2}r_{0}
for some
r_{1}\in R
and
r_{2}\in R\setminus rR
. Since the set

\{s\in R\,|\,r_{2}s\in rR\}

is a right ideal of
R
, we have

\{s\in R\,|\,r_{2}s\in rR\}=r_{3}R

for some
r_{3}\in R
. Note that
r_{3}
cannot be a unit since otherwise we would have
r_{2}\in rR
.
Both
r
and
r_{0}
belong to
r_{3}R
because
Rr=rR
and
r_{2}r_{0}=r_{1}r\in rR
. Therefore, there exists an irreducible element
r^{\prime}
that is a left factor of both

r
and
r_{0}
. Since
r
is an Inv-atom, we see that
r
is the right bound of the left irreducible factor
r^{\prime}
of
r_{0}
, i.e.,
rR=ann(R/r^{\prime}R)
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Lemma
3.2
can be regarded as a generalization of Lemma A in Amitsur and Small’s paper
[
1
]
.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
R
be a PID, and let

S=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
be a skew polynomial ring over
R
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that all structural endomorphisms of
S
are automorphisms.
Assume that
R
has infinitely many distinct maximal ideals
Rr=rR
such that
rS\subseteq Sr
.
Then
S
is an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
, and consequently, for every maximal left ideal
M
of
S
,
M\cap R
is a maximal left ideal of
R
.

Proof.

Let us first note that the assumption
rS\subseteq Sr
, where
Rr
is a maximal ideal, implies that
R\sigma_{i}(r)=Rr
for all
i
. Let
I_{\textbf{i}}=Ra_{\textbf{i}}
, where
\textbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, be a family of left ideals of
R
satisfying
\sigma^{\textbf{j}}(I_{\textbf{i}})\subseteq I_{\textbf{i}+\textbf{j}}
for all
\textbf{i},\textbf{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. We need to show that there exists
r\in R\setminus\{0\}
such that
r
is not left invertible,

rS\subseteq Sr
, and

(I_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r))\subseteq(rI_{\mathbf{i}}:\sigma^{\mathbf{i}}(r))
for all
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Since
R
is a PID, we can find finitely many
\textbf{i}_{1},\dots,\textbf{i}_{m}
with the property that
for every
i
, there exists some
1\leq l\leq m
such that

\textbf{i}-\textbf{i}_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
I_{\textbf{i}}=

\sigma^{\textbf{i}-\textbf{i}_{l}}

(I_{\textbf{i}_{l}})
.
It follows from the assumption and Lemma
3.2
that there exists a maximal ideal
Rr_{0}=r_{0}R
in
R
such that

r_{0}S\subseteq Sr_{0}
and
(I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})\subseteq(r_{0}I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})
for all
\textbf{i}=\textbf{i}_{1},\dots,\textbf{i}_{m}
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.2
,
r_{0}
can be chosen such that
r_{0}
is not the right bound of any irreducible left factor of
a_{\textbf{i}}
for all
\textbf{i}=\textbf{i}_{1},\dots,\textbf{i}_{m}
. We claim that
(I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})\subseteq(r_{0}I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})
for all
i
. Assume, on the contrary, that there exists some
I_{\textbf{i}}
which does not satisfy the condition. Then there exists some
1\leq l\leq m
such that

\textbf{i}-\textbf{i}_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
I_{\textbf{i}}=

\sigma^{\textbf{i}-\textbf{i}_{l}}

(I_{\textbf{i}_{l}})
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we have

r_{0}R=ann(R/r^{\prime}R)
for a left irreducible factor
r^{\prime}
of
\sigma^{\textbf{i}-\textbf{i}_{l}}

(a_{\textbf{i}_{l}})
. We have

r_{0}R=\sigma^{-\textbf{i}+\textbf{i}_{l}}(r_{0})R=ann(R/\sigma^{-\textbf{i}+\textbf{i}_{l}}(r^{\prime})R),

that is,
r_{0}
is the right bound of the irreducible left factor
\sigma^{-\textbf{i}+\textbf{i}_{l}}

(r^{\prime})
of
a_{\textbf{i}_{l}}
. This contradicts the choice of
r_{0}
. Therefore,
(I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})\subseteq(r_{0}I_{\textbf{i}}:r_{0})
for all
i
. Since
R\sigma^{\textbf{i}}(r_{0})=Rr_{0}
, we see that

(I_{\textbf{i}}:\sigma^{\textbf{i}}(r_{0}))\subseteq(r_{0}I_{\textbf{i}}:\sigma^{\textbf{i}}(r_{0}))

for all
i
. This completes the proof. The second statement follows from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

4
Examples of Amitsur–Small Extensions

This section presents some examples of Amitsur–Small extensions and establishes a skew Amitsur–Small Theorem.
We begin with the one-variable case.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
R
be a PID,
\sigma:R\to R
be an automorphism, and
\delta:R\to R
be a
\sigma
-derivation. If
R
has infinitely many distinct maximal ideals invariant under
\sigma
and
\delta
, then the Ore extension
R[x;\sigma,\delta]
is an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
, and consequently, for every maximal left ideal
M
of
R[x;\sigma,\delta]
, we have
M\cap R\neq 0
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, we only need to show that
R
has infinitely many maximal ideals
Rr=rR
such that
rR[x;\sigma,\delta]\subseteq R[x;\sigma,\delta]r
. This follows immediately from the assumption that
\sigma(Rr)\subseteq Rr
and
\delta(Rr)\subseteq Rr
for infinitely many distinct maximal ideals
Rr
in
R
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

In the case where
R
is commutative, the second conclusion of the proposition holds under the weaker assumption that
R
is a Dedekind domain, as proved by Bavula
[
2
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Turning to the several-variable case, we begin with the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
R
be a PID with infinitely many distinct maximal ideals. Then the polynomial ring
R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
in central indeterminates is an Amitsur–Small extension of
R
, and consequently, for every maximal left ideal
M
of
R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, we have
M\cap R\neq 0
.

Proof.

For any term order
<
,
R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]=R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};<]
is a skew polynomial ring in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with respect to
<
. The result now follows from Theorem
3.3
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The converse of the proposition holds true for commutative rings. More precisely, if
M\cap R\neq 0
for every maximal ideal
M
of the polynomial ring
R[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
over a commutative PID
R
in central indeterminates, then
R
has infinitely many maximal ideals. One can prove this result using the language of G-rings. For more details, see
[
7
, Theorem 27 and Theorem 147]
.

To present a skew version of the Amitsur–Small Theorem, we need the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
D
be a division ring,
\sigma:D\to D
an automorphism and
\delta:D\to D
a
\sigma
-derivation. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1) The center of the Ore extension
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
contains a nonconstant polynomial.

(2)
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
has infinitely many distinct maximal ideals.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2): Let
h_{0}(x)
be a nonconstant polynomial of the least degree in the center of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
. Let
q(x)
be a polynomial in
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
of the least degree such that
D[x;\sigma,\delta]q(x)=q(x)D[x;\sigma,\delta]
. By Cauchon’s description of the ideal structure of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
(see
[
3
]
), every ideal of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
is generated by a nonconstant polynomial
h(x)q(x)^{n}
, where
h(x)\in C(D)_{\sigma,\delta}[h_{0}(x)]
and
n\geq 0
. Here,
C(D)_{\sigma,\delta}
is the field

C(D)_{\sigma,\delta}=\{a\in D:\sigma(a)=a,\delta(a)=0,\forall b\in D\quad ab=ba\}.

It follows that for every irreducible polynomial
g(t)\in C(D)_{\sigma,\delta}[t]
, the ideal of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
generated by
g(h_{0}(x))
is maximal. It is well known that the polynomial ring
F[t]
over a commutative field
F
has infinitely many monic irreducible polynomials, from which (2) follows.

(2)
\implies
(1): If the center of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
does not contain a nonconstant polynomial, then by Cauchon’s result, every ideal of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
is generated by a polynomial
q(x)^{n}
for some
n\geq 1
, in which case
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
would have a single maximal ideal. This proves the implication (2)
\implies
(1).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

It is easy to prove that if the center of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
contains a nonconstant polynomial, then
\sigma
has a finite inner order and
\delta
is quasi-algebraic. For more details, see
[
8
]
and the references therein.

Let
D
be a division ring. Let
\sigma:D\to D
be an automorphism and
\delta:D\to D
be a
\sigma
-derivation such that
\sigma\delta=\delta\sigma
. Consider the ring
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
, generated over
D
by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, subject to the relations
x_{i}x_{j}=x_{j}x_{i}
,
x_{i}a=\sigma(a)x_{i}+\delta(a)
for all
i,j
and
a\in D
. It can be shown that
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
is an iterated skew polynomial ring. More precisely, we have

D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]=D[x_{1};\sigma_{1};\delta_{1}][x_{2};\sigma_{2};\delta_{2}]\dots[x_{n};\sigma_{n};\delta_{n}],

where
\sigma_{i}|_{R}=\sigma,\delta_{i}|_{R}=\delta,\sigma_{j}(x_{i})=x_{i},\delta_{j}(x_{i})=0
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
. See also
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
D
be a division ring. Let
\sigma:D\to D
be an automorphism and
\delta:D\to D
be a
\sigma
-derivation such that
\sigma\delta=\delta\sigma
. If the center of
D[x;\sigma,\delta]
contains a nonconstant polynomial, then any simple
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
-module is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
D
.

Proof.

The discussion preceding the theorem shows that

S=D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]=D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta][x_{2},\dots,x_{n};<_{l}]

is a skew polynomial ring over
D[x_{1},\sigma,\delta]
in variables
x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
, where
<_{l}
denotes the lexicographical order with
x_{2}<...<x_{n}
(see Example
2.1
). By Proposition
4.3
,
D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta]
has infinitely many maximal ideals. As shown in the proof fo the proposition, every maximal ideal of
D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta]
is generated by a central polynomial. Therefore, there are infinitely many maximal ideals
D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta]f
in
D[x_{1},\sigma,\delta]
such that
fS\subset Sf
. It follows from Theorem
3.3
that
S
is an Amitsur–Small extension of
D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta]
. In particular, for every maximal left ideal
M
in
S
, we have
M\cap D[x_{1};\sigma,\delta]\neq 0
. By symmetry, for every maximal left ideal
M
in
S
, we have
M\cap D[x_{i};\sigma,\delta]\neq 0
for all
i
. Interpreting this result in the context of
D[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma,\delta]
-modules yields the desired conclusion.
∎

As a special case, we record the following result.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
F
be a finite field and
\sigma:F\to F
be an automorphism. Then every simple
F[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma]
-module is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
F
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\sigma
has a finite order, say
m
. Then
x^{m}
belongs to the center of
F[x;\sigma]
. By Theorem
4.4
, every simple
F[x_{1},\dots,x_{n};\sigma]
-module is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
F
.
∎
