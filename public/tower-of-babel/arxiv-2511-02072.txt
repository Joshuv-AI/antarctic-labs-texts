Conformal hypersurface invariants and Bach-type Boundary Problems
Samuel Blitz ♭ & Rod Gover ♯

Abstract
. Using variational considerations, we establish that there exists a new symmetric trace-free tensor conformal invariant of hypersurfaces embeddings in even dimensional conformal manifolds. This conformal invariant completes the family of conformal invariants known as conformal fundamental forms. The object has important links to global problems. In the context of the even dimensional boundary-value Poincaré–Einstein problem, the image of the Dirichlet–to–Neumann map is conformally invariant. Recent investigations [ 15 ] established that this image is the pullback of a particular Riemannian invariant to the odd-dimensional boundary. We show here that, in fact, that image arises as the restriction of the new conformal invariant constructed here. As a consequence of the proof, we are able to construct several new global conformal invariants of the boundary. Finally, we use our variational results to establish that compact Bach-flat manifolds with umbilic boundary must admit a (formal to all orders) Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class of its interior. Keywords: Conformal geometry, extrinsic conformal geometry and hypersurface embeddings, Bach flat equations, Poincaré-Einstein metrics

Introduction

This work concerns the geometric study of conformal
hypersurfaces
, meaning embedded submanifolds of codimension one in
conformal manifolds of dimension
d
. Hypersurfaces are especially
important, in part because they arise as the boundaries of domains and
manifolds with boundary and in these settings their geometry plays a
pivotal role in many geometric boundary problems. Turning this around
boundary problems can sometimes be used to help recover the geometry
of hypersurfaces; this idea has been effectively applied in number of
recent settings
[
38
,
34
,
8
,
33
,
16
,
15
]
and it will also be exploited here.

While describing the local extrinsic geometry of Riemannian
submanifolds is a classical subject
[
21
,
39
,
43
]
, the corresponding
conformal theory has, apart from a few basic elements (see for example
[
24
]
), been developed far more recently and is considerably more subtle and
rich than its Riemannian counterpart. Even obtaining local invariants
effectively is not trivial. For a conceptual and practical approach it
is natural to exploit the conformally invariant Cartan connection

[
17
]
or its associated bundle analogue known as the tractor
connection
[
10
]
, and the associated calculus. A
comprehensive basic conformal submanifold theory is developed in

[
20
]
building on
[
18
,
44
]

and this can be used to proliferate local invariants.

A distinguished class of extrinsic hypersurface invariants was
recently found and constructed in a rather different way

[
16
,
13
]
. These
higher conformal fundamental forms

\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt},\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt},\cdots,\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}\qquad k\leqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d-1&d\,\,\mbox{even}\\
\frac{d+1}{3}&d\,\,\mbox{odd}\end{array}\right..

are natural trace-free symmetric tensors, each of which is an
extrinsic hypersurface conformal invariant. Here
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}
is simply the
trace-free part of the usual second fundamental form, while
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
is
the trace-free part of a conformal invariant due to Fialkow (that also
arises naturally in the tractor picture

[
20
,
44
,
46
]
), but the higher
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}

are new objects that probe higher jets of the embedding. They are
central objects in two ways. First they provide a generating basis of
extrinsic invariants in the sense that any local irreducible natural
hypersurface invariant (with not too high a transverse order—see
below for the meaning of this) can be expressed by partial contraction
polynomials which involve only intrinsic hypersurface invariants and these
fundamental forms
[
14
]
.

The second reason is through their relation to Poincaré-Einstein
manifolds. On a manifold
M
with boundary
\Sigma:=\partial M
an
interior Riemannian metric
g_{+}
is said to be conformally compact if
takes the form
g_{+}=r^{-2}g
where
g
is a metric on
M
and
r
is a
defining function for the boundary – meaning
\Sigma=r^{-1}(0)
and

dr
is nowhere zero along
\Sigma
. If
g_{+}
is Einstein then its
scalar curvature is negative and
|dr|^{2}_{g}
is constant (and, by
scaling
g_{+}
, is set to 1) along
\Sigma
, then it is said to
be a
Poincaré-Einstein (PE) metric
. It is well known that

g_{+}
being PE also implies
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}=0
, and also that the trace-free
part of the Fialkow tensor
{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}
vanishes
[
24
,
28
]
, but
in fact each of the
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}
must vanish
[
16
]
. Thus the higher
conformal fundamental forms provide order-by-order obstructions to the
PE condition.

We henceforth restrict to the case of
d
even. At order
k=d

(involving the
d-1
-jet of
(r,g)
) we do not expect local
obstructions to the PE condition, as this is the order at which global
data enters the asymptotics
[
37
,
15
]
. However this does
not exclude the existence of a conformal fundamental form at this
order. Indeed in the work
[
15
]
the authors describe a local
curvature invariant of PE manifolds that precisely captures the image
of the Dirichlet-Neumann type map associated with the PE filling
problem. A natural question, first posed in
[
15
]
, is whether this invariant is the
restriction to PE manfolds of a local conformal hypersurface invariant
(that exists on any even conformal manifold with boundary). The
invariant

{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}:=C_{n(ab)}^{\top}+HW_{nabn}-\bar{\nabla}^{c}W_{c(ab)n}^{\top}

of
[
16
]
is an invariant of hypersurfaces in conformal
manifolds of dimension
d=4
that is exactly of this type: when
applied along the boundary of a PE manifold, it recovers the invariant
\operatorname{DN}^{(4)}
of
[
15
]
. Our main result is that a similar
result is available in all even dimensions.

Theorem 1.1
.

For a hypersurface embedded in a conformal manifold
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
of even dimension
d
there
is natural conformal extrinsic invariant
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}\in\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}\Sigma[3-d])
such that
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
has transverse order
d-1
.

In the above and what follows, the
transverse order
of an
extrinsic invariant is the integer
k
such that, when the invariant

I[g]
is evaluated on a hypersurface embedding
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M,g+s^{\ell}h)
with
s
a defining function for

\Sigma
and for any choices of metric
g
and rank-2 tensor
h
, we
find that
I[g]=I[g+s^{\ell}h]
for all
\ell>k
, and
I[g]\neq I[g+s^{k}h]
. The transverse order of a differential operator is
similarly defined.
See
[
16
,
15
]
for more details.

It is
straightforward to verify that the transverse order, weight (as described in Section
2
), and
symmetry properties of
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
above are sufficient to uniquely
characterize the leading transverse-order structure of this invariant
(see
[
16
]
). Moreover this uniqueness is enough to give the
following:

Theorem 1.2
.

On even-dimensional conformal manifolds
(M^{d},\bm{c})
with boundary that
admit a Poincaré–Einstein structure, the map

(1.1)

\left(\partial M\hookrightarrow(M,\bm{c})\right)\mapsto\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}

recovers the Poincaré–Einstein Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of
[
15
]
.

This Theorem is a key motivation for our work here. Fixing a manifold
with boundary
(M,\Sigma=\partial M)
, the Poincaré-Einstein filling
problem is the PDE problem of finding a Poincaré-Einstein metric

g_{+}
on the interior that is compatible with a given conformal
structure
\bm{c}_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
. The latter is viewed as the
Dirichlet data for this non-linear problem and the corresponnding
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, introduced in
[
37
]
and studied
in, for example
[
26
,
48
,
15
]
, is the map
that extracts the key non-locally determined data in the metric
asymptotics. Thus the map carries information about the global
structure of
(M,\Sigma,\bm{c}_{\Sigma})
. It is not obvious that there
should generally be an invariant of conformal hypersurface embeddings

\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M,\bm{c})
that upon restriction recovers this
important quantity, but the Theorem establishes that this is the case.

In Riemannian geometry the first fundamental form of a hypersurface
is the induced metric thereon. In this sense for a conformal
hypersurface
\Sigma
we may think of the induced conformal metric as the
first fundamental form, this is not trace-free but naturally its variations are given by sections of
S^{2}_{o}T^{*}\Sigma[2]
. Then
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
is the dual object in that
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
has the right symmetries
and weight to pair along the boundary with such variations:
For any such
h_{ab}

(1.2)

\int_{\Sigma}h^{ab}\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}_{ab}\,dv_{g_{\Sigma}}

is conformally invariant. Furthermore, the divergence
\bar{\nabla}^{a}\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}_{ab}
is also conformally invariant, and it appears as an obstruction to a conformal manifold with boundary admitting a Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class on its interior
M_{+}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M,\bm{c})
be an even-dimensional conformal manifold with boundary. The divergence

\bar{\nabla}^{a}\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}_{ab}\in\Gamma(T^{*}\Sigma[1-d])

is conformally invariant and is an obstruction to
\bm{c}|_{M_{+}}
admitting a Poincaré–Einstein metric.

This invariant also plays a role in the Bach-flat (see Equation (
1.5
for a definition of the Bach tensor) boundary-value problem in four dimensions. In particular, one finds that the normal-tangential projection of the Bach tensor along the conformal boundary depends on
\bar{\nabla}^{a}{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}
.

The observation (
1.2
) above is part of a more general
obervation that weights of the conformal fundamental forms are such
that they yield a family of extrinsic conformally invariant integrals along the conformal boundary:

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
be a conformal hypersurface embedding with
d
even. Then, for each
k\in\{2,\ldots,\tfrac{d}{2}\}
, the integral

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}^{ab}\,\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d+1-k}}}}_{ab}dV_{\bar{g}}

is conformally invariant.

This proposition follows simply from weight considerations and the
conformal invariance of the conformal fundamental forms. The pairings
above are linked to conformally invariant Dirichlet and Neumann data
for PDE boundary problems constructed around the Fefferman-Graham
obstruction tensor—and similar natural tensors. The main results are
proved in Section
3
. In
Section
4
, we discuss variational problems
linked to these boundary problems and treat the “grooming” of the
boundary terms to manifest the pairings (
1.3
). We then examine
the conditions that lead to well-posed variational problems on
manifolds with boundary. In this same section, we treat in detail the
explicit variation in four dimensions. In Section
5
,
we discuss the conditions in which Fefferman–Graham-flat manifolds
with boundary admit Poincaré–Einstein structures. In dimension 4,
the Fefferman–Graham tensor is the well-known Bach tensor
B
and
the Bach-flat condition, namely
B=0
, is a strict weakening of the
Einstein condition. Thus, in particular, PE manifolds are Bach flat. As mentioned above a PE manifold also necessarily has
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}=0
along the the boundary.
We prove that this necessary condition is also
sufficient for a Bach-flat 4-manifold with boundary to admit a
Poincaré–Einstein metric on its interior:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M^{4},\bm{c})
be a smooth Bach-flat conformal manifold with umbilic boundary. Then the conformal class of metrics on the interior of
M
contains a metric that is, formally to all orders, Poincaré–Einstein.

In fact, in Section
5
, we prove a stronger
result that manifests how the metric arises from the conformal class,
see Theorem
5.1
.

1.1.
Conventions

In this article,
M^{d}
will refer to a smooth (meaning
C^{\infty}
) manifold with dimension
d
. When paired with a metric
g
or an equivalence class
[g]=[\Omega^{2}g]
for
\Omega\in C^{\infty}M
, such a manifold is a Riemannian or conformal manifold, respectively. Given a Riemannian manifold (or a representative of a conformal manifold), the curvature tensor associated with the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
is denoted by
R
and is defined according to

(1.4)

\displaystyle R(x,y)z=(\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}-\nabla_{y}\nabla_{x})z-\nabla_{[x,y]}z\,,

where
x,y,z\in\Gamma(TM)
are smooth vectors on
M
and
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Lie bracket of vector fields.

For notational convenience, we implement Penrose’s abstract index notation, where a subscript Latin index indicates a section of the cotangent bundle, a superscript Latin index indicates a section of the tangent bundle, and multiple indices indicates a section of the corresponding tensor product of the tangent and cotangent bundles, with repeated indices following the Einstein convention. To that end, we may express Equation (
1.4
) as

x^{a}y^{b}R_{ab}{}^{c}{}_{d}z^{d}=x^{a}y^{b}(\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}-\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a})z^{c}\,.

Furthermore, the metric
g_{ab}
has an inverse
g^{ab}
which may be used to raise and lower indices in the usual way. Indices may be symmetrized over (with unit normalization) using round brackets, e.g.
X_{(ab)}=\tfrac{1}{2}(X_{ab}+X_{ba})
, and antisymmetrized with square brackets, e.g.
X_{[ab]}=\tfrac{1}{2}(X_{ab}-X_{ba})
. The tracefree part of a tensor can be indicated via the symbol
\circ
, so that, for example,
X_{(ab)_{\circ}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(X_{ab}+X_{ba}-\tfrac{1}{d}g_{ab}g^{cd}X_{cd})
.

Occasionally, we will use “dot product” notation to denote contractions using the appropriate metric; that is, when
v,u\in\Gamma(TM)
, we write
u\cdot v:=g(u,v)
. This notation can generalize to a vector contracted into a tensor, so that if
t\in\Gamma(\otimes^{k}TM)
, then we may write
(v\cdot t)^{a_{2}\cdots a_{k}}:=g_{ab}v^{a}t^{ba_{2}\cdots a_{k}}
.

For conformal geometries, there exists a useful decomposition of the Riemann curvature tensor into the Weyl curvature tensor and the Schouten tensor, given by

R_{abcd}=W_{abcd}+g_{ac}P_{bd}-g_{bc}P_{ad}-g_{ad}P_{bc}+g_{bd}P_{ac}\,,

where the Schouten tensor is defined according to

P_{ab}:=\tfrac{1}{d-2}\left(Ric_{ab}-\frac{Sc}{2(d-1)}g_{ab}\right)\,,

with the Ricci curvature convention given by
Ric_{ab}=R_{ca}{}^{c}{}_{b}
and scalar curvature
Sc=Ric_{a}{}^{a}
. Certain derivatives of the Weyl tensor appear in conformal geometry quite frequently. The Cotton tensor is defined by

C_{abc}:=\nabla_{a}P_{bc}-\nabla_{b}P_{ac}=\tfrac{1}{d-3}\nabla^{d}W_{dcab}\,,

and the Bach tensor is given by

(1.5)

\displaystyle B_{ab}:=\Delta P_{ab}-\nabla^{c}\nabla_{a}P_{bc}+P^{cd}W_{acbd}=\nabla^{c}C_{cab}+P^{cd}W_{acbd}\,.

(In the above,
\Delta=g^{ab}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}
is the negative Laplacian.)

1.2.
Riemannian hypersurface geometry

For later use in this article, we record conventions and identities for hypersurface embeddings
\iota:\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M,g)
, which we always assume to be smooth embeddings into compact manifolds. Going forward, we will identity the manifold
\Sigma
with its image under the embedding
\iota(\Sigma)
.

When
\Sigma
is the boundary of
M
, we denote by
n\in\Gamma(TM|_{\Sigma})
the inward pointing unit normal vector. When
\Sigma
is merely an embedded hypersurface,
n
denotes some unit normal vector. From this unit normal vector, we may construct the induced metric on
\Sigma
via

\bar{g}_{ab}=\iota^{*}g=g_{ab}|_{\Sigma}-n_{a}n_{b}\,.

Then, for any extension of the unit normal vector to
TM
, the second fundamental form may be expressed as

{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}=\nabla_{a}^{\top}n_{b}\,,

where
\nabla^{\top}_{a}=\bar{g}^{b}_{a}\nabla_{b}
is the projection of the Levi-Civita connection on
(M,g)
to
\Sigma
, with the mean curvature defined according to

H:=\tfrac{1}{d-1}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{a}^{a}\,.

The induced connection on
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
is related to this projection according to

\bar{\nabla}_{a}v^{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}\nabla_{a}^{\top}v^{b}+n^{b}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ac}v^{c}\,,

where
v\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
and, on the right hand side is extended arbitrarily into
TM
.

The Gauss equation relates the induced curvature to the projection of the curvature, so that

\bar{R}_{abcd}=R^{\top}_{abcd}+{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ac}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{bd}-{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ad}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{bc}\,.

Following from this equation, we find the trace-free Fialkow–Gauss equation:

(1.6)

\displaystyle\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{2}_{(ab)_{\circ}}-W_{nabn}=(d-3)(P_{ab}^{\mathring{\top}}-\mathring{\bar{P}}+H\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab})=:(d-3){\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}_{ab}\,,

where
{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}_{ab}
is the trace-free Fialkow tensor. The Codazzi–Mainardi equation, on the other hand, takes the form

R_{abcn}^{\top}=\bar{\nabla}_{a}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{bc}-\bar{\nabla}_{b}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ac}\,,

with its trace-free part taking the form

(1.7)

\displaystyle W_{abcn}^{\top}=\bar{\nabla}_{a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{bc}-\bar{\nabla}_{b}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ac}+\tfrac{1}{d-2}\left(\bar{g}_{bc}\bar{\nabla}\cdot\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{a}-\bar{g}_{ac}\bar{\nabla}\cdot\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{b}\right)\,.

2.
Conformal Geometry and Tractor Calculus

Here a conformal manifold means a smooth manifold
M
of
dimension
n\geqslant 3
equipped with an equivalence class
\bm{c}
of Riemanian
metrics, where
g_{ab}
,
\widehat{g}_{ab}\in\bm{c}
means that

\widehat{g}_{ab}=\Omega^{2}g_{ab}
for some smooth positive function

\Omega
.

On an
n
-manifold
M
the top exterior power of the tangent bundle
\Lambda^{n}TM
is a line bundle. Thus its square

\mathcal{K}:=(\Lambda^{n}TM)^{\otimes 2}
is
canonically oriented and so one can take oriented roots of it. Given

w\in\mathbb{R}
we set

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}[w]:=\mathcal{K}^{\frac{w}{2n}},

and refer to this as the bundle of conformal densities. For any vector
bundle
\mathcal{U}
, we write
\mathcal{U}[w]
to denote
\mathcal{U}[w]:=\mathcal{U}\otimes\mathcal{E}[w]
.
For example,
\mathcal{E}_{(ab)}[w]
denotes the symmetric second tensor power
of the cotangent bundle tensored with
\mathcal{E}[w]
, i.e.
S^{2}T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{E}[w]
on
M
.

On a fixed Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{K}
is canonically
trivialised by the square of the volume form, and so
\mathcal{K}
and its
roots are not usually needed explicitly. However if we wish to change
the metric conformally, or work on a conformal structure then these
objects become essential.
It is straightforward to show a weight
w
conformal density
\phi
,
that is a section
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}[w])
, can be interpreted as a
double equivalence class
\phi:=[g;f]=[\Omega^{2}g;\Omega^{w}f]

where
f\in C^{\infty}M
and we will use this henceforth without further mention.

A weight
w=1
conformal density
\tau
will be termed a

choice of scale
as, at all points where it is non-zero, it
determines a representative of the conformal class of metrics
g_{\tau}

via the distinguished representative
(g_{\tau};1)
in the equivalence
class
\tau=[g_{\tau};1]
. When
\tau>0
we refer to it as a

true scale
.
On a conformal manifold there is evidently a canonical section
\bm{g}

of
\odot^{2}T^{*}M[2]
given by
\bm{g}=[g;g]
. This is called the
conformal metric.
The metric determined by
\tau\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}[1])
then arises as
g_{\tau}=\tau^{-2}\bm{g}
. In this article, when we do explicit computations we will pick a metric and trivialize density bundles accordingly.

Given a true scale
\tau
one has the corresponding Levi-Civita
connection
\nabla^{g_{\tau}}
. Clearly this acts naturally on
densities and, in fact, as an operator on
\Gamma(\mathcal{E}[w])
, is
recovered by the formula
\nabla^{\tau}=\tau^{w}\circ d\circ\tau^{-w}
. It follows that
\nabla^{g_{\tau}}
preserves the
conformal metric
\bm{g}
and so we may use that to raise and lower
indices without confusion. It turns out that this simplifies many
computations, and so we will do this.

On a general conformal manifold
(M,\bm{c})
, there is no
distinguished connection on
TM
. But there is an invariant and
canonical connection on a closely related bundle, namely the conformal
tractor connection on the standard tractor bundle.
Here we review the basic conformal tractor calculus on
Riemannian and conformal manifolds. See

[
10
,
19
,
30
]
for more details.
Unless stated otherwise, calculations will be done with the use of generic
g\in\bm{c}
.

Given a metric
representative
g\in\bm{c}
, the
standard tractor bundle

\mathcal{T}M

is isomorphic to the rank
d+2
vector bundle

\mathcal{T}M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\cong}}\mathcal{E}M[1]\oplus TM[-1]\oplus\mathcal{E}M[-1]\,.

Sections of this bundle (and tensor products involving this bundle) are denoted using capital latin letters for abstract indices, so that for example

(2.2)

T^{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{=}}(\tau^{+},\tau^{a},\tau^{-})\,.

While the
isomorphism holds for any metric representative, the isomorphism
transforms non-trivially under conformal rescalings:

(2.3)

T^{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega^{2}g}}{{=}}(\tau^{+},\tau^{a}+\Upsilon^{a}\tau^{+},\tau^{-}-\tau^{a}\Upsilon_{a}-\tfrac{1}{2}|\Upsilon|^{2}_{g}\tau^{+})\,,

where
\Upsilon:=d\log\Omega
.

The tractor bundle decomposition determined by a metric
g\in\bm{c}
, as given in (
2.2
), is captured by three bundle maps or

injectors
:
X^{A}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{T}M[1])
,
Z^{A}_{a}\in\Gamma(T^{*}M\otimes\mathcal{T}M[1])
, and
Y^{A}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{T}M[-1])
characterised by

T^{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{=}}\tau^{+}Y^{A}+\tau^{a}Z^{A}_{a}+\tau^{-}X^{A}\,.

These have conformal transformations determined by (
2.3
). In particular
X^{A}
is independent of the
choice of metric representative; we
label it the
canonical tractor
. The other injectors depend on
a the choice of metric representative. Nevertheless another choice-independent
tractor may also be constructed from these injectors: the tractor
metric, expressed in a choice of metric representative
g\in\bm{c}
:

h^{AB}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{:=}}\bm{g}^{ab}Z^{A}_{a}Z^{B}_{b}+2X^{(A}Y^{B)}\in\Gamma(\odot^{2}\mathcal{T}M)\,.

As it is invertible this identifies
\mathcal{T}M
with its dual
\mathcal{T}^{*}M
.

Additionally, one may use these injectors to construct sections of tractor bundles from sections of tensor products of the (co)tangent bundle (or the weighted equivalent). Doing so invokes differential operators known as
splitting operators
(see
[
45
]
for a complete characterization). It is sufficient for us to note that these are operators with left-inverses. One relevant example of a splitting operator takes the form

(2.4)

\displaystyle q_{AB}^{ab}:\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[w+2])

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}\mathcal{T}M[w])

\displaystyle t_{ab}

\displaystyle\mapsto Z_{A}^{a}Z_{B}^{b}t_{ab}-\tfrac{2}{d+w}X_{(A}Z_{B)}^{a}\nabla^{b}t_{ab}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{(d+w)(d+w-1)}X_{A}X_{B}(\nabla^{a}\nabla^{b}t_{ab}+(d+w)P^{ab}t_{ab})\,.

Note that this operator lives in the kernel of contraction with
X
.

The

tractor connection

\nabla^{\mathcal{T}}
is defined, in a choice of scale

\tau
, by

\nabla_{a}^{\mathcal{T}}T^{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{=}}(\nabla_{a}^{\tau}\tau^{+}-\bm{g}_{ac}\tau^{c},\nabla_{a}^{\tau}\tau^{b}+\bm{g}_{a}^{b}\tau^{-}+(P^{g_{\tau}})_{a}^{b}\tau^{+},\nabla_{a}^{\tau}\tau^{-}-(P^{g_{\tau}})_{ab}\tau^{b})\,.

It
is straightforward to check via the tractor transformation identity
that
\nabla^{\mathcal{T}}
is indeed a choice-independent
connection on conformal manifolds.

From its definition, one observes that, for
a choice of metric
g\in\bm{c}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}^{\mathcal{T}}X^{A}=

\displaystyle Z^{A}_{b}

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}Z^{A}_{a}=

\displaystyle-(P^{g})_{ab}X^{A}-\bm{g}_{ab}Y^{A}

\displaystyle\nabla_{b}Y^{A}=

\displaystyle(P^{g})_{b}^{a}Z^{A}_{a}\,.

where on the left hand side we have coupled the tractor connection to
the Levi-connection of
g_{\tau}
. These relations are useful for
computations and, in particular, using these one can verify that the
tractor connection preserves the tractor metric

\nabla^{\tau}_{a}h_{BC}=0.

It follows that it sensible to use
h_{BC}
and its inverse to lower and raise indices.

A non-trivial fact is that one may couple the tractor connection and
the Levi-Civita connection in a way to produce a tractor-valued
differential operator, called the
Thomas-
D
operator
, that
acts on any weighted tractor bundle, and is independent of the choice
of metric representative. This is defined, in a choice of scale, by

D^{A}T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g_{\tau}}}{{:=}}(d+2w-2)wY^{A}T+(d+2w-2)\bm{g}^{ab}Z^{A}_{a}\nabla_{b}^{\mathcal{T}}T-X^{A}(\Delta^{\mathcal{T}}+wJ^{g_{\tau}})T\,,

where
T
is weight-
w
section of a tractor bundle.

Closely linked to the tractor connection (see e.g.
[
10
]
) is the
conformally invariant differential operator
D_{ab}:\Gamma(\mathcal{E}[1])\to\odot_{o}^{2}T^{*}M[1])
given by

(2.5)

D_{ab}\tau=\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}\tau+\mathring{P}_{ab}\tau.

This is sometimes called the
almost-Einstein operator
because non-zero
solutions to
D_{ab}\tau=0
are scales such that
g=\tau^{-2}\bm{g}
is
Einstein on the open dense set (we assume
M
connected) where
\tau
is non-zero, and such solutions correspond to parallel standard tractors
[
29
,
28
]
.

Next, for our purposes it is useful to recall from
[
10
]
the
notion of the normal tractor. This is a natural section of
\mathcal{T}|_{\partial M}
along the
boundary
\partial M
of a conformal manifold
M
with boundary. In a choice of
metric representative, this is given by

N^{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{:=}}(0,n^{a},-H^{g})\in\Gamma(\mathcal{T}M|_{\Sigma})\,,

where
n
is the inward-pointing unit normal vector to the boundary

\Sigma
satisfying
|n|^{2}_{g}=1
and
H^{g}
is its mean curvature for
the representative
g
. Elsewhere, we use the same symbol
n
to refer to any extension of
n
off
\Sigma
unless specified. It is easily checked that the display
transforms according to (
2.3
), and so
N^{A}
is a conformally
invariant tractor field.

Now, given a conformal hypersurface embedding
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
, one may always (formally) solve the singular Yamabe problem to an order determined by the dimension of the manifold, see
[
7
]
, and, for the way we describe it here,
[
33
, Theorem 4.5]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
be a smooth conformal hypersurface embedding with
\sigma_{0}:=[g;s]\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[1])
, where
s
is any defining function for
\Sigma
. Then there exists a distinguished density
\sigma\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[1])
unique modulo terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{d+1}_{0})
satisfying

\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}|D\sigma|^{2}=1+\sigma^{d}\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}\,,

for some smooth density
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[-d])
.

We call the density
\sigma
, distinguished by the above proposition,
the
singular Yamabe density
, the metric it determines

g^{o}:=g/s^{2}
the
singular Yamabe metric
, and

\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}|_{\Sigma}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}\Sigma[-d])
the

obstruction density
; note that the obstruction density is
independent of the choice of
\sigma_{0}
and thus is an invariant of
the embedding.

From this result, and the conformal invariance of the almost-Einstein operator (
2.5
), it follows immediately
that associated to any conformal hypersurface embedding one may
construct a canonical
almost-Einstein tensor

E_{ab}
, unique to order

\sigma^{d-1}
:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
be a smooth conformal
hypersurface embedding with singular Yamabe density
\sigma:=[g;s]\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[1])
. Then

E_{ab}:=D_{ab}\sigma

is uniquely determined modulo terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{d-1})
.

The above results are local. But from the works of
[
41
,
9
,
7
]
(see the discussion in the introduction of
[
35
]
for more details), there is a stronger, global result:

Proposition 2.3
.

On a compact conformal manifold with boundary, there exists a unique
solution
\sigma
to the singular Yamabe problem. Furthermore, if the
obstruction density vanishes, then that solution is smooth.

Remark 2.4
.

For convenience, we will refer to a singular Yamabe metric as smooth when the singular Yamabe density it arises from is smooth.

As a corollary, when the obstruction density vanishes, the almost Einstein tensor
E
above is determined uniquely by the conformal embedding.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(M^{d},\bm{c})
be a compact conformal manifold with smooth boundary embedding
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M^{d},\bm{c})
and vanishing obstruction density. Then,
E\in\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[1])
is smooth up to the boundary and is uniquely determined by the boundary embedding and the conformal structure
(M,\bm{c})
.

Proof.

From Proposition
2.3
, we have that there exists a
unique smooth singular Yamabe density
\sigma
. But
E
is defined by
a conformally invariant differential operator on
\sigma
, and hence
as
\sigma
only depends on the boundary embedding and the conformal
structure, so does
E
. Furthermore, as
\sigma
is smooth up to the
boundary (and the underlying conformal structure is also), so is
E
.

∎

3.
Fefferman–Graham Obstruction Tensors and the Main Results

In
[
22
,
23
]
, it is shown that on each even-dimensional conformal manifold
(M^{d},\bm{c})
, the so-called
Fefferman–Graham
obstruction tensor
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}
arises as a distinguished, divergence-free section of
\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[2-d]
. These tensors get their name from the fact that they appear as the smoothness obstruction to a formal solution of the Poincaré–Einstein equation on a
(d+1)
dimensional manifold. More specifically, given a conformal manifold embedded as the boundary of a smooth manifold
(\Sigma,\bar{\bm{c}})\hookrightarrow M^{d}
(with
d
odd), the existence of a formal extension
\bm{c}
of
\bar{\bm{c}}
to
M
containing a Poincaré–Einstein metric as a reprensentative is obstructed by the Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor of
(\Sigma,\bar{\bm{c}})
. These tensors are of special interest as, aside from the Weyl tensor, they are the only conformally-invariant curvature tensors that are linear in the curvature at leading order
[
22
]
. For our purposes, this fact is critical.

The Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor also arises variationally from a conformally invariant energy functional

(3.1)

\displaystyle\int_{M^{d}}Q_{d}\,dV

where
Q_{d}
is Branson’s
Q
-curvature in
d
dimensions
[
36
]
. While
Q
-curvatures are not conformally-invariant themselves, they transform invariantly with weight
-d
modulo a divergence—hence, on a closed manifold, Display (
3.1
) is an integrated conformal invariant. However, for our purposes, we require an integral whose integrand is pointwise conformally-invariant and whose variation is the Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor. That such a pointwise-invariant exists is captured in the following two propositions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(M^{d},\bm{c})
be an even-dimensional conformal manifold. Then, the ambient scalar invariant
L_{d}:=|\tilde{\nabla}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\tilde{R}|^{2}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[-d])
is the unique (modulo terms cubic or higher order in curvature) natural conformal invariant that is quadratic in curvature at this weight.

Proof.

From the construction provided by Fefferman and Graham
[
23
, Proposition 9.1]
, the ambient invariant
L_{d}:=|\tilde{\nabla}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\tilde{R}|^{2}
is a scalar conformal invariant. Furthermore, it is straightforward to check that it is quadratic at leading order.
It thus remains to show that this conformal invariant is unique.

To do so, we note from the construction provided by Fefferman and Graham, that, for a fixed
d
, there exists a dimension-parametrized family of formulas for conformal invariants
\{L_{d}^{d^{\prime}}\}_{d^{\prime}}
where for each even
d^{\prime}>d
, the formula is given by

L_{d}^{d^{\prime}}:=|\tilde{\nabla}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\tilde{R}|^{2}\,.

Now, because the ambient curvature has vanishing Laplacian and divergence in sufficiently high dimension and a vanishing Ricci curvature to sufficiently high order, it follows that the ambient tensor
\tilde{\nabla}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\tilde{R}
is trace-free modulo lower-order curvature terms. Furthermore, from the Bianchi identity and the Ricci identity, it follows that we may not antisymmetrize over more than two indices without this expression being expressible in terms of lower-order invariants. It follows from straightforward representation theory that
L_{d}^{d^{\prime}}
is a formula for a unique (modulo terms cubic or higher order in the curvature) scalar conformal invariant for all
d^{\prime}>d
.

Now for contradiction suppose that there are two distinct invariants (at quadratic order in curvature) of the same form when
d^{\prime}=d
. Then, those two invariants would be realized as formulas that produce two distinct families of conformal invariants all even dimensions
d^{\prime}>d
. However, from the uniquness argument above in
d^{\prime}>d
, these families of invariants must agree at quadratic order in curvature. This then implies that there exist at least one family of curvature identities that hold in all dimensions
d^{\prime}>d
that does not hold in
d
dimensions. But no such curvature identities exist—a contradiction. This establishes that the invariant
L_{d}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[-d])
is the unique conformal invariant (of this weight that is quadratic at leading order) constructible from the ambient metric. However, from
[
23
, Theorem 9.4]
, all natural conformal invariants at this weight are constructible from the ambient metric. So uniqueness follows.
∎

Low-lying invariants of this form are known explicitly. In four dimensions, we have the (clearly) invariant density
L_{4}:=|W|^{2}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[-4])
. In six dimensions, Fefferman and Graham presented another invariant,

L_{6}:=|V|^{2}+16W^{abcd}(\nabla_{a}C_{dcb}-P_{a}^{e}W_{ebcd})+16C_{abc}C^{abc}\,,

where
V_{abcde}
has leading term
\nabla_{a}W_{bcde}
and subleading terms that are pure trace.

Now we show that it is precisely this family of pointwise conformal invariants whose integrals vary into the Fefferman-Graham obstruction tensors.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(M^{d},g)
with
d
even be a manifold without boundary. Then, there exists at least one pointwise conformal invariant
\mathcal{L}_{d}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}M[-d])
that is quadratic in curvature at leading order such that

\delta\int_{M}\mathcal{L}_{d}dV=\int_{M}(\delta g)^{ab}\mathcal{O}^{(d)}_{ab}dV_{g}\,,

where
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}
is the Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor in
d
dimensions. Furthermore, all such pointwise conformal invariants are proportional to
L_{d}
modulo terms of cubic or higher order in curvature, and their functional
L^{2}
-gradient are the Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor modulo terms quadratic in curvature.

Proof.

From
[
36
]
, there exists a natural scalar invariant called the Branson
Q
-curvature sastisfying

\delta\int_{M}Q^{g}dV_{g}=\int_{M}h^{ab}\mathcal{O}^{(d)}_{ab}dV_{g}\,.

Furthermore,
\int_{M}Q^{g}dV_{g}
is a global conformal invariant. Now from
[
3
]
it thus follows that the
Q
-curvature is decomposable as a sum of a Pfaffian, a pointwise conformal invariant, and a divergence. As the Pfaffian term is topological, it cannot contribute to the metric variation. Furthermore, divergences can be omitted because the manifold is closed.
Thus, there exists a pointwise conformal invariant that varies into the Fefferman–Graham obstruction tensor. By fiat, we identify an arbitrary such pointwise conformal invariant with
\mathcal{L}_{d}
.

Now, at leading order it is known that
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}\propto\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}B
.
But because
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}
is linear at leading order in curvature, it can only arise variationally from curvature invariants that are at most quadratic (at leading order) in curvature. However, because the only conformal invariants that are linear in curvature are the Weyl curvature and the obstruction tensor
[
22
]
, there are no conformally-invariant scalars that are linear in curvature. Hence, it follows that
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}
arises variationally from a conformal invariant that is quadratic in curvature at leading order. Thus
\mathcal{L}_{d}
is as required by the proposition. Furthermore, by the uniqueness provided in Proposition
3.1
, all possible choices of
\mathcal{L}_{d}
must be proportional to
L_{d}
modulo terms that are at least cubic in curvature.
∎

That such pointwise conformal-invariants exist will be instrumental in constructing
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
. However, before we may proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we first need to provide some technical results regarding the existence of certain conformally-invariant normal derivative operators.

To construct the required normal derivative operators, we reproduce the following propositions.

\displaystyle\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[2])\rightarrow\Gamma(T^{*}\Sigma[1-k])

\displaystyle\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[2])\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathcal{E}\Sigma[-k])\,,

\displaystyle-\tfrac{2}{d-2}(\bar{Q}^{*}_{2}\circ\mathring{\top}\delta^{k}D_{[A}q^{bc}_{B]C})(h_{bc})

\displaystyle-\tfrac{2}{d-2}(X^{A}N^{B}N^{C}\delta^{k}D_{[A}q^{bc}_{B]C})(h_{bc})\,,

where

We are now ready to prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

As noted in Proposition
3.2
, on a manifold without boundary,
\int_{M}\mathcal{L}_{d}dV
varies into the obstruction tensor. Note that because
\mathcal{L}_{d}
is quadratic in curvature at leading order, integration by parts occurs at least twice in order to obtain the leading structure of the obstruction
\int h^{ab}(\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}B_{ab}+\text{ subleading})
. Now such a term can only arise (up to identities) at leading order via integration by parts from an integral of the form

-\int\left(\alpha\nabla_{c}\Delta^{\frac{d-6}{2}}B_{ab}+(1-\alpha)\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{cab}\right)\nabla^{c}h^{ab}\,,

for some constant
\alpha
, which follows because
B_{ab}=\nabla^{c}C_{cab}+\text{lower order}
.
Indeed, after integrating by parts, we are left with (modulo subleading terms),

\int\left[h^{ab}\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}B_{ab}-\nabla^{c}\left(\alpha h^{ab}\nabla_{c}\Delta^{\frac{d-6}{2}}B_{ab}+(1-\alpha)h^{ab}\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{cab}\right)\right]\,,

as required.

Now for a manifold without boundary, the divergence term vanishes—however, when the manifold has a boundary, the boundary term (again, modulo subleading terms) takes the form

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}n^{c}h^{ab}\left(\alpha\nabla_{c}\Delta^{\frac{d-6}{2}}B_{ab}+(1-\alpha)\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{cab}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}n^{c}h^{ab}\left(\alpha\nabla_{c}\Delta^{\frac{d-6}{2}}\nabla^{d}C_{dab}+(1-\alpha)\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{cab}\right)+\text{subleading}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\alpha n^{d}h^{ab}\nabla_{n}^{2}\Delta^{\frac{d-6}{2}}C_{dab}+(1-\alpha)n^{c}h^{ab}\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{cab}+\text{subleading}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}h^{ab}n^{d}\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{dab}+\text{subleading}\,,

where

is an inward pointing unit normal vector.
In particular, this term has a non-vanishing coefficient. Our goal now is to show that this leading transverse-order integrand on the boundary combines with lower order terms to produce a pointwise conformal invariant with leading structure
n^{d}\Delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}C_{d(ab)}
contracted into the metric variation along the boundary.

By construction, the variation of the integral in question is conformally invariant as is the bulk integral itself (which can be established by examining the variation of the integral on a manifold without boundary). It follows that the boundary integral arising from the variation is separately conformally invariant.

To show pointwise invariance of the integrand along the boundary, we now must decompose the integral along
\Sigma
.

In general, the integrand of the boundary integral can be expressed in terms of bulk curvatures, the normal vector, the metric variation, and their derivatives. Furthermore, those derivatives can be decomposed into intrinsic hypersurface derivatives and transverse derivatives. Moreover because the boundary has no boundary, we may integrate by parts to remove intrinsic hypersurface derivatives from the metric variation, leaving only transverse derivatives on metric variations contracted into curvatures (whether intrinsic or extrinsic) and copies of the normal vector. Then we perform a tangential decomposition of those transverse derivatives on metric variations into four independent components:

\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k}\mathring{h}_{ab}\qquad\top n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{k}\mathring{h}_{ab}\qquad n^{a}n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{k}\mathring{h}_{ab}\qquad\nabla_{n}^{k}h_{a}^{a}\,.

Observe that the first integration by parts to remove derivatives from

may leave up to

\int_{\Sigma}dV_{\bar{g}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}\left(\bar{O}_{(i)}\nabla_{n}^{i}h^{a}_{a}+\bar{P}_{(i)}n^{a}n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{i}\mathring{h}_{ab}+\bar{Q}_{(i)}^{a}\top(n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{i}\mathring{h}_{ab})+\bar{R}_{(i)}^{ab}\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{i}\mathring{h}_{ab}\right)

3.1.
Conserved Currents

Having constructed
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}
, we now may use it to further glean information about the way the boundary of a conformal manifold is embedded. Indeed, we may straightforwardly prove Corollary

.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

First, observe that by a straightforward computation, one may show that for any section

Next, from Theorem

, it follows that

is the image of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the Poincaré–Einstein problem. But then its divergence vanishes, see
[
15
, Remark 3.10]
.
∎

In light of Corollary
1.3
, we may improve the
definition found in
[
16
]
for an asymptotically
Poincaré–Einstein manifold. To do so we first requires a standard definition:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
(M,\bm{c})
be a conformal manifold with boundary. Recall
g^{o}\in\bm{c}|_{M_{+}}
is conformally compact if
g_{s}=s^{2}g^{o}
extends smoothly
as a metric to the boundary, for
s\in C^{\infty}M
some boundary
(equivalently any) defining function. In this case the scalar
curvature
Sc^{g^{o}}
extends smoothly to the boundary, and we use

Sc^{g^{o}}
to denote that extension. Let
g^{o}\in\bm{c}|_{M_{+}}
be a
conformally compact metric such that

Sc^{g^{o}}=-d(d+1)+\mathcal{O}(s)\,.

We call such a metric representative an

asymptotically hyperbolic
metric.
∎

Similarly, from the conformal invariance of the almost-Einstein
operator (
2.5
) it follows that, on a conformally compact
manifold
(M,g^{o})
, the quantity
s\mathring{P}^{g^{o}}
(with
s
as
above) also extends smoothly to the boundary. As the vanishing of this
quantity is equivalent to the Einstein condition, we are now able to
define a special kind of asymptotically hyperbolic metric:

Definition 3.8
.

Let
(M,\bm{c})
be a conformal manifold with boundary and let
g^{o}
be an asymptotically hyperbolic metric on its interior. Then
g^{o}
is said to be
asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein
if

s\mathring{P}^{g^{o}}=\mathcal{O}(s^{d-2}),

where
s\in C^{\infty}M
that is a defining function for the boundary,
and the boundary embedding
\partial M\hookrightarrow(M,\bm{c})
satisfies
\bar{\nabla}\cdot\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d}}}}=0

∎

Observe that the first condition for a metric

to be
asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein is directly inspired. However the second
condition can not be easily read off from the Einstein condition
alone, but is rather suggested by the result in Corollary
1.3
.

In view of Definition
3.8
and Propositions
2.1

and
2.3
, it is evident that when there exists an

of the singular Yamabe solution. (The point here is that any PE metric
is, in particular, a singular Yamabe solution, so if a PE metric exists, the uniqueness of the singular Yamabe metric implies the PE metric is also unique.) For brevity, we will
say that a conformal manifold with boundary admits an asymptotically
Poincaré–Einstein metric when there exists such a metric in the
conformal class of the interior of the manifold.

As a consequence of the main theorem of
[
16
]
and the Definition

Corollary 3.9

Let
(M,\bm{c})
be an even-dimensional conformal manifold with boundary. Then
(M,\bm{c})

\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}=0

On the other hand, when

4.
Conformally-Invariant Variational Problems on Manifolds with Boundary

As a consequence of the construction used in Section
3
, we find applications in, among other things, variational approaches to the Fefferman–Graham flat problem, meaning the boundary problem associated to the PDE
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}=0
. To see this, consider the classical case of the Einstein–Hilbert action on a 4-manifold:

S_{EH}=\tfrac{1}{16\pi}\int_{M}Sc\,dV_{g}\,,

where
Sc
is the scalar curvature. On a manifold with boundary, it is known that
[
11
]

(4.1)

\displaystyle 16\pi\delta S_{EH}=-\int_{M}G_{ab}h^{ab}dV_{g}+\int_{\partial M}(2H\bar{g}^{ab}-\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{ab})h^{\top}_{ab}dV_{\bar{g}}-6\int_{\partial M}\delta(HdV_{\bar{g}})\,.

On a manifold without boundary, solving the problem
\delta S_{EH}=0
is equivalent to solving the Einstein field equations
G_{ab}=0
in a vacuum. Furthermore, it is known that the partial differential equation given by
G_{ab}=0
, for metrics with Euclidean signature, is, after gauge-fixing, elliptic
[
12
]
. Now because
\delta H
depends on the 1-jet of
h
along
\Sigma
, and we may choose
h
arbitrarily, it is clear that all three of the integrals must vanish independently to ensure that
\delta S_{EH}=0
. But enforcing this then amounts to specifying both
g|_{\partial M}
and its first (normal) derivative. (Note that one may alternatively fix the coefficient of
h^{\top}
in the second integral to vanish—this does not change the claim here.) In contrast, strictly fewer boundary conditions are required to make the gauge-fixed Einstein problem an elliptic boundary problem (of Fredholm index
0
)
[
5
]
. So, we find that that, as written, the variational version of the boundary-value problem is overdetermined.

To resolve this problem, Gibbons, Hawking, and York modified the Einstein–Hilbert action. Consider the energy functional

S=\tfrac{1}{16\pi}\int_{M}Sc\,dV_{g}+\tfrac{3}{8\pi}\int_{\partial M}HdV_{\bar{g}}\,,

Varying this functional, we have that

16\pi\delta S=-\int_{M}\sqrt{g}G_{ab}\delta g^{ab}dV_{g}+\int_{\partial M}(2H\bar{g}_{ab}-\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab})\delta\bar{g}^{ab}dV_{\bar{g}}\,,

Comparing with
[
5
]
, it is now clear that solving the variational system is at least not overdetermined (in fact, it is underdetermined as gauge-fixing is not required to solve the variational problem). Alternatively, one may instead prescribe that
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}=0
and find the same. That is, the (no longer overdetermined) variational problem requires that either
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}=0
or that we fix
\bar{g}
.

Note that this procedure picks out an important ordered pair of extrinsic tensors for the Einstein equation on a manifold with boundary: the induced metric
\bar{g}
and a trace-corrected form of the second fundamental form
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
,
(\bar{g},{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}-3H\bar{g})
. Note that this is
not
the trace-free part of
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
.

For later comparison, it is helpful to record the variation of the Einstein–Hilbert action in terms of another set of variables. Unpacking Equation (
4.1
), we find that

16\pi\delta S_{EH}=-\int_{M}G_{ab}\delta g^{ab}dV_{g}\\
+\int_{\partial M}\left[(-\delta g)_{ab}^{\top}(\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{ab}+H\bar{g}^{ab})+3(\delta g)_{nn}H-(\nabla_{n}\delta g_{ab})\bar{g}^{ab}\right]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

Observe that in this form, the overdetermined nature of the system is more manifest: without the imposition of extrinsic constraints, one would need both
\delta g|_{\partial M}
and
\nabla_{n}\delta g|_{\partial M}
to vanish as they are independent. In this form, the natural ordered pair of the Dirichlet data
\bar{g}
and the Neumann data
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}-3H\bar{g}
is less evident. However, one can still infer that, if
\delta g|_{\partial M}
is to be free, then the second fundamental form must vanish.

Consider now the variational form of the Fefferman–Graham flat
problem, as discussed in Proposition
3.2
,
except we now wish to treat a manifold with boundary. From
Equation (
3.4
), it is clear that the boundary
terms would depend non-trivially on both the variation of
g
along
the boundary
as well as
normal derivatives of that
variation. On the other hand the equation
\mathcal{O}^{(d)}=0
is
elliptic of order
d
, up to the need for gauge fixing due to
diffeomorphism invariance and conformal invariance

[
1
]
.
Thus one should not need to specify more than
d/2
sections of
\odot^{2}T^{*}\partial M
.
Interestingly, when
d=4
, for the Fefferman–Graham flat
problem (i.e. the
Bach flat problem
), we see that indeed only
two sections of
\odot^{2}T^{*}\partial M
must be fixed to force the
boundary terms to vanish. On the other hand, for all even

,
the system appears to become “overdetermined”. This suggests that one
should modify the energy functional by a boundary term in the spirit of
the Gibbons–Hawking–York boundary term. To see how this happens, we
first prove the following technical lemma:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma\hookrightarrow(M,g)
be a smooth hypersurface embedding. Then, with respect to metric variations, the trace-free second fundamental form of
\Sigma

\delta\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{R}\mathring{h}_{ab}+2n_{(a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}h_{nn}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}\,.

Proof.

To compute the variation of the trace-free fundamental form, we use the identity

{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{c}\frac{n_{d}}{\sqrt{|n|^{2}_{g}}}\,.

Note that for a given metric
g_{0}
, we may always choose for
\Sigma
a defining function

such that

, with
|n|^{2}_{g}=1
(see e.g.
[

as fixed (such that

\delta(g^{-1}(ds,ds))\not\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
in general, as we allow the metric to change.

Now, we must find the variations of
\bar{g}_{a}^{c}
,
\nabla
, and
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|n|^{2}_{g}}}
(viewed as quantities along
\Sigma
) with respect to

As
\sqrt{g_{0}^{-1}(n,n)}=1

\delta\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|n|^{2}_{g}}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta g^{-1})(n,n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}\tfrac{1}{2}h_{nn}\,.

Next, note that

\displaystyle\delta\bar{g}_{a}^{b}

\displaystyle=\delta g_{a}^{b}-\delta(\tfrac{1}{|n|^{2}_{g}}n_{a}g^{bc}n_{c})

\displaystyle=-h_{nn}n_{a}n^{b}+n_{a}h^{b}_{n}\,.

Finally, we use variation of the connection, which is a standard result:

(\delta\nabla)_{a}v_{b}=-C^{c}_{ab}v_{c}\,,

where

(4.2)

\displaystyle C^{a}_{bc}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{ad}(\nabla_{b}h_{dc}+\nabla_{c}h_{bc}-\nabla_{d}h_{bc})\,.

Putting all this together and using that
|n|^{2}_{g_{0}}=1
, we have that

\displaystyle\delta{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle(-h_{nn}n_{a}n^{c}+n_{a}h_{n}^{c})\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{c}n_{d}+\bar{g}_{a}^{c}(-h_{nn}n_{b}n^{d}+n_{b}h_{n}^{d})\nabla_{c}n_{d}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}n^{e}(\nabla_{c}h_{de}+\nabla_{b}h_{de}-\nabla_{e}h_{dc})+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{c}(h_{nn}n_{d})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle n_{a}h_{n}^{c}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{cb}+n_{b}h_{n}^{c}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ca}-\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}n^{e}(\nabla_{c}h_{de}+\nabla_{d}h_{ce}-\nabla_{e}h_{cd})+\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}h_{nn}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\bar{g}_{(a}^{d}\nabla_{b)}^{\top}(h_{dn}^{\top}+n_{d}h_{nn})+{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{n}h_{cd}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}h_{nn}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\bar{g}_{(a}^{d}\nabla_{b)}^{\top}h_{dn}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}h_{nn}+{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{n}h_{cd}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}h_{b)n}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}h_{nn}+{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{n}h_{cd}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}h_{nn}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}h_{b)n}^{\top}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{g}_{a}^{c}\bar{g}_{b}^{d}\nabla_{n}\mathring{h}_{cd}

\displaystyle+2Hn_{(a}h_{b)n}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}H\bar{g}_{ab}h_{nn}+Hh^{\top}_{ab}+\tfrac{1}{2d}\bar{g}_{ab}\nabla_{n}h_{c}^{c}\,.

In the second equality, we have used the fact that
\nabla n={\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
and that
n\cdot{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}=0
. In the third equality, we have expressed covariant derivatives in terms of the tangential and normal parts, i.e.
\nabla=\nabla^{\top}+n\nabla_{n}
, and then expressed
h_{na}
in terms of its projections along
\Sigma
. In the fourth equality, we have combined terms and simplified, using that
\nabla n={\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
as before. In the fifth equality, we have used the Gauss formula, namely that for any
v\in\Gamma(T^{*}\Sigma)
, we have that
\nabla_{a}^{\top}v_{b}=\bar{\nabla}_{a}v_{b}-n_{b}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{a}^{c}v_{c}
, where
\nabla_{a}^{\top}v_{b}
is defined using any extension of
v
to
T^{*}M
. In the final equality, we have expressed
\nabla_{n}h
in terms of
\nabla_{n}\mathring{h}
and all instances of
{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
in terms of
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}
.

Now, to compute the variation of the trace-free second fundamental form, recall that

\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}={\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}-\tfrac{1}{d-1}\bar{g}_{ab}\bar{g}^{cd}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{cd}\,.

To compute its variation, note that
\bar{g}=g-\tfrac{1}{|n|^{2}_{g}}n\otimes n
, so

\delta\bar{g}_{ab}=h_{ab}+\delta(|n|^{2}_{g})n_{a}n_{b}=h_{ab}-n_{a}n_{b}h_{nn}\,.

Because
\bar{g}^{ab}\bar{g}_{bc}=\bar{g}_{c}^{a}
, we also have that
\delta\bar{g}^{ab}=-\bar{g}^{ac}\bar{g}^{bd}h_{cd}
. Putting these together, we have that

\displaystyle\delta\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\delta{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{ab}-\tfrac{1}{d-1}\left[(d-1)(h_{ab}-n_{a}n_{b}h_{nn})H-\bar{g}_{ab}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{cd}h_{cd}+\bar{g}_{ab}\bar{g}^{cd}\delta{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{cd}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle(\delta{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI})_{(ab)_{\circ}}-(h_{ab}-n_{a}n_{b}h_{nn})H+\tfrac{1}{d-1}\bar{g}_{ab}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{cd}h_{cd}\,.

We can now finish the computation:

\displaystyle\delta\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}h_{nn}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}-\tfrac{1}{d-1}\bar{g}_{ab}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{cd}h_{cd}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}h_{b)_{\circ}n}^{\top}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}\mathring{h}_{cd}

\displaystyle+2Hn_{(a}h_{b)n}^{\top}+Hh^{\top}_{ab}-\tfrac{1}{d-1}H\bar{g}_{ab}\bar{g}^{cd}h_{cd}-Hh_{ab}+n_{a}n_{b}Hh_{nn}+\tfrac{1}{d-1}\bar{g}_{ab}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}^{cd}h_{cd}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}h_{nn}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}h_{b)_{\circ}n}^{\top}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}\mathring{h}_{cd}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2n_{(a}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{b)}h_{nc}^{\top}-\tfrac{1}{2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}h_{nn}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{(a}^{c}h^{\top}_{b)c}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\mathring{\top}}^{(1)}\mathring{h}_{cd}\,.

In the third equality above, we have used Equation (
4.4
). So the lemma follows.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(M^{4},g)
be a compact manifold with boundary
\partial M
. Then, the functional variation of
S=\frac{1}{4}\int_{M}|W|^{2}\,dV
with respect to metric variations is given by

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle-\int_{M}B^{ab}h_{ab}dV_{g}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial M}\big[\tfrac{4}{3}W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{bc}\mathring{h}_{nn}-({\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{ab}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{c}^{(a}W_{n}{}^{b)_{\circ}c}{}_{n})\mathring{h}_{ab}^{\mathring{\top}}-W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\delta^{(1)}_{\mathring{\top}}\mathring{h}_{bc}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

where
\delta g=h
.

Proof.

In order to use more standard variation formulas, we first translate the action into an integral of Riemann tensors and its traces:

S=\int_{M}\left(\tfrac{1}{4}R_{abcd}R^{abcd}-\tfrac{1}{2}Ric_{ab}Ric^{ab}+\tfrac{1}{12}Sc^{2}\right)dV\,.

Now, defining
h=\delta g
and using the (standard result for the) variation of the covariant derivative
(\delta\nabla)_{a}v_{b}=-\gamma^{c}_{ab}v_{c}
, where
\gamma
is defined according to

(4.3)

\displaystyle\gamma^{a}_{bc}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{ad}(\nabla_{b}h_{dc}+\nabla_{c}h_{bd}-\nabla_{d}h_{bc})\,,

we may compute the variation of the Riemann tensor (see
[
47
, Section 7.5]
). To do so, let
v\in\Gamma(T^{*}M)
.

\displaystyle(\delta R_{abc}{}^{d})v_{d}=

\displaystyle\delta(\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}v_{c}-\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a}v_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta\nabla_{a})\nabla_{b}v_{c}+\nabla_{a}(\delta\nabla_{b})v_{c}-(\delta\nabla_{b})\nabla_{a}v_{c}-\nabla_{b}(\delta\nabla_{a})v_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma^{d}_{ab}\nabla_{d}v_{c}-\gamma^{d}_{ac}\nabla_{b}v_{d}-\nabla_{a}(\gamma^{d}_{bc}v_{d})+\gamma^{d}_{ba}\nabla_{d}v_{c}+\gamma^{d}_{bc}\nabla_{a}v_{d}+\nabla_{b}(\gamma^{d}_{ac}v_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma^{d}_{ac}\nabla_{b}v_{d}-\nabla_{a}(\gamma^{d}_{bc}v_{d})+\gamma^{d}_{bc}\nabla_{a}v_{d}+\nabla_{b}(\gamma^{d}_{ac}v_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(\nabla_{b}\gamma^{d}_{ac})-(\nabla_{a}\gamma^{d}_{bc})\right]v_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}g^{de}\left[\nabla_{b}(\nabla_{a}h_{ce}+\nabla_{c}h_{ae}-\nabla_{e}h_{ac})-\nabla_{a}(\nabla_{b}h_{ce}+\nabla_{c}h_{be}-\nabla_{e}h_{bc})\right]v_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}g^{de}(\nabla_{b}\nabla_{c}h_{ae}-\nabla_{b}\nabla_{e}h_{ac}-\nabla_{a}\nabla_{c}h_{be}+\nabla_{a}\nabla_{e}h_{bc})v_{d}

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}g^{de}(R_{bac}{}^{f}h_{fe}+R_{bae}{}^{f}h_{cf})v_{d}\,.

So, it follows that

\delta R_{abcd}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla_{b}\nabla_{c}h_{ad}-\nabla_{b}\nabla_{d}h_{ac}-\nabla_{a}\nabla_{c}h_{bd}+\nabla_{a}\nabla_{d}h_{bc})+\tfrac{1}{2}R_{abc}{}^{e}h_{ed}-\tfrac{1}{2}R_{abd}{}^{e}h_{ce}\,.

Using this formula and its traces, we come to the variation formula

\delta S=\int_{M}\left(W^{cabd}\nabla_{a}\nabla_{b}h_{cd}-\tfrac{1}{2}W^{acde}W^{b}{}_{cde}h_{ab}+\tfrac{1}{8}|W|^{2}h^{a}_{a}-W^{abcd}P_{bd}h_{ac}\right)dV_{g}\,.

Integrating by parts to isolate
h_{cd}
and applying the divergence theorem (noting that
n
is inward pointing), we have that

\delta S=-\int_{M}B^{ab}h_{ab}dV_{g}+\mathcal{B}\,,

where

\mathcal{B}:=-\int_{\partial M}\left(n^{c}C_{cab}h^{ab}-n_{a}W^{abcd}\nabla_{c}h_{bd}\right)dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

Note that the integrand of
\mathcal{B}
is pointwise conformally-invariant.

We can now handle the boundary integral
\mathcal{B}
. First, rewrite
\nabla_{c}h_{bd}=(\nabla_{c}^{\top}+n_{c}\nabla_{n})h_{bd}
and then express

h_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}h_{ab}^{\top}+n_{a}h_{nb}^{\top}+n_{b}h_{na}^{\top}+n_{a}n_{b}h_{nn}\,,

yielding

\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\left[-n^{c}C_{cab}h^{ab}+n_{a}W^{abcd}\nabla_{c}^{\top}(h_{bd}^{\top}+2n_{(b}h_{d)n}^{\top}+n_{b}n_{d}h_{nn})+n_{a}W^{abcd}n_{c}\nabla_{n}h_{bd}\right]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

Using the fact that
\nabla^{\top}n={\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\big[

\displaystyle-n^{c}C_{cab}h^{ab}+n_{a}W^{abcd}\nabla_{c}^{\top}h_{bd}^{\top}+2n_{a}W^{abcd}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{c(b}h_{d)n}^{\top}

\displaystyle+n_{a}n_{d}W^{abcd}\nabla_{c}^{\top}h_{bn}^{\top}+n_{a}n_{d}W^{abcd}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{bc}h_{nn}+n_{a}W_{abcd}n_{c}\nabla_{n}h_{bd}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

Now using the Gauss formula

\nabla_{a}^{\top}v_{b}=\bar{\nabla}_{a}v_{b}-n_{b}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{a}^{c}v_{c}\,,

where
v\in\Gamma(T^{*}\Sigma)
and
\nabla^{\top}v
is defined using any extension of
v
to
T^{*}M
, we have that we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\big[

\displaystyle-n^{c}C_{cab}h^{ab}+n_{a}W^{abcd}(\bar{\nabla}_{c}h_{bd}^{\top}-n_{d}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{c}^{e}h_{be}^{\top})+2n_{a}W^{abcd}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{c(b}h_{d)n}^{\top}

\displaystyle+W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\bar{\nabla}_{b}h_{cn}^{\top}+W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{bc}h_{nn}-W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\nabla_{n}h_{bc}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

[
16
]
for

(4.4)

\displaystyle\delta^{(1)}_{\mathring{\top}}u_{ab}:=\mathring{\top}[\nabla_{n}u_{ab}-2\bar{\nabla}_{(a}u_{nb)_{\circ}}^{\top}]\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\big[

\displaystyle-n^{c}C_{cab}h^{ab}+n_{a}W^{abcd}(\bar{\nabla}_{c}h_{bd}^{\top}-n_{d}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{c}^{e}h_{be}^{\top})+2n_{a}W^{abcd}{\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI}_{c(b}h_{d)n}^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\big[

{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}:=C_{n(ab)}^{\top}+HW_{nabn}-\bar{\nabla}^{c}W_{c(ab)n}^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\int_{\partial M}\big[

\displaystyle-{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}h^{ab}+C^{a}{}_{nn}h_{na}^{\top}-W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{c}^{e}h_{be}^{\top}+n_{a}W^{abcd}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{cb}h_{dn}^{\top}

\displaystyle C_{ann}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{d-3}n^{b}n^{c}\nabla^{d}W_{dcab}

Remark 4.3
.

Observe that a natural variant of the fourth conformal fundamental form in
d=4
, as suggested by the variational problem in Proposition
4.2
, is

{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{(a}W_{nb)_{\circ}cn}=C_{n(ab)}^{\top}+HW_{nabn}-\bar{\nabla}^{c}W_{c(ab)n}^{\top}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{(a}W_{|n|b)_{\circ}cn}\,.

Now as a consequence of Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
(in particular Equation
4.5
), we may re-express the variation of the energy functional
\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{M}|W|^{2}dV
on a 4-manifold with boundary as

\delta S=-\int_{M}B^{ab}h_{ab}dV_{g}\\
+\int_{\partial M}\Big(-[{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{ab}-\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{c}^{(a}W_{n}{}^{b)c}{}_{n}]h_{ab}-2W_{n}{}^{ab}{}_{n}\delta\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}\Big)dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

In this form, two natural ordered pairs arise from the Bach flat problem:

(\bar{g}_{ab},-{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{(a}W_{|n|b)cn})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab},-2W_{nabn})\,.

More generally than Lemma
4.1
, it is easy to see (from the fact that, in coordinates adapted to the hypersurface embedding,
k
th conformal fundamental forms have leading term
\mathring{\top}\partial_{s}^{k-1}g
) that, at leading order,

\delta\,\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{k}}}}_{ab}\propto\delta_{\mathring{\top}}^{(k-1)}\mathring{h}_{ab}+\text{ lower order terms.}

Such a relation allows for two observations. First, it implies a canonical pairing of sections of
\odot^{2}T^{*}\Sigma
between those with transverse order
m
and
d-m-1
, expressible in terms of conformal fundamental forms and the induced metric of the form
\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{m+1}}}}\cdot\delta(\mathring{\underline{\overline{\rm{d-m}}}})
. Second, in
d\geqslant 6
, we may express the boundary contribution to the variation of the Fefferman–Graham action exhibited in Equation (
3.4
) in terms of conformal fundamental forms, their variations, and
I
. As such, it is straightforward to add conformally-invariant integrals of the form found in Display (
1.3
) to the Fefferman–Graham action to remove the problem of overdeterminedness, when necessary. Such integrals play the same role as the Gibbons-Hawking-York boundary term does.

When such terms are added, the variational problems become (at least) not overdetermined. In that case, we may view the pairings as between Dirichlet data and Neumann data. Because Fefferman–Graham flat problems are elliptic after gauge-fixing, it is sufficient to specify
just
the Dirichlet data to solve the problem globally (or alternatively, one may specify just the Neumann data). At least in the case of the Bach-flat problem where the splitting of the boundary data is clear from the variational problem, when there exists a unique solution the Bach-flat problem on a given 4-manifold with boundary, there exists a well-defined Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, sending

(4.6)

\displaystyle(\bar{g}_{ab},\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab})\mapsto(-2W_{nabn},-{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{c}_{(a}W_{|n|b)cn})\,.

When such a map exists, it is interesting to consider its specializations. This is discussed in Section
5
.

5.
Fefferman–Graham Flat Obstructions to Poincaré–Einstein

The uniqueness of Bach-flat manifolds with a given
set of boundary data is not fully understood. It is straightforward to show, and well known,
that all self-dual, anti-self-dual and Einstein manifolds are Bach-flat.
We are interested here in the link to Poincaré-Einstein metrics. Maldacena
showed
[
42
]
that at the level of small perturbations, when

\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}=0
, solutions to the linearized Bach-flat boundary problem are
also solutions to the linearized Poincaré–Einstein
equation. In
[
2
]
, the fully non-linear equations were
considered; they found that an asymptotically hyperbolic metric
satisfies
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}={\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0=\bar{\nabla}\cdot{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
(exactly in agreement with Corollary
3.9
), has a
vanishing obstruction density, is a formal solution to the Bach-flat
boundary-value problem, and satisfies a fourth constraint not relevant
for our discussion if and only if that asymptotically hyperbolic
metric is a formal solution to the Poincaré–Einstein equation.

Inspired by these results, we prove the stronger result that a Bach-flat manifold with umbilic boundary is sufficient to guarantee that the manifold’s singular Yamabe metric is a formal solution to the Poincaré–Einstein equation.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M^{4},\bm{c})
be a smooth compact Bach-flat conformal manifold with umbilic boundary. Then the singular Yamabe metric of Proposition
2.3
is smooth and, formally to all orders, Poincaré–Einstein.

Proof.

Let
(M^{d},\bm{c})
be a Bach-flat conformal manifold with an umbilic boundary. To prove the theorem, we begin by proving that
(M^{d},\bm{c})
contains an asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class of its interior. To do this, we rely on Corollary
3.9
: that the existence of an asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class of the interior is equivalent to the conditions that
{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}=0
and that
\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0
.

We begin by considering the Weyl-squared action,
S=\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{M}|W|^{2}dV_{g}
. From diffeomorphism invariance, the variation of this action
\delta S
with respect to a variation of the form
\delta g_{ab}=2\nabla_{(a}k_{b)}
vanishes.
Recall from Proposition
4.2
that we have an identity for the variation of this action:

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle-\int_{M}B^{ab}h_{ab}dV_{g}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial M}\big[\tfrac{4}{3}W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{bc}\mathring{h}_{nn}-({\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{ab}+\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{c}^{(a}W_{n}{}^{b)_{\circ}c}{}_{n})\mathring{h}_{ab}^{\mathring{\top}}-W_{n}{}^{bc}{}_{n}\delta_{\mathring{\top}}^{(1)}\mathring{h}_{bc}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}\,.

We now specialize to the case where our original manifold is Bach-flat, the boundary is umbilic, and the variation arises from the pullback of an infinitesimal diffeomorphism. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial M}\big[-{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{ab}(\nabla_{(a}k_{b)_{\circ}})^{\mathring{\top}}-W_{n}{}^{ab}{}_{n}\delta_{\mathring{\top}}^{(1)}(\nabla_{(a}k_{b)_{\circ}})\big]dV_{\bar{g}}=0\,.

Now observe that

\displaystyle\delta_{R}(\nabla_{(a}k_{b)_{\circ}})=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[\nabla_{n}\nabla_{(a}k_{b)_{\circ}}-2\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(\bar{g}_{b)}^{c}n^{d}\nabla_{(c}k_{d)})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[\nabla^{\top}_{(a}(\nabla_{n}k_{b)_{\circ}})^{\top}+R_{n(ab)_{\circ}d}k^{d}-\bar{g}^{c}_{(a}H\nabla_{|c|}k_{b)_{\circ}}-2\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(\bar{g}_{b)}^{c}n^{d}\nabla_{(c}k_{d)})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[\nabla^{\top}_{(a}(\nabla_{n}k_{b)_{\circ}})^{\top}+R_{n(ab)_{\circ}d}k^{d}-H\nabla_{(a}^{\top}k_{b)_{\circ}}-2\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(\bar{g}_{b)}^{c}n^{d}\nabla_{(c}^{\top}k_{d)})

\displaystyle-2\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(\bar{g}_{b)}^{c}n^{d}n_{(c}\nabla_{n}k_{d)})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[\nabla^{\top}_{(a}(\nabla_{n}k_{b)_{\circ}})^{\top}+R_{n(ab)_{\circ}d}k^{d}-H\bar{\nabla}_{(a}k^{\top}_{b)_{\circ}}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(n^{d}\nabla_{b)}^{\top}k_{d})-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(\bar{g}_{b)}^{c}\nabla_{n}k_{c})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[R_{n(ab)_{\circ}d}k^{d}-H\bar{\nabla}_{(a}k^{\top}_{b)_{\circ}}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}(n^{d}\nabla_{b)}^{\top}k_{d})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[R_{n(ab)_{\circ}d}k^{d}-H\bar{\nabla}_{(a}k^{\top}_{b)_{\circ}}-\bar{\nabla}_{(a}\bar{\nabla}_{b)}k_{n}+\bar{\nabla}_{(a}Hk^{\top}_{b)}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}[W_{n(ab)n}k_{n}-(\mathring{\bar{P}}_{ab}+{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}_{ab})k_{n}-\bar{\nabla}_{a}\bar{\nabla}_{b}k_{n}]\,.

In the last line above, we used the trace-free part of the Gauss–Fialkow Equation (
1.6
) and the trace-free part of the Codazzi–Mainardi Equation (
1.7
).
Furthermore, when
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}=0
and
d=4
, from the trace-free part of the Gauss–Fialkow Equation (
1.6
) we have that
W_{nabn}=-{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}_{ab}
, so we can integrate by parts to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial M}\big[(\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{b})k^{\top}_{b}-2|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}k_{n}-\mathring{\bar{P}}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}k_{n}-(\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt})k_{n}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}=0\,.

When
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}=0
, we can compute:

\displaystyle B_{nn}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle n^{b}n^{c}\nabla^{\top\,a}C_{abc}+|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}+\bar{P}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-n^{b}\nabla^{\top\,a}C_{nab}+|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}+\bar{P}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-n^{b}\nabla^{\top\,a}C_{nab}^{\top}+\nabla^{\top\,a}C_{ann}+|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}+\bar{P}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\nabla}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}+|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}+\bar{P}\cdot{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}\,,

where the first equality follows from the fact that
B_{ab}=\nabla^{c}C_{cab}+P^{cd}W_{adbc}
and the last equality follows from the identity

C_{ann}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}-\tfrac{1}{d-2}\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}_{ab}\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}^{b}+\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}_{a}-\tfrac{1}{2(d-1)}\bar{\nabla}_{a}|\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}|^{2}\,.

Then, we have that

B_{nn}-|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}-\mathring{\bar{P}}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}-\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0\,,

and so in the Bach-flat case, the integral identity in question reduces to

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial M}\big[(\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}^{b})k^{\top}_{b}-|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}k_{n}\big]dV_{\bar{g}}=0\,.

Recall that the above identity holds for any
k\in\Gamma(TM)
, and so in particular we may choose
k|_{\Sigma}=n
; this implies that

\int_{\partial M}|{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}|^{2}dV_{\bar{g}}=0\,.

So it follows that
{\hskip 1.70717pt}\mathring{\!{F}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}=0
. Alternatively, we may choose
k|_{\Sigma}\in\Gamma(T\Sigma)
arbitrarily in Equation (
5.1
), finding that
\bar{\nabla}\hskip-2.13394pt\cdot\hskip-2.13394pt{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0
. This establishes that the conformal class metric admits an asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein metric in the interior.

Observe that because
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}={\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0
, one may easily establish (according to
[
25
]
) that the obstruction density
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma}
of Proposition
2.1
vanishes, and hence the singular Yamabe density is smooth, following from Proposition
2.3
, and hence so is the singular Yamabe metric. Thus, from Corollary
2.5
, we have that there exists a unique almost Einstein tensor
E_{ab}
determined by the boundary embedding and the conformal structure. Note that the transverse jets of
E
on
\Sigma
capture the higher conformal fundamental forms
[
16
]
.

Now, to show that
(M^{d},\bm{c})
admits a (formal to all orders) Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class of its interior, it is sufficient to show that the almost Einstein tensor
E_{ab}
above vanishes to all orders
[
28
]
—that is, that
\nabla_{n}^{k}E_{ab}|_{\Sigma}=0
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
.
As the formal vanishing of
E_{ab}
is a conformally-invariant condition, it is sufficient to check that
\nabla_{n}^{k}E_{ab}|_{\Sigma}=0
in some choice of metric representative
g\in\bm{c}
. To fix the metric
g
, we pick any metric
\bar{g}\in\bar{\bm{c}}
and then extend it to
g\in\bm{c}
in such a way (according to
[
31
, Proposition 7.15]
) that each
T
-curvature vanishes—that is, the mean curvature
H^{g}
vanishes, and all higher transverse order analogs also vanish.

Having established already that
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}={\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0
, we already have that
\nabla_{n}^{k}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
for
k=0,1
. By explicit calculation (see
[
16
, Proof of Corollary 4.9]
), we see that
\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{2}E_{ab}|_{\Sigma}
only involves at most one normal derivative of the metric, and similarly
n^{a}\nabla_{n}^{2}E_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
, as may be seen from
[
16
, Proof of Lemma 4.11]
. As the
T
-curvatures vanish, it follows from the classification result of
[
14
]
(namely, that Riemannian invariants are expressible in terms of intrinsic curvatures, conformal fundamental forms, and
T
-curvatures) that
\nabla_{n}^{2}E|_{\Sigma}
is expressible in terms of (intrinsic) curvatures of
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
. However,
\nabla_{n}^{2}E|_{\Sigma}
has weight
-1
, and there exist no non-vanishing sections expressible in terms of curvatures of
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
with odd weight. Thus,
\nabla_{n}^{2}E|_{\Sigma}=0
.

Next, consider the case where
k=3
.
Because
\sigma
solves the singular Yamabe problem exactly, we may apply the Thomas-
D
operator to the equation
\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}|D\sigma|^{2}=1
to obtain (via the Leibniz failure lemma of
[
33
]
)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{d^{2}}(D^{B}\sigma)(D_{A}D_{B}\sigma)

\displaystyle=X_{A}|E|^{2}\,.

Evaluating the
X
slot of the above equation and noting that we have already established that
E=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})
, we therefore have that

\sigma(\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot E+(d-1)P\cdot E)=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{6})\,,

or

\sigma\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot E=-(d-1)\sigma P\cdot E+\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{6})=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{4})\,.

Hence,

\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot E=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{3})\,.

As
\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot E=n^{a}n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{2}E_{ab}+\text{ltots}
, and the lower transverse order terms vanish to higher order in
\sigma
, it follows that

n^{a}n^{b}\nabla_{n}^{3}E_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0\,.

Next, we consider the irreducible component
\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{3}E
. Observe from
[
16
, Lemma 4.7]
, the operator
\delta_{4,1}^{(3)}:\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}M[1])\rightarrow\Gamma(\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}\Sigma[-2])
is well-defined and has leading (transverse) symbol
\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{3}
. Thus, from the classification offered in
[
14
]
, it follows that

\delta_{4,1}^{(3)}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}{\mathring{{\bf\rm V}}}{}+\text{curvatures invariants of }(\Sigma,\bar{g})+\text{terms dependent on }{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}\,.

But note that
{\mathring{{\bf\rm V}}}{}
vanishes on 4-dimensional Bach-flat manifolds, and there are no non-vanishing sections expressible in terms of curvatures of
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
of
\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}\Sigma[-2]
on a 3-manifold. Finally, as
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
has weight
-1
, there are no (non-vanishing) sections of
\odot^{2}_{\circ}T^{*}\Sigma[-2]
that are constructible from
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
and solely intrinsic geometric quantities—so this term may not contribute. But then it follows that

0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{3}E+\operatorname{ltots}(E)\,,

where
\operatorname{ltots}
are lower transverse order differential operators, which vanish by the above computations, hence
\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{3}E=0
.

Finally we consider the component
(n^{a}\nabla_{n}^{3}E_{ab})^{\top}
. Again from the classification result of
[
14
]
, the tensor in question must be expressible in terms of curvatures intrinsic to
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
and
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
(as all other quantities appearing in the classification result vanish). It is easy to establish that the only natural sections of
T^{*}\Sigma[-3]
that depend on
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
are
\bar{\nabla}\cdot{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
and
n^{a}{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}_{ab}
. However, both of these invariants vanish—the former by the above proof of the manifold admitting an asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein metric in its interior and the latter by definition. Thus,
(n^{a}\nabla_{n}^{3}E_{ab})^{\top}
cannot depend on
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
, and hence is expressible entirely in terms of curvature invariants of
(\Sigma,\bar{g})
. But no such non-vanishing Riemannian invariants exist, and so it follows that
(n^{a}\nabla_{n}^{3}E_{ab})^{\top}=0
.

From the considerations above, we have thus established that, in the metric representative where
T_{!}=T_{2}=T_{3}=T_{4}=0
, we have that
\nabla_{n}^{3}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
.

It now remains to show that
\nabla_{n}^{k}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
for every
k\geqslant 4
. We do so via induction using
k=3
as the base case. Suppose that
\nabla_{n}^{\ell}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
for all
\ell<k
. We would now like to examine
\nabla_{n}^{k}E|_{\Sigma}
. From
[
16
, Proof of Lemma 4.11]
, we have that since
k\neq d-1=3
,

n^{a}\nabla_{n}^{k}E_{ab}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0\,.

Thus we must only examine
\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k}E|_{\Sigma}
. In what follows, we will implicitly set to zero those terms that must vanish by the induction, and
I^{A}:=\tfrac{1}{d}D^{A}\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the unique singular Yamabe density determined above. For a choice of metric representative
g\in\bm{c}
such that
\sigma=[g;s]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k}Z^{C}_{(a}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}I_{C}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-1}\left[(\nabla_{n}Z_{(a}^{C})\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}I_{C}+Z_{(a}^{C}\nabla_{n}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}I_{C}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-1}\left[Z_{(a}^{C}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}\nabla_{n}I_{C}+Z^{C}_{(a}\Omega_{nb)_{\circ}C}{}^{D}I_{D}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-1}\left[\nabla_{(a}\nabla_{n}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}s+Z_{(a}^{C}\Omega_{nb)_{\circ}C}{}^{D}I_{D}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-1}\left[-2\nabla_{(a}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}s\rho+Z_{(a}^{C}\Omega_{nb)_{\circ}C}{}^{D}I_{D}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-1}\left[Z_{(a}^{C}\Omega_{b)_{\circ}nC}{}^{D}I_{D}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-2}\nabla^{d}\left[Z_{(a}^{C}\Omega_{b)_{\circ}dC}{}^{D}I_{D}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathring{\top}\nabla_{n}^{k-2}\Omega_{d(a|C}{}^{D}\nabla^{d}Z_{b)_{\circ}}^{C}I_{D}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 0\,.

The first identity follows from the fact that
Z^{C}_{(a}\nabla_{b)_{\circ}}I_{C}=E_{ab}

[
33
]
. The third identity follows by noting that
Z^{C}\nabla_{n}I_{C}
has lower transverse order than
k+1
and thus vanishes by the induction hypothesis. The fourth identity follows covariant derivatives of
Z
and the induction hypothesis, and the fifth identity also follows from the induction hypothesis and the observation
[

that
\nabla_{n}s=1-2s\rho
, where
\rho=-\tfrac{1}{d}(\Delta s+Js)

must be differentiated, yielding lower order terms which vanish by the induction hypothesis. The seventh identity follows from the identity

, and the eigth identity follows from a critical result which holds on a 4-dimensional Bach-flat manifold

\nabla\cdot\Omega=0

\nabla_{n}^{k}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{=}}0
for all

Remark 5.2
.

Observe that, in the above proof, we could have terminated our efforts once we had established that the singular Yamabe metric is asymptotically Poincaré–Einstein and instead invoked the result of
[
2
]
. To do so would have required establishing their higher-order constraint alluded to above.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that Theorem
5.1
is equivalent to the statement that, when there exists a unique solution to the boundary-value Bach-flat problem on a given 4-manifold with umbilic boundary, its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map in Display (
4.6
) becomes

(\bar{g}_{ab},0)\mapsto(0,-\operatorname{DN}^{(4)})\,,

where
\operatorname{DN}^{(4)}
is the image of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the Poincaré–Einstein problem described in
[
15
]
.

As a corollary, additional hypotheses ensure that the formal Poincaré–Einstein structure fixed by the above theorem is in fact a genuine Poincaré–Einstein structure in a collar neighborhood of the boundary.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(M^{4},\bm{c})
be an analytic compact Bach-flat conformal manifold with umbilic boundary
\Sigma
. Then there exists a Poincaré–Einstein metric in the conformal class of the interior of a collar neighborhood of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

As
(M^{4},\bm{c})
has an umbilic boundary
\Sigma
and is Bach-flat, it follows from Theorem
5.1
that the almost Einstein tensor vanishes formally to all orders, i.e.
E=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{\infty})
. As the conformal manifold is analytic, it follows that the induced conformal class of metrics on the boundary is also analytic, as is any extrinsic data (including the fourth fundamental form
{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
). From
[
6
]
, it follows that when the boundary data (the induced metric and the image of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
g^{(3)}
) is analytic, there exists a unique Poincaré–Einstein metric in the interior of a collar neighborhood of the boundary. But the image of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\operatorname{DN}^{(4)}
is
-{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}
, which is analytic. The corollary follows.

∎

It is well-known and easily verified (see e.g.
[
22
]
) that on an Einstein 4-manifold, the Bach tensor vanishes everywhere. However, a simple counting shows that for most Bach-flat conformal 4-manifolds manifolds with boundary, these manifolds are not Poincaré–Einstein.
Having established Theorem
5.1
, we now understand why that is the case: a Bach-flat manifold with boundary need only have an umbilic boundary to ensure that it is (at least asymptotically) Poincaré–Einstein. While this is still quite a strong condition, it is far weaker than might have been observed naively, namely that
\hskip 1.13809pt\mathring{\rm{I\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.0pt}={\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptI\hskip-0.56905ptI}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0

[
16
]
and that
\bar{\nabla}\cdot{\mathring{{\bf\rm I\hskip-0.56905ptV}}{\hskip 0.56905pt}}{}=0
. Nonetheless, we can provide a method of constructing many such manifolds.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
(M^{4},g)
be a closed Einstein manifold and let
\iota:\Sigma\hookrightarrow M
be an embedded separating hypersurface such that
M=M_{+}\sqcup\Sigma\sqcup M_{-}
. Then, there exists a unique metric
g_{+}
in the conformal class of
g|_{M_{+}}
