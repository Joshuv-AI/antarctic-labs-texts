Cancer burden among adults aged 65 years and older in Asia, 1990–2023: a systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023
Authors: Tianjiao Zhou, Enhui Zhou, Wen Lu, Jianyin Zou, Jingyu Zhang, Chenyang Li, Hangdong Shen, Chen Xu, Ying Shen, Jianxiang Li, Huaming Zhu, Chuan Xu, Hongliang Yi, Shankai Yin, Weijun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults bear a substantial and increasing share of the cancer burden in Asia, yet its temporal trends, geographical heterogeneity, and inequalities remain insufficiently characterised. Using estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, we assessed cancer incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) among adults aged 65 years and older across 34 Asian countries from 1990 to 2023, and examined demographic and epidemiological drivers, variations by location, sociodemographic index, age, and sex, with projections to 2050. In 2023, this population experienced 4.59 million new cancer cases, 3.27 million deaths, and 57.49 million DALYs. East Asia had the highest burden, whereas South Asia had the lowest rates but the fastest increases in incidence and mortality. Lung cancer remained the leading cancer, and digestive system cancers accounted for the greatest system-level burden. Population growth and population aging were the main contributors to rising cases and deaths, while favourable epidemiological changes were observed only in East and Southeast Asia. By 2050, the age-standardised incidence rate is projected to reach 1146.25 per 100,000 in females and 1518.97 in males. These findings call for more equitable and age-responsive cancer prevention and care strategies across Asia.Subject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Cancer remains a major public health challenge worldwide, accounting for a substantial and growing proportion of morbidity, mortality, and disability1. Over the past three decades, global cancer burden has risen steadily in both incident cases and deaths, driven largely by population aging, population growth, and transitions in environmental and lifestyle exposures2,3. Asia, home to more than 4.5 billion people and nearly half of the global population, plays a decisive role in shaping global cancer patterns4. The region encompasses substantial socioeconomic diversity—from high-income settings with advanced health systems to low-income regions facing persistent limitations in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment—resulting in marked differences in cancer spectrum, time trends, and system performance5–7.

Existing evidence shows that cancer profiles in Asia differ considerably from those in other global regions8,9. Substantial heterogeneity also exists within Asia, with variation in cancer type distribution across East, Central, South, and Southeast Asia reflecting differences in demographic structure, health system capacity, and epidemiological transitions6,10,11. Although national or regional studies have examined individual cancer types or selected populations, systematic and methodologically consistent assessments integrating incidence, mortality across Asian countries remain scarce12–14. Variability in data quality and analytic approaches further limits cross-country comparability, leaving the magnitude, trends, and inequalities in cancer burden insufficiently defined15–17. Notably, despite rapid population aging, comprehensive assessments focusing specifically on adults aged 65 years and older —who account for a disproportionately high share of cancer cases and deaths—remain particularly limited18,19.

Nevertheless, cross-subregional heterogeneity in cancer burden among older adults has not been systematically assessed, and sex- and age-specific patterns remain insufficiently quantified across countries20. The influence of sociodemographic development, reflected by the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), on cancer burden and cancer control progress in this population is also unclear. Furthermore, evidence remains limited regarding which Asian countries have achieved sustained reductions in age-standardized rates and which continue to experience increasing trends. Addressing these gaps requires standardized, comparable data across countries and over time.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2023 study provides an opportunity to address these gaps. Using standardized analytic frameworks, comprehensive data synthesis, and consistent definitions of incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), the GBD enables cross-country and temporal comparisons that are not feasible using primary data sources alone21,22. Annual estimates from 1990 to 2023, together with estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), allow for detailed evaluation of temporal patterns at regional, subregional, and national levels. Stratification by sex, age, and SDI offers essential insight into demographic and socioeconomic inequalities in cancer burden among older adults across Asia.

In this study, we provide the comprehensive assessment to date of cancer burden among adults aged ≥65 years in Asia, according to the GBD regions, from 1990 to 2023. We quantify incidences, deaths, and DALYs across 34 Asian countries and territories and estimate temporal trends using EAPCs. We further examine disparities across sex, age subgroups within older adults, and SDI strata and identify vulnerable populations. In addition, we rank the burden of specific cancer types and characterize the distribution of cancer burden across major organ system. By delineating the magnitude and trajectory of cancer burden in this rapidly growing demographic group, our findings aim to inform evidence-based cancer control strategies and support regionally tailored policy interventions.

Results

Cancer burden in adults aged ≥ 65 years

In 2023, adults aged ≥65 years in Asia had 4.59 million incident cancer cases (95% UI 3.43 to 5.93), 3.27 million deaths (2.41 to 4.28), and 57.49 million DALYs (42.63 to 75.32). The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 1,025.20 (764.58 to 1325.45) per 100,000, with higher rates in males (1,319.32 [950.20 to 1,763.23]) than in females (778.56 [534.91 to 1075.30]). The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) were 742.32 (544.76 to 971.63) and 12,600.90 (9329.19 to 16,497.69) per 100,000 population, respectively, with sex-specific estimates provided in Table 1. From 1990 to 2023, the EAPC in ASIR was 0.05 (–0.03 to 0.13), while ASMR and ASDR decreased, with EAPCs of –0.84 (–0.95 to –0.74) and –1.04 (–1.14 to –0.93), respectively (Table 1). Given the disproportionate concentration of cancer burden among older adults, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR in this age group were 582.14%, 685.43%, and 453.33% higher than those in the all ages (Table S3). To further characterize the heterogeneity within older adults, we assessed cancer burden across specific 5-year age groups. In 2023, among older adults in Asia, the highest incidence rate occurred in the 90–94 years age group (1,751.45 [1,206.10 to 2,277.55] per 100,000), whereas the highest mortality and DALY rates were observed among those aged 95 years or older, reaching 2,109.75 (1,393.91 to 2,812.74) and 17,313.21 (11,461.86 to 23,024.26) per 100,000, respectively (Fig. S1). Between 1990 and 2023, the highest EAPCs for incidence, mortality, and DALYs were observed in the 95+ years age group, at 2.42 (2.29 to 2.55), 1.81 (1.67 to 1.96), and 1.80 (1.65 to 1.94), respectively. Furthermore, we assessed geographic heterogeneity across Asian subregions. Discussion

In this analysis across 34 Asian countries and territories, we found that adults aged ≥65 years experienced 4.58 million cancer cases, 3.27 million deaths, and 57.49 million DALYs in 2023—substantially exceeding the absolute burden observed in 1990. Although ASMR (EAPC –0.84) and ASDR (EAPC –1.04) declined overall in Asia, South Asia showed increasing rates (EAPC 0.70 and 0.60, respectively), and the absolute number of deaths continued to rise because of rapid population growth and population aging23,24. Older adults bore a disproportionately higher cancer burden than the all-age population (Table S3). These findings align with WHO warnings on population aging and highlight that older adults have become a principal driver of rising cancer burden in Asia25–27.

Decomposition analysis showed that increases in cancer burden since 1990 were overwhelmingly driven by demographic forces in Asia. Population growth and aging structure accounted for most increases in incidence (206.8% and 13.4%), deaths (187.9% and 18.9%), and DALYs (180.9% and 4.8%), whereas epidemiological effects varied across regions. In subregions, East and Southeast Asia exhibited negative epidemiological effects—indicating reductions in underlying risk or improvements in early detection and treatment—consistent with substantial progress in gastric cancer screening, lung-cancer control, and hepatitis-related liver cancer management in Japan, Korea, and China28–30. In contrast, South Asia showed adverse epidemiological effects (35.2%, 31.4%, and 32.8%, respectively), with all burden indicators rising faster than in other regions (APC 0.82%, 0.87%, and 0.75%, respectively)(Fig. S18). These findings suggest that risk-factor control, early diagnosis, and cancer-system strengthening function as essential “burden modifiers”, and that regionally coordinated cancer control strategies may help mitigate the aging-related rise in burden31,32.

Marked heterogeneity was observed across Asian subregions, consistent with earlier estimates33. Similar to the 2022 GLOBOCAN report20, East Asia had the highest ASIR (1307.83), ASMR (861.49), and ASDR (14,496.97) among older adults—primarily driven by Japan, Korea, and Taiwan (Province of China)—reflecting advanced population aging, comprehensive cancer registries, and wide screening coverage34,35. South Asia, although exhibiting the lowest burden levels, showed the fastest increases across all indicators, driven by rapid aging, rising metabolic risks, and constrained healthcare resources36–38. The sharp rise in mortality and DALY rates at advanced ages, driven by biological vulnerability and multimorbidity39–41, underscores the urgent need for strengthened geriatric oncology, palliative services42 and chronic-care systems in rapidly aging East and Southeast Asia43. Building on these subregional differences, substantial national variations were also observed. Japan recorded the highest ASIR (1,960.72), consistent with high diagnostic intensity, mature registry systems, and long life expectancy44,45. Conversely, Mongolia had the highest ASMR (1,350.44) and ASDR (23,858.02), echoing prior evidence linking high smoking prevalence, heavy alcohol consumption, endemic hepatitis infection, and limited treatment access to adverse cancer outcomes46–48. Georgia showed the fastest rise in ASMR (EAPC 2.03), reflecting gaps in geriatric oncology capacity and limited specialised services37,49,50. These subregional and national disparities highlight the uneven progression of cancer burden across Asia and underscore the urgent need for context-specific cancer-control strategies.

Cancer composition evolved markedly over the past three decades. Lung cancer remained the leading cancer in most years among older adults, reflecting persistent tobacco exposure and air pollution in Asia8. Breast, prostate, and non-melanoma skin cancers increased steadily.

The rise of colorectal cancer to second place among older adults in Asia is consistent with the global increase in late-life colorectal cancer51,52. Meanwhile, declines in gastric and liver cancer in East Asia are likely driven by sustained improvements in Helicobacter pylori control, vaccination, and hepatitis management53–56. Notably, the rapidly rising incidence of neuroblastoma and peripheral nervous cell tumours in older adults (ASIR EAPC 2.90) likely reflects improvements in diagnosis and coding rather than biological increases, but warrants continued surveillance57. Against the backdrop of rapid population aging, these shifts in cancer composition underscore the need for targeted prevention policies, and robust surveillance to anticipate and respond to the evolving cancer landscape.

Despite general improvements, inequalities in cancer burden remain substantial58. The positive correlations between SDI and cancer burden among older adults do not suggest worse outcomes in high-income regions59. These associations are likely influenced by, rather than solely attributable to, deeper population aging, more complete diagnosis, and the larger share of deaths attributed to cancer in long-lived populations29. They should therefore be interpreted as macro-level contextual patterns rather than direct evidence of individual-level etiologic mechanisms. In particular, lower cancer incidence in the oldest age groups in low-SDI settings should not be interpreted as evidence that lower development itself reduces individual cancer risk, because differences in survival selection, competing mortality, and diagnostic capture may alter the composition of the population that survives to very old age60–62. Although absolute inequalities narrowed over three decades (e.g., ASMR SII declined from 981.75 to 530.18; ASDR SII from 16,918.05 to 8,088.65), relative inequalities persisted, which may be related to shortages in oncology workforce63,64,uneven availability of precision diagnostics and radiotherapy65, and limited affordability and access to cancer care in many countries66,67. Future burden estimates further underscore the urgency of action. BAPC projections indicate that, by 2050, the majority of Asian countries will experience continued increases in cancer incidence and mortality among older adults, with particularly rapid growth in women (e.g., ASMR annual increase 3.39% vs 2.07% in men). These findings suggest that the pace of capacity expansion in cancer prevention, screening, early diagnosis, and geriatric oncology may remain far below what is required to offset demographic pressures68. Without substantial investments in these domains, the cancer burden among older adults in Asia is likely to continue its sharp rise over the coming decades.

This study has several strengths, including comprehensive geographic coverage within Asia, consistent methodology, integration of incidence, mortality, and DALYs, stratification by sex, age group, and subregion, and use of advanced methods such as decomposition analysis, frontier analysis, inequality metrics, and BAPC forecasting. However, limitations include substantial variation in data quality across Asian countries, potential influence of temporal changes in cancer coding8, the assumption of historical trend continuation in BAPC forecasting, and potential underestimation of multimorbidity-related disability among older adults69,70. Because the forecasting analyses were conducted using GBD point estimates rather than repeated across posterior draws, uncertainty from the upstream GBD estimation process was not fully propagated into the projection models. Projections for the oldest age groups should be interpreted cautiously, as smaller population denominators at advanced ages may reduce stability and increase susceptibility to random fluctuation. Another limitation is that lower cancer incidence at advanced ages in low-SDI settings may partly reflect selective survival and competing mortality, rather than only lower underlying risk. Moreover, contemporaneous SDI may not fully capture the historical context relevant to cancer etiology in older adults. Current SDI should therefore be interpreted as a marker of current development status rather than a direct proxy for life-course disease risk, and future studies could explore lagged or cohort-specific development indicators. These associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously and not as direct evidence of individual-level causal mechanisms.

In summary, the cancer burden among adults aged ≥65 years in Asia continues to rise, driven mainly by rapid population aging and uneven epidemiological progress across countries. Wide heterogeneity persists: gains in East Asia contrast with accelerating incidence and mortality in South and parts of Southeast Asia, reflecting gaps in prevention, early detection, and access to high-quality cancer care. Persistent inequalities—linked to shortages in oncology workforce, diagnostics, and treatment capacity—further amplify outcome disparities. Strengthening geriatric-focused prevention, screening, treatment, and palliative care—supported by sustained investments in cancer-system capacity—will be essential to confront the predictable rise in cancer burden among older adults in Asia.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted an integrative analysis of cancer burden among adults aged ≥65 years in Asia using data from the GBD 2023 study. GBD 2023 applies standardized data sources, case definitions, and modelling strategies to generate internally consistent estimates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs for 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2023. Cancer burden estimates are derived from vital registration systems, population-based cancer registries, verbal autopsy studies, and other epidemiological data sources, with adjustments for under-reporting, misclassification, and incomplete coverage. Methodological procedures were adopted from the published GBD 2023 analysis8,19 . Following previous GBD analyses71, we included 34 Asian countries and territories (30 from the GBD Asia region and 4 from the High-income Asia Pacific region), grouped into four Asian subregions (Table S1). The country-specific SDI values and SDI level classifications for the corresponding years are detailed in Table S2. The study population comprised adults aged ≥65 years72, grouped into 5-year age categories (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and ≥95 years). For each cancer type, we extracted from GBD 2023 the mean point estimates and corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for incidence, mortality, and DALYs by country or territory, year, sex, and 5-year age group. The analytic dataset was structured at the country–year–sex–age-group level. Relevant age groups were aggregated where required for regional or overall analyses. Downstream statistical analyses were performed using the GBD mean point estimates rather than the full set of posterior draws. The published GBD uncertainty intervals were used to summarize uncertainty in the original burden estimates but were not explicitly propagated into the subsequent regression or projection models.

Ethical compliance

This cross-sectional study used publicly available, anonymized data and did not involve identifiable personal information. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine granted an exemption from ethical approval and informed consent (study #2024-MS-001). Clinical trial registration was not applicable.

Case definition and cause classification

Cancer was defined as all malignant neoplasms, consistent with the GBD cause hierarchy and mapped using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) -9/ICD-10 codes to mutually exclusive categories22. Site-specific cancers were classified into 33 cancer types based on the GBD 2023 cause list, excluding “Other neoplasms,” which comprise benign, in-situ, and behaviour-unspecified tumors. These 33 cancers were grouped into 10 organ-system categories according to ICD-1073. The full ICD-10 mapping and organ-system grouping are provided in the Supplementary Information. Incident cases were defined as newly diagnosed cancer cases within a given year. Cancer deaths were defined as deaths for which the underlying cause was a malignant neoplasm. DALYs were defined as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs), consistent with the standard GBD framework.

Age-standardization for adults aged 65 years and older

To ensure comparability across countries and over time, age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates for adults aged ≥65 years were calculated using the WHO world standard population. Seven age groups were included: 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94, and ≥95 years.

The RW2 prior penalizes second-order differences and thus captures smooth curvature in the age, period, and cohort effects while reducing overfitting. For the age component, the RW2 prior was used as a smoothing constraint across adjacent age groups to obtain biologically plausible age-specific baseline risk profiles and to reduce instability caused by sparse observations in some strata. Temporal trends and future projections were primarily driven by the period and cohort components. To reduce instability in sparse age strata, projections were modeled in 5-year age groups and smoothed using RW2 priors across adjacent age, period, and cohort strata; no additional priors were specified specifically for the 85+ age groups.

Posterior inference was conducted using integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA), which efficiently approximates Bayesian posterior distributions for latent Gaussian models. Posterior predictive distributions for future years (2024–2050) were generated by sampling from the fitted APC structure. This provides both point estimates and fully Bayesian uncertainty intervals for each projected rate. These uncertainty intervals represent posterior predictive uncertainty from the BAPC model conditional on the input GBD point estimates and do not incorporate the full uncertainty from the upstream GBD estimation process.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were based on GBD 2023 estimates produced by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation.
