Exosomes as novel biomarkers in sepsis and sepsis related organ failure
Authors: Yixuan Yuan, Yujie Xiao, Jiazhen Zhao, Lixia Zhang, Mengyang Li, Liang Luo, Yanhui Jia, Kejia Wang, Yuxi Chen, Peng Wang, Yuhang Wang, Jingtao Wei, Kuo Shen, Dahai Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis, a severe and life-threatening condition arising from a dysfunctional host response to infection, presents considerable challenges to the health care system and is characterized by high mortality rates and substantial economic costs. Exosomes have garnered attention as potential diagnostic markers because of their capacity to mirror the pathophysiological milieu of sepsis. This discourse reviews the progression of sepsis classification from Sepsis 1.0 to Sepsis 3.0, highlighting the imperative for sensitive and specific biomarkers to facilitate timely diagnosis and optimize patient outcomes. Existing biomarkers, such as procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP), exhibit certain limitations, thereby prompting the quest for more dependable diagnostic indicators. Exosomal cargoes, which encompass proteins and miRNAs, present a trove of biomarker candidates, attributable to their stability, pervasive presence, and indicative nature of the disease status. The potential of exosomal biomarkers in the identification of sepsis-induced organ damage, including cardiomyopathy, acute kidney injury, and acute lung injury, is emphasized, as they provide real-time insights into cardiac and renal impairments. Despite promising prospects, hurdles persist in the standardization of exosome extraction and the need for extensive clinical trials to validate their efficacy. The combination of biomarker development and sophisticated exosome detection techniques represents a pioneering strategy in the realm of sepsis diagnosis and management, underscoring the significance of further research and clinical validation.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Sepsis, Exosome, Biomarker, MiRNA, Organ failure

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to quintessential infection [1, 2], and has been described as the quintessential medical disorder in our contemporary era. Sepsis is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients despite improvements in medical care for patients with various disorders. In 2020, the Global Burden of Diseases reported that nearly 50 million patients develop sepsis globally each year [3]. In China, the mortality rates of sepsis in hospitals and intensive care units (ICUs) are 33.5–48.7%, while the mortality rate of sepsis combined with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome is as high as 60%. Deaths caused by sepsis and multiple organ failure account for 60–70% of burn deaths [4]. Moreover, sepsis imposes enormous economic burdens on patients and the health care system. Billions of dollars from the health care system are spent on sepsis annually because it is common for sepsis patients to stay in ICUs for weeks or months. In the United States alone, annual spending on sepsis diagnosis and treatment exceeds $16.7 billion [5]. Furthermore, patients who survive sepsis have a longer-term risk of rehospitalization and death [6, 7]. For example, nearly 50% of sepsis survivors are rehospitalized at least once within a year, and one-in-six sepsis survivors do not survive in the first year. Considering the profound impact of this devastating disease, the need for effective and inchoate diagnostic approaches for sepsis cannot be understated.

During sepsis, inflammatory substances such as pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs) are released into the bloodstream, which combine with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) located on the surface of immune cells, such as Toll-like receptors. This combination initiates a series of signals that lead to the production and release of molecules and cytokines that promote inflammation, contributing to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [8]. However, excessive inflammatory factors and reactive oxygen species are a double-edged sword that not only eliminate invading pathogens but also cause damage to normal tissues and organs, leading to the dysfunction of various organs [9]. When two or more organ systems or organs are affected sequentially or simultaneously, multiorgan dysfunction ensues, resulting in the inability of the body to maintain stable internal conditions. If the injury progresses further, it can lead to septic shock, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and even death. Notably, sepsis is not a steadily progressive immunological process that follows infection or injury, but there are currently numerous unresolved issues, particularly problems with the diagnosis of sepsis. At present, clinicians continue to adhere to the diagnostic criteria outlined in Sepsis 3.0, which is characterized by the presence of infection coupled with a SOFA score exceeding two points. In cases where sepsis is suspected, prompt culture of blood and other bodily fluids is imperative to ascertain the type of infection and identify the most appropriate antibiotic therapy. Furthermore, imaging studies, such as X-ray, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasonography are instrumental in pinpointing the site of infection. In addition to these diagnostic measures, the management of sepsis necessitates swift action, encompassing the control of the source of infection, the administration of antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, vasoactive drug support, and organ function support [10, 11]. In recent years, immune-modulating therapy has garnered increasing attention. This therapeutic approach is designed to mitigate excessive inflammatory responses and prevent immunosuppression, thereby reducing organ dysfunction and improving patient outcomes. It may encompass the use of anti-inflammatory medications, immunoglobulins, blood purification techniques, and, in certain cases, the administration of glucocorticoids to suppress excessive immune reactions [12]. Furthermore, assessing the immune status of sepsis patients is of paramount importance, as restoring immune function and achieving immunological equilibrium is crucial for safeguarding organ function. The goal of treatment is to reestablish a balanced immune state, enhance organ performance, and thereby increase the cure rate of sepsis.

However, the use of sepsis 3.0 still faces numerous challenges in practical applications. These issues include, but are not limited to, insufficient sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic criteria, and inadequate assessment of disease severity, which may lead to delayed diagnosis and affect patient outcomes and prognosis [13, 14]. The introduction of biomarkers offers new hope for the diagnosis of sepsis [15]. Biomarkers are molecules related to diseases that can be detected within living organisms, including proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolic products [16]. These markers can reflect the presence, development, and prognosis of diseases, providing clinicians with more precise diagnostic tools. For example, procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP) have been proven to be of significant value in the early diagnosis of sepsis, but they also face some challenges [17, 18].

Exosomes, as important means of intercellular communication, have received widespread attention in biomedical research in recent years. Exosomes are small vesicles released by cells into the extracellular environment; these vesicles contain biological molecules such as proteins, lipids, DNA, and RNA, which can influence the function and behavior of recipient cells [19]. The role of exosomes in the pathogenesis of sepsis is gradually being revealed. Studies have shown that the content and composition of exosomes in sepsis patients change during disease development, which is closely related to key pathological processes such as inflammatory responses, endothelial cell function, and multiorgan dysfunction in sepsis [20]. There is a substantial body of research on the role of exosomes as biomarkers in sepsis, which sufficiently underscores their importance. Consequently, we aim to conduct a comprehensive review of new advances in the use of exosomes in sepsis diagnosis and prognosis. We elaborate on this review in term of five aspects: the development of diagnostic definitions of sepsis; current diagnostic biomarkers for sepsis; the use of exosomes as new potential biomarkers in sepsis; the identification of novel exosomal diagnostic biomarkers for sepsis-related organ injury; and the current challenges associated with the use of exosomes as diagnostic biomarkers.

Diagnosis of sepsis

“Sepsis’’ is an antiquity concept dated from ancient Egypt that was used to describe patients with a continuum of organ function abnormalities. Hippocrates used the term “sepo” to describe the process of decay or decomposition of organic matter, adding “when continuing fever is present, it is dangerous if the outer parts are cold, but the inner parts are burning hot”. As the understanding of microbes developed, sepsis tends to be used to describe occasions in which patients appear to die from the body’s response to infection. However, all these explanations were wraparound concepts.

Early diagnosis is a prerequisite for improving the curative ratio of sepsis, and early intervention greatly reduces the incidence of organ dysfunction and mortality. However, until recently, sepsis has been finely defined. In 1991 [21], experts in the field of critical care medicine jointly elaborated on the nature of sepsis and clearly defined sepsis, severe sepsis, septic shock, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which is Sepsis 1.0. SIRS induced by infection was defined as sepsis.This is the so-called Chicago standard. Sepsis 1.0 provides a standardized definition for sepsis, which is crucial for clinical practice and research. This criterion emphasizes the systemic inflammatory characteristics of sepsis and highlights the progression from sepsis to severe sepsis and septic shock, which can be life-threatening. This framework is crucial for the subsequent research and treatment of sepsis. With its widespread application in clinical practice, the shortcomings of the definition of sepsis and diagnostic criteria in sepsis 1.0 have become increasingly obvious [22, 23], such as excessive sensitivity and low specificity, overlooking the complexity of the host response [24], and a limited understanding of the pathophysiology of sepsis. These issues led to the revision of subsequent sepsis definitions, such as Sepsis 2.0 and Sepsis 3.0, which attempted to improve diagnosis and treatment through more precise standards and a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of sepsis [25].

In 2001 [26], the diagnostic criteria for sepsis were revised. The meeting proposed a diagnostic criterion consisting of more than 20 assessment indicators of clinical symptoms and signs, that is, Sepsis 2.0. However, the complexity of this standard, coupled with a dearth of robust research foundation and empirical evidence, has hindered its clinical acceptance and application, leading to the continued prevalence of the Sepsis 1.0 criteria in practice. Furthermore, as a result of diverse interpretations and applications of the consensus definitions, variable estimates of both the incidence and prognosis of sepsis have been obtained [27–29].

In January 2014, 19 experts in critical care, infectious diseases, surgery, and respiratory diseasesdeveloped new definitions and diagnostic criteria for sepsis and septic shock through evidence-based exploration and discussion. Published in 2016 [30], the third international consensus on sepsis emphasized the use of the sepsis-associated sequential organ failure score (SOFA) system for organ function assessment and risk stratification. The “infection + quick SOFA score (qSOFA) ≥ 2 points” can be used for rapid diagnosis of suspected sepsis patients, that is, Sepsis 3.0. It includes 3 elements: infection, host response and organ dysfunction, emphasizing that disorders of the host response and fatal organ dysfunction are crucial differences between sepsis and infection. Sepsis 3.0 represents an achievement of the international intensive care community in term of understanding the underlying mechanism and performing clinical exploration, and the application of Sepsis 3.0 significantly promoted aggressive diagnosis and treatment. However, Sepsis 3.0 is still not satisfactory. The definition is inaccurate, such as the criterion of suspected infection, which is subjective. The SOFA and qSOFA rely on organ dysfunction and fatality rates rather than the sensibility and specificity of sepsis. Studies have shown that compared with the qSOFA score, the modified early warning score (MEWS), national early warning score (NEWS), and SIRS scoring system are more accurate in the early diagnosis of sepsis and have great specificity [31]. In addition, the MEWS, NEWS, and SIRS are more valuable in predicting admission to the ICU and death in non-ICU patients. Therefore, both higher sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic methods—biomarkers of sepsis—are needed. Machine learning, a subfield of artificial intelligence (AI) that harnesses data and algorithms to mimic human learning, is instrumental in processing extensive datasets and uncovering significant patterns of information. This technology can be leveraged to aid in the diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic intervention of sepsis. The upcoming revision of sepsis definitions (possibly Sepsis 4.0) anticipates that sepsis and septic shock will be redefined not as a syndrome but as a spectrum of identifiable conditions, each distinguished by unique cellular changes and corresponding biomarkers. The complexity of this endeavour extends beyond merely understanding the pathophysiology of sepsis; thus, comprehensive observational studies of patients coupled with in-depth laboratory investigations are needed. Biomarkers of sepsis

Biomarkers, as emerging modalities for disease diagnosis, can help shorten the time for identifying suspected sepsis and play a vital role in the identification of pathogens. Not surprisingly, an increasing number of biomarkers are available for sepsis, the number of which is greater than 250 [32]. Ideal biomarkers should be easily detected and highly specific, sensitive, and predictive.

Currently, several categories of biomarkers are used for diagnosing and predicting the progression of sepsis, including acute-phase reactants, cytokines, tissue injury biomarkers, and DAMPs [33]. CRP is widely used in the clinical environment, because it reflects the sensitivity of specific inflammation in the body, and its level can indicate the severity and prognosis of infectious diseases. Therefore, CRP is one of the most used early diagnostic biomarkers for sepsis and has good application value because it is easy to detect [34]. However, its sensitivity is high, its specificity is inadequate, and its levels can increase due to various factors, such as trauma, surgery, and inflammation, making it difficult to distinguish between infectious and noninfectious injuries effectively [35]. Furthermore, CRP levels can be affected by several factors, such as age, sex, smoking status, body weight, lipid levels, and blood pressure, which may interfere with the accuracy of the use of CRP as a sepsis marker [36, 37].

PCT is a member of the calcitonin superfamily, and is secreted in healthy individuals only in neuroendocrine cells of the thyroid gland [38]. PCT was first defined in 1984, but until 1993, it was considered an indicator of infection. During infection, however, PCT is released from almost all tissues and cells, making it an effective indicator of bacterial infection, even at the early stage. In addition, the prognostic implications of PCT have also shown that elevated PCT serum levels are positively associated with disease severity, especially in septic patients [39]. Although PCT assays have shown great promise, the cost-effectiveness of these tests continues to be debated. Current research has shown that these tests are already being overused because there are currently no adequate guidelines for when these tests should and should not be obtained [40]. Therefore, the clinical significance of these tests needs to be more thoroughly researched on a large scale and through randomized clinical trials so that guidelines can be implemented to ensure the practice of cost-effective medicine [41].

Several inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α, can also be used as biomarkers for sepsis diagnosis, and inflammatory mediators are essential for serving as vanguards against pathogens and as catalysts for the activation of the adaptive immune response. A study revealed that critically ill sepsis patients exhibited a progressive increase in IL-10, IL-6, PAI-1, and IL-8, distinguishing them from their noncritically ill counterparts [42]. However, several limitations are associated with the use of these cytokines as sepsis markers. The dynamic nature of cytokine expression and their unstable properties mean that their levels can fluctuate over time, which may affect the accuracy of their use as biomarkers [43]. Furthermore, these cytokines are susceptible to degradation by proteolytic enzymes present in the bloodstream and the window of detection for cytokines is often narrow, meaning that the optimal time frame for their measurement is limited [44].

In-depth research on sepsis biomarkers provides new references for the early diagnosis of sepsis, the timing of antibiotic use, the assessment of the severity of the patient’s condition, and prognosis. Although the above biomarkers have been shown to have potential utility in the diagnosis and prognosis of sepsis, the current application and implementation of biomarkers in the clinical management of sepsis still have great limitations. First, there is no single biomarker that reliably predicts sepsis diagnosis or related outcomes. There are wide variations in the timing and method of biomarker measurements, as well as outcomes of interest, making it difficult to determine the optimal use of biomarkers in the clinical treatment of sepsis. Second, there are still some practical problems in the detection of sepsis biomarkers that need to be solved in future studies [15], such as a lack of specificity and sensitivity and cost-effectiveness concerns. Third, these tests are currently being overused owing to a lack of clear guidelines on when they should be conducted. Accordingly, in the clinical setting, there is a need for new sepsis biomarkers that possess the following characteristics: increased specificity, rapid detection, increased stability, increased cost-effectiveness, and dynamic monitoring capability to allow for consistent and reproducible detection in a hospital setting.

Exosome as a potential biomarker

Exosomes exist in various types of body fluids, and their greatest advantage in clinical applications lies in the convenience of sampling. Exosomes have emerged as important cell-to-cell communication tools and are widely used as biomarkers for various diseases. Exosomes are produced through a process in which the plasma membrane undergoes double invagination, leading to the formation of intracellular multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that contain intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). Eventually, these ILVs are released as exosomes, which have diameters ranging from approximately 40 to 160 nm. This release occurs through the fusion of MVBs with the plasma membrane, followed by exocytosis [45]. Exosomes, which are extracellular vesicles originating from a variety of cell types such as endotheliocytes [46], stem cells [47] and even cancer cells [48], can exhibit distinct functionalities and compositions [45]. While exosomes vary in size, composition, and origin, some fundamental characteristics can be summarized. Regardless of their size, exosomes are subcellular structures that are spherical or oval. They are composed of phospholipid bilayers and contain various membrane-bound or plasma membrane-associated molecules. These nanoscale entities mirror their parent cells, encapsulating a snapshot of the cell’s physiological state and exosomal surface proteins play a pivotal role in enhancing immune capture and enrichment processes. Consequently, they have emerged as versatile biomarkers, holding significant promise in the diagnosis and monitoring of a spectrum of diseases [19]. For example, plasma-derived exosomal circLPAR1 has cancer-specific characteristics, significantly increasing its diagnostic accuracy in detecting colorectal cancer, as evidenced by an increase in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) to 0.875 [49]. The exosome-encapsulated macromolecular repertoire, emanating from the allograft and immune cells, constitutes a promising panel of biomarkers that can be strategically utilized to evaluate the functionality and status of acceptance or rejection of the transplanted grafts [50].

The following biological characteristics endow exosomes with advantages as biomarkers for various diseases. (1) Wide distribution: The availability of highly sensitive assays capable of quantifying cellular and/or circulating exosomes and their compounds has facilitated the identification of “signatures” that may represent diagnostically significant biomarkers in nearly any biological sample. These samples include blood [51], urine [52, 53], cerebrospinal fluid [54], and nasal fluid [55]. Compared with blood samples, other body fluid samples can achieve true noninvasiveness, which results in the convenient acquisition of samples, including exosomes. For example, saliva is a recognized valuable noninvasive diagnostic sample, and its exosomes can reflect the systemic disease status, especially for oral diseases [56]. The formation of exosomes is closely related to the status of the source cells. Therefore, extracellular vesicle surface markers can replace to some extent the need for source cell biopsy, which is particularly meaningful for diseases that are difficult to biopsy [57]. (2) Rich content: The contents of exosomes include proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, among others. Studies on different components of exosomes have been extensive and detailed. For example, research on protein components includes surface proteins, internal proteins, and post translational modifications. Nucleic acids include RNA and DNA, with more research on RNA, involving various types of markers such as miRNAs [58], mRNAs [59], long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [60], and circular RNAs (circRNAs) [61]. But in recent years, there has been increasing focus on DNA components, which participate in regulating tumour immunity, and mutation information and its methylation information can be used as biomarkers for disease diagnosis [62]. The abundance of cargo within extracellular vesicles, specifically exosomes, renders them prime candidates for biomarker carriers and endows exosomes with considerable physiological and pathological potential. (3) Comprehensive functions: Extracellular exosomes participate in the regulation of multiple important physiological and pathological processes and orchestrate the modulation of intracellular pathways and the delicate balance between survival and death. Studies have shown that exosomes can change the behaviour of target cells by delivering functional contents such as nucleic acids and proteins, which play important roles in maintaining intracellular homeostasis and intercellular material and information exchange [63]. Furthermore, these nanoscale vesicles play a seminal role in the aetiology and progression of various diseases. Increasing evidence suggests that exosomes affect various disease-related processes such as cell proliferation, tumour metastasis, angiogenesis, tumour resistance, and immune reactions [64]. Therefore, exosomes are useful biomarkers that can reflect the process of disease and homeostasis through subcellular structural units (single vesicles or cells) for disease diagnosis and treatment. (4) Greater stability: To be considered potential biomarkers, any compound must exhibit sufficient stability to allow for consistent and reproducible detection in a hospital setting. As mentioned above, exosomes are generated through the double invagination of the cell membrane and are expelled from the cell via exocytosis, indicating that exosomes are vesicular structures enclosed by a phospholipid bilayer [19], which endows the contents within exosomes with increased stability, safeguarding them from metabolic degradation and thereby more accurately reflecting the original information [65]. Hence, owing to their aforementioned characteristics, exosomes have been widely used as biomarkers in cancer [66], cardiovascular diseases [67, 68], and diabetic complications [69]. In summary, exosomes stand at the forefront of cellular communication, serving as versatile regulators of cellular fate and function. Their role in disease pathogenesis and their ability to mirror the state of their originating cells underscore their importance in both fundamental biomedical research and clinical applications. Exosomes indeed serve as exceptional biomarkers for the diagnostic and therapeutic purpose of sepsis.

Different exosomal cargoes in sepsis diagnosis

Exosome-derived proteins and miRNAs play important roles in the diagnosis, progression, treatment, and prognosis of sepsis [70]. These cargoes can be detected via the following methods: immunoblotting, flow cytometry, mass spectrometry, miRNA sequencing and several new techniques, such as colloidal gold immunoassays, magnetic bead separation, and some label-free detection technologies [45]. While the analysis of exosomal cargo typically requires large pools of purified exosomes, it is important to note that not all exosomes contain the same abundance of a particular cargo. This variation can be observed, for example, in the case of miRNA exosomal cargo [71]. Proteomic analyses of exosomes have revealed the presence of diverse markers within exosomes, indicating heterogeneity. This heterogeneity should be taken into consideration when designing experiments that rely on purification methodologies based on specific markers [72]. The heterogeneity of exosomes can also contribute to the differences observed between exosomes from healthy individuals and those from sepsis patients. Thus, the various constituents within exosomes can reflect the status of sepsis, and given their abundance and detectability, they serve as effective indicators of sepsis. In this context, we have described biomarkers that reflect alterations in the cargo carried by exosomes during sepsis.

Proteins

Exosomes contain a variety of proteins with different functions, such as cytokines, chemokines, PAMPS, DAMPS, etc [73]. Research has revealed that the protein profiles of exosomes in the body fluids of sepsis patients are significantly altered. These alterations in protein profiles are potentially associated with disease progression in sepsis [74]. Exosome proteins as sepsis biomarkers

Aminopeptidase N (APN/CD13) is an ectopeptidase that is dependent on Zn2 + and is located on the cell membrane. It plays a role in catalysing the cleavage of the N-terminal amino acids of short peptides. APN/CD13 is considered a cancer-specific biomarker and is highly expressed on the surface of the cell membrane [89]. Quantitative proteomic analyses have investigated the protein profile of exosomes and revealed that the levels of exosomal APN/CD13 are increased in the plasma of patients with sepsis-induced ALI [83]. Exosomal APN/CD13 derived from macrophages governs the process of necroptosis in pulmonary epithelial cells through its interaction with the cell membrane receptor TLR4, thereby instigating the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), impairment of mitochondrial function, and activation of the NF-κB pathway [83].

HMGB1 is an omnipresent nuclear protein that can be liberated by stimulated macrophages to coordinate inflammatory reactions [90]. Furthermore, the secretion and release of HMGB1 are influenced by ROS and redox signals, which facilitate various post-translational modifications [91]. Investigations have indicated that lactate can induce HMGB1 acetylation through Hippo/YAP-mediated suppression of the deacetylase SIRT1 and β-arrestin2-mediated recruitment of the acetylases p300/CBP to the nucleus via G protein-coupled receptor 81 (GPR81). The lactylated/acetylated form of HMGB1 is then excreted from macrophages through exosome secretion, thereby increasing endothelial permeability [75].

Extracellular cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (eCIRP) serves as a crucial DAMP. In various clinical scenarios, such as sepsis, ischaemia/reperfusion injuries, and haemorrhagic shock, CIRP can translocate from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and even to extracellular regions, manifesting as eCIRP [92]. Notably, the levels of eCIRP in exosomes significantly increased following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) treatment in the supernatants of macrophage cultures, mouse serum, and caecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic mouse serum [76]. Furthermore, eCIRP can induce ferroptosis in septic lungs by diminishing the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) while concurrently increasing lipid ROS levels [93].

In addition to the plasma-derived exosome biomarkers, urine serves as a significant reservoir of exosomes, particularly in relation to sepsis-induced kidney injury. Urinary exosomes, which contain apical membranes and intracellular fluid, are typically expelled into the urine from various segments of the nephron and may harbour biomarkers such as proteins and microRNAs, which are indicative of renal dysfunction and structural damage [94]. One of these biomarkers is Na+/H + exchanger isoform 3 (NHE3), which is expressed predominantly in the apical membrane of the proximal convoluted tubule and thick ascending limb in mammals [95]. Notably, the urinary exosomal level of NHE3 was found to be elevated in sepsis-induced AKI patients compared with healthy volunteers, suggesting its potential utilization as a predictive diagnostic biomarker for AKI in the future [84]. Furthermore, ATF3, a transcription factor protected within urine exosomes, has been detected in the urine of both AKI patients and animal models [96]. Considering that uATF3 is largely undetectable in sepsis patients without AKI, the qualitative assessment of uATF3 might prove sufficient for detecting sepsis-induced AKI [85].

MiRNAs

In addition to analysing exosomes based on vesicle surface antigens, investigating cargo contents, such as miRNAs, has emerged as a compelling research approach [97]. miRNAs are short non-coding sequences, typically ranging from 19 to 25 nucleotides in length, which are actively and passively packaged into exosomes. These miRNAs can modulate gene expression by interfering with the translation of messenger RNA [98]. The maturation process of miRNAs has been extensively elucidated [99]. Initially, miRNAs are transcribed by RNA polymerase II and subsequently processed by two key enzymes, namely, Drosha ribonuclease III and Dicer 1 ribonuclease III. Dicer, along with the transactivation response element RNA-binding protein (TRBP), facilitates the recruitment of Argonaute proteins, leading to the assembly of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [100]. miRNAs exert their regulatory function by targeting cytosolic mRNAs through complementary base pairing, typically with the 3’ untranslated regions of the target mRNAs within the RISC complex. Remarkably, a single miRNA has the potential to influence the expression of hundreds of mRNAs molecules. As a result, miRNAs are considered integral components of intricate regulatory networks involved in gene expression, contributing to both physiological and pathophysiological processes.

Dysregulated miRNA expression has been implicated in tightly controlled mechanisms such as development, ageing, and cell death [101], as well as in the onset of complex diseases including infection, inflammation, and sepsis [102]. In a preclinical study on sepsis, miR-223 was shown to play a role in the regulation of haematopoiesis and is considered a promising biomarker for immune response dysregulation [103]. When combined with other miRNAs, miR-223 has the potential to aid in distinguishing between non-septic and septic patients [104].

MiRNAs have been detected in the blood and urine and might serve as biomarkers. Certain miRNAs exclusively reside within cellular exosomes, whereas others are expressed across diverse cellular lineages [105]. miRNAs in exosomes are very stable under conditions that normally degrade most RNAs, such as high or low temperatures, pH changes, and repeated freezing and thawing cycles [106]. In addition to their stability, miRNAs have several advantages over “conventional” protein-based markers: miRNAs are relatively small, do not undergo post-processing modifications, and have a less complex chemical structure. MiRNAs in exosomes as sepsis biomarkers

In a recent investigation aimed at verifying the differential expression of miRNAs derived from neutrophil-derived exosomes in patients with non-septic inflammatory cardiomyopathy and septic inflammatory cardiomyopathy compared with healthy individuals, significant alterations were observed. The profiling analysis of miRNAs revealed that a total of sixty-eight miRNAs from neutrophil-derived exosomes exhibited significant changes between healthy controls and patients without septic cardiomyopathy, with 61 miRNAs upregulated and 7 downregulated [117]. Evaluating their diagnostic performance, the area under the curve (AUC) for miR-150-5p, NT-pro BNP, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and the SOFA score was 0.941, whereas the AUC for miR-150-5p alone was 0.855. This demonstrates a notable advantage for miRNAs over other risk factors associated with SICM, such as NT-pro BNP, LVEF, and SOFA score, which achieved AUC values of 0.677, 0.646, and 0.708, respectively [117]. Moreover, miR-150-5p has additional advantages, as it can mitigate LPS-induced apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells and modulate renal inflammatory responses and oxidative stress by regulating the MEKK3/JNK pathway [122]. However, it is worth noting that miR-150-5p derived from rat renal tubular epithelial cell-derived exosomes following hypoxia treatment, can exacerbate renal fibrosis by activating rat kidney fibroblasts [123].

Research findings have indicated that exosomes derived from sepsis patients exhibit increased miR-885-5p levels, decreased HMBOX1 expression, elevated levels of IL-1β and IL-18, and increased pyroptosis in human myocardial cells [118]. The upregulation of miR-885-5p can increase the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD-N, which serve as biomarkers for pyroptosis, thereby contributing to sepsis-induced myocardial damage [118]. Elevated levels of exosomal miR-30d-5p derived from polymorphonuclear leukocytes have been implicated in sepsis-induced ALI through the induction of M1 macrophage polarization and the priming of macrophage pyroptosis via activation of the NF-κB pathway [113]. Additionally, abnormal levels of miR-30d-5p have been associated with various human tumour types, including lung cancer, colorectal cancer, esophageal cancer, prostate cancer, liver cancer, cervical cancer, and breast cancer [124]. Furthermore, exosomal miR-93-5p has been shown to regulate thioredoxin-interacting protein, thereby alleviating sepsis-induced acute kidney injury by directly influencing pyroptosis in renal epithelial cells [112].

Neutrophils employ three main strategies in their arsenal: phagocytosis, degranulation, and NETs release. During inflammation, neutrophils release NETs, and excessive NET formation contributes to inflammation and tissue damage in sepsis [125]. Recent studies have revealed the involvement of miR-15b-5p and miR-378a-3p derived from platelet-derived exosomes in inducing NET formation in neutrophils through the Akt/mTOR autophagy pathway, thereby promoting acute lung injury [114]. Furthermore, these studies also indicated that IκB kinase regulates the secretion of platelet-derived exosomes in septic shock [114]. Additionally, one of the upregulated miRNAs identified in exosomes derived from the plasma of septic patients identified is miR-7-5p. Consequently, miR-7-5p holds promise as a potential biomarker for sepsis [107]. Notably, miR-7-5p plays a role in mitigating T lymphocyte apoptosis by directly suppressing Bad, thereby attenuating sepsis-induced immunosuppression [107].

lncRNAs and CircRNAs

In exosomes from patients with sepsis, other types of non-coding RNAs, including lncRNAs and circRNAs, are also altered. lncRNAs are functional RNA molecules exceeding a length of 200 nucleotides. lncRNAs within the nucleus are associated with specialized subnuclear compartments and play pivotal roles in modulating nuclear processes such as chromatin organization, RNA transcription, and splicing. In the cytoplasm, lncRNAs engage with membrane-less cytosolic structures. Consequently, they exert regulatory control over mRNA transport, stability, and translation, as well as protein stability, post-translational modification, and function [126, 127]. They have emerged as significant contributors to the functionality and regulatory processes of nearly all genes [128]. LncRNAs are strongly related to the progression and prognosis of various diseases, such as cancer, metabolic diseases, and sepsis [129]. Compared with those in control groups, the levels of exosome-derived FGD5-AS1, which can interact with p300, increase in pancreatic cancer groups, resulting in STAT3 acetylation, thus promoting the nuclear localization and transcriptional activity of STAT3/NF-κB and ultimately causing M2 macrophage polarization to promote the malignant behaviours of tumour cells [130]. Moreover, platinum sensitivity-related lncRNAs from ascites-derived exosomes can increase platinum sensitivity in ovarian cancer to decrease cisplatin resistance [127]. Recently, researchers have reported the significant value of the lncRNA H19 for the early diagnosis of sepsis and sepsis-induced ALI. The ROC curve, which was utilized to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of lncRNA H19, indicated an AUC of 0.8197 for the early identification of sepsis. Furthermore, the AUC for diagnosing sepsis patients with ALI was 0.9141 (95% CI, 0.87–0.96), and for those without ALI, it was 0.8399 [131]. LncRNAs from exosomes can also serve as biomarkers to predict and diagnose sepsis. Nuclear-enriched transcript 1 (NEAT1) is a lncRNA that plays a pivotal role in cellular processes such as the cell cycle, proliferation, and apoptosis of tumour cells. It has a cancer-promoting effect [132]. The lncRNA NEAT1, which is transported by exosomes, facilitates the expression of transferrin receptor and glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1, thereby exacerbating sepsis-associated encephalopathy through the promotion of ferroptosis [133]. Furthermore, studies have revealed that the lncRNA IGF2-AS is also upregulated in the exosomes of sepsis patients. It regulates nucleotide metabolism by mediating HMGA1 to induce pyroptosis in endothelial progenitor cells in sepsis patients [134].

Compared with traditional linear RNAs, circRNAs are a class of specialized non-coding RNA molecules that exist in a covalently closed circular structure and exhibit greater stability and tissue-specific expression patterns [135]. The formation of circRNAs primarily occurs through back-splicing of exons, thereby generating a continuous loop structure. Owing to this configuration, circRNAs are resistant to degradation by exonucleases, demonstrating exceptional stability within cells. They serve as miRNA sponges, orchestrating gene expression regulation and actively engaging in the progression and manifestation of numerous pathological states [136]. The high stability and unique immunogenicity of circRNAs offer promising prospects for translational applications. Recent research revealed abnormal circRNA expression in the blood/serum, cells, tissues, and exosomes of colorectal cancer patients and identified circRNAs as potential markers for colorectal cancer diagnosis and prognosis [137]. In a study of SICM, researchers investigated the role of circRNAs in autophagy via an acute sepsis mouse model, and the findings indicated potential circRNA‒mRNA interactions in SICM autophagy, suggesting new targets for pathogenesis research [138]. Emerging research suggests that circRNAs serve as promising diagnostic biomarkers for sepsis because of their role in modulating immune reactions and the regulation of miRNAs [139]. Recently, researchers have reported that CircFADS2 is underexpressed across sepsis samples and may protect lung cells from LPS-induced apoptosis [140]. Studies have revealed the upregulation of circRNA_104484 and circRNA_104670 in serum exosomes from patients with sepsis, as determined by circRNA microarray analysis. Additionally, ROC analysis demonstrated the diagnostic potential of circRNA_104484 and circRNA_104670 in serum exosomes as markers for sepsis [141].

In summary, as members of the non-coding RNA family, lncRNAs and circRNAs have significant research and application value in the biomedical field because of their unique structure and diverse functions. With ongoing research, lncRNAs and circRNAs are expected to become vital tools for sepsis diagnosis and treatment in the future (Table 3).

Mitochondrial danger-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) include mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and mitochondrial formyl peptide 4, which activate chemotaxis via formyl peptide receptors 1 and 2 [144]. In the context of sepsis, inflammatory and infectious stimuli induce the release of active mtDAMPs through exosomes. Research has demonstrated that elevated levels of mtDNA are linked to poor clinical prognosis in sepsis [145, 146] and inflammatory lung injury [147]. Furthermore, mtDAMPs derived from monocyte-derived exosomes can inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis in a dose-dependent manner through a toll-like receptor-9-dependent mechanism [77].

Role of exosomes in sepsis-related organ injury

Sepsis, a condition characterized by infection and organ dysregulation (SOFA system for organ function assessment) resulting from pathogen‒host interactions, stands as a major cause of mortality in ICUs worldwide. This syndrome can induce systemic multiorgan damage, posing a grave threat to patients. Notably, exosomes generated during sepsis actively regulate the progression and prognosis of sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction. A comprehensive understanding of the pivotal role played by exosomes as influential messengers in the development of sepsis has significant implications for uncovering their diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential in combating this syndrome. In this context, we present a discussion on the involvement of exosomes in sepsis-related organ dysfunction.

Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM)

Myocardial injury is a fatal manifestation of sepsis-related multiple organ failure and is strongly associated with high mortality rates among patients [148]. The majority of sepsis patients exhibit abnormal cardiac function, characterized by systolic dysfunction, impaired perfusion, and elevated levels of biomarkers, such as circulating troponin levels [149]. Like in other organs, cardiac dysfunction in sepsis arises from an imbalanced host response to infection. Several mechanisms contribute to SICM, including the presence of circulating myocardial depressant substances, downregulation of the adrenergic pathway, oxidative and nitrosative stress, alterations in the coronary microvasculature, and abnormalities in calcium handling and myofilament sensitivity [149]. Importantly, SICM may exhibit transiently reversible myocardial dysfunction, suggesting that cardiac homeostasis can be restored following the resolution of septic stress [150, 151]. Advances in diagnostic techniques have increased the sensitivity of detecting myocardial abnormalities in sepsis patients, but few specific biomarkers that more accurately reflect the cellular status are available. Consequently, exosomes, which can mirror source cells, can serve as biomarkers that reflect the dynamic changes in the heart during sepsis by representing the status of sepsis-related cells.

Extensive research has indicated that exosomes serve as biomarkers for SICM and are implicated in the pathogenesis of this condition. Researchers have shown that the endothelial-derived exosomal heat shock protein A12B can be taken up by macrophages to modulate inflammatory reactions in polymicrobial sepsis and is associated with SICM and mortality [152]. Furthermore, in a previous study, circulating platelet-derived exosomes from septic patients induced myocardial dysfunction in isolated hearts through the generation of nitric oxide (NO) and an increase in the myocardial nitrate content [153]. Notably, exosomes derived from septic individuals presented higher levels of ROS than normal exosomes did, leading to increased formation of podosome clusters. This, in turn, induces endothelial hyperpermeability and cardiac dysfunction [154]. The development of endothelial hyperpermeability was found to be associated with podosome cluster-mediated fragmentation and depletion of zonula occludens-1 at the cell periphery [154]. Podosome clusters are highly regulated subcellular structures composed of actin that facilitate the localized release of lytic enzymes, contributing to matrix degradation in processes such as immune surveillance and cancer metastasis [155]. Furthermore, exosomes derived from the serum of sepsis patients presented elevated expression levels of miR-1262, which decreased the expression of SLC2A1, leading to the inhibition of glycolysis and promotion of apoptosis in human myocardial cells, ultimately contributing to myocardial injury [73]. From these perspectives, it becomes clear that exosomes can reflect the cardiac functional status in real-time and may precipitate SICM. Nonetheless, the existing body of research on this topic is relatively limited, underscoring the need for a substantial expansion of studies moving forwards.

Sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (AKI)

AKI is a prevalent complication observed in hospitalized and critically ill patients, increasing the risk of developing chronic comorbidities and resulting in a significantly high mortality rate [156]. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced AKI, which is distinct from AKI induced by ischemia/reperfusion injury [157], include inflammation [158], microcirculatory dysfunction [159], and metabolic reprogramming [160]. Renal tubular epithelial cells express Toll-like receptors, particularly TLR2 and TLR4, which interact with damage- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns that are filtered through the glomerulus or neighbouring peritubular capillaries. Consequently, proximal TECs exhibit an increase in ROS production and mitochondrial impairment [161]. Furthermore, TEC-derived exosomal miR-19b-5p leads to the polarization of the M1 phenotype by targeting NF-κB/SOCS-1, thus initiating tubulointerstitial inflammation [162]. Additionally, alterations in tissue perfusion, which may not be contingent upon systemic circulatory abnormalities, play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of sepsis-induced AKI.

Nonetheless, delayed detection of sepsis-induced AKI is a risk factor for mortality and chronicity. Therefore, early diagnosis is crucial to initiate appropriate treatment strategies. While serum creatinine is commonly used as a biomarker, its elevation in plasma occurs only in the advanced stages of AKI. On the other hand, exosomes are closely associated with sepsis-induced AKI and hold promises as potential biomarkers for early detection, enabling timely intervention and assessment of severity. As previously mentioned, miR-181a-5p and miR-23b-3p derived from serum exosomes exhibited higher expression levels in sepsis-induced AKI rats than in control animals. Bioinformatics analysis further revealed that both miRNAs are associated with the expression of proinflammatory cytokines [111]. Furthermore, several urinary biomarkers in exosomes, including NHE3 protein [84], and uATF3 [85], have been identified as noninvasive indicators for predicting and diagnosing sepsis-induced AKI. However, each biomarker currently has its limitations. Moreover, the integration of these biomarkers into routine clinical practice has been slow, potentially due to factors such as cost, availability of testing platforms, variations in assay techniques and results, and lack of regulatory approval. To enhance early AKI detection, further identification of additional exosomal biomarkers is necessary.

Sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI)

The lung is often the first and most affected organ in sepsis, leading to the development of ALI or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [163]. The pathophysiological characteristics of ALI involve widespread damage to microvascular endothelial cells and alveolar epithelial cells, pulmonary oedema, compromised alveolar‒capillary membrane integrity, and excessive neutrophil migration [164]. In sepsis-induced ALI, both bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and circulation contain an increased number of exosomes [120, 165]. Studies have revealed that exosomes derived from murine alveolar macrophages exhibit elevated expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines following stimulation with LPS [166]. Moreover, exosomal miR-30d-5p derived from polymorphonuclear neutrophils stimulated by TNF-α can promote M1 macrophage polarization and induce macrophage pyroptosis by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby participating in the pathophysiological processes of sepsis-induced ALI [113]. Similarly, miR-92a-3p is enriched in exosomes derived from alveolar epithelial cells in sepsis-induced ALI. It can activate alveolar macrophages and the NF-κB pathway, leading to increased pulmonary inflammation and increased alveolar permeability [167].

Notably, RNA sequencing was performed on lung tissue exosomes obtained from mice in the sham and CLP groups, and thirty lncRNA‒miRNA‒mRNA interactions were identified, including two upregulated lncRNAs, 30 upregulated miRNAs, and two downregulated miRNAs in the lung tissue exosomes of a mouse sepsis model [168]. Serum exosomes from patients with sepsis-induced ALI exhibit significantly high levels of serum amyloid A1, endotoxin, TNF-α, and IL-6 [169]. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that macrophage-derived exosomal miR-155-5p can drive macrophage activation and M1 polarization to induce sepsis-induced ALI [121].

These studies highlight the distinct pathological processes of organ damage in sepsis patients and the corresponding variability in exosomal biomarkers. Compared with other biomarkers, such as IL-1 and CRP, exosomes offer more reflective insight into organ damage, as they carry more information and hold greater potential as biomarkers for different types of organ injury in sepsis. However, many challenges remain to be overcome. For example, research in this area has been superficial and lacking in depth. Specific exosomes associated with damaged myocardial cells and alveolar epithelial cells have yet to be identified. Additionally, there is a paucity of clinical validation and an absence of diagnostic tools that can detect exosomes indicative of organ function across different stages of sepsis. These gaps warrant further investigation to fully understand the potential of exosomes as biomarkers of sepsis-related organ damage (Fig. 2).

Exosomes as biomarkers in sepsis-induced specific organ failure. Upon exposure to inflammatory stimuli, hypoxia, or pathogen invasion, macrophages and endothelial and epithelial cells can release exosomes, which encapsulate a variety of cargoes, such as proteins, miRNAs, lncRNAs and circRNAs. These molecular constituents not only act as diagnostic biomarkers for sepsis and its associated organ injuries but also play a regulatory role in the disease trajectory

Isolation and storage methods for exosomes

To obtain a substantial quantity of highly pure exosomes, the reliability of separation techniques is paramount. There is no absolute optimal method for exosome isolation; however, efficient recovery and high specificity are universally acknowledged as fundamental requirements and are particularly crucial for the diagnosis of sepsis as biomarkers [170]. Most current separation technologies struggle to completely differentiate exosomes from lipoproteins and extracellular vesicles derived from nonexosomal pathways, which possess similar biophysical characteristics, leading to lower exosome purity. Consequently, the effective collection of exosomes remains a significant challenge. The prevalent exosome isolation techniques include differential ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, ultrafiltration, polymer precipitation, and immunoaffinity-based isolation methods, among others. Differential ultracentrifugation is still considered the gold standard for exosome isolation and was first applied by Johnstone et al. to isolate exosomes from reticulocyte tissue culture medium [171]. Nevertheless, this approach is laborious and time intensive, yielding low quantities of exosomes. This necessitates a high level of technical proficiency from operators and relies on intricate equipment. Moreover, it carries the risk of inducing structural damage to the exosomes. These exosome isolation techniques are mostly not widely applicable in clinical settings, thereby limiting the potential of exosomes as biomarkers for sepsis diagnosis. In recent years, extraction methods based on various separation principles and commercial kits have been developed; these methods are easy to operate, time-efficient, labour-saving, and yield high volumes, such as microfluidic technology, based on fluid properties. Microfluidic technology can integrate sample preparation, reaction, separation, and detection processes on a chip of just a few square centimetres, with the greatest advantage- low sample consumption, high integration of functional components, short analysis time, and high sensitivity [172]. Microfluidic technology optimizes the separation and detection of exosomes from both temporal and spatial perspectives, surpassing traditional ultracentrifugation methods in both separation speed and efficiency. Moreover, immuno-biochip technology has shown remarkable performance in tumour diagnosis. Researchers have combined antibodies that recognize exosomal surface membrane proteins and pancreatic cancer protein markers, phosphorylated epithelial cell kinase (EphA2), on immunochromes, allowing only exosomes derived from pancreatic cancer cells to bind simultaneously to both antibodies, thereby achieving the differentiation of early pancreatic cancer patients from pancreatitis patients and healthy individuals [173]. Additionally, merging DNA signal amplification techniques with the signal boosting capabilities of nanomaterials has shown promise in overcoming the challenges of low sensitivity, poor specificity, and high costs associated with detecting tumour exosomes [174]. Characteristics of exosome isolation methods

As previously discussed, exosomes possess a phospholipid bilayer vesicular structure that shields internal biomolecules from various enzymes in body fluids, thereby maintaining their integrity and bioactivity. However, the integrity and bioactivity of exosomes after extraction may also be influenced by factors such as the storage medium, temperature, and duration. The most common storage method is cryopreservation, but this may lead to alterations in the shape and physical properties of exosomes and induce the formation and aggregation of multilayer vesicles, and repeated freeze‒thaw cycles may cause changes in the biological characteristics, content, and marker composition of surface molecules on exosomes [175]. Exosomal DNA in serum remains stable under various storage conditions, while RNA in plasma significantly degrades when stored at 4 °C, and even long-term storage at − 20 °C leads to the degradation of total RNA in plasma exosomes, but miRNA is highly stable, which also implies the potential of exosomal miRNAs as biomarkers [176]. Notably, due to their heterogeneity, exosomes from different sources, may exhibit significant differences in stability when stored under the same conditions. Therefore, the optimal storage conditions for exosomes from different types of sources still need to be further explored. Although storing exosomes at − 80 °C is recommended, maintaining such low temperatures during processing or transportation is sometimes challenging. Furthermore, the nature and content of exosomes are significantly influenced by the storage media used [177].

However, high-purity extraction and preservation of exosomes are crucial for their application as diagnostic biomarkers in sepsis. Mainstream separation technologies have several limitations, but emerging techniques, such as microfluidics and immuno-biochips, characterized by their efficiency, speed, and high sensitivity, show potential in the isolation of exosomes. The heterogeneity in isolation methodologies and limited loading mechanisms for cargo also contribute to inconsistent results and limited therapeutic utility. Moreover, the storage conditions of exosomes are equally critical; cryopreservation may lead to structural alterations, and the stability of RNA and DNA within exosomes can vary under different storage environments. Therefore, optimizing extraction and preservation methods, as well as specific storage conditions tailored to exosomes from various sources, is essential to fully harness the potential of exosomes in the diagnosis of sepsis.

Practical use of exosomes as biomarker and delivery systems

Exosomes have emerged as pivotal biomarkers in clinical settings, offering a wealth of potential applications in disease diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutics. In the context of cancer, exosomes have demonstrated significant potential as diagnostic biomarkers. They can reflect the mutational status of source cells and directly regulate gene expression and cellular function in recipient cells, making them ideal for “liquid biopsy” approaches and early cancer detection [178]. For example, exosomal miRNAs have been investigated as novel cancer biomarkers, presenting challenges and opportunities in disease management.

Exosomes also hold promise in targeted drug delivery because of their biocompatibility, payload capacity, and reduced immunogenicity. They can be engineered to carry therapeutic agents directly to exosome-recipient cells, suggesting a novel approach for treatment. In clinical trials, exosomes are being evaluated for their potential as noninvasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, as well as their capacity to act as nanocarriers for drug delivery [179]. By employing contemporary pharmaceutical techniques, drugs such as resveratrol and triptolide can be encapsulated within exosomes, synergistically combating the excessive inflammatory response and immune dysregulation characteristic of sepsis [180]. For example, folate-functionalized exosomes have been established as an exemplary targeted drug delivery system capable of more effectively transporting therapeutic agents to affected areas, such as the lungs, and colocalizing with macrophages. By modulating the NF-κB and ERK1/2 signalling pathways, these exosomes exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive activities, reducing the levels of proinflammatory cytokines and preventing acute lung injury and multiorgan dysfunction [180]. Researchers have also developed neutrophil membrane-engineered ginsenoside exosomes loaded with miR-182-5p to treat sepsis-induced ALI. These engineered exosomes significantly improved ALI by targeting the NOX4/Drp-1/NLRP3 signalling pathway, suggesting a new therapeutic approach for sepsis-related ALI [181].

In summary, exosomes are being investigated in various clinical trials, with applications ranging from biomarkers to cell-free therapeutic agents, drug delivery carriers, and cancer vaccines [182]. As the field has developed, further multidisciplinary research is needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms directing the roles of exosomes and to improve their clinical translational applications. Engineered exosomes offer novel strategies and hope for the treatment of sepsis, yet their clinical application necessitates overcoming various technical and safety challenges. As research progresses, there is optimism for the future development of more effective and secure exosomal therapies.

Conclusion and further prospects

Sepsis is a common clinical disease that is highly dangerous and often leads to MODS and even death, making it a focus and difficult point of clinical treatment. In-depth studies of sepsis biomarkers provide new means for the early diagnosis of sepsis, timing of antibiotic use, assessment of the severity of patients’ conditions, and prognosis. Currently, any single biomarker has difficulty diagnosing sepsis accurately, but a combination of multiple biomarkers and sepsis scores can improve the accuracy of sepsis diagnosis and prognosis. However, specific markers to help clinical physicians identify multiple organ dysfunction syndrome caused by sepsis in the early stage are lacking.

Exosomes are composed of a lipophilic outer shell and a hydrophilic inner core. Their composition changes according to the pathophysiological state of the parent cell and external stimuli. Exosomes have a double-layered membrane structure that can resist ribonucleases in the blood, making exosomal proteins, miRNAs and other molecules highly stable. They may become ideal biomarkers in circulating body fluids. With the deepening understanding of exosome biogenesis and the important biological information they carry, research on the use of exosomes as a “liquid biopsy” tool is increasing [183]. However, most of the current applications of exosomes are still in the early stages of research; for example, the mechanism of enrichment of tumour-specific markers is not fully understood, and most studies are limited to analysing a specific exosomal marker at a specific time. In addition, the isolation and purification methods of exosomes have not been standardized, which is also one of the main factors affecting the development of exosomal diagnosis. Various new techniques for isolating and purifying exosomes, including microfluidic and immuno-biochip technologies, have been developed. Different isolation methods may lead to differences in the qualitative and quantitative analysis of exosomes. Therefore, it is particularly important to establish standard operating procedures related to the isolation and detection of exosomes and to develop identification models of exosomes as disease-specific markers to improve their clinical application. Furthermore, the advent of novel exosome detection technologies, such as single-vesicle analysis, tissue exosome profiling, colloidal gold detection kits, and ELISA kits, holds significant promise. These technological advancements could transform exosomes into more effective prognostic indicators and biomarkers for organ damage in sepsis. Nevertheless, further in-depth research is imperative to substantiate these possibilities. Large-scale clinical trials are necessary to validate the effectiveness and reliability of these new technologies and to compare them with existing sepsis biomarkers. Such comparisons will be instrumental in refining the diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for early intervention in sepsis patients. By addressing these research gaps and conducting comprehensive clinical validations, the medical community can increase the precision of sepsis diagnostics and treatment, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes. The integration of advanced exosome detection methods with the ongoing development of exosomal biomarkers is a promising avenue that warrants dedicated effort and resources to maximize its potential impact on sepsis management.
