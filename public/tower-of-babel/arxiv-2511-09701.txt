Optimal control of Volterra integral diffusions and application to contract theory
Dylan Possamaï, Mehdi Talbi

Abstract
This paper focuses on the optimal control of a class of stochastic Volterra integral equations. Here the coefficients are regular and not assumed to be of convolution type. We show that, under mild regularity assumptions, these equations can be lifted in a Sobolev space, whose Hilbertian structure allows us to attack the problem through a dynamic programming approach. We are then able to use the theory of viscosity solutions on Hilbert spaces to characterise the value function of the control problem as the unique solution of a parabolic equation on Sobolev space. We provide applications and examples to illustrate the usefulness of our theory, in particular for a certain class of time-inconsistent principal–agent problems. As a by-product of our analysis, we introduce a new Markovian approximation for Volterra-type dynamics.

Keywords.
Stochastic Volterra equations, optimal control, contract theory, stochastic target.

Introduction

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
denote a fixed probability space, endowed with a standard
(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
–Brownian motion
W

of dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
. We consider the following type of controlled stochastic Volterra integral equation

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\alpha}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b_{r}(t,X_{r}^{\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(t,X_{r}^{\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}W_{r},

(1.1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha
lives in an appropriate space of controls
{\cal A}
taking their values in
some Polish space
A
, and
b:[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma:[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times A\longrightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\cal M}_{d,n}(\mathbb{R})}
are continuous in all their variables. We are interested in the optimal control problem

\displaystyle V_{0}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},\alpha_{t})\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{T}^{\alpha})\bigg],

(1.2)

where
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
are Borel-measurable functions.

Given their large scope of applications, stochastic control problems of the form (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) have received strong attention in the scientific literature. They have for example raised interest in medical sciences (see
e.g.

Schmiegel 2006

[
37
]
and
Saeedian et al. 2017

[
36
]
) or in finance, in particular in the study of rough volatility models (see e.g.
Bayer et al. 2016

[
6
]
or
Gatheral et al. 2018

[
20
]
).

More recently,
Hernández and Possamaï 2024
showed in
[
26
]
that Volterra-type control problems naturally arise in contracting problem involving some form of time-inconsistency. These problems have quite specific features, and could be referred as
extended Volterra control problems
. More precisely, the state process in this class of problem is a uncountable family of processes
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}\coloneqq\{X^{\alpha,s}\}_{\{s\in[0,T]\}}
, with

X_{t}^{\alpha,s}=x^{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{r}(X_{r}^{\alpha,r},X_{r}^{\alpha,s},\alpha_{s})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(X_{r}^{\alpha,r},X_{r}^{\alpha,s},\alpha_{s})\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

(1.3)

Note in particular that, when
b
and
\sigma
do not depend on
X_{r}^{\alpha,r}
, the diagonal process
t\longmapsto X_{t}^{\alpha,t}
satisfies the stochastic Volterra-integral equation (
1.1
).

Volterra-type control problems have also attracted strong attention for their challenging mathematical features. Indeed, due to the presence of the
t
in
b
and
\sigma
, it is well known that the optimisation problem (
1.2
) is time inconsistent:
X^{\alpha}
is not Markov or not even a semi-martingale in general, and therefore the flow property does not apply. Various techniques have been considered to overcome this difficulty. One rather popular method is to handle the problem through a maximum principle approach, see
e.g.

Agram and Oksendal 2015

[
4
]
,
Agram et al. 2019

[
3
]
,
Lin and Yong 2020

[
29
]
and

Hamaguchi 2023b

[
23
]
. We also mention the recent contribution of
Cárdenas et al. 2022

[
11
]
, who search for an optimal control in a relaxed form.

A large number of papers focus on recovering time-consistency by embedding the problem in a larger space, in which the new state process satisfies the usual flow property. Those are often referred as using the
lifting
approach. In the contributions of
Abi Jaber et al. 2021

[
2
]
,
di Nunno and Giordano 2023

[
13
]
,
Hamaguchi 2023a

[
22
]
and
Gasteratos and Pannier 2025

[
19
]
, the kernel of the stochastic Volterra integral equation writes as the linear transform of some element defined on a appropriate Banach space (and even in a Hilbert space in the case of
[
22
]
and
[
19
]
). This linear transformation involves some semi-group structure, so that it is possible to write the state process
X^{\alpha}
as the image of an infinite dimensional process
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
by the same transformation, with
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
satisfying an infinite dimensional stochastic differential equation (SDE for short) and therefore satisfying the Markov property. In a slightly different approach,
Viens and Zhang 2019

[
40
]
lifts the state process—typically a fractional Brownian motion—in the Banach space of continuous path, treating the ‘Volterra time’ (the
t
in
b
and
\sigma
in (
1.1
)) as a parameter. This approach has been used in several subsequent works, such as the ones of
Wang et al. 2022

[
41
]
and
Wang et al. 2023

[
42
]
.

We propose a new lifting in the same spirit as
[
40
]
, in the case of regular kernels. More precisely, we also treat the ‘Volterra time’ as a parameter, thus lifting the state process in a space of paths. We shall however assume that the coefficients
b
and
\sigma
of the equation (
1.3
) are differentiable in this parameter, in the sense of Sobolev derivatives. This provides a Hilbertian structure as well as continuity in the parameter, which is crucial to connect the original problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) with the lifted one. We are then able to recover time-consistency for the lifted problem, and to study the control problem through a dynamic programming approach. In particular, we can resort to the standard theory of viscosity solutions on Hilbert spaces, see
e.g.

Lions 1988

[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
Inaddition, and unlike the existing works in the literature, our approach enables us to study extended Volterra control problems (
1.3
) and to treat the control of the ‘classical’ Volterra dynamics (
1.1
) as a special case.

The paper is organised as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce precisely the lifted space and process, and highlight their main properties. In
Section

3
, the value function of the infinite dimensional problem is characterised as the unique viscosity solution to a parabolic equation on Sobolev space. A special attention is also given to the case of uncontrolled volatility. We apply our theory to some examples in
Section

4
, with a particular emphasis on the contracting problem with sophisticated agent.
Section

5
discusses an interest by-product of our analysis, which is a new Markovian representation of stochastic Volterra processes. Finally,
Section

6
compares our contribution with some of the aforementioned references and
Section

7
discusses the case of singular kernels.

2
The infinite dimensional problem

Our main requirements to define a ‘good’ lifting are the following

\quad\bullet
the state space must be a Hilbert space, and the lifted state process must satisfy some flow property, as this will enable us to apply the standard theory of viscosity solutions on Hilbert space for a large class of stochastic control problems;

\quad\bullet
if the original process writes as (
1.1
), it must write as a continuous function of the lifted process.

2.1
Choice of the state space

Let
\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T],\mathrm{d}t)
denote the equivalence class of square integrable functions
\varphi:[0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
C_{c}^{1}((0,T))
the set of
C^{1}
functions
\psi
on
(0,T)
such that
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
have compact support.
Introduce the Sobolev space

\displaystyle W^{1,2}([0,T])\coloneqq\bigg\{u\in\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T],\mathrm{d}t):\exists u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T],\mathrm{d}t),\;\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}u^{\prime}(t)\varphi(t)\mathrm{d}t,\;\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}((0,T))\bigg\},

(2.1)

as well as its scalar product
\langle u,v\rangle_{W^{\text{$1$}\text{$,$}\text{$2$}}}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}u(t)v(t)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}u^{\prime}(t)v^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t
. Let
H
be the space defined by

H\coloneqq\big\{{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}):{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in W^{1,2}([0,T])\ \forall\ k\in\{1,\dots,n\}\big\},

endowed with the scalar product

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle{\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\rangle_{W^{\text{$1$}\text{$,$}\text{$2$}}},\;(x,y)\in H^{2},

and the corresponding norm
\lVert{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{H}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{H}}
,
x\in H
.

(H,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H})
is a Hilbert space, which will be fundamental for us when resorting to the theory of viscosity solutions; furthermore, we have the following important result, which is crucial for the well-posedness of our lifted problem. This uses the notation
{\cal C}\coloneqq C^{0}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for the space of continuous functions from
[0,T]
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have
H\subset{\cal C}
. Moreover, the injection is compact, that is to say that there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\lVert{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{H},\;\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in H,

where
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
denotes the continuous representative of
{\mathbf{x}}
.

Proof.

We prove the result in the case
n=1
. The general result is recovered by reiterating the proof coordinate-wise. Each element
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
has a continuous representative
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in{\cal C}
(see
e.g.

Brézis 2011

[
9
, Theorem 8.2]
). Moreover, for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
, we have for any
s\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}\rvert+\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{T}|{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t)|\mathrm{d}t+\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}\rvert,

where we denote
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathbf{x}}(s)
for all
s\in[0,T]
. Therefore, integrating with respect to
s
leads to

\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{T}\lvert{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}(t)\rvert\mathrm{d}r+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert{\mathbf{x}}(r)\rvert\mathrm{d}r.

Thus, taking the supremum over
t
on the left-hand side and using Cauchy–Schwarz’s inequality and standard concavity inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\lVert{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{H},

(2.2)

for some constant
C
depending only on
T
.

Remark 2.2
.

As a consequence of the above estimate, the mapping

\Phi:[0,T]\times H\ni(t,{\mathbf{x}})\longmapsto\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is continuous and even Lipschitz-continuous in
{\mathbf{x}}
.

2.2
The infinite dimensional dynamics

Let
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times A\longrightarrow{\cal M}_{d,n}(\mathbb{R})
, and introduce the
H
-valued mapping

B(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)(\cdot)\coloneqq b_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a),

as well as the
{\cal L}(\mathbb{R}^{d},H)
-valued mapping

\Sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)(\cdot)\coloneqq\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a),

for all
(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\in[0,T]\times H\times A
, with
{\cal L}(\mathbb{R}^{d},H)
being the set of linear functions from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
H
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space endowed with a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
W
, and denote by
\mathbb{F}^{W}
its natural filtration. Denote by
{\cal A}^{W}
the set of
\mathbb{F}^{W}
–progressively measurable processes taking their values in
A
such that the
H
-valued SDE

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\alpha}={\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;t\in[0,T].\;\mathbb{P}--\text{\rm a.s.},

(2.3)

has a unique
strong
solution. Our first result provides
concrete
sufficient conditions to guarantee the existence and the uniqueness of a
H
-valued solution to (
2.3
), at least whenever the control
\alpha
is constant. The point here is that standard Lipschitz-continuity may not be sufficient in general, unlike in the finite-dimensional case.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
\phi\in\{b,\sigma\}
, assume that there exists two functions
\phi^{1}
and
\phi^{2}
such that

\phi_{t}(s,x,y,a)=\phi_{t}^{1}(s,x,a)+\phi_{t}^{2}(s,a)y,\;\mbox{\rm for all $(t,s,x,y,a)\in[0,T]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times A$}.

Assume furthermore that

(i)

\phi^{1}
is continuous in all its variables, Lipschitz-continuous in
x
uniformly in
(t,s,a)
, and admits a Sobolev derivative w.r.t.
s
which is also continuous in all its variables and Lipschitz-continuous in
x
uniformly in
(t,s,a);

(ii)

\phi^{2}
is continuous and uniformly bounded in all its variables, and admits a Sobolev derivative w.r.t.
s
which is also continuous and uniformly bounded in all its variables.

Then (
2.3
) has a unique solution in
H
.

Proof.

We check the assumptions of
Gawarecki and Mandrekar 2011

[
21
, Theorem 3.3]
.
For simplicity, we assume without loss of generality that
n=1
; the adaptation of the proof for the general multidimensional case is straightforward.
We first verify that
B
and
\Sigma
take their values in
H
whenever
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
. Indeed, we have, for all
s\in[0,T]
,

B(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)=b_{t}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s},a)=b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)+b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}.

By the assumptions made above and the fact that
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
, it is clear that
s\longmapsto b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)
and
s\longmapsto b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}
admit Sobolev derivatives. Therefore,
B
takes its values in
H
. We handle the case of
\Sigma
in the exact same way.

We secondly verify that
B
and
\Sigma
have linear growth in
{\mathbf{x}}
, uniformly in
(t,a)
. We denote by
\partial_{s}
the derivation w.r.t.
s
in the Sobolev sense. We have

\displaystyle\lVert B(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\big(b_{t}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s},a)\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\big(\partial_{s}(b_{t}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s},a))\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\big(b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)+b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\big(\partial_{s}b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)+\partial_{s}b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}+b_{t}^{2}(s,a)(\widetilde{\partial_{s}{\mathbf{x}}})^{s}\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Since
b^{1}
and
\partial_{s}b^{1}
are Lipschitz-continuous in their space variable, uniformly in
(t,s,a)
, we have for some constant
C

\lvert b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)\rvert+\lvert\partial_{s}b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)\rvert\leq C(1+\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\rvert)\leq C(1+\lVert{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{H}),

see (
2.2
) for the latter inequality. Then, we easily deduce from the boundedness of
b_{t}^{2}
and
\partial_{s}b_{t}^{2}
that
B
, and similarly
\Sigma
, have quadratic growth in
{\mathbf{x}}
.

We finally prove that
B
and
\Sigma
are Lipschitz-continuous in
{\mathbf{x}}
. Fix
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in H^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert B(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)-B(t,{\mathbf{y}},a)\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\big(\lvert b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)-b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{t},a)\rvert+|b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}-b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{s}|\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\partial_{s}b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)-\partial_{s}b_{t}^{1}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},a)|+|\partial_{s}b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}-\partial_{s}b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}|

\displaystyle\qquad+|b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\widetilde{\partial_{s}{\mathbf{x}}}^{s}-b_{t}^{2}(s,a)\widetilde{\partial_{s}{\mathbf{y}}}^{s}|\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{t}|+|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{s}|\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{s}|+|\widetilde{\partial_{s}{\mathbf{x}}}^{s}-\widetilde{\partial_{s}{\mathbf{y}}}^{s}|\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg),

for some constant
C>0
, where we used the Lipschitz-continuity of
b^{1}
and
\partial_{s}b^{1}
and the boundedness of
b^{2}
and
\partial_{s}b^{2}
. Recalling (
2.2
), we have

|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{t}|\leq\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert{\mathbf{x}}^{t}-{\mathbf{y}}^{t}\rVert_{H},

from which we finally deduce that
B
is Lipschitz-continuous in
{\mathbf{x}}
, uniformly in
(t,a)
.
We proceed similarly for
\Sigma
.

.

The linear dependence on
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{s}
is required to obtain the Lipschitz-continuity of the coefficients. Without this, we could still obtain existence of weak solutions
(
in the sense of martingale problems
)
, see

. Note that when

, the term in

), and therefore the well-posedness of (

).

Remark 2.5
.

This infinite dimensional process enjoys two important properties regarding the original Volterra-type dynamics (

)

(i)

is solution to a stochastic differential equation, whereas the process

(ii)
for

, denote
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbf{p}}(x)}
the element of

such that
\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}(x)^{t}=x
for all

. Assume
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}^{\alpha}={\mathbf{p}}(x)
, and that

and

write as in

. Then, the diagonal process

satisfies

(
1.1
). Therefore, if uniqueness holds for the latter, the diagonal process is equal to the original controlled dynamics,
\mathbb{P}
–
a.s.
The question of the existence and uniqueness of solutions of (

) has been studied for example by

[

]

(
see also

[

]
and

]
)
, and is mostly obtained by assuming that

and

’

2.3
The infinite dimensional control problem

From now, for
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
, we shall abuse the notation and still denote by
{\mathbf{x}}
its continuous representative. Given the dynamics (
2.3
), we consider the control problem

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{0}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}^{\text{$W$}}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}F(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r+G({\mathbf{X}}_{T}^{\alpha})\bigg],

for some

and

. The following result states that this control problem is connected to the control problem of a Volterra-type SDE (

) in the following way.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume
b
and
\sigma
write as in
Equation

1.1
, and that
F(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)=f(t,x^{t},a)
and

for all

, with

and

also from

. Assume furthermore that uniqueness holds for (

). Then

.

Proof.

As pointed in
Section

, when the initial condition of the lifted process
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
is
{\mathbf{p}}(x)
, and when uniqueness holds for
Equation

1.1
, the diagonal process pf
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
and the original Volterra-type dynamics

are equal

–a.s. By definition of

and

, and since we optimise on the same set

Remark 2.7

(Choice of the set of controls)
.

The choice of the set of controls is crucial for the above proposition. Indeed, if we choose to consider closed-loop controls for either (

) or (
2.4
), then we might have

, as the filtration generated by

, the filtration generated by

are different in general. However, if we consider the infinite dimensional control problem (

) as an object on its own—for example motivated by the study of moral hazard questions for time-inconsistent agents, see
[

]
—then we may either consider closed-loop or open-loop controls: both cases can be encapsulated in our dynamic programming approach.

3
Dynamic programming equation

3.1
The value function

We introduce a dynamic version of the control problem (
2.4
). Denoting by
{\mathbf{X}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}
the solution of (
2.3
) such that
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}={\mathbf{x}}
, we define:

\displaystyle{\cal V}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}F(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r+G({\mathbf{X}}_{T}^{\alpha,t,{\mathbf{x}}})\bigg],\;\mbox{for all $(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H$}.

(3.1)

Proposition 3.1

(Regularity of the value function)
.

Assume that

(i)

F
is uniformly continuous in
(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times B_{H}(0,R)
, uniformly in
a\in A
, for all
R\geq 0
, where
B_{H}(0,R)
denotes the ball of radius
R
and centre
0
for the metric
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H};

(ii)

G
is uniformly continuous in
{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{H}(0,R)
for all
R\geq 0;

(iii)

F
and
G
have polynomial growth in
{\mathbf{x}}
, uniformly in the other variables.

Then the value function
{\cal V}
is uniformly continuous on all the sets
[0,T]\times B_{H}(0,R)
,
R\geq 0
, and has polynomial growth in
{\mathbf{x}}
uniformly in
t
.

Proof.

This a direct application of
[
17
, Proposition 3.61]
.

Our lifted control problem (
2.4
) falls under the scope of Markovian control problems on Hilbert spaces, and we may therefore naturally formulate the following dynamic programming principle.

Proposition 3.2

(Dynamic programming principle)
.

Under the assumptions of
Section

3.1
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal V}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\theta}F\big(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{r}\big)\mathrm{d}r+{\cal V}(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha})\bigg],\;\forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times H,\;\text{\rm and}\;\theta\in{\cal T}_{t,T},

(3.2)

where
{\cal T}_{t,T}
denotes the set of
[t,T]
-valued
\mathbb{F}
–stopping times.

Proof.

This is a direct application of
[
17
, Proposition 2.24]
.

3.2
Viscosity solutions

For any smooth
\varphi:[0,T]\times H\longmapsto\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\partial_{t}\varphi
the derivative of
\varphi
with respect to
t\in[0,T]
, and by
D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi
and
D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}\varphi
the first- and second-order Fréchet derivatives of
\varphi
with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
. For all
(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H
, by Riesz’s representation theorem,
D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})
can be identified to an element of
H
, and
D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})
to an endomorphism of
H
.

The purpose of this section is to show that the value function
{\cal V}
of (
2.4
) can be characterised as the unique viscosity solution of the dynamic programming equation

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\sup_{a\in A}\bigg\{\big\langle D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),b_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a),D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\sigma(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+F(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\bigg\}=0,

(3.3)

with terminal condition
u|_{t=T}=G
.

Definition 3.3

(Viscosity solutions)
.

Let
u:[0,T]\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be locally bounded.

(i)

u
is said to be a viscosity super-solution of (
3.3
) if
u(T,\cdot)\geq G
and, for all
\varphi\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times H)
such that
u-\varphi
has a local minimum in
(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, we have

-\partial_{t}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\sup_{a\in A}\bigg\{\big\langle D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}}),b_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a),D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})\sigma(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+F(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\bigg\}\geq 0.

(ii)

u
is said to be a viscosity sub-solution of (
3.3
) if
u(T,\cdot)\leq G
and, for all
\varphi\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times H)
such that
u-\varphi
has a local maximum in
(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, we have

-\partial_{t}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\sup_{a\in A}\bigg\{\big\langle D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}}),b_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a),D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})\sigma(\cdot,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t},{\mathbf{x}}^{\cdot},a)\big\rangle_{H}+F(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\bigg\}\leq 0.

(iii)

u
is said to be a viscosity solution of (
3.3
) if it is both a viscosity super-solution and viscosity sub-solution of (
3.3
).

Then, applying standard viscosity theory on Hilbert space (see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 3.67]
), we may formulate the following characterisation of
{\cal V}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that

(i)

t\longmapsto B_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)
is continuous, uniformly in
({\mathbf{x}},a)\in B_{H}(0,R)\times A
for all
R>0;

(ii)

\sigma
has linear growth in
\tilde{x}^{t}
and
\tilde{x}^{s}
, uniformly in the other variables.

Then
{\cal V}
is the unique continuous viscosity solution of (
3.3
) with polynomial growth.

Proof.

Let
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\text{$\star$}}}
be an orthonormal basis of
H
. We essentially have to check
[
17
, Assumption (3.155)]
, that is

\underset{N\to\infty}{\lim}\sup_{a\in A}\Big\{{\rm Tr}\big[\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)^{\top}{\cal Q}_{N}\big]\Big\}=0,\;\forall(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H,

(3.4)

where
{\cal Q}_{N}
is the orthonormal projection onto the family
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\text{$\star$}}\setminus\{1,\dots,N\}}
. Note that
\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)^{\top}
corresponds to the endomorphism of
H

{\mathbf{y}}\longmapsto\langle\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{x}^{t},\tilde{x}^{\cdot},a),{\mathbf{y}}\rangle_{H}\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{x}^{t},\tilde{x}^{\cdot},a).

Then, denoting by
\sigma^{k}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)
the projection of
\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)
onto
e_{k}
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, we have

{\rm Tr}\big[\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)^{\top}{\cal Q}_{N}\big]=\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}|\sigma^{k}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)|^{2}.

However, we have

|\sigma^{k}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)|=\lvert\langle\sigma_{t}(\cdot,\tilde{x}^{t},\tilde{x}^{\cdot},a),e_{k}\rangle_{H}\rvert\leq C(1+|\tilde{x}^{t}|)|\langle 1,e_{k}\rangle_{H}|+|\langle{\mathbf{x}},e_{k}\rangle_{H}|,

and therefore

{\rm Tr}\big[\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)^{\top}{\cal Q}_{N}\big]\leq 2(1+|\tilde{x}^{t}|)^{2}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}|\langle 1,e_{k}\rangle_{H}|^{2}+2\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}|\langle{\mathbf{x}},e_{k}\rangle_{H}|^{2}.

Since both
1
(as a constant mapping) and
{\mathbf{x}}
belong to
H
, the two sums on the right-hand side go to
0
as
N\longrightarrow\infty
. Since this term is independent from
a
, we deduce that (
3.4
) holds true, and we may therefore conclude by applying
[
17
, Theorem 3.67]
.

3.3
The case of uncontrolled volatility

In this paragraph, we assume that
\sigma
does not depend on
a
, and that there exists a bounded
\theta:[0,T]\times H\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
B_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)=\Gamma_{t}({\mathbf{x}})+\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}})\theta_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)
. We show that the value function of the infinite dimensional control problem can be expressed as the solution of a backward SDE. To this end, we reformulate the lifted control problem in weak formulation. Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be the unique strong solution of the
H
-valued SDE

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$--a.s.}

Let
\alpha\in{\cal A}
. By the existence of the function
\theta
introduced above, it follows from Girsanov’s theorem that there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
equivalent to
\mathbb{P}
such that

W_{t}^{\alpha}\coloneqq W_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r,

is a
\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
–Brownian motion. Therefore

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r})dr+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r})dW_{r}^{\alpha},\;\mbox{$\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}$--a.s.},

and we may reformulate the control problem in the following way

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{0}^{w}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})=\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}F_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r})dr+G({\mathbf{X}}_{r})\bigg].

(3.5)

We can easily see that an analogue of
Section

2.3
holds true here; indeed, if
X
writes

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{r}(t,X_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$--a.s.,}

and

V_{0}(x_{0})\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}f_{r}(X_{r},\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r+g(X_{T})\bigg],

then
V_{0}(x_{0})={\cal V}_{0}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})
whenever
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}={\mathbf{p}}(x_{0})
. Let now
{\cal H}:[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the Hamiltonian defined by
{\cal H}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z)=\sup_{a\in A}\{z\theta_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)+F_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)\}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that

(i)

{\cal H}
is Lipschitz-continuous in
z;

(ii)

F
has linear growth in
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
, uniformly in the other variables
;

(iii)

\Gamma
and
\Sigma
are Lipschitz-continuous in
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
.

Then
{\cal V}_{0}=Y_{0}
, where
(Y,Z)
is the unique solution of the backward
SDE

Y_{t}=G({\mathbf{X}}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}{\cal H}_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},Z_{r})\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

Furthermore, if there exists a measurable mapping
\psi:[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow A
such that

{\cal H}(t,{\mathbf{x}},z)=z\theta_{t}\big({\mathbf{x}},\psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z)\big)+F_{t}\big({\mathbf{x}},\psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z)\big),

(3.6)

then
\alpha_{t}^{*}\coloneqq\psi_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{t},Z_{t})
is an optimal control for (
3.5
).

Proof.

For
\alpha\in{\cal A}
, let
(Y^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha})
denote the solution of the backward SDE:

Y_{t}^{\alpha}=G({\mathbf{X}}_{T})+\int_{t}^{T}\big(F_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r})+Z_{r}^{\alpha}\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r})\big)\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

By the Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions made on
{\cal H}
,
\Gamma
,
\Sigma
and
F
and the boundedness of
\theta
, there exists a unique solution
(Y^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha})
to be above equation (see e.g.
Pardoux and Peng 1990

[
33
]
,
El Karoui et al. 1997

[
16
]
or
Zhang 2017

[
43
]
).
As the equations solved by
(Y,Z)
and
(Y^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha})
satisfy the usual Lipschitz and measurability conditions, and by definition of
{\cal H}
, the comparison principle for backward SDEs ensures that
Y_{0}^{\alpha}\leq Y_{0}
. Since
\alpha
is arbitrary, this shows that
{\cal V}_{0}\leq Y_{0}
.

Fix now
\varepsilon>0
. By measurable selection, there exists a measurable mapping
\psi^{\varepsilon}:[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

{\cal H}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z)\leq F_{t}({\mathbf{x}},\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z))+z\theta_{t}({\mathbf{x}},\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t}({\mathbf{x}},z))+\varepsilon.

Introducing
\alpha_{t}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\psi^{\varepsilon}_{t}({\mathbf{X}}_{t},Z_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle Y_{t}-Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\big({\cal H}_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},Z_{r})-F_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}}\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})\big)\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}\big(Z_{r}-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}})\mathrm{d}W_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\Big(\big({\cal H}_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},Z_{r})-F_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})-Z_{r}\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})\big)+(Z_{r}-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}})\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})\Big)\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}\big(Z_{r}-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}})\mathrm{d}W_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\big({\cal H}_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},Z_{r})-F_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})-Z_{r}\theta_{r}({\mathbf{X}}_{r},\alpha_{r}^{\varepsilon})\big)\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}\big(Z_{r}-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}})\mathrm{d}W_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon(T-t)-\int_{t}^{T}\big(Z_{r}-Z_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}})\mathrm{d}W_{r}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}}.

Thus, we have
Y_{0}\leq Y_{0}^{\alpha^{\text{$\varepsilon$}}}+T\varepsilon
. By arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
, this implies that
Y_{0}\leq{\cal V}_{0}
, and therefore the desired equality holds true. In particular, when (
3.6
) holds, we have
{\cal V}_{0}^{w}=Y_{0}=Y_{0}^{\alpha^{\text{$\star$}}}
, which means that
\alpha^{\star}
is an optimal control.

Remark 3.6
.

Let us discuss what the assumption
B_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)=\Gamma_{t}({\mathbf{x}})+\Sigma_{t}({\mathbf{x}})\theta_{t}({\mathbf{x}},a)
means in the context of the control of stochastic Volterra integral equations. If one wants to be able to apply Girsanov’s theorem, the real-valued mapping
\theta
must only depends on
{\mathbf{x}}
and the
‘
regular time
’

t
, and not on the
‘
Volterra time
’

s
. This means that the dependency on
s
must be the same
b
and
\sigma
. This is for instance the case for the following dynamics, considered by
di Nunno and Giordano 2023

[
13
]

X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-r)\Big(\big(b^{1}_{r}(X_{r})+\sigma_{r}(X_{r})b^{2}_{r}(X_{r},\alpha_{r})\big)\mathrm{d}r+\sigma_{r}(X_{r})\mathrm{d}W_{r}\Big).

(3.7)

Therefore, this approach cannot be applied when the volatility is not Volterra, as it would enforces the drift to be standard as well.

4
Examples

4.1
A—very—simple starter

We start with a simple example to familiarise the reader with our methodology. Consider the uncontrolled non-Volterra SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=X_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

The corresponding lifted dynamics (starting at time
t
at some
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
) is given by the flow
{\mathbf{X}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}}}\coloneqq(X^{t,{\mathbf{x}},s})_{0\leq s\leq T}
such that

X_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},s}={\mathbf{x}}^{s}+\int_{t}^{r}X_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta+W_{\theta}-W_{t},\;\mbox{for all $s\in[0,T]$}.

Our goal is to show that the function
u
defined by

u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[X_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},T}\big],

satisfies the dynamic programming equation (
3.3
). For a fixed
t
, we introduce the function
y(s)\coloneqq\int_{t}^{s}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[X_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\theta}\big]\mathrm{d}\theta
, for
s\in[t,T]
. We easily see that
y
satisfies the ODE

y^{\prime}(s)=x^{s}+y(s),\;\mbox{with $y(t)=0$},

from which we deduce that
y(s)=\mathrm{e}^{s}\int_{t}^{s}x^{r}\mathrm{e}^{r}\mathrm{d}r
, and finally that

u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=y(T)=x^{T}+\mathrm{e}^{T}\int_{t}^{T}x^{s}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\mathrm{d}s.

Note that this can be rewritten

u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{0}^{T}x^{s}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\mathbf{1}_{[t,T]}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(4.1)

We then compute
\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=-x^{t}\mathrm{e}^{T-t}
,
and, observing that the Fréchet derivative of
u
with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}
can be identified to the càdlàg function

D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(s)=\mathrm{e}^{T-s}\mathbf{1}_{[t,T]}(s).

We emphasise that this is simply a convenient representation of the differential of
u
, which is strictly speaking a linear form on
H
. According to Riesz’s representation theorem, we could also write
D_{\mathbf{x}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
as an element of
H
. However, this would not be of any use in the present context where
u
is smooth. We recall that the lifting on the Sobolev space is essentially a way to apply the standard infinite dimensional viscosity solutions theory to our problem, which has no reason to be involved when we look for smooth value functions.

Next we compute

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(s)x^{t}\mu(\mathrm{d}s)

\displaystyle=-x^{t}\mathrm{e}^{T-t}+\int_{t}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{T-s}\mu(\mathrm{d}s)

\displaystyle=-x^{t}\mathrm{e}^{T-t}+\int_{t}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{T-s}\mathrm{d}s+1=-x^{t}e^{T-t}+(\mathrm{e}^{T-s}-1)+1=0.

Thus
u
satisfies (
3.3
).

4.2
Linear–quadratic control problem with kernel

Let
\phi:[0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous. We consider the controlled Volterra-type dynamics

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\phi(t-s)\big((X_{s}+\alpha_{s})\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big),

and the control problem

V_{0}\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(X_{s}^{2}+\alpha_{s}^{2})\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

The corresponding lifted problem writes

V(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big((X_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},s})^{2}+\alpha_{s}^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}s\bigg],

(4.2)

where the flow
{\mathbf{X}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}}}\coloneqq(X^{t,{\mathbf{x}},s})_{0\leq s\leq T}
is such that

X_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},s}={\mathbf{x}}^{s}+\int_{t}^{r}\phi(s-t)\big((X_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\theta}+\alpha_{\theta})\mathrm{d}\theta+\mathrm{d}W_{\theta}\big),\;\mbox{for all $s\in[0,T]$}.

We easily see that the dynamic programming equation corresponding to this problem is

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r,s)\phi(r-t)\phi(s-t)\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s+x^{t}\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r)\phi(r-t)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r)\phi(r-t)\mathrm{d}r\bigg)^{2}+\frac{(x^{t})^{2}}{2}=0,

(4.3)

with boundary condition
u(T,\cdot)=0
, as there is no terminal reward. Here we used Lebesgue measure instead of
\mu
as reference measure (since there is no terminal reward) and
\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T],\mathrm{d}t)
as reference space to represent the Fréchet derivatives of
u
(which does not make a difference in the context of classical solutions).

Our objective is to find a solution
u
of the form

u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}c(t,r,s)x^{r}x^{s}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s,

where
c
is a measurable
\mathbb{R}
-valued function defined on
[0,T]^{3}
, which is symmetric in its last two variables. Let us compute formally the derivatives of
u

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}\partial_{t}c(t,r,s)x^{r}x^{s}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s,\;D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r)=\int_{0}^{T}c(t,r,s)x^{s}\mathrm{d}s,\;D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r,s)=c(t,r,s).

Observing that
x^{t}=\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{t}(s)\mathrm{d}s
, where
\delta_{t}
is the Dirac mass at
t
, we also compute

\displaystyle x^{t}\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r)\phi(r-t)\mathrm{d}r=\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}c(t,r,s)\phi(r-t)\delta_{t}(s)x^{r}x^{s}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s,\;(x^{t})^{2}=\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}\delta_{t}(r)\delta_{t}(s)x^{r}x^{s}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s,

and

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})(r)\phi(r-t)\mathrm{d}r\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{[0,T]^{\text{$2$}}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}c(t,r,\theta)\phi(\theta-t)\mathrm{d}\theta\bigg)\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}c(t,\tau,s)\phi(\tau-t)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg)x^{r}x^{s}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s.

Introduce the following notations

(c\star\phi)(t,r)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}c(t,r,\theta)\phi(\theta-t)\mathrm{d}\theta.

Note that, since
c
is symmetric in
r
and
s
, we also have

(c\star\phi)(t,s)=\int_{0}^{T}c(t,\tau,s)\phi(\tau-t)\mathrm{d}\tau.
Plugging all these expressions in the dynamic programming equation (
4.2
), we see that
a
,
b
and
c
satisfy the following equation

\partial_{t}c(t,r,s)=-(c\star\phi)(t,r)(c\star\phi)(t,s)-2\delta_{t}(s)(c\star\phi)(t,r)-\delta_{t}(r)\delta_{t}(s),

(4.4)

with terminal conditions
c(T,\cdot,\cdot)=0
. Intuitively, it makes sense to look for
c
such that

c(t,r,s)=0,\;\mbox{whenever $(r,s)\notin[t,T]^{2}$},

since the Volterra dynamics that we consider is ‘triangular (
i.e.
, the ‘Volterra time is always greater than
t
for the problem starting at time
t
). So we expect
c(t,\cdot,\cdot)
to be zero on
[(t,T]^{2})^{c}
and to be smooth on
[t,T]^{2}
. This should roughly be a structure similar to (
4.1
) from the previous section, where

u(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{0}^{T}b(t,s)x^{s}\mu(\mathrm{d}s)

with
b(t,s)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\mathbf{1}_{[t,T]}(s)
, except that this time the function still depends on
t
on
[t,T]
, as a consequence of the Volterra structure. Then the size of the Dirac masses in (
4.4
) accounts for the boundary conditions of
c
in
t
, that is the values of
c(t,t,\cdot)=c(t,\cdot,t)
and
c(t,t,t)
. Finally, our infinite dimensional Riccati system takes the following form

\partial_{t}c(t,r,s)=-(c\star\phi)(t,r)(c\star\phi)(t,s),\;\mbox{for all $(s,r)\in(t,T]^{2}$,}

(4.5)

with boundary conditions

\begin{cases}c(t,t,r)=c(t,r,t)=2(c\star\phi)(t,r),\;\mbox{for all $r\in(t,T]$,}\\
c(t,t,t)=1,\\
c(T,\cdot,\cdot)=0.\end{cases}

(4.6)

Note that
a=b=0
satisfy the two first equalities, and that the third one only depends on
c
, thus the system becomes

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}c(t,r,s)=-(c\star\phi)(t,r)(c\star\phi)(t,s),\\
c(t,t,r)=c(t,r,t)=2(c\star\phi)(t,r),\\
c(t,t,t)=1,\\
c(T,\cdot,\cdot)=0,\end{cases}\mbox{for all $(s,r)\in(t,T]^{2}$.}

(4.7)

Remark 4.1

[

]
, although we chose to consider simpler coefficients for the sake of clarity. Note also that (

in

as well as

4.3
Time-inconsistent contract theory

One of our main motivation is related to the works of
Hernández and Possamaï 2024

[
25
,
26
]
, which focuses on principal–agent contracting problems in presence of a form of time-inconsistency in the agent’s problem. We first recall the setting of the problem and the main results of
[
25
,
26
]
when the time-inconsistency is due to the presence of a non-exponential discount factor.

4.3.1
The agent’s and principal’s problems

Given an output process

X_{t}^{\alpha}\coloneqq X_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\alpha_{r}\mathrm{d}r+W_{t},

where the effort
\alpha
takes its value in some compact
[0,\bar{a}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and a payment
\xi
given by the principal, the agent wants to solve the control problem

V_{0}^{\text{$\rm A$}}(\xi)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[U_{\text{$\rm A$}}(0,\xi)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}c_{r}(0,\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r\bigg],

where
U_{\text{$\rm A$}}
corresponds to his utility function,
c
his cost function and
f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
to the (possibly non-exponential) discount factor. The dynamic version of the agent’s problem,
i.e.
the control problem seen from any date
t\in[0,T]
, takes the following form

V_{t}^{\text{$\rm A$}}(\xi)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[U_{\text{$\rm A$}}(t,\xi)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}c_{r}(t,\alpha_{r})\mathrm{d}r\bigg].

Clearly, such a problem may not be handled through the traditional dynamic programming approach. Instead, the authors of
[
25
]
assume that the agent plays a leader–follower game with the future versions of himself, therefore looking for a Stackelberg equilibrium, see
[
25
, Definition 2.6]
. In particular, they show that

V_{t}^{\text{$\rm A$}}(\xi)=Y_{t}^{t},

where the family of processes
(Y^{s})_{\{s\in[0,T]\}}
satisfies the backward system

Y_{t}^{s}=U_{\text{$\rm A$}}(s,\xi)-\int_{t}^{T}c_{r}^{\star}(s,Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{r}^{s}\mathrm{d}W_{r},

with
c_{r}^{\star}(z)\coloneqq c_{r}(a_{r}^{\star}(z))
, where
a^{\star}
corresponds to a Stackelberg equilibrium. Assuming
c
is continuous in both
r
and
a
and non-negative, we observe that, by boundedness of the controls
\alpha
,
c^{\star}
takes its values in some compact
[0,\bar{c}]
.
In the spirit of
[
12
]
, this system is rewritten in a forward way and the principal optimises on
\xi
by maximising with respect to
Z
and
Y_{0}

V^{\text{$\rm P$}}=\sup_{\{\xi:V^{\text{$\rm A$}}(\xi)\geq R\}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\big[U_{\text{$\rm P$}}(X_{T},\xi)\big]=\sup_{Y_{0}^{\cdot}\geq R}V(Y_{0}^{\cdot}),

where
R
is the participation constraint
(i.e.
the minimal utility guaranteed to the agent so that he accepts to sign the contract), and
V
is defined by

V(Y_{0}^{\cdot})\coloneqq\sup_{Z\in{\cal Z}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[U_{\text{$\rm P$}}(X_{T}^{Z},Y_{T}^{0,Z})\big],

(4.8)

where
Y^{s,Z}
has the forward dynamics under
\mathbb{P}

Y_{t}^{s,Z}=Y_{0}^{s}+\int_{0}^{t}c_{r}^{\star}(s,Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{r}^{s}\mathrm{d}W_{r},

(4.9)

and
{\cal Z}
is the set of square integrable doubly indexed processes such that

U_{\text{$\rm A$}}^{(-1)}(s,Y_{T}^{s})=U_{\text{$\rm A$}}^{(-1)}(0,Y_{T}^{0}),\;\forall s\in[0,T],

(4.10)

where
U_{\text{$\rm A$}}^{(-1)}
denotes the inverse of
U_{\text{$\rm A$}}
with respect to the second variable. Our goal is to study the control problem (
4.8
) by using the setting developed in the present paper.

4.3.2
Reformulation as a control problem with stochastic target constraints

We assume that the control
{\mathbf{Z}}\coloneqq\{Z^{s}\}_{\{s\in[0,T]\}}
is such that
{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}\in H
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
It is clear that those conditions ensure that
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\mathbf{Z}}\coloneqq(Y^{Z,s})_{\{s\in[0,T]\}}
takes its values in
H
as well. We then rewrite the principal problem as

V({\mathbf{y}})=\sup_{{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\cal Z}_{\text{$H$}}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[U_{\text{$\rm P$}}(X,Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}})\big],

(4.11)

where
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{Y}}_{0}^{{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
{\cal Z}_{H}
is the set of square integrable
H
-valued processes such that (
4.10
) is satisfied, or equivalently (abusing the notation and denoting a Sobolev function and its continuous representative the same way)

Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}}=\psi\big(s,Y_{T}^{s,{\mathbf{Z}}}\big),\;\forall s\in[0,T],

with
\psi(s,y)\coloneqq U_{A}\big(0,U_{A}^{(-1)}(s,y)\big)
for all
(s,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the Principal must solve a stochastic control problem with stochastic target constraints on a Hilbert space. Note that this constraint is equivalent to

\overline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{\mathbf{Z}})\leq Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}}\leq\underline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{\mathbf{Z}}),

(4.12)

with

\underline{g}({\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\min_{0\leq s\leq T}\psi(s,\tilde{x}^{s}),\;\mbox{and}\;\overline{g}({\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\max_{0\leq s\leq T}\psi(s,\tilde{x}^{s}).

Reachability set.

The first step for the principal is to determine her reachability set,
i.e.
, the family of sets
{\cal V}(t),t\in[0,T]
in which the state process
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\mathbf{Z}}
must lie at each time
t
so that the target (
4.12
) can be reached. In general, and following the original ideas of

[]
in the finite-dimensional case (and more recently of

[]
in a particular infinite-dimensional case, namely the Wasserstein space), we expect to be able to relate the following map

w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\coloneqq\inf\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\underline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}})\geq\hat{Y}_{T}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}\geq\overline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}),\;\mbox{for some}\;{\mathbf{Z}}\big\},

to its associated geometric dynamic programming equation—typically in the sense of discontinuous viscosity solutions—which we conjecture to be

-\partial_{t}u+\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{y}},D_{\mathbf{y}}u(t,{\mathbf{y}}))}\Big\{-\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}u(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}u(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\Big\}=0,

(4.13)

where

{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{p}})\coloneqq\{{\mathbf{z}}\in H:\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{\mathbf{y}}u(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}=0\}.

This equation is an infinite dimensional extension of the one of
Soner and Touzi 2002a

[
38
]
. We prove in
Appendix

A
that the value function of epigraph-type stochastic target problems in Hilbert spaces is indeed a solution to a geometric PDE. Deriving the more general equation (
4.13
) from the geometric dynamic principle in our context is much more complex, and beyond the scope of the present paper. As such, in the rest of this section, we discuss how we conjecture that the general problem can be related to the epigraph case (though we insist that the arguments coming later for the principal–agent problem do not rely on the conjecture below).

As explained above, it is actually natural to expect that (
4.12
) will involve, at least intuitively, the two following epigraph-type target problems

\displaystyle\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\hat{Y}_{T}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}\geq\overline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}),\;\mbox{for some}\;{\mathbf{Z}}\big\},

\displaystyle\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\hat{Y}_{T}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}\leq\underline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}),\;\mbox{for some}\;{\mathbf{Z}}\big\},

for
(t,{\mathbf{y}})\in[0,T]\times H
, where
\hat{Y}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}
is the process following starting from
\hat{Y}_{t}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}=y
following the same SDE as
Y^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}}
.

As studied in
Appendix

A
(note that the supremum problem can be straightforwardly re-written as an infimum problem), the value functions are proved to satisfy, under some regularity assumptions, the following equations on
[0,T]\times H

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})+\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{y}},\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{y}}$}}\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}))}\bigg\{c_{t}^{\star}(0,z^{t})-\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\bigg\}=0,\;\underline{w}(T,{\mathbf{y}})=\overline{g}({\mathbf{y}}),

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})+\inf_{{\mathbf{z}}\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{y}},\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}))}\bigg\{c_{t}^{\star}(0,z^{t})-\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\bigg\}=0,\;\overline{w}(T,{\mathbf{y}})=\underline{g}({\mathbf{y}}),

with

{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{p}})\coloneqq\big\{{\mathbf{Z}}\in H:Z^{0}-\langle{\mathbf{Z}},{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}=0\big\}=\big\{{\mathbf{Z}}\in H:\langle{\mathbf{Z}},{\mathbf{v}}_{0}-{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}=0\big\},

where
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
is the element of
H
such that
\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{0},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{H}=\tilde{x}^{0}
for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in H
.

We now informally describe how we expect the reachability set to be related to these two functions. First, the following inclusion is obvious

{\cal V}(t)\subset\big\{{\mathbf{y}}\in H:\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\leq y^{0}\leq\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\big\}.

Indeed, if
{\mathbf{y}}\in{\cal V}(t)
, then there exists
{\mathbf{Z}}
such that

\overline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}})\leq Y_{T}^{0,t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}\leq\underline{g}({\mathbf{Y}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}).

Note that, as the
(Y^{s,t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}})_{s\in[0,T]}
only interact through the control
{\mathbf{Z}}
, we have in fact
Y^{0,t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}=\hat{Y}^{0,t,y,{\mathbf{Z}}}
,
\mathbb{P}
–a.s., whenever
y^{0}=y
. Therefore,
{\mathbf{Z}}
is admissible for both stochastic target problems (
4.3.2
), which implies that
y^{0}\in[\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}}),\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})]
. The converse inclusion is more challenging, though we refer to
Hernández et al. 2024

[
27
, Lemma 5.3]
for more details about how it can be proved in a finite dimensional context.

Assuming the same can be done here, we shall consider in what follows that the closure of the principal’s reachability set is equal to the previous set

{\rm cl}\big({\cal V}(t))=\big\{{\mathbf{y}}\in H:\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\leq y^{0}\leq\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\big\}.

(4.14)

Dynamic programming equation with epigraph-type reachability set.

Given this characterisation of the reachability set, the principal’s problem may be reformulated as a state-constrained control problem

V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\coloneqq\sup_{\{{\mathbf{Z}}:{\mathbf{Y}}_{r}^{\text{$t$}\text{$,$}\text{${\mathbf{y}}$}\text{$,$}\text{${\mathbf{Z}}$}}\in{\cal V}(r).\forall r\in[t,T]\}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[U_{\text{$\rm P$}}(X_{T}^{t,x,{\mathbf{Z}}},Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}})\big],

with
(X^{t,x,{\mathbf{Z}}},{\mathbf{Y}}^{t,{\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}}})
following the dynamics

\displaystyle X_{s}^{t,x,{\mathbf{Z}}}=x+\int_{t}^{s}a_{r}^{\star}(Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r+W_{s}-W_{t},\;{\mathbf{Y}}^{t,{\mathbf{Y}},{\mathbf{Z}}}={\mathbf{y}}+\int_{t}^{s}c_{r}^{\star}(\cdot,Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{t}^{s}{\mathbf{Z}}_{r}\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;s\in[t,T]

Introduce the sets

{\cal O}(t)\coloneqq\big\{{\mathbf{y}}\in H:\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})<y^{0}<\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\big\},\;\underline{{\cal O}}(t)\coloneqq\big\{{\mathbf{y}}\in H:\underline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})=y^{0}\big\},\;\overline{{\cal O}}(t)\coloneqq\big\{{\mathbf{y}}\in Hy^{0}=\overline{w}(t,{\mathbf{y}})\big\},\;t\in[0,T].

Within the model of
[
27
]
, who relies on the previous work of
Bouchard et al. 2010

[
8
]
, we then formally derive the principal’s dynamic programming equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})+F\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},\big(\partial_{x}V,\partial_{xx}^{2}V,D_{\mathbf{y}}V,D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}V,\partial_{x}D_{\mathbf{y}}V\big)(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\big)=0,\;(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\;{\mathbf{y}}\in{\cal O}(t),\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})+\underline{F}\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},\big(\partial_{x}V,\partial_{xx}^{2}V,D_{\mathbf{y}}V,D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}V,\partial_{x}D_{\mathbf{y}}V\big)(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\big)=0,\;(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\;{\mathbf{y}}\in\underline{{\cal O}}(t),\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})+\overline{F}\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},\big(\partial_{x}V,\partial_{xx}^{2}V,D_{\mathbf{y}}V,D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}V,\partial_{x}D_{\mathbf{y}}V\big)(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\big)=0,\;(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\;{\mathbf{y}}\in\overline{{\cal O}}(t),\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle V(T,\cdot)=U_{P},\end{cases}

(4.15)

where

\displaystyle F\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},p,A,{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{q}\big)\coloneqq\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in H}\bigg\{a_{t}^{\star}(z^{t})p+\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big(A+\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}+2\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{H}\big)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\underline{F}\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},p,A,{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{q}\big)\coloneqq\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\underline{H}(t,{\mathbf{y}},\underline{w})}\bigg\{a_{t}^{\star}(z^{t})p+\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big(A+\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}+2\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{H}\big)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\overline{F}\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},p,A,{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{q}\big)\coloneqq\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\overline{H}(t,{\mathbf{y}},\overline{w})}\bigg\{a_{t}^{\star}(z^{t})p+\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big(A+\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}+2\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{H}\big)\bigg\},

with the sets
\underline{H}
and
\overline{H}
defined by

\displaystyle\underline{H}(t,{\mathbf{y}},w)\coloneqq\bigg\{{\mathbf{z}}\in H:\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}-z^{0}=0,\;\mbox{and}\;c_{t}^{\star}(0,z^{t})-\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}w(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\geq 0\bigg\},

\displaystyle\overline{H}(t,{\mathbf{y}},w)\coloneqq\bigg\{{\mathbf{z}}\in H:\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}-z^{0}=0,\;\mbox{and}\;c_{t}^{\star}(0,z^{t})-\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}w(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\leq 0\bigg\},

for all
(t,{\mathbf{y}})\in[0,T]\times H
and smooth
w:[0,T]\times H\to\mathbb{R}
.

General dynamic programming equation.

In the general case, the reachability set
{\cal V}
might not be represented through functions of the state process. We therefore provide a general equation with general geometric constraints. The intuition is the same as above: if the state variable lies in the interior of the reachability set, then we obtain the ‘standard’ dynamic programming equation; if it lies at the border of
{\cal V}(t)
, we need go bring the state process back in the interior, which is obtained by sending the volatility to
0
and pushing the drift in the appropriate direction. Let
w:[0,T]\times H\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
{\cal V}(t)=\{{\mathbf{y}}\in H:w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\leq 0\}
(take for example
w(t,{\mathbf{y}})=1-\mathbf{1}_{{\mathbf{y}}\in{\cal V}(t)}
). We formally derive the following system of HJB equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})+F\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},\big(\partial_{x}V,\partial_{xx}^{2}V,D_{\mathbf{y}}V,D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}V,\partial_{x}D_{\mathbf{y}}V\big)(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\big)=0,\;(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\;{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathrm{Int}({\cal V}(t)),\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})+\partial F\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},\big(\partial_{x}V,\partial_{xx}^{2}V,D_{\mathbf{y}}V,D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}V,\partial_{x}D_{\mathbf{y}}V\big)(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\big)=0,\;(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R},\;{\mathbf{y}}\in\partial{\cal V}(t),\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle V(T,\cdot)=U_{P},\end{cases}

(4.16)

where
F
is defined as above and

\displaystyle\partial F\big(t,x,{\mathbf{y}},p,A,{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{q}\big)\coloneqq\sup_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\partial H(t,{\mathbf{y}},w)}\bigg\{a_{t}^{\star}(z^{t})p+\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),{\mathbf{p}}\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\big(A+\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}+2\langle{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{H}\big)\bigg\},

with the sets
\partial H
defined by:

\displaystyle\partial H(t,{\mathbf{y}},w)\coloneqq\bigg\{{\mathbf{z}}\in H:\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}=0,\;\mbox{and}\;\langle c_{t}^{\star}(\cdot,z^{t}),D_{\mathbf{y}}w(t,{\mathbf{y}})\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbf{z}},D_{{\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}w(t,{\mathbf{y}}){\mathbf{z}}\rangle_{H}\leq 0\bigg\},

for all
(t,{\mathbf{y}})\in[0,T]\times H
and smooth
w:[0,T]\times H\to\mathbb{R}
.

4.3.3
The exponential utility case

In this section, we assume the agent’s utility function is given by

V_{t}^{A}(\xi)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\text{$\alpha$}}}\bigg[U_{\text{$\rm A$}}\bigg(f(T-t)\xi-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{T}f(r-t)\alpha_{r}^{2}\mathrm{d}r\bigg)\bigg],

with
U_{\text{$\rm A$}}(x)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\gamma_{\text{$\rm A$}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{\text{$\rm A$}}x}
and
f(t)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}
, with positive
\gamma_{\text{$\rm A$}}
and
\rho
. We also assume that the principal is risk neutral, that is

U_{\text{$\rm P$}}(x,y)\coloneqq x-y,\;\mbox{for all}\;(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

We shall discuss the conjecture made in
[
26
]
, claiming that the optimal incentive
{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}
designed by the principal in this setting is deterministic, similarly to the case where
U_{\text{$A$}}=\mathrm{Id}
. In this setting, the dynamics controlled by the principal writes

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{s}=\bigg(\mathrm{e}^{-\rho(t-s)}\frac{(Z_{t}^{t})^{2}}{2}+\gamma\frac{(Z_{t}^{s})^{2}}{2}\bigg)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma Z_{t}^{s}\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Assume there exists a deterministic optimal control
{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}_{t}={\mathbf{z}}_{t}
. We first deduce from the constraint

Y_{T}^{s,{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}}=\frac{f(T-s)}{f(T)}Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}}=\mathrm{e}^{\rho s}Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}},\;\mbox{for all $s\in[0,T]$},

that
\int_{0}^{T}({\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{s}-\mathrm{e}^{\rho s}{\mathbf{z}}_{r}^{0})\mathrm{d}W_{s}
is deterministic, from which we deduce that
{\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{s}=\mathrm{e}^{\rho s}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{\mathrm{0}}
for Lebesgue–a.e.
(t,s)\in[0,T]^{2}
.

Then, given the dynamics of
(X,{\mathbf{Y}})
, it is clear that the dynamic value function
V:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the principal is smooth, as

V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[X_{T}^{t,x,{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}}-Y_{T}^{0,t,{\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{Z}}^{\star}}\big]=x-y^{0}+\int_{t}^{T}\bigg(z_{r}^{r}-\mathrm{e}^{-\rho r}\frac{(z_{r}^{r})^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{\text{$\rm A$}}\frac{(z_{r}^{0})^{2}}{2}\bigg)\mathrm{d}r.

Therefore, by the dynamic programming equation (
4.15
), if
(x,{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathrm{Int}({\cal V}(t))
, we have

-\partial_{t}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})-\sup_{{\mathbf{Z}}\in H}\bigg\{\partial_{x}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}})Z^{t}+\bigg\langle D_{\mathbf{y}}V(t,x,{\mathbf{y}}),\mathrm{e}^{-\rho(t-\cdot)}\frac{(Z^{t})^{2}}{2}+\gamma\frac{{\mathbf{Z}}^{2}}{2}\bigg\rangle_{H}\bigg\}=0,

observing that the second order derivatives of
V
are equal to
0
. Given that
\partial_{x}V=1
and
D_{\mathbf{y}}V={\mathbf{v}}_{0}
, the first order condition in the Hamiltonian writes

{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}z^{t}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\gamma z^{0}{\mathbf{v}}_{0}={\mathbf{v}}_{t}-z^{0}{\mathbf{v}}_{t}-\gamma z^{0}{\mathbf{v}}_{0}=0,

which is clearly impossible. Therefore, the conjecture formulated in
[
26
]
is false whenever there exists
t\in[0,T)
such that
\mathrm{Int}({\cal V}(t))\neq\emptyset
.

Remark 4.3
.

Of course, the previous argument does not invalidate the conjecture if it turns out that
\mathrm{Int}({\cal V}(t))
is empty, which we have not been able to rule out. One possible strategy to prove that this cannot happen would be to find a smooth super-solution
w^{\text{$+$}}
of the
PDE
associated to the reachability set. Indeed, in this case the non-empty domain
w^{\text{$+$}}\leq 0
would be included in the reachability set. Despite several attempts, we have not been able to construct such a function.

4.3.4
Study for a special discount factor

We end the discussion on the principal–agent problem with an example where a reduction of dimension can be obtained. Consider the case where the agent’s time-inconsistency only comes from the presence of a non-exponential discount factor

U_{A}(s,\xi)=f(T-s)U_{\text{$\rm A$}}(\xi),\;c_{t}(s,a)=f(t-s)c_{t}(a).

Moreover, we assume that the function
f
takes the form

f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho_{\text{$k$}}t},

for some
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, where the sequences
(\beta_{k})_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
and
(\rho_{k})_{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
are non-negative, such that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}=1
and
\rho_{k}\neq\rho_{j}
for all
(k,j)\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}

k\neq j

Lemma 4.4
.

For all
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}

\phi_{k}(s)\coloneqq\beta_{k}\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{\text{$k$}}s}

Proof.

First observe that the stochastic target constraint (

Y_{T}^{s,{\mathbf{Z}}}=\frac{f(T-s)}{f(T)}Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}}=\frac{1}{f(T)}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\rho_{k}T}Y_{T}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}},

Y_{t}^{s,{\mathbf{Z}}}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}f(T-s)c_{r}^{\star}(Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\bigg]=\frac{1}{f(T)}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{k}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\rho_{\text{$k$}}T}\Bigg(Y_{t}^{0,{\mathbf{Z}}}+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}f(T)c_{r}^{\star}(Z_{r}^{r})\mathrm{d}r\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\bigg]\Bigg),

as

or, equivalently, that

\max_{k\in\{2,\dots,N\}}\bigg\{\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{(\rho_{\text{$k$}}-\rho_{\text{$1$}})T}y_{k}\bigg\}\leq y_{1}\leq\min_{k\in\{2,\dots,N\}}\bigg\{\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{(\rho_{\text{$k$}}-\rho_{\text{$1$}})T}y_{k}\bigg\},

which writes again as the combination of two epigraph-type constraints. Then, the Principal must solve the finite dimensional control problem under stochastic target constraint

\sup_{\tilde{Z}:\tilde{Y}_{T}\in{\cal D}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[U_{\text{$\rm P$}}\bigg(X,\sum_{k=1}^{N}\beta_{k}\tilde{Y}_{T}^{k}\bigg)\bigg],

Note that this falls under the setting of

5
Markovian representation and approximation

In this section, we discuss how our framework provides a natural Markovian approximation for the Volterra-type dynamics (
1.1
). For the sake of clarity, we omit the dependence on the control
\alpha
for now. When
b
and
\sigma
are as in (
1.1
), we recall that the Volterra-type process
X
is related to the infinite dimensional process
{\mathbf{X}}
from (
2.3
) in the following way

X_{t}=\phi(t,{\mathbf{X}}_{t}),\;\mbox{for all $t\in[0,T]$},

where the mapping
\phi:[0,T]\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\phi(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\tilde{x}^{t},\;\mbox{for all $(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H$.}

By (
2.2
), the mapping
\phi(t,\cdot)
is a continuous linear form on
H
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, by Riesz’s representation theorem, there exists an
H
-valued mapping
t\longmapsto v_{t}
such that

\phi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\langle v_{t},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle_{H},\;\mbox{for all $(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H$}.

Note that
v_{t}
is a
Sobolev
solution of the equation

v_{t}-\partial_{s}^{2}v_{t}=\delta_{t},\;\mbox{for all $t\in[0,T]$}.

(5.1)

Note that
t
is only a parameter here. Solving the equation on
(0,t)
and
(t,T)
, and using the continuity condition of
s\longmapsto v_{t}^{s}
in
t
, we see that
v
has the form

v_{t}^{s}=\frac{1}{2}\big(A\mathrm{e}^{s}+B\mathrm{e}^{-s}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathrm{e}^{s-t}-\mathrm{e}^{-(s-t)}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{s\geq t\}},\;\mbox{$(A,B)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

(5.2)

Let
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\text{$\star$}}}
be an orthonormal basis of
H
. Denote for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
by
v_{t}^{k}
and
X_{t}^{k}
the projections of
v_{t}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
on
e_{k}
. We have

X_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{t}^{k}X_{t}^{k}.

(5.3)

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
X^{k}
is a diffusion process solving the SDE

X_{t}^{k}=x_{k}+\int_{0}^{T}b_{r}^{k}(X_{r})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{r}^{k}(X_{r})\mathrm{d}W_{r},

where

x_{k}\coloneqq\langle{\mathbf{x}},e_{k}\rangle_{H},\ b_{t}^{k}(x)\coloneqq\langle b_{t}(x),e_{k}\rangle_{H},\ \mbox{and}\ \sigma_{t}^{k}(x)\coloneqq\langle\sigma_{t}(x),e_{k}\rangle_{H},\;\forall(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}^{\star}.

Note also that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
,
t\longmapsto v_{t}^{k}
is
C^{1}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle v_{t}^{k}=\langle v_{t},e_{k}\rangle_{H}=\int_{0}^{T}v_{t}^{s}e_{k}^{s}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{s}v_{t}^{s}\partial_{s}e_{k}^{s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big((A\mathrm{e}^{s}+B\mathrm{e}^{-s})e_{k}^{s}+(A\mathrm{e}^{s}-B\mathrm{e}^{-s})\partial_{s}e_{k}^{s}\big)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{T}\big((\mathrm{e}^{s-t}+\mathrm{e}^{-(s-t)})e_{k}^{s}+(\mathrm{e}^{s-t}-\mathrm{e}^{-(s-t)})\partial_{s}e_{k}^{s}\big)\mathrm{d}s,

which is clearly
C^{1}
in
t
. Thus, we may formally differentiate (
5.3
) to obtain

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\partial_{t}v_{t}^{k}X_{t}^{k}\mathrm{d}t+v_{t}^{k}\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{k}.

Then, denoting
X^{0}\coloneqq X
, we see that the infinite dimensional system
(X^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\text{$\star$}}}
follows the Markovian dynamics

\begin{cases}\displaystyle X_{t}^{0}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\partial_{t}v_{r}^{k}X_{r}^{k}+v_{r}^{k}b_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0})\big)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{r}^{k}\sigma_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\\[8.00003pt]
\displaystyle X_{t}^{k}=x_{k}+\int_{0}^{T}b_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}.\end{cases}

(5.4)

Of course, a natural finite-dimensional approximation is obtained by simply truncating the sums in the above dynamics; thus, for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, we may consider the
(n+1)
-dimensional dynamics

\begin{cases}\displaystyle X_{t}^{0,n}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{t}v_{r}^{k}X_{r}^{k,n}+v_{r}^{k}b_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0,n})\bigg)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{r}^{k}\sigma_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0,n})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\\[8.00003pt]
\displaystyle X_{t}^{k,n}=x_{k}+\int_{0}^{T}b_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0,n})\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{r}^{k}(X_{r}^{0,n})\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},\end{cases}

(5.5)

which is a standard finite dimensional SDE.

Proposition 5.1
.

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|X_{t}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{t}^{n}\big|^{2}\bigg]=0,\;\text{\rm and}\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[|X_{t}^{0,n}-X_{t}^{0}|^{2}\big]=0,\;\forall t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Introduce for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}

\bar{X}_{t}^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}X_{t}^{k},\;\mbox{and}\;R_{t}^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}v_{t}^{k}X_{t}^{k}.

Recalling (
5.3
), we have for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}

|X_{t}^{0,n}-X_{t}^{0}|^{2}\leq 2|X_{t}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{t}^{n}|^{2}+2|R_{t}^{n}|^{2}.

(5.6)

Since
X_{t}^{0,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}X_{t}^{k,n}
, we have for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}

X_{t}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{t}^{n}=\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}\big(b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})\big)\bigg)\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}\big(\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})\big)\bigg)\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

By using Cauchy–Schwarz’s inequality and Doob’s inequalities, we have for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}\big|X_{u}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{u}^{n}\big|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2T\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}\big(b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})\big)\bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+8\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{t}^{k}\big(\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})\big)\bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2T\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v_{t}^{k}|^{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-b_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})|^{2}\bigg)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+8\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v_{t}^{k}|^{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\Bigg[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0,n})-\sigma_{s}^{k}(X_{s}^{0})|^{2}\bigg)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2T\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[\lVert b_{s}(\cdot,X_{s}^{0,n})-b_{s}(\cdot,X_{s}^{0})\rVert_{H}^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+8\int_{0}^{t}\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[\lVert\sigma_{s}(\cdot,X_{s}^{0,n})-\sigma_{s}(\cdot,X_{s}^{0})\rVert_{H}^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}s.

Recalling that
b
and
\sigma
are Lipschitz-continuous as
H
-valued functions and the estimate from
Equation

5.6
, we have

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\sup_{u\in[0,t]}\big|X_{u}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{u}^{n}\big|^{2}\bigg]\leq 4(T+4)L^{2}\lVert v^{\star}\rVert_{H}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{s}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{s}^{n}\big|^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[|R_{s}^{n}|^{2}\big]\Big)\mathrm{d}s,

(5.7)

with
\lVert v^{\star}\rVert_{H}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{H}
. Therefore, by Grönwall’s lemma, we have

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\big|X_{u}^{0,n}-\bar{X}_{u}^{n}\big|^{2}\bigg]\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[|R_{s}^{n}|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}s\exp\big(4(T+4)L^{2}T\lVert v^{\star}\rVert_{H}^{2}.

(5.8)

Notice that

\big|R_{s}^{n}\big|^{2}\leq\Bigg(\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}|v_{t}^{k}|^{2}\Bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}|X_{t}^{k}|^{2}\Bigg)\leq\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{H}^{2}\lVert{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\rVert_{H}^{2},

and that
R_{s}^{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
,
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathbb{P}
–a.e., so that we may apply the dominated convergence theorem to conclude that

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[|R_{s}^{n}|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}s\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

We then combine this limit to obtain the first result, and plug it into (
5.6
) to obtain the second one.

6
Comparison with other results in the literature

6.1
Lifting approach

In this section, we discuss how our methodology completes pre-existing results regarding the control of stochastic Volterra integral equations. It is common in the literature (see e.g.
Abi Jaber et al. 2021

[
2
]
or
Hamaguchi 2023a

[
22
]
) to assume that the control process has the following dynamics

X_{t}^{\alpha}=x+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-r)\big(b(X_{r}^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}r+\sigma(X_{r}^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}W_{r}\big),

(6.1)

where the kernel
K
writes
K(t)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\mu(d\theta)
for some signed measure
\mu
. Note that such kernels may be singular at
0
. In
[
13
]
,
di Nunno and Giordano 2023
generalise this structure by writing

K(t)=\langle g,{\cal S}_{t}\nu\rangle_{Y\times Y^{\text{$\star$}}},

(6.2)

where
Y
is a UMD Banach space,
Y^{\star}
its dual,
{\cal S}
a semi-group acting on
Y^{\star}
and
g
,
\nu
two elements in
Y
and
Y^{\star}
respectively. We easily see that the examples of
[
2
]
and
[
22
]
are covered by this structure. Then, switching the integrals in
\mathrm{d}r
and
\mathrm{d}W_{r}
and the duality bracket
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{Y\times Y^{\text{$\star$}}}
, we can write

X_{t}^{\alpha}=x+\langle g,{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\alpha}\rangle_{Y\times Y^{\text{$\star$}}},

where
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
is a
Y^{\star}
-valued process satisfying the infinite dimensional Markovian SDE

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{t}{\cal A}^{\star}{\mathbf{X}}_{r}\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\nu b\big(\langle g,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha}\rangle_{Y\times Y^{\text{$\star$}}}\big)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\nu\sigma\big(\langle g,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha}\rangle_{Y\times Y^{\text{$\star$}}}\big)\mathrm{d}W_{r},

thanks to the semi-group structure of
{\cal S}
. The problem of controlling
X^{\alpha}
is then reduced to the problem of controlling the infinite dimensional Markovian dynamics
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
. In their recent contribution
[
19
]
,
Gasteratos and Pannier 2025
propose to lift uncontrolled Volterra dynamics with convolution type singular coefficients in a weighted Sobolev space, therefore enjoying a Hilbertian structure.

Our approach generalises this reduction for the case of regular kernels. In particular, we do not require any semi-group structure. Recall our general Volterra-type dynamics

X_{t}^{\alpha}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b_{r}(t,X_{r}^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}r+\sigma_{r}(t,X_{r}^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

Then, assuming that
b
and
\sigma
have Sobolev regularity in the ‘Volterra time’
t
, we may write

b_{r}(t,x)=\langle b_{r},(\cdot,x),v_{t}\rangle_{H},\;\mbox{and}\;\sigma_{r}(t,x)=\langle\sigma_{r},(\cdot,x),v_{t}\rangle_{H},

where
v_{t}
is defined by (
5.2
) for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then, observing that
x=\langle{\mathbf{p}}(x),v_{t}\rangle_{H}
, we obtain

X_{t}^{\alpha}=\langle{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\alpha},v_{t}\rangle_{H},

where
{\mathbf{X}}^{\alpha}
follows the
H
-valued Markovian SDE:

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\alpha}={\mathbf{p}}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{r}\big(\cdot,\langle{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha},v_{r}\rangle_{H}\big)\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{r}\big(\cdot,\langle{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{\alpha},v_{r}\rangle_{H}\big)\mathrm{d}W_{r}.

6.2
PDE approach

We now mention other works connecting Volterra dynamics to partial differential equations, which are often used jointly to a lifting approach. We first mention the contribution of
Viens and Zhang 2019

[
40
]
, whose methodology is in spirit the closest to ours. Their idea is the following: given a process
X
of the form

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K(r,t)\mathrm{d}W_{r},

(6.3)

with
K
a possibly singular kernel, one wants to find a PDE characterising the process

Y_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}f(r,X_{r})\mathrm{d}r+g(X_{T})\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\bigg].

(6.4)

As usual, the trick is to find a function
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and an adapted process
\tilde{X}
so that
Y_{t}=u\big(t,\tilde{X}_{t}\big)
. Of course, the connection between the stochastic representation above and the PDE is derived through an appropriate form of Itô’s formula, for which a semi-martingale structure on
\tilde{X}
is necessary. Since
X
defined in (
6.3
) is obviously not a semi-martingale, the authors of
[
40
]
introduce the family of auxiliary processes
\Theta
defined by

\Theta_{s}^{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}K(r,s)\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;\mbox{for all $s\geq t$},

(6.5)

and prove that

Y_{t}=u(t,X\otimes_{t}\Theta^{t}),

where
(X\otimes_{t}\Theta^{t})_{s}\coloneqq X_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{s<t\}}+\Theta_{s}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{s\geq t\}}
and
u
satisfies the path-dependent PDE

\partial_{t}u(t,\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\omega\omega}^{2}u(t,\omega){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(K(t,\cdot),K(t,\cdot))}+f(t,\omega)=0,\ u(t,\omega)=g(T,\omega),

(6.6)

for all
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times C^{0}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
, where the path derivative is defined in the spirit of Dupire
[
14
]
, on the space of càdlàg paths. This characterisation assumes that the derivatives of the function
u
are well defined. This approach has been extended by
Wang et al. 2022
in
[
41
]
to the case of Volterra-type forward–backward SDEs. In
[
42
]
,
Wang et al. 2023
uses this dynamic programming equation to characterise the value function of a linear–quadratic problem by a system of path-dependent Riccati equations.

Let us now compare this approach with our work. We easily see that the family of random variables
(\Theta_{s}^{t})_{s\in[t,T]}
corresponds to our
(X_{t}^{s})_{s\in[t,T]}
, the only difference being that the domain of
(t,s)
in
[
40
]
is triangular (
i.e.
, one requires
s>t
) due to the potential singularity of
K
, whereas it is rectangular in our framework. Furthermore, for fixed
t
, we easily see that
u(t,\omega)=u(t,\omega\mathbf{1}_{[t,T]})
in this example. Therefore, introducing
\Theta
defined by (
6.5
) is almost equivalent to our lifting from
X
to
{\mathbf{X}}=(X^{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
, with the difference that the space where the new state process takes its values is not the same. In
[
40
]
, this would be the Banach space of càdlàg paths, whereas we chose the Hilbert space of Sobolev functions on
[0,T]
. Although this forces us to have stronger regularity assumptions on the coefficients of our dynamics, this dramatically reduces the need for regularity on
u
, as we can resort to the standard theory of viscosity solution on Hilbert space to derive our dynamics programming equation.

We mention again the recent contribution of
di Nunno and Giordano 2023

[
13
]
, who characterise the solution of a Volterra control problem by means of a backward SDE and as the mild solution to the corresponding dynamic programming equation. More precisely, as highlighted in Section
3.3
, they consider the controlled dynamics (
3.7
), with the extra assumption that the kernel
K
has the structure (
6.2
). They are then able to prove that, given that the value function has a first order Gâteaux derivative, the problem is characterised by mean of a semi-linear PDE on an UMD Banach space, satisfied in the sense of mild solutions. In our context, since we operate our lifting in a Hilbert space, this connection between backward SDEs and mild solution is standard (see
e.g.

Briand and Confortola 2008

[
10
]
), and thus our stochastic representation of the value function in
Section

3.3
immediately implies that (
3.3
) is satisfied in the mild sense.

7
Case of singular kernels

In this section, we discuss how we could adapt our approach to the case of singular kernels. Consider for example that the (non-lifted) state process writes

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K(t-r)\mathrm{d}W_{r},\;t\in[0,T],

with
K:(0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
K(t){\longrightarrow}_{t\to 0}\infty
and
K\in\mathbb{L}^{2}((0,T],\mathrm{d}t)
. Control problems involving this type of dynamics naturally arise in rough volatility models, see
e.g.
the portfolio optimisation problem studied by
Fouque and Hu 2019

[
18
]
. In our context, our lifting writes
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\coloneqq\{X_{t}^{s}\}_{(t,s)\in\Delta}
, with

X_{t}^{s}=\int_{0}^{t}K(s-r)dW_{r},\;t\in[0,T],\;\mbox{\rm and}\;\Delta\coloneqq\{(t,s)\in[0,T]^{2}:t<s\},

as the kernel
K
is not defined on non-positive times. Since our lifting requires the
\{X_{t}^{s}\}_{\{t<s\leq T\}}
to all belong to the same space, we could consider to artificially extend
K
on non-positive times, so that
(t,s)
belong to the rectangular space
[0,T]^{2}
instead of the triangular space
\Delta
. Then we have

X_{t}^{s}=\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}K(s-r)\mathrm{d}W_{r}\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{\{0\leq s\leq t\}}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}K(s-r)\mathrm{d}W_{r}\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{\{s>t\}}=X_{s}\mathbf{1}_{\{0\leq s\leq t\}}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}K(s-r)\mathrm{d}W_{r}\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{\{s>t\}},

which corresponds to the approach proposed by
Viens and Zhang 2019
in
[
40
]
. However, although
s\longmapsto X_{t}^{s}
is continuous a.s., the possible roughness of
X_{\cdot}
also prevents us to embed this lifting in a Sobolev space. The lifted control problem should then be studied in the Banach space of continuous paths on
[0,T]
. One could use the functional Itô’s formula of
[
40
]
(based on Gâteaux derivatives) to derive the corresponding dynamic programming equation for smooth value functions; however, finding the appropriate notion of viscosity solutions for this problem would remain challenging, as the absence of semi-martingale structure prevents use from using the theory developed by
Ekren et al. 2014

[
15
]
, ans is therefore left for future research.

Appendix A
A class of stochastic target problems in Hilbert spaces

We propose an extension of the results of
Soner and Touzi 2002b

[
39
]
to an infinite dimensional setting.
In this paragraph,
H
denotes an arbitrary Hilbert space, and
W
a standard
\mathbb{R}
-valued Brownian motion. Let
{\mathbf{X}}
and
Y
be the solution of the SDEs:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}

\displaystyle=B\big(tr{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{r}\big)\mathrm{d}r+\Sigma\big(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{r}\big)\mathrm{d}W_{r},

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}}y,,\alpha}

\displaystyle=b\big(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},y,\alpha},\alpha_{r}\big)\mathrm{d}r+\Sigma\big(r,{\mathbf{X}}_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{r}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},y,\alpha},\alpha_{r}\big)\mathrm{d}W_{r},

with
({\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{t}^{t,{\mathbf{x}}y,,\alpha})=({\mathbf{x}},y)
, where
(B,\Sigma)
and
(b,\sigma)
are respectively
H
-valued and
\mathbb{R}
-valued coefficients satisfying the usual regularity conditions, and
\alpha
lives in a set of controls
{\cal A}
. We assume that the elements of
{\cal A}
are càdlàg, progressively measurable and take their values in a convex, open and unbounded Polish space
\mathbb{A}
.

We consider the following problem : given
g:H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
(t,{\mathbf{x}},y)\in[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}
, we want to determine the following set of controls:

{\cal A}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y)\coloneqq\big\{\alpha\in{\cal A}:Y_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},y,\alpha}\geq g({\mathbf{X}}_{T}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},y,\alpha})\big\}.

Similarly to
[
39
]
, we observe that the monotonicity of
Y
in its initial condition
y
along with the independence of
{\mathbf{X}}
from
y
provides the following result

{\cal A}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y)\neq\emptyset\Longrightarrow{\cal A}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y^{\prime}),\;\forall y^{\prime}\geq t.

This leads us to define and study the following function

w(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\inf\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}:{\cal A}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y)\neq\emptyset\big\}.

(A.2)

As in
[
39
]
, we shall prove that
w
is a viscosity solution to some second order partial differential equation (here on the Hilbert space
H
). Our first observation is that
w
satisfies the dynamic programming principle (DPP for short)

Proposition A.1
.

For all
(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times H
and all
[t,T]
-valued stopping time
\theta
, we have

w(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\inf\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\alpha\in{\cal A},\;Y_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},y,\alpha}\geq w(\theta,{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}),\;\mathbb{P}\mbox{\rm--a.s.}\big\}.

(A.3)

Proof.

We observe that the proof of the DPP in
[
38
, Theorem 3.1]
applies to infinite dimensional state and control spaces. Indeed, it relies on the following assumptions

\bullet

{\cal A}
is separable and stable by concatenation, which is the case since our control set is a Polish space of càdlàg paths; in particular, those assumptions allow to apply Jankov–von Neumann theorem for measurable selection;

\bullet
the assumptions Z1–Z5 in
[
38
]
on the state process, which are easily satisfies here as
({\mathbf{X}},Y)
satisfies a standard SDE in a Hilbert space.

We can now derive the corresponding dynamic programming equation. First, for
(t,{\mathbf{x}},y,{\mathbf{p}})\in[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\times H
, we introduce:

{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{p}})\coloneqq\{a\in\mathbb{A}:\sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},y,a)-\langle\Sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},a),{\mathbf{p}}\rangle=0\}.

Then, we consider the following equation on
[0,T]\times H

-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\sup_{a\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}}))}\Big\{b(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),a)-{\cal L}^{a}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\Big\}=0,\;u(T,{\mathbf{x}})=g(x),

(A.4)

with

{\cal L}^{a}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\langle B(t,{\mathbf{x}},a),D_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},a),D_{{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\Sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},a)\rangle.

Definition A.2

(Viscosity solutions)
.

Let
u:[0,T]\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous.

(i)

u
is said to be a viscosity super-solution of (
A.4
) if
u(T,\cdot)\geq G
and, for all
\varphi\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times H)
such that
u-\varphi
has a local minimum in
(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, we have

-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\sup_{a\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}}))}\Big\{b(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),a)-{\cal L}^{a}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\Big\}\geq 0.

(ii)

u
is said to be a viscosity sub-solution of (
A.4
) if
u(T,\cdot)\leq G
and, for all
\varphi\in C^{1,2}([0,T]\times H)
such that
u-\varphi
has a local maximum in
(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, we have

-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\sup_{a\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}}))}\Big\{b(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),a)-{\cal L}^{a}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\Big\}\leq 0.

(iii)

u
is said to be a viscosity solution of (
A.4
) if it is both a viscosity super-solution and viscosity sub-solution of (
A.4
).

Theorem A.3
.

Assume that
w
is continuous, and that
{\cal N}
is continuous in the sense that if
a_{0}\in{\cal N}(t_{0},{\mathbf{x}}_{0},y_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{0})
, then there exists a mapping
\hat{a}:[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\times H\longrightarrow\mathbb{A}
such that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\hat{a}(t_{0},{\mathbf{x}}_{0},y_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{0})=a_{0},\\
\displaystyle\hat{a}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y,{\mathbf{p}})\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},y,{\mathbf{p}})\ \forall(t,{\mathbf{x}},y,{\mathbf{p}})\in[0,T]\times H\times\mathbb{R}\times H.\end{cases}

Then
w
is a viscosity solution of (
A.4
).

Proof.

The argument is very similar to
[
39
]
. However, as we are in an infinite dimensional setting and that we do not require
\mathbb{A}
to be compact, we detail the proof.

(i)
We first show the super-solution property. Let
\varphi
be a test function; we may assume without loss of generality that
\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=w(t,{\mathbf{x}})
and that the minimum in the tangency property is global. Let
\alpha\in{\cal A}(t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}))
and introduce, for
\delta>0
,

\theta_{\delta}\coloneqq\inf\big\{s\geq t:(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha})\notin[t,t+\delta)\times B_{\delta}({\mathbf{x}})\big\},

(A.5)

where
B_{\delta}({\mathbf{x}})
is the ball of radius
\delta
and centre
{\mathbf{x}}
in
H
. As a consequence of the DPP (
A.3
), we have

Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha}\geq w\big(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha}\big)\geq\varphi\big(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha}\big),\;\mathbb{P}\mbox{\rm--a.s.}.

Therefore, applying Itô’s formula between
t
and
\theta_{\delta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\theta_{\delta}}\Big(b\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big)-\partial_{t}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)-{\cal L}^{\alpha_{s}}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{\theta_{\delta}}\Big(\sigma\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big)-\langle\Sigma\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big),D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)\rangle\Big)\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq 0,

\mathbb{P}
–a.s. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now introduce the measure
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\coloneqq{\cal E}\bigg(-n\int_{t}^{T\wedge\theta_{\text{$\delta$}}}\big(\sigma(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha},\alpha_{s})-\Sigma(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{s})\big)\mathrm{d}W_{s}\bigg),

so that taking the expectation under
\mathbb{P}^{n}
in the above inequality provides

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{n}}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\theta_{\text{$\delta$}}}\Big(b\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big)-\partial_{t}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)-{\cal L}^{\alpha_{s}}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-n\int_{t}^{\theta_{\text{$\delta$}}}\Big(\sigma\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},Y_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big)-\langle\Sigma\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha},\alpha_{s}\big),D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi\big(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha}\big)\rangle\Big)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]\geq 0.

Then, by standard argument and the fact that this inequality must be true for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

b\big(s,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha_{t}\big)-\partial_{t}\varphi(s,{\mathbf{x}})-{\cal L}^{\alpha_{t}}\varphi(s,{\mathbf{x}})\geq 0,

with
\alpha_{t}
such that

\big(\sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},w(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\alpha_{t})-\langle\Sigma(t,{\mathbf{x}},\alpha_{t}),D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})\rangle\big)^{2}=0,

which proves the super-solution property.

(ii)
We now prove the sub-solution property. Given a test function
\varphi
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=w(t,{\mathbf{x}})
and that

\inf_{(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in\partial_{\text{$p$}}B_{\delta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})}\varphi(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-w(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\geq\delta>0,

(A.6)

where
\partial_{p}B_{\delta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\{t+\delta\}\times{\rm cl}(B_{\delta}({\mathbf{x}}))\cup[t,t+\delta]\times\partial B_{\delta}({\mathbf{x}})
is the parabolic border of
B_{\delta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq[t,t+\delta)\times B_{\delta}({\mathbf{x}})
. This can be achieved for example by adding a term in
\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}\rvert_{H}^{4}
to the test function.

We shall prove the sub-solution property by contradiction. Assume that

-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\sup_{a\in{\cal N}(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}u(t,{\mathbf{x}}))}\big\{b(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),a)-{\cal L}^{a}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\big\}>0.

(A.7)

By continuity of
{\cal N}
,
\delta
can be chosen so that

-\partial_{t}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\big\{b\big(t,{\mathbf{x}},u(t,{\mathbf{x}}),\hat{a}(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\varphi(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\mathbf{x}}f(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\big)-{\cal L}^{a(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\varphi(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),D_{\text{${\mathbf{x}}$}}f(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}u(t,{\mathbf{x}})\big\}\geq\delta,

for all
(s,\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\in B_{\delta}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
, with the mapping
\hat{a}
as in the assumptions of the theorem. Fix now
\eta>0
, and let
({\mathbf{X}}^{\eta},Y^{\eta})
be the solution of the SDEs (
A.1
) such that

{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\eta}={\mathbf{x}},\ Y_{t}^{\eta}=w(t,{\mathbf{x}})-\eta,

and controlled by
\hat{\alpha}_{s}\coloneqq\hat{a}(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{\eta},Y_{s}^{\eta},D_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(s,X_{s}^{\eta}))
. Let also
\theta_{\delta}
be as in (
A.5
). We have

\displaystyle Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta}-w(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})

\displaystyle=Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta}-\varphi(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})+\varphi(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})-w(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})\geq Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta}-\varphi(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})+\delta,

by (
A.6
). Introduce now the process
\hat{Y}_{s}^{\eta}\coloneqq\varphi(s,{\mathbf{X}}_{s}^{\eta})-\eta
. As in
[
39

, we observe that
\hat{Y}^{\eta}
satisfies the same SDE as

with a lower drift term, due to our hypothesis (

\hat{Y}^{\eta}\leq Y^{\eta}
. Coming back to the previous inequalities, we have

\displaystyle Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta}-w(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})

\displaystyle\geq Y_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta}-\hat{Y}_{\theta_{d}}^{\eta}+\hat{Y}_{\theta_{d}}^{\eta}-\varphi(\theta_{\delta},{\mathbf{X}}_{\theta_{\delta}}^{\eta})+\delta\geq-\eta+\delta.

Thus, taking
