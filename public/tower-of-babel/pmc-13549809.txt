Perceived training needs and barriers among Korean residents: a cross-sectional survey for competency-based education
Authors: Tae Hyun Kim, Min-jung Lee, Seung Hee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCurrent South Korean resident education relies heavily on apprenticeship models rather than the systematic curricula needed for multifaceted competency development. Therefore, a rigorous needs assessment is required for effective program design, particularly to target year-specific educational differences.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of all residents at a single hospital branch was conducted in December 2023. An online questionnaire reviewing residents’ perceptions of the current program, needs assessment for a proposed institutional core competency program, and evaluation of preferred educational topics was performed. Responses were analyzed utilizing Kruskal-Wallis, McNemar’s, and chi-square tests, and compared in terms of education areas and residency years.ResultsA survey of 117 residents revealed that while the current residency program is perceived as strong in clinical training, residents ideally desire a more comprehensive education encompassing clinical practice, research, and general competencies. There was a high willingness to participate in a new institutional core competency program, with a strong preference for online learning formats and supportive measures such as workload reduction to overcome barriers. Importantly, the analysis of preferred topics showed that educational needs evolve significantly throughout the residency, shifting from foundational clinical skills and stress management in junior years to advanced topics like research methodology, academic writing, and health systems in senior years.ConclusionThese findings support that bridging the discrepancy between residents’ current clinical-focused training and their desired comprehensive education necessitates the implementation of stage-specific curricula, blended learning models, and organizational support systems for an effective, competency-based resident education.

Introduction

Residents are expected to develop balanced, multifaceted competencies including not only clinical practice skills but also research, education, and self-management as they progress toward becoming specialists [1]. International competency frameworks such as Canada’s CanMEDS especially emphasize roles beyond that of a medical expert [2].

In Korea, the Ministry of Health and Welfare announced a year-specific training curriculum for residents in 2019, presenting common competencies to be acquired during training [3]. However, competency-based medical education (CBME) implementation remains only in its early stages [1]. Due to the absence of a systematic curriculum, the quality of education varies largely especially in the ‘general competency’ area [4]. Moreover, residents often struggle to participate in educational programs due to heavy clinical workloads [5], and systematic evaluation and feedback remain inadequate, suggesting a need for overall training environment improvement.

A learner-centered approach is essential for developing effective resident education programs, where the educational objectives should harmoniously reflect the learners’ needs and institutional goals [6]. Such learner-centered education requires systematic needs assessment that identifies gaps between defined competencies and actual performance levels, thereby providing the basis for curriculum development and enabling efficient utilization of limited resources [7].

Resident training progressively develops specialist competencies, with clinical experience and responsibility varying by year. Junior residents sometimes experience excessive burden transitioning to direct patient care and face autonomy challenges [5]. Conversely, senior residents increasingly engage in teaching and team leadership [8].

Educational needs differ by training stage. Junior residents show higher demand for consultation response methods, basic epidemiology, and stress management, while senior residents express greater need for legal dispute response, patient-centered interviewing, research presentation, and career development [4]. Therefore, a stage-specific tailored approach establishing milestones by residency year according to CBME principles may be beneficial.

Training needs assessment (TNA) is a systematic process of identifying what is needed for effective education, establishing the basis for curriculum design and priority-setting [7]. In medical education, TNA has been utilized for quality management tailored program development [9]. Educational needs have been conceptualized in various ways, ranging from discrepancy-based approaches to those grounded in learner’s perceived needs. In this study, educational needs are operationally defined as the educational topics that residents perceive as necessary for a competency-based training program, as expressed through their selection from a predefined topic list organized by competency area.

Since residents are expected to perform roles not only in clinical practice but also as researchers and educators, identifying educational needs for each area can serve as the starting point for establishing stage-specific training objectives and developing educational content. However, existing studies have mainly examined broad competency frameworks without exploring specific educational topics or their optimal timing across residency years.

This study addresses these gaps by investigating residents’ perceptions of their current program, identifying needs for an institutional core competency program, aligned with institutional educational objectives, and exploring important educational topics across different training years. We conducted a topic-based exploratory TNA, focusing on topics residents perceive as needed in the clinical practice, research, and general competency areas. Rather than rating specific competency levels, we identified the perceived training needs through topic selection and explored when each topic should be introduced during training based on year-specific developmental considerations. Through this approach, we aimed to answer the following questions:

Question 1. What is the perception of the residents regarding the residency program?

Question 2. How may an institutional core competency program be implemented?

Question 3. What are the topics that can be proposed for an institutional core competency program?

By analyzing residents’ role perceptions, educational needs, and their variations across residency years, this study aims to provide foundational data for designing differentiated, stage-specific tailored educational programs that respond to the evolving needs of residents throughout their training.

Methods

1. Study design and participants

This study utilized a cross-sectional survey design. We developed a questionnaire organized into three main sections to address the research questions, with a total of 30 questions (including four demographic questions). The first section assessed the current status of the residency program, ideal objectives of the program, and contribution of attending physicians to the program through four questions in 5-point Likert scale. The second section inquired about the willingness to participate in an institutional core competency program, reasons for their choice, preferred learning formats, and desired supportive measures through 18 questions. The final section listed potential topics for inclusion in such a program through four separate questions in multiple-response questions.

The questionnaire utilized a 5-point Likert scale for evaluative questions and multiple-response questions for preferences regarding future measures. The options for the multiple-response questions were selected based on a review of prior researches. For example, a question on the current status of the residency program was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). An example item is: “Do you think that education and training in clinical practice, research & general competency area are well implemented in your current training institution?” On the other hand, preferences for educational topics were assessed using multiple-response questions where respondents selected their preferred subjects from a predefined list of clinical and general competency topics. Demographic variables, especially age data were collected as a categorical variable, rather than a continuous exact value, to protect participant anonymity. All residents of a single hospital branch were the target population. The survey was distributed online via email and mobile messages in December 2023.

2. Survey instrument development

To develop the survey items, we conducted a comprehensive literature review. We also referenced the hospital’s mission and vision, previously established resident core competencies by residency years and examples of existing competency-based programs. Based on the analysis of prior research, the educational content required to achieve common core competencies—applicable across all specialties—was categorized into two areas: clinical practice area and research & general competency area. A preliminary draft of the questionnaire was developed through consensus meetings of the research team consisting of three medical education experts. To ensure the appropriateness, readability, comprehensibility of the response options and to establish content validity, a peer validation was conducted involving five residents and five medical education experts. The structure of the questionnaire was subsequently refined based on the feedback. Because this questionnaire was designed as an exploratory needs assessment to identify specific educational preferences rather than a psychometric scale measuring a single latent construct, an exploratory factor analysis was not performed. Instead, validity was established through rigorous content validation by the expert panel. The internal consistency reliability of the final questionnaire was established, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.83.

3. Statistical analysis

Responses from the 5-point Likert scale were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s test for post-hoc analysis. To compare categorical variables (e.g., participation willingness and preferred learning format) between the clinical practice and research & general competency areas, McNemar’s test was utilized for categorical variables to account for the paired, within-subject nature of the data. For branched subsample analyses (e.g., reasons for participation), where the groups were partially overlapping due to different branching paths, Pearson’s chi-square test was applied. In these cases, the analytic unit was the individual resident, and the resulting sample overlap was treated as a limitation in statistical independence.

For multiple-response questions, each topic was treated as an independent binary variable (selected vs. not selected) to account for the multiple responses per individual. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to identify significant associations across the training years, and standardized residual analysis was performed to determine which options were selected with a significantly higher or lower frequency than expected. Additionally, to evaluate the magnitude of these differences, Cramér’s V was calculated as a measure of effect size. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software ver. 4.1.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

4. Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No., E-2509-109-1676). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the anonymous nature of the survey and minimal risk to participants. All study methods adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

A total of 117 residents participated in the survey. The demographic details of the respondents are outlined in Table 1. The respondents were from various specialty tracks, including medical specialties (n=54), and surgical specialties (n=26), and other departments (n=30), with seven participants not providing this information. The distribution by training year was as follows: interns (n=43; 36.8%), junior residents (n=29; 24.8%), senior residents (n=45; 38.4%).

1. Evaluation of the current residency program (section 1)

In section 1, questions regarding the current status of the residency program and contribution of attending physicians to the program was asked. For the program’s current status, the clinical practice area received the highest mean score (3.34), followed by the research area (3.07) and the general competency area (2.71). This difference in perception across the three areas was statistically significant (p<0.001). A similar trend was observed when residents evaluated the contribution of attending physicians to their program. The highest perceived contribution was shown in the clinical practice area (3.60), followed by the research area (3.49) and the general competency area (3.02). This difference was statistically significant as well (p<0.001). When asked about the ideal objectives of a residency program, residents showed varied perspectives. The most common viewpoint, held by 33% of respondents, was that the program should provide a comprehensive education covering clinical practice, research, and general competency equally. Of the residents, 25% believed the program’s primary objective should be focused solely on clinical practice. The remaining respondents perceived the program’s objective as a combination of clinical practice with either research (22%) or general competency (20%).

2. Needs assessment for an institutional core competency program (section 2)

As shown in Table 2, residents were asked upon their willingness to participate in an institutional core competency program. Majority of them expressed the willingness to do so, both in clinical practice area (92%) and research & general competency area (81%). However, there was a significantly higher willingness to participate in programs for the clinical practice area compared to the research & general competency area (p=0.011).

Among residents willing to participate, the most common motivations were “To enhance my expertise” and “Interest in acquiring new knowledge” for both categories. Statistically significant differences were found for two associated reasons: “For self-development” was cited more frequently for the research & general competency area (p=0.027), whereas considering the program a “mandatory education” was a more common reason for the clinical practice area (p=0.019).

For the minority of residents unwilling to participate, the primary reasons cited were a heavy workload and the burden of education hours. No statistically significant differences were observed in the reasons for non-participation between the two areas.

Online learning was strongly a preferred format over offline learning for programs in both the clinical practice area (n=79 online vs. n=38 offline) and the research & general competency area (n=77 online vs. n=40 offline), with no significant difference in this preference between the areas (p=0.832) (Table 3).

Among those who chose online learning, “Autonomously watching educational videos on open platforms (e.g., YouTube)” was the most popular specific mode for both categories. For those who preferred offline learning, “Utilizing regular in-hospital conference hours” and “During international/national/regional/local meetings” were the most selected options. There were no statistically significant differences in the preferences for any specific modes of online or offline education.

Preferred supportive measures to facilitate resident participation in the institutional core competency program is outlined in Table 3 as well. For the clinical practice area, the most desired support was “Providing in-house education and training programs” (n=69). For the research & general competency area, the top choices were “Providing financial support for external training opportunities” (n=56), “Providing in-house education and training programs” (n=56), and “Reducing workload and providing substitute personnel” (n=53). While some differences were noted, none of the preferences for supportive measures reached statistical significance between the two areas.

3. Preferred educational topics by residency year (section 3)

Table 4 and Table 5 present the preferred educational topics in the clinical practice and research & general competency areas, stratified by training years (postgraduate year [PGY]1 interns, PGY2–3 junior residents, PGY4–5 senior residents). We identified statistically significant differences in several topics across the residency years. The effect sizes of these significant differences, measured by Cramer’s V, ranged from 0.240 to 0.378, indicating a moderate and practically meaningful magnitude of difference.

Preferences for clinical topics is outlined in Table 4. The preference varied significantly across different residency years. Interest in “endoscopy training” (p=0.003) and “transplant patient management” (p=0.035) was significantly higher among interns and junior residents but decreased among senior residents. Conversely, interest in the “health insurance system” (p=0.003) was significantly lower among interns and highest among senior residents. Junior residents showed a significantly stronger preference for “advanced organ-specific anatomy” (p=0.010) and “wound dressing” (p=0.012) compared to other groups.

Preferences for research & general competency area is outlined in Table 5. Significant differences by residency year were also prominent for research and general competency topics. Interest in “stress management” (p=0.003) and “research equipment utilization” (p=0.009) was highest among interns and decreased significantly in senior years. In contrast, interest in “research methodology” (p=0.002), “statistical software” (p=0.034), “academic paper writing” (p=0.007), and “AI application on research” (p<0.001) was lowest among interns and increased significantly among senior residents. Interest in “career path” (p=0.011) was lowest for interns but peaked among junior residents.

Discussion

This study conducted a TNA of residents at a university hospital to systematically analyze their role, perception, willingness to participate in education, preferred learning formats, and educational needs throughout the training stage. While residents perceived the current program as strongest in the Clinical Practice, most recognized their roles extending beyond clinical practice to encompass research and general competencies. The majority of residents expressed willingness to participate in an institutional core competency program, preferring online learning, in-house education, and workload reduction. Educational needs showed clear difference across residency years, shifting from foundational skills and stress management in junior years to research methodology, academic writing, and health system competencies in senior years. These findings suggest that stage-specific, tailored approaches and structural support are necessary for competency-based training education in postgraduate medical education.

Residents rated their current program as strongest in clinical practice area, followed by the research area and the general competency area, consistent with previous findings [4]. However, when asked about ideal objectives of residency program, only a minority focused solely on clinical practice; most recognized the need for comprehensive education covering all three areas, exhibiting a formation of a multifaceted professional identity.

This aligns with professional identity formation theory, which describes residents’ gradual expansion from clinical-centered to academic and educational roles [8,10]. Our results suggest that roles outlined by the CanMEDS—scholar, communicator, and collaborator—are being increasingly internalized by Korean residents.

Residents perceived that faculty mainly contributed to the clinical practice area and research area, whereas they themselves recognized all three areas more evenly. This contrasts with previous studies where attending physicians prioritized professionalism-based competencies while residents emphasized practical skills [4,11], suggesting that educational objectives conveyed by faculty may not be explicitly received by residents. This perception gap suggests that regular dialogue between faculty and residents regarding educational goals, particularly in the general competency area, may be necessary to ensure alignment of objectives.

Most residents expressed willingness to participate in education, consistent with previous studies [8,12]. Motivations differed by area: professional development for the research and general competency area versus required competence for the clinical practice area, suggesting residents view clinical competency as fundamental obligation while considering research and general competency as voluntary professional growth opportunities.

Heavy workload and time constraints were the greatest barriers, aligning with findings that Korean residents consider common curricula ‘necessary but unfeasible’ [1]. Residents’ dual role as learner and healthcare provider results in insufficient protected learning time, with previous studies reporting feelings of being ‘cheap labor’ and experiencing work-learning imbalance [5].

Residents proposed in-house education, financial support, and workload reduction—solutions supporting previous recommendations for protected learning time and workload adjustment [4]. The FIRST Trial demonstrated that flexible duty-hour policies improved care continuity without compromising patient safety [13]. Educational program success therefore requires both leveraging learners' motivation and providing organizational support to ensure balance between education and clinical work [1].

Residents’ strong preference for in-house education and asynchronous online learning reflect their aspiration for time flexibility and accessibility, thereby seeking learning methods compatible with heavy clinical workloads. This aligns with previous studies showing residents value the ability to learn at their own pace through virtual courses and prefer online individual learning [5]. The preference for asynchronous learning reflects practical constraints that make physical attendance at fixed times difficult.

However, preference for offline education especially during the existing conference hours were noted as well. Given the limited protected learning time, this preference reflects a practical strategy to integrate core curriculum content into already-established departmental learning routines.

These findings support the necessity of a blended learning model. Previous research has shown that hybrid formats combining seminars with web-based components were most beneficial [14]. Theoretical content should be provided asynchronously online while procedural training should occur in-person with mentoring. This approach, especially when integrated with external resources such as academic conference sessions would effectively address residents’ preferences and practical constraints.

Clear differences in educational needs between junior and senior residents were noted. Junior residents expressed greater need for topics dealing with immediate clinical adaptation and personal well-being, while senior residents demonstrated relatively higher demand for topics related to academic productivity and health system understanding. These results align with previous studies [4,11]. This suggests that educational needs evolve from foundational clinical skills and personal adaptation to advanced scholarly competencies and system-level understanding as residency progresses.

Year-specific differences were also evident in the clinical practice area. Previous studies similarly showed that junior residents preferred observation and simulation-based education, while senior residents favored research presentation and career development activities, demonstrating a shift in educational focus from skill acquisition to academic and leadership development [15].

Therefore, stage-specific educational objectives considering the competence continuum are necessary. This stepwise change was also observed in research competencies. Senior residents’ increased research education needs align with their greater experience and time invested in academic activities [11]. However, the consistently high demand for research methodology across all years suggests this area should be included in the core curriculum throughout the training period, preferably in a stepwise manner.

Based on the results of this study, two evidence-based recommendations can be proposed for designing effective resident education programs. First, stage-specific curriculum by residency year is necessary. The significant differences in topic preferences across residency years directly support year-specific educational objectives: junior residents should receive core education focusing on practical adaptation and personal well-being, while senior residents should be provided with advanced education for strengthening research and health system competencies. Second, blended learning models and organizational support considering structural constraints are required. Residents’ strong preference for online learning combined with their practical interest in utilizing existing conference hours for offline education, as identified in this study, collectively support a hybrid approach; their expressed barriers—particularly heavy workload—further underscore the need for structural measures such as workload reduction and protected learning time. These recommendations are additionally reinforced by prior literature demonstrating the effectiveness of hybrid learning formats [14] and the importance of organizational support for sustainable resident education [1,4,13].

This study has limitations in generalizability due to its single-institution setting and insufficient reflection of specific competency levels and faculty perspectives. Furthermore, for the branched subsample analyses, the compared groups were partially overlapping as they were derived from the same pool of respondents. This partial dependency may have affected the independence assumption required for the Pearson’s chi-square test. Additionally, regarding the statistical analysis of multiple-response questions, we performed multiple chi-square tests without applying stringent multiple comparison corrections. Because this study was designed as an exploratory needs assessment to broadly identify potential educational trends and demands rather than as a confirmatory trial, we chose to retain partially overlapping sub-analyses and not to correct for multiple comparisons to avoid inflating Type II errors. Thus, the statistical findings for specific topics should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. Nonetheless, it provides a practical case of organizational change, with findings currently informing the implementation of an institutional residency core competency program at our hospital. By exploring residents’ role perception, participation motivation, educational barriers, format preferences, and year-specific content needs in a comprehensive manner, this study offers a practical foundation for educational program development at other institutions as well.

Future research should enhance generalizability and depth through multi-institutional and mixed-methods studies, develop and validate programs based on the identified needs, and specify implementation strategies for protecting the learning time and organizational support systems.

In conclusion, this study systematically analyzed residents' educational needs and provided direction for effective educational program design. Based on the results directly obtained in this study, two core recommendations emerge: a stage-specific curriculum aligned with residency year, and a blended learning model that accommodates clinical workload constraints. Residents demonstrated high learning motivation, but heavy workload served as the primary barrier, with preferences for asynchronous online and in-house education. These results, taken together with prior evidence on organizational support and protected learning time, further suggest that structural support is an equally essential condition for sustainable resident education. This study provides evidence for developing CBME-based resident curriculum through learner-centered needs assessment.
