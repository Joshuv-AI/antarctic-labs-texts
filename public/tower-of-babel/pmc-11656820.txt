Tim-3 pathway dysregulation and targeting in sepsis-induced immunosuppression
Authors: Jialiu Luo, Cong Zhang, Deng Chen, Teding Chang, Shunyao Chen, Zhiqiang Lin, Chengla Yi, Zhao-Hui Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a major medical problem which causes millions of deaths worldwide every year. The host immune response in sepsis is characterized by acute inflammation and a simultaneous state of immunosuppression. In the later stage of sepsis, immunosuppression is a crucial factor that increases the susceptibility of septic patients to secondary infection and mortality. It is characterized by T cell exhaustion, excessive production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, hyperproliferation of immune suppressor cells and aberrant expression of immune checkpoint molecules. T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (Tim-3), an immune checkpoint molecule, is found on the surface of various cells, including macrophages, NK cells, NKT cells, and T cells. There are four different ligands for Tim-3, and accumulating evidence indicates that Tim-3 and its ligands play a crucial role in regulating immune cell dysfunction during sepsis. Anti-Tim-3 antibodies have been applied in the field of cancer immunotherapy and have achieved positive therapeutic effects in some clinical trials. However, the therapeutic efficacy of Tim-3 blockade is still controversial in animal models of sepsis. These challenges highlight the need for a deeper understanding of Tim-3 signaling in sepsis. This review examines the comprehensive effect of Tim-3 signaling in the development of sepsis-induced immunosuppression and the therapeutic efficacy of Tim-3 blockade.Keywords: Immune checkpoint, Tim-3, Sepsis, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Sepsis is characterized by life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from an aberrant immune response to infection [1]. Sepsis represents a significant and under-recognized health-care problem, impacting approximately 48.9 million individuals worldwide annually and leading to 11 million fatalities [2]. Sepsis is not only solely characterized by acute inflammation, but also by a simultaneous state of immunosuppression that can endure for an extended period [3–5]. In the early phase of sepsis, the host responds by rapidly mobilizing immune cells and generating a significant surge of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This excessive inflammatory response contributes to the first peak of mortality associated with sepsis, as the overwhelming inflammation can cause severe damage to vital organs and systems. The process is promptly counterbalanced by several immunomodulatory mechanisms, such as soluble mediators and membrane-bound molecules, which aim to limit immunopathology and subsequent organ failure. However, as organ damage increases, individuals who survive sepsis become immunocompromised due to the prolonged activation of these immunomodulatory mechanisms. Therefore, in the later phase of sepsis, the persistent immunosuppression resulting from impaired immune responses is associated with compromised immunity, nosocomial infections, multiple organ failures, and mortality [6–10]. During this phase, a second peak of mortality associated with sepsis is observed, primarily due to secondary infections that arise as a result of sepsis-induced immunosuppression. It is characterized by abnormal death of immune effector cells, T cell exhaustion, excessive production of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-33), hyperproliferation of immune suppressor cells (Tregs and MDSCs) and aberrant expression of immune checkpoint molecules [11–15]. The pathophysiology of sepsis-induced immunosuppression has been extensively reviewed in recent years [16–18]; however, our work aims to address a gap in the existing literature by examining the role of an important immune checkpoint molecule, Tim-3, in sepsis-induced immunosuppression.

Immune checkpoint molecules bind to specific ligands found on various cell types. Immune checkpoint molecules include programmed cell death receptor-1 (PD-1), cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4), B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA), lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3), T cell immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT), and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain 3 (Tim-3) [19]. These molecules play a critical role in limiting immunopathology by regulating the immune response. Immune checkpoint molecules are like two-faced swords that act as gatekeepers of the immune system [20]. In the early phase of sepsis, the role of immune checkpoint molecules is to maintain immune balance and reduce tissue damage caused by excessive inflammatory response. The dysregulated immune response during sepsis is characterized by the persistence of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory mechanisms even after recovery from the early phase. In the later phase of sepsis, their upregulation on immune cells is believed to contribute to sepsis-induced immunosuppression by impairing cell functions [21]. These molecules play an important role in modulating T cell activation and inhibition, leading to T cell apoptosis and anergy during sepsis-induced immunosuppression [22, 23]. The utilization of checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) to blockade immune checkpoints has demonstrated improved host resistance to infections and outcomes in the pre-clinical studies and early clinical trials of sepsis [24–26]. Consequently, elucidating the involvement of immune checkpoint molecules in the pathogenesis of sepsis holds significant promise for sepsis treatment.

Tim-3, an immune checkpoint molecule, is expressed on various immune cells and plays an immunosuppressive role [27, 28]. The investigation of Tim-3 in sepsis is still insufficient in contrast to other immune checkpoints molecules, such as PD-1 or CTLA-4. In this review, we examine the existing literature and discuss the comprehensive effect of Tim-3 signaling in the development of sepsis-induced immunosuppression and the therapeutic efficacy of Tim-3 blockade. Our objective is to enhance the understanding of this emerging immune checkpoint molecule as a potentially effective immunomodulatory target in the management of sepsis.

Tim-3

Tim-3 is a type I transmembrane protein encoded by the HAVCR2 gene. It consists of an amino-terminal IgV domain, a mucin domain, a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic tail [29, 30]. Tim-3 contains a CC′ loop and an FG loop, with the CC′ loop forming a distinctive ligand-binding pocket [31]. A distinguishing characteristic of Tim-3 is the absence of recognized inhibitory signaling motifs in its cytoplasmic tail. Instead, the carboxyl-terminus has five tyrosine residues in cytoplasmic tail, two of them (Tyr256 and Tyr263) essential for Tim-3 signaling via interactions with HLA-B-associated transcript 3 (Bat3) [32]. If Tim-3 is not bound to a ligand, Bat3 can bound to Tyr256 and Tyr263 and recruit active Lck. Although the precise intracellular signaling pathway is still not completely elucidated, the current theory holds that Tim-3 permits to immune cell activation when Tim-3 is not bound to its ligand (Fig. 1) [33].

Tim-3 is expressed on various cell types, including T cells, macrophages, NKT cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, Treg cells, and monocytes. Literatures indicates that the downregulation or dysfunction of Tim-3 leads to autoimmune diseases by regulating macrophage function [34, 35]. Tim-3 expression is linked to elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in NKT cells, and increased Tim-3 expression in these cells leads to enhanced NKT cell apoptosis [27]. In addition, Tim-3 signaling can induce the differentiation of naive T cells into Tregs, thereby mediating immunosuppression in sepsis [36].

Tim-3 signaling

To date, Tim-3 has four known ligands: Galectin-9, carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 1 (Ceacam-1), phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), and high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB-1) [37–39].

Galectin-9 is widely produced by immune cells, such as T cells and mast cells, as well as by other cells in the parenchyma, including the gastrointestinal tract epithelium, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells [40–42]. Previous studies have shown that Galectin-9 binds to non-glycosylated Tim-3 with low affinity; however, this binding affinity increases when Galectin-9 binds to glycosylated Tim-3, causing significant structural changes in the Tim-3/Galectin-9 complex. This structural change may facilitate Tim-3’s ability to bind to other ligands, such as Ceacam-1 [43, 44]. Upon binding to Galectin-9, Tyr263 and Tyr256 in the cytoplasmic tail of Tim-3 become phosphorylated, leading to the dissociation of Bat3 from Tim-3 and the recruitment of Fyn. This process is widely believed to disrupt immune synapse formation, leading to phosphatase recruitment and resulting in immune cell anergy or apoptosis [33, 40]. Notably, Tim-3 can also be recruited to the immune synapse between APCs and T cells, where, under certain conditions, it provides a costimulatory signal without visibly disrupting the synapse [45].

HMGB-1 can be released by most types of cells under stress, such as trauma and infection, and it plays a role in regulating nuclear transcription, dendritic cell (DC) differentiation, and immune responses [46, 47]. By analyzing how Tim-3+ dendritic cells are impaired in their response to nucleic acids, researchers discovered that HMGB-1 is one of the ligands of Tim-3 [39]. HMGB-1 can bind to released RNA and activate the TLR-4 pathway, inducing innate immune cell activation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In tumors, however, the binding of Tim-3 to HMGB-1 can interfere with this process, inhibiting the activation of the innate immune response [30]. Tim-3 is expressed by tumor-infiltrating DCs in the tumor microenvironment, where it competes with nucleic acids produced by tumor cells for binding to HMGB-1, thereby blocking the entry of nucleic acids into endosomes. Consequently, Tim-3 mediates negative regulation of innate immune responses, thereby suppressing the function of innate immune cells through pattern recognition receptor for nucleic acids [39].

Phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) exhibits lower affinity for binding to Tim-3 compared to the above-mentioned ligands [40]. The combination of PtdSer and Tim-3 is important for the clearance of apoptotic cells by efferocytosis. During apoptosis, PtdSer undergoes redistribution to the outer membrane. A typical characteristic of apoptotic cells is PtdSer valgus, which is a crucial signal for macrophage recognition. The PtdSer exposed on apoptotic cells, which is a critical signal for inducing cell phagocytosis, can bind to Tim-3 expressed on macrophages [48]. The engagement of PtdSer promotes the phosphorylation of Tim-3, leading to PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 signaling resulting in dysfunction of NK cells [49].

Ceacam-1 is a cell–cell adhesion-related glycoprotein involved in immunomodulation, activation, and cell death. It is expressed on various cell types, including T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes, and tumor cells [50]. The Tim-3 CC′ and FG loops can bind to Ceacam-1, forming a specific heterodimer either in cis or trans. Knocking out Ceacam-1 in T cells lowers the expression levels of Tim-3 and reduces the production of immunosuppressive cytokines in these cells. This hyperinflammatory state can be reversed by re-expressing Ceacam-1 in T cells [38]. Simultaneous blockade of Tim-3 and Ceacam-1 enhances the immune response and reduces tumor burden in a mouse model of colorectal cancer [51].

The role of Tim-3 signaling pathway in sepsis-induced immunosuppression

During sepsis, Tim-3 is upregulated in immune cells and is thought to play a major role in immune cell dysfunction, thereby impairing the immune response needed to eliminate invasive pathogens [52]. The expression of Tim-3’s four ligands is also increased in various cells and organs during sepsis-induced immunosuppression [53–55]. These ligands have different receptors that regulate immune responses through complex signaling pathways. While some studies on sepsis-induced immunosuppression have examined the interaction between Galectin-9 and Tim-3, most research on the Tim-3 signaling pathway has not focused on specific ligands [55–57]. Furthermore, there is currently no evidence indicating which ligand is more effective in promoting immunosuppression. The following sections will discuss the role of the Tim-3 signaling in sepsis-induced immunosuppression.

Tim-3 and NKT cells

Tim-3 has been demonstrated to mediate NKT cell apoptosis [27, 58]. The expression of Tim-3 in NKT cells is elevated in septic patients and NKT cell apoptosis is significantly increased during sepsis. Our recent studies revealed that, in patients 1 day after sepsis, most apoptotic NKT cells were associated with Tim-3 expression [57]. This suggests that Tim-3+ NKT cells are more susceptible to apoptosis, resulting in extensive NKT cell death and correlating with poor prognosis in sepsis-induce immunosuppression (Fig. 2) [57]. In severe COVID-19 infection, the number of NKT cells is reduced, while the number of Tim-3+ NKT cells is increased [59]. Therefore, Tim-3 overexpression in NKT cells promotes apoptosis and may play a pivotal role in sepsis-induced immunosuppression.

Tim-3 expression levels are also linked to the severity and prognosis of sepsis. Our recent studies found that the percentage of Tim-3+ NKT cells was significantly higher in the septic shock group compared to both the sepsis and healthy control groups. Moreover, the percentage of Tim-3+ NKT cells was independently associated with 28-day mortality in sepsis [57]. Similar findings were observed in CD4+ T cells from septic mice. In a recurrent sepsis model, the percentage of Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells was significantly higher than in the acute sepsis model, which correlated with a worse prognosis [60].

Tim-3+ T cells are often considered as exhausted T cells, characterized by defects in their proliferative potential or cytokine production [61]. However, according to our previous findings, Tim-3 was predominantly expressed in active and proliferating NKT cells. Tim-3+ NKT cells had a more activated phenotype (CD25, CD69) compared to Tim-3− NKT cells. During sepsis, Fas ligand (FasL) expression in NKT cells was elevated, with Tim-3+ NKT cells showing a higher expression of FasL [57]. NKT cells from septic mice had stronger IFN-γ secretion capacity than those from sham-operated mice. Furthermore, Tim-3+ NKT cells from septic mice produced higher levels of IFN-γ compared to Tim-3− NKT cells [62]. The same phenomenon was observed in severe COVID-19 patients, where Tim-3+ NKT cells exhibited a greater capacity to secrete IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-4 compared to Tim-3− NKT cells [59]. In the early phase of sepsis, NKT cells rapidly secrete IFN-γ, which enhances the immune response by stimulating other immune cells to release more pro-inflammatory cytokines. While IFN-γ generally aids in infection clearance, elevated plasma levels of IFN-γ early in clinical sepsis have been linked to a higher susceptibility of developing secondary infections [63]. At the same time, Tim-3+ NKT cells inhibit the pro-inflammatory response by secreting classical anti-inflammatory cytokines and deteriorated the sepsis-induced immunosuppression through massive and rapid apoptosis of NKT cells. In conclusion, Tim-3 mediates the complex inflammatory response of sepsis by regulating the activation, cytokines production, and cytotoxicity of NKT cells.

Immune checkpoint molecules, including Tim-3, PD-1, and LAG-3, are critical for the function of NKT cells. Research confirms that immune checkpoint molecules influence each other. The upregulation of Tim-3 is linked to the failure of anti-PD1 immunotherapy in cancer [64]. In severe COVID-19 patients, the expression of PD-1 and LAG-3 in Tim-3+ NKT cells is higher compared to that in Tim-3− NKT cells [59]. Elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules further exacerbates sepsis-induced immunosuppression by inhibiting NKT cell function.

Tim-3 and macrophages

Tim-3 is also expressed on macrophages during sepsis-induced immunosuppression [39, 65, 66]. Macrophages have several pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) which can sense pathogens and activate an innate immune response. Tim-3 may induce autoimmune disease through regulating macrophage activation. Macrophages are categorized into two general subtypes: M1 macrophages and M2 macrophages. M2 macrophages are significantly increased during sepsis-induced immunosuppression, and blocking Tim-3 can further enhance the M2 response. Furthermore, the enhanced M2 response is associated with increased immunosuppression [65].

Tim-3 inhibits TLR-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokines production in macrophages [67]. TLRs can recognize pathogen-associated molecular pattern molecules (PAMPs), which are conserved microbial molecules. LPS can bind to TLR4, which signals through MyD88 and TRIF to upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines [68]. Research by Yang suggests that Tim-3 signaling plays a critical role in suppressing NF-κB activation in macrophages. This is achieved through the upregulation of A20, an inhibitor of NF-κB, and enhanced activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway, which together modulate the immune response during sepsis. This results in a significant decrease in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages (Fig. 3) [65]. Thus, the sepsis-induced immunosuppression state may be further aggravated in the later phase of sepsis.

Tim-3 and CD8+ T cells

Tim-3 has recently emerged as a key molecule in CD8+ T cell exhaustion during sepsis. Expression of Tim-3 on CD8+ T cells varies across different organs, with a marked increase observed in the spleens of septic mice [69]. The expression of Tim-3 by CD8+ T cells in the liver of septic mice showed unique changes. Our recent study found that Tim-3 expression on CD8+ T cells in the liver peaked 8 h after CLP, decreased thereafter, and rose again to a second peak at 36 h [28]. The expression of Tim-3 by CD8+ T cells has been associated with CD8+ T cell exhaustion during sepsis-induced immunosuppression [56]. Tim-3 signaling decreases the production of proinflammatory cytokine (IFN-γ) in CD8+ T cells and promotes upregulation of CD44 expression [55]. Tim-3 is also known to mediate apoptosis of CD8+ T cells in cancer and chronic virus infections, and its immunoregulation role in CD8+ T cells has been confirmed in sepsis [61, 70]. Evidence suggests that CD8+ T cell apoptosis increases in the later phase of sepsis [69, 71]. A recent study showed that the percentage of Tim-3+ CD8+ T cells is significantly elevated in septic patient, and these Tim-3+ CD8+ T cells are more prone to apoptosis [28]. Furthermore, CD8+ T cells apoptosis during sepsis is significantly reduced in mice expressing an enhanced-function mutant of Tim-3 [28]. This finding suggests that Tim-3-mediated sepsis-induced immunosuppression may be linked to apoptosis of liver CD8+ T cell.

Tim-3 and CD4+ T cells

Following stimulation, CD4+ T cells have the potential to develop into many different subtypes: Th1, Th2, Th9, Th17, regulatory T cells (Tregs). These subtypes play roles in activating multiple immune cells and contribute to sepsis-induced immunosuppression by regulating cytokine production [72]. Tim-3 is specifically expressed on Th1 cells, Th17 cells, and Treg cells [17, 29, 73]. Tim-3 expression on CD4+ T cells was significantly elevated during sepsis-induced immunosuppression. Furthermore, mortality in sepsis patients was correlated with the percentage of Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells [23, 60]. Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells display an exhausted phenotype during sepsis-induced immunosuppression. Compared to Tim-3− CD4+ T cells, Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells showed diminished proliferative capacity and increased expression of inhibitory markers such as LAG-3 and 2B4. Recurrent sepsis exacerbates immunosuppression, driving CD4+ T cells toward a more pronounced exhaustion phenotype. Compared with acute sepsis, Tim-3 expression in CD4+ T cells was increased in spleen from recurrent septic mice, which aggravates CD4+ T cell exhaustion and reduces the antiviral immune response including decreased CD4+ T cell infiltration, increased viral titers, and decreased inflammatory cytokines [60]. Tim-3 may suppress the function of CD4+ T cell by regulating the production of cytokines. Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells exhibit a marked increase in the production of the inhibitory cytokine IL-10, while the production of effector cytokines, like TNF-α and IL-1β, is significantly decreased [23]. A recent study demonstrated that Tim-3+ CD4+ T cells exhibited decreased Toll-like receptor, LPS-stimulated MAPK, and T helper cell differentiation signaling. Compared to WT mice, the mortality of Cd4CreTim-3fL/fL mice with sepsis decreased by maintaining organ function [23]. This provides evidence for blocking Tim-3 to treat immunosuppression in sepsis.

Tim-3 and other immune cells

Tim-3 is also expressed in monocytes, DC, NK cells, and Tregs, but their role in these cells’ biology during sepsis-induced immunosuppression has received less attention compared to other immune cells.

Monocytes are classified into different subgroups based on CD16 expression. The CD14+ CD16+ monocytes were significantly expanded in septic patients and these cells were involved in several immune responses [74]. Xia et al. used flow cytometry to detect the expression level of Tim-3 on monocytes from patients with sepsis. They found a reduction in Tim-3 expression only in CD14+CD16+ monocytes, while no significant changes were found in CD14+CD16− monocytes. When monocyte subgroups were not classified by CD16 expression, Tim-3 expression on CD14+ monocytes did not differ significantly between septic patients and healthy volunteers [75]. Zhang et al. found that TLR activation in monocytes can lower Tim-3 expression, which is associated with infection and elevated endotoxin [76]. In addition, studies have shown that reduced Tim-3 expression in decompensated cirrhosis is associated with impaired monocyte function [77]. Based on these findings, we speculate that Tim-3 regulates sepsis-induced immunosuppression by regulating the function of CD16+ monocytes.

NK cells are known for their cytotoxic ability to eliminate infected cells. They also produce cytokines, such as IFN-γ, which contribute to organ damage during sepsis [78]. Studies found that both the number and function of NK cells decline in the early phase of sepsis, leading to NK cell exhaustion [79, 80]. This exhaustion is linked to the development of an immunosuppressive phenotype, which may weaken the host defense against infections in the later phase of sepsis. The expression of Tim-3 in mice NK cells shows increase after 24 h of LPS injection [55]. NK cells are the primary producers of IFN-γ, and overexpression of Tim-3 was found to reduce IFN-γ production by these cells. Hou et al. indicated that the cytotoxicity of Tim-3+ NK cells decreased 24 h after LPS injection in mice. The expression of the degranulation marker CD107a was lower on Tim-3+ NK cells compared to Tim-3− NK cells [81].

Th17 cells, a subset of CD4+ T cells, play a key role in clearing pathogens during sepsis by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-17 and IL-22 [82]. These cytokines help recruit neutrophils to the site of infection and are crucial for maintaining mucosal immunity and barrier integrity, which prevents pathogen invasion. However, the role of Th17 cells in sepsis is complex. Prolonged Th17 cell activation may worsen immunosuppression, making the host more vulnerable to secondary infections [83–85]. During the hyperinflammatory phase of sepsis, the percentage of Th17 cells is elevated [86–88]. Th17 cells can secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and trigger immune response to clear infection. Tim-3 is expressed by Th17 cells and elevated during sepsis [40]. A recent study demonstrated that administration of mesenchymal stem cell (MSCs) improved the survival rate and protected organ function in septic mice [89]. The underlying mechanism is that MSCs mediated the hyperinflammatory phase by inducing IL-10 and FOXP3 expression via the Galectin-9/Tim-3 pathway and reducing the percentages of Th17 cells. During the immunosuppressive phase, the proportion of Th17 cells decreases [86, 90]. The immune checkpoint Tim-3 promotes immunosuppression by inducing Th17 cell apoptosis and inhibiting Th17-related pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion [89, 91].

Tregs are another key subset of T lymphocytes. These cells are known to suppress immune responses through various mechanisms, including the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β, direct inhibition of effector T cells, and regulation of antigen-presenting cells [92, 93]. In sepsis, Tregs play a dual role. In the early phase of sepsis, their inhibitory function may prevent excessive inflammatory responses and reduce organ damage. However, in the later phase of sepsis, they may promote immunosuppression, thus greatly increasing the risk of secondary infection [94–96]. The percentage of Tregs was elevated during sepsis-induced immunosuppression [60, 73]. Galectin-9/Tim-3 signaling induces the differentiation of naive T cells into Tregs [36]. The suppressive function of Tim-3+ Treg cells was enhanced in in vitro suppression assays compared to Tim-3− Treg cells, and Tim-3+ Treg cells exhibit robust CD39 and IL-10 expression. Furthermore, the expression of other immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, LAG-3, and CTLA-4 was significantly increased on Tim-3+ Treg cells [97–99].

Targeting Tim-3 signaling pathway during sepsis

Previous studies have demonstrated the inhibitory effects of Tim-3 on immune cell function in autoimmune, immune tolerance disorders, tumors, and sepsis [40, 100]. Since immune cell exhaustion may be reversible, the new potential target Tim-3 has received a lot of interest. The blockade of Tim-3 has shown positive therapeutic effects in some clinical trials for cancer immunotherapy [101–103]. However, the possible effects of Tim-3 blockade on the immune function and prognosis of sepsis remain unknown.

α-Lactose

α-Lactose is a disaccharide sugar that binds to Galectin-9, thereby preventing Galectin-9 from interacting with Tim-3 [61]. Our previous studies demonstrated that administering α-lactose to hinder Tim-3 signaling can reduce the mortality in septic mice [57, 62]. In septic mice, α-lactose was administered at 0, 2, 6, 18, and 36 h after CLP, resulting in a decrease in 28-day mortality from 75 to 30% with a 10% α-lactose treatment. Furthermore, a high dose of α-lactose (10%) led to a higher survival rate compared to a lower concentration (5%) [57]. Notably, in Tim-3 mutant mice—where the cytoplasmic domain of Tim-3 is removed—α-lactose treatment did not improve survival rates [57]. Although the survival rate of Tim-3 mutant mice with sepsis treated with α-lactose showed a slight increase, it was not statistically significant [57].

α-Lactose can prevent NKT cell apoptosis and increase the percentage of NKT cells in liver and spleen. In an in vitro experiment, rhGal-9-induced apoptosis in NKT cells was inhibited after preincubation with α-lactose [57]. Furthermore, α-lactose suppressed the expression of Tim-3 in NKT cells during sepsis by blocking the Galectin-9/Tim-3 pathway and inhibiting IL-12 production. Decreased expression of Tim-3 may prevent the potentially deleterious effects of massive apoptosis of NKT cells [62]. A similar phenomenon can be observed in CD8+ T cells, and the expression of Tim-3 was also significantly reduced in liver CD8+ T cells in septic mice treated with α-lactose [28].

α-Lactose can improve outcomes of septic mice by attenuating production of inflammatory cytokines. It was shown that administering α-lactose in the early phase of sepsis reduces the expression of cytokines, thereby mitigating its excessive production [62]. Recent study showed that α-lactose can reduce the production of IL-15, which is important for NKT cell proliferation. The increase in the liver NKT cell population after α-lactose treatment during sepsis is due to reduced NKT cell apoptosis rather than cell proliferation [62]. In addition, α-lactose helps decrease liver inflammation and damage during sepsis. Septic mice treated with α-lactose at 0, 2, 6, 18, 36 and 72 h post-CLP showed reduced liver damage and inflammation, with significantly lower ALT and AST activity levels [28].

Many clinical trials of immunomodulators for sepsis have failed, alerting researchers to the potential harmful effects of these drugs, which can have both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory effects [104]. Recent studies suggest that α-lactose holds promise as a novel and cost-effective immunomodulator for sepsis. As a disaccharide derived from glucose, α-lactose is neither pro-inflammatory nor anti-inflammatory. It has been approved as a common pharmaceutical excipient in over 2300 oral capsule. Currently, α-lactose has few reported adverse reactions and can be administered intravenously. In conclusion, α-lactose shows potential as an immunomodulator for sepsis treatment and warrants further investigation.

Anti-Tim-3 antibodies

At present, the therapeutic efficacy of anti-Tim-3 antibodies in preclinical models of sepsis remains controversial. Some earlier studies showed that the blockade of Tim-3 with anti-Tim-3 antibodies resulted in decreased survival in septic mice [55, 65]. However, recent evidence has reached the reverse conclusion that the survival rate was elevated in septic mice treated with anti-Tim-3 antibody [69]. Table 1 shows a summary of the outcomes of Tim-3 blockade in preclinical studies of sepsis. Recent work by Huang et al. [23] indicated that blocking Tim-3 in the later phase of sepsis reduced mortality, whereas blockade during the hyperinflammatory phase did not have this effect. Based on these findings, we suggest that Tim-3 may help limit excessive inflammation in the early phase of sepsis. Thus, blocking Tim-3 during this early phase could potentially worsen inflammation and increase organ damage. Conversely, Tim-3 blockade in the later phase of sepsis may counteract its immunosuppressive effects and improve survival rates [23]. This may help explain the contradictory results observed in previous studies.

Some previous preclinical studies have indicated that blocking Tim-3 can increase sepsis mortality. Yang et al. found that administering anti-Tim-3 antibodies increased mortality in septic mice. Tim-3 blockade affected macrophage responsiveness, leading to a substantial upregulation of mRNA levels for proinflammatory cytokines and Toll-like receptors (TLR2 and TLR4) in macrophages [65]. In another study by the same group, blocking the Tim-3 pathway with soluble Tim-3 increased both the number and activity of M1 macrophages in septic mice. In addition, Tim-3 signaling blockade heightened T cell apoptosis and reduced proinflammatory cytokine production [105]. Similar findings were reported by Wang et al., where Tim-3 pathway blockade increased mortality in septic mice, while activating the Tim-3 pathway via Galectin-9 reduced mortality. Anti-Tim-3 antibody administration elevated TNF-α and IFN-γ levels 24 h after LPS injection [55]. These researchers hypothesized that Tim-3 blockade exacerbates the uncontrolled inflammatory response in the early phase of sepsis, leading to reduced survival rates.

Recent research suggests that Tim-3 blockade can improve the prognosis of septic mice. In a study by Liu et al. [69], Tim-3 blockade significantly reduced mortality in CLP mice. Mice in the treatment group received an injection of 50 μg of anti-Tim-3 antibody through the tail vein 30 min post-CLP, which attenuated sepsis-induced organ damage and excessive inflammatory response. In addition, anti-Tim-3 antibody treatment decreased apoptosis rates, particularly in CD8+ T cells. Blocking the Tim-3 pathway promoted T lymphocyte proliferation, increased TNF-α and IL-10 production, and restored CD8+ T cell responses against viral infections [75]. In vitro experiments further confirmed that anti-Tim-3 antibodies counteract the pro-apoptotic effects of rhGal-9 on NKT cells [57]. Other studies have also shown that Tim-3 blockade increases the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ, enhances cytotoxic activity, and reduces apoptosis in NK cells [81].

Recent work by Huang et al. [23] found systemic Tim-3 deletion and conditional deletion of Tim-3 in CD4+ T cells both decreased mortality in mice with sepsis by maintaining liver and kidney organ function. Furthermore, the protective effect only occurs in secondary infection (Streptococcus pneumoniae injection) rather than first infection (CLP), suggesting the different effect of Tim-3 blockade in different phase of sepsis. The blockade of Tim-3 in the hyperinflammatory phase of sepsis maybe ineffective. The sepsis second-hit model by intra-nasal infection S. pneumoniae (Sp) in surviving mice 4 days after CLP simulates the immunosuppressed status during sepsis. Therefore, the blockade of Tim-3 may decrease the mortality by attenuating sepsis-induced immunosuppression. Research by Wu et al. [57] indicated that impaired Tim-3 activity provides a protective effect in septic mice, with an elevated survival rate observed in Tim-3 mutant mice. In these Tim-3 mutant mice with sepsis, the percentage of apoptotic splenic NKT cells was lower compared to wild-type (WT) mice.

Currently, anti-Tim-3 antibodies have not been tested in clinical trials for sepsis; however, several Phase I/II clinical trials in cancer are underway with these antibodies. Preliminary data indicate that anti-Tim-3 antibodies are safe and no serious adverse events reported [106–108]. The positive effects and favorable safety profile of these antibodies suggest Tim-3 is a promising therapeutic target for sepsis. However, negative effects have been associated with administering Tim-3 antibodies at least 1 day prior to the onset of sepsis [55, 65]. Tim-3 blockade may reduce mortality by mitigating sepsis-induced immunosuppression rather than by addressing hyperinflammation.

Conclusions and perspective

Currently, novel treatment strategies beyond conventional therapies are urgently needed for managing sepsis-induced immunosuppression. The advancement of immune checkpoint therapy offers a new therapeutic approach. Tim-3 is upregulated on various types of immune cells in sepsis patients, and our recent studies have shown that blocking the Tim-3 pathway with α-lactose prevented lymphocytes apoptosis and reduces mortality in septic mice. Although the literature supports a role of Tim-3 in sepsis-induced immunosuppression, the mechanisms driving Tim-3 upregulation and whether this checkpoint molecule remains elevated in sepsis survivors are still unclear. Future studies are needed to investigate the specific intracellular signaling pathways of Tim-3 and its overall impact on sepsis-induced immunosuppression. Further challenges include identifying the optimal timing for Tim-3 blockade and establishing effective biomarkers to determine which subgroups of sepsis patients might benefit most from Tim-3-targeted therapies.
