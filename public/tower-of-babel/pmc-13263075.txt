Neuroma Prevention with a Novel In Situ Forming Hydrogel in a Rat Sciatic Nerve Model
Authors: Sophia Campbell, Yong Ren, Zoe Mote, Joshua G. Vose, Corinne Bright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Symptomatic neuromas are a common source of chronic pain after transection injury to peripheral nerves. This study evaluated a novel, bioresorbable in situ forming hydrogel nerve cap (allay Nerve Cap, Tulavi Therapeutics) for its ability to reduce the risk of neuroma formation in a rat model of sciatic nerve transection neurectomy.Methods:Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 54) underwent surgical transection of the left sciatic nerve, followed by placement of the hydrogel nerve cap (n = 18), a conventional nerve cap (n = 18), or a sham procedure (n = 18) with no cap. Animals were evaluated weekly for recovery of sensorimotor function (Hargreaves test) and euthanized at 4 weeks and 3 months for gross and histopathologic evaluation.Results:At 4-week and 3-month necropsy, no animals in the hydrogel cap group (0 of 14), 3 of 9 animals in the conventional cap, and 6 of 10 animals in the sham group had developed neuromas. Animals in the hydrogel cap group demonstrated faster return to baseline sensitivity than other groups. Histologically, the hydrogel prevented leukocyte infiltration to the nerves. The observed inflammatory response to the hydrogel cap was consistent with that expected of a resorbable implant. Histological neuroma scores were lower for the hydrogel cap versus conventional cap (P = 0.0157) and sham (P = 0.0007).Conclusions:The in situ forming hydrogel-based nerve cap prevented neuroma formation in a rat model of peripheral nerve transection over a clinically relevant timeframe. Although translational study in humans is needed, these results suggest that the hydrogel cap may be capable of preventing neuroma formation following peripheral nerve injury.

Takeaways

Question: Can a novel, bioresorbable in situ forming hydrogel nerve cap reduce the risk of neuroma formation after peripheral nerve transection in a rat model?

Findings: No rats that underwent sciatic nerve transection followed by hydrogel nerve cap implantation (0 of 14) developed neuromas after 4 or 12 weeks, compared to 3 of 9 animals with a conventional nerve cap and 6 of 10 animals with no cap.

Meaning: Pending further clinical study, the novel hydrogel nerve cap may represent a new option for preventing formation of symptomatic neuromas and associated pain after amputation or other transecting peripheral nerve injuries.

INTRODUCTION

Neuromas are common, benign, noncancerous tumors that arise from damaged nerves after amputation or transecting injury.1 They are characterized by disorganized, aberrant axonal nerve outgrowth coupled with extensive collagen deposition.2 Approximately 4%–15% of patients with below-knee amputations and 5%–25% with upper-limb amputations develop symptomatic neuromas with chronic pain, which can profoundly affect quality of life and productivity in patients already adjusting to limb loss.1 There are approximately 55,000 upper-extremity, 540,000 lower-extremity, and 45,000 finger amputations per year,3,4 resulting in a significant patient burden.

The biological mechanism of neuroma formation remains incompletely understood.1,5 What is known is that nerve regeneration attempts are limited to a finite timeframe, based on coordinated signals from the neuron, immune cell environment, and distal Schwann cells. Disorganized axons and neuroma can be observed histologically by 28 days,5 and neuromas have been observed to be fully formed and stable within the first 3–4 months after injury,1 indicating that factors critical to neuroma formation are present early after injury.

Traditional methods for treating or preventing neuromas include traction neurectomy, burying the nerve into muscle or bone, or capping with allograft or biologic materials.1,6–10 Unfortunately, these approaches have demonstrated limited overall efficacy, and conventional nerve caps available today leave space, albeit confined, around the nerve and thus do not effectively inhibit axonal outgrowth.9,11

New surgical approaches, including targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI), have focused on giving the nerve “something to do and somewhere to go.”12,13 Such methods attempt to encourage muscle reinnervation by grafting injured nerves onto a target nerve or a denervated muscle flap, respectively.1,14 However, these techniques require specialized instruments; training in current microsurgery techniques; a low patient comorbidity burden; and, in the case of TMR, extended anesthesia time to accommodate a longer surgical procedure.15–17The original developers of the RPNI procedure have suggested that factors including immunosuppression, nicotine use, and vascular disease could be risk factors of poor revascularization of RPNI and should be avoided.17–19 Thus, additional, high-quality research is needed to understand patient-associated risk factors for these procedures to guide optimal patient selection.

Considering the magnitude of the problem, the limitations of current techniques and their modest efficacy, there remains a significant and meaningful clinical need for the development of a reliable and widely accessible method for treating and preventing symptomatic neuromas. Recently, a bioresorbable in situ forming hydrogel was developed to reduce the development of neuromas in injured peripheral nerves. Unlike conventional nerve caps, the hydrogel completely conforms and adheres to the nerve surface to encapsulate the proximal nerve stump, creating a mechanical barrier between the nerve and the surrounding tissue. By limiting neuroma formation overall, the hydrogel could potentially limit the emergence of both asymptomatic and symptomatic neuromas.

The present preclinical study was designed to test the hypothesis that covering the proximal end of a transected nerve with the hydrogel cap after a sciatic nerve transection would block axonal outgrowth and inflammatory cell infiltration, reducing gross neuroma formation compared to a sham transection or transection and treatment with a conventional capping device.

METHODS

Study Overview and Ethics

This preclinical study evaluated the ability of a hydrogel nerve cap to reduce the risk of development of neuroma, compared to a sham procedure and a conventional, commercially available nerve capping device, in an established rat model of sciatic nerve transection. Neuroma formation in vivo was assessed by blinded gross and histopathologic evaluation of the transected nerves and surrounding tissue at 4 weeks and 3 months postsurgery. The study protocol was approved by the NAMSA Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Minneapolis, MN) and all animal care and procedures were carried out by trained staff, under veterinary supervision, in an American Association for Accredation of Laborarory Animal Care–accredited facility and according to US Food and Drug Administration Good Laboratory Practice regulations.

Test Article Description

The hydrogel in this study (allay Nerve Cap, Tulavi Therapeutics, Los Gatos, CA) was granted a De Novo classification from the US Food And Drug Administration in 2024 and is labeled as a physical barrier to separate the peripheral nerve end from the surrounding environment to reduce the risk of the development of a symptomatic neuroma. Its principal chemical component is a sterile powder of functionalized multi-arm polyethylene glycol (PEG) molecules suspended in a diluent buffer. PEG is a fully synthetic hydrophilic polymer with a lack of available cell binding sites, making it very biologically inert and resistant to cell ingrowth when crosslinked.20 To form a nerve cap, the PEG suspension is mixed with an accelerator during delivery around the proximal end of a transected peripheral nerve, within a temporary silicone form. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which shows hydrogel nerve cap preparation and placement procedure, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E940.) The accelerator buffer facilitates crosslinking of the PEG monomers in situ within seconds of combining to form a flexible, transparent, bioabsorbable hydrogel that encapsulates the nerve stump and fully conforms to the shape of the transected end (Fig. 1A–C). Over time, the hydrogel is degraded by hydrolysis into chemically inert PEG molecules of very low toxicity,21 which are then eventually excreted in the urine and feces.22,23

The formulation and characteristics of the adaptive polyethylene glycol hydrogel, with a schematic of multi-arm polyethylene glycol crosslinking (A), a visual demonstration of the hydrogel’s flexibility and strength (B), and hydrogel delivery on the end of a mock nerve (C). The elastic modulus of the hydrogel is slightly greater than that of native nerve tissue (D) and simulated degradation experiments show that the hydrogel persists for longer than the typical time course of neuroma formation (E).

Hydrogel Physical Characterization

Specific physical characteristics of the hydrogel were evaluated, including time course of degradation and mass loss, functional life, elastic modulus, and permeability to small and large molecules, as detailed in Supplemental Digital Content 2. (See document, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which shows physical characterization of the hydrogel, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E941.)

Sciatic Nerve Transection Neurectomy

The rat sciatic nerve model is an established and clinically relevant model for studying nerve regeneration after peripheral nerve injury.24–28 Nerve transection alone results in classic neuroma formation within 3–4 months, with gross and histologic evaluation used to evaluate their incidence and characteristics.

Surgery was performed on adult female Sprague-Dawley rats, using an aseptic technique and anesthesia according to Good Laboratory Practice. In all animals, the left sciatic nerve was exposed using a standard gluteal-muscle splitting incision. The nerve was then transected, and approximately 10 mm of nerve was removed to prevent the proximal and distal stumps from reconnecting.

For animals in the sham procedure group (n = 18), the surgical site was closed after nerve transection, without further treatment. For animals in the control-device group (n = 18), a conventional nerve cap (Neurocap, Polyganics/Regenity, Paramus, NJ) was sutured into place around the nerve stump according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E941). For animals in the hydrogel group (n = 18), the nerve cap was placed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplemental Digital Content 1 and 2, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E940; https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E941). All animals received buprenorphine postoperatively and as needed for pain or discomfort throughout the study.

Assessments

Animal health and behavior were monitored postoperatively per protocol. Recovery of sensorimotor function in the left hindleg was evaluated weekly using the Hargreaves paw-withdrawal latency assessment,29 in which shorter paw-withdrawal latencies signified earlier return to the preprocedure baseline response to painful stimulus. Any animals showing autotomy behavior, which is common in nerve transection models in which a large gap is created and the nerve cannot regenerate to a distal target, were euthanized early. Early-terminated animals were reassigned to the 4-week analysis group if they were euthanized within prespecified assessment windows (+10/−4 d for the 4-wk group, or 60–90 d for the 3-mo group).

Randomly selected groups of animals from each experimental cohort were euthanized at 4 weeks and 3 months for gross and histopathologic evaluation of the transected nerves and the perineural tissues. Tissue specimens were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The primary assessments were the extent of neuroma formation at each time point and the biocompatibility of the hydrogel in nerve and surrounding tissue. Gross neuroma assessments were performed by a blinded independent neurosurgeon (T.J.W.). All other microscopic assessments were performed by a blinded, independent pathologist (T.C.). The extent of neuroma, fibrosis, and inflammation were scored semiquantitatively using a scale of 0 (none) to 4 (extensive). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which shows the histological evaluation scoring method for biological response, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E942.)

Data Analysis

Means and SDs were reported for continuous variables and proportions were reported for categorical variables. The Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn test for multiple comparisons was performed to compare presence of neuroma grossly. Hargreaves assessments and histological scoring were compared using two-way analysis of variance with the Tukey multiple comparisons test (α = 0.05).

RESULTS

Hydrogel Physical Characteristics

In vitro evaluation of the hydrogel showed that the stiffness remained greater than that of nerve tissue (12–54 kPa)30 (Fig. 1D), with a decrease of only 16.0% ± 1.0% when activation was delayed 30 minutes after reconstitution. In the accelerated aging experiment, 92.7% ± 5.5 % of the solid-mass residue of the hydrogel remained at 4 months and 75.1% ± 3.4% at 6 months, with complete degradation by 8 months (Fig. 1E).

Evaluation of permeability showed that although the small-molecule dye FD&C Blue No.1 (749 Da) diffused freely, diffusion of a large molecular-weight, protein-based dye, TRITC-BSA (tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate-bovine serum albumin; 66 kDa), was prevented. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 4, which shows the permeability of the adaptive polyethylene glycol hydrogel, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E943.)

Sciatic Nerve Transection and Capping

Surgical procedures were completed on 54 rats (n = 18 per group). Following the surgical procedures, the blue-tinted, polymerized hydrogel nerve cap was plainly visible covering each proximal nerve stump (Fig. 2A). During the 12-week postsurgical observation period, a total of 27 (n = 27) study animals were euthanized early, all due to autotomy. The number of animals euthanized was 8 of 17 (47%) in the sham group, 11 of 8 (61%) in the conventional nerve cap group, and 8 of 18 (44%) in the hydrogel nerve cap group. Early terminations that fell within range of the assessment period were analyzed for neuroma grossly and histologically (8 animals), and the remaining 19 animals were excluded from neuroma analysis. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 5, which shows animal disposition for gross neuroma and histological assessment, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E944.)

Nerve cap and neuroma formation showing the blue hydrogel nerve cap at delivery surrounding the proximal sciatic nerve stump (A) with no gross changes to the nerve stump after 3 months (C) compared to a sham procedure after 3 months, in which a neuroma has formed distal to the transected nerve end (D). Paw-withdrawal latency (B) increased from baseline in all animals immediately after the surgical procedure then returned to baseline sensitivity over time in the hydrogel and conventional nerve cap groups, and neuroma was evident at necropsy in the conventional cap and sham groups but not the hydrogel group (E).

After nerve transection, animals in all three groups displayed a decrease in sensitivity to painful stimulus (ie, increased paw-withdrawal latency during the Hargreaves test) in the left hindleg compared to the preprocedure baseline measurement (Fig. 2B). By week 9, paw-withdrawal latency had returned to baseline in animals in the hydrogel group but not in the sham procedure group (P = 0.035). Similarly, paw-withdrawal latency remained elevated in the conventional cap group compared to the hydrogel group at 3 and 6 weeks (P = 0.0267, P = 0.0053).

At necropsy at 4 weeks and 3 months, gross evaluation showed no (0%) evidence of neuroma formation in the hydrogel group (0 of 14) (Fig. 2C). In the sham procedure group, 60% (6 of 10) grossly demonstrated neuroma formation, characterized by disorganized regeneration attempted from the transection site (Fig. 2D). Neuromas formed in 33% (3 of 9) of animals in the conventional cap group (Fig. 2E).

Histologic Findings

Neuromas were not observed in any animals treated with the hydrogel (0%). The hydrogel nerve cap was visible on histologic examination at 28 and 90 days, creating a clear demarcation between the nerve and surrounding tissue without leukocyte infiltration (Fig. 3A, B). Inflammatory response adjacent to the hydrogel and conventional cap sites were consistent with known tissue response to a resorbable implant. A minimal or mild tissue response was limited to the implant-tissue interface and was characterized by infiltration with macrophages and multinucleated giant cells, with few lymphocytes and rare polymorphonuclear cells and fibrosis.

Hematoxylin and eosin histology of sciatic nerve transection site at 90 days in the hydrogel group (A, B) showing the implanted hydrogel capsule (asterisks), clear margins around the end of the nerve, mild mesenchymal/Schwann cell proliferation, and no neuroma formation; sham procedure (C, D) showing moderate mesenchymal/Schwann cell proliferation and moderate neuroma formation; and conventional cap implant (E, F) showing mild mesenchymal/Schwann cell proliferation, and minimal neuroma formation. Histological scoring of nerve features (G) demonstrated that neuroma formation was significantly reduced in hydrogel cap animals.

Histologically, neuroma formation after sham procedures was evident by the integration of nerve tissue into fibrotic and mesenchymal tissue within the muscle bed (Fig. 3C, D). Histology in animals implanted with the conventional nerve cap showed mild but evident cellular proliferation, deposition of fibrous tissue, and visible evidence of the nerve cap material persisting in situ (Fig. 3E, F).

Hydrogel animals demonstrated a significantly lower neuroma score compared to the conventional cap (P = 0.0157) and sham (P = 0.0007) groups, with a negligible fibrosis score (0) (Fig. 3G). The inflammatory response was similar across groups, consistent with neural injury and resolution.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the ability of a novel hydrogel nerve cap to reduce the risk of neuroma formation in transected peripheral nerves. The PEG hydrogel formulation inhibited neuroma formation in an established rat model of sciatic nerve transection, yielding a significantly reduced incidence of gross neuroma formation compared to sham control and a conventional nerve cap.

Neuroma formation is a frequent consequence of peripheral nerve transection, resulting from disorganized regeneration of axons that form a mass of nerve and scar tissue.1 Not all neuromas are symptomatic, and the factors that enable some neuromas to exhibit painful and debilitating symptoms are not completely understood.1,31 However, the ability to consistently prevent neuroma formation using a simple hydrogel cap is a promising first step toward potentially alleviating undesired clinical outcomes after peripheral nerve injury which, in cases of limb loss, cause 85% of patients to report residual pain or phantom sensations.1

The durability of the hydrogel was assessed to ensure a polymer functional life that supports practical use in a surgical environment. Assessments performed at 0 and 30 minutes after suspension confirmed that the hydrogel precursors maintain functionality to crosslink, with acceptable stiffness characteristics, for up to 30 minutes after suspension. The protective nature of the hydrogel was supported by these findings, including a stiffness similar to or slightly greater than nerve tissue that isolated the nerve from the local environment and created a physical barrier to diffusion of macromolecules and to infiltration by inflammatory cells. This barrier also blocked the axonal outgrowth associated with the natural history of neuroma formation, as evidenced by the lack of tissue ingrowth from the transected nerve end into the gel. The marked, overall decrease in neuroma formation suggests that both asymptomatic and symptomatic neuromas were suppressed.32

Importantly, there was no evidence that the hydrogel increased inflammation or fibrosis in the local environment following implantation compared to the sham or conventional cap control. Furthermore, in vivo, the hydrogel nerve cap was associated with earlier return to preprocedure baseline sensitivity to painful stimulus, and a shorter paw-withdrawal latency compared to the sham, indicating that it supported postsurgical recovery of sensorimotor function and did not impair healing of the limb. Presumably, other nerves that innervate the rat hindlimb, including the femoral and saphenous nerves, were able to recover function more rapidly in the hydrogel treated animals.

This study’s central hypothesis was that covering the proximal end of a transected nerve with the conformable PEG hydrogel would reduce neuroma formation compared to a sham transection procedure or a conventional nerve capping device. It has previously been shown that in the absence of distal trophic support, or in the event of a reinjury to the proximal stump, the period of attempted regeneration and reinnervation is limited to 3-4 months.5 Schwann cell-derived neurotrophic factors that regulate axon regeneration peak around 1 month after injury, decline to around 50% of peak levels around 2–3 months, and return to very low levels by around 6 months.33 The hydrogel in this study was formulated specifically to degrade slowly, hydrolyzing and resorbing over a period of approximately 8 months. As observed in the in vitro and in vivo experiments, degradation is initially slow, remaining intact in implanted animals at the 3-month time point, by which time neuromas had formed in the sham procedure animals. Degradation begins at approximately 4 months, resulting in eventual hydrogel softening and then liquefaction, transport, and excretion over time. Without a reinjury to the area, the stimuli for nerve growth should be resolved, leaving a quiescent nerve end.

The current surgical alternatives to nerve capping include TMR and RPNI. The advantages of these techniques are that they are deviceless and offer the potential benefit of reinnervation for future control of a myoelectric prosthetic. A recent meta-analysis of 14 studies evaluating TMR in 366 patients and 3 studies evaluating RPNI in 75 patients determined that, when used for pain treatment, TMR and RPNI improved neuroma pain to some degree in 75%–100% of patients and phantom limb pain in 45%–80% of patients.19 Prophylactic use of the techniques resulted in 48%–100% of patients reporting no neuroma pain and 45%–87% reporting no phantom limb pain.

Despite this promising outlook, the published clinical experience with TMR and RPNI remains limited, with reported efficacy outcomes based mainly on studies with small populations and heterogeneous study designs that complicate interpretation.18 Although further investigation and refinement of TMR and RPNI are warranted, the results of the present study suggest that the hydrogel cap may represent a reliable and simple alternative to reducing the risk of neuroma formation, especially when no additional motor nerves or local muscle are available for TMR or RPNI, respectively, or in challenging anatomical situations such as nerve-transecting injury of the digits. Future studies are needed to determine optimal patient selection for each of the available approaches.

Limitations

There are several key limitations to this study. First, this was a preclinical study in a rat model of nerve transection, and therefore, the results require clinical confirmation. However, the selected model is well established for the study of neuroma, and therefore, the results remain promising.

Second, although neuromas are common after peripheral nerve transection, the degree and presentation of nerve impairment in rat neuroma is highly variable based on strain. Symptoms of cold and heat allodynia and hypersensitivity have been widely noted from neuromas formed with the sciatic nerve transection model.27 Neuroma tissues contain disorganized scar tissue and axonal growth, which can cause unpredictable compression and cytokine secretion that may influence neuropathic pain.1 There are not reliable methods to distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic neuromas histologically. In rats, neuromas are quantified using gross dissection and histology, but that is not feasible in a clinical setting. Thus, it is not unexpected that observed neuroma rates in rats are higher than published rates in humans. In humans, the incidence of symptomatic neuroma for a lower limb amputation has been reported to be approximately 19%.34 It is our opinion that prevention of all neuromas should be the primary goal of any clinical treatment.

The Sprague-Dawley rats used in this model are known to engage in high levels of autotomy to the insensate foot after nerve transection. For humane reasons, assessment out to 8 months, when the hydrogel is expected to be cleared entirely from the repair site, was not possible. However, as neuroma formation has been shown to be stable at 3–4 months,5 the inflammatory and neurotrophic environment is expected to be resolved by the time of hydrogel clearance. Additional study will be necessary to determine whether neuroma suppression is durable after resorption.

In conclusion, these experiments demonstrated that an in situ forming hydrogel nerve cap that conforms to the proximal end of a transected nerve can effectively prevent neuroma formation in an established rat model. Use of the hydrogel is simple and straightforward, resulting in nerve protection over a clinically relevant duration, after which it degrades and is fully resorbed. Further clinical study is warranted to understand human outcomes in preventing formation of symptomatic neuromas and associated pain after nerve injury.
