Single-nuclei transcriptomics reveals cellular senescence in pediatric anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy
Authors: Diwakar Turaga, Chang-Ru Tsai, Yuka Morikawa, Hanna J Tadros, Yi Zhao, Lalita Wadhwa, Iki Adachi, Xiao Li, James F Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemotherapy drugs that fight childhood cancer can age heart cells prematurely. We found this “cellular aging” throughout the hearts of children with treatment-related heart failure.Keywords: Cardiology, OncologyKeywords: Heart failure

To the Editor: Anthracyclines such as doxorubicin are integral to pediatric cancer treatment, yet their use carries a substantial long-term cardiovascular burden. Pediatric anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy is a major late effect, with progression to advanced heart failure and the need for transplantation in a subset of survivors (1). Although anthracycline cardiotoxicity has been extensively studied in experimental models and adult populations (2, 3), the developing pediatric myocardium, characterized by distinct metabolic states, stress-adaptive responses, and cell-cycle regulation, may respond differently to chemotoxic injury. As a result, the in vivo, cell type–specific transcriptional consequences of anthracycline exposure in children remain poorly defined, underscoring the need for direct interrogation of human pediatric myocardial tissue.

We performed single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on left ventricular myocardium to generate a high-resolution, cell type–resolved transcriptomic atlas of pediatric anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy (pAICM). We integrated data from 5 female pediatric patients exposed to anthracyclines who progressed to end-stage heart failure requiring transplantation, along with age- and sex-matched patients with pediatric dilated cardiomyopathy (pDCM) with end-stage heart failure and nonfailing pediatric donors (pDonors) (Figure 1A and Supplemental Table 1; supplemental material available online with this article; https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI204909DS1). Following quality control and integration, the final dataset comprised 63,826 nuclei from patients with pAICM, 31,973 from patients with pDCM, and 29,190 from pDonors (Figure 1B). Unsupervised clustering identified 11 major cardiac cell populations, annotated using established canonical markers, with cell type composition varying across groups (Figure 1C and Supplemental Figure 1, A–D).Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis in pAICM cardiomyocytes revealed selective enrichment of the senescence pathway (Figure 1D). Cellular senescence is a stress-induced cell fate characterized by stable cell-cycle arrest and a proinflammatory, hypersecretory phenotype (4). We then applied a curated composite gene set integrating canonical senescence pathways and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) components (Senescence Signature Genes; Figure 1A). Using this signature, pAICM cardiomyocytes demonstrated significantly higher senescence scores compared with pDCM and pDonor cardiomyocytes (P < 0.0001; Figure 1E). Elevated senescence scores were also observed in pAICM-derived endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and myeloid cells (P < 0.0001 for all), relative to cells derived from pDCM and pDonor (Figure 1E), indicating this is not explained by end-stage systolic dysfunction alone.

Consistent with these global transcriptional changes, key senescence-associated genes (including ANGPTL4, STAT3, JUN, and CDKN1A) were selectively upregulated in pAICM cardiomyocytes (Figure 1F). Immunostaining for p21, a canonical mediator of senescence-associated cell-cycle arrest encoded by CDKN1A, demonstrated robust nuclear expression in pAICM myocardium, with minimal staining in pDonor tissue (Figure 1G). Quantification of cardiomyocyte p21 positivity, aggregated at the disease level, revealed a marked increase in the proportion of p21+ cardiomyocytes in the pAICM group compared with that in donor controls (P <0.001; Figure 1H).

Applying our senescence signature to a published adult AICM dataset (5) revealed no substantial elevation in senescence scores across major cell types relative to adult donors, suggesting that multicellular senescence may represent a pediatric-specific response to anthracycline cardiotoxicity (Supplemental Figure 1, E and F). Subcluster analysis across pAICM cardiomyocyte, endothelial cell, fibroblast, and myeloid populations revealed that senescence scores were elevated uniformly across all cell states, supporting a pan-cellular senescence phenotype (Supplemental Figure 2). Differential gene expression and Gene Ontology enrichment analysis comparing cells derived from patients with pAICM with pDonor cells and cells from patients with pDCM revealed distinct pathway signatures across cell types, underscoring the broad transcriptional effect of anthracycline exposure on the pediatric myocardium (Supplemental Figure 3).

In summary, we identified increased cellular senescence across multiple cardiac cell types in pAICM. While these data do not establish causality, the absence of comparable senescence activation in age-matched patients with pDCM and adult patients with AICM supports a pediatric anthracycline-specific effect. A major limitation of this study is the small, female-only cohort, reflecting the extreme rarity of pediatric transplant myocardium with documented anthracycline exposure. Nonetheless, end-stage human pediatric tissue offers otherwise unattainable insight and establishes a resource for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies in childhood cancer survivors.
