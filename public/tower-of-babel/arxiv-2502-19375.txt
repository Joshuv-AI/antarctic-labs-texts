Extracting scattering amplitudes for arbitrary two-particle systems with one-particle left-hand cuts via lattice QCD
André Baião Raposo, Raul A. Briceño, Maxwell T. Hansen, and Andrew W. Jackura

Abstract
We derive a general formalism that relates the spectrum of two-particle systems in a finite volume to physical scattering amplitudes, taking into account the presence of any left-hand branch cuts due to single-particle exchanges. The method first relates the finite-volume spectrum to an infinite-volume short-range quantity, denoted ℳ 0 \mathcal{M}_{0} , and then relates the latter to the physical scattering amplitudes via known integral equations. The derivation of both relations is performed using all-orders perturbation theory and is exact up to neglected exponentially suppressed volume dependence. The relations hold for arbitrary two-particle systems with any number of coupled channels, non-identical and non-degenerate particles, and any intrinsic spin.

Introduction

In recent decades, remarkable progress has been made in applying numerical lattice quantum chromodynamics (QCD) to systematically and rigorously determine properties of the low-lying spectrum directly from the fundamental theory. To reach this understanding, the field has had to overcome two major obstacles:

First, because of the non-perturbative nature of QCD, analytic attempts to systematically and quantitatively understand its low-energy properties are limited, only applicable for certain observables, and with sources of systematic uncertainty that can be difficult to quantify. The lattice paradigm circumvents this challenge by placing the theory in a finite, discretized, Euclidean spacetime, where reliable numerical determinations of correlation functions can be achieved by applying Monte Carlo importance sampling on high-performance computers.

Second, the vast majority of states in QCD are unstable resonances that decay to two or more hadrons via the strong force, and their existence must be reconstructed from the analytic properties of multi-hadron scattering amplitudes.

This formalism will not only impact the first-principles characterization of QCD resonances, but will also play an important role in future constraints on two- and three-nucleon interactions, which will help to constrain ab initio nuclear methods. A closely related application is the prediction of single and double beta decay rates, which can play a role in precision tests of the Standard Model. Currently, the lattice QCD community is still resolving the nucleon-nucleon spectrum at unphysical heavy quark masses, with the most reliable calculations in the range
400\,{\rm MeV}\lesssim m_{\pi}\lesssim 700\,{\rm MeV}
. As these calculations begin to approach the physical point, the OPE branch point at
s=(2m_{N})^{2}-m_{\pi}^{2}
, where
m_{N}

[
1

Reference
[
1
]
solves the OPE cut problem for elastic scattering of a single channel of identical particles, using
NN
as an example. The derivation is based on a skeleton expansion which represents a finite-volume correlation function as a geometric series of Feynman diagrams with irreducible vertex functions. The loops in these diagrams are summed over the discrete set of finite-volume momentum modes and integrated over the time components; as a result, the vertex functions are sampled at off-shell momenta, i.e. four-momenta
k
that do not satisfy
k^{2}=m^{2}
. In the usual derivations of finite-volume scattering formulas, the vertex functions can be expanded around their on-shell points, and the off-shell dependence can be absorbed into new infinite-volume quantities up to exponentially suppressed volume effects. However, as highlighted in ref.
[
1
]
, this on-shell placement creates problems on the OPE cut. By using partially off-shell kinematics to avoid the corresponding singularities, an alternative relation is derived that remains valid down to the two-pion exchange branch cut starting at
s=(2m_{N})^{2}-(2m_{\pi})^{2}
. The new relation allows to constrain an intermediate quantity
\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}(s)
, which can be related to the scattering amplitude via integral equations also derived in the published work.

In addition to the extension to any number and type of two-particle channels, in this work, we also provide some technical results that are important for the practical implementation of the formalism. In particular, we use a recently developed formalism for the partial-wave projection of integral equations for three-particle systems
[
145

146

to derive improved integral equations for the two-particle sector that are partial-wave projected.

The need to generalize the formalism of ref.
[

We also note that for some systems, such as the

This manuscript is organized as follows. In section
2.1
, we present a derivation of the quantization condition for any number of channels, without intrinsic spin. In section
2.2

2.3

The quantization condition requires only one set of dynamical inputs, namely the couplings associated with the OPE, and the finite-volume spectrum. From this, one can constrain an infinite-volume intermediate quantity denoted
\mathcal{M}_{0}

\mathcal{M}_{0}

We begin by stating the first of our two main results of this work, the quantization condition relating finite-volume energies to intermediate infinite-volume quantities. We find it useful to lead with this to emphasize the essential message before going into details of the derivation. Our main result for a generic system with coupled two-particle channels is that, up to neglected exponentially suppressed effects, the finite-volume energies satisfy

\displaystyle\det_{aJm_{J}\ell S}\big[\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1}+F+\mathcal{C}_{L}\big]=0\,.

2.1
Derivation for coupled-channel systems with no intrinsic spin

In this section, we derive a quantization condition in a complementary but equivalent fashion to ref.
[
1
]
, extending the results of that paper to non-identical particles and multiple scattering channels.

Our main task in this section is to obtain an expression for the finite-volume analog of the two-body scattering amplitude,
\mathcal{M}_{L}
. In the context of a generic effective theory of hadrons, this object is defined through the same expansion in terms of Feynman diagrams as the physical infinite-volume amplitude,
\mathcal{M}
, but evaluated instead in a periodic cubic finite spatial volume of side length
L
. In a finite volume, loop integration over continuous spatial momentum is replaced by a sum over the discrete set of spatial momenta allowed by the periodic boundary conditions,
\mathbf{k}=(2\pi/L)\mathbf{n}
with
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
:

\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}~\longrightarrow~\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\,.

(2)

The infinite-volume amplitude
\mathcal{M}
is recovered from
\mathcal{M}_{L}
by introducing an
i\epsilon
prescription on the energy
E\to E+i\epsilon
, and taking the infinite-volume limit
L\to\infty
, so that the sums over discrete momentum become Feynman integrals.

In processes involving multiple open two-particle scattering channels, we look at the finite- and infinite-volume amplitudes as matrices in the space of channels. For example, we take
\mathcal{M}
to have entries
\mathcal{M}_{a^{\prime}a}
, where the indices
a
and
a^{\prime}
specify the channels of the incoming and outgoing states, respectively.

By first deriving an expression for
\mathcal{M}_{L}
, we are then able to obtain a quantization condition for the finite-volume spectrum. This will also help us derive expressions satisfied by the physical infinite-volume amplitude, which we present in section
3
. As in ref.
[
1
]
, we restrict our attention to the kinematic region bounded from below by the nearest left-hand cut associated with two or more particle exchanges (across all channels considered) and bounded from above by the threshold for production of
n
-particle states, where
n\geq 3
.

Power-law finite-volume corrections to observables result from singularities in the physical region. In a finite volume, these singularities can only be poles arising from intermediate states going on shell. Other finite-volume corrections are exponentially suppressed as
\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\mu L}\right)
, where
\mu
is typically the mass of the lightest particle that can be exchanged, and are generally neglected. The goal throughout this derivation is to isolate all possible pole contributions and thus track the power-law volume dependence.

Before giving explicit expressions for these exchanges, it is crucial to unambiguously define what is meant by

Let us illustrate this by looking at a few examples. In a single-channel elastic process such as
DD^{*}\to DD^{*}

Specifying a labeling scheme as outlined above gives a clear definition of single

-channel exchanges, which we now describe in detail. These contributions can be written in terms of two classes of functions: The first type is the fully-dressed propagator
\Delta_{x}(q^{2})

\Delta_{x}(q^{2})=\frac{1}{q^{2}-m_{x}^{2}-\Pi_{x}(q^{2})+i\epsilon}\,,

is its pole mass and

This series puts into evidence where two-particle intermediate states can arise, namely the propagator pairs, exposing the possible sources of power-like volume effects.

The Bethe-Salpeter kernel used is defined as the sum of all diagrams contributing to the amplitude which are two-particle irreducible in the
s
-channel, i.e. those which contain no intermediate two-particle states. As a result, the infinite- and finite-volume versions of this object differ only by exponentially suppressed volume corrections. We will neglect these in the following derivation, always considering the infinite-volume kernel. Note that, according to the definition above, the Bethe-Salpeter kernel includes the contributions from
t
- and
u
-channel exchanges,
i\mathcal{M}_{t}^{(1)}
and
i\mathcal{M}_{u}^{(1)}
. Due to their role in the generation of the nearest left-hand cut, it is convenient to define a subtracted kernel,
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
, from which we have stripped the pole pieces of the exchanges,
i\mathcal{T}
and
i\,\mathcal{U}
. This is shown diagrammatically in figure

\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
contains only the non-singular pieces of the exchanges,
i\mathcal{B}_{t}^{(1)}
and
i\mathcal{B}_{u}^{(1)}

Before giving an all-orders expression for the amplitude based on the series described, let us examine second-order contributions to the finite-volume amplitude, i.e. contributions with a single two-particle loop between two kernels. Our goal is to analyze this loop in finite volume and isolate the two-particle intermediate state singularity, which corresponds to the source of power-law volume dependence.

t
-channel exchange pole piece only
\mathcal{T}
on the left-hand side and a generic kernel
\mathcal{A}

\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}=\sum_{b}\int\!\frac{{\rm d}k_{0}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\bigg(i\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}b}(p_{3},p_{4};k,P-k)\\
\xi_{b}\,iD_{b1}(k^{2})\,iD_{b2}((P-k)^{2})\,i\mathcal{A}_{ba}(k,P-k;p_{1},p_{2})\bigg)+\big(i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,1)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}\,.

In the first term, we have kept only the dressed propagator pole pieces

b2
is not itself on shell since
(P-k)^{2}\neq m_{b2}^{2}
. Contributions from other poles in
k^{0}
are absorbed into the kernel
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,1)}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,2)}

Simplifying the propagator pole piece

E=\omega_{b1}({\bf k})+\omega_{b2}({\bf P-k})

q_{b}^{\star}\equiv\frac{1}{2\sqrt{s}}\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{b1}^{2},m_{b2}^{2})\,,

(17)

\lambda(x,y,z)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-2(xy+xz+yz)

b

\displaystyle(\Delta_{2,L})_{b^{\prime}b}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{L^{3}}\,\xi_{b}\,\frac{\omega_{b1}^{\star}}{\omega_{b1}(\mathbf{k})}\frac{\delta_{b^{\prime}b}\,\delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}\,H_{b}(\mathbf{k}^{\star})}{2E^{\star}\big[(q_{b}^{\star})^{2}-(\mathbf{k}^{\star})^{2}+i\epsilon\big]}\,,

(18)

where we have defined
\omega_{b1}^{\star}\equiv\sqrt{(q_{b}^{\star})^{2}+m_{b1}^{2}}
and introduced an analytic cut-off function
H_{b}(\mathbf{k}^{\star})
that equals

at the pole
|{\mathbf{k}}^{\star}|=q_{b}^{\star}
and goes to zero exponentially as
|\mathbf{k}^{\star}|\to\infty
. The dependence on

\displaystyle\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime},b}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}\Big(\big[i\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}(p_{3},p_{4};k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime})\big]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{b^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}\,(i\Delta_{2,L})_{b^{\prime}b}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\big[i\mathcal{A}_{ba}(k,P-k;p_{1},p_{2})\big]_{k^{0}=\omega_{b1}(\bf k)}\Big)+\big(i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\equiv\big(i\mathcal{T}\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot i\mathcal{A}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}+\big(i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}\,,

(20)

where the difference is absorbed into a new kernel via
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,2)}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}

An identical analysis can be carried out for contributions with a
u
-channel pole piece
\mathcal{U}
or a kernel
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
on the left side of the loop, which we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2)}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{(1)}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{B}^{(1)}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\equiv i\mathcal{B}^{(1)}\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot i\mathcal{A}+i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{B}^{(1)}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}\,.

For
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}
, however, we define this notation with a subtle difference. The analog of eq. (
19
) in this case reads

\displaystyle\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b^{\prime},b}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}\Big(\big[i\,\mathcal{U}_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}(p_{3},p_{4};k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime})\big]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{b^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}\,(i\Delta_{2,L})_{b^{\prime}b}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\big[i\mathcal{A}_{ba}(k,P-k;p_{1},p_{2})\big]_{k^{0}=\omega_{b1}(\bf k)}\Big)+\big(i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}\,.

(21)

Noting that the quantities
\omega_{b1}(\mathbf{k})
and
{E-\omega_{b2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k})}
must coincide at the two-particle pole, we make the replacement

\big[i\,\mathcal{U}(p_{3},p_{4};k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime})\big]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{b1}(\bf k^{\prime})}\to\big[i\,\mathcal{U}(p_{3},p_{4};k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime})\big]_{k^{\prime 0}=E-\omega_{b2}(\bf P-\bf k^{\prime})}\,,

(22)

with the difference between the two objects being absorbed through
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,3)}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,4)}
. We define the shorthand notation to take this modification into account, such that

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A},L}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\equiv i\,\mathcal{U}\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot i\mathcal{A}+i\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{U}\!\mathcal{A}}^{(2,4)}\,.

(23)

The motivation for this replacement is ultimately to avoid the presence of a spurious singularity in the energy in the partially on-shell
u
-channel exchange. This is explained in more detail later in this section.

Putting all ingredients together, the overall second-order contribution to the finite-volume amplitude,
i\mathcal{M}_{L}^{(2)}
, can be written compactly as

i\mathcal{M}_{L}^{(2)}=\big(i\mathcal{B}^{(1)}+i\mathcal{T}+i\,\mathcal{U}\big)\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot\big(i\mathcal{B}^{(1)}+i\mathcal{T}+i\,\mathcal{U}\big)+i\mathcal{B}^{(2)}\,,

(24)

where we take into account all possibilities for the objects on the left and right sides of the loop. The kernel
\mathcal{B}^{(2)}
collects all non-singular kernels arising from the analysis described above.

This expression can then be applied to all two-particle loops at all orders of the amplitude series. By doing so, we can obtain the following all-orders expression for the finite-volume amplitude:

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{L}

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{B}+i\mathcal{T}+i\,\mathcal{U}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(i\mathcal{B}+i\mathcal{T}+i\,\mathcal{U}\big)\left[\,\cdot\,i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot\big(i\mathcal{B}+i\mathcal{T}+i\,\mathcal{U}\big)\right]^{n}\,.

(25)

The kernel
\mathcal{B}
here has been obtained by starting from the non-singular kernel
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
(shown in figure
3
(a)) and subsequently absorbing all non-singular kernels arising from the two-particle loops at each order of the amplitude series, e.g. the second-order kernel
\mathcal{B}^{(2)}
in eq. (
24
). This absorption is depicted in figure
3
(c).

An important point to consider is that the “
\,\cdot\,i\Delta_{2,L}\,\cdot\,
” operation places the neighboring objects in specific partially off-shell kinematic configurations. More concretely, for
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}
appearing between these operations in the series, we will have the placements

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)

\displaystyle\to\left[\mathcal{B}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)\right]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\,k^{0}=\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k})}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)

\displaystyle\to\left[\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)\right]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\,k^{0}=\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k})}\,,

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt=\frac{-g_{13}g_{24}}{(\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})-\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}-(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})^{2}-m_{e}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)

\displaystyle\to\left[\mathcal{U}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)\right]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\,k^{0}=E-\omega_{a2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k})}\,

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt=\frac{-g_{23}g_{14}}{(\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})-\omega_{a2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k}))^{2}-(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})^{2}-m_{e}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,.

(28)

For
\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}a}
, we have set
k=(\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{k})
and
k^{\prime}=(\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
, and hence one can show that
t=(k^{\prime}-k)^{2}\leq(m_{a^{\prime}1}-m_{a1})^{2}
for any choice of real
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
. With these kinematics, we cannot reach the single-exchange pole at
t=m_{e}^{2}
, in contrast to the fully on-shell
t
-channel exchange. The value of
u=(P-k^{\prime}-k)^{2}
, on the other hand, depends explicitly on the total energy
E
and is not bounded. The situation is reversed for
\mathcal{U}_{a^{\prime}a}
, where we have set
k=(\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{k})
and
k^{\prime}=(E-\omega_{a2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
. We can then show that
u\leq(m_{a^{\prime}1}-m_{a2})^{2}
for real
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, while
t
is unconstrained. This means we cannot hit the
u=m_{e}^{2}
pole and was the motivation for our earlier kinematic replacement in eq. (
22
).

In previous derivations of the standard Lüscher quantization condition, e.g. ref.
[
10
]
, the Bethe-Salpeter kernel, including the
t
- and
u
-channel exchange poles, is placed fully on shell at this stage. This is done by setting the magnitudes of the CM spatial momenta to their values at the two-particle poles in the relevant channels, e.g.
|\mathbf{k}^{\star}|=q_{a}^{\star}
and
|\mathbf{k}^{\prime\star}|=q_{a^{\prime}}^{\star}
above. As discussed in ref.
[
1
]
, doing this for the
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}
exchanges, would introduce unphysical poles. As a result, we leave the
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}
exchanges as is, and we introduce a new exchange (
\mathcal{E}
) function that includes the sum of both exchanges,

\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime};\mathbf{k})\equiv\left[\mathcal{T}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)\right]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\,k^{0}=\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k})}\\
+\left[\mathcal{U}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime},P-k^{\prime};k,P-k)\right]_{k^{\prime 0}=\omega_{a^{\prime}1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),\,k^{0}=E-\omega_{a2}(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{k})}.

(29)

The kernel
\mathcal{B}
, on the other hand, does not contain these poles and can be placed fully on shell without further issues.

The contributions arising from the difference between off- and on-shell kernels generate terms that do not contain two-particle poles. These can be absorbed into
\mathcal{B}
and the resulting object can then be set on shell. This iterative process can be applied to each order of the amplitude series of eq. (
25
). We are then able to reorganize the series and, hence, write down an equation that must be satisfied by the finite-volume amplitude:

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{L}

\displaystyle=i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\mathcal{E}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\mathcal{E}\big)\left[{}\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot\big(i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\mathcal{E}\big)\right]^{n}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle=i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\mathcal{E}+\big(i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\mathcal{E}\big)\cdot i\Delta_{2,L}\cdot i\mathcal{M}_{L}\,.

(31)

Here,
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
is the on-shell kernel obtained from
\mathcal{B}
through the absorption of successive contributions arising from on-shell placements as described above. This object contains no singularities nor branch points in the kinematic region considered. For identical particles, the
\mathcal{E}
function double counts the contributions from exchanges in loops. This over counting is compensated by the
\xi
symmetry factors in the definition of
\Delta_{2,L}
in eq. (
18
).

From this form, it can be deduced that the finite-volume poles are generated from the all-orders sum shown explicitly. To make use of this, it is necessary to give more explicit definitions of both
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}

Let us start by looking at
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
. This object satisfies eq. (
32
) involving only the non-singular on-shell kernel
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
. The standard trick of writing the finite-volume correction of the two-particle loops in terms of on-shell quantities and a single finite-volume function
F

[

. This allows us to relate
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
to its infinite-volume counterpart,
\mathcal{M}_{0}

To do so, we first need to express
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0}
as matrices in the angular momentum basis. We decompose their angular dependence in the CM frame into spherical harmonics. For
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
, we can write

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{0,L})_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\ell m}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime\star},q_{a^{\prime}}^{\star})\,(\mathcal{M}_{0,L})_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};a\ell m}\,\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{p}^{\star},q_{a}^{\star})\,,

(35)

where
\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p}
are one of the outgoing and incoming spatial momenta, respectively, and we suppress the dependence on the total energy

and momentum

. We have also introduced

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{p}^{\star},q_{a}^{\star})

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{4\pi}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{p}^{\star}|}{q_{a}^{\star}}\right)^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star})\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}^{T}(\mathbf{p}^{\star},q_{a}^{\star})

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{4\pi}\left(\frac{|\mathbf{p}^{\star}|}{q_{a}^{\star}}\right)^{\ell}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star})\,,

(37)

with
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}\equiv\mathbf{p}^{\star}/|\mathbf{p}^{\star}|
. Identical decompositions apply also to the infinite-volume object
\mathcal{M}_{0}
or the kernel
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
.

We must address two important aspects here: Firstly,
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
or
\mathcal{M}_{0}
have all external momentum arguments on shell, meaning that there is a redundancy in their dependence on
\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p}
. We can see this by expressing these objects in terms of CM-frame-boosted momenta
\mathbf{p}^{\prime\star},\mathbf{p}^{\star}
, whose magnitude is fixed to
q_{a^{\prime}}^{\star}
and
q_{a}^{\star}
(
a^{\prime},a
specify the corresponding channels), leaving only their directions, i.e.
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\star}
and
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}
, unspecified. As such, we can look at eq. (
35
) as providing an off-shell extension of
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
in momentum space, and similarly for
\mathcal{M}_{0}
or
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
. Secondly,
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
is a finite-volume object and, in principle, only valued in the set of discretized finite-volume momenta, while projection to harmonics relies on dependence on continuum CM spatial momentum. This is resolved by noting that the dependence on the external momenta is carried by infinite-volume objects only, namely the kernels
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
, for which the extension to continuous infinite-volume spatial momentum is straightforward.

Using the spherical harmonic projections and combining the finite-volume eq. (
32
) and its infinite-volume analog, we can express
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
in terms of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
through the following matrix equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0,L}

\displaystyle=\left[{\mathcal{M}}_{0}^{-1}+F\right]^{-1}\,.

(38)

The
F
matrix, as standard in the literature, encodes the difference between the finite- (
\Delta_{2,L}
) and infinite-volume (
\Delta_{2,\infty}
) two-particle loops and has elements
F_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\ell m}
given by

\displaystyle F_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};a\ell m}(E,\mathbf{P})

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\,\xi_{a}\left[\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}-\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\right]\frac{\omega_{a1}^{\star}}{\omega_{a1}(\mathbf{k})}\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\star},q^{\star})\,\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{k}^{\star},q^{\star})\,H_{a}(\mathbf{k}^{\star})}{2E^{\star}\big[(q_{a}^{\star})^{2}-(\mathbf{k}^{\star})^{2}+i\epsilon\big]}\,.

(39)

Using the standard technology to evaluate the imaginary part of two-particle loops, it is also straightforward to show that
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{0}^{-1}={\mathcal{K}}_{0}^{-1}-i\rho\,,

(40)

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
is a real-valued non-singular function and
\rho
is the standard relativistic two-body phase space factor, represented here as a diagonal matrix in angular momentum and channel space with elements

\displaystyle\rho_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,a\ell m}\equiv\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\,\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m^{\prime}m}\,\xi_{a}\frac{q_{a}^{\star}}{8\pi\sqrt{s}}\,.

(41)

, this object does not have its external legs on shell. Consequently, it is most conveniently expressed as a matrix in the momentum basis, as opposed to the angular momentum basis. For a given cutoff function

in eq. (

2.2
Generalization to particles with spin

In deriving eq. (
49
), we allowed for any number of intermediate two-particle channels to go on shell, our only assumption being that the particles considered had no intrinsic spin. This appeared in two places: the propagator structure and the analytic structure of the vertex functions. These two points are, of course, closely related.

In this section, we lift this assumption and allow for particles of arbitrary spin. Because our main interest is QCD, where the left-hand cuts are primarily generated by one-pion exchanges, we assume that the exchange particle is still spinless. This assumption only simplifies the details when partial-wave projecting the resulting one-particle exchange, as will be done in section
3.3
, and has no impact on the steps discussed in this section.

We build largely on the work of refs.
[
19
,
149
,
1
]
, which have previously considered two-body systems with non-zero spin. The first step is to note that the propagator for a particle
x
with arbitrary spin
s
can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}(q)=\sum_{m_{s}}\frac{\eta_{x,m_{s}}(q)\,\bar{\eta}_{x,m_{s}}(q)}{q^{2}-m_{x}^{2}+i\epsilon}+S_{x}(q),

(50)

where
\eta_{x,m_{s}}(q)
is the spin wave function for the particle
x
with spin quantum numbers
(s,m_{s})
, and
S_{x}
is a smooth function of momentum in the kinematic region considered. Here we assume that the spin is quantized along a fixed
z
-axis, but, as discussed in section
3.3
, one can equivalently do this using the helicity basis.

In general,
\eta_{x,m_{s}}(q)
is a column vector in the appropriate representation space of the Lorentz group. For example, for spin-1/2 particles,
\eta_{x,m_{s}}(q)
would be a spinor in Dirac space. Meanwhile,
\bar{\eta}_{x,m_{s}}(q)
is a row vector in this same space. For clarity, let us explicitly write eq. (
50
) with indices
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
in the representation space,

\displaystyle\left[\Delta_{x}(q^{2})\right]_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{s}}\frac{\left[\eta_{x,m_{s}}(q)\right]_{\alpha^{\prime}}\left[\bar{\eta}_{x,m_{s}}(q)\right]_{\alpha}}{q^{2}-m_{x}^{2}+i\epsilon}+\left[S_{x}(q^{2})\right]_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{s^{\prime}},m_{s}}\left[\eta_{x,m_{s^{\prime}}}(q)\right]_{\alpha^{\prime}}\left[D_{x}(q^{2})\right]_{m_{s^{\prime}}m_{s}}\left[\bar{\eta}_{x,m_{s}}(q)\right]_{\alpha}+\left[S_{x}(q)\right]_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}\,,

(51)

where we introduced

\displaystyle\left[D_{x}(q^{2})\right]_{m_{s^{\prime}}m_{s}}\equiv\frac{\delta_{m_{s^{\prime}}m_{s}}}{q^{2}-m_{x}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,.

(52)

This explains all modifications to the dressed propagators necessary for analyzing the skeleton expansion.

We turn now to the kernels appearing between loops in that expansion. As before, we isolate the OPE poles to define the objects
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}
. All non-singular contributions to the Bethe-Salpeter kernel are collected into the object
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}

will acquire indices associated with the appropriate representation space,

\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{U}
specifically, the tensor structure will be embedded into the vertex couplings
g

g

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\delta_{S^{\prime}S}\sum_{m^{\prime},m,m_{S}}\braket{J^{\prime}m_{J^{\prime}}|\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},Sm_{S}}F_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,a\ell m}\braket{\ell m,Sm_{S}|Jm_{J}}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}_{L})_{a^{\prime}J^{\prime}m_{J^{\prime}}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,aJm_{J}\ell S}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\sum_{m^{\prime},m_{S^{\prime}},m,m_{S}}\braket{J^{\prime}m_{J^{\prime}}|\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},S^{\prime}m_{S^{\prime}}}(\mathcal{C}_{L})_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}S^{\prime}m_{S^{\prime}};\,a\ell mSm_{S}}\braket{\ell m,Sm_{S}|Jm_{J}}\,.

(78)

Having these quantities in the desired basis, we can now rewrite the quantization condition to include an arbitrary number of channels and spin,

\displaystyle\det_{aJm_{J}\ell S}\big[\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1}+F+\mathcal{C}_{L}\big]=0\,,

(79)

where the determinant now runs over the larger index space. Note that, in the limit where the exchanges couplings are set to
0
, the
\mathcal{C}_{L}
term vanishes and one recovers the quantization condition for two-body systems with arbitrary spin, first derived in refs.
[
19
,
149
]
.

2.3
Equivalence to the quantization condition in ref.
[
1
]

In ref.
[
1
]
, quantization conditions are provided for single-channel systems of two identical particles of arbitrary spin, taking into account the nearest left-hand cut arising from one-particle exchanges. In this section, we show their equivalence to the results presented in this work, namely eqs. (
49
) (spinless particles) and (
79
) (arbitrary spin).

The condition for identical spinless particles, given in eq. (5.3) of ref.
[
1
]
, can be stated in the form

\det_{\ell m}\Big[\big(\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}\big)^{-1}+F^{\mathcal{E}}\Big]=0\,,

(80)

where
\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}
is a scheme-dependent, non-singular, infinite-volume function and
F^{\mathcal{E}}
is a finite-volume function, defined as

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{E}}\equiv v^{T}S\frac{1}{1+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}S}\,v=v^{T}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S\big(-\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}S\big)^{n}v\,,

(81)

corresponding to eq. (5.4) of ref.
[
1
]
. The objects
S
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
are matrices in a mixed finite-volume momentum and angular momentum basis, with elements given by

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}\ell m}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{L^{3}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{\star}}{\omega(\mathbf{k})}\frac{4\pi\,|\mathbf{k}^{\star}|^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\,Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\star})\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\star})\,\delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}\,H(\mathbf{k}^{\star})}{2E^{\star}\big[(q^{\star})^{2}-(\mathbf{k}^{\star})^{2}+i\epsilon\big]}\,,

(82)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}\ell m}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{k}^{\prime\star}|^{\ell^{\prime}}|\mathbf{k}^{\star}|^{\ell}}\left[\int\!{\rm d}\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\star}}\!\int\!{\rm d}\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}}\,Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime\star})\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star})\right]_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\mathbf{p}^{\prime\star}|=|\mathbf{k}^{\prime\star}|\\[2.0pt]
\hskip 0.0pt|\mathbf{p}^{\star}|=|\mathbf{k}^{\star}|\end{subarray}}\,,

(83)

cf. eqs. (3.21) and (3.64) in ref.
[
1
]
.

Here, kinematic quantities are defined as in section
2.1
and we drop particle and channel labels since we are considering identical particles in a single channel. As before,
H(\mathbf{k}^{\star})
is a smooth regulator function,
\mathcal{E}
is the exchange function defined in eq. (
29
), and
\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}}
denotes integration over the angular degrees of freedom of the CM-boosted momentum
\mathbf{p}^{\star}
. Finally,
v
and
v^{T}
are trivial column and row vectors of ones, respectively, in momentum space:
v_{\mathbf{k}}=1
. These contract with the momentum indices of the internal block in eq. (
81
) to produce the matrix
F^{\mathcal{E}}
which is defined in the angular momentum index space. Note that there are a few minor differences between the definitions given here versus ref.
[
1
]
, which we discuss at the end of the section. We also deviate from the original notation to align more closely with the notation and conventions used in this work.

From its definition, we see that
S
carries the two-particle pole and is therefore closely related to the object
\Delta_{2,L}
defined in eq. (
18
). Similarly,
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is the matrix of spherical harmonic projections of the exchange function
\mathcal{E}
, evaluated at finite-volume momenta. As such, we can provide the following relations between their elements:

\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}\ell m}

\displaystyle=(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\star},q^{\star})\,(\Delta_{2,L})_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}}\,\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\star},q^{\star})\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\,\ell m}(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\star},q^{\star})\,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\mathbf{k}\ell m}\,\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\star},q^{\star})\,,

(85)

where we omit the trivial channel indices in
\Delta_{2,L}
and
\mathcal{E}
and use the appropriate form of these objects for identical particles.

Looking at the series definition of
F^{\mathcal{E}}
in eq. (
81
), we can apply these relations and move around the spherical harmonics and powers of on-shell momentum to obtain

\displaystyle F^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}

\displaystyle=(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Y}^{T}\Delta_{2,L}\left[-\mathcal{E}\Delta_{2,L}\right]^{n}\mathcal{Y}\bigg)_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}\,,

(86)

\displaystyle=(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\big(\mathcal{Y}^{T}\Delta_{2,L}\,\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{Y}^{T}\Delta_{2,L}\,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}\,\Delta_{2,L}\,\mathcal{Y}\big)_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}\,,

(87)

with
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{T}
as defined in eqs. (
36
) and (
37
). In the second line, we use eq. (
43
) to replace the infinite series with
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}
. It is now straightforward to see that the second term inside the brackets corresponds to the sum
\mathcal{C}_{L}
defined in eq. (
46
). The first term can be identified as the sum term of the Lüscher finite-volume function
F
in eq. (

I
for the matching integral term:

I_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\omega^{\star}}{\omega(\mathbf{k})}\frac{\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\star},q^{\star})\,\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{k}^{\star},q^{\star})\,H(\mathbf{k}^{\star})}{2E^{\star}\big[(q^{\star})^{2}-(\mathbf{k}^{\star})^{2}+i\epsilon\big]}\,,

(88)

we are now able to rewrite the elements of
F^{\mathcal{E}}
as

F^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}=(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}\big(F+\mathcal{C}_{L}+I\big)_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}\,.

(89)

The factors of

in the expressions above arise from the different normalization of the barrier factors in ref.
[

]
.

Let us turn now to the kernel
\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}

[
1
]

introduced in section

[
1

and its infinite-volume version matches eq. (4.18) of the earlier work. Consequently, we can identify

with
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
and use this to relate it to
\mathcal{M}_{0}

(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}(\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1})_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}=\big[\big(\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}\big)^{-1}\big]_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}+(q^{\star})^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}I_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,\ell m}\,.

(90)

We can now substitute eqs. (
89
) and (
90
) into the quantization condition eq. (

) and to remove the extra factors of on-shell momentum, yielding the expected condition eq. (

For the arbitrary spin case, we proceed almost identically to the spinless case. The relevant objects
\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os}
,
F^{\mathcal{E}}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
and

. Besides accommodating the additional spin indices, eqs. (

We close this section by commenting on key differences between the expressions given in eqs. (

) and the original expressions in ref.
[

: Firstly, a minor detail is that the we use the finite-volume momentum
\mathbf{k}
here as an index, instead of the CM-frame-boosted
\mathbf{k}^{\star}

above is defined using the projections
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
of the exchange function
\mathcal{E}
. This replaces

in the previous article, where only the projection of the

(91)

with
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}

\Big[(\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os})^{-1}+F^{\mathcal{E}}\Big]^{-1}=\Big[(\overline{\mathcal{K}}{}^{\sf os})^{-1}+F^{\mathcal{T}}\Big]^{-1}\,,

up to the absorption of non-singular contributions into

. The two-particle pole in eq. (

3
Physical scattering amplitudes

The derivation of the physical amplitude
\mathcal{M}
is relatively straightforward now that we have defined the finite-volume amplitude in Secs.
2.1
and
2.2
. This can be directly obtained from
\mathcal{M}_{L}
in eq. (
47
) by taking the ordered double limit

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{L}\,.

(93)

In practice, this means one must replace the sums over discrete momentum with integrals over continuous momentum:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}~\longrightarrow~\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,,

(94)

such that finite-volume loops become infinite-volume loops. Using this prescription, the expression relating
\mathcal{M}
to the quantities
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
in momentum space will look essentially identical to eq. (
47
) without the
L
subscripts, where
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
are defined as the ordered infinite-volume limits of
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}
, respectively. This will then provide a direct relation between
\mathcal{M}_{0}
, the quantity appearing in the quantization conditions derived in section
2
, to the desired physical amplitude
\mathcal{M}
.

The following subsection deals with the partial-wave-projected infinite-volume amplitude for the case of particles with zero intrinsic spin. In section
3.2
, we generalize this to particles with non-zero spin. We close with section
3.3
, which details the general procedure for projecting OPEs to definite total angular momentum and parity (
J^{P}
) and provides explicit expressions for the cases of spinless-spinless and vector-spinless scattering.

Because we are interested in physical amplitudes that are Lorentz invariant in this section, we restrict our attention to the CM frame, where the total four-momentum is simply
P=(\sqrt{s},0)
. As such, we will omit the
\star
superscripts when denoting CM kinematics, to avoid notation clutter.

3.1
Partial-wave projected amplitudes for spinless particles

The goal of this subsection is to show that, after partial-wave projecting the amplitude to a definite
J^{P}
, it can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}+\left(1+i\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}\right)\,\left[\left(\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{0}\right)^{-1}+\mathcal{C}^{J^{P}}\right]^{-1}\left(1+i\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}\right),

(95)

where we have suppressed the dependence on the total energy of the system, as well as indices over channel space. The objects appearing in this equation, which we define in detail below, are all matrices in channel space. As the reader may note, this equation closely resembles the expression for the finite-volume amplitude in terms of the auxiliary amplitudes
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}
given in eq. (
47
).

Let us now proceed to define each one of the building blocks above, starting with the partial-wave-projected auxiliary amplitudes
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}
. The functions
\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}
,
\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{J^{P}}
are projections of the infinite-volume analogs of
\mathcal{R}_{L}
,
\mathcal{L}_{L}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{L}
defined in eqs. (
44
), (
45
), and (
46
), respectively. Since these depend on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, we will address them last.

In order to define
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}
, we first write down the integral equations satisfied by
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
and then partial-wave project. These equations are the infinite-volume limit, as defined in eq. (
93
), of those satisfied by
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E},L}
, presented in eqs. (
32
) and (
33
), respectively. Explicitly,

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{0}

\displaystyle=i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}+i\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}\,\cdot i\Delta_{2,\infty}\cdot\,\,i\mathcal{M}_{0}\,,

(96)

\displaystyle i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{E}+i\mathcal{E}\,\cdot i\Delta_{2,\infty}\cdot\,i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\,,

(97)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
and
\mathcal{E}
are the same driving terms appearing in the definition of the finite-volume amplitudes. Here, we have also introduced
\Delta_{2,\infty}
and a dot product notation. These are jointly defined via

\displaystyle\left[i\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\,\cdot i\Delta_{2,\infty}\cdot\,i\mathcal{A}\right]_{a^{\prime}a}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b}\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}~i\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}b}(\mathbf{k})\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{b}(\mathbf{k})\,i\mathcal{A}_{ba}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(98)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b}\int\!\frac{{\rm d}^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}~i\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}b}(\mathbf{k})\,\xi_{b}\,\frac{\omega_{1}(q_{b}\hat{\mathbf{k}})}{\omega_{1}(\mathbf{k})}\frac{i\,H_{b}(\mathbf{k})}{2E\big[q_{b}^{2}-\mathbf{k}^{2}+i\epsilon\big]}\,i\mathcal{A}_{ba}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(99)

where
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are generic infinite-volume quantities, and we omit their dependence on external momentum argument. Note that
\Delta_{2,\infty}
is the infinite-volume version of
\Delta_{2,L}
, defined in eq. (
18
).

The integral equation for
\mathcal{M}_{0}
can be solved algebraically, by first writing it in the partial-wave basis. By considering an arbitrary number of open channels composed of spinless distinguishable particles, the partial wave basis is obtained using eq. (
35
),

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime};\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\ell m}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},q_{a^{\prime}})\,\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,a\ell m}\,\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{p},q_{a})\,,

(100)

where again we have suppressed the dependence on the total energy of the system for the projected amplitudes, which are fixed to be on-shell. Since
\mathcal{M}_{0}
does not depend on the azimuthal quantum number
m
, and the orbital angular momentum
\ell
is conserved in channels with two spinless bosons, we can write
\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,a\ell m}\equiv\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m^{\prime}m}\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}
, simplifying the equation above to

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\ell m}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},q_{a^{\prime}})\,\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}\,\mathcal{Y}^{T}_{\ell m}(\mathbf{p},q_{a})\,.

(101)

The orbital angular momentum
\ell
fixes the total angular momentum (
J
) and parity (
P
) of the system,

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}=\left(\mathcal{M}_{0}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}\delta_{J\ell}\,,

(102)

and
P=(-1)^{\ell}\,\pi_{a1}\pi_{a2}
, where
\pi_{a1}
and
\pi_{a2}
are the intrinsic parity of the two particles in channel
a
.

There is a subtle point worth addressing about the partial-wave projection used in eq. (
101
). In particular, this partial-wave projection is slightly unconventional due to the explicit presence of the barrier factors embedded in the definition of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{T}
in eqs. (
36
) and (
37
), respectively. This definition is forced upon us from the partial-wave projection of
\mathcal{M}_{0,L}
in eq. (
35
). The need for this is to avoid unphysical singularities in finite-volume functions, such as
F
in eq. (
39
), at threshold. Because we have used these barrier factors in writing our quantization condition in terms of partial-wave projected quantities, this is propagated onto the definition of the partial-wave projected
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
kernels. This is to say, this is yet another way in which the
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
kernels are scheme-dependent. Consequently, the integral equation relating
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}^{\,J^{P}}
to
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}
depends on these barrier factors, a dependence subsequently inherited by the projections
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}
.

In contrast to
\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we are not aware of an algebraic solution for the integral equation for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, eq. (
97
), that gives the correct analytic structure. In this case, however, we know the driving term of this integral equation, namely the OPE function
\mathcal{E}
, explicitly. Consequently, we can attempt to solve this equation numerically. Remarkably, since it closely resembles the integral equations for three-body systems, we may adopt the techniques already in use to solve these
[
150
,
151
,
145
,
146
,
130
]
. The first step towards this goal is to write the integral equation for the partial-wave-projected
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}
. The only quantity that makes this step non-trivial is
\mathcal{E}
, since it carries angular dependence in the exchange propagator, as well as in the couplings for systems with non-zero spin. In section
3.3
, we give a detailed description of how the OPE can be projected to definite
J^{P}
in general. For spinless particles, this procedure is relatively straightforward.

As discussed above, when partial-wave projecting
\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we are “forced” to introduce the barrier factors. Since we did not project
\mathcal{E}
in the derivation of the finite-volume formalism, we have more freedom in defining this projection now. In turn, we choose to define a more standard projection without barrier factors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})=\sum_{\ell m}4\pi\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\,\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}(|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|,|\mathbf{p}|)\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,,

(103)

where we again drop azimuthal quantum number indices in the projections and use the fact that orbital angular momentum is conserved for spinless particles. Another notable distinction between this equation and eq. (
101
) is the fact that the projections depend on the spatial momentum magnitudes,
|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|
and
|\mathbf{p}|
. This is because
\mathcal{E}
is not fully on shell (note
(P-p)^{2}\neq m_{2}^{2}
and
(P-p^{\prime})^{2}\neq m_{4}^{2}
), unlike
\mathcal{M}_{0}
above.
5
5

5

It is worth commenting that because
\mathcal{E}
is a partially off-shell quantity, we could have very well introduced barrier factors when partial-wave projecting. This difference would just be absorbed into the definition of the projections
\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}
, as done in ref.
[
1
]
.
Again, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}a}^{J^{P}}=\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}\,\delta_{J\ell}\,.

(104)

Using similar arguments, we can write analogous expressions for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, namely

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\right)_{a^{\prime}a}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell m}4\pi\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\,(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}})_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}(|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|,|\mathbf{p}|)\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,,

(105)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\right)_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}\delta_{J\ell}\,.

(106)

Inserting eqs. (
103
) and (
105
) into the integral equation for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, eq. (
97
), and performing the integration over the angular degrees of freedom, we obtain an integral equation for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}
:

\displaystyle\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}(p^{\prime},p)

\displaystyle=i\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}a}^{J^{P}}(p^{\prime},p)+\sum_{b}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}~i\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}b}^{J^{P}}(p^{\prime},k)\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{b}(k)\,\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{ba}(k,p)\,,

(107)

where the integral is now one-dimensional, over the magnitude of spatial momentum
k
.
6
6

6

We point the reader to ref.
[
146
]
, where a nearly identical projection of integral equations was done for three-particle systems.
Note that all arguments here are spatial momentum magnitudes, not four-momenta.

Although the integral runs to
\infty
, the cut-off function in the definition
\Delta_{2,\infty}
allows one to truncate the integral at some value of
k
, which we denote
k_{\rm max}
. Given a value for
k_{\rm max}
, one can discretize the momentum, and write eq. (
107
) as a matrix equation over generally off-shell momenta. This is most easily solved by fixing

and

to be in the set of discrete momenta. As discussed in great detail in ref.
[

\mathcal{E}^{J^{P}}
and the pole singularity present in
\Delta_{2,\infty}
. After solving for the amplitude along the discrete values of the momenta defined along the contour, one can use the integral equation, eq. (
107
), to evaluate
\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}
for on-shell momenta. For more details on this procedure, we point the reader to ref.
[
151
]
and references within.

To briefly summarize, given the finite-volume spectrum, we can determine
\big(\mathcal{M}_{0}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}
from the quantization condition, eq. (

\mathcal{E}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}a}

\big(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}
from its integral equation, eq. (
107

The last three quantities we need to examine before arriving at the expression for
\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}

95

\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}
,
\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}
, and
\mathcal{C}^{J^{P}}

\mathcal{R}_{L}

\mathcal{L}_{L}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{L}

45
), and (

\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{L}
using spherical harmonics:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime};\,a}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell m}\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m^{\prime}m}\mathcal{R}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}(|\mathbf{p}|)\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})=\mathcal{R}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime};\,a\ell^{\prime}}(|\mathbf{p}|)\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,,

(108)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a^{\prime};\,a\ell m}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\,\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m^{\prime}m}\,\mathcal{L}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}(|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|)=\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\prime})\,\mathcal{L}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}(|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|)\,.

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}_{a^{\prime}\ell m;\,a\ell m}\equiv\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\,\delta_{m^{\prime}m}\,\mathcal{C}_{a^{\prime}\ell;\,a\ell}

(110)

Using eq. (
105

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
within the definitions of
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle i\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}a}(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{k}{q_{a^{\prime}}}\right]^{J}\!(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a^{\prime}}(k)\,\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}(k,p)\,,

(111)

\displaystyle i\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}a}(p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}a}(p^{\prime},k)\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a}(k)\left[\frac{k}{q_{a}}\right]^{J}\,,

(112)

(113)

As alluded to at the start of the section, the infinite-volume limit of eq. (
47

\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}

\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{M}

3.2
Generalization to particles with spin

At this stage, including the possibility that the particles have intrinsic spin is conceptually straightforward. As discussed in section
2.2
, by introducing spin, each of the building blocks considered becomes a matrix in spin space. Take, for example, the exchange functions, which acquire indices associated with the spin of the initial and final particles:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}a}(k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2};k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}m_{s_{1}}^{\prime}m_{s_{2}}^{\prime};\,am_{s_{1}}m_{s_{2}}}(k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2};k_{1},k_{2})\,.

(114)

Given this, one can use the steps described in section
2.2
to change these objects to the
Jm_{J}\ell S
basis and, thus, to definite
J^{P}
. Let us label the resulting projections by
\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}^{J^{P}}(|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}|,|\mathbf{p}|)
. Note that, since the particles now have intrinsic spin, these are no longer diagonal in orbital angular momentum.

Performing the appropriate projections and changes of basis, one can rewrite the integral equation for the projections of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
, eq. (
107
), in the form

\big(\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(p^{\prime},p)=i\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}^{J^{P}}(p^{\prime},p)\\
+\sum_{b}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}S^{\prime\prime}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\,\,\,i\mathcal{E}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,b\ell^{\prime\prime}S^{\prime\prime}}^{J^{P}}(p^{\prime},k)\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{b}(k)\,\big(i\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{J^{P}}\big)_{b\ell^{\prime\prime}S^{\prime\prime};\,a\ell S}(k,p)\,.

(115)

This can be solved, as was done for three-body systems in ref.
[
146
]
, by solving this as a matrix equation in momentum and
\ell S
space.

The rest of the equations in the previous section can be immediately be generalized to systems with spin by promoting everything to include
\ell S
indices,

\displaystyle i\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(p)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{k}{q_{a^{\prime}}}\right]^{\ell^{\prime}}\!(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a^{\prime}}(k)\,(i{\mathcal{M}}^{J^{P}}_{\mathcal{E}})_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(k,p)\,,

(116)

\displaystyle i\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\,(i{\mathcal{M}}^{J^{P}}_{\mathcal{E}})_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(p^{\prime},k)\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a}(k)\left[\frac{k}{q_{a}}\right]^{\ell}\,,

(117)

\displaystyle i\mathcal{C}^{J^{P}}_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{\prime 2}{\rm d}k^{\prime}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{k^{\prime}}{q_{a^{\prime}}}\right]^{\ell^{\prime}}\!(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 110.0pt\times(i\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\mathcal{E}})_{a^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,a\ell S}(k^{\prime},k)\,(i\Delta_{2,\infty})_{a}(k)\left[\frac{k}{q_{a}}\right]^{\ell}\,.

(118)

Given this enhanced matrix space, the main equation for
\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}
, eq. (
95
), remains unchanged.

Of course, this hinges upon having an expression for
\mathcal{E}^{J^{P}}
. In the subsequent section we explain how this can be done. In particular, we use the helicity basis to arrive at some key examples.

3.3
Evaluating the one-particle exchange

In this section, we explain how the OPE can be partial-wave projected onto the
\ell S
basis. Given that in QCD the nearest left-hand cuts are due to pseudoscalar exchanges, we will assume that the exchange particle is spinless.

An example we will emphasize is
D^{*}D\to D^{*}D
scattering in the
T_{cc}
channel, where the nearest left-hand cut is due to
u
channel
\pi
-exchange. Given this motivating example, let us look at a generic
u
-channel OPE, which, following section
2.2
, can generically be written in the form

\displaystyle i\,\mathcal{U}=\left(\bar{\eta}_{4}\,i\Gamma_{14}\,\eta_{1}\right)\,\frac{i}{u-m_{e}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,\left(\bar{\eta}_{3}\,i\Gamma_{23}\,\eta_{2}\right)\,,

(119)

where
u=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}
is the usual Mandelstam invariant,
m_{e}
is the mass of the exchange particle and the
\Gamma
tensors result from the on-shell reduction of the vertex functions. We use the shorthand notation
\eta_{j}\equiv\eta_{j,\lambda_{j}}(p_{j})
for the spin wave-functions, where
j
labels the particle,
p_{j}
is its four-momentum and
\lambda_{j}
is its helicity. We omit channel indices as these will not be relevant for the following. We find that working in the helicity basis is particularly advantageous when writing explicit expressions for the vertex functions for amplitude analyses and projecting to partial waves. To express the amplitude in terms of fixed-
z
spin projections, as is needed for the form of the QC presented in eq. (
79
), one must apply the following unitary transformations on the wave functions (see refs.
[
152
,
153
]
):

\displaystyle\eta_{m_{s}}(p)=\sum_{\lambda=-s}^{s}\eta_{\lambda}(p)\,D_{\lambda m_{s}}^{(s)}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,,

(120)

where
D_{\lambda m}^{(s)}
is the Wigner
D
rotation matrix element and
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
is the orientation of the particle momentum which can be expressed in terms of the polar,
\theta
, and azimuthal,
\varphi
, angles of the particle,
\hat{\mathbf{p}}=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)
. The specific form of the wave functions and vertex factors depends on the particles involved in the reaction. Generally, for integer spin particles, the wave functions are (cf. refs.
[
154
,
155
,
156
]
)

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(p)\Big\rvert_{s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\longrightarrow\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p,\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}\braket{1\lambda^{\prime},(s-1)\lambda^{\prime\prime}|s\lambda}\,\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}}(p,\lambda^{\prime})\,\varepsilon^{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p,\lambda^{\prime\prime})\,,

(121)

where
\braket{j_{1}m_{1},j_{2}m_{2}|jm}
is the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient and
\varepsilon^{\mu}(p,\lambda)
is the standard helicity basis spin-1 polarization four-vector. For half-integer spin particles, the wave functions are

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda}(p)\Big\rvert_{2s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\longrightarrow u^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p,\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}}\braket{{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{\prime},(s-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}})\lambda^{\prime\prime}|s\lambda}\,u(p,\lambda^{\prime})\,\varepsilon^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}(p,\lambda^{\prime\prime})\,,

(122)

where
u(p,\lambda)
is the standard helicity basis Dirac spinor.

The OPE in eq. (
119
) can be projected into a partial wave via the following procedure: First, we write the propagator as a function of the CM frame scattering angle
\theta
, defined via
\mathbf{p}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{3}=\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\cos\theta
,

\displaystyle u=(p_{1}-p_{4})^{2}

\displaystyle=m_{1}^{2}+m_{4}^{2}-2\omega_{1}\omega_{4}-2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\cos\theta\,,

\displaystyle=u_{0}-2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\,(1+\cos\theta)\,,

(123)

where
u_{0}=m_{1}^{2}+m_{4}^{2}-2\omega_{1}\omega_{4}+2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert
is the backward limit of
u
. We can therefore write eq. (
119
) as

\displaystyle i\,\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\mathcal{H}_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}(\theta)}{2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert(\zeta+i\epsilon-\cos\theta)}\,,

(124)

where
\zeta
corresponds to the value of
\cos\theta
when the propagator goes on mass-shell,
u=m_{e}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\zeta\equiv\cos\theta\rvert_{u=m_{e}^{2}}=-1-\frac{m_{e}^{2}-u_{0}}{2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert}\,,

(125)

and
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}(\theta)
is the product of vertex functions. In general,
\mathcal{H}
is a complicated function of the scattering angle
\theta
. However, the dependence can be easily written for low-lying spins, see ref.
[
145
]
, which we follow.

Following that work, here we assume that the reaction plane is oriented such that the azimuthal angle is zero, which we are free to do. Given this, the partial wave projection of eq. (
124
) over the CM frame scattering angle is

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{J}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\\
\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\end{subarray}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\,\sin\theta\,\frac{d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J)}(\theta)}{2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert(\zeta-\cos\theta)}\,\mathcal{H}_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}(\theta)\,,

(126)

where
\lambda=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}
and
d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J)}
is the Wigner little
d
matrix element.
7
7

7

We use the usual convention for rotation matrices,
e^{-i\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{\theta}}
, to define the Wigner

matrix. In using our results, one should note that some software may use different conventions, for example,
Mathematica
uses a sign convention opposite of ours.
While the helicity basis is useful in constructing exchange amplitudes of definite
J
, our target amplitude should be of definite spin-parity
J^{P}
. Therefore, we apply a unitary transformation on the helicity partial wave amplitudes to those in the spin-orbit, or
\ell S
, basis
[

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\ell^{\prime}S^{\prime};\,\ell S}^{J}=\sum_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}\sum_{\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{3}\lambda_{4}}({}^{2S^{\prime}+1}\ell^{\prime}_{J})\,\mathcal{U}^{J}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\\
\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\end{subarray}}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}({}^{2S+1}\ell_{J})\,,

(127)

where the coefficients

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}({}^{2S+1}\ell_{J})=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{2J+1}}\,\braket{J\lambda|\ell 0,S\lambda}\,\braket{S\lambda|s_{1}\lambda_{1},s_{2}\,(-\lambda_{2})}\,,

(128)

where the total angular momentum
J
is restricted as
\lvert S-\ell\rvert\leq J\leq\ell+S
. Similar relations hold for the final state. Upon projecting to definite
J
, and recoupling to the
\ell S
basis, the partial wave amplitudes take the form
[
145
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{J^{P}}=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{J^{P}}+\mathcal{C}_{2}^{J^{P}}\,Q_{0}(\zeta+i\epsilon)\,,

(129)

where
Q_{0}
is the Legendre function of the second kind, the coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
which are matrices in
\ell S
space to be tabulated for a target
J^{P}
. The
Q_{0}
function has branch points at
\zeta=\pm 1
, which yields a branch cut, the so-called left-hand cut, in the complex
s
plane of the scattering amplitude. The remainder
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
coefficients are assured to be non-singular functions in the kinematic region considered.

We do not attempt writing a generic amplitude and partial wave projection for any spin, but instead follow the procedure of ref.
[
145

to construct partial wave OPE amplitudes and illustrate it by examining some cases of interest to the community.

3.3.1
Spinless-Spinless scattering via Spinless Exchange

Let us consider a generic process involving spinless particles in the external states. Since the wave functions are trivial, the OPE is

\displaystyle i\,\mathcal{U}=ig^{\prime}\,\frac{i}{u-m_{e}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,ig\,,

(130)

where
g
and
g^{\prime}
are the couplings of particles 1 and 4 to
e
and particles 2 and 3 to
e
, respectively. In this case, it is easy to show that the partial wave projected OPE is

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{J}=\frac{g^{\prime}g}{2\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert}\,Q_{J}(\zeta+i\epsilon)\,.

(131)

One can show that this is equivalent to eq. (
129
) using an identity implemented in ref.
[
145
]
. This follows from the fact that
Q_{J}
can be written as an integral of the Legendre polynomial
P_{J}
. Then, one can use the recursion relation for the
P_{J}
, to find

\displaystyle Q_{J}(\zeta)=P_{J}(\zeta)\,Q_{0}(\zeta)-W_{J-1}(\zeta)\,,

(132)

where
W_{J-1}
is a polynomial in
\zeta

\displaystyle W_{\ell-1}(\zeta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{n}P_{n-1}(\zeta)\,P_{\ell-n}(\zeta)\,.

(133)

3.3.2
Vector-Spinless scattering via Spinless Exchange

Next, we consider the
u
-channel exchange in a vector-spinless reaction, e.g.,
D^{*}D\to D^{*}D
via
\pi

[
155

), which must satisfy the transversity condition

\displaystyle i\,\mathcal{U}=i[g^{\prime}\,p_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{*}(p_{3},\lambda_{3})]\,\frac{i}{u-m_{\pi}^{2}+i\epsilon}\,i[g\,p_{4}\cdot\varepsilon(p_{1},\lambda_{1})]\,.

(134)

The polarization vector in the helicity basis (in the reaction plane) is defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p,\pm)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(0,\mp\cos\theta,-i,\pm\sin\theta)\,,

(135)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}(\lvert\mathbf{p}\rvert,\omega\sin\theta,0,\omega\cos\theta)\,.

(136)

By direct evaluation, the
\mathcal{H}
function
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}}\equiv-g^{\prime}g\,[p_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{*}(p_{3},\lambda_{3})][p_{4}\cdot\varepsilon(p_{1},\lambda_{1})]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\pm\pm}=-\mathcal{H}_{\pm\mp}

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}g\,\frac{1}{2}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\sin^{2}\theta\,,

(137)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\pm 0}

\displaystyle=\mp g^{\prime}g\,\frac{\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert}{\sqrt{2}\,m_{1}}\sin\theta\,\left(\omega_{4}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert+\omega_{1}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\,\cos\theta\right)\,,

(138)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm g^{\prime}g\,\frac{\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert}{\sqrt{2}\,m_{3}}\sin\theta\,\left(\omega_{2}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert+\omega_{3}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\,\cos\theta\right)\,,

(139)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{00}

\displaystyle=-\frac{g^{\prime}g}{m_{1}m_{3}}\left(\omega_{4}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert+\omega_{1}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert\,\cos\theta\right)\left(\omega_{2}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{3}\rvert+\omega_{3}\lvert\mathbf{p}_{1}\rvert\,\cos\theta\right)\,.

(140)

Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be the product of the intrinsic parities for the initial and final state, respectively. For the case of
D^{*}D\to D^{*}D
, we have
\pi=\pi^{\prime}=+
. Thus, the allowed parity of the system is
P=(-1)^{\ell}=(-1)^{\ell^{\prime}}
. A particularly interesting case is the
J^{P}=1^{+}
channel, where the
T_{cc}
has been discovered by the LHCb collaboration. Here, both
S
and
D
waves contribute. For example, following ref.
[
145
]
, we find the
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}^{1^{+}}({}^{3}S_{1}\to{}^{3}S_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{g^{\prime}g}{6}\left[\frac{2}{3}(1-\gamma_{3})(1-\gamma_{1})-\gamma_{3}\gamma_{1}+\zeta f-f^{\prime}\right]\,,

(141)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{1^{+}}({}^{3}S_{1}\to{}^{3}S_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{g^{\prime}g}{6}\left[(1-\zeta^{2})f+\zeta f^{\prime}\right]\,,

(142)

where
\gamma_{j}=1/\sqrt{1-\beta_{j}^{2}}
with
\beta_{j}=\lvert\mathbf{p}_{j}\rvert/\omega_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,4
, and

\displaystyle f=\gamma_{3}\left(\frac{\beta_{3}}{\beta_{2}}+\zeta\right)+\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{4}}+\zeta\right)-\zeta\,,

(143)

and

\displaystyle f^{\prime}=\gamma_{3}\gamma_{1}\zeta\left(\frac{\beta_{3}}{\beta_{2}}+\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{4}}+\zeta\right)+\gamma_{3}\gamma_{1}\frac{\beta_{3}\beta_{1}}{\beta_{4}\beta_{2}}\,.

(144)

Further cases can be evaluated similarly. Note that one can relate the results presented in ref.
[
145
]
by identifying
\mathcal{H}=(-g^{\prime}g/3)\,q_{p}^{\star}q_{k}^{\star}\,\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, where
q_{k}^{\star}
,
q_{p}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are as defined in that work (the latter without the prime).

4
Conclusions

We have derived a generic non-perturbative framework that relates finite-volume energy spectra to two-body scattering amplitudes, including nearby left-hand singularities arising from single-particle exchanges. The formalism allows for an arbitrary number of two-body channels in which the particles can have arbitrary spin. Applications of this framework proceed in two steps: first, the new quantization condition is used to constrain the intermediate quantity
\mathcal{M}_{0}
that is insensitive to the left-hand cut; second, the full scattering amplitude is reconstructed from
\mathcal{M}_{0}
via a set of integral equations that also incorporate all single-particle exchanges.

It is straightforward to generalize this work to include branch cuts due to two- or more particles using models due to these exchanges, as was done recently in ref.
[
157
]
. While higher-particle exchanges can be incorporated similarly, we expect their effects will be suppressed compared with OPE, thus we leave their investigation for future applications.

We conclude by emphasizing two key messages. First, this formalism is a conceptually simple modification of previously published two-particle formalisms that do not account for the presence of the one-particle exchange. Second, the formalism is complete and ready for immediate application.
