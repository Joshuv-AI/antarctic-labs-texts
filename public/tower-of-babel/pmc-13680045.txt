Cancer Survivorship Care in Bavaria: General Practitioners’ Perspectives and a Regional Capacity Analysis
Authors: Laura Oestreich, Jörg Schelling, Michael Schoenberg, Michael von Bergwelt-Baildon, Karin Berger-Thürmel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rising number of cancer survivors in Germany increases the need for long-term, coordinated cross-sectoral care. General practitioners (GPs) play a key role, yet evidence on regional structures and GPs’ perspectives is limited. This study examines cancer survivorship care in Bavaria by combining a regional capacity analysis with a statewide GP survey.MethodsA mixed-methods study was conducted in Bavaria, combining a regional structural analysis of physician availability and projected survivorship care demand with a statewide anonymous quantitative online GP survey on care experiences, collaboration, digital tools, training needs, vaccination practices, and reimbursement.ResultsIn 2024, 9553 Bavarian GPs cared for approximately 380000 cancer survivors, with 17% living in areas of GP undersupply. Northern and Eastern Bavaria showed particularly high patient loads and projected shortages. Among 574 surveyed GPs, 72.7% perceived survivorship care as challenging. Key barriers included insufficient cross-sectoral collaboration (76.9%), limited knowledge of co-treating colleagues’ activities (72.6%), incomplete treatment information (31.7%), and inadequate reimbursement (95.2%). While most GPs (71.5%) considered the electronic patient record (ePA) promising, only 56.9% used KIM at least occasionally. 73.1% expressed interest in dedicated training and guidance, particularly regarding vaccination responsibilities and timing (64.1%).ConclusionGPs in Bavaria face increasing structural and organizational challenges due to rising survivor numbers and regional workforce shortages. Improved information exchange, strengthened cross-sectoral collaboration, consistent digital communication, targeted GP training, and continuous regional monitoring, are essential to ensure sustainable, high-quality survivorship care across Germany.

Introduction

In Germany, the number of cancer survivors is increasing due to both rising cancer incidence due to demographic aging and improved survival following advances in screening, diagnostics, and therapy 1 2 . The most widely used international definition considers cancer survivorship as beginning at diagnosis and continuing throughout life 3 , regardless of disease stage or prognosis. A narrower classification differentiates survivors who are disease free (cured), live with a well-controlled disease and mild symptoms (chronic condition), or remain in long-term clinical remission (long-term survivors) 4 , commonly defined from five years after diagnosis 5 . In Germany, around 3.1 million people were long-term survivors in 2017 6 , although more recent data are lacking.

Longer survival, however, does not necessarily equate to restored health. More than one-third of survivors still identify as “cancer patients” 5–16 years after diagnosis 7 . Up to 60% experience late and long-term effects such as cardiotoxicity, fatigue, secondary malignancies, or psychosocial distress requiring ongoing care 4 8 . Novel therapies, including immunotherapies, have introduced additional late effects such as heart failure and musculoskeletal dysfunction 9 . As advances in cancer treatment have substantially improved long-term survival, increasing numbers of patients live long enough for late and long-term effects to become clinically relevant and require structured survivorship care. Consequently, long-term survivorship care must extend beyond oncology follow-up, encompassing tertiary prevention, proactive diagnostics, preventive measures like vaccinations, and comprehensive management of physical and psychosocial sequelae.

Effective long-term survivorship care requires interdisciplinary and cross-sectoral collaboration, integrating oncology specialists, rehabilitation services, psycho-oncology, and – crucially – general practitioners (GPs). A clearly defined coordinator is essential; internationally, this role is often assumed by GPs. In Germany, around two-thirds of all cancer patients consult their GP during diagnosis and treatment 10 , underlining their function as first contact and long-term companion. Their continuity of care, accessibility, and knowledge of comorbidities and social context make them pivotal in survivorship care. Yet, unlike other European countries as the Netherlands, GPs in Germany are not system-wide gatekeepers to other healthcare providers.

In health systems such as Germany’s, where primary care plays a central role, strengthening GPs’ involvement offers important opportunities to improve continuous, holistic, and accessible survivorship care across sectors. However, the GP perspective on survivorship care in Germany is underexplored. In Bavaria, this knowledge gap coincides with a dual demographic challenge: a growing number of cancer survivors and a shrinking, aging GPs’ and specialists’ workforce 11 . However, the extent of these developments has not yet been systematically analyzed.

Against this background, our study combined a statewide survey of GPs in Bavaria on their experiences, challenges, and training needs with an analysis of regional GP workforce capacity, age distribution, and patient loads. This dual perspective provides an evidence base to inform future-oriented models of survivorship care that strengthen GPs, foster interdisciplinary collaboration, and ensure sustainable, high-quality cancer survivorship care in Germany’s federal healthcare system, where regional analyses are indispensable.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional mixed-methods study combined (i) a structural analysis of the regional availability of cancer survivorship care providers, including GPs and office-based hematologists/oncologists, and the projected population demand for survivorship care, and (ii) an anonymous online survey of GPs in Bavaria, Germany, assessing their experiences, challenges, and needs in providing cancer survivorship care. The structural analysis provided objective indicators of capacity, while the survey captured GPs’ subjective experiences of workload, coordination, and training needs, allowing linkage between structural conditions and professional perceptions.

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the CHERRIES checklist for online surveys 12 . The Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty, LMU Munich, confirmed that formal ethical approval was not required (reference number: 25–0073).

Structural analysis: availability of cancer survivorship care providers and population demand

The structural analysis evaluated the current and future capacity of GP- and specialist-based survivorship care in Bavaria by combining provider- and population-level data to identify regional disparities.

GP and hematologists/oncologists workforce data for 2024, including regional distribution, age structure, and projected developments, were obtained from the Bavarian Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (KVB) and the Versorgungsatlas 13 14 .

Population demand for survivorship care was estimated using data from the Bavarian State Office for Statistics and the Bavarian Cancer Registry , providing regional information on population size, age structure, projected growth, and cancer incidence.

Based on these data, patient loads (number of residents aged ≥65 years and new cancer cases) per GP and hematologist/oncologist were calculated.

Survey

Development

The survey questionnaire was developed following a systematic literature review in PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar using the keywords “oncology”, “cancer”, “general practitioners”, and “cancer survivorship” in German or English. An interdisciplinary team of oncologists, GPs, and healthcare researchers designed the questionnaire in collaboration with GP representatives. Pilot testing with GPs ensured its feasibility, clarity, and acceptability.

Content

The final questionnaire had 21 questions ( Supplementary Material ). It included sociodemographic characteristics of the GPs and their practices (place of residence, type of specialty), GPs’ clinical experiences with cancer survivorship care, perceived challenges and needs, vaccinations, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Recruitment

All actively practicing GPs in Bavaria (n = 9297 at the time of the survey) were eligible. Data collection took place between February and June 2025 using LimeSurvey (Version 5.6.65). GPs were invited to participate via mail and newsletter distributed by the Bavarian Association of General Practitioners (Bayerischer Hausärzteverband) and the KVB. A reminder was sent after six weeks. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated.

Statistical analyses

Survey findings were analyzed in relation to structural indicators to assess whether regional characteristics (patient loads, urbanity, GP undersupply) correlated with reported challenges in survivorship care. In terms of patient load, regions were compared by terciles (highest vs. lowest patient load). Urban -rural classifications followed the Federal Institute for Research on Building, Urban Affairs, and Spatial Development in Germany as of 31 December 2023 based on postal codes and settlement size. Following the KVB definitions, regions were classified as undersupplied (coverage < 75%) or at risk of undersupply (coverage 75%–110%) in terms of GP coverage when demographic projections, expected retirements, and expected replacements indicated potential shortages.

Group differences were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied. Data analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.3.3) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0). Maps were created with QGIS (version 3.40.4).

Results

Structural analysis: availability of cancer survivorship care providers and population demand

In 2024, a total of 9.553 GPs and 245 hematologists/oncologists were active in Bavaria 13 14 , which corresponds to an average of 1386 residents per GP and 54077 residents per hematologist/oncologist. Mean GP age was 55.1 years (36.5% aged ≥60 years), mean hematologist/oncologist age 52.8 years (28.2% aged ≥60 years) 13 14 .

Between 2023 and 2043, Bavaria’s population is projected to increase by 4.3% to 13736700 inhabitants 15 . The old-age dependency ratio (people aged ≥65 per 100 individuals aged 20–64) is expected to rise by 25%, from 36.1 in 2024 to 44.4 in 2043 15 . Four districts included planning regions classified as undersupplied in terms of GPs, and 17 districts were categorized as at risk of undersupply 16 , affecting in total approximately 17% of the Bavarian population. In 20 districts, no office-based hematologist/oncologist is available, affecting approximately 20% of the Bavarian population 14 .

The median age at diagnosis was 67 years for women and 69 years for men 17 . Survey

A total of 574 GPs (response rate 6.2%) participated in the survey. Recruitment was geographically well distributed, with 80% of districts achieving participation rates between 3% and 9%. Overall, 45.1% of GPs practiced in urban and 54.9% in rural areas. Most participants (78.1%) were trained in general medicine, while the remainder were predominantly specialized in internal medicine. Detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Material ( Supplement 1 ).

Long-term survivorship care by GPs and perceived challenges

Cancer survivor patients most frequently consulted GPs for blood monitoring, referrals to other specialists or clinics, counselling, applications to pension or social welfare agencies, and supportive conversations. The latter were particularly relevant in rural regions: 33.7% of rural GPs reported that patients always sought consultations for psychological support, compared to 24.0% in urban areas (p = 0.016). Further frequent concerns included support for everyday needs, rehabilitation, cancer follow-up examinations, and management of treatment side-effects.

Overall, 72.7% of GPs considered the long-term cancer survivorship care challenging, particularly in urban regions (urban: 76.9%, rural: 69.3%, p = 0.050). Fig. 3 shows regional differences in perceived challenges.

Only 4.8% of GPs considered reimbursement adequate for the complexity and time demands of cancer survivorship care.

Collaboration and information exchange

Collaboration with co-treating healthcare providers was often described as inadequate. Only a minority of GPs felt adequately informed about the activities of other providers in their region, though this was more common in rural than in urban areas (rural: 31.8%, urban: 22.2%, p = 0.017). 29.6% of GPs reported local co-treating colleagues as unknown. The frequency of consultations with other healthcare providers is shown in Fig. 4 . Although 63.2% of all GPs were satisfied with the content of information exchange, 80.5% perceived the process too time consuming. Information exchange most frequently concerned aftercare measures (87.9%), innovative treatments (49.3%), vaccinations (44.5%), and further diagnostics (36.9%). However, only 59.3% of GPs reported having all necessary information on the current treatment status of their cancer survivor patients most of the time. This proportion was higher in regions with a higher cancer patient load per GP (highest tercile 66.9%; lowest tercile: 55.7%; p = 0.015).

Barriers to cross-sectoral collaboration were perceived by many GPs (76.9%). The most frequent reported obstacles were poor accessibility of clinics and specialists (88.1%) and a lack of structured communication channels (60.4%). Additionally, 35.8% of GPs stated that information exchange is not required by oncology specialists. According to the GPs, smooth coordination with oncological experts would require direct accessibility, reliable communication of findings, consistent use of the secure communication system “Kommunikation im Medizinwesen” (KIM), and a shared case file for structured information exchange.

Despite these barriers, 69.6% of GPs wished for a more active role in cancer survivorship care and closer communication with specialists, particularly in low cancer patient load regions per GP (lowest tercile: 80.6%, highest tercile 64.2%; p < 0.001).

Digital exchange

61.8% of GPs supported introducing a virtual case record to improve information exchange, accelerate processes, and strengthen cross-sectoral care. The electronic patient record (ePA) was considered a promising tool for information exchange by 71.5% of GPs. KIM was used at least occasionally by 56.9% of GPs to communicate with other healthcare providers and more frequently in high patient load regions per GP (highest tercile 65.0%, lowest tercile 51.5%; p = 0.015).

Training and information needs

The survey revealed a broad demand for training: 73.1% of all respondents wished for dedicated training programs and/or guidelines for survivorship care. Interest in training and preferred topics and formats are shown in Fig. 5 .

51.4% of GPs reported a lack of structured processes to inform long-term survivors about upcoming vaccinations. This was accompanied by a high demand for clarity regarding vaccination responsibilities: 67.8% of respondents wished for information from oncologists/hematologists on necessary and pending vaccinations, 64.1% expected guidance from professional associations on clear indications and timing, and 56.5% emphasized the need for better coordination within the treatment network regarding responsibilities and who administers vaccinations. Only 5.5% reported no need for additional information or action in relation to vaccinations for long-term cancer survivors.

Discussion

This study is the first to examine long-term cancer survivorship care from the perspective of GPs in Bavaria by linking structural regional GP capacity and cancer survivor demand with survey-based insights into clinical practice, care collaboration, digitalization, and information needs. Overall, the findings reveal substantial and growing challenges in primary-care-based survivorship care, including barriers in cross-sectoral collaboration, limited awareness of local networks and their activities, incomplete treatment information, and time-consuming care processes. Digital tools could alleviate some of these challenges by improving information exchange and strengthen cross-sectoral care; however, uptake remains inconstant. Dedicated survivorship training and practical vaccination guidance could further support GPs. As demographic aging, rising survivor numbers, and uneven GP distribution converge, these challenges will intensify. Given that similar trends occur across Germany, the Bavarian findings may serve as a blueprint for other federal states facing strained healthcare infrastructures 20 .

Structural analysis

The structural analysis indicated that demographic and GP aging will further intensify existing regional disparities in GP and hematologist/oncologist coverage and increase demand for primary-care-based cancer survivorship services. Several Bavarian regions — particularly parts of Northern and Eastern Bavaria — already exhibit structural vulnerability, characterized by high demand on primary care, limited specialist availability, and demographic trends that will exacerbate these pressures. Similar patterns have been documented in other federal states of Germany 20 21 , where geographic dispersion, shrinking workforces, and aging populations strain traditional care models. Moreover, young physicians express reluctance to establish primary care practices in less populated regions, citing working conditions and lack of incentives 22 .

These developments underscore the need for strategic workforce planning and innovative, decentralized care models tailored to underserved regions. Approaches such as GP-oncologist shared-care models, survivorship centers focusing on the long-term needs of cancer survivors, including the management of late effects associated with cancer and its treatment, structured survivorship pathways, telemedicine-supported case management, and regionally coordinated networks, may help mitigate gaps in specialist availability 23 24 . Other countries have implemented such models successfully; for example, nurse-led survivorship programs, GP-oncologist shared-care protocols, and structured handover processes in countries such as the Netherlands and Australia 25 26 27 . Bavaria and other federal states could benefit from piloting and adapting these approaches.

To support prioritization and regional planning, a more robust and recurring monitoring infrastructure is urgently needed, ideally linking cancer registry data, workforce data, and regional care utilization patterns. At present, responsibility for such monitoring is fragmented across institutions 28 , resulting in a responsibility gap in systematic, long-term surveillance. However, continuous data collection, analysis, and evaluation are essential for prioritization, planning, and informed decision-making.

Long-term survivorship care by GPs, perceived challenges, and collaboration

Consistent with recent studies from Germany and the Netherlands demonstrating the central role of GPs in survivorship care 29 30 , GPs in this study reported extensive involvement in long-term medical, administrative, and psychosocial care tasks.

However, 72.7% of GPs described survivorship care as challenging. In line with previous research 31 32 , insufficient cross-sectoral exchange emerged as a major issue, mainly related to limited specialist accessibility and lack of structured communication. This lack of systematic information flow, observed also in other studies on survivorship care 32 33 , underscores persistent weaknesses in coordination across sectors within German cancer care. Strengthening collaboration will require clearer role definitions, structured communication pathways, consistent use of secure communication tools (e.g., KIM), and regionally anchored care networks. Personal professional networks appear particularly critical in urban areas, where complex provider landscapes and professional anonymity contribute to fragmented care.

Digital exchange

Our results align with earlier studies 34 suggesting that secure digital communication tools, such as KIM and ePA, hold significant potential to streamline information flow and enhance coordination. Many GPs expressed interest in shared virtual case records and broader ePA use, indicating that standardized and user-friendly digital infrastructure could address several identified deficits and align with national and European digital health strategies 35 . The ePA in particular could strengthen information continuity and support informed patient participation 36 . At the time of the survey, implementation and routine use of digital communication tools was still evolving and limited. Only 56.9% of GPs reported at least occasional KIM use. Higher uptake in high-burden regions suggests that workload pressures may encourage digitalization. To realize the potential of digital tools, structured implementation support, reduced administrative complexity, and user-friendly software are essential. In survivorship care specifically, digital tools could play a crucial role in ensuring continuous information flow, access to initial oncological treatment summaries, and guidance on managing cancer treatment late effects.

Training and information needs

More than 70% of GPs expressed a desire for targeted survivorship training. Current oncological training programs rarely address cross-sectoral coordination, late and long-term effects, or the specific role of primary care. Vaccination management emerged as a major area of uncertainty, particularly regarding responsibilities, indications, and timing. Since vaccination uptake is critical for survivors 37 38 , joint guidance on indications, timing, and coordination of vaccinations from oncology societies, GP associations, and public health institutions is needed, ideally supported by standardized documentation such as an integrated electronic vaccination record 39 .

Strengths and limitations

The survey response rate was moderate (6.2%), yet comparable to other German GP surveys 40 . However, a selection bias towards GPs more engaged in cancer care cannot be excluded. As data were self-reported they reflect subjective experience and may not fully capture actual practice behavior Nonetheless, the strong regional representativeness of GP respondents supports the generalizability of the findings. Because district-level survivorship prevalence data were unavailable, long-term burden had to be inferred from incidence, which may vary by district due to different survival rate. Outcomes of survivorship care were not assessed but should be examined in future longitudinal studies. Such studies should evaluate care trajectories over time, linking healthcare utilization with patient outcomes and clarifying the respective roles of GPs and specialists in survivorship care. Furthermore, future research should include the perspectives of cancer survivors, oncologists, hematologists, and other specialists involved in survivorship care, as evidence on the challenges of cancer survivorship care from these stakeholder groups is currently lacking.

Conclusion

This study underscores the increasing structural and organizational challenges in long-term cancer survivorship care, driven by demographic change, rising survivor numbers, and an aging office-based physician workforce. The results emphasize the need for future-oriented care models that respond to these developments and leverage the pivotal role of GPs and enhance the quality and sustainability of long-term cancer survivorship care. Such models should strengthen interdisciplinary and cross-sectoral collaboration, establish structured communication channels between healthcare professionals, promote shared digital records to enable low-threshold information exchange, and clearly define vaccination responsibilities. Until the ePA is fully functionally implemented, interim solutions, such as local low-threshold contact points or nurse-led support structures, may help bridge current information and coordination gaps. Continuous healthcare monitoring will be essential, particularly as the ongoing hospital reform is likely to reshape responsibilities and care pathways, issues that warrant further investigation in future research.
