Telehealth implementation for military combat casualty care and evacuation: a qualitative study
Authors: Mohsen Fooladzadeh Dehghan, Abdollah Saghafi, Masoud Arabfard, Seyed Tayeb Moradian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe application of telehealth in combat zones offers transformative potential for casualty care but presents unique and complex challenges. Successfully leveraging this technology requires a deep understanding of its foundational requirements and operational barriers in high-stakes military environments. This study aimed to explore the foundational requirements, challenges, and enablers for the successful implementation of telehealth systems in military combat environments from the perspective of experienced personnel.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using a conventional content analysis approach. We recruited ten military personnel through purposive and snowball sampling until reaching data saturation. We collected data via in-depth, semi-structured interviews and interviewer notes, and analyzed them using the five-step Graneheim and Lundman method. We ensured the trustworthiness of the data using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.ResultsThe analysis resulted in the extraction of two main categories: “Disruption in the Care and Evacuation Chain: Operational and Structural Vulnerabilities” and “Designing a Resilient Military Health Ecosystem: Requirements and Vision” which encompassed 14 generic categories and 43 subcategories. Key findings highlight the necessity of a resilient, mission-oriented technical infrastructure and human-centered design. Crucially, the study underscores the paramount importance of a robust “Cyber Defense Shield,” featuring end-to-end encryption and remote data wipe capabilities to navigate the paradox between data accessibility and operational security. Furthermore, redefining organizational culture to view telehealth as a “strategic imperative” rather than an option, and “empowering the human element” through continuous, integrated training were identified as critical determinants of success.ConclusionThe successful deployment of telehealth in combat zones requires a holistic, socio-technical approach. It demands more than advanced technological solutions, necessitating integrated strategies that address robust cybersecurity, standardized governance, comprehensive human factor development, and strong organizational and command-level commitment to ensure clinical effectiveness and tactical advantage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14789-4.

Background

Armed conflicts and war have consistently posed one of the most significant challenges to healthcare systems, often causing severe damage to health infrastructure [1]. In the contemporary era, the paradigm of military conflict has increasingly shifted toward asymmetric and complex warfare, in which the rapid evacuation of casualties from the battlefield and their transfer to medical facilities are often not feasible [2]. This paradigm shift has given rise to the concept of Prolonged Field Care (PFC), a concept that requires medics to sustain casualties on the battlefield for hours using limited resources and fundamental life-saving interventions [3]. Recent studies indicate that under such circumstances, medics’ knowledge and skills in the field are often insufficient without external support [3]. Telehealth has emerged as a powerful tool to bridge this gap, supporting military forces and frontline medics. Telehealth, broadly defined as the provision of healthcare services, clinical information, and health education over a distance using telecommunication technologies, is a powerful tool for supporting military forces and medics in the field [4, 5]. Research has demonstrated that in conflict- or crisis-stricken zones, telehealth connects primary care providers with remote specialist nurses and physicians for vital consultations [5, 6]. Essentially, telehealth empowers battlefield medics to administer initial life-saving interventions under the direct guidance of healthcare specialists, to reduce combat mortality and the incidence of limb loss [1]. Furthermore, the application of telehealth on the battlefield aims to support human performance and manage cognitive load in the high-stress environment of combat [7]. However, translating telehealth design from civilian contexts to military combat scenarios presents distinct structural and operational challenges. Civilian telehealth systems are typically designed around stable, high-speed infrastructure, synchronous video capabilities, and predictable environments [8]. In stark contrast, combat scenarios are characterized by Disconnected, Intermittent, and Limited (DIL) bandwidth environments, high kinetic threats, severe weather, and stringent noise and light discipline [1, 9]. Consequently, standard civilian telehealth architectures do not directly transfer to the battlefield. Recent literature highlighting evolving technologies—such as asynchronous store-and-forward systems, ruggedized wearables, and low-bandwidth communication protocols—underscores the necessity of context-specific military designs [1]. Despite these technological advancements, integrating these tools into the highly volatile chain of command and extreme physical conditions of combat remains fundamentally challenging [5].

While a cursory review of recent literature reveals growing attention to telehealth usage trends, general barriers, and technological opportunities in military settings [10–12], there remains a profound research gap. Much of the existing literature focuses on top-down technical specifications or high-level policy guidelines, often overlooking the socio-technical dynamics of the battlefield. Specifically, it is difficult to ascertain how these evolving technologies align with the actual human-system interactions and cognitive loads of combat medics under fire. There is a critical lack of qualitative, user-centered research that translates the lived, operational experiences of frontline medical personnel into concrete design requirements.

Given the high-stakes nature of military casualty care, an in-depth qualitative study is warranted to capture the perspectives of personnel directly involved in combat operations. Addressing this gap is of paramount importance for moving beyond generic technological solutions to developing resilient, purpose-built systems. Therefore, the objective of this study is to elucidate the structural challenges and establish specific socio-technical requirements for designing and employing telehealth in military combat casualty care and evacuation.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a qualitative study. To achieve the study’s objectives, data were primarily collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews, supplemented by interviewer notes. Subsequently, a conventional qualitative content analysis approach was employed to analyze the collected data. Content analysis is a credible and robust method for analyzing qualitative data to generate new knowledge and insights, making it a suitable methodology for exploring individuals’ experiences of phenomena within specific contexts [13]. We selected this methodology considering the complex and underexplored nature of telehealth application in combat zones, along with the imperative for an in-depth exploration of individual experiences to achieve the study’s objectives. Participants and setting

The study population comprised military personnel and specialists with direct or observational experience in the application of telehealth in combat zones. Given the sensitive nature of the topic and the difficulty in identifying and accessing these individuals, we employed a snowball sampling technique [14]. The inclusion criteria were: a history of deployment in a combat zone; direct engagement with telehealth systems for casualty care; the absence of any diagnosed psychological conditions that would affect participation; and a willingness to participate in the study. The sole exclusion criterion was the unwillingness to continue with the research process.

We continued recruitment and participant interviews until achieving data saturation. Saturation was defined as the point at which no new data emerged that could contribute to the development of the existing categories and subcategories [15]. In total, we interviewed 10 participants, and collected and analyzed the data over a six-month period. To clarify their roles and extent of engagement with telehealth, the majority of the participants (n = 7) were frontline combat medics and first responders who directly operated telehealth tools at the point of injury to communicate with rear-echelon medical staff. The remaining participants (n = 3) included remote clinical providers (physicians and specialized nurses) who received these communications to provide specialized medical direction to the frontline. All participants were male, with ages ranging from the 35–69 years old; the majority (n = 5) fell within the 40–49 age range. Regarding educational background, four participants held a graduate or professional degree, three possessed an undergraduate degree, and three had a high school diploma or its equivalent. Furthermore, all participants had combat zone experience, with durations of up to 12 months (n = 5), 13–18 months (n = 3), and 19–24 months (n = 2).

Data collection

We collected and analyzed data over a six-month period between June 2025 and January 2026. As our primary method, we conducted face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with each participant. In addition to the interviews, we employed direct interviewer notes as a supplementary data source. During these sessions, we took notes on the participants’ non-verbal communication (e.g., tone of voice, body language, silences) and the environmental context. We used these interviewer notes to contextualize the interview data and ensure a deeper understanding of the participants’ narratives. We used an interview guide featuring open-ended questions derived from the study’s objectives to initiate and guide the conversation. As the dialogue unfolded, we asked probing questions to delve deeper into participants’ narratives and explore emergent themes. To foster a comfortable and conducive environment, we chose the interview location based on each participant’s preference. With each participant’s informed consent, we audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed all interviews verbatim. Additionally, we completed these notes immediately after each session to capture non-verbal cues and contextual details. The duration of the interviews ranged from 30 to 45 min. The semi-structured interview guide developed for this study is available as a supplementary file (see Supplementary file 1).

Data analysis

We analyzed the qualitative data—including both interview transcripts and interviewer notes—using the conventional content analysis method based on the five steps outlined by Graneheim and Lundman [16]. First, we immediately transcribed the recorded interviews verbatim. Second, we listened to the audio recordings repeatedly and thoroughly read the transcripts and corresponding interviewer notes to develop a comprehensive understanding. Third, we identified meaning units and generated initial codes. To ensure analytical rigor, this and subsequent steps were conducted independently by two researchers (the correspondence and first authors) who coded the transcripts in parallel. Fourth, through abstraction, we classified similar initial codes into broader categories. Any disagreements in coding or categorization between the two researchers were resolved through discussion and consensus meetings. If a consensus could not be reached, a third senior qualitative researcher was consulted. Fifth, we identified the central concept and underlying theme of each category. Given the highly specialized nature of the frontline responders’ experiences, the interviews possessed high “information power,” yielding exceptionally rich and dense data despite the sample size of 10 participants. In line with our qualitative paradigm, the prominence of the resulting themes was determined by their depth of meaning, recurrence across multiple narratives, and centrality to the research question, rather than by quantitative frequency counts. Finally, we used MAXQDA software (Version 2020) to manage and analyze the dataset.

Trustworthiness and rigor

To ensure data trustworthiness and rigor, we applied the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985) [17]:

Credibility: We established credibility through prolonged engagement with the subject, member checking (participant review), and peer debriefing, and investigator triangulation via the use of multiple independent coders.

Dependability: We detailed the data collection and analysis steps to establish a clear and reproducible audit trail. Furthermore, in qualitative inquiry, rather than calculating statistical inter-rater reliability (IRR), we ensured dependability through independent parallel coding and consensus-building.

Confirmability: We strived to maintain objectivity by bracketing personal biases, holding reflexive discussions, and continuously cross-referencing our interpretations with the collected interviewer notes.

Transferability: We provided thick descriptions and direct quotations to enable others to judge the applicability of our findings to similar contexts.

Results

Analysis of the 10 in-depth interviews and their corresponding interviewer notes revealed the core challenges and requirements of military telehealth. Through this process, we extracted 291 conceptual codes, 43 subcategories, 14 generic categories, and two main categories (Table 1).

To synthesize our findings and explicitly link the identified challenges with their corresponding solutions, we developed a conceptual model (Fig. 2). This model bridges the gap between the isolated themes by mapping “Operational and Structural Vulnerabilities” against the “Resilient Military Health Ecosystem” requirements, visually demonstrating how each proposed requirement directly mitigates a specific structural flaw. Furthermore, the model highlights the ultimate “Value Proposition” resulting from the system’s implementation as a key outcome.

Main category 1: Disruption in the care and evacuation chain: operational and structural vulnerabilities

Fragility of communication channels in combat environments and infrastructural deficiencies

A fundamental vulnerability identified was the fragility of communication infrastructure in combat zones. Participants reported unreliable connections, frequent signal drops, and poor bandwidth, which directly hampered the quality and accuracy of medical consultations. This disruption was not merely a technical glitch; it had profound psychological impacts on the medical staff on the ground:

There were many problems, like losing connection due to combat conditions or lack of signal, and poor audio and video quality, which sometimes meant the physician couldn’t provide an accurate consultation.” (Participant 3)

There were many problems, like losing connection due to combat conditions or lack of signal, and poor audio and video quality, which sometimes meant the physician couldn’t provide an accurate consultation.” (Participant 3)

Furthermore, the physical equipment available was often inadequate—limited to non-medical, general-purpose tools like basic walkie-talkies that were insecure and ill-suited for medical dialogue. As one participant emphasized the inadequacy of these tools:

Our only tool was a walkie-talkie. That’s it—a basic tool designed for short military messages, not for long conversations and medical consultations. The walkie-talkie wasn’t made for this. Its range is limited, it’s full of noise, and most importantly, it’s not secure. (Participant 1)

Compounding these issues was the critical logistical challenge of sustaining power for electronic devices in harsh, mobile environments. Over-reliance on battery-powered systems creates vulnerabilities, where recharging batteries under fire was described as a nightmare:

Providing a sustainable energy supply is a huge logistical challenge. All this advanced equipment is power-hungry. Batteries run out, and charging them under fire, in dust, or in extreme cold is a nightmare. Over-reliance on batteries can be a deadly weakness. (Participant 9)

Technological gaps and equipment inadequacy on the battlefield

Beyond the fragility of communication networks, participants prominently highlighted a severe scarcity of ruggedized medical devices engineered for austere operational environments. The physical resilience of hardware is a critical necessity in combat zones, yet there is a significant shortage of equipment capable of withstanding harsh physical conditions. Emphasizing the critical need for durability over standard medical devices, one participant noted:

Electronic equipment needs to be resistant to impact, moisture, dust, extreme temperatures, and battlefield vibrations. This means we need rugged devices, which are more expensive and might also be bulkier. (Participant 8)

Electronic equipment needs to be resistant to impact, moisture, dust, extreme temperatures, and battlefield vibrations. This means we need rugged devices, which are more expensive and might also be bulkier. (Participant 8)

This physical hardware deficiency is further compounded by a stark lack of specialized, integrated, and user-friendly software. Basic communication applications fail to meet the unique, high-stakes clinical needs of field medics operating under extreme pressure. Instead of utilizing purpose-built telehealth platforms designed for combat medicine, medical personnel are often forced to rely on rudimentary workarounds:

As I mentioned, in the Syrian warzone, we didn’t have anything called specialized telehealth technology or software. We just used limited voice calls and two-way radios.” (Participant 3)

As I mentioned, in the Syrian warzone, we didn’t have anything called specialized telehealth technology or software. We just used limited voice calls and two-way radios.” (Participant 3)

Governance gaps and ambiguity in legal frameworks

The successful deployment of telehealth in combat zones is severely hindered by a systemic absence of codified protocols and comprehensive legal frameworks. Participants repeatedly highlighted that operations were largely ad hoc, lacking standardized operating procedures to guide medical personnel. This unstructured environment creates immense operational uncertainty; as Participant 4 noted, “We had no organized system, protocol… Everything was case-by-case and ad hoc, which made it difficult to make the right decisions.” This lack of standardized protocols inevitably leads to profound ambiguities regarding medical and legal liability. In high-stakes remote treatments, establishing accountability remains a complex challenge. Highlighting this critical regulatory void, Participant 8 questioned: “If a misdiagnosis or an error occurs in the telehealth system, who is responsible? The field medic? The remote consulting physician? The software developer?”

Beyond legal uncertainties, military telehealth suffers from profound structural fragmentation at both the macro-policy and operational levels. Nationally, there is a critical lack of inter-sectoral coordination among key entities such as the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Communications, and the Armed Forces. Participant 2 emphasized that without “macro-level policymaking and coordination” between these bodies, centers operate in silos with weak or non-existent integration. This disjointed approach at the macro level is closely mirrored on the battlefield by a persistent organizational divide between medical and operational commands. Because medical hierarchies are often structurally separated from military leadership, operational commanders frequently lack a comprehensive understanding of frontline clinical needs. Consequently, as Participant 1 explained, “this separation prevents a sense of need for a telehealth system from being created in their minds,” further stalling its prioritization and implementation.

Cultural resistance and personnel skill gaps

Beyond physical and legal barriers, the implementation of military telehealth is heavily impeded by profound cultural resistance and a lack of organizational institutionalization. Senior commanders and medical officials often exhibit a fundamental mindset barrier, failing to recognize telehealth as an operational necessity. This top-down cultural resistance prevents telehealth from becoming a standardized, institutionalized practice within the military framework. Consequently, this lack of institutional priority cascades down to frontline operations, resulting in a stark absence of specialized training programs. Because telehealth is neither culturally valued nor structurally embedded, field medics are often deployed to combat zones with critical skill gaps, having received only basic combat training rather than specialized telehealth instruction. Reflecting on this cascading failure from leadership mindset to frontline readiness, Participant 1 provided a comprehensive account of these systemic barriers, supported by the interviewer notes:

Our main challenge was that the mindset of the medical officials was not ready to use telehealth. We still haven’t been able to establish in our commanders’ minds that telehealth is an essential principle and must be used. (Participant 1)

Our main challenge was that the mindset of the medical officials was not ready to use telehealth. We still haven’t been able to establish in our commanders’ minds that telehealth is an essential principle and must be used. (Participant 1)

The security vs. access paradox: risks of information leakage and enemy exploitation

A critical paradox emerges when implementing battlefield telehealth: the tension between ensuring rapid access to vital medical data and safeguarding it from enemy exploitation. Participants noted that in combat zones, medical data transforms from a private, individual concern into a highly sensitive strategic asset. This vulnerability manifests across three major domains. First, there is a high susceptibility to cyber-attacks and electronic warfare, where adversaries might not only intercept communications but actively alter medical orders in transit, potentially leading to fatal consequences. Second, the very transmission of this data risks exposing critical operational intelligence; simply emitting a radio signal can reveal troop positions, while leaked health statuses of key personnel allow the enemy to assess combat power and exploit tactical vulnerabilities. Finally, beyond electronic threats, there is a severe physical security risk, as captured devices like tablets and smartphones can provide the enemy with direct, unauthorized access to a wealth of sensitive information. Participant 9 eloquently detailed this multifaceted threat landscape, highlighting both the cyber and operational risks:

Imagine the enemy could infiltrate the system; they could change the medical orders in transit. For example, a doctor gives an order, but the message that reaches the medic is a wrong order that leads to the soldier’s death (Participant 9)

Imagine the enemy could infiltrate the system; they could change the medical orders in transit. For example, a doctor gives an order, but the message that reaches the medic is a wrong order that leads to the soldier’s death (Participant 9)

The enemy is desperate for our medical information. Why? To assess our combat power. If the enemy finds out that a key unit’s commander is wounded, their troops’ morale will be shattered, and this is an excellent opportunity for the enemy to attack. Every radio signal is a blinking light that shouts: ‘I am here!’ This means exposing the troops’ position to the enemy. (Participant 9)

Main category 2: Designing a resilient military health ecosystem: requirements and vision

Ensuring communication continuity: a multi-layered network architecture

To counter the infrastructure fragility identified in the previous section, participants articulated a vision for a multi-layered, hybrid communication architecture. Fundamentally, this requires a transition from reliance on single-channel commercial networks (like 4G) toward redundant systems capable of switching seamlessly between radio, satellite, and mesh networks. This resilience must be paired with strict data optimization; given the combat reality of limited bandwidth, systems should be engineered to prioritize vital telemetry and text over high-bandwidth video. Furthermore, to address inevitable coverage dead zones, participants proposed innovative tactical solutions, such as deploying aerial assets to create temporary network bridges.

We need stable communications that don’t require high-speed internet. Wired internet or even 4G might be unavailable on the battlefield. So, the systems must be able to work with radio, satellite, or emergency networks and transfer critical data with the smallest possible size to save time. (Participant 6)

We need stable communications that don’t require high-speed internet. Wired internet or even 4G might be unavailable on the battlefield. So, the systems must be able to work with radio, satellite, or emergency networks and transfer critical data with the smallest possible size to save time. (Participant 6)

Using communication drones, meaning drones that fly at a high altitude and act like a mobile communication tower, establishes a connection between ground units. In reality, they create temporary but vital communication coverage. (Participant 9)

Using communication drones, meaning drones that fly at a high altitude and act like a mobile communication tower, establishes a connection between ground units. In reality, they create temporary but vital communication coverage. (Participant 9)

Development of battlefield-compatible hardware

Beyond network architecture, the physical characteristics of telehealth equipment dictate its viability on the frontlines. Participants stressed that any hardware carried by combat medics must be rigorously designed for austere environments—specifically, it must be ruggedized, ultra-lightweight, and highly portable. Crucially, the physical form factor is inseparable from power consumption. Because highly mobile units operate off-grid, relying on standard military or civilian power infrastructures is impossible. If devices are not extremely low-power, the requisite batteries transform advanced medical tools into heavy, burdensome liabilities. Therefore, sustainable, grid-independent energy solutions are a strict prerequisite, requiring devices that function seamlessly with minimalistic power sources. This physical constraint and the necessity for tailored power solutions were clearly articulated by participants and vividly demonstrated during the interview sessions:

Medical equipment must also be portable, lightweight, and rugged, like a pulse oximeter or a handheld ECG that fits in an aid bag and can be charged with a power bank or a solar panel. (Participant 5)

Medical equipment must also be portable, lightweight, and rugged, like a pulse oximeter or a handheld ECG that fits in an aid bag and can be charged with a power bank or a solar panel. (Participant 5)

It’s essential to use independent energy sources like long-lasting batteries or small generators that can keep the equipment powered without relying on the civilian or military power grid. (Participant 2)

It’s essential to use independent energy sources like long-lasting batteries or small generators that can keep the equipment powered without relying on the civilian or military power grid. (Participant 2)

Smart integration and data-driven decision-making

Beyond hardware constraints, participants emphasized the need for intelligent software ecosystems capable of turning raw battlefield data into actionable medical decisions. This requires a three-pronged approach: rapid identification, resilient data capture, and automated triage. To establish immediate clinical context, participants proposed integrating digital identifiers—such as uniform-mounted QR codes—to instantly access a soldier’s baseline health records, including blood type and allergies. However, capturing real-time injury data requires robust software designed for intermittent connectivity. Thus, an “offline-first” architecture is critical; applications must seamlessly cache telemetry and clinical notes locally, automatically synchronizing with command centers during fleeting windows of network availability. Finally, as data from multiple casualties aggregates at the centralized command, the risk of cognitive overload necessitates artificial intelligence. Participants envisioned an AI-driven decision-support system that functions akin to air traffic control, autonomously analyzing physiological parameters to prioritize triage and coordinate medical evacuation dynamically. These interconnected concepts of smart identification, resilient data management, and AI-assisted triage were clearly articulated by the participants:

Every soldier has a QR code on their uniform or ID card that, when scanned, quickly shows their basic medical information like blood type, allergies, and specific diseases. This way, the medic knows who they’re dealing with from the very first moment. (Participant 4)

Every soldier has a QR code on their uniform or ID card that, when scanned, quickly shows their basic medical information like blood type, allergies, and specific diseases. This way, the medic knows who they’re dealing with from the very first moment. (Participant 4)

For telehealth to really work on the battlefield, the applications and systems must be offline-first. They should store simple information like vital signs, wound photos, or location, and then synchronize the information when a connection is made. (Participant 5)

Establishing integrated governance and standardized process architecture

Participants emphasized that the technological implementation of military telehealth must be underpinned by a robust, yet flexible, governance architecture. Rather than relying solely on technological solutions, success depends on an integrated command-and-control structure capable of formulating cohesive, overarching policies. However, centralized governance must not equate to rigid, uniform execution; participants unanimously rejected “one-size-fits-all” approaches. Operational models must be inherently native and mission-oriented, adapting to drastically different geographic constraints, combat doctrines, and localized threats. Within these highly tailored environments, clinical execution demands standardized, ultra-simplified, and rapid medical protocols that prevent confusion and cognitive overload during crises. Finally, to operationalize these protocols safely, a transparent legal framework is mandatory. Clear regulations must delineate clinical responsibilities, define authority limits, and establish accountability for data privacy and potential medical errors, thereby legally protecting frontline personnel. These multifaceted governance requirements—ranging from macro-level command to micro-level legal protection—were distinctly highlighted by the participants:

For telemedicine to succeed on the battlefield, an integrated management system must be created that is responsible for defining protocols, overseeing implementation, and coordinating between different units. (Participant 3)

Cyber defense shield: securing vital data

The integrity of a military telehealth system relies on a multi-layered “Cyber Defense Shield” designed to balance rigorous security with rapid clinical accessibility. Participants argued that data protection in combat zones extends beyond standard patient privacy to operational security; a breach could compromise mission secrecy by revealing troop locations or unit readiness. Consequently, the consensus dictated a three-tiered security architecture: (1) End-to-end encryption to secure data in transit against enemy interception; (2) Strict authentication protocols to ensure only authorized medical personnel can access sensitive information; and (3) A “remote kill switch” capability. This final layer addresses the unique risk of physical capture in the battlefield, allowing command centers to irreversibly wipe data from devices lost to the enemy. These critical security layers—protecting data at rest, in transit, and upon device compromise—were highlighted by the participants:

If I were to explain it simply and cohesively, the most important security consideration for telehealth on the battlefield is to ensure patient information is completely protected, while at the same time, authorized medical personnel can access it quickly. Therefore, communications must have strong encryption. (Participant 5)

Empowering the human element: from digital literacy to operational proficiency

The successful deployment of military telehealth is fundamentally predicated on the human element, shifting the focus from technological sophistication to pedagogical institutionalization. Participants argued that telehealth proficiency must not be treated as an elective skill but integrated into the very fabric of initial military service training. This training must transcend basic device operation, encompassing a multi-disciplinary curriculum that includes advanced clinical procedures, technical troubleshooting, and cybersecurity awareness. However, participants noted a critical gap between classroom theory and battlefield reality; they advocated for high-fidelity, stress-inoculated simulations—such as “blackout drills” and mass casualty maneuvers—to expose systemic vulnerabilities. Ultimately, the goal of this training is to foster “resilient autonomy.” While medics must be experts in using the system, they must also be trained to resist technological dependency, ensuring they remain capable of decisive, independent medical action when systems inevitably fail or are compromised. The urgency of this human-centric readiness was underscored by participants and observed during field sessions:

We must simulate all possible scenarios by conducting combined and simulated exercises: from communication blackouts and cyber-attacks to adverse weather conditions and mass casualties. This is where weaknesses reveal themselves, not on paper. (Participant 9)

Redefining organizational culture: from a desirable option to a strategic imperative

Interview analysis indicates that the most critical barrier to telehealth adoption is not technological, but cultural. Participants stressed the urgent need to transition telehealth from a demonstrative phase—often perceived by macro-level management as merely a “fancy or showcase project” confined to exhibitions and conferences (Participants 3 and 4)—into a recognized strategic imperative for future warfare. To operationalize these systems in real-world settings, it is essential to foster strategic acceptance among operational and military commanders. This requires a fundamental shift in narrative: telehealth must be presented not merely as a clinical service, but as a vital force multiplier that protects combat strength. Achieving this paradigm shift depends on finding a conceptual bridge with operational leaders to demonstrate how robust medical support directly enhances mission success, a point vividly captured by one participant:

A field commander’s primary concern is advancing and securing territory. Medical issues aren’t their primary concern until they have casualties to treat. We have to find a common language with them. We need to explain that this system helps them conduct operations more confidently, knowing they have robust and rapid medical support, which ultimately helps them achieve their mission. (Participant 8)

Integrated architecture for a smart chain of care: integrating data, expertise, and operations

The envisioned telehealth architecture must function as an uninterrupted continuum of data, expertise, and operations, rather than a collection of isolated tools. Participants conceptualized this architecture as a continuous loop starting at the point of injury. They advocated for passive, continuous monitoring using wearable sensors integrated directly into combat gear, capable of autonomously transmitting vital data and alerts to rear echelons without burdening the combat medic. This real-time data flow is critical not only for immediate tactical guidance but also for destination preparedness. By transmitting physiological and procedural data ahead of the casualty’s arrival, destination facilities can eliminate critical diagnostic delays and initiate definitive care immediately upon the patient’s arrival (Participant 7). Orchestrating this seamless flow requires a paradigm shift in the support center’s structure. Rather than a traditional medical desk, participants described an integrated command-and-control hub where clinical experts, IT support, and cybersecurity teams operate in absolute synchrony to prevent system paralysis (Participant 8). The operational necessity of this autonomous, edge-level data generation was vividly emphasized by one participant:

Instead of a smartwatch that counts steps, we need sensors integrated with the uniform or body armor that automatically monitor vital signs. These sensors could detect a drop in blood pressure or even exposure to a chemical agent and automatically alert the command center. (Participant 9)

Value-creating dimensions of the system: integrating

The core value proposition of the integrated telehealth ecosystem lies in its capacity to fundamentally restructure battlefield casualty care across three interconnected dimensions. First and most critically, telehealth enhances the safety of highly specialized medical personnel by enabling the transfer of expertise directly into the conflict zone, negating the need to deploy experts into high-risk areas. Second, this remote guidance directly maximizes survival rates during the crucial “golden hour.” By bringing virtual trauma specialists to the casualty’s side, frontline medics can perform advanced life-saving procedures, preventing fatalities from otherwise survivable injuries caused by geographical or logistical barriers (Participant 4). Finally, the strategic integration of this data dramatically improves the speed, accuracy, and quality of operational decision-making. Real-time awareness of casualty severity and location (e.g., exact coordinates of ‘Code Red’ cases) transforms resource deployment—such as helicopter dispatch and destination hospital preparation—from blind, reactive measures into precise, calculated management tasks (Participant 6). The primary strategic benefit—safeguarding specialists while extending their life-saving reach—was vividly articulated by one participant:

With telehealth, instead of sending a specialist under enemy fire, we can easily send their expertise there. In places where we used telehealth, our specialist was practically kilometers away from the hot zone, and we were able to apply that expertise through the medic or corpsman operating in that dangerous area. (Participant 1)

Discussion

Principal findings

This study was conducted to elucidate the challenges and requirements of deploying telehealth for military medical aid and battlefield evacuation. Our qualitative analysis culminated in a conceptual model (Fig. 2) structured around two principal categories of findings: “Disruption in the Care and Evacuation Chain: Operational and Structural Vulnerabilities” and “Designing a Resilient Military Health Ecosystem: Requirements and Vision.” Our findings demonstrate that despite the high clinical potential of telehealth, its implementation is hindered by complex infrastructural, security, and cultural barriers. Overcoming these barriers requires a shift from viewing telehealth as a mere technological add-on to adopting a systemic, process-oriented redesign of military medical strategies. This Discussion is organized accordingly, first interpreting the identified vulnerabilities in light of existing literature, and then exploring the proposed requirements for a resilient system. We will subsequently address the theoretical and practical implications stemming from these findings.

Disruption in the care and evacuation chain: operational and structural vulnerabilities

Fragility of Communication Infrastructure: A primary catalyst for telehealth failure in combat zones is the inherent unreliability of communication lines. Relying on standard national internet and conventional infrastructure under unstable conditions inevitably leads to operational breakdown. This corroborates Haimi [1], who identified the disruption of communication and energy infrastructure as a principal damage of war. The crippling effect of limited bandwidth further complicates deployment, extending the practical limitations of high-bandwidth dependency noted by DeSoucy et al. [18] into the military context. Consequently, designing systems contingent upon high bandwidth is a fundamental structural flaw—a potential weakness also highlighted in the U.S. military’s telehealth systems by Hoyt et al. [3].

Technological and Equipment Inefficiency: The failure of commercial medical devices under battlefield duress presents another critical gap. Our data provides empirical support for Babcock et al. [19] demonstrating that standard equipment frequently malfunctions under the intense vibrations and high acceleration of combat transport. This underscores that civilian healthcare hardware is fundamentally ill-suited for the demanding conditions of warfare.

Governance and Legal Ambiguities: The operational paralysis caused by absent protocols, ambiguous medical liabilities, and opaque legal processes strongly resonates with the obstacles described by Haimi [1]. This absence of unified policy means systems are often halted due to liability or privacy concerns, contextualizing the primary barriers identified by Panter et al. [20] and Cetin et al. [9] within the high-stakes chain of command, where such ambiguity urgently requires strict legal and ethical regulations.

Cultural Resistance and Skill Gaps: Persistent resistance and a lack of digital proficiency among medics align with well-established barriers documented by Gagnon et al. [21] and Kruse et al. [22]. Interestingly, while post-COVID-19 studies report decreased resistance and improved attitudes in civilian settings [23, 24], our findings contrast with this trend, highlighting enduring cultural challenges unique to the military environment. This fits within the broader explanatory framework of the NASSS model by Greenhalgh et al. [25], which cites entrenched habits and cultural unpreparedness as primary reasons for digital health failures.

The Security-Operational Paradox: Our findings reveal a fundamental tension: balancing the need for rapid clinical data access against stringent military security requirements. Apprehensions regarding data leakage and communication interception often lead to the abandonment of telehealth. This echoes Jalali et al. [26] and Kruse et al. [27], who noted that digital reliance without robust cybersecurity frameworks makes health data prime targets for attacks. Furthermore, this highlights the strategic risk emphasized by Linkov et al. [28] and Ahmed et al. [5] as the potential disclosure of force locations transforms health data into a vulnerability that alters operational equations.

Designing a resilient military health ecosystem: requirements and vision

Ensuring Communication Continuity. To directly mitigate these structural vulnerabilities, as illustrated in our conceptual model (Fig. 2), participants proposed ensuring communication continuity through a multi-layered network architecture. The system must seamlessly switch among platforms (satellite, radio, mesh networks, and drones) to maintain connectivity. This hybrid approach enhances resilience against threats, supporting the findings of Kuikka et al. [29] and aligning with modern transport diversity strategies (LEO, MEO, GEO) [30]. Furthermore, this vision operationalizes the updated Joint Trauma System (JTS) guidelines, which mandate a PACE (Primary, Alternate, Contingency, and Emergency) strategy [31]. Ultimately, architecture must be designed around the assumption of disconnection rather than constant connectivity, relying heavily on applications capable of operating under low-bandwidth conditions to avoid the strategic errors warned against by Pamplin et al. [32].

Developing Battlefield-Compatible Hardware. Overcoming technological gaps requires strict adherence to military standards. As Babcock et al. [19] argued, simply miniaturizing equipment is insufficient. System resilience demands lightweight, portable, and inherently rugged devices capable of withstanding extreme temperatures, dust, moisture, and impact [33], ensuring patient safety throughout the evacuation chain regardless of software superiority.

Workforce Empowerment and Cultural Redefinition. By triangulating participants’ lived experiences with our interviewer notes, our study strongly reinforces this evidence: without targeted investments in training and cognitive shifts in personnel mindsets, telehealth cannot achieve its operational potential, rendering infrastructural readiness futile. Empowering the workforce necessitates a departure from traditional, episodic training toward comprehensive integration of digital literacy and simulated disconnected decision-making into foundational doctrines, addressing the structured training gap identified by Madsen et al. [7]. Culturally, telehealth must transition from an “option” to a “strategic necessity” in the minds of commanders, a shift that Haleem et al. [34] identify as the most critical step in change management, requiring integration into core processes beyond mere demonstration pilots.

Cybersecurity Shield and Intelligent Automation. To resolve the security paradox, robust measures including end-to-end encryption, multi-layered access control, and remote data wiping are imperative, corroborating the security priorities of Ahmed et al. [5] and Hoyt et al. [3]. Furthermore, managing cognitive load and supporting human decision-making under extreme pressure necessitates transitioning from isolated manual systems to integrated, intelligent architectures. However, from a resilience engineering perspective, it must be recognized that such sophisticated technologies do not eliminate human error; rather, they introduce new systemic complexities, adaptations, and vulnerabilities. Incorporating AI for mass casualty triage and utilizing automated data flow via wearable sensors and barcode scanners is essential, echoing the transformative vision of modern combat medicine articulated by Ventura et al. [35] and Haleem et al. [34].

Theoretical Implications

This study advances the theoretical discourse on military digital health by challenging the prevailing techno-centric paradigm, which often treats telehealth as an isolated technological overlay. Instead, we propose conceptualizing the military health ecosystem through a socio-technical systems lens, where technology, human cognition, and rigid hierarchical structures are deeply intertwined. By mapping telehealth vulnerabilities directly onto the “care and evacuation chain,” this research extends traditional models of medical logistics. It provides a novel theoretical framework that shifts the focus from simple “technology adoption” to “systemic resilience,” arguing that in austere environments, digital health interventions must be theorized as complex adaptive components rather than plug-and-play tools.

Practical and design implications

For military planners, policymakers, and technology developers, these findings offer actionable pathways for designing resilient telehealth ecosystems. From a design perspective, our results dictate a fundamental shift away from commercial, high-bandwidth-dependent platforms toward systems specifically engineered for Disconnected, Intermittent, and Limited (DIL) bandwidth environments. This includes prioritizing asynchronous communication architectures and interoperable, low-resource nodes. Practically, the study highlights the urgent need to overhaul military medical governance by establishing unified command structures dedicated to digital health. Furthermore, operational readiness requires embedding simulated telehealth decision-making into standard field exercises, ensuring that digital triage and remote consultation become reflexive capabilities for combat medics rather than emergency workarounds.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that successfully implementing military telehealth requires far more than merely adopting commercial digital tools; it demands a strategic paradigm shift from connectivity-dependent systems to intelligent, resilient, and offline-first ecosystems. The findings confirm that conventional urban telehealth models are insufficiently effective due to infrastructure instability and security threats posed by warfare. We conclude that the future architecture of military telehealth must rest on three fundamental pillars: [1] Infrastructural Resilience, driven by multi-layered networks and ruggedized, grid-independent hardware; [2] Operational Intelligence, leveraging artificial intelligence for automated triage and opportunistic data synchronization; and [3] Impenetrable Cybersecurity. Ultimately, operationalizing this technology hinges on establishing unified governance and revolutionizing the military organizational culture—transforming telehealth from a supplementary option into a strategic, lifesaving imperative during the golden hour.

Study limitations

This study faced certain limitations. The sensitive nature of the topic made it difficult to reach some military participants, which was addressed primarily through snowball sampling. Additionally, given the qualitative nature of the study, the generalizability of the results should be carefully considered, taking into account the specific context and setting of each military force. Furthermore, while our findings provide in-depth insights into the socio-technical complexities of battlefield telehealth, the relatively small sample size (n = 10) limits the strength of this study in definitively confirming or contrasting with previous large-scale or quantitative studies. Although our discussion connects extensively with existing literature to contextualize the findings, these connections should be viewed as exploratory. The primary value of our study lies in uncovering nuanced, lived experiences and generating specific design requirements in a highly restricted setting, rather than providing broad statistical validation.
