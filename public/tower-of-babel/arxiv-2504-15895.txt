Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning Models
Chenxu Yang, Qingyi Si, Yongjie Duan, Zheliang Zhu, Chenyu Zhu, Qiaowei Li, Zheng Lin , Li Cao, Weiping Wang

Abstract
Recent advances in large reasoning language models (LRLMs) rely on test-time scaling, which extends long chain-of-thought (CoT) generation to solve complex tasks. However, overthinking in long CoT not only slows down the efficiency of problem solving, but also risks accuracy loss due to the extremely detailed or redundant reasoning steps. We propose a simple yet effective method that allows LLMs to self-truncate CoT sequences by early exit during generation. Instead of relying on fixed heuristics, the proposed method monitors model behavior at potential reasoning transition points (e.g.,"Wait" tokens) and dynamically terminates the next reasoning chain’s generation when the model exhibits high confidence in a trial answer. Our method requires no additional training and can be seamlessly integrated into existing o1-like reasoning LLMs. Experiments on 10 reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH-500, AMC, GPQA, AIME and LiveCodeBench) show that the proposed method is consistently effective on 11 cutting-edge reasoning LLMs of varying series and sizes, reducing the length of CoT sequences by an average of 19.1% to 80.1% while improving accuracy by 0.3% to 5.0%. 1 1 1 The code is available at https://github.com/iie-ycx/DEER. .

Motivations and Observations

In this section, we analyze the overthinking problem in LRLMs and explore the impact of static early exits on model performance.
We define the critical point where the reasoning information becomes just sufficient as the pearl reasoning. Fig.

Additionally, we observed that there are some samples for which the correct answer can only be obtained through early exits (e.g., Questions 11, 19 and 26 in Fig.

These observations validate our motivation that early exiting at the pearl reasoning position and directly outputting conclusion improves accuracy while reducing computational costs.
The quantitative results in Fig.

3
Method

3.1
Preliminaries

The Generation Pattern of Large Reasoning Language Models.

In contrast to traditional large language models (
System 1
), large reasoning language models (
System 2
)
[
23
]
exhibit distinct generation patterns during the inference stage. (1) LRLMs use delimiters to divide the output into two processes: slow thinking and conclusion. LRLMs conduct systematic and thorough reasoning in the slow thinking, ultimately summarizing the thought process and providing the final answer in the conclusion. (2) During the slow thinking process, LRLMs engage in complex thinking actions, such as Problem Restatement & Comprehension, Approach Exploration, and Result Verification
[
24
]
. We refer to each thinking action as a thinking chunk, and the transitions between these chunks are often marked by
action transition points
, including
"Wait"
,
"Alternatively"
, and
"Hmm"
.

\textit{System 1:}\;[\text{Prompt}]+[\text{Completion}],

\textit{System 2:}\;[\text{Prompt}]\,+\langle\text{think}\rangle+\,[\text{Slow Thinking}]\,+\langle\text{/think}\rangle+\,[\text{Conclusion}],

where
\langle\text{think}\rangle
and
\langle\text{/think}\rangle
are begin-of-thinking and end-of-thinking delimiters respectively.

Budget Forcing.

The budget forcing method proposed by
Muennighoff et al. 2025
cleverly leverages the unique generation patterns in LRLMs to control test-time computation, thus achieving performance improvement without additional training or modules. They proposed a simple decoding-time intervention by forcing a maximum and minimum number of slow-thinking tokens at test time. Specifically, upon reaching the maximum token count, they appended the end-of-thinking token delimiter along with
"Final Answer:"
to early exit the thinking stage. To enforce a minimum, they suppressed the generation of the end-of-thinking token delimiter and appended action transition points to the LRLM’s ongoing thought process, thus encouraging the model to double-check its answer or attempt new reasoning approaches. However, the budget forcing method they proposed is static, leaving significant room for improvement. We further explored the unique generation patterns in LRLMs and introduced a dynamic budget forcing method to enable early exits.

3.2
Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning

In this section, we introduce the Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning (DEER) method to determine optimal positions for early exits (pearl reasoning path), thereby alleviating the overthinking issue.

The core idea behind DEER is that a model’s confidence in its trial answer dynamically indicates whether the thinking information required for LRLMs to generate the final answer is sufficient. We observe that when the model’s reasoning process is incomplete or flawed, the trial answer tends to exhibit significantly lower confidence. Conversely, when the reasoning is comprehensive and logically sound, the model generates answers with higher confidence, as illustrated in Fig.
14
. This suggests that while the model’s parameter space inherently encodes an implicit awareness of the occurrence of the Pearl Reasoning, the oversight of dynamically varying lengths of reasoning chains during training prevents it from explicitly terminating reasoning early. Our method addresses this limitation by explicitly activating this implicit reasoning-awareness to enable dynamic early exits.

Figure 3
:
An overview of the Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning (DEER) method.

As shown in Fig.
3
, DEER involves three designs to determine whether to exit early: reasoning transition monitor, answer inducer, and confidence evaluator.
As mentioned in Section
3.1
, LRLM incorporates action transition points between thinking chunks, which DEER regards as potential early exit points. The reasoning transition monitor is designed to track the emergence of these points, preparing for the identification of optimal early exit opportunities. When the LRLM pauses at a potential early exit point, the trial answer inducer module prompts the model to generate an intermediate answer based on the reasoning content produced so far. We incorporated the answer delimiters (
\boxed
{}) into the prompt to facilitate a more precise identification of the trial answers, as follows:
\bm{A}=\text{LRLM}(\bm{P},\bm{T},\bm{I})

where
\bm{P}
denotes the input prompt,
\bm{T}
denotes the generated thoughts,
\bm{I}
denotes the answer inducer prompt, and
\bm{A}=[a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]
is the trial answer.

The confidence evaluator module computes the confidence of the induced trial answer.
We take the maximum predicted probability of each token as its confidence. For multi-token trial answers, the overall confidence is computed as the mean confidence across all constituent tokens as follows:

p(a_{t})=\text{softmax}(\mathcal{M}(\bm{P},\bm{T},\bm{I},\bm{a_{<t}})),\quad\mathcal{C}=\left({\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max_{a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}}p(a_{t})}\right)^{1/n}

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the LM Head of the LRLM and its preceding components, with logits as the output.

Finally, the comparison between the obtained confidence and the empirical threshold
\lambda
determines whether to exit early. If
\mathcal{C}>\lambda
, we consider the reasoning information currently generated by the LRLM to be sufficient, indicating that the model has reached the Pearl Reasoning. At this point, DEER stops further reasoning actions and proceeds to deliver the conclusion. Otherwise, the model reverts to the previous transition point to generate the next thinking chunk.

3.3
Branch-Parallel Decoding Acceleration

Intuitively, the computation of
Answer Inducer
and
Confidence Evaluator
in DEER introduces additional latency during inference, especially for the code generation task where the trial answers are still long. This reduces the efficiency gains we’ve earned by greatly shortening the generated CoT sequences. To solve this problem, we integrate DEER with a branch-parallel acceleration strategy (Fig.
11
) to further address these efficiency limitations: (1) Multiple branches are linearized into a single sequence and generated in parallel using a special causal attention mask; (2) Dynamic KV cache management through confidence-based pruning. This strategy enables temporal overlap between the trial answer evaluation and the ongoing reasoning-chain generation, thus optimizing overall inference efficiency.

6
Conclusion

This paper verifies the rationale behind the early exit motivation in CoT generation, and accordingly
proposes a training-free dynamic early exit algorithm, which makes the reasoning model withdraw from subsequent thinking when the thinking amount is just enough. Our method comprehensively evaluated across reasoning models of varying model sizes and demonstrates superior performance with fewer tokens on ten classical reasoning benchmarks (including math, science, and programming tasks), which offers a win-win solution to the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency commonly encountered in test-time scaling.

Limitations

Our proposed dynamic early-exit framework exhibits three key limitations:

Fundamental Assumption Dependency

The method specifically addresses overthinking in LRLMs. Should future post-training techniques achieve strictly progressive and non-redundant reasoning paths (e.g., RL with thought processes reward), our approach would become obsolete. However, empirical evidence from the most advanced Qwen3 confirms persistent overthinking, suggesting our method will remain practically valuable during the transitional phase of reasoning model development.

Language-Specific Reasoning Transition Tokens

For English scenarios, this paper identifies and uses specific words ("wait", "alternatively") as the reasoning transition tokens for early-exit decisions. For other languages, equivalent linguistic markers must be identified separately. However, regardless of language, reasoning models inherently produce such transition tokens during generation.

Model-Specific Threshold Sensitivity

For certain reasoning models (e.g., Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1
[
65
]
) that exhibit consistently low confidence during generation, we correspondingly lower the threshold in early-exit conditions. However, mainstream open-source models such as Deepseek-distill and Qwen3 all demonstrate comfortably similar threshold ranges.

Ethical Considerations

The proposed early-exit inference framework DEER operates strictly on frozen pre-trained language models without parameter modification or additional training, which introduces no new data bias or privacy concern, and preserves the original model’s safety alignment. Our experiments are conducted on standard academic benchmarks under Apache 2.0/MIT licenses. DEER significantly reduces average token generation compared to baselines, inherently lowering computational costs. The authors conclude no ethical risks beyond those inherent in the base LLMs, with the environmental benefits of reduced energy consumption constituting a positive societal impact.

Appendix C
More Experiment Setup

Metrics.

The goal of DEER is to maintain the correctness performance of LRLMs while avoiding the redundant token overhead caused by overthinking. To this end, we selected
Accuracy
(ACC) and
Generation Length
(LEN) as the evaluation metrics.
Accuracy
(ACC) is calculated as follows:
\textit{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{LRLM}({x}_{i}))=y_{i}\}
, where
x_{i}
is the question and
y_{i}
is the ground-truth answer from the dataset.
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
extracts the answer from the LRLM’s response.
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
is an indicator function that determines whether the inside given condition is valid. The accuracy evaluation is based on the evaluation framework publicly released by
Ye et al. 2025
(LIMO). Intuitively, the longer the generated text, the greater the inference cost for LRLMs. Therefore, we calculate the average generation tokens per sample to evaluate the cost as follows:
\textit{Generation Length}(LEN)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\mathcal{LRLM}({x}_{i})|
, where
|\cdot|
measures the number of generated tokens. For the two programming benchmarks, we use the Pass@1 metric to measure generated code correctness.

Implementation details.

All evaluations are conducted in a Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) setting with the following prompt:
"Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed
{}
."

For the decoding strategy, we employ greedy decoding with a single sample for the correctness evaluation. The ground-truth answers to the evaluation problems in our experiments are all well-structured numerical values or options. Therefore, we apply rule-based evaluations directly to verify mathematical equivalence. We set the maximum generation length at 16,384 to ensure that the evaluation captures complete problem-solving attempts.
