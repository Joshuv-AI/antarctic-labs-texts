Abstract
. Although the λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -calculus is a natural fragment of the λ \lambda -calculus, obtained by forbidding the erasure of arguments, its equational theories did not receive much attention. The reason is that all proper denotational models studied in the literature equate all non-normalizable λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -terms, whence the associated theory is not very informative. The goal of this paper is to introduce a previously unknown theory of the λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -calculus, induced by a notion of evaluation trees that we call “Ohana trees” . The Ohana tree of a λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -term is an annotated version of its Böhm tree, remembering all free variables that are hidden within its meaningless subtrees, or pushed into infinity along its infinite branches. We develop the associated theories of program approximation: the first approach — more classic — is based on finite trees and continuity, the second adapts Ehrhard and Regnier’s Taylor expansion. We then prove a Commutation Theorem stating that the normal form of the Taylor expansion of a λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -term coincides with the Taylor expansion of its Ohana tree. As a corollary, we obtain that the equality induced by Ohana trees is compatible with abstraction and application. Subsequently, we introduce a denotational model designed to capture the equality induced by Ohana trees. Although presented as a non-idempotent type system, our model is based on a suitably modified version of the relational semantics of the λ \lambda -calculus, which is known to yield proper models of the λ ​ 𝖨 \lambda\mathsf{I} -calculus when restricted to non-empty finite multisets. To track variables occurring in subterms that are hidden or pushed to infinity in the evaluation trees, we generalize the system in two ways: first, we reintroduce annotated versions of the empty multiset indexed by sets of variables; second, we introduce a separate environment to account for the free variables present in these subterms. We show that this model retains the standard quantitative properties of relational semantics and that the induced theory precisely captures Ohana trees. We conclude by discussing the cases of Lévy-Longo and Berarducci trees, and possible generalizations to the full λ \lambda -calculus.

1.
Introduction

In the pioneering article

“The calculi of lambda-conversion”

[
Church41
]

Alonzo Church introduced the
\lambda
-calculus
together with its fragment without weakening,
called the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, where each abstraction
must bind
at least
one occurrence of a variable.
Historically, the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus has proven to be a useful framework for establishing results such as the finiteness of developments and standardization, which were successfully proven for the full
\lambda
-calculus only decades later.
Despite that, in the last forty years there have been
no groundbreaking advances in its study.

The study of models and theories of
\lambda
-calculus flourished in the 1970s
[
Bare
]
and remained central in theoretical computer science for decades
[
BarendregtM22
]
.
Although the equational theories of
\lambda
-calculus, called
\lambda
-theories
, form a complete lattice of cardinality
2^{\aleph_{0}}

[
LusinS04
]
, only a handful are of interest to computer scientists.
Most articles focus on
\lambda
-theories generated through some notion of
“evaluation tree”
of a
\lambda
-term, which means that two
\lambda
-terms are equated in the theory exactly when their evaluation trees coincide.
The definitions of evaluation trees present in the literature follow the same pattern: they collect in a possibly infinite tree all stable portions of the output coming out from the computation, and replace the subterms that are considered
meaningless
by a constant
\bot
, representing the lack of information.
By modifying the notions of
“stable”
and
“meaningless”
,
one obtains Böhm trees
[
Barendregt77
]
,
possibly endowed with some form of
extensionality
[
Nakajima.75
,
SeveriV17
,
IntrigilaMP19
]
,
Lévy-Longo trees
[
Levy76
,
Longo83
]

and Berarducci trees
[
Berarducci96
]
.
In the modern language of infinitary term rewriting systems, these trees can be seen as the transfinite normal forms of infinitary
\lambda
-calculi
[
EndrullisHK12
,
Czajka20
]
.
Another popular method for defining
\lambda
-theories is via observational equivalences
[
MorrisTh
]
: two
\lambda
-terms are equivalent if they display the same behaviour whenever plugged in any context.
For instance, the theory of Scott’s
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
model
[
Scott72
]
captures the maximal consistent observational equivalence
[
Hyland75
,
Wadsworth76
]
.

Since the
\lambda
-calculus is an equationally conservative extension of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, every theory of the former is also a theory of the latter, namely a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory
, while the converse does not hold.
A typical example is the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}
generated by equating all
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms that are not
\beta
-normalizing. The maximal consistent observational equivalence
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\eta}
is similar, except for the fact that it is extensional.
Note that equating all
\lambda
-terms without a
\beta
-normal form gives rise
to an inconsistent
\lambda
-theory, but in the context of
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus it is
consistent and arises naturally: all
“proper”
denotational models of
the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus studied so far induce either
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}
or

\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\eta}

[
HonsellL93
]
.
This is somewhat disappointing, since models and theories are most valuable when they capture non-trivial operational properties of programs, and normalization is relatively elementary in this setting.
In this paper we introduce a new
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory, induced
by a notion of evaluation trees inspired by Böhm trees, but explicitly
designed for
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms.

Böhm trees and Taylor expansion for the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus

An important feature of Böhm trees is that, because of their coinductive nature, they are capable of pushing some subterms into infinity.
Consider for instance a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term
M_{xf}
containing free variables
x,f
and satisfying

M_{xf}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}f(M_{xf})\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}f(f(M_{xf}))\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}f(f(f(M_{xf})))\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}f^{n}(M_{xf})\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}\cdots

then the Böhm tree of
M_{xf}
is the limit of this infinite
sequence, namely
f^{\omega}=f(f(f(\cdots)))
.
Observe that
x
is
“persistent”
in the sense that it is never
erased along any finite reduction, but disappears in the limit.
We say that
x
is
pushed into infinity
or
forgotten

in the Böhm tree of
M_{xf}
.
To some extent, also the variable
f
is pushed into infinity, but it occurs infinitely often in
f^{\omega}
.
A variable
x
can also disappear because it is hidden behind a meaningless term like
\mathsf{\Omega}=(\lambda y.yy)(\lambda y.yy)
.
For instance, the Böhm tree of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term
N=\mathsf{\Omega}x
is just
\bot
and we say that
x
is
left behind
.
As a consequence there are
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms, like
\lambda xf.M_{xf}
and
\lambda xy.y(\mathsf{\Omega}x)
, whose Böhm tree is not a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-tree
[
Bare
, Def. 10.1.26]
since the variable
x
is abstracted, but does not appear in the tree.
This shows that Böhm trees are not well-suited for the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, and the question of whether other notions of trees can model
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms in a more faithful way naturally arises.

In the first part of this paper (
Section

3
)
we introduce a notion of Böhm trees keeping tracks
of the variables persistently occurring along each possibly infinite path,
or left behind a meaningless subterm.
We call them
Ohana trees
as they remember all the variables present
in the terms generating them, even if these variables are never actually
used in their evaluations:
no variable is
left behind
or
forgotten
.
This is obtained by simply annotating such variables on the branches of the Böhm tree, and on its
\bot
-nodes, but bares interesting consequences: since Ohana trees are invariant under
\beta
-conversion, showing that two terms have distinct Ohana trees becomes a way of separating them modulo
=_{\beta}
.
Once the coinductive definition of Ohana trees is given
(
Section

3.1
), we develop the corresponding theory
of continuous program approximation based on finite trees.
Perhaps surprisingly, in the finite approximants, the only variable annotations that are actually required are those on the constant
\bot
, as all other labels can be reconstructed when taking their supremum.
Our main result in this setting is the Continuous Approximation Theorem
(
Theorem

9
) stating that the Ohana tree of a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term is
uniquely determined by the (directed) set of all its finite approximants.

Inspired by quantitative semantics of linear logics, Ehrhard and Regnier
proposed a theory of linear program approximation for regular
\lambda
-terms
based on Taylor expansion
[
EhrhardR03
,
Ehrhard.Reg.08
]
.
A
\lambda
-term is approximated by a possibly infinite power-series of programs living in a completely linear
resource calculus
, where no resource can be duplicated or erased along the computation.
This theory is linked to Böhm trees by a Commutation Theorem
[
Ehrhard.Reg.06
]
stating that the normal form of the Taylor expansion of a
\lambda
-term is equal to the Taylor expansion of its Böhm tree.

The second part of our paper (
Sections

4
and
5
)
is devoted to explore the question of whether it is possible
to define a Taylor expansion for the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus
capturing the Ohana tree equality.
In
Section

4
we design a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-resource calculus with memory,
mixing linear and non-linear features.
Our terms are either applied to non-empty
bags
(finite multisets) of
non-duplicable resources, or to an empty bag
1_{X}
annotated with a set
X

of variables (the non-linear parts of the terms).
The reduction of a resource term
t
preserves its free variables
\mathrm{fv}(t)
: if the reduction is valid then
t
consumes all its linear resources and each variable is recorded in some labels, otherwise an exception is thrown and
t
reduces to an empty program
\mathbf{0}_{X}
, where
X=\mathrm{fv}(t)
.

First we prove that the resource calculus so-obtained is confluent and strongly normalizing, then we use it as the target language of a Taylor expansion with memory, capable of approximating both
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms and Ohana trees.
Our main result is that the Commutation Theorem from
[
Ehrhard.Reg.06
]

extends to this setting: the normal form of the Taylor expansion with memory
of a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term always exists and is equal to the Taylor expansion with
memory of its Ohana tree (
Theorem

19
).
As consequences (
Corollaries

25
and
26
),
we obtain that:

(1)

The equality induced on
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms by Ohana trees coincides with the equality induced by the normalized Taylor expansion with memory;

(2)

The equality induced by Ohana trees is compatible with application and abstraction, in the sense of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, and it is therefore a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory.

Denotational semantics of Ohana trees

In the last part of this paper (
Section

6
),
we construct a denotational model of Ohana trees
based on the relational semantics of linear logic.
As is common, we present this model as
a multi-type (or non-idempotent intersection type) system,
where the interpretation of a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term is given
by the set of its typing judgments.
Finite multisets
[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]

occur on the left-hand side of an arrow type:

\Gamma\vdash M:[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]\multimap\beta

holds if
M
needs to use its argument
n
times
to produce an output of type
\beta
.
In our setting, we also have
\Gamma\vdash\lambda x.M:[]_{\vec{y}}\multimap\beta
, expressing that
M
may receive an argument whose free variables are
\vec{y}
, but will not use it to produce the output; instead, the argument is hidden or pushed into infinity.
A distinctive feature of our system is the introduction of a separate environment
\Delta
, as in
\Gamma;\Delta\vdash M:[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]\multimap\beta
, which ensures proper
\alpha
-conversion by tracking the correspondence between the variables
x
occurring in
M
and the variables
\vec{y}
of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms that are semantically substituted for
x
.
We show that this type system satisfies quantitative versions of subject reduction and subject expansion, and that it validates our Taylor expansion (
).
As a corollary of the Commutation Theorem, two
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms with the same Ohana tree share the same denotation, while the converse is established by inductive techniques.
Hence, the model captures exactly the equality induced by Ohana trees (
).

We consider this work a first step in a broader line of research, which we discuss further in the conclusion of the paper.
In particular, we aim to extend our formalism to the full
\lambda
-calculus and explore its applicability to other kinds of trees.

Related works

On the syntactic side, the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus can be translated
into MELL proof-nets without weakening
studied in
[
CarvalhoF12
,
CarvalhoF16
]
.
Our Ohana trees can be presented as labelled Böhm trees, and therefore share
similarities with Melliès’s (infinitary)
\lambda
-terms
“with
boundaries”
, whose branches are annotated by booleans
[
Mellies17
]
.
However, our labels are describing additional points occurring at transfinite positions, a phenomenon reminiscent of the non-monotonic pre-fixed point construction defined in
[
Berardid17a
]
.
They can also be seen as an instance of the transfinite terms considered in
[
KetemaetAl09
]
, but simpler, because no reduction is performed at transfinite positions.
Thus, Ohana trees should correspond to normal forms of an infinitary labelled
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, just like Böhm trees are the normal forms of the infinitary
\lambda
-calculus
[
KennawayKSV95
]
.
We wonder whether a clever translation of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus into the process calculus in
[
DufourM24
]
would allow us to retrieve the Ohana trees and the associated Taylor expansion, in which case our Commutation Theorem might become an instance of their general construction.

Concerning denotational semantics, certain
relevant
intersection type systems
[
Bakel92
,
Ghilezan96
,
AlvesF22
]
can be used to represent models of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, as they describe reflexive objects in the SMCC of cpos and strict functions
[
Jacobs93
]
. In
[
HonsellR92
]
, Honsell and Ronchi Della Rocca constructed a non-semi-sensible filter model that never equates a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term with a proper
\lambda
-term; however, the induced notion of equivalence does not appear to have an obvious tree-like representation. In
[
HonsellL93
]
, the authors show that the model introduced in
[
EgidiHR92
]
is fully abstract for the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\eta}
. The results in
[
CarvalhoF16
]
suggest that the relational semantics, endowed with the comonad of finite
non-empty
multisets, contains models whose theory is either
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}
or
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{I}}^{\eta}
. No relational graph model can induce the Ohana tree equality, since they equate all
\lambda
-terms having the same Böhm tree
[
PaoliniPR17
,
BreuvartMR18
]
. The relational model
\mathcal{E}
defined in
[
CarraroES10
]
, using the comonad of finite multisets with possibly infinite coefficients, appeared more promising because it separates
M_{xf}
from
M_{yf}
, and
\mathsf{\Omega}x
from
\mathsf{\Omega}y
when
x\neq y
; however, we will see that it still fails to capture the Ohana tree equality.

2.
Preliminaries

This section is devoted to recall some basic notions and results concerning the
\lambda
-calculus, and its fragment without weakening known as the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus.
However, we assume that the reader is already familiar with the rewriting theory of
\lambda
-calculus, its equational theories and its models based on Böhm trees.
For more details, we refer to Barendregt’s book
[
Bare
]
.

2.1.
The
\lambda
-calculus, in a nutshell

Let us fix an infinite set
\mathcal{V}
of variables. The set
\Lambda
of

\lambda
-terms
is defined by induction

\Lambda\quad\ni\quad M,N\mathrel{\Coloneqq}x\mid\lambda x.M\mid MN\quad(\text{for $x\in\mathcal{V}$})

We use parentheses when needed.
We assume that application associates on the left,
and has higher precedence than abstraction.

E.g.
,
\lambda x.\lambda y.\lambda z.xyz
stands for
\lambda x.(\lambda y.(\lambda z.(xy)z))
.
We write
\lambda x_{1}\ldots x_{n}.M
or even
\lambda\vec{x}.M

for
\lambda x_{1}.\ldots\lambda x_{n}.M
,
and
N^{n}(M)
for
N(N(\cdots N(M)\cdots))
, where
N
occurs
n
times.
In particular, when
n=0
, we get
\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{n}.M=N^{n}(M)=M
.

The set
\mathrm{fv}(M)
of
free variables of
M
is defined as usual, and we say that
M
is
closed
when
\mathrm{fv}(M)=\varnothing
.
Hereafter,
\lambda
-terms are considered modulo
\alpha
-conversion

(see
[
Bare
, §2.1]
).

{exa}

The
\lambda
-terms below are used throughout the paper to construct examples:

\mathsf{I}\coloneqq\lambda x.x,\quad\mathsf{K}\coloneqq\lambda xy.x,\quad\mathsf{F}\coloneqq\lambda xy.y,\quad\mathsf{B}\coloneqq\lambda fgx.f(g(x)),\quad\mathsf{D}\coloneqq\lambda x.xx,\quad\mathsf{\Omega}\coloneqq\mathsf{DD}.

The
\lambda
-term
\mathsf{I}
is the identity,
\mathsf{K,F}
are the projections,
\mathsf{B}
is the composition,
\mathsf{D}
is the diagonal and
\mathsf{\Omega}
a looping term.
The
pairing of
M,N
is encoded as
[M,N]\coloneqq\lambda x.xMN
, for
x
fresh.

{defi}

(1)

The
\beta
-reduction

\to_{\beta}
is generated by the rule

(\lambda x.M)N\to M[N/x]

where
(\lambda x.M)N
is called a
\beta
-redex
and
M[N/x]
stands for the
capture-free substitution
of
N
for all free occurrences of
x
in
M
.

(2)

We let
\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}
(resp.
=_{\beta}
) be the reflexive, transitive (and symmetric) closure of
\to_{\beta}
.

(3)

We say that
M

is in
\beta
-normal form (
\beta
-nf)
if
M
does not contain any
\beta
-redex.

(4)

We say that
M

has a
\beta
-nf
if
M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}N
, for some
N
in
\beta
-nf.
Since
\rightarrow_{\beta}
is confluent, such

must be unique.

Remark 1
.

Any
\lambda
-term
M
can be written in one of the following forms: either
M=\lambda\vec{x}.yM_{1}\cdots M_{k}
, in which case
M
is called a
head normal form
(hnf) and

its
head variable
; or
M=\lambda\vec{x}.(\lambda y.P)QM_{1}\cdots M_{k}
, where
(\lambda y.P)Q
is referred to as its
head redex
.

The
head reduction

\rightarrow_{h}
is the restriction of
\rightarrow_{\beta}
obtained by contracting the head redex.

{defi}

A
\lambda
-term
Y
is a
fixed-point combinator
(fpc) if it satisfies
Yx=_{\beta}x(Yx)
.

Notice that we do not require that fixed-point combinators are closed
\lambda
-terms.

{exa}

We recall Curry’s fixed-point combinator
\mathsf{Y}
and define, for all
l\in\mathcal{V}
,
a parametrized version of Turing’s fpc
\Theta_{l}
and Klop’s

\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}

]
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda f.\mathsf{D}_{f}\mathsf{D}_{f},

\displaystyle\text{ where }\mathsf{D}_{f}\coloneqq\lambda x.f(xx),

\displaystyle\Theta_{l}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{VV}l,

\displaystyle\text{ where }\mathsf{V}\coloneqq\lambda vlf.f(vvlf),

\displaystyle\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda e.\mathsf{BYB}el.

It is easy to check that
\mathsf{Y}
and
\Theta_{l}
are fpcs.
Regarding the

\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}

\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}x=_{\beta}\mathsf{BYB}xl=_{\beta}\mathsf{Y}(\mathsf{B}x)l=_{\beta}\mathsf{B}x(\mathsf{Y}(\mathsf{B}x))l=_{\beta}x(\mathsf{Y}(\mathsf{B}x)l)=_{\beta}x(\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}x).

\mathsf{O}_{x}=_{\beta}{[}\mathsf{\Omega}x,[\mathsf{\Omega},\mathsf{O}_{x}]]

\mathrm{BT}(\mathsf{E}_{x})=\mathrm{BT}(\mathsf{O}_{x})=[\bot,[\bot,[\bot,[\bot,[\,\cdots]]]]]\hfill\textrm{ (inline depiction of a tree)}

We start by defining the approximants with memory, corresponding to finite Ohana trees.

{defi}

(1)

3.3.
Digression: Ohana trees for the full
\lambda
-calculus.

It is natural to wonder whether it makes sense to
extend the definition of Ohana trees to arbitrary
\lambda
-terms.
The idea is to consider the set of

permanent free variables
of
M\in\Lambda
:

\mathrm{pfv}(M)\coloneqq\{x\mid\forall N\in\Lambda\,.\,M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}N\textrm{ implies }x\in\mathrm{fv}(N)\}

Intuitively,
\mathrm{pfv}(M)
is the set of free variables of
M
that are never
erased along any reduction.

{defi}

The
Ohana tree
of a
\lambda
-term
M
can be then defined by setting:

•

\mathrm{OT}(M)\coloneqq\lambda\vec{x}.y\cdot^{\mathrm{pfv}(M_{1})}\mathrm{OT}(M_{1})\cdots^{\mathrm{pfv}(M_{k})}\mathrm{OT}(M_{k})
,
if
M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{h}\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{n}.yM_{1}\cdots M_{k}
;

•

\mathrm{OT}(M)\coloneqq\bot_{\mathrm{pfv}(M)}
, otherwise.

For
M\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
the definition above is equivalent to

Section

3.1
, since
\mathrm{pfv}(M)=\mathrm{fv}(M)
.
For an arbitrary
M\in\Lambda
, one needs an oracle to compute
\mathrm{pfv}(M)
,
but an oracle is already needed to determine whether
M
has an hnf.

It is easy to check that the above notion of Ohana tree remains invariant by
\rightarrow_{\beta}
. The key property which is exploited to prove this result is that
\mathrm{pfv}(\lambda\vec{x}.yM_{1}\cdots M_{k})=\{y\}\cup\mathrm{pfv}(M_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathrm{pfv}(M_{k})
.
The main problem of
Section

3.3
is that the equality induced
on
\lambda
-terms is not a
\lambda
-theory because it is not compatible with
application.
The following counterexample exploits the fact that, in general,
\mathrm{pfv}(MN)\neq\mathrm{pfv}(M)\cup\mathrm{pfv}(N)
.
Define:

\mathsf{W}=\lambda xy.yxy,\qquad M=\lambda z.\mathsf{W}(zv_{1}v_{2})\mathsf{W},\qquad N=\lambda z.\mathsf{W}(zv_{2}v_{1})\mathsf{W},\qquad\textrm{ for }v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}.

We have
M\rightarrow_{h}\lambda z.(\lambda y.y(zv_{1}v_{2})y)\mathsf{W}\rightarrow_{h}M
and
N\rightarrow_{h}\lambda z.(\lambda y.y(zv_{2}v_{1})y)\mathsf{W}\rightarrow_{h}N
, whence
\mathrm{OT}(M)=\mathrm{OT}(N)=\bot_{\{v_{1},v_{2}\}}
.
By applying these terms to
\mathsf{K}
, we obtain
M\mathsf{K}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}\mathsf{W}v_{1}\mathsf{W}
and
N\mathsf{K}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\beta}\mathsf{W}v_{2}\mathsf{W}
, therefore
\mathrm{OT}(M\mathsf{K})=\bot_{\{v_{1}\}}\neq\bot_{\{v_{2}\}}=\mathrm{OT}(N\mathsf{K})
.
We conclude that the equality induced on
\Lambda
is not a
\lambda
-theory, because it is not compatible with application.
This counterexample is particularly strong because it is independent from the fact that Ohana trees are generalizations of Böhm trees and not, say, Berarducci trees.
Indeed, the
M,N
constructed above have the same
Böhm tree, Lévy-Longo tree and Berarducci tree.

4.
A resource calculus with memory

We now introduce the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-resource calculus
refining the (finite) resource calculus
[
EhrhardR03
]
, best known as the target language of Ehrhard and Regnier’s Taylor expansion
[
Ehrhard.Reg.08
]
.
So, the resource calculus is not meant to be a stand-alone language, but rather another theory of approximations for the
\lambda
-calculus.
Before going further, we recall its main properties.

We consider here the promotion-free fragment of the resource calculus introduced in
[
PaganiT09
]
.
Its syntax is similar to the
\lambda
-calculus, except for the applications that are of shape
s\bar{t}
, where
\bar{t}=[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
is a multiset of resources called a
bag
.
The resources populating
\bar{t}
are linear as they cannot be erased or copied by
s
, they must be used
exactly once
along the reduction.
When contracting a term of the form
s=(\lambda x.s^{\prime})[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]
there are two possibilities.

(1)

If the number of occurrences of
x
in
s^{\prime}
is exactly
n
, then each occurrence is substituted by a different
t_{i}
.
Since the elements in the bag are unordered, there is no canonical bijection between the resources and the occurrences of
x
.
The solution consists in collecting all possibilities in a formal sum of terms, the sum representing an inner-choice operator.

(2)

If there is a mismatch between the number of occurrences and the amount of resources, then
s
reduces to the empty-sum,
\mathbf{0}
. From a programming-language perspective, this can be thought of as a program terminating abruptly after throwing an uncaught exception.

The first-class citizens of the resource calculus are therefore
finite sums
of resource terms, that are needed to ensure the (strong) confluence of reductions. Another important property is strong normalization, that follows from the fact that no resource can be duplicated.

4.3.
The operational semantics

We now equip resource expressions and resource sums with a notion of reduction, denoted
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
. We first define reduction on a single
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-resource term and then extend it to resource sums in the usual way. Note that contracting a single redex in a term
t\in\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)
already produces a finite sum of terms in
\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
, due to the way substitution is defined (
Lemma

11
).

{defi}

(1)

For each
X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}
, define the
resource reduction
as a relation between
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-resource terms and resource sums,
i.e.

\mathord{\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}}\subseteq\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)\times\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
:

(\lambda x.s)\bar{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}s\left\langle\bar{t}\middle/x\right\rangle\qquad\lambda x.s\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\lambda x.\mathbf{s}^{\prime}s\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\qquad s\bar{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\bar{t}s\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\qquad s\bar{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}s\bar{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}\bar{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\bar{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}\qquad[s]\cdot\bar{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}{[\mathbf{s}^{\prime}]}\cdot\bar{t}s\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}

(2)

We extend the reduction relation
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
to resource sums
\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]\times\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
, and simultaneously introduce its reflexive closure
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}
, as follows:

s+\mathbf{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\lx@proof@logical@and s\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\qquad\mathbf{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\qquad\mathbf{t}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}

(3)

We denote by
\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}
the reflexive and transitive closure of
the relation
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
given in
2
.

Observe that
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
is well-defined because
\mathrm{fv}((\lambda x.s)\bar{t})=\mathrm{fv}(s\left\langle\bar{t}\middle/x\right\rangle)
.
Indeed
\mathrm{fv}((\lambda x.s)\bar{t})=\mathrm{fv}(s)-\{x\}\cup\mathrm{fv}(\bar{t})

with
x\in\mathrm{fv}(s)
, whence
\mathrm{fv}(s\left\langle\bar{t}\middle/x\right\rangle)=\mathrm{fv}(s)\left\{\mathrm{fv}(\bar{t})\middle/x\right\}=\mathrm{fv}(s)-\{x\}\cup\mathrm{fv}(\bar{t})
.

{exa}

We use the resource
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-terms
\mathsf{I}
and
\mathsf{D}_{n}
from

Section

4.1
.

(1)

\mathsf{D}_{0}[z]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}(x1_{\{x\}})\langle[z]/x\rangle=(x\langle[z]/x\rangle)(1_{\{z\}})+(x\langle 1_{\{z\}}/x\rangle)(1_{\{x\}}\langle[z]/x\rangle)=z1_{\{z\}}+\mathbf{0}_{\{z\}}=z1_{\{z\}}
.
Similarly,
\mathsf{D}_{0}[\mathsf{I}]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{I}1_{\varnothing}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}_{\varnothing}
.

(2)

(\lambda x.\mathsf{D_{0}}[x])[z]
has two redexes. Contracting the outermost first gives
(\lambda x.\mathsf{D_{0}}[x])[z]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{D_{0}}[z]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}z1_{\{z\}}
.
Contracting the innermost
(\lambda x.\mathsf{D_{0}}[x])[z]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}(\lambda x.x1_{\{x\}})[z]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}z1_{\{z\}}
.

(3)

\mathsf{D}_{1}[\mathsf{I},\mathsf{I}]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{I}[\mathsf{I}]+\mathsf{I}[\mathsf{I}]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{I}[\mathsf{I}]\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{I}=2.\mathsf{I}

Thus, sums can arise from single terms.

We now show that
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
enjoys the properties of
strong normalization and strong confluence,
which are the key features of such a resource calculus.

Theorem 14
.

(1)

The reduction
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
is strongly normalizing.

(2)

\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}
is strongly confluent in the following sense:
for all
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]

such that
\mathbf{s}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{2}

there exists
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
such that

\mathbf{t}_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{t}_{2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{u}
.

\mathbf{s}_{\neq 2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 2}
.

There are two possible cases.

•

If
s_{1}=s_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}_{\neq 1}=\mathbf{s}_{\neq 2}
,
then by
Lemma

17
applied to

s_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1}
and
s_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
,
we obtain
\mathbf{t}_{1}
such that

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{1}
.
By the induction hypothesis on
\mathbf{s}_{\neq 1}
,
we obtain
\mathbf{t}_{\neq 1}
such that

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\neq 1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{\neq 1}

and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{\neq 1}
.
Therefore we can set
\mathbf{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{t}_{1}+\mathbf{t}_{\neq 1}
.

•

If
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
, then we can write

\mathbf{s}=s_{1}+s_{2}+\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12}
, with:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\neq 1}

\displaystyle=s_{2}+\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12},

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\neq 1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{2}+\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\neq 12},

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\neq 12},

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\neq 2}

\displaystyle=s_{1}+\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12},

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 12},

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12}

\displaystyle\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 12}.

By
Lemma

17
applied to
s_{1}

we obtain
\mathbf{t}_{1}
such that

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{1}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{1}
.
By the same lemma applied to
s_{2}

we obtain
\mathbf{t}_{2}
such that

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{2}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{2}
.
By the induction hypothesis on
\mathbf{s}_{\neq 12}

we obtain
\mathbf{t}_{\neq 12}
such that

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{\neq 12}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{\neq 12}

and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}_{\neq 12}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}^{?}\mathbf{t}_{\neq 12}
.
Finally, we can set
\mathbf{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{t}_{1}+\mathbf{t}_{2}+\mathbf{t}_{\neq 12}
.

By
Theorem

14
every
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
has
a unique
\mathrm{r}
-normal form, which is denoted by
\mathrm{nf}(\mathbf{s})
.

5.
Taylor approximation for Ohana trees

In its original formulation, Ehrhard and Regnier’s Taylor expansion
translates a
\lambda
-term as a power series of iterated
derivatives
[
Ehrhard.Reg.08
]
.
We now introduce a
qualitative
Taylor expansion
[
ManzonettoP11
]
specifically designed for the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus, having as target the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-resource calculus.

5.1.
The Taylor approximation

Intuitively, a qualitative Taylor expansion should associate each term
M\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}(X)
with a
set
of resource approximants
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\subseteq\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X)
.
Therefore the codomain of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(-)
should be
\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
, and what we actually define is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\coloneqq(X,\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{\bullet}(M))
with
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{\bullet}(M)\subseteq\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X)
.
Notice that, whereas all previous constructions of disjoint unions

\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}
were formed from genuinely disjoint sets,
this is no longer the case here, as
\varnothing\in\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
, for all
X
.

{nota}

(1)

For
X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
,
we write
\mathrm{fv}(X,\mathcal{X})\coloneqq X
.

(2)

We let
\mathord{\subseteq^{\bullet}}
denote the order relation
on
\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))

such that
(X,\mathcal{X})\subseteq^{\bullet}(Y,\mathcal{Y})

whenever
X=Y
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.
We write
\cup^{\bullet}
for the corresponding least upper bounds.

(3)

We write
s\in^{\bullet}(X,\mathcal{X})
to mean that
s\in\mathcal{X}
.

(4)

Given
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
,

\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{!}}(Y))-\{\varnothing\}

and
x\in X
, we write:

\displaystyle\lambda x.(X,\mathcal{X})\coloneqq(X-\{x\},\{\lambda x.s\ |\ s\in\mathcal{X}\}),

\displaystyle(X,\mathcal{X})\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq(X\cup Y,\{s\bar{t}\ |\ s\in\mathcal{X},\ \bar{t}\in\mathcal{Y}\}).

{defi}

(1)

The
Taylor expansion

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\in\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
of a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-term
M
, is defined together with
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(M)\subset\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{!}}(\mathrm{fv}(M))
by mutual induction:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(x)&\coloneqq(\{x\},\{x\}),&\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\lambda x.M)&\coloneqq\lambda x.\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M),&\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(MN)&\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(N),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(M)\coloneqq\{1_{\mathrm{fv}(M)}\}\cup\{[t_{0},\dots,t_{n}]\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ t_{0},\dots,t_{n}\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\}.

(2)

The above definition is extended to Ohana trees by setting,
for all
M\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M))\coloneqq\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A),\qquad\text{together with }\left\{\begin{array}[]{r!{\eqdef}l}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\bot_{X})\lx@intercol\coloneqq&(X,\varnothing),\\
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(\bot_{X})\lx@intercol\coloneqq&\{1_{X}\}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Remark 18
.

For all
M
, we have
\mathrm{fv}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\mathrm{fv}(M)
.
Similarly,
\mathrm{fv}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M)))=\mathrm{fv}(M)
,
due to
Remark

6
and the way
we ordered
\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))
.

{exa}

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathsf{I})=(\varnothing,\{\mathsf{I}\})
,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathsf{D})=(\varnothing,\{\mathsf{D}_{n}\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N}\})
.

(2)

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(\mathsf{Y}f))=(\{f\},\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{Y}f})
and

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}f))=(\{f,l\},\mathcal{X}_{\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}f})
,
where the sets of approximants can be described
as the smallest (in fact, unique) subsets of
\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}
such that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{Y}f}

\displaystyle=\{f1_{\{f\}}\}\cup\{f[t_{0},\dots,t_{n}]\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ t_{0},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{Y}f}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}f}

\displaystyle=\{f1_{\{f,l\}}\}\cup\{f[t_{0},\dots,t_{n}]\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ t_{0},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{\scalebox{0.8}{\faIcon{bible}}}_{\,l}f}\}.

We now describe how resource reduction acts on Taylor expansions,
i.e.
on potentially infinite sets of resource expressions.

{nota}

(1)

Given
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X)]
,
we denote by
|\mathbf{s}|\in\coprod_{X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(X))

its
support
, defined by

|\mathbf{0}_{X}|\coloneqq(X,\varnothing)
and

|s+\mathbf{t}|\coloneqq(X,\{s\})\cup^{\bullet}|\mathbf{t}|
.
Notice that
s\in^{\bullet}|\mathbf{t}|
whenever
s

bears a non-zero coefficient in
\mathbf{t}
(
i.e.

\mathbf{t}=s+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
, for some
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
).

(2)

Given
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}^{\!(!)}}(X))
, we write

\mathrm{nf}(X,\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{s\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathrm{nf}(s)|
.
Notice that
\mathrm{fv}(\mathrm{nf}(X,\mathcal{X}))=X
, and that

\mathrm{nf}(X,\mathcal{X})=(X,\varnothing)
if and only if

s\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{0}_{X}
, for all
s\in\mathcal{X}
.

This allows us to state the following theorem,
adapting
[
Ehrhard.Reg.06
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 19

(Commutation)
.

For all
M\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M))
.

We outline a proof in the following sequence of lemmas,
similar to the one in
[
Barbarossa.Man.19
]
;
alternatively, the variants of
[
Ehrhard.Reg.06
,
Ehrhard.Reg.08
]
,

[
OlimpieriVaux22
]
and
[
Cerda.24
,
CerdaVaux23
]

could also be adapted.

Lemma 20
.

For all resource sums
\mathbf{s}
such that
|\mathbf{s}|\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
,
there exists a reduction
M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{β}N

such that
|\mathrm{nf}(\mathbf{s})|\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.

Proof 5.1
.

By induction on the length of the longest reduction

\mathbf{s}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathrm{nf}(\mathbf{s})
.
If it is
0
, take
N\coloneqq M
.
Otherwise, the longest reduction is

\mathbf{s}=t+\mathbf{u}\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathrm{nf}(\mathbf{s})
.
By firing all redexes in
M
and
\mathbf{u}
at the same position
as the redex fired in
t\rightarrow_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}

(formally we do this by induction on this reduction),
we obtain
M\rightarrow_{β}M^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{u}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
such that

|\mathbf{t}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}|\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M^{\prime})
.
By confluence,
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}+\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathrm{nf}(\mathbf{s})
.
We conclude by induction on this (shorter) reduction.

Lemma 21
.

If
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
is in
\mathsf{r}
-nf, then
there exists
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
such that
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)
.

Proof 5.2
.

By induction on
t
. An
\mathsf{r}
-nf is also a hnf, hence

t=\lambda\vec{x}.y\bar{t}_{1}\cdots\bar{t}_{k}
and

N=\lambda\vec{x}.yN_{1}\cdots N_{k}
.
For
1\leq i\leq k
,
\bar{t}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(N_{i})
.
If
\bar{t}_{i}=1_{\mathrm{fv}(N_{i})}
define
A_{i}\coloneqq\bot_{\mathrm{fv}(N_{i})}
.
If
\bar{t}_{i}=[t_{i}^{1},\dots,t_{i}^{n}]
,
each
t_{i}^{j}
is in
\mathsf{r}
-nf, by induction
there is
A_{i}^{j}\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N_{i})
,
t_{i}^{j}\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A_{i}^{j})
.
Define
A_{i}\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{n}A_{i}^{j}\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N_{i})
.
Finally,
A\coloneqq\lambda\vec{x}.yA_{1}\cdots A_{k}\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)

and
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)
.

Lemma 22

(Monotonicity of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(-)
)
.

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A^{\prime})
if and only if
A\sqsubseteq A^{\prime}
.

(2)

For all
N\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\omega_{\mathsf{m}}(N))\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.

Proof 5.3
.

Immediate inductions (i) on
A
and
A^{\prime}
,
and (ii) on the head structure of
N
.

Lemma 23

(Simulation of
\rightarrow_{β}
)
.

If
M\rightarrow_{β}N
, then
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)=\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)}|\mathbf{t}_{s}|

for resource sums
\mathbf{t}_{s}\in\mathbb{N}[\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(\mathrm{fv}(M))]

such that
\forall s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M),\ s\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{s}
.

Proof 5.4
.

One first shows by induction on
P
that
for all
P,Q\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
and
x\in\mathrm{fv}(P)
,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(P[Q/x])=\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(P)}\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{\bar{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(Q)}|s\left\langle\bar{t}\middle/x\right\rangle|
.
Then the proof is an induction on a derivation of
M\rightarrow_{β}N
,
using the previous equality in the base case.
See the details of a similar proof in

[
CerdaVaux23
, Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2]
.

Lemma 24
.

If
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{m}}
then all
s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)

are in
\mathsf{r}
-nf.

Proof 5.5
.

Immediate induction on
A
.

Everything is now in place to prove the Commutation
Theorem

19
.

Proof 5.6

(Proof of
Theorem

19
)
.

By
Remark

18
,
\mathrm{fv}(\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)))=\mathrm{fv}(M)=\mathrm{fv}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M)))
.
Thus, it is sufficient to prove that,
for
t\in\mathord{\Delta_{\mathsf{I}}}(\mathrm{fv}(M))
:

t\in^{\bullet}\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))
if and only if
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M))
.

Take
t\in^{\bullet}\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))
,
i.e.

t\in|\mathrm{nf}(s)|

for some
s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
.
Then, by
Lemma

20
, there exists
a reduction
M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{β}N

such that
|\mathrm{nf}(s)|\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.
Hence
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
and
t
is in
\mathsf{r}
-nf:

Lemma

21
ensures that
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)

for some
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)=\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
(by
Proposition

8
).
This means exactly that
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M))
.

Conversely, take
t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\mathrm{OT}(M))
,
i.e.

t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)
for some
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
.
By definition there is a reduction
M\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{β}N

such that
A\sqsubseteq\omega_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.
By
Lemma

22
,

t\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\omega_{\mathsf{m}}(N))\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N).

By iterated applications of
Lemma

23
,
there is an
s\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
such that

s\mathrel{\rightarrow\mathrel{\mkern-14.0mu}\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{t}_{s}
and
t\in|\mathbf{t}_{s}|
.
By

,
t
is in
\mathsf{r}
-nf,
therefore

.

5.2.
The
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory of Ohana trees

Consider the equivalence
\mathcal{O}
on
\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,
defined by
M=_{\mathcal{O}}N
if and only if
\mathrm{OT}(M)=\mathrm{OT}(N)
.
Thanks to
Theorem

19
,
we are now able to show that this equivalence is a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory.

Corollary 25
.

For
M,N\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,
M=_{\mathcal{O}}N
if and only if
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N))
.

Proof 5.7
.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Immediate by
Theorem

19
.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Take
M,N
such that
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N))
.
By
Theorem

9
, it is sufficient to prove

\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)=\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.
Take
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)
.
If
A=\bot_{X}
, then
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
is trivial
since
\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
is downward closed.
Otherwise, by hypothesis and
Theorem

19
,

\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)\subseteq^{\bullet}\mathop{\bigcup\nolimits^{\mathrlap{\bullet}}}\displaylimits_{B\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(B)
.
There is a
B\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
such that

[\![A]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(B)
,
where
[\![A]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(A)
is defined by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}[\![x]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}&\coloneqq&x,\\
[\![\lambda\vec{x}.yA_{1}\cdots A_{k}]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}&\coloneqq&\lambda\vec{x}.y[\![A_{1}]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}^{!}\cdots[\![A_{k}]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}^{!},\\
\end{array}\qquad[\![A]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}^{!}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1_{X},&\textrm{if }A=\bot_{X},\\
{[[\![A]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}]},&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By induction on
A
, one shows that

[\![A]\!]_{\mathsf{r}}\in^{\bullet}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(B)
implies
A\sqsubseteq B
,
hence
A\in\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
by downward-closure
(
Lemma

7
2
).
This shows that
\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\subseteq\mathrm{App}_{\mathsf{m}}(N)
.
The converse inclusion is symmetric.

Corollary 26
.

\mathcal{O}
is a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory.

Proof 5.8
.

The relation
=_{\mathcal{O}}
is clearly an equivalence.
By
Propositions

8
and
9
,
for all
M,N\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,
M=_{\beta}N
entails
M=_{\mathcal{O}}N
.

For compatibility with abstraction,
take
M=_{\mathcal{O}}N
and
x\in\mathrm{fv}(M)\cap\mathrm{fv}(N)
.
By
Corollary

25
, we get

\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\lambda x.M))=\lambda x.\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\lambda x.\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(\lambda x.N))
.

For compatibility with application,
by
Theorem

14
observe that for all
M,N\in\Lambda_{\mathsf{I}}
,

\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(MN))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M)\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(N))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(N)))
,
where
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}^{!}(N))
is defined by adapting

Section

5.1
to sets of resource bags.
Therefore,
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M^{\prime}))
and
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(N^{\prime}))

imply
\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(MN))=\mathrm{nf}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{m}}(M^{\prime}N^{\prime}))
, and
we can conclude the proof by
Corollary

.

6.
A multi-type system characterizing
\mathcal{O}

Once established that Ohana trees induce a
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-theory, the question naturally arises whether it is possible to design a denotational model whose theory is exactly
\mathcal{O}
. In the conference version of this work
[
Cerda.Man.Sau.25
]
, this was presented as Problem 46.
In fact, this problem immediately leads to a further difficulty:
since
\mathcal{O}
is not a
\lambda
-theory
(as explained in
Section

3.3
),
it cannot be the theory of a denotational model of the
\lambda
-calculus,
but only of a denotational model of the
\lambda\mathsf{I}
-calculus —
which is not a particularly well-defined notion.
Although some proposals exist
[
EgidiHR92
,
Jacobs93
]
, unifying them and ensuring their compatibility with the present framework is left for future work.

In this section we present a first step towards such a denotational model by introducing a multi-type (or non-idempotent intersection type) system characterizing
\mathcal{O}
just like the usual system characterizes the theory
\mathcal{B}
induced by equality of Böhm trees

[
Rocca82
,
BreuvartMR18
]
:

\forall M,N\in\Lambda,\quad\mathrm{BT}(M)=\mathrm{BT}(N)\Longleftrightarrow\{(\Gamma,\sigma)\ |\ \Gamma\vdash M:\sigma\}=\{(\Gamma,\sigma)\ |\ \Gamma\vdash N:\sigma\}.

By interpreting the set

[\![M]\!]\coloneqq\{(\Gamma,\sigma)\ |\ \Gamma\vdash M:\sigma\}

in the category
\mathbf{Rel}
of sets and relations
this usually gives rise to
the relational model of the
\lambda
-calculus

6.1.
A multi-type system

Let us first introduce a grammar generating the multi-types.
We adapt the standard definition along the same lines as in the treatment of the resource calculus and the Taylor expansion in
Sections

4.1
and
5.1
:
empty multisets
[]_{X}
are annotated with a finite set
X
of variables, and the types of a term are extended with a type
\mathbf{0}_{X}
playing the role of the first component of the Taylor expansion.

{defi}

Given a countable set
\mathcal{A}
of
atoms
,
the set
\mathord{?\mathbb{T}}
of
multi-types
is defined inductively by:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad\ni\quad}r@{\quad\eqbnf\quad}l@{\qquad}r}\mathord{?\mathbb{T}}&\sigma,\tau,\dots\quad\mathrel{\Coloneqq}&\mathbf{0}_{X}\ |\ \alpha&X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V}\\[8.0pt]
\mathbb{T}&\alpha,\beta,\dots\quad\mathrel{\Coloneqq}&\ast\ |\ \bar{\alpha}\multimap\beta&\ast\in\mathcal{A}\\[8.0pt]
\mathord{!\mathbb{T}}&\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},\dots\quad\mathrel{\Coloneqq}&[]_{X}\ |\ [α_{0},\dots,α_{n}]&X\subseteq_{\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{V},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\end{array}

The key feature of our typing system is that, along the usual environment
\Gamma
associating a multiset of types to all the free variables of the typed term,
we introduce a second environment
\Delta
in each sequent:
to each free variable
x
of the typed term
M
this environment associates
the set of the free variables of the term that will ultimately be substituted to
x
in
M
.
This should become clear in the statement and the proof of the substitution
,
and
below.

{defi}

An
environment
is a pair denoted by
\Gamma\mathrel{;}\Delta
,
where:

•

A
(type) environment

\Gamma
is a map
\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbb{T})
whose support
\support(\Gamma)\coloneqq\{x\mid\Gamma(x)\neq[]\}
is finite.
In other words, we have that
\Gamma(x)\neq[]
holds for finitely many variables
x
.

–

Such a
\Gamma
can be presented as a sequence
x_{1}:\bar{\alpha}_{1},\dots,x_{n}:\bar{\alpha}_{n}
where the
x_{i}
are supposed distinct.
Observe that the notation
x_{i}:\bar{\alpha}_{i}
is slightly improper here, since these
\bar{\alpha}_{i}
live in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbb{T})
, not in
\mathord{!\mathbb{T}}
.
Concretely, this means that when
\bar{\alpha}=[]_{X}\in\mathord{!\mathbb{T}}
is used in an environment
\Gamma,x:\bar{\alpha}
, we silently forget the annotation
X
and set
\bar{\alpha}=[]
.

–

Such environments are equipped with an operation
+
performing pointwise multiset union:

(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2})(x)=\Gamma_{1}(x)+\Gamma_{2}(x),\textrm{ for all $x\in\mathcal{V}$.}

•

A
(variable) environment
is any finite partial map
\Delta:\mathcal{V}\rightharpoondown\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{V})
.

–

Such a
\Delta
is presented as a sequence
x_{1}:X_{1},\dots,x_{n}:X_{n}
where the
x_{i}
are implicitly distinct and
X_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{V})
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

–

We denote by
\domain(\Delta)
the domain of
\Delta
,
and we define
\cup\mathrm{im}(\Delta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.

–

We write
\Delta_{1}\coh\Delta_{2}
if, for all
x\in\domain(\Delta_{1})\cap\domain(\Delta_{2})
, we have
\Delta_{1}(x)=\Delta_{2}(x)
.

–

For
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
such that
\Delta_{1}\coh\Delta_{2}
, we define

(\Delta_{1}\vee\Delta_{2})(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Delta_{1}(x),&\text{if }x\in\domain(\Delta_{1}),\\
\Delta_{2}(x),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

–

We write
\Delta_{1}\subseteq\Delta_{2}
whenever
\domain(\Delta_{1})\subseteq\domain(\Delta_{2})
,
and
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
coincide on
\domain(\Delta_{1})
.
Observe that
\Delta_{1}\subseteq\Delta_{2}\coh\Delta_{3}
entails
\Delta_{1}\coh\Delta_{3}
.

{defi}

\Gamma\mathrel{;}\Delta\vdash M:\sigma

and

\Gamma\mathrel{;}\Delta\vdash^{!}M:\bar{\alpha}

by mutual induction as follows:
