Convergence-rate and error analysis of sectional-volume average method for the collisional breakage equation with multi-dimensional modelling
Prakrati Kushwah, Anupama Ghorai, Jitraj Saha

Abstract
. Recent literature reports two sectional techniques, the finite volume method [Das et al., 2020, SIAM J. Sci. Comput., 42(6): B1570-B1598] and the fixed pivot technique [Kushwah et al., 2023, Commun. Nonlinear Sci. Numer. Simul., 121(37): 107244] to solve one-dimensional collision-induced nonlinear particle breakage equation. It is observed that both the methods become inconsistent over random grids. Therefore, we propose a new birth modification strategy, where the newly born particles are proportionately allocated in three adjacent cells, depending upon the average volume in each cell. This modification technique improves the numerical model by making it consistent over random grids. A detailed convergence and error analysis for this new scheme is studied over different possible choices of grids such as uniform, nonuniform, locally-uniform, random and oscillatory grids. In addition, we have also identified the conditions upon kernels for which the convergence rate increases significantly and the scheme achieves second order of convergence over uniform, nonuniform and locally-uniform grids. The enhanced order of accuracy will enable the new model to be easily coupled with CFD-modules. Another significant advancement in the literature is done by extending the discrete model for two-dimensional equation over rectangular grids.

1.
Introduction

In disperse system, particles are encountered by several physical processes such as aggregation, breakage, nucleation, evaporation etc. The particle encounters can be induced by some external force or due to Brownian motion among the fellow particles. During such encounters, particles disintegrate into smaller fragments, thus leading to the breakage process.

Thus, particle volume evolves in a closed system over a period of time and the equations representing these mechanisms are called breakage population balance equations (PBEs). PBEs are initial valued integro-partial differential equations.
Mathematical model where breakage PBEs are induced by external force is represented by a linear equation
[
16
]
. On the other hand, mathematical model for breakage PBEs,
driven by the collisions between particles is nonlinear equation
[
11
]
. Note that the collisions between two mother particles can lead to an outcome in which one mother particle breaks into smaller fragments while other remains unchanged, acting as a catalyst.
This study focuses on
the collision-induced nonlinear breakage process that has several applications in real life.
The collision-induced breakage process can be experienced during the formation of raindrops and bubbles. Prat et al.

[
15
]
have identified that during the formation of raindrop and bubbles, the collisions occur among the raindrops (which can also be treated as particles) and raindrops disintegrate into smaller ones. They have modeled this phenomena with the help of the discrete nonlinear equation and
explored the solution (which is the raindrop size distribution) through Monte Carlo simulations.
On a furthernote, the collisional nonlinear breakage equation plays crucial role for productions of powders with particular density distribution for drug formation and to create specified sizes tablets from wet granulation process
[
17
]
. Various other applications of such breakage model are found in dense disperse system for fluidized beds
[
13
]
,
astrophysics
[
14
]
, the crushing or milling processing, mineral processing, material science and both batch and continuous granulation processes.

1.1.
The continuous collisional nonlinear breakage model

In this article, we consider the continuous collision-induced nonlinear breakage equation which is first introduced by Cheng and Redner
[
1
,
2
]
. The time evolution of density distribution function
n(x,t)
of particles of volume
x\geq 0
at time
t\geq 0
due to the collision induced breakage process is governed by the following equation:

(1.1)

\frac{\partial n(x,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{x}^{\infty}\beta(x|y;z)\mathcal{K}(y,z)n(y,t)n(z,t)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(x,y)n(x,t)n(y,t)\mathrm{d}y,

with the initial condition

(1.2)

n(x,0)=n_{0}(x)\geq 0,\quad\text{for any}\quad x\geq 0.

Here, functions
\beta(x|y;z)
and
\mathcal{K}(y,z)
are the breakage distribution function and the collisional rate kernel respectively.
The first term on the right hand side (R.H.S.) of (
1.1
) is the birth term which represents the formation of
x
volume particles as a result of collision between two particles of
y

(>x)
and
z
volume. The mother particle of
y
volume breaks into the smaller particle of volume
x
whereas particle of volume
z
acts like a catalyst and remain unchanged during collision. The second term on R.H.S. of (
1.1
) is the death term that accounts the depletion of particles of volume
x
from the system.
In general, the collisional kernel and the breakage distribution function satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{K}(y,z)
is nonnegative and symmetric with respect to the arguments
y
and
z
i.e. satisfies the conditions,

(1.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{K}(y,z)=\mathcal{K}(z,y),\quad\text{for all}\quad y,z>0,

(ii)

\beta(x|y;z)
is nonnegative satisfying the condition
\beta(x|y;z)=0
when
x>y
and the volume conserving property,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y}x\beta(x|y;z)\mathrm{d}x=y,\quad\text{for all}\quad y,z>0,

and total number of fragments produced during a breakage event is calculated as

(1.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y}\beta(x|y;z)\mathrm{d}x=\zeta(y,z),\quad\text{where}\quad 2\leq\zeta(y,z)\leq\bar{\zeta}<\infty,\quad\text{for all}\quad y,z>0.

For population balance equations, conservation of particles properties such as mass and number of particles of the system plays a key role to determine the density evolution for certain kinetic rates. In this regard, some integral properties
of the density function play a crucial role to indicate several significant physical properties.
For this purpose, the
r^{\text{th}}
order moment is denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{r}(t)
and is defined by

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{r}n(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69046ptr=0,1,2,\dots...

Consider, a function
\varphi(x)
of positive real numbers. Now, multiplying the breakage equation (
1.1
) with
\varphi(x)
on both sides and taking integration over
x
, we obtain the corresponding moment equation for the collisional nonlinear breakage equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)n(x,t)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{y}\varphi(x)\beta(x|y;z)\mathrm{d}x-\varphi(y)\right)\mathcal{K}(y,z)n(y,t)n(z,t)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z.

Setting
\varphi(x)=1
in equation (
1.7
) and using property (
1.5
), we obtain the time evolution of zeroth moment given as

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}_{0}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\zeta(y,z)-1\right]\mathcal{K}(y,z)n(y,t)n(z,t)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z.

Likewise, setting
\varphi(x)=x
in equation (
1.7
) and by property (
1.5
),
we obtain the time evolution of the first moment of the particles as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{implies}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathcal{M}_{1}(t)=\mathcal{M}_{1}(0),\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046ptt\geq 0.

provided all the integrals in the R.H.S. of equation (
1.7
) exists. Thus, for a suitable choice of kinetic rates the volume conservation law holds appropriately.

1.2.
The state of art and motivation

The evolution of raindrop size distribution undergoing the collision induced nonlinear breakage process
[
18
,
15
]
is extensively used to describe the precipitation dynamics, weather modeling, and radar meteorology. Moreover, several other applications in real life and industrial processes urge researchers to explore more on the collisional nonlinear breakage equation. In literature, this equation is mostly theoretically analysed
[
7
,
6
]
and less numerically investigated.
Since achieving analytical solutions of the collisional nonlinear breakage equation is very challenging, so the primary motivation lies on securing efficient numerical solutions.
In this regard, various numerical methods including sectional discretization methods
[
3
,
11
]
,
Monte Carlo simulations
[
5
]
and semi-analytical methods
[
19
]
are implemented in the literature to solve the collisional nonlinear breakage equation. Among these numerical methods, the sectional discretization methods are formulated on the basis of distributing the particles in the representatives as well as neighboring nodes (or pivots) for capturing the required integral properties accurately.
Monte Carlo method needs a significant large number of data to achieve higher accuracy, thus involving a significant computational cost. On the other hand, semi-analytical method uses the analytical toolbox of the supporting software and hence very slow to produce results for higher iterations. Additionally, this method fails to give solution of any complex, nonlinear equations in closed form except specific conditions. Thus, the sectional discretization methods are efficient to address and overcome these limitations for generating results with better accuracy. In last few decades, these section based methods includes finite volume methods (FVM)
[
8
,
4
]
, fixed pivot techniques (FPT)
[
16
,
9
]
and cell average techniques (CAT)
[
10
,
12
]
to solve the linear breakage equation. In recent literature, there are few works documented to address the numerical treatment of the collisional nonlinear breakage equation, employing sectional discretization methods including FVM
[
3
]
and FPT
[
11
]
to solve the equation (
1.1
) but these existing schemes have its limitations. For FVM, the allocation of the new born particles is restricted to a single pivot whereas in FPT, two pivots are used for birth modification. Moreover, FVM is highly acccurate for a particular choice of grids that is the geometric, but for other choice of grids it struggles with performance. Additionally, FVM fails on coarser grids. On the other hand, FPT offers some definite improvement, yet it fails over randomly generated grids. For overall accuracy, it achieves first order convergence rate.
In this context, we propose an improved sectional discretization method based on particles averaged volume in a particular cell named as
volume average method
(VAM). The cell allocation is done in three nighboring cells based on the properties of particles volume average, which are expected to be preserved. The birth terms are modified in order to achieve consistency with total volume and number of particles. The nonnegative solution obtained from the numerical scheme and consistency of the scheme are examined in detail followed by a discussion on discretization error over different grid types. It is observed that VAM is first order convergent over uniform, nonuniform and locally-uniform grids. Most significant observation is that VAM shows first order convergent over random grids.
We also study the performance of VAM over oscillatory grids which is first evidence where consistency analysis is reported for the collisional nonlinear breakage equation in the literature and this new method achieves first order accurate. An important observation is discussed where conditions depending upon the kernels for which the VAM shows second order convergence over uniform, nonuniform and locally-uniform grids and maintains first order accuracy over random and oscillatory grids. The accuracy of the new scheme VAM is validated against the existing fixed pivot technique.
It is worth mentioning here that the existing FPT is inconsistent over random grids. Furthermore, we carry out the stability analysis of the new scheme using Lipschitz criterion. The model is further extended for two-dimensional case over the rectangular grids. For numerical results, two examples are solved, where the total particle properties and the particle hypervolume are calculated against their exact values.
Importantly, the results show that VAM performs better than the existing FPT.

The article is organized as follows: in section
2
, we describe the cell adaptive mathematical formulation of VAM for the collisional nonlinear breakage equation (
1.1
). Section
3
discusses the convergence and consistency analysis. Two-dimensional model is presented in section
4
and numerical discussion is done for different text problems in section
5
. The findings of the work are highlighted in section
6
.

2.
The cell volume average method

We first truncate the continuous collisional breakage equation (
1.1
) by considering a finite computational domain as
\Lambda=[0,x_{\max}]
with
x_{\max}<\infty
: for all
x\in\Lambda
and
t\geq 0
and the truncated equation (
1.1
) reads as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n(x,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{0}^{x_{\max}}\int_{x}^{x_{\max}}\beta(x|y;z)\mathcal{K}(y,z)n(y,t)n(z,t)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z-\int_{0}^{x_{\max}}\mathcal{K}(x,y)n(x,t)n(y,t)\mathrm{d}y,

with the initial condition

(2.2)

\displaystyle n(x,0)=n_{0}(x)\quad\text{for any}\quad x\in\Lambda.

We now discretize the volume domain
\Lambda
into
I(<\infty)
discrete cells. Let
\Lambda_{i}
denotes the
i^{\text{th}}
cell,
\Lambda_{i}:=[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]
with
x_{1/2}:=0
and
x_{I+1/2}:=x_{\max}
and
\Delta x_{i}:=x_{i+1/2}-x_{i-1/2}
. A finitely discretized computational domain is shown in Figure
1
. The midpoint
x_{i}:=\displaystyle\frac{x_{i+1/2}+x_{i-1/2}}{2}
of
\Lambda_{i}
is the cell representatives or pivots. It is assumed that the particle property is concentrated at these pivots.
Consider
\Delta x_{i}\leq\Delta x_{i+1}
and
\Delta x_{\text{min}}=\min\limits_{i}\Delta x_{i}\leq\Delta x_{i}\leq\Delta x=\max\limits_{i}\Delta x_{i}\hskip 5.69046pt\text{with}\hskip 5.69046pt\displaystyle\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta_{\text{min}}}\leq\alpha
, a constant, for all
1\leq i\leq I
.
Let
N_{i}(t)
be the discrete number density in the
i^{\text{th}}
cell at time
t
defined by

(2.3)

\displaystyle N_{i}(t)=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}n(x,t)\mathrm{d}x.

Using relation
\displaystyle n(x,t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{I}N_{i}(t)\delta(x-x_{i})
in the truncated equation (
2.1
), we obtain the collisional nonlinear breakage equation as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{N}_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{B}_{i}(t)-\hat{D}_{i}(t),\quad\text{ with initial conditions}\quad N_{i}(0)=N_{i}^{0},

for all
i=1,2,\dots,I
.
Here, birth terms and death terms are defined as respectively

(2.5)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{i}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=i}^{I}\beta_{i,j}^{k}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k}){N}_{j}(t){N}_{k}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{D}_{i}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j}){N}_{i}(t){N}_{j}(t),

with

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}^{k}=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{j}^{i}}\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad p_{j}^{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{i},&\quad\mbox{if}\quad i=j,\\
x_{i+1/2},&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Note that positivity of all the terms appearing on the R.H.S. of
B_{i}(t)
and
D_{i}(t)
defines positivity of the both
B_{i}(t)
and
D_{i}(t)
.
The
r^{th}
order discrete moment is defined as

\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{r}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}x^{r}_{i}N_{i}(t)
, for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Recalling equation (
1.7
) the discrete moment equation is written as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\varphi_{i}N_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k}){N}_{j}(t){N}_{k}(t)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{i}^{j}}\varphi_{i}\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x-\varphi_{j}\right),

where
\left\{\varphi_{i}\right\}_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of positive real numbers.
Consequently, setting
\varphi_{i}=1
and
x_{i}
in equation (
2.8
), the time evolution of the discrete zeroth moment and first moment are written as respectively

(2.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k}){N}_{j}(t){N}_{k}(t)\left[\zeta(x_{j},x_{k})-1\right],

and

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k}){N}_{j}(t){N}_{k}(t)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{i}^{j}}x_{i}\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x-x_{j}\right).

Remark 2.1
.

The discrete formulation (
2.4
) is not consistent with discrete first order moment.
[
5
]

Since the discrete formulation (
2.4
) fails to preserve the total mass of the particles in system, it is not suitable to approximate the collisional nonlinear breakage equation (
1.1
). To overcome this limitation, we formulate a new numerical method which modifies the birth term of equation (
1.1
) and conserves the total mass as well as number of the particles.

2.1.
Cell volume average based birth rate modification

To capture the birth rate of the particles more precisely,
the daughter particles are assigned to the neighboring pivots depending upon the position of the average volume
\bar{v}_{i}
in
\Lambda_{i}
. In this context, to obtain the average volume, the discrete volume flux over
\Lambda_{i}
is defined as

(2.11)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=i}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k}){N}_{j}(t){N}_{k}(t)\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{j}^{i}}x\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x,

and recalling
B_{i}(t)
from equation (
2.5
) the volume average
\bar{v}_{i}
of all newborn particles in
\Lambda_{i}
as

(2.12)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{i}=\frac{\hat{V}_{i}(t)}{\hat{B}_{i}(t)},\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\dots,I.

There are two possibilities for the particle allocation in a cell as follows:

(1)

(Less likely event) if the average volume
\bar{v}_{i}
of the particles in
\Lambda_{i}
matches with the volume of cell representative (happens very rarely) i.e.
\bar{v}_{i}=x_{i}
, then the total birth
B_{i}
can be allocated to the node
x_{i}
. Here, all properties corresponding to the average volume of particles are preserved trivially.

(2)

(Most likely event) if the average volume
\bar{v}_{i}
of the particles in
\Lambda_{i}
does not match with the volume of cell representative, that is either
\bar{v}_{i}>x_{i}
or
\bar{v}_{i}<x_{i}
, then the particles are distributed to the neighboring pivots such that the total number of particles and mass are preserved. Then, depending upon the position of average volume, we see the contribution of fractions of birth term in the neighboring pivots.

\bar{v}_{i}>x_{i}

x_{i}

Then, to preserve number of particles and volume allocated in

\displaystyle c_{1}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i})+c_{2}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i+1})=\hat{B}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{i}c_{1}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i})+x_{i+1}c_{2}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i+1})=\bar{v}_{i}\hat{B}_{i}.

Solving relations (

(2.14)

\displaystyle c_{1}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i})=\hat{B}_{i}\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad c_{2}(\bar{v}_{i},x_{i+1})=\hat{B}_{i}\lambda_{i+1}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i}),\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda_{i}^{\pm}(x)=\frac{\displaystyle x-x_{i\pm 1}}{\displaystyle x_{i}-x_{i\pm 1}}.

x_{i}

(2.15)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{N}_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(t)-\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(t),\hskip 2.84544pt\text{with initial conditions,}\quad\hat{N}_{i}(0)=\hat{N}_{i}^{0}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{B}}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 11.38092ptb^{(2)}_{i}+b^{(3)}_{i+1},&\quad i=1,\\

where

\displaystyle b^{(1)}_{i-1}:=

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i-1})H(\bar{v}_{i-1}-x_{i-1})\hat{B}_{i-1}(t),\quad b^{(2)}_{i}:=\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i})H(x_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})\hat{B}_{i}(t)+\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i})H(\bar{v}_{i}-x_{i})\hat{B}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\hat{N}_{i}(t)\hat{N}_{j}(t).

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{I}x_{i}\hat{N}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\hat{N}_{j}(t)\hat{N}_{k}(t)\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{i}^{j}}x\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x

(2.26)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}x_{i}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\hat{N}_{i}(t)\hat{N}_{j}(t).

Using volume preserving property (
1.4
) in equation (
2.26
) and simplifying calculation, it yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{I}x_{i}\hat{N}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}x_{j}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\hat{N}_{j}(t)\hat{N}_{k}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}x_{i}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\hat{N}_{i}(t)\hat{N}_{j}(t)=0,

that is
\mathcal{\hat{M}}_{1}(t)=\mathcal{\hat{M}}_{1}(0)
for all
t\geq 0
, which is the volume conservation law.

The time evolution of the discrete zeroth moment is calculated by taking summation over
i
on discrete equation (
2.15
) and calculating in similar way as volume conservation and using property (
1.5
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\hat{N}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=i}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\hat{N}_{j}(t)\hat{N}_{k}(t)\sum_{i=1}^{j}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{j}^{i}}\beta(x|x_{j};x_{k})\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\hat{N}_{i}(t)\hat{N}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\hat{N}_{j}(t)\hat{N}_{k}(t)\zeta(x_{j},x_{k})-\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{i},x_{j})\hat{N}_{i}(t)\hat{N}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\hat{N}_{j}(t)\hat{N}_{k}(t)\left[\zeta(x_{j},x_{k})-1\right].

This is equivalent with the time evolution of discrete zeroth moment (
2.9
).
∎

Here, we are a position to introduce the semi-discrete system in a vector form described by reformulation(
2.15
) as follows:

\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}=\left\{\hat{N}_{1},\hat{N}_{2},\dots,\hat{N}_{I}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
is the numerical solution of the following semi-discrete system

(2.27)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}):=\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}^{0}\geq 0,

where
i^{th}
components of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\hat{B}}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{{\hat{D}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
are defined by
\mathcal{\hat{B}}_{i}
(
2.1
) and
\mathcal{\hat{D}}_{i}
(
2.19
) and also the numerical flux
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}=\left\{{\hat{J}}_{1},{\hat{J}}_{2},\dots,{\hat{J}}_{I}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
is a non negative vector whose
i^{\text{th}}
component is given by

{\hat{J}}_{i}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{i}-\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}.

3.
Stability and consistency

3.1.
Preliminary definitions and theorems

Here, we assume the space
X=\mathbb{R}^{I}
equipped with
the discrete
L^{1}
-norm defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)||=\sum_{i=1}^{I}|\hat{N}_{i}(t)|,\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for all}\hskip 5.69046pt0\leq t\leq T.

Definition 3.1
.

(Spatial truncation error). The discretization residue obtained due to the substitution of the exact solution in the discrete scheme (
2.27
) is called truncation error and is mathematically calculated as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sigma(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{N}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for all}\hskip 5.69046pt0\leq t\leq T

where
\sigma=\left\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{I}\right\}
is a vector whose
i^{\text{th}}
component is given by
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(t)=\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{N}_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}{\hat{N}}_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}
.

Definition 3.2
.

(Global discretization error). A numerical method is called the
p
th order convergent if for
\Delta x\to 0

(3.3)

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{N}(t)-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{p})\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for all}\hskip 5.69046pt0\leq t\leq T.

Definition 3.3
.

(Lipschitz condition). A mapping
\hat{J}
which follows

(3.4)

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{g})-\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{h})||\leq\gamma||\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{h}||\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for all}\hskip 5.69046pt\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{I},

is said to be satisfy the Lipschitz condition with
\gamma<\infty
as Lipschitz constant.

Definition 3.4
.

(Nonnegativity). The system of ODEs in
\mathbb{R}^{I}
defined by (
2.27
) is called nonnegative or nonnegativity preserving if

(3.5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(0)\geq 0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{implies}\hskip 5.69046pt\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)\geq 0\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Definition 3.5
.

(Consistency). A numerical scheme is called
p^{\text{th}}
order consistent if for
\Delta x\to 0

(3.6)

\displaystyle||\sigma(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{p})\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ uniformly for all}\hskip 5.69046pt0\leq t\leq T.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
be the numerical flux defined by the equation (
2.27
) in the new scheme VAM. Then
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\hat{M}},\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
be two solutions satisfying the discrete system (
2.27
).

\displaystyle||\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\bigg|\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i-1})H(\bar{v}_{i-1}-x_{i-1})\left(\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182pt+\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i})H(x_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})\left(\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right)+\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i})H(\bar{v}_{i}-x_{i})\left(\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182pt+\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i+1})H(x_{i+1}-\bar{v}_{i+1})\left(\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i-1})H(\bar{v}_{i-1}-x_{i-1})\left|\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}^{-}(\bar{v}_{i})H(x_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})\left|\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i})H(\bar{v}_{i}-x_{i})\left|\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|

(3.7)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}^{+}(\bar{v}_{i+1})H(x_{i+1}-\bar{v}_{i+1})\left|\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|.

By the definitions of
\lambda_{i}^{\pm}(x)
and
H(x)
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{i}^{\pm}(x)H(x)\leq 1.

Thereby, estimation (
3.7
) can be written as

\displaystyle||\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left|\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left|\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|

(3.8)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left|\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{B}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|.

Using definition of
\hat{\beta_{i}}
and property (
1.5
), bounds of
\mathcal{K}
(
2.20
) and boundedness of the discrete zeroth moment
\mathcal{\hat{M}}_{0}
(
2.22
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right|=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=i}^{I}\mathcal{K}(x_{j},x_{k})\left|\hat{M}_{j}\hat{M}_{k}-\hat{N}_{j}\hat{N}_{k}\right|\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{p_{j}^{i}}\beta(x|x_{j},x_{k})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}C(x_{\max})\sum_{k=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\frac{1}{2}\left|\left(\hat{M}_{j}+\hat{N}_{j}\right)\left(\hat{M}_{k}-\hat{N}_{k}\right)+\left(\hat{M}_{j}-\hat{N}_{j}\right)\left(\hat{M}_{K}+\hat{N}_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\zeta}C(x_{\max})}{2}\times 2\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{I}\left|\hat{M}_{j}+\hat{N}_{j}\right|\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{I}\left|\hat{M}_{k}-\hat{N}_{k}\right|\bigg)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\bar{\zeta}C(x_{\max})\beta(T)||\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||.

In similar way, for death term we obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle||\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||\leq 2C(x_{\max})\beta(T)||\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||.

Thereby birth term from(
3.9
) and death term from (
3.10
), combinedly we may write

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})\right)-\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\right)\right|\right|\leq

\displaystyle||\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||+||\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||

\displaystyle 2(\bar{\zeta}+1)C(x_{\max})\beta(T)\;||\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta||\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||,

where

is a constant, independent of the grids.

Finally, we can write

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{M}})-\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||\leq\eta||\boldsymbol{\hat{M}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||,

which implies that the
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition irrespective of the grids.
∎

3.2.
Nonnegativity of the solution

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})

be a continuous mapping satisfying Lipschitz condition with respect to
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
. Then the semi-discrete system (
2.27
) is nonnegative, if and only if for any vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
satisfying

(3.11)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0,\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{N}_{i}=0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{implies}\hskip 5.69046pt\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})}\geq 0,

which proves that the solution by the new scheme VAM (
2.15
) is nonnegative.

Proof.

Assume that the semi-discrete system (
2.27
) is nonnegative. Let
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
be the solution of the system (
2.27
) with initial conditions
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}(0)}\geq 0
. By definition
3.4
of the ODE system, we have
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0
. Now,
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0
with
\hat{N}_{i}=0
implies

\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\geq 0\hskip 2.84544pt\text{and}\hskip 2.84544pt\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})=0\hskip 2.84544pt\text{for all}\quad i\in\left\{1,2,\dots,I\right\}
.
So,
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0
with
\hat{N}_{i}=0
deduces that
{\hat{J}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\geq 0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,I\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ which implies}\hskip 2.84544pt\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})\geq 0
.

Conversely, we have the criteria

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0,\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{N}_{i}=0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{implies}\hskip 5.69046pt\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})}\geq 0.

The above expression implies
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\hat{N}_{i}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\displaystyle\geq 0.

To prove the nonnegativity, we will use the Lipschitz condition on

\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
. Since the solution
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
can not cross the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}:\hat{N}_{i}=0\right\}
so there exists sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\geq 0,\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{N}_{i}=0\hskip 5.69046pt\text{implies}\hskip 5.69046pt\frac{\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\hat{N}}_{i}}{\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t}\geq\varepsilon>0.

This will satisfy the the perturbed ODE system with

\displaystyle{\tilde{J}}_{i}={\hat{J}}_{i}+\varepsilon,\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\dots,I.

By the Lipschitz condition of
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
and using the standard stability argument for ODEs, if
\varepsilon\to 0
, the solution of the unperturbed system with given initial condition will be approximated with exact solution of the perturbed system. This above argument and the criteria (
3.11
), we can write that
the system (
2.27
) is nonnegative.

For the second part, we have already proved that the system of ODEs (
2.27
) is nonnegative in the first part.
This directly implies that the solution by the new scheme VAM is nonnegative.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta x^{2}_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i+1}}C_{i}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x_{i}^{2})\leq\frac{\Delta x^{2}}{\Delta x_{\text{min}}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x_{i}^{2})\leq\alpha\Delta x+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x_{i}^{2}).

Similar argument is applicable for
Case II
.
Therefore, from (
3.26
) we can easily say that the order of consistency is

(3.30)

\displaystyle||\sigma(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x).

3.4.5.
Type E: Oscillatory grids

In order to track the oscillatory behavior (unlikely event) in discretized points due to the effect of collisions, let us consider oscillatory grids defined as

\displaystyle\Delta x_{i+1}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\Delta x_{i},&\quad\text{if}\quad i\quad\text{is even}\\
2\Delta x_{i}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad i\quad\text{is odd}.\end{cases}

By performing this above defined oscillatory grids in (
3.26
), we obtain

\displaystyle||\sigma(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x).

Therefore, we can say that VAM is first order consistent on oscillatory grids.

Remark 3.3
.

It is observed that over uniform, nonuniform and locally-uniform grids, the VAM executes first order consistency same as the FPT
[
11
]
. Over random grids, VAM exhibits first-order consistency, whereas the FPT becomes inconsistent over random grids
[
11
]
. This employs that VAM offers better accuracy in terms of consistency compared to FPT for solving the collisional nonlinear breakage equation over random grids. Furthermore, it is found that VAM achieves first-order consistency by applying to oscillatory grids.

Remark 3.4
.

Compiling the Remark
3.1
and
3.2
, we compute the spacial truncation error over all four different regions
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},R_{4}
. The order of consistency is obtained as

\displaystyle\qquad||\sigma(t)||=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{R}_{1}}|\sigma_{i}(t)|\sum_{i\in{R}_{2}}|\sigma_{i}(t)|+\sum_{i\in{R}_{3}}|\sigma_{i}(t)|+\sum_{i\in{R}_{4}}|\sigma_{i}(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{R}_{1}}\left|C^{\prime}_{i}\left(\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i+1}}-\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i-1}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i-1}}\right)\right|+\sum_{i\in{R}_{2}}\left|C^{\prime}_{i}\bigg(\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i+1}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i+1}}-\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i-1}}\bigg)\right|

\displaystyle+|\sigma_{1}(t)|+\left(|\sigma_{I_{1}}(t)|+|\sigma_{I_{2}}(t)|+\dots+|\sigma_{I_{m}}(t)|\right)+|\sigma_{I}(t)|+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{R}_{1}}\left|C^{\prime}_{i}\left(\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i+1}}-\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i-1}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i-1}}\right)\right|+\sum_{i\in{R}_{2}}\left|C^{\prime}_{i}\bigg(\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i+1}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i+1}}-\frac{\Delta x^{3}_{i}}{\Delta x_{i}+\Delta x_{i-1}}\bigg)\right|

(3.31)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{2}).

Thus VAM shows second order consistency that is
||\sigma(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{2})
over uniform, nonuniform and locally-uniform grids, whereas
||\sigma(t)||=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)
over random and oscillatory grids.

3.5.
Convergence

To prove the consistency and convergence of the new scheme VAM, the following theorem is utilized.

Theorem 3.2
.

[Convergence theorem]
Assume that the mapping
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
satisfies Lipschitz condition for
0\leq t\leq T
and for all
\boldsymbol{N},\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
where
\boldsymbol{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}
are projected exact and numerical solutions defined in (
2.4
) and (
2.15
) respectively. Then a consistent discretization method is also convergent and the order of the convergence is the same order as the consistency.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}(t)
be the global discretization error and is defined by
\boldsymbol{E}(t)=\boldsymbol{N}(t)-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)
for all
\boldsymbol{N},\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
. Now, we can write

(3.32)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{E}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{N}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{N}(t))}-\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t))}.

From Proposition
3.1
,
\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}
satisfies Lipschitz condition, so there exist Lipschitz constant
\gamma<\infty
such that

(3.33)

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{N})-\boldsymbol{\hat{J}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}})||\leq\gamma||\boldsymbol{N}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||\hskip 5.69046pt\text{ for all}\hskip 5.69046pt\boldsymbol{N},\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}.

Using Lipschitz condition (
3.33
) in (
3.32
), we obtain

(3.34)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}||\boldsymbol{E}(t)||}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq||\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{N}(t))}-\boldsymbol{\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}(t))}||\leq\gamma||\boldsymbol{N}-\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}||=\gamma||\boldsymbol{E}(t)||.

The above inequality implies that

(3.35)

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{E}(t)||\leq||\boldsymbol{E}(0)||\exp{(\gamma t)}.

When
\Delta x\to 0
, with the relation
||\boldsymbol{E}(0)||=0
and by the definition of global dicretization error, we deduce that
||\boldsymbol{E}(t)||=0
that is if the new discretization method is consistent, then it is convergent and the order of the convergence is same as the order of consistency.
∎

Thus, from the convergence theorem
3.2
, we can say that the numerical VAM (
2.15
) is convergent and the order of convergence of the VAM is same as the order of consistency.

4.
Two-dimensional collisional breakage equation

In particulate process, a particle is characterized by different properties such as volume, energy, moisture content etc. These properties need to be defined by more than one-dimension. Here, we assume two particle properties
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. The corresponding collisional nonlinear breakage equation in two particle properties (or dimensions)is written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n(x_{1},x_{2},t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{x_{1}}^{\infty}\int_{x_{2}}^{\infty}\beta(x_{1},x_{2}|y_{1},y_{2};z_{1},z_{2})\mathcal{K}(y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2})n(y_{1},y_{2},t)n(z_{1},z_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{d}z_{1}

(4.1)

\displaystyle-n(x_{1},x_{2},t)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})n(y_{1},y_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1},

with the initial condition

(4.2)

n(x_{1},x_{2},0)=n_{0}(x_{1},x_{2})\geq 0,\quad\text{for}\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Here, all the functions present in equation (
4.1
) are defined similar to the one-dimensional collisional nonlinear breakage equation.
The moments of the particles with properties
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
is given by

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{r_{1},r_{2}}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}x_{1}^{r_{1}}x_{2}^{r_{2}}n(x_{1},x_{2},t)\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1},\quad\text{for all}\quad r_{1},r_{2}=0,1,2,\dots.

Here, the zeroth order moment
\mathcal{M}_{0,0}(t)
represents the total number of particles, whereas the moments
\mathcal{M}_{1,0}(t)
and
\mathcal{M}_{0,1}(t)
denotes the total amount of property with respect to
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively and the moment
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}(t)
describes the hypervolume of the particles.

We truncate the model in the finite computational domain
[0,x_{\max}]^{2}
with
x_{\max}<\infty
and the time is taken as
[0,T]
. Next we discretize the computational domain as cartesian grid into
I_{1}I_{2}
cells as
\Lambda_{i,j}:=[x_{1,i-1/2},x_{1,i+1/2}]\times[x_{2,j-1/2},x_{2,j+1/2}]
with
1\leq i\leq I_{1}
and
1\leq j\leq I_{2}
. Moreover,

x_{1,1/2}:=0,\quad x_{2,1/2}:=0,\quad x_{1,I_{1}+1/2}:=R_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2,I_{2}+1/2}:=R_{2}.

The representative of the particle population in the cell
(i,j)
is denoted by
(x_{1,i},x_{2,j})
where

x_{1,i}:=\frac{x_{1,i-1/2}+x_{1,i+1/2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2,j}:=\frac{x_{2,j-1/2}+x_{2,j+1/2}}{2},

and also

\Delta x_{1,i}:=x_{1,i+1/2}-x_{1,i-1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta x_{2,j}:=x_{2,j+1/2}-x_{2,j-1/2}.

Let
N_{i,j}(t)
be the discrete number density in
\Lambda_{i,j}
at time
t
is defined by

(4.4)

\displaystyle N_{i,j}(t)=\int_{\Lambda_{i,j}}n(x_{1},x_{2},t)\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

Integrating equation truncated equation (
4.1
) with respect to the volume variables
x_{1,i}
and
x_{2,j}
over each cell
\Lambda_{i,j}
, we obtain

(4.5)

\frac{\mathrm{d}N_{i,j}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=B_{i,j}(t)-D_{i,j}(t).

Here,
B_{i,j}(t)
is the birth rate and
D_{i,j}(t)
is the death rate of particles and are given by

\displaystyle B_{i,j}(t):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda_{i,j}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\int_{x_{1}}^{R_{1}}\int_{x_{2}}^{R_{2}}\beta(x_{1},x_{2}|y_{1},y_{2};z_{1},z_{2})\mathcal{K}(y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2})

(4.6a)

\displaystyle\hskip 108.12054pt\times n(y_{1},y_{2},t)n(z_{1},z_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1},

and

(4.6b)

\displaystyle D_{i,j}(t):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda_{i,j}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})n(x_{1},x_{2},t)n(y_{1},y_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

Putting
\displaystyle n(x_{1},x_{2},t)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{I_{2}}N_{i,j}(t)\delta(x-x_{1,i})\delta(x-x_{2,j})
in the above equations (
4.5
)-(
4.6
), we get the discrete formulation of two-dimensional collisional nonlinear breakage equation as

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{N}_{i,j}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{B}_{i,j}(t)-\hat{D}_{i,j}(t),

where
\hat{N}_{i}(t)
discrete approximation of the density function at time
t\geq 0
.
Here, birth terms and death terms are defined by

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{i,j}(t):=

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{1}=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{p_{2}=1}^{I_{2}}\sum_{q_{1}=i}^{I_{1}}\sum_{q_{2}=j}^{I_{2}}\beta_{i,j}^{q_{1},q_{2}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}},x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}}){N}_{q_{1},q_{2}}(t){N}_{p_{1},p_{2}}(t),

(4.8b)

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{i,j}(t):=

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{1}=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{p_{2}=1}^{I_{2}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1,i},x_{2,j},x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}}){N}_{i,j}(t){N}_{p_{1},p_{2}}(t),

where
\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}^{q_{1},q_{2}}=\int_{x_{1,i-1/2}}^{p_{1,i}^{q_{1}}}\int_{x_{2,j-1/2}}^{p_{2,j}^{q_{2}}}\beta({x}_{1},{x}_{2}|x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}};x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}})\mathrm{d}{x}_{2}\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}
and for all
k=1,2
with
l=i,j
,

\displaystyle p_{k,l}^{q_{k}}=\begin{cases}x_{k,l},&\quad\mbox{if}\quad q_{k}=l,\\
x_{k,l+1/2},&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The total mass flux of the property for
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively over
\Lambda_{i,j}
are given by

\displaystyle V_{{x_{1},i}}(t):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda_{i,j}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\int_{x_{1}}^{R_{1}}\int_{x_{2}}^{R_{2}}x_{1}\beta(x_{1},x_{2}|y_{1},y_{2};z_{1},z_{2})\mathcal{K}(y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 108.12054pt\times n(y_{1},y_{2},t)n(z_{1},z_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1},

and

\displaystyle V_{x_{2},i}(t):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda_{i,j}}\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\int_{0}^{R_{2}}\int_{x_{1}}^{R_{1}}\int_{x_{2}}^{R_{2}}x_{2}\beta(x_{1},x_{2}|y_{1},y_{2};z_{1},z_{2})\mathcal{K}(y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2})

(4.10)

\displaystyle\hskip 108.12054pt\times n(y_{1},y_{2},t)n(z_{1},z_{2},t)\mathrm{d}y_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

The discrete mass flux of property for
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively over
\Lambda_{i,j}
are obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{x_{1},i}(t):=

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{1}=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{p_{2}=1}^{I_{2}}\sum_{q_{1}=i}^{I_{1}}\sum_{q_{2}=j}^{I_{2}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}},x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}}){N}_{q_{1},q_{2}}(t){N}_{p_{1},p_{2}}(t)

(4.11)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\int_{x_{1,i-1/2}}^{p_{1,i}^{q_{1}}}\int_{x_{2,j-1/2}}^{p_{2,j}^{q_{2}}}x_{1}\beta({x}_{1},{x}_{2}|x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}};x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}})\mathrm{d}{x}_{2}\mathrm{d}{x}_{1},

and

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{x_{2},j}(t):=

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{1}=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{p_{2}=1}^{I_{2}}\sum_{q_{1}=i}^{I_{1}}\sum_{q_{2}=j}^{I_{2}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}},x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}}){N}_{q_{1},q_{2}}(t){N}_{p_{1},p_{2}}(t)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\int_{x_{1,i-1/2}}^{p_{1,i}^{q_{1}}}\int_{x_{2,j-1/2}}^{p_{2,j}^{q_{2}}}x_{2}\beta({x}_{1},{x}_{2}|x_{1,q_{1}},x_{2,q_{2}};x_{1,p_{1}},x_{2,p_{2}})\mathrm{d}{x}_{2}\mathrm{d}{x}_{1}.

The average property values of all new born particles for property with respect to
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively in
\Lambda_{i,j}
are given by

(4.13)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{{1,i}}:=\frac{\hat{V}_{x_{1},i}}{\hat{B}_{i,j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{v}_{{2,j}}:=\frac{\hat{V}_{x_{2},j}}{\hat{B}_{i,j}}.

Similar argument is used as one-dimensional case to formulate the numerical scheme VAM.

Here,
\boldsymbol{\hat{N}}=\left\{\hat{N}_{i,j}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{I_{2}}
be nonnegative vector for
i=1,2,\dots,I_{1}
and
j=1,2,\dots,I_{2}
.

4.1.
Cell volume average method in two-dimension

The numerical scheme for two-dimensional collisional nonlinear breakage equation by VAM is defined by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{N}_{i,j}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}\lambda_{i,j}^{-,-}(\bar{v}_{{1,i-p}},\bar{v}_{{2,j-q}})H[(-1)^{p}({x}_{1,i-p}-\bar{v}_{1,i-p})]H[(-1)^{q}({x}_{2,j-q}-\bar{v}_{2,j-q})]\hat{B}_{i-p,j-q}(t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}\lambda_{i,j}^{-,+}(\bar{v}_{{1,i-p}},\bar{v}_{{2,j+q}})H[(-1)^{p}({x}_{1,i-p}-\bar{v}_{1,i-p})]H[(-1)^{q}({x}_{2,j+q}-\bar{v}_{2,j+q})]\hat{B}_{i-p,j+q}(t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}\lambda_{i,j}^{+,-}(\bar{v}_{{1,i+p}},\bar{v}_{{2,j-q}})H[(-1)^{p}({x}_{1,i+p}-\bar{v}_{1,i+p})]H[(-1)^{q}({x}_{2,j-q}-\bar{v}_{2,j-q})]\hat{B}_{i+p,j-q}(t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=0}^{1}\sum_{q=0}^{1}\lambda_{i,j}^{+,+}(\bar{v}_{{1,i+p}},\bar{v}_{{2,j+q}})H[(-1)^{p}({x}_{1,i+p}-\bar{v}_{1,i+p})]H[(-1)^{q}({x}_{2,j+q}-\bar{v}_{2,j+q})]\hat{B}_{i+p,j+q}(t)

(4.14)

\displaystyle-\sum_{p=1}^{I_{x_{1}}}\sum_{q=1}^{I_{x_{2}}}\mathcal{K}(x_{1,i},x_{1,p},x_{2,j},x_{2,q})\hat{N}_{i,j}\hat{N}_{p,q},

where
\hat{B}_{i,j}(t)
defined by (
4.8
) and
\displaystyle\lambda_{i,j}^{\pm,\pm}(\bar{v}_{1,i},\bar{v}_{2,j}):=\frac{\displaystyle(\bar{v}_{1,i}-x_{1,i\pm 1})(\bar{v}_{2,j}-x_{2,j\pm 1})}{\displaystyle(x_{1,i}-x_{1,i\pm 1})(x_{2,j}-x_{2,j\pm 1})}

and
H
is the Heaviside function defined as in previous one-dimensional case.

6.
Conclusion

This study provided a comprehensive stability and consistency analysis of the new scheme volume average method for the collisional nonlinear breakage equation. It documented the mathematical and numerical results in comparison with the existing scheme fixed pivot techniques. It is noteworthy that the new scheme achieves first order convergence rate over random grids, whereas the existing scheme fixed pivot techniques are inconsistent over random grids. Moreover, it is shown that the new scheme is first order convergence rate over oscillatory grids. Furthermore, our considered equation is extended for two-dimensional case over rectangular grids.
Most importantly, numerical experiments have been performed successfully for one and two-dimensional collisional nonlinear breakage equation with two test examples for each case. Additionally, we explored a detailed error estimation for the new scheme.
