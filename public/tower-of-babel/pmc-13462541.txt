Synthesis and sorption performance of imidazole-based hydrogels for selective palladium recovery in green hydrometallurgical processes
Authors: Saad Alrashdi, Nourah Alshahrani, Hamdy Khamees Thabet, Sobhi M. Gomha, Magdi E. A. Zaki, Amr A. Sayed Alahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel poly(ethylene glycol)/acrylic acid/imidazole (PEG/AAc/IMZ) hydrogel was synthesized via free-radical polymerization and evaluated as an adsorbent for the recovery of Pd(II) ions from aqueous solutions. The synthesized hydrogel was characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), confirming the successful incorporation of the functional groups responsible for metal-ion binding. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of contact time, solution pH, initial Pd(II) concentration, phase ratio (V/m), and temperature on the adsorption performance. The adsorption efficiency was strongly influenced by the solution pH, with optimum Pd(II) uptake achieved at pH 4. Under the optimized conditions (contact time = 60 min, V/m = 0.1 L/g, initial Pd(II) concentration = 100 mg/L, and temperature = 25 °C), the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel exhibited an adsorption efficiency of 85%. Equilibrium data were best described by the Langmuir isotherm model (R2 = 0.989), yielding a maximum adsorption capacity of 24.26 mg/g, which is in good agreement with the experimental value (25.15 mg/g). Kinetic studies revealed that Pd(II) adsorption followed the pseudo-second-order model, indicating that the adsorption rate is governed by the availability of active adsorption sites. Thermodynamic investigations demonstrated that the adsorption process was spontaneous and exothermic in nature. Furthermore, selectivity experiments performed in the presence of competing metal ions (Pt, Ni, and Zn) confirmed the preferential uptake of Pd(II), following the order: Pd > Ni > Pt > Zn. The developed PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel therefore represents a promising adsorbent for the selective recovery of Pd(II) from multicomponent aqueous systems.

Introduction

The extraordinary qualities of the platinum group metals (PGMs) make them indispensable in many different sectors. Because of their critical role in contemporary technology and new sustainable solutions, the global demand for these metals is rising at an alarming rate. Palladium is a well-known PGM that has many respected uses in fields as varied as electronics1, medicine2, and jewelry3.

Complex mineral assemblages containing palladium are common in ores of the precious metal4, which is often found with nickel, copper, platinum, rhodium, and gold5. From simple sulfide deposits to intricate layered intrusions, the mineralogy of these ores can vary greatly6–8. The presence of palladium in these ores can manifest in several ways, such as individual mineral phases9 or solid solutions embedded in host minerals10. Cooperite, braggite, and vysotskite are notable minerals that contain palladium11. Palladium plays an indispensable role in modern clean-energy technologies. As a key platinum-group metal (PGM), it is widely used in automotive catalytic converters for emission control, in three-way catalysts (TWCs) that reduce NOx, CO, and hydrocarbons. In the hydrogen economy, palladium serves as an electrocatalyst in proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) and as a hydrogen-selective membrane for ultra-high-purity H₂ production. It is also essential in electronics (multilayer ceramic capacitors) and in emerging CO₂ electroreduction systems, where robust palladium hydride catalysts enable high-efficiency formation with excellent CO tolerance. This growing demand, coupled with the scarcity and geographical concentration of primary palladium resources (mainly Russia and South Africa), makes efficient extraction and recovery critically important12,13.

Due to the high demand for palladium in the automobile industry, spent catalytic converters from these vehicles are the primary raw materials for palladium recycling. There are considerable quantities of palladium and other precious metals in these converters14–16.

Sorption is a well-known physicochemical method that recovers metal ions from low-concentration effluents with little space occupied, minimal operating costs, and through the passive sorption and/or complexation of metals by sorbents17 especially in the extraction and separation of precious metal ions18–20.

PEG, which has a unique branched/linear conformation, can act as a ligand for the complexation of PGMs. To make this material stable, several studies have incorporated its crosslinked products into biopolymers, such as chitosan21, and carrageenan22,23. The imidazole-bearing resin shown high efficacy for the recovery of Pd and Pt from dilute acidic solutions24. The zirconium‑based metal–organic framework Zr‑L1/L2, which incorporates both amino (L1) and imidazole‑diimine (L2) functional groups, efficiently extracts palladium and gold from river water with high adsorption capacities25.

Recent efforts have focused on developing new materials with high adsorption capacities, superior selectivity, high stability (longer shelf life), and low cost. Over the past fifty years, hydrogels have been used in numerous fields, including medicine, pharmaceuticals, agriculture, and the environment. However, the use of hydrogels as adsorbent phases has recently garnered increasing attention, primarily due to their structure of highly hydrophilic polymer networks capable of absorbing and retaining large quantities of water without dissolving26. The ability to modify the chemical and physicochemical properties of these materials allows for the production and use of more selective phases. PEG/AAC is an example of such a hydrogel, as it contains groups that give the hydrogel unique properties, such as COOH and OH groups27.

the recent development for green hydrometallurgy is to use of solvents such as ionic liquids (ILs) and deep eutectic solvents has made it possible to eliminate the use of volatile organic solvents in solvent-extraction systems28–31. Additionally, processes such as sorption32–34, precipitation35, membrane separation36, and solvent extraction37.

In this study, a PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel was developed by modifying it and adding imidazole to PEG and AAc to prepare hydrogel adsorbent for green metallurgical separation of palladium (II) from aqueous solution. The recovery of palladium, a valuable critical metal, from aqueous solutions is linked to the sustainability of the current effort. Such recovery lessens the environmental impact of palladium-containing waste stream discharge, encourages resource conservation, and permits metal recycling. Therefore, the application of hydrogel rather than the hydrogel’s inherent composition is the main source of the study’s environmental advantage. The synthesis procedure and characterization of PEG/AAc/IMZ were systematically described using the appropriate analytical techniques. Furthermore, the effects of key adsorption parameters, including solution acidity, contact time, adsorbent dosage, and initial Pd(II) concentration, were thoroughly investigated and discussed. A synthetic solution for recovery of palladium from other interfering ions was also investigated. A comparative evaluation of previously reported adsorbent materials was conducted to assess the adsorption performance of the PEG/AAc/IMZ/Pd(II) system.

Experimental

Materials

PEG and AAc were procured from Merck & Co. (Darmstadt, Germany) and were used as received. Potassium persulfate (KPS) and N,N′-Methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 2-vinylimidazole and palladium chloride were obtained from sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All chemicals employed in this work are of analytical grade (AR) and used without further purification.

Preparation of PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel

The PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel was prepared using free-radical grafting polymerization, with KPS as the initiator and MBA as the crosslinking agent. A 5% PEG solution was prepared by dissolving 5 g in 100 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, 10 mL of this solution (corresponding to 0.5 g PEG) was transferred into a 100 mL conical flask. Then, 0.2 g of 2-vinylimidazole was dissolved in different amount of acrylic acid (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 mL), followed by the addition of 0.01 g MBA and 0.005 g KPS, and this mixture was added to the reaction mixture with continuous stirring for 60 min. Nitrogen gas was then introduced into the solution for 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen. The required amount of potassium persulfate was added to the reaction mixture. The polymerization process was carried out for four hours at 80 °C. After completion, the process was stopped and allowed to cool to room temperature. The hydrogel was immersed in water for two days to remove unreacted monomers and the initiator. The final hydrogel was then dried in a heated oven at 50 °C for 24 h. Characterization

PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel before and after extraction of palladium were analyzed by FTIR using Shimadzu infrared spectrometer (Model 400, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) with FTIR spectra spanning the spectral range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ using the KBr pellet technique. Results and discussion

FTIR

Figure 1 show the differences in the FT-IR spectra of the PEG/AAc/IMZ before and after extraction of Pd(II). As shown in Fig. 1a the detected signal at 3311.7 cm− 1 was attributed to O-H stretching, whereas the peak at 1723.6 cm− 1 corresponded to C=O for the carboxylic group of AAc. The peaks at 1564.8 and 1401.2 cm− 1 correspond to the C=N and C-N bonds of the imidazole ring. After sorption of Pd(II), the stretching vibration peaks of the C=N and C-N bonds in the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel were shifted to 1542.4 cm− 1 and 1454.6 cm− 111 (Fig. 1b), respectively, suggesting that IMZ was probably coordinated to Pd(II) via the N atom in the imidazole ring. These shifts give evidence that Pd(II) coordinates with the nitrogen donor atom of the imidazole ring through the creation of coordinate bonds.

The SEM and EDX analysis

The SEM image of the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel, both free and Pd-extracted, is shown in Fig. 2. The hydrogel exhibits a highly porous structure before extraction (Fig. 2a), after which there is a notable reduction in pore size (Fig. 2b). To verify the existence of Pd(II) in the hydrogel, EDX spectra of the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel and the Pd-extracted sample are examined (Fig. 2a, b). The EDX spectrum of the prepared hydrogel exhibits carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen content of 44.22%, 10.08%, and 45.70%, respectively, therefore confirming the existence of imidazole in the prepared PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel. Also, the EDX spectrum of the intact hydrogel did not exhibit the typical peak of Pd, but the EDX spectrum of the Pd-loaded hydrogel clearly displayed the Pd peak with 8.88%, with intensity according to the metal concentration. The presence of the Pd element in the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel is confirmed by EDX spectra.

The gel fraction and swelling properties

Gel fraction was used to ascertain the hydrogel’s uncross-linked polymer fraction. It was found that gel fraction of hydrogels rose along with increased concentration of acrylic acid as shown in Fig. 3a. The prepared hydrogel with 0.5 ml of acrylic acid was insufficient to form a hydrogel. With increasing the acrylic acid concentration to 1 ml, the gel fraction reached 59.36%, and this rose further to 85.6% with 2.5 ml of acrylic acid. The swelling ratio of the PEG/AAc/IMZ hydrogel was examined, achieving roughly 18.39 g/g in water until equilibrium was attained after approximately 7 h, Fig. 3, at which juncture it included almost 94% water by weight. Consequently, its elevated porosity and water permeability facilitate the fast passage of palladium ions from the solution into the aqueous gel.

Batch investigation

Effect of solution pH

Figure 4a shows the results of the study on the effects of solution pH on the adsorption of Pd(II) by PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent. The significant affinity for Pd(II) sorption at a low pH (1.0–2.0) gives low uptake efficiency but at higher pH results in higher efficacy (3.0–5.0). From 1.0 to 5.0, researchers looked at how different starting pH levels affected the sorption of Pd(II) on PEG/AAc/IMZ, Fig. 4a which indicate that sorption process was most effective between pH 3.0 and 5. The sorption of the Pd(II) on PEG/AAc/IMZ under study is clearly caused by various attractions38.

PEG/AAc/IMZ was subjected to additional sorption studies at pH 4.0 for Pd(II) ions for other research experiments39.

Effect of contact time

Sorption of Pd(II) ions onto PEG/AAc/IMZ was investigated over a time span of 10 min to 120 min. Figure 4b show that after 60 min, the adsorption efficacy reached 85% of Pd(II) metal ions, and after that, the amount of Pd(II) that was sorbed remained steady at 85 % till time reached 120 min., indicating a quicker sorption process using the prepared PEG/AAc/IMZ. So that the sorption of palladium ions reached equilibrium after 60 min, the sorption happened within 10–60 min, at suggested conditions (V/m = 0.1 L/g, [Pd]=100 mg/l, PH = 4 and T = 25 °C) as shown in Fig. 4b.

The impact of the PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent weight

Another crucial aspect that has a major impact on the sorption process is the dose PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent, which determines how well the sorbent can bind to a specific beginning concentration of the metal ions being studied. the impact of PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent dose on the sorption of 100 mg/L Pd(II) from aqueous solution of pH = 4 in the range of 0.01–0.05 gm at contact time 60 min was thus studied, Fig. 4c. Based on the results, increasing the PEG/AAc/IMZ weights from 0.01 to 0.05 gm dramatically improved the adsorption efficiency (uptake%). Adding more adsorbent further improved the efficiency40. Also, because unsaturated accessible for metal ions as the there isn’t enough Pd(II) to fill all of the sorption sites at a constant concentration, the capacity decreased as the dosage of PEG/AAc/IMZ increased. It may also be associated with the buildup of adsorbents, which has been observed at larger dosages. The results showed that 0.025 g of adsorbent was the best dosage for PEG/AAc/IMZ across all trials.

The effect of Pd(II) concentration

Knowing the adsorption mechanism and the rate and efficiency of metal ion uptake from water solutions are both necessary for designing the adsorption system. Researchers tested solutions with varying concentrations of Pd(II) in the rage 100 to 500 mg/L using PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent and phase contact time = 60 min at pH = 4. For various starting concentrations of Pd(II) ions, Fig. 4d shows that adsorption efficiency of Pd(II) was affected by the initial Pd(II) concentration which reached to 85% at concentration 100 mg/L and sharply decreased to 50% at 500 mg/L. Adsorption of Pd(II) ions is shown to have a three-step kinetics. Adsorption of Pd(II) onto PEG/AAc/IMZ is rapid in the first (initial) stage because there are many available free adsorption sites; subsequent stages are slower until equilibrium is reached. The remaining sites become more difficult to access due to the increased repulsive forces caused by the deposited Pd(II) ions on the ion exchanger surface41,42. At higher initial concentrations of Pd(II), the kinetic curves are flatter, and the system equilibrium take more time to achieve.

Isotherm study

When planning sorption systems, the equilibrium adsorption isotherm plays a significant role. The Langmuir isotherm relies on the adsorbent’s active sites supporting monolayer adsorption. Adsorption on a heterogeneous (multi-layered) surface with homogeneous energy is explained by the Freundlich isotherm43. Empirical models can be utilized to conveniently estimate the maximal absorption of precious metals from experimental data such Pd(II) using) PEG/AAc/IMZ, but they cannot give a mechanistic insight of the adsorption events.

Figure 5a–e presents the adsorption isotherms of Pd(II) onto PEG/AAc/IMZ together with the corresponding fits obtained using the Langmuir, Freundlich, Redlich–Peterson, Temkin, and Halsey models. The calculated isotherm parameters are summarized in Table 1. Among the investigated models, the Langmuir isotherm provided the best fit to the experimental data, as evidenced by its highest correlation coefficient (R² = 0.998). Furthermore, the maximum adsorption capacity predicted by the Langmuir model (qmax = 24.26 mg/g) was in good agreement with the experimentally determined value (qexp = 25.15 mg/g). These findings suggest that Pd(II) adsorption onto PEG/AAc/IMZ predominantly occurs through monolayer coverage on a homogeneous distribution of active adsorption sites.

Sorption kinetics of PEG/AAc/IMZ-Pd(II) system

The kinetic parameters of the sorption process were established to complete the evaluation of the sorption mechanism of the Pd(II) using PEG/AAc/IMZ that were studied. This was accomplished by conducting sorption experiments on Pd ions (100 mg/L) under ideal conditions with 0.025 g (wet adsorbent) of the PEG/AAc/IMZ that is now under evaluation at pH = 4. Assumptions and verifications were made using the pseudo-first (PFO) and pseudo-second (PSO) adsorption kinetic models46. With the use of the graphical dependency of log (qe-qt) vs. t for the PFO and 1/qt vs. 1/t for the PSO models of adsorption kinetics, Fig. 6a, b, we were able to experimentally calculate the adsorption rate constants (k1 and k2) and basic kinetic parameters like the amount of adsorbed metal ions at equilibrium (qe). Using the regression coefficient, we could see whether the experimental data were compatible with the adsorption kinetics model that was postulated. Table 2 displays the kinetic parameters that were found for the processes that were evaluated.

According to the results, the pseudo-second order kinetics model works well for the adsorption kinetics of Pd onto PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent as R2=998 and the calculated capacity qe=9.1 mg/g is close to the experimental value qexp=10.5 mg/g. Thus, ion exchange or complexing processes, among others, are likely to represent the rate-limiting step in the process of metal ion binding to the adsorbent surface47.

Effect of temperature and thermodynamic studies

Under the optimized adsorption conditions (contact time = 60 min and V/m = 0.1 L/ g), the effect of temperature on the adsorption of 100 mg/L Pd(II) at pH 4 using PEG/AAc/IMZ beads was investigated over the temperature range of 15–65 °C. As shown in Fig. 7a, the sorption efficiency decreased markedly with increasing temperature, indicating that elevated temperatures are unfavorable for Pd(II) uptake. The thermodynamic parameters are presented in Fig. 7b; Table 3. The ΔG° values were negative at all studied temperatures, confirming the spontaneous nature of the adsorption process. However, the ΔG° values became less negative with increasing temperature, indicating a gradual decrease in adsorption spontaneity. Furthermore, the negative ΔH° value (− 34.22 kJ/mol) confirms that the adsorption process is exothermic, which is consistent with the observed decline in sorption efficiency at higher temperatures. The negative ΔS° value suggests a decrease in randomness at the solid–solution interface during the adsorption of Pd(II) onto PEG/AAc/IMZ beads.

Conclusions

Within this research, PEG/AAc/IMZ adsorbent was successfully employed for the recovery of Pd(II) from aqueous solutions. Under the optimized conditions, a sorption efficiency of 85% was achieved. Thermodynamic studies revealed that the adsorption process was spontaneous, as indicated by the negative ΔG° values, while the negative ΔH° value confirmed the exothermic nature of the process. The Langmuir model provided the best fit to the equilibrium data, with a maximum monolayer adsorption capacity of 24.15 mg/g at 298 K. Kinetic investigations showed that the adsorption process followed the pseudo-second-order model with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.9989. Selectivity studies demonstrated the preferential uptake of Pd(II) over competing metal ions in the order Pd > Ni > Pt > Zn. Furthermore, Pd(II) could be selectively desorbed using 0.5 M thiourea, indicating the potential applicability of PEG/AAc/IMZ for the selective recovery of palladium from multi-component aqueous solutions. A comparison with previously reported adsorbents highlights the promising adsorption performance of the developed material for Pd(II) recovery.
