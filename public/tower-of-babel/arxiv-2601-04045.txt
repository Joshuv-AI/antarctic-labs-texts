Recursive Program Synthesis from Sketches and Mixed-Quantifier Properties
Derek Egolf, Stavros Tripakis

Abstract
We present a novel approach to the automatic synthesis of recursive programs from mixed-quantifier first-order logic properties. Our approach uses Skolemization to reduce the mixed-quantifier synthesis problem to a ∀ ∗ \forall^{*} -synthesis problem, synthesizing witness-generating functions for introduced Skolem symbols alongside the target program. We tackle ∀ ∗ \forall^{*} -synthesis using a sketching-based, enumerative, counterexample-guided approach. Our algorithm learns syntactic constraints from counterexamples to prune the candidate space and employs a prophylactic pruning technique to avoid enumerating invalid candidates altogether. We evaluate our technique on 42 benchmarks, demonstrating that both counterexample generalization and prophylactic pruning significantly improve performance.



1
Introduction

We present a novel approach to the automatic synthesis of recursive programs
from
mixed-quantifier
first-order logic properties. Note that it is
undecidable even to verify that a candidate program satisfies a
mixed-quantifier property. Recursive programs have been synthesized from
examples
[
12
,
1
,
9
,
15
,
7
]
and from universally
quantified properties
[
14
,
13
,
17
,
11
]
. To our knowledge, ours
is the first approach to synthesize recursive programs from mixed-quantifier
properties.

It is often very natural to specify the desired behavior of a recursive program
using mixed-quantifier properties. For example, suppose we want to synthesize a
function
prefixb
that decides whether one list is a prefix of another.
The soundness of this function is naturally expressed as

\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{xs},\texttt{ys}::\text{({prefixb} \ {xs} \ {ys} )}\implies\langle\!\langle\exists\texttt{suffix}::\texttt{ys}=\texttt{xs}\cdot\texttt{suffix}\rangle\!\rangle\rangle\!\rangle

There is no equivalent property that uses only universal quantification and the
same set of function symbols. We address the presence of existential
quantifiers using a Skolemization-based approach. The Skolem function symbols
introduced by this technique pose difficulties for synthesis. We address these
difficulties, in essence, by synthesizing the
witness-generating function

for each Skolem function symbol, alongside the target program. This technique
allows us to reduce the mixed-quantifier synthesis problem to a synthesis
problem involving only universal quantification (
\forall^{*}
-synthesis).

We tackle the
\forall^{*}
-synthesis problem using the classical sketching
paradigm
[
19
]
. In this paradigm, the user provides a
sketch
,
which is a partial program with holes, and the synthesis tool fills in the
holes to produce a complete program that satisfies the given specification.
Sketching allows the user to syntactically bias the space of candidate programs
using their expert knowledge. Sketching does not trivialize the synthesis
problem. Typically, a sketch still characterizes an infinite candidate space.
Even if we bound the size
n
of the expressions used to fill the sketch, the
candidate space usually grows exponentially in
n
.

To address this problem, we adopt a novel enumerative and counterexample-guided
approach. We carefully enumerate the space of candidate programs using a lazy,
on-the-fly approach. When (and only when) we encounter a candidate program, we
use a state-of-the-art counterexample generator
[
4
]
to search for a
counterexample. Our algorithm learns syntactic constraints on the candidate
programs from each counterexample. These syntactic constraints are used to
prune the candidate space. Our pruning technique is
prophylactic
: we make
a best effort to avoid enumerating pruned candidates altogether, rather
than iterating over them and then rejecting them. We evaluate our technique on
42 benchmarks with encouraging results.

Our
\forall^{*}

Our synthesis algorithm works by (carefully) enumerating candidate programs and
checking them against the specification. If a candidate program is incorrect, a

counterexample generator

demonstrates that the program is incorrect. This suggests a

programs is typically far too large. To address this challenge, our algorithm
also

each counterexample, producing a constraint on the
syntax of candidate programs. As the algorithm explores a candidate at each
step, new counterexamples are found, and the set of syntactic constraints
becomes larger, ruling out more and more of the search space.

3.1
Lazy, On-the-fly Enumeration without Pruning

A brute force algorithm can enumerate candidate programs in two phases: first
enumerate all concepts from the grammar
G
(up to some size bound), and then
combine these to obtain all possible emergents. Recall that emergents are
tuples of concepts and candidate programs (sketch completions) are obtained by
substituting emergents into sketches from the multi-sketch
\mathcal{M}
. In
Fig.
2

\mathcal{U}
on line
4
by a candidate space object
\mathcal{U}
, which is
instantiated on line

as a function of the grammar
G
and the
multi-sketch
\mathcal{M}
. Internally,
\mathcal{U}
maintains two objects: a concept space
and a list of emergent spaces (one for each sketch in
\mathcal{M}
). We now describe
each of these objects in turn and then describe how they can be combined to
obtain lazy, on-the-fly enumeration of candidate programs.

Concept Enumeration.

First, note that the concept space is independent of the multi-sketch
\mathcal{M}

It depends only on the grammar
G
. Initially, the concept space
enumerates
terminal concepts
(i.e., constants and variables) from the
grammar using

of the form
N\to a
, where

terminal symbol or constant. A cache tracks each concept that has already been
enumerated, along with the non-terminal(s) used to generate each. Once all
terminal rules have been exhausted, the concept space enumerates a new

from
the grammar. It then draws, if possible, a concept for each
N_{i}
from the
cache, and applies the function
f
to obtain a new concept. If it is not
possible to draw a combination of concepts for the
N_{i}
in such a way that
obtains a new concept, the concept space repeats the process with a
different rule. If
none
of the rules can be used to obtain a new concept,
the concept space indicates that it has been exhausted.

As a simple example, suppose the grammar contains the rules
\texttt{E}\to\texttt{x}

and
\texttt{E}\to\text{({+} \ {E} \ {E} )}
. Our strategy first enumerates the
concept
x
, using the first rule (a terminal rule). Then, using the
second rule,
it would enumerate the following concepts:

(+ x x )
,

(+ x
(+ x x )
)
,

(+
(+ x x )
x )
,

(+
(+ x x )

(+ x x )
)
, etc.
Notice in this case that when the cache contains
x
, there is only one way
to combine concepts. But when the cache also contains
(+ x x )
, there are 4 ways to combine concepts. In general, the number of ways to
obtain a new concept is the product of the number of cached concepts for each
non-terminal in the selected rule.

A naive implementation of this enumeration procedure might maintain, for each
rule, something like an explicit list of all appropriate combinations of cached
concepts and then extend it (careful to avoid duplicates) whenever a new
concept is added to the cache. This approach is inefficient. Instead, we use an
on-the-fly product construction to lazily enumerate these combinations for each
rule. We construct new lazy products every time a new concept is added to the
cache, and we lazily chain the products together so that we can obtain a stream
of concepts without maintaining an explicit list.

Emergent Enumeration.

Suppose we have an explicit list
L
of concepts from the grammar
G
and that
there is just one sketch
S
in
\mathcal{M}
. Let
L_{h}
be the list of concepts in

L
that are generated by the non-terminal of the hole
h
; i.e., those
concepts in
L
that are appropriate to use to fill
h
. To enumerate all
emergents for
S
, we need only enumerate all tuples in
L_{h_{1}}\times\ldots\times L_{h_{k}}
, where
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
are the holes in
S
. To enumerate
completions, we simply substitute each tuple into
S
. In general, there are
multiple sketches in
\mathcal{M}
, and we can apply this procedure to each sketch
independently and take a union. As for concept enumeration, we can use an
on-the-fly product construction to lazily enumerate
L_{h_{1}}\times\ldots\times L_{h_{k}}
for each sketch.

On-the-fly Candidate Enumeration.

We have shown how to lazily enumerate concepts from the grammar
G
and how to
lazily enumerate emergents from an explicit list of concepts.
Fig.
3
shows how to lazily enumerate emergents from a lazily
enumerated list of concepts (rather than an explicit list). At a high level,
the method repeatedly tries to obtain a new emergent by querying each emergent
space and querying the concept space for a new concept when all
emergent spaces are exhausted under the current list of concepts.
Line
3
loops over each emergent space, and queries each
one for a new emergent (line
4
). If an emergent is found, it
is returned (line
6
). If no emergent is found, the concept
space is queried for a new concept. If the concept space is exhausted
(line
8
), then the entire candidate space is exhausted,
and
None
is returned. Otherwise, the new concept is added to each emergent
space (lines
10
and
11
). Once a new
concept is added, the loop repeats and the emergent spaces have a chance
to produce an emergent with the new concept. The emergent spaces maintain
a chain of lazy products and they extend this chain with new lazy products
whenever a new concept is added. These new lazy products capture all emergents
that use the new concept along with the previously cached concepts.

Although on-the-fly enumeration allows us to obtain a new candidate program
without generating an explicit list of programs all at once, this enumeration
strategy is still not sufficient in practice. We will now discuss how to learn
syntactic constraints from counterexamples to prune the search space, then we
return to enumeration strategies that take advantage of these constraints.

3.2
Counterexample Generalization

In this section, we show how to generalize counterexamples to obtain syntactic
constraints on candidate programs. Counterexamples can witness various kinds of
violations: contract violations (i.e., the program is contract inadmissible),
measure violations (i.e., the program is non-decreasing), or property
violations.

Contract Violations.

A key idea of our approach is to use the input contracts of background
functions to prune the search space. In particular, if a candidate program
violates an input contract of a background function, we can analyze this
violation to obtain syntactic constraints that rule out not only the
current candidate but also many other candidates that would exhibit the
same contract violation.

3.3
Retrospective and Prophylactic Pruning

We can incorporate the syntactic constraints learned from counterexample
generalization into the enumeration procedure described in
Section
3.1
. In particular, we can modify the procedure so that
emergents violating any constraint are skipped. We call the modification
retrospective pruning
. In particular, when an emergent space is queried for a
new emergent, it checks if the next emergent satisfies the accumulated
syntactic constraints. If it does, that emergent is returned. If it does not,
that emergent is skipped, and a new emergent from the lazy product is obtained.
This filtering is repeated until either a valid emergent is found or the
emergent space is exhausted. We call this approach retrospective because in
order to identify that an emergent is invalid, we first have to iterate over it
and only then can we check if it satisfies the constraints.

A better approach is
prophylactic pruning
, which avoids iterating over
many invalid emergents in the first place. This approach exploits the fact that
in many cases, the syntactic constraints only mention a small number of holes.
This observation allows us to identify constraint violation after only filling
a subset of the holes in a sketch. For instance, suppose there are 20 holes and
a constraint mentions only holes
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
. After filling
h_{1}
and

h_{2}
, we can check the partial emergent against the constraint. If the partial
emergent violates the constraint, we can skip filling the remaining 18 holes,
backtrack, and try a new combination of concepts for
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
.

3.4
Multi-function Synthesis

Our algorithm thus far handles the synthesis of a single function. No major
changes are required to handle multi-function synthesis. For purposes of
enumeration, it makes no difference if the emergents fill holes in one function
body or holes that are spread across multiple function bodies. Therefore, the
enumeration procedure need not change to accommodate multiple functions.
Generalizing from contract violations and measure violations is also unaffected
by the shift to multi-function synthesis because these violations are always
localized to a single function body. Generalizing from property violations in
the single function setting requires us to obtain a trace from executing the
function on a counterexample input. The multi-function setting is identical
except that we may cross function boundaries during this execution and a
generalization may simultaneously constrain the contents of holes residing in
distinct functions.

3.5
Mixed-Quantifier Synthesis

Our algorithm thus far handles only
\forall^{*}
-synthesis, where all
properties contain only universal quantifiers. However,
Problem
1
allows properties with both universal and existential
quantifiers. We now explain how to solve the general Problem
1

by reducing it to
\forall^{*}
-synthesis through Skolemization.

In a nutshell, our reduction computes a
\forall^{*}
-property involving a
witness-generating function
for each existentially quantified variable. We
then ask the
\forall^{*}
-synthesis algorithm to synthesize these witness
generating functions along with the original functions to be synthesized.

Part of this reduction uses the standard
Skolemization

algorithm
[
8
]

to eliminate the existential
quantifiers from the property. For instance, the property

\langle\!\langle\forall x::\langle\!\langle\exists y::P(x,y)\rangle\!\rangle\rangle\!\rangle
becomes
\langle\!\langle\forall x::P(x,g(x))\rangle\!\rangle
under Skolemization. But
our reduction is not as simple as applying Skolemization. In particular, the
function symbol
g
in the Skolemized example is not defined by the background
theory, nor is
g
one of the
f_{1},...,f_{n}
being synthesized. Our
counterexample generation procedure expects each function symbol to have an
executable definition, which the Skolem function symbol
g
lacks.

Our solution to this challenge is to treat
g
as any other function being
synthesized. That is, we synthesize
g
alongside
f_{1},...,f_{n}
, as well as any
other Skolem function symbols introduced by Skolemization. Hence, we note that
Problem
1
requires the user to
annotate
each existentially
quantified variable with a multi-sketch to describe the space of possible
witness-generating functions for that Skolem function symbol. We infer the
signatures for these functions and automatically generate the non-existential
properties. The alternative to this automation is for the user to manually
encode an instance of Problem
1
as a
\forall^{*}
-synthesis
instance, which is tedious and error-prone.

Bit-vector Monotonicity (monotonic, benchmark #38).

A bit-vector is monotonically non-decreasing (“monotonic” for brevity) if it
is of the form
\texttt{0}^{p}\texttt{1}^{s}
for some
p,s\geq 0
. For instance,

000111
and
111
are monotonic, but
10
and
010
are not.
The soundness of an algorithm for checking monotonicity is naturally stated as

\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{xs}::\langle\!\langle\exists\texttt{p},\texttt{s}::\texttt{(monotonic xs)}\implies\texttt{xs}=\texttt{0}^{\texttt{p}}\cdot\texttt{1}^{\texttt{s}}\rangle\!\rangle\rangle\!\rangle
. This property includes multiple
existential quantifiers, i.e, we existentially quantify over both
p
and

s
.

Prefix Minimum (prefixmin, benchmark #39).

An integer
x
is a prefix minimum of an integer list
xs
if
x
is
a minimum of some prefix of
xs
. For instance,
2
is a prefix minimum
of
(3 4 2 2 5 1)
because
(3 4 2)
is a prefix,
2
is a member of
that prefix, and
2
is less than all other members of that prefix. On the
other hand,
4
is not a prefix minimum of
(3 4 2 2 5 1)
because all
prefixes that contain
4
also contain
3
. We can state the soundness
of
prefixmin
, a prefix minimum checker, as

\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{x},\texttt{xs}::\langle\!\langle\exists\texttt{i}::\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{j}::\texttt{(prefixmin x xs)}\implies(\texttt{x}=\texttt{xs}[i]\land(j<i\implies\texttt{x}<\texttt{xs}[j]))\rangle\!\rangle\rangle\!\rangle\rangle\!\rangle

I.e., if the synthesized function identifies
x
as a prefix minimum
of
xs
, then there must exist an index
i
such that
x
is the
i
’th element of
xs
and
x
is less than all elements of
xs
that
come before index
i
(i.e., at index
j
for all
j
<
i
). This
property is an example with a universal quantifier nested inside an existential
quantifier.

Guarantees and Post-hoc Verification

Our synthesis tool offers the following guarantee: if it returns a program, the
counterexample generator did not find a violation of the given properties.
Although this does not necessarily imply correctness, we were able to formally
prove correctness of all 41 benchmarks solved by
Proph
. Specifically, of the
41 benchmarks solved by
Proph
, all but 7 were
automatically
verified by
ACL2s without user intervention. The remaining 7 benchmarks required a
relatively small manual effort, namely, the addition of helper lemmas and/or
the specification of an induction scheme. Our artifact includes successful
proof scripts for all 41 benchmarks solved by
Proph
. Since this
verification problem is undecidable in general, it is reasonable for our
tool to only guarantee correctness modulo counterexample generation. Our
tool inherits the same guarantees as the state-of-the-art in counterexample
generation and automated theorem proving, and will improve as the
state-of-the-art improves.

Related Work

Recursive Program Synthesis from Examples.

All of
[
12
,
1
,
9
,
15
,
7
]
synthesize recursive programs
from input-output examples. Our work differs from these works in that we
synthesize programs from general first-order specifications. Of these works,
only
Smyth

[
12
]
supports user-provided sketches.

Para

[
9
]
internally uses templates and fills them with
recursion-free expressions, but these templates
are not provided by the user.

Recursive Program Synthesis from Properties.

All of
[
14
,
13
,
17
,
11
]
synthesize recursive programs from
properties. To our knowledge, these works do not support mixed-quantifier
specifications; only universal quantification is supported.
Furthermore,
[
13
,
17
,
11
]
place restrictions on the type of
universally quantified specifications that can be used; they cannot express,
for instance,
\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{x},\texttt{y}::\text{({add} \ {x} \ {y} )}=\text{({add} \ {y} \ {x} )}\rangle\!\rangle
, nor can they express
\langle\!\langle\forall\texttt{xs}::\text{({rev} \ \text{({rev} \ {xs} )})}=\texttt{xs}\rangle\!\rangle
. Except for
[
11
]
, none of these works support
user-provided sketches. Moreover, while
[
11
]
takes a sketch
body
as input, it does not take user-provided grammars for the holes as input.
Although these tools support less expressive properties, this restriction may
ease the burden of automatically verifying synthesized programs.
Due to their lack of sketch support and restrictions on the types of
universal specifications that they can handle, it is not appropriate to provide
experimental comparisons with these tools in our evaluation
(c.f. Section
4
).

In addition to the major differences mentioned above, our approach differs
algorithmically from those of
[
14
,
13
,
17
,
11
]
.
[
17
]

uses refinement types to specify and synthesize recursive programs through
top-down decomposition.
[
11
]
combines deductive synthesis rules with
enumerative search to synthesize recursive programs from pre/post-conditions.

[
13
]
synthesizes recursive programs in a bottom-up fashion by
exploiting angelic semantics and iteratively strengthening the specification.

[
14
]
synthesizes programs from general universally quantified
specifications using constraint-annotated tree automata.

Syntax-Guided Synthesis.

Syntax-Guided Synthesis (SyGuS)
[
2
]
is a framework for
program synthesis that uses grammars to restrict the search space of candidate
programs. The syntax of the SyGuS Language Standard (version
2.1)
[
16
]
allows grammars with recursive function definitions.
Despite this, none of the 6000+ publicly available SyGuS competition
benchmarks
[
20
]
involve recursive functions—recursion is never
explicitly enabled, nor is it implied by the presence of a function name in its
own grammar. Furthermore, the state-of-the-art SyGuS solver
CVC5
[
18
,
3
]
does not currently support the recursion
feature
[
21
]
. Our work focuses specifically on
recursive program synthesis, and our benchmarks require recursive solutions.
Therefore, it is inappropriate to use the SyGuS benchmark suite to evaluate our
approach, and it is also not appropriate to compare our tool against current SyGuS solvers
in our evaluation.

Both our work and
[
18
]
enumerate concepts from a grammar. A key
difference between our work and
[
18
]
is that we then enumerate
combinations of concepts
(emergents) in order to fill a sketch. An
optimization employed by
[
18
]
is to discard equivalent concepts during
enumeration. For instance, if
x+y
has been enumerated, then
y+x
is
redundant and they only use
x+y
(and not
y+x
) as a building block for
larger concepts. We would refer to this kind of optimization as
concept
reduction
, as it avoids iterating over redundant concepts. In contrast, our
prophylactic pruning technique focuses on
emergent reduction
, which
avoids iterating over
emergents
. Our tool has some support for concept
reduction, but we did not notice significant performance improvements when
using it. This may be explained by the fact that our benchmarks tend to involve
list/S-expression manipulating programs, where operations like
cons
,
append
,
etc. tend not to be, e.g., commutative unlike addition, conjunction, union, etc.

6
Conclusions

We have presented a novel method for solving the mixed-quantifier synthesis
problem for recursive programs. To our knowledge, ours is the first work to
address this problem. Our algorithm reduces the mixed-quantifier synthesis
problem to a
\forall^{*}
-synthesis problem using a Skolemization-based
approach. This reduction works by creating additional obligations for the
synthesis tool; the tool must synthesize witness-generating functions for the
Skolem function symbols introduced by Skolemization. Then, our algorithm solves
the
\forall^{*}
-synthesis problem using a sketching-based, enumerative,
counterexample-guided approach. Our algorithm learns syntactic constraints from
counterexamples to prune the candidate space and we use a prophylactic
technique to avoid enumerating pruned candidates altogether. We have
illustrated our technique through experimental evaluation involving 42
benchmarks. We saw that both our counterexample generalization and our
prophylactic pruning techniques significantly improved the performance of our
algorithm.

Going forward, we plan to investigate how generalizations of counterexamples
can be shared across sketches. We would also like to investigate how a
counterexample generator can be automatically configured or extended to improve
the speed and likelihood of finding counterexamples by leveraging all the
information obtained during synthesis (e.g., failed candidate programs,
counterexamples). We are also interested in whether this information can be
leveraged to automatically generate the helper lemmas, induction schemes, etc.
that are needed for verifying the synthesized programs.
