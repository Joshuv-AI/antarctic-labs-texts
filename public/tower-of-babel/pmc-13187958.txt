Protocol for a randomized controlled trial of a cognitive-behavioral therapy–based smoking cessation intervention in incarcerated individuals: The PRISANTABAC Study
Authors: Mélanie Rome, Xavier Laqueille, Carine Meslot, Katia Illel, Delphine Bourdeau, Lucia Romo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking prevalence in French prisons exceeds 70%, far higher than in the general population. Few rigorous smoking cessation interventions have been evaluated in correctional settings, and existing studies show substantial heterogeneity and high risk of bias. Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) is effective in the general population but has rarely been adapted to prison contexts. The PRISANTABAC trial addresses this gap by evaluating a brief group-based CBT intervention for smoking cessation in a French prison.ObjectivesTo evaluate the effectiveness of group CBT, compared with a health education intervention and a wait-list control, on smoking abstinence at 1-month (M1) and 3-month (M3) follow-ups, and to explore individual predictors of abstinence.MethodsPRISANTABAC is a three-arm randomized controlled trial conducted in a French correctional facility. Participants are randomized (1:1:1) to a three-session CBT group, a health education group, or a wait-list control. The primary outcome is biochemically validated 7-day point prevalence abstinence at M3 (0 cigarettes/day; expired CO < 8 ppm); abstinence at M1 is a key secondary outcome. Secondary outcomes include nicotine dependence, craving, motivation to quit, self-efficacy, and cigarettes smoked per day. Abstinence will be analysed using logistic regression, and repeated continuous outcomes using linear mixed-effects models. Analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle.DiscussionThis trial will provide the first rigorously designed evaluation of a brief group-based CBT smoking cessation intervention in a French prison setting and is expected to inform the development of evidence-based cessation interventions adapted to correctional environments.

Introduction

Scientific background and rationale

Tobacco use is one of the leading causes of premature and preventable death worldwide, accounting for more than 8 million deaths annually [1]. In France, 75,000 deaths each year are attributable to tobacco, representing nearly 13% of all deaths [2]. The burden of smoking is particularly concentrated in socially vulnerable populations, contributing to increasing health inequalities.

Incarcerated individuals constitute one of these high-risk groups. Smoking prevalence in prisons far exceeds that of the general population. Recent European data estimate prevalence rates of 70 to 80% in prison populations [3]. In France, 73% of incarcerated individuals smoke, compared with approximately 25% in the general population [4,5]. This alarming prevalence occurs in a context of marked vulnerability, with high rates of psychiatric disorders, polysubstance use, and somatic comorbidities [6]. Moreover, tobacco holds specific functions in detention settings (emotional regulation, social exchange, a marker of group belonging) which contribute to maintaining smoking behaviors [7,8]. Nonetheless, many incarcerated individuals report being willing to participate in a smoking cessation program [9], making incarceration a potential opportunity for cessation interventions.

In the general population, the effectiveness of smoking cessation treatments is well established: pharmacological treatments, psychological interventions, including those based on cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and combined approaches significantly increase abstinence rates [[10], [11], [12]]. CBT for smoking cessation aims to modify maladaptive thoughts and beliefs related to smoking, identify high-risk situations and craving triggers, develop alternative coping strategies, and strengthen self-efficacy [13]. In contrast, health-education interventions focused on information provision generally show modest and variable effects [14].

In correctional settings, evidence regarding the effectiveness of psychological interventions is far more limited. A recent meta-analysis conducted by our team [15] identified few trials, substantial heterogeneity across protocols, and an overall high risk of bias, resulting in uncertain conclusions. Very few studies have assessed brief, structured group-based CBT programs tailored specifically to the characteristics of smoking in detention, and none, to our knowledge, has been conducted in France.

Furthermore, research in the general population has identified several factors influencing the likelihood of long-term abstinence, including dependence severity, craving, motivation to quit, self-efficacy, impulsivity, and psychiatric comorbidities [13,[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. These factors are particularly relevant to examine in prison settings, where psychiatric disorders and addictions are highly prevalent [6]. Their investigation may help optimise and individualise cessation interventions in detention.

The PRISANTABAC study therefore pursues two main objectives:(1)To evaluate the effectiveness of a brief group-based CBT intervention, compared with a health-education intervention and a waitlist control, on smoking abstinence and key psychological indicators (dependence, craving, motivation, self-efficacy, and number of cigarettes smoked per day).(2)To explore predictors of treatment response, including dependence severity, craving, motivation, self-efficacy, impulsivity, and psychopathological dimensions.

Hypotheses

Primary hypothesis

Smoking abstinence rates will be significantly higher in the CBT group than in the Health Education and wait-list control groups at 1-month (M1) and 3-month (M3) follow-ups, with the expected hierarchy CBT > Health Education > Control.

Secondary hypotheses

Compared with the Health Education and control groups, participants allocated to the CBT intervention will show greater improvements from baseline to follow-up (M1 and M3) in tobacco-related psychological and behavioural outcomes, including nicotine dependence, craving, motivation to quit, self-efficacy, and number of cigarettes smoked per day.

Methods

Study design

This study is a single-center, three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial. Randomization is performed at the end of the baseline visit, after written informed consent, eligibility re-confirmation, and completion of baseline questionnaires. Participants are randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups: (1) a CBT group receiving a group-based intervention delivered in three 90-min sessions (total 4.5 h); (2) a Health Education group receiving a single 1-h session focused on tobacco-related risks and cessation benefits; or (3) a wait-list control group receiving no intervention during the study period, with access to either intervention after study completion.

The Health Education group serves as a comparison condition, while the wait-list control allows assessment of changes in the absence of intervention, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the CBT intervention. The total study duration is estimated at 24 months (June 2025 to June 2027).

Setting and context

The study is conducted at Maison d’Arrêt Paris–La Santé, a correctional facility that houses exclusively adult males. French Maisons d'arrêt primarily accommodate individuals on remand awaiting trial or serving short sentences (i.e., ≤2 years). Unlike many countries where correctional facilities have implemented total smoking bans, smoking remains permitted in French prisons, including in cells and courtyards.

The study population consists of incarcerated adults who smoke. The interventions and all study assessments (baseline and follow-up visits) are conducted within the Addiction Unit (CSAPA) of Paris–La Santé, located inside the prison's healthcare department.

Participant recruitment is planned over an 18-month period (June 2025 to November 2026) in order to reach the target sample size of 204 participants.

Recruitment and consent

Within the first ten days following their arrival, incarcerated individuals who smoke and are housed in the prison intake unit are systematically informed about the PRISANTABAC study as part of a continuous recruitment process. Eligible individuals are provided with a registration form, which they may return to the CSAPA via the internal mail system. Recruitment is conducted on a rolling basis, with successive intervention groups formed until the target sample size is reached.

Upon receipt of the registration form, a pre-inclusion interview is scheduled. During this interview, an investigator presents the study objectives, procedures, and participants’ rights in accordance with ethical and regulatory requirements and verifies eligibility criteria. If eligibility is confirmed, individuals are invited to participate and are provided with the participant information sheet and informed consent form.

A reflection period of one to two weeks is granted before the inclusion visit. This interval also ensures that participants have been incarcerated for more than one month, thereby limiting the potential influence of the initial incarceration shock on baseline assessments. Written informed consent is obtained at the beginning of the inclusion visit, prior to any study-related procedure.

Interventions and control group

Description of the interventions

First intervention: CBT group

The group-based cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) intervention is grounded in core CBT principles and integrates behavioral, cognitive, and emotional strategies, with selected elements of motivational interviewing. The program was adapted from the brief CBT protocol developed by Richmond et al. (23). To accommodate group delivery, session duration was extended to 90 min and an additional session was added, resulting in three weekly 90-min sessions.

At the beginning of the first session, participants receive a standardized manual containing all intervention content, worksheets, and between-session exercises; a slide deck summarising key components is used during each session.

The thematic progression of the intervention is as follows: Session 1 focuses on strengthening motivation for change (decisional balance, benefits of cessation, identification of craving triggers, and goal setting); Session 2 addresses coping with smoking urges (craving management, relaxation and emotional regulation techniques, coping planning, and cognitive restructuring); and Session 3 is devoted to progress review and relapse prevention (analysis of progress and barriers, distinction between lapses and relapses, recovery processes, and relapse prevention strategies).

Although content is standardized, delivery remains flexible to allow adaptation to daily prison contexts. Fidelity is ensured through use of standardized materials and delivery by the same trained psychologist.

Participant adherence is monitored through systematic recording of session attendance. In accordance with the protocol, participants who miss more than one consecutive session without attending a subsequent scheduled group are withdrawn from the study. Adherence data will be incorporated into the statistical analyses to examine the association between participation level and treatment outcomes.

Second intervention: health education group

The Health Education group serves as one of the comparison conditions in this study. This intervention draws on general principles of health education to strengthen participants’ knowledge and motivation regarding health-promoting behaviours. Its primary objective is to provide structured and interactive information on smoking, its effects, and the benefits of cessation. Participants assigned to this group attend a single 1-h group session, facilitated by the same psychologist-nurse duo who conduct the CBT intervention.

The session is organized into two distinct parts. The first part consists of a presentation of health education elements related to tobacco use, focusing on its effects and associated risks. These topics are addressed interactively through a true/false quiz, designed to encourage reflection and stimulate group discussion. The second part of the session presents the short-, medium-, and long-term physiological benefits of smoking cessation.

A dedicated slide deck was developed for this intervention, including all quiz questions and the full set of cessation benefits. This standardized visual support ensures consistency and fidelity across sessions.

Although the session follows a standardized structure, facilitators may adjust pacing and examples to respond to spontaneous interactions and group dynamics. Nonetheless, strict adherence to the protocol is required, and all planned content must be presented.

Adherence to the intervention is assessed based on actual participation in the session. In the event of absence, the participant is rescheduled for the next available group; if they fail to attend this second opportunity, they are withdrawn from the study in accordance with the protocol.

Coding of behavior change techniques (BCTs)

The interventions were coded using the Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy v1 (BCTTv1) (24) to identify the active components implemented in each study condition. The CBT intervention incorporates a wide range of behaviour change techniques spanning multiple domains, including goal setting, self-monitoring, action planning, social support, and cognitive and emotional regulation strategies. In contrast, the Health Education intervention relies predominantly on techniques focused on the provision of information about smoking, its determinants, health risks, and the benefits of cessation. This coding highlights the conceptual distinction between an active, skill-based CBT approach and an information-based health education intervention. Fidelity and adherence

To ensure protocol fidelity and consistency of delivery, both facilitators (a psychologist and a nurse) received specific training in the approved protocol. CBT sessions are delivered using a standardized participant manual and slide deck, while Health Education sessions rely on a dedicated standardized slide deck. Additionally, a fidelity checklist is completed by the facilitator after each session of the CBT and Health Education interventions, recording whether each key component was delivered as planned. The psychologist, who contributed to protocol development, oversees consistency of delivery across groups. Fidelity is monitored through regular team meetings within the CSAPA to supervise intervention delivery and verify adherence. No strategies beyond standard study procedures are implemented to enhance adherence. Participants are informed of the session schedule during pre-inclusion and inclusion visits, and a replacement session is offered in the CBT group in case of absence. Adherence is assessed through systematic recording of session attendance and completion in the electronic case report form.

Criteria for discontinuation of the intervention

Discontinuation of the allocated intervention may occur at the participant's request, in cases of non-adherence (absence from more than one CBT group session without replacement), or if psychological or cognitive conditions compromise study participation, with appropriate clinical referral initiated.

Wait-list control group

Participants allocated to the wait-list control group receive no group intervention during the 3-month study period but complete the same assessment schedule as participants in the intervention groups. After the final follow-up, they are offered access to the Health Education programme, the CBT programme, or both.

Concomitant care

Participants may continue or initiate usual care within the CSAPA during the trial, including smoking cessation support and nicotine replacement therapy. All additional cessation support will be documented and accounted for in the analyses.

Modifications or deviations

No protocol modifications or deviations have occurred since ethics approval. Any future modification will be documented and submitted for approval prior to implementation.

Outcomes

Primary and secondary outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is smoking abstinence, assessed by self-report and biologically validated using expired carbon monoxide (CO, parts per million). Abstinence is defined as 7-day point prevalence abstinence (0 cigarettes per day) with a CO level <8 ppm and is assessed at 1 month (M1) and 3 months (M3) post-intervention. The outcome focuses on cigarette smoking; other tobacco and nicotine products (e.g., cigars, chewing tobacco) were not assessed as only manufactured and roll-your-own cigarettes are available for purchase in French correctional facilities. In cases of discordance between self-report and biological verification (CO ≥ 8 ppm), participants are classified as smokers. Participants abstinent at M1 who relapse by M3 are classified as smokers at M3 for the primary analyses.

Secondary outcomes

In addition to the primary outcome, several secondary measures will be assessed to examine the effects of the intervention on smoking behavior, motivation to change, and associated psychological determinants.

Assessments will take place at baseline and at the month 1 (M1) and month 3 (M3) follow-ups

Allocation (randomization and blinding)

Randomization sequence

Randomization is performed at the end of the inclusion (baseline) visit, after written informed consent, eligibility re-verification, and completion of baseline assessments. Randomization is stratified by nicotine dependence level assessed with the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND; 0–4 vs. 5–10). The allocation sequence is generated in RStudio by the DRCI using simple randomization within strata and a 1:1:1 allocation ratio. Study groups are anonymized (A, B, C), and the randomization list is transmitted to the sponsor for archiving and implementation in accordance with institutional procedures.

Allocation concealment mechanism

The trial is partially blinded: participants are unaware of their group assignment prior to randomization and only discover their allocation at the time of the intervention. Investigators and facilitators, however, are aware of group assignments.

Allocation sequence generation and implementation

The randomization sequence is stored in a centralized and secure computerized system accessible only to the study sponsor and the designated statistician. Investigators do not have access to the allocation list prior to randomization. Group assignment is automatically generated by the system at the end of the inclusion visit, once eligibility criteria have been confirmed and baseline data recorded.

Blinding

The trial is open-label. Participants are informed of the existence of a randomization process designed to allocate them randomly to one of the study groups, but they do not know their group assignment prior to randomization nor before actually starting the intervention. Facilitators and outcome assessors are aware of participants’ allocation, as this information is required for delivering and monitoring the intervention. The psycho-behavioural nature of the interventions does not allow for credible masking of participants. Due to the limited number of research staff available in the correctional setting, outcome assessors could not be blinded to group allocation. The absence of blinding of outcome assessors is acknowledged as a limitation of this design; however, the use of biochemical validation of abstinence (expired CO) partially mitigates the risk of assessment bias.

Sample size and statistical analyses

Sample size

The sample size was estimated using a chi-square test of independence in G∗Power 3.1.9.7. Abstinence was defined as 7-day point prevalence smoking cessation (0 cigarettes/day) with biological validation by expired CO < 8 ppm. In the absence of prior comparative data, a medium effect size (w = 0.25) was assumed. With a two-sided α = 0.05, 80% power, the required sample size was 155 participants. Allowing for 30% attrition at the primary endpoint, the target sample was set at 204 participants (68 per group), ensuring balanced allocation across strata.

Statistical methods

All analyses will be conducted using RStudio. Baseline characteristics will be summarised by group using means ± SD or frequencies and percentages, and 95% confidence intervals will be reported for all main estimates. For all primary and secondary regression analyses (excluding exploratory prediction analyses), intervention group (CBT, Health Education, wait-list control) will be included as a categorical fixed effect. Two model specifications will be estimated: a primary model adjusted for baseline nicotine dependence (FTND 0–4 vs. 5–10), and a sensitivity model additionally adjusting for nicotine replacement therapy use and/or individual smoking cessation counselling. Estimates will be considered robust if results are consistent across model specifications.

Primary and secondary effectiveness analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle and include all randomized participants, regardless of adherence. Per-protocol analyses will be conducted as exploratory sensitivity analyses. In addition, an adherence analysis within the CBT group will compare participants who completed the full program with those who attended two sessions to examine whether effectiveness varies according to participation level.

Primary analysis of intervention effectiveness

Intervention effectiveness will be assessed using logistic regression, with biochemically validated smoking abstinence at 3 months (M3) as the primary outcome. Intervention group will be included as a fixed effect, with baseline nicotine dependence (FTND; 0–4 vs. 5–10) as an adjustment covariate. Results will be reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, using a two-sided significance threshold of 0.05. An identical model will be estimated for abstinence at 1 month (M1), considered a key secondary outcome. Sensitivity analyses will assess an alternative imputation rule classifying participants abstinent at M1 but relapsing at M3 as abstinent at M1.

Secondary analyses

Psycholological and behavioral outcomes

Continuous outcomes (FTND, FTCQ-12, self-efficacy VAS, cigarettes per day) will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for group, time (baseline, M1, M3), and their interaction, and a random intercept for participants. Estimates will be obtained using REML and reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Appropriate transformations or non-Gaussian models will be applied if distributional assumptions are violated. Post-hoc contrasts will compare groups at each time point and estimate within-participant changes from baseline to M1 and M3, with multiple testing controlled using Holm's method.

Exploratory analysis of predictors of treatment response (smoking cessation)

Predictors of smoking cessation at M1 and M3 will be explored using baseline psychological and behavioural measures (FTND, FTCQ-12, Q-MAT, self-efficacy VAS, UPPS-P, HADS, ASRS, PCL-5, ASSIST). Analyses will be conducted primarily among participants in the active intervention groups (CBT and Health Education combined) and, if sample size permits, separately by intervention. Univariable logistic regressions will be combined with penalized variable selection using the LASSO method, followed by multivariable logistic regression models. Results will be reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

A detailed Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) will be finalised prior to database lock and will define analysis populations (intention-to-treat and per-protocol), data management procedures, model specifications, and rules for result reporting. The SAP will be validated by the lead statistician and approved by the study sponsor.

Missing data

For the primary outcome, missing data will be handled using penalized imputation as the primary analysis, with participants lacking validated abstinence data at M1 or M3, or meeting a study-exit criterion (e.g., release, transfer, withdrawal), classified as smokers. Given that this assumption has been questioned in the literature, particularly when dropout may be differential across conditions [38,39], sensitivity analyses will be conducted to examine the robustness of findings. These will include: (a) multiple imputation, and (b) an analysis distinguishing between treatment-related dropout (e.g., withdrawal) and non-treatment-related dropout (e.g., release, transfer). Reasons for study exit are systematically recorded in the electronic case report form to support these analyses. For continuous secondary outcomes, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) will be conducted if missing data are substantial and will be interpreted as robustness assessments only. Operational details will be specified in the Statistical Analysis Plan.

Oversight and management

Data management and security

Data are collected at baseline and at 1- and 3-month follow-ups by trained investigators and entered into an electronic case report form (eCRF) using REDCap. All data are pseudonymised using a unique study identifier, with no directly identifying information stored; data quality is ensured through automated validation checks. Follow-up assessments are systematically proposed at each scheduled time point and conducted whenever possible, including for participants who discontinue or deviate from the allocated intervention, provided consent is not withdrawn. Data from participants who discontinue prematurely (e.g., release, transfer, withdrawal) are retained and analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle.

Data provided to the sponsor and statistician are non-identifiable. All data processing complies with GDPR requirements and the French Reference Methodology MR-001, and data will be retained for 15 years after study closure.

Trial monitoring

Trial conduct is monitored by a clinical research associate appointed by the sponsor, in collaboration with the principal investigator and study staff. Monitoring visits follow a predefined plan adapted to trial progress to ensure protocol adherence, data completeness, and compliance with Good Clinical Practice; any deviations are reported to the sponsor and managed according to institutional procedures. Given the minimal-risk, single-center design, no independent data monitoring committee, interim analyses, or formal stopping guidelines are planned.

Potential risks and adverse event monitoring

The psychological interventions involve minimal risk. Adverse events will be monitored throughout the study, with psychological distress systematically assessed at baseline and at 1- and 3-month follow-ups using the HADS. Participants exceeding predefined thresholds will be referred for appropriate care.

Audit and quality assurance

The sponsor ensures the quality of the research in accordance with Good Clinical Practice. Audits may be conducted at any time by the sponsor or regulatory authorities to assess protocol compliance and data quality. Investigators agree to facilitate these inspections.

Discussion

The PRISANTABAC trial addresses a major gap in the evaluation of smoking cessation interventions in correctional settings by testing a brief, group-based cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) programme adapted to incarceration. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial conducted in France to evaluate a structured psychological smoking cessation intervention specifically designed for a prison context.

A major strength of this protocol is its methodological rigor, including randomized allocation, a three-arm design comprising an educational comparison condition and a wait-list control, and biological validation of smoking abstinence. The brief group-based format was selected to enhance feasibility and scalability within correctional healthcare systems, where access to care is often limited. In addition, the assessment of psychological and clinical predictors of abstinence may help refine and better target future cessation interventions in prison populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged, including the single-centre design, the absence of blinding of outcome assessors, the relatively short follow-up period with no post-release assessment, and the risk of attrition related to release or transfer. Additionally, the difference in intervention dose between the CBT and Health Education conditions (4.5 h vs. 1 h) limits the ability to isolate CBT-specific effects from non-specific factors such as therapeutic contact time. E-cigarette use was not formally assessed, which may limit the interpretation of abstinence outcomes. The trial would also have benefited from a qualitative process evaluation, in line with the MRC framework for complex interventions [40,41], which should be considered in future research. Finally, although cannabis use was assessed at baseline using the ASSIST scale, repeated assessment throughout the trial was not included; future studies should consider longitudinal monitoring of cannabis use to examine its potential influence on tobacco cessation outcomes.

Despite these constraints, this trial is expected to provide valuable evidence on the feasibility and effectiveness of CBT-based smoking cessation interventions in correctional environments and to inform the development of future high-quality trials in this field.
