Is there an alternative to grant-funding for sexual and reproductive health advocacy? A survey of the income base of AmplifyChange grantees
Authors: Alex le May, Lucie Hazelgrove-Planel
Published: 2023

Background

A strong and diverse civil society is critical to establishing and sustaining sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR). The role of civil society organisations (CSOs) in progressive social reform is well recognised. CSOs play different roles in initiating and organising social movements. They design and deliver campaigns to engage public involvement in policy debates. They engage policymakers directly in debates over rights to safe abortion, LGBTI+ rights, comprehensive sexuality education, tackling sexual violence and other SRHR themes. Local CSOs also play a leading role in the inclusion of specific social groups – e.g. people living with disabilities or specific social or religiously excluded populations – and representing their perspectives in movements and the definition of sexual and reproductive health rights in laws, policies and social norms (Figure 1).

Grants fund the vast majority of CSOs working in SRHR who responded to the survey

It is difficult to track international financing trends for SRHR. The best we have in terms of international comparison and track trends are consolidated data on “Population Policies/ Programmes & Reproductive Health”. The proportion of these funds being directed to family planning is growing, but most of this funding is directed towards HIV prevention.1 In 2018, the USA was by far the largest funder, providing over $6.7 billion, followed by The Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (over $1 billion) and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation ($620 million). Philanthropic donors probably play a comparatively large role in funding SRHR programmes, but consistent information varies across donors about the type, amount and destination of support.

International aid budgets are under pressure. The COVID-19 pandemic has exposed the vulnerability of many commitments to international grant-making (see, for example, Loft and Brian2 or USAID3). Certain governments – such as the United Kingdom – have restricted their contributions as part of a reorganisation of public finance in the face of increased domestic spending to manage the pandemic and its aftermath. The war in Ukraine and the economic shocks accompanying it have created even more pressure on government spending for international grant-making, especially amongst European governments. Many other comparatively generous donor governments face pressure to divert funding away from the Global South to support resettlement programmes for refugees. Although important new private donors have emerged, some organisations have found their support cut from private sources, including philanthropic foundations, in the face of financial uncertainties and stock-market volatility. Many fundraisers also complain that raising mass contributions from less wealthy households to international grant-making has become harder.

Across different policy areas, not specifically to SRHR, we know that donations through grants from wealthy country governments are a critical source of funding for civil society action in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Yet, local CSOs in LMICs access a much smaller share of grant-funding than their international partners. Surveys conducted by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) found that there were between $1–1.4 billion in grants made to developing country-based CSOs between 2010 and 2018.4 The survey also revealed that CSOs based in donor countries received between $12.5 and $14 billion in the same period, whilst international CSOs received between $2.8 and $5.2 billion.*

The OECD surveys did not collect information on aid flows to local CSOs from CSOs based in donor countries or international CSOs. The survey does not provide information about contributions from private individuals or foundations, which have increasingly played a large role in international civil society action. However, the research provides substance to a well-recognised concern about the limited share of international grant-funding received directly by local CSOs compared to their larger international and Global North-based partners. The research underlines how scarce international grant-funding is for local CSOs in LMICs.

A lack of financial sustainability remains the greatest challenge to CSOs, especially in sub-Saharan Africa.7 Notwithstanding the considerable contribution of unpaid volunteer labour power and foregone income by activists, local CSOs have basic running costs to manage. Effective campaigns – especially at a national scale – require funding, often significant. As agents of social change, CSOs and social movements cannot rely on being able to use the social and communications infrastructure to manage their debate or influence fellow citizens and policymakers during a campaign. Engaging meaningfully in public debate and informing policymakers, therefore, requires financial and labour resources that can be expensive and far beyond the means of individual volunteer activists. Activists for SRHR also operate in contexts where policymakers face competition for their time from various, often well-funded, interest groups. Money matters for upholding rights as much as any policy agenda.

Where will the money for LMIC CSOs engaged in SRHR advocacy and activism come from? The implicit assumption is that grants should finance civil society activism for SRHR. But what of alternatives? This includes mass-contribution models, membership fees, social enterprise revenue and domestic grant-making from government or non-governmental funds. Organised campaigns run by civil society in Europe and N. America can often mobilise volunteer resources and cash from large contributor bases – often high volumes of small contributions by individual supporters that can aggregate to an effective working budget.

But do the organisations we support in LMICs use similar models to fund their work? Our tacit knowledge accumulated from more than eight years of supporting CSOs working for SRHR in Africa and South Asia suggests that most groups rely on funding from grants but the range of alternative funding sources is limited.8 International trends in the last decade have encouraged entrepreneurship, but this requires capacity and resources, which many CSOs do not have.9

This ever-present awareness of the limits of funds available led us to explore alternative sources of funding that local CSOs in LMICs can access.

Why is AmplifyChange – a grant-maker – asking questions about alternative sources of income?

AmplifyChange was established as a mechanism to fund civil society in the Global South – prioritising locally founded and registered organisations. Since 2014, the fund – now an independent not-for-profit organisation but initially hosted as a consortium with African Women’s Development Fund, Global Fund for Women and MannionDaniels – has run 51 funding rounds and supported over 950 CSOs, many with no previous access to funding for advocacy. We disbursed £96 million across 64 countries where SRHR are most limited. We have contributed to local CSOs to expand movements for change in SRHR. We have a broad thematic focus within SRHR – eliminating gender-based violence, challenging stigma and discrimination based on gender identity and sexual orientation, increasing access to reproductive health for marginalised groups, removing barriers to safe abortion and improving the sexual health of young people.

AmplifyChange is among a growing number of organisations involved in international support to civil society that have begun to specialise or shift their models to direct funding to civil society in the Global South. This is a highly welcome movement in international development, not limited to SRHR.

Most of our grants (87%) go to small or medium-sized CSOs with annual operating budgets of less than £200,000; most are based in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Ninety-seven per cent of them work in countries rated as Closed, Repressed or Obstructed by CIVICUS monitor. In our current portfolio, 53% of our grants are women-led, 17% LGBTI-led, 12% youth-led and 5% are disabled person organisations (some organisations belonging to more than one of these categories).

Selection of applicants to date has been organised through a results-oriented, open and transparent challenge fund with relatively low-eligibility barriers for new applicants. Consequently, we have received more than ten times as many applications compared to the number that we can fund – a ratio which illustrates the level of demand from civil society for funding. Each organisation selected for funding undergoes a rigorous, multistage selection process largely undertaken by independent reviewers. Until 2020, AmplifyChange also ran a competitive “renewals” programme to allocate follow-on grant-funding for the strongest performing organisations when their grants entered their final phases.† Therefore, the organisations selected through this process represent some of the most effective organisations working to strengthen SRHR.

As an organisation whose mission is to support CSOs and build stronger, more inclusive movements for SRHR, we wanted to learn how our financial contributions to CSOs working for SRHR relate to the broader income mix on which CSOs cover their core operating costs and their campaigns. As a grant-maker funding civil society activism, we permanently manage resourcing challenges. These set the fundamental parameters or limits on our support to civil society activism. Our day-to-day decision-making, as well as our overarching strategic work, is regularly framed around the following questions:

AmplifyChange’s role in the SRHR financing architecture, therefore, offers a comparatively unique opportunity to learn about the funding base of civil society. It is dedicated to SRHR but not exclusive to specific topics within the broad range of SRHR issues. The open selection process and mechanisms to renew based on performance make for a more representative “potential sampling frame” of civil society than closed networks of affiliates, a federation or a country branch structure. Whilst the organisations funded are not entirely representative of civil society, they may offer a useful sample of high-performing CSOs or organisations playing leading roles within domestic social movements for SRHR.

The fund reached its highest grant-making volume in 2020. At this point, we were supported by the Hewlett Foundation to conduct a survey with our grantees to explore their funding situation. Methods

We developed a standard online questionnaire in SurveyMonkey to explore CSO fundraising experiences and learn about funding sources. The questionnaire was piloted with 20 organisations and emailed to all organisations funded between November and December 2020 through an email content manager to 349 grant partners of AmplifyChange. We followed this questionnaire with a series of interviews with selected respondents who generated income from alternative sources of funding to grant-making.

We received responses to our survey from 210 organisations (60.2%). Responses were received from organisations based in Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Benin, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Congo (Republic of), The Democratic Republic of the Congo, Côte D'Ivoire, Ethiopia, Gabon, Ghana, Guatemala, Guinea, India, Kenya, Lesotho, Madagascar, Malawi, Mauritania, Namibia, Nepal, Nigeria, Nicaragua, Pakistan, Rwanda, Senegal, Sierra Leone, South Africa, South Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda, Zambia and Zimbabwe.

The questionnaire collected information from grant-receiving CSOs about their past expenditure, current funding sources, future fundraising activities and financial outlook of their organisation. Selected respondents (n = 8) to the survey were chosen to participate in a series of focus groups and interviews to enable the sharing of experiences between like-minded organisations and create case studies on organisations that had successfully raised funds from non-grant sources. The resulting case studies were shared on the AmplifyChange Learn platform.

Results

Funding sources

Respondents surveyed who answered questions on their financing (n = 127) managed total funds worth EUR55.7 million – the vast majority was raised through grants. More than 85.3% was sourced from donor grants based in OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) contributing countries. National or local government funding accounted for 10.37% of overall funding to the CSOs surveyed. Local philanthropic funding accounted for 3.27% of funding.

Alternative sources of revenue to grants accounted for 1.07% of funding – only a marginal level compared to grants. The main alternative sources included membership dues (0.39%) community donations (0.12%) followed by social enterprise (0.09%).

Sources of grant-funding

In total, respondents received grant-funding through 348 different sources other than AmplifyChange (the common source of funding for all respondents). Over two-thirds of funding was sourced directly from grant-makers, including foundations and multilateral, national and international government sources (Figure 2).

Fundraising outlook

Over two-thirds of respondents (64%) raised concerns about the impact of COVID-19 on their fundraising abilities for the two years following the survey (2021–2022). Respondents’ concerns included a lack of open funding opportunities during the COVID period, concerns about the redirection of funding from SRHR to COVID-19 response and overseas development assistance budgets being reduced due to the economic impact of COVID-19 in donor countries.

Alternative sources of funding

Overall, alternative sources to grant-funding represented only a small share of civil society funding amongst AmplifyChange’s grant partners. Although the revenue raised from alternative fundraising sources was small compared to grant-funding, we found a wide diversity of non-grant fundraising initiatives to support SRHR activities amongst respondents. Alternative revenue streams included livestock farming, crowdfunding, consultancy services, charging membership fees, raising funds from community donations and other forms of social enterprise such as renting out spare facilities such as meeting rooms.

Interested by these non-grant income streams and in an effort to encourage the sharing of experiences between grant holders, we brought together organisations who, in the survey, self-identified as using a range of fundraising initiatives. These themed focus groups, each mobilising four to six organisations, gave us greater insight into the range of fundraising techniques and approaches used by CSOs and their contextual relevance. The organisations were eager to discuss the histories of their fundraising and learn from each other’s experiences,

To share these learnings with the wider SRHR community, the research team identified six organisations that reported raising funds from alternative funding sources and conducted short interviews to create learning-oriented case studies on the initiatives of each of the organisations. Case study contributors had generally started their resource mobilisation planning intending to raise funding from grants. Still, limitations of grant-funding had motivated them to generate different, more flexible sources of funding.

Initially, our fundraising strategy had been aimed at increasing funding through grants and donations, which we successfully did. However, as our programming evolved and the number and diversity of beneficiaries increased, we encountered funding needs that could not be addressed through grants. We sometimes had situations where girl protection and safety concerns needed immediate action or financial support, for example, during legal proceedings. This motivated us to diversify our funding portfolio. (Tawina Jane Kopa-Kamanga, TAWINA, Malawi10)

Because of the urgency and the lack of guaranteed outcomes, we did not feel we could rely on the regular calls for funding applications, so we turned to crowdfunding. (Ann Gloria Njoki, Deaf Outreach Program, Kenya11)

Our fundraising situation is linked to the policy that governs the civil society organisations (CSOs) and non-governmental organisations (NGOs) in Ethiopia and the funding direction that donors and governments take. In 2015, the global funding direction shifted from aid to trade, which affected our organisation’s ability to fundraise. The shift also included the need to work more with consortium partners than as individual organisations. (Berhanu Demissie, Development Expertise Centre, Ethiopia12)

Although small, the importance of these alternative sources of fundraising was recognised across the case study contributors.

The financial resources raised by the contributions have enabled us to cope with certain administrative burdens, particularly with regard to state taxes. (Viviane Sebahire, Solidarité des femmes pour le développement intégral (SOFEDI), DRC13)

Case study contributors also pointed out the importance of non-financial contributions – especially volunteer labour power to the success and health of civil society.

… the [organisation] is made up of volunteers and employees, whom we employ in the context of projects and grants. When we don’t have funding, most still stay with us. We have sensitised all of our staff and volunteers to take ownership of the organisation as if it were their own, so should we let the boat sink? No. The staff stay but work when they can and at flexible hours. (Irène Capet, NGO Sainte Philomène, l'Espérance (OSPE), Côte d'Ivoire14)

The value of alternative fundraising extends beyond generating revenue for the organisation to develop stronger relationships and ownership by the communities served.

The membership dues also enabled [our organisation] to integrate meaningfully with the community. The whole community were all invited to our events and so benefited from our activities. Some of our members were also members of other groups in the community, such as the women’s group at the town hall and the church. As a result, people felt that the organisation was part of the community and belonged to them. (Sybil Nkeiruka Nmezi, Generation Initiative for Women and Youth Network (GIWYN), Nigeria15)

Discussion

Limitations

The sampling of AmplifyChange grant recipients was helpful for our organisation and partners to learn more about the financial base of the CSOs we work with. The sampling frame – organisations selected for grants through an extensive and open selection process from a wide pool of applications – was a unique opportunity to develop insights. However, this research did not set out to establish a representative sample, so caution is required when drawing concrete conclusions about the overall financing picture of civil society. Further research using more sophisticated sampling methods would be helpful.

AmplifyChange has a wide remit to support civil society working across the spectrum of SRHR priorities. This includes organisations that are comparatively underfunded, stigmatised or, in many cases, face legal restrictions on their freedom to organise and associate. For example, AmplifyChange has a strong track record in funding abortion rights activism, action against female genital mutilation/cutting, organisations working to strengthen comprehensive sexuality education and organisations run by and for LGBT activists. It may be unsurprising that subjects still encounter high levels of stigma and taboo and struggle to establish a diverse financing base. But again, this feature of the sample may limit the extrapolation of insights from the research to other less stigmatised subjects on which civil society is engaged.

Dealing with vulnerable financial foundations

Notwithstanding the limitations of the sample, we found that grants from governments or foundations in OECD DAC contributing countries play a large role in the budgets of civil society activists working to strengthen SRHR. Whilst grants are issued through an increasingly wide range of organisations – most of whom are likely to be intermediaries from the original taxpayer or philanthropic grant sources – we found that at least 85% of funding was grant income.

Funds raised directly from the community via donations, membership dues, social enterprise, etc., were almost marginal in comparison to grant-funding. Whilst the sample for this research leaves much to be desired, the extremely small nature of these resource mobilisations suggests that a grant-based funding base is the main source of income for most SRHR organisations in the countries where CSOs participating in the survey were based.

Our research also pointed to the value of community-generated, non-grant income to enable organisations to work flexibly. Grants are rarely given without conditions and limitations. The extent and nature of those limitations or conditions vary widely across grant-makers and through the grant-making chain from one organisation to another, each of which may add conditions and limitations to subsequent on-grants. CSOs who had been able to raise their funds were able to support costs that may be limited or slowed by compliance requirements.

The study did not explore potential barriers to generating funding from alternative income sources. This is an important future direction for research and product development. We are considering low-income communities with limited disposable income for the most part. CSOs are already stretched for time. While it is indeed important to raise local funding, which also has many non-financial benefits, the extent of such funding will likely always be very small. The returns to investment in terms of time and effort are also never high unless small contributions can be taken to scale. Innovations in digital finance may offer a route to achieving small contributions at scale, but even community-based CSOs with modest budgets would need to devote significant effort towards fundraising.

The skill sets required to run successful enterprises are often very different from those possessed by the CSO leadership, which is driven by idealism and social justice motives. Also, there may be a contradiction between a profit-making venture to raise financial resources and running a social justice CSO.

Further product development and best practice sharing should be prioritised. Grant funders may also want to consider supporting the development of community financing strategies to benefit CSO or campaign financing. Future research could play a role in evaluating different approaches or understanding the relationship between community resource mobilisation, increased community stake in a campaign and the potential to unlock more volunteer labour power.

Money matters, but it’s not everything

Whilst it is difficult to envision a campaign meaningfully engaging citizens in debate, discussion and the democratic process at the national scale without funding or a prior infrastructure or network, CSOs working to change norms, policies or strengthen their local services often start with nothing but volunteer labour and time. Stories told by CSOs about their organisational journeys often begin with a group of activists with little or no finance at their disposal coming together to establish an organisation to promote SRHR interests. Examples of these stories can be found in a recent set of videos produced as part of a Participatory Video Project organised alongside our financing survey.

The case studies produced in this work pointed to activists’ motivation to continue their work despite funding limitations. Examples of staff working unpaid when grant-funding runs out speak to the motivational value of civic action for SRHR – although the capacity of individuals or organisations to maintain unpaid work should be an obvious and serious concern for SRHR movements.

Conclusion

Although limited, our research points towards some important realities about financing SRHR activism within low- and middle-income settings. Inequalities in financial distribution between different types of CSO have been a strong concern of international civil society discussions and those taking place within many countries. However, this research should remind us that all organisations share a common vulnerability – a heavy reliance on international grant-funding and limited access to finance generated at or close to the communities served by CSOs. Many organisations have experimented with means to generate alternatives to grant income and to maximise the value of volunteer labour or in-kind contributions. The development of new research, products and practices to significantly scale up non-grant income and harness non-financial contributions of active citizens is a priority to sustain and increase the impact of social movements for SRHR.
