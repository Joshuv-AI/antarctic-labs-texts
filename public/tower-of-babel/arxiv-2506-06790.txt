Adam assisted Fully informed Particle Swarm Optimzation ( Adam-FIPSO ) based Parameter Prediction for the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
SHASHANK SANJAY BHAT 1, PEIYONG WANG 1,2 , AND UDAYA PARAMPALLI. 1

Abstract
The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is a prominent variational algorithm used for solving combinatorial optimization problems such as the Max-Cut problem. A key challenge in QAOA lies in efficiently identifying suitable parameters ( γ , β ) (\gamma,\beta) that lead to high-quality solutions. In this paper, we propose a framework that combines Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (FIPSO) with adaptive gradient correction using the Adam Optimizer to navigate the QAOA parameter space. This approach aims to avoid issues such as barren plateaus and convergence to local minima. The proposed algorithm is evaluated against two classes of graph instances, Erdos Renyi and Watts-Strogatz. Experimental results across multiple QAOA depths consistently demonstrate superior performance compared to random initialization, underscoring the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed optimization framework.

I
Introduction

In recent years, quantum computing has emerged as a promising approach to solving complex optimization problems such as TSP (Traveling Salesman Problem)
[
1
]
, VRP (Vehicle Routing Problem)
[
2
]
, and the Max-Cut problem
[
3
]
. We are currently in the NISQ (Noisy Intermediate Scale Quantum) era
[
4
]
, characterized by devices with limited numbers of qubits that are susceptible to noise and decoherence. These constraints have inspired the development of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms, which leverage the strengths of both paradigms. In such frameworks, quantum circuits handle computationally intensive tasks, while classical optimizers iteratively refine results
[
5
]
.

One such hybrid quantum-classical algorithm is QAOA
[
6
]
, which is particularly suited for tackling combinatorial optimization problems like the Max-Cut problem. Max-Cut problem is a NP-hard problem that involves partitioning a graph’s vertices into two subsets to maximize the number of edges between them. QAOA operates by preparing candidate solutions on a quantum circuit using parameters
\gamma
and
\beta
. A classical optimizer then adjusts these parameters to improve the solution quality
[
8
]
.

Finding optimal parameters for QAOA can be challenging due to issues like barren plateaus
[
7
]
and local minima in the optimization landscape
[
10
]
. To address these challenges, effective warm-start methods are crucial for ensuring that the algorithm begins its search near promising regions of the solution space.

To address these challenges in parameter optimization for QAOA, we propose using Adam-assisted Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (Adam-FIPSO) to predict near-optimal parameters. By combining a custom objective function with Adam-FIPSO, our method effectively searches for parameters that balance performance and optimization efficiency. The key components of our approach are listed below:

•

Custom Objective Function:
We introduce a custom objective function that aims to achieve near-optimal performance while incorporating the Approximation Ratio (AR). This function helps in guiding the optimization process to improve the quality of the predicted solution.

•

Adam-Assisted FIPSO for Parameter Prediction:
We employ the Adam-FIPSO algorithm to predict near-optimal QAOA parameters
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
and
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
for varying circuit depths (
p=1,2,3
). This optimization approach enhances the search process by utilizing the collective knowledge of the swarm.

•

Improved Approximate Ratios:
Evaluations using the approximate ratio parameter (comparing the quality of the cut found with the best possible cut) demonstrate that our Adam-FIPSO-based initialization significantly outperforms random initialization.

II
Related Work

Several recent studies have investigated machine learning techniques to improve parameter initialization in the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) for combinatorial problems such as Max-Cut. Liang et al.
[
13
]
employ Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to learn mappings from graph structures to effective QAOA parameters, aiming to reduce reliance on iterative quantum evaluations. Similarly, Meng et al.
[
14
]
introduce a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) to generate high-quality parameter samples that serve as strong initializations.

While these methods are model-driven and rely on training over large graph datasets, they do not explicitly utilize the QAOA cost landscape during optimization. In contrast, our approach directly optimizes QAOA parameters by evaluating the expectation value of the QAOA circuit for each graph instance. This is achieved using an Adam-assisted Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (Adam-FIPSO) strategy, which combines swarm-based exploration with gradient-based refinement.

By operating directly on the QAOA objective function without the need for learned surrogates, our method provides a flexible and model-free alternative for parameter tuning across varying graph types and circuit depths.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

QAOA

QAOA is a variational quantum algorithm designed to solve combinatorial optimization problems by leveraging quantum mechanics
[
6
]
. It operates by encoding an optimization problem into a quantum circuit consisting of alternating cost and mixer Hamiltonians, whose parameters are optimized to approximate the optimal solution.

Fig. 1:
The QAOA Circuit mechanism showing the cost and mixer circuit components

QAOA applies a sequence of unitary transformations using the cost and mixer Hamiltonians. Given a depth parameter
p
, the quantum state evolves as:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{p}(\gamma,\beta)}=e^{-i\beta_{p}H_{M}}e^{-i\gamma_{p}H_{C}}

(1)

\displaystyle\dots e^{-i\beta_{1}H_{M}}e^{-i\gamma_{1}H_{C}}\ket{+}^{\otimes n}.

•

The cost Hamiltonian evolution
e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}
encodes problem constraints.

•

The mixer Hamiltonian evolution
e^{-i\beta H_{M}}
introduces transitions between states.

•

The initial state
\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
is a uniform superposition:

\ket{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1}).

(2)

The circuit alternates between these operators for
p
layers, with variational parameters
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
optimized to minimize the expectation value of
H_{C}
:

\langle H_{C}\rangle=\bra{\psi_{p}(\gamma,\beta)}H_{C}\ket{\psi_{p}(\gamma,\beta)}.

(3)

III-B

Max Cut Problem

The Max-Cut problem is a well-known combinatorial optimization problem where the goal is to partition the vertices of a given graph
G(V,E)
into two sets such that the number of edges crossing the partition is maximized. Formally, for a graph
G
with
|V|=n
vertices and edges
(i,j)\in E
, the problem can be expressed as maximising the objective function:

C(z)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{i}z_{j}),

(4)

where
z_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
represents the assignment of vertex
i
to one of the two partitions. The corresponding cost Hamiltonian in QAOA is:

H_{C}=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1}{2}(1-Z_{i}Z_{j}),

(5)

where
Z_{i}
is the Pauli-Z operator acting on the
i
th qubit. The ground state of
H_{C}
corresponds to the optimal Max-Cut partition.

IV
Methodology

IV-A

Problem Definition

This work addresses the problem of optimizing the variational parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})
in the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) for solving the MaxCut problem over diverse graph instances. Given a set of graphs
(G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{n})
, the goal is to identify high-quality QAOA parameters that yield near-optimal cut values for each instance and depth. However, the QAOA optimization landscape is known to be highly non-convex, oscillatory, and problem-dependent, making traditional gradient-free methods prone to premature convergence or stagnation in local optima.

Conventional Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) techniques, while effective in simpler domains, often struggle to efficiently explore such complex energy landscapes due to limited adaptation to local curvature. Fully Informed PSO (FIPSO) partially mitigates this by incorporating social knowledge from all particles, but still lacks fine-grained control over per-dimension learning dynamics. In contrast, the proposed Adam-assisted Fully Informed PSO (Adam-FIPSO) augments the social guidance of FIPSO with adaptive moment estimation inspired by the Adam optimizer. This enables the algorithm to adjust learning rates dynamically based on both first- and second-order statistics of the swarm’s movements, significantly improving robustness and convergence behavior.

In our approach, we apply Adam-FIPSO to optimize QAOA parameters
(\gamma_{1},\beta_{1}),(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2}),\\
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})
for circuit depths
p=1,2,3
, respectively. The optimization is driven by a MaxCut-based objective function, where the performance of candidate parameters is evaluated using a quantum simulation backend. The swarm of particles iteratively refines its positions using both social information and Adam-corrected updates to navigate the rugged QAOA landscape effectively. For each graph instance, the framework converges toward parameter configurations that achieve high-quality cuts, demonstrating improved generalization and optimization efficiency compared to baseline methods.

IV-B

Particle Swarm Optimization

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is a population-based stochastic optimization algorithm originally introduced by Kennedy and Eberhart in 1995
[
15
]
. It draws inspiration from the collective behavior observed in natural systems, such as bird flocking and fish schooling, wherein individual agents (particles) share information and adapt their trajectories based on both individual experiences and social interactions.

In PSO, each particle represents a candidate solution in the search space and iteratively updates its position by two factors: the best solution it has encountered so far which is called the personal best and the best solution identified by any particle in the swarm which is known as the global best. This combination allows the swarm to balance exploration and exploitation during the optimization process.

At each iteration
t
, the velocity update is given by the following equation:

v_{i}^{(t+1)}=w\cdot v_{i}^{(t)}+c_{1}r_{1}\left(p_{\text{best},i}-x_{i}^{(t)}\right)+c_{2}r_{2}\left(g_{\text{best}}-x_{i}^{(t)}\right)

(6)

and the position update is given by the following equation:

x_{i}^{(t+1)}=x_{i}^{(t)}+v_{i}^{(t+1)}

(7)

where
x_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the position of particle
i
at iteration
t
, and
v_{i}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents its velocity. The term
p_{\text{best},i}
refers to the best position discovered by particle
i
so far, while
g_{\text{best}}
indicates the best position found by any particle in the entire swarm. The scalar
w
is the inertia weight that controls the influence of the previous velocity, and
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the cognitive and social acceleration coefficients, respectively. Finally,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are independent random variables sampled from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
, used to introduce stochasticity into the learning components.

The inertia component
w\cdot v_{i}^{(t)}
encourages particles to continue in their current direction (exploration), while the cognitive and social components bias the motion toward promising regions discovered by the individual or the swarm (exploitation). This iterative process continues until a termination criterion is met, such as convergence to a solution or a maximum number of iterations.

IV-C

Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization

Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (FIPSO) is an enhanced version of the standard PSO algorithm. In FIPSO, each particle uses information from all other particles in the swarm to update its velocity and position, rather than just using its personal best and the global best
[
16
]
. This allows the particles to have a more collaborative and informed search for the optimal solution. In essence, each particle is influenced by the positions and velocities of all other particles in the swarm, leading to a more global search and often better convergence behavior.

The velocity and position update in Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (FIPSO) is given by the following equations:

\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(t+1)}=w\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(t)}+c_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[r_{1,ij}\big(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(t)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}\big)\Big]

(8)

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}+\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(t+1)}

(9)

where
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{(t)}
denotes the velocity of particle
i
at iteration
t
, and
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(t)}
is its corresponding position. The variable
N
represents the total number of particles in the swarm. The term
r_{1,ij}
is a random scalar drawn from a uniform distribution, representing the stochastic influence of particle
j
on particle
i
. The inertia weight
w
controls the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, while the acceleration coefficient
c_{1}
governs the magnitude of social influence exerted by the swarm on each particle.

In this update, instead of just following its own best position and the global best, each particle considers the influence of all other particles in the swarm. This results in each particle receiving a more ”fully informed” update and contributes to more diverse exploration of the search space.

IV-D

Adam Assisted FIPSO

Adam-Assisted Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (Adam-FIPSO) combines the strengths of Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (FIPSO) and the Adam optimization algorithm, which is widely used for adaptive learning rate adjustments in machine learning
[
17
]
. The idea behind Adam-FIPSO is to enhance the convergence speed and stability of the original FIPSO by incorporating Adam’s adaptive learning rates into the velocity update mechanism of the particles. The adaptive learning rate helps the particles move efficiently through the search space, particularly in high-dimensional or complex optimization problems.

In Adam-FIPSO, the velocity update equation is modified to include the adaptive moment estimates used in the Adam optimizer, which adjusts the learning rates based on both the first and second moments of the gradients.

For each particle
i
and dimension
d
, an influence-driven gradient component is first computed using:

g_{i}^{(t),d}=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j\in N_{i}}c_{j}r_{j}\left(p_{\text{best},j,d}-x_{i}^{(t),d}\right),

(10)

where
N_{i}
denotes the set of neighbors influencing particle
i
,
c_{j}
is a constant weight coefficient, and
r_{j}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
is a random scalar modulating the influence from neighbor
j
. This term is analogous to a gradient, constructed via fully informed interactions in the swarm.

The first moment estimate is updated using the following equation:

m_{i}^{(t+1),d}=\beta_{1}m_{i}^{(t),d}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{i}^{(t),d}.

(11)

while the second moment is updated as:

v_{i}^{(t+1),d}=\beta_{2}v_{i}^{(t),d}+(1-\beta_{2})\left(g_{i}^{(t),d}\right)^{2}.

(12)

To account for initialization bias in early iterations, bias-corrected estimates are computed:

\hat{m}_{i}^{(t+1),d}=\frac{m_{i}^{(t+1),d}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t+1}},\quad\hat{v}_{i}^{(t+1),d}=\frac{v_{i}^{(t+1),d}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t+1}}.

(13)

The final velocity update combines the inertia term and the adaptive Adam step:

v_{i}^{(t+1),d}=w\cdot v_{i}^{(t),d}+\alpha\cdot\frac{\hat{m}_{i}^{(t+1),d}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{i}^{(t+1),d}}+\epsilon}.

(14)

Where
w
is the inertia weight and
\alpha
is the learning rate. The particle position is then updated as:

x_{i}^{(t+1),d}=x_{i}^{(t),d}+v_{i}^{(t+1),d}.

(15)

In these equations,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are exponential decay rates for the first and second moment estimates, typically set to
0.9
and
0.999
, respectively. The term
\epsilon
is a small constant (e.g.,
10^{-8}
) included for numerical stability during division.

In a multi-dimensional setting, each particle explores a high-dimensional parameter space, where each dimension corresponds to a variational parameter (
\gamma
or
\beta
). The particles leverage both social knowledge from neighboring particles and local gradient-like information to guide their movement. The Adam component introduces per-dimension momentum and adaptive scaling based on first and second moment estimates, allowing each particle to take appropriately sized steps in every dimension. This combination enables efficient navigation of the complex, non-convex landscapes typical of QAOA optimization.

IV-F

Objective Function

The objective function for the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is defined as:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\text{MSE}(\hat{C},C_{\text{target}})+\lambda\cdot\text{AR penalty},

(16)

where:

•

\hat{C}=\text{predicted cut}
, the output of the QAOA cost function.

•

C_{\text{target}}
is the target cut calculated classically.

•

MSE stands for the Mean Squared Error:

\text{MSE}=(\hat{C}-C_{\text{target}})^{2}

•

AR represents the Approximate Ratio, which is calculated as:

AR=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\hat{C}}{C_{\text{target}}}&\text{if }y_{\text{target}}\neq 0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The approximate ratio measures how close the predicted cut is to the target cut.

•

The AR penalty is defined as the squared difference between 1 and the approximate ratio:

\text{AR penalty}=(1-AR)^{2}

This term penalizes predictions that are far from the target cut in terms of accuracy ratio.

•

\lambda
is a regularization parameter that controls the weight of the AR penalty in the objective function. If the AR penalty is larger, the objective function will put more emphasis on minimizing the difference in accuracy ratio.

The final objective function,
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, thus combines both the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss and the AR penalty to guide the optimization towards both accurate predictions and maintaining a reasonable accuracy ratio.

IV-G

Synthesis

Each graph instance, generated from either the Erdős–Rényi (ER) or Watts–Strogatz (WS) model, is first encoded as an adjacency matrix and used to construct the corresponding QAOA circuit. The edges in the graph correspond to the connections between the qubits and the gates in the circuit. Figure
3
shows the equivalent QAOA circuit for a 4 node graph. The depth of the QAOA circuit, defined by the number of alternating mixer and cost layers, is varied to evaluate performance across multiple configurations. To optimize the QAOA parameters
(\gamma,\beta)
, the Adam-assisted Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (Adam-FIPSO) algorithm is employed. At each iteration, candidate solutions are refined using gradient information estimated via finite differences, and the Adam optimizer is applied to adaptively update the search trajectory. This combination of collective intelligence and gradient-based correction ensures both exploration and precision. For every graph and circuit depth, the optimizer seeks parameter values that maximize the expected cut value as estimated by the QAOA circuit.

V
Evaluation Methodology

We evaluate our QAOA optimization framework on randomly generated graphs drawn from two families: Erdős–Rényi (ER)
[
18
]
and Watts–Strogatz (WS)
[
19
]
. Graph sizes range from 3 to 16 nodes. For each graph model and node size, five unique graph instances are generated and evaluated. Below, we describe the graph generation process, QAOA configuration, and how performance is assessed across different initialization strategies.

V-A

Graph Generation

For each graph size
n\in[3,16]
, we generate five unique instances for both ER and WS models. Erdős–Rényi graphs are constructed using a fixed edge probability
p=0.5
. Watts–Strogatz graphs are generated with a rewiring probability
p=0.3
and neighborhood parameter
k
, which is set to 4 when
n\geq 4
, and adjusted to 2 otherwise to ensure a valid WS structure.

Each graph is represented as an adjacency matrix and used to build the corresponding QAOA circuit.

V-B

Parameter Optimization and Evaluation

For each graph instance and QAOA depth
p\in\{1,2,3\}
, the initial cut value is computed using randomly sampled QAOA parameters from the range
[-\pi,\pi]
. These are then compared against the optimized parameters produced by the Adam-assisted Fully Informed Particle Swarm Optimization (Adam-FIPSO) algorithm.

Performance is evaluated in terms of the cut value obtained from the QAOA circuit, with the classical cut value computed using the one-exchange approximation algorithm as a reference. The improvement percentage of PSO-optimized parameters over random initialization is also reported for each configuration.

\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{Adam\_FIPSO}}=\frac{\mathrm{QAOA\text{-}Cost}(\gamma_{\mathrm{Adam\_FIPSO}},\ \beta_{\mathrm{Adam\_FIPSO}})}{\mathrm{MaxCut}(G)}

(17)

V-C

Random Baseline

To establish a baseline for comparison, a single random sample of QAOA parameters
(\gamma,\beta)
is drawn from a uniform distribution over the range
[-\pi,\pi]
. This process is repeated separately for each QAOA depth
p\in\{1,2,3\}
.

The resulting QAOA cost is computed for each graph instance and normalized by the classical MaxCut value to yield the approximation ratio.

\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{random}}=\frac{\mathrm{QAOA\text{-}Cost}(\gamma,\beta)}{\mathrm{MaxCut}(G)}

(18)

This approximation ratio quantifies the performance of a randomly initialized QAOA circuit without optimization. It serves as a baseline for evaluating the effectiveness of the optimized parameters produced by Adam-FIPSO across different circuit depths.

Improvement Metric

The performance enhancement achieved through Adam-FIPSO is quantified using the normalized approximation ratio (AR). Let
\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{Adam\_FIPSO}}
denote the mean approximation ratio obtained using Adam-FIPSO initialized QAOA parameters, and
\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{random}}
represent the average approximation ratio under random parameter initialization. The improvement is defined as the relative percentage increase over the baseline and computed as:

\mathrm{Improvement}=\frac{\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{Adam\_FIPSO}}-\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{random}}}{\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{random}}}\times 100\%

This framework is particularly well-suited for domains where MaxCut formulations are frequently encountered, such as statistical physics, circuit layout design, portfolio optimization, and image segmentation. In these applications, the ability to rapidly compute high-quality solutions is critical, especially under real-time or near-real-time constraints. In scenarios where problem instances are dynamic or evolve continuously, a lightweight, population-based optimizer like Adam-FIPSO could offer a practical advantage by producing near-optimal QAOA parameters without requiring full retraining.

The Adam-FIPSO framework demonstrates robust and effective performance in guiding QAOA parameter initialization, consistently outperforming random baselines in terms of approximation ratio. This advantage is particularly evident in low-depth QAOA circuits and smaller graph instances, where the optimization landscape tends to be smoother and the parameter space more tractable.

While higher-depth QAOA circuits allow for more expressive quantum states and potentially better approximation ratios, they also impose greater computational demands. Within these practical constraints, Adam-FIPSO effectively identifies high-quality parameters, and further gains are expected with enhanced computational resources and deeper circuit implementations.

V-G

Future Work

The strong performance of our Adam-FIPSO-initialized QAOA across small- to medium-sized graph instances highlights its viability as a classical pre-optimizer within hybrid quantum-classical workflows. However, as graph sizes increase, so do the dimensionality and complexity of the parameter landscape, making optimization increasingly resource-intensive. Future work would explore parallelized variants of Adam-FIPSO, distributing particle evaluations and gradient computations across multiple cores or GPUs. This would significantly reduce wall-clock training time and enable the algorithm to scale to higher-order graphs with larger parameter spaces and deeper QAOA circuits.

Moreover, real-world quantum applications often require optimizing multiple conflicting objectives - for instance, maximizing cut quality while minimizing circuit depth, gate count, or execution time. Incorporating multi-objective optimization frameworks into our approach could enable the generation of Pareto-optimal parameter sets that balance fidelity with hardware efficiency. Constraint-aware variants of PSO and adaptive weighting strategies can be particularly useful here, allowing users to prioritize trade-offs based on application-specific tolerances. Finally, expanding the optimization scope beyond MaxCut to include combinatorial problems like graph coloring, weighted Max-cut, or scheduling can help assess the generalizability and practical utility of our framework across quantum-ready problem domains.
