On the local convergence of integer-valued Lipschitz functions on regular trees.
Nathaniel Butler, Kesav Krishnan, Gourab Ray, Yinon Spinka

Abstract
We study random integer-valued Lipschitz functions on regular trees. It was shown by Peled, Samotij and Yehudayoff [ 22 ] that such functions are localized, however, finer questions about the structure of Gibbs measures remain unanswered. Our main result is that the weak limit of a uniformly chosen 1-Lipschitz function with 0 boundary condition on a d d -ary tree of height n n exists as n → ∞ n\to\infty if 2 ≤ d ≤ 7 2\leq d\leq 7 , but not if d ≥ 8 d\geq 8 , thereby partially answering a question from [ 22 ] . For large d d , the value at the root alternates between being almost entirely concentrated on 0 for even n n and being roughly uniform on { − 1 , 0 , 1 } \{-1,0,1\} for odd n n , leading to different limits as n n approaches infinity along evens or odds. For d ≥ 8 d\geq 8 , the essence of this phenomenon is preserved, which obstructs the convergence. For d ≤ 7 d\leq 7 , this phenomenon ceases to exist, and the law of the value at the root loses its connection with the parity of n n . Along the way, we also obtain an alternative proof of localization. The key idea is a fixed point convergence result for a related operator on ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} , and a procedure to show that the iterations get into a ‘basin of attraction’ of the fixed point. We also prove some accompanying analogous ‘even-odd phenomenon’ type results about M M -lipschitz functions on general non-amenable graphs with high enough expansion (this includes for example the large d d case for regular trees). We also prove a convergence result for 1-Lipschitz functions with { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} boundary condition. This last result relies on an absolute value FKG for uniform 1-Lipschitz functions when shifted by 1 / 2 1/2 .

Proof outline:

Let us briefly outline the ideas behind the proofs.
Theorem

1.1
is the major result in this article and takes up most of the analysis. There are two separate parts, convergence for
2\leq d\leq 7
and non-convergence for
d\geq 8
, the latter being much simpler. The basic idea for the convergence part is a contraction argument: we think of the probability distribution at the root as an element of
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z})
and then we apply iterates of an appropriate operator
F
. The goal then reduces to proving that the iterates of
F
(applied to
\delta_{0}
) converge in an appropriate norm. The proof of this would have been somewhat straightforward if the map
F
turned out to be a contraction (but it isn’t). Nevertheless, we show that after a number of iterations, we get into a certain “basin of attraction”, on which the map becomes a contraction. To show the latter, we prove that the operator norm of the derivative of
F
is strictly less than 1 in this basin. After this, the proof concludes by a simple application of the Banach fixed point theorem.

In practice, directly applying this idea to
F
soon becomes intractable. The way we work our way through is to look at ratios of the consecutive values, which gets rid of the normalizing factor in a good way. For the non-convergence part of the theorem, we show that the iterates oscillate between two separated ‘basins’, ruling out the possibility of convergence (though we still expect convergence of
\mu^{0}_{n,d}
along even
n
). The details of these ideas can be found in
Section

2
.

Theorem

1.2
follows from a certain “FKG for absolute value” result when the height function is shifted by
1/2
. Finally
Theorem

1.3
is a consequence of a variation of a Peierl’s type argument following that in
[
22
,
23
]
.

\psi_{n}(x)\leq x_{n-1}^{d}\left(\frac{1+x_{n}+cx_{n}}{1+x_{n-1}+x_{n-1}x_{n}}\right)^{d}.

Since the latter expression is increasing in

(this can be seen easily by dividing both the numerator and denominator by

) and

so that

Since this expression is increasing in
x_{n}
(follows from (

\psi_{n}(x)\leq b^{d}\left(\frac{1+c+c^{2}}{1+b+bc}\right)^{d}.

thereby completing the proof.
∎

2.3
Convergence for
2\leq d\leq 7

We shall prove a generalization of the convergence result stated in
Theorem

1.1
.
We say a sequence of weights
w=(w_{i})\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{Z}}
is
good
if it is symmetric (i.e.
w(i)=w(-i)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
),
w(0)>0
and there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and a
c\in[0,1)
such that

w(i)=w(i+1)\quad\text{for all }0\leq i<k,\qquad\text{and}\qquad w(i+1)\leq c\cdot w(i)\quad\text{for all }i\geq k.

In other words, the distribution is flat in a symmetric interval around 0, and decays at least at an exponential rate
c
outside this interval. Let
\mu_{n,d}^{w}
be the probability distribution on Lipschitz functions on
T_{n,d}
where each Lipschitz function
f
is weighted by the product of
w(f(v))
over the leaves
v
. Note that
\mu_{n,d}^{\{-k,\ldots,k\}}
is a special case of such a measure.

Theorem 2.6
.

Fix
2\leq d\leq 7
. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n,d}^{w}
exists for all good weight sequences
w
and is independent of
w
. In particular,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n,d}^{\{-k,\ldots,k\}}
exists for all
k\geq 0
and is independent of
k
.

Note that
\mu_{n,d}^{w}
is unaffected by a scaling of
w
. Thus, we may assume henceforth that
w
is itself a probability distribution. Note in particular that this means it belongs to
\mathcal{E}
(it is symmetric and its support is an interval or all of
\mathbb{Z}
).
Our broad goal is to prove that the iterates of
\psi
starting from
\varphi(w)
converge pointwise. Along the way we also argue that all iterates stay in the set
\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c}
, whose preimage under
\varphi
is a tight family of distributions.
Consequently, we argue that
f_{n}(\rho)
converges in distribution and that
\mu^{0}_{n,d}
converges weakly.

The major step is to prove the following.

Proposition 2.7
.

Fix
2\leq d\leq 7
. Let
w
be a good sequence of weights normalized to be a probability distribution. There exists
c\in(0,1)
such that the
\psi^{(k)}({\mathsf{R}}(w))
belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c}
for all
k
large enough and converges pointwise as
k\to\infty
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.6
assuming
Proposition

2.7
.

It is not hard to check that
{\mathsf{R}}^{-1}
is continuous on its domain
\mathcal{R}
(when both the domain and range are endowed with the pointwise topology).
Since
F^{(k)}(w)={\mathsf{R}}^{-1}\circ\psi^{(k)}\circ{\mathsf{R}}(w)
, we conclude from
Proposition

2.7
that
F^{(k)}(w)
converges pointwise and that
F^{(k)}(w)
belongs to
{\mathsf{R}}^{-1}(\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c})
for all
k
large enough. Observe that
{\mathsf{R}}^{-1}(\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c})
is a tight family of distributions.
Thus, letting
f_{n}\sim\mu_{n,d}^{w}
and recalling
Claim

2.1
(or rather its straightforward extension from
\mu^{0}_{n,d}
to
\mu^{w}_{n,d}
) , this implies that
f_{n}(\rho)
converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
.

It is now not too hard to see that
f_{n}
converges locally. We very briefly give the idea. Fix
r\geq 0
and consider the restriction
f_{n,r}
of
f_{n}
to
L_{r}
(assume
n
large). Then, for a given Lipschitz function
\xi
on
L_{r}
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(f_{n,r}=\xi)
is proportional
\prod_{v}\mathbb{P}(f_{n-r}(\rho)=\xi(v))
, where the product is over the leaves of
L_{r}
. Since this product converges as
n\to\infty
, we see that the distribution of
f_{n,r}
converges as
n\to\infty
, which shows that
f_{n}
converges locally.
∎

To prove
Proposition

2.7
we employ the following strategy.
First, we shall show that

(2.10)

if
w
is good then there exist
k\geq 0
and

such that

.

This reduces the problem to understanding the iterations of
\psi
starting from an initial point in
\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c}
.
For this we will show that there exist numbers

with

such that

(2.11)

\text{for every $0\leq c<1$ there exists $k$ such that $\psi^{(k)}(\mathcal{S}_{0,1,c})\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{a_{*},b_{*},c_{*}}$},

and

(2.12)

\text{$\psi$ preserves the convex set $\mathcal{S}_{a_{*},b_{*},c_{*}}$ and is a contraction on it}.

The contraction referred to in (

\|x\|:=\begin{cases}\sup_{i\geq 1}|x_{i}|&\text{if }d=2\\

3
Long-range order for graphs with large expansion

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.3
.
Recall that
G
is an infinite bipartite connect graph with maximum degree
d
and Cheeger constant
h
. We fix a finite subset
G^{\prime}
of
G
. Recall that
\nu^{a,b}_{G^{\prime},M}
is the uniform measure on all
M
-Lipschitz functions on
G^{\prime}\cup\partial G^{\prime}
whose values on
\partial G^{\prime}
are constrained to be in
\{a,\ldots,b\}
for white vertices or in
\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}
for black vertices.

We now fix two integers
a,b
with
0\leq b-a\leq 2M
. To facilitate the description of these Gibbs measures, we shall consider the following sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(f)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathbb{T}_{2n}^{d}\text{ is odd},f(v)\not\in\{b-M,\ldots,a+M\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathbb{T}_{2n}^{d}\text{ is even},f(v)\not\in\{a,\ldots,b\}\}.

and define
B(f):=\mathcal{O}(f)\cup\mathcal{E}(f)
. Let
A(f,v)
denote the distance two connected component of
B(f)
containing
v
. To elaborate: connect two vertices if they are at distance at most two, and let
A(f,v)
denote the connected component of
v
thus formed.
Our goal in the next proposition will be to prove that the size of
A(f,v)
has exponential tail once
h
is much larger compared to
M
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
h\geq 4M\log(d^{4}(4M+1))
and let
f\sim\nu^{a,b}_{G^{\prime},M}
. Then for all
v_{0}\in G^{\prime}
and
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(|A(f,v_{0})|=n)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{hn}{4M}\right).

The upshot of
Proposition

3.1
is that most of the odd vertices take values in
\{b-M,\ldots,a+M\}

and most of the even vertices take values in
\{a,\ldots,b\}
.

Let us illustrate some special cases. If
a=b=0
and
M=1
(1-Lipschitz case with 0 boundary conditions on even vertices), most of the even vertices take the value 0 and the odd vertices take values in
\{-1,0,1\}
(roughly uniformly at random as they are likely to be surrounded by 0s). Also, if
a=0,b=1
(as in
Theorem

1.2
), then both even and odd vertices are likely to take values in
\{0,1\}
. In general, if
b-a=M
, the even and odd vertices play a symmetric role in the measure (and in fact bipartiteness of
G
is not needed).

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

We begin with an elementary fact (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Lemma 1.8]
). Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of
M
-Lipschitz functions on
G^{\prime}
satisfying the required boundary constraints. Let
\mathcal{E}
be a subset of
\mathcal{L}
and suppose that
T
is a map from
\mathcal{E}
to subsets of
\mathcal{L}
. Also assume that there exist
s,t>0
such that
|T(g)|\geq t
for all
g\in\mathcal{E}
, and
|\{g\in\mathcal{A}:g^{\prime}\in T(g)\}|\leq s
for all
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(f\in\mathcal{E})=\frac{|\mathcal{E}|}{|\mathcal{L}|}\leq\frac{s}{t}.

Throughout, we write
A(f)
for
A(f,v_{0})
and observe that
A(f)
cannot contain any vertex of
\partial G^{\prime}
by definition. Fix a distance two connected set
A
containing
v_{0}
but not intersecting
\partial G^{\prime}
and let
\mathcal{E}:=\{g\in\mathcal{L}:A(g)=A\}
. We shall construct a map
T
as described in the previous paragraph so that
s/t
is small, where
s,t
are as in the previous paragraph. Consequently,

\mathbb{P}(A(f)=A)=\mathbb{P}(f\in\mathcal{E})\leq\frac{s}{t}.

Our goal is to make
\frac{s}{t}
exponentially small in the size of
A
with a suitably large constant, so that we can then apply a union bound over all possible realizations of
A(f)
and conclude.

Let us describe the map
T
. For
g\in\mathcal{E}
, let
T(g)
be the set of
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfying

(3.1)

g^{\prime}(x)\begin{cases}=g(x)&\text{if }x\not\in A\cup\partial A\\
\in\{a,\dots,b\}&\text{if }x\in A\cup\partial A,x\text{ even,}\\
\in\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}&\text{if }x\in A\cup\partial A,x\text{ odd.}\\
\end{cases}

The point of this definition is that, as we will see, it allows much more possibilities in
A\cup\partial A
for
g^{\prime}\in T(g)
compared to
g
. Specifically, we claim
T
satisfies the requirement with

(3.2)

s:=(4M+1)^{|A|}(b-a)^{|\{v\in\partial A:v\text{ odd}|}(a-b+2M)^{|\{v\in\partial A:v\text{ even}|},

and

(3.3)

t:=(b-a+1)^{|\{v\in A\cup\partial A:v\text{ odd}|}(a-b+2M+1)^{|\{v\in A\cup\partial A:v\text{ even}|}.

Before proving this claim, let us show how it yields the proposition.
We have

\frac{s}{t}\leq(4M+1)^{|A|}\left(\frac{b-a}{b-a+1}\right)^{|\{v\in\partial A,v\text{ odd}|}\left(\frac{a-b+2M}{a-b+2M+1}\right)^{|\{v\in\partial A,v\text{ even}|}

Since
x\mapsto\frac{x}{x+1}
is increasing and
0\leq b-a\leq 2M
, and using the fact that
|\partial A|\geq h|A|
(since recall that
A
cannot contain any vertex in the boundary ),

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}(A(f)=A)\leq\frac{s}{t}\leq(4M+1)^{|A|}\left(\frac{2M}{2M+1}\right)^{|\partial A|}\leq e^{|A|\log(4M+1)-\frac{h|A|}{2M}}

Finally, the number of distance two connected sets
A
with
|A|=n
and
v_{0}\in A
is at most
d^{4n}
. Union bounding, we get

\mathbb{P}(|A(f)|=n)\leq e^{n(4\log d+\log(4M+1)-\frac{h}{2M})}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{hn}{4M}\right),

as desired.

It remains to prove that
T
satisfies the required upper and lower bounds with (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
We begin with the lower bound. Let
g\in\mathcal{E}
.
Since
|x-y|\leq M
for
x\in\{a,\dots,b\}
and
y\in\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}
, and since
g(v)\in\{a,\dots,b\}
for any even
v\in\partial(A\cup\partial A)
and
g(v)\in\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}
for any odd
v\in\partial(A\cup\partial A)
, we see that every function satisfying (
3.1
) must necessarily belong to
\mathcal{L}
. Hence,

|T(g)|=(b-a+1)^{|\{v\in A\cup\partial A:v\text{ odd}|}(a-b+2M+1)^{|\{v\in A\cup\partial A:v\text{ even}|}=t.

We now move on to the upper bound. Fix
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
. We need to upper bound the number of
g\in\mathcal{E}
such that
g^{\prime}\in T(g)
. Clearly,
g
is uniquely determined outside
A\cup\partial A
. Note that if a vertex
u
in
A
is at distance 2 from the complement of
A\cup\partial A
, then the number of possible values of
g(u)
is at most
4M+1
. By choosing the values of
g
on
A
one after another, in any order consistent with their distance from the complement of
A\cup\partial A
, we see that there are at most
(4M+1)^{|A|}
possible choices for
g
on
A
. To show that
|\{g\in\mathcal{E}:g^{\prime}\in T(g)\}|\leq s
, it remains to show that, given
g
outside
\partial A
, the number of choices for
g
on
\partial A
is at most
\prod_{v\in\partial A}\ell_{v}
, where

l_{v}:=\begin{cases}b-a&\text{if $v$ is even }\\
2M+a-b&\text{if $v$ is odd}\end{cases}.

For this, it suffices to prove a vertex-by-vertex bound, namely, that the number of choices for
g(v)
is at most
\ell_{v}
for any
v\in\partial A
. To this end, fix
v\in\partial A
, and let
u\in A
be any neighbor of
v
(which exists since
v\in\partial A
).
Suppose first that
v
is even.
First note that since
v\in\partial A
and
v
is even,
g(v)\in\{a,\ldots,b\}
.
Similarly, since
u\in A
and
u
is odd,
g(u)\notin\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle a+1\leq g(u)-M\leq g(v)\leq b,\qquad\text{if $g(u)\geq a+M+1$},

\displaystyle a\leq g(v)\leq g(u)+M\leq b-1,\qquad\text{if $g(u)\leq b-M-1$.}

(One consequence of the above inequalities is that
v
cannot be even if
a=b
.)
Therefore, the number of possible values of
g(v)
is at most
\ell_{v}
, as claimed.
Suppose next that
v
is odd.
We similarly have that
g(v)\in\{b-M,\dots,a+M\}
and
g(u)\notin\{a,\dots,b\}
, so that

\displaystyle b-M+1\leq g(u)-M\leq g(v)\leq a+M\qquad\text{if $g(u)\geq b+1$, }

\displaystyle b-M\leq g(v)\leq g(u)+M\leq a+M-1\qquad\text{if $g(u)\leq a-1$.}

(Again,
v
cannot be odd if
b-a=2M
.)
Therefore, the number of possible values of
g(v)
is again at most
\ell_{v}
, as claimed.
∎

The following corollary is a simple generalization of (

), and is what we will actually need in the proof of

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}
be disjoint two connected sets of total size

. Then the probability that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
are distance two connected components of
B(f)
is at most

\exp\left(n\big(\log(4M+1)-\tfrac{h}{2M}\big)\right).

Proof of

1.3
.

Let
(G_{n})
be an exhaustion.
Note that it is enough to prove the following: for all

,

and for all connected

such that

so that they agree on

with probability tending to

as

.

To construct the coupling, let
f\sim\nu_{H,M}^{a,b}
and
f^{\prime}\sim\nu_{H^{\prime},M}^{a,b}
be sampled independently and let
B(f)
and
B(f^{\prime})
be the set of ‘atypical’ vertices as defined before

3.1
. Let
U=B(v,R)

be the union of the distance two connected components of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A(f,f^{\prime},U)=A)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{A_{1}\cup A_{2}=A}\exp((|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|)(\log(4M+1)-\tfrac{h}{2M}))

\displaystyle\leq 3^{|A|}\exp(|A|(\log(4M+1)-\tfrac{h}{2M}))).

Since there are at most
(2k)^{|U|}d^{4k}

\mathbb{P}(|A(f,f^{\prime})|=k)\leq\exp\left(k(\log(3(4M+1))-\tfrac{h}{2M}+4\log d)+|U|\log(2k)\right)\leq\exp(-\tfrac{hk}{4M}+|U|\log(2k)).

Since

is fixed, this bound decays exponentially in

.
In particular, as
R^{\prime}\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}(A(f,f^{\prime},U)\subset B(v,R^{\prime}))\to 1.

It remains to show that
f

f^{\prime}
can be coupled so that they agree on
B(v,R)
on the event
\{(A(f,f^{\prime},U)\subset B(v,R^{\prime})\}
. We proceed via exploration from the boundary.

. Note that, by definition of

.

can be coupled to agree on

We prove this for

(the proof for

, since any

Now we show part (b) of the theorem, which is an easy consequence of
Proposition

3.1
.
Let

. Fix an odd vertex

and denote

. Let
\mathcal{N}^{-}
and
\mathcal{N}^{+}
be the minimum and maximum elements in
\mathcal{N}
, respectively.
Since

is conditionally uniformly distributed in
\{\mathcal{N}^{+}-M,\dots,\mathcal{N}^{-}+M\}
given
\mathcal{N}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{N}^{-},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{+}\}\subsetneq\{a,\dots,b\})

\displaystyle\leq(2M+1)\cdot\mathbb{P}(f(u)\notin\{b-M,\dots,a+M\})

\displaystyle\leq(2M+1)\cdot\mathbb{P}(|A(f,u)|\geq 1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M+1}{e^{h/4M}-1}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{N}^{-},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{+}\}\not\subset\{a,\dots,b\})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}^{-}<a\text{ or }\mathcal{N}^{+}>b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(f(v)\notin\{a,\dots,b\}\text{ for some }v\in N(u))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in N(u)}\mathbb{P}(|A(f,v)|\geq 1))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{e^{h/4M}-1}.

Together, we get that

\mathbb{P}(\{\mathcal{N}^{-},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{+}\}\neq\{a,\dots,b\})\leq\frac{d+2M+1}{e^{h/4M}-1}\leq\frac{d+2M+1}{3d^{4}(4M+1)-1}<\frac{1}{2},

which means that

is the largest atom of
\{\mathcal{N}^{-},\dots,\mathcal{N}^{+}\}
, so that it is uniquely determined by
\nu^{a,b}_{G,M}
.
∎

. Also,
g_{b}(x)>x
if and only if
x<c_{0}(b)
.

Proof.

We have

\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\frac{db^{d}(bx^{2}+2bx+2x+1)(x^{2}+x+1)^{d-1}}{(bx+b+1)^{d+1}}

and

\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x^{2}}=db^{d}(x^{2}+x+1)^{d-2}(bx+b+1)^{-d-2}\\
(b^{2}((d-1)x^{4}+4(d-1)x^{3}+(4d-2)x^{2}+2x+2)+2b(2(d-1)x^{3}+(5d-4)x^{2}+(2d-1)x+1)+d(2x+1)^{2}-2x^{2}-2x+1)

It can be easily checked from these expressions that
\partial g/\partial x
and
\partial^{2}g/\partial x^{2}
are both strictly positive for
d\geq 2
.

For
b<1
, since
g(b,0)=b^{d}>0
and
g(b,1)<g(1,1)=1
and
g
is strictly increasing and convex, it must be the case that
g
has a unique fixed point in
(0,1)
. If
b=1
, although
g(1)=1
and we further note that

\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}|_{x=1}=\frac{2d}{3}>1

if
d\geq 2
. Thus
g
has a unique fixed point in
(0,1)
in this case also. This takes care of the ‘also’ part in the lemma as well.

The fact that
g^{(i)}(c)
converges to the fixed point

is immediate from the fact that
c_{0}<g(x)<x
if
x>c_{0}
and
c_{0}>g(x)>x
if
x<c_{0}
, and hence the iterates monotonically converge to
c_{0}
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

6.3
.

Note that with the new notation used in this section

{\sf i}(x)=f_{1}\circ f_{1+c}(x);\qquad\qquad{\sf j}(x)=f_{1+c}\circ f_{1}(x)

One can easily plot the derivatives of these functions for a range of choices of
c
and check the results for
0<x<1
, and perhaps that is the most practical way to be convinced of the lemma. We could not find a straightforward algebraic way to prove this lemma, so we employ the following rather undesirable case by case analysis.

First observe that by (
6.4
),
f_{1}
and
f_{1+c}
are both decreasing and hence both
{\sf i}
and
{\sf j}
are increasing in
[0,1]
.
We will show that for all
2\leq d\leq 7
,
|{\sf i}^{\prime}(x)|<1
for all
x\in(0,1)
. This ensures that both
{\sf i},{\sf j}
are contractions in
(0,1)
, thus the iterations of
{\sf i}
and
{\sf j}
must converge to a fixed point. Furthemore by Rolle’s theorem, this fixed point must be unique.

Our strategy is as follows. Since both
f_{1},|f^{\prime}_{1}|
are decreasing in
[0,1]
, and since

|f^{\prime}_{1+c}(x)|\leq d\frac{(1+(1+c)x)^{d-1}}{(1+2x)^{d+1}}\leq d\frac{(1+(1+c_{d})x)^{d-1}}{(1+2x)^{d+1}},

for any interval
[a_{1},a_{2}]\subset[0,1]
, we conclude

\displaystyle 0\leq{\sf i}^{\prime}(x)\leq|f_{1}^{\prime}(f_{1+c}(a_{2}))||f^{\prime}_{1+c}(a_{1})|\leq|f_{1}^{\prime}(f_{1}(a_{2}))|d\frac{(1+(1+c_{d})a_{1})^{d-1}}{(1+2a_{1})^{d+1}}\text{ for }a_{1}\leq x\leq a_{2}

\displaystyle 0\leq{\sf j}^{\prime}(x)\leq|f_{1+c}^{\prime}(f_{1}(a_{2}))||f^{\prime}_{1}(a_{1})|\leq|f^{\prime}_{1}(a_{1})|d\frac{(1+(1+c_{d})f_{1}(a_{2}))^{d-1}}{(1+2f_{1}(a_{2}))^{d+1}}\text{ for }a_{1}\leq x\leq a_{2}

Denote

\xi(x)=\xi_{c_{d}}(x)=d\frac{(1+(1+c_{d})x)^{d-1}}{(1+2x))^{d+1}},\qquad\eta(x)=d\frac{(1+x)^{d-1}}{(1+2x)^{d+1}}.

With this new notation, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq{\sf i}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\eta(f_{1}(a_{2}))\xi(a_{1})\text{ for }a_{1}\leq x\leq a_{2}

\displaystyle 0\leq{\sf j}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\xi(f_{1}(a_{2}))\eta(a_{1})\text{ for }a_{1}\leq x\leq a_{2}

Clearly
\xi(x)\leq\eta(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, thus we can further approximate

(6.6)

\max\{{\sf i}^{\prime}(x),{\sf j}^{\prime}(x)\}\leq\xi(f_{1}(a_{2}))\xi(a_{1})\text{ for }a_{1}\leq x\leq a_{2}

It is now enough to pick a
c_{d}>c_{g}
and then find a partition
[\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})\cup[\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})\cup\ldots\cup[\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}]
of
[0,1]
so that
\xi(f_{1}(\beta_{i}))\xi(\alpha_{i})<1
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
