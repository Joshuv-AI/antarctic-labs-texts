Photon pair generation via down-conversion in III-V semiconductor microrings: modal dispersion and quasi-phase-matching
Samuel E. Fontaine, 1 Colin Vendromin, 1 Trevor J. Steiner, 2 Amirali Atrli, 1 Lillian Thiel, 3 Joshua Castro, 3 Galan Moody, 3 John Bowers, 2,3 Marco Liscidini, 4 and J. E. Sipe 1

Abstract
We explore how III-V semiconductor microring resonators can efficiently generate photon pairs and squeezed vacuum states via spontaneous parametric down-conversion by utilizing their built-in quasi phase matching and modal dispersion. We present an analytic expression for the biphoton wave function of photon pairs generated by weak pump pulses, and characterize the squeezed states that result under stronger pumping conditions. Our model includes loss, and captures the statistics of the scattered photons. A detailed sample calculation shows that for low pump powers conversion efficiencies of 10 -5 , corresponding to a rate of 39 39 MHz for a pump power of 1 μ \mu W, are attainable for rudimentary structures such as a simple microring coupled to a waveguide, in both the continuous wave and pulsed excitation regimes. Our results suggest that high levels of squeezing and pump depletion are attainable, possibly leading to the deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states.

I
Introduction

Spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) is routinely used to create photon pairs as well as squeezed states
[
1
]
; the former are used for quantum key distribution
[
2
]
, single heralded photon sources
[
3
]
, and quantum communications
[
4
]
, while the latter are a resource for optical quantum computing
[
5
]
and quantum metrology
[
6
]
. In SPDC, pump photons interact with a material that has a second-order nonlinear susceptibility (
\chi^{(2)}
), and fission into pairs of lower energy signal and idler photons. The rates of these SPDC interactions are limited by the magnitude of
\chi^{(2)}
and phase matching issues. Yet, in integrated resonant structures, such as microring resonators, the light confinement leads to large field intensities that enhance the effectiveness of the nonlinear susceptibility
\chi^{(2)}
, and for certain ring designs and certain choices of resonant modes the usual phase matching difficulties are alleviated
[
7
,
8
]
.

For microrings fabricated out of III-V semiconductors, a “built-in” quasi-phase-matching condition is present due to the difference between the crystal’s natural reference frame and the local reference frame in which the fields propagate
[
9
,
10
]
. In addition, III-V semi-conductors have very high values of
\chi^{(2)}
compared to other materials commonly used for nonlinear interactions
[
11
]
, leading to more efficient generation of quantum states. Also, recent advances in fabrication techniques have resulted in high-Q microring resonators useful for down-conversion from the visible to the telecommunication wavelengths have been developed
[
7
,
12
]
. By adequately designing the microring resonator, the quasi-phase-matching condition can easily be satisfied, as the signal and idler effective refractive indexes can have values similar to that of the effective refractive index of the pump due to modal dispersion
[
7
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

Experiments where high rates of photon pairs were generated have been reported
[
7
,
8
]
, and different approaches to arrive at rate equations or conversion efficiencies for microrings have been presented
[
10
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In older work losses were neglected
[
10
,
17
,
18
]
, and in more recent work, they have been treated by an approach based on Langevin equations
[
15
,
16
]
. In this work we extend these earlier calculations by treating the SPDC interaction shown in
[
18
]
with the strategy for lossy structures presented in
[
19
]
. We also present extensive numerical simulations of realistic microring resonators from III-V semiconductors for down-conversion from the visible to the telecommunication range. Our calculation also extends beyond the pair regime, providing efficiencies for the squeezing regime.

We present the generation efficiencies of photon pairs by SPDC in a lossy III-V semiconductor microring resonator point-coupled to a waveguide, where the waveguide is pumped by cw or pulsed excitation in regimes where pump depletion can be neglected. We account for loss by adding a point-coupled “phantom” waveguide. Our approach uses an expansion in terms of asymptotic-in and asymptotic-out fields, which can be generalized to more complicated structures. This provides us with the statistics of not only the pairs of generated photons exiting the waveguide, but also the statistics of the pairs where both the photons are scattered, and those of the pairs where one photon is scattered and the other leaves the waveguide
[
19
,
18
]
. Since the information of the scattered photons is not lost, we retain a full characterization of the outgoing quantum state. The results from our approach can also be used directly to treat more sophisticated problems involving high squeezing, pump-depletion, and non-Gaussian state generation
[
20
]
.

In the text we treat the scenario where the signal and idler photons generated are associated with different ring resonances (nondegenerate SPDC), but in Appendix B we present the corresponding expressions for the scenario where the signal and idler photons are associated with the same ring resonance (degenerate SPDC).

For excitation by pump pulses we address two different regimes. In the “pair regime,” the generated state consists approximately of vacuum and a small probability amplitude of a pair of photons that can be written from first-order perturbation theory as

\lvert\psi\rangle\approx\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle+\beta\lvert{\mathrm{II}}\rangle+...,

(1)

where
\lvert\mathrm{II}\rangle
is the ket characterizing the pair of photons, and
|\beta|^{2}
can be identified as the probability that a pair is generated. We find an analytic expression for the biphoton wave function (BWF) of the signal and idler photons. In order for Eq. (
1
) to be a valid approximation, we require that
|\beta|^{2}\ll 1
. We also study realistic ring systems and pumping scenarios where more than one pair is typically generated by each pump pulse, and Eq. (
1
) no longer holds. We consider the regime where pump depletion can nonetheless still be neglected, and refer to this as the “lowest-order squeezing (LOS) regime.” Here the ket for the generated photons is well approximated by

\lvert\psi\rangle\approx e^{\beta\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\varphi(k_{1},k_{2})a^{\dagger}(k_{1})b^{\dagger}(k_{2})-\mathrm{H.c.}}\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle,

(2)

where
\varphi(k_{1},k_{2})
is the joint spectral amplitude, and the
\beta
that appears here plays the role of a “squeezing parameter.”

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Sec.
II
, we begin by presenting the system and the theory to obtain the rate equation for cw excitation in the pair regime. We also present the BWF and the number of photons in the generated squeezed state for pulsed excitation for both the pair regime and the LOS regime. In Sec.
III
, we present a detailed sample calculation of the generation efficiencies of a realistic, simple structure: a microring coupled to a single physical waveguide, both fully encapsulated in silicon oxide (SiO
2
) cladding. We show a number of structures for which conversion efficiencies are on the order of
10^{-5}
at critical coupling for cw low pump powers, where the quasi-phase-matching and the dispersion properties of the system are taken into account, and show how the different coupling regimes compare with the one of critical coupling. We present various BWFs for the optimized structures for pulsed excitation for various pulse durations. The outgoing states have a range of different Schmidt numbers (
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
). We also show that our optimized structures are potentially able to generate high amounts of squeezing and could possibly lead to pump depletion – which in turn could lead to deterministic generation of non-Gaussian states
[
24
]
– for relatively-low-energy pump pulses. Finally, in Sec.
IV
, we include a brief conclusion, and discuss future work.

II.1
The linear Hamiltonian

The linear Hamiltonian of our system consists of the sum of the Hamiltonians of its components and the Hamiltonians describing the coupling between them. We begin with an isolated ring, for which the Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\mathrm{ring}}=\sum_{u}\hbar\omega_{u}b^{\dagger}_{u}b_{u},

(3)

where the resonant frequencies are denoted by
\omega_{u}
, and the operators
b_{u}
satisfy the commutation relations

[b_{u},b_{u^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{uu^{\prime}}.

(4)

The associated displacement field inside the ring is given by

\bm{\mathsf{D}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{u}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{u}}{2\mathcal{L}}}\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta)b_{u}e^{i\kappa_{u}\zeta}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(5)

where
\zeta
indicates the distance along the circle at the nominal radius
\mathcal{R}
of the ring, and
\bm{r}_{\perp}
is a vector in the local
zx
plane (see Fig. 1),
\kappa_{u}=2\pi m_{u}/\mathcal{L}
are the resonant wavenumbers in the ring with
m_{u}
the associated mode numbers
[
22
]
, of which the positive ones will be relevant here, and
\mathcal{L}=2\pi\mathcal{R}
; the
\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta)
are the field amplitudes, normalized according to

\begin{split}\int\frac{v_{p}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})}{v_{g}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})}\frac{\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}^{*}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\cdot\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp})}{\epsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}=1,\end{split}

(6)

where
\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\omega_{u})
is the relative permittivity,
v_{g}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})
is the local group velocity of the material,
and
v_{p}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})
is the local phase velocity of the material, all at frequency
\omega_{u}

[
22
]
. Here we have used the fact that the dot product
\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}^{*}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta)\cdot\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta)
is independent of
\zeta
, and we have written it simply as
\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}^{*}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\cdot\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp})
. In the systems treated here we consider pump frequencies near a ring resonance frequency that we denote by
\omega_{P}
, signal frequencies near a ring resonance that we denote by
\omega_{S}
, and idler frequencies near a ring resonance that we denote by
\omega_{I}
; for each pump scenario we also consider a range of different
\omega_{S}
and
\omega_{I}
.

Next we consider an isolated waveguide, and focus first on the actual waveguide in Fig. 1 in this isolated limit. We write the total displacement field as

\bm{D}(\bm{r})=\sum_{u}\bm{D}_{u}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{H.c.},

(7)

where here each
u
labels a frequency “bin” with centre at the ring resonance
\omega_{u}
, and extending over all frequencies of light in the waveguide relevant for coupling into that ring resonance. So we have

\bm{D}_{u}(\bm{r})=\int\mathrm{d}k\bm{D}_{uk}(\bm{r})a_{u}(k),

(8)

and the integral ranges over the
k
in frequency bin
u
. The ladder operators satisfy the usual commutation relations
[
22
]

[a_{u}(k),a_{u}^{\dagger}(k^{\prime})]=\delta(k-k^{\prime}),

(9)

with all the other commutators vanishing, and

\bm{D}_{uk}{(\bm{r})}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{uk}}{4\pi}}\bm{d}_{uk}(\bm{r}_{\perp})e^{iks},

(10)

where
\bm{r}_{\perp}
is the vector in a cross-section of the waveguide, perpendicular to the direction of propagation, which is indicated by increasing
s
; for the actual waveguide in Fig. 1, these correspond respectively to the
y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
plane and the direction
x^{\prime}
. In analogy with Eq. (
6
), the field amplitudes are normalized
[
22
]
according to

\begin{split}\int\frac{v_{p}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{uk})}{v_{g}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{uk})}\frac{{\bm{d}}_{uk}^{*}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\cdot\bm{d}_{uk}(\bm{r}_{\perp})}{\epsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\omega_{uk})}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}=1.\\
\end{split}

(11)

Here we have written the frequency associated with wavenumber
k
within bin
u
as

\omega_{uk}=\omega_{u}+v_{u}(k-K_{u}),

(12)

where group velocity dispersion and higher-order terms are neglected for the small frequency ranges in each frequency bin
u
; the group velocity associated with bin

is denoted by
v_{u}=\left(\partial\omega_{uk}/\partial k\right)_{K_{u}}
, with
K_{u}
the value of
k
at the centre frequency
\omega_{u}
.
It is then convenient to introduce a channel operator associated with each frequency bin,

\psi_{u}(s)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{\sqrt{2\pi}}a_{u}(k)e^{i(k-K_{u})s},

(13)

and using Eq. (
12

[
22
]
as

\begin{split}H_{\mathrm{wg}}&=\sum_{u}\Bigg[\int\hbar\omega_{u}\psi^{\dagger}_{u}(s)\psi_{u}(s)\mathrm{d}s\\
&-\frac{i\hbar v_{u}}{2}\int\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{u}(s)\frac{\partial\psi_{u}(s)}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial\psi^{\dagger}_{u}(s)}{\partial s}\psi_{u}(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\Bigg].\end{split}

(14)

We adopt a point coupling model between the waveguides and the ring resonator. For the actual waveguide, we take the coupling point at
s=0
and the coupling Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\mathrm{cpl}}=\sum_{u}(\hbar{\gamma}_{u}b^{\dagger}_{u}\psi_{u}(0)+\mathrm{H.c.}),

(15)

where
\gamma_{u}

. This expression is valid in the high finesse regime

]

Up to this point, only expressions for the actual waveguide have been introduced. We can generalize these to refer to either the actual or the phantom waveguide by introducing an index
\lambda\in\{\mathrm{ac},\mathrm{ph}\}
. Then for the actual (phantom) waveguide, the direction of increasing propagation is

(

), with the field operator denoted by
\psi_{u}^{\mathrm{ac}}(s)
(
\psi_{u}^{\mathrm{ph}}(s)

\gamma_{u}^{\mathrm{ac}}
(
\gamma_{u}^{\mathrm{ph}}

\omega_{uk}^{\lambda}=\omega_{u}+v_{u}^{\lambda}(k-K_{u}^{\lambda}),

(16)

to apply to both waveguides, where

\psi_{u}^{\lambda}(s)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{\sqrt{2\pi}}a_{u}^{\lambda}(k)e^{i(k-K_{u}^{\lambda})s},

\left[a_{u}^{\lambda}(k),\left\{a_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})\right\}^{\dagger}\right]=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta(k-k^{\prime}).

Using these definitions, the total linear Hamiltonian of our system in Fig.

\begin{split}H_{L}&=H_{\mathrm{ring}}+H^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\mathrm{wg}}+H^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\mathrm{wg}}+H^{\mathrm{ac}}_{\mathrm{cpl}}+H^{\mathrm{ph}}_{\mathrm{cpl}}.\end{split}

(19)

II.2
Asymptotic fields

In calculating the nonlinear response of structures such as the ring resonator we consider here, one could of course proceed by using an expansion of the full displacement field in terms of the “ring mode fields” from Eq. (
5
) and the “waveguide mode fields” from Eq. (
7
). But since there is linear coupling between these elements of the structure, that would complicate the analysis of nonlinear effects. An alternate strategy is to employ “asymptotic-in mode fields” and “asymptotic-out mode fields”
[
18
]
, an extension of the use of asymptotic-in and asymptotic-out states in scattering theory
[
25
]
.

Figure 2:
Schematic of the a) asymptotic-in field for the actual waveguide, b) asymptotic-out field for the actual waveguide, c) asymptotic-out field for the phantom waveguide.

For the system we are considering here the asymptotic-in mode fields form a complete set, as do the asymptotic-out mode fields, so we can write either

\bm{D}(\bm{r})=\sum_{u}\bm{D}_{u}^{\mathrm{out}}(\bm{r})+\rm{H.c.}

(20)

or

\bm{D}(\bm{r})=\sum_{u}\bm{D}_{u}^{\mathrm{in}}(\bm{r})+\rm{H.c.},

(21)

with

\begin{split}\bm{D}_{u}^{\rm{out}}(\bm{r})&=\sum_{\lambda}\int\mathrm{d}k\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(\bm{r})a_{u}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k),\\
\bm{D}_{u}^{\rm{in}}(\bm{r})&=\sum_{\lambda}\int\mathrm{d}k\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(\bm{r})a_{u}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(k),\end{split}

(22)

where the sum over
\lambda
is summing over the different waveguides. Both the fields
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(\bm{r})
and
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(\bm{r})
are generally nonzero in the ring and in both waveguides, and in the absence of nonlinearity the Heisenberg operator versions of
a_{u}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k)
and
a_{u}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(k)
are respectively
a_{u}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k)e^{-i\omega_{uk}^{\lambda}t}
and
a_{u}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(k)e^{-i\omega_{uk}^{\lambda}t}
. That is, the asymptotic-in and -out mode fields identify modes of the full linear Hamiltonian (Eq. (
19
)).

The mode fields
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(\bm{r})
are constructed (see Fig. 2b and 2c) so that they are equal to
\bm{D}_{uk}(\bm{r})
for waveguide
\lambda
and
s>0
(indicated by the red arrows), and vanish for the other waveguide for
s>0
; the mode fields
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(\bm{r})
(see Figure 2a for the asymptotic-in mode field for the actual waveguide) are constructed so that they are equal to
\bm{D}_{uk}(\bm{r})
for waveguide
\lambda
and
s<0
(indicated by the red arrow) and vanish for the other waveguide for
s<0
. So the asymptotic-out expansion is appropriate for the signal and idler fields exiting the structure, while the asymptotic-in expansion is appropriate for the pump field entering the structure
[
18
,
19
,
25
]
.

We assume that the nonlinearity is effective only in the ring resonator, where the fields are enhanced. Thus, in constructing the nonlinear interaction Hamiltonian we need the expressions for the fields
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(\bm{r})
and
\bm{D}_{uk}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(\bm{r})
in the ring. We denote these by
\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{uk}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(\bm{r})
and
\bm{\mathsf{D}}_{uk}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}(\bm{r})
, and they are given by
[
19
]
:

\begin{split}&\bm{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}_{uk}(\bm{r})=-\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{u}}{4\pi}}\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta){F}_{u+}^{\lambda}(k)e^{i\kappa_{u}\zeta},\\
&\bm{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathrm{in},\lambda}_{uk}(\bm{r})=-\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{u}}{4\pi}}\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta){F}_{u-}^{\lambda}(k)e^{i\kappa_{u}\zeta},\\
\end{split}

(23)

where

{F}_{u\pm}^{\lambda}(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}}}\left(\frac{({\gamma}_{u}^{\lambda})^{*}}{v_{u}^{\lambda}(K_{u}^{\lambda}-k)\pm i\bar{\Gamma}_{u}}\right)

(24)

describes the resonant enhancement, with
\bar{\Gamma}_{u}
the total decay rate of the resonator, which is related to the decay rates into the actual and phantom waveguides by

\bar{\Gamma}_{u}=\sum_{\lambda}\Gamma_{u}^{\lambda}=\Gamma_{u}^{\mathrm{ac}}+\Gamma_{u}^{\mathrm{ph}}.

(25)

In the point coupling model we adopt these decay rates follow from the group velocities and coupling constants in the Hamiltonian
[
19
]
:

\Gamma_{u}^{\lambda}=\frac{|{\gamma}_{u}^{\lambda}|^{2}}{2v_{u}^{\lambda}}.

(26)

We introduce the loaded quality factor
Q_{u}^{\mathrm{(load)}}
, which is related to the total decay rate
\bar{\Gamma}_{u}
of the ring resonance
u
,

\begin{split}\bar{\Gamma}_{u}&=\frac{\omega_{u}}{2Q_{u}^{\mathrm{(load)}}},\end{split}

(27)

and the quality factors associated with the coupling between the ring and the waveguide
{\lambda}
are given by

\begin{split}{\Gamma}_{u}^{\lambda}&=\frac{\omega_{u}}{2Q_{u}^{\lambda}}.\end{split}

(28)

Since the phantom waveguide models loss,
Q_{u}^{\mathrm{ph}}
is the “intrinsic quality factor,” and
Q_{u}^{\mathrm{ac}}
is the “extrinsic quality factor,” and

\frac{1}{Q_{u}^{\mathrm{(load)}}}=\frac{1}{Q_{u}^{\mathrm{ac}}}+\frac{1}{Q_{u}^{\mathrm{ph}}.}

(29)

II.3
The nonlinear Hamiltonian

We assume that the only relevant nonlinear response coefficient is
\chi^{(2)}
. We neglect the third-order processes such as self-phase modulation (SPM) and cross-phase modulation (XPM). However, for the sample calculations we present in Sec.
III
we find that the shifts in the pump, signal, and idler resonances can be considered negligible – approximately two orders of magnitude smaller than the resonance linewidth – for continuous-wave pump powers below
100\,{\rm\mu W}

[
26
]
.

With the assumption that the nonlinearity is important only in the ring, where the fields are enhanced, the nonlinear Hamiltonian takes the form
[
22
]

H_{NL}=-\frac{1}{3\epsilon_{0}}\int\Gamma^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r}){\mathsf{D}}^{i^{\prime}}(\bm{r}){\mathsf{D}}^{j^{\prime}}(\bm{r}){\mathsf{D}}^{k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})\mathrm{d}\bm{r},

(30)

where
{\mathsf{D}}^{l^{\prime}}(\bm{r})
is the
l^{\prime\mathrm{th}}
Cartesian field component inside the ring, the integration ranges over the ring, and for frequencies around
\omega_{P}
,
\omega_{S}
, and
\omega_{I}
, we have

\Gamma^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})=\frac{\chi^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})}{\epsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r};\omega_{S})\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r};\omega_{I})\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r};\omega_{P})},

(31)

where
\chi^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})
is the nonlinear susceptibility at the frequencies of interest
[
22
]
.

We note here that the primed indices

to the ring frame, as described in Appendix
A
and indicated in Fig.

where the displacement operators

To derive Eq. (
35

22

\bm{\mathsf{d}}_{u}(\bm{r})=\epsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}(\bm{r};\omega_{u})\bm{\mathsf{e}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp},\zeta).

In the ring frame, at points inside the ring the elements of

\begin{split}\chi^{(2)}_{[xxz]}(\bm{r})&=\bar{\chi}^{(2)}\sin{(2\phi)},\\

\begin{split}W_{SIP}^{(\pm)}(\bm{r}_{\perp})&=\Bigg\{\sum\limits_{i,j,k=[xyz]}[{\mathsf{e}}_{S}^{i}(\bm{r}_{\perp}){\mathsf{e}}_{I}^{j}(\bm{r}_{\perp})]^{*}{\mathsf{e}}_{P}^{k}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\\

&+\,\,\,\,\,\sum\limits_{i,j,k=[yyz]}[{\mathsf{e}}_{S}^{i}(\bm{r}_{\perp}){\mathsf{e}}_{I}^{j}(\bm{r}_{\perp})]^{*}{\mathsf{e}}_{P}^{k}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\Bigg]\Bigg\},\end{split}

(39)

with the notation
{i,j,k=[abc]}
under the sum denoting that the
(i,j,k)
triplet is summed only over the distinct permutations of
\{a,b,c\}
, and

\begin{split}V_{SIP}^{(\pm)}&=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{L}}e^{i(\kappa_{P}-\kappa_{S}-\kappa_{I}\pm\frac{2}{\mathcal{R}})\zeta}\mathrm{d}\zeta\\
&=2\mathcal{R}e^{i\pi\mathcal{R}\Delta\kappa}\frac{\sin(\pi(\mathcal{R}\Delta\kappa\pm 2))}{\mathcal{R}\Delta\kappa\pm 2},\end{split}

(40)

with
\Delta\kappa=\kappa_{P}-\kappa_{S}-\kappa_{I}

(41)

which is an integer, we see that

vanishes unless

(42)

which are the possible quasi-phase-matching conditions. Further, at most one of

can vanish; when the

(
-
) quasi-phase-matched condition is met, the value of

(
V_{SIP}^{(-)}
) is equal to
2\pi\mathcal{R}
(
2\pi\mathcal{R}
) and the
V_{SIP}^{(-)}
(

) term is zero, and so at most one term in Eq. (

\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{SIP}
.
We write the “
\pm
matched” version of Eq. (

) by combining Eq. (

terms,

\begin{split}\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{SIP}^{(\pm\,\mathrm{matched})}&=2\epsilon_{0}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}\pi\mathcal{R}\int_{\rm{ring}}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}W_{SIP}^{(\pm)}(\bm{r}_{\perp}).\end{split}

(43)

To capture the effective area of the waveguide mode that is relevant for the nonlinear interaction, we can define

\begin{split}{A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(\pm)}}&=\frac{{{N_{S}N_{I}N_{P}}}}{\left|\int_{\mathrm{ring}}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}W_{SIP}^{(\pm)}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\right|^{2}},\end{split}

(44)

where

N_{u}=\int\frac{n(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})/\bar{n}_{u}}{v_{g}(\bm{r}_{\perp};\omega_{u})/\bar{v}_{u}}\bm{\mathsf{e}}_{u}^{*}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\cdot\bm{\mathsf{e}}_{u}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp},

(45)

with
\bar{v}_{u}
and

typical values of the group velocities and group
refractive indices, respectively, introduced here just for convenience. Note that Eqs. (

) and (

N_{u}=\bar{v}_{u}/(c\epsilon_{0}\bar{n}_{u})
. In terms of the effective area from Eq. (

\begin{split}\bar{\mathsf{K}}_{SIP}^{(\pm\,\mathrm{matched})}&=\frac{2\bar{\chi}^{(2)}\pi\mathcal{R}}{\epsilon_{0}^{1/2}c^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}_{S}\bar{v}_{I}\bar{v}_{P}}{\bar{n}_{S}\bar{n}_{I}\bar{n}_{P}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(\pm)}}}.\\
\end{split}

(46)

II.4
Continuous-wave excitation

As a first calculation – and to help identify the target parameters for the system – we consider a monochromatic classical pump at frequency

\begin{split}H^{\mathrm{SPDC}}_{NL}(t)&\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{}}_{NL}(t)=-\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\mathsf{M}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\\

\end{split}

\begin{split}\Omega_{SI}(k_{1},k_{2})\equiv\omega_{0}-\omega_{Sk_{1}}^{\lambda}-\omega_{Ik_{2}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}=A-v_{S}^{\lambda}k_{1}-v_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}k_{2},\end{split}

II.4.1
Rates

As shown earlier
[
19
]
, using Eq. (

and

R^{SI}=\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}

(52)

where

R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\delta(\Omega_{SI}(k_{1},k_{2}))|\mathsf{M}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})|^{2}

(53)

is the rate of generation of photon pairs where the signal photon (
S
) is exiting through the
\lambda
waveguide and the idler photon (
I
) is exiting through the
\lambda^{\prime}
waveguide.
After integrating over both
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, we find

\begin{split}R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}=&\frac{P_{P}P_{\rm{vac}}\left(\bar{\chi}^{(2)}\right)^{2}\sqrt{\omega_{S}\omega_{I}}\bar{\Gamma}_{P}\eta_{S}^{\lambda}\eta_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}\eta_{P}^{\rm{(ac)}}}{2\hbar\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{3}\bar{\Gamma}_{S}\bar{\Gamma}_{I}\mathcal{R}}\\
&\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\times{\frac{\bar{v}_{S}\bar{v}_{I}\bar{v}_{P}}{\bar{n}_{S}\bar{n}_{I}\bar{n}_{P}}}\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(\pm)}}\frac{1}{\left[(\delta\omega)^{2}+(\bar{\Gamma}_{P})^{2}\right]},\end{split}

(54)

which scales linearly with the pump power
P_{P}
, where we have defined the pump detuning
\delta\omega\equiv\omega_{0}-\omega_{P}
and the escape efficiencies
\eta_{u}^{\lambda}
,

\eta_{u}^{\lambda}\equiv\Gamma_{u}^{\lambda}/\bar{\Gamma}_{u}.

(55)

We note that the rate expression from Eq. (

) is for a given set of resonances with resonant wavenumbers satisfying the quasi-phase-matching condition given by Eq. (
41

or

) is chosen by which quasi-phase-matching condition is met from Eq. (

) (either
+
or

). We have written the rate as a function of the vacuum power

,
19
]

P_{\rm{vac}}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{S}\omega_{I}}\bar{\Gamma}_{S}\bar{\Gamma}_{I}(\bar{\Gamma}_{S}+\bar{\Gamma}_{I})}{(\Delta\omega)^{2}+(\bar{\Gamma}_{S}+\bar{\Gamma}_{I})^{2}}.

(56)

The rates for a different combination of exit waveguides
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
, are easily deduced from Eq. (

)
[
19
]
,

\frac{R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}}{R^{SI}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}}=\frac{\eta_{S}^{\lambda}\eta_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{\eta_{S}^{\mu}\eta_{I}^{\mu^{\prime}}}=\frac{\Gamma_{S}^{\lambda}\Gamma_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{\Gamma_{S}^{\mu}\Gamma_{I}^{\mu^{\prime}}}.

(57)

Of central interest will be the total rate at which both signal and idler photons leave the actual channel,

R^{\mathrm{total}}_{\mathrm{ac},\mathrm{ac}}=\hat{R}^{SS}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}+\sum_{S>I}R^{SI}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}

(58)

where
S
and

label the distinct frequency bins, with

indicating that the centre frequency of the signal bin (
\omega_{S}
) is taken greater than the centre frequency of the idler bin (

). The expression for the nondegenerate rate

follows from Eq. (

), and the degenerate efficiency
\hat{R}^{SS}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}
is that of Eq. (
118
). We can also introduce a generation efficiency
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{total}}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}
, defined as the ratio of the rate at which photon pairs are generated in the actual channel to the rate at which pump photons are incident,

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{total}}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}={R}^{\mathrm{total}}_{\mathrm{ac},\mathrm{ac}}\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{P_{P}}.

(59)

II.4.2
Discussion

We present numerical results in Sec.
III
, but we briefly mention here how certain parameters affect the rate of pair generation and thus the efficiency.

First, the rate is inversely proportional to the effective area, and so it scales with the square of the integral in Eq. (
43
). Maximizing this integral involves identifying the best overlap between the electric-field mode profiles.

Second, we note that the rate is inversely proportional to the radius of the ring
\mathcal{R}
, implying that lowering the radius of the ring might improve rates, although of course we can expect the intrinsic
Q
to decrease as lower radii are considered due to increased scattering loss
[
12
]
.

Third, consider an
R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
that does not vanish; this arises when the quasi-phase-matching condition from Eq. (
42
) is satisfied. If the pump frequency were set to the ring resonance frequency
\omega_{P}
, and we wanted the signal and idler generated at the ring resonance frequencies
\omega_{S}
and
\omega_{I}
respectively, then from energy conservation we would require
\omega_{P}-\omega_{S}-\omega_{I}=0
. In general this cannot be satisfied together with the quasi-phase-matching condition, which is required to have
R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\neq 0
. So the signal and the idler will not be generated exactly at the ring resonance frequencies, which will weaken the generation rate.

More generally, if the pump frequency were detuned from the ring resonance
\omega_{P}
, set to a value
\omega_{0}\neq\omega_{P}
but still within the resonance centred at
\omega_{P}
, the pump intensity in the ring would be lower than if the excitation were at
\omega_{P}
, and this in itself would decrease the generation rate, as indicated by the nonzero value of
\delta\omega
in the expression from Eq. (
54
) for

R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
. Then the condition for energy conservation, with the signal and idler generated at the ring resonance frequencies
\omega_{S}
and
\omega_{I}
respectively, would be
\Delta\omega\equiv\omega_{0}-\omega_{S}-\omega_{I}=0
. This in general will not be satisfied, but the generation rate is largest when this is as small as possible, with the signal and idler light as close as possible to
\omega_{S}
and
\omega_{I}
respectively. This effect is captured by the
P_{\mathrm{vac}}
term in
R^{SI}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
from Eq. (
56
), which peaks as
\Delta\omega\rightarrow 0
.

To maximize the rate, both
\Delta\omega
and
\delta\omega
should be as close to zero as possible; specifically, to have a non-negligible rate, we should have
{|\Delta\omega|}\lesssim\bar{\Gamma}_{S}+\bar{\Gamma}_{I}
and
{|\delta\omega|}\lesssim\bar{\Gamma}_{P}
. The latter condition can be satisfied by choosing the pump frequency. The former condition, which we call the
frequency bin matching condition
, is a challenge to obtain, and is more difficult as rings of higher finesse are considered.

II.5
Pulsed excitation

We now consider excitation with a pump pulse, and formulate the problem in terms of “input” and “output” kets,
\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
and
\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle
respectively
[
27
]
. For a pulse incident on the structure, the input ket is the ket that would describe the state to which the incident pulse would evolve at
t=0
were there no nonlinearity, and the output ket is the ket at
t=0
that would evolve to the actual state at later times, including the effects of the nonlinearity, if there were no nonlinearity. We take the input ket to be a coherent state,

\begin{split}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle=e^{(\alpha A^{\dagger}_{P}-\mathrm{H.c.})}\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle,\end{split}

(60)

where

\begin{split}A^{\dagger}_{P}=\int\mathrm{d}k_{3}\phi(k_{3})\left[a_{P}^{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{ac}}(k_{3})\right]^{\dagger},\end{split}

(61)

is the supermode pump creation operator
[
27
]
. The pulse distribution function
\phi(k_{3})
is normalized according to

\int\left|\phi(k_{3})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}k_{3}=1,

(62)

and the expectation value of the total number of photons in the pump pulse is
N_{P}\equiv|\alpha|^{2}
.

II.5.1
pair regime

We first look at the pair regime, where the outgoing state approximately consists of only pairs of photons. In this case, the output ket can be written as
[
27
,
19
]

\begin{split}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle&\approx\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle+\beta(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}})^{\dagger}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(63)

where
|\beta|^{2}
is the probability of generating a pair of photons. The pair generation operator is

\begin{split}(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}})^{\dagger}\equiv\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}&\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\\
&\times\left[{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k_{1}){a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{2})\right]^{\dagger},\end{split}

(64)

which is written in terms of the components of the biphoton wave function (BWF)
\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})
associated with the signal photon exiting the
\lambda
waveguide and the idler photon exiting the
\lambda^{\prime}
waveguide,

\begin{split}\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\equiv\frac{i\alpha}{\beta\hbar}&\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}t\int\mathrm{d}k_{3}\mathsf{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};t)\phi(k_{3}),\end{split}

(65)

where the first (i.e.,
k_{1}
) and second (i.e.,
k_{2}
) arguments in the function relate to the signal and idler respectively, and range only over those resonances; the function is normalized according to

\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\left|\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\right|^{2}=1.

(66)

Since the operators for the asymptotic-out fields commute with those for the asymptotic-in fields, Eq. (
63
) for the output ket can be written as

\begin{split}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle=e^{(\alpha A^{\dagger}_{P}-\mathrm{H.c.})}\lvert\psi\rangle,\end{split}

(67)

where
\lvert\psi\rangle
is the state of the generated photons, which we write in the pair regime as

\lvert\psi\rangle\approx\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle+\beta\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle.

(68)

The ket
\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle
represents a two-photon state where the signal photon exits by waveguide
\lambda
and the idler photon exits by waveguide
\lambda^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}&k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\\
&\times\left[a_{S}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k_{1})a_{I}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{2})\right]^{\dagger}\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle.\end{split}

(69)

The states
\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle
and
\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\rangle
are orthogonal for
\lambda\neq\mu
and
\lambda^{\prime}\neq\mu^{\prime}
,

\begin{split}\langle\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\,|\,\mathopen{}{\mathrm{II}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}}\rangle=&\delta_{\lambda\mu}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}\\
&\times\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})\varphi^{\mathrm{II}*}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2}).\end{split}

(70)

We can write the full two-photon ket by summing over the output waveguides,

\lvert\mathrm{II}\rangle=\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\lvert\mathrm{II}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle,

(71)

where

\langle\mathrm{II}\,|\,\mathopen{}\mathrm{II}\rangle=1.

(72)

Evaluating the two integrals in Eq. (
65
) and neglecting group velocity dispersion over the pump bandwidth, we obtain an analytical expression for the components of the BWF

\begin{split}\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})=\frac{2\pi i\alpha}{\beta{v_{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}}\hbar}\mathsf{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},X_{P})\phi\left(X_{P}\right),\end{split}

(73)

where

X_{P}\equiv K_{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}-\frac{(\omega_{P}-\omega_{Sk_{1}}^{\lambda}-\omega_{Ik_{2}}^{\lambda^{\prime}})}{v_{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}}.

(74)

In this pair regime, the probability
|\beta|^{2}
that a pair of photons is generated is determined by using the expression from Eq. (
73
) for the components of the biphoton wave function in the normalization condition from Eq. (
66
). We find

\begin{split}|\beta|^{2}&=\frac{4\pi^{2}E_{P}}{(v_{P}^{\mathrm{ac}})^{2}\hbar^{3}\omega_{0}}\\
&\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\times\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\left|\mathsf{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},X_{P})\phi\left(X_{P}\right)\right|^{2},\end{split}

(75)

where
E_{P}
is the pump pulse energy; this
expression scales linearly with the energy of the pump pulse for a fixed pulse duration. We can define the probabilities from the different combinations of output waveguides

\begin{split}|\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|^{2}\equiv&\frac{4\pi^{2}E_{P}}{(v_{P}^{\mathrm{ac}})^{2}\hbar^{3}\omega_{0}}\\
&\times\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\left|\mathsf{K}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},X_{P})\phi\left(X_{P}\right)\right|^{2},\end{split}

(76)

where the total probability is given by

|\beta|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|^{2}.

(77)

Dividing by the number of pump photons, the efficiency for the pair regime, which does not vary within this regime, can be identified as

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pulse;pairregime}}^{SI}&=\frac{|\beta|^{2}}{|\alpha|^{2}},\end{split}

(78)

and the efficiency for each output channel is

\begin{split}[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pulse;pairregime}}^{SI}]_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}&=\frac{|\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|^{2}}{|\alpha|^{2}},\end{split}

(79)

and similar to Eq. (
57
) we have

\begin{split}\frac{|\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}|^{2}}{|\beta_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}|^{2}}=\frac{[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pulse;pairregime}}^{SI}]_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}}{[\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pulse;pairregime}}^{SI}]_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}}=\frac{\eta_{S}^{\lambda}\eta_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{\eta_{S}^{\mu}\eta_{I}^{\mu^{\prime}}}=\frac{\Gamma_{S}^{\lambda}\Gamma_{I}^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{\Gamma_{S}^{\mu}\Gamma_{I}^{\mu^{\prime}}}.\end{split}

(80)

II.5.2
Lowest-order squeezing regime

To explore the lowest-order squeezing (LOS) regime, where we must consider amplitudes for the generation of more than one photon pair by the pump pulse, we employ a “backwards Heisenberg picture” approach
[
27
]
, and perform the calculation to first order in the operator dynamics (hence “lowest order squeezing”), which leads to an undepleted pump approximation. The result is equivalent to a more standard calculation assuming a classical, undepleted pump from the start, and neglecting time-ordering corrections in the evolution operator. Using either approach, we find

\begin{split}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle=&e^{\left[\beta(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}})^{\dagger}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right]}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle,\end{split}

(81)

where within the approximations identified above the pair generation operator is the same as previously defined in Eq. (
64
), and the norm of
\beta
is still given by Eq. (
75
), but now
\beta
(the “squeezing parameter”
[
23
]
), no longer immediately gives the probability of generating pairs, and
\varphi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})
is more properly identified as the “joint spectral amplitude.” The ket for the generated light in the LOS regime is given by a multimode squeezed vacuum state

\lvert\psi\rangle=e^{\left[\beta(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}})^{\dagger}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right]}\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle.

(82)

Using the state in Eq. (
82
) we now calculate the number of squeezed photons and other useful quantities such as the moments and the Schmidt number. To do this we discretize the wavevectors in the actual and phantom waveguides. We begin by discretizing
Eq. (
64
):

\begin{split}\left(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{ij}\sqrt{\Delta k_{1}\Delta k_{2}}\Phi^{\mathrm{II}(ij)}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\left[{a}_{Si}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}{a}_{Ij}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda^{\prime}}\right]^{\dagger},\end{split}

(83)

and we combine the indices
\lambda
and
i
to
m
, and the indices
\lambda^{\prime}
and
j
to
m^{\prime}
. The index discretizing
k_{1}
(
k_{2}
) is
i
(
j
) and
i=\left\{1,\dots,\ell_{1}\right\}
(
j=\left\{1,\dots,\ell_{2}\right\}
), and since
\lambda
(
\lambda^{\prime}
) is summed over the two indices “ac” and “ph”:
m
(
m^{\prime}
) is summed over
2\ell_{1}
(
2\ell_{2}
) indices. We can then write Eq. (
83
) as

\begin{split}\left(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{\dagger}=\sum_{mm^{\prime}}\sqrt{\Delta k_{1}\Delta k_{2}}\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{mm^{\prime}}\left[{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m}{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\right]^{\dagger},\end{split}

(84)

and we define a
2\ell_{1}\times 2\ell_{2}

squeezing matrix
\bm{J}
(
[
20
]

\begin{split}J_{mm^{\prime}}=\beta\sqrt{\Delta k_{1}\Delta k_{2}}\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{mm^{\prime}},\end{split}

(85)

which allows us to write Eq. (

\begin{split}\left(A_{\mathrm{II}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{mm^{\prime}}J_{mm^{\prime}}\left[{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m}{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\right]^{\dagger},\end{split}

(86)

and to finally write our generated state as

\begin{split}\lvert\psi\rangle=&\exp\left\{\sum_{mm^{\prime}}J_{mm^{\prime}}\left[{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m}{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\right]^{\dagger}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right\}\lvert\mathrm{vac}\rangle.\end{split}

(87)

The matrix
\bm{J}
is built from the four discretized joint spectral amplitude matrices:

\bm{J}=\beta\sqrt{\Delta k_{1}\Delta k_{2}}\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\mathrm{ac,ac}}&\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\mathrm{ac,ph}}\\
\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\mathrm{ph,ac}}&\Phi^{\mathrm{II}}_{\mathrm{ph,ph}}\end{matrix}\right].

(88)

The moments of the squeezed state of Eq. (
87
) are given by

\displaystyle\centering N_{mm^{\prime}}^{S}\@add@centering

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert\left[{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m}\right]^{\dagger}{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle,

(89)

\displaystyle N_{mm^{\prime}}^{I}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert\left[{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m}\right]^{\dagger}{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle,

(90)

\displaystyle M_{mm^{\prime}}^{SI}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert{a}_{S}^{\mathrm{out},m}{a}_{I}^{\mathrm{out},m^{\prime}}\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle.

(91)

To calculate these moments we perform a Schmidt decomposition
[
22
,
21
]
of the joint spectral amplitude in Eq. (

\bm{J}
as

\bm{J}=\bm{F}_{S}\bm{R}\left[\bm{F}_{I}\right]^{\mathrm{T}},

(92)

where
\bm{F}_{S}
and
\bm{F}_{I}
are two square unitary matrices of rank
2\ell_{1}
and
2\ell_{2}
respectively, and the matrix
\bm{R}
is a
2\ell_{1}\times 2\ell_{2}
diagonal matrix of rank
\ell=\min\left\{2\ell_{1},2\ell_{2}\right\}
with entries
r_{n}
, for
n=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
. Using Eq. (
92
) for
{\bm{J}}
it is straightforward to show
[
22
]
that the moments in Eq. (
90
) can be written as

\displaystyle N_{mm^{\prime}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\left[\bm{F}_{S}\sinh^{2}(\bm{R})\bm{F}^{\dagger}_{S}\right]_{mm^{\prime}},

(93)

\displaystyle N_{mm^{\prime}}^{I}

\displaystyle=\left[\bm{F}_{I}\sinh^{2}(\bm{R})\bm{F}^{\dagger}_{I}\right]_{mm^{\prime}},

(94)

\displaystyle M_{mm^{\prime}}^{SI}

\displaystyle=\left[\bm{F}_{S}\cosh(\bm{R})\sinh(\bm{R})[\bm{F}_{I}]^{\mathrm{T}}\right]_{mm^{\prime}}.

(95)

The total number of pairs of signal and idler photons in the squeezed state,
N_{SI}
, is obtained by summing over the diagonal entries of either the signal or idler moment in Eqs. (
93
) or (
94
) respectively,

\displaystyle N_{SI}=\sum_{m}N^{S}_{mm}=\sum_{m}N^{I}_{mm}=\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sinh^{2}(r_{n}).

(96)

An expression similar to Eq. (
59
) for the generation efficiency in the cw limit, and similar to Eq. (
78
) for the efficiency for the pair regime, can then be introduced here for the LOS regime,

\mathcal{E}^{SI}_{\mathrm{pulse}}\equiv\frac{N_{SI}}{N_{P}}.

(97)

As we will see in our sample calculation,
\mathcal{E}^{SI}_{\mathrm{pulse}}
coincides with
\mathcal{E}^{SI}_{\mathrm{pulse;pairregime}}
for sufficiently low pulse energies and powers, as we would expect. To study the separability of the squeezed state in Eq. (
87
) we calculate its Schmidt number
[
22
,
21
]

K=\frac{\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sinh^{2}(r_{n})\right)^{2}}{\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\sinh^{4}(r_{n})},

(98)

where
r_{n}
are the entries of the diagonal matrix
\bm{R}
in Eq. (
92
). Within the approximations of this section, the Schmidt number in the LOS regime is unchanged from that in the pair regime, since the same pair generation operator from Eq. (
64
) appears in Eq. (
63
) from the pair regime and expression (
81
) from the LOS regime for the output ket. That is, within the approximations made here the joint spectral amplitude in the LOS regime is the same as the biphoton wave function in the pair regime.

IV
Conclusion and Future Work

We have derived general formulas for the generation rate of photon pairs, and the generation of squeezed light in the lowest-order squeezing (LOS) regime, from the process of SPDC in ring resonator structures. Since our approach treats the scattering losses quantum mechanically, we can also analyze the statistics of the scattered photons. We have focused on microring resonators made of III-V semiconductors, point-coupled to a waveguide, and our approach can easily be extended to multiple input waveguides, and to microrings made from different materials.

With the integrated photonic structures we have considered, where modal dispersion is significant, the quasi-phase-matching associated with the tensor nature of the nonlinearity does not play an essential role in obtaining high efficiencies, although it does affect what structures will lead to the highest efficiencies. From our sample calculations, we obtain optimized structures yielding high generation efficiencies of
2.72\times 10^{-5}
in the pair regime, and we have detailed how both the rate at which pairs of generated photons exit the channel without loss, and the heralding efficiencies, depend on the coupling between the channel and the resonator.

In the LOS regime we find efficiencies in excess of
1\times 10^{-2}
for realistic structures, and indeed such structures should lead to elevated levels of squeezing and pump depletion beyond the LOS regime.

In future work we will extend this calculation to treat the higher levels of squeezing that are possible, and include third-order processes such as self- and cross-phase modulation that will affect the dynamics of the squeezing.
In the high-squeezing regime our calculations predict significant pump depletion. As pointed out earlier
[
24
]

As we have shown, high efficiency microring resonators made from III-V semiconductor materials are a promising platform for investigating non-Gaussian states generated via nonlinear interactions. In this Appendix we treat degenerate SPDC, where both of the generated photons are within one frequency bin labelled by the centre frequency
\omega_{S}
. For variables that differ from those for nondegenerate SPDC, we will include a “hat” for the degenerate case. The Hamiltonian that describes the degenerate SPDC interaction is

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\mathrm{SPDC}}_{NL}=-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\int\Gamma^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})&\left[{\mathsf{D}}_{S}^{\mathrm{out}(i^{\prime})}(\bm{r})\right]^{\dagger}\left[{\mathsf{D}}_{S}^{\mathrm{out}(j^{\prime})}(\bm{r})\right]^{\dagger}\\
&\times{\mathsf{D}}_{P}^{\mathrm{in}(k^{\prime})}(\bm{r})\mathrm{d}\bm{r}+\mathrm{H.c.}.\end{split}

(113)

We rewrite the Hamiltonian in a form similar to Eq. (
33
):

\begin{split}\hat{H}^{\mathrm{SPDC}}_{NL}&=-\sum_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}k_{1}\mathrm{d}k_{2}\mathrm{d}k_{3}\hat{\mathsf{K}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}({k}_{1},{k}_{2},{k}_{3})\\
&\times\left[a_{S}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda}(k_{1})\right]^{\dagger}\left[a_{S}^{\mathrm{out},\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{2})\right]^{\dagger}a_{P}^{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{ac}}(k_{3})+\mathrm{H.c.},\end{split}

(114)

where here

\begin{split}\hat{\mathsf{K}}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=&\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{P}}{4\pi}\frac{\hbar\omega_{S}}{4\pi}\frac{\hbar\omega_{S}}{4\pi}}\left[F_{S+}^{\lambda}(k_{1})F_{S+}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(k_{2})\right]^{*}\\
&\times F_{P-}^{\mathrm{ac}}(k_{3})\times\hat{\bar{\mathsf{K}}}_{SSP},\end{split}

(115)

and

\begin{split}\hat{\bar{\mathsf{K}}}_{SSP}&=\epsilon_{0}\int\chi^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})[{\mathsf{e}}_{S}^{i^{\prime}}(\bm{r}){\mathsf{e}}_{S}^{j^{\prime}}(\bm{r})]^{*}{\mathsf{e}}_{P}^{k^{\prime}}(\bm{r})\\

&=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{0}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}\Big[V_{SSP}^{(+)}\int_{\rm{ring}}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}W_{SSP}^{(+)}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\\
&\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+V_{SSP}^{(-)}\int_{\rm{ring}}\mathrm{d}\bm{r}_{\perp}W_{SSP}^{(-)}(\bm{r}_{\perp})\Big].\end{split}

(116)
