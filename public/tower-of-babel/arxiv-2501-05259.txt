Reversible Computation with Stacks and “Reversible Management of Failures”
Matteo Palazzo, Luca Roversi

Abstract
This work focuses on making certain computational models reversible. We start with the idea that “reversibilizing” should mean a process that gives a computational model an operational semantics capable of interpreting each term as a bijection. The most commonly used method of reversibilization creates operational semantics that halt computation when it is not possible to uniquely determine the starting state from a produced computational state; thus, terms are interpreted as partial bijective functions. We introduce S-CORE , a language of terms that allows manipulation of variables and stacks. For S-CORE , we define the operational semantics R-semantics . With the help of a proof assistant, we certify that R-semantics makes S-CORE a reversible imperative computational model where all terms are interpreted as total bijections on an appropriate state space.

Introduction

Landauer’s Principle
[
4
]
established that irreversible, i.e. classical, computation necessarily dissipates energy because it typically erases bits of information. The observation led to explore reversible computational models, or paradigmatic programming languages, alternative to classical ones, inherently devoid of information erasure, i.e. useless dissipation energy.

Fredkin and Toffoli gates were a first basis for designing reversible algorithms. Since then, reversible computational models have been introduced to deepen our understanding of computations, whose distinctive and intuitive meaning can be summarized as follows:

•

concerning the set of computable functions that we can express in it, reversible computation can only deal with injective functions
[
1
]
;

•

concerning the programs we can write, every step in the forward direction from what we typically take as inputs to its output can always be traced back, from output to the corresponding inputs.

Possible practical benefits for everyday programming follow.

•

Reversible computation models can simplify debugging and testing by allowing programs to be run backward, enabling programmers to trace errors and understand program behavior more effectively
[
5
]
.

•

Reversible computing naturally supplies primitives that facilitate efficient checkpointing and rollback mechanisms. This is crucial for long-running applications, as they allow programs to revert to a previous state without losing progress. This can ensure that systems can recover from errors and interruptions smoothly
[
14
]
.

Back to Landauer’s Principle, by avoiding the unavoidable energy loss of classical computation, reversible computation can theoretically operate with zero energy dissipation, leading to significant energy savings. A truly reversible hardware available will help to mitigate the environmental impact of technology.
