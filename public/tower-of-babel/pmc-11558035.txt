Blood Metal Ion Levels After Hip Resurfacing: A Comparison of 2 Different Implants
Authors: Renee Ren, Ryan Cheng, Andrew Jordan, Jonathan Spaan, Rachelle Hornick, Walter L. Taylor, Edwin P. Su
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile hip resurfacing arthroplasty has been shown to be an effective prosthetic solution for end-stage osteoarthritis, prior studies have also reported an increasing concern regarding blood metal ion levels following the use of metal-on-metal articulations. The purpose of this study was to compare early and midterm blood metal ion levels to functional outcomes and implant survivorship for patients treated with the Birmingham Hip Resurfacing (BHR) implant system and the ReCap Magnum.MethodsA retrospective review identified 104 patients who underwent 134 hip resurfacing arthroplasties using BHR (n = 67) and ReCap (n = 67) at a single institution between 2006 and 2018. ReCap and BHR patients were matched 1:1 by sex, femoral head and acetabular cup sizes, age, and year of surgery. The primary outcome of interest was cobalt (Co) and chromium (Cr) ion levels.ResultsThe ReCap cohort had lower median metal ion levels compared to the BHR cohort at 1-2 y (Co: 1.5 vs 1.9 parts per billion [ppb], P = .018; Cr: 1.3 vs 2.8 ppb, P = .008) and 3-5 y (Co: 1.1 vs 1.9 ppb, P = .001; Cr: 1.2 vs 2.2 ppb, P = .003) after surgery. Correlation analysis showed no significant associations between Co and Cr ion levels and pre- and postoperative patient-reported outcomes. Indications for revision differed between groups. Three BHR hips were revised due to adverse reactions to metal debris, whereas 2 ReCap hips required revisions: one for instability and another for periprosthetic fracture.ConclusionsBHR patients had higher metal ion levels than ReCap patients at 1-2 and 3-5 y after surgery, though these metal levels are still low and in line with prior studies. Indications for revision differed between patients treated with BHR and ReCap. Surgeons should be aware of these outcomes when counseling patients regarding these metal-on-metal articulations.

Introduction

Hip resurfacing arthroplasty (HRA) is an alternative prosthetic solution to total hip arthroplasty (THA) for end-stage osteoarthritis, particularly for male patients under the age of 65 who aim to return to high impact activities. [1] The advantages of HRA include bone preservation, lower risk of dislocation due to a larger femoral head component, greater range of motion, and easier revision, if necessary. [2] A matched-cohort study also found that patients who undergo HRA experience higher levels of function and are less likely to limp, experience thigh pain, and have perceptions of limb length discrepancy compared to patients who undergo THA. [3]

Though HRA offers the potential for significant improvements in quality of life, [[1], [2], [3]] studies have also reported an increasing concern with the metal-on-metal (MoM) articulations utilized in the procedure. [[4], [5], [6], [7]] MoM bearings can increase cobalt (Co) and chromium (Cr) blood ion levels, [4,5] deposit metallic debris into periprosthetic soft tissue and bone, [8] and can lead to complications such as pain, swelling, tissue necrosis, pseudotumors, and corrosive osteolysis, among others. [[9], [10], [11]] Furthermore, MoM hip resurfacing systems have had significantly higher all-cause revision rates compared to conventional, non-MoM THA prostheses in certain patient populations. [6,11,12]

The Birmingham Hip Resurfacing (BHR) implant system (Smith & Nephew Inc., London, United Kingdom) and the ReCap Magnum (Biomet Inc., Warsaw, Indiana) are 2 of the most widely used HRA systems. The BHR prosthesis was the first HRA system to be approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2006. [13] The cemented ReCap femoral component and uncemented Biomet Magnum acetabular component have also been used since the early 2000s, [14] though the use of them together for hybrid hip resurfacing are considered an off-label use in the U.S. Studies have shown that both BHR and ReCap devices have excellent implant survivorship through 10-y of follow-up. [[15], [16], [17]] Though prior studies have reported on the survivorship of the BHR and ReCap HRA devices, the literature comparing blood metal ion levels and its association with functional outcomes following the use of these 2 MoM HRA systems is limited. The few studies that have investigated blood metal ion levels after HRA have focused on the BHR system and have not assessed postoperative patient-reported outcomes. [18,19] Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate and compare early and midterm blood metal ion levels in hips implanted with BHR and ReCap. Secondary aims were to (1) examine the relationship between blood metal ion levels and implant survivorship and patient-reported outcomes while controlling for patient-level characteristics and surgical variables such as implant position and (2) compare implant survivorship and indications for revision for BHR and ReCap patients.

Material and methods

Patient population and implant designs

A single-surgeon database was reviewed to identify all patients who underwent an HRA at a single institution between February 2, 2006 and April 11, 2018 using ReCap and/or BHR devices. The BHR prothesis consists of a cemented as cast cobalt-chromium (CoCr) femoral component and an uncemented as cast CoCr hydroxyapatite coated acetabular component. [13] The ReCap system utilizes uncemented as cast CoCr femoral and acetabular components and has a spherical geometry at the nonarticulation femoral component, [14] whereas the BHR implant has a chamfer design. The fixation surface of the Magnum acetabular shell and ReCap femoral implant is a titanium plasma spray. Patients who had a MoM THA or HRA performed on their contralateral hip were excluded from our study. Those who did not have follow-up CoCr laboratory results or respond to all pre- and postoperative patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were also excluded. ReCap and BHR patients were matched 1:1 by sex, femoral head and acetabular cup sizes (±2 millimeters), age at surgery (±5 y), and year of surgery (±5 y). This study was approved by the institutional review board.

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome of interest was CoCr metal ion levels. Secondary outcomes of interest included implant survivorship, functional outcomes, as measured by PROMs such as the Harris Hip Score [20,21] and Lower Extremity Activity Scale, [22] and revisions secondary to metal reactions. Additional covariates of interest included anteversion and acetabular cup inclination angles, which were assessed by 2 independent reviewers. The inclination angle was obtained by assessing the angle between the horizontal line parallel to the bottom of the image, and a line across the greater diameter of the cup face, after calibration with the size of the acetabular cup or femoral head. Anteversion angles were measured on cross-table lateral X-rays using -Bild-Roentgen-Analysis software. [23]

Statistical analyses

Variables were assessed for normalcy. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Descriptive statistics were reported with medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. Spearman and Pearson correlation coefficients were used to measure associations between metal ion levels and PROMs. Kaplan-Meier estimates were calculated for implant survivorship and reported with 95% confidence intervals. Log-rank tests were utilized to compare differences in survivorship between groups. All tests were 2-tailed. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio 1.1.463 (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA).

Metal ion levels

At 1-2 y after surgery, median Co and Co blood ion levels for the BHR cohort were 1.9 (IQR: 1.4-2.7) parts per billion (ppb) and 2.8 ppb (1.7-3.5), respectively. In comparison, the ReCap cohort had lower Co (1.5 ppb; 1.0-1.9) and Cr (1.3 ppb; 0.9-2.0) ion levels. There was a significant difference between groups in both Co (P = .018) and Cr (P = .008) ion levels. At 3-5 y after surgery, median Co and Cr blood ion levels were 1.9 ppb (1.4-2.5) and 2.2 ppb (1.6-3.0) for the BHR group. For the ReCap group, Co and Cr ion levels were still relatively lower, at 1.1 (0.7-1.5) and 1.2 (0.7-2.5) respectively. These differences in Co and Cr ion levels at longer follow-up were also significant (P = .001, P = .003).

Implant survivorship and indications for revision

Average follow-up was 8.0 y (standard deviation [SD]: 2.8) for the BHR group, 7.2 y (SD: 3.1) for the ReCap group, and 7.6 y (SD: 3.4) for the overall cohort. For the BHR treatment group, implant survivorship was 95.7% at 5 y. Three hips (2 patients) in the BHR cohort (5%) were revised at an average of 6.4 y (range: 6.1-6.9), all due to adverse reactions to metal debris. One patient who required revision had Co and Cr levels of 16.8 ppb and 16.3 ppb at 1-2 y after surgery and 16.7 ppb and 16.1 ppb at the 3-5 y follow-up. Another patient required bilateral revision of her BHR implants and had Co levels of 3.7 ppb and Cr levels of 3.0 ppb at both follow-ups. Implant survivorship was 97.1% for the ReCap group at 5 y. 2 hips (3%) in the ReCap cohort required revisions: one for instability at 7 y post-HRA and another for a periprosthetic fracture after a fall 7.7 y post-HRA. Discussion

Understanding the postoperative Co and Cr ion levels for HRA patients is critical, especially given that the metallic debris associated with MoM HRA systems can lead to a variety of complications. [[5], [6], [7], [8],[10], [11], [12]] Though prior studies have reported on the survivorship of MoM articulations [15,16,24] and metal ion levels following the use of the BHR system, [18] few have compared the postoperative metal ion levels of patients treated with BHR and ReCap HRA devices and examined the relationship between metal ion levels and PROMs. The current matched-cohort study included a large sample of patients treated with BHR and ReCap systems and identified significantly higher levels of Co and Cr for BHR patients compared to ReCap patients at both 1-2 y and 3-5 y after surgery. Though metal ion levels were not associated with patient-reported outcomes scores and implant survivorship did not differ between groups, our BHR cohort underwent 3 revisions due to adverse reactions to metal ions while no ReCap patients required a subsequent procedure for metal ion-related issues. Our findings suggest that, even though PROMs and implant survivorship are comparable, the types of complications and indications for revision surgery may differ between patients treated with BHR and ReCap systems.

Blood ion levels vs previous studies

Prior research has also examined blood metal ion levels for patients who underwent HRA. Pietiläinen et al., for instance, assessed postoperative changes in metal ion levels for 233 patients (274 hips) who were treated with the BHR HRA system. [18] While the study demonstrated that Co and Cr levels decreased from 2.4-1.5 ppb and 2.1-1.6 ppb, respectively, the time points at which these measurements were collected varied greatly between patients (9 months-7 y). Van der Straeten et al. conducted a similar study in 2012 and reported on serum Co and Cr ion levels following 453 unilateral and 139 bilateral HRAs. [19] However, this study categorized patients by preoperative function (high- vs low-functioning), did not control for patient-level characteristics and surgical variables, and included 8 types of HRA prostheses, all of which may have had varying effects on postoperative metal ion levels. In the current study, we matched patients treated with BHR and ReCap systems 1:1 by sex, age at the time of surgery, femoral head and acetabular cup size, and year of surgery, which allowed for a more accurate comparison of postoperative Co and Cr blood ion levels. BHR patients had higher metal ion levels than ReCap patients at both 1-2 y and 3-5 y after surgery, though these metal levels are still low and in line with prior studies. [18,25] Similar to the results reported in previous studies, [18] Co and Cr ion levels for both of our cohorts decreased over time.

Indications for revision surgery

While we found no association between metal ion levels and PROMs and no differences in overall implant survivorship between the 2 treatment groups, the indications for revision surgery differed for the BHR and ReCap cohorts. Three hips that were implanted with BHR resulted in adverse tissue reactions due to elevated CoCr metal ion levels >7 ppb and required subsequent revision to dual mobility THA, whereas no incidents of metallosis were reported for patients who received ReCap devices. A potential contributor to the slight differences in postoperative metal ion levels between our BHR and ReCap cohorts is the variation in coverage angles, which has been identified as a crucial determinant of articular wear, especially for the BHR system. [[26], [27], [28]] Prior literature has suggested that coverage angle depends on 3 factors: (1) bearing profile arc of the implant design, (2) implant size, and (3) inclination angle. [14,29,30] Though the current study matched patients by implant size, patients were not matched by inclination angle. Differences in average inclination angles between BHR and ReCap treatment groups were not statistically significant but trended toward significance (P = .070). As a result, this may have contributed to variations in coverage angles. Small coverage angles, which are most frequently encountered with smaller femoral head sizes, higher acetabular component inclination angles, or inadequate anteversion angles, result in smaller contact patch to rim distance. [26,28,31] Contact patch to rims <10 mm can then increase edge loading, disrupt the lubrication mechanism of the implants, and ultimately elevate rates of wear at the cup rim. [26,32] As such, surgeons who are considering the use of BHR and ReCap implant systems for their HRA patients should be aware of the differences in postoperative metal ion levels, their associated complications, and the factors that may contribute to increased implant wear reported in our study. Future research should aim to establish guidelines to determine which patients benefit most from BHR or ReCap HRA implants.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations: (1) all procedures were performed by 1 surgeon at a single teaching institution and therefore, may not be generalizable to all other orthopedic departments. Nevertheless, the findings from our study are impactful in that they demonstrate that patients treated with BHR systems may have higher postoperative Co and Cr ion levels and be at a higher risk for subsequent revision surgery for metallosis compared to those treated with ReCap systems. It should be noted that these differences in postoperative metal ions is still small and it is unclear as to whether these differences are clinically significant. (2) Our study included a wide time range of interest and reported on HRAs performed by a single surgeon between February 2006 and April 2018. As such, surgeon experience varied greatly depending on the time of the procedure. Though we did not control for surgical experience at the time of surgery, we matched BHR and ReCap patients by year of surgery (±5 y) and were ultimately able to produce one of the largest matched-cohort studies examining blood metal ion levels at 1-2 y and 3-5 y after HRA to date.

Conclusions

In conclusion, differences in Co and Cr blood ion levels between BHR and ReCap treatment groups were statistically significant but potentially not clinically significant, at 1-2 y and 3-5 y after surgery. Though no significant correlation was found between metal ion levels and PROMs and implant survivorship did not differ between groups at any time point, indications for revision surgery were different for patients with BHR and ReCap systems.
