Adjacent cycle-chains are e e -positive
Foster Tom, Aarush Vailaya

Abstract
We describe a way to decompose the chromatic symmetric function as a positive sum of smaller pieces. We show that these pieces are e e -positive for cycles. Then we prove that attaching a cycle to a graph preserves the e e -positivity of these pieces. From this, we prove an e e -positive formula for graphs of cycles connected at adjacent vertices. We extend these results to graphs formed by connecting a sequence of cycles and cliques.

Introduction

For a graph
G
, the chromatic symmetric function
X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})
was first defined by Stanley in
[
14
]
, as a function on
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
, an infinite sequence of variables. The chromatic symmetric function can be written in many bases, and one particular field of interest is the positivity of the coefficients in these bases. One specific basis of interest is the elementary basis, or the
e
-basis. The famous Stanley–Stembridge conjecture in
[
16
, Conjecture 5.5]
claims that incomparability graphs of
\mathbf{(3+1)}
-free partially ordered sets are
e
-positive, meaning the coefficients of the chromatic symmetric functions of these graphs in the
e
-basis are all positive. This conjecture is closely related to the immanants of Jacobi-Trudi matrices
[
16
]
, the cohomology of Hessenberg varieties
[
2
]
, and the characters of Kazhdan–Lusztig elements of the Hecke algebra
[
1
]
. Gasharov in
[
9
]
proved that such graphs are Schur-positive, a weaker condition than
e
-positivity. Guay-Paquet in
[
11
]
reduced the Stanley–Stembridge conjecture to proving that all natural unit interval graphs are
e
-positive. The converse is not true, as there are many non-unit interval graphs (such as cycles) that are
e
-positive. It is generally unknown when a graph is
e
-positive or not, but many papers have proven the
e
-positivity of specific families of graphs and derived explicit formulas for certain families of graphs
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
17
,
18
]
.

Many results related to
e
-positivity have been achieved working with certain generalizations or alternate versions of the chromatic symmetric function, such as the chromatic symmetric function in non-commuting variables
[
10
]
or the quasisymmetric refinement
[
13
]
.

The first author in
[
17
]
found a new method of finding the chromatic symmetric function for certain unit interval graphs, using objects called forest triples. With these he proved that
K\!
-chains, which are cliques connected at single vertices, are
e
-positive. We will use a similar method to prove that adjacent cycle chains are also
e
-positive. More precisely, our paper is structured as follows.

Section
2
introduces the definitions to describe the chromatic symmetric function, the
e
-basis, unit interval graphs, and the Stanley–Stembridge conjecture. In Section
3
, we present a way to calculate the
e
-expansion of the chromatic symmetric function of a graph using objects called forest triples. In Section
4
we find a way to decompose the chromatic symmetric function into multiple pieces, which we conjecture are all
e
-positive. Section
5
presents a new proof of the already-known
e
-positivity of cycles, using forest triples and involutions. In Section
6
, we prove that given an involution on “cycle+tree” graphs, attaching a cycle to a certain graphs at a single vertex preserves
e
-positivity. From this, we get that adjacent cycle chains are
e
-positive. Additionally, we derive explicit formulas for certain graphs, such as the graph of two cycles connected at a single vertex,
C_{a}+C_{b}
, with

X_{C_{a}+C_{b}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\models a,\beta\models b+\alpha_{1}\\
\len(\beta)\geq 2,\beta_{1}\leq\alpha_{1},\beta_{2}\geq\alpha_{1}\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{1}-1)\cdots(\alpha_{l}-1)\cdot(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}+1)(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}-1)\cdot\\[-20.0pt]
(\beta_{3}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)\cdot e_{\sort(\alpha\setminus\alpha_{1}\cdot\beta\setminus\beta_{1}\cdot(\beta_{1}-1))}.

(1.1)

Finally, Section
7
proves the involution on “cycle+tree” graphs used in Section
6
, through similar methods as Section
5
.

2
Background

In this paper,
G
always references a loopless non-directed graph with
n
vertices labeled
1
through
n
, and with a fixed total ordering on the edges. The vertex set of a graph is denoted as
V(G)
and edge set as
E(G)
. The number of vertices in a graph is denoted as
|G|
. The clique
K_{n}
is the graph with
n
vertices and an edge between every pair of vertices.

Definition
.

A
proper coloring
of graph
G
is a function
\kappa:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that if
(i,j)\in E(G)
, then
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
.

Definition
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{i}\;\text{for all}\;i\in\mathbb{N})
be an infinite tuple of variables. Then, the
chromatic symmetric function
is defined as

X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\text{$\kappa$ is proper}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{\kappa(i)}\right).

Remark
.

The function is symmetric since
X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=X_{G}(\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}))
for any permutation
\sigma
. The function is also homogeneous, since the degree of every term is
n
. Note that
X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})
is independent of the labeling of
G
and the ordering of the edges of
G
.

We use different bases to write symmetric functions without needing to use an infinite number of variables. The basis of interest in this paper is the
e
-basis. First, we must define a partition.

Definition
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
be a tuple of positive integers. Then,
\lambda
is a
partition
of
n
if
\lambda
is a weakly decreasing sequence such that
\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}=n
. We let
\len(\lambda)=l
be the length of partition
\lambda
, which is the number of positive integers in the tuple
\lambda
.

Now, we define an elementary symmetric function.

Definition
.

For some partition
\lambda
of
n
, the
elementary symmetric function

e_{\lambda}
of degree
n
is

e_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\len(\lambda)}e_{(\lambda_{i})},\text{ where }e_{(k)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\\
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}.

Because every chromatic symmetric function is both symmetric and homogeneous, they can be written uniquely as the sum of finitely many elementary symmetric functions
[
15
, Theorem 7.4.4]
.

Example
.

If
G
is the path graph with two vertices, its chromatic symmetric function can be written as

X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{N}\\
i<j\end{subarray}}2x_{i}x_{j}=2e_{(2)}.

(2.1)

Definition
.

A graph
G
is
e
-positive
if the expansion
X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}c_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}
has only non-negative coefficients.

Now, we define unit interval graphs.

Definition
.

Graph
G
with labeled vertices
1
through
n
is a
natural unit interval graph
if for all
i<j<k
where
(i,k)\in E(G)
, both
(i,j)\in E(G)
and
(j,k)\in E(G)
.

We can finally state the Stanley–Stembridge conjecture.

Conjecture 2.2

(
[
16
, Conjecture 5.5]
)
.

If
G
is a natural unit interval graph, then it is
e
-positive.

3
Forest Triples

Forest triples provide a way to calculate the chromatic symmetric function for a graph. First, we introduce the concept of a composition.

Definition
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})
be a tuple of positive integers. Then,
\alpha
is a
composition
of
n
(denoted as
\alpha\models n
) if
\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}=n
. The length of the composition is denoted as
\len(\alpha)
, which is the number of positive integers, or
parts
, in the composition. We now define a few properties on compositions. The notation
\alpha_{l}
always denotes the last part of composition
\alpha
.

For some
\alpha
, we denote
\alpha\setminus\alpha_{i}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})
. For two compositions
\alpha
and
\beta
, we define
\alpha\cdot\beta=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l})
.

We denote a tuple of compositions as
\left(\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(m)}\right)
, where
m
is the number of compositions. This allows us to index the tuple for a specific composition and a specific part of the composition. For example, the first part of the second composition can be denoted as
\alpha_{1}^{(2)}
.

We can link a composition to a partition by sorting the elements.

Definition
.

If
\alpha
is a composition of
n
, then
\sort(\alpha)
is the partition of
n
formed by sorting the elements of
\alpha
in non-increasing order.

Now, we will introduce the concept of broken circuits. Recall that
G
is a graph with labeled vertices
1
through
n
and ordered edges.

Definition
.

For every cycle
C
that is a subgraph of
G
, the corresponding
broken circuit

B\subset G
is the subgraph of
C
without the largest edge of
C
.

Definition
.

A
non-broken
circuit
F
of graph
G
is a subgraph of
G
such that no subgraph of
F
is a broken circuit.

Every non-broken circuit is a forest, since if there is a cycle in
F
, then there exists a subgraph of that cycle that is a broken circuit.

Definition
.

A
tree triple
is a tuple
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\alpha,r)
where
T
is a tree in some non-broken circuit
F
,
\alpha
is a composition of
|T|
, and
r
is an integer with
1\leq r\leq\alpha_{1}
.

We can finally define what a forest triple is.

Definition
.

A
forest triple

\forest
of
G
is a set of tree triples
\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{m}\}
where
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}=(T_{i},\alpha^{(i)},r_{i})
and the trees
T_{1}
through
T_{m}
are the trees of some non-broken circuit
F
of graph
G
.

The type of a forest triple,
\type(\forest)
, is the partition formed by combining and sorting all the elements in every composition in decreasing order. The sign of a forest triple can be defined as

\mathrm{sign}(\forest)=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\len(\alpha^{(i)})-1\right)}.

Definition
.

A tree triple is
unit
if
\len(\alpha)=1
. A
unit
forest triple is one where every tree triple is unit.

Remark
.

Note if
\forest
is unit, then
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(1-1)}=1
. Additionally, if
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\alpha,r)
is unit, then
\alpha_{1}=|T|
.

Let
\mathrm{FT}(G)
be the set of all forest triples of
G
. We can now state the following theorem regarding forest triples.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
17
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

The chromatic symmetric function of any graph
G
is

X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(G)}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type(\forest)}.

(3.2)

Remark
.

Note that given a graph
G
with labeled vertices, the set
\mathrm{FT}(G)
depends on how the edges are ordered. However, the formula in Equation
3.2
provides the same result for any arbitrary ordering of the edges, since the chromatic symmetric function of a graph does not depend on any ordering of the edges.

.

P_{2}
, and how the sum of
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type(\forest)}
results in
X_{P_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})=2e_{(2)}
as calculated in Equation
2.1
.

4
Sign-Reversing Involutions

Now, we describe a way to prove a graph is
e
-positive using forest triples.

Definition
.

A
sign-reversing involution
is a function
\varphi:\mathrm{FT}(G)\rightarrow\mathrm{FT}(G)
with the following properties:

1.

It is an involution, meaning
\varphi(\varphi(\forest))=\forest
.

2.

It preserves type, so
\type(\forest)=\type(\varphi(\forest))
.

3.

If
\forest\neq\varphi(\forest)
, then
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\neq\mathrm{sign}(\varphi(\forest))
.

4.

If
\forest=\varphi(\forest)
, then
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)=1
, and we say that
\forest
is a fixed point.

If there exists a sign-reversing involution on
\mathrm{FT}(G)
, then we can pair every forest triple with a negative sign to a non-fixed forest triple with a positive sign, meaning

X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(G)\\
\text{$\forest$ is fixed under $\varphi$}\end{subarray}}e_{\type(\forest)}.

(4.1)

We will look at sign-reversing involutions with an additional property.

Definition
.

For
\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(G)
, let
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{\min}=(T_{\min},\alpha^{(\min)},r_{\min})
reference the unique tree triple where the smallest vertex of
G
is in
V(T_{\min})
.

Definition
.

Let
\varphi:\mathrm{FT}(G)\rightarrow\mathrm{FT}(G)
be a sign-reversing involution. Suppose all fixed points in
\varphi
are units. If for all
\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(G)
, letting
\forest^{\prime}=\varphi(\forest)
with
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{\min}=(T^{\prime}_{\min},\alpha^{(\min)\prime},r^{\prime}_{\min})\in\forest^{\prime}
, we have
\alpha^{(\min)}_{1}=\alpha^{(\min)\prime}_{1}
and
r_{\min}=r^{\prime}_{\min}
, then
\varphi
is a
first-preserving involution
.

Definition
.

Let
\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)
be the set of forest triples where
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}=i
and
r_{\min}=1
.

Letting
\forest=\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{\min},\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{m}\}
, we define
\type^{\prime}(\forest)=\sort(\alpha^{(\min)}\setminus\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}\cdot\alpha^{(2)}\cdots\alpha^{(m)})
. Note that
e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}=e_{\type(\forest)}\mathbin{/}{e_{\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}}}
.

If
\mathrm{FT}(G)
has a first-preserving involution, then there exists sign-reversing involutions on the subsets
\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)
.

Definition
.

For a graph
G
and integer
i
, we denote

X^{(i)}_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}.

(4.2)

We see
X^{(i)}_{G}
is a homogeneous symmetric function of degree
|G|-i
.

Note that
X^{(i)}_{G}
has non-negative coefficients for all integers
i
if and only if
G
has a first-preserving involution
\varphi
, letting Equation
4.2
be rewritten as

X^{(i)}_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)\\
\text{$\forest$ fixed under $\varphi$}\end{subarray}}e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}.

(4.3)

The chromatic symmetric function can be written as

X_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{|G|}e_{i}\cdot i\cdot X_{G}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(4.4)

Example
.

Returning to the forest triples of
G=P_{2}
shown in Figure
1
, we have
X_{G}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
and
X_{G}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x})=1
.

The first author proved in
[
17
, Theorem 4.10]
that all
K\!
-chains, which are cliques joined at single vertices, have a first-preserving involution (for a specific labeling of vertices and ordering of edges). It is conjectured in
[
17
, Section 5]
that all natural unit interval graphs with a specific ordering of edges have a first-preserving involution.

To prove that adjacent cycle chains are
e
-positive, we will try to find a first-preserving involution for them. We will first look at cycles.

5
Cycle Graphs

In this section, we find a first-preserving involution on the set of forest triples of cycle graphs.

Definition
.

For
a\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
C_{a}=\left([a],E=\{(1,2)<\cdots<(a-1,a)<(a,1)\}\right)
as the cycle graph with
a
vertices.

Previous papers have found the chromatic symmetric function for cycle graphs. We will prove the same result using forest triples.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
8
, Corollary 6.2]
)
.

The cycle graph with
a
vertices,
C_{a}
, has a chromatic symmetric function equal to

X_{C_{a}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\alpha\models a}e_{\sort(\alpha)}\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot(\alpha_{1}-1)\cdots(\alpha_{l}-1).

(5.2)

Example
.

The graph
C_{6}
has

X_{C_{6}}\mathrel{\mathop{(\boldsymbol{x})}\limits^{\vbox to8.82028pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\alpha\hskip 2.84544pt=$}\vss}}}=\overbrace{30e_{6}}^{(6)}+\overbrace{12e_{4,2}}^{(4,2)}+\overbrace{12e_{3,3}}^{(3,3)}+\overbrace{6e_{4,2}}^{(2,4)}+\overbrace{2e_{2,2,2}}^{(2,2,2)}.

Let
\forest=\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}=(T_{1},\alpha^{(1)},r_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{m}\}\in\mathrm{FT}(C_{a})
be a forest triple with
m
tree triples. We order the tree triples such that
1\in V(T_{m})
and for
1\leq i<j\leq m-1
, we have
\min(V(T_{i}))<\min(V(T_{j}))
.

For each
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}=(T,\alpha,r)\in\forest
, we can find the size of
T
based on
\alpha
(since
\alpha\models|T|
). Thus we can identify
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}
with a tuple
(v,\alpha,r)
, where
v
is the vertex such that
V(T)=\{(v,v+1),\ldots,(v+|T|-2,v+|T|-1)\}
, with vertices taken mod
a
. We then denote forest triples as

\forest=\left\langle(v_{1},\alpha^{(1)},r_{1}),\ldots,(v_{m},\alpha^{(m)},r_{m})\right\rangle,

where
T_{i}
has edges
\{(v_{i},v_{i}+1),\ldots,(v_{i+1}-2,v_{i+1}-1)\}\!\!\mod a
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}=(T_{i},\alpha^{(i)},r_{i})
. A first-preserving involution preserves
\alpha^{(m)}_{1}
and
r_{m}
, since vertex
T_{\min}=T_{m}
.

An example is given in Subfigure
2(a)
.

To define a first-preserving involution on
\mathrm{FT}(C_{a})
, we break it into disjoint subsets such that every forest triple is in exactly one subset. Then, if there exists a first-preserving involution on each subset, they can be combined together to form a first-preserving involution on
\mathrm{FT}(C_{a})
.

Say
\gamma:S_{1}\rightarrow S_{2}
is a bijective function between two disjoint subsets of
\mathrm{FT}(C_{a})
, where
\gamma
preserves
\type(\forest)
and
\alpha_{1}^{(m)}
and
r_{m}
, but reverses
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)
. Then, we can define a first-preserving involution
\varphi
on
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
, where
\varphi
either applies
\gamma
or its inverse. This involution has no fixed points.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We now define the involution in three steps, breaking
\mathrm{FT}(C_{a})
into five subsets.

Step 1:

We define sets
A
and
B
with indexed subsets
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle A_{i}=\left\{\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(C_{a}):m-1\geq i;\;r_{i}=1;\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(i)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{i-1}\geq 2\right\},

\displaystyle B_{i}=\{\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(C_{a}):m\geq i;\;\len(\alpha^{(i)})\geq 2;\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(i-1)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{i-1}\geq 2;\;

\displaystyle\text{either $m-1\geq i$ or $\alpha_{l}^{(m)}\leq a-v_{m}+1$}\}.

Intuitively, if
\forest\in A_{i}
or
\forest\in B_{i}
, then
i
is the smallest integer such that either
\len(\alpha^{(i)})\geq 2
or
r_{i}=1
. We define function
\tjoin_{i}:A_{i}\rightarrow B_{i}
, where
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)
replaces
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1}
in
\forest
with tree triples

S=\left(v_{i},\alpha^{(i+1)}\cdot\alpha^{(i)},r_{i+1}\right).

The inverse map
\tbreak_{i}:B_{i}\rightarrow A_{i}
replaces
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
in
\forest
with

S_{1}=\left(v_{i},(\alpha_{l}^{(i)}),1\right),S_{2}=\left(v_{i}+\alpha_{l}^{(i)},\alpha^{(i)}\setminus\alpha_{l}^{(i)},r_{i}\right).

Notice that if
\forest\in A_{i}
, then tree triples
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
through
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i-1}
all have
\len(\alpha)=1
and
r\geq 2
. Those tree triples are untouched in
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)
, while
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1}
are replaced by
S
. Since
S
has a composition with length at least 2, then
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)\in B_{i}
. Applying
\tbreak_{i}(\tjoin_{i}(\forest))
breaks
S
while preserving the other tree triples, resulting in
\forest
again. Thus,
\tjoin_{i}
and
\tbreak_{i}
are inverses, meaning
\tjoin_{i}
is a bijection.

If
\forest\in A_{m-1}
, then
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)
replaces
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{m-1}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{m}
with
S
. Since every
\forest
has
|T_{m-1}|\leq a-v_{m-1}+1
, then after joining

has
\alpha_{l}^{(m)}\leq a-v_{m}+1
, explaining that condition. Also note that
\tbreak_{i}
preserves
\type(\forest)
,
\alpha_{1}^{(m)}
,
r_{m}
, and reverses
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)
. We then use this function to form a first-preserving involution on sets
A\cup B
.

. We look at forest triples where

, and denote this subset
B_{\forest^{\prime}}
.

Let
\first(\forest)=\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}\}
and
\last(\forest)=\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{l}\}
. Note that
\first(\forest)\in\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+T_{i}|_{G^{\prime}})=\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+T^{\prime}_{\min})
, where
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}_{\min}\in\forest^{\prime}
. By Lemma
6.1
,
\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+T^{\prime}_{\min})
has a first-preserving involution, which we will call
\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}
. We can define a first-preserving involution
\varphi:B_{\forest^{\prime}}\rightarrow B_{\forest^{\prime}}
, where

\varphi(\forest\in B_{\forest})=\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}(\first(\forest))\cup\last(\forest).

Then, we have

\varphi(\varphi(\forest))=\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}(\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}(\first(\forest)))\cup\last(\forest)=\first(\forest)\cup\last(\forest)=\forest,

so
\varphi
is an involution. Since
\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}
is a first-preserving involution, we see
\varphi
preserves
\type(\forest)
and
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}
and
r_{\min}
. Additionally,
\varphi
reverses
\mathrm{sign}(\forest)
, unless
\forest
is a fixed point. These fixed points in
B_{\forest^{\prime}}
under
\varphi
have
\first(\forest)
fixed under
\varphi_{\forest^{\prime}}
, meaning fixed points are unit. Thus,
\varphi:B_{\forest^{\prime}}\rightarrow B_{\forest^{\prime}}
is a first-preserving involution.

Note that for
\forest\in B_{\forest^{\prime}}
, then

\type(\last(\forest))=\type^{\prime}(\forest^{\prime}),\;e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}=e_{\type^{\prime}(\first(\forest))}\cdot e_{\type(\last(\forest))},\;\mathrm{sign}(\forest)=\mathrm{sign}(\first(\forest)).

We can now combine the first-preserving involutions on
A
and
B
, to make a new first-preserving involution on the set

, and we can then calculate the value of

\displaystyle X^{(i)}_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)\\
\text{$\forest$ fixed under $\varphi$}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest^{\prime}\in\mathrm{FT}(G^{\prime})\\
r_{\min}=1\\
\text{$\forest^{\prime}$ fixed under $\varphi^{\prime}$}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\forest\in(B_{\forest^{\prime}})\cap(\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G))}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|G^{\prime}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest^{\prime}\in\mathrm{FT}^{(k)}(G^{\prime})\\
\text{$\forest^{\prime}$ fixed under $\varphi^{\prime}$}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{(i)}(G)\\
\forest|_{G^{\prime}}=\forest^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|G^{\prime}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest^{\prime}\in\mathrm{FT}^{(k)}(G^{\prime})\\

Definition

The graph

a

\forest\in\mathrm{FT}(C_{a}+U_{k})

(a,1)
. Then, we write

.

A first-preserving involution always preserves
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}
and
r_{\min}
for
\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+U_{k})
. We will define this involution in 5 steps.

Step 1:

This step is nearly identical to Step 1 in the cycle involution. We define sets
A
and
B
with indexed subsets
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle A_{i}=\left\{\forest:m\geq i;\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(i)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{i-1}\geq 2;\;r_{i}=1\right\},

\displaystyle B_{i}=\left\{\forest:m\geq i;\;\len(\alpha^{(i)})\geq 2;\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(i-1)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{i-1}\geq 2\right\}.

Intuitively, if
\forest\in A_{i}
or
\forest\in B_{i}
, then

is the smallest integer such that either
\len(\alpha^{(i)})\geq 2
or
r_{i}=1
. We define function
\tjoin_{i}:A_{i}\rightarrow B_{i}
, where
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)
replaces
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1}
in

The inverse map
\tbreak_{i}:B_{i}\rightarrow A_{i}
replaces
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
in

with

\forest\in A_{i}
, then tree triples
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
through
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i-1}
all have
\len(\alpha)=1
and

. Those tree triples are untouched in
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)
, while
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
and
\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1}
are replaced by

. Since

has a composition with length at least 2, then
\tjoin_{i}(\forest)\in B_{i}
. Applying
\tbreak_{i}(\tjoin_{i}(\forest))

S
while preserving the other tree triples, resulting in
\forest
again. Thus,
\tjoin_{i}
and
\tbreak_{i}
are inverses, meaning
\tjoin_{i}
is a bijection.

\displaystyle\len(\alpha^{(\min)})\geq 2;\;\alpha_{2}^{(\min)}\geq a-v_{\min}+1;\;v_{1}-1-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}=i\},

where for the final restriction on
H_{i}
, if
\forest=\{\mathrm{\mathcal{T}}_{\min}\}
has
m=0
, then
v_{1}=v_{\min}
.
We define a bijection,
\rotatejoin_{i}:G_{i}\rightarrow H_{i}
, with

\rotatejoin_{i}(\forest)=\Big\langle\!\left(v_{1}+i,\alpha^{(1)},r_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(v_{m}+i,\alpha^{(m)},r_{m}\right),\\
\left(a-r^{\prime}+1,\alpha^{(\min)}\cdot\alpha^{\prime},r_{\min}\right)\!\Big\rangle.

Say
\forest_{G}\in G_{i}
and
\forest_{H}=\rotatejoin_{i}(\forest_{G})
. Note that in
\forest_{G}
, we have
v_{1}=|T_{\min}|+1=\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}+1
. After rotating,
v_{1}
in
\forest_{G}
becomes
v_{1}+|T^{\prime}|-k-r^{\prime}+1
in
\forest_{H}
. In other words,

(v_{1})
in
\forest_{H}

\displaystyle=\text{$(v_{1}+|T^{\prime}|-k-r^{\prime}+1)$ in $\forest_{G}$, so}

(v_{1}-1-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)})
in
\forest_{H}

\displaystyle=\text{$(v_{1}+|T^{\prime}|-k-r^{\prime}+1-1-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)})$ in $\forest_{G}$}

\displaystyle=|T^{\prime}|-k-r^{\prime}+1=i,

meaning
\forest_{H}\in H_{i}
. The inverse
\rotatebreak_{i}:H_{i}\rightarrow G_{i}
is defined as

\rotatebreak_{i}(\forest)=\Big\langle\!\left(v_{1}-i,\alpha^{(1)},r_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(v_{m}-i,\alpha^{(m)},r_{m}\right),\\
\left(v_{\min}-i,\alpha^{(\min)}\setminus\alpha_{1}^{(\min)},a-v_{\min}+1\right),\left(1,(\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}),r_{\min}\right)\!\Big\rangle.

Step 5:

The remaining forest triples are in sets
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}=\{\forest:(a,1)\in E(T);\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(m)})=\len(\alpha^{(\min)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{m-1}\geq 2;\;

\displaystyle\text{either $r_{m}\geq 2$ or $|T_{m}|\leq k-1$}\},

\displaystyle I_{2}=\{\forest:(a,1)\not\in E(T);\;\len(\alpha^{(1)})=\cdots=\len(\alpha^{(m)})=\len(\alpha^{(\min)})=1;\;r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\geq 2;\;

\displaystyle r^{\prime}\geq|T^{\prime}|+|T_{\min}|-k+1\}.

First, note that if
\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+U_{k})
does not have edge
(a,1)
, then it must have
\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\geq k
. If
\len(\alpha^{\prime})\geq 2
, then

r^{\prime}\leq\alpha_{1}^{\prime}\leq|T^{\prime}|-\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\leq|T^{\prime}|-k\leq|T^{\prime}|+|T|-k,

meaning
\forest\not\in I_{2}
. Thus,
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
contain only unit forest triples.

Combining the bijections on sets
A
through
H
as well as the identity function on
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
lets us construct a first-preserving involution on
\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+U_{k})
, where the set of fixed points is
I_{1}\cup I_{2}
.

Computing Sum of Fixed Points:

The last step is to find an expression for the sum of fixed points, by counting the forest triples in
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
. We recall

B_{a,k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{\prime(i)}(C_{a}+U_{k})}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forest\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}\\
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}=i,r_{\min}=1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sign}(\forest)\cdot e_{\type^{\prime}(\forest)}.

For
\forest\in I_{1}
(where edge
(a,1)\in E(T)
), we define
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)=\alpha^{(m)}\cdots\alpha^{(1)}
. For
\forest\in I_{2}
(where edge
(a,1)\not\in E(T)
), we define
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)=\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\alpha^{(m)}\cdots\alpha^{(1)}
. Note that
\type^{\prime}(\forest)=\sort(\comp^{\prime}(\forest))
, and
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)\models a+k-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}-1
.

Case 1:

We will first find
B_{a,k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})
where
i\leq k-1
. Let
\beta\models a+k-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}-1
be some composition, we want to count the number of forest triples where
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)\models\beta
.

For
\forest\in I_{1}
, note that
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}=\alpha_{l}^{(\min)}\geq k
for
\forest\in\mathrm{FT}^{\prime}(C_{a}+U_{k})
, so
\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}\neq i
, and we have zero forest triples.

For
\forest\in I_{2}
, since edge
(a,1)\not\in E(T_{\min})
, there is only one way to place the tree triples so that
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)=\beta
. Note that
\alpha^{\prime}_{1}=\beta_{1}\geq k
. There are
|T_{m}|-1=\beta_{2}-1
possible values for
r_{m}
, and
|T_{m-1}|-1=\beta_{3}-1
possible values for
r_{m-1}
, and we use similar logic up until
r_{1}
. There are
k-|T_{\min}|=k-i
possible values for
r^{\prime}
. Thus, there are
(k-i)\cdot(\beta_{2}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)
forest triples in
I_{2}
where
\comp^{\prime}(\forest)=\beta
. This means

B_{a,k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\displaystyle{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\models a+k-i-1\\
\beta_{1}\geq k\end{subarray}}}(k-i)\cdot(\beta_{2}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)\cdot e_{\sort(\beta)},\hskip 28.45274pt\text{if }i\leq k-1.

Case 2:

We will now find
B_{a,k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})
where
i\geq k
. Let
\beta\models a+k-\alpha_{1}^{(\min)}-1
, we proceed like before.

First, for
\forest\in I_{1}
, there are
|T_{\min}|-k=i-k
ways to place the first tree triple, and then one way to place the remaining tree triples. For
r_{m-1}
, there are
|T_{m-1}|-1=\beta_{2}-1
possible values. For
r_{m-2}
, there are
\beta_{3}-1
possible values, and this is true till
r_{1}
. If
|T_{m}|=\beta_{1}\leq k-1
, then there are
\beta_{1}
possible values for
r_{m}
(since
r_{m}
can equal 1), otherwise there are
\beta_{1}-1
possible values (since
r_{m}\neq 1
).

If
\forest\in I_{2}
, note that
r^{\prime}\geq|T^{\prime}|+|T_{\min}|-k+1
, but since this case
|T_{\min}|=i\geq k
, we get
r^{\prime}\geq|T^{\prime}|+1
. However,
\forest
must have
r^{\prime}\leq|T^{\prime}|
, so there are no forest triples to count in this case. Thus, summing up the forest triples in
I_{1}
gets

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{a,k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{x})=&\sum_{\beta\models a+k-i-1}(i-k)\cdot(\beta_{1}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)\cdot e_{\sort(\beta)}\\
+&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\models a+k-i-1\\
\beta_{1}\leq k-1\end{subarray}}(i-k)\cdot(\beta_{2}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)\cdot e_{\sort(\beta)}\end{split}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\models a+k-i\\
\beta_{1}\leq k\end{subarray}}(i-k)\cdot(\beta_{2}-1)\cdots(\beta_{l}-1)\cdot e_{\sort(\beta_{1}-1,\beta\setminus\beta_{1})},\hskip 28.45274pt\text{if }i\geq k.

The final step involves combining the two sums into a single sum over compositions
\beta\models a+k-i
, where if
\beta_{1}=1
, the sum gives the value of the top summation, while if
2\leq\beta_{1}\leq k
, the sum gives the value of the second summation. Note that if
i=k
, the sum becomes 0.

Thus, we have derived Equation
6.3
, and using Equation
6.4
and some composition manipulation, we can derive Equation
6.2
.

Attaching at Cut Vertices:

We can generalize the method used for adjacent cycle chains to both non-adjacent cycle chains and more generally graphs attached at cut vertices. Say
G^{\prime}
is an arbitrary graph and
A
is a graph with
a
vertices. Then, we define
B_{A,k}(\boldsymbol{x})
similarly to how we defined it in Section
6
, but instead looking at subsets of
\mathrm{FT}(A+U_{k})
. The chromatic symmetric function of
A+G^{\prime}
can be written similar to Equation
6.6
as

X_{A+G^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{|G^{\prime}|}X^{(k)}_{G^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot B_{A,k}(\boldsymbol{x}),

using the results from Section
6
. Note that
G^{\prime}
may not have a first-preserving involution, in which case the value of
X^{(k)}_{G^{\prime}}
would be negative for some integer
k
.

If
G^{\prime}
does have a first-preserving involution and
B_{A,k}(\boldsymbol{x})
is non-negative for all integers
k
, then the graph
A+G^{\prime}
is
e
-positive. Moreover, if
B_{A,k}^{(i)}
is always
e
-positive for integer pairs
k,i
, then
A+G^{\prime}
also has a first-preserving involution. This paper proved this true for
A=C_{a}
, shown in Section
7
, and a similar method could be used to prove that chains formed by connecting other graphs at single vertices are
e
-positive.
