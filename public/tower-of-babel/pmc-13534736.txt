High-performance zinc tungstate photocatalyst: tailoring crystallinity, bandgap, and charge separation via controlled calcination
Authors: A. M. Abdelraheem, Hend Abd-Allah, D. Hamad, Hani Negm, Alaa M. Abd-Elnaiem, Nagih M. Shaalan, Atta Y. Abdel-Latief, M. A. Abdel-Rahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zinc tungstate (ZnWO4) nanoparticles were successfully synthesized via a simple co-precipitation method and calcined at a temperature range of 400–700 °C to optimize their catalytic performance. Structural characterization confirmed the formation of monoclinic wolframite-phase ZnWO4, with crystallite size increasing from 9.6 to 17.5 nm with the calcination temperature. Optical studies revealed that the bandgap can be tuned from 2.5 (as-prepared) to 3.2 eV (500 °C), with a decrease to 2.6 eV at 700 °C. ZnWO4 calcined at 500 °C exhibited optimal properties, including balanced crystallinity, surface area (8.58 m2/g), and charge separation efficiency, leading to exceptional photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue under UV irradiation (96.5% removal in ~ 4.3 h). Kinetic analysis followed a pseudo-first-order model, with the highest rate constant (~ 1.24 × 10−2 min−1) observed for the 500 °C-calcined sample. Scavenger tests identified hydroxyl radicals (·OH) as the dominant reactive species, with holes contributing moderately and superoxide radicals playing a negligible role. Photoelectrochemical measurements confirmed enhanced charge carrier separation, and cycling tests demonstrated remarkable stability (> 95% efficiency retention after five uses). XRD analysis of the used catalyst confirmed structural stability with negligible phase changes. This work highlights the critical role of calcination temperature in tailoring the structural, optical, and photocatalytic properties of ZnWO4, establishing it as a highly efficient, stable, and cost-effective photocatalyst for wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Transition metal tungstates (MWO4) have emerged as important functional materials with diverse applications in photocatalysis, optoelectronics, and sensing technologies1,2. MWO4 is classified into two structural types based on cation radius: scheelite (larger cations) and wolframite (smaller cations). Zinc tungstate (ZnWO4), which crystallizes in a monoclinic wolframite structure (space group P2/c) with edge-sharing WO6 and ZnO6 octahedra, has attracted significant attention due to its unique combination of properties, including high thermal stability, low radiation damage, and excellent photocatalytic activity3–5. ZnWO4 has demonstrated remarkable potential for environmental remediation, particularly in the degradation of organic pollutants such as methylene blue (MB), rhodamine B (Rh B), and various pharmaceutical compounds under UV irradiation6–8. Comparative studies have shown that ZnWO4 exhibits superior photocatalytic performance compared to commercial TiO2 (P25) and other tungstates like Bi2WO6 in UV irradiation, though its wider bandgap (3.7–5.7 eV) limits visible-light responsiveness9.

The photocatalytic efficiency of ZnWO4 is governed by crystallinity, surface area, particle size, and charge carrier recombination rates, all of which can be optimized through synthesis and thermal treatment conditions8,10. Various strategies have been explored to enhance performance, including surface area modification, elemental doping, and heterojunction formation (e.g., with N-doped graphene) to improve charge separation11,12. These interdependent factors ultimately determine the density of active sites, charge carrier dynamics, and interfacial reaction kinetics. While several synthesis methods have been reported (hydrothermal, sol–gel, sonochemical), the co-precipitation technique offers distinct advantages of simplicity, low-temperature processing, and scalability2,8. However, systematic studies on the effects of calcination temperature on co-precipitated ZnWO4 nanoparticles remain limited. There are various synthesis methods that have been employed to prepare ZnWO4 with distinct morphological and photocatalytic properties. Hydrothermal synthesis typically produces ZnWO4 with a monoclinic wolframite structure and nanorod morphology, where particle size can be precisely controlled by adjusting reaction parameters, i.e., increasing from 30 at 100 to 120 nm at 200 °C for fixed 12 h reactions, or from 40 at 6 to 90 nm at 24 h when maintaining 150 °C13. These hydrothermally synthesized samples exhibit relatively high surface areas (34.7–48.2 m2/g), with maximum values achieved at 100 °C, and demonstrate excellent photocatalytic activity, completely degrading Rh B within 45 min under UV irradiation. A co-precipitation method followed by calcination at 500 °C for 4 h yields ZnWO4 with a similar monoclinic structure but different morphology, showing aggregated particles whose size increases with calcination temperature or duration7. These samples display lower surface areas (19.20 m2/g at 400 °C, decreasing to 0.76 m2/g at 600 °C) yet maintain good photocatalytic performance, achieving 90% Rh B removal in 25 min. Previous studies have reported varying photocatalytic efficiencies towards MB for ZnWO4, ranging from 2.7% using co-precipitation14 and 74% via solution combustion15 to approximately 100% using template-free hydrothermal synthesis16, while microwave-assisted synthesis achieved > 50% degradation17, highlighting the strong dependence of photocatalytic performance on synthesis method and conditions. For specialized applications, high-temperature solid-state reactions have been employed, involving calcination at 350 °C for 12 h followed by 900 °C for 20 h to produce ZnWO4 for dielectric and optical property studies18. Composite materials like ZnWO4/WO2 have been developed to enhance photoelectrochemical water oxidation performance9, while fundamental studies have examined the luminescent properties of ZnWO4 and related compounds19. These comparative studies highlight how synthesis method selection critically determines the structural characteristics, surface properties, and ultimately the functional performance of ZnWO4 materials for various applications. Recent advances in thermal treatment strategies have established calcination temperature as a critical parameter governing the structural evolution of photocatalysts, directly influencing crystallite size, defect chemistry, and surface characteristics that collectively determine photocatalytic performance20–23. Concurrently, emerging studies emphasize the importance of translating these fundamental physicochemical modifications into sustainable wastewater treatment solutions, driving the demand for catalysts that combine high stability, exceptional efficiency, and cost-effectiveness24. Despite these advances, comprehensive investigations that systematically elucidate the interdependent effects of calcination temperature on the crystalline structure, bandgap modulation, and charge carrier dynamics of ZnWO4 remain surprisingly limited, representing a significant gap in the rational design of high-performance tungstate-based photocatalysts.

In this work, we present a comprehensive investigation of ZnWO4 nanoparticles synthesized via co-precipitation using zinc nitrate and sodium tungstate precursors. The study focuses on elucidating the relationship between calcination temperature (400–700 °C) and the material’s structural, morphological, surface area, optical, and catalytic properties. The nanoparticles were characterized using XRD, FTIR, UV–Vis spectroscopy, and BET surface area analysis, with their photocatalytic performance evaluated through MB degradation under UV light. MB adsorption dynamics and photocurrent response were quantitatively analyzed and contextualized with existing literature, demonstrating that our ZnWO4 system achieves exceptional interfacial adsorption and charge transport properties. This work provides valuable insights into optimizing ZnWO4 photocatalysts through controlled thermal treatment while demonstrating the effectiveness of the co-precipitation method in producing high-performance photocatalytic materials. Although multiple co-precipitation studies on ZnWO4 photocatalysts exist in the literature, our work introduces several key distinctions that set it apart. Compared to Ref.25, which used a zinc chloride precursor and calcination at 300–500 °C for 2 h, we employed zinc nitrate tetrahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·4H2O) over an extended temperature range (400–700 °C for 3 h), enabling identification of optimal performance at 500 °C not previously reported. Relative to Ref.7, which examined Rh B and formaldehyde degradation under various calcination conditions (400–600 °C for 1–16 h), our study provides comprehensive mechanistic insights, including band edge position analysis, photocurrent measurements, and PL spectroscopy to explain temperature-dependent activity toward MB degradation, beyond the crystallinity and surface area focus of prior work. These systematic investigations establish optimal calcination conditions specifically for MB removal, offering a new understanding of structure–activity relationships in ZnWO4 photocatalysts.

Experimental details

The synthesis of ZnWO4 nanoparticles was carried out using the co-precipitation method2. High-purity zinc nitrate tetrahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·4H2O) and sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O), obtained from Alpha Chemika, were used as starting materials. Figure 1 presents a schematic diagram of the co-precipitation synthesis procedure for ZnWO4 nanoparticles, including precursor preparation and post-treatment steps that lead to crystalline nanoparticle formation. Equimolar solutions (1 M) of each precursor were prepared by dissolving them in 50 mL of deionized (DI) water. The sodium tungstate solution was slowly added dropwise to the zinc nitrate solution under constant magnetic stirring at 80 °C for 1 h, during which the pH was maintained at 8 by adding ammonia solution. This resulted in the immediate formation of a white precipitate, which was then separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min. The collected precipitate underwent multiple washing cycles with DI water and ethanol (EtOH) to remove impurities, followed by drying at 100 °C for 24 h. To investigate the effect of thermal treatment, the dried powder was calcined at different temperatures (400, 500, 600, and 700 °C) for 3 h in a muffle furnace.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis of ZnWO4 by the co-precipitation method.

Schematic illustration of the synthesis of ZnWO4 by the co-precipitation method.

Thermal behavior was investigated through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), derivative TGA (DTG), and differential thermal analysis (DTA) using a LINSEIS DTA PT 1600 instrument at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in air from room temperature up to 500 °C. The crystal structure of the synthesized nanoparticles was characterized using X-ray diffraction (Philips PW 1700 diffractometer) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. Chemical bonding characteristics were analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (Thermo-Nicolet 6700) in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 using KBr pellets. Morphological examination was performed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Quattro S, Thermo Fisher) operated at 20 kV. Surface area analysis was conducted through Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) measurements using nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 77.35 K (Quantachrome TouchWin™ software), with samples degassed at 300 °C for 2 h before analysis. Photoluminescence spectroscopy (JASCO FP-6300) with an excitation wavelength of 310 nm. Optical absorbance spectra were recorded using a double-beam UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2101) over 200–800 nm at room temperature. Diffuse reflectance spectra were collected for the ZnWO4 sample calcined at 500 °C using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Evolution 220 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UK), with BaSO4 serving as the reflectance standard. Transient photocurrent responses of ZnWO4 nanoparticles calcined at 500 °C were measured using an electrochemical workstation with a three-electrode system under intermittent UV light irradiation at bias potentials ranging from 0.5 to 2.0 V.

Photocatalytic activity was assessed by monitoring the degradation of MB dye under UV–vis light irradiation. In a typical experiment, 0.01 g of ZnWO4 catalyst was dispersed in 6 mL of MB solution (10−5 M). The suspension was irradiated using a 1800 W UV lamp, and the degradation process was tracked by measuring the absorbance at regular intervals (20, 35, 60, 90, 100, 130, 200, and 260 min) using a PerkinElmer Lambda 750 spectrophotometer. The percentage degradation was calculated based on the decrease in absorbance at the characteristic MB peak (664 nm). Control experiments were conducted to verify that the observed degradation was indeed photocatalytic. Radical trapping experiments were performed to identify the dominant reactive species, using isopropanol (IPA, 1 mM) for hydroxyl radicals (·OH), p-benzoquinone (PBQ, 1 mM) for superoxide radicals (·O2−), and disodium EDTA (1 mM) for holes (h⁺), each added separately to the MB solution before irradiation. The reusability of the 500 °C-calcined ZnWO4 catalyst was evaluated over five consecutive MB degradation cycles. After each cycle, the catalyst was recovered by centrifugation, washed with distilled water and ethanol, dried at 80 °C, and reused in a fresh MB solution. To assess structural stability, XRD patterns of the fresh and reused catalyst (after five cycles) were compared under identical conditions.

Results and discussion

Thermal and structural properties

The thermal behavior of as-prepared ZnWO4 nanoparticles was thoroughly investigated through TGA-DTA analysis to establish appropriate calcination conditions and understand the material’s thermal decomposition pathway. Figure 2 shows TGA, DTG, and DTA profiles of the as-prepared ZnWO4 nanoparticles at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The TGA analysis revealed three distinct thermal events occurring between room temperature and 500 °C. The first significant weight loss (7.7%) between 33 and 212 °C corresponds to the removal of physisorbed water molecules, as confirmed by the characteristic endothermic peak at 123 °C in the DTA curve. A subsequent smaller mass loss (1.0%) in the 214–344 °C range is attributed to the elimination of water of crystallization from the material’s structure8, evidenced by an exothermic DTA peak at 310 °C indicating structural reorganization. Above 345 °C, the gradual weight loss suggests decomposition of residual hydroxyl groups, with the accompanying exothermic peak at 397 °C marking the complete formation of crystalline ZnWO4. The DTG curve reveals a single distinct peak at 123 °C corresponding to physisorbed water removal, while the higher temperature regions show no discernible peaks despite continued gradual weight loss in the TGA. This indicates that the elimination of crystallization water and hydroxyl groups occurs through slow, continuous processes without abrupt structural changes. These thermal transitions provide critical insights for optimizing the calcination process, particularly noting that temperatures above 400 °C yield thermally stable ZnWO4 while simultaneously influencing its crystallinity and surface properties—key factors governing photocatalytic performance. The observed thermal stability beyond 400 °C supports our calcination temperature selection (400–700 °C) for subsequent characterization and photocatalytic studies, as this range ensures complete crystallization while allowing systematic investigation of temperature-dependent properties.Fig. 2TGA, DTG, and DTA profiles, at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, of the as-prepared ZnWO4 nanoparticles.

The crystallographic evolution of ZnWO4 nanoparticles under thermal treatment was systematically investigated through XRD analysis. Figure 3 presents the XRD patterns of both as-prepared and calcined ZnWO4 at 400–700 °C, with all diffraction peaks perfectly indexed to the monoclinic wolframite structure (JCPDS 73-0554). The dominant (111) peak at 2θ = 30.6° confirms the preferred crystalline orientation, while the absence of secondary peaks (ZnO/WO₃) verifies phase purity. Notably, the progressive narrowing of diffraction peaks with increasing calcination temperature demonstrates significant improvement in crystallinity, reflecting enhanced long-range atomic order and reduced lattice strain. The crystallization behavior of ZnWO4 under various synthesis conditions has been extensively studied, with results that corroborate and contextualize our findings. Previous work has demonstrated that calcination of co-precipitated ZnWO4 leads to peak intensification and sharpening without phase transformation, indicating crystallite growth with increasing temperature—a trend fully consistent with our observations7. Similar thermal effects have been reported for hydrothermally synthesized ZnWO4, where annealing at 550 °C increased crystallite size from 19.6 nm to 46.8 nm32, and for co-precipitated samples showing growth from 30 (300) to 65 nm (500 °C)25. The hydrothermal method proves particularly sensitive to synthesis parameters, with studies showing that pH optimization (pH = 7) maximizes crystallinity16, while temperature variations (140–160 °C) in microwave hydrothermal synthesis yield moderate size increases from 11.7 to 13.9 nm33. Crystallization kinetics are equally important, as demonstrated by the amorphous-to-crystalline transition at 180 °C, requiring 24 h for optimal crystal development13. Morphological control is another critical factor, with different hydrothermal conditions producing nanowires versus nanorods of varying crystallite sizes34, while extreme mechanical processing like ball milling at room temperature results in mixed-phase products containing ZnWO4, WO3, and/or ZnO depending on milling conditions35. These collective findings underscore the universal importance of thermal and kinetic parameters in governing ZnWO4 crystallization across different synthesis methods, with calcination time and temperature being particularly influential factors10, all of which align with and help explain the crystallite size trends observed in our work.

The molecular bonding characteristics and hydration state of ZnWO4 nanoparticles were investigated through FTIR (Fig. 4). The spectra reveal distinct vibrational signatures in the 400–1000 cm−1 range, corresponding to fundamental metal–oxygen bonding configurations in the wolframite structure36. In additon, there three characteristic regions, in the FTIR spectrum of as-prpeared ZnWO4 nanoparticles, provide critical structural information : (1) The broad absorption band at 3300–3500 cm−1, coupled with the sharp peak at 1622 cm−1, arises from O−H stretching and H−O−H bending vibrations of surface-adsorbed and structural water molecules. (2) The intermediate frequency region (800–900 cm−1) contains distinctive Zn–W–O bridging vibrations, with peaks at 835 cm−115, 881 cm−1, and 808 cm−1 corresponding to asymmetric and symmetric stretching of Zn−O−W linkages35. (3) The low-frequency region (400–700 cm−1) exhibits fingerprints of octahedral coordination, featuring deformation modes of ZnO₆ and WO₆ units at 468–472 cm−1 (Zn−O bending) and 548–649 cm−1 (W−O stretching), respectively40. Notably, the thermal treatment induces progressive dehydration, evidenced by the systematic attenuation of hydroxyl-related bands (3346 and 1622 cm−1) with increasing calcination temperature. This dehydration process accompanies the crystallization enhancement observed in XRD analysis, suggesting a correlation between water removal and structural ordering. The persistence of metal–oxygen vibrational modes across all calcination temperatures confirms the thermal stability of the fundamental ZnWO4 framework, while the temperature-dependent intensity variations provide insight into the evolution of surface chemistry and defect states that are crucial for photocatalytic applications. Calcination-induced sharpening of Zn−O−W (835 cm−1) and W−O (468 cm−1) FTIR bands demonstrates enhanced structural order, mirroring stabilization effects observed in doped ZnWO437 and confirming improved thermal stability over as-prepared samples. The FTIR spectral features align perfectly with reference data for ZnWO425,38, with characteristic metal–oxygen vibrations appearing at expected wavenumbers regardless of preparation method, confirming successful phase formation.Fig. 4FTIR spectra of the as-prepared and calcined ZnWO4 nanoparticles.

Morphology and textural properties

The morphological evolution of ZnWO4 nanoparticles under thermal treatment was systematically investigated through FE-SEM, as presented in Fig. 5. The FE-SEM images reveal a distinct calcination temperature-dependent transformation in particle morphology and size distribution. At lower calcination temperatures (400–500 °C), the nanoparticles maintain a predominantly spherical morphology, for both the as-prepared and calcined ZnWO4, with relatively uniform size distribution, suggesting limited atomic mobility for structural reorganization (Fig. 5a–c). As the temperature increases to 600 °C, the onset of morphological transition becomes apparent, with the emergence of polyhedral and accaumulated-like structures indicating the beginning of crystallographic facet development (Fig. 5d). This temperature-induced morphological evolution correlates well with XRD results, which show increased crystallinity at higher temperatures, where enhanced atomic mobility enables the formation of lower-energy crystal facets. These morphological changes have significant implications for photocatalytic performance, as the transition from spherical nanoparticles to nanorods alters both surface area and the exposure of catalytically active crystal facets. The observed morphological features are in good agreement with previously reported studies on tungstate-based materials (MWO4, where M = Zn, Cd, Bi, etc.)2,7,25,39. The morphological evolution of ZnWO4 under thermal treatment has been extensively documented across various synthesis methods, with trends that corroborate our observations. Previous studies report that co-precipitated ZnWO4 undergoes progressive particle interconnection and aggregation with increasing calcination temperature7, mirroring our findings of crystallite growth from 9.6 to 17.5 nm across 400–700 °C. Hydrothermal synthesis offers additional morphological control, producing nanorods (70–80 nm width, 900–1100 nm length) whose dimensions vary with reaction parameters16, while conventional hydrothermal samples show similar temperature-dependent growth (20–30 nm below 500 °C expanding to 100–200 nm at 550 °C)32. The microwave hydrothermal method further demonstrates this temperature sensitivity, yielding rectangular nanorods of varying sizes at 140–160 °C33. These literature reports collectively confirm that thermal history fundamentally governs ZnWO4 morphology regardless of synthesis route, though our optimized co-precipitation/calcination approach achieves superior control over nanoparticle size (15.9 nm at 500 °C) compared to these alternative methods.

The obtained adsorption–desorption isotherms reveal a systematic decrease in specific surface area from 25.5 (400) to 4.2 m2/g (700 °C), demonstrating an inverse correlation with calcination temperature. This temperature-dependent behavior follows conventional nanoparticle growth models, where elevated thermal energy overcomes surface energy barriers, enabling atomic diffusion and grain boundary migration. The progressive reduction in surface area has significant implications for photocatalytic applications, as it affects both active site availability and mass transport properties. The surface area characteristics of ZnWO4 nanoparticles exhibit strong dependence on synthesis methods and thermal treatment, with trends that closely align with our observations. Hydrothermal synthesis of ZnWO4 produces a high surface area of 34.7–48.2 m2/g, with surface area decreasing as processing temperature increases7,13, consistent with our observed trend of reduced surface area at higher calcination temperatures. The thermal effect is further demonstrated by hydrothermal samples annealed at 550 °C, showing a dramatic reduction from 44.71 to 3.5 m2/g32, consistent with our observed surface area decrease from 25.5 (400) to 4.2 m2/g (700 °C). Alternative preparation methods show varying results—mechanochemical synthesis produces intermediate surface areas (12–22 m2/g) that decrease with extended milling due to particle aggregation35, while conventional wet impregnation yields low surface area materials (4.7 m2/g) that can be significantly enhanced through doping (156.9 m2/g)37. The 500 °C-calcined nanoparticles demonstrated good stability, maintaining > 95% of their initial degradation efficiency through five consecutive MB photodegradation cycles under identical UV irradiation conditions. The absence of deactivation mechanisms—such as photocorrosion, active site poisoning, or morphological changes—positions ZnWO4 as a particularly robust photocatalyst for wastewater treatment applications. These findings significantly enhance the material’s practical viability, addressing a critical challenge in heterogeneous photocatalysis where many materials suffer from rapid performance degradation. The sustained photocatalytic activity across multiple cycles can be attributed to the optimal calcination temperature (500 °C), achieving the perfect balance between crystallinity for stability and surface defects for activity, as evidenced by complementary characterization techniques throughout this study.

To identify the primary reactive species responsible for MB photodegradation, scavenger tests were performed using benzoquinone (PBQ), isopropanol (IPA), and disodium EDTA as quenchers for superoxide radicals (·O2−), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and holes (h+), respectively, as shown in Fig. 15a. Compared to the control experiment without any scavenger (96.5% degradation), the addition of IPA resulted in a dramatic drop in degradation efficiency to 7.9%, indicating that hydroxyl radicals are the dominant reactive species in the photocatalytic process. In contrast, the addition of PBQ and EDTA yielded efficiencies of 96.3% and 64.1%, respectively, suggesting that superoxide radicals play a negligible role while holes contribute moderately to the degradation mechanism. The structural stability of the optimized ZnWO4 photocatalyst was further investigated by comparing XRD patterns of the fresh sample and after five consecutive degradation cycles (Fig. 15b). The XRD patterns of the reused ZnWO4 catalyst (after five cycles) show the characteristic diffraction peaks of monoclinic ZnWO4 (JCPDS 73-0554), confirming that the overall crystal structure is preserved. However, careful comparison reveals minor differences: (1) a slight shift of the diffraction peaks corresponding to a lattice strain change; and (2) the appearance of small additional peaks which may indicate trace surface-adsorbed species or minor surface transformation. These changes likely arise from surface modifications, residual organic adsorption, or minor lattice distortion during photocatalytic reactions, rather than bulk structural degradation. The main diffraction peaks remain well-defined, and the overall structure is maintained, confirming the catalyst’s good stability under repeated use. The minor XRD peak shifts and intensity variations observed after recycling are in agreement with the literature and likely arise from surface-adsorbed dye species or trace organic residues2,49. This structural stability, combined with the identified radical mechanism, establishes ZnWO₄ calcined at 500 °C as a durable and efficient photocatalyst for wastewater treatment applications.

The photocatalytic mechanism of ZnWO4 nanoparticles was elucidated through a comprehensive analysis of its energy-band structure and reactive species generation, as illustrated in Fig. 16. When photon energy (hν ≥ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{g}$$\end{document}) excites the ZnWO4 catalyst, valence band electrons (e−) are promoted to the conduction band, leaving behind holes (h+) in the valence band50. This charge separation initiates a cascade of redox reactions: the photogenerated e− reduces adsorbed O2 to form superoxide radicals (·O2−), while h+ oxidizes either H2O or OH− to produce hydroxyl radicals (·OH)51. These highly reactive species (with ·OH possessing an oxidation potential of 2.8 eV) subsequently mineralize MB through progressive oxidation to CO2 and H2O. The exceptional performance of our 500 °C-calcined ZnWO4 (96.5%) can be attributed to three synergistic factors: (1) Optimal band structure facilitating efficient charge separation (confirmed by photocurrent measurements), (2) Reduced e−−h+ recombination due to controlled defect density (evidenced by PL analysis), and (3) Enhanced surface reactivity from tailored crystallinity and morphology (demonstrated by XRD and FE-SEM). Comparative analysis with literature values (Table 4) confirms that our material achieves superior or comparable degradation rates despite using significantly lower catalyst loading, highlighting the advantages of the optimized synthesis approach. The proposed photocatalytic mechanism begins with photoexcitation, where ZnWO4 absorbs UV light (hν ≥ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{g}$$\end{document}), generating electron–hole pairs (e−CB + h+VB)8. The photogenerated holes react with H2O or OH− to produce hydroxyl radicals (·OH), while electrons reduce dissolved O2 to form superoxide radicals (·O2−). These reactive species subsequently attack MB molecules, leading to degradation through three pathways: direct hole oxidation, hydroxyl radical attack, or superoxide-mediated degradation, ultimately mineralizing the dye to CO2 and H2O. The comparative analysis in Table 4 demonstrates that our co-precipitated ZnWO4 calcined at 500 °C achieves exceptional photocatalytic performance (96.5% MB degradation in 260 min), outperforming most reported tungstate-based systems regardless of synthesis methodology. While template-free hydrothermal ZnWO416 shows comparable efficiency (∼100% in 60 min), it requires specialized preparation conditions, whereas our approach utilizes a simpler, scalable co-precipitation method. Notably, our system surpasses other co-precipitated ZnWO₄ catalysts (2.72–87% efficiency14,25) by 9–93 percentage points, demonstrating the critical advantage of our optimized calcination protocol. Compared to doped perovskite systems (72.9–96% efficiency52–54), our undoped ZnWO₄ achieves similar performance without requiring expensive rare-earth elements (Rh/V). The 5 wt% Cu2O/ZnWO4 composite55 shows marginally lower efficiency (91% in 90 min) despite incorporating noble metal oxides, further highlighting the effectiveness of our pure-phase catalyst. While some systems achieve faster degradation (e.g., 60–120 min), they typically employ higher-intensity light sources (250–500 W versus our 1800 W) or specialized lamps (Xe/Hg). Importantly, our catalyst maintains superior performance even when compared to other co-precipitated tungstates like NiWO4 (92.5%14) and CuWO4 (78%56), validating ZnWO4’s inherent photocatalytic advantages. This work establishes 500 °C calcination for 3 h as optimal for MB degradation using zinc nitrate-derived ZnWO4, offering distinct advantages over previously reported co-precipitation protocols7,25 through comprehensive mechanistic insights into band edge positions, charge carrier dynamics, and defect engineering. This comprehensive comparison establishes that our thermally optimized ZnWO₄ represents an ideal balance of (1) high efficiency comparable to complex composites, (2) simple preparation avoiding dopants or templates, and (3) robust performance under moderate irradiation conditions—making it particularly suitable for practical wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the structure–property relationships in ZnWO4 nanoparticles synthesized via a facile co-precipitation method and calcined at 400–700 °C. Comprehensive characterization revealed three critical temperature-dependent effects: (1) crystallite growth from 9.6 to 17.5 nm with increasing calcination temperature, (2) tunable bandgap from 2.5 (as-prepared) to 3.2 eV (500 °C), and (3) optimal surface area (8.58 m2/g) and defect engineering at 500 °C. These synergistic effects culminated in exceptional photocatalytic performance, with ZnWO₄ calcined at 500 °C achieving 96.5% MB degradation within 260 min (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{app}$$\end{document} = 1.24 × 10−2 min−1), outperforming both lower- and higher-temperature counterparts. Scavenger tests confirmed that hydroxyl radicals (·OH) are the dominant reactive species, while superoxide radicals play a negligible role, consistent with calculated band edge positions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{CB}$$\end{document} above O2/·O2− potential). The catalyst maintained > 95% efficiency across five reuse cycles, with XRD confirming structural stability. Compared to the literature, our optimized ZnWO4 system achieves superior degradation rates with minimal catalyst loading (0.01 mg), highlighting both economic and performance advantages. These findings provide fundamental insights into thermal optimization of tungstate photocatalysts while establishing a simple, scalable synthesis protocol for wastewater treatment applications.
