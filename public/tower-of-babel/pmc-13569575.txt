Obesity-related Metabolic Dysfunction and Triple-negative Breast Cancer: Epidemiologic Signals, Mechanistic Pathways, and Opportunities for Prevention

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for more than 2.3 million new cases annually and representing a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Despite advances in early detection, molecular classification, and systemic therapy, substantial geographic and population-level disparities persist in both incidence and tumor subtype distribution [1]. In 2021, high BMI was associated with an estimated 356,740 cancer deaths and 8.89 million disability-adjusted life years globally, with substantial variation by sex, cancer type, and geographic region [2]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—defined clinically by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression and HER2 overexpression/amplification—accounts for approximately 15–20% of breast cancers and is associated with aggressive clinical behavior, earlier recurrence, and fewer established targeted therapeutic options compared with hormone receptor–positive disease [3, 4]. TNBC is overrepresented among younger women and women of non-European ancestry, particularly women of African ancestry [5–7]. Data from Latin America, South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the Middle East suggest that aggressive breast cancer phenotypes may be more prominent in several populations undergoing rapid demographic, nutritional, and metabolic transition, often alongside a growing breast cancer burden and, in some settings, younger age at diagnosis; however, prospective subtype-specific epidemiologic data and registry coverage remain heterogeneous or limited [8–14]. These regions are therefore highlighted both as settings in which to study metabolic–subtype relationships prospectively and as an equity priority for strengthening molecular pathology, biospecimen, and registry infrastructure. Figure 1B provides broader population context by showing the global burden of selected cancer deaths attributable to high BMI, including breast cancer.

The global rise in obesity has reshaped the epidemiology of cancer and other chronic diseases. Well over one billion individuals now live with obesity worldwide, reflecting a major shift in population-level metabolic health [15]. In breast oncology, the relationship between excess adiposity and cancer risk is best established for postmenopausal hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, in part through increased peripheral aromatization and estrogen exposure [16, 17]. However, this mechanism does not fully explain the broader biological effects of obesity, including insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, adipokine imbalance, immune remodeling, and alterations in the adipose stromal microenvironment.

Accumulating evidence suggests that obesity-related metabolic dysfunction may also be relevant to receptor-negative and triple-negative breast cancer, particularly among premenopausal women and metabolically high-risk populations [16, 18]. Figure 1C illustrates the rising global burden of cancers attributable to high BMI from 1990 to 2021 [2]. Although these trends are not subtype-specific, they provide population-level context for investigating whether metabolic dysfunction contributes to aggressive breast cancer biology in selected high-risk populations. Pooled analyses generally report modest but reproducible associations between excess adiposity and TNBC, whereas selected population-specific cohorts have reported larger effect estimates [18]. More recent US data also suggest that obesity-associated subtype patterns may vary by race and population context [7]. These differences should be interpreted cautiously because the previously reported associations vary by study design, obesity definition, menopausal status, subtype classification, ancestry, adjustment variables, underlying metabolic burden, and residual confounding. Nevertheless, the recurring observation of subtype-specific associations raises the possibility that metabolic dysfunction may influence not only overall breast cancer risk but also biological pathways linked to aggressive receptor-negative disease.

Several mechanistic studies provide biological plausibility for the association between obesity and TNBC. Obesity-associated adipose tissue dysfunction is characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy, macrophage infiltration, crown-like structures, altered adipokine secretion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and sustained inflammatory signaling [19–24]. These processes can activate pathways such as STAT3, NF-κB, insulin/IGF-1 signaling, and mechanotransduction networks that promote epithelial plasticity, stemness-associated features, immune evasion, and tumor-promoting stromal–epithelial crosstalk.

This review evaluates the epidemiologic, mechanistic, and translational evidence linking obesity-related metabolic dysfunction to TNBC risk and biology. Rather than proposing metabolic dysfunction as a proven determinant of tumor phenotype, we present an evidence-graded framework in which obesity may contribute to a breast microenvironment that favors inflammatory, metabolic, and stromal conditions associated with aggressive receptor-negative disease. We also identify key limitations, alternative explanations, and research priorities for biomarker-driven risk stratification and prevention studies.

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

This narrative review was informed by a structured literature search rather than conducted as a systematic review or meta-analysis. We searched MEDLINE/PubMed and Embase for English-language articles published from January 1, 2000, through June 30, 2026. Search concepts combined terms for adiposity and metabolic dysfunction (“obesity,” “body mass index,” “visceral adiposity,” “insulin resistance,” “type 2 diabetes”), breast cancer subtype (“triple-negative breast cancer,” “TNBC,” “ER-negative,” “basal-like”), and tumor-microenvironment biology (“adipocytes,” “cancer-associated fibroblasts,” “IL-6,” “IL-8,” “leptin,” “p16,” “STAT3,” “NF-kappaB,” “epithelial-mesenchymal transition”). The Boolean structure was adapted to the syntax of each database, and reference lists of relevant primary studies, meta-analyses, and reviews were hand-searched for additional publications. Because the search was undertaken for a narrative review rather than a prospectively registered systematic review, it was iterative and refined as relevant mechanistic and epidemiologic concepts emerged. The principal search concepts are summarized here, and the database-specific search strategies are provided in Table S1.

Eligible evidence included human observational studies (prospective cohorts, retrospective cohorts, case–control studies, and cross-sectional analyses), quasi-experimental studies of sustained weight loss or bariatric surgery, Mendelian randomization studies, and mechanistic investigations using human tissue, primary cells, co-culture systems, or in vivo models. Case reports, editorials, conference abstracts without full text, and studies without relevance to obesity or metabolic exposures, breast cancer subtype, or the tumor microenvironment were excluded. Potentially relevant records were screened at the title/abstract level, followed by full-text assessment against these criteria. Because the purpose was evidence synthesis and conceptual integration rather than exhaustive effect estimation, formal meta-analysis, risk-of-bias scoring, and PRISMA-based record accounting or flow-diagram reporting were not performed. Searches were conducted by the authors (N.E. and A.A.) using the strategies detailed in Table S1; disagreements on study inclusion were resolved by consensus.

Restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language bias, particularly for evidence from underrepresented regions. We therefore interpreted geographic comparisons cautiously and used reference-list screening to improve retrieval of relevant regional studies.

Definitions: Adiposity, Obesity, and Metabolic Dysfunction

In this review, adiposity and metabolic dysfunction are treated as overlapping but non-equivalent exposures. Obesity is generally defined anthropometrically by body mass index (BMI), whereas visceral adiposity refers to the distribution of metabolically active fat and may be assessed by waist-based or imaging measures. Metabolic dysfunction describes a broader phenotype that may include insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, adipokine imbalance, ectopic fat, and altered nutrient metabolism. Individuals with similar BMI may therefore have markedly different metabolic profiles. Metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO), and metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW) illustrate this dissociation, although operational definitions vary across studies. Accordingly, studies that directly assess visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, inflammatory activation, adipokines, or other metabolic features distinguish BMI-based epidemiologic associations throughout.

Obesity and Breast Cancer: Beyond Estrogen-dependent Mechanisms

The link between obesity and breast cancer is best established for postmenopausal hormone receptor–positive disease. Excess adiposity increases peripheral aromatization of androgens to estrogens in adipose tissue, raising circulating estradiol levels and supporting estrogen receptor signaling. This mechanism provides a well-established biological explanation for the increased risk of ER-positive postmenopausal breast cancer observed among women with obesity [25, 26].

However, estrogen-mediated signaling represents only one component of the obesity–breast cancer relationship. Over the past decade, additional mechanisms, including insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, adipokine imbalance, immune remodeling, extracellular matrix remodeling, adipose stromal dysfunction, and alterations in the gut–breast axis and microbiome, have increasingly been recognized as contributors to obesity-associated carcinogenesis [27–30]. These pathways may be particularly relevant to receptor-negative and triple-negative breast cancer, where estrogen-dependent mechanisms are less likely to provide a complete explanation.

The framework is intended to identify testable translational pathways rather than to imply established phenotype interception, TNBC prevention, or deterministic subtype conversion. Epidemiologic studies have reported associations between excess adiposity and ER-negative or triple-negative breast cancer, especially among premenopausal women and metabolically high-risk populations [16, 18]. These associations should be interpreted cautiously due to their variation across different populations and study designs, as well as the potential contributions of residual confounding, subtype misclassification, reproductive factors, ancestry, and screening patterns to the observed heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the recurrence of subtype-specific associations across multiple settings supports further investigation into non-estrogenic pathways linking metabolic dysfunction to aggressive breast cancer biology.

The following sections examine the epidemiologic and mechanistic evidence linking obesity-related metabolic dysfunction to TNBC risk and biology. Rather than treating metabolic and inflammatory pathways as alternatives to estrogen biology, we consider them complementary mechanisms that may operate alongside hormonal, genetic, reproductive, and social determinants of breast cancer risk.

The Epidemiologic Signal: Obesity and Subtype-specific Risk

A. Consistency and Magnitude of Association

Prospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and pooled analyses support a modest average association between excess adiposity and TNBC, particularly in premenopausal women [16]. More recent syntheses reinforce this interpretation: a 2024 systematic review of premenopausal molecular subtypes and a 2026 meta-analysis of 33 studies including more than 2.1 million women both reported modest subtype-specific associations, with the latter estimating a pooled OR of 1.30 (95% CI 1.15–1.47) for TNBC among premenopausal women with higher BMIs [31, 32]. These contemporary pooled estimates provide the principal benchmark for interpreting larger population-specific effects.

Against this pooled background, larger effect sizes have been reported in selected populations characterized by early-onset disease or high metabolic burden. In a Saudi Arabian cohort with a median age at diagnosis of 45 years, obesity was associated with increased odds of TNBC among premenopausal women (adjusted OR 4.32; 95% CI 2.52–7.42) [18]. This estimate should be regarded as a context-specific high-effect estimate rather than as representative of the average obesity-TNBC association, because population structure, exposure prevalence, case mix, reproductive factors, and subtype ascertainment may differ substantially from those in pooled analyses.

Within the same cohort, the association also varied by breast cancer subtype, with the strongest estimate for TNBC and weaker estimates for HER2-enriched and luminal tumors [18]. This gradient is hypothesis-generating but should not be interpreted as evidence that obesity determines subtype, because age, reproductive factors, ancestry, healthcare access, residual confounding, and subtype misclassification may contribute to the observed pattern.

The corresponding population-attributable fraction (PAF) was approximately 43% for TNBC among premenopausal women [18]. Using Levin’s formula, PAF = Pe(OR-1)/[1 + Pe(OR-1)], the calculation used obesity prevalence among controls (Pe = 23.1%) and the adjusted OR of 4.32. This estimate is conditional on several assumptions: that the association is causal, that the OR reasonably approximates the relative risk in the source population, that exposure prevalence is correctly specified, and that residual confounding and selection bias are limited. It should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that 43% of TNBC cases are preventable.

PAF estimates from different populations should not be compared directly when exposure definitions, obesity prevalence, effect measures, study designs, population structure, or covariate adjustment differ. Published estimates from African American and Western populations remain informative within their source populations [16, 17, 33], but they should not be converted into or interpreted as directly comparable regional PAF estimates when exposure prevalence, effect measures, study design, and covariate adjustment differ. In the revised epidemiologic framework, PAF is used as a study-specific measure of potential population burden under explicit causal assumptions rather than as a cross-regional benchmark.

Parallel observations in African American and Asian populations further indicate that adiposity–TNBC associations vary according to population context, menopausal status, and the measure of adiposity used [5–7, 34]. Beyond BMI, metabolic phenotype may capture additional risk dimensions. A recent meta-analysis found elevated cancer risk among metabolically unhealthy individuals, including some with normal BMI [35], and a 2026 nationwide Korean cohort of more than 2.1 million women demonstrated that breast cancer risk differs across metabolic obesity phenotypes and menopausal status [36]. These data reinforce the need to analyze adiposity and metabolic dysfunction separately rather than treating BMI as a complete measure of metabolic risk.

B. Sources of Heterogeneity Across Studies

The reported associations between obesity and TNBC risk vary widely, from null findings to odds ratios exceeding four. This heterogeneity should not be interpreted simplistically as either inconsistency or evidence of biological amplification. Instead, it likely reflects a combination of biological, methodological, demographic, and social factors that modify or obscure the observed associations.

Age and Menopausal Status

In several studies, the association between obesity and TNBC was strongest among premenopausal women [16, 18]. In younger women, estrogen-dependent postmenopausal mechanisms are less dominant, and metabolic or inflammatory pathways may be relatively more important. However, age and menopausal status are also correlated with parity, breastfeeding, hormonal exposure, screening intensity, and tumor biology, all of which may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Cohorts enriched for women aged 40 years or younger may therefore show stronger obesity–TNBC associations, but these findings require careful adjustment and replication [18, 37].

Populations with high prevalence of obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and sedentary lifestyles provide important settings in which to examine whether metabolic burden modifies associations between adiposity and aggressive breast cancer phenotypes [7, 18, 38]. This pattern is biologically plausible because BMI may serve as a surrogate for a broader metabolic state encompassing hyperinsulinemia, chronic inflammation, adipokine imbalance, and an altered tissue microenvironment. Nevertheless, population-level metabolic burden is difficult to separate from ancestry, socioeconomic conditions, reproductive patterns, diet, physical activity, and healthcare access. These factors should be explicitly considered when interpreting cohorts with high effect sizes.

Ancestry, reproductive factors, and social determinants

TNBC is more common among certain non-European ancestry populations, including women of African ancestry, and is also associated with a younger age at diagnosis and specific reproductive patterns [5–7, 38]. These factors may interact with obesity, but they may also confound it. Differences in parity, age at first birth, breastfeeding, oral contraceptive exposure, socioeconomic status, screening access, and diagnostic pathways may influence both obesity prevalence and subtype distribution. Future studies should therefore integrate metabolic phenotyping with reproductive, genetic, and social determinants, rather than attributing differences in subtypes to adiposity alone.

Genetic Susceptibility and Gene–environment Interaction

TNBC is enriched for germline BRCA1 mutations and for homologous recombination deficiency. High-burden cohorts have observed independent associations between family history, obesity, and TNBC risk [18]. Experimental data indicate that metabolic stress can induce oxidative DNA damage [39], and clinical studies suggest that obesity may promote DNA damage in the breast epithelium from BRCA1/2 mutation carriers [40]. These observations raise the possibility of additive or multiplicative gene–environment interactions, although formal interaction analyses remain limited. At present, this remains a plausible hypothesis rather than an established explanation for subtype-specific risk.

C. Causal Inference and Triangulation of Evidence

Observational associations alone cannot establish that obesity causes TNBC or determines tumor phenotype. However, causal inference can be strengthened when multiple evidence streams—observational epidemiology, intervention-associated risk reduction, genetic inference, and mechanistic studies—point in the same direction and have different sources of bias.

Observational Epidemiology

Subtype-specific observational studies provide the main evidence linking obesity with TNBC risk [16–18]. Their strengths include direct relevance to human populations and the ability to examine clinically defined tumor subtypes. Their limitations include residual confounding, measurement error in BMI or adiposity assessment, incomplete metabolic phenotyping, variation in menopausal classification, and potential subtype misclassification. Observational data are therefore best interpreted as evidence of association and hypothesis generation, rather than as proof of causal subtype specification.

Bariatric Surgery and Weight-loss Studies

These studies may be considered quasi-experimental because they evaluate the long-term effects of surgical weight loss on cancer incidence in non-randomized, real-world cohorts. In the Swedish Obese Subjects study, bariatric surgery was associated with reduced overall cancer incidence among individuals with obesity, including a reduction in breast cancer incidence among women [41]. Longer-term follow-up suggests that risk reduction may extend to female-specific cancers, but subtype-specific analyses remain limited and underpowered [42]. These studies support the broader concept that sustained weight loss and metabolic improvement may reduce cancer risk, but they do not directly establish that weight loss prevents TNBC specifically or alters breast cancer subtype distribution.

Mendelian Randomization (MR)

Mendelian randomization analyses using genetic instruments for BMI have generally reported inverse associations with overall breast cancer risk, including premenopausal disease [43, 44]. These findings contrast with positive observational associations reported for adult adiposity in postmenopausal breast cancer and in some TNBC populations. The discrepancy may reflect life-course timing, instrument selection, pleiotropy, and the inability of BMI-based instruments to capture visceral adiposity or metabolic dysfunction. Because these studies did not directly evaluate TNBC-specific metabolic phenotypes, their findings cannot be interpreted as supporting a positive causal effect of adult metabolic dysfunction on TNBC. More informative causal analyses will require genetic instruments targeting specific metabolic traits rather than BMI alone.

Mechanistic Convergence

Several experimental studies have shown that obesity-associated adipocytes, inflammatory cytokines, and stromal signals can promote epithelial plasticity, mesenchymal features, stemness-associated phenotypes, immune remodeling, and tumor-promoting behavior [19, 20]. These mechanisms provide biological plausibility for epidemiologic associations. However, EMT changes, stemness promotion, and inflammatory signaling should not be interpreted as equivalent to de novo conversion into a clinically receptor-negative phenotype in vivo. Mechanistic evidence strengthens plausibility but does not independently establish subtype causation.

Taken together, the epidemiologic evidence supports a reproducible but heterogeneous association between obesity and TNBC, with stronger signals in some premenopausal and metabolically high-risk populations. Triangulation across observational, interventional, genetic, and mechanistic evidence supports further investigation of obesity-related metabolic dysfunction as a contributor to receptor-negative breast cancer risk and biology. However, the current evidence is insufficient to conclude that obesity directly determines TNBC phenotype. These observations instead support an evidence-graded framework in which metabolic dysfunction may shape the breast microenvironment and influence pathways associated with aggressive receptor-negative diseases.

D. Summary and Transition

Taken together, observational dose–response patterns, quasi-experimental evidence that substantial weight loss may reduce overall breast cancer incidence, and mechanistic studies support further investigation of obesity-related metabolic dysfunction as a possible contributor to receptor-negative breast cancer risk and biology. However, discordant findings from BMI-based Mendelian randomization studies suggest life-course metabolic phenotyping, subtype-specific analyses, and genetic instruments targeting more specific metabolic traits.

These findings direct attention to the tumor microenvironment as a potential interface between systemic metabolic dysfunction and aggressive breast cancer biology. The following section examines mechanistic evidence linking obesity-associated adipose dysfunction, inflammatory signaling, immune remodeling, and stromal–epithelial crosstalk with biological processes relevant to TNBC.

Mechanistic pathways linking obesity-related metabolic dysfunction with TNBC-relevant biology

A. Obesity-induced Adipose Inflammation

Evidence for obesity-associated adipose inflammation comes from studies of human breast adipose tissue, primary mammary adipocyte models, histologic analysis of crown-like structures, and rodent models of diet-induced obesity. Obesity is marked by persistent, low-grade inflammation originating in adipose tissue. Adipocyte hypertrophy can lead to local hypoxia, adipocyte stress and death, and the recruitment of macrophages that form crown-like structures, a histologic feature documented in adipose tissue from obese individuals [45, 46]. These immune cells, together with hypertrophic adipocytes, secrete pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 [47, 48].

Studies using primary mammary adipocytes isolated from obesity-related breast adipose tissue have shown elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8, and leptin, along with downregulation of the tumor suppressor protein p16 [19]. These findings suggest that obesity may induce a persistent state of adipocyte activation. This persistence has been interpreted as a form of metabolic or inflammatory imprinting, potentially involving durable transcriptional or epigenetic changes that sustain pro-inflammatory signaling beyond transient weight fluctuations [49]. However, most human data remain cross-sectional, limiting inference about temporality and reversibility.

Beyond classical inflammatory macrophages, metabolically activated macrophage phenotypes have been identified in mammary adipose tissue in obesity models. In preclinical studies, metabolically activated macrophages secrete IL-6 in a NOX2-dependent manner and signal through GP130 on TNBC cells, thereby enhancing stem-like properties and tumor initiation [50]. Weight loss in these models reverses this inflammatory macrophage phenotype and attenuates obesity-driven TNBC tumor formation [50]. These findings provide mechanistic support for inflammatory mediation, but translation to human TNBC prevention remains to be established.

B. Stromal Activation and Inflammatory Feedback Loops

Evidence for stromal activation derives from primary human cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) and their counterparts isolated from histologically normal tumor-adjacent tissues, indirect co-culturing experiments using breast epithelial cell lines, and studies of human breast cancer stroma. Inflammatory signals from CAAs can activate JAK2/STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways in breast epithelial cells, both non-tumorigenic and cancer-derived lines [19, 20, 51]. These pathways induce expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and IL-8 and may reinforce autocrine and paracrine signaling loops.

In CAAs, increased NF-κB activity promotes IL-8 upregulation, further amplifying the inflammatory microenvironment [20]. These reciprocal signaling circuits may help maintain a tissue niche that supports proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and tumor-promoting epithelial–stromal communication.

Fibroblasts are also key components of the breast tumor microenvironment. However, direct evidence linking obesity-associated adipocyte signaling to fibroblast activation in human breast tissue remains limited [52]. It is biologically plausible that adipocyte-derived cytokines contribute to broader stromal remodeling, but this hypothesis requires further validation using primary human stromal–epithelial systems, spatial profiling, and longitudinal tissue sampling.

C. The p16–miRNA–Leptin Regulatory Axis

Evidence for the p16–miRNA–leptin axis came primarily from primary human mammary adipocytes derived from cancer-free lean and obese women, human breast adipose tissue, and co-culture models using cell lines. In primary mammary adipocytes from women with obesity, p16/CDKN2A expression is reduced at both the mRNA and protein levels [19]. This reduction is associated with increased leptin expression and secretion, linking adipocyte dysfunction with a pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic adipokine profile.

Mechanistic studies indicate that p16 can regulate microRNAs, including miR-141 and miR-146b-5p, that target the leptin transcript [53]. Downregulation of p16 reduces the levels of these microRNAs, thereby increasing leptin expression and adipocyte activation [19, 53]. Secreted leptin can activate JAK2/STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways in breast epithelial and cancer cells, promoting processes such as EMT, angiogenesis, and immune modulation [54, 55].

This axis provides a biologically coherent link between adipocyte dysfunction and sustained stromal signaling. However, leptin should not be presented as the sole or dominant explanation for the obesity–TNBC relationship. Obesity-associated cancer promotion has also been observed in experimental contexts where leptin signaling alone cannot fully account for the phenotype, indicating that leptin likely acts alongside insulin resistance, inflammation, immune remodeling, extracellular matrix changes, and other adipokine networks. Thus, the p16–miRNA–leptin pathway is best interpreted as a component of a broader obesity-associated microenvironmental program rather than a single causal mechanism.

D. Insulin/IGF-1 and Metabolic Signaling

Evidence supporting the roles of insulin and IGF-1 signaling in linking obesity to breast carcinogenesis includes epidemiological research, biomarker data from clinical studies, and experimental models examining the growth of breast cancer cells and their survival. Obesity is often associated with insulin resistance, elevated insulin levels (hyperinsulinemia), altered bioavailable IGF-1 signaling, dyslipidemia, and changes in nutrient availability [56, 57]. These metabolic alterations can provide mitogenic (cell growth-promoting) and anti-apoptotic (preventing cell death) signals that may, at least in part, act independently of estrogen receptor signaling.

Insulin and IGF-1 receptor activation can stimulate downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK signaling, supporting proliferation, survival, and metabolic adaptation [56]. These pathways are relevant to breast cancer broadly and may be particularly important in receptor-negative tumors, where growth may be less dependent on estrogen signaling. Emerging clinical data linking insulin receptor expression and hyperinsulinemia to TNBC further support the plausibility of metabolic signaling as a contributor to aggressive receptor-negative biology [58].

Nevertheless, this evidence has important limitations. BMI is an imperfect surrogate for insulin resistance, and many epidemiologic studies lack direct measures of fasting insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR, visceral adiposity, adipokines, or inflammatory biomarkers. Moreover, insulin/IGF-1 signaling is not specific to TNBC and may influence multiple breast cancer subtypes. Future studies should therefore distinguish adiposity from metabolic dysfunction and evaluate whether insulin-related biomarkers improve the prediction of receptor-negative disease beyond BMI and established risk factors.

E. Paracrine Induction of Epithelial Plasticity

Evidence for paracrine induction of epithelial plasticity largely derives from direct and indirect co-culture systems and from neutralizing antibody studies using primary adipocytes and breast epithelial or cancer cell lines. Exposure to conditioned media from obesity-activated primary human adipocytes or p16-deficient adipocytes promotes mesenchymal features in breast epithelial cell models, including increased expression of N-cadherin and vimentin, enhanced migratory capacity, and increased invasive behavior [19, 20].

Neutralization of IL-8 or leptin attenuated these mesenchymal changes, indicating that these adipokines contribute to EMT-like and pro-invasive signaling in vitro [20]. Combined inhibition does not appear to be fully additive, suggesting convergence on shared downstream pathways. Co-culture experiments with p16-deficient adipocytes similarly support a role for adipocyte reprogramming in epithelial plasticity [19].

Additional adipokines may also contribute. For example, apelin has been implicated in obesity-associated tumor growth and metastatic potential in murine models and human TNBC cell lines [59]. Collectively, these findings indicate that obesity-associated adipocyte signaling can promote epithelial plasticity and aggressive tumor behavior in experimental systems. However, EMT-like changes, migration, invasion, and stemness-associated phenotypes should not be equated with the de novo emergence of a clinical TNBC subtype in humans. Direct evidence that these paracrine effects cause loss of ER, PR, or HER2 expression in vivo remains lacking.

F. Immune Remodeling and Stemness-associated Features

Evidence for obesity-related immune remodeling derives from murine obesity models, human adipose tissue analyses, and tumor immune profiling studies. Obesity can reshape the immune microenvironment by promoting macrophage infiltration, myeloid-derived suppressor cell accumulation, altered T-cell function, and inflammatory cytokine production [60–63]. In tumor-bearing animal models, myeloid-derived suppressor cells can suppress cytotoxic T-cell responses and promote tumor progression [60–63].

These immune changes are relevant to TNBC biology because TNBC often displays immune-rich but immunologically complex microenvironments, high proliferation, and stemness-associated features [3, 4]. Obesity-activated adipocytes and inflammatory macrophage phenotypes can enrich stemness-associated markers in experimental systems, including ALDH1 activity and CD44-high/CD24-low phenotypes [19, 50]. Such features overlap with basal-like and aggressive breast cancer biology and may contribute to therapy resistance, invasion, or recurrence.

However, the relationship between obesity, immune remodeling, and TNBC remains incompletely defined in humans. Immune alterations observed in obesity are not specific to TNBC and may influence tumor initiation, progression, and treatment response across multiple cancer types. The question of whether obesity directly enriches a cancer stem-like population in normal human breast epithelium or selectively promotes TNBC precursor states remains unresolved and necessitates prospective tissue-based and single-cell studies.

G. Extracellular Matrix Remodeling and Mechanotransduction

Evidence for extracellular matrix remodeling comes from human tissue studies, animal models, and mechanistic studies of fibrosis, tissue stiffness, and mechanotransduction. Obesity is associated with adipose tissue expansion, fibrosis, collagen deposition, altered matrix architecture, and changes in tissue stiffness [14, 64]. These changes can modify stromal–epithelial interactions and create mechanical cues that affect how tumor cell behave.

Mechanotransduction pathways, including YAP/TAZ-related signaling, may link obesity-associated matrix remodeling to proliferation, invasion, and aggressive tumor behavior [64]. Increased collagen deposition and matrix stiffness can promote cell migration, alter epithelial polarity, and facilitate the acquisition of invasive phenotypes. These processes provide a mechanism by which obesity may influence the physical and biochemical properties of the breast microenvironment beyond soluble cytokines and adipokines.

This pathway extends the mechanistic model beyond leptin, IL-6, IL-8, and STAT3/NF-κB signaling. At present, the strongest inference is that matrix remodeling may create a permissive environment for invasion and aggressive tumor behavior.

H. Microbiome and the Gut–breast Axis

Evidence for the effect of obesity on the microbiome and the gut–breast axis is still emerging and comes from preclinical models, human observational studies, and broader studies of obesity-associated systemic inflammation [65]. Obesity can alter the composition of the gut microbiome, intestinal permeability, bile acid metabolism, circulating metabolites, immune tone, and systemic inflammatory signaling [27]. These changes may indirectly influence cancer biology through metabolic and immunologic pathways [27, 66].

The relevance of the microbiome to breast cancer may include effects on estrogen metabolism, inflammatory mediators, microbial metabolites, and immune regulation [66]. These mechanisms are potentially relevant to obesity-associated breast cancer biology, including receptor-negative disease, because they may shape systemic inflammatory and metabolic conditions that interact with the breast microenvironment [27, 66]. However, evidence linking obesity-associated microbiome changes specifically to TNBC risk or subtype distribution remains limited [67].

Accordingly, the microbiome should be presented as an emerging component of the broader obesity–cancer framework rather than as a central established mechanism in TNBC [65, 67]. Its inclusion acknowledges the expanding mechanistic literature and addresses the need to avoid an overly narrow leptin- or cytokine-centered model, but its causal relevance to TNBC requires further validation [27, 67].

I. Reversibility and Therapeutic Tractability

A key translational question is whether obesity-associated stromal and inflammatory reprogramming is reversible. Evidence primarily comes from preclinical mouse models, weight-loss models, and pharmacologic inhibition studies in cell lines and co-culture systems. In diet-induced obesity models, weight loss can reverse metabolically activated macrophage phenotypes and attenuate obesity-driven TNBC tumor formation [50]. These findings suggest that some obesity-associated inflammatory states may be reversible.

Pharmacologic targeting of inflammatory pathways also supports mechanistic tractability. Inhibiting IL-8 signaling via CXCR1/2 antagonism, including reparixin, can reduce inflammatory signaling, attenuate STAT3 activation, and diminish pro-tumorigenic stromal effects in experimental systems [20, 68]. More broadly, interventions targeting insulin resistance, inflammatory signaling, adipokine imbalance, and metabolic dysfunction have been proposed as strategies to modify cancer-relevant host physiology [69].

However, these approaches remain experimental for TNBC prevention. Weight loss, GLP-1 receptor agonists, metformin, CXCR1/2 blockade, STAT3/NF-κB inhibition, and related strategies should not be presented as established methods for preventing TNBC or changing the distribution of breast cancer subtypes. Their appropriate role at present is in biomarker-enriched feasibility and window-of-opportunity studies designed to evaluate short-term metabolic, inflammatory, stromal, and tissue-based endpoints. Such studies should address eligibility, safety, exposure duration, and biomarker responsiveness before subtype-specific prevention endpoints are considered. Such trials would require careful attention to eligibility criteria, safety, exposure duration, metabolic and inflammatory endpoints, tissue-based biomarkers, and the distinction between reducing overall breast cancer risk and altering subtype-specific burden.

J. Summary and Interpretive Caveats

Taken together, mechanistic studies support biological plausibility across human tissue, primary-cell, animal, and cell-line systems, but most pathways described here are shared with other breast cancer subtypes and are not TNBC-specific. The evidence is strongest for tumor-promoting inflammation, metabolic adaptation, immune remodeling, invasion, and epithelial plasticity; evidence for de novo receptor loss or subtype specification in humans is lacking. Host Metabolic State, Microenvironmental Selection, and Aggressive Breast Cancer Biology

An Ecological and Evolutionary Perspective

Cancer develops within an ecological system in which tumor-intrinsic genetic and epigenetic alterations interact with host conditions. In this framework, metabolic dysfunction is considered a potential modifier of tissue ecology rather than a substitute for intrinsic oncogenic drivers. Germline BRCA1 susceptibility, TP53 alterations, homologous recombination deficiency, lineage-specific transcriptional programs, and other genomic or epigenomic events remain central to receptor-negative and basal-like breast cancer biology.

Obesity-related metabolic dysfunction can alter inflammatory, hypoxic, nutrient, immune, and extracellular-matrix conditions within breast tissue. These changes may create selective pressures that favor cells with stress tolerance, metabolic adaptability, invasive capacity, or reduced dependence on hormone-driven growth. Large-scale tumor profiling and experimental studies are consistent with obesity-associated differences in inflammatory microenvironments, stromal remodeling, myeloid-cell recruitment, and extracellular-matrix organization [14, 70, 71].

Clinical associations with higher grade, proliferative features, adverse gene-expression patterns, and poorer outcomes further support biological relevance [72–74], but they cannot distinguish effects on initiation, subtype development, progression, treatment delivery, or competing cardiometabolic risk. Figure 3 therefore depicts metabolic context as interacting with, rather than replacing, tumor-intrinsic determinants. Precision Risk Stratification

The convergence of epidemiologic associations and mechanistic evidence suggests that obesity-related metabolic dysfunction may help identify women at increased risk of aggressive receptor-negative breast cancer [27]. However, BMI alone is an incomplete measure of metabolic risk [27, 75]. Individuals with similar BMI may differ substantially in visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, adipose inflammation, adipokine balance, and ectopic fat. Conversely, metabolically unhealthy normal-weight individuals may exhibit adverse metabolic profiles despite normal BMI, while some individuals with obesity remain relatively metabolically healthy. These phenotypes reinforce the need to separate anthropometric adiposity from metabolic dysfunction in risk models.

A precision framework for risk stratification should therefore move beyond anthropometric classification alone. Fasting insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR, leptin, adiponectin, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein are biologically plausible markers, while visceral adiposity, crown-like structures (CLS), and stromal signatures may provide tissue-level context [76–78]. Across individual adipokines, reproducibility remains variable: leptin has a comparatively broad mechanistic and observational evidence base, whereas findings for apelin and adiponectin are less extensively replicated and may vary by population, menopausal status, metabolic phenotype, and assay methodology [54, 55, 59, 79]. No single marker is currently sufficiently reproducible or specific for TNBC prediction across cohorts. Multi-marker panels that combine metabolic, inflammatory, adipokine, imaging, and genomic information are therefore more plausible than reliance on an isolated biomarker but require prospective calibration and external validation before clinical use.

Integrating metabolic phenotyping with germline susceptibility, family history, reproductive factors, ancestry, and social determinants may clarify whether obesity-related metabolic dysfunction modifies subtype-specific breast cancer risk in selected populations [27, 80]. This approach may be particularly relevant for premenopausal women, women with a strong family history or BRCA1-associated risk, and populations with high burdens of obesity, insulin resistance, and early-onset breast cancer [80]. Subtype-specific incidence should be considered a later-stage endpoint only after reproducible biomarker modulation and safety have been demonstrated.

Research priorities for translating metabolic–stromal biology into TNBC prevention

Integrating metabolic phenotyping into prospective breast cancer cohorts will be essential to clarify whether obesity-related metabolic dysfunction predicts subtype-specific risk beyond BMI and established risk factors. Such phenotyping should include measures of visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, inflammatory activation, adipokine balance, immune status, and, where feasible, tissue-level markers of breast adipose inflammation or stromal remodeling [75]. Longitudinal evaluation will be required to determine whether circulating or tissue-based biomarkers independently predict receptor-negative disease, beyond age, ancestry, reproductive history, germline susceptibility, socioeconomic factors, screening access, and other established determinants of breast cancer risk [75].

Early translational studies should align interventions with measurable metabolic and inflammatory risk states rather than BMI categories alone [82, 84]. Lifestyle and weight-loss interventions, metformin, and newer anti-obesity therapies including GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists such as tirzepatide can modify weight, insulin sensitivity, leptin, adiponectin, and cardiometabolic profiles [79, 85–88]. These properties justify mechanistic investigation but not claims of TNBC prevention. Initial studies should therefore prioritize short-term biomarker and tissue endpoints, with larger prevention trials considered only if a reproducible signal is established [89].

Regions undergoing rapid demographic, nutritional, and metabolic transitions including parts of the Middle East, Latin America, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa are important settings for future research because rising obesity and insulin resistance may coexist with younger age at breast cancer diagnosis, limited screening infrastructure, and incomplete subtype-specific data. Cross-regional PAF estimates are not used as direct benchmarks because their underlying exposure prevalence, study design, and adjustment structures differ. Priority should instead be given to harmonized prospective cohorts, biospecimen repositories, standardized receptor testing, and scalable molecular-pathology capacity that permit comparable analyses across settings.

Alternative Explanations and Limitations

Several alternative interpretations warrant consideration. Reverse causality—whereby early, undetected malignancy induces systemic metabolic changes—cannot be entirely excluded, although prospective cohort designs and Mendelian randomization studies reduce this concern. Detection bias may also contribute. Women with obesity may have different patterns of healthcare contact, screening participation, imaging sensitivity, diagnostic delay, or clinical follow-up compared with women without obesity. These factors could influence the stage at diagnosis or tumor detection patterns, although they would not readily account for consistent subtype-specific associations across multiple settings.

Residual confounding remains a major limitation of the observational literature. Obesity is correlated with reproductive factors, including parity, age at first birth, breastfeeding history, menopausal timing, and exogenous hormone exposure, all of which may influence breast cancer subtype distribution [90]. Socioeconomic status, diet, physical activity, diabetes, medication use, environmental exposures, and access to screening or molecular pathology may also confound or modify reported associations. In populations undergoing rapid demographic and nutritional transitions, these factors may cluster with obesity, making it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of metabolic dysfunction.

Ancestry and genetic susceptibility also warrant careful consideration. TNBC is more common in certain non-European-ancestry populations and is enriched among carriers of germline BRCA1 mutations and in tumors with homologous recombination deficiency. Therefore, observed associations between obesity and TNBC may partly reflect underlying differences in genetic susceptibility, population structure, reproductive history, or subtype ascertainment. Subsequent research ought to amalgamate germline data, ancestry-informed analyses, family history, and comprehensive reproductive and metabolic phenotyping to elucidate whether obesity-related metabolic dysfunction independently modifies subtype-specific risk or does so via interaction with inherited susceptibility.

Subtype misclassification is another important limitation. TNBC is clinically defined by the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, but it is not synonymous with basal-like breast cancer. Conversely, not all basal-like tumors are clinically triple-negative. Differences in receptor testing, assay thresholds, tissue handling, HER2 classification, Ki-67 availability, and molecular subtyping can all affect how subtypes are assigned in different studies. Throughout this review, TNBC refers to clinically defined ER-negative, PR-negative, HER2-negative breast cancer, whereas basal-like disease is discussed only when molecular subtype data are available.

TNBC comprises a heterogeneous group of tumors with distinct genomic, transcriptomic, immune, and metabolic features. Some receptor-negative diseases likely arise through pathways other than obesity-associated inflammation or stroma. For example, tumors with luminal androgen receptor signaling, homologous recombination deficiency, immune-enriched biology, or mesenchymal features may differ in their relationship to metabolic dysfunction. Some TNBC subsets may be more metabolically responsive than others, but this remains poorly defined.

Finally, most available studies rely on BMI as the primary exposure, despite its limited ability to capture visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, sarcopenic obesity, ectopic fat, or metabolic health status. These should be treated as distinct exposures rather than interchangeable variants of obesity. Future cohorts should therefore combine repeated anthropometry with waist-based or imaging measures of visceral and ectopic fat, metabolic biomarkers, body-composition measures where feasible, germline susceptibility, social determinants, and standardized tumor molecular profiling [91]. This approach will be essential for distinguishing causal relationships from correlated associations and for identifying which breast cancer subsets, if any, are most influenced by metabolic dysfunction.

Conclusion

Current evidence supports a modest but reproducible association between excess adiposity and TNBC, particularly in premenopausal women, together with biological plausibility linking metabolic dysfunction to inflammatory, stromal, immune, and metabolic processes relevant to aggressive receptor-negative disease. These data do not establish that obesity determines tumor phenotype or causes de novo receptor loss in humans.

The evidence also differs by intended inference. Causal pathways remain incompletely established; potentially modifiable processes include insulin resistance, adipose inflammation, adipokine imbalance, and stromal remodeling, although reversibility in human breast tissue is uncertain; and proposed predictive biomarkers remain investigational and insufficiently validated for clinical TNBC risk prediction. This distinction between causal, modifiable, and predictive evidence should guide both interpretation and study design.

The next priority is disciplined validation. Prospective studies should integrate detailed adiposity and metabolic phenotyping with germline and somatic genomic data, reproductive and social determinants, standardized receptor testing, longitudinal biospecimens, and nested spatial or single-cell profiling. Biomarker-enriched feasibility and window-of-opportunity studies should precede large prevention trials and should distinguish overall breast cancer risk, TNBC-specific risk, subtype distribution, and treatment response as separate endpoints.
