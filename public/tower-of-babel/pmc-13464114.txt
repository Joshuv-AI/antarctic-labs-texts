A Rare Case of Bilateral Mature Teratoma With Disseminated Deposits and Ascitic Fluid Involvement in a Young Female: An Exceptional Case Report
Authors: Rajashree Jeyaraman, Roobashri Murugan, Bhawana Badhe, Debasis Gochhait
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Teratoma is a germ cell neoplasm of the ovary. There are two main types of teratomas: mature teratoma and immature teratoma. Teratomas can rarely be associated with disseminated deposits. It is most commonly seen in immature teratomas. Mature teratomas with deposits are exceedingly rare. Glial tissue is the most widely implanted tissue in the case of teratomas. We report a case of immature teratoma of the ovary, postneoadjuvant chemotherapy with abdominal pain and distension. Imaging revealed an ovarian mass. Following this, total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy were performed. The patient was diagnosed with bilateral mature teratoma with disseminated deposits and ascitic fluid involvement. These deposits comprised elements with derivatives of all three germ cell layers. This case will help to shed light on the literature regarding the status of disseminated deposits and ascitic fluid involvement in mature teratoma of the ovary.

1. Introduction

Teratoma is a germ cell neoplasm of the ovary. There are two main types of teratoma: mature teratoma and immature teratoma [1].Mature teratoma is a benign tumour that can occur at any age but is most commonly reported in the reproductive age group. The incidence of malignant transformation in a teratoma is 0.17%–2% [2]. Gliomatosis peritonei is the implantation of mature neural tissue onto the peritoneal surface. It is mainly seen in immature teratomas and rarely in mature teratomas [3].

Growing teratoma syndrome (GTS) is a rare and unusual complication that may arise during or after treatment of a malignant germ cell tumour. This syndrome involves the transformation of immature teratomatous elements into mature components following chemotherapy. Khandelwal and Giri et al. has documented cases in males with testicular germ cell tumours, where recurrences posttherapy consisted exclusively of mature teratoma [4]. The diagnostic criteria of GTS include [1] a progressively enlarging mass during or after chemotherapy, [2] normalisation of previously elevated tumour markers and [3] histopathological confirmation of mature teratoma without malignant elements [5].

In this report, we present a case of a 22‐year‐old female, diagnosed with ovarian immature teratoma, who developed GTS following chemotherapy. We also provide a focused review of the literature to understand this rare entity.

2. Case History

A 22‐year‐old female presented with abdominal distension and intermittent lower abdominal pain for 3 months. Contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) revealed a larger heterogenous right ovarian mass measuring 11 × 10 × 8 cm with multiple peritoneal deposits; serum levels of alpha fetoprotein (AFP) were elevated (526 ng/mL), whereas beta human chorionic gonadotrophin (Beta‐HCG) was within normal limits. The patient was initially evaluated at an outside hospital, where a diagnosis of immature teratoma was rendered on biopsy. However, the pretreatment histopathological slides and blocks are not available for review at our institute. The patient subsequently received three cycles of bleomycin, cisplatin and etoposide. It was administered at 3 weekly intervals.

After 6 months, she presented with complaints of abdominal pain and abdominal distension. Serum AFP levels at this time point is 5.8 ng/mL. Serial tumour marker evaluation revealed a progressive decline in serum AFP levels following chemotherapy (4.8 ng/dL). Postchemotherapy CECT imaging revealed a large heterogeneous solid mass measuring 18 × 15 × 10 cm with coarse calcifications, fat areas and fluid attenuation areas arising from the right ovary (Figure 1). The left ovary was also enlarged. There was massive ascites. There were suspicious subdiaphragmatic deposits (Figure 2).

Following this, a cytoreduction surgery was done. A total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy and rectal resection was performed. The right ovary was grossly enlarged, measuring 12 × 11 × 8 cm. The capsule was intact. No surface deposits were seen. The cut surface revealed a solid cystic lesion. The solid area was 80%, which was gray‐tan to brown and vaguely nodular with gelatinous and yellowish areas. Few places were gritty to cut. No areas of necrosis, papillary excrescences or haemorrhage were seen. The cystic area was filled with mucinous material. The right ovary was adhered to the rectum. However, the tumour did not penetrate the rectum (Figure 2). The left ovary was completely cystic and filled with mucinous material. The uterus and bilateral fallopian tubes were unremarkable. We also received specimens of caecal deposit, subdiaphragmatic deposit, liver deposit, sigmoid deposit and omentum.

Extensive sampling was performed. Histopathological sections from the left ovary showed a tumour composed of mature ectodermal, mesodermal and endodermal elements. The elements are hair follicles, sebaceous gland, benign neural tissue, cartilage, bone, smooth muscle, fibro adipose tissue, salivary gland and intestinal and respiratory epithelium (Figure 3). No immature elements or necrosis were identified. The deposit was on the surface of the right ovary and all the other specimens that were received showed deposits consisting of derivatives from all three germ cell layers. No immature elements were seen in any of them. In the absence of the review of the pretreatment histopathological material at our institute, the diagnosis was considered suggestive of GTS based on the available clinical history, chemotherapy exposure, radiological progression, normalised tumour markers and mature teratomatous histology on the resection specimen.

3. Discussion

Mature teratoma accounts for over 10%–20% of all ovarian tumours. It is the most common germ cell tumour of the ovary [6]. The complications of cystic teratoma are torsion, malignant transformation, rupture and infections [7]. Teratomas arising from the extragonadal location are rare. Thyroid, retroperitoneum and mediastinum are the common extragonadal sites of teratoma [8]. In contrast, immature teratomas are malignant tumours. They are composed of immature embryonal components. The prognosis of this depends on the percentage of immature components [9].

GTS is related to both ovarian and testicular tumours. The reported incidence of GTS following testicular germ cell tumours ranges from 1.9% to 7.6%. The frequency in ovarian germ cell tumours remains uncertain. The pathogenesis of GTS is not completely understood. The hypotheses that have been proposed are (1) the elimination of chemotherapy‐sensitive, immature germ cell elements with persistent proliferation of the mature germ cell elements and (2) chemotherapy‐induced differentiation of immature elements into mature elements [10, 11]. The hallmark of GTS is the presence of mature somatic elements. No malignant germ cell elements should be present after adequate sampling. Hence, meticulous sampling and histological evaluation are required to exclude residual immature elements [6].

The present case fulfilled the criteria of GTS, including postchemotherapy decline in serum tumour markers, persistence of radiological lesions and histopathological demonstration of mature teratomatous tissue without immature malignant elements.

Grossly, GTS lesions are well circumscribed, solid cystic with areas of calcification, ossification and cartilage formation. These features, although benign, can mimic malignancy radiologically. This emphasises the confirmation on histopathological examination [12].

Differential diagnosis includes residual nonseminomatous germ cell tumour, teratoma with somatic transformation and posttherapy fibrosis or necrosis [13]. Complete surgical resection of GTS lesions is recommended. It is a curative approach. Incomplete resection is a causative factor for recurrence. Hence, long term follow up is recommended [14].

Similar to the previously published reports, the present case demonstrated enlargement of the deposits, despite chemotherapy. Johnson et al. [15] described a paediatric ovarian immature teratoma with massive ascites and peritoneal deposits. Postchemotherapy resection in this case revealed mature teratomatous tissue.

Similarly, Senger and Kulkarni [16], reported progressive increase in size of pelvic mass during chemotherapy, in spite of normalisation of AFP levels. Emphazing paradoxical growth pattern is typical of GTS.

Peritoneal dissemination in GTS occurs as mature teratomatous implants or gliomatosis peritonei, both of which can mimic persistent malignant disease. Liu et al. [17] documented extensive abdominal deposits associated with mature teratomatous transformation following chemotherapy. Patients with teratoma can present with Meigs syndrome, which includes ovarian mass, massive ascites and pleural effusion [18]. Ascitic fluid involvement can be observed in immature teratomas. Mature teratomas with ascitic fluid involvement are rare, with only very few cases reported in the literature [3].

Our case is a sporadic case of mature teratoma with multiple peritoneal deposits and ascitic fluid dissemination. The deposits contained components derived from all three germ layers: hair follicles, sebaceous gland, benign neural tissue, cartilage, bone, smooth muscle, fibro adipose tissue, salivary gland, intestinal epithelium and respiratory epithelium. Ascitic fluid cytology smears showed mature squamous cells and anucleate squames. One important differential consideration in the ascitic fluid cytology is the rupture of the ovarian contents, mimicking true peritoneal dissemination. In the present case, the possibility of the presence of radiologically demonstrable peritoneal deposits and an intact capsule in both the ovaries favoured true peritoneal involvement.

The limitation in the present case was the nonavailability of pretreatment histopathological material for review, as the patient had been initially diagnosed and treated in an outside hospital. Therefore, correlation with original histopathological data could not be performed at our institute. The diagnosis was based on histomorphological features. The absence of immunohistochemical correlation represents the limitation of the present study.

4. Conclusion

Mature teratomas with deposits and ascitic fluid involvement are exceedingly rare. The presence of components composed of derivatives from all three germ cell layers in the deposits site is highly unusual. Histopathological analysis plays a crucial role in identifying this rare entity of GTS.
