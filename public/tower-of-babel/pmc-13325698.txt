Introducing Chemical Vapor Etching (CVE): A Material‐Selective Approach for Surgically Precise Tarnish Removal
Authors: Yumei Chai, Aranzazu Sierra‐Fernández, Yuxing Qin, Raydel Pérez Menéndez, Jianfeng Zhu, Mato Knez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe controlled removal of corrosion products is a persistent challenge in fields ranging from industrial alloy protection to cultural heritage conservation. Achieving high precision and selectivity in subtractive treatments remains difficult because of chemical complexity and structural heterogeneity of the corrosion layers. Inspired by the surface‐chemistry principles of Atomic Layer Etching (ALE), we developed a vapor‐phase strategy termed Chemical Vapor Etching (CVE), which is conceptually analogous to chemical vapor deposition operated in reverse. CVE is demonstrated on artificially corroded Cu‐Sn‐Pb bronze that simulates marine‐aged artifacts. Using Hexafluoroacetylacetone (Hhfac) as a volatilizing agent, corrosion products, including oxides, hydroxides, carbonates, and chloride‐containing phases, are selectively converted into volatile species and removed, whereas the underlying substrate remains unaffected. SEM and XRD confirmed the preservation of surface morphology and phase composition, whereas EDS/XPS revealed the efficient removal of sulfur species and strong reduction of chlorine. The process exhibits tarnish‐limited, self‐terminating behavior, with etching effectively ceasing once a stable Cu/Cu2O interface is reached. By combining chemical selectivity, time‐controlled removal, and non‐line‐of‐sight capability, CVE provides a controllable alternative to conventional mechanical, laser, and plasma‐based cleaning approaches. Beyond bronze conservation, this strategy offers a chemically precise route for managing corrosion in technologically relevant, copper‐based systems.

Introduction

Copper‐based alloys, including bronze and brass, have always been employed in various architectural elements, industrial components, and exist as cultural heritage artefacts for their excellent mechanical strength, thermal and electrical conductivity, and appealing aesthetics [1, 2, 3]. From the bronze masks and chariots of China's Sanxingdui civilization over three millennia ago [4], to the bronze warrior statues of ancient Greece [5], the Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor, and the majestic equestrian sculptures that adorn European squares [6], copper and bronze artefacts have transcended time, bearing witness to the progress of human civilization and the evolution of aesthetic express. However, in humid, chloride‐rich marine climates, copper and its alloys are highly susceptible to oxidation and electrochemical corrosion, leading to the formation of stable copper oxides, basic copper salts, and highly reactive chloride‐bearing corrosion products, which severely compromise their structural integrity and surface characteristics [7, 8, 9]. The accumulation of these species undermines both the structural integrity and surface appearance of the substrate [10, 11, 12]. For high‐value items, such as historic buildings, significant artefacts, or precision‐engineered copper components, corrosion not only shortens service life and diminishes aesthetic value but may also cause irreversible loss of surface morphology and embedded cultural information [13, 14, 15]. Consequently, the removal of tarnish from copper‐based materials has become a central focus in conservation research and practice, leading to the continuous development and refinement of cleaning techniques.

Current tarnish removal techniques for bronze are undergoing a shift from traditional mechanical and chemical approaches toward more gentle, controllable, and selective strategies. Conventional methods such as mechanical grinding, acid dissolution, and chelation offer simplicity and high efficiency, but often pose risks of substrate damage and incomplete tarnish removal, which restrict their suitability for valuable artefacts or precision components [16, 17, 18]. In recent years, electrochemical approaches have come into the spotlight due to their ability to selectively reduce corrosion products and modulate interfacial potentials, making them especially effective against CuCl and other active chlorides [19]. Advanced surface treatment and material removal techniques increasingly emphasize controllability, precision, and minimization of substrate disturbance, driven by innovative near‐surface engineering strategies [20, 21]. Laser cleaning techniques enable highly localized and contact‐free material removal with excellent spatial resolution; however, their application to copper alloy artefacts may be constrained by thermal accumulation, phase transformation risks, and limited selectivity when dealing with multilayered or chemically heterogenous corrosion products. Plasma‐based methods offer rapid surface activation and effective removal of loosely bound contaminants, yet their reliance on energetic species and physical sputtering mechanisms may compromise fine surface features or induce unintended chemical modification of the underlying substrate [22, 23, 24, 25]. Enzyme‐based green methods are applied for specific degradation of organic contaminants under mild conditions, but their application is still limited by challenges in activity, stability and substrate compatibility [26, 27]. Although these techniques have achieved considerable progress, a critical challenge remains in balancing corrosion removal and surface protection. This trade‐off becomes particularly pronounced in multilayered, heterogeneous corrosion systems containing reactive chlorides, where existing methods struggle to reconcile thorough cleaning with substrate preservation [28, 29, 30, 31]. This highlights an urgent need for new strategies that are both precise and minimally invasive.

In contrast to laser‐based cleaning, where material removal is governed by photothermal or photomechanical interactions that may induce subsurface heating, phase transformation, or localized stress, or plasma‐based approaches that rely on energetic ion bombardment and physical sputtering mechanisms, a chemistry‐driven strategy offers a fundamentally different mode of selectivity. Rather than relying on energy deposition or momentum transfer, material removal can be achieved through targeted surface reactions that preferentially convert reactive corrosion phases into volatile species that can be easily removed by purging. Such an approach enables corrosion‐phase selectivity while minimizing collateral modification of the underlying metallic substrate, thereby defining a distinct niche among advanced non‐contact cleaning technologies.

In recent years, atomic layer etching (ALE) has demonstrated great potential in achieving extreme precision and controllability during subtractive surface treatments [32, 33]. Various processes have been developed for the precise etching of transition metal surfaces [34, 35]. For example, Cl− ions have been used to chemically modify Co surfaces to form CoCl, which can be selectively removed through adsorption and volatilization using the etchant hexafluoroacetylacetone (Hhfac) at temperatures above 140°C [36, 37]. Similarly, etching of metallic copper typically relies on a combination of surface oxidation and ligand‐assisted volatilization mechanisms [38]. With specific organic reagents such as Hhfac, acetylacetone (Hacac), and formic acid, selective removal of Cu and other transition metals has been successfully achieved [39]. These advanced etching strategies, featuring both selectivity and controllability, have provided important inspiration for the present study.

Here, we extend the chemical principles of atomic layer deposition (ALE) to substrates that differ fundamentally from those typically addressed by this technique (Figure 1a). In conventional ALE, material removal is achieved through two sequential, self‐limiting half‐reactions: surface modification (Half‐reaction A), followed by ligand‐assisted volatilization (Half‐reaction B), resulting in a discrete thickness change per cycle. In the present case, however, the corrosion layer to be removed, composed of oxides, hydroxides, carbonates, and chloride‐containing phases, already corresponds chemically to the product of Half‐reaction A (Figure 1b). Consequently, the initial surface modification step becomes unnecessary, and the process can be simplified by directly exploiting the volatilization reaction (half‐reaction B) alone.

Conceptual comparison between cycle‐based atomic layer etching (ALE) and time‐dependent chemical vapor etching (CVE). (a) In ALE, material removal proceeds through alternating, self‐limiting half‐reactions, resulting in discrete thickness reduction controlled by the number of cycles; (b) In CVE, only the ligand‐assisted volatilization step is performed, and the thickness evolution is governed by rection time rather than cycle number. Etching proceeds selectively within chemically active corrosion layers and self‐terminates upon exhaustion of the tarnish, while the underlying bronze substrate remains essentially unaffected. The schematic highlights the fundamental distinction between cycle‐limited ALE and chemically selective, self‐terminating CVE (not to scale).

The use of a single precursor naturally evokes analogies with chemical vapor deposition (CVD), in which continuous precursor exposure leads to time‐dependent film growth (Figure 1b). Here, however, this paradigm is inverted: instead of deposition, selective chemical vapor etching (CVE) occurs. In contrast to ALD/ALE, where thickness evolution is governed by cycle number, the extent of material removal in CVE is controlled by reaction time. Importantly, CVE preserves a key advantage of ALE, its non‐line‐of‐sight nature, allowing highly conformal access to rough, recessed, or porous surfaces. As illustrated schematically in Figure 1b, the CVE process is chemistry‐limited rather than cycle‐limited. Etching proceeds selectively within the corrosion layer and progressively slows as reactive species are depleted. Once the tarnish is exhausted and a stable Cu2O‐rich interface or metallic bronze substrate is reached, the reaction rate decreases dramatically, effectively terminating the etching process without further substrate consumption. In this sense, CVE is best described as a self‐terminating, tarnish‐limited etching process, rather than a self‐limiting one in the classical ALE sense.

Based on this mechanistic framework, we developed a CVE strategy using hexafluoroacetylacetone (Hhfac) as a selective volatilization agent. At moderate temperatures, that is, around 150°C (or higher than it), Hhfac reacts preferentially with copper‐ and bronze‐based corrosion products to form volatile metal–β‐diketonate complexes that are removed in the vapor phase, thereby exposing a clean and chemically stable underlying surface suitable for subsequent conservation or protection strategies.

Materials and Methods

Material Sources

High‐purity copper (99.995%), tin (99.995%), extra‐pure lead granules for ternary bronze fabrication were purchased from Jinou Materials Co., Ltd (China). Copper sheets (0.25 mm, ≥99.98%) were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). Analytical‐grade NaCl, NaHCO3, Na2SO4 were supplied by Thermo Scientific (USA). A seawater analogue was sourced from NATURITAS (Spain). The etchant precursor hexafluoroacetylacetone (Hhfac, 1,1,1,5,5,5‐Hexafluoro‐2,4‐pentanodiona, 98+%) was purchased from Thermo Scientific (USA). The selection of Hhfac as the etchant precursor was based on its high volatility, controllable reactivity toward copper‐based corrosion products, and comparatively low tendency to generate persistent surface residues [34, 38, 40]. These characteristics enable selective and well‐controlled vapor‐phase etching processes.

The main equipment used for fabrication and processing included a graphite crucible, a muffle furnace (KSL1400X, Zhengzhou Kejing, China), a cylindrical clay mold with a recessed groove (ø 20 mm), and a diamond wire saw (Shenyang Kejing Automation Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Sample preparation was carried out using a polishing machine (BOSCH PEX 220 A, Germany) equipped with 1000# sandpaper, followed by cleaning in an ultrasonic bath (Elma, Germany). Etching reactions were conducted in a Savannah S100 atomic layer deposition (ALD) system (Cambridge Nanotech. Inc., USA).

Sample Preparation

A ternary bronze alloy was fabricated in the laboratory to simulate commonly used historical copper‐based material. Copper, tin, and lead granules or powders were weighed according to a mass ratio of Cu:Sn:Pb = 89:10:1 and melted in a crucible at 1200°C in a muffle furnace, followed by a holding period of 30 min. The molten alloy was then cast into preheated (200°C) clay molds, which were embedded in moist loose soil. After natural cooling, the solidified alloy was cut into thin sheets with a thickness of approximately 0.7 mm using a diamond wire saw to serve as experimental samples. For comparison, commercially available pure copper sheets (0.5 mm thick) were cut into 8 mm*8 mm squares.

The corrosion solution was prepared by mixing artificial seawater with a compound electrolyte (0.028 mol/L NaCl, 0.01 mol/L Na2SO4, and 0.016 mol/L NaHCO3) in a volume ratio of 1:1. All samples were polished with 1000‐grit sandpaper, ultrasonically cleaned with absolute ethanol, and immersed in the corrosion solution for two weeks to develop a representative corrosion layer. Etching Experiments

Reaction Parameters

Etching reactions were performed in a Savannah ALD system (Cambridge Nanotech, USA). The liquid precursor Hhfac was delivered into the reaction chamber in vapor form under a system pressure of 0.26–0.29 mbar, using N2 as both carrier and purge gas. The reaction temperature was set to 150°C based on two considerations: First, Hhfac exhibits sufficient vapor pressure and thermal stability at this temperature to ensure efficient gas‐phase transport without condensation. Second, previous studies have shown that ligand‐assisted volatilization of copper oxides via Hhfac proceeds effectively near 150°C, whereas significant interaction with compact metallic Cu or Cu2O surfaces typically requires higher temperatures (>200°C) [41, 42]. Operating at 150°C therefore provides adequate activation for corrosion‐phase conversion while minimizing the risk of substrate modification.

Each etching cycle comprised three steps: (1) precursor pulsing (0.015 sec), (2) reaction hold (wait 1, variable), and (3) purging (wait 2, 20 sec). The short pulse duration (0.015 s) was selected to introduce a controlled dose of precursor sufficient to achieve surface exposure under low‐pressure conditions while avoiding excessive gas‐phase accumulation or condensation. Under these conditions, the process remains reaction‐limited rather than mass‐transport‐limited. The reaction hold time allowed surface conversion of reactive corrosion species, and the subsequent purge ensures removal of volatile metal‐β‐diketonate products and unreacted precursor.

Experimental Procedure and Design Strategy

The reaction hold time (wait 1) was systematically varied (10, 20, and 30 s) to determine the optimal exposure duration for corrosion removal. The selection criterion was based on comparative evaluation of surface morphology and corrosion‐phase removal efficiency, as assessed by optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Based on this analysis, a hold time of 20 s was identified as the optimal condition and was therefore adopted for subsequent experiments. Detailed morphological comparisons and justifications of this selection are presented in Section 3.1.

Polished bronze alloy substrates (prepared using 1000# sandpaper) were ultrasonically cleaned for 10 min and subsequently treated under the same conditions to evaluate the influence of CVE on substrate morphology and structural integrity.

Characterization and Testing Methods

The surface morphology of the samples before and after treatment was analyzed using an optical microscope (OM, Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH DM4000M, Germany) and an environmental scanning‐electron microscope (ESEM‐FEI Quanta 250, USA). Phase composition was analyzed by x‐ray diffraction (Malvern Panalytical B.V., Almelo, The Netherlands) over a range of 10°‐85°, with an accelerating voltage of 45 kV, and a current of 40 mA. Surface chemical states and structural changes were characterized by x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific Nexsa, USA) equipped with a monochromatic AI Kα source (1486.6 eV), and by Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia Confocal, UK) with 532 nm laser excitation source. The elemental composition of the corrosion products was further analyzed by energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) integrated into the SEM system (ESEM‐FEI Quanta 250, USA).

Results and Discussion of Corrosion Removal

Preliminary Determination of Effective Reaction Parameters

In the CVE process, reaction time is a critical parameter governing the extent of interaction between the precursor and surface‐active sites, thereby affecting both etching efficiency and byproduct formation. To establish suitable reaction conditions, the hold (reaction) time (wait 1) was systematically varied while keeping all other parameters constant (150°C, precursor pulse time of 0.015 s, purge time of 10 s). Reaction times of 10, 20, and 30 s were investigated, and the resulting surface morphologies of corroded bronze samples were examined by OM and SEM, as summarized in Figure 3.

Surface morphology of corroded bronze subjected to different CVE exposure times. (a–c) Optical microscopy (OM) images and (d–f) corresponding SEM images: (a,d) before etching, (b,e) after 10 s per cycle, and (c,f) after 20 s per cycle. Green arrows highlight representative corrosion regions. Insets in (a–c) show macroscopic photographs of the corresponding samples. Insets (c1) and (c2) compare the surface before and after solvent cleaning, illustrating removal of loosely adhered residues. The inset in (e) provides a higher‐magnification view of the etched surface. The 30 s per cycle condition is omitted, as its morphology was comparable to that observed at 20 s per cycle.

Prior to etching, the corroded bronze surface exhibited a characteristic bluish‐green appearance (Figure 3a), commonly associated with basic copper hydroxycarbonates such as azurite or malachite, together with scattered dark regions attributed to copper oxides. SEM imaging (Figure 3d) revealed a highly roughened surface composed of densely accumulated columnar, flake‐line, and powdery corrosion products, indicative of a heterogeneous and disordered corrosion layer. After etching for 10 s (200 cycles), a pronounced change in surface appearance was observed (Figure 3b). The bluish‐green corrosion layer was largely removed, while abundant white deposits appeared on the surface. SEM imaging (Figure 3e) showed a substantially smoother morphology with residual block‐like features surrounded by fine particulate matter. A magnified view (inset in Figure 3e) revealed that these deposits consist of loosely packed, irregular nano‐microparticles forming a continuous but weakly adhered layer. Extending the etching time to 20 s per cycle resulted in further evolution of the surface morphology (Figure 3c). The original corrosion products were almost eliminated, although some white byproducts remained locally (Figure 3c1). Importantly, these residues were readily removed by gentle rinsing with water, as shown in Figure 3c2. After removal of the corrosion layer, the surface exhibited a uniform reddish‐brown coloration under OM, consistent with exposure of a cuprous oxide (Cu2O) layer. SEM imaging (Figure 3f) confirmed a smooth and homogeneous surface, indicating effective corrosion removal.

Although a reaction time of 30 s per cycle was also tested, no further improvement in surface morphology was observed compared with the 20s condition. Both treatments produced comparable surface uniformity and residual features, indicating that prolonged reaction times do not lead to additional benefits and may unnecessarily increase substrate exposure. Therefore, 20 s was selected as the optimal reaction hold time for the investigated corrosion system. It should be noted that this optimal parameter is intrinsically linked to the characteristics of the tarnish, including its thickness, chemical composition, and degree of aging. These factors influence the kinetics of corrosion product removal and, consequently, the optimal etching duration.

Assessment of Substrate Integrity

Because the tarnish is inherently heterogeneous while the precursor uniformly covers the entire sample surface during reaction, it was essential to evaluate whether the CVE process affects the underlying alloy substrate. Given that Cu is the major constituent of bronze, pure copper samples were examined alongside bronze to disentangle potential alloy‐related effects. As shown in Figure 4a, the bronze surface exhibited uniformly distributed granular protrusions, attributed to second‐phase particles precipitated during solid solution treatment, mainly Sn‐Pb enriched regions. After etching, sporadically distributed particulate aggregates loosely adhered to the surface (Figure 4b and its inset). These deposits were easily removed by water rinsing, after which the original morphology was largely restored (Figure 4c). No pitting, grain‐boundary attack, or surface roughening, indicative of substrate degradation, was observed. Similar behavior was found for pure copper, which displayed clear polishing lines prior to reaction (Figure 4d) and particulate deposits after etching (Figure 4e and its inset), both removable by rinsing. The physical properties of these residues are consistent with the deposits discussed in Section 3.1 and are attributed to reaction‐by‐products rather than substrate modification.

Surface morphology (SEM) of bronze substrate: (a) before, (b) after reaction, and (c) after washing; SEM images of copper: (d) before, (e) after reaction; (f) Comparison of XRD patterns of bronze and copper. Insets show magnified views of selected regions.

The corresponding XRD patterns (Figure 4f) further corroborate these observations. Both bronze and copper samples exhibit consistent peak positions before and after treatment, indicating that no new crystalline phases were introduced during CVE. No peak shifts or additional reflections associated with new oxide, intermetallic, or decomposition phases were detected. The slight reduction in peak intensity observed after etching can be attributed to the presence of surface residues or minor preferred‐orientation effects rather than structural damage. The broad envelope feature (dashed box) appearing after treatment is indicative of amorphous species, most likely originating from precursor‐derived organic or metal‐organic by‐products formed during ligand‐assisted volatilization. This amorphous contribution is consistent with the loosely adhered white residues observed in SEM (Figure 4b,e), suggesting that both phenomena arise from reaction by‐products rather than from structural modification of the alloy substrate. These residues differ fundamentally from the more abundant corrosion‐related deposits discussed in Figure 3c,d, as they are removable by simple rinsing and do not correspond to crystalline corrosion phases. Importantly, extended exposure under the investigated conditions (e.g., 30 s compared to 20 s) did not produce measurable additional morphological changes or detectable reduction in substrate crystallinity, indicating that the clean alloy exhibits a negligible etching rate at 150°C.

Previous studies have shown that hexafluoro acetylacetone (Hhfac) primarily undergoes adsorption and ligand exchange reactions on oxidized copper surfaces, with Cu(hfac)2 formation reported around 425 K (∼152°C) [43]. However, significant direct interaction with compact metallic Cu or stable Cu2O layers generally requires temperatures above 200°C, with more pronounced decomposition and substrate reaction occurring near 300°C. At the applied reaction temperature of 150°C, the native Cu/Cu2O interface is thermodynamically stable and kinetically less reactive toward Hhfac. These findings confirm that CVE selectively removes corrosion products while preserving the underlying Cu‐Sn‐Pb alloy. The reaction effectively halts once the reactive tarnish layer is depleted, supporting a tarnish‐limited, self‐terminating behavior rather than continuous substrate etching. Such selectivity is particularly advantageous for bronze artefacts exhibiting corrosion and localized substrate exposure, as it enables precise corrosion removal while minimizing impact on the original material.

Phase and Composition Changes on the Corrosion Surface

After confirming that the substrate integrity was preserved, the effect of CVE on the corrosion layers was systematically investigated. Phase composition and structural evolution were evaluated by combining x‐ray diffraction (XRD) and vibrational spectroscopy (Raman and FTIR) on the bronze surface before and after treatment (Figure 5). Figure 5a provides a schematic representation of the proposed reaction pathway, in which chemically active corrosion products undergo ligand‐assisted reactions with Hhfac, leading to their removal, while more stable oxide phases remain on the surface.

(a) Schematic illustration of the proposed ligand‐assisted CVE mechanism, showing selective reaction of Hhfac with chemically active corrosion products and the retention of a residual oxide layer. (b) XRD patterns of bronze and copper surfaces before and after CVE, showing the attenuation of corrosion‐related phases and the predominance of Cu2O after treatment. (c) Raman spectra of bronze surfaces before and after CVE, highlighting the suppression of chloride‐and carbonate‐related features and the emergence of new bands attributed to metal‐organic reaction intermediates; Inset: enlarged low‐frequency Raman region (around 150–300 cm−1) comparing spectra acquired after various reactions cycle numbers. (d) FTIR spectra of bronze surfaces before and after CVE, showing the removal or carbonate‐ and hydroxy‐containing corrosion products and residual vibrational features associated with surface reaction by‐products.

As shown in Figure 5b, XRD patterns indicated that simulated corrosion layers formed on bronze and copper consist of phases typical of humid or marine environments, including oxides, chlorides, and carbonates [44, 45, 46]. The corrosion layer on bronze is structurally more complex than that on pure copper, as evidenced by peak broadening, small peak shifts, and reduced intensities of Cu‐related reflections. These features are consistent with the presence of mixed corrosion products and second‐phase oxides with alloying elements [47, 48, 49]. Following CVE (Figure 5b, red curve), diffraction peaks associated with basic corrosion products, such as hydroxycarbonates, and chlorides, are strongly attenuated, together with a partial reduction of CuO‐and SnO2‐related signals. The remaining diffraction features are dominated by Cu2O, with only minor contributions from CuO, indicating that CVE selectively removes reactive corrosion phases while leaving a residual cuprous oxide layer. A similar trend was observed for pure copper, where weak metallic Cu reflections at ∼50.4° (Cu (200)) and ∼74.1° (Cu (220)) remain detectable after treatment. This observation reflects partial removal of the corrosion layer and exposure of an underlying Cu2O‐rich interface, rather than complete stripping to metallic copper. Such residual metallic diffraction signals are commonly reported for non‐uniform corrosion layers, where incomplete surface coverage and local variations in corrosion thickness allow diffraction from the underlying metal substrate, particularly on rough or heterogeneous surfaces [50, 51, 52, 53]. Notably, the treated XRD patterns (Figure 5b, red and green curves) exhibit a weakly arched background. In analogy with the amorphous envelope observed after CVE on pure metal surfaces (Figure 4f), this feature is attributed to residual organic or metal‐organic species formed during ligand‐ reactions between Hhfac and corrosion products. The broader and more gradual character of this background suggests contributions from a distribution of surface‐bound reaction intermediates rather than crystalline corrosion phases.

Raman spectra (Figure 5c) provide further insight into the chemical selectivity of CVE. Before etching, the bronze corrosion layer exhibits multiple low‐frequency bands in the 150–250 cm− 1 range, which are characteristic of chlorine‐containing corrosion products, including Cu2(OH)3Cl [54, 55]. After CVE, these bands were largely eliminated, demonstrating efficient suppression and removal of chloride‐bearing phases. A broad Raman feature spanning approximately 186–230 cm−1 is observed before treatment and can be assigned to overlapping Cu─O vibrational modes from basic copper carbonates (CuCO3(OH)2) and cuprous oxide (Cu2O) [56]. After CVE, this band is significantly weakened but did not completely disappear entirely, consistent with preferential removal of basic carbonates while retaining a Cu2O contribution. Comparison of spectra acquired after different reaction cycle numbers (inset) shows that the low‐frequency feature near ∼200 cm−1 remains centered at a similar position but exhibits progressive broadening with increasing cycle number. Such broadening has been reported for nanoscale or structurally disordered copper oxides, where phonon confinement and disorder effects lead to broadened, pseudo‐Voigt‐type line shapes. Accordingly, contributions from structurally disordered or nanoscale Cu‐O species cannot be excluded [57, 58, 59]. Concomitantly, the relative intensity of Cu2O‐related peak at ∼233 cm−1 increases after CVE, indicating progressive exposure of a cuprous oxide layer following the removal of outer corrosion products. Raman bands associated with sulfate‐, carbonate‐, and oxalate‐containing corrosion phases (≈330, 770, 1055, 1071, and ∼1200 cm−1) [60, 61, 62] are markedly attenuated or disappear after treatment, confirming the efficient removal of chemically active corrosion components. In contrast, new Raman bands appearing near ∼410, ∼450, and ∼639 cm−1 were detected after CVE. These features are consistent with disordered metal‐organic species and are tentatively assigned to Cu‐β‐diketonate complexes, such as Cu(hfac)2, or related reaction intermediates. Additionally, an increased background signal in the 1200–1800 cm−1 region indicates fluorescence contributions from residual organic or fluorinated by [63, 64]. Together, these spectral features provide evidence that CVE proceeds through ligand‐assisted reactions that generate transient metal‐organic species during corrosion removal.

FTIR spectra of the bronze samples before and after CVE treatment (Figure 5d) were compared following the XRD and Raman analyses. Compared with the untreated surface, the treated sample exhibits a pronounced reduction or complete disappearance of absorption bands in the 1200–1500 cm− 1 region, which is commonly associated with vibrational modes of copper carbonate corrosion products (Figure 5d). This result confirms the effective removal of carbonate‐based corrosion phases by CVE. In addition, the broad absorption band in the 2690–3689 cm− 1 range, corresponding to O─H stretching vibrations of structural water and hydroxyl groups, is significantly weakened after treatment (Figure 5d), indicating efficient elimination of hydrated corrosion phases and adsorbed moisture.

In the low‐wavenumber region, absorption features at approximately 697 and 757 cm− 1 are strongly attenuated after CVE (Figure 5d). These bands have been reported for chloride‐related corrosion products and metal–oxygen vibrations [65], and their suppression further demonstrates the selective removal of chemically active corrosion components from the bronze surface. In contrast, the treated sample exhibits enhanced absorption near ∼1083 cm− 1, together with the appearance of new features or peak shifts around ∼1257 and ∼1531 cm− 1 (Figure 5d). These spectral changes are attributed to residual species introduced during the CVE process. According to previous studies, absorptions in this region may originate from C─F or C─CF stretching vibrations, C─O‐related functional groups, or intermediate metal‐organic complexes formed through interactions between the metal surface and organic ligands [66, 67, 68]. Due to spectral overlap and the inherent complexity of surface chemistry, these assignments remain tentative and require further verification using complementary surface‐sensitive techniques.

To evaluate the environmental stability of the treated surface, FTIR spectra were re‐collected after five months of exposure to ambient air (Figure S1). The spectra remain largely unchanged, without emergence of new absorption bands. Only a slight increase in intensity near ∼1115 cm− 1 is observed, which may be associated with the slow formation of stable oxide species under atmospheric conditions. Importantly, no reappearance of low‐wavenumber features associated with chloride‐related corrosion products is detected, indicating that the CVE‐treated surface does not undergo significant reactivation of aggressive corrosion phases during long‐term air exposure.

Taken together, the combined XRD, Raman, and FTIR analyses demonstrate that CVE effectively removes corrosion products from the bronze surface, with high selectivity toward aggressive species such as chlorinated salts and carbonate‐based phases. At the same time, the retention of stable oxide phases, particularly Cu2O, is expected to contribute to surface passivation. These results highlight the efficacy and chemical selectivity of the CVE approach.

Comparative Elemental Analysis of the Corroded Area

To further verify the removal of corrosion products, EDS mapping and spectra were performed (Figure 6). Before etching (Figure 6a), the surface exhibited strong enrichment in C, O, Cl, and S, with uneven distribution and localized accumulation, resulting in a rough surface morphology (inset in Figure 6a). After etching (Figure 6b), the elemental composition was dominated by O and Cu, with trace Cl, while sulfur was no longer detected. The elemental distribution was more homogeneous, and SEM imaging confirmed a smoother surface (inset of Figure 6b). Profilometry revealed a decrease in average roughness from 23.32 to 10.99 µm, consistent with the removal of corrosion deposits and improved uniformity.

Elemental distribution maps and corresponding EDS spectra of the corroded bronze surface (a,c) before and (b,d) after CVE treatment, illustrating the reduction of corrosion‐related elements following etching.

A semi‐quantitative EDS analysis, based on standardless quantification with built‐in matrix correction (Figure 6c,d), further supports these observations. The relative carbon content decreased to ∼1%, consistent with the removal of carbonate species; oxygen decreased by more than 50%, reflecting the reduction of oxide and hydroxide phases; and chlorine decreased by over 50%, indicating effective removal of chloride‐related corrosion products by CVE. Owing to the intrinsic limitations of EDS, including interaction volume and matrix effects, the reported atomic percentages are considered semi‐quantitative and are intended for comparative evaluation under identical acquisition conditions rather than absolute compositional determination. The compositional trends obtained by EDS are in good agreement with XRD and Raman analyses, collectively corroborating the selective corrosion removal achieved by CVE.

Evaluation of Surface Valence State and Structural Changes

To further elucidate, the chemical mechanisms underlying the CVE process, x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to probe surface valence states, ligand coordination, and residual reaction by‐products. Due to its high surface sensitivity and quantitative capability, XPS enables direct assessment of changes in metal oxidation states and the presence of organic or fluorinated species resulting from ligand‐assisted reactions [69]. Combined analysis of C 1s, O 1s, Cu 2p, and Sn 3d core levels provided insight into the selective removal of corrosion products, the formation of transient metal‐β‐diketonate species, and the chemical nature of the residual surface layer following CVE treatment (Figure 7).

High‐resolution XPS spectra of the bronze surface before and after CVE treatment: (a,a1) C 1s, (b,b1) O 1s, and (c,c1) Cu 2p regions, showing changes in surface chemical states and ligand‐derived species. Depth profile of selected elements upon gradual Ar+ sputtering (d) before CVE, (d1) after CVE, and (d2) after CVE, followed by 9 days of ambient air exposure, illustrating the selective removal of corrosion products and the compositional stability of the residual surface layer.

The C 1s spectra of the bronze surface before and after CVE are shown in Figure 7a,a1 . Prior to treatment, the surface exhibited components corresponding to C─C/─CH (284.8 eV), C─O─C/C─OH (286.9 eV), carbonyl/carboxyl (C═O/O─C ═O, 289.2 eV), and carbonate species (∼290.8 eV) [70, 71]. After CVE, the carbonate‐related peak disappeared completely, whereas the carboxyl/carbonyl contribution decreased by approximately 75% in atomic percentage and shifted to 288.5 eV. This residual signal is primarily attributed to C═O functional groups associated with remaining organic species. However, the pronounced C‐F vibrational features observed in the Raman spectra (Figure 5b), together with the detection of fluorine in XPS depth profiles (Figure 7d1, d2), indicate that this binding energy range may also include contributions from C─F [72, 73]. Accordingly, the signal at 288.5 eV is most likely dominated by C═O groups from residual organics, with additional contributions from fluorinated species introduced by Hhfac. These changes confirm the effective removal of carbonate‐based corrosion products and support the presence of minor residual ligand‐derived species after CVE.

The O 1s spectra before and after treatment are shown in Figure 7b and Figure b1 ). In the untreated sample, peak A at 530.68 eV is assigned to metal oxides (e.g., CuO/Cu2O) [74], whereas peak B at 531.83 eV corresponds to hydroxides and organic oxygen species (e.g., ─OH and O─C═O) [75, 76, 77]. Peak C at 535.42 eV originates from Na Auger interference [77, 78]. After CVE, the relative intensity of peak A decreased significantly (from 70.31% to 12.23%), indicating substantial removal of oxide‐rich corrosion phases. Concurrently, peak B shifts to higher binding energy (532.6 eV), consistent with oxygen coordinated to organometallic ligands or other oxygen‐containing organic species [79, 80]. The presence of residual ligand‐derived species after CVE correlates with the increase in water contact angle observed for the treated surfaces (Figure S3), indicating a modification of the surface energy. Such changes are consistent with the incorporation of fluorinated or organic functional groups, which are known to reduce surface free energy and alter wettability [81]. A decrease in surface energy may influence the adsorption thermodynamics of water and dissolved oxygen at the metal‐environment interface. However, the relationship between these surface‐chemical modifications and long‐term corrosion kinetics has not been quantitatively established in the present study. Therefore, any potential contribution of residual organometallic species to corrosion suppression should be regarded as a working hypothesis that requires systematic electrochemical and aging studies for validation.

The Cu 2p spectra (Figure 7c,c1) display characteristic features of copper in multiple oxidation states. Peak A at 932.99 eV corresponds to metallic copper (Cu0) or cuprous oxide (Cu+), whereas peak B at 934.91 eV corresponds to divalent copper species, such as CuO or Cu(OH)2. The associated satellite peaks (C‐E) are characteristics fingerprints of Cu2+ species [82, 83, 84, 85]. After CVE the intensity of Cu2+‐related peaks (including satellite peaks) decreased markedly, indicating substantial removal of Cu2+ corrosion products and exposure of the underlying Cu0/Cu+ states. Nevertheless, a small fraction of Cu2+ signals remains (atomic percentage ∼6.5%), suggesting that the treated surface is not composed exclusively of metallic or cuprous species. A slight positive shift in Cu2+ binding energy was also observed, which is typically associated with changes in the local coordination environment of copper and is more consistent with ligand coordination than with a simple inorganic oxide [86, 87, 88, 89].

To assess the interaction of Hhfac with non‐copper components in the corrosion layer, Sn 3d spectra were analyzed (Figure S2). Prior to treatment, the Sn 3d region is strongly affected by Na Auger signal, and only a weak Sn─O component at approximately 486.6 eV can be discerned, indicating that Sn is predominantly present in an oxidized state within the corrosion layer. Due to the low signal intensity and spectral overlap, reliable deconvolution was not feasible before treatment. After CVE, pronounced changes are observed: the Na Auger signal decreased and changed in line shape, while the Sn 3d signal became more distinguishable, revealing a new component at around 487.0 eV. This binding energy is higher than that typically reported for Sn─O species and can be reasonably attributed to fluorine‐coordinated Sn species (e.g., Sn─O─F or Sn‐Fx), consistent with the transient formation of Sn(hfac)x intermediates during CVE, as reported for β‐diketonate coordination with Sn species in previous studies [90, 91]. These observations indicate that Hhfac interacts not only with copper‐based corrosion products but also modifies the chemical environment of Sn‐contaning phases.

Overall, when considered together with the emergence of new Raman bands at approximately 410, 450, and 630 cm− 1 after CVE (Figure 5c), the subtle preferred‐orientation changes observed by XRD (Figure 5b), and the structural modifications revealed by FTIR (Figure 5d), the XPS results provide converging evidence for the presence of residual metal‐organic ligand‐derived species or transient reaction intermediates on the treated surface, rather than the formation of new stable bulk phases. These findings are consistent with a ligand‐assisted etching mechanism in which chemically active corrosion products are selectively transformed and removed, while minor surface residues remain.

Depth profiles of selected elements were obtained upon Ar+ sputtering of bronze samples before CVE, after CVE, and after 9 days of exposure to air (Figure 7d–d2 ). In the untreated sample (Figure 7d), the surface Cu concentration is initially below 10% and gradually increases with sputtering, reaching approximately 60% at the maximum analyzed depth (∼6.23 µm). The persistence of corrosion‐related elements throughout the whole depth profile indicates that the corrosion layer was not fully probed. In contrast, the CVE‐treated sample (Figure 7d1 ) exhibits an initial Cu concentration of ∼30%, which rapidly increases during sputtering and stabilizes above 90% at a depth of ∼3.2 µm. Concurrently, corrosion‐related elements decrease sharply to near‐zero levels, while the emergence of Sn confirms passing the complete corrosion layer and exposure of the bronze alloy substrate beneath. Minor surface residues remain, reflecting incomplete removal of all reaction by‐products. Consequently, elemental concentrations measured during sputtering may include contributions from such residues or secondary environmental exposure, which should be considered when interpreting near‐surface compositions.

To assess post‐etching stability, CVE‐treated samples were exposed to ambient air (20°C–25°C, 45%–60% relative humidity) for 9 days and re‐analyzed at multiple positions (Figure 7d2). The resulting depth profiles remain largely unchanged compared to those obtained immediately after treatment, supporting the effective removal of chemically active species (e.g., Cl─ and S─ containing ions) and the persistence of a chemically stable surface state under ambient conditions within the investigated scale.

To further evaluate the surface stability following CVE, complementary tests were conducted under both long‐term air exposure and short‐term humid conditions. As shown in Figure S4, bronze samples with different initial corrosion durations (2 days, 2 weeks, and 2 months) were subjected to CVE and subsequently exposed to ambient air for five months. In contrast to untreated samples, the treated surfaces do not exhibit further development of visible corrosion products or surface attack, indicating enhanced stability under dry atmospheric conditions. The white deposits observed after a large number of etching cycles originate from residual surface species rather than newly formed corrosion products.

The behaviour of CVE‐treated surfaces under humid conditions was examined through short‐term wet–dry cycling immersion in seawater (composition detailed in Section 2.2). Comparative OM, SEM, and FTIR analyses were performed on bronze samples corroded for three months and their CVE‐treated counterparts after five months of air exposure (Figure S5). Following immersion, both sample sets exhibit surface deposits primarily attributed to NaCl accumulation, as confirmed by EDS analysis (Figure S5a–e). Correspondingly, FTIR spectra showed noticeable changes (Figure S5f,g). For the CVE‐treated samples, organic‐related vibrational features observed prior to immersion disappeared after soaking, while no new distinct infrared‐active bands emerge. This behaviour is consistent with the removal or dissolution of residual surface species during immersion, accompanied by the formation of inorganic corrosion products such as copper oxides, which typically exhibit weak infrared responses under ATR‐FTIR measurement conditions.

Furthermore, a similar analytical approach applied to corroded copper surfaces revealing peak‐fitting trends broadly consisting of those observed for bronze samples (Figure S6). However, the evolution of the C 1s spectra under identical CVE conditions differs between the two substrates. While the carbonate‐related component disappears in both cases, the remaining C 1s components on copper shift toward higher binding energies (e.g., from ∼286.1 and 288.5 eV to ∼286.7 and 289.2 eV), accompanied by the emergence of pronounced C─O─C─F and CF‐ related components (Figure S6c1). This behaviour indicates a higher degree of fluorinated carbon species on copper surfaces, likely reflecting the simpler chemical environment of pure Cu compared to bronze and the absence of compelling alloy‐related interactions. The strong electron‐withdrawing character of fluorinated groups accounts for the observed binding energy shifts. Depth profiling further highlights the contrast between the two systems (Figure S7). In untreated copper, the Cu atomic percentage remains around 93% even after sputtering to depths of ∼6.23 µm, indicating incomplete removal of the tarnish layer. In contrast, the CVE‐treated copper surface reaches a stabilized Cu content close to 100% at a depth of ∼2.2 µm, with other elements nearly eliminated, confirming rapid and efficient removal of corrosion products.

Overall, the markedly reduced concentrations of corrosion‐related elements, the shallower detection of the bulk substrate, and the observed stability under ambient exposure collectively demonstrate the high efficiency and chemical stability of the CVE process in removing corrosion layers, while maintaining substrate integrity and short‐term environmental stability. However, the limited stability observed under humid or aqueous environments indicates that CVE should be regarded as a selective corrosion‐removal and surface‐stabilization step rather than a permanent protective barrier under aggressive conditions, providing a controlled substrate for subsequent conservation coating strategies.

Conclusions and Outlook

This study establishes Chemical Vapor Etching (CVE) as a chemically selective, vapor‐phase strategy for controlled removal of tarnish from bronze surfaces using hexafluoroacetylacetone (Hhfac) as the etchant. By systematically varying temperature, reaction duration, and cycle number, we demonstrate that CVE preferentially targets chemically active corrosion products while preserving the structural integrity of the underlying Cu‐Sn alloy. Under the investigated conditions, the process exhibits a tarnish‐limited, self‐terminating behavior: Etching proceeds through reactive corrosion phases and becomes strongly suppressed once the stable near‐substrate Cu/Cu2O interface is reached. SEM, XRD, and extended exposure experiments collectively indicate negligible modification of the clean alloy substrate, confirming that the etching rate of the metallic phase is minimal at 150°C.

CVE effectively removes aggressive constituents of bronze corrosion layers, including chloride‐and sulfur‐containing phases, together with carbonate‐ and oxide‐based corrosion products. Across complementary characterization techniques (XRD, Raman, FTIR, EDS, and XPS), these transformations are reflected by the attenuation or disappearance of signatures associated with basic copper salts and reactive anions, accompanied by a marked reduction in corrosion‐related elements. Importantly, a compact cuprous oxide (Cu2O) layer is retained at the substrate interface. Owing to its comparatively low reactivity and solubility, this Cu2O layer acts as a passivating interfacial phase that limits oxygen and ion transport and suppresses corrosion reactivation in air within the timescales examined.

Beyond chemical selectivity, CVE benefits from its vapor‐phase, non‐line‐of‐sight character. The use of gaseous ligand and volatile reaction products enables access to recessed and occluded geometries that are difficult to treat using conventional mechanical or liquid‐based approaches, making the method particularly attractive for complex and heterogeneous corrosion systems. This capability is particularly relevant for the conservation of complex cultural heritage objects, where localized corrosion, heterogeneous multilayer structures, and restricted accessibility pose critical challenges.

It is important to clarify that the term “clean,” as used in this study, refers to the effective removal of chemically unstable and corrosion‐active phases rather than the complete absence of all analytically detectable surface residues. Under the investigated conditions, trace ligand‐derived species may remain at the topmost surface. Nevertheless, CVE‐treated samples exhibit negligible alteration of the underlying substrate and demonstrate enhanced stability during prolonged exposure to ambient air compared with untreated controls. In contrast, the behavior observed under humid or aqueous environments indicates that CVE should be regarded primarily as a selective corrosion‐removal and surface‐stabilization step, rather than a standalone long‐term corrosion barrier.

Future work will focus on (i) elucidating and controlling intermediate reaction byproducts (e.g., white residues) through parameter optimization (e.g., reaction temperature, residence time) and post‐treatment protocols, (ii) establishing robust and practical endpoint criteria, potentially integrating in situ monitoring approaches such as mass spectrometric detection of volatile metal‐β‐diketonate species, optical emission analysis, or real‐time spectroscopic ellipsometry and etching kinetics, and (iii) integrating compatible protective coatings to extend durability. Systematic studies addressing the influence of tarnish thickness, composition, and aging state on etching behavior, together with accelerated aging and electrochemical evaluation following protective layer deposition, will be essential to translate CVE into reproducible and conservation‐relevant treatment workflows.
