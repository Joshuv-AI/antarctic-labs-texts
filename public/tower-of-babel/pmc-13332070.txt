Meteorin-like protein may be a mediator of exercise-related reproductive disorders in female rats
Authors: Nazife Ulker Ertugrul, Feyza Keskin Buyukbudak, Nurcan Delice, Ebru Gokdere, Meryem Sedef Dogru, Tugrul Ertugrul, Serife Tutuncu, Sinan Canpolat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExercise provides significant benefits for most women, but adverse effects on reproduction can and do occur. Increased frequency, duration, and intensity of exercise are associated with a higher risk of female infertility. In particular, exercise-induced reproductive dysfunction is linked to alterations in the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis. However, the role of meteorin-like protein (metrnl), as an exercise-induced myokine, in female reproductive function and the HPG axis is not yet fully understood. In this study, we investigated the effects of metrnl, compared with a swimming exercise protocol, on sexual motivation and the HPG axis in female rats. Female rats with regular estrous cycles were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 9 per group), and subjected to deionized water (1 mL/kg), metrnl (1 µg/day) or swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise (30 min/day, 5 days/week) for about 19 consecutive days. The results showed that metrnl administration reduced the time spent near the male rat and the male preference ratio, indicators of sexual motivation. It also decreased hypothalamic GnRH expression (in the median eminence and preoptic area), serum concentrations of reproductive hormones (follicle-stimulating hormone and anti-Müllerian hormone), and the numbers of ovarian follicles (primordial and primary follicles), similar to the effects observed after the swimming exercise protocol. Furthermore, metrnl concentrations in serum remained unchanged in female rats treated with metrnl and exercise. In conclusion, metrnl treatment reduced sexual incentive motivation and altered HPG axis regulation in female rats. These findings suggest that exercise-induced metrnl may be a potential exerkine associated with changes in female sexual motivation and HPG axis regulation due to exercise.Keywords: Meteorin-like protein (metrnl), Moderate-intensity exercise, Reproduction, Sexual incentive motivation, HPG axis, GnRHSubject terms: Endocrinology, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Over the last three decades, the number of women participating in exercise—from general physical activity to elite sports—has increased more rapidly than that of men1. While exercise is known to have substantial health benefits, to date, numerous studies have examined the relationship between exercise and fertility, yet the results remain debatable and unclear2,3. For example, some studies have shown that greater hours of vigorous or intense exercise reduce the likelihood of ovulation, while others have reported negative effects of excessive or prolonged exercise on pregnancy rates. Additionally, increasing the frequency, duration, or intensity of physical activity under high training loads has been associated with decreased fertility in women, regardless of body mass index2,4,5.

A healthy reproductive cycle is a complex process involving the coordinated interaction of neurotransmitter systems, hypothalamic releasing factors, anterior pituitary hormones, gonadal sex steroid hormones, and various growth factors6. The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG, also referred to as the GnRH-gonadotropins-steroids axis) has essential and multifaceted effects on the female reproductive system. The HPG axis controls the estrous cycle by coordinating fluctuations in GnRH, pituitary gonadotropins, and ovarian steroid hormones. Therefore, irregular estrous cycles can occur due to dysfunction at the level of the hypothalamus, pituitary, or ovaries7. Metabolic status (such as leptin, ghrelin, insulin, and adiponectin) and energy balance, key regulators of the HPG axis, play a crucial role in reproductive function. Alterations in energy balance resulting from changes in exercise can also impact the reproductive system, and a reduction in energy availability in female athletes can suppress the HPG axis8,9. Consequently, athletes are generally more susceptible to reproductive dysfunction than nonathletes10.

In female athletes, while moderate-intensity exercise is generally associated with beneficial effects on health, intense physical exercise, insufficient energy availability, or both can lead to adverse reproductive outcomes10,11. Exercise-associated reproductive dysfunction in women, which depends on exercise intensity, appears to be related to hypothalamic dysfunction and disruption of the gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse generator. Disrupted GnRH pulsation due to intensive exercise and/or lower energy availability can lead to low luteinizing hormone (LH) secretion, menstrual dysfunction, including luteal phase deficiency and anovulation, and ultimately infertility10. GnRH, synthesised in the median eminence (ME), is the central neuropeptide that controls the HPG axis of all vertebrates. In addition to this well-established neuroendocrine output, GnRH is also released specifically in the preoptic area (POA). The biological pattern of GnRH release is essential for maintaining normal reproductive function12,13.

The physiological mechanisms governing energy balance are interconnected with those regulating reproduction, indicating that exercise-induced changes in energy balance can influence reproductive processes14. Exercise-induced soluble factors, inducing adipokines, myokines, cytokines, and osteokines, play significant roles in the body’s response to exercise15. At this point, exercise-induced adipomyokines such as irisin and fibroblast growth factor 21—both involved in fat browning—have been shown to influence reproductive function in ways similar to exercise itself16,17. However, it remains unclear whether the novel adipo-myokine associated with exercise and energy metabolism, meteorin-like protein (metrnl), acts as an effective factor in modulating the reproductive axis. To fill this gap in the existing literature, our study aims to comparatively assess the effects of metrnl and exercise on the function of the female reproductive axis in rats.

Metrnl is produced in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue following exercise and cold exposure, respectively, and secreted into the bloodstream18. Metrnl is highly expressed in skeletal muscle after exercise, in activated monocytes, and adipose tissue, and in various organs such as the digestive tract, skin, lung, and brain19. Research indicates that metrnl is crucial for glucose homeostasis, thermogenesis, and adipose browning. Accordingly, increased metrnl expression in mice enhances energy expenditure, stimulates thermogenic gene expression, and increases the presence of beige adipocytes15,18,20. Given that metrnl can augment whole-body energy expenditure, it seems imperative to explore its potential influence on the neuroendocrine reproductive axis.

Considering the dynamics of exercise on the reproductive system, the present study aimed to determine whether metrnl triggers the HPG axis. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of metrnl administration on estrous cycle, sexual incentive motivation toward a partner, reproductive hormones, reproductive organs, and GnRH expression in the ME and POA of female rats, in comparison with a swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise. Our findings provide valuable insight into the role of metrnl in the female reproductive system and enhance understanding of how exercise influences mammalian reproductive function. Considering the clinical relevance of exercise-associated reproductive disorders in physically active women, clarifying the potential role of metrnl may further elucidate the neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying these conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

This study was approved by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Firat University (Elazig, Turkey) (Ethical Approval Number and Date: 2023/16 − 06 and 20.09.2023) and conducted in accordance with the governmental guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals at Firat University. Twenty seven female Sprague-Dawley rats with at least two consecutive regular estrous cycles (200–250 g; 2–3 months old) were obtained from the Firat University Experimental Research Center. The rats were kept under controlled conditions of humidity (50–55%) and temperature (21 ± 1 °C), with a 12:12 h light: dark cycle (lights on from 07:00–19:00). The rats were randomly divided into three groups: control, metrnl, and exercise (n = 9 per group). Animals were housed in groups of three per cage and were provided ad libitum access to standard food and tap water throughout the experimental period.

Metrnl administration

Rats in the metrnl group were intraperitoneally administered metrnl at a dose of 1 µg/day (CSB-EP719323RA, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China). The metrnl dose was determined based on the dose reported in a previous study21. Metrnl was dissolved in deionized water and administered in a volume of 1 mL/kg. Rats in the control group received an intraperitoneal injection of deionized water in the same volume. Metrnl and deionized water were administered daily for about 19 consecutive days. The duration of metrnl administration was designed to parallel the moderate-intensity swimming exercise protocol adapted from a previously reported study22. All injections were performed between 13:00 and 14:00 h.

Exercise training

In the present study, swimming was selected as a commonly used experimental model of physical exercise in rodents23. The rats in the exercise group underwent a swimming exercise protocol designed as a moderate-intensity paradigm adapted from previous studies22,24,25. Briefly, the rats swam in cylindrical tanks (25 cm diameter x 60 cm height) filled with tap water at 32 ± 1 °C. The water depth was adjusted so that the rats’ tails could not touch the bottom of the tanks. During the first 7 days prior to the swimming exercise, all animals were acclimated to water for 5 min daily between 13:00 and 15:00 h. After the adaptation period, the rats performed swimming exercise for 30 min per day, 5 days per week, for an average of 19 consecutive days. After each swimming session, rats were dried with towels and returned to their cages. All rats in the control and metrnl groups remained sedentary in their home cages throughout this period. During the swimming exercise and injections of deionized water or metrnl, daily body weights of rats in all experimental groups were monitored.

Estrous cycle evaluation

In female laboratory rodents, assessing the stage of the estrous cycle over several consecutive days enables short- and long-term monitoring of each animal’s cyclicity and reproductive health26. Therefore, since the average estrous cycle length in rats is 4–5 days27, we typically monitored 2–3 consecutive estrous cycles in our study. Between the 7th and 16th days of swimming exercise and injections of deionized water or metrnl (i.e. for 10 consecutive days–typically covers 2–3 consecutive estrous cycles), vaginal smears were collected daily between 15:00 and 16:00 h to assess the estrous cycle pattern (frequency). Vaginal smear samples were collected from the rat vaginal opening using a plastic pipette filled with 0.2 mL of physiological saline. The samples were then carefully placed on glass slides and immediately examined under a light microscope. The estrous cyclicity was monitored by microscopic examination of the predominant cell type in the vaginal smears. Accordingly, each stage of the estrous cycle was classified cytologically as follows: presence of leukocytes together with cornified and nucleated epithelial cells (metestrus); predominance of leukocytes (diestrus); predominance of nucleated epithelial cells (proestrus); and predominance of cornified epithelial cells (estrus)27. Since the cytology and hormone profiles of metestrus and diestrus are similar, both phases were combined together for analysis.

Sexual incentive motivation test and active investigation test

After estrous cycle monitoring was completed, vaginal smears were continued to be collected from all animals, and a 10-minute sexual motivation test was performed on all female rats determined to be in the estrus phase (the phase in which the female is sexually receptive to the male). To ensure cycle consistency, vaginal cytology samples were collected immediately prior to each behavioral test to verify the estrous stage on the day of testing. Accordingly, all sexual motivation tests were conducted between the 16th and 19th days of swimming exercise and injections of deionized water or metrnl, in a room equipped with a night vision camera system, under dim red light, between 17:00 and 19:00 h, during the reversed dark phase of the light-dark cycle. The testing procedures were modified from previous studies28–30.

Stimulus animals

In the sexual incentive motivation test and active investigation test, separate female Sprague-Dawley rats (200–250 g; 2–3 months old) were used as social stimuli, and male Sprague-Dawley rats (300–350 g; 3–4 months old; gonado-intact and sexually experienced) were used as sexual stimuli. These animals were maintained under the same housing conditions as the experimental female rats but in different rooms. Before being used as social stimuli, these stimulus female rats were surgically ovariectomized under anesthesia (intraperitoneal injection of ketamine-xylazine) to eliminate endogenous hormonal fluctuations, and subsequently brought into estrus by a subcutaneous injection of estradiol benzoate (10 µg/rat; Sigma, E8515) and progesterone (500 µg/rat; Sigma, P0130) to standardize sexual receptivity during behavioral testing31.

Testing apparatus and habituation

Sexual incentive motivation and active investigation were evaluated in a three-compartment rectangular transparent Plexiglas cage (91.44 × 31.75 × 40-cm). The main compartment, located in the center of the apparatus, housed the experimental female rat, while two smaller compartments (16.51 cm long) on each end contained the sexual and social stimulus animals, respectively. The compartments were separated from each other by Plexiglas barriers with holes (1 cm in diameter) that allowed the experimental female rat and stimulus animals to see, hear, and smell one another without physical contact.

All experimental female rats were habituated to the testing apparatus prior to the sexual motivation tests. First, the rats were allowed to acclimate to the apparatus alone for 30 min per day for two days. On the testing day, they underwent an additional 5-minute adaptation period immediately before the start of testing. The stimulus animals were absent from the apparatus during habituation. Before each adaptation period and every experimental session, the test arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate residual odors.

Behavioral testing procedure

In all intact experimental female rats, sexual incentive motivation test and active investigation test were performed simultaneously in the testing apparatus. On the test day, the experimental female rat was first given a 5-minute adaptation period and then returned to its home cage. Subsequently, the social and sexual stimulus animals were placed individually in the side compartments, and then the experimental female rat was positioned in the main compartment of the apparatus. The female rat was allowed to move freely in the testing apparatus for 10 min, and the session was video-recorded. In sexual incentive motivation test, when all four paws of the experimental female rat were within a 12.7 cm zone adjacent to the barriers separating it from the stimulus animals, the rat was considered to be spending time near that stimulus. The following parameters were evaluated: time spent near the male rat (TWM) or the female rat (TWF); total social time (TST, calculated as TWM + TWF); and male preference ratio (TWM/TST).

In active investigation test, the experimental female rat’s active investigation behaviours—including sniffing, licking, chewing, and climbing the barriers in attempts to reach the stimulus animals—were recorded. The following parameters were analyzed: duration of investigating the male rat (TIM) or the female rat (TIF); total investigation time (TIT, calculated as TIM + TIF); and male investigation preference ratio (TIM/TIT). Each 10-minute video recording was manually scored using a stopwatch by a researcher blinded to the experimental conditions. Additionally, the activity index, defined as the number of times the experimental female rat crossed the center of the apparatus, was manually counted by the same researcher using a hand counter.

Collection of blood and tissues

Between the 16th and 19th days of swimming exercise and injections of deionized water or metrnl, each experimental female rat in the estrus phase that completed the sexual motivation test was immediately euthanized by decapitation without anesthesia to prevent potential confounding effects of anesthetic agents on GnRH analysis, as previously reported32. Blood samples were collected from the trunk approximately 4–5 h after the last swimming exercise and injections of deionized water or metrnl. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 5 min at + 4 °C, then immediately stored at – 80 °C for later analysis. The ovary, uterus, and brain were carefully dissected and then fixed in 10% formalin for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. In this study, all experimental female rats were decapitated at the same stage of the estrous cycle (estrus) to eliminate the influence of hormonal fluctuations on the analyzed parameters.

Serum hormone assay

Serum levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (E-EL-R0391; Elabscience, USA), LH (E-EL-R0026; Elabscience, USA), prolactin (E-EL-R3006; Elabscience, USA), 17-β estradiol (E2) (E-EL-0152; Elabscience, USA), progesterone (E-EL-0154; Elabscience, USA), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) (E-EL-R3022; Elabscience, USA), metrnl (201-11-2697; SunRed, China), and corticosterone (201-11-0497; SunRed, China) were measured using validated Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance values were read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Scientific, USA). The assay sensitivities for FSH, LH, prolactin, E2, progesterone, AMH, metrnl, and corticosterone were 1.88 ng/mL, 0.94 mIU/mL, 1.88 ng/mL, 1 pg/mL, 0.15 ng/mL, 37.5 pg/mL, 0.071 ng/mL, and 5.005 ng/mL, respectively.

Histological evaluation of the ovary and uterus

After fixation, the ovary and medial portion of the uterine horns were embedded in paraffin blocks following standard histological procedures. Five serial Sect. (5 μm thick, 30 μm apart) from each paraffin block were stained with Crossman’s triple staining or hematoxylin and eosin and mounted using Entellan. Slides were examined under a Nikon E-80i microscope and photographed with a Nikon Digital Sight imaging system. For each animal, histological evaluations were performed on five ovarian sections, and the mean value was calculated per animal.

In the ovary, the ovarian follicles (primordial, primary, secondary, and Graafian) and corpus luteum numbers were counted in each section. The classification of ovarian follicles followed the morphological criteria defined by Mazaud et al.33. For corpus luteum angiogenesis, ten randomly selected fields per section were evaluated semi-quantitatively using a scoring system from 0 to 3 (0: none, 1: mild, 2: moderate, and 3: severe) under 40x magnification. Additionally, fibroblast counts were obtained from ten randomly chosen areas per ovarian section using a 100-square ocular micrometer under 40x magnification. The arithmetic mean for each rat was calculated, and data were expressed as the number of fibroblast cells per 1 mm234.

In the uterine horn, a general histological examination was performed, and the epithelial and wall thickness (including endometrium, myometrium, and perimetrium) were measured.

GnRH Immunohistochemistry and histoscore analysis

The median eminence (ME) and preoptic area (POA), including its medial (mPOA) and lateral (lPOA) subregions, were identified according to the Paxinos and Watson atlas (ME: bregma − 2.12 mm to -2.30 mm; POA: bregma − 0.26 mm to -0.92 mm)35. The accuracy of ME and POA sectioning was verified microscopically after cutting with a microtome.

GnRH expression and localization were determined in the ME and POA using immunohistochemistry as previously described34. Briefly, after deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed on 5 μm sections, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary GnRH antiserum (ME: 1/500 dilution; POA: 1/100 dilution; EPR24529-70; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Immunoreactivity was visualized using a goat anti-polyvalent horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (Patolab, PL-125-HL) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Patolab, PL-125-HD). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and examined microscopically. For each animal, immunohistochemical evaluation was performed on three serial sections taken at 30 μm intervals in both the ME and POA, and the mean value was calculated per animal.

For immunohistochemical scoring (histoscore), both the intensity and percentage of GnRH immunostaining in the ME (from seven rats per group) and in the mPOA and lPOA (from all animals) were evaluated under 20x magnification. Immunostaining intensity was scored on a scale from 0 to 3 (0 = negative; 0.5 = trace; 1 = light; 2 = moderate; and 3 = intense). The percentage of GnRH-immunopositive area was scored from 0 to 0.9 (0 = no staining; 0.1 = < 25% staining; 0.4 = 26–50% staining; 0.6 = 51–75% staining; and 0.9 = 76–100% staining, near homogeneity). To simultaneously assess both the intensity and uniformity of the GnRH immunostaining, the average intensity value for each brain region was multiplied by the average percentage of stained area to calculate a composite histoscore (i.e., histoscore = intensity x area)36. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were conducted independently by two investigators blinded to the experimental group allocation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 15.0, and all data were expressed as mean + SEM (standard error of the mean). Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that were normally distributed and showed homogeneity of variances (assessed with Levene’s test), a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons between groups. If the data were normally distributed but did not show homogeneity of variances, Tamhane’s T2 post-hoc test was used. When the assumptions of normality were not satisfied, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

In this study, the G*Power (version 3.1.9) software was used to determine the appropriate total sample size37. With an α level of 0.05, a power of 80%, and an effect size of 0.65, the minimal number of animals required for the total sample size was calculated to be 27 (i.e., n = 9/group).

Effects of metrnl administration and swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise on sexual incentive motivation

Using the sexual incentive motivation test, the preference for the sexual stimulus was reduced in the metrnl-treated and exercising female rats relative to deionized water-treated controls. Compared with the control group, all rats in the metrnl and exercise groups spent significantly less time near the sexual stimulus (TWM; p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, F2,24=6.73, η2 = 0.36; respectively; Fig. 1A) and more time near the social stimulus (TWF; p < 0.05 and p = 0.001, F2,24=9.54, η2 = 0.44; respectively; Fig. 1B). It was found that, compared with the control group, metrnl administration did not alter the total social time (TST; Fig. 1C), whereas exercise training significantly increased TST (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). This increase in TST in the exercise group was also significant compared to the metrnl group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Based on relavant changes in TWM and/or TST, metrnl administration and exercise training significantly decreased the male preference ratio (TWM/TST) in female rats compared with deionized water-treated controls (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, F2,24=8.17, η2 = 0.41; respectively; Fig. 1D). In addition, the total activity (number of crossings from the center of the testing apparatus) in the metrnl and exercise groups did not differ significantly from that of the control group (Fig. 1E).

Effects of metrnl administration and swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise on active investigation

Results from the active investigation test showed that metrnl administration did not alter the active investigation behavior of female rats toward male and female incentives (Fig. 2). Effects of metrnl administration and swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise on the serum levels of reproductive hormones, metrnl, and corticosterone

The data revealed that metrnl administration or exercise training in female rats had no significant effect on the serum LH, prolactin, 17-β estradiol, progesterone, and metrnl levels compared with the deionized water-treated control group (Fig. 3). Effects of metrnl administration and swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise on the GnRH immunoreactivity in the median eminence and preoptic area

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that GnRH was specifically expressed, as indicated by brown-colored staining, in the median eminence (ME) of the female rat brain (Fig. 6). Overall, GnRH immunoreactivity was highest in the caudal ME compared with the rostral and medial regions (Fig. 6A-C). Compared with the control group, GnRH immunoreactivity in the ME was significantly reduced in both the metrnl and exercise groups (p < 0.05; F2,24=10.12, ε²=0.53; Fig. 6D).

Discussion

To date, no studies have examined the effects of metrnl on the female reproductive system in rats with such detail. The present study is the first to comparatively reveal the effects of metrnl administration and exercise training on sexual motivation and the HPG axis in female rats, unlike studies investigating the effects of exercise-induced myokines on female reproductive function16,17. In particular, it is important to investigate the effects of metrnl administration and exercise training on hypothalamic GnRH expression in female rats. According to our data, exogenous metrnl administration decreased sexual incentive motivation toward sexual partner, GnRH immunoreactivity in the ME and POA, serum FSH and AMH levels, the numbers of primordial and primary follicles and ovarian stromal fibroblasts in the ovary. It also increased the number of corpus luteum and corpus luteum angiogenesis, similarly to swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise training. Meanwhile, serum metrnl levels in metrnl-treated female rats were comparable to those in all other groups, including female rats subjected to swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise and those treated with deionized water. Collectively, these results shed new light on how the administration of metrnl, one of the myokines, may influence female sexual function and alter HPG axis regulation. Furthermore, exercise-induced muscle-derived metrnl may be associated with the observed changes in the female reproductive axis following swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise.

Exercise, including moderate-intensity exercise, is widely recognized for its numerous benefits to overall health, fitness, and quality of life11,38. However, exercise also represents a physiological stress paradigm that engages many regulatory endocrine systems to maintain homeostasis. Previous studies have shown that swimming exercise can induce stress responses that vary among rodents4,38,39. In particular, forced swimming may impose both physical and psychological stress for animals due to their struggle for survival. Therefore, forced swimming is also known to be a model of chronic stress40,41. Accordingly, although our protocol was designed as a moderate-intensity exercise paradigm based on the existing literature, the reproductive changes observed in female rats in the present study may partly reflect stress-related neuroendocrine responses associated with repeated swimming exposure, rather than representing a generalized effect of moderate-intensity exercise itself. This interpretation is further supported by the increase in serum corticosterone levels associated with exercise training in our study.

Numerous lifestyle factors, including nutrition, weight management, psychological stress, and exercise, play an important role in determining reproductive health and can have either favorable or adverse effects on fertility5. In particular, exercise variables such as frequency, intensity, duration, and type significantly influence female sexual function. For example, regular vigorous exercise can significantly enhance sexual function, whereas excessive intense physical activity may adversely affect it3,42. Research indicates that women who engage in prolonged or high levels of physical activity may be at increased risk of lubrication disorders or dyspareunia43,44. In our study, the effects of a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week on female sexual function were demonstrated through sexual motivation tests. Accordingly, it was found that the time spent near the sexual stimulus, the male preference ratio, duration of investigating the male rat, and the male investigation preference ratio decreased in exercising female rats, indicating that this swimming exercise protocol reduced sexual motivation in female rats. In addition, since stress is known to reduce the quality of sexual function, especially its motivational aspects45, the decreased sexual motivation observed in the exercised female rats in our study may be attributable to stress induced by repeated swimming-based exercise.

Myokines are known to mediate various exercise-induced changes, including metabolism, muscle hypertrophy, tissue regeneration, and growth46. Metrnl has been characterized as both an exercise-inducible myokine and an adipokine19. It has been suggested that metrnl may partially mediate the positive effects of regular exercise on overall body function21. In our study, similar to a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week, metrnl administration reduced sexual incentive motivation toward sexual partner in female rats, as evidenced by decreased time spent near the sexual stimulus and male preference ratio. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of the myokine metrnl on female rat sexual function. However, a previous study we conducted revealed that administration of irisin, an exercise hormone similar to metrnl hormone, also reduced sexual incentive motivation in female rats30. In conclusion, these results raise the possibility that exercise-secreted myokines may contribute to pathways related to exercise-associated alterations in female sexual motivation.

The female HPG axis consists of GnRH neurons and their terminals in the hypothalamus, the pituitary gonadotropins (LH and FSH) and the ovary, and normal function at all HPG levels is required for reproduction. In particular, GnRH is considered the primary regulatory hormone of the HPG reproductive axis, its release in the median eminence (ME) serves as the main signal controlling reproductive function. Additionally, GnRH processes in the preoptic area (POA) contribute to the regulation of GnRH release12,47. The modulatory effects of exercise on the HPG axis have been demonstrated, as exercised rats exhibit decreased GnRH levels alongside alterations in testosterone, LH, and FSH48. Similarly, Arisha & Moustafa49 reported that chronic swimming exercise reduced the expression of GnRH mRNA and its receptor in the hypothalamus of rats. Recent studies further indicate that different exercise modalities exert distinct effects on hypothalamic GnRH gene expression: high-intensity interval training on a treadmill inhibits GnRH expression, whereas moderate-intensity continuous treadmill exercise enhances it50. Previous research also shows that hypothalamic GnRH expression may increase, decrease, or remain unchanged depending on exercise intensity and duration51,52. In line with these findings, our present study detected reduced GnRH expression in the ME and POA of rats following a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week. Furthermore, this study provides the first evidence that administration of metrnl reduces hypothalamic GnRH immunoreactivity in female rats. The HPG axis is inhibited at multiple levels by components of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Corticosterone may play a role in the suppression of GnRH neurons, resulting in reproductive dysfunction4,53. Therefore, the increased serum corticosterone levels observed following metrnl administration and exercise training may have contributed to the decrease in GnRH expression observed in our study. Collectively, these results suggest a potential role for metrnl, an exercise-induced myokine, in processes associated with exercise-induced disruption of GnRH release.

It is well established that disruption of the normal pulsatile secretion of GnRH can critically affect gonadotropin release54. Previous studies have shown that reproductive abnormalities in female athletes result from suppression of pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH secretion. This suppression, in turn, decreases LH and FSH pulsatility and leads to cessation of ovarian stimulation53,55. Additionally, Danforth et al.56 reported that GnRH antagonists alone reduce the number of primordial follicles in the murine model, likely through a direct effect on the ovary. Consistent with these findings, our results indicated that a decrease in hypothalamic GnRH expression—induced by metrnl administration or exercise training—may underlie the reduction in serum FSH levels and the number of ovarian primordial follicles. These changes were also associated with other alterations in the HPG axis regulation in metrnl-treated and exercising female rats. Interestingly, although GnRH expression decreased in the ME and POA in the metrnl and exercise groups, serum LH levels remained unchanged. LH secretion occurs in a pulsatile manner57. Therefore, serum measurements obtained at a single time point in our study may not adequately reflect dynamic alterations in LH release.

Physical and psychological factors can influence the endocrine equilibrium that regulates female reproductive function. Exercise is a key factor affecting this balance58. Numerous studies have demonstrated that different exercise modalities and intensities impact HPG axis hormones in distinct ways. For instance, Njoku et al.59 reported that prolonged moderate-to-vigorous exercise decreased serum estrogen, LH, and FSH levels, while increasing serum AMH and progesterone in premenopausal women. In sedentary women, resistance exercise has been shown to increase serum FSH levels while decreasing serum AMH levels60. Moreover, several studies have indicated that vigorous physical activity is associated with lower AMH concentrations in women61. In addition, physiological stress has been associated with decreased AMH levels, and low serum AMH concentrations have been linked to stress, particularly in women with infertility62. Rahnama et al.63 reported that estradiol concentrations increased, whereas progesterone, LH, and FSH levels remained unchanged in female middle-distance runners. Consistent with these findings, our data showed that swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise decreased serum FSH and AMH levels, without affecting serum LH, prolactin, 17-β estradiol, and progesterone levels in female rats. These changes may be associated with stress-related endocrine responses, reflecting the elevated corticosterone levels observed in our study. Interestingly, similar changes in reproductive hormones were also observed in the metrnl-treated female rats. Taken together, these findings suggest that administration of metrnl, an exercise-responsive myokine, and swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise produce comparable adverse effects on FSH and AMH levels in female rats.

Frequency, duration, and intensity of physical activity correlate with subfecundity and infertility and increased physical activity is believed to cause various disorders in the female reproductive axis2,4,5,10. Numerous studies in rodents have demonstrated that different types and intensities of exercise have distinct effects on ovarian follicles. Notably, various swimming training protocols, including forced or exhaustive swimming, have been shown to cause histopathological reductions in ovarian follicle numbers, including growing follicles, secondary, and Graafian follicles64,65. Similarly, in the present study, we have demonstrated that a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week, as well as exogenous metrnl, led to reductions in ovarian follicles, including primordial and primary follicles, in rats. Histologically, the term “reserve” refers to the number of primordial follicles present in the ovaries at a given age. The reserve of primordial follicles that initiate folliculogenesis determines the population of developing follicles available for ovulation and directly regulates female reproductive efficiency. In addition, ovarian reserve is associated with serum AMH levels and the number of ovarian antral follicles66,67. Serum FSH levels, critical for folliculogenesis and female fertility, are also commonly used as biological markers to evaluate ovarian reserve59. Concerning AMH’s role in primordial follicle recruitment, Durlinger et al.68 reported that female AMH-null mice exhibited early depletion of primordial follicles, suggesting that AMH inhibits their entry into the growing follicle pool. Consistently, in the present study, decreases in serum AMH and FSH levels were accompanied by reductions in primordial follicle numbers and, consequently, ovarian reserve in rats treated with exogenous metrnl or subjected to a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week. In particular, the decreased serum AMH levels may reflect altered regulation of early follicular recruitment. This reduction in AMH could explain the selective decrease in primordial and primary follicles while leaving the number of secondary and Graafian follicles unaffected. Moreover, the adverse effects of metrnl and a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week on ovarian tissue were evidenced by decreased numbers of ovarian stromal fibroblast cells, impaired folliculogenesis, and increased corpus luteum numbers and angiogenesis, which may reflect delayed luteolysis.

Regarding the effects of exercise variables on the reproductive organs, Mosavat et al.69 reported that low- or high-intensity jumping exercise reduced the thickness of the uterine endometrium and/or myometrium in rats. Notably, reductions in uterine thickness were linked to the detrimental effects of high-intensity jumping exercise on FSH, LH, and progesterone levels. In the present study, we observed that metrnl, an exercise mediator, and a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week adversely affected the female reproductive tract of rats by decreasing uterine endometrial or myometrial thickness. These findings suggest a potential impact of metrnl administration and exercise training on endocrine regulatory mechanisms.

Previous studies suggest that the effect of exercise on circulating metrnl levels may depend on exercise variables, including modality and intensity70–72. In the present study, serum metrnl concentrations remained unchanged after swimming-based moderate-intensity exercise and exogenous metrnl administration in female rats. However, this does not preclude the biological effects of metrnl upon sexual incentive motivation and the HPG axis in female rats. These results suggest that metrnl may exert local effects on target tissues of the HPG axis, and the absence of a serum metrnl response could be due to sampling timing or its short half-lives73. To sum up, considering the adverse effects of exogenous metrnl and a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week on the female reproductive axis, circulating metrnl concentrations may be clinically relevant in exercise-related alterations of the reproductive axis. However, extrapolating these findings to humans requires further investigation due to differences in reproductive physiology among species.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations of the present study. First, although our study indicated that exogenous metrnl and exercise produced similar modulatory effects on the female reproductive axis, a significant limitation is that a direct causal link for metrnl was not established. To definitively demonstrate causality, mechanistic interventions such as metrnl inhibition, neutralization, receptor blockade, or knockdown would be required. Second, functional fertility outcomes, such as mating success and pregnancy rates, were not assessed in the present study. Third, another methodological limitation relates to the euthanasia procedure: the experimental animals were euthanized by rapid decapitation, and therefore, the potential impact of acute stress on hormonal outcomes cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, exercise intensity was not objectively verified using physiological or performance-based parameters.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that metrnl administration and a 30-min swimming exercise performed 5 days per week reduced sexual motivation toward a partner and altered HPG axis regulation in female rats in a similar manner, as evidenced by decreased hypothalamic GnRH expression, reduced FSH and AMH levels, impaired ovarian folliculogenesis, and other abnormalities. Additionally, it was revealed that serum metrnl levels were similar in the metrnl-treated and exercising female rats. These findings suggest that exercise-induced metrnl (as a myokine) may contribute to the adverse effects of exercise on the female reproductive axis.
