A tip of the iceberg: genome survey indicated a complex evolutionary history of Garuga Roxb. species
Authors: Dongbo Zhu, Rui Rao, Yu Du, Chunmin Mao, Rong Chen, Liangliang Yue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Garuga Roxb. is a genus endemic to southwest China and other tropical regions in Southeast Asia facing risk of extinction due to the loss of tropical forests and changes in land use. Conducting a genome survey of G. forrestii contribute to a deeper understanding and conservation of the genus.ResultsThis study utilized genome survey of G. forrestii generated approximately 54.56 GB of sequence data, with approximately 112 × coverage. K-mer analysis indicated a genome size of approximately 0.48 GB, smaller than 0.52GB estimated by flow cytometry. The heterozygosity is of about 0.54%, and a repeat rate of around 51.54%. All the shotgun data were assembled into 339,729 scaffolds, with an N50 of 17,344 bp. The average content of guanine and cytosine was approximately 35.16%. A total of 330,999 SSRs were detected, with mononucleotide repeats being the most abundant at 70.16%, followed by dinucleotide repeats at 20.40%. We conducted a preliminary ploidy assessment using Smudgeplot and observed a clear bimodal distribution in G. forrestii at 1/2 relative coverage depth and total coverage depth (2n), suggesting a potential diploid genome structure. A pseudo chromosome of G. forrestii and a gemone of Boswellia sacra were used as reference genome to perform a primer population resequencing analysis within three Garuga species. Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated three distinct groups, but genome wide phylogenetics represented conflicting both between the dataset of different reference genomes and between maternal and nuclear genome.ConclusionIn summary, the genome of G. forrestii is small, and the phylogenetic relationships within the Garuga genus are complex. The genetic data presented in this study holds significant value for comprehensive whole-genome analyses, the evaluation of population genetic diversity, investigations into adaptive evolution, the advancement of artificial breeding efforts, and the support of species conservation and restoration initiatives. Ultimately, this research contributes to reinforcing the conservation and management of natural ecosystems, promoting biodiversity conservation, and advancing sustainable development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10917-8.Keywords: Genome survey, K-mer, Flow cytometry, SNP, Phylogenetic, Garuga forrestii, Nuclear-cytoplasmic conflict

Background

Garuga Roxb. is an endemic genus to southwest China and other tropical regions in Southeast Asia. These deciduous trees bloom between March and April, prior to the onset of the rainy season, produce fruits maturing from May to November, with the main maturation phase occurring in July and August. There are approximately five species or varieties within this genus, including G. forrestii W. W. Smith, G. floribunda var. gamblei (King ex Smith) Kalkm., G. floribunda var. floribunda Decne., G. pierrei Guill., and G. pinnata Roxb. Four of these species are found in China, except G. floribunda var. floribunda. G. forrestii is an endemic species of China [1]. Previous report indicated the chromosome number of G. pinnata is 2n = 26 [2]. However, chromosome reports of other species of Garuga are excluded till now.

Garuga forrestii, is a Chinese endemic species of this genus, distributed in the arid and warm river valleys of Yunnan, S.W. China, such as Jinshajiang River (JR), Lancangjiang River (LR), Red River (RR), Nanpanjiang River (NPR) and their tributaries. It is also the only species distributed beyond to the Tropic of Cancer, in the JR, extra north to the tropical regions. It extends to Leibo, Sichuan, making it the highest latitude distribution within the Burseraceae. It distributed upper to the altitudes nearing 1600 m at many points in the JR. It demonstrates an extraordinary ability to flourish under arid conditions. G. floribunda var. gamblei and G. pinnata grow in dense tropical forests of South Yunnan, further to Guangxi, Guangdong and Hainan. Our survey along the middle LR unveiled the coexistence of three species: G. forrestii, G. floribunda var. gamblei and G. pinnata. But individuals of this genus are all in danger of extinction due to the loss of tropical forests and changes of land uses.

The taxonomy of Garuga has experienced substantial reorganization, resulting in alterations to their taxonomic classifications. G. pierrei previously considered as a variety of G. pinnata, exhibits leaf characteristics similar to G. pinnata [3]. However, typical G. pierrei specimens were not found during our fieldwork. Furthermore, the fruiting attributes of G. floribunda var. gamblei share similarities with those of G. forrestii. In the comparison within each group, we found notable differences manifest in terms of hair coverage, pedicel, fruit dimensions and shape. Within the Lancang-Mekong River region, these three species coexist, displaying a continuum of variations in tree structure and leaf morphology. Consequently, the current taxonomic classification needs further tests by phylogenetic and phylogenomic evidence [1].

The Sino-Himalaya region is in the southeast margin of the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau (QTP) hosting several Asian rivers, including the Mekong, RRB and JRB, along with their tributaries. Presently, these rivers flow separately to the ocean [4, 5]. However, they once flowed southward into the Paleo-Red River (PRR), creating an extensive drainage network that ultimately emptied into the South China Sea millions of years before [6, 7]. The PRR system disassembled subsequently due to uplift of QTP and river capture events since the late Miocene [7]. This reorganization disrupted the previously continuous distribution pattern, resulting in distinctive genetic and biogeographical attributes, facilitating species’ genetic differentiation giving rise to new taxa [8, 9]. The river captures also made separate ranges unique promoting lineage fusion and diversity accumulation [10].

Genome sequencing technology enables the thorough investigation of species evolution [11]. Next-generation sequencing, NGS, technique has greatly reduced the cost of DNA sequencing [12], and was widely used in most fields of genetics and genomics, providing researchers with a more convenient, refined, and comprehensive investigation method [13]. Genome surveys provide a time-saving and cost-effective method for discovering new SSR marker sequences in plants [14]. Furthermore, genome surveys also offer information about the genomic structure of plant species, including estimates of genome size, heterozygosity levels, and repetitive content. A genome survey isa convenient strategy to provide a rough reference genome for those species without whole genome data [15–17].

This study employed genome survey to explore the genome size and characteristics of G. forrestii. The gathered genomic information from this research will serve to (1) learn deeper in our comprehension of the G. forrestii genome; (2) strive to construct preliminary long contigs or even draft scaffolds, providing a foundation for future evolutionary surveys of this genus.

Materials and methods

Sampling and DNA isolation

Fresh leaves from a mature G. forrestii individual were collected for genome survey during the summer of 2023 near to the Gangou Bridge in the Red River valley, Yunnan, China. Fresh leaves from other 25 individuals of three species (G. floribunda var. gamblei, G. pinnata and G. forrestii) in this genus were also tested. Among these, 6 individuals are of G. floribunda var. gamblei, 8 of G. pinnata, and 11 of G. forrestii (Fig. 1; Tab. S1). No specific permissions were required for the collection of specimens for this study in which there were neither privately owned nor protected species. We checked the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. After the formal identification of the plant material was carried out by Liangliang Yue, voucher specimens were prepared and deposited at the herbarium of College of Wetlands, Southwest Forestry University (Tab. A total of 26 samples were sequenced. The genomic DNA was isolated using the CTAB method [18]. DNA was detected by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, while DNA was quantified by UV spectrophotometer.

Genome size estimation by flow cytometry

To determine the genome size of G. forrestii. Solanum lycopersicum L. (genome size: 900 Mb) was selected as an internal reference to detect the genomic DNA content of G. forrestii. One to two tender leaves from G. forrestii and S. lycopersicum (tomato) were placed into a Petri dish and 0.8 mL MGb Buffer was added. Meanwhile, a sharp razor was used to chop up both leaves. The homogenate was filtered through a 40 μm nylon filter. Then, nuclei DNA was stained with PI (50 µg/mL) and RNAase (50 µg/mL) staining solution [19, 20]. The samples were incubated on ice for 10 min. A green argon laser with 488 nm wavelength was used to analyze over 10,000 nuclei in the sample. Genome sequencing and quality control

Dried leaves from the mature tree of G. forrestii were sent to Personalbio company for paired-end sequencing. We followed the standard protocol using Illumina’s TruSeq DNA PCR-free prep kit reagents for sequencing library preparation. Extracted DNA sample underwent random shearing through ultrasonication, followed by end repair to eliminate overhanging bases at the 5’ end and to add a phosphate group while filling in missing bases at the 3’ end. To prevent self-ligation of DNA fragments and ensure compatibility with sequencing adaptors, an A base was added to the 3’ end of the DNA sequence. Sequencing adaptors with library-specific tags were ligated to the 5’ end of the DNA sequence, facilitating the immobilization of DNA molecules onto the Flow Cell. We used AMPureXP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) for a selective removal of adaptor-ligated fragments and purification of the resulting library system. Subsequently, PCR amplification was performed on DNA fragments ligated with adaptors to enrich the sequencing library templates. A second purification step with BECKMAN AMPure XP Beads was carried out to purify the enriched library products. Finally, we conducted 2% agarose gel electrophoresis to select and purify the library’s final fragments. The resulting library insert fragments were approximately 400 bp in size. We performed paired-end sequencing with 2 × 150 bp reads using an Illumina NovaSeq instrument (Table 1).

Assessment of raw data quality

The software fastp [22] was used to qualify the raw data. sliding window method with a window size of 5 bp was employed, moving from the 3’ end to the 5’ end. Within each window, the average quality (Q) value of bases was computed. Bases with a Q value < 20 were excluded, and sliding ceased when a Q value ≥ 20 was reached. Moreover, reads with a length ≤ 50 bp or containing ≥ 5 ambiguous (N) bases at either end were discarded.

Ploidy level estimation and k-mer analysis

In many cases, researchers studying a novel organism may not know the ploidy a priori. For this reason, Smudgeplot has been implemented to visualize genome structure and infer ploidy directly from the k-mers present in sequencing reads [23].

Smudgeplot software [23] conducts genome structural analysis by comparing the cumulative coverage of k-mer pairs (CovA + CovB) and their relative coverage (CovB / (CovA + CovB)). This enables the quantification of heterozygous k-mer pairs, facilitating genome structure analysis. The jellyfish software [24], was used to estimate the genome size of G. forrestii by analyzing the distribution of k-mer frequencies and the counts of 19-mers, providing data on its heterozygosity and the proportion of repetitive sequences.

De novo assembly and guanine plus cytosine (GC) content analysis

The pair-end DNA sequencing data was de novo assembled with standard parameters using the MEGAHIT [25]. Contigs were further assembled into scaffold levels using SOAPdenovo [26] software. Sliding window calculations were performed using a window size of 10 kb. The average depth and GC content of each window were computed. The quality of genome assembly was assessed using the Assembly-stats [27] utility, which calculated metrics such as N50, scaffold count, scaffold size, scaffold length, and genome length.

Before chloroplast assembly, low-quality sequences were filtered using SOAPnuke [28], and de novo assembly was performed following the pipeline of GetOrganelle [29].

Identification and verification of microsatellite motifs

Microsatellite Identification Software (MISA) [30] was utilized to detect microsatellite patterns within the scaffolds generated above. The minimum number of repeats required for identifying mononucleotide, dinucleotide, trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeat sequences is set as follows: mononucleotide repeats with less than 10 repetitions, dinucleotide repeats with less than 6 repetitions, and all other repeat types with less than 5 repetitions [30].

Variant calling using whole genome resequencing

In this study, we used two sets of reference genomes. The first set consists of long contigs or scaffolds generated in this study, while the second set includes published chromosome-level assemblies of Boswellia sacra Flück [31]. We concatenated all the long contigs or scaffolds into one linear sequence, which was then split into ten pseudo-chromosomes to facilitate the process of SNP calling, using Fatools in iTools [32]. The Picard [33], BWA [34], and Samtools [35] software are used to build the genome index and perform processes such as mapping, sorting, and deduplication, all aimed at subsequent variation detection. The GATK [36] software was employed to perform variant calling using HaplotypeCaller for each sample. The GATK software was applied to filter and extract SNPs and INDELs.

Phylogenetic reconstruction and principal components analysis (PCA)

Three sets of alignments were used for phylogenomic reconstruction. Two sets are from genome scanning, one set is from complete chloroplast genome. One of the two genome scanning alignment is referred by the scaffolds generated in this study, another is referred by the published scaffold level assamblies of B. sacra. The plastid genomes used in this study are all newly assembled in this study. The genome scanning datasets were converted into SNP format by deleting all the conserved loci.

We employed Plink [37] software to execute LD (Linkage Disequilibrium) filtering on the SNP dataset, resulting in the generation of a refined VCF file. This file was utilized to generate an alignment for phylogenomic reconstruction and PCA analysis, ensuring that the SNP matrix used for PCA did not incorporate LD sites. The phylogenomic reconstructions are performed using iqtree [38] with the default parameters under the model of SNPs, using B. sacra as outgroup. Phylogenomic trees were visualized using Figtree [39]. The PCA based on the SNP data extracted from the 25 samples was conducted using Plink [37] and GCTA [40] software, followed by visualization of the results using an R script. The plastid phylogenomic reconstruction is performed also using iqtree [38] with the default parameters.

The utilization of flow cytometry analysis produced a detailed high-resolution histogram (Fig. 2; Tab. Genome survey sequencing and quality evaluation

Insertion of paired-end libraries with a size of 400 bp (Table 1) yielded approximately ~ 54.56 GB of raw sequence (Tab. S3). The percentage of bases with quality score ≥ Q20 was 97.46% and ≥ Q30 was 92.86% (Tab. S3). Approximately 1.1% of the low-quality data was filtered, and the remaining ~ 53.97 GB was used for downstream analysis (Tab. S4).

The base distribution showed no separation of AT and GC either at the R1 end or the R2 end (Fig. S1), which laid the foundation for the subsequent quantitative analysis. The average sequencing error rates for single-base loci were all less than 0.1% (Fig. S2). All the results demonstrated that the sequencing quality was good enough for further analysis.

Genomic characteristics predicted by k-mer analysis and ploidy level estimation

K-mer analysis provides an estimate of the genome size based on the substrings of length k contained in the biological sequence. The data volume was multiplied to achieve a coverage of 112x. Besides, this it also indicates low quality or contamination in the sequences. 19-mer frequency analysis identified the 90X depth as the dominant peak based on the k-mer count (Fig. 3). After dividing the k-mer number by 90, the genome size was predicted to be ~ 483 Mb (483,508,243 bp). The other peak at 1/2 the depth of the main peak (45X) is most likely due to heterozygosity (~ 0.54%), While the peak at twice the depth of the main peak (180X) is caused by the repetitive sequence (~ 51.54%).

Preliminary genome assembly

A total of 360 million paired-end reads were utilized to establish the initial genome assembly of G. forrestii. The genome, assembled using SOAPdenovo software, comprises 339,729 scaffolds (509.9 Mb) with scaffolds exceeding 200 bp chosen to exclude low-quality sequences. The genome size, excluding Ns, accounts for 499,986,768 bp, and the final assembly exhibits a low value of Ns content of roughly 0.01%. The largest scaffold spans 345,990 bp, with N50 = 17,344 bp, N90 = 293 bp (Table 2).

Identification and characteristics of microsatellite motifs

The MISA software was used for SSR search among the scaffolds. It yielded 330,999 SSR motifs (Table 3). Among these, mononucleotides were the largest in number (232,225; 70.16%), dinucleotides were the second in number (67,518; 20.40%), which was followed by trinucleotide (23,506; 7.10%), tetranucleotide (5,103; 1.54%), pentanucleotide (1,472; 0.44%), and hexanucleotide (1,175; 0.35%) SSR markers (Fig. 6a).

Discussion

Genome features

The 19 K-mer showed that the G. forrestii genome was approximately 483.51 Mb, slightly smaller than that by flow cytometry (~ 520.00 Mb) (Figs. 2 and 5). We observed similar variations in other published studies and statistically analyzed the differences among these 15 species (Tab. S5). The variation in genomic size derived from these two methods, as illustrated in published papers, ranged from 16.00 Mb to 138.09 Mb. In our study, the genomic size deviation observed for G. forrestii falls within this range reported previously. The variability in laboratory processes leads to fluctuations in genome size estimations obtained using the cell-flow method [20, 41]. K-mer approach emerged as a computational technique to generate a k-mer frequency distribution (similar to a Poisson distribution) by plotting the coverage distribution of all k-mers in a sequence, where the peak of the distribution would be centered on the average sequencing depth of the genome. The genome size inferred by k-mer analysis could be more accurate [42].

A GC bias that is introduced during DNA amplification by PCR has been identified as a major source of variation in coverage [43]. Therefore, GC content can lead to sequence bias in Illumina sequencing libraries, which can impact genome assembly [13]. In this study, the GC content of the G. forrestii genome was 35.16% (Fig. 5), falling within the acceptable range of 25–65% as summarized by previous authors for genome assembly [13, 44–46]. However, the assembly result of higher-depth three-generation sequencing may be better.

In this study, the G. forrestii genome exhibited relatively low heterozygosity (0.54%, less than 1%), as shown in Fig. 4, making de novo assembly feasible. This high value of genome repeat rate (51.54%) is likely from expansion of gene family [47], gene transposition activity [48] and genome recombination [49]. To gain deeper insights into the G. forrestii genome and enhance our understanding of its speciation and population dynamics, a chromosome-level assembly is necessary.

We investigated several species exited also in warm and hot valleys, Terminalia franchetii Gagnep [50]. , Osteomeles schwerinae Schneid [51]. , Nouelia insignis Franch [52]. , Buddleja crispa Benth [53]. and Excoecaria acerifolia Didr [54]. for a comparison analysis. No raw reads of four species mentioned can be found in the GenBank for genome survey. Our data would provide a first view for evolutionary pattern of this area.

Multi backbones of phylogenomic trees and applications

While conflicts in topographies between phylogenomic trees derived from plastid and nuclear genomes are commonly observed, this study reveals the fascinating phenomenon of three distinct evolutionary patterns emerging from three different datasets, underscoring the intricate diversity of evolutionary processes (Fig. 7).

When we map our sequenced reads onto a reference genome to produce an informative matrix, the raw reads tend to map more frequently to conserved regions within the reference genomes. These regions are primarily within the coding sections of gene sequences. The effect of this method could be akin to the single-copy genomic protocol [55]. As the result, the phylogenomic reconstruction based on distantly related species appears to represent phylogenetic relationship of highly conserved backbone genes. Phylogenomic reconstruction based on the assemblies by this survey enables the mapping of raw reads to higher variable regions, offering more information (Tab. S6). This method produced longer matrix in our study, by which we can get higher resolution among branches of the phylogenetic tree and population dynamic simulations.

The inconsistent phylogenetic framework may be due to hybridization, but other factors such as incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) and various sources of error and noise cannot be excluded [56–58]. Hybridizations are common, but need broader sampling to make sure of it, as indicated within the genus Ostryopsis Decne in upper Lancangjiang River Valley [59]. Different Garuga species, which exhibit sympatric distribution in river valleys, may undergo interspecific hybridization. This process is facilitated when previously disconnected river systems become linked, allowing species to migrate and spread along the valleys. G. pinnata and G. forrestii demonstrate sympatric distribution. The chloroplast phylogenetic tree (Fig. 7c) suggests that some individuals of G. pinnata may have originated as a hybrid species, with G. forrestii as the maternal parent. It suggests that G. floribunda var. gamblei expanded northward, diverged subsequently and generated clade of G. pinnata and G. forrestii. However, this hypothesis needs validation through additional samples and different taxa in the future. The evolutionary relationships among the three species appear to be complex (Fig. 7a and c). Therefore, to address this issue in the Garuga genus, we plan to conduct further Hi-C and Hi-Fi sequencing in the future to assemble the genomes of the three species for comparative genomic research.

Conclusion

This study conducted a preliminary investigation into the genome size and characters of G. forrestii for the first time and reconstructed the phylogenomic relationships of three species within the Garuga genus. It provided valuable genomic data for the further exploration within the Garuga genus. The conflicts of topology among the three phylogenomic reconstructions indicated that we should pay more attention when selecting references.

In this preliminary survey, we discovered intriguing traits within the evolutionary histories of these three species. These findings could enhance our understanding of the historical relationship between the flora of Tropical Asia and East Asia, offering new insights into the mechanisms of plant diversity in Southwest China. We plan to use single-nucleus sequencing to assemble the whole genomes of these three species and perform comparative analyses. This will deepen our knowledge of the evolutionary history of these tropical tree species and provide valuable genomic information for their conservation, breeding, and utilization.

The whole genome sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Warehouse in National Genomics Data Center, Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences / China National Center for Bioinformation, under accession number GWHERCJ00000000 that is publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwh. The chloroplast assembly and annotation data for this study have been deposited in NCBI under the accession numbers ON361002 and PP337695-PP337718.
