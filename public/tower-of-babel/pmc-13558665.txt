Introduction

Aortic valve stenosis is the most common valvular heart disease in the elderly population, with an insidious disease progression. Once symptoms appear without effective intervention, the 5‐year survival rate is only about 20% (Rosalia et al. 2023). Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is currently the dominant minimally invasive interventional procedure for the treatment of severe aortic stenosis. It offers distinct advantages including minimal trauma, rapid postoperative recovery and a short hospital stay, with most patients being discharged within 3–7 days after surgery (Carroll et al. 2020). However, the out‐of‐hospital rehabilitation period represents a high‐risk window for various complications, placing stringent requirements on patients' ability to conduct in‐home self‐management.

Research indicates that up to 50% of delayed high‐grade atrioventricular blocks occur within 30 days post‐discharge, creating a tension between early discharge strategies and the need for prolonged rhythm surveillance (Muntané‐Carol et al. 2021). Subclinical leaflet thrombosis and structural valve deterioration may compromise long‐term prosthetic function, necessitating regular monitoring of valve hemodynamics and timely antithrombotic regimen adjustments (Makkar et al. 2020). Heart failure symptoms and fluid volume overload are common causes of readmission after TAVR, requiring close post‐discharge monitoring of body weight, dyspnoea and medication adherence—particularly diuretic management (Kolte et al. 2024; Goel et al. 2020). Collectively, these risks impose a complex home‐based management burden and present considerable challenges to patients' and caregivers' capacity to cope with the disease.

Digital health interventions (DHIs) offer unique advantages in addressing these challenges. By integrating wearable sensors, mobile applications, remote monitoring platforms and AI‐driven alerting systems, DHIs enable simultaneous surveillance across multiple risk domains: continuous ECG monitoring for conduction abnormalities (Fan et al. 2024; Natarajan et al. 2022); physiological parameter tracking—including weight, heart rate and oxygen saturation—for volume and haemodynamic status assessment (Liu et al. 2024; Ogami et al. 2025); structured rehabilitation guidance to counteract physical deconditioning (Brocki et al. 2023; Lindman et al. 2021); and automated medication reminders to support adherence (Jia et al. 2025). This integrated home‐based management model can reduce readmissions and facilitate early discharge (Bellemare et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2022, 2023). In recent years, relevant studies on DHIs for TAVR patients have emerged rapidly at home and abroad.

Several systematic reviews and evidence syntheses have examined digital technologies or cardiac rehabilitation in cardiac surgery and TAVR populations from complementary perspectives. Ahuja et al. (2023) conducted a systematic review of DHIs for patients undergoing cardiac surgery, covering mobile applications, wearable devices and telehealth platforms across the perioperative continuum. Jia et al. (2024) synthesized best evidence for home‐based cardiac rehabilitation in TAVR patients, summarizing 26 recommendations across seven domains. More recently, Rauseo et al. (2026) and Skalidis et al. (2026) reviewed computational modelling and digital twin technologies for pre‐procedural TAVR planning and complication prediction. However, these existing reviews address either broad cardiac surgery populations, evidence synthesis rather than primary evidence mapping, or pre‐procedural decision support—none have systematically mapped the application forms, functional modules, outcomes and implementation barriers of DHIs specifically for post‐discharge home self‐management in TAVR patients. Therefore, the present review fills this gap by providing a comprehensive synthesis of DHI applications in the post‐TAVR home setting, with particular attention to the range of technologies employed, their functional capabilities, the outcomes reported and the barriers to implementation. This focused scope distinguishes our review from broader syntheses and complements existing work by offering a TAVR‐specific, home‐centred evidence map to inform clinical practice and future research.

The Review

Aim

To systematically map the application forms, functions, outcomes and implementation barriers of digital health interventions (DHIs) in home self‐management for patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).

Design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (Jordan et al. 2022; Tricco et al. 2018). The completed PRISMA‐ScR checklist is provided in Supporting Information S1. The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (available at: https://osf.io/kuz7b/overview).

Search Methods

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, CNKI and Wanfang Databases. The search strategy was formulated by combining medical subject headings (MeSH) and keywords, with the search time limit from the establishment of the databases to 26 August 2026. The Boolean string included terms such as: (‘Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement’ OR ‘TAVR’ OR ‘TAVI’ OR ‘transcatheter aortic valve implantation’ OR ‘aortic valve stenosis’) AND (‘Digital Health’ OR ‘Telemedicine’ OR ‘Mobile Health’ OR ‘Remote Sensing Technology’ OR ‘digital health’ OR ‘eHealth’ OR ‘mHealth’ OR ‘telehealth’ OR ‘telemedicine’ OR ‘remote monitor’ OR ‘mobile app’ OR ‘smartphone’ OR ‘wearable device’ OR ‘telemonitor’) AND (‘Self‐Management’ OR ‘Self Care’ OR ‘Patient Discharge’ OR ‘Aftercare’ OR ‘Postoperative Care’ OR ‘self‐manag’ OR ‘self‐care’ OR ‘post‐discharge’ OR ‘postoperative’ OR ‘home‐based’ OR ‘rehabilitation’ OR ‘medication adherence’). The detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Supporting Information S2–S8.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligibility criteria were determined based on the Participants, Concept and Context (PCC) framework (Jordan et al. 2022):

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants: Studies involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) who had undergone transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR/TAVI). (2) Concept: Studies that evaluated any form of digital health intervention (DHI). This included, but was not limited to, mobile health (mHealth) applications, wearable devices, remote patient monitoring platforms, telehealth systems and AI‐driven tools for patient management, education or support. (3) Context: Studies where the DHIs were implemented primarily in a home or community‐based setting for postoperative self‐management, rehabilitation or follow‐up care. (4) Study Type: Peer‐reviewed original research articles, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi‐experimental studies, observational cohort studies and mixed‐methods studies. No restrictions on publication date were applied.

Exclusion criteria: (1) conference abstracts, books, book chapters, editorials, letters, protocols, reviews and case reports; (2) studies not focused on the post‐discharge/home self‐management phase; (3) full text unavailable; (4) duplicate publications.

Search Outcome

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X9.1 to remove duplicates. Two trained researchers independently conducted initial screening by reading titles and abstracts and secondary screening by reading full texts in accordance with the criteria. In case of discrepancies during the screening process, discussions were held with a third researcher to make a decision.

Quality Assessment

In compliance with scoping review methodology (Peters et al. 2020; Tricco et al. 2018), no quality assessment of included studies will be conducted, as our goal is to rapidly map the literature.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by two researchers (S.Z. and L.L.Z.) using a standardized data extraction form developed in Microsoft Excel. The form was developed a priori and comprised the following fields: author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, type of DHI technology, application functions, outcome indicators, and implementation barriers. The form was piloted on five randomly selected studies to ensure all relevant information was adequately captured, and minor adjustments were made based on this pilot. Prior to the formal extraction, the two researchers reviewed the inclusion/exclusion criteria and discussed the extraction fields to ensure a consistent understanding of each variable. Each researcher independently extracted data from all included studies. Extracted data were then cross‐checked. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion; when consensus could not be reached, a third researcher (H.L.C.) made the final decision. To ensure data accuracy, key characteristics (e.g., sample size, outcome indicators) were double‐checked against the original articles, and a third researcher performed spot checks on 20% of the included studies.

Synthesis

Data were synthesized narratively and presented using descriptive statistics to illustrate the main findings. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study characteristics (e.g., number and percentage of studies by country, study design, DHI type and outcome categories). Themes were identified through a hybrid approach. The categories of application forms and functional modules were deductively predetermined based on the study aim and existing literature. In contrast, the categories of implementation barriers were inductively derived from the extracted data: the two researchers independently reviewed all barrier‐related data, generated initial codes, and subsequently grouped them into broader themes through iterative discussion. We synthesized information on the study designs, technical characteristics of telemedicine interventions, therapeutic approaches used and outcomes assessed. To structure the synthesis of findings and identify key implications for research and practice, the findings were organized according to the PAGER (Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence for practice, Research recommendations) framework, as recommended for scoping reviews (Bradbury‐Jones et al. 2022).

Ethical Considerations

This scoping review did not involve human or animal experiments, and thus no ethical approval or informed consent was required.

Results

Study Inclusion

Our search yielded 623 potentially relevant records. After removing 335 duplicates, 288 reports were screened by title and abstract, of which 233 were excluded. The remaining 55 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 36 were excluded due to inconsistent research themes, inappropriate study types, or mismatched research populations. Finally, 19 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this scoping review (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Bellemare et al. 2022; Brocki et al. 2023; Cheng et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2024; Fischer et al. 2026; Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024; Mohr et al. 2022; Natarajan et al. 2022; Ogami et al. 2025; De Lucia et al. 2023; Tarakji et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). Application Forms

Based on the 19 included studies, the application forms of DHIs in home self‐management of TAVR patients can be summarized into seven types: Wearable devices: 11 studies (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Brocki et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2024; Fischer et al. 2026; Lindman et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024; Natarajan et al. 2022; De Lucia et al. 2023; Tarakji et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023); Mobile applications: 7 studies (Bellemare et al. 2022; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023; Ashikaga et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023); Remote monitoring platforms: 6 studies (Liu et al. 2024; Mohr et al. 2022; Ogami et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023; Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024; Natarajan et al. 2022); AI voice virtual assistants: 1 study (Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024); Remote video training platforms: 2 studies (Brocki et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023); Social media platforms: 2 studies (Cheng et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025); Integrated management platforms: 4 studies (Jia et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024; Bellemare et al. 2022; Ogami et al. 2025).

In clinical practice, combined application of multiple DHI technologies better addresses the multidimensional, dynamic and heterogeneous care needs of post‐TAVR patients: 10 studies comprehensively applied two or more electronic health technologies (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Bellemare et al. 2022; Brocki et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024; Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). The typical combination modes include: Wearable devices + mobile applications (Brocki et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023; Lindman et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023), which form a closed ‘collection‐transmission‐feedback’ loop; Mobile applications + social media/telephone (Bellemare et al. 2022; Jia et al. 2025; Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023), strengthening patient education and behavioural support; Remote monitoring platforms + green channel/early warning system (Liu et al. 2024; Mohr et al. 2022), triggering interventions driven by abnormal events.

Functional Modules

Digital health interventions for post‐TAVR home self‐management encompass five core functional modules (see Table 2). Remote ECG monitoring utilizes wearable or patch‐based devices for continuous cardiac rhythm surveillance (Dang et al. 2023; De Lucia et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2024; Fischer et al. 2026; Mohr et al. 2022; Natarajan et al. 2022; Tarakji et al. 2022), with AI algorithms in some studies automatically detecting arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation and conduction disturbances and triggering real‐time clinical alerts (Mohr et al. 2022; Natarajan et al. 2022). Multi‐parameter physiological monitoring extends beyond ECG to synchronous collection of multi‐modal data including heart sounds, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and body weight, with systems such as the Wenxin Xiaoyi patch (Liu et al. 2024) and Medtronic Care Management Service (Ogami et al. 2025) enabling fusion analysis and threshold‐based alerts; smartwatch‐based monitoring of heart rate, sleep, and physical activity was also employed (Zhang et al. 2023). Home‐based exercise rehabilitation combines wearable devices with mobile applications to deliver personalized goal setting, real‐time feedback and remote supervision, with multiple studies demonstrating improvements in exercise capacity and adherence (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Brocki et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021). Health education modules leverage social media platforms and dedicated applications to provide educational content, peer support and algorithm‐driven personalized feedback (Bellemare et al. 2022; Cheng et al. 2023; Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023). Finally, AI‐driven remote follow‐up, exemplified by Herrero‐Brocal et al.'s voice virtual assistant (Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024), enables automated telephone‐based structured follow‐up with natural language processing and three‐level alert generation, achieving high acceptance rates (89% of calls completed by patients themselves) in elderly populations without requiring internet access or smartphone applications.

Outcomes Measured

Functional outcomes were reported in all 19 included studies. Quality of life was evaluated in 6 studies (32%) (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Bellemare et al. 2022; Brocki et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Venkatraman et al. 2022). Patients' subjective experience and acceptance of the DHI system were less common, included in only 2 studies (Venkatraman et al. 2022; Venkatraman et al. 2023). Adverse events were monitored in 11 studies (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Bellemare et al. 2022; Brocki et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2024; Fischer et al. 2026; Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Mohr et al. 2022; Natarajan et al. 2022), though no serious adverse events related to DHI use were reported. Only 1 study conducted a cost analysis (Mohr et al. 2022).

Across the included studies, exercise‐based interventions delivered via telemedicine demonstrated varied outcomes for patients undergoing TAVR. Improvements in physical fitness and functioning were observed, including increases in 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) ranging from 48 to 82 m (Brocki et al. 2023; Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024). Improvements in sit‐to‐stand test performance (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Lindman et al. 2021) and handgrip strength (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Venkatraman et al. 2022) were also reported. Compared with the control group, the intervention group achieved a higher daily step count, though the difference was not statistically significant (+769 steps/day, 95% confidence interval −244 to +1783, p = 0.14) (Lindman et al. 2021).

Interventions positively influenced quality of life and readmission outcomes. Improvements in quality of life, measured by KCCQ‐12, showed a trend toward improvement (3.8‐point difference, p = 0.06) (Bellemare et al. 2022). Readmission rates were reduced in multiple studies, with 30‐day readmission reduced by 33.5% (Bellemare et al. 2022) and 90‐day unplanned readmission reduced by 45% (Venkatraman et al. 2023).

Health economic evaluation was notable for its near‐complete absence. Only Mohr et al. (2022) conducted a cost analysis of the MCOT system, comparing resource use and costs between patients with (n = 283) and without (n = 3881) post‐TAVR MCOT monitoring using Medicare claims data. They found that MCOT use was associated with significantly lower index hospitalization costs (US$40,569 vs. US$43,289, p = 0.003) and improved hospital contribution margins (US$7087 vs. US$5177, p = 0.047), despite a higher rate of pacemaker insertion in the MCOT group (6.6% vs. 2.1%, p = 0.007). No significant cost differences were observed during the 60‐day post‐discharge period.

Patient Barriers

Patient‐level barriers were the most frequently cited obstacles to DHI implementation, clustering into four main categories. First, recruitment difficulties and follow‐up attrition were prominent: Brocki et al. (2023) reported a recruitment rate of only 37% (15/41) and a retention rate of 47% (7/15) in their telerehabilitation program, with lack of internet or poor network coverage (32%), post‐operative fatigue, non‐cardiac readmission, reduced cognition, and fluctuating health cited as the primary reasons for withdrawal; Tarakji et al. (2022) observed that adherence to extended ECG patch monitoring declined progressively from 100% pre‐TAVR to 84% post‐discharge and further to 74% at 2‐month follow‐up. Second, low digital literacy and limited IT skills posed a substantial barrier: De Lucia et al. (2023) found that 11.04% (18/163) of patients failed device training—7.3% due to incompatible smartphone operating systems, 1.8% due to cognitive decline combined with lack of caregiver support, and 1.8% due to reduced mobility that prevented proper device operation—with an additional 10 patients refusing the device altogether, while Venkatraman et al. (2022) reported that 37% of app users added a caregiver proxy to navigate the platform. Third, physical discomfort contributed to attrition, with Tarakji et al. (2022) reporting that 5.2% (5/96) of patients withdrew from patch‐based monitoring due to skin irritation or discomfort. Fourth, selection bias toward healthier, more technologically proficient patients was consistently observed: Venkatraman et al. (2023) found that app users were significantly younger (76.4 vs. 78.1 years, p = 0.03) and had lower STS risk scores compared to non‐users.

Technical Barriers

Technical barriers primarily involved device connectivity failures, single‐lead ECG limitations, and insufficient system usability. Brocki et al. (2023) observed that Bluetooth connection failures resulted in incomplete data records for all participants in their telerehabilitation program, with daily step count uploads fluctuating greatly due to unstable connections. Ashikaga et al. (2023) reported eight cases of prolonged exercise sessions triggered by internet connection errors, which stemmed from low network speeds or operational errors by patients. Regarding single‐lead ECG limitations, Zhang et al. (2023) systematically compared smartwatch single‐lead ECG against 24‐h Holter monitoring and found substantially lower diagnostic sensitivity for left bundle branch block (LBBB: 0.61) and right bundle branch block (RBBB: 0.60), attributing this to the Holter's ability to detect transient conduction abnormalities; Dang et al. (2023) corroborated these findings, documenting false positives and false negatives in smartwatch arrhythmia detection, and Fan et al. (2024) further noted that single‐lead ECG technology remains in its nascent stages, facing unresolved challenges in data handling, analysis, storage, security and privacy. Regarding system usability and algorithm performance, Herrero‐Brocal et al. (2024) reported that their AI‐powered voice virtual assistant tends to overidentify clinical symptoms, with excessive false detections of dizziness commonly observed in elderly patients. Such redundant alarm notifications may substantially increase the operational burden on medical staff.

Study Barriers

Across the included studies, restrictive exclusion criteria were commonly applied, with multiple studies excluding patients without home internet access, smartphones, or adequate cognitive capacity (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Brocki et al. 2023; De Lucia et al. 2023; Jia et al. 2025; Lindman et al. 2021; Venkatraman et al. 2022, 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). The majority of studies were single‐center, with Natarajan et al. (2022) and Fischer et al. (2026) reporting multi‐center design. Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from small feasibility studies (n = 7 in Brocki et al. 2023; n = 11 in Ashikaga et al. 2023) to large cohort studies (n = 1078 in Ogami et al. 2025; n = 4164 in Mohr et al. 2022). Follow‐up durations were generally short, with most interventional studies reporting outcomes at 12 weeks or less; however, a minority of studies extended follow‐up to 12 months (Bellemare et al. 2022) or captured long‐term outcomes through registry data (Ogami et al. 2025; Mohr et al. 2022).

Discussion

Technological Evolution: From Single‐Function Monitoring to Integrated Intervention

The development of DHIs for post‐TAVR home self‐management has shown a clear evolutionary trend from single‐function monitoring to multi‐functional integrated intervention. Early studies focused on the diagnostic efficacy and pacemaker predictive value of single electrocardiographic monitoring devices (e.g., MCOT (Mohr et al. 2022), Zio Patch (Tarakji et al. 2022)). After 2019, the intervention goal extended from detecting abnormalities to actively improving functional status, leading to the emergence of home‐based exercise rehabilitation platforms (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Brocki et al. 2023; Lindman et al. 2021), AI voice follow‐up systems (Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024), and full‐cycle integrated management platforms (Jia et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024). In contrast, single technical path studies still have irreplaceable clinical value in specific scenarios, especially showing unique advantages in two directions: age‐friendly design and low‐cost universality. For example, the AI voice virtual assistant (Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024) with zero technical threshold is highly suitable for elderly post‐TAVR patients with limited digital literacy, and simple wearable devices can achieve large‐scale popularization due to their low cost.

Imbalance in the Evidence Strength of Outcome Indicators

The 19 included studies exhibited significant heterogeneity in outcome measures. All 19 studies assessed functional outcomes, including the 6‐min walk distance (6MWD), physical mobility, daily step count, readmission rates, and permanent pacemaker implantation. Quality of life was evaluated in 6 studies (32%), predominantly using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) or EuroQol visual analog scale. However, only one study (Venkatraman et al. 2022) analysed usability‐related outcomes such as user satisfaction and willingness to recommend the system. The absence of usability metrics may undermine the long‐term real‐world effectiveness of digital health interventions (DHIs). Even if DHIs demonstrate clinical efficacy, suboptimal user experience will reduce patient engagement and adherence, thereby compromising their value for clinical translation.

A further limitation of the included studies lies in the scarcity of hard clinical endpoints. The majority of positive study findings were derived from surrogate endpoints, such as improved 6MWD, increased step counts, and enhanced medication adherence. Only four studies reported hard clinical endpoints: Mohr et al. (2022) and Ogami et al. (2025) adopted all‐cause mortality as a primary or secondary outcome, Liu et al. (2024) reported a 6‐month composite endpoint (including all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, stroke, conduction block, myocardial infarction, heart failure rehospitalization, and major bleeding), and Fischer et al. (2026) reported a 1‐year composite endpoint of sudden cardiac death, arrhythmic syncope/presyncope, and stroke (1.9% vs. 6.6%, HR = 0.27, p = 0.011). Nevertheless, none of the included studies possessed sufficient statistical power to detect intergroup differences in mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events. While surrogate endpoints facilitate the formulation of research hypotheses, they cannot reliably predict long‐term clinical outcomes that are clinically meaningful to patients. Without robust data verifying that surrogate parameter improvements induced by DHIs translate to fewer clinically significant adverse events, the widespread integration of these interventions into routine clinical practice remains uncertain.

Moreover, there was a notable lack of health economic evaluations. Only Mohr et al. (2022) performed a cost analysis of Mobile Cardiac Outpatient Telemetry (MCOT) using federal medical insurance claims data, comparing healthcare resource utilization and expenditures between monitored and unmonitored cohorts. None of the included studies conducted standardized cost‐effectiveness, cost‐utility, or cost–benefit analyses from the perspective of society or healthcare payers. This fundamental gap restricts the translation of DHIs research evidence into clinical policy, as economic evidence constitutes an essential prerequisite for insurance reimbursement approval and clinical pathway optimization.

The Digital Divide

DHIs have shown positive effects on multiple outcome indicators of home management in post‐TAVR patients, but the transformation from research results to real‐world clinical practice still faces systematic obstacles. The core of these obstacles is the increasingly deepening digital divide—the huge gap between the rapid development of digital technology and the actual acceptance capacity of the TAVR patient population (Azizan 2026).

At the patient level, post‐TAVR patients are characterized by advanced age, multimorbidity and high incidence of frailty. Cognitive decline, visual and auditory impairment, and weakened fine motor skills make the operation of complex digital devices a huge challenge (El‐Sabawi et al. 2023). Beyond these intrinsic barriers, many older adults hold negative attitudes toward technological change, perceiving digital health tools as a source of anxiety rather than reassurance. Brocki et al. (2023) program reported a retention rate of only 47%, PROs completion dropped from 76.7% preoperatively to 27.9% at 1 month post‐discharge (Venkatraman et al. 2022), and 5.2% of patients withdrew from patch‐based monitoring due to skin irritation (Tarakji et al. 2022). However, it is important to acknowledge that age alone is not an insurmountable barrier; successful implementation has been demonstrated in older populations when interventions incorporate simplified interfaces, caregiver‐mediated support, and age‐appropriate instructional design (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Herrero‐Brocal et al. 2024; Lindman et al. 2021). These successful models indicate that with careful attention to human factors, DHIs can be effectively adopted even among octogenarians.

At the technical level, unstable Bluetooth connection and data upload failure are common obstacles (Brocki et al. 2023; Hajiaghajani et al. 2023), and the accuracy and clinical applicability of algorithms also face challenges (Zhang et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023). Zhang et al. (2023) demonstrated that smartwatch‐derived single‐lead ECG had substantially lower sensitivity for detecting LBBB and RBBB. Dang et al. (2023) corroborated these findings, documenting false positives and false negatives in smartwatch arrhythmia detection. A central challenge underlying these inaccuracies is the difficulty of establishing appropriate clinical alert thresholds. Low specificity can lead to excessive early warning, and frequent false positives cause clinical ‘early warning fatigue’; low sensitivity leads to missed diagnosis, making patients and medical staff fall into a ‘sense of false security’ and miss the intervention opportunity (Phan et al. 2024). These technical problems not only affect the clinical effect of DHIs but also reduce patient and medical staff acceptance, further hindering their clinical translation.

At the policy level, the costs of DHI devices and platforms have not been included in the scope of medical insurance payment (van Kessel et al. 2023), which limits the large‐scale clinical promotion of technologies. In addition, existing studies generally take ‘no smart phone/no Internet/cognitive impairment’ as exclusion criteria, resulting in a significant difference between the research population and the real‐world TAVR population. DHIs may exacerbate rather than narrow health inequalities, and the accessibility of vulnerable groups needs urgent attention.

Future Research Directions

Based on the current research status and existing problems, future research on DHIs for post‐TAVR home self‐management should be systematically laid out around four main lines: evidence upgrading, precise intervention, equitable accessibility, and value transformation to promote the evidence‐based practice and clinical translation of DHIs.

First, it is necessary to break through the limitations of existing RCTs with small sample sizes, short cycles and obvious selection bias, and carry out pragmatic randomized controlled trials with multi‐center, long‐term follow‐up and wide inclusion criteria, with readmission rate, frailty state transition and cost‐utility as the core endpoints, and preset digital literacy stratified analysis to systematically evaluate the heterogeneity of intervention effects.

Second, use machine learning methods to integrate preoperative digital literacy assessment, psychological scales, and social support scores to develop and verify a TAVR‐specific digital health compliance prediction model, and then construct an adaptive intervention strategy (such as reducing push frequency for those with high attrition risk, increasing family incentives, and dynamically switching to low‐technical threshold paths), so as to realize the model transformation from ‘unified push’ to ‘dynamic adaptation’.

Third, it is necessary to face up to the health equity crisis caused by the general exclusion of populations with ‘no smart phone/no Internet/cognitive impairment’ in current research. Future research should target rural, low‐education, low‐income, and ethnic minority vulnerable groups, evaluate whether DHIs exacerbate or narrow health inequalities, and systematically explore a hybrid promotion model of community nurse home visits + low‐technical threshold digital tools.

Fourth, embed prospective health economic evaluation synchronously, calculate the quality‐adjusted life year using preference measurement tools such as the EuroQol Five‐Dimension Five‐Level Scale (EQ‐5D‐5L), and generate high‐level cost‐utility evidence that can serve medical insurance payment decisions, providing key support for DHIs to move from ‘clinically effective’ to ‘payment accessible’ (Joelson et al. 2021).

Implications for Nursing Practice

With the continuous development of DHIs in the medical field, nurses should continuously update their reservoir of digital nursing knowledge, achieve in‐depth integration of digital technologies with specialized nursing, and adapt to the new requirements for nursing positions brought by the era of smart healthcare. Based on the findings of this scoping review, the application of DHIs should be embedded into the entire process of home‐based self‐management for TAVR patients in nursing practice.

First, nurses should incorporate digital literacy screening into routine pre‐discharge assessments. Given that insufficient digital competence constitutes a major barrier to the adoption of DHIs (De Lucia et al. 2023; Venkatraman et al. 2022), clinical nurses may identify patients requiring additional support through simple screening questions, such as those with limited smartphone‐using experience, cognitive impairments, or lack of caregiver assistance. On the basis of such assessment outcomes, nurses can deliver personalized health education and select DHI tools adapted to patients' capabilities (e.g., voice‐based tools or application‐based tools).

Second, when introducing DHIs to elderly patients, nurses should adopt structured, age‐appropriate teaching protocols. Teach‐back methods, hands‐on practice, and clear visual guidance can improve patients' confidence and operational proficiency in using wearable devices and mobile applications (Ashikaga et al. 2023; Lindman et al. 2021). Meanwhile, family caregivers are recommended to be involved in training. Existing studies have demonstrated that support from caregivers can enhance patient adherence and help resolve device malfunctions (Venkatraman et al. 2022).

Third, nursing managers and nursing educators should consider integrating digital‐health‐related competencies into nurses' pre‐service training and continuing education programs. As DHIs become increasingly prevalent in post‐discharge care, nursing staff need to acquire basic knowledge of commonly‐used dDHI devices, platforms and troubleshooting, so as to provide professional guidance and answer patients' questions. They shall also be aware of privacy and safety considerations when utilizing patients' health data.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. First, despite a comprehensive search across seven major databases, we may have missed relevant studies indexed in other databases or published in languages other than English and Chinese. Second, as with any scoping review, we did not formally assess the quality or risk of bias of included studies, which limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about intervention effectiveness. Third, the heterogeneity of outcome measures across studies precluded quantitative synthesis. Finally, the rapid evolution of digital health technologies means that newly emerging interventions may not be captured in this review.

Conclusion

DHIs has diverse application forms and increasingly integrated functions in the home self‐management of TAVR patients, with preliminary evidence suggesting positive effects in improving patients' exercise capacity and reducing readmission rates. However, the evidence for hard endpoints is weak, and health economic evaluations are lacking. Issues such as technology accessibility, usage compliance, and payment security caused by the digital divide have restricted the exertion of its effects. In the future, it is necessary to optimize technology combinations and aging‐friendly designs, conduct multi‐center long‐term follow‐up studies to fill the evidence gap, promote hierarchical care and medical insurance payment reforms, and at the same time pay attention to the accessibility for vulnerable groups, so as to promote the evidence‐based practice and clinical transformation of DHIs.
