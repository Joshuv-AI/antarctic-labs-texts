On Identifying Critical Network Edges via Analyzing Changes in Shapes (Curvatures)
Bhaskar DasGupta, Katie Kruzan

Abstract
In recent years extensions of manifold Ricci curvature to discrete combinatorial objects such as graphs and hypergraphs (popularly called as “network shapes”), have found a plethora of applications in a wide spectrum of research areas ranging over metabolic systems, transcriptional regulatory networks, protein-protein-interaction networks, social networks and brain networks to deep learning models and quantum computing but, in contrast, they have been looked at by relatively fewer researchers in the algorithms and computational complexity community. As an attempt to bring these network Ricci-curvature related problems under the lens of computational complexity and foster further inter-disciplinary interactions, we provide a formal framework for studying algorithmic and computational complexity issues for detecting critical edges in an undirected graph using Ollivier-Ricci curvatures and provide several algorithmic and inapproximability results for problems in this framework. Our results show some interesting connections between the exact perfect matching and perfect matching blocker problems for bipartite graphs and our problems.

1.1
Quantifying the Shape of a Network: Short Informal Overview

Curvatures are very natural measures of anomaly of higher dimensional objects in
mainstream physics and mathematics,
e.g.
, in general relativity.
However, extending these measures to networks need to overcome many key
challenges since networks are discrete objects that do not necessarily have
an associated natural geometric embedding. There are several ways previous researchers
have attempted to formulate notions of curvatures of networks and other
combinatorial objects.

An earliest quantification
of network shapes
is
Gromov-hyperbolicity

defined via properties of geodesic triangles or

4
-node conditions or
Gromov-product nodes
[
5
,
19
]
.
This measure assigns one scalar value to the entire network.
There is a large body of research works dealing with theoretical and empirical aspects of this measure.

More recently, researchers have attempted to extend the concept of Ricci curvature for Riemannian
manifolds to networks in two major ways.
One of them is
via extending Forman’s discretization of Ricci curvature for
(polyhedral or CW) complexes
[
18
]

to networks.
Informally, the Forman-Ricci curvature is applied to networks by
topologically associating components (sub-networks) of a given network with higher-dimensional
objects.
Although formulated relatively recently, there are already a number of
papers regarding empirical application of
this measure
[
41
,
44
,
12
,
7
]
.
The other approach, which is used in this article,
is by using Ollivier’s discretization of Ricci curvature.
For an informal understanding, consider
transporting a infinitesimally small ball centered at a point of a manifold
along a specific direction
to measure the “distortion”
of that ball due to the shape of the surface.
Ollivier’s discretization
[
36
,
34
,
35
,
33
]
.
provides appropriate network-theoretic analogs of such concepts.
Intuitively, it tries to mimic the concept of the sectional curvature at a point
in the manifold.
The
Ollivier-Ricci curvature
has already found its way to a plethora of applications in
biological and social networks (
e.g.
, see
[
38
,
42
]
).
More recently, both these curvatures have been extended to directed and undirected
hypergraphs.
Both these approaches yield a scalar (number) for each edge
and therefore may be considered as a measure of the “local shape” of the network at
a given edge.

2.2
Quantifying Criticality of an Edge

It is well-known that

positive curvatures and negative curvatures
define very different topological worlds

[
36
]
;
for example,
positive
Ollivier-Ricci curvature
allow rapid mixing of a natural Markov process based on the distributions

{\mathbb{P}}_{u}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{v}

[
6
]

and
see
[
20
]

for further extensive discussions on the sign and geometric meaning of curvature.
Based on this observation, we adopt the extremal anomaly detection framework used in the context of Gromov-hyperbolic
curvature of undirected unweighted graphs in
[
12
]
to measure the criticality of
an edge in the following way.
For a given
edge
{\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}}=\{u,v\}\in E
.
we measure the
criticality of the edge
by the amount of topological alterations
of
G
that we need to perform to change the value of

\mathsf{sign}(\!\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!G}({\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}}))
,
where

\mathsf{sign}(x)=1
if
x>0
,

\mathsf{sign}(x)=0
if
x<0
,
and

\mathsf{sign}(x)

is undefined otherwise.
We have several variations of the problem depending on the following attributes:

Unweighted vs. positively edge-weighted graphs (uw vs. wt):

For weighted graphs, we assume that the edge weights are integers selected from

\{1,2,\dots,W\}
where
W
is polynomial in
\mathsf{deg}_{G}(v)
.
We do not allow an edge weight to be zero since

\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!G}(e)
is undefined for this case and also to preserve
the metric property needed for any curvature definition.
Our justification of allowing only integers as opposed to (polynomial-size) rational numbers
stems from the fact that

\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!G}(e)
is
“scale-invariant” (
i.e.
, multiplying all edge weights of a graph by a fixed positive
number does not change the value of

\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!G}(e)
), and thus if the edge weights are from the set

\{\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}},\dots,\frac{a_{k}}{b_{k}}\}

then as long as LCM
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})
is a polynomial-size number we can scale all the
weights to be integers of polynomial size.

Nature of topolical change: deletion vs. insertion of edges (del vs. ins):

We allow only insertion or deletion of edges in the graph, but not both.
For weighted graphs, we assume that the weight of an inserted edge is
of polynomial size. However, it is not difficult to see that we do not need edges
arbitrarily large, namely if an inserted edge has a weight greater than the diameter
of the given graph then the insertion of the edge has no effect on the shortest path
between any two nodes and thus there is no need to insert such an edge.
Assuming that the given graph has weights from the set

\{1,\dots,W\}
,
this provides an upper bound of
(|V|-1)W
on the weight of an inserted edge.

Restricted vs. unrestricted candidate edges for insertion/deletion (rt vs. ut):

We define two cases of permissible edges
for insertion or deletion.

\blacktriangleright

When insertions of edges are allowed then
for the restricted case only those new edges (not in
E
)
can be added that whose
one end-point is in
\mathsf{Nbr}_{G}(u)\setminus\{v\}

and
the other end-point is in
\mathsf{Nbr}_{G}(v)\setminus\{u\}
.
For the unrestricted case, any new edge can be added.

\blacktriangleright

If only deletion of edges are allowed then,
for either case, the edge
{\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}}
itself cannot be deleted.
For the restricted case, only those edges
\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}\in E

for which
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\notin\{u,v\}

can be deleted.
For the unrestricted case, any edge in
E
, except
{\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}}
, can be deleted.

Note that for restricted edge insertions/deletions the neighborhoods of the two nodes
u
and
v

remain the same, but that is not the case for unrestricted insertions/deletions.

Nature of curvature change: positive-to-negative vs. negative-to-positive (ptn vs. ntp):

The two cases correspond to whether the topological changes cause

\mathsf{sign}(\!\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!}({\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}}))
to change from
1
to
0
or from
0
to
1
.

A version of the problem will be denoted by combining the various attribute values in
the same order they were presented in the above discussion,
e.g.
,
Problem wt-ut-del-ptn or
Problem uw-ut-ins-ntp.
It is easy to see that for unweighted graphs, insertions (
resp.
, deletions)
of edges can only decrease (
resp.
, increase) the value of

\text{{\sc Ric}}_{\!\!}({\mathsf{e}_{\mathrm{critical}}})
.
Thus, the problems
uw-ut-del-ptn,
uw-rt-del-ptn,
uw-ut-ins-ntp and
uw-rt-ins-ntp have no feasible solutions and need not be studied.
However, the situation is not so simple for weighted graphs even for restricted insertions/deletions.
To see this, note that when we insert
(
resp.
, delete)
an edge, it may decrease
(
resp.
, decrease)
the distance values between many nodes but it may also change
the value of
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)
and therefore it is possible for the ratio

\frac{\text{{\sc Emd}}_{\!\!G}(u,v)}{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)}

to either increase or decrease.

Observation 1

(
a
)

Consider
Problem
wt-rt-ins-ntp and suppose that
w(u,v)\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Then the value of
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)
remains the same no matter which edges are inserted
and therefore
insertion of any edge
cannot
increase the value of

\frac{\text{{\sc Emd}}_{\!\!G}(u,v)}{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)}
.
Thus, in this case
we may assume that in any optimal solution
if an edge between two nodes are inserted
then the weight of the edge is
1
since it provides the maxmimum
decrese of the distance between nodes in the graph and consequently
the maximum decrease in

\text{{\sc Emd}}_{\!\!G}(u,v)

(
this is used in the proof of
Theorem
4
)
.

(
b
)

By the same reasoning as in

(
a
)
,
for
Problem
wt-ut-ins-ntp if
w(u,v)\in\{1,2\}

then
insertion of any edge cannot decrease the value of

\frac{\text{{\sc Emd}}_{\!\!G}(u,v)}{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)}
.
since
w(u,v)
does not change, and we may assume that in any optimal solution
all inserted edges are of weight
1

(
this is used in the proof of
Theorem

.

2.3
Clarifying Remarks on the Time Complexity Issues

To analyze time-complexity for Ricci curvature problems, it is customary
to assume that the distances between relevant nodes have already been
computed and thus the size of the input is proportional to

\mathsf{deg}_{G}(u)+\mathsf{deg}_{G}(v)
(
e.g.
, see publications such as
[
11
]

and
the discussion in the paragraph
preceding Section III in
[
31
]
).
In other words, it is assumed that
that
we are given access to an oracle

\mathcal{O}

that given two query nodes
x
and
y
of the input graph
G

provides the value of
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(x,y)
.
Following the same approach, we also consider
our input size is taken to be proportional to

\mathsf{deg}_{G}(u)+\mathsf{deg}_{G}(v)
.
In this article, we are mainly interested in distinguishing
between polynomial-time computations vs. computational
intractability via
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness and inapproximability results,
so we do not concern ourselves with the precise nature of the polynomial
for polynomial-time algorithms. However, for articles that
do concern with this issue, a revised notion of the input size
should involve the

\mathsf{deg}_{G}(u)+\mathsf{deg}_{G}(v)

plus the actual number of calls made to the oracle
\mathcal{O}
.
Note that the
number of calls made to the oracle
\mathcal{O}

is at most a polynomial in

\mathsf{deg}_{G}(u)+\mathsf{deg}_{G}(v)

and the number of edges inserted/deleted.
This more fine-grain determination of the input size
has no effect on the
nature (polynomial-time vs.
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness and inapproximability results)
on the results in this article.

Conclusions and Future Research

This article
provides a formal framework for studying algorithmic and computational complexity issues
for detecting critical edges in an undirected graph using Ollivier-Ricci curvatures.
Since there are
12
non-trivial version of problems and we have covered only a few of them,
there is considerable opportunity for studying algorithmic aspects of the remaining versions.
As for improvements for results in this article,
curiously the exact perfect matching problem has a polynomial-time
deterministic algorithm if the matching is allowed to differ just by one edge
from perfect matching
[
48
]

and one possible future research work may involve
de-randomizing
our randomized algorithms
using the ideas/results in
[
48
]
.

Another possible future direction of research could be to redefine the curvature
measure by using another distance between two probability distribution instead
of
Emd
. The simplest such distance which can be easily computed is the
total variation
distance
, however this distance would not be very useful in practice. Note that
distances which are not symmetric (
e.g.
, the Kullback-Leibler divergence)
are not be appropriate for usage in curvature measure.
