Effect of different irrigation regimen on sealability and push-out bond strength of biodentine when used for repair of furcal perforation in mandibular permanent molars: An in vitro study
Authors: Karthikeyan Moorthy, Smita Surendran, Selvakarthikeyan Ulaganathan, Velmurugan Natanasabapathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the influence of different irrigation regimens on the sealability and push-out bond strength of Biodentin used for furcal perforation repair in mandibular molars.Materials and Methods:Fifty-six extracted human mandibular molars with standardized 1.6 mm furcal perforations were divided into seven groups according to the irrigation protocol: (1) 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) +17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), (2) 1% NaOCl + 7% maleic acid, (3) 1% NaOCl + 9% HEDP, (4) 2% chlorhexidine (CHX) +17% EDTA, (5) 2% CHX + 7% Maleic Acid, (6) 2% CHX + 9% HEDP, and (7) saline (control). Biodentin was placed to seal the perforation and allowed to set for 24 h. Pushout bond strength was measured using a universal testing machine, and marginal sealability was analyzed under the scanning electron microscopy. Statistical analysis was performed using the one-way analysis of variance and post hoc Tukey tests.Results:Group 3 (1% NaOCl + 9% HEDP) showed the highest bond strength (31.33 ± 25.89 MPa) and the least marginal gap (1.28 ± 0.45 μm), indicating superior adhesion and sealing.Conclusion:The irrigation regimen of 1% NaOCl combined with 9% HEDP enhances Biodentin’s bonding and sealing ability in furcal perforation repair, suggesting its clinical advantage over other protocols.

INTRODUCTION

Perforations are accidental or pathological communications between the pulp chamber or root canal system and the periodontal ligament space. There are various types of perforations namely furcal, lateral root, apical, strip, accidental/mechanical, and pathological perforations.[1] Furcal perforations occur on the floor of the pulp chamber, and in mandibular permanent molars, endodontic therapy is often challenging and it compromises the treatment outcomes if not managed efficiently. Perforation may result from iatrogenic factors such as misaligned access cavity and extensive caries progression. These perforations create an open path between the pulp chamber and periodontium, enabling bacterial contamination, inflammation, and treatment failure if not sealed properly. Timely and effective sealing is critical for preventing microbial ingress and ensuring long-term success.[2] An ideal perforation repair material should have excellent biocompatibility, dimensional stability, easy manipulation, resistance to blood and moisture, and dentin-like mechanical properties.[3]

Biodentine, a calcium silicate-based material, offers high alkalinity, short setting time, and the capability to induce dentin bridge formation. It seals well and supports the healing of periradicular tissues. Its strength and bioactivity make it ideal for furcal perforation repair. However, its success depends on dentin surface conditions, smear layer presence, and the irrigation regimen used before application.[4] Irrigation plays a vital role in endodontic disinfection and affects the bonding characteristics of restorative materials. Different irrigants can influence biodentine’s sealability and push-out bond strength, thus affecting treatment outcomes.[5] The common irrigants include sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), chlorhexidine (CHX), maleic acid, and new bioactive solutions. These agents help eliminate bacteria and may modify the smear layer to improve Biodentine bonding. However, some may weaken the dentin and compromise bonding.[4] Irrigants can alter dentinal tubule structure, affect the setting of Biodentine, and impact adaptation.[6]

7% maleic acid (pKa 1.9) has emerged as a promising smear layer remover, offering better dentin preservation than EDTA.[7] Hydroxyethylidene diphosphonic acid (HEDP) is a biocompatible chelator compatible with NaOCl, removing the smear layer without reducing its antibacterial properties.[8] However, the effects of these chelators on the sealability of biodentine remain untested.

Sealability refers to a material’s ability to form a hermetic seal at the interface between the perforation site and the restorative material, which is critical for preventing microleakage, infection, and treatment failure. The seal’s integrity depends on biodentine’s dimensional stability, adaptability, and interaction with irrigants.[9] Irrigants can alter dentinal tubule structure, affect the setting of biodentine, and impact adaptation. Research shows that smear layer modification can significantly influence sealing effectiveness.[10]

Push-out bond strength measures biodentine’s ability to adhere to dentin. This is essential to resisting masticatory forces and for long-term stability. Irrigation protocols influence dentin’s demineralization-remineralization dynamics, thereby affecting its bond strength. Aggressive irrigants may weaken dentin, whereas others may improve bonding by modifying the smear layer and forming a hybrid layer. Strong, stable bonding is necessary for structural integrity and long-term success.

Although extensive research exists on irrigants and biomaterials, few studies have examined how different irrigation protocols affect the sealability and bond strength of Biodentine in furcal perforation repairs. This study aims to fill this gap by assessing the impact of irrigants on the performance of biodentine in mandibular molars.

Aim

To evaluate the effect of different irrigation regimens on the sealability and push-out bond strength of Biodentine as a repair material for furcal perforations in mandibular permanent molars.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol was presented to the Institutional Review Board at Meenakshi Ammal Dental College, and ethical clearance for the same was obtained (MADC/IEC/II/30/2023).

Sample preparation

Freshly extracted human permanent mandibular molars were collected and stored in 0.9% saline. Teeth were cleaned with an ultrasonic cleaner to remove residual soft tissue or debris. A diamond disk under continuous water irrigation was used to resect the teeth 6 mm from the roof of the furcation. Teeth were mounted in acrylic blocks to provide a stable experimental base. To simulate periodontal ligament conditions, a gelatin sponge (Goodwill Hemosponge, India) was placed beneath the furcation to create a relief space.

Root canal orifices were sealed with gutta-percha to prevent irrigants and materials from entering the canal system. Furcal perforations were created centrally in the furcation area using an Endo Access Bur size 2, (Dentsply, Switzerland) producing a standardized diameter of 1.6 mm for all the samples.

The samples were randomly assigned into seven groups based on the irrigation regimen, with eight samples per group (n = 8). The sample size was determined after a pilot study with 35 samples (5 per group).

Each irrigation cycle used a 27G open-ended needle (SuperEndo, India). This systematic design enabled the standardized comparison of how different irrigants influence Biodentine’s bond strength and sealing properties in furcal perforation repair.

Furcation repair and irrigation protocol

All specimens had perforation sites repaired using Biodentine, mixed as per the manufacturer’s instructions and placed with a micro-applicator under a dental microscope [Figure 1]. It was allowed to set for 24 h. Postsetting, respective irrigation regimens were applied for 60 s using a 27G needle, followed by a distilled water rinse and gentle drying with absorbent paper points.

Perforation sealed with biodentine

Push-out bond strength testing

After 24-h moist storage, samples underwent push-out bond strength testing with a universal testing machine (Instron, USA). Positioned axially in a custom fixture, compressive load was applied at 1 mm/min until debonding. Maximum force (N) was recorded and bond strength calculated in MPa [Figure 2].

Hard tissue microtome for sectioning of tooth and push-out bond strength assessed using the instron testing machine

Microleakage assessment and scanning electron microscopy analysis

Postbond testing, samples were sectioned with a hard tissue microtome (Fig No: 3). The sealed perforation was gold sputter-coated and examined under the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Tescan, Czech Republic) at various magnifications to evaluate interfacial gap and material adaptation.

Scanning electron microscopy images for interfacial analysis

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. Since the assumptions of normality and equal variances were met, parametric tests were applied. To assess intergroup differences across the seven experimental groups for each variable, namely force at displacement (n), push-out bond strength (MPa), and marginal gap (μm). A one-way analysis of variance followed by post hoc Tukey HSD test was performed. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 considered highly significant.

RESULTS

Push-out bond strength of biodentine after irrigation

Significant differences in force of displacement were observed among irrigant groups (F = 12.398, P < 0.001). Group 3 (1% NaOCl + 9% HEDP) showed the highest mean force (1105.70 ± 673.55 N), followed by Group 1 (1% NaOCl + 17% EDTA) (586.60 ± 84.02 N), and Group 6 (2% CHX + 9% HEDP) (438.69 ± 154.53 N). The lowest was Group 7 (saline control) (123.09 ± 99.94 N). Significant differences in bond strength (F = 2.607, P = 0.028) were also noted, with Group 3 showing highest (31.33 ± 25.89 MPa), followed by Group 1 (30.74 ± 28.26 MPa) and Group 7 lowest (4.59 ± 11.96 MPa). Post hoc analysis confirmed Group 3’s superior performance (P < 0.001) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Successful repair of furcal perforations remains a pivotal challenge in endodontics critical for preserving tooth integrity and avoiding treatment failure. Furcal perforations in mandibular molars occur in 0.9%–17.6% of endodontic procedures.[11] These defects occurring in the furcation area can compromise success and must be promptly sealed with a biocompatible material.[1] The push-out bond strength of repair materials reflects their sealing ability under stress without leakage. Such materials should allow the safe placement of postendodontic restoratives without extrusion beyond the defect. The push-out test, applying increasing pressure until debonding, reliably evaluates adhesion under clinical-like conditions.[12]

In this in vitro study, Biodentine (Septodont, France), an alternative to mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) was used offering sealing ability, biocompatibility and biomineralization. The capsule formulation enhances its physical properties and its tag-like calcium-phosphate-rich structures promote dislodgement resistance.[13] Compared to ProRoot MTA, Biodentine has higher radiopacity and shorter setting time with less discoloration and higher push out bond strength.[1415] Biodentine has been widely studied and time tested for it’s performance in furcal perforation repairs as seen in previous lab-based studies.[1617]

Mandibular molars were selected due to their vulnerability to iatrogenic furcal perforations, especially during mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canal access. A 1.6-mm diameter perforation was created using an Endo Access Bur size 2 (Dentsply Maillefer). Simulating clinical disinfection, combinations of NaOCl and chelators (EDTA, Maleic Acid and HEDP) were applied. Two percent CHX, a broad-spectrum antimicrobial was also tested.

Push-out bond strength

Biodentine’s ability to resist dislodgment depends on adhesion to dentin. HEDP’s superior performance lies in its smear layer removal and dentin preservation. Unlike EDTA, HEDP enables continuous chelation with NaOCl, exposing collagen while maintaining mineral structure.[18] Krishnegowda et al., stated that Biodentine demonstrated higher push out bond 1 day after its setting when a combination of NaOCl and HEDP was used.[19] An earlier study using, BioAKT (Silver doped citric acid) was shown to have higher pushout bond strength possibly because of better micromechanical retention and biomineralizing properties.[20] The combination of HEDP with hypochlorite has not been evaluated before in this clinical simulation.

Sealability

SEM analysis of marginal gaps confirmed with superior results in Group 3 (NaOCl + HEDP, 1.28 μm) and least with Group 4: (CHX + 17% EDTA, 37.60 μm). Group 4 precipitates impairs bonding and shows larger gaps due to over demineralization.[21] Whereas in Group 3, HEDP preserves collagen fibrils supporting micromechanical adhesion and removes smear layer without damaging the dentin.[22] Saline left natural smear layer, superficially aiding Biodentine adaptation.[22] Maleic Acid, though a potent chelator, may cause over-demineralization, reducing structural integrity.[7]

Clinical implications

Group 3 (1% NaOCl + 9% HEDP) is recommended for improved bonding and sealability in furcal repairs. CHX-based protocols, despite antimicrobial benefits, are less suitable for bonding.[23] Saline is ineffective as a sole irrigant.

Limitations and future directions

In vitro nature limits real-world relevance; future studies should assess long-term bonding under stress. Advanced imaging (e.g., Transmission electron microscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy) could clarify Biodentine-HEDP interactions. Exploring bioactive irrigants and surfactants may enhance adhesion further.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitation of this study, it was concluded that the current study provides evidence supporting the use of 1% NaOCl combined with HEDP as the most effective irrigation regimen for optimizing the performance of Biodentine in furcal perforation repair.
