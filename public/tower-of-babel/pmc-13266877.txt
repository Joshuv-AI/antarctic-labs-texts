Segmentectomy for treating centrally located pulmonary hamartoma: a retrospective analysis of 22 cases
Authors: Xiaohui Xu, Yushang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the characteristics and outcomes of 22 pulmonary hamartoma patients with centrally localized lesions undergoing segmentectomy, and to propose a new classification system for this disease based on its location.MethodsConsecutive pulmonary hamartoma patients who underwent segmentectomy from January 1, 2016 to June 30, 2021 were included in this retrospective study. Patients’ age, sex, symptoms, lesion location in the lung parenchyma, size, type of operation, and adverse events were analyzed.ResultsAll lesions were completely removed by segmentectomy with lung parenchyma and function maximumly preserved. None of the patients experienced in-hospital mortality, severe postoperative complications, or poor recovery outcomes. No recurrence of hamartomas was observed during postoperative follow-up. Pulmonary hamartoma was classified as the peripheral, central-superficial, or central-core type based on its location, with corresponding recommended surgical approaches of wedge resection, enucleation, segmentectomy, or lobectomy, respectively.ConclusionsA new, objective, and reproducible classification system based on lesion location was established. The corresponding surgical approach also reflects real-world practice. Segmentectomy is safe and effective for the treatment of the central-core type of pulmonary hamartoma, providing an alternative option for patients that previously required lobectomy.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Segmentectomy is indicated for the treatment of central core-type hamartomas, and its safety and efficacy have been demonstrated in this cohort of cases.

What is known and what is new?

• Peripheral pulmonary hamartomas, as benign tumors, are typically best treated with wedge resection. Prior to the maturation of segmental resection techniques, central core-type pulmonary hamartomas had to be managed with lobectomy.

• Through the study of pulmonary hamartoma distribution within the lung, building upon the existing central-peripheral classification of pulmonary tumors, we propose three subtypes of pulmonary hamartoma distribution: peripheral type, central superficial type, and central core type. We further validated that central core-type hamartomas are suitable for segmentectomy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our newly proposed classification system for pulmonary hamartomas and the corresponding treatment approaches have been validated in this cohort of cases. However, due to the relatively small proportion of central core-type hamartomas, the sample size remains limited. Clinicians may encounter challenges and risks not yet encountered in this cohort during clinical practice. Therefore, our recommendations require further refinement as the case volume increases.

Introduction

Hamartoma is a benign tumor that can arise in multiple human organs. Pulmonary hamartoma, which typically occurs in the lungs, is the most common benign lung tumor, accounting for 77% of cases (1,2). Pulmonary hamartoma can be diagnosed at any age, but is most common in patients aged 60–69 years. There is no evidence of sex predilection. Popcorn-like calcifications are the classic radiologic feature of pulmonary hamartoma. Following the widespread use of high-resolution computed tomography (CT), an increasing number of pulmonary hamartomas are being detected in the clinic.

The underlying mechanisms of hamartoma are not fully understand; however, recombinations between chromosomal bands 6p21 and 14q24 have been identified, suggesting that hamartomas represent neoplasms rather than developmental abnormalities (3,4). Pulmonary hamartomas are composed of various components, including smooth muscle, mucous gland, cartilaginous, and fibrous tissue, and are classified as adenoleiomyomatous hamartoma, fibroleiomyomatous hamartoma, cystoadenomatous hamartoma, and pulmonary hamartoma with mucous glands based on the dominant tissue content. The complex tissue composition of pulmonary hamartoma results in diverse and atypical imaging. Only 23–46% of pulmonary hamartomas show calcification on CT images, and most lesions do not exhibit the popcorn-like calcification pattern (5,6). Pulmonary hamartoma, carcinoid, and early-stage lung cancer present with similar morphologic features on CT, making differential diagnosis and the selection of patients for surgical resection or radiological follow-up difficult.

Even pulmonary hamartomas identified as benign by CT-guided biopsy have to be treated by surgical resection. Wedge resection and enucleation are the preferred treatments for patients with pulmonary hamartoma, as benign tumors do not normally require aggressive excision margins (5-7). However, lobectomy may be appropriate for centrally localized lesions due to limitations related to diagnosis and treatment, and patient anxiety. In a study of 433 patients with pulmonary hamartoma who underwent surgery resection at Peking Union Medical College Hospital from 2000 to present, 83.3% underwent wedge resection and 6.5% underwent enucleation, as the lesions were located close to hilum. Before 2016, over 10% of lesions required lobectomy (7). We applied segmentectomy, which was initially developed for the treatment of early-stage lung cancer, to central type pulmonary hamartomas unsuitable for wedge resection or enucleation, achieving curable outcomes while preserving pulmonary function. This study retrospectively analyzed the efficacy and safety of segmentectomy in 22 patients with central pulmonary hamartoma, and proposed a new classification system for this disease based on its location.

Methods

Patients

This study included 22 pulmonary hamartoma patients with centrally localized lesions who underwent segmentectomy from January 1, 2016 to June 30, 2021 at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Patients were excluded from the study if they had indications for wedge resection or enucleation, or had lung cancer or other malignancies. The patients’ age, sex, symptoms, lesion location in the lung parenchyma, size, type of surgery, and adverse events were recorded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Peking Union Medical College, and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Surgical techniques

All 22 patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia with double-lumen tube tracheal intubation. Twenty patients underwent three-port video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) with a 1.5-cm incision for the camera through the 7th intercostal space at the midaxillary line, a 3.0–5.5-cm utility port through the 4th intercostal space at the anterior axillary line, and an additional 1.5-cm utility port at the internal lateral aspect of the inferior angle of the scapula through the 7th intercostal space. Two patients with mass diameters greater than 5.0 cm underwent posterolateral thoracotomy through the 5th intercostal space. The surgeon detected the lesion location on the surface of the lung by finger or oval forceps. However, it was difficult to detect lesions less than 1.0 cm on the collapsed lobe in our study.

Guided by high-resolution CT and Vitaworks, the arteries and segmental bronchi of the targeted segment were dissected, the intersegmental plane was then identified using the inflation-deflation line method, the resection line was marked by energy equipment, and the targeted segment containing the lesion was removed using a stapling device. For lesions in S6 or S8, the segmental arteries were dissected at the entrance of the thoracic cavity, which revealed the boundary between the pink, incompletely collapsed, targeted segment and the collapsed, unaffected segments. Therefore, the inflation-deflation line method was sometimes unnecessary, and the resection could be performed directly along the formed borderline.

The nodule was retrieved from the resected segment and sent for frozen pathology examination. Intraoperative frozen section evaluation revealed neuroendocrine differentiation in one patient, for whom a segmental lymph node (group 12) was also sent for frozen section evaluation, and specimens from the hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes were collected. None of the thoracoscopic surgeries required conversion to open thoracotomy. The mean operative time was 84.5±20.3 minutes (range, 53–133 minutes). The mean blood loss was 90.9±51.5 mL (range, 50–210 mL). The mean postoperative hospital stay was 4.0±2.5 days (2–6 days). Postoperative pathology on paraffin-embedded sections confirmed hamartoma in all patients.

Statistical analysis

The data collected in this study were analyzed using the SPSS 19.0 statistical software package. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Of the study patients, 13 were male (59.1%) and nine were female (40.9%) (Table 1). The patients were aged 32–67 years (three were aged 30–39 years, three were aged 40–49 years, seven were aged 50–59 years, and nine were aged 60–69 years). Six patients in our study were smokers (five males and one female). Most patients (18, 81.8%) were asymptomatic, while three (13.6%) presented with lung infections and one (4.5%) with coughing.

All patients underwent preoperative chest CT, with a mean mass diameter of 2.8 cm (0.9–9.2 cm) on pulmonary windows. Three patients (13.6%) had lesions <1.0 cm, seven (31.8%) had lesions 1.0–2.0 cm (including 2.0 cm), four (18.2%) had lesions 2.0–3.0 cm, four (18.2%) had lesions 3.0–4.0 cm, two (9.1%) had lesions 4.0–5.0 cm, one (4.5%) had a lesion 6.0–7.0 cm, and one(4.5%) had a lesion 9.0–10.0 cm. Calcifications were observed in eight patients (36.4%) and fat density was observed in two patients (9.1%) on the mediastinal window images. Twelve (54.5%) patients underwent additional positron emission tomography-CT, revealing a mean standardized uptake value (SUV) of 1.58, with a SUV maximum <1.5 in eight cases (96%), a SUV maximum between 1.5 and 2.5 in three cases, and a SUV maximum >2.5 in one patient.

All the patients had a single nodule. The lesions were localized in the right lung in 13 cases (59.1%) and in the left lung in nine cases (40.9%). Specifically, eight (36.4%) lesions were located in right upper lobe, five (22.7%) in the right lower lobe, two (9.1%) in the left upper lobe, and seven (31.8%) in the left lower lobe. Four of the 22 patients had lesions located in S9, S10, or S9+10, for whom three-dimensional visualization reconstruction was conducted using DICOM data through Vitaworks. The location of lesions in each segment was summarized (Table 2). No patient underwent percutaneous lung biopsy or bronchoscopic biopsy before surgery. The time for radiographic follow-up (from the date of first radiographic diagnosis to the date of surgery) was less than 1 year for 18 patients, and was 15 months, 3 years, 12 years, and 15 years for the remaining four patients, respectively.

No deaths or re-operations were reported during the postoperative period. Abnormal laboratory test results included increased white blood cell counts, elevated D-Dimer levels, and oxygen desaturation, all of which resolved after symptomatic treatments, such as antibiotics, low-molecular-weight heparin, and oxygen inhalation. Seven patients suffered from hemoptysis 3 days after surgery; their symptoms were alleviated after the administration of Yunnan Baiyao. Continuing air leakage was observed in three patients under closed thoracic drainage on the second post-operative day, which was treated with a intrathoracic injection of hypertonic glucose and postural drainage, and resolved within 1 week.

The mean follow-up period was 50.2±17.9 months (range, 16–73 months). No local recurrence or new lesions were reported. The remaining lung inflated well after the removal of the affected segment. Aware that pulmonary hamartomas are unlikely to recur once removed, most patients (n=20) refused to undergo lung function testing. Two female patients—a 38-year-old who underwent RS3 resection and a 32-year-old who underwent LS6 resection—were re-examined 6 months after surgery, and their results showed that the segmentectomy had not adversely affected their postoperative lung function.

Discussion

Tumor characteristics and patient preferences are the two main factors influencing decision-making in the management of solid tumors. Surgery is indicated for patients who are willing to undergo surgical resection or for those in whom malignancy cannot be excluded based on clinical and supplementary examinations. In cases of suspected pulmonary hamartoma presenting as a solitary solid nodule with characteristic features such as a popcorn‑like appearance, punctuate calcifications on mediastinal windows, low SUV uptake, or hamartoma pathologically confirmed by fine-needle aspiration, follow-up may be considered for patients unwilling to undergo surgery; otherwise, surgery is warranted. The operative strategies of hamartoma differ from those for lung malignancy in terms of the procedures used to preserve pulmonary function. To achieve function-preserving radical resection, preoperative evaluation of the location of the solid tumor is essential to optimize surgical planning to maximize the preservation of lung parenchyma and function while removing the tumor.

Lung cancer is commonly classified as central-type or peripheral-type based on lung location. Central-type tumors affect the segmental, lobar, or main bronchus, while peripheral-type tumors affect the remaining part of the lung (8). Although this classification is used to guide the selection of lobectomy, sleeve lobectomy, or carinoplasty in lung cancer, it is not applicable to pulmonary hamartoma. Bandoh et al. conducted a survey to determine the definition of central-type tumors; however, the definition remained unclear due to a lack of uniformity (8). In the survey, using the hilum as the center, 84% of the respondents defined central lung tumors as those located in the inner one-third of the hemithorax, and peripheral lung tumors as those located in the outer one-third, although there was controversy regarding tumors located in the middle third (Line A in Figure 1). In addition, 14% of the respondents defined tumors based on straight lines in the sagittal plane of the chest (Line B in Figure 1). However, none of these definitions are suitable for the classification of pulmonary hamartoma.

Methods to delineate the thirds of the hemithorax: A lines (concentric lines arising from hilum) and B lines (straight lines in sagittal plane).

Based on previous research and our 10 years of experience in the surgical treatment of over 400 hamartomas, we proposed a new classification system for pulmonary hamartoma comprising the peripheral-type, central-superficial-type, and central-core-type (Figure 2). The peripheral type is defined as tumors located outside the midpoint between the hilum and the chest wall, which can generally be removed by wedge resection for fast-frozen pathology. The central-superficial type is defined as tumors located in the midpoint between the hilum and the chest wall, without bronchi or vessels located between the lesion and the nearest pulmonary membrane; Wedge resection is not suitable for this type of tumor due to its proximity to the hilum; however, enucleation surgery can be performed. The central-core type is defined as tumors located in the inner half of the hemithorax, with segmental bronchi or vessels located between the lesion and the nearest pulmonary membrane, which can be injured by enucleation surgery, resulting in a loss of lung function.

Dark blue region indicates peripheral type, light blue region indicates central-superficial-type, and yellow region indicates central-core type. Nodules 1 and 2 were peripheral-type nodules that could be resected by wedge resection, nodule 3 was central-superficial type that could be resected by enucleation, and nodule 4 was central-core type that could be resected by segmentectomy.

Prior to the development of segmental resection, lobectomy was the only surgical option for the treatment of central-core type tumors. Even when masses were strongly suspected to be benign, previous studies reported that most pulmonary hamartomas were treated with lobectomy. As a benign tumor, a negative surgical margin is not required for pulmonary hamartoma; therefore, segmentectomy is preferable for tumor removal, enabling pathological diagnosis while maximizing lung function preservation. If a malignancy, such as non-small cell lung cancer, carcinoid, or adenoid cystic carcinoma, is detected by fast-frozen pathology during segmentectomy, further lobectomy plus lymph node dissection can be performed immediately (Figure 3).

According to the analysis of the 22 pulmonary hamartomas resected during the study period, the ratio of the number of tumor in left to right lung (103 vs. 119) was similar to the ratio of the number of involved segments in the left to right lung (8 vs. 10), indicating that pulmonary hamartomas are evenly distributed in the lungs without any particular location predilection. In the current study, the location of lesions was evenly distributed across all lobes and segments with the exception of the middle lobe and the lingual segment. It may be that the tetrahedron-like shape of the middle lobe and lingual segment provides more directions for performing enucleation surgery without injuring great vessels (Figure 4).

Right middle lobe (A) and LS4+5 (B) both have similar tetrahedron shapes with surfaces adjacent to upper lobe, lower lobe, mediastinum, pericardium, and chest wall.

Additionally, we recommend segmentectomy for central-core-type hamartomas based on the emerging evidence that hamartomas may coexist with lung cancer. Currently, (I) there is no evidence of malignant transformation in hamartoma patients; (II) hamartoma is not a risk factor associated with lung cancer; and (III) pulmonary hamartoma and lung cancer are associated with similar risk factors. However, previous studies have shown that patients with hamartoma have a 6% higher probability of developing lung cancer than the general population, and hamartomas coexisting with lung cancer have also been observed in the clinic (9). Therefore, segmentectomy is the preferred option for preserving adequate lung function in hamartoma patients, enabling them to tolerate further resection for lung cancer.

The operation technique employed in this study was similar to that employed for segmentectomy in early-stage lung cancer and was not associated with severe post-operative complications. However, it should be noted that the hard texture of pulmonary hamartomas makes them mobile in the lung parenchyma, which can cause tumor displacement under the pressure of the stapler when cutting the segment, leaving the tumor near the surface of the segment in the body. One solution is to remove the tumor when it is near the cutting surface of the segment before the target segment is completely resected, followed by resection of the remaining segment.

Lobectomy can be used in the treatment of central-core-type pulmonary hamartomas, and was performed in some cases during the study period, including cases with concurrent hamartoma and lung cancer in different segments, as well as cases in which pulmonary parenchyma was damaged by hamartoma-induced pneumonia. Lobectomy continues to play a critical role in the treatment of hamartoma. Efforts should be made to maximize lung function preservation in patients with hamartoma by using wedge resection, enucleation, or segmentectomy. Limited thoracotomy may be used instead of thoracoscopic surgery when the goal is to achieve minimal resection of pulmonary parenchyma rather than minimal incisions.

Our study had some limitations. Data on the precise preoperative diagnosis was limited, which was also a common issue in previous studies. Hamartoma is difficult to diagnose before surgery. CT or contrast-enhanced CT is the most common evaluation method for hamartoma; however, classical features such as fat alternating with foci of calcification have been observed in only 21% of cases, even with additional magnetic resonance imaging (10,11). Further, only 26% of patients can be definitively diagnosed by percutaneous transthoracic needle biopsy (12). If definitively diagnosed before surgery, hamartoma can be effectively managed for years with conservative treatment or observation, while improper treatment can cause lung damages and a decline in quality of life. Future research should focus on the precise preoperative diagnosis of hamartoma to avoid unnecessary surgery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, segmentectomy is safe and effective for patients with centrally localized pulmonary hamartoma, enabling the maximal preservation of pulmonary parenchyma. Based on our experience in treating hamartomas since 2000, we proposed a new classification system consisting of the peripheral-type, central-superficial-type, and central-core-type. This classification system is also applicable for the treatment of other benign tumors of the lung, such as pulmonary sclerosing hemangioma. Further studies in larger patient populations are underway to assess the feasibility of this new classification system.
