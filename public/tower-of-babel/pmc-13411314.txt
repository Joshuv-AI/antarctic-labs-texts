Investigating spiritual well-being and its relationship with health behaviors, depression, and academic achievement in medical students: a structural equation modeling study
Authors: Mehrdad Karimi, MoradAli Zareipour, Esmayil Zeynali, Rahim Sharafkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSpiritual well-being (SWB) is a key health dimension crucial for quality of life. For university students facing significant academic pressures, SWB may be a vital resource associated with lower depression, healthier behaviors, and higher academic achievement. This study aimed to investigate the complex interrelationships among these variables in medical students.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 362 students from Khoy University of Medical Sciences via census sampling. The participants had a mean age of 21.67 ± 2.98 years. The majority of participants were female (55.5%) and single (88.7%). Validated self-report questionnaires were used to measure spiritual well-being (Paloutzian SWB scale), health behaviors (Ahmadi Questionnaire), depression (PHQ-9), and academic performance (GPA). Data were analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to test the direct and indirect pathways within the conceptual model.ResultsThe final structural model demonstrated good fit (χ²/df = 2.34, RMSEA = 0.060, CFI = 0.91). Spiritual well-being had significant direct positive association with all health behaviors: physical activity (β = 0.35, p < 0.001), healthy diet (β = 0.48, p < 0.001), and no smoking (β = 0.58, p < 0.001). SWB also had a significant direct negative association with depression (β=-0.50, p < 0.001). Among health behaviors, only no smoking showed a significant direct association with lower depression (β=-0.18, p = 0.04). Depression showed a significant negative direct association with academic performance (β=-0.35, p = 0.007). Significant indirect effects revealed an association where SWB was related to better academic performance through its association with increased health behaviors and lower depression.ConclusionSpiritual well-being is a significant factor in comprehensive student health, with its associations with healthier behaviors and lower depression being subsequently linked to academic achievement. Integrating spiritual well-being into university support and educational programs is essential, underscoring the need for targeted interventions in this domain.Keywords: Academic performance, Depression, Health behavior, Spirituality, Structural equation modeling, Students, Well-being

Introduction

The university period is a time of significant personal, intellectual, and social transformation for students, often marked by academic pressures, psychological issues such as depression, and the need for effective health behaviors [1]. Spiritual well-being (SWB) defined as beliefs, values, meaning in life, and connection to a higher power enhances resilience, reduces stress, and improves quality of life, helping individuals maintain psychological balance when facing challenges [2].

Students frequently face stressors (academic, social, and financial) that are known to be associated with poorer mental health, including depression and unhealthy behaviors. In this context, SWB has been described as a protective factor, with previous studies showing correlations with greater hope, life meaning, and spiritually-oriented social support, as well as lower depressive symptoms and greater motivation for healthy behaviors [3, 4]. Doolittle et al. reported a strong inverse correlation between spiritual involvement and depression [5]. Similarly, Moura et al. found that medical students with higher levels of spirituality exhibited significantly greater resilience, which was in turn correlated with lower vulnerability to depression [6], and a study in Morocco confirmed that religiosity was associated with lower depression among nursing and medical students [7].

Additionally, individuals with stronger SWB typically exhibit more constructive health behaviors, such as a healthy diet, regular physical activity, and effective stress management. This association likely operates through spiritual beliefs shaping health-related attitudes and subjective norms [8, 9]. Bożek et al. demonstrated that both spirituality and health-promoting behaviors contribute positively to psychological well-being, with stronger spirituality linked to greater engagement in health-enhancing behaviors [10]. Alinejad et al. also found that SWB strongly predicted the adoption of health-promoting lifestyles [11]. Depression remains one of the most significant psychological challenges among students, with widespread negative correlations with academic performance and quality of life [12]. However, SWB has been identified as a significant correlate of lower depression; students with higher SWB tend to report greater mental resilience and fewer depressive symptoms [13, 14]. Previous research has also suggested that SWB is associated with lower anxiety and depression, higher intrinsic motivation, and better concentration, which in turn are related to academic achievement (AA) [13, 15]. Napone et al. reported a positive correlation between students’ spirituality and their academic performance, suggesting that higher SWB is associated with better studying effectiveness through greater motivation and concentration [16]. It is hypothesized that depression is a mediator in the association between SWB and AA, suggesting a pathway of higher SWB → lower depression → higher AA.

Despite these promising findings, previous research has largely examined these variables separately. No known study has simultaneously modeled SWB, health-promoting behaviors, depression, and AA within an integrated analytical framework. This gap is notable because students, as future builders of society, face multiple concurrent pressures that affect their mental health and academic performance simultaneously [17, 18]. Addressing this gap is essential for developing comprehensive prevention and intervention strategies in higher education settings.

Therefore, the present study was conducted to examine the structural associations between SWB, Health Behaviors (HB), Depression (D), and AA in a student population using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). Based on the theoretical framework, the hypothetical path model considers SWB as the main exogenous variable, which is hypothesized to show direct and indirect associations with AA, with HB and D acting as hypothesized mediators. Specifically, it is hypothesized that SWB is positively and directly associated with HB and AA, while SWB is negatively and directly associated with D. Furthermore, D is expected to be negatively and directly associated with both AA and HB, whereas HB is hypothesized to be positively and directly associated with AA. Finally, it is predicted that D mediates the association between SWB and AA, and similarly, HB mediates the association between SWB and AA.

Methods

This study utilized a cross-sectional analytical study to investigate the interrelationships among spiritual well-being, health behaviors, depression, and academic performance in medical students. To examine these complex relationships and test the hypothesized structural paths, we employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). A cross-sectional approach is suitable for this purpose as it allows for the simultaneous assessment of these variables and the proposed mediation pathways within the target population. The study was conducted among students at Khoy University of Medical Sciences. At the time of data collection, the total eligible population consisted of 500 enrolled students. We used a census sampling method, inviting every student to participate. Regarding the sampling procedure, the researchers visited different classrooms and educational departments in person. The objectives of the study were clearly explained to all potential participants, with strong emphasis placed on the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. Following this, written informed consent was obtained from students who agreed to participate. The participants then completed the validated self-report questionnaires in person (paper-and-pencil format) under the supervision of the research team. Data collection took place over a 15-day period. Out of the total population, 362 students completed the survey, resulting in a participation rate of 72.4%. Students who chose not to participate or who did not return a fully completed questionnaire were not included in the final analysis. Regarding the suitability of our sample size for SEM analysis, we adhered to established methodological guidelines. It is recommended that SEM studies have a sample size exceeding 200 cases and maintain a favorable ratio of observations to the number of estimated parameters in the model [19]. Our final model estimated 75 parameters. With a final sample size of 362, our study meets these recommended thresholds, ensuring adequate statistical power for robust parameter estimation and reliable model testing. The study protocol received ethical approval from the relevant committee (IR.KHOY.REC.1402.006). Before participation, the aims of the study were clearly explained to all potential participants, with strong emphasis placed on the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. Following this, written informed consent was obtained. Data collection took place over a 15-day period, during which participants self-administered the validated questionnaires.

Measurement tools

The questionnaire consisted of four sections: demographic information, spiritual well-being, health behaviors, and depression severity.

Demographic information

The demographic questionnaire included sections on age, gender, marital status, field of study, year of university entry, place of residence (local or non-local), and self-assessed economic status. Academic performance was measured using the students’ Grade Point Average (GPA) throughout their academic period.

Spiritual Well-Being (SWB)

The Spiritual Well-Being (SWB) questionnaire developed by Paloutzian was utilized to assess the spiritual well-being of the participants. This 20-item questionnaire is designed to measure two main dimensions: religious well-being and existential well-being. Each item is rated on a Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of the individual’s Spiritual Well-Being. The SWB questionnaire has been widely used in various studies and has demonstrated high reliability and validity. It provides valuable insights into the participants’ sense of purpose, life satisfaction, and connection to a higher power or spiritual beliefs. The scores from the questionnaire can range from 20 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater spiritual well-being. The Persian version of the SWB questionnaire has been extensively validated through various methods, including content validity, construct validity, and concurrent validity. The reliability of the Persian version has been assessed using methods such as test-retest reliability and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha). These characteristics make the Persian version of the SWB questionnaire a reliable and valid tool for assessing spiritual well-being in various populations, including students [20]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Spiritual Well-Being (SWB) scale was found to be 0.88.

Health behavior questionnaire

To assess health behaviors, the standard questionnaire developed by Ahmadi et al. [21] was employed. This instrument consists of 9 items measuring three dimensions: physical activity (1 item), diet (5 items), and cigarette and hookah smoking (3 items). The items are scored on a three-point Likert scale including “always” (scored 3), “sometimes” (scored 2), and “never” (scored 1). Accordingly, the total score ranges from 9 to 27, with higher scores indicating better health behaviors. It is worth noting that the content validity and reliability of this questionnaire were confirmed in the study by Ahmadi et al. [21], with a reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.74.

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was used to measure depression severity. This instrument was previously validated by Dadfar et al. [22]It consists of 9 items scored on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Higher scores indicate greater severity of depressive symptoms. In the present study, the reliability of this instrument was obtained with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.89.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24 and AMOS version 24. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Prior to inferential analyses, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The results indicated that the distributions of the main study variables did not significantly deviate from normality (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric statistical tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine bivariate relationships among spiritual well-being, health behaviors, depression, and academic performance.

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was applied to evaluate the hypothesized direct and indirect relationships among variables. Spiritual well-being was specified as the exogenous latent variable, health behaviors (physical activity, healthy diet, and non-smoking) and depression as mediators, and academic performance as the endogenous outcome variable. Model fit was assessed using multiple fit indices, including Chi-square to DF1, SRMR1, RMSEA2, GFI3, AGFI4, NFI5, IFI6, CFI7, TLI8., based on commonly accepted thresholds [23].

Demographic variables including gender, semester, economic status, and residence were entered as control variables in the modified model. These variables were selected based on previous literature indicating their potential associations with the main study variables. Specifically, previous studies have shown that gender is associated with differences in spiritual well-being, depression, and health behaviors [24, 25]; academic semester may reflect cumulative exposure to academic stress, which is related to mental health outcomes [17, 26]; economic status has been linked to academic performance and health behaviors [27] .; and residency status (dormitory vs. native) may influence students’ quality of life and psychological well-being [28, 29]. Non-significant paths (p > 0.20) were removed to improve model parsimony and interpretability.

The normality of the continuous observed variables was assessed prior to SEM analysis. Given the sufficient sample size (n = 362), which provides robustness against non-normality for Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation, we evaluated skewness and kurtosis values, with all indices falling within acceptable ranges (absolute values < 2). Visual inspection of Q-Q plots also supported the normality assumption. Therefore, ML estimation, which is robust under these conditions, was deemed appropriate for the SEM analysis [19, 30]. The validity of the measurement model was first assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for the latent constructs were calculated from the CFA results based on Fornell and Larcker’s (1981) criteria [31]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which requires that the square root of the AVE for each construct be greater than its highest correlation with any other construct. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Assessment of discriminant validity

To address the concern regarding high correlations among constructs, we assessed discriminant validity using the Fornell-Larcker criterion. According to this criterion, discriminant validity is established if the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (√AVE) for each construct is greater than its highest correlation with any other construct.

As shown in Table 2, the √AVE values were 0.714 for SWB, 0.678 for religious well-being, 0.693 for existential well-being, 0.632 for health behavior, 0.728 for diet, 0.656 for no smoking, and 0.721 for depression.

The high correlations observed in Table 3 between SWB and its subscales (religious: r = 0.85; existential: r = 0.86) naturally exceed the √AVE of SWB (0.714). This is theoretically expected, as subscales are components of the same higher-order construct and share substantial common variance. This reflects adequate internal consistency rather than a lack of discriminant validity. Importantly, the correlation between the two subscales (religious and existential) was 0.47, which is lower than their respective √AVE values (0.678 and 0.693), supporting discriminant validity between them.

Furthermore, the correlations between depression and other constructs (e.g., SWB: r = -0.34) were all lower than √AVE of depression (0.721). Similarly, the correlation between health behavior and depression (r = -0.25) was lower than √AVE of health behavior (0.632).

Thus, despite some expected high correlations between a latent construct and its dimensions, the Fornell-Larcker criterion confirms adequate discriminant validity across distinct constructs in this study. No multicollinearity issues were detected that could affect the stability of structural model estimates.

Direct effects

Spiritual Well-Being (SWB) showed significant positive direct associations with all components of health-promoting behaviors: Physical Activity (β = 0.35, p < 0.001), Healthy Diet (β = 0.48, p < 0.001), and No Smoking (β = 0.58, p < 0.001). Furthermore, SWB showed a substantial negative direct association with Depression (β = -0.50, p < 0.001). Among the health behaviors, only ‘No Smoking’ showed a significant direct association with lower depression (β = -0.18, p = 0.04). Finally, Depression showed a significant negative direct association with Academic Performance (GPA) (β = -0.35, p = 0.007). The direct path from SWB to GPA was not statistically significant (β = 0.15, p = 0.47), which is consistent with the presence of mediation.

Indirect effects and mediation

The analysis of indirect pathways revealed the pathways through which SWB is associated with academic achievement. SWB had a significant total indirect association with GPA (β = 0.15, p = 0.03). This association was primarily carried through two key mediated sequences: (1) SWB → Health Behaviors (particularly No Smoking, β = 0.58) → lower Depression (β = -0.18) → higher GPA (β = -0.35), and (2) SWB → lower Depression (β = -0.50) → higher GPA (β = -0.35). This pattern is consistent with full mediation of the SWB-GPA association by health behaviors and depression.

Role of demographics

Demographic variables also played significant roles, both directly and indirectly via SWB. For instance, female gender was directly associated with higher SWB (β = 0.21, p = 0.002) but also higher depression (β = 0.16, p = 0.009). However, through the mediating role of SWB, being female had significant positive indirect association with health behaviors and ultimately on GPA. Advancing in semester was linked to a decrease in SWB (β = -0.15, p = 0.02), which in turn negatively related to physical activity and had positive association with depression indirectly. Native residency was associated with higher SWB (β = 0.15, p = 0.015), leading to beneficial indirect effects on all health behaviors, lower depression, and higher GPA.

Total effects

The total associations (direct plus indirect) for each pathway are also presented in Table 4. The total association between SWB and GPA was significant (β = 0.30, p = 0.038), indicating that although the direct path from SWB to GPA was not significant (β = 0.15, p = 0.47), the combined indirect pathways through health behaviors and depression showed a significant overall association. Furthermore, the total association between SWB and depression was significant (β = -0.56, p = 0.001), which was largely attributable to its strong direct association (β = -0.50, p < 0.001). Among demographic variables, gender showed a significant total association with physical activity (β = -0.26, p = 0.001), and economic status showed a significant total association with performance (β = -0.22, p = 0.006). These total association estimates provide a comprehensive summary of the magnitude and significance of each pathway in the model.

Discussion

The present study examined the structural relationships among spiritual well-being, health behaviors, depression, and academic achievement in university students. Overall, the findings suggest that spiritual well-being is positively associated with health behaviors and academic achievement and negatively associated with depression. In addition, depression was negatively correlated with academic achievement, suggesting that psychological distress may be an important correlate linking spiritual well-being to educational outcomes.

Spiritual well-being showed a significant positive association with health behaviors. This finding should be interpreted as indicating a general tendency toward healthier lifestyles rather than a direct and uniform effect on all specific behaviors or outcomes. This interpretation is consistent with prior research suggesting that spirituality may enhance motivation, self-regulation, and engagement in health-promoting behaviors. For example, Bożek et al. reported that spirituality is associated with better stress management and greater involvement in behaviors such as nutrition and physical activity [10]. Similarly, Kruk emphasized the potential role of faith-based approaches in supporting healthier routines [32]. However, in the present study, not all specific behavioral pathways showed significant effects on academic achievement or depression, and therefore conclusions regarding direct behavioral impacts should be made cautiously.

The findings also indicated an inverse association between spiritual well-being and depression. Rather than simply restating this relationship, it can be interpreted that spiritual well-being may be a psychological resource that is associated with greater coping capacity and emotional regulation. This is consistent with previous studies showing that individuals with stronger spiritual resources tend to report lower levels of depression and greater resilience [33]. For instance, Mangotara and Hilmy identified an inverse relationship between spirituality and depression [34, 35], while Noer highlighted the role of spirituality in shaping adaptive attitudes and behaviors [36]. Habibi also reported similar findings among Iranian dental students [33]. Taken together, these findings suggest that spiritual well-being may buffer the psychological impact of academic and life stressors.

With regard to academic achievement, the present findings suggest that spiritual well-being may show both direct and indirect associations, particularly through its relationship with depression. This supports the interpretation that lower psychological distress may be one of the key correlates linking spirituality to academic success. Previous research has similarly emphasized that spiritual well-being is associated with greater meaning in life, resilience, and social connectedness, which in turn are related to academic functioning [13, 15]. Al-Shoubaki also reported that spiritual well-being may improve academic performance through reduced depression and enhanced existential thinking [13]. However, the current model indicates that not all indirect pathways especially those involving specific health behaviors were statistically significant, and therefore the influence of spiritual well-being on academic achievement should be viewed as multifaceted rather than uniformly direct.

The findings also suggest that demographic and contextual factors may influence these relationships. Female students reported higher spiritual well-being but also lower physical activity, less healthy dietary patterns, and higher levels of depression. This pattern may reflect gender-related differences in emotional expression, social roles, and coping strategies rather than a simple causal relationship. Previous studies have similarly shown that women often report higher levels of spirituality alongside greater psychological vulnerability [24, 25]. Although spiritual well-being appeared to have some protective influence, it did not fully offset these differences, highlighting the importance of considering gender-specific factors.

Academic and environmental conditions also appear to play an important role. Students in higher academic semesters showed lower levels of spiritual well-being, which may reflect cumulative academic stress and reduced psychological resources over time. This interpretation is consistent with findings indicating that academic pressure can weaken psychological and spiritual resilience [17, 26]. At the same time, healthier dietary patterns and lower depression observed in higher semesters may reflect increased maturity and improved coping skills rather than the direct effect of spiritual well-being. Dormitory living conditions may also contribute to these patterns, as previous studies have shown that environmental stressors can negatively affect quality of life, mental health, and spiritual well-being [28, 29].

Economic status was also identified as an important factor influencing academic achievement. Students with more favorable economic conditions demonstrated better academic performance, which is consistent with previous findings that financial constraints can limit access to resources and increase psychological stress [27]. These findings suggest that spiritual well-being alone cannot fully compensate for structural and socioeconomic disadvantages.

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that spiritual well-being should be understood as a broad protective and supportive factor rather than a direct determinant of all health and academic outcomes. Its effects appear to operate through multiple psychological and behavioral pathways, particularly through reducing depression, while some expected pathways (e.g., via specific health behaviors) may be weaker or non-significant. Therefore, interpretations should remain cautious and context-sensitive. Future research is recommended to further examine these relationships using longitudinal designs and more diverse populations.

These findings highlight the importance of integrating spiritual well-being into student support and health promotion programs. Interventions may focus on enhancing meaning, coping skills, and emotional resilience rather than assuming direct behavioral change. At the same time, it is essential to address broader contextual factors such as academic pressure, living conditions, and economic challenges, as these also significantly influence student well-being and academic success.

Conclusion

This study’s findings are consistent with the view that spiritual well-being is an important correlate of medical students’ health and academic outcomes. Higher spiritual well-being was directly associated with healthier behaviors, including physical activity, healthy diet, and smoking avoidance, as well as lower depression. Furthermore, spiritual well-being showed indirect associations with better academic performance through hypothesized mediating mechanisms, particularly lower depression and healthier behaviors. These associations were more pronounced among specific subgroups, such as female students and those living in dormitories. Taken together, the findings underscore the potential value of integrating spiritual well-being into university support programs and educational interventions, adopting a holistic approach that encompasses spiritual, psychological, and environmental dimensions to enhance student health and academic success.

Study limitations

The present study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between spiritual health and the outcomes under investigation; future longitudinal studies are warranted. Secondly, reliance on self-report instruments may introduce response biases, such as social desirability bias. Thirdly, the generalizability of these findings may be limited by the specific cultural and academic context of the participating student population.
