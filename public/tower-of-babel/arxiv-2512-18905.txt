Abstract
. This paper proposes a weak Galerkin (WG) finite element method for elliptic interface problems defined on nonconvex polygonal partitions. The method features a built-in stabilizer and retains a simple, symmetric, and positive definite formulation. An optimal-order error estimate is rigorously derived in the discrete H 1 H^{1} norm. Furthermore, a series of numerical experiments are provided to verify the theoretical results and to demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed WG method for elliptic interface problems.

1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the development and analysis of a weak Galerkin (WG) finite element method for elliptic interface problems posed on both convex and nonconvex polygonal partitions. We consider the following model problem: find a function
u
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a}}\nabla u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f,\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(1.2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma,

(1.3)

\displaystyle{[[u]]}_{\Gamma}=u|_{\Omega_{1}}-u|_{\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{D},\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

(1.4)

\displaystyle{[[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a}}\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}]]}_{\Gamma}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a_{1}}}\nabla u|_{\Omega_{1}}\cdot{\bf n}_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a_{2}}}\nabla u|_{\Omega_{2}}\cdot{\bf n}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{N},\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded domain decomposed as

\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, with interface
\Gamma=\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
.
Here,
a_{i}=a|_{\Omega_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, and
{\bf n}_{1}
and
{\bf n}_{2}
denote the unit outward normal vectors to
\Gamma
with respect to
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively. The diffusion tensor
a
is assumed to be symmetric and uniformly positive definite in
\Omega
. For simplicity of presentation,
a
is taken to be a constant matrix; however, all theoretical results extend in a straightforward manner to sufficiently smooth variable coefficients.

A weak formulation of (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) reads as follows: find
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
u=g
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
,
{[[u]]}_{\Gamma}=g_{D}
on
\Gamma
, and

(1.5)

({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a}}\nabla u,\nabla v)=(f,v)+\langle g_{N},v\rangle_{\Gamma},\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

where
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

Elliptic interface problems arise in numerous scientific and engineering applications, including biological systems
[
19
]
, material science
[

, fluid dynamics
[

, and computational electromagnetics
[

This lack of smoothness presents substantial challenges for the construction and analysis of accurate and robust numerical methods, particularly of high order.

A variety of numerical methods have been proposed to address elliptic interface problems. Broadly, these approaches can be divided into unfitted mesh methods and interface-fitted mesh methods. Unfitted mesh methods allow the computational mesh to be generated independently of the interface geometry, thereby avoiding complex mesh generation when interfaces are intricate or time dependent. One class of unfitted methods modifies basis functions locally near the interface to incorporate jump conditions directly into the approximation space. Representative examples include immersed interface methods
[
21
,
18
,
3
]
, ghost fluid methods
[
22
]
, and hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
11
,
14
]
.

Another class of unfitted methods enforces interface conditions weakly through penalty or stabilization terms. This category includes extended finite element methods
[
61
,
4
]
, unfitted finite element methods
[
15
]
, cut finite element methods
[
1
]
, and high-order hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
17
]
. Although unfitted methods have demonstrated considerable flexibility, their analysis becomes increasingly intricate for complex interface geometries, and rigorous convergence results—especially for high-order schemes—remain challenging.

Interface-fitted mesh methods, by contrast, align the computational mesh with the interface and are well suited for problems involving complex geometries, irregular elements, or hanging nodes. Typical examples include discontinuous Galerkin methods
[
17
,
23
,
35
]
, the matched interface and boundary method
[

,

]
, virtual element methods
[

An important extension of the WG framework is the primal-dual weak Galerkin (PDWG) method

In this work, we propose a simplified weak Galerkin finite element method for elliptic interface problems on both convex and nonconvex polygonal partitions. The method incorporates an intrinsic stabilization mechanism and does not require explicitly added stabilizer terms, which are commonly employed in classical WG formulations. In contrast to existing stabilizer-free WG methods
[
64
]
, the proposed scheme applies to nonconvex polygonal meshes and admits flexible choices of polynomial degrees. The essential analytical tool is the construction of suitable bubble functions, which enables stability without additional stabilization terms. Although this approach necessitates higher-degree polynomial spaces for computing discrete weak gradients, it facilitates the development of auto-stabilized WG methods on nonconvex elements.

The resulting scheme preserves the size and global sparsity pattern of the stiffness matrix, thereby significantly simplifying implementation relative to stabilizer-dependent WG methods. A rigorous error analysis demonstrates optimal-order convergence of the WG approximation in the discrete
H^{1}
norm. These results confirm that the proposed auto-stabilized WG method provides an efficient and theoretically sound framework for elliptic interface problems on nonconvex polygonal meshes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the weak gradient operator and its discrete counterpart. Section 3 presents the WG discretization for elliptic interface problems on convex and nonconvex polygonal partitions. Section 4 establishes existence and uniqueness of the discrete solution. An error equation is derived in Section 5, followed by optimal-order error estimates in the discrete
H^{1}
norm in Section 6. Numerical results validating the theoretical analysis are reported in Section 7.

2.
Weak Gradient and Discrete Weak Gradient

In this section, we briefly review the definition of the weak gradient and its discrete counterpart, following
[
54
,
36
]
.

Let
T
be a polygonal element with boundary
\partial T
. A
weak function
on
T
is defined as

v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}
, where
v_{0}\in L^{2}(T)
and
v_{b}\in L^{2}(\partial T)
. The components
v_{0}
and
v_{b}
represent the values of
v
in the interior of
T
and on
\partial T
, respectively. In general,
v_{b}
is treated as an independent variable and is not required to coincide with the trace of
v_{0}
. In the special case
v_{b}=v_{0}|_{\partial T}
, the weak function
v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}
is uniquely determined by
v_{0}
and may be identified with
v_{0}
.

Denote by
W(T)
the space of all weak functions on
T
, namely,

(2.1)

W(T)=\{v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}:v_{0}\in L^{2}(T),\;v_{b}\in L^{2}(\partial T)\}.

The weak gradient, denoted by
\nabla_{w}
, is defined as a linear operator from
W(T)
into the dual space of
[H^{1}(T)]^{2}
. For any
v\in W(T)
, the weak gradient
\nabla_{w}v
is the bounded linear functional satisfying

(2.2)

(\nabla_{w}v,{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}=-(v_{0},\nabla\cdot{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}+\langle v_{b},{\bm{\varphi}}\cdot{\bf n}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall{\bm{\varphi}}\in[H^{1}(T)]^{2},

where
{\bf n}
denotes the unit outward normal vector to
\partial T
.

Let
r\geq 0
be an integer and let
P_{r}(T)
denote the space of polynomials on
T
of total degree at most
r
. The discrete weak gradient on
T
, denoted by
\nabla_{w,r,T}
, is a linear operator from
W(T)
to
[P_{r}(T)]^{2}
. For any
v\in W(T)
, the discrete weak gradient
\nabla_{w,r,T}v
is the unique polynomial vector in
[P_{r}(T)]^{2}
satisfying

(2.3)

(\nabla_{w,r,T}v,{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}=-(v_{0},\nabla\cdot{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}+\langle v_{b},{\bm{\varphi}}\cdot{\bf n}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall{\bm{\varphi}}\in[P_{r}(T)]^{2}.

If
v_{0}\in H^{1}(T)
is sufficiently smooth, then applying integration by parts to the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.3
) yields

(2.4)

(\nabla_{w,r,T}v,{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}=(\nabla v_{0},{\bm{\varphi}})_{T}+\langle v_{b}-v_{0},{\bm{\varphi}}\cdot{\bf n}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall{\bm{\varphi}}\in[P_{r}(T)]^{2}.

3.
Weak Galerkin Algorithms

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
be a finite element partition of the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
into polygonal elements, assumed to be shape regular in the sense of
[
54
]
. Denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}
the set of all edges in
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
and by
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{0}={\mathcal{E}}_{h}\setminus\partial\Omega
the set of all interior edges. For each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, let
h_{T}
denote the diameter of
T
, and define the mesh size
h=\max_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{T}
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
q\geq 0
be integers satisfying
k\geq q
. For each element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, define the local weak finite element space by

(3.1)

V(k,q,T)=\{\{v_{0},v_{b}\}:v_{0}\in P_{k}(T),\;v_{b}\in P_{q}(e),\ e\subset\partial T\}.

By patching these local spaces together through a single-valued boundary component
v_{b}
on each interior edge in
{\mathcal{E}}_{h}^{0}
, we obtain the global weak finite element space

V_{h}=\big\{\{v_{0},v_{b}\}:\ \{v_{0},v_{b}\}|_{T}\in V(k,q,T),\ \forall T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\big\}.

Let
V_{h}^{0}
denote the subspace of
V_{h}
consisting of functions with vanishing boundary values on
\partial\Omega
, namely,

V_{h}^{0}=\{v\in V_{h}:\ v_{b}=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\}.

For notational simplicity, we write
\nabla_{w}v
to denote the discrete weak gradient computed elementwise by (
2.3
); that is,

(\nabla_{w}v)|_{T}=\nabla_{w,r,T}(v|_{T}),\qquad\forall T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}.

For any edge
e\subset\Gamma
shared by two adjacent elements
T_{1}\subset\Omega_{1}
and
T_{2}\subset\Omega_{2}
, define the jump of
v_{b}
across
e
by

[[v_{b}]]_{e}=v_{b}|_{\partial T_{1}\cap\Gamma}-v_{b}|_{\partial T_{2}\cap\Gamma}.

For
v,w\in V_{h}
, define the bilinear form

a(v,w)=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a}}\nabla_{w}v,\nabla_{w}w)_{T}.

Let
Q_{b}
denote the
L^{2}
projection onto
P_{q}(e)
on each edge
e
.

Weak Galerkin Algorithm 1
.

A weak Galerkin scheme for the weak formulation (
1.5
) of the interface problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) seeks
u_{h}=\{u_{0},u_{b}\}\in V_{h}
such that

u_{b}=Q_{b}g
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
and

{[[u_{b}]]}_{\Gamma}=Q_{b}g_{D}
, and satisfies

(3.2)

\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{a}}\nabla_{w}u_{h},\nabla_{w}v)_{T}=(f,v_{0})+\sum_{e\subset\Gamma}\langle g_{N},v_{b}\rangle_{e},\quad\forall v\in V_{h}^{0}.

4.
Solution Existence and Uniqueness

Recall that
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
is a shape-regular finite element partition of the domain
\Omega
. Therefore, for any
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
and
\phi\in H^{1}(T)
,
the following trace inequality holds true
[
54
]
; i.e.,

(4.1)

\|\phi\|^{2}_{\partial T}\leq C(h_{T}^{-1}\|\phi\|_{T}^{2}+h_{T}\|\nabla\phi\|_{T}^{2}).

If
\phi
is a polynomial on the element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, the following trace inequality holds true
[
54
]
; i.e.,

(4.2)

\|\phi\|^{2}_{\partial T}\leq Ch_{T}^{-1}\|\phi\|_{T}^{2}.

For any
v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}\in V_{h}
, we define
the following discrete energy norm

(4.3)

{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}v{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}=\Big(\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}v,\nabla_{w}v)_{T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the following discrete
H^{1}

(4.4)

\|v\|_{1,h}=\Big(\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}(a\nabla v_{0},\nabla v_{0})_{T}^{2}+h_{T}^{-1}\|v_{0}-v_{b}\|_{\partial T}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
36
]

For
v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}\in V_{h}
, there exists a constant
C
such that

\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{T}\leq C\|\nabla_{w}v\|_{T}.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
36
]
There exists two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for any
v=\{v_{0},v_{b}\}\in V_{h}
, we have

(4.5)

C_{1}\|v\|_{1,h}\leq{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}v{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}\leq C_{2}\|v\|_{1,h}.

Theorem 4.3
.

The WG Algorithm
1
has a unique solution.

Proof.

Assume
u_{h}^{(1)}\in V_{h}
such that
u_{b}^{(1)}=Q_{b}g
on
\partial\Omega\backslash\Gamma
,
{[[u_{b}^{(1)}]]}_{\Gamma}=Q_{b}g_{D}
and
u_{h}^{(2)}\in V_{h}
such that
u_{b}^{(2)}=Q_{b}g
on
\partial\Omega\backslash\Gamma
,
{[[u_{b}^{(2)}]]}_{\Gamma}=Q_{b}g_{D}
are two different solutions of the WG Algorithm
1
. Then,
\eta_{h}=u_{h}^{(1)}-u_{h}^{(2)}\in V_{h}^{0}
satisfies

\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}\eta_{h},\nabla_{w}v)_{T}=0,\qquad\forall v\in V_{h}^{0}.

Letting
v=\eta_{h}
gives
{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}\eta_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}=0
. From (
4.5
) we have
\|\eta_{h}\|_{1,h}=0
, which yields

\nabla\eta_{0}=0
on each
T
and
\eta_{0}=\eta_{b}
on each
\partial T
. Using the fact that
\nabla\eta_{0}=0
on each
T
gives
\eta_{0}=C
on each
T
. This, together with
\eta_{0}=\eta_{b}
on each
\partial T
and
\eta_{b}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, gives
\eta_{0}\equiv 0
and further
\eta_{b}\equiv 0
and
\eta_{h}\equiv 0
in the domain
\Omega
. Therefore, we have
u_{h}^{(1)}\equiv u_{h}^{(2)}
. This completes the proof of this theorem.
∎

5.
Error Equations

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a partition of the domain
\Omega
. On each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, let
Q_{0}
be the
L^{2}
projection onto

. On each edge or face

(Q_{h}w)|_{T}:=\{Q_{0}(w|_{T}),Q_{b}(w|_{\partial T})\},\qquad\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.

Let

for a nonconvex polygon with
N
edges, and
r=N+k-1
for a convex polygon with
N
edges. See
[
36
]
for details. We denote by

.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
36
]

The weak gradient satisfies the following property:

(5.1)

\nabla_{w}u=Q_{r}\nabla u,\qquad\forall u\in H^{1}(T).

Let
u
and
u_{h}
be the exact solution of the elliptic interface problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) and its numerical approximation arising from the WG Algorithm
1
, respectively. We define the error function, denoted by
e_{h}
, as:

(5.2)

e_{h}=u-u_{h}.

Lemma 5.2
.

The error function
e_{h}
defined in (
5.2
) satisfies the following error equation:

(5.3)

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}e_{h},\nabla_{w}v)_{T}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle(I-Q_{r})a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T},\qquad\forall v\in V_{h}^{0}.

Proof.

Using (
5.1
) and setting
{\bm{\varphi}}=Q_{r}(a\nabla u)
in (
2.4
), we obtain:

(5.4)

\begin{split}&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}u,\nabla_{w}v)_{T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(Q_{r}(a\nabla u),\nabla_{w}v)_{T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(Q_{r}(a\nabla u),\nabla v_{0})_{T}+\langle Q_{r}(a\nabla u)\cdot{\bf n},v_{b}-v_{0}\rangle_{\partial T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla u,\nabla v_{0})_{T}+\langle Q_{r}(a\nabla u)\cdot{\bf n},v_{b}-v_{0}\rangle_{\partial T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(f,v_{0})_{T}+\langle g_{N},v_{0}\rangle_{\Gamma}-\langle a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}\\
&\quad+\langle(I-Q_{r})(a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}),v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(f,v_{0})_{T}+\langle g_{N},v_{0}\rangle_{\Gamma}-\langle g_{N},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\Gamma}\\
&\quad+\langle(I-Q_{r})(a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}),v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}\\
=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(f,v_{0})_{T}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle(I-Q_{r})(a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}),v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}+\sum_{e\subset\Gamma}\langle g_{N},v_{b}\rangle_{e},\end{split}

where we used (
1.5
) and it follows from (
1.4
) that

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}=\langle[[a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}]]_{\Gamma},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\Gamma}=\langle g_{N},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\Gamma}.

Subtracting (
3.2
) from (
5.4
) yields:

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}e_{h},\nabla_{w}v)_{T}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle(I-Q_{r})a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume the exact solution
u
of the elliptic interface problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) satisfies the regularity condition
u\in H^{k+1}(\Omega_{i})
for
i=1,2
. There exists a constant
C
such that the following error estimate holds:

(6.4)

{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}\leq Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}}).

Proof.

Consider the right-hand side of the error equation (
5.3
). Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the trace inequality (
4.1
), the estimate (
6.1
) with
m=k
and
s=0,1
, and the norm equivalence (
4.5
), we obtain:

(6.5)

\begin{split}&\Big|\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle(I-Q_{r})a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n},v_{0}-v_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}\Big|\\
\leq&C\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|(I-Q_{r})a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n}\|^{2}_{T}+h_{T}^{2}\|\nabla((I-Q_{r})a\nabla u\cdot{\bf n})\|^{2}_{T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\quad&\cdot\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}^{-1}\|v_{0}-v_{b}\|^{2}_{\partial T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\leq&Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})\|v\|_{1,h}\\
\leq&Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}}){|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}v{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}.\end{split}

Substituting (
6.5
) into (
5.3
) gives:

(6.6)

(a\nabla_{w}e_{h},\nabla_{w}v)\leq Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}}){|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}v{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, letting
v=Q_{h}u-u_{h}
in (
6.6
), and applying estimate (
6.3
), we have:

\begin{split}&\quad\ {|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}^{2}\\
&=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}(u-u_{h}),\nabla_{w}(u-Q_{h}u))_{T}+(a\nabla_{w}(u-u_{h}),\nabla_{w}(Q_{h}u-u_{h}))_{T}\\
&\leq\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{w}(u-u_{h})\|^{2}_{T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{w}(u-Q_{h}u)\|^{2}_{T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\quad+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(a\nabla_{w}(u-u_{h}),\nabla_{w}(Q_{h}u-u_{h}))_{T}\\
&\leq{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-Q_{h}u{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}+Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})\\
&\quad\ \cdot\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{w}(Q_{h}u-u_{h})\|^{2}_{T}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq C{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}h^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})+Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})\\
&\quad\ \cdot\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{w}(Q_{h}u-u)\|^{2}_{T}+\|a^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{w}(u-u_{h})\|^{2}_{T}\right)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq C{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}h^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})+Ch^{2k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}})^{2}\\
&\quad\ +Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}}){|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}.\end{split}

Simplifying the inequality gives:

{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}u-u_{h}{|\hskip-1.4457pt|\hskip-1.4457pt|}\leq Ch^{k}(\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{1}}+\|u\|_{k+1,\Omega_{2}}).

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
