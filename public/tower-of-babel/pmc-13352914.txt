Cross-training digital therapeutics for pediatric ADHD: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xi Zhao, Yuanhao Cai, Xia Zhang, Tong Xu, Yan Qian, Wenbi Hao, Yujiao Wang, Jing Yuan, Tengguang Cai, Lin Zhou, Haihua Li, Lei Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in children can result in academic and occupational failure, poor socialization, and an increased risk of accidental injuries. Digital therapeutics (DTx) leverage computers and smartphones to provide game-based treatments targeting ADHD’s cognitive and behavioral aspects. Although the evidence base for DTx in ADHD is expanding, most DTx focus on a single mechanism without leveraging potential synergies between them. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a cross-training DTx for children aged 6–12 with ADHD.MethodsThe DTx study is a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial that employs a cross-training approach integrating neurofeedback and executive function training. A total of 210 participants will be randomly assigned to one of three groups: the medication group (MED), the DTx group (DTX), or the combination group (COM). DTX will receive home‑based digital training, MED will receive methylphenidate, and COM will receive both. The primary outcome is change in Swanson, Nolan, and Pelham Rating Scale (SNAP‑IV) total score from baseline to 8 weeks post-randomization. Non‑inferiority will be tested between DTX and MED with a non-inferiority margin of Δ = 4 points; superiority between COM and MED will be defined as the lower bound of the two‑sided 95% confidence interval for the difference being greater than 0.DiscussionThe clinical trial protocol presented here outlines a randomized controlled trial that evaluates DTx utilizing cross-training approach. The aim of this trial is to assess the efficacy and acceptability of the cross-training DTx for children aged 6 to 12 years with ADHD. The primary outcome is the degree of improvement in the core symptoms of ADHD (measured by SNAP‑IV). Additionally, we will evaluate compliance and potential side effects associated with the DTx. The results of this study may provide an optional non-pharmacological intervention/supplementary treatment option for children with ADHD.Trial registrationNCT06369714. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent and age-inappropriate core symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. Its prevalence in school-age children is 5.3% [1] and symptoms often persist into adulthood [2]. The core symptoms lead to school and occupational failure, poor socialization, and increase the risk of accidental injuries [3–5]. ADHD often co-occurs with other psychiatric disorders [6] and somatic conditions [7].

Most clinical guidelines endorse pharmacotherapy as the primary intervention for ADHD [8, 9], and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have substantiated its efficacy [10–12]. However, side effects and caregiver concerns limit its use[13]. Non‑pharmacological interventions have fewer side effects and target symptoms poorly responsive to medication, such as defiant behavior and low self‑esteem [14, 15]. Common approaches include cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), dietary/exercise interventions, electroencephalogram (EEG) neurofeedback, and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) [16]. Behavioral therapy is recommended for preschool‑aged children before medication [17–19] and has demonstrated efficacy in reducing disruptive behaviors and improving parental skills[16, 20]. Nevertheless, non‑pharmacological treatments face access barriers, insufficient insurance coverage, and a shortage of trained child psychiatrists. Digital therapeutics (DTx) for ADHD leverage computers, smartphones, and wearable devices providing therapeutic game products and remote monitoring for patients [21].Typical forms of DTx comprise neurofeedback techniques, computerized cognitive training, internet-based training, and game-based training. A large RCT of a game-based attention training demonstrated small but statistically significant improvements in attention as measured by the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA) [22]. Despite the rapid increase in clinical evidence for DTx, a specific limitation is that most DTx focus on a single mechanism—either neurofeedback or computerized cognitive training—without leveraging potential synergies between them [23, 24].

We therefore developed a refined cross-training approach that integrates neurofeedback training with executive function training for children aged 6 to 12 who have been diagnosed with ADHD. Neurofeedback directly trains self‑regulation of brain activity, specifically aiming to reduce the theta/beta ratio in the prefrontal cortex and thereby to facilitate more efficient suppression of task‑irrelevant default mode network (DMN) [25–27] activity during externally oriented attention. Executive function tasks focus on the executive dysfunction pathway of ADHD [28], which provide a behavioral context in which the individual practices goal‑directed attention regulation, reinforcement of controlled processing, and the resistance of intrusive off‑task mental activity. The central mediator is the reduction in the theta/beta ratio, which is hypothesized to improve DMN suppression and causally improve working memory and inhibitory control (as assessed by the BRIEF and DCT). We hypothesize that the combination produces a mutually reinforcing loop—improved neural regulation facilitates better performance on executive function tasks, while successful executive function performance provides positive feedback that consolidates neurofeedback learning—and thus synergy is expected. The EEG headband used in this study and its proprietary concentration algorithm have been internally validated in pilot studies but have not been independently published due to intellectual property protection.

This protocol describes a RCT designed to evaluate the efficacy and acceptability of the cross-training DTx for children aged 6 to 12 years with ADHD. The trial employs a three-arm parallel design to evaluate the non-inferiority of DTx compared to medication in reducing ADHD core symptoms, and the superiority of combination therapy (DTx + medication) over medication. By integrating non-inferiority and superiority hypotheses within a single trial, this design efficiently addresses both the viability of DTx as a standalone alternative and its role in augmenting traditional pharmacotherapy.

The remainder of this protocol details the methodology, including participant recruitment, intervention, outcome measures, and ethical considerations, in accordance with SPIRIT guidelines. This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06369714) prior to participant enrollment. The completion of the trial will be reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement [29]. A detailed statistical analysis plan (SAP) will be finalized and made publicly available prior to analyses.

Overall objective

The primary aim of this trial is to evaluate the efficacy of a cross-training DTx for children aged 6–12 years with ADHD, providing a novel home-based therapeutic option.

Trial design {8}

This study is a multicenter (two sites), open-label, three-arm, parallel-group, RCT with a hybrid non-inferiority-superiority framework. Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to three arms: the medication group (control group, MED), the digital therapeutics group (experimental group 1, DTX), and the combination group (experimental group 2, COM). The primary outcome is the change in SNAP‑IV total score from baseline to 8 weeks. The non‑inferiority of DTX versus MED will be tested first using a prespecified margin Δ = 4 points. Only if non‑inferiority is established will the superiority of COM versus MED be tested. If non‑inferiority fails, the superiority hypothesis will not be tested.

Rationale for combining non‑inferiority and superiority in a single trial

This hybrid design is justified because it efficiently answers two clinically relevant questions within one study: (1) whether DTx can serve as an acceptable alternative to medication (non‑inferiority) for patients who cannot or prefer not to take medication, and (2) whether adding DTx to medication provides additional benefit (superiority) for those already on pharmacotherapy. By sharing the same medication control group and using a fixed hierarchical testing order, the design controls type I error without requiring separate trials, thus reducing sample size and research costs while maintaining interpretational clarity.

Rationale for the 8-week endpoint and follow‑up assessments

The 8‑week duration is selected based on prior neurofeedback and cognitive training studies, which indicate that this period is sufficient for observable changes to emerge [35, 36]. This duration also balances the feasibility of maintaining family adherence. Improvements in core ADHD symptoms are hypothesized to plateau by the end of the 8‑week intervention, with no further substantial gains without continued training. However, it is anticipated that acquired self‑regulation skills may be maintained or may partially decay over the follow‑up period. The follow‑up assessments will directly inform this question.

Internal pilot phase

An internal pilot phase will be conducted during the first 30 recruited participants to assess feasibility metrics, including recruitment rate (target: ≥ 10 participants/month), retention rate (≥ 80% at 4 weeks), and protocol adherence (≥ 70% for DTx training and medication). Predefined progression criteria are: continue to full trial if all three targets are met; modify recruitment/retention strategies if one target is missed; terminate the trial if two or more targets are missed after attempted remediation. The pilot data will be included in the main analysis.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

This trial will be conducted as a multicenter study in the pediatric departments of two affiliated teaching hospitals in Shanghai, China. Participants will access the DTx using their personal devices in home settings, with technical support provided through a dedicated helpdesk. Assessments and data collection will be conducted in person at the hospitals by medical staff. A complete list of study sites is available on the trial registry website [https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06369714].

Eligibility criteria {10}

Inclusion criteria are as follows: children aged 6–12 years who met the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‑5) criteria for ADHD (predominantly inattentive, hyperactive/impulsive, or combined type); had not received any ADHD‑specific pharmacologic or non‑pharmacologic treatment within 4 weeks prior to enrollment; with no sex restrictions; and from whom both child verbal assent and legal guardian written informed consent will be obtained.

Exclusion criteria included comorbid psychiatric or neurodevelopmental disorders other than ADHD; intellectual disability or a Full Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) < 70 on the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‑Fourth Edition (WISC‑IV); neurological disorders or other severe uncontrolled diseases; prior participation in the present study; physical or functional inability to operate the study software; substance dependence within the past 6 months; and any other conditions deemed inappropriate for enrollment by the investigator (further details are provided in the Supplementary Appendix).

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

A physician not directly involved in the child’s clinical care will obtain written informed consent. During the recruitment process, the physician will explain the study’s purpose, risks, benefits, and randomization procedures using a standardized script. Parents will have the opportunity to review the paper consent form and ask any questions they may have. Written consent will be obtained on paper, and a copy will be provided to the parents.

A trained nurse from the clinical team, who is not involved in treatment decisions, will obtain verbal assent from the children. After parental consent, the nurse will explain the study using a brief animation. Questions from the children will be answered in a neutral manner. Verbal assent will be documented in the trial database, with the signature of another nurse present as a witness.

Participants may withdraw their consent or assent at any stage without facing any consequences; data collected prior to withdrawal will be retained unless a request for deletion is explicitly made. This dual consent framework aligns with ethical guidelines for pediatric research, ensuring that parents understand the process while also respecting the child’s autonomy.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Participant data, including clinical assessments, medication adherence records, and demographic information, will be recorded on numbered, non-identifiable paper Case Report Forms (CRFs). These CRFs will be securely stored in locked filing cabinets within a restricted-access hospital archive room. Only authorized research staff will be permitted to handle the CRFs. The CRFs will be scanned into password-protected PDFs within 7 days of collection, with the originals retained for 5 years post-trial before secure shredding. The scanned PDFs will remove direct identifiers and replace them with unique study codes. Participants will be asked to provide explicit consent for the use of de-identified data in future ADHD-related research and will retain the right to request the physical destruction of original CRFs and associated scans unless anonymization is irreversible. However, aggregated results in publications cannot be withdrawn. This trial does not involve the collection or storage of biological specimens.

Intervention description {11a}

Digital therapeutics group (DTX)

The intervention for the DTX integrates neurofeedback and executive function training through a gamified software [39] that is intended to target attention and executive function in children with ADHD (detailed information on the software is provided in the Supplementary Appendix). Participants in the DTX will use a tablet at home to receive an 8‑week DTx. They are required to complete at least 30 days of valid training within the 8 weeks, where a valid day is defined as a cumulative training duration of ≥ 30 min with intact EEG signals.

Medication group (MED)

Participants in the MED will receive methylphenidate hydrochloride prolonged-release tablets (18 mg/tablet, Janssen Pharmaceuticals), starting at 18 mg taken once daily in the morning, with the dose adjusted to a maintenance level that controlled core symptoms, up to a maximum of 54 mg/day, for 8 weeks.

Combination group (COM)

Participants in the COM will receive both interventions simultaneously. Training requirements are the same as those for the DTX, and medication requirements are the same as those for the MED.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants may discontinue or modify their allocated interventions based on predefined safety, efficacy, and ethical criteria.

Discontinuation is mandated in cases of SAEs, including hypertension that exceeds the 95th percentile for age, cardiovascular abnormalities (e.g., arrhythmia, sustained tachycardia), or psychiatric events (e.g., hallucinations, severe agitation, or suicidal ideation) in the MED or COM. For the DTX and COM, discontinuation applies to unresolved technical failures (e.g., persistent device malfunctions), DTx-related AEs (e.g., motion sickness, seizures, or severe eyestrain), psychological distress (e.g., anxiety or frustration) reported by participants or caregivers, or failure to complete more than 50% of scheduled sessions within 2 consecutive weeks. Voluntary withdrawal requests from participants or guardians are honored without the need for justification. Additionally, worsening ADHD symptoms (a 25% or greater increase in SNAP-IV score) despite adherence or protocol violations (e.g., unauthorized use of additional treatments) will also trigger discontinuation.

Modifications to interventions are allowed under specific circumstances. In the MED or COM, a temporary suspension of up to 3 days may occur for non-SAEs (e.g., transient insomnia or decreased appetite), with resumption contingent on symptom resolution. For the DTX or COM, task difficulty may be reduced (e.g., simplifying Sequence Recall levels) if persistent frustration or low accuracy is observed, and sessions may be rescheduled to prevent circadian disruptions (e.g., delayed sleep onset).

All decisions adhere to standardized procedures: AEs (AEs) are documented in real-time through CRFs. A blinded safety committee, comprising independent psychiatrists, neurologists, and statisticians, will review all SAEs and any discontinuation or modification decisions within 24 h of occurrence. The committee will evaluate causality, severity, and the appropriateness of the proposed action. All modification and discontinuation decisions, along with the committee’s rationale, will be recorded in a dedicated decision log and signed off by the committee chair. Decisions will be communicated to the research team via a secure email notification system within 12 h of the committee’s determination. The principal investigator (PI) is responsible for informing the participant’s caregivers verbally and in writing. Participants who withdraw receive necessary clinical care, and pre-withdrawal data are retained unless explicit deletion is requested. Ethical safeguards ensure participant autonomy, including the right to withdraw freely and the requirement for written approval from the Principal Investigator (PI) for any modifications.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Caregivers and children will participate in a structured in-person orientation session to review the intervention protocol, device usage (for the DTX and COM), and medication administration (for the MED and COM). Study staff will train participants on the digital therapeutics software and monitor device usage for a minimum of 30 min to ensure that participants can adhere to their assigned intervention protocols. Participants in the DTX and COM will receive in-app reminders for daily sessions. Pediatricians will monitor training progress in real time through the backend database and conduct brief weekly phone calls to reinforce adherence, address challenges, and provide troubleshooting guidance. Participants in the DTX and COM will earn virtual outfits and unlockable game content for completing their sessions.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Permitted concomitant care

Participants may continue their existing medications for comorbid conditions, such as asthma, if the regimen has been stable for at least 4 weeks prior to enrollment and remains unchanged throughout the trial. Additionally, participants may receive urgent medical interventions that are unrelated to ADHD. All such events must be documented in the CRFs.

Prohibited concomitant care

The use of stimulants, non-stimulants, or herbal supplements specifically aimed at alleviating ADHD symptoms is prohibited. Additionally, CBT, neurofeedback, and computerized cognitive training outside the trial protocol are also prohibited during the 8-week intervention period. Participants must avoid initiating new dietary regimens or intensive exercise programs aimed at managing ADHD symptoms.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

Participants who experience AEs related to the trial interventions will receive free follow-up care at the trial sites until their condition is resolved or stabilized. The trial sponsor will cover all medical expenses directly associated with trial participation, in accordance with Chinese clinical trial regulations. Participants in the MED and COM will transition to standard clinical care, with prescriptions managed by their primary pediatricians.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome

The SNAP-IV Inattention and Hyperactivity/Impulsivity subscales will serve as the primary outcome measures to assess the severity of ADHD symptoms. Parents will rate 18 items (9 per subscale) on a 4-point scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “very much”). Subscale scores will be calculated as the mean of the item scores, with lower values indicating better outcomes [40]. The primary analysis will evaluate the mean change in SNAP-IV total scores (0–54 points) from baseline to 8 weeks post-randomization. The SNAP-IV was selected for its validated sensitivity to ADHD symptoms changes, widespread clinical use, and its alignment with established diagnostic criteria [41].

Secondary outcomes

The SNAP-IV Inattention and Hyperactivity/Impulsivity subscales will be assessed at 20 and 32 weeks post-randomization to evaluate the sustainability of the intervention effects. No continued use of the DTx or medication is permitted after the 8‑week intervention phase. Mean changes from baseline to these time points will be analyzed to determine the long-term impact on symptoms.

The DCT will evaluate visual attention and processing speed. Participants’ performance, measured by the total number of correct cancellations, will be recorded, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. Mean changes from baseline to Week 8 and follow-ups will be analyzed. The DCT has established validity [34].

The BRIEF will assess executive functioning through parent-reported responses. Age-adjusted T-scores will be analyzed, with lower scores reflecting better executive function [32]. Changes from baseline to Week 8 and follow-up time points will be evaluated using linear mixed models. The BRIEF has established validity [33] and provides clinically actionable insights into daily executive challenges, directly reflecting the effectiveness of the executive function training module.

Adherence to DTx will be automatically recorded by the system’s backend database, which tracked the number and duration of training sessions; sufficient adherence was predefined as completing ≥ 27 valid days (90% of the required 30 days) of training. Adherence to medication will be assessed using pill counts, with sufficient adherence predefined as taking ≥ 80% of the prescribed medication (confirmed by pill count).

AEs will be documented through structured parent diaries and clinician reports. The severity, causality, and resolution timelines will be aggregated as proportions. Safety monitoring will ensure the timely identification of intervention-related risks, balancing the evaluation of efficacy with the welfare of participants.

The sample size calculation is based on the primary endpoint of the mean change in SNAP-IV total scores from baseline to 8 weeks post-randomization. For the non-inferiority comparison between DTX and MED, the non-inferiority margin for the total score of the SNAP-IV was set at Δ = 4 points. This margin was determined based on clinical judgment, representing approximately 50% of the clinical benefit observed with methylphenidate compared with placebo [42], and is smaller than the Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) of 6.6 points for the ADHD Rating Scale (ADHD-RS) [43]. The rationale for this choice is supported by the structural similarity between the SNAP-IV and ADHD-RS, both of which were developed based on the DSM criteria and use the same scoring approach to reflect the severity of core ADHD symptoms [40, 41]. Based on pilot data, the difference between DTX and MED in the change in SNAP-IV total score was assumed to be −1.3, with a common standard deviation (SD) of 5. The non-inferiority margin was Δ = 4. Parameter settings included a one-sided significance level of 0.025, power of 80%, and a 1:1 allocation ratio. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, 60 participants are required per group. The difference between COM and MED in the change in SNAP-IV total score was assumed to be 2.5, with a common SD of 5. Parameter settings included a two-sided significance level of 0.05, power of 80%, and a 1:1 allocation ratio. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, 70 participants are required per group. The larger value was taken as the final sample size, i.e., 70 participants per group.

Recruitment {15}

Children aged 6–12 years with a formal ADHD diagnosis will be recruited primarily through the pediatric outpatient departments of Shanghai Changhai Hospital and Shanghai Changzheng Hospital, both of which are tertiary hospitals with specialized ADHD clinics. Outpatient physicians will review medical records during routine visits and refer eligible families to the study team. To expand outreach, the trial will collaborate with non-profit organizations to disseminate information via WeChat and Douyin (TikTok), utilizing short videos to explain the study’s purpose and eligibility criteria. Additionally, posters displayed in public areas of the outpatient departments will further raise awareness among visiting families. Flexible scheduling, including weekend and evening clinic hours, will be implemented to accommodate working parents and increase enrollment efficiency. All recruitment materials will avoid coercive or persuasive language, emphasize voluntary participation, and undergo pre-approval by the Ethics Committee to ensure clarity, transparency, and compliance with ethical standards.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

The allocation sequence will be generated using SAS 9.4, with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio for the three arms (DTX, MED, COM). Block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 3 and 6 will be used, stratified by study center (two centers). A simple randomized list of 210 unique numbers will be created, with each number linked to a specific group assignment.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

The sequence will be concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). Envelopes are sequentially numbered, opaque, and sealed with tamper‑evident tape. The envelope can only be opened by tearing the tape, which leaves visible evidence of tampering. The independent statistician who prepares the envelopes does not participate in participant enrollment or outcome assessment. Each envelope, labeled with a unique sequential number, contains a card indicating the assigned group. The envelopes are stored in a locked cabinet accessible only to an unblinded coordinator.

Implementation {16c}

Clinicians enroll eligible participants and obtain informed consent. The unblinded coordinator retrieves the next sequential envelope, opens it in the participant’s presence, and records the allocation. Opened envelopes are archived for auditing purposes.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

This trial is an open-label study because the DTx and medication protocols inherently reveal group assignments, making full blinding impractical. Consequently, participants, care providers, and the coordinator responsible for administering intervention will not be blinded to group assignments. To mitigate detection bias, the following measures are implemented: (1) Clinicians responsible for administering objective assessments (e.g., DCT) and instructing parent‑reported scales remain blinded to group allocation. (2) Statisticians analyzing trial data work with anonymized datasets where group labels are replaced with Group A, B, and C. (3) To reduce recall bias from a single informant, the SNAP‑IV scale is also administered to the teacher who knows the participant well at enrollment, and the scores are compared with those from the parent‑rated scale.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

After completing the primary analysis, the relationships between Group A/B/C and DTX/MED/COM will be disclosed to the statisticians for final interpretation and reporting. No unblinding of outcome assessors is anticipated during the trial, as any medical emergencies requiring urgent knowledge of group allocation will be managed by unblinded clinical staff who are independent of outcome assessment and data analysis.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Outcome assessments will include both parent-reported questionnaires and investigator-administered objective test to comprehensively evaluate ADHD symptoms, executive function, attention, and social functioning. The SNAP-IV scale will be collected via an online questionnaire completed by the primary caregiver. Other secondary outcomes (DCT, BRIEF, QCD) will be assessed through on-site testing of participants and paper-based questionnaires completed by primary caregivers.

Assessments will be conducted at Weeks 0 (baseline), 8 (primary endpoint), 20, and 32 (follow-ups). Clinicians administering on-site assessments will receive standardized training to ensure consistency.

AEs and adherence data will be recorded in real-time using structured parent diaries and clinician reports.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

Participants and caregivers will receive a small gift upon achieving 100% session completion or completing all assessments. Flexible scheduling, including weekend and evening clinic hours, will accommodate family routines. A dedicated coordinator will send personalized reminders via WeChat and conduct monthly check-in calls to address any barriers. Courier services will be provided for the return of devices.

Caregivers may contact the technical support team during working hours (8 AM to 6 PM) to address device malfunctions, Bluetooth pairing failures, software crashes, or any other technical or logistical issues. If a participant misses three consecutive sessions due to unresolved technical issues, the coordinator will arrange an in‑person support visit. If the issue persists beyond five sessions, the participant may be offered a replacement device. All technical problems and resolution steps will be recorded in the CRF. Participants who discontinue the intervention or withdraw from the trial will be invited to complete an abbreviated remote final assessment (primary outcomes only) to capture endpoint data, unless they explicitly refuse. Reasons for discontinuation will be documented in the CRF and analyzed. Outcome data collected prior to withdrawal will be retained unless a formal request for deletion is made.

Data management {19}

Participant data, including clinical assessments, medication adherence records, and demographic information, will be recorded on numbered, non-identifiable paper CRFs during clinic visits. Researchers will enter the data into a structured EpiData database (version 4.6) hosted on encrypted offline computers. To ensure accuracy, double-entry verification will be conducted, with two independent researchers inputting the data. Any flagged discrepancies will be resolved by a third reviewer through source document reconciliation.

Data validation will be enforced through range constraints, mandatory fields, and logical checks. All data modifications will be documented in a manual audit log, which will record the date, time, researcher’s name, specific changes made, and the rationale for each modification. Additionally, weekly encrypted backups of the EpiData database will be archived to preserve historical records of all changes. The PI will review the manual audit logs and versioned backups weekly to ensure compliance with data integrity standards.

The original paper CRFs will be securely stored in locked filing cabinets within a restricted-access archive room at Shanghai Changhai Hospital. Electronic data will be encrypted and stored on offline hospital computers. Participant identifiers will be replaced with unique study numbers during data entry, and the linking file will be stored on a separate encrypted drive accessible only to the PI. Weekly backups will be conducted on password-protected external hard drives. Paper CRFs will be retained for 5 years post-trial before secure shredding, while electronic data will be archived for 10 years on encrypted drives before permanent deletion.

Confidentiality {27}

All personal information collected during the trial, including participant names, birthdates, contact details, and medical records, will be rigorously protected. Participants will be assigned unique study numbers upon enrollment. Identifiable information will be stored separately from research data in a password-protected file, accessible only to authorized staff responsible for participant communication. No identifiable data will be included in publications or reports, nor will it be shared with external parties.

All data collected from the digital therapeutic device (EEG signals, training logs, and performance metrics) are encrypted during transmission and stored on a password‑protected cloud server located within the country. To register for the digital therapeutic account, participants provide a mobile phone number, nickname, sex, and date of birth, which generate a unique registration ID. No real names, family information, or medical identifiers are entered into the backend database or shared with the device manufacturer. The linkage between the registration ID and the participant’s true identity (including name and study number) is known only to the unblinded coordinator at the study site and is stored separately in a password‑protected hospital database. Participants and guardians are informed of these measures during the informed consent process.

Plans for collection, laboratory evaluation and storage of biological specimens for genetic or molecular analysis in this trial/future use {33}

No biological specimens will be collected from participants for use in this trial.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

The main analysis will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle [44, 45], including all randomized participants in their originally assigned groups regardless of protocol adherence or missing data. Analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 28.0.

Primary outcome

For the primary outcome (mean change in SNAP-IV total scores from baseline to week 8), both absolute (raw mean difference) and standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) will be reported, along with their 95% confidence intervals. The primary outcome will be analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The model will include the baseline SNAP-IV total score as the covariate. The primary analysis did not include study center as a covariate due to the limited sample size of the sub-center; however, a sensitivity analysis adding center as a fixed effect will be conducted to assess the robustness of the results [46]. For hypothesis testing, the non-inferiority of DTX compared to MED will be evaluated by constructing a one-sided 97.5% confidence interval for the adjusted mean difference. Non-inferiority will be declared if the lower bound of this confidence interval exceeds −4 points, which is the predefined margin. Additionally, the superiority of COM over MED will be assessed by constructing a two-sided 95% confidence interval for the adjusted mean difference. Superiority will be declared if the lower bound of the two‑sided 95% confidence interval is greater than 0.

To control the family‑wise type I error rate for the two primary hypotheses (non‑inferiority and superiority), a fixed hierarchical testing procedure will be used: non‑inferiority is tested first at one‑sided α = 0.025; only if non‑inferiority is established will superiority be tested at two‑sided α = 0.05. No additional multiplicity adjustment is required because the hierarchical order protects the overall type I error.

To assess the impact of protocol adherence on the primary outcome, a per‑protocol analysis will be conducted. Protocol deviations are documented in CRFs. Participants who meet the exclusion criteria for the per‑protocol analysis (fewer than 27 days of valid training, medication adherence < 80%, lack of dose adjustment to control core symptoms, use of other medications, non‑pharmacological treatments, dietary regimens, or exercise plans specifically for ADHD symptoms after baseline, or failure to complete the endpoint assessment) will be excluded from the per‑protocol analysis but remain in the ITT analysis. The per‑protocol results will be compared with the primary ITT analysis to evaluate the robustness of the conclusions.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes include SNAP-IV total scores at 20 and 32 weeks, which will be analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. This model will incorporate fixed effects for treatment group, time (baseline, 8 weeks, 20 weeks, and 32 weeks), group-by-time interaction, and baseline SNAP-IV total score. Random intercepts for each participant will be included to account for repeated measurements. Other secondary outcomes, such as mean change in QCD, BRIEF, and DCT, will be analyzed separately at 8 weeks using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests.

Because the secondary outcomes are exploratory and not part of the primary confirmatory hypotheses, no adjustment for multiple comparisons will be applied. Results for secondary outcomes will be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‑generating, with emphasis on effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than p‑values.

Adherence

Sufficient adherence rates as described in section {12} will be compared among groups using the chi-square test (two-sided α = 0.05). Training parameters (total training days, valid training days, session duration) between DTX and COM will be compared with the Mann–Whitney U test, reported as median (interquartile range).

AEs

The incidence rates of AEs will be summarized as proportions with 95% confidence intervals (Clopper-Pearson method) and compared across treatment groups using Fisher’s exact tests (two-sided α = 0.05). Events will be categorized by severity (mild, moderate, severe) and by causality (related or unrelated to the intervention).

Interim analyses {21b}

No formal interim analyses for efficacy or futility are planned. Independent safety monitors will review anonymized aggregate AEs data every month. Trial termination may be recommended if ≥ 5% of participants experience SAEs related to interventions or if any life-threatening event attributable to the interventions occurs. Statisticians will receive safety data labeled as Group A/B/C to maintain blinding. Unblinded personnel will not have access to interim safety reports. The Ethics Committee will review all safety monitor recommendations and hold the authority for final termination decisions.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses will explore potential heterogeneity in treatment effects based on the following baseline characteristics:

Study Center: Shanghai Changhai Hospital vs. Changzheng Hospital.

Baseline ADHD severity: SNAP-IV total score < 36 (moderate) vs. ≥ 36 (severe).

Age Categories: 6–8 Years vs. 9–12 Years.

These analyses are purely exploratory and hypothesis‑generating. For the primary outcome, ANCOVA models will be repeated within each subgroup using the ITT population, while adjusting for baseline scores. For secondary outcomes, subgroup-specific mixed-effects models or ANCOVA will be applied as appropriate. To formally assess heterogeneity, treatment-by-subgroup interaction terms will be added to the primary ANCOVA model (two-sided α = 0.10 for interaction tests). Given the exploratory nature, p‑values will not be adjusted for multiplicity, and results should be interpreted as descriptive only, with no confirmatory conclusions drawn.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the primary analysis results under various assumptions regarding missing data, sensitivity analyses will be conducted. For multiple imputation (MI), 20 imputed datasets [47] will be generated via chained equations based on the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption. The imputation models include baseline variables (SNAP-IV total scores, age, sex, study center) and post-baseline outcomes. For complete-case analysis (CCA), participants with missing primary outcome data will be excluded, assuming the data are missing completely at random (MCAR). The results from MI and CCA will be compared to evaluate the consistency and robustness of the primary analysis results. Significant differences in the results will indicate that the choice of imputation method may have a substantial impact on the conclusions. Additionally, as described in the Primary Outcome Analysis, a sensitivity analysis adding study center as a fixed effect will be conducted to assess the robustness of the results.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

Analysis populations

The primary analysis will follow the ITT principle, including all randomized participants in their originally assigned groups, regardless of protocol adherence or missing data. A per-protocol analysis (as described in {20b}) will be conducted to assess the impact of protocol adherence on the primary outcome.

Handling missing data

MI (as described in {20a}) will be applied to the ITT population to handle missing data, ensuring that all available information is utilized while accounting for the uncertainty associated with missing values. If the proportion of missing primary outcome data exceeds 20%, a sensitivity analysis using worst‑case and best‑case scenario imputation will be performed to assess the robustness of the conclusions. CCA (as described in {20b}) will be used for comparison, where participants with missing primary outcome data will be excluded, assuming the data are MCAR. The ITT results will be prioritized in efficacy reporting. Per-protocol and missing data sensitivity analyses will be explicitly labeled as exploratory, providing additional insights into the robustness of the findings.

Plans to give access to the full protocol, participant level-data and statistical code {31c}

The complete study protocol and statistical analysis code are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author, subject to confidentiality agreements and institutional approvals. To protect participant privacy and comply with local regulations, individual participant data will not be publicly shared. Anonymized datasets may be provided to qualified researchers under a formal data-sharing agreement, contingent upon approval by the ethics committee and trial sponsors.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating centre and trial steering committee {5d}

This multicenter trial is designed and coordinated by Changhai Hospital, with collaborative execution by both Changhai Hospital and Changzheng Hospital. Daily operations are managed by the core research teams as follows:

The PI oversees the conduct of the trial, approves protocol deviations, and assumes medical responsibility.

The Study Coordinator is responsible for cross-center coordination, managing allocation envelopes, coordinating study visits, and maintaining trial registration.

The Data Manager is responsible for organizing data capture, performing quality checks, and resolving data discrepancies.

Clinicians are responsible for screening potential participants, obtaining informed consent, administering interventions, and conducting clinical assessments.

Collaboration is facilitated through monthly online meetings held across sites to review recruitment progress, ensure data consistency, and address AEs.

Although there is no trial steering committee, the Investigator-Initiated Trial (IIT) Management Office at Changhai Hospital offers support for protocol development and ensures regulatory compliance. The Ethics Committee, also based at Changhai Hospital, conducts centralized reviews of the protocol, consent forms, and amendments, while providing ongoing oversight by reviewing aggregated safety reports and addressing participant complaints.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

The Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) is an independent panel comprising a pediatric psychologist specializing in ADHD, a statistician responsible for the methodological oversight of safety data interpretation, and an ethicist who ensures the protection of participants’ rights while balancing risks and benefits. All members have no financial or institutional conflicts with the trial sponsor, investigational products, or competing therapies, and are required to submit disclosures regarding any potential conflicts of interest. Given the open-label design and the absence of interim efficacy analyses, the DMC focuses on safety monitoring by reviewing aggregated safety data on a quarterly basis. It assesses whether the rates of AEs exceed predefined thresholds. The DMC also ensures adherence to the protocol by identifying systemic deviations and recommending corrective actions. Additionally, it provides ethical oversight by evaluating whether the risks remain justified in light of emerging safety data. The DMC will submit recommendations, such as protocol modifications or decisions regarding continuation or termination, to the sponsor and will share anonymized safety summaries annually with ethics committees. Any significant changes to the protocol will be subject to review by the DMC.

AE reporting and harms {22}

Although the digital therapeutics is generally considered low‑risk, several possible adverse effects may occur. First, prolonged use of screen‑based devices may contribute to digital fatigue or eye strain. Second, the gamified and reward‑based design could theoretically reinforce maladaptive reward‑seeking behaviors. Third, some children may experience frustration or disengagement when task difficulty exceeds their ability, potentially leading to reduced adherence or emotional distress. Fourth, the EEG headband may cause local discomfort, headache, or skin irritation in a small subset of users. These risks are explicitly discussed with caregivers during the informed consent process, and participants are instructed to report any such events.

In this trial, AEs and SAEs will be systematically monitored through a combination of active and passive surveillance methods. Participants will complete standardized AE questionnaires at each study visit and will receive structured inquiries during weekly follow-up calls. Additionally, spontaneous reports can be submitted via a 24/7 hotline. All AE data will be recorded in CRFs within 24 h of identification, with severity graded as mild, moderate, or severe, and causality assessed to determine the likelihood of intervention-relatedness. SAEs will trigger immediate notification to the PI for preliminary assessment, followed by reporting to regulatory and ethics committees within 7 calendar days for events deemed possibly or definitively related to the interventions. Emergency protocols will be activated for SAEs, including medical intervention and temporary suspension of trial procedures, while non-SAEs will be managed through weekly follow-ups until resolution. Specific risks will be prioritized: pharmacological interventions will undergo focused monitoring for known adverse drug reactions and metabolic interactions, whereas DTx will be evaluated for privacy breaches, screen time-related behavioral changes, and visual fatigue.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Auditing responsibilities are managed by the PI and the Quality Assurance (QA) team from Changhai Hospital’s Clinical Research Center. Given the trial’s low-risk profile—characterized by non-invasive interventions, established safety data for the medication, and minimal-risk digital tools—the PI will coordinate annual audits that focus on critical areas: verification of informed consent documentation, consistency between source data and CRFs, and SAE reporting compliance. Audits will combine remote monitoring of DTx logs and on-site checks for medication dispensation records. The PI will submit interim quality reports to the ethics committee biannually. The QA team, independent of the study team, will report audit findings directly to the ethics committee. Significant deviations—such as unresolved data discrepancies of 5% or greater, or protocol violations that impact safety—will necessitate corrective actions.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

All significant protocol amendments will be communicated timely to all investigators and study team members through investigator meetings to ensure their awareness and adherence to the updated protocol requirements. Any significant protocol amendment will be submitted to the ethics committee for review and approval. For amendments that may affect the rights or safety of participants, participants will be informed through written notices or supplementary informed consent forms. Participants will have the opportunity to ask questions or withdraw before continuing their participation in the trial. All significant protocol amendments will be promptly updated on ClinicalTrials.gov to ensure transparency and openness of trial information.

Dissemination plans {31a}

After the trial concludes, participants will be sent a plain-language summary of the trial results via email or postal mail to ensure their understanding of the outcomes and their implications. The trial’s principal findings are planned to be published in international and domestic peer-reviewed journals, along with presentations or poster displays at relevant academic conferences.

Discussion

This trial evaluates a home‑based, integrated DTx that combines neurofeedback and executive function training for children with ADHD. The cross‑training approach combines two active mechanisms—real‑time EEG‑based neurofeedback and adaptive executive function tasks—within a single, alternating protocol. The main contribution of the study lies in the practical, scalable, home‑based deployment of this combined training within an all‑in‑one platform that automatically adjusts task difficulty based on both behavioral performance and EEG‑derived engagement. This implementation advantage addresses common barriers to access and may improve real‑world adherence and reach. Moreover, the hybrid non‑inferiority‑superiority trial design itself represents a methodological distinction. Prior DTx trials have typically used placebo or waitlist controls, or sequentially conducted separate placebo‑controlled and superiority trials [22, 48]. In contrast, our study simultaneously tests two clinically relevant hypotheses within one trial: (1) whether DTx alone is non‑inferior to an active pharmacological comparator (methylphenidate), and (2) whether DTx added to medication is superior to medication alone. By sharing the same medication control group and using a fixed hierarchical testing order, we control type I error, reduce sample size, and lower research costs while maintaining interpretational clarity. All comparisons are performed in the same population, time frame, and with identical assessment procedures, minimizing confounding from cross‑trial heterogeneity.

The protocol has several limitations. As this is an open‑label trial, participants, caregivers, and intervention providers will be aware of group assignments. This may introduce expectation bias, particularly for parent‑reported outcomes such as the SNAP‑IV. Parents who believe the digital therapy is innovative might overestimate its benefits, while those skeptical of non‑pharmacological approaches might underestimate them. Beyond general expectation bias, the novelty of the EEG headband, the engaging gamified interface, and the visible enthusiasm of caregivers for a high-tech solution could all generate non-specific improvements that are not attributable to the active training mechanisms. To mitigate this, we included teacher‑rated SNAP‑IV at baseline and used objective attention measures (DCT) as secondary outcomes, providing independent sources of information. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that expectancy effects influenced the primary results. The non‑inferiority and superiority findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, and future studies using blinded assessors or sham controls are warranted to confirm these findings. Additionally, the absence of a well‑validated continuous performance test (CPT) as a co‑primary outcome is a notable limitation. While the DCT provides an objective measure of attention, it does not capture the full range of vigilance and impulsivity deficits assessed by established CPTs. Including a validated CPT would have provided a more robust, objective primary endpoint less susceptible to reporter bias. Future trials should consider incorporating an established CPT as a co‑primary outcome to strengthen the evidence base for digital therapeutics in ADHD.

The cross‑training DTx comprises several components, including neurofeedback, executive function training, gamification, adaptive difficulty algorithms, and caregiver involvement. While this integrative package reflects a real‑world implementation, it is difficult to isolate which specific component drives the observed effects, and the absence of mechanistic biomarkers (e.g., fMRI or EEG metrics) restricts insights into how these components synergistically modulate neuroplasticity. Therefore, this trial tests the effectiveness of the DTx as a whole package rather than the independent contribution of any single mechanism; claims about synergy must be interpreted with caution. Future research should employ factorial or dismantling designs to systematically manipulate the presence or absence of each candidate component, while mechanistic studies could address this gap by incorporating multimodal neuroimaging to disentangle intervention-specific effects.

Although the trial includes only two tertiary pediatric hospitals in Shanghai, this constitutes a multicenter design that broadens generalizability beyond a single institution. However, we acknowledge that results may not directly generalize to primary care settings or other geographic regions, and the limited number of centers precludes formal meta‑analysis of site heterogeneity, which will be noted as a limitation.

Furthermore, the exclusion of children with intellectual disabilities or comorbid ASD strengthens internal validity but limits the generalizability to real-world ADHD populations with complex presentations. Scalability remains a concern; if efficacy is demonstrated, future implementation studies must evaluate cost-effectiveness and integration into school systems or public health platforms. Long-term follow-up beyond the trial period is critical to assess the durability of effects, particularly for interventions relying on neuroadaptive changes.

Despite these limitations, this protocol offers a pragmatic framework for hybrid digital-pharmacological trials. By addressing methodological trade-offs, this study aims to generate robust, actionable evidence to guide clinicians in personalizing ADHD care.
