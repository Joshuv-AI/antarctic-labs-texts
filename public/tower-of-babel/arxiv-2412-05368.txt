Multi- and Infinite-variate Integration and L 2 L^{2} -Approximation on Hilbert Spaces with Gaussian Kernels
M. Gnewuch, K. Ritter, R. Rüßmann

Abstract
. We study integration and L 2 L^{2} -approximation in the worst-case setting for deterministic linear algorithms based on function evaluations. The underlying function space is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with a Gaussian kernel of tensor product form. In the infinite-variate case, for both computational problems, we establish matching upper and lower bounds for the polynomial convergence rate of the n n -th minimal error. In the multivariate case, we improve several tractability results for the integration problem. For the proofs, we establish the following transference result together with an explicit construction: Each of the computational problems on a space with a Gaussian kernel is equivalent on the level of algorithms to the same problem on a Hermite space with suitable parameters.



1.
Introduction

We consider integration and
L^{2}
-approximation of functions
f\colon{\mathfrak{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of
d
variables in the cases
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d=\infty
,
where the underlying measure
\mu
is the
d
-fold product of
the univariate standard normal distribution
\mu_{0}
.
The functions
f
are assumed to be
elements of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H(M)
with a reproducing
kernel
M
of
tensor product form with domain
{\mathfrak{X}}:={\mathfrak{X}}(M)
. For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
the
domain is given by
{\mathfrak{X}}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(and
\mu
is the

d
-dimensional standard normal distribution), while
for
d=\infty
the domain
{\mathfrak{X}}
is a proper
subset of the sequence space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1
.
This setting, with
\mu_{0}
as before or with

\mu_{0}
being the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
,
is frequently studied in the literature on multivariate
or infinite-variate integration and approximation.

Throughout this paper, we use the following notation.
We put
J:=\{1,\dots,d\}
if
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

J:={\mathbb{N}}
if
d=\infty
, and

h_{\nu}
denotes the univariate Hermite polynomial of
degree
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, normalized in
L^{2}(\mu_{0})
.

We study two particular kinds of reproducing kernels.
Primarily, we are interested in Gaussian kernels

L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j\in J}\exp\left(-\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot(x_{j}-y_{j})^{2}\right)

with
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
,
which are specified by sequences
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in J}
of shape
parameters
\sigma_{j}>0
.
Additionally, we consider Hermite kernels

K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j\in J}\Biggl(\,\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\beta_{j}^{\nu}\cdot h_{\nu}(x_{j})\cdot h_{\nu}(y_{j})\Biggr)

with
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, which
are specified by sequences
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=(\beta_{j})_{j\in J}
of base
parameters
0<\beta_{j}<1
.
See Section
2
for the study of
the function spaces
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and, in the case
d=\infty
,
of the corresponding domains
{\mathfrak{X}}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
Here we only mention the following monotonicity properties:
If
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}
, then

{\mathfrak{X}}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}})\subseteq{\mathfrak{X}}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})

and the restriction
f\mapsto f|_{{\mathfrak{X}}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}})}

defines a continuous mapping from

H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
to
H(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}})
;
analogously, if
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}
, then

{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}})\subseteq{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})

and the restriction
f\mapsto f|_{{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}})}

defines a continuous mapping from

H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
to
H(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}})
.

For both kernels,
M:=L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
M:=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, and for both problems,
integration and
L^{2}
-approximation, we analyze deterministic linear
algorithms that are based on a fixed number of function
evaluations in the worst-case setting on the unit ball in

H(M)
. Concerning the definition of cost we distinguish
two cases: For finite
d
the cost per function evaluation at any
point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
is
one, the most natural choice, so that the worst-case cost of an algorithm is equal
to the number of function evaluations.
For
d=\infty
it is widely accepted that
the analogous definition of cost is too generous and
non-predictive for computational practice.
Therefore we employ the unrestricted subspace
sampling model, which has been introduced in

Kuo et al. (2010)
to define the worst-case cost of algorithms
for computational problems on spaces of functions of infinitely many
variables. Accordingly, function evaluation is only permitted at points

{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(x_{j})_{j\in J}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
with finitely many non-zero components

x_{j}
, and
\cost({\boldsymbol{x}})
, the cost for the evaluation at
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, depends
non-decreasingly on the number of non-zero components.
The worst-case setting is presented in detail in Section
3
.

A key quantity in the analysis is
the
n
-th minimal error
e_{n}(M)

for integration and for
L^{2}
-approximation, which is, roughly speaking,
the smallest worst-case error on the unit ball in
H(M)

that can be achieved by any algorithm with worst-case cost at most

n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

In this framework integration and
L^{2}
-approximation on
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

are studied in

Fasshauer et al. (2012)
,

Kuo and Woźniakowski (2012)
,

Kuo et al. (2017)
,

Karvonen and Särkkä (2019)
,
and

Karvonen et al. (2021)
,
see also

Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018)
.
On
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
these problems are studied in

Irrgeher et al. (2015)

and in

Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
,
see also
Irrgeher et al. (2016b)
.
Actually, the kernels
K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, which define spaces
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
of
functions of infinite smoothness, are only particular elements of
the broader class of Hermite kernels, which may also define
spaces of functions of finite smoothness.
Here we refer to

Irrgeher and Leobacher (2015)
,

Dick et al. (2018)
,
and
Leobacher et al. (2023)

for results on integration and
L^{2}
-approximation with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

For
d=\infty
we are not aware of results on
integration and
L^{2}
-approximation on spaces
with Gaussian kernels, while spaces with Hermite kernels, including the
kernels
K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, have been studied for both problems in

Gnewuch et al. (2024)
.

Numerous models in probability theory
are ultimately based on a sequence of independent random variables,
and in every such model
expectations may be represented as integrals with respect to product
measures.
This motivates the study of integration of functions
f
defined
on the sequence space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
or subspaces thereof.
Let us mention, for instance, the
Lévy-Ciesielski (or Brownian bridge) representation of Brownian motion,
which is based on an independent sequence of standard normally
distributed random variables and thus leads to the measure

\mu
that is studied in this paper. In this setting, the results
derived in this paper may potentially be used to compute
expectations of smooth functionals of Brownian motion.

In this paper we first of all
establish a transference result for integration and a transference
result for
L^{2}
-approximation, which
yield
for each of these problems
and suitably related spaces
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

the equivalence
on the level of algorithms.
The transference results only require that

(1.1)

\sum_{j\in J}\sigma_{j}^{2}<\infty,

which is trivially satisfied in the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
In the proofs we employ a particular
isometric isomorphism between Hilbert spaces with Gaussian and with
Hermite kernels, see Theorem
2.7
, which is due to
Gnewuch et al. (2022)
.

For integration with any
d
the transference result reads as follows.
Assume that the shape and base parameters are related by

1-\beta_{j}=\frac{1}{1+2\sigma_{j}^{2}}

for every
j\in J
. Moreover,
let
e_{0}(M)
denote the norm of the integration functional on
H(M)
.
Then for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a bijection

A\mapsto B
on the set of all
n
-point quadrature formulas
such that

e(A,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})=e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\cdot e(B,K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

for the worst-case errors
e(A,L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and
e(B,K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

of
A
on the unit ball in
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
and of

B
on the unit ball in
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. See Theorem
4.3
.
The bijection is given
explicitly in Remark
4.4
, and the worst-case cost of
A

and
B
coincide also in the case
d=\infty
. Consequently,
the minimal errors satisfy

e_{n}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})=e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\cdot e_{n}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

For
L^{2}
-approximation with any
d
an analogous transference result is
established in Theorem
5.1
and Remark
5.2
, assuming
that the shape and base parameters are related by

1-\beta_{j}=\frac{2}{1+\left(1+8\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}

for every
j\in J
.

Let
d=\infty
.
We combine the transference results
with known results for the decay of the minimal errors

e_{n}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
from
Gnewuch et al. (2024)
to determine the decay of
the minimal errors
e_{n}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
.
For any non-increasing sequence
{\boldsymbol{e}}:=(e_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of positive real numbers its decay is given by

(1.2)

\decay({\boldsymbol{e}}):=\sup\{\tau>0\colon\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(e_{n}\cdot n^{\tau}\right)<\infty\}

with the convention that
\sup\emptyset:=0
. Put

\rho:=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\frac{\ln(1/\sigma_{j}^{2})}{\ln(j)},

and assume that (
1.1
) is satisfied, which implies
1\leq\rho\leq\infty
. Under a very mild assumption on the cost function

{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto\cost({\boldsymbol{x}})
, see (
4.15
), we obtain

\decay\left((e_{n}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho-1),

see Theorem
4.14
for integration and
Theorem
5.4
for
L^{2}
-approximation.
Algorithms showing that the polynomial decay rate of
the minimal errors
e_{n}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
is at least
(\rho-1)/2

are obtained by
applying the transference results to properly chosen
multivariate decomposition methods on the spaces
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
,
see Section
4.4
.

Now, we turn to the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In the sequel
we survey known results from the literature
together with the findings from this paper for
the integration problem. To this end we fix
sequences
(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\beta)_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of shape
parameters and base parameters, respectively, and we either have

M_{d}:=L_{(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})}
for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

or
M_{d}:=K_{(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})}
for
all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

At first, we
note that exponential convergence of
e_{n}(M_{d})

holds for every fixed dimension
d
:
For every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a constant
c>0
such
that

(1.3)

e_{n}(M_{d})\leq\exp\bigl(-c\cdot n^{1/d}\bigr)

for all
n\geq 1
.
For
d=1
we have a matching lower bound, i.e.,
there exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0

such that

(1.4)

e_{n}(M_{1})\geq\exp\bigl(-c^{\prime}\cdot n\bigr)

for all
n\geq 1
. For
d\geq 2
it is only known that

(1.5)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(e_{n}(M_{d})\cdot\exp\bigl(c\cdot n^{p}\bigr)\right)=\infty

for all
p>1/d
and
c>0
.

For Hermite kernels all of these results
have been established in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
.
For Gaussian kernels the situation is as follows:
The upper bound (
1.3
) is due to

Kuo et al. (2017)
and
Karvonen et al. (2021)
under the additional assumptions

\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\sigma_{j}<1/2
and

\sigma_{1}=\dots=\sigma_{d}
, respectively;
from the latter one can easily deduce (
1.3
) in full generality.
The lower bound (
1.4
) follows from Theorem
4.6
and improves the lower bound
from
Kuo et al. (2017)
, which is super-exponentially small in
n
.
Moreover, (
1.5
) is due to

Kuo et al. (2017)
in the case
\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\sigma_{j}<1/2
,
and the monotonicity property mentioned above immediately yields
(
1.5
) in full generality.

Next, we discuss tractability results, which deal with
the information complexity
n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d})
with
0<\varepsilon<1

and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
for the normalized error criterion.
Roughly speaking,
\varepsilon\mapsto n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d})
is the inverse
function to
n\mapsto e_{n}(M_{d})/e_{0}(M_{d})
.
Concerning tractability, the exponential convergence
(
1.3
) suggests to control the behavior of

\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d}))
as
d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty
in terms
of
\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})
and a power of
d
, which leads to
the following definition, see
Kuo et al. (2017)
.
Exponential convergence
(t,\kappa)
-weak tractability, for short
EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT, holds for
(M_{d})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
if

\lim_{d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty}\frac{\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d}))}{d^{t}+\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})^{\kappa}}=0.

At first, we address the case
(t,\kappa)=(1,1)
, where we put

\gamma_{j}:=\sigma_{j}
in the Gaussian case and
\gamma_{j}:=\beta_{j}

in the Hermite case and assume that

(\gamma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is non-increasing to obtain the following result:
EC-
(1,1)
-WT is equivalent to
\lim_{j\to\infty}\gamma_{j}=0
.
For Hermite kernels
\lim_{j\to\infty}\beta_{j}=0

has been established as a necessary condition in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
and
as a sufficient condition in
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
.
For Gaussian kernels a characterization of
EC-
(1,1)
-WT has not been available. Under the additional
assumption that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is bounded by a constant less than
1/2
,
it was known that
\lim_{j\to\infty}\sigma_{j}=0
is a necessary
condition, while exponential convergence of the shape parameters
towards zero had been established as sufficient condition, see

Kuo et al. (2017)
.

To study the case
t>1
and
\kappa>0
we put

\gamma_{j}:=\sigma_{j}
in the Gaussian case and

\gamma_{j}:=1/(1-\beta_{j})
in the Hermite case
to obtain the following result:
If
(\gamma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is polynomially bounded,
then EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT holds for all
t>1
and
\kappa>0
.
See Theorems
4.10
and
4.12
for more
general results that permit exponentially growing shape parameters

\sigma_{j}
and exponentially shrinking gaps
1-\beta_{j}
for the
base parameters
\beta_{j}
.
For Hermite kernels the case
t>1
has not been studied before, and
for Gaussian case it was only known that
EC-
(t,1)
-WT holds for all
t>1
and
\kappa\geq 1
, if
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is non-increasing
and bounded by a constant less than
1/2
, see
Kuo et al. (2017)
.

For any sequence
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=(\omega_{j})_{j\in J}

\omega_{j}\geq 0

Let

and

{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(x_{j})_{j\in J}

and

We put
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{x}}:=(\tau_{j}x_{j})_{j\in J}
and

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{z}:=(\tau_{j}^{z})_{j\in J}
if
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is positive,
i.e.,
\tau_{j}>0
for every
j\in J
.
Moreover,
{\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(1-\tau_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
If
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is positive and
\sum_{j\in J}|\tau_{j}-1|<\infty
then

(1.7)

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}:=\prod_{j\in J}\tau_{j}>0.

For any Hilbert space

with reproducing kernel

\|\cdot\|_{H(M)}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H(M)}
to denote

2.
The Function Spaces

In this paper we study two different types of
reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs), namely spaces with
Gaussian kernels and spaces
with a particular kind of Hermite kernels.
For Gaussian and for
Hermite kernels tensor products are used
to proceed from the univariate case
d=1
to the multivariate case

d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and the infinite-variate case
d=\infty
.
In this section
we mainly present known results from
Steinwart et al. (2006)
, see also

Steinwart and Christmann (2008)
and
Minh (2010)
, and from

Gnewuch et al. (2022)
, most importantly a
particular one-to-one correspondence between both types of RKHSs.
For basic facts about RKHSs and their kernels we refer to
Aronszajn (1950)

and
Paulsen and Raghupathi (2016)
.

2.1.
Univariate Gaussian Kernels

The univariate Gaussian kernel
\ell_{\sigma}
with shape parameter

\sigma>0
is defined by

\ell_{\sigma}(x,y):=\exp\left(-\sigma^{2}\cdot(x-y)^{2}\right)

for
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Despite a different use in stochastic analysis,
the Hilbert space
H(\ell_{\sigma})
will be called a Gaussian space
throughout this paper.

For the analysis of
Gaussian spaces we refer to
Steinwart et al. (2006)
, see also

Steinwart and Christmann (2008, Sec. 4.4)
. In particular, each function

f\in H(\ell_{\sigma})
is the real part of an entire function
g

restricted to the real line, where
g
belongs to the complex
reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
\ell_{\sigma}

extended to
{\mathbb{C}}
in the obvious way.
Since

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle f,\ell_{\sigma}(\cdot,x)-\ell_{\sigma}(\cdot,y)\rangle_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|^{2}_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}\cdot\|\ell_{\sigma}(\cdot,x)-\ell_{\sigma}(\cdot,y)\|^{2}_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}

\displaystyle=2\,\|f\|^{2}_{H(\ell_{\sigma})}\cdot(1-\ell_{\sigma}(x,y))

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the functions from

H(\ell_{\sigma})
are bounded.
No Gaussian space contains any non-zero polynomial, and
obviously
\ell_{\sigma}
is translation-invariant, i.e.,

\ell_{\sigma}(x,y)=\ell_{\sigma}(x-y,0)
.
Moreover, we have the following monotonicity property
of Gaussian spaces:

(2.1)

\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}\Rightarrow H(\ell_{\sigma_{1}})\subsetneq H(\ell_{\sigma_{2}})

with a non-compact continuous identical embedding of norm

\sqrt{\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}}
.

2.2.
Univariate Hermite Kernels

Let
0<\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\dots
with

\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot\nu^{-1/2}<\infty.

Then

k(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}^{-1}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y),

which is well defined for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, yields a
reproducing kernel. The mapping
k
and the corresponding Hilbert
space
H(k)
are called a Hermite kernel and a Hermite space,
respectively.

For the analysis of
Hermite spaces we refer to
Irrgeher and Leobacher (2015)
,
Gnewuch et al. (2022)
,
and
Leobacher et al. (2023)
. In particular,

(2.2)

H(k)=\left\{\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}c_{\nu}\cdot h_{\nu}\colon\text{$c_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{R}}$ with $\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot c_{\nu}^{2}<\infty$}\right\}

and

(2.3)

\|f\|_{H(k)}^{2}=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\alpha_{\nu}\cdot\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f\cdot h_{\nu}\,d\mu_{0}\right)^{2}

for
f\in H(k)
. Consequently, every Hermite space contains
all polynomials. Hermite kernels are not translation invariant,
since
\lim_{x\to\infty}k(x,x)=\infty
.

In the present paper we consider Hermite spaces with

\alpha_{\nu}:=\beta^{-\nu}

for
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
with a parameter
0<\beta<1
,
which will be called the base parameter of the kernel
k_{\beta}

given by

k_{\beta}(x,y):=\sum_{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\beta^{\nu}\cdot h_{\nu}(x)\cdot h_{\nu}(y)

for
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The elements of
H(k_{\beta})
are real analytic
functions, and we have the following monotonicity property
of Hermite spaces, which follows immediately from (
2.2
) and
(
2.3
):

(2.4)

\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}\Rightarrow H(k_{\beta_{1}})\subsetneq H(k_{\beta_{2}})

with a compact continuous identical embedding of norm one.

2.3.
Tensor Products of Gaussian Kernels

In this paper we study
d
-fold tensor products of Gaussian kernels and of
Hermite kernels. For the Gaussian kernels we consider a sequence

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in J}
of shape parameters, i.e.,
\sigma_{j}>0
.

In addition to the weighted
\ell^{2}
-space
\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
, see
(
1.6
), we also consider the space
\ell^{\infty}
of all
bounded sequences in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
The following observation will be used
in the study of the domains of tensor products of Gaussian and of
Hermite kernels in the case
d=\infty
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every positive sequence
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=(\omega_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}

with
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}<\infty
we have

\ell^{\infty}\subsetneq\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\omega}}))=1.

Proof.

The first statement obviously holds true, and the second statement
follows from

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}x_{j}^{2}\,d\mu_{0}(x_{j})=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}<\infty,

which implies that
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\omega_{j}\cdot x_{j}^{2}<\infty
holds for
\mu
-almost every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

For every sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

and
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
we define

(2.5)

\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}):=\prod_{j\in J}\ell_{\sigma_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})=\exp(-\sum_{j\in J}\sigma_{j}^{2}\cdot(x_{j}-y_{j})^{2})

with the convention that
\exp(-\infty):=0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, we have
H(\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\subseteq L^{2}(\mu)
with a compact identical
embedding.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})=1
for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
,
the kernel
\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
has finite trace, i.e.,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{J}}\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})<\infty.

The latter implies the statement of the lemma, see, e.g.,
Steinwart and Scovel (2012, Lemma 2.3)

Lemma 2.3
.

For every sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, with

Proof.

2.4.

For the study of tensor products of Hermite kernels we consider a

3.
The Worst-Case Setting

L^{2}
-approximation and integration on Gaussian and on Hermite spaces
will be studied within the following framework,
where
{\mathfrak{X}}:=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
and
M:=L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under the
assumption (
2.6
) or
{\mathfrak{X}}:=\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})
and
M:=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
under the
assumption (
2.9
). Recall that
\mu({\mathfrak{X}})=1
and
H(M)\subseteq L^{2}(\mu)
in both cases, see Lemmata
2.2
,
2.4
.(i), and
2.5
.

For
L^{2}
-approximation of functions
f\in H(M)

we consider linear sampling methods

(3.1)

A(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot a_{i}

with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, nodes
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, and coefficients

a_{i}\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
As error criterion we consider the worst-case error of
A
on the
unit ball in
H(M)
, which is defined by

e(A,M):=\sup_{\|f\|_{H(M)}\leq 1}\|f-A(f)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

For integration of functions
f\in H(M)

we consider quadrature formulas

(3.2)

A(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot a_{i}

with
n
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
as before, but with
coefficients
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The worst-case error of
A
on the unit ball in
H(M)
is defined by

e(A,M):=\sup_{\|f\|_{H(M)}\leq 1}|I(f)-A(f)|,

where

I(f):=\int_{{\mathfrak{X}}}f\,d\mu.

The worst-case cost of an algorithm
A
of the form (
3.1
) or
(
3.2
) is defined by

(3.3)

\cost(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cost({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}),

where
\cost\colon{\mathfrak{X}}\to{[0,\infty[}\cup\{\infty\}
denotes
the cost of a single function evaluation,

Of course, the most natural choice of the cost function is

\cost({\boldsymbol{x}}):=1
for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
, which means that the
functions from
H(M)
may be evaluated anywhere in the domain
{\mathfrak{X}}

at a constant cost one. We will use this model throughout the paper
in the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
{\mathfrak{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
In the case
d=\infty
, where
J={\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathfrak{X}}
is an
infinite-dimensional space,
we employ the unrestricted subspace
sampling model, which has been introduced in
Kuo et al. (2010)
.
This model is based on a non-decreasing cost function

\$\colon{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\to\left[1,\infty\right[

in the following way. For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
the
number of active variables is defined by

\Act({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\#\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon x_{j}\neq 0\},

and the evaluation of any function
f\in H(M)
is permitted at
any point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
for which
\Act({\boldsymbol{x}})
is finite; in this case
the corresponding cost is given by
\$(\Act({\boldsymbol{x}}))
.
We add that
\Act({\boldsymbol{x}})<\infty
already implies
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.

Accordingly, we define

(3.4)

\cost({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\begin{cases}\$(\Act({\boldsymbol{x}}))&\text{if $\Act({\boldsymbol{x}})<\infty$,}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
x\in{\mathfrak{X}}
.

As the key quantity, we study the

n
-th minimal worst-case error, which is defined by

(3.5)

e_{n}(M):=\inf\{e(A,M)\colon\cost(A)\leq n\}

for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For
n=0
the corresponding quantity is

e_{0}(M):=e(A,M)

with
A:=0
, i.e.,

e_{0}(M)=\sup_{\|f\|_{H(M)}\leq 1}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

for
L^{2}
-approximation and

e_{0}(M)=\sup_{\|f\|_{H(M)}\leq 1}|I(f)|

for integration.

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
explicit formulas are availablefor the norm of the embedding of
H(M)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
,
see
Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018)
and
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
,
and for the norm of the integration functional on
H(M)
,
see
Kuo et al. (2017)
and
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
.
In the following lemma we cover the case
d=\infty
, too.

Lemma 3.1
.

If (
2.6
) is satisfied then

e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\prod_{j\in J}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\left(1+\left(1+8\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}}

for
L^{2}
-approximation and

e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\prod_{j\in J}\frac{1}{\left(1+4\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/4}}

for integration.
If (
2.9
) is satisfied then

e_{0}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=1

for
L^{2}
-approximation and for integration.

Proof.

We use the tensor product structure of the
embedding of
H(M)
into
L^{2}(\mu)
and of the integration
functional on
H(M)
, see

Gnewuch et al. (2022, Sec. A.4–A.6)
for a general account in the case

d=\infty
, together with the known results for
d=1
. First of all,

e^{2}_{0}(\ell_{\sigma_{j}})=\frac{2}{1+\left(1+8\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}<1

for
L^{2}
-approximation,
see, e.g.,
Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018, Eqn. (10))
,
and

e^{4}_{0}(\ell_{\sigma_{j}})=\frac{1}{1+4\sigma_{j}^{2}}<1

for integration,
see
Kuo et al. (2017, Eqn. (1.5))
,
while
e_{0}(k_{\beta_{j}})=1
for
L^{2}
-approximation and for
integration,
see
Irrgeher et al. (2016a, p. 104)
and
Irrgeher et al. (2015, p. 385)
,
respectively.
Consequently,

e_{0}(M)=\prod_{j\in J}e_{0}(m_{j})

with
m_{j}:=\ell_{\sigma_{j}}
or with
m_{j}:=k_{\beta_{j}}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Consider the reproducing kernel
\widetilde{L}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

on the domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, as in (

). If (

Similarly, one can show that

It is well-known that in the worst-case setting

-approximation

based on function values and with information cost at most

;
for more details see

.

4.
Integration

In what follows, we derive new results for integration and

L^{2}
-approximation on Gaussian and Hermite spaces. This will be done by
transferring known results from Hermite spaces with the help of the
isometric isomorphism from Section
2.5
to Gaussian
spaces and vice versa. Since the actual transference mechanisms
for integration and
L^{2}
-approximation
differ significantly, we discuss both applications separately in
Sections
4
and
5
, respectively.

Throughout this section,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in J}
and

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=(\beta)_{j\in J}
denote
sequences of shape parameters and base parameters, respectively.
Initially, we do not impose any summability requirements on

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
or
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. For the integration problem, an appropriate
relation between
\sigma_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
, which is
different from (
2.10
), will be
determined later, see (
4.4
).

4.1.
The Transference Result

For any positive sequence
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
we define

t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{J}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
by

t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}({\boldsymbol{x}}):={\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(\tau_{j})_{j\in J}
denote a positive
sequence with
\sum_{j\in J}|\tau_{j}-1|<\infty
.
The image measure
t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mu
of
\mu

with respect to
t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
has the density

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}\cdot\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}

with respect to
\mu
.

Proof.

To establish the statement of the lemma, it suffices to show for an
arbitrary cylinder set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{J}
that

t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mu(A)=\int_{A}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}\cdot\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}\,d\mu
.

In the case
d<\infty
any cylinder set is of the form

A:=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{d}

with measurable sets
A_{j}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. Since

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(\tau_{j}A_{j})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\cdot\int_{\tau_{j}A_{j}}\exp(-x_{j}^{2}/2)\,dx_{j}

\displaystyle=\tau_{j}\cdot\int_{A_{j}}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\biggr)\,d\mu_{0}(x_{j}),

we obtain

t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mu(A)=\mu\left(t^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(A)\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{0}(\tau_{j}A_{j})={\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}\cdot\int_{A}\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}),

which in turn yields the claim.

In the case
d=\infty

we put
J_{0}:=\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon\tau_{j}<1\}
.
Using the monotone convergence theorem we obtain

(4.1)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}\exp\Biggl(-\sum_{j\in J_{0}}\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\Biggr)\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{j\in J_{0}}\tau_{j}^{-1}<\infty.

For
d=\infty

any cylinder set is of the form
A:=A_{1}\times\dots

with measurable sets
A_{j}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, where
A_{j}={\mathbb{R}}
for
j>j_{0}
. For every
j_{1}>j_{0}
we obtain

\displaystyle t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{j_{1}}\mu_{0}(\tau_{j}A_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{j_{1}}\biggl(\tau_{j}\cdot\int_{A_{j}}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\biggr)\,d\mu_{0}(x_{j})\biggr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{j_{1}}\tau_{j}\cdot\int_{A}\exp\Biggl(-\sum_{j=1}^{j_{1}}\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\Biggr)\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}).

Clearly, we have

\exp\Biggl(-\sum_{j=1}^{j_{1}}\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\Biggr)\leq\exp\Biggl(-\sum_{j\in J_{0}}\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}-1}{2}\cdot x_{j}^{2}\Biggr)

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, due to (
4.1
),
the dominated convergence theorem yields

t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mu(A)={\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}\cdot\int_{A}\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,d\mu({\boldsymbol{x}}),

which in turn establishes the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{c}}:=(c_{j})_{j\in J}
and

{\boldsymbol{\tau}}:=(\tau_{j})_{j\in J}
denote positive
sequences with
\sum_{j\in J}|c_{j}-1|<\infty
and

\sum_{j\in J}|\tau_{j}-1|<\infty
.
If

(4.2)

\tau_{j}^{2}=\frac{c_{j}^{2}+1}{2}

for every
j\in J
then we have

(4.3)

I(Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}f\circ t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\ast}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ast}^{1/2}}\cdot I(f)

for every
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
.

Proof.

By definition,

Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}f\circ t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}={\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ast}^{1/2}\cdot\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\circ t_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\cdot\left(f\circ t_{{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right)=\left({\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ast}^{1/2}\cdot\left(\varphi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\circ t_{\boldsymbol{c}}\right)\cdot f\right)\circ t_{{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}.

Note that
\sum_{j\in J}\omega_{j}<\infty
for

Lemma

.

.

∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that (

Moreover, assume that

(4.4)

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+2\sigma_{j}^{2}},

(4.5)

Proof.

At first, we only assume that (

Next, we apply Theorem

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, which leads to

.

4.2.
Functions of a Single Variable

Let us mention prior work concerning integration on
H(\ell_{\sigma})

and on
H(k_{\beta})
, where explicit upper bounds for the worst-case
error
e(A,M)
of particular quadrature formulas
A
and explicit
lower bounds for
e_{n}(M)
are obtained. Of course, these upper bounds
immediately yield upper bounds for minimal errors.
Gauss-Hermite rules are analyzed in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
on Hermite spaces and
in
Kuo and Woźniakowski (2012)
as well as
Kuo et al. (2017)

on Gaussian spaces. Moreover, scaled Gauss-Hermite rules are
studied in
Karvonen et al. (2021)
on Gaussian spaces.
Lower bounds for the
n
-minimal
errors have been established in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
for Hermite
spaces and in
Kuo et al. (2017)
for Gaussian spaces.

In the following we combine these results in the best possible way
with the transference result established in Theorem
4.3
and
Corollary
4.5
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let

\displaystyle C_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=2^{-1}\cdot(1+4\sigma^{2})^{-1/4},

\displaystyle C_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\pi^{-1/4}\cdot(1+2\sigma^{2})^{-1/2},

\displaystyle C(\beta)

\displaystyle:=\pi^{-1/4}\cdot(1-\beta^{2})^{1/4}.

For every shape parameter
\sigma>0
and every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

the
n
-th minimal error
e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})

for integration on the Gaussian space
H(\ell_{\sigma})
satisfies

C_{1}(\sigma)\cdot\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+2\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2n}\cdot(n+1)^{-2}\leq e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})\leq C_{2}(\sigma)\cdot\left(\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{1+2\sigma^{2}}\right)^{n}\cdot n^{-1/4}.

For every base parameter
0<\beta<1
and every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

the
n
-th minimal error
e_{n}(k_{\beta})

for integration on the Hermite space
H(k_{\beta})
satisfies

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2n}\cdot(n+1)^{-2}\leq e_{n}(k_{\beta})\leq C(\beta)\cdot\beta^{n}\cdot n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

The upper bound for
e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})
follows immediately
from
Karvonen et al. (2021, Thm. 2.5)
, and the
lower bound for
e_{n}(k_{\beta})
is established in
Irrgeher et al. (2015, Thm. 2)
.

Assume that

1-\beta=\frac{1}{1+2\sigma^{2}}.

First of all, Corollary
4.5
and Lemma
3.1
yield

e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})=(1+4\sigma^{2})^{-1/4}\cdot e_{n}(k_{\beta}).

Moreover, we have

\beta=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{1+2\sigma^{2}}

and

1-\beta^{2}=\frac{1+4\sigma^{2}}{(1+2\sigma^{2})^{2}}.

Consequently,
the upper bound for
e_{n}(k_{\beta})
follows from the upper bound
for
e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})
and
the lower bound for
e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})
follows from the lower bound
for
e_{n}(k_{\beta})
.
∎

The lower bound for
e_{n}(\ell_{\sigma})
from Theorem
4.6

improves the lower bound from
Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 4.1)
,
which is super-exponentially small in
n
, while
the upper bound for
e_{n}(k_{\beta})
from Theorem
4.6

only slightly improves the upper bound from
Irrgeher et al. (2015, Prop. 1)
.
Obviously, the upper and lower bounds from Theorem
4.6
are not
tight at all.

4.3.
Functions of Finitely Many Variables

In this section we study the multivariate case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
where upper bounds for the minimal errors
e_{n}(M)
have been
established in

Kuo et al. (2017)
and
Karvonen et al. (2021)
for Gaussian spaces as well as
in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
and
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)

for Hermite spaces.
In all three papers, the upper bounds are obtained by
quadrature formulas
that are full tensor products of suitable univariate

.
For lower bounds and for the study of tractability concepts

.

4.3.1.
Exponential Convergence

At first,
we consider the case of
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
being fixed,
and thus (
2.6
) and (
2.9
) are trivially satisfied for any
choice of parameters
\sigma_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
, respectively.

Let either
M:=L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
or
M:=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
Motivated by Theorem
4.6
,
we study whether there exist
constants
C_{1},C_{2},p>0
fulfilling

(4.9)

e_{n}(M)\leq C_{1}\cdot\exp({-C_{2}\cdot n^{p}})

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In this case,
we say that exponential convergence is achieved for the integration
problem on
H(M)
and

p^{*}(M):=\sup\{p\geq 0\colon\exists\,C_{1},C_{2}>0\ \forall\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon e_{n}(M)\leq C_{1}\cdot\exp({-C_{2}\cdot n^{p}})\}

is the most relevant quantity.

Exponential convergence has been studied by
Kuo et al. (2017)
and

Karvonen et al. (2021)
for Gaussian spaces, as well as by
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
for
Hermite spaces.
We present a moderate generalization of the result for Gaussian
spaces, together with a proof that employs the transference mechanism.

Theorem 4.7
.

Exponential convergence is achieved for the integration
problem on
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
with

p=1/d=p^{*}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}).

Proof.

After a possibly necessary reordering of the univariate
kernels, the shape
parameters satisfy
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{d}
, which implies

\beta_{1}\geq\dots\geq\beta_{d}
for the base parameters given by
(
4.4
). Thus we may employ

Irrgeher et al. (2015, Thm. 1.1)
in the specific case

\omega:=1/\mathrm{e}
as well as
a_{j}:=\ln(1/\beta_{j})
and

b_{j}:=1
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which yields exponential
convergence on
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
with
p^{*}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=1/d
.
Furthermore, (
4.9
) is satisfied
for
M=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in the extremal case
p=1/d
,
see
Irrgeher et al. (2015, Thm. 4)
.

Corollary
4.5
implies that (
4.9
) holds for

M=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
if and only if it holds for

M=L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
with
C_{1}
being replaced by

C_{1}\cdot e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, but with
C_{2}
and
p
unchanged.
Thus, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Theorem
4.7
has been established in

Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 1.1)
under the additional assumption

\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\sigma_{j}^{2}<1/2,

while the general case has been left open for future research.
In the case

\sigma_{1}=\dots=\sigma_{d}>0

the exponential convergence on
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
with
p=1/d
follows directly
from
Karvonen et al. (2021, Cor. 2.11)
.

Alternatively to the transference mechanism, the following
monotonicity property allows to establish Theorem
4.7
in
full generality and working exclusively with Gaussian spaces.
Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(1)}\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(2)}
,
i.e.,
\sigma^{(1)}_{j}\leq\sigma^{(2)}_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
Using (
2.1
) we conclude that
H(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(1)}})\subseteq H(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{(2)}})
with a continuous identical embedding.
Therefore we obtain
p^{*}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\leq 1/d
for every
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
from

Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 1.1.(b))
, and exponential convergence with

p=1/d
for every
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
from
Karvonen et al. (2021, Cor. 2.11)
.

4.3.2.
Exponential Convergence Weak Tractability

Exponential convergence has been studied alongside uniform
exponential convergence and several notions of tractability in

Irrgeher et al. (2015)
,
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
, and
Kuo et al. (2017)
. In this paper,
the setting is specified by arbitrary sequences

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=(\beta_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of shape parameters
\sigma_{j}>0
and base parameters

0<\beta_{j}<1
. For every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we put

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}:=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})
and

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}:=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
, and we consider
either
M_{d}:=L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}}
for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
or
M_{d}:=K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}}

for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

By definition, we have uniform exponential convergence
if there exists a constant
p>0
with the following property: For every

d\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

e_{n}(M_{d})\leq C_{1}\cdot\exp({-C_{2}\cdot n^{p}})

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For all sequences
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, Theorem
4.7

excludes uniform exponential convergence, and the lack of this property
excludes exponential convergence polynomial
tractability, see
Kuo et al. (2017, p. 832)
. We are thus led
to the study of a weaker tractability concept,
which is discussed in the following.

For
0<\varepsilon<1

the information complexity for the absolute and the normalized
error criterion are defined by

n^{\mathrm{abs}}(\varepsilon,M_{d}):=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon e_{n}(M_{d})\leq\varepsilon\}

and

n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d}):=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon e_{n}(M_{d})/e_{0}(M_{d})\leq\varepsilon\},

respectively.
Lemma
3.1
yields

n^{\mathrm{abs}}(\varepsilon,M_{d})\leq n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d})
,
with equality if
M_{d}
is a Hermite kernel.

Let
t\geq 1
and
\kappa>0
. By definition, exponential convergence

(t,\kappa)
-weak tractability, for short EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT,
holds for
(M_{d})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the normalized error criterion, if

\lim_{d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty}\frac{\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,M_{d}))}{d^{t}+(\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))^{\kappa}}=0.

For Hermite spaces, the case
t=\kappa=1
is studied
in
Irrgeher et al. (2015)
as well as
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
,
while for Gaussian spaces, the case
t\geq 1
and
\kappa\geq 1

is studied in
Kuo et al. (2017)
.

First, we give a necessary condition and a sufficient condition for
EC-
(1,1)
-WT in the case of Gaussian spaces and the
normalized error criterion.
For non-increasing shape parameters the two conditions coincide
and hence we even obtain a characterization
of this tractability property.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
be a sequence of shape parameters.
If EC-
(1,1)
-WT holds for

(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the normalized error criterion, then we have

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}=0.

If
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is non-increasing and

\lim_{j\to\infty}\sigma_{j}=0,

then EC-
(1,1)
-WT holds for

(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the normalized error criterion.

Proof.

For both statements, we make use of corresponding results for Hermite
spaces, similarly to the proof of Theorem
4.7
. Therefore,
let
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
be the sequence of base parameters given by (
4.4
).

For the first statement, suppose that
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}>0

holds. Then, we also have
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta_{j}>0
, which means
there exists a
constant sequence
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
of base parameters such that
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

is bounded from below by
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
. Thus, we may use

Irrgeher et al. (2015, Thm. 1.3)
in the specific case
\omega:=1/e

as well as
a_{j}:=\ln(1/\beta_{j})
and
b_{j}:=1
, which shows that
EC-
(1,1)
-WT does not hold for
(K_{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Further, for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have identical embeddings of

H(K_{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{d}})
into
H(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}})
of norm one,
cf. (
2.4
), which implies

e_{n}(K_{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{d}})\leq e_{n}(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}})
for all

d,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus, EC-
(1,1)
-WT does not hold for
(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Together with Corollary
4.5
, this shows the claim.

To show the second statement, we assume that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is non-increasing
and converges to 0. Then,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is also
non-increasing and converges to 0. Thus, by
Irrgeher et al. (2016a, Thm. 3)
,
again in the specific case

\omega:=1/e
as well as
a_{j}:=\ln(1/\beta_{j})
and
b_{j}:=1
,
we have that EC-
(1,1)
-WT holds for
H(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Again by Corollary
4.5
, this shows the claim.
∎

Theorem
4.8
improves the previously best known sufficient
condition for EC-
(1,1)
-WT for
Gaussian spaces, namely, that the sequence of shape parameters
is non-increasing and converges to 0 exponentially fast,
see
Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 1.1(e))
. On the other hand, the
necessity of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
converging to 0 was already known under the
additional assumptions that the boundedness condition

(4.10)

\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}^{2}<1/2

is satisfied and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is non-increasing, see

Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 1.1(f))
. We remark that the first result for Hermite
spaces we have used in the proof,

Irrgeher et al. (2016a, Thm. 3)
, is fully constructive.
Therefore, via the transference principle,
the sufficient condition in Theorem
4.8

is also obtained constructively.

Our next goal is to study EC-
(t,k)
-WT in the case
t>1
.
As an intermediary step, we derive a new upper bound for

\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}}))
with an explicit dependence
on
\varepsilon
,
d
, and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
.
For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we put

h(d):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $d\leq 2$},\\
\ln(\ln(d))&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Theorem 4.9
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}}))\leq C\cdot\left(d\,h(d)+d\ln(\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\ln(\sigma_{j})\right)^{+}\right)

for every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every
\varepsilon\in{]0,1/3]}
, and every
sequence
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of shape parameters.

Proof.

Put

\zeta_{j}:=\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right).

From
Karvonen et al. (2021, Thm. 2.10)
we get

e_{N}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1+2\sigma_{j}^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}(1+4\sigma_{i}^{2})^{-1/4}\cdot\exp(-n_{j}\zeta_{j})

for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
with
N:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{j}
. Together with Lemma
3.1
this
implies

\frac{e_{N}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})}{e_{0}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}(1+2\sigma_{j}^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot(1+4\sigma_{j}^{2})^{1/4}\cdot\exp(-n_{j}\zeta_{j})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\exp(-n_{j}\zeta_{j}).

Choosing

n_{j}:=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(d/\varepsilon)}{\zeta_{j}}\right\rceil

we obtain
\exp(-n_{j}\zeta_{j})\leq\varepsilon/d
and therefore

n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})\leq N
, i.e.,

(4.11)

n(\varepsilon,d):=n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})\leq\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\ln(d/\varepsilon)}{\zeta_{j}}+1\right),

cf.
Kuo et al. (2017, Eqn. (3.9))
.

For
z\geq 0
we define

\varphi(z):=\ln(1+z),

and we put
\tau:=\ln(d/\varepsilon)\geq 0
as well as

J_{1}:=\{j\in\{1,\dots,d\}\colon\tau\leq 2\zeta_{j}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{2}:=\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus J_{1}.

Clearly,

\varphi(z)\leq z
for every
z\geq 0
and

\varphi(z)\leq 3\ln(z)
if
z>2
,
and therefore

\displaystyle\ln(n(\varepsilon,d))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(\tau/\zeta_{j}+1)\leq\sum_{j\in J_{1}}\tau/\zeta_{j}+3\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\ln(\tau/\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle\leq 2d+3\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\ln(\tau/\zeta_{j}).

In the sequel, we assume that
\varepsilon\in{]0,1/3]}
, so
that
\ln(\tau)\geq\ln(\ln(3))>0
. With
c_{1}:=2/\ln(\ln(3))+3

we obtain

(4.12)

\ln(n(\varepsilon,d))\leq c_{1}d\ln(\tau)+3\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\ln(1/\zeta_{j}).

Let us consider the particular case that

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma_{j}\geq 2.

We put

\psi(y):=\ln\left(1+1/(2y^{2})\right)

for
y\geq 2
, so that
\zeta_{j}=\psi(\sigma_{j})
. Since

\lim_{y\to\infty}y^{2}\cdot\psi(y)=1/2
, we have

c_{2}:=\inf_{y\geq 2}y^{2}\cdot\psi(y)\in{]0,1/2]}.

With
c_{3}:=2+\ln(1/c_{2})/\ln(2)
we obtain

\ln\left(1/\psi(y)\right)\leq\ln(y^{2}/c_{2})=\ln(y)\cdot\left(2+\ln(1/c_{2})/\ln(y)\right)\leq c_{3}\ln(y)

for every
y\geq 2
. Consequently,

\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\ln(1/\zeta_{j})=\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\ln\left((1/\psi(\sigma_{j})\right)\leq c_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(\sigma_{j}).\

Together with (
4.12
), this implies

\ln(n(\varepsilon,d))\leq c_{1}d\ln(\tau)+3c_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(\sigma_{j}).

Observe that the upper bound in (
4.11
) is a monotonically
increasing function in each of the variables
\sigma_{j}
.
For any sequence of shape parameters we therefore have

\ln(n(\varepsilon,d))\leq c_{1}d\ln(\tau)+3c_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(\max(\sigma_{j},2)).

Since

\ln(\max(\sigma_{j},2))\leq(\ln(\sigma_{j}))^{+}+1,

we obtain

\ln(n(\varepsilon,d))\leq c_{1}d\ln(\tau)+3c_{3}d+3c_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\ln(\sigma_{j}))^{+}.

Finally, there exists a constant
c_{4}>0
such that

\displaystyle\ln(\tau)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\ln(d)+\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)\leq\ln\left(2\max(\ln(d),\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\ln\left(\max(\ln(d),\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))\right)\leq c_{4}\left(h(d)+\ln(\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))\right)

for all
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon\in{]0,1/3]}
.
∎

Next, we present a sufficient condition for EC-
(t,k)
-WT to hold
for some
t>1
and every
\kappa>0
.

Theorem 4.10
.

If there exist
c,\alpha\geq 0
such that the shape parameters satisfy

\sigma_{j}\leq\exp(cj^{\alpha})

for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then we have EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT for

(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the normalized and the absolute
error criterion for every
t>\alpha+1
and every
\kappa>0
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the normalized error criterion. We apply the upper bound for

n(\varepsilon,d):=n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\min(\varepsilon,1/3),L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}})\geq n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{d}}).

from Theorem
4.9
. Clearly, we have

\lim_{d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty}\frac{d\,h(d)+d\ln(\ln(\max(\varepsilon^{-1},3)))}{d^{t}+\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})^{\kappa}}=0

even for
t>1
, so it suffices to show

\lim_{d+\varepsilon^{-1}\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\ln(\sigma_{j})\right)^{+}}{d^{t}+\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})^{\kappa}}=0

for
t>\alpha+1
. This is indeed true, since

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\ln(\sigma_{j})\right)^{+}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}cj^{\alpha}\leq cd^{\alpha+1}.\qed

Theorem
4.10
extends the result established in

Kuo et al. (2017, Thm. 1.1.(h))
,
where the boundedness condition (
4.10
)
is shown to imply EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT for every
t>1
and
every
\kappa\geq 1
under the additional assumption
that
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is non-increasing.
Theorem
4.10
yields, in particular, that the latter already holds if

(\sigma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is polynomially bounded.

Next, we apply the transference result to obtain new
results for Hermite spaces, where the normalized and the
absolute error criterion coincide.

At first, corresponding to Theorem
4.9
,
we provide a new upper bound for
\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}}))

with an explicit dependence on
\varepsilon
,
d
, and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

Theorem 4.11
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\ln(n^{\mathrm{norm}}(\varepsilon,K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}}))\leq C\cdot\left(d\,h(d)+d\ln(\ln(\varepsilon^{-1}))-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\ln(1-\beta_{j})\right)

for every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every
\varepsilon\in{]0,1/3]}
, and every
sequence
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=(\beta_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of base parameters in
(0,1)
.

Proof.

Since (
4.4
) implies
2\sigma_{j}^{2}<1/(1-\beta_{j})
, the
statement follows from Corollary
4.5
and Theorem
4.9
.
∎

Next, corresponding to Theorem
4.10
,
we give a sufficient condition for EC-
(t,\kappa)-WT
to hold
for some
t>1
and every
\kappa>0
in the case of Hermite spaces.

Theorem 4.12
.

If there exist
c,\alpha\geq 0

such that the base parameters satisfy

\beta_{j}\leq 1-\exp(-cj^{\alpha})

for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then we have EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT for

(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}})_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the normalized error criterion
for every
t>\alpha+1
and every
\kappa\geq 1
.

Proof.

The assumption on the shape parameters
\beta_{j}
implies

-\ln(1-\beta_{j})\leq cj^{\alpha}
. Thus we may proceed as in the
proof of Theorem
4.10
to verify the claim.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

On the one hand, rather mild assumptions on the shape parameters or
the base parameters imply EC-
(t,\kappa)
-WT for every
t>1

regardless of choice of
\kappa
, see
Theorems
4.10
and
4.12
. On the other hand, in the case

t=1
, much stronger
properties are required even for EC-
(t,1)
-WT to hold, see
Theorem
4.8
and
Irrgeher et al. (2015, Thm. 1.3)
.
We add that necessary conditions for EC-

-WT are only known
in the case

and

.

4.4.
Functions of Infinitely Many Variables

In this section, we consider the case
d=\infty
.
For
M=L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
or
M=K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we study the decay

(4.13)

\dec(M):=\decay\left((e_{n}(M))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\right)

of the
n
-th minimal worst-case errors, see (
1.2
).
Note that a lower bound for
\dec(M)
corresponds to an upper bound for
the
n
-th minimal errors
e_{n}(M)
and vice versa.
Our goal is to determine
\dec(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
, and to this end we put

(4.14)

\rho:=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\frac{\ln(1/\sigma_{j}^{2})}{\ln(j)}.

Note that (
2.6
) implies
\rho\geq 1
, see, e.g.,

Gnewuch et al. (2019, Lemma B.3)
.

Recall that
\$(m)
denotes the cost of evaluating a function

f\colon\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})\to{\mathbb{R}}
at any point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
with exactly
m

non-zero components, see Section
3
.
We assume that
there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

(4.15)

c_{1}\cdot m\leq\$(m)\leq\exp(c_{2}\cdot m)

for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 4.14
.

Assume that (
2.6
) is satisfied.
The polynomial decay rate of
n
-th minimal errors of integration on the
Gaussian space
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
is

\dec(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{1}{2}(\rho-1).

Proof.

Consider the sequence
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of base parameters given by (
4.4
). Corollary
4.5
immediately yields

(4.16)

\dec(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\dec(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

and thus it suffices to show
\dec(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\frac{1}{2}(\rho-1)
.
This has already been established in
Gnewuch et al. (2024)

for an arbitrary sequence
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of base parameters such that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta_{j}<\infty
.
Indeed, put
r_{j}:=-\log_{2}(\beta_{j})
.
After a possibly necessary reordering of the kernels, we have

r_{1}=\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}r_{j}
. Then the weights

\beta_{j}^{-\nu}=2^{r_{j}\cdot\nu}
with
\nu,j\in{\mathbb{N}}
are
exponentially growing Fourier weights in the sense of

Gnewuch et al. (2024, Sec. 2.1)
.
Hence we obtain from
Gnewuch et al. (2024, Cor. 4.10)
that the polynomial
decay rate of
n
-th minimal errors of integration on the
Hermite space
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is

\dec(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{\rho}-1),

where

\widetilde{\rho}:=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\frac{r_{j}\cdot\ln(2)}{\ln(j)}.

Given (
4.4
) we have
\beta_{j}\asymp\sigma_{j}^{2}
, and therefore

\rho=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\frac{\ln(1/\beta_{j})}{\ln(j)}=\widetilde{\rho}.\qed

Algorithms that yield
\dec(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\geq(\rho-1)/2

are obtained by applying the transference result to properly chosen
multivariate decomposition methods (MDMs) on the space
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
Here we sketch the construction of the MDMs,
see
Gnewuch et al. (2024, Sec. 4.3)
for details and further references.

Let
{\boldsymbol{U}}
denote the set of all finite subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
we use
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{0}}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}})

to denote the point
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}
given by
y_{j}:=x_{j}
if
j\in{\boldsymbol{v}}

and
y_{j}=0
otherwise. Due to Lemma
2.5
,
we always have
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{0}}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}})\in{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.
The construction of MDMs
is based on the anchored decomposition

(4.17)

f=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\boldsymbol{U}}}f_{\boldsymbol{u}}

of the functions
f\in H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, where

f_{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{u}}}(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{u}}\setminus{\boldsymbol{v}}|}\cdot f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{0}}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}^{c}})

for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. The anchored components

f_{\boldsymbol{u}}
of
f
only depend on the variables with indices
in
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, so that
f_{\boldsymbol{u}}
may be considered as a multivariate
function with domain
{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\boldsymbol{u}}|}
. We stress that (
4.17
) is
a non-orthogonal decomposition in the space
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.

At first, we choose a finite set
{\mathcal{B}}
of non-empty elements of

{\boldsymbol{U}}
, which specifies the anchored components
f_{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
whose integrals with respect to the
|{\boldsymbol{u}}|
-dimensional standard
normal distribution will be approximated.
For each
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{B}}
we employ a Smolyak formula
B_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n_{\boldsymbol{u}}}
,
which uses
n_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
function values for the integration
of the functions
f_{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
The corresponding MDM is given by

(4.18)

B(f):=f({\boldsymbol{0}})+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{B}}}B_{{\boldsymbol{u}},n_{\boldsymbol{u}}}(f_{\boldsymbol{u}}).

As building blocks for the Smolyak formulas, we use
a sequence
(B_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of univariate quadrature formulas,
where
B_{n}
uses
n
function evaluations and
e(B_{n},k_{\beta_{1}})

converges to 0 sufficiently fast, see again
Gnewuch et al. (2024, Sec. 4.3)
for details.
For instance, we may use Gauss-Hermite rules
B_{n}
, which yield

e(B_{n},k_{\beta_{1}})\leq C\cdot\beta_{1}^{n}

with an explicitly known absolute constant
C
, see
Irrgeher et al. (2015, Prop. 1)
in the
specific case
\omega:=1/e
as well as
a:=\ln(1/\beta_{1})
and
b:=1
.
For alternatives, see Section
4.2
.
For the specific choice of
{\mathcal{B}}
and of
n_{\boldsymbol{u}}
for
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathcal{B}}

we refer again to
Gnewuch et al. (2024, Sec. 4.3)
.

5.
L^{2}
-Approximation

As before, we consider sequences
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}:=(\sigma_{j})_{j\in J}
and

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=(\beta)_{j\in J}
of shape parameters and base parameters,
respectively.

5.1.
The Transference Result

In contrast
to integration, we may use the relation (
2.10
) directly to establish
a transference result for
L^{2}
-approximation in a straightforward
way.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that (
2.6
) is satisfied. Moreover, assume that
(
2.10
) and (
2.11
) are satisfied for every
j\in J
.
For every linear sampling method
A
with nodes from
\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
we
have

e(A,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})=({\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\ast}^{1/2}\cdot e(Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}AQ_{\boldsymbol{c}},K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}).

Proof.

We apply Theorem
2.7
. Put
Q:=Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}
.
Since
Q_{|H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}
is an isomorphism from

H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
to
H(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})
with

\|Qf\|^{2}_{H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}=\|f\|^{2}_{H(({\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\ast}K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}=({\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\ast}^{-1}\cdot\|f\|^{2}_{H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}

for every
f\in H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, we have

e(A,L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})=({\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}})_{\ast}^{1/2}\cdot\sup_{\|f\|_{H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}\leq 1}\|Qf-AQf\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

Furthermore,

\|Qf-AQf\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\|f-Q^{-1}AQf\|_{L^{2}(\mu)},

since
Q
is an isometric isomorphism on
L^{2}(\mu)
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Let the assumptions from Theorem
5.1
be satisfied.
For a linear sampling method
A
on the Gaussian space
H(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

according to (
3.1
) with nodes from
\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})

the linear sampling method
Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}AQ_{\boldsymbol{c}}

on the Hermite space
H(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is easily
determined explicitly: For
f\colon\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})\to{\mathbb{R}}
we have

Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}AQ_{\boldsymbol{c}}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})\cdot b_{i}

with
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\in\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})
and
b_{i}\in L^{2}(\mu)
given by

{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}:={\boldsymbol{c}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{i}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})}{\varphi_{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{z}})}\cdot a_{i}({\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{z}}),

see Lemma
2.8
.

It is easy to see
that
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},a_{i})\mapsto({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i},b_{i})
defines a bijection on the set

\ell^{2}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2})\times L^{2}(\mu)
of pairs of nodes and coefficients.
Furthermore,

\cost(A)=\cost(Q_{\boldsymbol{c}}^{-1}AQ_{\boldsymbol{c}}),

cf. Remark
4.4
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Assume that (
2.6
) and (
2.10
) are satisfied.
For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\frac{e_{n}(L_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}})}{e_{0}(L_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})}=\frac{e_{n}(K_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})}{e_{0}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}=e_{n}(K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),

i.e., the normalized
n
-th minimal errors for
L^{2}
-approximation on
Gaussian spaces and on the corresponding Hermite spaces, related by
(
2.10
), coincide.

Proof.

Combine Theorem
5.1
, Remark
5.2
, and Lemma
3.1
.
∎

5.2.
Functions of Finitely Many Variables

L^{2}
-approximation in the case of finitely many variables has been
studied previously by
Fasshauer et al. (2012)
and
Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018)
for Gaussian
spaces and by
Irrgeher et al. (2016b)
and
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
for Hermite spaces.
While the latter paper employs linear sampling methods as described
in Section
3
,
Irrgeher et al. (2016b)
and
Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018)
allow a larger
class of
algorithms, namely linear algorithms
A
based on linear information,
i.e.,

(5.1)

A(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(f)\cdot a_{i}

with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, bounded linear functionals
\lambda_{i}
on
H(M)

and coefficients
a_{i}\in L^{2}(\mu)
;
Fasshauer et al. (2012)
consider both
classes of algorithms. In the case of general linear algorithms,
the same tractability concepts have been
studied on the one hand in
Fasshauer et al. (2012)
and
Sloan and Woźniakowski (2018)
for
Gaussian spaces, and on the other hand in

Irrgeher et al. (2016b)
for Hermite spaces, with rather complete and
matching results for both types of function space.

It was already observed in
Irrgeher et al. (2016a)
that the linear sampling
methods studied there achieve the same results as
algorithms of the form (
5.1
) for several
tractability concepts.
Indeed, as shown in
Krieg et al. (2023)
, the results from
Dolbeault et al. (2023)
are applicable and lead to rather complete results
for tractability
based on linear sampling methods, though in a non-constructive way.
Therefore, we do not apply our transference result to this case here.
Our results for infinitely many variables, however,
are new.

5.3.
Functions of Infinitely Many Variables

As in Section
4.4
, we consider
\rho
given by
(

) and assume that the function
\$
obeys (

).

The next result follows easily from

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume that (
2.6
) is satisfied.
The polynomial decay rate of
n
-th minimal errors of

-approximation on the Gaussian space

, and
