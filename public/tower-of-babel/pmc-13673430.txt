Trochlear Morphological Parameters Based on Measurements at the Trochlear Groove Entry Plane Demonstrate Excellent Predictive Efficacy for Identifying the High‐Grade J‐Sign
Authors: Jianzhong Sun, Yang Liu, Daofeng Wang, Zhengjie Tang, Chengcheng Lv, Zhijun Zhang, Tianjiao Mao, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe correction of high‐grade J‐sign is critical for improving clinical outcomes in patients with trochlear dysplasia, but insufficient attention has been given to anatomical abnormalities at the trochlear groove entry plane, the established gold standard for describing trochlear morphology. We aim to compare the diagnostic accuracy and reliability of axial computed tomography (CT) measurements at the trochlear groove entry plane versus the Roman arch plane in predicting the J‐sign among patients with patellar instability.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 120 knees diagnosed with recurrent patellar dislocation. Based on patellar tracking assessments, the J‐sign was categorized into a low‐grade group (J‐sign negative and Grade I) and a high‐grade group (J‐sign Grades II and III). Preoperative measurements of trochlear depth, sulcus angle (SA), and lateral trochlear inclination (LTI) were obtained at both the trochlear groove entry plane and Roman arch plane using computed tomography (CT). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was utilized to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the three parameters measured at both anatomical planes for J‐sign severity.ResultsParameters measured at the trochlear groove entry plane demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy on ROC curves compared with those obtained at the Roman arch plane. In both planes, the trochlear depth and LTI of the high‐grade group were significantly lower than that of the low‐grade group, while the SA was significantly higher than that of the low‐grade group (all p < 0.01). At the trochlear groove entry plane, the area under the curve (AUC) values for distinguishing low‐grade and high‐grade J‐sign were as follows: AUCTrochlear depth = 0.858, AUCSA = 0.912, and AUCLTI = 0.803. For SA, sensitivity was 0.87, specificity 0.87, and the optimal cutoff value 149.8°. In contrast, measurements at the Roman arch plane exhibited lower discriminative performance with AUCTrochlear depth = 0.714, AUCSA = 0.740, and AUCLTI = 0.694. Specifically, SA demonstrated reduced sensitivity of 0.67, comparable specificity of 0.77, and a lower cutoff threshold of 140.2°.ConclusionsWhen evaluating knee parameters in patients with recurrent patellar instability, axial CT measurements at the trochlear groove entry plane demonstrate superior predictive efficacy for the J‐sign compared to the Roman arch plane. Specifically, CT‐based measurement of the SA enables enhanced differentiation between low‐grade and high‐grade J‐sign subgroups, providing critical preoperative insights of patellar maltracking.

Introduction

Proximal patellar mal‐tracking has been documented in approximately 80% of patients with recurrent patellar dislocation (RPD) [1]. In most of these cases, the condition is characterized by lateral displacement of the patella from the trochlear groove during knee extension. This pathological movement pattern is designated as the J‐sign in physical examination. A positive J‐sign indicates the excessive lateral deviation of patellar tracking, which has been attributed to osseous anatomical abnormalities or imbalanced medial‐lateral soft tissue tension [2]. Previous studies have demonstrated that preoperative high‐grade J‐sign serves as an independent risk factor for residual graft laxity after medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction, with an odds ratio of 11.9 [3]. The incidence of postoperative residual patellar mal‐tracking has been reported to reach 54% in patients exhibiting preoperative J‐sign [4].

The previous series of studies has identified that patients with high‐grade J‐sign demonstrate a higher prevalence of structural bone risk factors [1]. A significant increase in lower limb rotational deformities, including femoral anteversion and excessive knee torsion, has been observed in those exhibiting the jumping sign [5]. However, the correlation analyses between trochlear dysplasia and patellar mal‐tracking remain underrepresented in the previous literature.

The capacity to quantitatively identify and predict patellar tracking through radiographic measurements has been established as clinically critical. Abnormal trochlear morphology has been recognized as one of the primary contributors to pathological patellar motion [6]. Multiple radiological parameters have been proposed to evaluate trochlear morphology [7, 8]. A systematic meta‐analysis identifying 39 unique metrics has reported that patellar tracking and trochlear morphology were the most commonly reported measurements for characterizing patellar instability [9]. Several quantitative measurements have been reported as radiographic references for evaluating patellar tracking: the sulcus angle (SA), trochlear depth, and lateral trochlear inclination (LTI) [9, 10, 11]. Quantitative analyses of trochlear anatomical morphology have been conducted at the Roman arch plane in multiple studies [12, 13, 14]. However, the trochlear groove entry plane should be considered the gold standard when describing the trochlear morphology [15]. Severe trochlear dysplasia may demonstrate more morphological abnormalities on imaging, while low‐grade dysplasia might be performed exclusively to the proximal trochlear segment [10, 15, 16].

The purpose of this study was to (1) evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of the trochlear groove entry plane versus the Roman arch plane in quantifying patellar tracking parameters characterized by a high‐grade J‐sign; (2) to preliminarily propose parameters demonstrating superior diagnostic performance for identifying and assessing high‐grade J‐sign. A comparative analysis of measurement reproducibility and correlation was systematically conducted between the two radiological reference planes. We hypothesize that the measurements of the trochlear groove entry plane exhibit improved diagnostic utility for predicting and characterizing patellar mal‐tracking compared to evaluations of the Roman arch axial cut.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital (Ethics Number: 01‐07‐2018). A retrospective screening was performed on RPD patients who underwent surgical treatment at Beijing Jishuitan Hospital between February 2023 and July 2024. A medical record review was conducted based on documented evidence of at least one patellar dislocation episode. All included patients were confirmed to have a documented history of patellofemoral instability. Clinical and demographic data were systematically collected from standardized patient registries. Inclusion criteria included (1) recurrent patellar instability (at least two documented episodes of patellar dislocation or subluxation) [12]; (2) complete preoperative knee imaging data of CT scans; and (3) preoperative documentation of J‐sign. (4) an upper age limit of 40 years. Exclusion criteria consisted of (1) previous knee surgery; (2) history of ipsilateral knee joint fractures and chondral injuries; (3) unclear determination of preoperative J‐sign; (4) nonstandard preoperative scan.

Clinical and Radiographic Measurements

The J‐sign was confirmed in all patients through standardized physical examinations conducted by the senior author (ZH). Patients were positioned in an upright seated posture with legs naturally hanging down, and then active knee extension was performed from 90° of flexion. A standardized video capture protocol was implemented to ensure a clear record and reproducible grade verification. Complete knee flexion‐extension cycles were recorded within a controlled 20‐s acquisition window, with a minimum of five repetitions systematically captured for each patient. The J‐sign was classified based on lateral patellar displacement observed during active knee extension from 90° flexion to terminal extension. Classification criteria were defined as follows: J‐sign negative for lateral displacement ≤ 25% of the maximum patellar transverse diameter, J‐sign grade I for lateral displacement > 25% but ≤ 50% of the maximum patellar transverse diameter, J‐sign Grade II for lateral displacement > 50% but ≤ 100% of the maximum patellar transverse diameter, and J‐sign Grade III for complete patellofemoral dislocation [1, 17]. Based on the classification criteria, patients were categorized into the low‐grade group (J‐sign negative and Grade I) and the high‐grade group (J‐sign grade II and III). All grading assessments were independently performed by two experienced sports medicine physicians (Z.H. and S.J.Z.). The interobserver kappa value of 0.81 was calculated for the identification of high‐grade J‐sign on standardized video analysis protocol.

Measurements were performed separately on CT scans at both the Roman arch axial cut and trochlear groove entry plane. CT scans were performed with the patient supine, knees fully extended, and quadriceps relaxed. Axial slices were acquired perpendicular to the femoral mechanical axis to ensure standardized multiplanar reconstruction. The Roman arch configuration was defined by optimal visualization of the rounded Roman arch appearance of the intercondylar notch, thereby enabling reproducible femoral morphology across different knee images [5]. The trochlear groove entry plane was defined as the most proximal axial slice where the cartilaginous contours of the medial and lateral trochlear facets were fully visible and continuous, indicating the entrance of the patella into the trochlear groove (Figure 1) [15]. In cases where osteophytic changes or substantial bony dysplasia obscure the typical bony contour at the proximal trochlea, the plane was identified as the most proximal level where the trochlear sulcus was discernible between the femoral condyles [15]. This anatomical reference plane was specifically selected to standardize measurements of proximal trochlear morphology, which has been biomechanically related to patellar tracking abnormalities in terminal knee extension. All measurements were determined using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer software.

The measurement of the sulcus angle is performed on axial computed tomography scans, which was described as the angle between the slopes of the medial and lateral trochlea: (A) trochlear groove entry plane. The region of the trochlear cartilaginous contour of the trochlear groove is indicated by a yellow arrow; (B) Roman arch plane. The rounded Roman arch appearance of the intercondylar notch was indicated by a yellow semi‐ellipse.

The sulcus angle was defined as the angle formed between two reference lines originating from the deepest point of the trochlear groove [18]. The first line extended to the prominent point of the lateral trochlear facet, while the second connected to the prominent point of the medial facet [13]. Measurement was performed using previously established anatomical landmarks (Figure 1).

LTI was measured as the angle between the posterior condylar axis and a line along the lateral facet of the trochlea [13, 16]. The posterior condylar axis was constructed by connecting the most posterior margins of the medial and lateral femoral condyles. Subsequently, a reference line was aligned with the lateral trochlear facet (Figure 2).

The measurement of the lateral trochlear inclination is performed on axial computed tomography scans, which was described as the angle between the posterior condylar axis and the lateral trochlear facet: (A) trochlear groove entry plane. The region of the trochlear cartilaginous contour of the trochlear groove is indicated by a yellow arrow; (B) Roman arch plane. The rounded Roman arch appearance of the intercondylar notch was indicated by a yellow semi‐ellipse.

In this study, a method established by Pfirrmann et al. was used to describe trochlear depth [19]. Three perpendicular measurements were obtained from the posterior condylar reference line to the peak of the lateral condyle, medial condyle, and the deepest point of the trochlear groove on the Roman arch axial plane and trochlear groove entry plane imaging, as have been previously presented. The final depth value was derived by subtracting the trochlear sulcus measurement from the mean condylar height (Figure 3).

Statistical Analysis

The normality of all continuous variables was assessed. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed, while nonnormally distributed data were characterized by median (interquartile range, IQR). For parameters demonstrating normal distribution characteristics, Student's t‐test was performed to compare mean values between the low‐grade and high‐grade groups and Mann–Whitney U tests for nonnormal distributions. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test. All analyses were performed with two‐tailed testing, and p values < 0.05 were determined to be significant.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed for all quantitative measurements. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of each parameter in predicting J‐sign severity. An AUC value > 0.7 was considered indicative of a valuable diagnostic test [12]. Differences in AUC values between models were compared with the DeLong test. Additionally, the Youden J statistic (sensitivity + specificity −1) was reported to identify optimal cutoff values for differentiating between low‐grade and high‐grade J‐sign classifications. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0.0.0; IBM Corp).

Interrater reliability was assessed by two independent observers using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Intrarater reliability was evaluated by one observer repeating measurements after a 2‐week interval. The final dataset was analyzed using a two‐way mixed‐effects model to evaluate measurement consistency, with observers as random effects and measurement parameters as fixed effects. ICC values range from 0 to 1, where a value higher than 0.75 indicates excellent agreement [20].

Study Design Demographic and Reliability Data

A total of 120 knees from 60 patients were included, including 22 male and 38 female patients with a mean age of 21.8 years (range: 14–40 years). The cohort comprised 60 patients with patellofemoral instability in the low‐grade J‐sign group (29 J‐sign negative; 31 Grade I) and 60 patients in the high‐grade J‐sign group (34 Grade II; 26 Grade III).

Diagnostic Performance of Parameters at Both Anatomical Planes

Table 3 presents the AUC and optimal cutoff values for each parameter derived from the calculations of Youden J. At the trochlear groove entry plane, the AUC values for distinguishing low‐grade and high‐grade J‐sign were as follows: AUCSA = 0.912, AUCLTI = 0.803, and AUCTrochlear depth = 0.858. For SA, sensitivity was 0.87, specificity 0.87, and the optimal cutoff value 149.8°. In contrast, measurements at the Roman arch plane exhibited lower discriminative performance with AUCTrochlear depth = 0.714, AUCSA = 0.740, and AUCLTI = 0.694. Specifically, SA demonstrated reduced sensitivity of 0.67, comparable specificity of 0.77, and a lower cutoff threshold of 140.2°. Overall, all parameters measured at both anatomical levels demonstrated diagnostic utility except LTI assessed at the Roman arch plane, with AUC values ranging from 0.69 to 0.91. The Delong test revealed that significant differences were present in the predictive performance of the SA (p < 0.001), LTI (p = 0.003), and trochlear depth (p < 0.001) between the Roman arch plane and the trochlear groove entry plane. The sulcus angle at the trochlear groove entry level exhibited the highest discriminative capacity (Figure 4).

Discussion

Summary of Main Findings

The primary objective of this study was to demonstrate differential diagnostic and predictive efficacy for patellar tracking between trochlear groove entry plane and Roman arch plane measurements of trochlear morphology using CT imaging. In this study, quantitative analyses demonstrated that the measurements of the SA, trochlear depth, and LTI at the trochlear groove entry plane exhibited superior diagnostic efficacy compared with the Roman arch plane, with the SA achieving the highest AUC of 0.912 (optimal cutoff value: 149.8°). Patients with high‐grade J‐sign demonstrated a significantly increased SA and significant reductions in trochlear depth and LTI (p < 0.001). These main findings may guide surgeons in utilizing radiographic parameters to objectively grade patellar tracking abnormalities and formulate morphology‐specific corrective protocols for patellofemoral instability.

Clinical Significance of Identifying High‐Grade J‐Sign

The quantitative evaluation of patellar tracking severity through axial imaging parameters provides critical preoperative insights for surgical decision‐making in cases of patellar maltracking. A retrospective multivariate analysis identified preoperative high‐grade J‐sign as the predominant risk factor for residual graft laxity following medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction [3]. In a minimum 3‐year follow‐up study of 211 post‐MPFL reconstruction patients by Sappey‐Marinier et al., those with positive J‐sign demonstrated a 3.9‐fold increased risk of graft failure compared to J‐sign‐negative cohorts [21]. Furthermore, abnormal patellar tracking has been mechanistically linked to elevated patellofemoral contact pressures, which may predispose patients to postoperative stiffness and accelerated progression of patellofemoral osteoarthritis [22, 23]. These findings collectively emphasize that it is necessary to consider the preoperative identification and correction of J‐sign‐associated tracking abnormalities to optimize surgical outcomes and mitigate complications.

Superiority of the Trochlear Groove Entry Plane

The most proximal axial imaging plane is universally recognized as the gold standard for evaluating trochlear morphology, as low‐grade trochlear dysplasia may be identified in the proximal trochlear segment. Therefore, the proximal axial cut may present superior positive detection efficiency for morphological abnormalities of the trochlear compared to the Roman arch plane located at the more distal level. The surgical interventions based on trochlear groove entry plane assessments may contribute to reduced long‐term surgical failure rates. Additionally, severe trochlear dysplasia might show more morphological abnormalities in the proximal axial plane of trochlea, thereby facilitating more precise characterization of pathological alterations. In this study, the trochlear groove entry plane was selected as the representative anatomical level for the proximal trochlear region, which was defined as the full cartilaginous contour of the trochlear groove becoming fully discernible. The present study further discerned that the trochlear groove entry plane shows greater diagnostic efficacy in identifying morphological abnormalities of the trochlea compared to the Roman arch plane, providing novel quantitative references for evaluating patellar tracking patterns with interrater reliability coefficients ranging from 0.877 to 0.970. The superior AUC values and diagnostic cutoffs for parameters measured at the trochlear groove entry plane indicate not only better diagnostic accuracy but also suggest a stronger potential association between proximal trochlear morphology and severe patellar maltracking, as captured by the high‐grade J‐sign. Future studies incorporating quantitative measures of patellar stability and correlation analyses with these morphological parameters are warranted to further elucidate their predictive relationship.

Comparison of Diagnostic Performance With Prior Studies on Trochlear Morphology

Patellofemoral instability is widely recognized as a multifactorial condition, in which osseous abnormalities including trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, increased tibial tubercle–trochlear groove distance, lower limb rotational malalignment, Q angle, and soft‐tissue deficiencies collectively contribute to the loss of patellar stability [24]. Within this complex framework, the morphological configuration of the femoral trochlea represents a cornerstone anatomical determinant of patellar tracking [10]. The present study was specifically designed to dissect and quantify the proximal trochlear morphology, as captured at the trochlear groove entry plane to the dynamic maltracking signified by the high‐grade J‐sign. Trochlear morphology, including the SA, LTI, and trochlear depth, has been established as critical quantitative parameters for evaluating patellofemoral instability [6, 13, 25]. Among these, the sulcus angle was identified as one of the most reproducible measurements [18, 24]. A prospective cohort study with a minimum 2‐year follow‐up conducted ROC curve analysis demonstrating that the SA ≥ 154° was associated with lateral patellar redislocation [26]. Tanaka et al. utilized MRI to measure the trochlear sulcus angle at both bony and cartilaginous landmarks for identifying patellar instability and established cutoff values of 145° for bony landmarks and 154° for cartilaginous landmarks [12]. In contrast, our analysis of the Roman arch plane reported an optimal cutoff value of 140.2° for detecting high‐grade J‐sign through ROC curve analysis (AUC = 0.74, sensitivity: 0.67 specificity: 0.77). This finding may investigate that the development from abnormal patellar tracking to patellar dislocation may be overlooked in clinical assessments, particularly when quantitative evaluation of proximal trochlear morphology is not systematically performed. Wang et al. revealed that over 90% of patients with high‐grade J‐sign presented with multiple lower structural bone abnormalities, with trochlear dysplasia identified as the most common factor [1]. The assessment of trochlear shape can provide direct insights into early interventions and understanding of patellofemoral kinematics.

The LTI was defined as the parameter having the greatest performance in the utility of diagnosing patellar instability. A previous study documented that patients with the J‐sign exhibiting the jumping sign demonstrated significantly flatter LTI angles compared to those presenting with the glide sign [5]. However, the potential correlation between LTI and patellar tracking abnormalities remains unexplored in prior investigations. We found that the cutoff value of LTI was 16° and 20° at the trochlear groove entry plane and the Roman arch plane, respectively, to distinguish the low‐grade J‐sign and high‐grade J‐sign. Biyani et al. created computational models from high‐resolution MRI scans to discern that the LTI tended to increase with increasing depth of the trochlear groove as the flexion angle increased. This relationship was mechanistically linked to variations in patellar tracking patterns, with LTI identified as the predominant determinant of divergence in patellar tracking [6]. Furthermore, Senavongse et al. demonstrated that a reduction in LTI to 0° was associated with a 70% decrease in the force required for lateral patellar displacement [27]. These findings collectively emphasize the critical need to explore the mechanistic relationship between LTI and patellar tracking patterns. Besides, this present study further demonstrated that trochlear depth has significant diagnostic utility in identifying pathological patellar motion patterns. A three‐dimensional computed tomography analysis reported that quantitative evaluation of trochlear depth facilitates reproducible characterization of patellar kinematics [28].

Advantages of Computed Tomography in Trochlear Assessment

Axial imaging provides comprehensive tomographic visualization for evaluating trochlear morphology. All anatomical parameters in this study were measured using CT scans. In contrast to X‐ray, CT imaging demonstrates superior multiplanar reconstruction capabilities and cross‐sectional resolution. This enhanced technical capacity enables a detailed assessment of trochlear morphology and patellofemoral alignment. Furthermore, CT offers distinct advantages over MRI, including faster acquisition times, lower operational costs, and superior visualization of cortical bone definition. These advantages contribute to its broader clinical applicability and enhanced utility in preoperative surgical planning [29].

Limitations

This study has limitations. Firstly, parameter measurements were performed by multiple observers, inevitably introducing measurement bias that may influence interobserver reliability. Secondly, statistically significant age differences were reported between the high‐grade J‐sign group and low‐grade group, potentially confounding quantitative analyses of trochlear morphology due to age‐related degenerative changes in cartilage thickness and subchondral bone density. Thirdly, Although CT provides excellent bony detail and is widely used for preoperative planning, it does not directly visualize cartilage thickness or soft tissue structures. MRI could offer complementary information on cartilaginous morphology, which may further refine the assessment of trochlear dysplasia. Finally, the single‐center design inherently limits generalizability, as the regional differences may lead to sampling bias.

Conclusion

In assessing patellar tracking in individuals with recurrent patellar instability, axial CT scans at the trochlear groove entry‐level show better predictive accuracy for identifying the J‐sign than those taken at the Roman arch level. Notably, the CT‐measured sulcus angle offers an improved distinction between mild and severe J‐sign cases, delivering valuable preoperative information for patellar maltracking.
