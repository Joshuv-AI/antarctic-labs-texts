Multiple External Invasive Root Resorption and Calcification in Systemic Sclerosis—Case Report
Authors: Jee‐Yun Leung, Geoffrey Heithersay, Richard Logan, Sharon Liberali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThere are few reports in the literature of multiple external invasive root resorption (EIRR) lesions in patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc), and an association between root resorption and SSc is not well established. We report the only case that comprehensively illustrates—with the combination of histopathology, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), orthopantomograms and photography—pulpal and periodontal ligament calcifications, multiple EIRR lesions progressing over time and the sequelae of treatment. Sixteen affected teeth, documented over 14 years, are presented.SummaryA 51‐year‐old female presented for overall oral management of progressive SSc. Extraction of symptomatic teeth 16 and 26 revealed palatal EIRR. Retrospective dental record review revealed four previously extracted affected teeth. Subsequent CBCT revealed a further 10 affected teeth. Rapid EIRR progression of teeth 25 and 47 ensued and were extracted with tooth 46. Histopathology revealed ankylosis with mature bony infill of EIRR lesions, bone abutting dentine with absent cementum and pulpal and PDL calcifications. The teeth 46–47 extraction site developed stage I medication‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw secondary to denosumab use, was managed with conservative measures and resolved.Key Learning PointsPatients with SSc may display a higher prevalence of EIRR. Awareness should be raised about this potential association. Dentists treating patients with SSc should conscientiously search for these easily unnoticed, often subgingival lesions, since early management may reduce the risk of teeth succumbing to pathological fracture. However, if multiple progressive lesions present, no intervention may be appropriate due to the aggressive nature of EIRR and potential futile outcome of treatment.Keywords: denosumab, invasive cervical resorption, multiple resorption, root resorption, scleroderma, systemic sclerosis

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc), otherwise known as scleroderma, is a rare progressive autoimmune connective tissue disease characterised by fibrosis, microvascular damage and immune dysfunction (van den Hoogen et al. 2013). These result in degenerative changes and hypertrophy of collagenous tissue involving the skin, bone and other internal organs.

Commonly reported orofacial manifestations include telangiectasia, a mask‐like expression, nasal‐beaking, microstomia, ankyloglossia, tongue depapillation, TMJ erosion and peri‐articular involvement, oral ulcerations, widened periodontal ligament (PDL) space without increased mobility, periodontal disease (associated with high clinical attachment loss and low bleeding on probing values), gingival recession, mandibular osteal erosion and increased risk of oral cancer (Nagy et al. 1994; Dagenais et al. 2015; Baron et al. 2015; Porter and Scully 2008; Jagadish et al. 2012; Crincoli et al. 2016; Hadj Said et al. 2016; Gomes da Silva et al. 2019). Dry mouth (often associated with Sjögren's syndrome) is common, and oral hygiene can be challenging to maintain due sclerodactyly‐related reduction in manual dexterity and microstomia (Baron et al. 2015; Hadj Said et al. 2016; Gomes da Silva et al. 2019). Accordingly, caries risk may be greater (Poole et al. 2005).

The American Association of Endodontists define resorption as ‘a condition associated with either a physiological or pathologic process resulting in a loss of dentine, cementum and/or bone’ (American Association of Endodontists 2020). There are multiple classification systems that categorise root resorption (Lin et al. 2022). These are largely divided into external resorption and internal resorption, and include extent of resorption (Heithersay 2007; Abbott and Lin 2022; Mavridou et al. 2022). Root resorption is typically caused by trauma, infection, or hyperplastic invasive processes (Heithersay 2007, 1999a). However, rare instances of unknown cause that do not fit these categories are deemed ‘idiopathic’ (Heithersay 2007).

To date, there have only been 12 patients with root resorption (with no other identifiable cause) believed to be associated with SSc described in the literature, across six authorship groups (Rout et al. 1996; de Figueiredo et al. 2008; Arroyo‐Bote et al. 2017; Matsuda et al. 2018; Memida et al. 2019). Seven of these patients displayed multiple (more than two) root resorption lesions (Arroyo‐Bote et al. 2017; Matsuda et al. 2018; Memida et al. 2019). Only one patient had both cone beam computerised tomography (CBCT) and histopathology investigations performed (Matsuda et al. 2018). The proposed aetiopathogenesis varies between authorship groups. Nevertheless, it has only recently been suggested that patients with SSc have an increased prevalence of root resorption, and hence roots need to be carefully assessed in patients with SSc (Memida et al. 2019).

Two case series found a higher prevalence of root resorption in patients with SSc than in unaffected patients (Rout et al. 1996; Memida et al. 2019). Though the presence of multiple root resorption lesions is an unusual and rarely reported finding in patients with SSc, this may be due to lack of awareness of the condition. The early changes are subtle and may be difficult to detect at the cervical to subgingival level. Unless the clinician is specifically looking out for the lesions, they can easily go unnoticed. Hence, it is important to add to the literature to raise awareness about this potential association.

This report presents the only case in the literature comprehensively illustrated with the combination of histopathology, orthopantomograms, CBCT and photography of a patient with SSc exhibiting multiple external invasive root resorption (EIRR) lesions progressing over time, pulpal and PDL calcifications and the sequelae of treatment. Sixteen affected teeth, documented over 14 years, are presented.

Report

This case report has been written according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Case reports in Endodontics (PRICE) 2020 guidelines.

A 51‐year‐old Italian‐ethnicity female patient with an 18‐year diagnosis of diffuse SSc was referred to the Special Needs Unit of the Adelaide Dental Hospital. Secondary to SSc, the patient exhibited CREST syndrome (calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, esophageal dysmotility, sclerodactyly and telangiectasia), gastro‐oesophageal reflux disease, digital ulcers, interstitial lung disease, hypertension, low body weight and had a history of left heel ischaemia with cellulitis. She was under future consideration for mycophenolate mofetil, since she was previously trialled on methotrexate, IV cyclophosphamide and methylprednisolone with little symptomatic improvement and poor tolerance. Other medical history included osteoporosis and iron deficiency anaemia. Medications at time of examination were: 5‐year history of 6‐monthly denosumab (which was commenced at least 3 years after EIRR was first detected), calcium, cholecalciferol, ramipril, ranitidine, Quick‐eze (antacid), acidophilus probiotic, iron supplements, psyllium husk, vitamin C and fish oil.

Clinical examination and orthopantomogram on 13/02/2019 revealed telangiectasia, nasal beaking, marked TMJ peri‐articular calcinosis with condylar erosion, microstomia with constriction of the oral orifice, reduced radiographic visibility of the bilateral inferior alveolar nerve canals and ankyloglossia (Figures 1 and 2). Generalised gingival recession was present, most notably on teeth 32 (buccal 11 mm), 43 (buccal 11 mm) and 42 (buccal 8 mm). Oral hygiene was satisfactory, despite sclerodactyly rendering this difficult, with minimal plaque in accessible areas and light plaque on the lower molars (Figure 1). Dental caries was present on surfaces: tooth 23 disto‐palatal, tooth 37 buccal and mesio‐occlusal (difficult access with dental handpiece) and tooth 47 buccal (inaccessible with dental handpiece). Naturally decoronated teeth 31 and 41 were splinted as a composite ribbon bridge. Tooth 35 root was retained (Figure 2).

Dental history revealed a regular dental attendance record. As a former dental assistant, the patient was highly motivated with oral hygiene maintenance. She cleaned twice daily with fluoridated toothpaste and a combination of an electric toothbrush, orthodontic toothbrush to access the posterior teeth, floss, flossettes and interdental brushes. Due to low weight, she was on a high calorific diet to meet her nutritional needs, consisting of: milk shakes, sugar in her coffee three times daily (followed by drinking water) and ice‐cream and cake at night (followed by tooth brushing). GC Saliva‐Check BUFFER revealed normal quality and quantity of saliva in a patient with a moderately restored dentition.

Given the patient was planned for mycophenolate mofetil, the known future deterioration of her disease, and that the risks of dental treatment would increase over her lifetime, a decision was made together with the patient for a shortened dental arch. Though a conservative approach of attempting restoration or topical fluoride application of the posterior teeth, with regular cleaning, was initially considered, there was concern she may become ‘inoperable’ in future should dental problems arise when SSc complications were more progressed.

Prior to the planned dental extractions, the patient had no previous diagnosis of MRONJ despite multiple recent extractions; she was not on concurrent systemic glucocorticosteroids or immunomodifying drugs, had no bisphosphonate history, denosumab dosing was non‐oncological, her last denosumab injection was 5 months prior and several CTX results suggested reasonable bone turnover rebound following denosumab washout. It was determined the benefits of extractions likely outweighed the risks of MRONJ (Ruggiero et al. 2022; Oral and Dental Expert Group 2019; Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme 2017).

Extractions of the symptomatic teeth and affected molar teeth (teeth 15, 25, 36, 37, 46 and 47) were planned for a step‐wise approach, with two extractions performed each time, 1–2 months prior to the end of each denosumab cycle. Though early intervention for preservation of the affected anterior teeth was considered, due to the sheer number of root resorption lesions detected, a possible poorer success rate due to the technical challenges associated with difficult oral access, concern that incomplete removal of clastic cells would allow the aggressive resorptive process to continue regardless, and the multiple extensive aggressive nature of the lesions, the decision was made to monitor the affected anterior teeth.

Due to the rapid progression of symptomatic tooth 25 and severity of tooth 47 resorption, these teeth were extracted first on 04/12/2019 (Figure 9). Tooth 47 had minimal mobility and required surgical extraction, indicating ankylosis. The teeth 46 and 47 extraction site developed American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS) stage I MRONJ, characterised by a slit of 2–3 mm exposed bone in the tooth 47 site without symptoms or signs of infection, and possible small bony sequestrum (Ruggiero et al. 2022). This was managed conservatively with chlorhexidine wound care, and resolved sometime between 15 weeks and 7 months post‐extraction (review appointments were delayed due to COVID‐19 restrictions). In light of the histological findings and MRONJ development, the remaining extractions were abandoned. If a coronal fracture were to transpire in the other affected teeth, the roots were to be left in situ, as had occurred previously with unproblematic tooth 35. If any teeth were to develop symptoms, these would be treated endodontically. Ongoing maintenance was to comprise conservative preventive oral hygiene measures, monthly recalls and tooth polishing in order to prevent calculus accumulation and superimposed infection, since there was concern that periodontal probing and scaling in areas of root resorption may risk lesion progression. The early buccal caries on tooth 37 was inaccessible with handpieces, and was arrested with silver fluoride (Creighton Dental CSDS).

The patient was recalled until June 2024 with no further significant findings or progression of lesions noted. She deceased in early 2025.

Discussion

A relationship between SSc and root resorption is not well established in the literature, and only features in isolated case reports. Cases with long clinical follow‐up, and the combination of 2D/3D imaging and histology to illustrate the disease process are lacking, thus making it important to contribute our findings.

The aetiology of EIRR is poorly understood. Potential predisposing factors include trauma, intra‐coronal bleaching, surgery, orthodontics, periodontal treatment, bruxism, delayed eruption, developmental defects, interproximal stripping, poor oral health, malocclusion, extraction of adjacent teeth, restorative work and exposure to cats (Lin et al. 2022; Heithersay 1999b; Von Arx et al. 2009; Mavridou et al. 2017).

While the successful management of most external cervical resorptions (ECRs) has been well documented, this is not the case with rare cases of multiple external invasive cervical root resorptions or external invasive radicular root resorptions either with specific predisposing factors as illustrated with the current report or with no obvious (idiopathic) aetiology. These multiple resorptive lesions are aggressively destructive and not amendable to attempted treatment. In the absence of superimposed infection, decoronation will ultimately occur with subsequent epithelial coverage of residual root structure.

Rout et al. (1996) first reported a higher prevalence of root resorption in patients with SSc. Four out of 12 dentate patients (five teeth) displayed either coronal or apical root resorption which, at 33%, was higher expected for general population. In the absence of other identifiable causes, root resorption was suggested to be due to regressive pulpal tissue changes secondary to SSc. However subsequent case reports have noted positive cold sensibility testing responses (de Figueiredo et al. 2008).

de Figueiredo et al. (2008) observed external apical resorption of tooth 38 coincident with mandibular bone erosion. The authors proposed that increased physical pressure from tight facial mucocutaneous tissues initiates cell signalling to inhibit anti‐invasion factor in cementum or PDL cells. Furthermore, they argued that since patients with SSc exhibit a decreased calcium‐phosphorous ratio and absence of magnesium in dentine, root resorption occurs together with bone resorption as a defence mechanism to maintain some normal bone structure and reduce pathological fracture risk.

Arroyo‐Bote et al. (2017) presented a case of ECR affecting teeth 35 and 36. Though a normal external cervical area was reported for tooth 47, it likely also had invasive cervical resorption as evidenced by the radio‐opaque line demarcating the pulp from invading resorptive tissue. They proposed that SSc causes bone resorption of the mandible and distal phalanges of fingers (acro‐osteolysis) due to impaired angiogenesis and vascularisation, caused by occlusive vasculopathy and fibrotic skin compression, resulting in an imbalance in bone remodelling favouring resorption and osteoclastogenesis (Siao‐Pin et al. 2016; Park et al. 2016). This stimulates odontoclastic processes, since dentine and cementum resorption are regulated by similar mechanisms to those controlling bone resorption.

Our patient displayed 16 teeth (16, 15, 25, 26, 37, 36, 35, 34, 31, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46 and 47) affected by EIRR over 14 years. Matsuda et al. (2018) reported the first histological analysis of root resorption in a patient with SSc. Multiple root and cervical resorption lesions (seven molars, five premolars) were detected, with an unclear transition from alveolar bone to root profile. Histopathology revealed resorption of fractured tooth samples, as well as irregular replacement of pulp and dentine by reparative bone‐like tissue, suggesting excessive bone formation in patients with SSc is related to the resorption. The Japanese group coined the term ‘multiple external root resorption’ (MERR) and subsequently performed the first cross‐sectional study in 2019 to examine MERR as a manifestation of SSc (Memida et al. 2019). MERR was detected in 4 of 41 SSc patients. Since only 30 cases of idiopathic MERR were reported between 1930 and 2015 in the general population (Wu et al. 2016), the group proposed that MERR is associated with SSc. No significant differences in prevalence of systemic involvement were found between non‐MERR and MERR patients, except digital ulcers. However the rate of maxillofacial calcinosis, facial skin sclerosis, limited mouth opening, temporomandibular joint disorder and tongue rigidity, were significantly higher in patients with MERR. Given that multiple teeth are affected in MERR, the group hypothesised that gene mutation or enzyme dysfunction play a role (Matsuda et al. 2018). They also proposed that cementum abrasion caused by occlusal forces may be a cause, since MERR in SSc patients are usually detected in the cervical region (Memida et al. 2019).

Other cases of MERR in SSc patients have since been reported. Journo et al. (2022) describe a case of invasive cervical resorption affecting four teeth. Elmatary et al. (2023) presented a case with eight maxillary teeth and six mandibular teeth affected by ECR on clinical examination and CBCT, without the characteristic vascularity of resorptive defects upon probing.

Pulpal and PDL calcifications were also detected in our patient. Jung et al. (2013) presented the first documented case of such calcifications in SSc, which were detected on CBCT. The authors suggested these manifestations could be linked to major pathogenic features of SSc such as calcinosis, vasculopathy and fibrosis, thus contributing to the phenotypic spectrum of the disease. Because calcinosis frequently occur at sites of recurrent microtrauma, they hypothesised that masticatory forces account for PDL calcifications. Vasculopathy may lead to reduced vascular flow and microhemorrhages, inducing pathologic periodontal mineralisation. Since pulp calcifications have been observed in other connective tissue disorders, such as Ehler‐Danlos and Marfan syndromes, they also propose a similar pathogenesis (Jung et al. 2013).

Incidentally, our patient also displayed extensive TMJ calcinosis, which may represent an unusual phenotype. However, since it did not affect our patient's quality of life enough to warrant intervention, this finding was not comprehensively investigated nor treated.

Finally, it is worth noting the increasing literature on a possible relationship between root resorption and denosumab use, with which our patient was medicated for osteoporosis (Wang et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2025; Mota et al. 2025). Osteoprotegrin (OPG)‐receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL) pathway is the key signalling pathway in bone remodelling, and has been recognised in root resorption (Wang and McCauley 2011; Hall and Guyton 2011). Denosumab binds to and neutralises RANKL activity (Tsourdi et al. 2019), thus inhibiting the maturation, activation and function of osteoclasts and, ultimately, bone turnover (Hall and Guyton 2011). Therefore, systemic factors such as denosumab that interfere with osteoclast function through the OPG‐RANKL pathway may contribute to external root resorption (Arroyo‐Bote et al. 2017; Wang and McCauley 2011).

Since progressive EIRR lesions were first detected 3 years prior to the commencement of denosumab in our patient, it is likely that SSc (rather than denosumab) was the initiating risk factor for this dental presentation. However, rather than acting as a confounder, there is a possibility denosumab acted as a compounder in the rate of lesion development and progression in this case, though this is difficult to ascertain.

Interestingly, neither osteoporosis nor denosumab were mentioned in any previous case reports associating SSc and EIRR, despite a higher prevalence of osteoporosis being reported amongst those with SSc compared to the general population (Dobrovolskaya et al. 2018; Bimal et al. 2021; Omair et al. 2013). SSc shares several risk factors with osteoporosis, including female sex, older age, vitamin D deficiency and low body mass index (Dobrovolskaya et al. 2018; Bimal et al. 2021; Omair et al. 2013). Additionally, many SSc‐related complications have been found to increase osteoporosis risk in international cohorts, such as the presence of calcinosis, sclerodactyly, lung complications, malnutrition, reflux, telangiectasia, disability and corticosteroid and other immunosuppressive therapy (Dobrovolskaya et al. 2018; Bimal et al. 2021).

Deeb et al. (2019) published the first case report associating denosumab use and discontinuation with multiple idiopathic cervical root resorption. In 2021 a retrospective case–control study found denosumab use as a predictor for developing ECR (Alyahya and Myers 2021). Interestingly, all 10 patients on denosumab developed ECR while using the drug, two of whom developed a progressive generalised form of ECR upon discontinuation (in agreement with Deeb et al.'s ‘rebound effect hypothesis’). Other case reports also described multiple vertebral fractures after denosumab discontinuation, with the hypothesis that the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving multiple vertebral fractures after denosumab discontinuation may also cause generalised cervical root resorption (Alyahya and Myers 2021; Tsourdi et al. 2017; Lamy et al. 2019). Mota et al. (2025) described a case of 2 teeth affected by invasive cervical root resorption in a patient who had discontinued denosumab 1 year prior. However, other case reports present patients who developed multiple idiopathic cervical root resorption during denosumab use, raising questions about the ‘rebound effect hypothesis’ (Beaumont et al. 2022; Mikušková et al. 2022). Notably, Mikušková et al. (2022) reported numerous active osteoclasts in the cervical resorption fossa, suggesting that RANKL‐independent pathways may be involved (Wang et al. 2023).

It has been proposed that inhibition of the RANK/RANKL pathway may lead to compensatory alternative resorptive pathways being overactivated, which may preferentially act on root surface osteoclasts and cementoclasts, resulting in resorption (Lin et al. 2025). Additionally, prolonged RANK/RANKL pathway inhibition can lead to preclast cell accumulation and activation in the presence of inflammatory stimulators TNF‐α, IL‐1, IL‐6 and lipopolysaccharides. Inflammatory signalling may promote osteoclast activity independent of RANKL (Feng et al. 2019). The evidence in the literature between denosumab and ECR is currently still being established, and an Internet site (https://www.ehealthme.com/ds/prolia/tooth‐resorption/) has been established to report cases of tooth resorption in patients treated with denosumab (eHealthMe 2025). It reports 49 cases of root resorption in patients receiving Prolia since 2013 (prevalence of 0.03%).

Conclusion

We document the only case of a SSc patient in the literature that comprehensively illustrates (with the combination of histopathology, orthopantomograms, CBCT and photography) pulpal and PDL calcifications, multiple (16) EIRR lesions progressing over a long 14‐year period (8 years of prior dental records, 6 years of active follow‐up), and the sequelae of treatment. The histology presented attempts to link the aetiopathogenesis to why these lesions occur in patients with SSc.

There are currently few cases of multiple root resorption lesions in patients with SSc described in the literature, possibly due to the difficulty in detecting these lesions. This report adds to the small but growing number of reports in the literature that patients with SSc may display a higher prevalence of root resorption.

It is important that awareness is raised about this potential association. Dentists treating patients with SSc should conscientiously search for these often cervico‐subgingival lesions, which can easily go unnoticed in the early stages, since early management is crucial for ensuring a reasonable prognosis before teeth succumb to pathological fracture. However, if multiple progressive resorptive lesions are detected, no intervention is considered appropriate due to the aggressive nature of EIRR and the potential futile outcome of any attempted treatment.
