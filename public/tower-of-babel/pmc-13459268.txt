Immersive ethical role-play in undergraduate nursing ethics education: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Yayun Song, Pu Lin, Gang Hu, Jia Qu, Zhenhong Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing ethics teaching often relies on lectures and paper-based cases, which may not adequately prepare students for ethically ambiguous clinical practice. Evidence on low-resource, narrative, immersive approaches for nursing ethics education remains limited.ObjectiveTo explore how undergraduate nursing students described their ethical reasoning, ethical positioning, and perceived learning after participating in an immersive ethical role-play simulation (IERS).DesignQualitative descriptive study.Setting and participantsA total of 102 second-year undergraduate nursing students at Hubei Polytechnic University in central China participated in November 2025.MethodsStudents took part in a 2.5-hour, peer-enacted IERS built around an end-of-life decision-making scenario. Anonymous post-session survey data were collected using one two-part mapping item and two open-ended questions. Introduction

Nursing ethics education is expected to prepare students not only to name ethical principles but also to use them in situations marked by uncertainty, hierarchy, emotional distress, and competing obligations [1, 2]. In many undergraduate settings, however, ethics teaching still relies heavily on lectures, textbook cases, and examination-oriented learning [3–5]. These approaches may strengthen declarative knowledge, yet they do not always help students develop situated ethical judgment, moral sensitivity, or the confidence to act within complex clinical environments [6].

The nursing education literature increasingly supports experiential approaches for ethics learning [7, 8]. Role-play may be particularly useful because it requires students to speak and decide from within a position rather than comment from outside it [9, 10]. Immersive role-play, adapted from immersive theater, has shown promise in clinical ethics education by placing learners inside emotionally and socially textured dilemmas rather than presenting them with neatly resolved cases [11]. Yet narrative-driven, student-enacted immersive formats designed specifically for nursing ethics remain uncommon, and evidence from Chinese undergraduate nursing education is limited.

End-of-life (EOL) care provides an especially relevant context for ethics teaching because it brings patient autonomy, family wishes, professional responsibility, and resource considerations into direct tension [12–14]. In China, these tensions are further shaped by rapid population ageing, expanding palliative care services, and the still-evolving implementation of advance care planning (ACP) [15–18]. Preparing nursing students for such decisions therefore requires learning approaches that can accommodate both principle-based reasoning and the relational realities of bedside practice.

In response, we designed an Immersive Ethical Role-Play Simulation (IERS), a structured, narrative, character-based teaching activity informed by immersive role-play [11] and adapted to a low-resource undergraduate context. The format also drew inspiration from Jubensha, a familiar Chinese social storytelling form [19, 20], while replacing professional performers with student participants to improve curricular feasibility. The aim of this study was to explore how undergraduate nursing students described their ethical reasoning, post-role ethical positioning, and perceived learning after participating in the IERS.

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative descriptive design [21]. The overall approach was conceptually informed by experiential learning [7], transformative learning [22], and immersive role-play [11], while remaining close to participants’ own descriptions of the activity. Three linked analytic components were used, with each approach selected according to the form and purpose of the corresponding survey prompt. First, descriptive content analysis was applied to Q1-2 responses because these items generated short character-to-principle mapping responses. This approach allowed us to summarize which bioethical principles students identified in relation to statements made by their characters during the simulation. Second, typological analysis [23] was used for Q3 responses because this item asked students to evaluate their relationship to the stance they had enacted. This approach allowed us to characterize recurring modes of post-role ethical positioning across roles. Third, reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) [24, 25] was used for Q4 responses because this item invited broader reflection on perceived learning gains. This approach allowed us to develop interpretive themes across the dataset. Together, the three analytic components moved from descriptive mapping, to ethical positioning, to broader experiential meaning-making, in alignment with the structure of the survey and the aims of the study.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at Hubei Polytechnic University, central China, in November 2025. A total of 102 s-year undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a nursing ethics course participated in the IERS, and all completed the post-session survey (response rate: 100%). No formal exclusion criteria were applied. The simulation was delivered across three class sessions, each including five or six concurrent groups of six students, yielding 17 groups in total. Participation was voluntary and did not affect course grading. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethics approval was granted by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. Hbpu2025061).

The immersive ethical role-play simulation

Clinical scenario

The IERS centered on Mr Liu Jianguo (pseudonym), a 74-year-old retired technician admitted to an intensive care unit with terminal lung cancer. The central dilemma was whether to proceed with endotracheal intubation, which might prolong life at the cost of additional suffering, or to transition to palliative care. The patient had verbally told his bedside nurse that he did not want further aggressive treatment, but this preference had not been formally documented, creating the ethical ambiguity at the core of the simulation.

Character design

Six characters were created to represent distinct stakeholder perspectives. Each role combined a publicly stated position with hidden motivations, pressures, or conflicts. The design principle was deliberate moral complexity: no role was written as ethically uncomplicated. This structure was intended to prompt perspective-taking, generate productive tension during discussion, and create material for post-role ethical reflection (Table 1).

Character design of the IERS

The nurse role was designed as a pivotal point of tension because it concentrated patient advocacy, professional hierarchy, and personal risk in a single position. The inclusion of roles with ethically complex motivations also created a basis for the post-simulation question asking students whether they still agreed with the stance they had defended.

Session structure

The IERS lasted 2.5 h and unfolded in six phases. Before character scripts were distributed, the facilitator gave a standardized briefing. Students were instructed to introduce themselves in the first person as their character, maintain that perspective during the activity, and read the script materials in the prescribed sequence (Table 2).

IERS session structure

Background audio materials consisted of a recorded intensive care soundscape including ventilator alarms, monitor beeps, and ambient family conversation. These were played at low volume during Phases 2 and 3 to support contextual immersion without requiring a simulated clinical environment.

The scenario and character materials were developed by the research team and reviewed by an experienced intensive care clinician for clinical plausibility and by nursing ethics educators for alignment with the educational aims of the session. The simulation was not graded, and students were informed in advance that survey responses would be anonymous.

Data collection

Following the individual debrief (Phase 5) and before the teacher-led discussion (Phase 6), students completed an anonymous digital survey comprising three open-ended prompts. Rather than using individual or group interviews, this study collected written narrative responses through a brief open-ended survey developed by the research team specifically for this study. The prompts were designed on the basis of the study aims and the learning objectives of the immersive ethical role-play session to elicit students’ in-role ethical reasoning, post-role positioning, and perceived learning gains. Two nursing ethics educators reviewed the prompts for clarity and alignment with the study aim before use. An English version of the survey prompts is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Q1-2 (character-to-principle mapping): “State one thing your character said during the session, and identify the bioethical principle this statement reflected.”

Q3: “Do you still agree with your character’s stated position? Why?”

Q4: “What did you gain from this simulation?”

Participants wrote their responses in Chinese; quotations are presented here in English translation for publication.

Data analysis

Q1-2 responses were coded independently by two members of the research team against the four canonical bioethical principles (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice). For tabulation, each response was assigned one primary principle; additional principles mentioned in the same response were noted narratively rather than double-counted. Coding differences were resolved through discussion.

Q3 responses were analyzed inductively using typological analysis [23] to identify recurring ways in which students positioned themselves in relation to the stance they had enacted.

Q4 responses were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis [24, 25]. Initial coding was conducted by the first author and then discussed iteratively with co-investigators who had not facilitated the sessions. Reflexive memos were kept throughout to document analytic decisions and assumptions.

To support interpretive resonance, preliminary themes and positioning types were discussed informally with 12 participants (two complete six-member groups). Participants indicated that the interpretations were recognizable, and no substantive changes were requested.

Q4 responses ranged from one sentence to approximately 200 Chinese characters (mean approximately 85 characters; total corpus approximately 8,670 characters). Although briefer than interview transcripts, the dataset was sufficiently large for pattern-oriented analysis across the full corpus. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, we focused on developing coherent themes across the dataset rather than claiming code or theoretical saturation [25].

Researcher reflexivity

The first author designed and facilitated the IERS sessions and also led the primary analysis. This insider role provided close familiarity with the pedagogical intentions, classroom dynamics, and ethical tensions embedded in the simulation. At the same time, it created a risk that the analysis might overemphasize intended learning outcomes or interpret student responses too favorably.

This positionality informed the analytic strategy. Because the first author had designed and facilitated the IERS, the analysis needed to balance interpretive engagement with safeguards against overinterpretation. The more structured analysis of Q1-2 helped keep the character-to-principle mapping close to participants’ written responses. The typological analysis of Q3 allowed different forms of post-role positioning to be described without assuming a single pattern of ethical reflection. For Q4, reflexive thematic analysis was used with memoing and team discussion to support interpretation of perceived learning gains while making the researchers’ assumptions more explicit.

To enhance reflexive rigour, the first author kept analytic memos documenting assumptions, uncertainties, and decisions during coding and theme development. Interpretations were discussed with co-investigators who had not facilitated the sessions, providing critical distance from the educational intervention. Preliminary themes and positioning types were also discussed informally with 12 participants, who indicated that the interpretations were recognizable.

Results

Participant characteristics

All 102 students who participated in the IERS completed the post-session survey. Data were collected across three sessions (Session 1: n = 36; Session 2: n = 36; Session 3: n = 30), comprising 17 groups in total. Role allocation was evenly distributed across the six characters (n = 17 per role).

Character-to-principle mapping (Q1-2)

Across the 102 Q1-2 responses, autonomy was the most frequently identified primary principle (50/102), followed by beneficence (16/102), justice (12/102), non-maleficence (11/102), and other or unclear responses (13/102). Primary bioethical principle identified in Q2 by character role (n = 102)

The distribution of primary principles varied by character role (Table 3). Among students assigned to Nurse Chen, autonomy was the most frequently identified principle (11/17). Elder daughter Liu Jin generated the highest number of other or unclear responses (5/17), which often invoked filial duty or emotional obligation. Justice appeared most often among responses from the younger son role (4/17). Physician-role responses were more widely distributed across categories.

Representative examples illustrate how students linked in-role statements to bioethical concepts. One student in the nurse role wrote, “This is the patient’s own decision,” and identified the statement as reflecting autonomy. Another student in the physician role noted that a statement could appear beneficent on the surface while also serving professional self-protection in context. These responses suggest that some students were already distinguishing stated ethical justification from underlying motive.

Students’ ethical evaluation of their character’s stance (Q3)

Typological analysis of Q3 responses identified three recurring positioning modes describing how students related to the stance they had enacted after the simulation.

Type A: Principle-based distancing

Students in this mode stepped back from their character and used explicit bioethical language to critique the position they had previously defended.

“I played the role of the elder daughter. I insisted on continued treatment because of her financial interests. When I reflected on this, I realised that what looked like filial piety was actually a violation of the autonomy principle.” (Elder daughter role, S35).

“I played the role of the elder daughter. I insisted on continued treatment because of her financial interests. When I reflected on this, I realised that what looked like filial piety was actually a violation of the autonomy principle.” (Elder daughter role, S35).

This mode was most often observed in the elder daughter, Professor Zhou, and Nurse Chen roles.

Type B: contextualized ambivalence

Students in this mode recognized ethical problems in their character’s stance while also acknowledging the situational pressures shaping that stance, such as hierarchy, grief, economic burden, or institutional accountability.

“I kept justifying my character’s choice, even though I could feel it was wrong. The pressure the nurse faces, including hierarchy, job security, and the weight of a patient’s unspoken wish, felt suffocating in a way that a textbook case never could.” (Nurse Chen role, S60).

“I kept justifying my character’s choice, even though I could feel it was wrong. The pressure the nurse faces, including hierarchy, job security, and the weight of a patient’s unspoken wish, felt suffocating in a way that a textbook case never could.” (Nurse Chen role, S60).

“Everyone has their own difficulties. The nurse is afraid of losing her job; the doctor is afraid of complaints; the social worker has referral targets; the children are broken by grief and debt. In this web, there is no uncomplicated hero.” (Younger son role, S92).

“Everyone has their own difficulties. The nurse is afraid of losing her job; the doctor is afraid of complaints; the social worker has referral targets; the children are broken by grief and debt. In this web, there is no uncomplicated hero.” (Younger son role, S92).

This mode appeared most often in the physician and younger son roles.

Type C: value-aligned identification

Students in this mode responded largely from within the relational or affective logic of the character, often without clearly distancing themselves from that perspective or using explicit principle language.

“Nurse Chen’s position, caught between the patient’s whispered wish, the doctor’s authority, and the fear for her job, felt unbearably real to me. She is placed in an impossible situation not of her making.” (Nurse Chen role, S86).

“Nurse Chen’s position, caught between the patient’s whispered wish, the doctor’s authority, and the fear for her job, felt unbearably real to me. She is placed in an impossible situation not of her making.” (Nurse Chen role, S86).

Across the dataset, Types A and B were more common than Type C.

Students’ perceived learning gains (Q4)

Reflexive thematic analysis of Q4 responses identified four themes describing students’ perceived learning gains from the IERS.

Theme 1: embodied understanding of bioethical principles

Students frequently contrasted prior theoretical knowledge with the situated understanding generated by the simulation.

“Before this, I only understood the four principles at a theoretical level. In the simulation, when I faced conflicting demands from different family members, I genuinely felt what impartiality means.” (Professor Zhou role, S2).

“Before this, I only understood the four principles at a theoretical level. In the simulation, when I faced conflicting demands from different family members, I genuinely felt what impartiality means.” (Professor Zhou role, S2).

“For the first time, I truly understood what respect for autonomy means: not just ticking a box, but actively protecting what a patient actually wants.” (Nurse Chen role, S45).

“For the first time, I truly understood what respect for autonomy means: not just ticking a box, but actively protecting what a patient actually wants.” (Nurse Chen role, S45).

Theme 2: moving beyond binary moral judgment

Students often rejected simplified oppositions such as “saving equals good” and “not saving equals abandonment”. Their responses instead framed ethical decisions as context-dependent, multi-causal, and morally uncomfortable.

“This simulation shattered my black-and-white thinking. I had naively believed that saving means being filial. But in reality, aggressive treatment can serve a family member’s needs, while palliative care may be the truest respect for the patient’s suffering.” (Elder daughter role, S10).

“This simulation shattered my black-and-white thinking. I had naively believed that saving means being filial. But in reality, aggressive treatment can serve a family member’s needs, while palliative care may be the truest respect for the patient’s suffering.” (Elder daughter role, S10).

Theme 3: perspective-taking and empathy

Many students described increased understanding of viewpoints they would previously have judged quickly, especially those involving professional fear, litigation risk, or family strain.

“I watched a classmate embody the doctor who has been scarred by prior litigation. I had always judged aggressive treatment harshly. But standing inside his perspective, I felt the weight of his fear. Judgment softened into understanding.” (Dr Sun role, S79).

“I watched a classmate embody the doctor who has been scarred by prior litigation. I had always judged aggressive treatment harshly. But standing inside his perspective, I felt the weight of his fear. Judgment softened into understanding.” (Dr Sun role, S79).

“Every character in this simulation is simultaneously a victim of their circumstances and a contributor to the dilemma. There are no clean hands.” (Social worker role, S98).

“Every character in this simulation is simultaneously a victim of their circumstances and a contributor to the dilemma. There are no clean hands.” (Social worker role, S98).

Theme 4: emerging professional identity

A fourth theme concerned students’ reflections on the kind of nurse they hoped to become. These responses often moved from abstract ethics to future bedside actions such as listening, documenting, and speaking up.

“This experience made me realise that nursing ethics is not a set of rules to recite in an exam. It shapes every micro-decision I will make at the bedside.” (Nurse Chen role, S82).

“This experience made me realise that nursing ethics is not a set of rules to recite in an exam. It shapes every micro-decision I will make at the bedside.” (Nurse Chen role, S82).

“As a nurse I will make it my practice to ask, to listen, and to write down what the patient says.” (Nurse Chen role, S70).

“As a nurse I will make it my practice to ask, to listen, and to write down what the patient says.” (Nurse Chen role, S70).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study suggests that a low-resource, peer-enacted immersive role-play can help students move beyond recalling ethical terminology toward using ethical concepts in a contested clinical situation. Across the dataset, students not only identified autonomy as central to the scenario, but also described shifts in judgment, empathy, and professional self-understanding after inhabiting ethically difficult roles.

The prominence of autonomy was unsurprising given that the scenario was organized around an undocumented patient wish. More important, however, was that students’ reflections did not collapse into a single “correct” answer. Responses showed ongoing negotiation among autonomy, beneficence, justice, institutional risk, family burden, and relational loyalty. This suggests that the IERS made ethical conflict visible rather than merely asking students to reproduce principle language.

Cultural and contextual ethics

The elder daughter role frequently elicited references to filial duty and relational obligation rather than straightforward use of canonical bioethical principles. Rather than treating these responses as misunderstandings, they can be read as evidence that students were drawing on culturally embedded moral repertoires alongside formal bioethics [26, 27]. For nursing ethics education in China, this is pedagogically important: students need opportunities not only to learn the four principles, but also to examine how those principles intersect with filial norms, family decision-making, and clinical hierarchy.

The nurse role appeared especially generative because it concentrated patient advocacy, documentation, hierarchy, and personal vulnerability in a single position. Students’ reflections on undocumented wishes and speaking up suggest that immersive role-play may help connect end-of-life ethics to concrete nursing actions such as listening carefully, documenting preferences, escalating concerns, and facilitating ACP-related communication.

Educational implications

The findings are compatible with experiential and transformative learning perspectives [7, 22]. Character immersion provided a concrete encounter with conflict, while debriefing and written reflection created space for reinterpretation. Importantly, this occurred in a format that did not require actors, mannequins, or specialized simulation facilities. That feasibility may make the approach attractive for routine ethics teaching.

Two practical implications follow. First, morally conflicted roles appear educationally productive when paired with structured debriefing. Second, the IERS may work best after students have already received introductory teaching on ethical principles, because the activity asks them to mobilize those concepts in context rather than encounter them for the first time.

Limitations and future research

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. Data came from a single university and an immediate post-session survey, so the study captures short-term perceptions rather than durable change. Survey responses were briefer than interview data and may have been shaped by social desirability. The study was not designed to compare the IERS with other teaching methods or to establish causal effects. Role selection was negotiated rather than randomized, and some roles may have prompted richer reflection than others. In addition, the survey was completed immediately after the individual debrief and before the teacher-led debrief. This timing was chosen to capture students’ immediate post-role reflections before instructor interpretation could shape their responses. However, it may also have introduced time pressure. Some students may have written briefer or less elaborated answers because they were completing the survey within the scheduled class session. This may have limited the depth of some responses, particularly for the broader reflective question on learning gains.

Future studies should pair this kind of qualitative work with pre-post measures, comparison conditions, and longer follow-up to assess sustained impact. It would also be valuable to examine how facilitator style, debriefing structure, and alternative ethical scenarios influence what students learn.

Conclusion

An immersive, peer-driven ethical role-play may offer a feasible way to strengthen undergraduate nursing ethics education by helping students apply bioethical principles in context, consider multiple stakeholder perspectives, and reflect on future professional responsibilities. In this study, students described learning that extended from principle recognition to empathy, moral complexity, and professional identity. The IERS therefore appears to be a useful complement to didactic ethics teaching, while further longitudinal and comparative work is needed to determine the durability and transferability of its educational effects.
