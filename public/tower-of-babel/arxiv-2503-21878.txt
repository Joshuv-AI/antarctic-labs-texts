Is Best-of-N the Best of Them? Coverage, Scaling, and Optimality in Inference-Time Alignment


Abstract
Inference-time computation offers a powerful axis for scaling the performance of language models. However, naively increasing computation in techniques like Best-of-N sampling can lead to performance degradation due to reward hacking. Toward a theoretical understanding of how to best leverage additional computation, we focus on inference-time alignment, which we formalize as the problem of improving the quality of responses drawn from a pre-trained policy, given a prompt of interest and access to an imperfect reward model. We analyze the performance of inference-time alignment algorithms in terms of (i) response quality, and (ii) compute, and provide new results that highlight the importance of the pre-trained policy’s coverage over high-quality responses for performance and compute scaling: 1. We show that Best-of-N alignment with an ideal choice for N N can achieve optimal performance under stringent notions of coverage, but provably suffers from reward hacking when N N is large, and fails to achieve tight guarantees under more realistic coverage conditions. 2. We introduce InferenceTimePessimism , a new algorithm which mitigates reward hacking through more sophisticated use of inference-time compute, implementing the principle of pessimism in the face of uncertainty via rejection sampling; we prove that its performance is optimal and does not degrade with N N , a property that we call scaling-monotonic . We complement our theoretical results with an experimental evaluation that demonstrate the benefits of InferenceTimePessimism across a variety of tasks and models.

Introduction

Inference-time computation
has emerged as a new axis for scaling in language models,
which has led to dramatic improvements in their capabilities

(
Brown et al., 2024
;
Snell et al., 2024
;
Wu et al., 2024b
;
OpenAI, 2024b
;
DeepSeek-AI, 2025
)

and played a central role in recent AI breakthroughs
(
Chollet et al., 2024
)
.
While there are a multitude of ways in which additional computation can be utilized during inference—e.g., to
generate long chains of thought
(
Wei et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2024b
)
,
have the model rate or correct its own responses
(
Zheng et al., 2023
;
Wu et al., 2024a
)
,
or implement planning and search
(
Yao et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
—even exceedingly simple methods like
Best-of-N (parallel) sampling

can provide significant performance gains
(
Lightman et al., 2023
;
Brown et al., 2024
)
,
and enjoy provable representational benefits
(
Huang et al., 2024a
)
. Such algorithms are also widely used throughout post-training for frontier models
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2025
;
Yang et al., 2024a
)
,
and thereby serve as a cornerstone of fine-tuning and inference.

Toward developing a deeper understanding of the algorithmic landscape for inference-time computation,
in this paper, we focus on the problem of
inference-time alignment
:
given a task and a reward model that is a proxy for task performance,
how can we best use inference-time computation to improve the quality of a response
chosen from many candidates generated by the base model? The Best-of-N heuristic is one of the most widely used inference-time alignment methods, and proceeds by generating
N
candidate responses for a given prompt,
then returning the response with the highest reward under a reward model

(
Stiennon et al., 2020
;
Nakano et al., 2021
;
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Gao et al., 2023
;
Eisenstein et al., 2023
;
Mudgal et al., 2024
)
.
While attractive in its simplicity,
Best-of-N and related heuristics are known to suffer from
reward overoptimization
or
reward hacking
when
N
increases

(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Stroebl et al., 2024
;
Chow et al., 2024
;
Frick et al., 2024
)
.
While one might hope that increasing computation to generate a larger number of candidates will
increase the likelihood of selecting a high-quality response,
reward model errors at the tail of the response distribution
can cause Best-of-N to return generations with high (modeled) reward, but poor task performance.

This overoptimization phenomenon raises two fundamental questions,
which we investigate in this paper:
(1) what is the extent to which the imperfect reward model limits the performance of inference-time alignment methods;
and (2) can more deliberate algorithm design
lead to performance gains that scale monotonically with increased computation?
Beyond guiding practical interventions for inference-time alignment,
answers to these questions contribute to a foundational understanding
of inference-time computation more broadly.

Our framework

To address the questions above, we pose
inference-time
alignment

as the task of extracting a high-quality response
\widehat{y}

for a prompt
x

from a pre-trained language model, or

base policy

\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
that maps a prompt
x
to a distribution over responses
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
The quality of the response
\widehat{y}
is determined by an underlying

true reward function

r^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,R_{\mathsf{max}}]
, that expresses, for example,
the correctness of a math proof or the helpfulness of a chat response.
The algorithm designer does not know
r^{\star}

and instead uses an
imperfect reward model
\widehat{r}
(e.g., one learned from preference-based feedback

(
Christiano et al., 2017
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2024b
)
) to maximize performance as follows.

Inference-Time Alignment

Given a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, use black-box access to
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
and
\widehat{r}
in the form of
(a)
sampling queries

y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)
, and (b)
evaluation queries

via reward labels
\widehat{r}(x,y)
and log-probabilities
\log\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid x)
, to produce a response
\widehat{y}
that approximately maximizes the true reward (i.e., minimizes regret to the optimal response):

\displaystyle r^{\star}(x,\widehat{y})\approx\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{\star}(x,y)

(2)

This formulation presents two central questions:

Q1
:

Regret.

How close to optimal can we make the reward
r^{\star}(x,\widehat{y})
in
Eq.

2
,
as a function of the quality of the reward model
\widehat{r}
?

Q2
:

Compute.

What is the computational cost—measured
by the number of sampling queries
y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)
, and
evaluation queries
\widehat{r}(x,y)
and
\log\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)
used by the algorithm—required for optimal reward?

Note that while our goal is to maximize the true reward
r^{\star}
,
the quality of the reward model
\widehat{r}
creates an
inherent, information-theoretic barrier to optimizing
r^{\star}
, since the latter unobserved.
Intuitively, we should not be able to maximize quality under
r^{\star}
if
\widehat{r}
is highly inaccurate.

1.1
Contributions

We show that Best-of-N alignment and related heuristics may fail to achieve optimal regret,
but that more sophisticated use of inference-time computation—namely, to extract additional information from the reward model and
quantify its uncertainty—can mitigate overoptimization,
and achieve optimal regret and compute scaling.

Statistical framework and necessity of coverage (
Section

2
)

Our formal framework, summarized above, reformulates inference-time
alignment as a
statistical problem
via query complexity.
This allows us to derive fundamental limits on the performance of any inference-time alignment algorithm.
We show that the best possible reward one can achieve, irrespective of computational cost,
is determined by the base policy’s
coverage
over high-quality responses,
along with the mean-squared error of
\widehat{r}
.
This serves as a skyline for our investigation into
improved algorithmic interventions.

Tight analysis of
BoN-Alignment
(
Section

3
)

Within our framework, we offer the first theoretical analysis of the regret of
Best-of-N alignment (
BoN-Alignment
). We show that
BoN-Alignment
can achieve optimal regret under a stringent
notion of coverage (“uniform” or
L_{\infty}
-type) when
N
is tuned appropriately, but:

1.

provably suffers from overoptimization, degrading in
performance once
N
scales past a critical threshold; and

2.

fails to achieve tight guarantees under weaker
notions of coverage (“average-case” or
L_{1}
-type),
thereby falling short of the skyline established in
Section

2
.

Optimal algorithm:
InferenceTimePessimism
(
Section

4
)

Motivated by the shortcomings of Best-of-N, we introduce an improved algorithm,
InferenceTimePessimism
. We prove that
InferenceTimePessimism
:

1.

is
regret-optimal
, in the sense that it can achieve the best possible reward in our framework,
thereby matching the skyline in
Section

2
; and

2.

is
scaling-monotonic
, in the sense that it is guaranteed to avoid
overoptimization beyond a certain point (determined by the
regularization parameter), even as
N\to\infty
.

To achieve this, our algorithm uses a novel rejection
sampling scheme to implement
\chi^{2}
-regularization—which is known to mitigate overoptimization via the principle of

pessimism in the face of uncertainty

(
Huang et al., 2024b
)
—purely at inference time.
Beyond achieving optimal regret, we show that
InferenceTimePessimism
uses near-optimal compute under our framework.

Empirical evaluation (
Section

5
)

To demonstrate the benefits of
InferenceTimePessimism
, we compare the algorithm to
BoN-Alignment
across several tasks, base policies, and reward models.
As predicted by our theory,
BoN-Alignment
degrades in performance as computation increases (via
N
),
while
InferenceTimePessimism
is scaling-monotonic and does not suffer
from this characteristic overoptimization phenomenon (
Fig.

1
).
We also observe several instances where
InferenceTimePessimism
outperforms

BoN-Alignment
in terms of maximal reward achieved when the computational budget is untuned, demonstrating the robustness of our algorithm.

Appendix

B
contains further
empirical results, including examinations of the reward model’s tail behavior
and demonstrations of
InferenceTimePessimism
’s robustness to choice of hyperparameter.

1.2
Related Work

To our knowledge, our work provides the first theoretical framework for understanding and mitigating
overoptimization in inference-time alignment.
Various works have analyzed
specific properties of the Best-of-N alignment algorithm

(
Yang et al., 2024b
;
Beirami et al., 2024
;
Mroueh, 2024
)

such as tradeoffs between reward and KL-divergence, but do not
ultimately provide guarantees on downstream performance
when the estimated reward model and true reward are mismatched.
We focus on analyzing Best-of-N specifically because it is the most widely used and foundational inference-time
alignment technique, but other algorithms
(
Welleck et al., 2024
)
including variants of
rejection sampling
(
Khanov et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Shi et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024a
;
Jinnai et al., 2024
)

and Monte-Carlo Tree Search
(
Feng et al.,
;
Yao et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, are also used in practice.

Our work is also closely related to research on
offline alignment at training time

(
Christiano, 2014
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
;
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
, which involves fitting a reward model
\widehat{r}
(typically through pairwise feedback), and then maximizing it using policy optimization techniques. A growing body of theoretical works on offline
alignment provide theoretical guarantees for mitigating reward
overoptimization that—like our work—depend on notions of
coverage
for the base policy
(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023a
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Cen et al., 2024
;
Fisch et al., 2024
;
Ji et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024b
)
. Our formulation for inference-time alignment can be viewed as a variant of this problem that abstracts away the reward model training; in particular, we take
\widehat{r}
as given and ask how to achieve the best possible performance on a
per-instance
basis, with respect to the true reward
r^{\star}
and the prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Our algorithm,
InferenceTimePessimism
, is closely related to
\chi
PO

(
Huang et al., 2024b
)
, a training-time offline alignment algorithm that aims to mitigate
overoptimization via regularization with the

\chi^{2}
-divergence.
InferenceTimePessimism
also leverages
\chi^{2}
-regularization,
but implements this purely at inference-time via a novel rejection sampling
scheme.
Finally, our analysis of
InferenceTimePessimism
, as well as
BoN-Alignment
,
draws on the treatment of
approximate rejection sampling
in
Block and Polyanskiy (2023)
, which tightly characterizes the performance of rejection sampling with unbounded likelihood ratios. Please see
Appendix

A
for an extended discussion
of related work.

A Statistical Framework for Inference-Time Alignment

In this section, we formally introduce our inference-time alignment
framework, then use it to prove lower bounds that highlight the
necessity of coverage. In our framework, the algorithm designer begins with a
base policy

\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, which is a conditional distribution
mapping
prompts

x\in\mathcal{X}
to
distributions over
responses

y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
and
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)
is the probability that the base policy generates
a response
y
given the prompt
x
.
1
1

1

The motivating special case is
autoregressive language modeling, where
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{V}^{H}
for a
vocabulary space
\mathcal{V}
and sequence length
H
, and
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
has the
autoregressive structure
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y_{1:H}\mid{}x)=\prod_{h=1}^{H}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y_{h}\mid{}y_{1:h-1},x)
for
y=y_{1:H}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

The algorithm designer also has access to an
imperfect reward model

\widehat{r}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,R_{\mathsf{max}}]
,
where
R_{\mathsf{max}}\geq 1
,
and the reward label
\widehat{r}(x,y)
estimates the quality of the response
y
for the prompt
x
.
This serves as a proxy for the
true reward model

r^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,R_{\mathsf{max}}]
,
which is unknown.

Given the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
, the reward model
\widehat{r}
, and a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
the goal is to produce a high quality response
\widehat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}

as measured by the true reward
r^{\star}
,
in the sense that the following
inference-time regret
is small.

\displaystyle\texttt{Reg}_{\pi^{\star}}(\widehat{y}\,;{}x)\vcentcolon=J(\pi^{\star}\,;{}x)-J(\widehat{y}\,;{}x)\leq\varepsilon,

(3)

Here,
\pi^{\star}
can be viewed as a comparator policy with high reward,
and
J(\pi;x)\vcentcolon={}\En_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid{}x)}\left[r^{\star}(x,y)\right]

denotes the expected reward under
r^{\star}
for any policy
\pi
.
When the response
\widehat{y}
is chosen by a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,
we use
\widehat{\pi}_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot\mid{}x)
to denote the conditional distribution over
responses induced by
\mathcal{A}
, and write regret as

\texttt{Reg}_{\pi^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi}\,;{}x)\vcentcolon=J(\pi^{\star}\,;{}x)-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathcal{A}}\,;{}x)
.

In applications, the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
represents a language model trained in some manner (e.g., through next-token prediction and post-training
steps such as SFT or RLHF).
The true reward
r^{\star}
can represent the extent to which
y

agrees with human preference, passes a proof checker, or passes a unit test suite.
The reward model
\widehat{r}
can be an open
source reward model, a model trained by the algorithm designer
themselves, or even derived directly from
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
itself

(
Wang et al., 2022
;
Huang et al., 2024a
;
Song et al., 2024
)
.

Reward model quality versus regret

There is no hope of making the regret in
Eq.

3

small without requirements on the fidelity of the reward model
\widehat{r}
.
For a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we measure the quality of
\widehat{r}
via
the expected squared error with respect to
r^{\star}
,
where responses are drawn by the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)\vcentcolon={}\En_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(x,y)-r^{\star}(x,y)\right)^{2}\right].

(4)

Empirically and in theory, one can minimize

Eq.

4
by fitting
\widehat{r}
to rewards or human
preference data for responses generated from
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}

(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023a
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Cen et al., 2024
;
Fisch et al., 2024
;
Ji et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024b
)
,
which is already standard in post-training pipelines.
As our focus is on
inference-time
interventions,
we simply abstract the reward model training step away
and assume we have a reward model
\widehat{r}
with error
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}
,
as defined in
Eq.

4
.
We aim to understand how small we can make the regret in
Eq.

3

as a function of the reward model quality
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}
,
and what algorithmic interventions are required to achieve this (cf.
Q1
).

{remark}

In practice, the reward estimation error in
Eq.

4

may not be the tightest notion of reward error, and thus our bounds may be conservative.
Nevertheless, it is arguably the most common formulation of reward mismatch
(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023a
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Gao et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024b
)
,
and serve as a foundation for future investigations
into other notions of error.

Measuring computational efficiency via query complexity

The next question we consider (cf.
Q2
) is how much computation is required to
minimize the inference-time regret in
Eq.

3
for a given
prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
To allow for computational understanding that is agnostic to the
architecture or description of
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
and
\widehat{r}
,
we draw inspiration from
Huang et al. (2024a)
and abstract it as a
statistical problem
,
where computational efficiency is quantified via
the number of samples and labels queried from the base policy and reward model.

{definition}

[Sample-and-evaluate framework]
In the
sample-and-evaluate
framework, the algorithm designer does
not have explicit access to the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
or the reward
model
\widehat{r}
. Instead, they
access
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
and
\widehat{r}
through
sample-and-evaluate queries
: For a
given prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, they can sample
N

responses
y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{N}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)
and observe
the likelihood
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y_{i}\mid{}x)
and reward model value

\widehat{r}(x,y_{i})
for each such response. The efficiency
(
query complexity
) of the algorithm is measured by the total number
of queries
N
.
This is a natural statistical abstraction for computation—analogous
to oracle/query complexity in optimization

(
Nemirovski et al., 1983
;
Traub et al., 1988
;
Raginsky and Rakhlin, 2011
;
Agarwal et al., 2012
)
,
and learning theory
(
Blum et al., 1994
;
Kearns, 1998
;
Feldman, 2012
;
Feldman, 2017
)
—and encompasses Best-of-N alignment and other schemes such as
rejection sampling

(
Khanov et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Shi et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024a
;
Jinnai et al., 2024
)
.

2.1
A Skyline for Performance: The Necessity of Coverage

To motivate our main algorithmic results, and as a step toward
answering question
Q1
, we begin by proving a lower bound on the
best possible regret one can hope to achieve,
as determined by the reward model’s error
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)
.
This lower bound highlights the importance of the base policy’s
coverage
,
defined formally for a comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)\vcentcolon={}\En_{y\sim\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid{}x)}\left[\frac{\pi^{\star}(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}\right].

(5)

{proposition}

[Necessity of coverage]

Fix a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
and base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. For any alignment
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and any

16\leq{}C^{\star}\leq\max_{\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\mathcal{C}^{\pi}
, there exists a
reward function
r^{\star}
and reward model
\widehat{r}
satisfying
Eq.

4
and comparator
policy
\pi^{\star}
with
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\leq C^{\star}
such that

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star};x)-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\mathcal{A}};x)\geq\frac{1}{4}\cdot\sqrt{C^{\star}\cdot{}\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)}.

(6)

Coverage in the sense of
Eq.

5
plays a central role in the
theoretical study of offline alignment
(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023a
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Cen et al., 2024
;
Fisch et al., 2024
;
Ji et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024a
;
Huang et al., 2024b
)
, and

Section

2.1
shows that it plays a similar role for
inference-time alignment.
Recent works show that standard language models can indeed exhibit favorable coverage
over desirable responses in standard tasks of interest,
which translate to performance gains at inference time
(
Brown et al., 2024
;
Snell et al., 2024
;
Wu et al., 2024b
)
.
In the remainder of the paper, we will explore the extent to which the skyline in
Eq.

5
can be achieved—through
existing techniques, or through new interventions.

3
Understanding Best-of-N Alignment: Guarantees and
Limitations

As our first step toward understanding of how to best leverage computation for
inference-time alignment, we give a sharp analysis of
Best-of-N alignment, highlighting the role of coverage in determining its scaling
properties and (sub-) optimality.

3.1
A Sharp Analysis of Best-of-N Alignment

Algorithm 1

)

1:

First, in

4
An Optimal Algorithm: Inference-Time Pessimism

We now present
InferenceTimePessimism
, an optimal inference-time alignment method
that implements a statistically sound regularizer purely at inference time.
In contrast to
BoN-Alignment
,

InferenceTimePessimism
separates the scaling parameter (
N
) from the regularization parameter,
and is thereby able to achieve the performance skyline in
Section

2.1
.
It is, as a result, also
scaling-monotone
—as

is increased,
it does not overfit or lose performance.
We first give an overview of the
algorithm, then state the main theoretical guarantee.

Parameter tuning and practicality

As presented in
Algorithm

2
,

InferenceTimePessimism
has two parameters,
\beta
and
N
, compared to the single parameter
N
used by
BoN-Alignment
.
This separation enables the optimal and scale-monotonic guarantees for
InferenceTimePessimism
,
and we also view it as a beneficial feature from a practical perspective:
by using two parameters, we achieve a clear separation between tuning the computational budget (through
N
)
and tuning the statistical performance (through
\beta
).
While to achieve
Eq.

21

\beta
must be chosen based on potentially unknown parameters
(
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)
and
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)
),
this represents a meaningful improvement over
BoN-Alignment
,
which cannot be tuned to achieve
Eq.

21
even when these parameters are known (
Footnote

3
).
This is because
BoN-Alignment
conflates computational and statistical considerations in its single parameter
N
.

Empirically, we find that for any fixed choice of
\beta
,

InferenceTimePessimism
is robust to overfitting when computation and sample size is increased,
with performance essentially monotonic as
N
grows (
Fig.

10
),
as predicted by the guarantee in
Eq.

20
.
Because of this robustness, it is easier to tune the parameter
\beta
,
which we also find is important to achieve strong performance;
further discussion and practical guidance is provided in
Section

5
.
Altogether, we believe it is most natural to interpret
InferenceTimePessimism
as a
“single-parameter” algorithm where only
\beta
needs to be tuned,
and
N
is as large as the computational budget allows.

Overview of analysis

The analysis of
InferenceTimePessimism
, and has three parts.
First, we show that the idealized
\chi^{2}
-regularized distribution in

Eq.

16
achieves the regret bound in

Section

4.2
, i.e.

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to0.0pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:chis_regret_ideal}\hfil}eq:chis_{r}egret_{i}deal}J(\pi^{\star};x)-J(\pi^{\chi}_{\beta};x)\lesssim\sqrt{\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)}+\beta\cdot\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)+\beta^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x).

(23)

This follows the same reasoning as the analysis of training-time
interventions based on
\chi^{2}
-regularization in

Huang et al. (2024b)
,
and uses the
property that for any function
\Delta(x,y)
(we use
\Delta(x,y)=\left\lvert\widehat{r}(x,y)-r^{\star}(x,y)\right\rvert
) and
policy
\pi
,

\En_{\pi}\left[\Delta(x,y)\right]\lesssim\sqrt{(1+D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi(x)\;\|\;{}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right))\cdot\En_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left[\Delta^{2}(x,y)\right]}.

(24)

Of course, we cannot sample from

\pi^{\chi}_{\beta}
itself because the distribution depends on the “true”
normalization constant
\lambda(x)
in
Eq.

17
.
To address this, we prove a robustness result showing that,
given a
\widehat{\lambda}
that approximately normalizes the distribution in
Eq.

16
,
the approximate
\chi^{2}
-regularized policy

\widetilde{\pi}^{\chi}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)\cdot\mathsf{relu}\left(\beta^{-1}(\widehat{r}(x,y)-\widehat{\lambda})\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y^{\prime}\mid{}x)\cdot\mathsf{relu}\left(\beta^{-1}(\widehat{r}(x,y^{\prime})-\widehat{\lambda})\right)}
achieves a regret bound that matches
Eq.

23
up to
absolute constants. From here, a concentration argument
implies that the
\widehat{\lambda}(x)
computed in

Eq.

14
is an approximate normalizer whenever

N\gtrsim\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta}
, with high probability.

Finally, we leverage analysis for rejection sampling to show that, as long as

N\gtrsim\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\widetilde{\pi}^{\chi}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}\log(1/\delta)
,
the rejection sampling procedure will terminate and return

y\sim\widetilde{\pi}^{\chi}_{\beta}(\cdot\mid{}x)
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Critically, due to the heavy-tailed nature of the
\chi^{2}
-regularizer,
this density ratio is bounded as

\frac{\widetilde{\pi}^{\chi}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}\lesssim\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta}
,
which yields the claimed query complexity bound. This observation highlights an important computational benefit of

\chi^{2}
-regularization that goes beyond its
statistical benefits, illustrated below.

{remark}

[Comparison to KL-regularization]

Liu et al. (2023)
;
Li et al. (2024a)

use
rejection sampling to simulate samples from the
KL-regularized distribution:

\pi^{\texttt{KL}}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)\vcentcolon=\argmax_{p\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\left\{\En_{y\sim{}p}\left[\widehat{r}(x,y)\right]-\beta\cdot D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\right\},

(25)

which satisfies

\pi^{\texttt{KL}}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)\propto\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\widehat{r}(x,y)}{\beta}\right).

(26)

This has two issues. First, as shown by
Huang et al. (2024b)
,
this distribution can fail to achieve the guarantee in
Eq.

21
,
no matter how
\beta
is chosen. Second, the density ratio is
exponential in general, i.e.

\frac{\pi^{\texttt{KL}}_{\beta}(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}\geq\exp\left(\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta}\right)
,
which means that
N\gtrsim\exp\left(\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta}\right)

sample-and-evaluate queries are required to simulate it with rejection sampling.

6
Conclusion

Our results reveal the interplay between coverage, scaling, and
optimality in inference-time alignment,
and highlight the benefits of scaling computation at inference-time in more deliberate and sophisticated forms.

We believe that, in addition to the sample-and-evaluate framework,
the following technical contributions may be of greater interest,
e.g., for drawing samples from other desirable distributions at inference time:
(i) our methods for efficiently approximating samples from regularized distributions,
(ii) lower bounds on query complexity, and
(iii) regret analysis based on approximate rejection sampling.

Beyond providing optimal
algorithms (
InferenceTimePessimism
) and insights into the performance of
BoN-Alignment
,
our framework can serve as starting point toward a foundational
understanding of inference-time computation more broadly.
In
particular, our work raises a number of interesting
directions for future research:

•

Opening the black box.
Our formulation of the
inference-time alignment problem treats the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
as a
black box, and does not take advantage of any specific properties of
the policy outside of coverage. An important direction is to
understand whether we can open this black box and use more refined
properties of the policy (e.g., the learned representations) to
derive improved inference-time alignment schemes.

•

Co-design of training-time and inference-time alignment
algorithms.
Recent work

(
Balashankar et al., 2024
;
Chow et al., 2024
)
has explored the
design of “inference-aware” training-time procedures that train a policy attempting to optimize Best-of-N performance at
inference-time. Since our work shows that inference-time Best-of-N can be suboptimal in general, a natural direction is to co-design better
inference-time and training-time interventions in parallel.

•

Inference-time exploration.
Our formulation—where
the learning algorithm must make decisions based on an imperfect
reward model—is inspired by the offline reinforcement learning
framework. An interesting direction, inspired by
online

reinforcement learning, is whether one can develop inference-time algorithms
that leverage exploration to learn as quickly as possible in the
online setting where the learner has access to the true reward
function or a high-quality verifier.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

In this section, we discuss additional related work in greater detail.

Theoretical analysis of inference-time alignment

Inference-time alignment has received limited theoretical
investigation so far. Most notably, various works have analyzed
specific properties of the Best-of-N alignment algorithm

(
Yang et al., 2024b
;
Beirami et al., 2024
;
Mroueh, 2024
)

such as tradeoffs between reward and KL-divergence, but do not
ultimately provide guarantees on downstream performance in the
presence of mismatch between the estimated reward model and true
reward. Our theoretical framework—which abstracts the role of the
base policy through sample-and-evaluate access—is inspired by

Huang et al. (2024a)
, who used a similar framework to give guarantees
for the complementary problem of language model self-improvement, but
our specific problem formulation and techniques are quite different.

Empirical algorithms for inference-time alignment

Empirically,
the Best-of-N alignment heuristic

(
Stiennon et al., 2020
;
Nakano et al., 2021
;
Touvron et al., 2023
;
Gao et al., 2023
;
Eisenstein et al., 2023
;
Mudgal et al., 2024
)

is perhaps the most widely used inference-time alignment heuristic; specific works that have observed the overoptimization phenomenon for
Best-of-N include
Gao et al. (2023, Figure 1)
,
Chow et al. (2024, Figure
3)
,
Frick et al. (2024, Figure 7)
, and
Stroebl et al. (2024)
.
There are also other algorithms based on more
sophisticated variants of Best-of-N or other techniques such as
rejection sampling
(
Liu et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Chakraborty et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
;
Shi et al., 2024
;
Qiu et al., 2024
;
Jinnai et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
)
,
though few of these works are explicitly designed to address these
issue of over-optimization. For example, most algorithms based on inference-time search, such as
Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) and
relatives
(
Feng et al.,
;
Yao et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, are designed with the complementary goal of maximizing a
fixed reward function of interest given exact access, and do not account for
overoptimization or mismatch between the reward function and task performance. Specific algorithms that
make use of rejection sampling include

Liu et al. (2023)
;
Li et al. (2024a)
;
Xiong et al. (2024)
;
Zhao et al. (2024)
;
Khaki et al. (2024)
,
though the specific distributions these works aim to sample from are
quite different from that used in
InferenceTimePessimism
. See also
Welleck et al. (2024)
for a survey of inference-time algorithms.

Other related but complementary line of work include (1) distilling
inference-time procedures into policies, thereby giving training time
procedures

(
Amini et al., 2024
;
Sessa et al., 2024
;
Gui et al., 2024
;
Pace et al., 2024
)
,
and (2) designing “inference-aware” training procedures which change the
training process to optimize performance of downstream inference-time
such as Best-of-N
(
Balashankar et al., 2024
;
Chow et al., 2024
)
.

A.1
Connection to Offline (Training-Time) Alignment

As discussed in
Section

1.2
, our problem formulation and
algorithms are closed related to a growing body of research on
theoretical algorithms for
offline alignment

(
Zhu et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023a
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2024b
;
Cen et al., 2024
;
Fisch et al., 2024
;
Ji et al., 2024
;
Huang et al., 2024b
;
Rashidinejad and Tian, 2024
)
,
which give training-time interventions that enjoy robustness to reward
model overoptimization under various notions of coverage. In
particular, our
InferenceTimePessimism
algorithm can be viewed as implementing an
“idealized” version of the
\chi^{2}
-regularized RLHF algorithms
introduced by
Huang et al. (2024b)
at inference-time.

Our formulation of inference-time alignment can be viewed
as a variant of the offline alignment problem that abstracts away the
process of training the reward model. Rather than concerning ourselves
with the details of training
\widehat{r}
from
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{pref}}
to minimize

Eq.

4
, we take
\widehat{r}
as a given (in the process, abstracting
away the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{pref}}
), and ask how to achieve the best
possible regret on a
per-instance
basis, both with respect to
the reward model
\widehat{r}
itself, and with respect to the prompt

x\in\mathcal{X}
(which is arbitrary and fixed, rather than assumed to be
i.i.d. as
x\sim\rho
). Naturally, our algorithms and analyses can be
combined with any reward estimation procedure that minimizes

\En_{x\sim\rho}\left[\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)\right]
from
\mathcal{D}_{\texttt{pref}}
to derive
end-to-end sample complexity guarantees for offline alignment.

Online alignment

A complementary line of theoretical research which is somewhat less related to our work
studies alignment with
online feedback

(
Xu et al., 2020
;
Novoseller et al., 2020
;
Pacchiano et al., 2021
;
Wu and Sun, 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2023b
;
Chen et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Du et al., 2024
;
Das et al., 2024
;
Ye et al., 2024
;
Xie et al., 2024
;
Cen et al., 2024
;
Xiong et al., 2024
;
Gao et al., 2024
;
Chang et al., 2024
;
Song et al., 2024
)
,
where feedback from the true reward model
r^{\star}(x,y)
is available.

.

C.1
Background

This section gives guarantees for the
ComputeNormConstant
subroutine
(
Algorithm

3
) used within
InferenceTimePessimism
. Given a set of response

\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the algorithm computes a normalization constant
\lambda

such that

\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\mathsf{relu}\left(\beta^{-1}(\widehat{r}(x,y)-\lambda)\right)=1

(29)

in time
O(N\log{}N)
using a dynamic programming-like procedure.
Note that such a
\lambda
always exists because

\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)
is a continuous, piecewise linear function
that is decreasing in
\lambda
,
with
\widehat{\Phi}(-\infty)=\infty
and
\widehat{\Phi}(\infty)=0
.
In what follows, we state and prove the main guarantee for the algorithm.
En route, we will also prove a guarantee for a more general version of
ComputeNormConstant
(
Algorithm

5
),
which computes a normalization constant such that

\Phi(\lambda)\vcentcolon=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)\mathsf{relu}\left(\beta^{-1}(\widehat{r}(x,y)-\lambda)\right)=1

in time
O\left(N\log N\right)
.

.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be fixed; we omit dependence on
x
going forward to keep notation compact.
We begin by defining a surrogate problem with response space

\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{M}\vcentcolon=\left\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}\right\}
,
where
y_{i}
is any response from
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
,
surrogate reward function
\widehat{r}^{\prime}
with
\widehat{r}^{\prime}(y_{i})=\vcentcolon{}\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}=\widehat{r}_{i}
,
and surrogate reference policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}
, where for
i\in[M]
we set

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}(y_{i})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{i}}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y).

(30)

Note that all responses in this collection have unique values under a surrogate reward function
\widehat{r}^{\prime}
.
Since
Algorithm

5
sorts rewards in ascending order,
we also have that the surrogate rewards are indexed in ascending order, i.e.,

\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}<\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i+1}
for all

.
We also define the following function,
which is the analog of
\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)
defined over the
M

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}(y_{i})\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}-\lambda\right)\right).

(31)

For any
\lambda
, it can be seen that
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)

\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{i}}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\right)\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(\widehat{r}_{i}-\lambda\right)\right)=\sum_{y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(\widehat{r}(y)-\lambda\right)\right)=\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda),

(32)

because each
y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
is binned into one of
\left\{\mathcal{Y}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{M}
,
and within each
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
all responses share the reward label
\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}
.

Next, define
\lambda^{\star}
to be such that
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})=1
, that is,
the true constant that normalizes the distribution over the
M

Our goal is to show that

5

\lambda^{\star}
,
which, given the previously shown equivalence, then proves the lemma statement since

1=\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})=\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda^{\star}).

\lambda^{\star}
is guaranteed to exist

\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)
is continuous and decreasing in
\lambda
.
Moreover, from the form of
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}
, it can be seen that there exists some index

\lambda^{\star}\geq\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle 1=\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda^{\star})=\beta^{-1}\sum_{i>j}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}(y_{i})\left(\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}-\lambda^{\star}\right).

(34)

In other words, there exists an index
j\in[M]

\lambda^{\star}\in[\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{j},\widehat{r}_{j+1}^{\prime}]
,
which also shows that
\lambda^{\star}\in\left[J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})-\beta,\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{M}\right]
.
Then to find
\lambda^{\star}
, that is,
a
\lambda
such that
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda)=1
,
it is sufficient to find an index
j
such that for

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\sum_{i>j}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}(y_{i})\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{i}-\beta}{\sum_{i>j}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime}(y_{i})},

(35)

we have
\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{j}\leq\lambda<\widehat{r}^{\prime}_{j+1}
.
This is exactly the output of
Algorithm

5
run on
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{M},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}^{\prime},\widehat{r}^{\prime},\beta
, which breaks at an index that satisfies the above conditions, and outputs the corresponding
\lambda
.
By inspection, such a
\lambda
satisfies
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\lambda)=1
,
and therefore also satisfies
\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)=1
.

Lastly, we discuss the computational complexity of
Algorithm

5
.
Sorting
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
and
\widehat{r}
require
O(N\log N)
time,
while binning is
O(N)
.
Finally, we make one forward pass through the responses,
which requires at most
N
iterations, each with
O(1)
computations, before termination.

Appendix D
Rejection Sampling

This section includes background on and guarantees for approximate rejection sampling,
which forms the basis for our analysis for
BoN
and
InferenceTimePessimism
.

The organization is as follows. First, in
Section

D.1
,
we describe the approximate rejection sampling algorithm
(
Algorithm

4
)—which is used within
InferenceTimePessimism
, as well as within
the analysis of
BoN-Alignment
—and introduce the fundamental concepts
that we will use
to analyze the sample complexity of
RejectionSampling
.
Then, in
Section

D.2
we provide
upper bounds on the sample complexity of
RejectionSampling
,
with matching lower bounds in
Section

D.3
,
in terms of the aforementioned measure,
demonstrating its fundamental nature and the tightness of our results.

D.1
Background

The rejection sampling algorithm
RejectionSampling
is shown in
Algorithm

4
.
The input parameters are the sample size
N
and the rejection threshold
M
,
a prompt
x
,
and an importance sampling weight
w
.

The algorithm first draws
N
samples from the conditional distribution of
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}

for the fixed prompt
x
,
i.e., it draws
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}=\left\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\right\}
where
y_{i}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)

for each
i\in[N]
.
Then, for each
y_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
, it samples a Bernoulli random variable
\xi_{i}

where the probability of observing
\xi_{i}=1

is given by the importance weight
w(y_{i}\mid{}x)
divided by the rejection threshold
M
,
truncated to be at most 1.
The algorithm returns any
y_{i}
for which
\xi_{i}=1
,
and if no such event is observed, returns the first response
(which is equivalent to randomly sampling from the base policy).

If
\pi(\cdot\mid{}x)=w(\cdot\mid{}x)\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)
is a valid distribution,
and the rejection threshold
M
upper bounds the importance weight (or now, likelihood ratio) uniformly, that is,

M\geq\frac{\pi(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}

for all
x
and
y
,
then
Algorithm

4
is identical to classical rejection sampling,
where it is known that the law of accepted samples matches the target distribution
\pi
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}\left(y\mid\xi=1,x\right)=\pi(y\mid{}x)
.
If
M
is not a uniform upper bound on the likelihood, the law of the accepted
samples is not identical to
\pi
.
This is because
Algorithm

4
effectively truncates the distribution of
\pi
to be at most
M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
,
and any mass above this threshold is effectively lost.
Nonetheless, the law of accepted responses is a close approximation to
\pi
when
M
is sufficiently large.

Approximate
rejection sampling under this regime was first analyzed
in
Block and Polyanskiy (2023)
, and our results in this section below borrow from their analysis.

For the remainder of this section, we will consider the problem of
sampling from a target distribution or policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
by calling
\texttt{RejectionSampling}_{N,M}({{\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}}}\,;{}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},x)
,
where
\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}
is its importance weight (or here, likelihood ratio)
over the base policy. Concretely, we are concerned with analyzing
how close the
RejectionSampling
response distribution is to
\pi
as a function
of the truncation level
M
.
For example, if there exists
y
such that
w(y\mid{}x)>0

but
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)=0

then it is not possible information-theoretically
to sample from this portion of the target distribution,
and similar reasoning applies to
y
with poor coverage under
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
.

Preliminaries:
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence

Based on the intuition above, a central object in our analysis will be
the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence in
Section

D.1

(
Polyanskiy, 2010
;
Block and Polyanskiy, 2023
)
,
which,
for an input rejection threshold
M
,
quantifies the mass of the target distribution
\pi

that is lost by truncating the importance weight
\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}
to
M
.

{definition}

[
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence ]
For a prompt
x
,
base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
and target policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
let
\pi(x)\vcentcolon={}\pi(\cdot\mid{}x)
refer to the distribution conditioned on
x
,
and define
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)
similarly.
Then for any rejection threshold
M\geq 1
,
the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\vcentcolon={}\En_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left[\left(\frac{\pi(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}-M\right)_{+}\right]=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}(x)}\pi(y\mid{}x)-M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x),

(36)

where
\mathcal{Y}_{M}(x)=\left\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:\pi(y\mid{}x)>M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)\right\}
.
In addition, the expected
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence over the prompt distribution
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\vcentcolon={}\En_{x\sim\rho}\left[\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\right].

(37)

Note that
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)
and
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)
are non-increasing in
M
in the sense that
they do not increase as
M
gets larger, and we will show in the
sequel that they control the approximation error for
RejectionSampling
.
In particular, the parameter
M
strikes a bias-variance tradeoff
in the
RejectionSampling
procedure.
When
M
is large, the algorithm requires a large number of samples in order to terminate;
but for
M
small, incurs larger bias from failing to sample from regions of the target density.

For the results that follow, it will be useful to define the smallest parameter
M

that guarantees
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\leq\varepsilon

for some error tolerance of interest

\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

{definition}

Given the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
, for a target policy
\pi
,
prompt
x
,
and any
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, define the smallest rejection threshold
M
that ensures

\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\leq\varepsilon
to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}\vcentcolon={}\min\left\{M\mid{}\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(38)

Similarly, define the smallest rejection threshold
M
that ensures
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\leq\varepsilon
to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}\vcentcolon={}\min\left\{M\mid{}\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(39)

Though the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence is perhaps the most natural object by which to quantiy the error of aproximate rejection sampling,
it can also be upper bounded by other information-theoretic divergences,
such as
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\pi}
,
which will be useful in the later analysis for
BoN-Alignment
and
InferenceTimePessimism
.
We state the result below for
\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}
for a fixed
x
,
which can be stated for
\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}
in a similar manner.

{proposition}

Given the base policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
,
for any target policy
\pi
and prompt
x
,
recall that
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\pi(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}
,
and define
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}(x)\vcentcolon={}\frac{1}{\alpha}\En_{y\sim\pi}\left[\left(\frac{\pi(y\mid{}x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]
for any
\alpha>1
,
so that
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\pi}(x)=\mathcal{C}^{\pi}(x)
.
Then for any
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
, we have

\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}\leq\min\left(\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi}(x),~~\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}(x)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\right).

(40)

In particular, for the coverage coefficient
\mathcal{C}^{\pi}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\pi}(x)
,
the above result shows that
\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}^{\pi}(x)}{\varepsilon}
.
The proof below utilizes
Block and Polyanskiy (2023, Example 7)
and the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}
controls the Renyi divergence of order
\alpha
between
\pi
and
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
.

Proof of
Section

D.1
.

As the prompt is fixed, we omit
x
dependencies for notational compactness.
Recall that for any
M
,

\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)=\pi(\mathcal{Y}_{M})-M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\mathcal{Y}_{M}).

The statement for
M=\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi}
follows directly from the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)
since
\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi}}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)=0
. For
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}
, it can be seen that
\pi(\mathcal{Y}_{M})\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}}{M^{\alpha-1}}
since

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}\geq\sum_{y}\frac{\pi(y)^{\alpha}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)^{\alpha-1}}\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[y\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}\right]>M^{\alpha-1}\sum_{y}\pi(y)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[y\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}\right]=M^{\alpha-1}\cdot\pi(\mathcal{Y}_{M}).

(41)

Then for
M
to satisfy

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}}{M^{\alpha-1}}-M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\mathcal{Y}_{M})\leq\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}}{M^{\alpha-1}},

(42)

it suffices to have
M=\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}^{\pi}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
.
∎

D.2
Guarantee for
RejectionSampling

This section contains sample complexity upper bounds for
Algorithm

4
,
which express the size of
N

required to ensure that the total variation distance between
the law of responses drawn from
Algorithm

4

and the target distribution
\pi
is small.
The main result,
Section

D.2
, that expresses sample
complexity in terms of
M
and
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)
.

For a fixed
N
,

Section

D.2
shows that the total variation distance
is bounded by the
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence corresponding to the input rejection threshold
M
,
as well as an exponential in
\frac{1}{N}
term that
bounds the error when the algorithm fails to terminate.
As expected, the former term decreases as
M
increases,
while the lattter increases.
In addition, while increasing
N
can reduce error from the latter term,
the former term is irreducible even as
N
tends too infinity,
and this too we expect given that
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)
is an information-theoretic measure of error
that is a property of the distributions and the choice of
M
.

{lemma}

[Per-prompt rejection sampling upper bound; adapted from Theorem 3 in
Block and Polyanskiy (2023)
]
For any valid policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
for a given prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
let
{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}(x)\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
be the law of responses induced by running

\texttt{RejectionSampling}_{N,M}({{\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}}}\,;{}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},x)
(
Algorithm

4
).
We have

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi(x),{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}(x)\right)\leq\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{N\cdot\left(1-\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\right)}{M}\right).

(43)

In particular, if
M=\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}
and
N\gtrsim\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
for some
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
,
we have that
D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\lesssim\varepsilon
.

Proof of
Section

D.2
.

As the prompt is fixed, we omit
x
dependencies for notational compactness.
Define the truncated pseudo-distribution
\widetilde{\pi}=\min\{\pi,M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\}
.
Define
A_{M}\vcentcolon={}\sum_{y}{\widetilde{\pi}(y)}
to be the total mass of the truncated policy.
Recalling that
\mathcal{Y}_{M}(x)=\left\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:\pi(y\mid{}x)>M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid{}x)\right\}
,

A_{M}
can be equivalent expressed as

\displaystyle A_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\min\{\pi(y),M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\}

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}}M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)+\sum_{y\notin\mathcal{Y}_{M}}\pi(y)

(45)

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right).

(46)

Recall that, for a single sample
y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
,

Algorithm

4
samples
\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})
, where

p_{y}\vcentcolon={}\min\left\{\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot M},1\right\}
is a Bernoulli random variable such that

\mathbb{P}_{\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})}\left(\xi=1\mid{}y\right)=p_{y}
.
Then the expected probability of acceptance for a single sample is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})}\left(\xi=1\right)

\displaystyle=\En_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=1\mid{}y\right)\right]

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot\min\left\{\frac{\pi(y)}{M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)},1\right\}

(48)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{y}\min\left\{\pi(y),M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\right\}

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{M}}{M},

(50)

and it can be seen that the law of the response conditioned on acceptance,

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{M}}M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)+\sum_{y\notin\mathcal{Y}_{M}}\pi(y)
,
is equivalent to the normalized version of
\widetilde{\pi}
since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(y^{\prime}=y\mid{}\xi=1\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})}\left(y^{\prime}=y,\xi=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})}(\xi=1)}=\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot\min\left\{\frac{\pi(y)}{M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)},1\right\}}{A_{M}/M}=\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(y)}{A_{M}}.

(51)

Next, given
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}=\left\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\right\}
,
let
\widehat{\xi}_{N}=\left\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\right\}
be the random draw of Bernoulli random variables,
where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y_{i}})
for all
i\in[N]
.
For short, we write
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}(\cdot)\equiv\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},\xi_{1}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(y_{1}),\ldots,\xi_{N}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(y_{N})}(\cdot)
.
Define also the following event, which is a random variable over the draw of
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
and
\widehat{\xi}_{N}
,
under which rejection sampling accepts one of the
N
responses
and
Algorithm

4
outputs the
i^{\star}
th response in
Line
6
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to0.0pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:rej-term}\hfil}eq:rej-term}\mathrm{stop}\vcentcolon={}\left\{\exists i^{\star}{}\in[N]\text{ s.t. }\xi_{i^{\star}}=1\right\}.

(52)

Let
\widehat{y}=\texttt{RejectionSampling}_{N,M}({{\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}}}\,;{}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},x)
be the response output by
Algorithm

4
,
which is a random variable over draws of
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
and
\widehat{\xi}_{N}
.
Due to independence, we have that the law of responses is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\widehat{y}=y\mid{}\mathrm{stop}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(y_{i^{\star}}=y\mid{}\exists i^{\star}\in[N]\text{ s.t. }\xi_{i^{\star}}=1\right)=\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(y)}{A_{M}}.

(53)

It can be observed that via union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\neg\mathrm{stop}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\xi_{i}=0,~\forall i\in[N]\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathbb{P}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{y})}(\xi=1)\right)^{N}

(55)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{A_{M}}{M}\right)^{N}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{N\cdot A_{M}}{M}}.

(57)

Recall that
{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}(y)=\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(y\right)
.
Now for the upper bound, we first decompose the total variation distance using
\widetilde{\pi}
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi,{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\pi(y)-{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}(y)\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\pi(y)-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert}_{\mathrm{(T{1})}}+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert{\pi}_{\mathsf{R}}(y)-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert}_{\mathrm{(T{2})}},

(58)

where, via
Section

D.1
, the first term is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T{1})}=\sum_{y}{}\pi(y)-\min\left\{\pi(y),M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\right\}=\sum_{y}{}\pi(y)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\pi(y)>M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\right]=\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right).

(59)

and we further bound the second term as

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T{2})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\widehat{y}=y\mid{}\mathrm{stop}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\widehat{y}=y\mid{}\neg\mathrm{stop}\right)-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\widehat{y}=y\mid{}\mathrm{stop}\right)-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert}_{\mathrm{(T{3})}}+\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}(\neg\mathrm{stop})

(61)

From
Eq.

57
, have
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}(\neg\mathrm{stop})\leq e^{-\frac{N\cdot A_{M}}{M}}
.
In addition, using
Eq.

53
and the fact that
A_{M}\leq 1
,
we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T{3})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N},\,\widehat{\xi}_{N}}\left(\widehat{y}=y\mid{}\mathrm{stop}\right)-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert

(62)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}{}\left\lvert\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(y)}{A_{M}}-\widetilde{\pi}(y)\right\rvert=\sum_{y}{}{\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(y)}{A_{M}}-\widetilde{\pi}(y)}

(63)

\displaystyle=1-A_{M}=\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right),

(64)

so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{(T{2})}\leq\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)+\exp^{-\frac{N\cdot A_{M}}{M}}.

(65)

Combining
\mathrm{(T{1})}
and
\mathrm{(T{2})}
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{(T{1})}+\mathrm{(T{2})}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp^{-\frac{N\cdot A_{M}}{M}},

(67)

and substituting the expression for
A_{M}=1-\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)
gives the result.
∎

.

,
which shows that there exists some

for which

(77)

The result now follows by noting that

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)-\widehat{\pi}(y))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}=\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}}-1\geq\frac{1}{8}C^{\star}-1\geq\frac{1}{16}C^{\star}.

(78)

Case 2:
\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{8}C^{\star}

In this case, we set

\pi^{\star}=\lambda\pi_{\texttt{max}}+(1-\lambda)\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}

(79)

for
\lambda^{2}\vcentcolon={}\frac{C^{\star}}{2\mathcal{C}^{\texttt{max}}}\in(0,1)
. We compute directly
that

\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}=\lambda^{2}\mathcal{C}^{\texttt{max}}+(1-\lambda^{2}),

(80)

so that
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\in\left[\frac{1}{2}C^{\star},C^{\star}\right]
. We invoke

Appendix

E
with
\pi^{\star}
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}
, which gives that there exists some
r^{\star}
for which

J_{r^{\star}}(\pi^{\star})-J_{r^{\star}}(\widehat{\pi})\geq\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}\cdot{}\sqrt{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{(\pi^{\star}(y)-\widehat{\pi}(y))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}}.

(81)

We further compute that

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{(\pi^{\star}(y)-\widehat{\pi}(y))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}=\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}+\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}}-2\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\pi^{\star}(y)\widehat{\pi}(y)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}\geq{}\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}-\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}},

(82)

where the last step follows by the AM-GM inequality, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\pi^{\star}(y)\widehat{\pi}(y)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}\leq{}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{(\pi^{\star}(y))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{(\widehat{\pi}(y))^{2}}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}.

(83)

By the assumption for this case, we have
\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{8}C^{\star}
,
so that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}-\mathcal{C}^{\widehat{\pi}}\geq{}\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}-\frac{1}{8}C^{\star}\geq{}\frac{1}{4}C^{\star}-\frac{1}{8}C^{\star}=\frac{1}{8}C^{\star},

(84)

completing the result.

∎

increases in

while the reward model
\widehat{r}
decreasess.
Because
\pi^{\star}
here is an exponential tilting of
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
with respect to the true reward,
the lower bound construction reflects the structure of language modeling,
where policies are parameterized as softmax functions and the response space is exponentially large,
which we believe may be of independent interest.

Preliminaries

The lower bound constructions below utilize only a single prompt
x
,
and in the proofs (not the theorem statements), we drop the
x
dependence for notational compactness.
For example,
\pi(y)\equiv\pi(y\mid{}x)
since
\mathcal{X}=\left\{x\right\}
,

\mathcal{C}^{\pi}\equiv\mathcal{C}^{\pi}(x)
, etc.
Lastly, we formally define what we mean by “any selection algorithm”
in the sample-and-evaluate framework.

{definition}
[Inference-time selection algorithm
\mathcal{A}
]
Under the sample-and-evaluate framework (
Section

2
),
an inference-time selection algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is any mapping from

\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(\cdot\mid{}x)
and
\left\{\widehat{r}(x,y_{i})\right\}_{i\in[N]}

to a response
y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
;
we define
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
to be
the law over responses that the algorithm induces.

F.2.1
Lower Bounds under
L_{\infty}
-Coverage

{theorem}

[Regret lower bound for
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}_{\infty}
]
For any
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}\in[0,1]
and
C\geq 1
,
there exists a comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
over contexts
\mathcal{X}=\{x\}
and responses
\mathcal{Y}

for which the following statements hold.

1.

There exists a problem instance
(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},r^{\star},\widehat{r})
with

\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}
and

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=C

(115)

such that, for any
N<\frac{C}{2}
,
any inference-time selection algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(
Section

F.2
)
has regret

J(\pi^{\star};x)-J(\pi_{\mathcal{A}};x)>\min\left\{2\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)},~1\right\}.

(116)

2.

For any
N\geq 1
,
there exists a problem instance
(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},r^{\star},\widehat{r})
with

\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}
and

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=C

(117)

such that
BoN-Alignment
suffers regret

J(\pi^{\star};x)-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}};x)\geq c\cdot\min\left\{\sqrt{N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)},~1\right\},

(118)

where
c
is a universal constant.

Proof of
Section

F.2.1
.

Because we condition on a single prompt
x
,
we omit
x
dependencies for ease of presentation; in particular, we abbreviate
\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\equiv\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star}(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)
.

Fix
N
and
\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}
and
C
.
The response space is
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{0},y^{\star},y_{\mathsf{bad}}\}
,
the comparator policy
\pi^{\star}(y)=\mathbb{I}\left[y=y^{\star}\right]
plays
y^{\star}
deterministically,
and the reference policy is

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y_{0})=1-\frac{1}{2N}-\frac{1}{C},&\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y^{\star})=\frac{1}{C},&\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})=\frac{1}{2N},\end{array}

(119)

for which we have
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}=\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}=C
.
Next, for some
\varepsilon\geq 0
recall
Section

D.1
,

\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}\vcentcolon={}\min\left\{M\mid{}\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(120)

For our choice of policies we can compute
\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}
in closed form,
since for any
M
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)=1-M\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y^{\star})=1-\frac{M}{C}.

(121)

Then any
M\geq C\cdot(1-\varepsilon)
is sufficient to have
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\leq\varepsilon
, therefore

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to0.0pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:mpix1}\hfil}eq:mpix1}\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}=C\cdot(1-\varepsilon).

(122)

Part 1 (Small
N
)

Define the reward functions

\begin{array}[]{l l l}r^{\star}(y_{0})=0&r^{\star}(y^{\star})=1&r^{\star}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})=0\\
\widehat{r}(y_{0})=0&\widehat{r}(y^{\star})=1-\min\left\{\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},1\right\}&\widehat{r}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})=0\end{array}

(123)

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\En_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(y)-r^{\star}(y)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{C}=\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}.

(124)

For some
\varepsilon
that we will set shortly, when
N<\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}
the regret is lower bounded as

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})

\displaystyle=\pi^{\star}(y^{\star})-\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}}(y^{\star})

(125)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(y^{\star}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right)

(126)

\displaystyle\geq 2\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right).

(127)

Then setting
\varepsilon=\min\left\{\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},\frac{1}{2}\right\}

and using the closed form of
\mathcal{M}_{{x,{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}
in
Eq.

122
,

Section

D.3
states that when

N<\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}=C\cdot\left(1-\min\left\{\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right)=C\cdot\max\left\{1-\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},~\frac{1}{2}\right\},

(128)

we have
\mathcal{E}_{{\mathcal{M}_{{{\varepsilon}}}^{\pi^{\star}}}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)>\varepsilon=\min\left\{\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},\frac{1}{2}\right\}
, and thus

J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})>\min\left\{2\sqrt{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},1\right\}~.

(129)

Part 2 (Large
N
)

Define the gap on
y_{\mathsf{bad}}
to be
\Delta\vcentcolon={}\min\left\{1,\sqrt{N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}\right\}
, and set the reward functions

\begin{array}[]{l l l}r^{\star}(y_{0})=0&r^{\star}(y^{\star})=1&r^{\star}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})=1-\Delta\\
\widehat{r}(y_{0})=0&\widehat{r}(y^{\star})=1-\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{C}{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}},1\right\}&\widehat{r}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})=1\end{array}

(130)

and we can check that

\displaystyle\En_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(y)-r^{\star}(y)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{2C}+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2N}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{2}+\frac{\min\left\{1,N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}\right\}}{2N}\leq\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}.

(131)

Note that for this construction,
\widehat{r}(y_{\mathsf{bad}})
is the largest reward under
\widehat{r}
, so
\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}}

will always play
y_{\mathsf{bad}}
if
y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
, which is an event
that occurs with at least constant probability under our choice for
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^{N}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(132)

Using this, we lower bound the regret as

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})\cdot\En_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\left[J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})\mid{}y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right]

(133)

\displaystyle~+\mathbb{P}(y_{\mathsf{bad}}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})\cdot\En_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\left[J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})\mid{}y_{\mathsf{bad}}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right]

(134)

\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}(y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})\cdot\En_{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\left[J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})\mid{}y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right]

(135)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(y_{\mathsf{bad}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})\cdot\Delta

(136)

\displaystyle\geq c\cdot\min\left\{1,\sqrt{N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}\right\},

(137)

where in the first inequality we use the fact that
\pi^{\star}
is optimal for
r^{\star}
, and in the last inequality we plug in the definition of
\Delta
.
∎

F.2.2
Lower Bounds under
L_{1}
-Coverage

{theorem}

[Regret lower bound for
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}
]
For any
\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}\in(0,1/4]

,
there exists a comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
over

\mathcal{X}=\{x\}
and response space
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
and universal constants

such that
the following statements hold for any

.

1.

For any
\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}},\tfrac{1}{4}]
,

(138)

(139)

(

(140)

2.

For any
N\gtrsim 1
,
there exists a problem instance
(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}},r^{\star},\widehat{r})
with
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}(x)\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=O(\log C)

(141)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}(x)=O(C),

(142)

(143)

Proof of
Section

F.2.2
.

As in the proof of
Section

F.2.1
,
x
-dependencies are ommitted in the proof below,
inclusive of complexity measures such as
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star}(x),\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)

Part 1 (
N
small).
We prove the statement for
\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\leq\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}^{-2}
, otherwise
\sqrt{\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}=\Omega(1)
.

For all
\varepsilon\in\left[\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}},\tfrac{1}{4}\right]
,
we will define
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
and
\pi^{\star}
to be the distributions from the construction in
Section

F.2.3
with
C
.
These policies are defined as follows for all
i\in\mathcal{Y}=\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}

(where we use
i
instead of
y
to index responses).

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(i)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4^{i}}

(144)

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}(i)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2^{i}}{3}\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(i),&\text{~if }i\leq I\vcentcolon={}\lceil\log C\rceil,\\
2^{I}\cdot\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(i),&\text{~otherwise.}\end{cases}

(145)

From the proof of
Section

F.2.3
, we know that

\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}=O(\log C),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\infty}^{\pi^{\star}}=O(C).

(146)

Now fix a choice of
\varepsilon\in\left[\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}},\tfrac{1}{4}\right]
.
We will now define the reward functions for the construction.
Let
k_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{Y}
is an index that will be specified shortly,
and, for
i\in\mathcal{Y}
, define

\Delta_{\varepsilon}(i)=2^{i}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{4\cdot k_{\varepsilon}}}
, and

\displaystyle r^{\star}(i)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i<k_{\varepsilon},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{\varepsilon}(k_{\varepsilon})}{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\qquad\widehat{r}(i)=\begin{cases}\Delta_{\varepsilon}(i)&\text{if }i<k_{\varepsilon},\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\Delta_{\varepsilon}(k_{\varepsilon})}{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(147)

For this choice of
\widehat{r}
and
r^{\star}
,
we verify that the estimation error is upper bounded as
\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}
,

\displaystyle\En_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(i)-r^{\star}(i)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=3\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\varepsilon}}4^{-i}\cdot\Delta^{2}_{\varepsilon}(i)+\Delta^{2}_{\varepsilon}(k_{\varepsilon})\sum_{i=k_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}\right)

(148)

\displaystyle=3\cdot k_{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{4\cdot k_{\varepsilon}}+\left(\frac{4^{k_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{4\cdot k_{\varepsilon}}\right)\cdot 4^{-k_{\varepsilon}}

(149)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{4}\left(3+\frac{1}{k_{\varepsilon}}\right)

(150)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}.

(151)

Next, we set

k_{\varepsilon}=\left\lfloor\log\left(\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{-p}\right)\right\rfloor.

(152)

We can check that
k_{\varepsilon}\leq I=\lceil\log C\rceil
,
again using the precondition that
C\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}}\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\lfloor\log\left(\frac{1}{\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}\right)^{p}}\right)\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}}\right)\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\log(C)\right\rfloor.

(153)

Then
Section

D.3
applied with

\varepsilon^{\prime}=2^{-k_{\varepsilon}}=c\cdot\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{p}
,
where
c
is an absolute constant, states that,
if
N<c\cdot\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{-p}
,
any selection algorithm (
Section

F.2
) has

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathcal{A}}\right)\geq\mathcal{E}_{{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)\gtrsim\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{p}.

(154)

We conclude by lower bounding the regret using
Eq.

154
.
When
N<c\cdot\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{-p}
,

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\varepsilon}}r^{\star}(i)\cdot\left(\pi^{\star}(i)-\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\right)+r^{\star}(k_{\varepsilon})\cdot\sum_{i=k_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}\left(\pi^{\star}(i)-\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\right)

(155)

\displaystyle=r^{\star}(k_{\varepsilon})\cdot\sum_{i=k_{\varepsilon}+1}^{\infty}\left(\pi^{\star}(i)-\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\right)

(156)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{\varepsilon}(k_{\varepsilon})}{2}\right)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)

(157)

\displaystyle>c_{2}\cdot\left(\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{p},

(158)

where we have applied
Eq.

154
in the last line,
and in the first inequality we use the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{{M}}\left(\pi^{\star},\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\right)
with

M=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=2^{k_{\varepsilon}}
,
since
\{k_{\varepsilon}+1,\ldots\}=\{i:\frac{\pi^{\star}(i)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(i)}\geq 2^{k_{\varepsilon}}\}
.

Part 2 (
N
large).

Fix
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.
We prove the result for
N\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}
, otherwise the stated bound holds trivially.
Let
k_{N}=\lfloor\log_{4}(N)\rfloor\leq I\vcentcolon={}\lceil\log C\rceil
,
so that
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(k_{N})\geq\frac{1}{N}
.

Next, let

\Delta_{N}(i)\vcentcolon={}2^{i}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8\cdot k_{N}}}
,
and define the reward functions

\displaystyle r^{\star}(i)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{N}(i)}{2}&\text{if }i<k_{N},\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}&\text{if }i=k_{N},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\qquad\widehat{r}(i)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\Delta_{N}(i)}{2}&\text{if }i<k_{N},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}&\text{if }i=k_{N},\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(159)

First, we check the reward ranges, and observe that

\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{8}\cdot N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}\leq 1
.
For the reward model error, we have

\displaystyle\En_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\left(\widehat{r}(i)-r^{\star}(i)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=3\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}4^{-i}\cdot\Delta_{N}^{2}(i)+k_{N}\cdot\Delta_{k_{N}}^{2}(k_{N})\sum_{i=k_{N}}^{\infty}4^{-i}\right)

(160)

\displaystyle=\frac{3(k_{N}-1)\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8k_{N}}+k_{N}\cdot\Delta_{N}^{2}(k_{N})\cdot 4^{1-k_{N}}

(161)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8}\left(\frac{3(k_{N}-1)}{k_{N}}+4\right)

(162)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}.

(163)

Second, we show that
J(\pi^{\star})-J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})\geq 0
as long as
k_{N}\geq 4
by computing the policies in closed form.
Recall that
\pi^{\star}(i)=2^{-i}
if
i\leq I
,
and
\pi^{\star}(i)=3\cdot 2^{I}\cdot 4^{-i}
otherwise.
Then by plugging in this expression and the definition of
r^{\star}
above,
we calculate its return as

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}2^{-i}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{N}(i)}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(2^{-k_{N}}+\sum_{i=k_{N}+1}^{I}2^{-i}+3\cdot 2^{I}\sum_{i=I+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}\right)

(164)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=k_{N}+1}^{I}2^{-i}+3\cdot 2^{I}\sum_{i=I+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}-2^{-k_{N}}\right)

(165)

Recall that

1=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Y}}{}\pi^{\star}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{I}2^{-i}+3\cdot 2^{I}\sum_{i=I+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}.

(166)

Then grouping terms and substituting this identity,

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}{}2^{-i}\Delta_{N}(i)+\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})\cdot\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}}2^{-i}-2^{-k_{N}}\right)\right)

(167)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}{}2^{-i}\cdot 2^{i}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8\cdot k_{N}}}+2^{k_{N}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8}}\cdot\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}}2^{-i}-2^{-k_{N}}\right)\right)

(168)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\left(k_{N}-1\right)\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8\cdot k_{N}}}+2^{k_{N}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{8}}\cdot\left(2^{-k_{N}}-2^{-k_{N}}\right)\right)

(169)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\left(k_{N}-1\right)\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{32\cdot k_{N}}}.

(170)

Also, for
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
we have

\displaystyle J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})

\displaystyle=3\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}4^{-i}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\Delta_{N}(i)}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(4^{-k_{N}}+\sum_{i=k_{N}+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}\right)+\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=k_{N}+1}^{\infty}4^{-i}-4^{-k_{N}}\right)\right)

(171)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{32k_{N}}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k_{N}-1}2^{-i}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}\cdot\left(4^{-k_{N}}-4^{-k_{N}}\right)

(172)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{32k_{N}}}\cdot\left(1-2^{1-k_{N}}\right)

(173)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{32k_{N}}}.

(174)

Together, we obtain that

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})\geq(k_{N}-4)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{32\cdot k_{N}}},

(175)

which is nonnegative whenever
k_{N}=\lfloor\log_{4}N\rfloor>4
, or
N\geq 256
.

Lastly, we lower bound the regret by considering two cases.

•

If
k_{N}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
, then
BoN
will choose
k_{N}
since
k_{N}=\argmax_{i}\widehat{r}(i)
, and

\En\left[J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})\mid{}k_{N}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{k}}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{k_{N}}\cdot\Delta_{N}(k_{N})}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{32}\cdot N\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}.

(176)

•

If
k_{N}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
, our design of
r^{\star}
and
\widehat{r}
ensures that
BoN
will always choose the response
y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}

with the smallest reward

, while
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
is equivalent to choosing

uniformly, thus

\En\left[J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})\mid{}k_{N}\notin\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}\right]\geq 0.

(177)

Formally, combining these cases gives

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\texttt{BoN}})

2. The construction is as follows. The response space

N\gtrsim\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\left(1+\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta}\right)\log\left(\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta\cdot\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}}\right)\right)
we have

\displaystyle J(\pi^{\star})-J(\widehat{\pi})\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}+\frac{3\beta}{4}\cdot\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{\beta}+2\varepsilon_{\texttt{RM}}.

(229)

We set
\beta=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{\texttt{RM}}}{\mathcal{C}^{\pi^{\star}}}}
to balance the terms for the final result.
∎

{lemma}

Let a reward function
r
and parameter
\alpha>0
be given, and define

\displaystyle\Delta_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Y})=\left\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{Y}}\mid{}\textstyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)=\alpha\right\}.

(235)

Then there exists a choice of
\lambda\in[J(\pi_{\mathsf{ref}})-\alpha\beta,\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r(y)-\alpha\beta]

such that
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)=\alpha
. Furthermore, given any
\lambda
such that
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)=\alpha
,

\displaystyle\pi(y)=\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(r(y)-\lambda\right)\right)

(236)

is the optimal solution to

\displaystyle\pi=\argmax_{\pi\in\Delta_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Y})}\left[J(\pi)-\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi}\right].

(237)

Proof of
Section

G.1.1
.

First we rewrite the objective in
Eq.

237
in primal form,

\displaystyle\pi=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{-J(\pi)+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi}\right\}.

(238)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)=\alpha

(239)

\displaystyle\quad-\pi(y)\leq 0,~\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}

(240)

and we can verify that Slater’s condition holds, because the objective is convex, since
J(\pi)
is affine and
\mathcal{C}^{\pi}
is (strongly) convex, and there exists at least one strictly feasible point, an example being the function
\pi^{\prime}
that sets
\pi^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\alpha}{|\mathcal{Y}|}
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Under strong duality, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for optimality;
further, the objective has a unique minimum due to strong convexity,
and therefore, to prove the theorem statement, it is sufficient to show that the proposed
\pi
satisfies the KKT conditions.

For primal variable
\pi
and dual variables
(\nu,\lambda)
, the Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}}{}\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathcal{Y}}}{}\mathcal{L}(\pi,\nu,\lambda)=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)r(y)+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pi}+\lambda\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(y)-\alpha\right)-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\nu(y)\pi(y)

(241)

and under strong duality, we know that the optimal primal and dual variables
(\pi,\nu,\lambda)
satisfy

•

\pi\geq{}0

•

\sum_{y}\pi(y)=\alpha

•

\nu\geq{}0

•

\pi(y)\cdot{}\nu(y)=0
for all
y
(complementary slackness).

•

\nabla{}_{\pi}\mathcal{L}(\pi,(\nu,\lambda))=0
(first-order condition).

From the first-order condition
\nabla{}_{\pi}\mathcal{L}(\pi,\nu,\lambda)=0
, we know that
\pi
satisfies for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
:

\displaystyle r(y)-\beta\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)}-\lambda+\nu(y)=0,

(242)

or after rearranging,

\displaystyle\pi(y)=\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot{}\beta^{-1}(r(y)-\lambda+\nu(y)).

(243)

Now consider a fixed
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. We consider three cases:

•

If
r(y)-\lambda<0
, then we must have

\nu(y)\geq{}-(r(y)-\lambda)>0
to satisfy
\pi(y)\geq{}0
by

Eq.

243
. By complementary slackness, this implies that

\pi(y)=0
.

•

If
r(y)-\lambda=0
, then
Eq.

243
gives

\pi(y)=\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\beta^{-1}\nu(y)
, which implies
\pi(y)=0
by
complementary slackness.

•

if
r(y)-\lambda>0
, then
r(y)-\lambda+\nu(y)>0
(since

\nu(y)\geq{}0
), which in turn gives
\pi(y)>0
by

Eq.

243
. This implies that
\nu(y)=0
by complementary slackness.

Combining these cases, we conclude that

\displaystyle\pi(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\quad r(y)-\lambda\leq{}0,\\
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\beta^{-1}(r(y)-\lambda)&\quad r(y)-\lambda>0.\end{array}\right.

This is equivalent to
\pi(y)=\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y)\cdot\mathsf{relu}(\beta^{-1}(r(y)-\lambda))
, which is also optimal under strong duality. Finally, the
condition
\sum_{y}\pi(y)=\alpha
implies that
\lambda
must be chosen
such
\pi\in\Delta_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Y})
normalizes to
\alpha
.
∎

{lemma}

Recall
\Phi(\lambda)=\En_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left[\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(r(y)-\lambda\right)\right)\right]
, and given
N
samples from
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
, define

\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{relu}\left({\beta}^{-1}\left(r(y_{i})-\lambda\right)\right).

(246)

Fix any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. With probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
\lambda\in\left[-\gamma,R_{\mathsf{max}}-\gamma\right]

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{7}{8}\Phi(\lambda)-\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda),\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)-\frac{9}{8}\Phi(\lambda)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8}+12\left(\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}+\gamma}{\beta}\right)\frac{\log\left(\frac{60R_{\mathsf{max}}}{\beta\delta}\right)}{N}.

(247)

Proof of
Section

G.1.1
.

We start with Bernstein’s inequality, which states that for a bounded random variable
X\in[a,b]
, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
,
the empirical mean

satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widehat{\En}[X]-\En[X]\right\rvert\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}[X]\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N}}+\frac{4\left(b-a\right)\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}{N},

(248)

where
\mathbb{V}[X]
is the variance of
X
.
Now fix
\lambda
, and let the random variable be
X=\mathsf{relu}\left(r(y)-\lambda\right)
.
Since
\lambda\in\left[-\gamma,R_{\mathsf{max}}-\gamma\right]
,
the random variable
X\in\left[0,\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}+\gamma}{\beta}\right]
,
and henceforth we refer to the range as
R=\frac{R_{\mathsf{max}}+\gamma}{\beta}
.
We can bound the variance of this random variable as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[X]\leq\En[X^{2}]\leq R\cdot\En[X]=R\cdot\Phi(\lambda).

(249)

Then with probability at least

, we have

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{R\Phi(\lambda)\log(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}})}{N}}+\frac{4R\log(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}})}{N}

(250)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{8}+\frac{8R\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}{N}+\frac{4R\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}{N}

(251)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{8}+\frac{12R\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}{N},

(252)

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)-\widehat{\Phi}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{8}+\frac{12R\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}{N},\quad\text{and}\quad

(253)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{8}+\frac{12R\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}{N},

(254)
