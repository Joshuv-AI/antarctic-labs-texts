The impact of helicopter teaching in nursing education and approaches to foster independent learning: A narrative review
Authors: Kalpana Thakur, Suresh K. Sharma, Thungchanbeni Ezung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ‘helicopter teaching’ in nursing education can be harmful to the development of clinical judgement, decision-making, and autonomy among nursing students because it involves excessive control, constant supervision, and over-dependence. This narrative review examines the impact of helicopter teaching on the academic and professional development of nursing students. It also discusses evidence-based approaches that encourage independent learning and resilience. We searched databases such as PubMed, CINAHL, and Scopus with a mixture of search terms and Medical Subject Headings to analyze the evidence between 2010 and 2024. The review identified several contributing factors behind this helicopter style of teaching including faculty anxiety, a lack of confidence in student competency, and institutional pressures relating to patient safety. The study also examined how such practices can negatively impact student learning and these include reduced confidence, limited thinking and dependence on instructors. The article also focused on educational theories like Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal Development or experiential learning models to propose evidence-based strategies for countering overprotection including scaffolded education, safe-to fail environments, reflection debriefings, growth zone challenges and peer coaching between faculty. These approaches can help nursing educators create a learning environment that balances patient care with the development of competent and independent future nurses to enter real-world clinical practices. Institutions must prioritize faculty development, curriculum changes and supervised autonomy as core components of nursing education.

Introduction

A teacher plays an integral and long-lasting impact on students’ lives, both academically and personally. Influence extends beyond simply delivering content; it shapes how students view themselves, their potential, and their future. Since the Vedic period, the role of the guru or teacher in Indian society has extended far beyond imparting knowledge-encompassing the moral, intellectual, and spiritual development of the student. Teachers were seen not only as guides who shaped individual lives, but also the course of society. Iconic figures like Chanakya, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar are examples of how these traditions used education to bring about social reform, and the impact was evident not only in their revolutionary ideas but also in the transformative achievements they achieved.[1,2] A report by the National Research Council highlighted the teaching strategies that promote active learning, such as problem-based and inquiry-based education, which improve student understanding and retention.[3]

Over the past few decades, teaching styles in nursing education have undergone a significant transformation, shifting from a traditional, authoritarian model to more student-centric and competency-based approaches. Historically, nursing education emphasized passive learning, where students absorbed information from instructors and had limited involvement in clinical decision-making. However, with the growing complexity of healthcare systems and the emphasis on producing practice-ready graduates, nursing education has adopted more active learning methods, such as problem-based learning, simulation, and collaborative practice models.[4,5] While these innovations have aimed to foster critical thinking and autonomy, they have also introduced new challenges—particularly regarding accountability, student preparedness, and clinical safety. In response, some educators have adopted overly cautious and protective teaching behaviors to prevent student errors in high-stakes environments. This hyper-vigilance has contributed to the rise of what can be termed helicopter teaching—a style characterized by constant supervision, intervention, and control over students’ clinical activities.[6] A study reported in BMC also found that abusive or overbearing supervisors lead to emotional exhaustion in nursing students and increase their intention to leave the profession.[7] Another correlational research confirms these findings, which indicate that the behaviors of clinical instructors have a strong impact on student self-efficacy, and too much control or too little autonomy can lead students to doubt their abilities.[8] These studies show that overprotective teaching can limit students’ critical thinking and confidence, but also lead to emotional exhaustion. Hence, it is important to have balanced pedagogical approaches that encourage both safety and independence.

Materials and Methods

This narrative review was conducted in order to synthesize the literature and evaluate it critically. Electronic databases were used to conduct a comprehensive search for relevant articles. Sources were chosen based on relevance, quality, and contribution rather than following a formal protocol.

Search strategy

The literature was searched to identify studies, reviews, and theoretical papers relevant to overprotective or helicopter teaching styles, their causes, impact, and improvement strategies. The electronic databases searched were PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The search was performed using a combination of keywords and Boolean operators such as:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To ensure that the results were relevant to current educational practices, filters were used to limit the search to peer-reviewed articles English language and published between 2010 and 2024. Theses and conference papers that have not been published, as well as predatory journal articles, were excluded.

Findings and Discussion

Helicopter teaching: Conceptual grounding

The term “helicopter parent” was first used in the fields of developmental psychology and educational studies to describe a style of parenting that involves excessive involvement with a child, such as hovering close, making decisions on their behalf, and protecting them against failure or discomfort. The term “helicopter parent” was first introduced by Dr. Haim Ginott in his 1969 book Between Parent and Teenager.[9] Researchers and authors such as Foster Cline and Jim Fay later popularized and expanded the concept in the 2006 book Parenting with Love and Logic.[10,11] The term has since transcended its original parenting context and can be used as a valuable framework for understanding the behavior and impact of overinvolved or overprotective educators. Just as helicopter parents “hover” over their children, making decisions on their behalf and shielding them from challenges, some nursing instructors exhibit similar patterns by closely monitoring students, providing excessive guidance, and intervening in tasks before learners can attempt them independently. This constant oversight, while often well-intentioned, can significantly hinder the development of clinical competence, confidence, and decision-making skills among students. Research has shown that students under such intense supervision often report increased anxiety, reduced autonomy, and poor critical thinking development.[12,13]

The helicopter-like teaching style creates an environment in which students may become dependent on instructor approval, avoid taking initiative, and feel unprepared for the realities of independent clinical practice. Helicopter teaching in nursing is becoming increasingly common as educators tend to provide students with all the answers, step-by-step instructions, and close supervision, which limits students’ involvement in critical thinking and independent problem-solving. This approach often stems from a desire to avoid any mistakes or complaints, particularly due to concerns about students’ poor coping abilities under pressure. While intended to support and safeguard students, this teaching style can unintentionally hinder their development into confident, competent, and autonomous nursing professionals.[13,14,15] The aim of this article is to explore the underlying reasons and impacts of helicopter teaching in nursing education and to propose practical solutions to promote student independence and professional growth.

Causes and impact of helicopter teaching in nursing

In nursing, overprotective teaching is often a result of educators’ fear of compromising the safety and security of patients. This leads them to limit student involvement in clinical tasks. Oermann and Gaberson[16] reported that a lack of trust in the students’ competency can also be a contributing factor, as instructors might perceive them as being unprepared or inexperienced. In addition, legal and institutional concerns may pressure educators to place safety above experiential learning. While well-intentioned, this overprotection negatively impacts students by reducing confidence, hindering the development of crucial clinical skills, and encouraging dependence on supervision. Furthermore, students may have difficulty with decision-making and be unprepared for independent practice after graduation.[17,18]

Helicopter teaching in nursing refers to an educational approach where educators monitor or control students too closely during clinical practice sessions, often due to institutional pressure, inexperienced instructors, or simply wanting to ensure patient safety.[18,19] Many nursing educators with limited teaching experience or who have suffered negative results may lack confidence in their ability to supervise students effectively and therefore prefer lingering closely and intervening frequently. As part of their efforts to minimize legal liability, some educators may feel pressured into adopting more controlling approaches—with potentially negative repercussions for student learning.[19,20,21,22] While such behavior might have good intentions behind it, it could still have significant negative implications on learning outcomes. Limiting students’ opportunities to practice independently restricts their growth as critical thinkers, clinical professionals, and confident individuals. As a result, students may become overly dependent on instructors, become anxious when performing tasks alone, and enter the workforce unprepared to handle real-world nursing responsibilities.[22,23] Research evidence also demonstrates that overprotective or perfectionist teaching styles may restrict students’ hands-on development, limiting readiness for autonomous practice [Table 1].

Micromanagement to mentorship: Shifting the teaching paradigm

Nursing educators can combat helicopter teaching styles by actively encouraging student autonomy while still maintaining adequate supervision to protect patient safety. One effective strategy for increasing student autonomy and confidence is releasing responsibility gradually over time. Teachers initially provide close guidance but gradually release more independence as students gain competence and confidence.[16] This approach promotes critical thinking and decision-making skills development while encouraging students to take ownership of their learning. Simulation-based education has been shown to increase students’ readiness for clinical practice by offering safe environments to practice complex skills before providing direct patient care.[25] By offering both structured supervision and hands-on experiences simultaneously, educators can reduce micromanagement tendencies while creating a supportive yet empowering learning environment for their students.

To deal with overprotective nursing teaching, it is necessary to shift away from instructor-centered strategies and towards student-centered ones based on established educational theories. Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development (ZPD) suggests students learn best when challenged beyond what they can independently do, with the appropriate support. Overprotective teachers often prevent students from entering the “growth zone” because they limit their opportunities to reflect and self-correct. Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory also emphasizes learning through experience. This includes making mistakes, reflecting, and applying new understanding. Instructors should slowly release control to counteract overprotection. They can move from demonstrations to guided practice and then to independent practice. Reflective debriefing is encouraged instead of immediate correction. By providing both challenge and support, educators are able to foster confidence, independence, and competence in future nurses.[26,27]

To address helicopter teaching in nursing, a shift is needed to pedagogical methods that promote critical thinking and reflective practice. Overprotective instruction, while well-intentioned and beneficial, can hinder the development of the clinical judgment and decision-making skills necessary for safe patient treatment. In scaffolded learning, educators gradually decrease support as students achieve competence. This allows them to progress from dependence to independent clinical skills. A safe-to-fail environment through simulation allows students to make errors without any real-life consequences. This promotes deeper learning and resilience.[28,29] Debriefings that are reflective instead of being corrected immediately help students to internalize lessons. Faculty calibration, peer coaching, and other methods of ensuring consistency are also used to support educators and avoid micromanagement. These strategies work together to transform nursing education from a teacher-centered model into one that is student-centered and equips students with the confidence, competence, and skills needed for clinical practice.[30,31] [Table 2].

Strategies or new approaches for students’ learning[16,25,26,27,28,29,30,31,32,33]

Maintaining professional equilibrium requires educators to foster open dialogue and reflective practice within clinical teaching environments. Encourage students to discuss their learning needs and challenges openly in order to adjust supervision levels accordingly, thus preventing unnecessary overprotection.[32] Reflective debriefing sessions provide both students and educators with an opportunity to evaluate clinical experiences critically, which builds trust between participants while creating tailored teaching strategies. Professional development for educators that emphasizes mentorship skills and emotional intelligence can strengthen educators’ confidence in managing student autonomy without jeopardizing safety.[33] Such combined strategies not only reduce helicopter teaching behavior but also foster a supportive educational climate that provides nursing students with an excellent chance to become competent practitioners.

Conclusion

Although helicopter teaching is often motivated by concerns for the safety of students and patients, it can undermine core goals in professional nursing education. Students may miss important opportunities for developing confidence, clinical reasoning, and self-efficacy when educators constantly intervene or control the learning experience. This study shows that overprotective instruction is not just a pedagogical problem, but also a barrier to nursing student development. Understanding the causes, such as fear of mistakes, institutional expectations or lack of faculty training, helps educators reflect on their practices. Adopting flexible yet structured strategies, such as scaffolded learning and reflective debriefing in safe simulation environments, peer coaching, and reflective debriefing, allows students to move from dependence to autonomy. Nursing institutions must prioritize faculty development, curriculum redesign, and evidence-based strategies for teaching that empower students, rather than overwhelm or shelter them. By creating growth zones and not comfort zones, educators are able to support nursing students as they become capable, confident, and accountable practitioners who are fully prepared for modern healthcare challenges.
