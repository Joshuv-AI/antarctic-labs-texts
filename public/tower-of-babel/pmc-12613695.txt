A case of hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia with hypoxemia onset in the neonatal period
Authors: Dongwei Zhang, Jianru Li, Gan Zhou, Diyuan Yang, Peiqiong Wu, Yingkang Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is an autosomal-dominant disease with high penetrance characterized by arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) in multiple organs and telangiectasia in the mucocutaneous. Neonatal presentations of HHT are rare, as this disorder typically presents in adolescence or beyond with epistaxis. Case presentationA male newborn was found to have hypoxemia, but screening for common causes was negative. His cerebrovascular malformations, including cerebral arteriovenous malformation (CVMs) and arteriovenous fistula (AVF), were discovered at 8 months of age. He showed no neurological symptoms after two embolization treatments, although hypoxemia of unknown origin persisted. At 29 months of age, transthoracic contrast echocardiography (TTCE) confirmed an intrapulmonary right-to-left shunt. Subsequent genetic testing revealed a de novo mutation in the ENG gene, confirming the diagnosis of HHT. However, his concurrent computerized tomography (CT) angiography unrevealed the size and location of the pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVMs), which make embolization unfeasible. Therefore, the current management plan is limited to observation and follow-up.ConclusionsThis patient highlights the importance of conducting comprehensive early screening for potential causes of hypoxemia in newborns or infants. Although rare, HHT with PAVMs remain a significant etiology of hypoxemia.

Introduction

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is an autosomal-dominant disease with high penetrance characterized by arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) in multiple organs and telangiectasia in the mucocutaneous [1]. The vast majority of patients carry disease-causing mutations in the endoglin (ENG) or activin A receptor type-II like 1 (ACVRL1) genes, causing HHT1 and HHT2 disease, respectively [2]. Neonatal presentations of HHT are rare, as this disorder typically presents in adolescence or beyond with epistaxis. To the authors’ knowledge, there is little literature describing significant hypoxemia secondary to pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVMs) during the neonatal period due to HHT.

Case presentation

A 29-month-old male presented to the respiratory department with a complaint of “persistent low oxygen saturation for over two years”. He was born via cesarean section at 33 + 2 weeks of gestation, due to “abnormal fetal heart monitoring” at an external hospital. Postnatally, he required neonatal intensive care unit admission, during which persistent low oxygen saturation (SpO2) levels (85%–92%) with cyanosis of the lips and extremities were noted. Despite multiple evaluations encompassing imaging studies (chest radiography and computed tomography), cardiac evaluation (echocardiography, enzyme analysis, and electrocardiography), laboratory investigations (hemoglobin profiling, carbon monoxide toxicity testing, and metabolic screening) and so on, no definitive diagnosis was made at that time.

In the neonatal period, cranial imaging (CT scan) revealed a subdural hematoma in the right parieto-occipital region. At 8 months, follow-up imaging indicated old hemorrhage with calcification or possible combined cerebrovascular malformation in the right parieto-occipital lobe and widening of the external spaces in the right parietal and temporal regions. Consequently, the patient was admitted to the neurosurgery department of our hospital for further evaluation and management. The cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed scattered focal abnormal tortuous vascular shadows in the right temporal lobe and bilateral parietal lobes (Fig. 1), suggesting multiple intracranial vascular malformations. These were accompanied by old hemorrhagic lesions and brain injury foci in the right temporal and parietal lobes. Additionally, a chest CT revealed an abnormally enhanced lesion in the left lower lobe of the lung, suggestive of a possible hemangioma(Fig. 2A), as well as slightly dilated and tortuous bronchial arteries. An electroencephalogram (EEG) indicated abnormal findings during the interictal period, including reduced physiological wave activity and increased slow-wave activity in the right hemisphere. The patient underwent a cerebral angiography and partial interventional embolization of multiple arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) and arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) in the bilateral parieto-occipital lobes. The postoperative recovery was uneventful. Four months post-surgery, a follow-up cranial MRI showed persistent focal abnormal tortuous vascular shadows in the right frontal, temporal, and bilateral parietal lobes, with a reduction in the number of large superficial vessels in the bilateral parietal lobes compared to previous imaging. A second interventional embolization of the left parietal arteriovenous fistula was performed, and the patient recovered smoothly postoperatively.

On physical examination, the patient showed no skin capillary malformations or telangiectasias.

Auxiliary examination

Laboratory examination

Blood Gas Analysis: PH 7.41(7.35–7.45), partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) 9.97 kPa(>10.64), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) 4.76 kPa(4.66–5.99). The biochemical indicators, including liver function, cardiac function, and renal function, showed no abnormalities. Coagulation parameters were within normal limits. Infection markers such as white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate were all normal. The complete blood count revealed a significantly elevated hemoglobin level of 156 g/L(112–149). Immunological indicators, including immunoglobulins, were within the normal range.

Imagological examination

Echocardiogram revealed structurally normal heart with normal cardiac showing no evidence of patent foramen ovale, ventricular septal defect, or atrial septal defect function. Abdominal ultrasound (liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen, and kidneys) showed no abnormalities. Electrocardiogram (ECG) indicated sinus tachycardia. A contrast-enhanced CT of the upper abdomen revealed no abnormalities, and portal vein reconstruction showed no definite anomalies. A contrast-enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated an abnormally enhanced lesion in the lateral basal segment of the left lower lung lobe, which showed slight enlargement compared to previous imaging and was suggestive of a hemangioma(Fig. 2B). Additionally, there was mild tortuosity and dilation of the bronchial arteries.

Transthoracic contrast echocardiography (TTCE)

Following the injection of agitated saline into the left antecubital vein, the right atrium and right ventricle were sequentially opacified. At rest (under sedation), the right atrium was opacified for three cardiac cycles before contrast bubbles appeared in the left atrium. After five cardiac cycles, persistent contrast bubbles were observed in the left atrium and left ventricle, with the number of microbubbles exceeding 30 (Fig. 4). These findings indicate a significant right-to-left shunt.

Genetic test results

The patient was found to harbor a heterozygous variant in the ENG gene: c.1748_1751delCAAG (p.Ser584Lysfs*55) (Table 1). The identified heterozygous frameshift variant in the ENG gene, was classified as Pathogenic according to the American college of medical genetics and genomics guidelines. This classification is based on the following evidence: PVS1 (null variant in a gene where loss-of-function is a known mechanism of disease), PS2 (confirmed de novo occurrence in the patient), and PS3 (the variant’s pathogenic effect is supported by functional studies in the literature [3, 4]). This variant was not detected in either parent. Currently, there is no available literature reporting the frequency of this variant in the general population.

Discussion

The described patient details a neonatal male presenting with hypoxemia and found to have a cerebral AVM as well as a suspected pulmonary AVM in addition to telangiectasias and epistaxis. This constellation of symptoms and findings is highly suggestive of HHT. HHT is a hereditary, relatively common, systemic disease characterized by multiple vascular bed telangiectasias and AVMs, affecting about 1 in 5,000–8000 individuals worldwide [5]. However, clinical manifestations of HHT exhibit an age-related penetrance, and telangiectasias and epistaxis are uncommon in children and predominantly seen in late childhood or adulthood, resulting in the diagnosis being frequently delayed until later stages. For example, epistaxis is the most common clinical manifestation of HHT, and the average age of onset is 12 years [6]. Diagnostic delays primarily occur because children seldom fulfill existing clinical criteria known as the Curaçao criteria including recurrent spontaneous epistaxis, multiple characteristic telangiectasias, visceral vascular malformation, first-degree family member meeting HHT criteria [1]. Throughout the entire course, this patient met only one diagnostic criterion, which does not qualify as HHT according to the diagnostic criteria. Thus, current standards lack sensitivity while maintaining specificity in pediatric populations.

For this patient, another factor contributing to the delayed diagnosis was the lack of attention to hypoxemia. Hypoxemia was detected in the patient shortly after birth, but no further investigation was pursued after excluding common clinical causes. It was not until this hospitalization that the pulmonologist considered the possibility of intrapulmonary shunting and confirmed it via TTCE. This patient illustrates that small intrapulmonary shunts may not be detected on chest contrast-enhanced CT, whereas they can be identified through TTCE. This emphasizes the complementary diagnostic value of TTCE in patients where CT findings are inconclusive, particularly in the context of unexplained hypoxemia. TTCE is also currently the guideline-recommended method for screening pulmonary arteriovenous malformations [1]. Clinicians need to improve their awareness of this aspect of diagnosis.

A right occipitoparietal subdural hematoma was detected one month after birth for the patient. Follow-up cranial imaging at 8 months of age revealed multiple AVMs in the bilateral occipitoparietal lobes and an old hemorrhagic lesion in the right parietal lobe, suggesting that the old hemorrhage might have been caused by the AVMs. However, the patient did not exhibit any corresponding neurological symptoms, such as seizures. Subsequently, a cerebral angiogram confirmed multiple AVMs with arteriovenous fistulas in the bilateral occipitoparietal lobes. The patient underwent two sessions of embolization therapy and currently remains free of neurological symptoms. Cerebral vascular malformations (CVMs) are seen in 10% to 20% of patients with HHT, including pial arterial-venous fistula (AFVs), arteriovenous malformation (AVMs) with a nidus, micro AVMs, or capillary vascular malformation with a higher prevalence in HHT1 [6, 7]. This patient is complicated by the coexistence of two CVM subtypes. From another perspective, genetic alterations are increasingly recognized as etiologic factors linked to the pathogenesis and development of CVMs. Recent reviews have reported more than 860 genetic mutations known to influence the development of cerebral AVMs and have identified various pathways that have roles in AVM development [8]. However, only 4.3% of cerebral AVMs are associated with HHT-related genes [9], which likely explains why HHT genetic testing was not initially considered in this patient during the early stages of the disease.

Compared to CVMs, pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVMs) exhibit a stronger association with HHT. Approximately 80–90% of PAVMs are HHT-related [6]. Therefore, when this patient revealed a PAVM, HHT was strongly suspected despite not meeting the Curaçao criteria. PAVMs are found in up to half of patients with HHT [10]. In pediatric patients, 44% of children with PAVMs experience signs and or symptoms, with exercise intolerance being the most common, followed by cyanosis and hypoxemia [6]. Collectively, the coexistence of PAVMs with unusually early symptom onset in this HHT patient represents a rare clinical scenario. Based on the diagnostic experience from this patient, TTCE should be considered for screening PAVMs in patients with hypoxemia who test negative on conventional examinations. Although guidelines recommend treating large PAVMs with decreased oxygen saturation to prevent serious complications [1], this patient’s concurrent CT angiography at 29 months of age unrevealed that the size and location of the PAVMs make embolization unfeasible. Therefore, the current management plan is limited to observation and follow-up for the patient. With current knowledge of the pathophysiological mechanisms of HHT, several antiangiogenic agents were tested on visceral AVMs, but no regression of visceral AVMs has been observed. While significant clinical progress has been achieved in managing HHT symptoms through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway inhibition [2]. This therapeutic approach may offer a future treatment option for our patient.

Considering the low sensitivity of clinical criteria in children, genetic testing plays an important role in the diagnosis of HHT in the pediatric population. Genetic testing of this patient revealed a heterozygous mutation in the ENG gene, identified as a de novo variant, confirming the diagnosis of HHT1. Secondly, the clinical manifestations of HHT patients are significantly correlated with their genotype. Cerebral AVMs and PAVMs are more common in HHT1 [6, 11]. This patient is precisely HHT1, which is consistent with the literature reports. PAVMs may be the only clinical criterion present in genetically confirmed HHT adult patients [12]. Therefore, for pediatric patients, even in the absence of a family history of HHT, the diagnosis of brain or PAVMs in young children should raise suspicion of a potential HHT diagnosis, prompting early genetic testing.

In conclusion, this patient highlights the importance of conducting comprehensive early screening for potential causes of hypoxemia in newborns or infants. Although rare, PAVMs remain a significant etiology of hypoxemia. Furthermore, a high index of suspicion for HHT with PAVMs is warranted in neonates and infants with unexplained hypoxemia. While TTCE is an established and highly sensitive screening tool for PAVMs in older children and adults with HHT, its application in the neonatal population requires careful consideration by clinicians. The decision to perform TTCE should balance the need for diagnosis against the potential risks, particularly in those with suspected high-grade shunts who may be at risk for cerebral embolism. Once PAVMs are identified, considering HHT as the underlying diagnosis is essential, as pediatric PAVMs are almost exclusively associated with HHT. Early diagnosis and implementation of a structured follow-up plan to prevent complications are particularly crucial in managing such patients.
