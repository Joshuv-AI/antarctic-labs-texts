Microcontroller-based water control system for evaluating crop water use characteristics
Authors: Daisuke Sugiura, Shiro Mitsuya, Hirokazu Takahashi, Ryo Yamamoto, Yoshiyuki Miyazawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change and the growing demand for agricultural water threaten global food security. Understanding water use characteristics of major crops from leaf to field scale is critical, particularly for identifying crop varieties with enhanced water-use efficiency (WUE) and stress tolerance. Traditional methods to assess WUE are either by gas exchange measurements at the leaf level or labor-intensive manual pot weighing at the whole-plant level, both of which have limited throughput.ResultsHere, we developed a microcontroller-based low-cost system that integrates pot weighing, automated water supply, and real-time monitoring of plant water consumption via Wi-Fi. We validated the system using major crops (rice soybean, maize) under diverse stress conditions (salt, waterlogging, drought). Salt-tolerant rice maintained higher water consumption and growth under salinity than salt-sensitive rice. Waterlogged soybean exhibited reduced water use and growth. Long-term experiments revealed significant WUE differences between rice varieties and morphological adaptations represented by altered shoot-to-root ratios under constant drought conditions in maize.ConclusionsWe demonstrate that the system can be used for varietal differences between major crops in their response to drought, waterlogging, and salinity stress. This system enables high-throughput, long-term evaluation of water use characteristics, facilitating the selection and development of water-saving and stress-tolerant crop varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-024-01305-0.

Background

Climate change-induced global warming and drought, as well as groundwater depletion, are making it difficult to secure agricultural water, which accounts for 70% of the world's total water withdrawals (FAO AQUASTAT). This is already having an impact on food production, as there have been reports that crop yields worldwide have decreased by about 10% due to drought and heat waves [1], and three-quarters of the world's major grain crops had been affected by drought [2]. Under these circumstances, in order to ensure global food security, it is necessary to clarify the water use characteristics of major crops that require large amounts of water for cultivation such as rice, soybeans, and maize from leaf to field scales [3]. Furthermore, establishment of rapid selection methods of highly productive and water-saving crop varieties, as well as development of water-saving cultivation technique, are expected.

One method for evaluating the water use characteristics of crops is to measure leaf-level gas exchange and calculate the water use efficiency (WUE), which is the ratio of the photosynthetic rate to the stomatal conductance or transpiration rate [4]. This method has elucidated that japonica rice varieties grown in cool temperate regions show higher WUE at the leaf level than indica varieties grown in tropical regions, suggesting that leaf-level WUE could be a useful breeding target for the development of water-saving varieties [5, 6]. Stomatal closure speed is also a determinant of plant water consumption [7–9]. Quantitative analysis of stomatal closure speed showed that monocotyledonous plants show a faster stomatal closure speed than dicotyledonous plants [10]. Ozeki et al. [11] showed that C4 crops such as sorghum and millet show rapid stomatal closure compared with C3 crops such as wheat and barley, which contributes to the higher water use efficiency of C4 crops [11]. However, these methods require a portable gas exchange system and are usually time-consuming and low throughput due to the limited number of available systems. Furthermore, water use characteristics of single leaf in the short-term does not necessarily reflect those at the whole-plant level in the long-term. Whole-plant WUE, which is expressed as the ratio of biomass to cumulative water consumption, has also been used before the establishment of gas exchange system [12]. This method has shown that the WUE of C4 plants is about two-times higher than that of C3 plants [13], and that WUE increases under drier conditions [14]. However, this method requires manual weighing and water supply, and is therefore labour-intensive. Dry-down experiments are widely used to evaluate whole-plant water use characteristics and growth responses to severe drought [15–17], while soil moisture can deplete rapidly, leading to plant death, which less likely to occur in the field. Therefore, the ideal approach would be maintaining sublethal drought and tracking water consumption under such conditions, which presents a significant challenge.

To address these challenges and enable high-throughput and continuous monitoring of plant water consumption, automated plant phenotyping systems that integrate pot weight measurement, irrigation, and other functions have been developed and deployed worldwide [18–20]. However, these platforms are currently limited in their availability due to the high cost of development, installation, and maintenance, and limited portability restricting their use to fixed locations.

We considered that these limitations could be overcome by utilizing the Arduino microcontroller platform. Arduino is an open-source system comprising the Arduino hardware board and the Arduino IDE software, which facilitates the rapid prototyping of diverse measurement systems. Notably, Arduino has been successfully employed in the development of automated irrigation systems for regulating soil moisture conditions [21] and a system for measuring CO2 and O2 fluxes from tree bark [22]. In this study, we aimed to develop a microcontroller-based system that integrates pot weighing, automated water supply, and real-time monitoring of plant water consumption via Wi-Fi by uploading data to Google spreadsheet while maintaining constant soil water conditions. Furthermore, since plants close stomata and reduce water consumption in response to abiotic stresses, including drought, waterlogging, and salinity [23–25], it may be possible to assess stress tolerance from measurements of water consumption, yet conventional manual measurements are time consuming and labor-intensive. Then we considered that our system could also be applied as a novel and easy method for evaluating stress tolerance and selecting stress-tolerant crop varieties [26].

The specific objectives of the present study were to (1) develop a low-cost microcontroller-based water control system and establish a method for estimating water consumption, (2) utilize the system for investigating the whole-plant water consumption and environmental responses of major crops such soybeans, rice, and maize, (3) evaluate the potential of the system for assessing crop stress tolerance.

Results

The present system enabled control of pot weights and calculation of cumulative water consumption

We completed a low-cost system (approximately 50,000 JPY or 300 USD) capable of managing the weight of up to 18 pots (Fig. 1, S1). The preliminary experiment demonstrated the system's ability to maintain pot weights under automated watering. Four control plants maintained the set weight for 7 days, while four drought-stressed plants decreased their weight until reaching the target weight and then stabilized (Fig. 2A). Calculations revealed increasing differences in cumulative and daily water consumption between treatments from 3 days after drought treatment (Fig. 2B, C). Discussion

In the present study, we established a robust method for estimating water consumption with the microcontroller-based water control system at low cost. While there was a similar system employing load cells and pumps to maintain constant pot weight [27], the need for a laptop computer to manage the system made it less cost-effective. We demonstrate that the present system can evaluate water use characteristics represented by cumulative and daily water consumption and WUE, and its applicability was confirmed not only in the environmentally controlled growth chambers but also in the glass houses under natural sunlight. Furthermore, the system was able to capture varietal differences in WUE and salt tolerance in rice, as well as responses to drought and waterlogging stress in soybean and maize. It is expected that the system will be used in the future for high-throughput evaluation of water use characteristics, leading to the selection and development of water-saving and stress-tolerant crop varieties.

Low-cost and high-throughput system can be developed by Arduino microcontroller

Key features of the present system include the ability to simultaneously measure water consumption in 18 pots while maintaining constant pot weight, real-time monitoring of pot weight via Wi-Fi, and the flexibility to be installed in various locations (Fig. 1). Compared to the large-scale phenotyping platforms that can control pot weight [19], the present system is simpler and more cost-effective. Data quality appears comparable to a similarly sized phenotyping system utilizing expensive data loggers [28]. Despite its low cost, the microcontroller-based system is considered sufficiently practical as it demonstrated the ability to control pot weights and evaluate cumulative and daily water consumptions in the long term (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7).

Strain gauge-based load cells, used for weight measurement, are susceptible to temperature changes. While this sensitivity might preclude their use in detecting minute weight fluctuations, water consumption of crops like rice, soybean, and maize is often at the several kilogram level within a month (Fig. S2), ensuring that the system provides sufficiently reliable water consumption estimates. Nevertheless, employing load cells with lower temperature dependence and higher precision would further enhance the temporal resolution and accuracy of water consumption measurements.

To estimate only plant transpiration, we subtracted the cumulative water consumption of unplanted pots, namely soil evaporation, from that of planted pots. However, plant growth gradually shades the soil surface, reducing direct sunlight exposure and reducing direct sunlight exposure and thus limiting soil temperature increases. Therefore, the soil evaporation rate of the unplanted pots might have been overestimated in the present study. To address this issue, using pots with lids or covering soil surface with plastic beads or aquarium gravel that prevent soil evaporation while allowing water supply could enable more accurate evaluation of water consumption at the whole-plant level (Fig. S1).

Different trends in water consumption at the leaf and whole-plant levels

In drought-rewatered plants, decrease and increase in whole-plant daily water consumption mirrored the trends in leaf-level gs (Fig. 3A, B). However, under control conditions, daily water consumption continued to increase as plants grow (Fig. 3A) while gs gradually declined (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, gs across all leaf positions at 23 DAS was higher in drought-rewatering plants (Fig. 3C), indicating that gs inadequately represented whole-plant water use dynamics and highlighting the importance of whole-plant assessment [29]. This discrepancy could be partly attributed to pot size limitations in control plants [30], emphasizing the importance of selecting appropriately sized pots for such experiments [31].

The system is useful for assessing various stress tolerances in different crops

Under salt stress conditions, the salt-sensitive rice cultivar Koshihikari showed significant reductions in leaf-level and whole-plant water consumption (Fig. 4A, C). In contrast, Daw Hawm, known for its high salinity tissue tolerance due to salt sequestration into vacuoles [32, 33], showed a less reduction in whole-plant water consumption (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that salt-tolerant cultivars maintain stomatal function and carbon assimilation under short-term salt stress, contributing to biomass stability (Fig. 4E). This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that salt-tolerant cultivars preserve higher photosynthetic activity and stomatal conductance under saline conditions [34, 35]. Consequently, the present system demonstrates potential utility for assessing salt stress tolerance and facilitating the selection of salt-tolerant cultivars.

The soybean cultivar Enrei, known for its low waterlogging tolerance [36], exhibited pronounced stomatal closure and reduced biomass when waterlogged, as successfully monitored by the present system (Fig. 5A, C). Notably, differences in leaf-level gs were detected only at 21 DAS (Fig. 5B), highlighting that non-continuous gas exchange measurements may not fully reflect whole-plant stress responses. Furthermore, the system revealed a greater difference in daily water consumption between control and waterlogged plants during cloudy or rainy days (Fig. 5A). This suggests that waterlogging-induced agricultural damage to soybeans, particularly during rainy season in Japan, results from stomatal closure and reduced photosynthesis, severely hindering growth [37]. Applying this system to diverse soybean lines could elucidate the physiological and molecular mechanisms underpinning secondary aerenchyma formation [38], ultimately aiding in the selection and development of waterlogging-tolerant varieties—a crucial step toward mitigating the adverse effects of climate change [39, 40].

Exploring varietal differences in water use characteristics and molecular mechanisms of drought response will be feasible

In Exp. 4, a month-long evaluation using our system confirmed the higher whole-plant water use efficiency (WUE) of japonica rice varieties compared to indica varieties (Fig. 6), consistent with previous research [41]. While extensive studies report higher leaf-level WUE (photosynthetic rate per transpiration rate or stomatal conductance) in japonica varieties compared to indica varieties [42], few studies have investigated whole-plant WUE of rice in the long term. This could be due to the labor-intensive nature of traditional pot weight management when studying water-demanding crops like rice. Our cost-effective system, capable of managing 18 pots, offers a potential solution for high-throughput WUE screening. By operating multiple systems in parallel, researchers can conduct large-scale experiments and overcom the time, cost, and labor limitations of conventional methods.

Exp. 5 demonstrated that the system incorporating a Wi-Fi microcontroller (ESP32C3) enabled real-time monitoring of pot weights for approximately one month while maintaining mild drought conditions (Fig. 7). Imposing and maintaining constant drought conditions at any timing allows the evaluation of plant drought response over long duration, offering a significant advantage over transient dry-down experiments. The Wi-Fi function further facilitates seamless data access obtained at remote experimental facilities, enabling rapid data collection and analysis for high throughput phenotyping.

Plants alter morphological traits such as shoot-to-root ratio in response to drought conditions (Fig. 7E) [43], preceded by changes in gene expression and metabolite profiles [44, 45]. Integration of real-time monitoring of plant water consumption with transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis will facilitate comprehensive understanding of the physiological and morphological changes and underlying molecular mechanisms associated with progressive drought stress. Including Arabidopsis in addition to crop species as research subjects offer an even broader perspective on these mechanisms.

Conclusion

We developed a novel and cost-effective microcontroller-based system for estimating crop water consumption. Extensive validation in both controlled growth chambers and environmentally fluctuating greenhouses demonstrated the system's capacity to track water use dynamics in major crops subjected to diverse stresses, including drought, rewatering, waterlogging, and salinity. Our experiments successfully revealed distinct responses of soybean to drought and waterlogging, varietal differences in salt tolerance in rice, and long-term WUE in rice and maize. The system's flexibility in deployment, cost-effectiveness, and integration of Wi-Fi connectivity for real-time monitoring of pot weight make it a valuable tool for high-throughput phenotyping. This has significant potential for accelerating the selection and development of water-saving and climate-resilient crop varieties to address challenges in global food security.

Four core cables from a load cell within a commercially available digital scale (CX-386, Keegoo, China; 5 kg capacity, 21.8 × 16.3 × 4 cm) or a separate load cell (SC133, 5 kg capacity, SENSORCON, China) were connected to an amplifier module (HX711, M5Stack, China) (Fig. 1B). For experiments utilizing SC133 load cells, custom scales were constructed using white acrylic plates (16 × 16 × 4 cm, 5 mm thickness). The calibration coefficient was determined with 3 kg of reference standard for each load cell. To account for evaporation from the soil, pots without plants (unplanted pots) were also prepared.

As shown in the system flowchart (Fig. S3), the pot weight was measured by the microcontroller every 30 s, and when the weight of each pot dropped below the set value, the water pump, which was connected to the board via digital I/O ports, in the water reservoir was driven by a command from the Arduino Mega microcontroller to maintain a preset constant pot weight by supplying water through a silicone tube. This system activates the pumps for a set duration (typically 5 s) once the pots reach their target weight., and the volume of water delivered during this activation depends on the length of the silicone tubing and the length and diameter of the pipes. The measurement data was recorded on an SD card at 1- or 5-min intervals. In Experiment 5, the data was uploaded to a Google spreadsheet via Wi-Fi at 5-min intervals by the ESP32C3 microcontroller. The cumulative evapotranspiration from the plant and soil surface was calculated by integrating the decrease in each pot weight, and the cumulative evapotranspiration and daily water consumption by the plant were calculated by subtracting the cumulative evapotranspiration of the unplanted pots.

Pre-experiment to test the validity of the micro-controller system

A pilot test of the present Arduino-based system was conducted with a C4 crop finger millet (Eleusine coracana). Eight plants were grown according to Ozeki et al. [11] in a growth room under a 12-h light/dark cycle with a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 300 μmol m−2 s−1 provided by LED light source. At 30 days after sown (DAS), four plants were put together in a container and placed on the system, and the other four plants were also put together in another container and placed on the system. Each container was either maintained at a target weight equivalent to a field water capacity (FWC) of 100% (control) or subjected to constant drought conditions with a target weight equivalent to a FWC of 50% for 7 days. While FWC of 100% is wet and may not be optimal for plant growth, we considered it acceptable for comparison with the dry conditions of FWC 50%. The cumulative water consumption and daily water consumption per plant were measured for 7 days. Unplanted pots were not prepared in this pilot test.

Experiment 1

Response of water use of soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr. “Enrei”) to drought and subsequent rewatering was evaluated. Soybean seeds were treated with Cruiser MAXX (Syngenta Japan) and sown in 1000 mL plastic pots without drainage holes filled with commercial soil (Kumiai Nippi, JA, Japan) containing fertilizer. Eight plants were grown in a phytotron under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with a PPFD of 250 μmol m−2 s−1 provided by fluorescent lamps, and day/night temperatures of 30/25 °C, respectively. Eight plants and two unplanted pots were placed on the system and grown for 13 days under control conditions with a FWC of 100%. 13 DAS, four plants and one unplanted pot were subjected to drought stress by withholding water for 5 days, followed by rewatering, returning to FWC of 100%, for 5 days. The stomatal conductance (gs) of the first leaf was measured using a porometer (LI-600, LICOR, Lincoln, NE, USA) from 13 DAS at the beginning of drought to 23 DAS at the end of the rewatering period. At 23 DAS, gs was measured on all leaves from fully expanded upper to lower leaves. After the gs measurements, plants were harvested and dried to obtain above-ground biomass.

Experiment 2

To identify the difference in salt tolerance, water use of salt- sensitive rice cultivar (Oryza sativa L, “Nipponbare”) and salt-tolerant cultivar (“Daw Hawm”) in response to salinity stress was evaluated. Rice seeds were germinated and transplanted in 1/10000 a Wagner pots (about 1.6L in volume) without drainage holes filled with 1.2 kg of sandy loam soil and 0.9 g of chemical fertilizer (N:P:K = 14:14:14, Itochu corporation, Japan). For each cultivar, eight plants were grown in the phytotron under the same conditions as the Exp 1 for 30 days. At 30 days after transplanting, four plants were subjected to salinity stress by replacing water with 0.4%NaCl solution (68 mM NaCl) for each cultivar. Eight pots and two unplanted pots were paced on the system from 2 to 7 days and from 1 to 5 days after the salt treatment in Koshihikari and Daw Hawm, respectively. During the salt treatment, tap water and 0.4%NaCl solution were supplied to control plants and stressed plants, respectively. In Koshihikari, gs of the fully expanded upper leaf was measured using the porometer. gs measurements could not be performed in Daw Hawm due to the narrow leaf width. Above-ground parts of the plants were harvested and dried after the salt treatment to obtain shoot biomass.

Experiment 3

To investigate when and how much waterlogging causes growth reduction in soybean (G. max (L.) Merr. “Enrei”), response of water use to waterlogging was evaluated. Soybean seeds were treated with Cruiser MAXX and sown in 1000 mL plastic pots without drainage holes filled with commercial soil containing fertilizer (Kumiai Nippi Engeibaido No. 1, Nihon Hiryo, Tokyo, Japan). Eight pots and two unplanted pots were placed on the system and grown under 100% FWC in a temperature-controlled glass house for 11 days, and four plants and one unplanted pot were subjected to waterlogging treatment by setting the pot weight equivalent to 120% FWC for 10 days. gs of the fully expanded upper leaf was measured using the porometer at 14, 17, 19, and 21 DAS. Plants were harvested and dried at 21 DAS to obtain whole-plant biomass. Average temperature was 31.1ºC during the growth period, and daily solar radiation (MJ m‒2 d‒1) was obtained from the Nagoya Meteorological Observatory.

Experiment 4

The varietal difference in water use between indica (“IR8”) and japonica (“Nipponbare”) variety was evaluated. Rice seeds were germinated and transplanted in 1/10000 a Wagner pots without drainage holes filled with sandy soil and 2 g of controlled-release nitrogen fertilizer (LP40, JCAM Agri, Tokyo, Japan) and 2 g of inorganic fertilizer containing phosphorous and potassium (PK40, Onoda chemical, Tokyo, Japan). Plants were grown under flooded conditions in a temperature-uncontrolled glass house. After thinning, four Nipponbare, four IR8, and one unplanted pot were placed on the system and grown for 28 days. At 28 days after thinning (DATh), plants were harvested, divided into leaves, stems, and roots, and dried to obtain whole-plant biomass and shoot to root ratio. Average temperature was 28.9ºC during the growth period, and daily solar radiation (MJ m‒2 d‒1) was obtained from the Nagoya Meteorological Observatory.

Experiment 5

Response of water use of maize (Zea mays (L.) “Honey bantam”) to long-term drought was evaluated. Maize seeds were sown in 2000 mL plastic pots without drainage holes filled with the commercial soil containing fertilizer (Kumiai Nippi Engeibaido No. 1). After thinning, eight pots and two unplanted pots were placed on the system and grown in the temperature-controlled glass house for 5 days under control conditions with a FWC of 100%. From 5 DATh, four plants and one unplanted pot were subjected to drought stress by setting the pot weight equivalent to 60% FWC. At 26 DATh, plants were harvested, divided into leaves, stems, and roots, and dried to obtain whole-plant biomass and shoot to root ratio. Average temperature was 31.37ºC during the growth period, and daily solar radiation (MJ m‒2 d‒1) was obtained from the Nagoya Meteorological Observatory.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed using R (http://www.r-project.org/). Significant differences in gs, biomass, and WUE between control and stressed plants were determined by Student’s t test. Significant differences in daily water consumption between control and stressed plants were not determined in Exp. 1, 4, and 5, due to large difference in plant size.
