A rare cause of abdominal pain: spontaneous pneumomediastinum with subcutaneous emphysema
Authors: Ouiam Taibi, Ismail Neftah, Rania Bouanane, Yahya El Harras, Ittimade Nassar, Kaoutar Imrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous mediastinal emphysema is rare and is usually associated with a sudden increase in alveolar pressure. The most common presenting symptom is acute chest pain. We report the case of a young man who presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain, which was ultimately diagnosed as spontaneous pneumomediastinum associated with cervical emphysema.Keywords: spontaneous pneumomediastinum, emphysema, abdominal pain

Introduction

Pneumomediastinum is defined as the presence of air in the mediastinum, most commonly secondary to trauma (accidental, assault-related, or iatrogenic). Rarely, it occurs without an identifiable cause, known as spontaneous pneumomediastinum, typically affecting young adults and resulting from factors that increase intrathoracic pressure [1]. The prognosis is usually favorable, with the most common presenting symptom being the acute onset of chest pain, and most patients respond well to conservative management [2].

We report the case of a young man with no significant past medical history who presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain. Further investigations revealed a spontaneous pneumothorax associated with cervical emphysema. The atypical clinical presentation, together with the lack of triggering factors, makes this case a rare manifestation of an otherwise uncommon condition.

Case report

A 28-year-old male, a chronic smoker, was admitted to the emergency department with severe acute abdominal pain, most pronounced in the epigastric region. His medical history was unremarkable, with no prior trauma or known respiratory or gastrointestinal diseases. On admission, he was conscious and hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed no abnormalities. Pulmonary auscultation was normal, and there were no signs of respiratory distress.

Given the sudden onset of severe abdominal pain in a smoker, an abdominal CT scan was performed with an initial suspicion of a perforated ulcer. No abdominal abnormalities, including pneumoperitoneum, were identified. However, the scan revealed soft-tissue emphysema of the dorsal and lumbar regions (Fig. 1). In the absence of an explanation for this finding, a thoracic CT scan was subsequently performed to complete the evaluation. This examination revealed a pneumomediastinum with subcutaneous emphysema extending to the cervical and thoracic soft tissues (Fig. 2). Despite the patient’s smoking history, no radiological evidence of pulmonary emphysema was observed on chest CT. Assessment of the trachea and its main bronchi, the esophagus, larynx, heart, and pericardium revealed no abnormalities. No osseous abnormalities were identified.

No apparent cause was identified to explain the patient’s pneumomediastinum and subcutaneous emphysema. Specifically, there was no history of recent trauma, invasive procedures, vigorous physical exertion, vomiting, coughing, or illicit drug use that could account for these findings. In the absence of any identifiable cause, the condition was classified as spontaneous pneumomediastinum associated with subcutaneous emphysema.

The patient was kept under observation for 24 h and received analgesic treatment, with no additional interventions. Given the clinical improvement, he was subsequently discharged. A follow-up chest X-ray performed one month later demonstrated complete resolution of the pneumomediastinum and subcutaneous emphysema.

Discussion

Hamman’s syndrome, also known as Macklin syndrome, is a rare condition first described in 1939 by Hamman [3]. It involves the spontaneous development of pneumomediastinum along with subcutaneous emphysema. The most commonly reported hypothesis in the literature is an increase in endobronchial pressure against a closed glottis (Valsalva maneuvers) [4]. This pressure rise is thought to cause alveolar rupture, allowing air to dissect through the interstitial spaces and track toward the mediastinum along the peribronchovascular sheaths. The air may then spread into the subcutaneous tissues and deep cervical spaces.

Most cases have been observed in women during the second stage of labor [5] and in young patients using inhaled medications [6]. It can sometimes result from intense coughing, vomiting, defecation, physical exertion, or the inhalation of recreational drugs [4]. In rare cases, it may also occur without any identifiable precipitating factor, as was the case in our patient.

The most frequent symptoms and signs are chest and neck pain, dyspnea, subcutaneous emphysema, hypotension, dysphagia, and cough. The particularity in our case is that the patient presented with abdominal pain without any other respiratory symptoms.

The diagnosis is based on chest radiography, which allows visualization of the pneumomediastinum and, in some cases, subcutaneous emphysema. However, ruling out other, more common causes of pneumomediastinum is essential to establish the diagnosis.

Chest computed tomography is indicated when an associated bronchial or esophageal injury is suspected and typically demonstrates air dissecting along the anatomical structures of the mediastinum and neck.

The differential diagnosis of Hamman’s syndrome mainly includes secondary causes of pneumomediastinum, which must be ruled out first, such as esophageal rupture (Boerhaave syndrome), thoracic trauma, and iatrogenic causes, including endoscopy or mechanical ventilation [1].

The natural course of spontaneous pneumomediastinum is typically benign, with resolution occurring within 48 to 96 h through direct absorption of air into the bloodstream. Management is generally conservative and symptomatic. Rarely, cases of compressive pneumomediastinum have been reported [7], presenting with cardiac tamponade requiring surgical drainage. Recurrences are exceedingly uncommon [1, 8].
