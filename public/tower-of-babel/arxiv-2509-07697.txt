Mode-coupling theory of the glass transition for a liquid in a periodic potential
Abolfazl Ahmadirahmat, Michele Caraglio, Vincent Krakoviack, Thomas Franosch

Abstract
We derive a microscopic theory for the structural dynamics in the vicinity of the glass transition for a liquid exposed to a one-dimensional periodic potential. The periodic potential breaks translational invariance, in particular, the density exhibits a periodic modulation. Using techniques familiar from solid-state theory, we define generalized intermediate scattering functions from fluctuating densities in wave-vector space. Exact equations of motion are derived within the Mori-Zwanzig projection-operator formalism reflecting the residual lattice symmetries. Due to the lack of rotational symmetry it is necessary to split the currents into components parallel and perpendicular to the modulation. We provide a closure of the equations in terms of a mode-coupling approximation for the force kernel. The theory reflects the usual analytic properties of correlation functions and encodes all phenomena known for mode-coupling theories. We prove that the theory reduces to the conventional mode-coupling theory in the case of vanishing amplitude of the modulation.



Published: Physical Review E 112, 015405 (2025)

I
Introduction

In many liquids the transition to a crystal can be circumvented by rapid cooling or compression leading to a metastable supercooled state where transport coefficients such as the viscosity or diffusion coefficients vary by orders of magnitude upon small changes of temperature or density
Götze 2009
. Once the structural relaxation time becomes macroscopic a glassy state is reached characterized by a structural arrest concomitant with the emergence of an amorphous elastic solid. The slowing down of transport is accompanied by a plethora of striking phenomena, such as the time-temperature superposition principle, the universal
\beta
-relaxation window, as well as the emergence of a two-time fractal. Many of the features have been rationalized within the mode-coupling theory of the glass transition (MCT)
Götze 2009
;
Janssen 2018
, a microscopic theory for the directly measurable intermediate scattering functions.
Although MCT is considered as incomplete since it misses certain relevant features, such as the ergodicity restoring processes, it serves as a benchmark for various complementary approaches.

For a better understanding of the merits and limitations of the MCT approximation, the theory has been explored in multiple directions by adding more experimentally accessible control parameters, going beyond simple one-component systems as well as considering situations where the liquid does not display all symmetries of a bulk liquid. For example,
mixing polymers and colloids introduces an effective short-range attraction leading to competing mechanisms for forming a glass, which results in a complex nonequilibrium state diagram characterized by higher-order singularities and reentrant behavior

Bergenholtz and Fuchs 1999
;
Fabbian et al. 1999
;
Dawson et al. 2000
;
Pham et al. 2002
.
Confining the liquid to disordered pores introduces the packing fraction of the quenched matrix as additional control parameter and yields nontrivial transition scenarios, including reentrant and higher-order singularities

Krakoviack 2005
;
*Krakoviack:PRE_75:2007
;
*Krakoviack:PRE_79:2009
;
Kurzidim et al. 2009
;
Kim et al. 2009
.
Reentrant behavior also emerges upon confining simple liquids to a slit due to the interplay of layering and local packing
Lang et al. 2010
;
Lang et al. 2012
;
Mandal et al. 2014
;
Jung et al. 2020a
;
Schrack et al. 2020
. In these confined systems the dynamics becomes anisotropic which suggested splitting the currents into components parallel and perpendicular to the walls yielding a mathematical structure somewhat different from the conventional MCT. However, the main mathematical features of the theory, such as the analytic properties of the solutions
Götze and Sjögren 1995
;
Franosch and Voigtmann 2002
;
Lang et al. 2013
and
the emergence of the
\beta
-scaling law
Jung et al. 2020b
can still be demonstrated. Splitting currents was also necessary for the structural dynamics to be independent of kinetic parameters for
molecules with additional orientational degrees of freedom
Franosch et al. 1997
;
Götze et al. 2000
;
Schilling and Scheidsteger 1997
;
Kämmerer et al. 1998a
;
*Kaemmerer:PRE58:1998a
. Beyond equilibrium dynamics, MCT has been tested successfully also for systems driven by shear
Fuchs and Cates 2002
;
Brader et al. 2007
;
Brader et al. 2008
, by active rheology
Gazuz et al. 2009
;
Şenbil et al. 2019
;
Gruber et al. 2020
, for driven granular gases
Kranz et al. 2010
;
Kranz et al. 2018
;
Kranz et al. 2020
,
or for active particles

Szamel et al. 2015
;
Liluashvili et al. 2017
;
Reichert et al. 2021
;
Debets and Janssen 2022
. To investigate the quality of the factorization,
generalized-mode-coupling theory
Mayer et al. 2006
;
Janssen and Reichman 2015
;
Luo and Janssen 2020
;
Luo et al. 2022

has been developed to postpone the factorization approximation to higher levels with the hope to achieve better quantitative agreement with simulations.

In this work, we continue in this direction
by exposing the liquid to a periodic external potential. For the case of colloids in suspensions such external potentials can be conveniently created using laser beams as has been introduced in the pioneering works by Ashkin on optical tweezers
Ashkin 1970
;
Ashkin 1980
;
Ashkin 1997
. Since then the use of optical fields has opened the possibility to control mesosized particles on the micrometer scale

Dholakia and Čižmár 2011
;
Maragò et al. 2013
.
Therefore colloidal suspensions are ideally suited to explore the material properties in strong external fields – strong means here way beyond the linear response regime – to push our understanding of condensed matter problems to frontiers that can be hardly reached in molecular systems.
The colloid-light interaction is approximately conservative, i.e. it can be well represented by an external potential proportional to the light intensity, while the optical scattering and absorption is often negligible
Grier 2003
;
Maragò et al. 2013
. The simplest non-trivial potential landscape then consists of a sinusoidal laser intensity which can be achieved experimentally by interference of two coherent laser beams
Smith et al. 1981
;
Chowdhury et al. 1985
. The relative incident angle can be varied to tune the wavelength of the potential modulation, while the intensity determines their amplitude.

Here, we address the structural dynamics of a liquid in such a periodic potential relying on the mode-coupling approach. Although our main application will be experimental realizations on colloidal monolayers
Dalle-Ferrier et al. 2011
;
Jenkins and Egelhaaf 2008
;
Capellmann et al. 2018
,
we shall formulate the mode-coupling theory for a
d
-dimensional system and a one-dimensional modulation. Also with a little more effort, our approach can be extended to arbitrary periodic potentials.
Although we are ultimately interested in colloidal dynamics we formulate the theory for Newtonian dynamics first, anticipating that the slow structural dynamics in the vicinity of the glass transition is independent of the details of the underlying dynamics
Götze 2009
;
Franosch et al. 1998
. The current work focuses on the formal derivation of the mode-coupling equations, consequences for the nonequilibrium state diagram and multiple reentrant behavior are elaborated for the case of a monolayer of hard disks in the companion paper
Ahmadirahmat et al. 2025
.

This work is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
we present the underlying microscopic model, introduce the relevant quantities of interest reflecting the symmetries of the set-up, and formulate appropriately generalized intermediate scattering functions (ISF). In Sec.
III
we employ the Mori-Zwanzig projection-operator formalism to derive formally exact equations of motion for the ISF in terms of suitable memory kernels. We account for the induced breaking of isotropy by splitting the currents into components parallel and perpendicular to the modulation resulting in generalized force kernels. In Sec.
IV
we use a mode-coupling approach to derive an approximate expression of the force kernels in terms of bilinear combinations of the ISF itself. Various technical derivations are deferred to the appendices.
For reference, in Sec.
V
we specialize the MCT equation to the long-time limits of the ISF. In Section
VI
we show that the theory reduces to the standard MCT of the glass transition in the case of vanishing modulation. A summary and concluding remarks are provided
in Sec.
VII
.

II
Model and observable quantities

II.1
Model set-up

We consider a simple
d
-dimensional liquid of
N
identical classical particles enclosed
in a hypercube of volume
V=L^{d}
in the presence of an external periodic potential. The thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty,V\to\infty
with fixed number density
n_{0}=N/V
will be anticipated.

The collection of positions and momenta of the particles are denoted by

\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N}=(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N})\in V^{N}
and
\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N}=(\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
.
The dynamics of the positions and momenta are prescribed by Hamilton’s equations driven by the Hamilton function

\mathcal{H}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N},\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}^{2}}{2m}+V(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N})+U(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N}),

(1)

where
m
denotes the mass of the particles.
Here the mutual interaction between the particles is assumed to be pairwise additive

V(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\mathcal{V}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{m}|),

(2)

where the pair potential
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)
depends only on the magnitude of the separation of the pair,
such that linear momentum and angular momentum is preserved.
Additionally, a periodic modulation is imposed by the external potential

U(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}(z_{n}),

(3)

where the single-particle potential
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)
acts only on the
z
-coordinates of the particles and
is assumed to be periodic with period
a
:

\mathcal{U}(z)=\mathcal{U}(z+a)
(with
L/a\in\mathbb{N}
). Generally we write for a spatial coordinate
\bm{\mathrm{x}}=(\bm{\mathrm{r}},z)
where
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
is the component perpendicular to the modulation and
z
along the modulation.

Then all lattice-periodic function can be expanded in terms of suitably adapted Fourier modes, e.g. the external potential

\mathcal{U}(z)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{U}_{\mu}\exp(-iQ_{\mu}z),

(4)

where the one-dimensional wavevectors are discrete
Q_{\mu}=2\pi\mu/a,\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
. The associated Fourier coefficients are obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{a}\mathcal{U}(z)\exp(iQ_{\mu}z)\mathrm{d}z.

(5)

For real
\mathcal{U}(z)
in real space,
\mathcal{U}_{\mu}^{*}=\mathcal{U}_{-\mu}\in\mathbb{C}
. For symmetric functions
\mathcal{U}(z)=\mathcal{U}(-z)
the Fourier coefficients additionally fulfill
\mathcal{U}_{\mu}=\mathcal{U}_{-\mu}
in particular
\mathcal{U}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
.

By the imposed external potential the liquid will no longer be translationally invariant nor isotropic. However, the Hamilton function remains invariant under permutations of the particles, a time shift, and time reversal. Furthermore it is translationally invariant by a uniform shift of the positions perpendicular to the modulation and invariant under a simultaneous reflection of all spatial coordinates on a plane that includes the direction of the modulation.
For
d\geq 3
a rotation
\mathcal{R}_{z}
leaving the direction of modulation fixed is also a symmetry transform of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N},\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N})=\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R}_{z}\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N},\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N})
. Last, by discrete symmetry
\mathcal{H}
remains unchanged by a uniform shift of all positions
by integer multiples of the primitive vector
\bm{\mathrm{a}}=a\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}
. Together with the continuous translational symmetry perpendicular to the modulation, the Hamilton function is invariant under uniform shifts by vectors

\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\Lambda:=\{\bm{\mathrm{r}}+n\bm{\mathrm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\mathrm{r}}\perp\bm{\mathrm{a}},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
of the degenerate Bravais lattice
\Lambda
. For symmetric potentials,
\mathcal{H}
also remains invariant under simultaneous reflection of all coordinates
(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{n},z_{n})\mapsto(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{n},-z_{n})
along the modulation. Together with the reflection symmetries for planes including the direction of modulation this is equivalent to spatial inversion symmetry
\mathcal{H}(-\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N},\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N})=\mathcal{H}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{N},\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{N})
. We assume that the liquid reflects all these symmetries, in particular, there is no spontaneous symmetry breaking to a crystalline or chiral phase, or quasi long-range order in the positions or orientations.

II.2
Observables

Here we are concerned with the dynamics of fluctuating densities

\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta[\bm{\mathrm{x}}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)],

(6)

where
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{l}(t)
denotes the position of particle
l
at time
t
.
We also define the equilibrium density

n(z):=\langle\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)\rangle,

(7)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
indicates canonical averaging. By the translational symmetry of the Bravais lattice and in the liquid phase it
depends only on
z
and is lattice-periodic:
n(z)=n(z+a)
. In particular it can be represented as in Eq.(
4
) by its associated Fourier components
n_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C},\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We shall also need the local volume,
v(z):=1/n(z)
. Then the convolution theorem yields the relation for the Fourier coefficients

\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}}n_{\mu-\kappa}\,v_{\kappa-\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu},

(8)

with the Kronecker symbol
\delta_{\mu\nu}=1
if
\mu=\nu
and 0 otherwise. Since the density and local volume in real space are non-negative
n(z)\geq 0,v(z)\geq 0
, one readily checks that the matrices
n_{\mu,\nu}:=n_{\mu-\nu},v_{\mu,\nu}:=v_{\mu-\nu}
are positive semidefinite.

We then introduce fluctuations around the mean
\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t):=\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)-n(z)
and define the density-density correlation function

G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t):=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\langle\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},0)\rangle,

(9)

also referred to as van Hove function
Hansen and McDonald 2006
.
By translational and rotational symmetry perpendicular to the modulation, the van Hove function
does not depend separately on
\bm{\mathrm{x}}=(\bm{\mathrm{r}},z),\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}=(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},z^{\prime})
, rather it depends only on the absolute distance of the perpendicular components
|\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}|
but explicitly on both parallel components
z,z^{\prime}
.
By time-reversal symmetry and time-translational symmetry
it is an even function of time and symmetric with respect to interchanging the positions

G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t)=G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},-t)=G(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{x}},t).

(10)

Furthermore,
a common shift of the positions by a vector
\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\Lambda
in the Bravais lattice leaves the van Hove function invariant

G(\bm{\mathrm{x}}+\bm{\mathrm{R}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathrm{R}},t)=G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t).

(11)

For a symmetric potential
G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t)
remains also invariant under simultaneous reflection
(\bm{\mathrm{r}},z)\mapsto(\bm{\mathrm{r}},-z),(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},z^{\prime})\mapsto(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},-z^{\prime})
.

Instead of relying on real space we shall formulate the theory in wave-vector space. For a finite volume we need to introduce discrete Fourier transforms, a summary can be found in Appendix
A
. Then the wave vectors
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
compatible with the finite volume
V
are discrete in
\lambda^{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/L)^{d}\}
.
We also introduce the reciprocal lattice
\Lambda^{*}
associated with the modulation
by requiring
\exp(i\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}})=1
for all
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}\in\Lambda^{*},\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\Lambda
which implies

\Lambda^{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}=(2\pi\mu/a)\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}:\mu\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subset\lambda^{*}
. Any wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}
can then be uniquely decomposed
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
such that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}:=\{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\lambda^{*}:-\pi/a<\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}\leq\pi/a\}
is in the first Brillouin zone (BZ) and a reciprocal lattice vector
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}\in\Lambda^{*}
. To simplify, we use the compact notation
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}:=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
.
We define the fluctuating density to such a wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu},t)=

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t):=\int_{V}\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)e^{i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)}-Vn_{\mu}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},0}.

(12)

Thus
\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
corresponds to the density modulation to wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
and we use the notation
\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu},t):=\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
interchangeably.
We refer to Greek letters
\mu,\nu,\ldots
as
mode indices
.

Let us consider the autocorrelation function
N^{-1}\langle\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)^{*}\delta\rho_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},0)\rangle
with
\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\text{BZ}
,
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
. Shifting all particle positions by a lattice vector
\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\Lambda

induces the mapping
\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)\mapsto\exp(i\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}})\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
.
Since this symmetry transform has to leave the correlation function invariant, it is nonzero only for
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}
.
We therefore define a properly generalized intermediate scattering function (ISF)

S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t):=\frac{1}{N}\langle\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)^{*}\delta\rho_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},0)\rangle,

(13)

in particular, the initial values
S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}):=S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t=0)
will be referred to as generalized static structure factors.
The observation that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
has to be identical in both fluctuating densities is a manifestation of conservation of crystal momentum. For
\mu=\nu
we recover the conventional intermediate scattering function to wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}
. The novelty of the modulation is to explicitly allow for contributions
\mu\neq\nu
such that the wave vectors differ by a reciprocal lattice vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\nu}\in\Lambda^{*}
.

By time inversion symmetry the ISF is even in time and by time-translation invariance it is a Hermitian matrix

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},-t)=S_{\nu\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)^{*}.

(14)

Moreover for every
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
the ISF
S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
is a
matrix-valued correlation function

with corresponding analytic properties
Gesztesy and Tsekanovskii 2000
;
Lang et al. 2013
since every contraction
\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}}y_{\mu}^{*}S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)y_{\nu}
for arbitrary
y_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}
is merely the autocorrelation function of
\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}y_{\mu}\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
. In particular, the generalized static structure factor
S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
is a positive semi-definite matrix.

For
d\geq 3
a rotation
\mathcal{R}_{z}
leaving the axis of the modulation invariant is a symmetry of the ISF
S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=S_{\mu\nu}(\mathcal{R}_{z}\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
. Furthermore, reflecting
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
at any plane containing the direction of modulation leaves the ISF invariant, in particular, in 2D the wave vectors
\bm{\mathrm{q}}=(q_{x},q_{z})
and
(-q_{x},q_{z})
yield the same ISF.
For symmetric potentials spatial-inversion symmetry implies

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)^{*}=S_{-\mu,-\nu}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}},t),

(15)

i.e. the matrices are real symmetric and invariant under a simultaneous change of sign of the mode indices.

The respective inverse transformation for the fluctuating densities reads (see Appendix
A
)

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)\exp[-\text{i}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}],

(16)

and one readily checks that the ISF is obtained from the van Hove correlation function by spatial Fourier transform

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\!\int_{V}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}\,G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}}e^{i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\nu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}},

(17)

and reversely

\displaystyle G(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime},t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)

\displaystyle\times e^{i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}}e^{-i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\nu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\prime}}.

(18)

II.3
Currents and the continuity equation

In this subsection we introduce the continuity equation for the particle density and define the associated currents. Their definition depends on the underlying dynamics. Although we have as main application a colloidal monolayer in mind, we formulate the theory here for Newtonian dynamics to keep the derivation of the subsequent mode-coupling theory as simple as possible.

From Hamilton’s equations of motion we derive
the continuity equation in real space for the particle density

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)+\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{j}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=0,

(19)

with the microscopic current

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{j}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t)}{m}\delta[\bm{\mathrm{x}}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)].

(20)

In the spatial Fourier domain the current becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{j}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu},t)=\bm{\mathrm{j}}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t):=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t)}{m}\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)].

(21)

The continuity equation in terms of these variables then reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)

\displaystyle=i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{j}}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t).

(22)

The breaking of rotational symmetry due to the periodic modulation suggests splitting the wavevector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
into a component parallel to the modulation
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}^{\parallel}:=(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z})\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}
and a perpendicular one
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}^{\perp}:=\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}^{\parallel}
. Note that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}^{\parallel}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\parallel}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
and
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\perp}
such that the direction of the associated unit vectors
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}_{\mu}^{\parallel}=\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\parallel}/q^{\parallel}=\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z},\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\perp}/q^{\perp}
depends only on the direction
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
. The currents therefore naturally split into

\displaystyle j_{\mu}^{\parallel}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t)}{m}\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)],

(23a)

\displaystyle j_{\mu}^{\perp}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t)}{m}\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)].

(23b)

We can unify the notation to

\displaystyle j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}

\displaystyle b^{\alpha}[\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t),\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n}(t)]

\displaystyle\times\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}(t)],

(24)

where the selector
b^{\alpha}(x,z):=x\delta_{\alpha,\perp}+z\delta_{\alpha,\parallel}
has been introduced.
We shall use Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,..
to indicate the relaxation channel and refer to these as
channel indices
.
The continuity equation then reads in terms of these variables

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)

\displaystyle=i(q^{\parallel}+Q_{\mu})j^{\parallel}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)+iq^{\perp}j^{\perp}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)

\displaystyle=i\sum_{\alpha=\parallel,\perp}(q+Q_{\mu})^{\alpha}j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t).

(25)

Here the superscript
\alpha=\parallel,\perp
refers to the component parallel, resp. perpendicular to the modulation.

We shall also need the correlation function of the currents at equal times

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\frac{1}{N}\langle{j}_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},0)^{*}j_{\nu}^{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},0)\rangle.

(26)

A simple calculation yields (see Appendix
B
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=v_{\text{th}}^{2}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\frac{n_{\nu-\mu}}{n_{0}},

(27)

with the thermal velocity
v_{\text{th}}=\sqrt{k_{B}T/m}
and
k_{B}T
the thermal energy.
The inverse matrix is then found using the convolution relation, Eq. (
8
),

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{v_{\text{th}}^{2}}n_{0}\delta^{\alpha\beta}v_{\nu-\mu}.

(28)

III
Mori-Zwanzig equations of motion

We use the Mori-Zwanzig projection operator formalism
Hansen and McDonald 2006
;
Götze 2009
to derive formally exact equations of motion for
the generalized intermediate scattering functions. In Hamiltonian mechanics the time evolution of an observable
A(t)\equiv A(\bm{\mathrm{x}}(t),\bm{\mathrm{p}}(t))
is driven by the canonical equations
of motion
\mathrm{d}A(t)/\mathrm{d}t=\{A(t),\mathcal{H}\}
where
\{\,.,.\}
denotes the Poisson bracket.
It is convenient to introduce the Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
via
i\mathcal{L}=\{\,.\,,\mathcal{H}\}
.
The formal solution then is
A(t)=\exp(i\mathcal{L}t)A
where we adopt the convention that if no time argument is provided, the observable is evaluated at time
t=0
.

The set of fluctuating observables
\delta A:=A-\langle A\rangle
naturally acquires the structure of a Hilbert space upon defining the Kubo scalar product
\langle A|B\rangle:=\langle\delta A^{*}\delta B\rangle
. One readily shows that the Liouville operator is Hermitian with respect to the Kubo scalar product
\langle A|\mathcal{L}B\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}A|B\rangle
. Time-dependent correlation functions can therefore be represented as matrix elements
\langle\delta A(t)^{*}\delta B\rangle=\langle A|\mathcal{R}(t)|B\rangle
of the backwards-time evolution operator
\mathcal{R}(t)=\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t)
.

The starting point of the Zwanzig-Mori formalism relies on the operator identity (see e.g.
Lang et al. 2012
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)\mathcal{P}+i\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{R}(t)\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}e^{-i\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}(t-t^{\prime})}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{R}(t^{\prime})\mathcal{P}=0,

(29)

valid for any orthogonal projector

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{P}^{2}
and
\mathcal{Q}=1-\mathcal{P}
its orthogonal complement.

The derivation of the equations of motion (e.o.m.) largely parallels the one for the slit geometry
Lang et al. 2012
.
First we choose the projection operator on the densities

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rho}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}|\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]_{\mu\nu}\langle\rho_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|,

(30)

where we introduced the matrix notation
[\mathbf{S}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]_{\mu\nu}=S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
. One readily checks that it fulfills the properties of an orthogonal projector. Then sandwiching the operator identity between bras and kets consisting of density modes
one finds the e.o.m.

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)+

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}K_{\mu\kappa}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]_{\kappa\lambda}S_{\lambda\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0.

(31)

To simplify notation, we switch to a matrix notation and suppress the dependence on the wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
in the equation as it is unchanged throughout the equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{S}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{K}(t-t^{\prime})\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{S}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0.

(32)

The microscopic expression for the memory kernel
[\mathbf{K}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)]_{\mu\nu}=K_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
is provided by

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\frac{1}{N}\langle\mathcal{L}\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|e^{-i\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}t}|\mathcal{L}\rho_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle.

(33)

In the jargon of the Mori-Zwanzig projection-operator formalism the operator
\exp(-i\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}t)
drives the reduced (backwards) time evolution with respect to the projector
\mathcal{P}_{\rho}
.

Inserting the particle-conservation law, Eq. (
II.3
), we find that the current kernel naturally splits

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=\parallel,\perp}(q+Q_{\mu})^{\alpha}\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)(q+Q_{\nu})^{\beta},

(34)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\frac{1}{N}\langle j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|e^{-i\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}t}|j_{\nu}^{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle.

(35)

Note that the initial value reduces to the static current correlation function
\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t=0)=\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
.

Next we derive an exact equation of motion for this current kernel. We introduce the projector on the currents

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{j}=N^{-1}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=\parallel,\perp}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}|j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle[\boldmath{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}\langle j_{\nu}^{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|.

(36)

Note that the projection operators commute
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{\rho}\mathcal{P}_{j}=\mathcal{P}_{j}\mathcal{P}_{\rho}
. Sandwiching the operator identity, Eq. (
III
) between current modes, we find another exact equation of motion

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)+

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma,\delta=\parallel,\perp}\sum_{\kappa,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha\gamma}_{\mu\kappa}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times[\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{\gamma\delta}_{\kappa\lambda}\mathcal{K}_{\lambda\nu}^{\delta\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0.

(37)

Here the force kernel is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\frac{1}{N}\langle\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}j^{\alpha}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|e^{-i\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{Q}t}|\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}j^{\beta}_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle,

(38)

with the projection operator
\mathcal{Q}:=\mathcal{Q}_{j}\mathcal{Q}_{\rho}
. Using a matrix notation with superindices consisting of the pair of a channel index and a mode index
(\alpha,\mu)
we can write the e.o.m. as

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{\mathfrak{M}}(t-t^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0.

(39)

It is also convenient to formulate the e.o.m in the Laplace domain since then the time convolutions become local in the frequency domain. We define Laplace transforms using the convention

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\mu\nu}(q,z)=i\int_{0}^{\infty}S_{\mu\nu}(q,t)e^{izt}\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\Imag[z]>0,

(40)

and similarly for all other quantities.
The e.o.m. assume the following form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}(z)=-[z\mathbf{S}^{-1}+\mathbf{S}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{K}}(z)\mathbf{S}^{-1}]^{-1},

(41)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}(z)=-[z\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}+\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\mathfrak{M}}}(z)\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}]^{-1}.

(42)

The equations of motion as well as the formally exact equations for the memory kernel are virtually identical to the ones for a slit geometry except that the wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
in general cannot be reduced to a scalar by suitable rotations. Employing the symmetries of a modulated liquid allows only restricting
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
to a two-dimensional cut of the degenerate Brillouin zone consisting of a single perpendicular directions and the parallel direction
-\pi/a<q_{z}\leq\pi/a
.

IV
Mode-coupling theory

The e.o.m. derived in the previous section are exact, however, in a certain sense also empty since they merely reformulate the original problem for the generalized intermediate scattering functions in terms of unknown memory kernels. The strategy of the mode-coupling approach
Götze 2009
is to close the equations of motion by providing an approximate relation between the unknown memory kernel and the intermediate scattering functions itself. To this end, the fluctuating force variables are represented as a linear superposition of density pair modes, concomitantly the reduced dynamics of the pair modes are factorized into a product of pair modes evolving in time by the original dynamics. As a result,
the mode-coupling procedure leads to an approximation of the force kernel as a bilinear functional of the generalized intermediate scattering functions.

Following literally the procedure of Ref.
Lang et al. 2012
, the mode-coupling approximation leads to

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N^{3}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}\in\text{BZ}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\\
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\end{subarray}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{X}^{\alpha}_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle\times S_{\mu_{1}\nu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},t)S_{\mu_{2}\nu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},t)\mathcal{X}^{\beta}_{\nu,\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})^{*},

(43)

where the complex-valued vertices arise from the overlap of the fluctuating forces with the density pair modes

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\alpha}_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle[\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{*}\delta\rho_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})\delta\rho_{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\times[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})]_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\mu_{1}}[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})]_{\mu_{2}^{\prime}\mu_{2}}.

(44)

The overlap can be expressed in terms of structural quantities only

\displaystyle\langle[\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}j_{\mu}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{*}\delta\rho_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})\delta\rho_{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Nv_{\text{th}}^{2}\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}\big\{(q_{1}+Q_{\mu_{1}})^{\alpha}S_{\mu-\mu_{1}+\mu^{\sharp},\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle+(1\leftrightarrow 2)-\sum_{\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{n_{\mu^{\prime}-\mu}}{n_{0}}(q+Q_{\mu^{\prime}})^{\alpha}[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]_{\mu^{\prime}\mu^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\times S_{\mu^{\prime\prime},\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\big\},

(45)

see Appendix
D
. Here in addition to the generalized static structure factors also the triple correlation function

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})=\frac{1}{N}\langle\delta\rho_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})^{*}\delta\rho_{\mu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})\delta\rho_{\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\rangle,

(46)

enters the expression. The triple correlation function is hard to obtain in praxis and to make progress additional approximations are invoked. Here we rely on the convolution approximation for inhomogeneous liquids as introduced by Rajan
et al

Rajan et al. 1978
generalizing the standard bulk convolution approximation
Hansen and McDonald 2006
. The application to the case of periodically modulated liquid reflecting the discrete translational symmetry is elaborated in Appendix
F
. Using this result the expression for the vertex drastically simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\alpha}_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\approx-Nn_{0}v_{\text{th}}^{2}\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[(q_{1}+Q_{\mu+\mu^{\sharp}}-Q_{\mu_{2}})^{\alpha}c_{\mu+\mu^{\sharp}-\mu_{2},\mu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})+(1\leftrightarrow 2)\right],

(47)

see Appendix
E
for a derivation. The vertex is expressed in terms of the Fourier representation
c_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
of the direct correlation function defined via the inhomogeneous Ornstein-Zernike relation

Hansen and McDonald 2006
, see Appendix
C
for details. In the Fourier domain for a modulated liquid it is related to the matrix of the static structure factor via

\displaystyle[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]_{\mu\nu}=n_{0}v_{\nu-\mu}-n_{0}c_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}).

(48)

The force kernel enters the Mori-Zwanzig equation of motion for the current kernel in the Laplace domain, Eq. (
42
), only in the combination
\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\hat{\bm{\mathfrak{M}}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},z)\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
which suggests introducing the effective force kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)

\displaystyle:=[\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\mathbf{\mathfrak{M}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}[\mathbf{S}(t),\mathbf{S}(t);\bm{\mathrm{q}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}\in\text{BZ}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\\
\nu_{1}\nu_{2}\end{subarray}\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}_{\mu;\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle\times S_{\mu_{1}\nu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},t)S_{\mu_{2}\nu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},t)\mathcal{Y}^{\beta}_{\nu;\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})^{*},

(49)

with new vertices

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{0}^{2}\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}}v^{*}_{\mu-\kappa}

\displaystyle\times\left[(q_{1}+Q_{\kappa+\mu^{\sharp}}-Q_{\mu_{2}})^{\alpha}c_{\kappa+\mu^{\sharp}-\mu_{2},\mu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})+(1\leftrightarrow 2)\right].

(50)

The explicit expression of the mode-coupling functional and the vertices, Eqs. (
IV
),(
IV
), together with the equations of motion of Sec.
III
is the main result of this work.
The structure of the vertices is reminiscent of the cases of a confined liquid
Lang et al. 2012
or a liquid of comprising of molecules
Schilling and Scheidsteger 1997
;
Franosch et al. 1997
.
As additional feature of the modulation we find that only modes can couple satisfying the selection rule
\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}\in\Lambda^{*}
which reflects the conservation of crystal momentum.

The notation for the mode-coupling functional
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}^{\alpha\beta}[\mathbf{S}(t),\mathbf{S}(t);\bm{\mathrm{q}}]
in Eq. (
IV
) highlights that the functional is bilinear in the matrices of the intermediate scattering functions, which is a general feature of the mode-coupling approach
Götze 2009
. The structure of the Mori-Zwanzig equations of motion, Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
),
together with the MCT closure,
Eq. (
IV
), falls into the class of general MCT equations with multiple decay
channels
Lang et al. 2013
for which various mathematical properties hold. In particular, for given parameters, i.e. the density profile
n_{\mu}
and static structure factors
S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
a unique solution of the MCT equations exists and the solution respects the mathematical properties of a correlation function.

V
Nonergodicity parameters

Here we show that the theory allows for a glass transition as encoded in a nonergodicity transition. Since our theory belongs to the class of MCT equations with multiple decay channels, this section merely summarize known results
Lang et al. 2012
;
Lang et al. 2013
and serves to fix the notation but also as a reference for future work.

The long-time limits of the intermediate scattering functions are denoted by

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t),

(51)

and referred to as nonergodicity parameters.
This limit is known to exist for pure relaxational dynamics
Götze and Sjögren 1995
;
Franosch and Voigtmann 2002
;
Jung et al. 2020b
and also for a broad class of Newtonian dynamics
Franosch 2014
. Liquid states are defined if
F_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\equiv 0
for all wave vectors
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
and mode indices
\mu,\nu
, and are referred to as ergodic
Götze 2009
. In contrast, glassy states are nonergodic
F_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\neq 0
. A convenient representation is obtained as the low-frequency limit in the Laplace domain

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=-\lim_{z\to 0}z\hat{S}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},z).

(52)

where the limit
z\to 0
is always understood to be performed in a sector in the upper half plane
\delta<\text{arg }(z)<\pi-\delta,\delta>0
. Since the matrix
\mathbf{S}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
is a matrix-valued autocorrelation function, the associated matrix of nonergodicity parameters
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
is positive-semidefinite for each wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
.

By the Mori-Zwanzig equations of motion, a nonergodic state can happen only if the long-time limit of the force kernels

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bm{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\bm{\mathcal{F}}[\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F};\bm{\mathrm{q}}],

(53)

becomes nonergodic simultaneously. The e.o.m. for the currents, Eq. (
42
), then shows that the low-frequency behavior of the current kernel in the Laplace domain is provided by
\hat{K}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},z)=zG_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})+o(z)
for
z\to 0
with

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}):=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=\parallel,\perp}(q+Q_{\mu})^{\alpha}[\bm{\mathcal{N}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(q+Q_{\nu})^{\beta}.

(54)

Similarly, the e.o.m., Eq, (
41
), reveals that the nonergodicity parameters are connected to the low-frequency behavior of the currents via

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\mathbf{S}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})-[\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})+\mathbf{G}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})]^{-1}.

(55)

Equations (
53
),(
54
),(
55
) have to be solved self-consistently. A vanishing nonergodicity matrix
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\equiv 0
is always a solution (in which case we formally associate the infinite matrix
\mathbf{G}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
with
\mathbf{G}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=0
), however nontrivial solutions may occur, provided the coupling encoded in the mode-coupling functional
\bm{\mathcal{F}}[\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F};\bm{\mathrm{q}}]
is sufficiently strong. The unique solution as obtained from the long-time limit of the solution of the e.o.m. is obtained by a convergent iteration scheme
Lang et al. 2012
;
Lang et al. 2013
without resorting to the full time-dependent solution.

VI
Comparison to bulk liquids

In this section, we demonstrate that in the absence of an external modulation the theory simplifies to the conventional MCT equations governing the glass transition in bulk systems.

The complete translational and rotational symmetry is restored in bulk liquids leading to a uniform equilibrium density,
n(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=n_{0}=1/v=\text{const.}
, which implies for the Fourier coefficients of the density and volume

\displaystyle n_{\mu}

\displaystyle=n_{0}\delta_{\mu 0},

(56a)

\displaystyle v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=v\delta_{\mu 0}.

(56b)

Similarly, the intermediate scattering function becomes diagonal with respect to the mode indices

S_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}S(k,t),

(57)

where
S(k,t)
refers to the conventional intermediate scattering function of a bulk system and we abbreviate in this section
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
. By isotropy it depends only on the magnitude
k=|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|
of the wave vector.
Specializing to time
t=0
implies the same relation for the generalized structure factor. Then the Ornstein-Zernike relation, Eq. (
48
), reveals

c_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\delta_{\mu\nu}c(k),

(58)

where
c(k)\in\mathbb{R}
is the ordinary bulk direct correlation function.

Similar considerations hold for the tensorial correlation functions.

For bulk liquids, isotropy holds which implies for the effective force kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}\left[\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{k}^{\beta}\mathcal{M}^{L}(k,t)+(\delta^{\alpha\beta}-\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{k}^{\beta})\mathcal{M}^{T}(k,t)\right],

(59)

where
\mathcal{M}^{L}(k,t)
and
\mathcal{M}^{T}(k,t)
denote the longitudinal and transversal components and
\hat{k}^{\alpha}=k^{\alpha}/k
. Similar relations hold for the current kernel
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)
. Note that by Eq. (
34
) only the longitudinal part
\mathcal{K}^{L}(k,t)
contributes to
K_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}K(k,t)
with
K(k,t)=k^{2}\mathcal{K}^{L}(k,t)
.

In the expression for the vertex, Eq. (
IV
), only the term
\kappa=\mu
contributes due to the constant volume per particle. Furthermore
\mu_{1}=\mu+\mu^{\sharp}-\mu_{2}
is enforced by the diagonality of the direct correlation function, which also implies
(q_{1}+Q_{\mu+\mu^{\sharp}}-Q_{\mu_{2}})^{\alpha}=k_{1}^{\alpha}
. Last, with
\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}}
and upon collecting results we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{0}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}}[k_{1}^{\alpha}c(k_{1})+(1\leftrightarrow 2)]

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}).

(60)

Note that the Kronecker delta reflects momentum conservation for fully restored translational invariance. Furthermore
\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})
transforms as a vector under rotations.

For the memory kernel, Eq. (
IV
), momentum conservation implies that it is only nonvanishing for
\mu=\nu
.
Observing that
\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\in\text{BZ}}\sum_{\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}\ldots=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}}\ldots
, we find for the force kernel in
MCT approximation

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)=\delta_{\mu\nu}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}\in\lambda^{*}}\mathcal{Y}^{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})

\displaystyle\times S(k_{1},t)S(k_{2},t)\mathcal{Y}^{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})^{*}.

(61)

In particular, the MCT approximation does not spoil the tensorial character of the effective force kernel, Eq. (
59
).
We find for the longitudinal part

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{L}(k,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{n_{0}^{2}}{2N}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}\in\lambda^{*}}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}}[\hat{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}c(k_{1})+(1\leftrightarrow 2)]^{2}

\displaystyle\times S(k_{1},t)S(k_{2},t).

(62)

The previous expression coincides with the conventional mode-coupling approximation for bulk liquids
Götze 2009
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006
.

Next we show that the e.o.m, Eqs. (
32
),
(
39
), reduce to the well-known equations for bulk liquid. First, for the generalized intermediate scattering function we find directly from Eq. (
32
)

\displaystyle\dot{S}(k,t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(k,t-t^{\prime})S(k)^{-1}S(k,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0,

(63)

with formal solution in the Laplace domain

\displaystyle\hat{S}(k,z)=-[zS(k)^{-1}+S(k)^{-1}\hat{K}(k,z)S(k)^{-1}]^{-1}.

(64)

Then we decompose the e.o.m. for the current kernel, Eqs. (
39
), in terms of the effective force kernel

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t)+\int_{0}^{t}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}){\mathcal{M}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t-t^{\prime})\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}},t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0,

(65)

into its longitudinal and transverse part. Since the projection operators
\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{k}^{\beta}
and
\delta^{\alpha\beta}-\hat{k}^{\alpha}\hat{k}^{\beta}
onto the longitudinal and transversal component
are orthogonal, and for an isotropic liquid
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=v_{\text{th}}^{2}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mu\nu}
, we find

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{K}}^{L}(k,t)+v_{\text{th}}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{M}^{L}(k,t-t^{\prime})\mathcal{K}^{L}(k,t^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}=0,

(66)

and similarly for the transversal parts. By Laplace transform, the solution is formally expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}(k,z)=-[z/v_{\text{th}}^{2}+\hat{\mathcal{M}}^{L}(k,z)]^{-1}.

(67)

Observing
\hat{K}(k,z)=k^{2}\hat{\mathcal{K}}(k,z)
, Eqs. (
64
),(
67
), can be combined to the conventional
form
Götze 2009
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006

\displaystyle\hat{S}(k,z)/S(k)=\frac{-1}{z-\Omega(k)^{2}/[z+\Omega(k)^{2}\hat{m}(k,z)]},

(68)

with the characteristic frequency
\Omega(k)^{2}=k^{2}v_{\text{th}}^{2}/S(k)
and
the memory kernel
\hat{m}(k,z)=\hat{\mathcal{M}}^{L}(k,z)S(k)/k^{2}
.

In summary, we find that the mode-coupling theory for modulated liquids reduces to the well-known MCT for bulk liquids. In particular, our calculation demonstrates the covariance of the theory. For a bulk liquid the introduction of a lattice should have no impact on the dynamics of the system, in particular, the orientation of the lattice and the lattice constant should be irrelevant. Since the Mori-Zwanzig formalism is merely an exact reformulation of the problem, it is anticipated that this covariance is reflected in the equations of motion. The nontrivial observation is that also the mode-coupling approximation for the force kernel preserves the covariance. A similar covariance property for mode-coupling theory has been demonstrated for the case molecular liquids
Schilling 2002
where the choice of a reference point on the molecules should not affect the dynamics.

VII
Summary and Conclusions

We have derived a mode-coupling theory for the dynamics of a liquid exposed to an external one-dimensional periodic potential. The liquid becomes inhomogeneous, in particular, a density modulation of the same period results. To account for the breaking of translational symmetry we have introduced appropriately generalized intermediate scattering functions reflecting the discrete translational symmetry, as familiar from the theory of crystalline solids. The loss of rotational symmetry suggests splitting the currents into components parallel and perpendicular to the modulation giving rise to Mori-Zwanzig equations of motion of a more general structure than for bulk liquids.
The force kernels are then approximated in terms of a mode-coupling approach which is known to yield a successful description of the structural relaxation in bulk systems but also for molecules and confined systems.

As a new feature of the discrete symmetry, momentum is not strictly conserved but may involve a reciprocal lattice vector. These
Umklapp
process are properly reflected in the construction of the mode-coupling functional which corroborates that the mode-coupling approach respects all underlying symmetries of the system. Similar observations hold for the conservation of momentum in (quasi-)confined systems
Lang et al. 2012
;
Schrack et al. 2020
or angular momentum for molecular liquids
Franosch et al. 1997
;
Schilling and Scheidsteger 1997
.

Whereas for molecular liquids the splitting of the currents was mandatory if the structural relaxation dynamics is assumed to be independent of inertial parameters such as masses and moments of inertia
Schilling 2002
,
the splitting of the currents in modulated or (quasi-)confined liquids is suggested by physical intuition. As a benefit the static current-density matrix
can be inverted without introducing additional structural information.
We anticipate that the MCT functional for a modulated colloidal liquid will be identical to the one derived in our theory. The derivation based on Brownian dynamics will be addressed in future work.

The present theory for modulated liquids shares all the mathematical properties derived for mode-coupling theories for multiple decay channels and correspondingly all mathematical properties can be directly inferred. In particular, the existence of unique solutions can be demonstrated and moreover these solutions belong to the class of admissible functions for general stochastic processes.

Formal equations for a mode-coupling theory for general inhomogeneous environments have been derived by Biroli
et al

Biroli et al. 2006
and recently also for generalized mode-coupling theory
Laudicina et al. 2022
. The main purpose there was to consider changes of the intermediate scattering functions in response to an infinitesimal external potential as encoded in higher-order density correlation functions in the absence of such a perturbation. These higher-order susceptibilities have been used to unravel divergent correlations and growing length scales as the glass transition is approached. By linear response, it is sufficient to apply a periodic modulation to derive these susceptibilities and correspondingly our theory should reproduce the results in the limiting case of small amplitude modulations.

A one-dimensional liquid exposed to a periodic potential has been introduced by Nandi
et al
Nandi et al. 2011
relying on a simplified mode-coupling approximation in order to discuss in a toy model the effects of confining hard walls. Our approach differs in several respects from their approach, in particular, we take into account all density modes as encoded in the matrix of the intermediate scattering functions necessary to reconstruct the real-space density-density correlation functions. Correspondingly, our theory properly accounts for
Umklapp
processes inherent to the residual discrete translational symmetry.

We have presented the theory for the case of a one-dimensional potential modulation, yet the generalization to arbitrary periodic potentials is straightforward at the price of few notational changes. For example, the Brillouin zone will no longer be a stripe and the reciprocal lattice will have more than one dimension. The currents need to be split into several components compared to the one-dimensional modulation. While the experimental realization of such arbitrary periodic potentials appears feasible, it will be more and more difficult to preempt the crystallization as the modulation promotes crystallization, a phenomenon also called ’light-induced freezing’
Sood 1996
;
Strepp et al. 2001
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2005
.

The set-up of a modulated liquid adds two more control parameters with respect to the same bulk liquid. The corresponding intermediate scattering functions and derived quantities provide relevant information on the competition of local packing and the imposed modulation. Depending on the period and strength of the modulation a non-trivial nonequilibrium state diagram follows already for the simplest conceivable interaction, the hard-sphere potential. In the companion paper
Ahmadirahmat et al. 2025
we evaluate the theory numerically for the nonergodicity parameters for the case of a monolayer of hard-sphere particles and determine the glass-transition lines and show a reentrant behavior emerges. We anticipate that upon tuning the parameters also higher-order singularities emerge similar to the colloid-polymer mixtures
Bergenholtz and Fuchs 1999
;
Fabbian et al. 1999
;
Dawson et al. 2000
;
Pham et al. 2002
or liquids in a porous host matrix
Krakoviack 2005
;
*Krakoviack:PRE_75:2007
;
*Krakoviack:PRE_79:2009
;
Kurzidim et al. 2009
;
Kim et al. 2009
.

The most promising realization of a modulated liquid consists of a colloidal monolayer where the external periodic potential is achieved in terms of interfering laser beams
Dalle-Ferrier et al. 2011
;
Jenkins and Egelhaaf 2008
;
Capellmann et al. 2018
. Our theory should directly apply to this situation, a comparison to experiments is the content of future work.

Appendix A
Lattice sums

In this appendix we collect some properties of discrete Fourier transforms in periodic systems, thereby introducing some useful notation.

The volume is considered to be a hypercube
V=L^{d}
of linear dimension
L
. In particular, the box size is an integer multiple of the period of the potential,
L=Ma
with
M\in\mathbb{N}
. All functions are considered to be periodically extended

f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=f(\bm{\mathrm{x}}+\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda})
where
\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda}\in\lambda:=\{\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in(L\mathbb{Z})^{d}\}
. Such functions will be referred to as
\lambda
-periodic.
Then Fourier modes are introduced according to

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})=\int_{V}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\exp(i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(69)

Since
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
is
\lambda
-periodic, a shift
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mapsto\bm{\mathrm{x}}+\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda}
with
\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda}\in\lambda
leaves the function invariant, and we have to impose

\displaystyle\exp(i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda})=1\qquad\text{for }\bm{\mathrm{R}}_{\lambda}\in\lambda,

(70)

which restricts the wave vectors to the
reciprocal lattice

\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}:=\{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/L)^{d}\}
.
The following relations are useful

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\exp(i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},0}\qquad\text{for }\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*},

(71a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}}\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\qquad\text{for }\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in V.

(71b)

Then we find also the representation of a
\lambda
-periodic function in terms of its Fourier modes

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}}\hat{f}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(72)

Now for modulated liquids we decompose
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
such that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}:=\{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\lambda^{*}:-\pi/a<\bm{\mathrm{q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}\leq\pi/a\},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}\in\Lambda^{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}=(2\pi\mu/a)\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}:\mu\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and use the compact notation
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}
to indicate the decomposition. The corresponding Fourier mode is abbreviated as
\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}):=\hat{f}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\mu})
. Then the Fourier decomposition can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\,f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}})],

(73a)

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}\hat{f}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\exp[-i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}].

(73b)

The orthogonality and completeness is encoded in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},0}\delta_{\mu,0},

(74a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}}\exp[-i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}]

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(74b)

where
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}\in\Lambda^{*},\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in V
.

It is also of interest to specialize the decomposition for functions that are already
\Lambda
-periodic,
\mathcal{U}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\mathcal{U}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}+\bm{\mathrm{R}})
with
\bm{\mathrm{R}}\in\Lambda=\{\bm{\mathrm{r}}+na\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z}:\bm{\mathrm{r}}\perp\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{z},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},0}V\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm{d}z\,\mathcal{U}(z)e^{iQ_{\mu}z}=\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},0}V\,\mathcal{U}_{\mu},

(75)

where
\mathcal{U}_{\mu}
is the Fourier coefficient of the one-dimensional modulation as defined in Eq. (
5
).

Appendix B
Static current-density correlator

In this appendix we calculate the thermal average of the current density correlation function that is needed for introducing the projector on the currents.

The static current density correlation matrix
\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=N^{-1}\langle j^{\alpha}_{\mu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})^{*}j^{\beta}_{\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})\rangle
is diagonal with respect to the channel indices
\alpha,\beta
since averages over unpaired momenta vanish. Inserting the current densities and pre-averaging over the momenta yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\Big\langle\langle b^{\alpha}[(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n})(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{m}),(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n})(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{m})]\rangle

\displaystyle\times\exp[-i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{m}]\exp[i(\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\nu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}]\Big\rangle\frac{\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{Nm^{2}}.

(76)

The thermal averages of the momenta follow from the equipartition theorem

\displaystyle\langle(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n})(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{m})\rangle=\langle(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{n})(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{q}}}^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{m})\rangle=\delta_{nm}mk_{B}T.

(77)

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{Nm}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\exp[-i(\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\nu})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{Nm}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\int_{V}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\delta(\bm{\mathrm{x}}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n})\rangle\exp[-i\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu-\nu}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{Nm}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\int_{V}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\,\,n(z)\exp[-iQ_{\mu-\nu}{z}]

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{Nm}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\frac{V}{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}z\,\,n(z)\exp[-iQ_{\mu-\nu}{z}]

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{mn_{0}}\delta^{\alpha\beta}n_{\nu-\mu}.

(78)

Note that the correlation functions for the split currents is expressible solely in terms of the density profile.

Appendix F
Convolution approximation

In this appendix, the convolution approximation for the triplet
structure factor of a fluid exposed to a periodic potential energy
landscape is derived. The procedure is based on the general
formulation of Rajan
et al.

Rajan et al. 1978
, valid for any
inhomogeneous system, and is similar to the one used in
Ref.
Lang et al. 2012
for a fluid in a slit pore.

We first consider the general case of an inhomogeneous
N
-particle
fluid enclosed in a
d
-dimensional hypercube of volume

V
, for which we lay down the necessary definitions and
notations. With

\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})=\sum_{l=1}^{N}e^{i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{l}},

(102)

where
\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n}
denotes the position of the
n
-th particle and
\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}
is a wave vector compatible with the finite box, see Appendix
A
,
the
Fourier transform of the one-body density, denoted with
n(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
,
is given by

\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})=\langle\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})\rangle.

(103)

As for the pair and triplet structure factors, they are defined from
the fluctuations

\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})=\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})-\langle\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})\rangle=\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}})-\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}),

(104)

as

\displaystyle S^{(2)}({\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}})=\frac{1}{N}\langle\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0})\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1})\rangle,

(105)

\displaystyle S^{(3)}({\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}})=\frac{1}{N}\langle\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0})\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1})\delta\rho(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})\rangle,

(106)

respectively. Note that, at variance with the main text, the
definitions of the structure factors in this Appendix do not involve
any complex conjugation in order to preserve the symmetry of the
working equations. It is also convenient to introduce the Fourier
components
\hat{v}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})
, Eq. (
69
), of the local specific volume

v(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=1/n(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, which obey generally

\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}-\bm{\mathrm{k}})\hat{v}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}-\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})=V\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}}.

(107)

Then, as shown in Ref.
Lang et al. 2012
, the convolution approximation
for
S^{(3)}({\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}})
obtained in
Ref.
Rajan et al. 1978
can be written as

\displaystyle S^{(3)}({\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2}})\approx\frac{N^{2}}{V^{6}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{2}\\
\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\end{subarray}}\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{2})\prod_{i=0}^{2}\hat{v}(-\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{i})S^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{i}).

(108)

Specialization to the case of a fluid in a periodic modulation is
almost straightforward. Here we present the derivation for the slightly more general case of an arbitrary Bravais lattice
\Lambda
with associated reciprocal lattice
\Lambda^{*}
and (first) Brillouin zone
BZ
.
First, a generic wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}\in\lambda^{*}
, see Appendix
A
, uniquely decomposes into
\bm{\mathrm{k}}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
where
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\in\Lambda^{*}
belongs
to the reciprocal lattice, and
\bm{\mathrm{q}}\in\text{BZ}
, to the first Brillouin zone. Accordingly, the sums
over wave vectors in Eq. (
F
) split into separate
sums over each type of wave vectors,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{2}\\
\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\end{subarray}}\ldots=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{2}\in\Lambda^{*}\\
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\in\Lambda^{*}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}_{2}\in\text{BZ}\\
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\in\text{BZ}\end{subarray}}\ldots.

(109)

Second, using the decomposition we write
S^{(2)}_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{0}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}):=S^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1})
, and
as a result of lattice periodicity this is non-vanishing only if
\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\in\Lambda^{*}
is a reciprocal lattice vector or equivalently if
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}=0
. Therefore we conclude

S^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1})=S^{(2)}_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{0}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{1}}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{0}}}.

(110)

Similarly the Fourier coefficients of a
\Lambda
-periodic functions such as the local volume fulfills

\hat{v}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})=Vv_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{0}}},

(111)

with the Fourier coefficient

\displaystyle v_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}}=\frac{1}{V_{P}}\int_{V_{P}}e^{i\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{x}}}v(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{x}},

(112)

where the integral extends only over a primitive cell
V_{P}
. The Eqs. (
111
),(
112
) are the proper generalizations of Eqs. (
75
),(
5
) to arbitrary periodic modulations.

It follows immediately that any nonvanishing term in the last product of
Eq. (
F
) has to be such that

\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}=-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{i}
and
\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}_{i}=-\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{i}
.
Last, some care is needed to deal with the remaining factor

\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{2})
, which, upon using the decomposition of the wave vectors into the Brillouin zone and reciprocal lattice, also can be written as
\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{2}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}^{\prime})

factor to be itself nonvanishing, it is required that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}
is equal to a reciprocal lattice vector

\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}\in\Lambda^{*}
. Therefore, one
finds

\hat{n}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime}_{2})=V\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}\in\Lambda^{*}}n_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{2}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}}.

(113)

Substituting these results into Eq. (
F
), the sums over
vectors of the first Brillouin zone can be explicitly performed and
one finds the result

\displaystyle S^{(3)}_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{0}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{2}}

\displaystyle({\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}):=S^{(3)}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{k}}_{2})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle n_{0}^{2}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}\in\Lambda^{*}}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{2}\in\Lambda^{*}\\
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\in\Lambda^{*}\end{subarray}}n_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{2}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=0}^{2}v_{-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}}S^{(2)}_{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{i}}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{i}),

(114)

where
n_{0}=N/V
. The overall factor
\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}}
encodes the conservation of
the crystal momentum at the level of the triplet structure factor. It
could have been introduced from the outset, but its deduction here
demonstrates the consistency of the convolution approximation with the
requirements of lattice periodicity. Note that
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}=\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}\in\Lambda^{*}
implies that
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\sharp}
is either
\bm{\mathrm{0}}
or a reciprocal vector neighboring the origin.

Last, let us specialize again to the case of a one-dimensional modulation and use the notation of the main text. We use the substitution rules
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{0}\mapsto-\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{0}\mapsto-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu}

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})=S^{(3)}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu_{1}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}+\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu_{2}})

\displaystyle\approx n_{0}^{2}\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{-\bm{\mathrm{q}}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{\prime},\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\mu^{\prime\prime},\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}n_{\mu^{\prime}+\mu_{1}^{\prime}+\mu_{2}^{\prime}+\mu^{\sharp}}v_{-\mu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime}}S_{-\mu^{\prime\prime},-\mu}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}})

\displaystyle\times v_{-\mu_{1}^{\prime}-\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime}}S_{-\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})v_{-\mu_{2}^{\prime}-\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}S_{-\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}).

(115)

Next we observe that
S_{-\mu^{\prime\prime},-\mu}(-\bm{\mathrm{q}})=S_{\mu\mu^{\prime\prime}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
and reverse the signs of the double primed summation indices

as well as the sign in
\mu^{\sharp}

\displaystyle S_{\mu,\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathrm{q}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2})\approx n_{0}^{2}\sum_{\mu^{\sharp}=0,\pm 1}\delta_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\mu^{\sharp}},\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{\prime},\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\mu^{\prime\prime},\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}n_{\mu^{\prime}+\mu_{1}^{\prime}+\mu_{2}^{\prime}-\mu^{\sharp}}v_{-\mu^{\prime}-\mu^{\prime\prime}}S_{\mu,\mu^{\prime\prime},}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})

\displaystyle\times v_{-\mu_{1}^{\prime}+\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime}}S_{\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{1}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{1})v_{-\mu_{2}^{\prime}+\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}S_{\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{2}).

(116)
