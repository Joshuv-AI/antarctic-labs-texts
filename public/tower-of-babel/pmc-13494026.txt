Facilitation of hippocampal long-term potentiation by astrocytic Gq signaling is compromised in an advanced Alzheimer’s disease model
Authors: Rasim Miftakhov, Polina Fortygina, Alena Zuzina, Pavel Balaban, Anastasia Borodinova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astrocytes communicate bidirectionally with neurons by releasing various signaling molecules. This astrocyte-neuron “dialogue” is essential for long-term processes in the nervous system, and its disruption correlates with various neuropathologies, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Neurogenetic approaches including opto- and chemogenetics allow targeted modulation of astrocyte functioning. Our study investigated how chemogenetic activation of the Gq-coupled pathway in hippocampal astrocytes modulates synaptic plasticity and gene expression in both healthy state and AD pathology. To selectively manipulate astrocytic Gq signaling, we expressed DREADD receptors hM3Dq in hippocampal astrocytes of both wild-type (WT) and transgenic 5xFAD mice, a model of familial Alzheimer’s disease, using adeno-associated viruses. In the acute brain slices, we assessed the amplitude of field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) and expression of immediate early genes following activation of DREADD receptors hM3Dq via application of specific agonist Compound-21 (C21), both under resting conditions and during the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP) via theta-burst stimulation (TBS). High concentrations of C21 (2–10 µM) non-specifically increased fEPSPs amplitude even in slices lacking DREADD expression, indicating significant off-target effects. At a lower concentration (1 µM), C21 did not alter baseline synaptic transmission in mice of both genotypes. In healthy animals, chemogenetic astrocyte stimulation with C21 (1 µM) can robustly enhance activity-dependent plasticity without altering the transcription of immediate early genes. In contrast, in 5xFAD slices, the fEPSPs amplitudes were already potentiated following TBS stimulation. On this background, chemogenetic astrocyte stimulation with C21 (1 µM) elicited no further increase in LTP. Our work highlights a common pitfall in chemogenetic studies by demonstrating that excessive DREADD agonist concentrations (more than 1 µM) produce significant off-target effects. We further show that stimulation of astrocytic Gq signaling with low agonist concentration exerts a facilitation of long-term hippocampal plasticity in slices from healthy animals. However, the efficacy of targeted astrocytic Gq-mediated modulation appears limited in advanced-stage AD. Introduction

For decades, the role of astroglia in plasticity was underestimated. It is now evident that astrocytes are integral components of synaptic structures, forming “tripartite synapses” with pre- and postsynaptic neurons1–3. Astrocytes can exchange information with neurons by responding to neuronal signals by elevation of intracellular calcium through activation of membrane receptors including various G protein coupled receptors (GPCR). This increased calcium activity drives diverse cellular responses, including but not limited to the uptake of potassium from the extracellular space4,5, changes in neuronal metabolism regulated by lactate and glucose6,7, and the release of specific sets of neuroactive signaling molecules (gliotransmitters)8–10. Particularly, activation of the Gq-coupled receptors triggers a massive release of calcium ions from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) via phospholipase C (PLC)-inositol trisphosphate (IP3)-dependent pathway. This, in turn, can stimulate the secretion of gliotransmitters, such as glutamate, D-serine, GABA, and ATP, into the synaptic cleft, where they modulate neuronal activity11–14.

Astrocytes can influence synaptic transmission and synaptic plasticity, underlying memory storage and learning15. Impaired astrocyte-neuron communications often correlate with the development of many neuropathologies, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Substantial structural and functional reorganization of neural circuits was observed during AD progression, including neuronal atrophy, synaptic dysfunction, altered excitability, and excitation/inhibition imbalance16–19. Growing evidence suggests significant astrocyte dysfunction during the AD progression that includes soma and processes hypertrophy20, changes in spontaneous and evoked calcium ion levels18,21–23, altered gene expression profiles24, and reduced astrocyte–neuron interactions at tripartite synapses25. These and other aspects of pathologically remodeled astrocyte-neuron communications can affect K⁺ homeostasis and metabolic function, impairing gliotransmission and leading to altered synaptic activity and plasticity4,6,7.

Recent studies highlight a pivotal role for dysregulated astrocytic Gq signaling in AD 26. For instance, activation of the endogenous astrocytic Gq-coupled receptor mGluR5 that increases intracellular Ca2+ via PLC-IP3-dependent pathway27,28 results in calcium hyperactivity, exacerbating amyloid pathology and cognitive deficits29.

This is consistent with the observation of aberrant astrocytic Ca²⁺ oscillations and long-range Ca²⁺ waves around senile plaques in AD brains, pointing to widespread disruption of calcium signaling23. Molecular analyses further show dysregulated expression of the key genes involved in GPCR signaling in astrocytes from AD brain30,31. Collectively, these findings underscore that altered astrocytic Gq-signaling can be a crucial factor underlying synaptic dysfunction in AD.

The selective modulation of astrocyte signaling is therefore essential for dissecting their role in synaptic functions under both normal and pathological conditions, which can guide future research and targeted AD therapies. Neurogenetic approaches such as optogenetics and chemogenetics were widely used to manipulate intracellular ionic concentrations and signaling cascades in astrocytes, and can be employed to compensate for pathological phenotypes in various neurological diseases32–38. Optogenetics uses light to precisely control activity of the cells, expressing ionotropic and metabotropic light-sensitive receptors39,40. These methods have been used in neuroglial studies to alter astrocyte activity in order to modulate synaptic transmission and long-term plasticity37,41–44. We previously demonstrated that, depending on the way of intracellular calcium accumulation (influx across the plasma membrane or release from intracellular stores), astrocytes can induce either long-term depression or long-term potentiation (LTP), which emphasizes their important integrative role42.

The chemogenetic approach uses synthetic, biologically inert ligands to activate specific intracellular cascades in selective populations of cells expressing genetically-encoded metabotropic receptors45. The most popular chemogenetic approach to studying neuroglial interactions is the designer receptors activated exclusively by designer drugs (DREADDs)45,46. DREADDs are genetically engineered variants of muscarinic cholinergic G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) that can be activated by binding to clozapine derivatives such as clozapine-N-oxide (CNO), Compound-21 (C21) and deschloroclozapine47. Previous studies have shown that activation of the Gq pathway in astrocytes using hM3Dq DREADD receptors triggers calcium release from the endoplasmic reticulum43,48,49. This leads to the subsequent release of gliotransmitters into the synaptic cleft50–53, causing de novo potentiation without additional stimuli43,50. However, studies investigating the effect of chemogenetic astrocytes stimulation on long-term potentiation are sparse38.

In this study, we used chemogenetic approach to elucidate the role of astrocytes in regulating synaptic plasticity and gene expression profiles in hippocampal slices from healthy B6SJL mice (WT) and transgenic B6SJL-Tg (5xFAD) mice, a model of familial form of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). We selectively expressed hM3Dq DREADD receptors in hippocampal astrocytes and applied DREADD agonist Compound-21 (C21) to activate astrocytic Gq pathway, which induces calcium accumulation in the cytoplasm and triggers downstream signal transduction. We found that under normal conditions, stimulation of astrocytes with 1 µM C21 can enhance the synaptic plasticity and plasticity-related gene expression. However, the efficacy of targeted astrocytic Gq-mediated modulation appears limited in an advanced-stage AD, which may be attributed to the pathologically remodeled astrocyte-neuron communications. Finally, it was found that at high concentrations (more than 1 µM), C21 exhibits significant off-target effects. Obtained results demonstrated the potential and limitations of astrocyte-targeted chemogenetic strategies in the correction of pathological phenotype, defining critical parameters for future applications.

Results

Extensive evidence indicates that astrocytic Gq-coupled receptor signaling, which triggers calcium release from intracellular stores, undergoes significant remodeling in pathological states, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). This dysregulation ultimately alters the capacity of astrocytes to modulate neuronal functions at both synaptic and molecular levels.

In this study, we used the 5xFAD mouse model of familial form of AD, where astrocyte-neuron interactions and astrocyte-dependent signaling are already impaired18,25,54. We tested whether restoring the pathological endogenous Gq signaling in hippocampal astrocytes using an AAV-based chemogenetic approach could rescue their ability to modulate neuronal functions. To this end, we selectively expressed an optimized Gq-coupled DREADD receptors hM3Dq (Supplementary Fig. S1, S2) in hippocampal astrocytes of transgenic B6SJL-Tg mice (5xFAD) and their age-matched healthy littermates B6SJL (wild-type, WT).

Upon exposure to the DREADD agonist Compound-21 (C21), we assessed in brain slices how the hM3Dq-mediated astrocyte stimulation affects field excitatory postsynaptic potentials’ (fEPSP) amplitude and immediate early genes (IEGs) expression (Fig. 1A) under resting conditions and during the induction of long-term synaptic potentiation (LTP).

High concentrations of Compound-21 used for chemogenetic stimulation of hippocampal astrocytes elicited significant off-target effects, whereas low concentration did not alter synaptic transmission in hippocampal slices under baseline conditions

In numerous studies, high doses of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) have been used for stimulation of signaling cascades in DREADD-expressing astrocytes43,50,55,56, despite its disadvantages compared to newer, more specific DREADD agonists such as Compound-21 (C21)57,58.

In this study, we used this less explored DREADD agonist C21 to stimulate hM3Dq chemoreceptors. Given that CNO and its derivatives induce off-target effects at high concentrations58–60, we first performed dose-response experiments with C21 on non-transduced hippocampal slices to evaluate potential off-target activity (Fig. 1B). It was demonstrated that at high concentrations (2–10 µM) C21 significantly increased the resting fEPSP amplitudes in non-transduced brain slices, but these non-specific effects were absent at lower concentrations of 1 µM (Fig. 1B, C).

Activation of astrocytic hM3Dq receptors with 1 µM C21 in transduced brain slices from both WT and 5xFAD mice did not affect the resting fEPSP amplitude (Fig. 1D-G). In contrast, 10 µM C21, a concentration that produced clear off-target effects, significantly increased the resting fEPSP amplitude in transduced brain slices, reaching levels comparable to LTP-induced responses (Fig. 1D, H: from 101.4 [100.65; 101.58] to 202.03 [187.74; 206.30], p = 0.027, and Fig. 1E, I: from 104.17 [102.22; 105.98] to 200.82 [194.98; 220.98], median [Q1;Q3] % of baseline, p = 0.027, Mann-Whitney U test). This phenomenon, called de-novo potentiation, was first reported by Adamsky and colleagues and later confirmed in other studies43,50. Our results raise reasonable concerns regarding the interpretation of de-novo potentiation phenomenon, suggesting it may represent the pharmacological artifacts rather than specific DREADD-mediated effects, but it requires additional careful examination.

In additional experiments, we verified that 1 µM C21 did not influence the amplitude of synaptic responses in non-transduced WT slices following LTP induction with a theta-burst protocol (TBS) (Fig. 2A, B: TBS group 125.52 ± 10.68% vs. 1 µM C21 + TBS group 145.2 ± 10.94% in non-transduced slices, mean ± s.e.m. % of baseline, p = 0.23, Student’s t-test).

Stimulation of astrocytes with low concentration of Compound-21 produced different responses to LTP induction in slices from WT and 5xFAD mice

Based on the existing literature, we hypothesized that chemogenetic stimulation of astrocytic Gq signaling would enhance synaptic plasticity under both normal and pathological conditions. To test this hypothesis, we examined how activation of astrocytic hM3Dq receptors influenced the synaptic plasticity during LTP induction using a theta-burst protocol (TBS).

Surprisingly, the chemogenetic stimulation of hippocampal astrocytes combined with TBS stimulation elicited significantly distinct synaptic responses in transduced WT and 5xFAD brain slices. In WT slices, TBS stimulation in the presence of 1 µM C21 (C21 + TBS group) significantly increased both fEPSP amplitude (Fig. 2C, D) and slope (Supplementary Fig. S3 A, B) compared to the TBS stimulation alone (amplitude: TBS-alone 125.52 ± 10.68% vs. 1µM C21 + TBS 203.40 ± 15.2% (p = 0.0016), ANOVA test followed by Tukey’s post hoc test; slope: TBS-alone 106.55 [88.95; 150.58] vs. 1µM C21 + TBS 196.87 [176.87; 241.89], median [Q1;Q3] % of baseline, p = 0.0044, Mann-Whitney U test). This elevated synaptic transmission efficiency was observed both during LTP induction and maintenance (Supplementary Fig.S4 A). Notably, at the higher C21 concentration, although the amplitude of synaptic responses was increased, this effect did not reach the statistical significance (Fig. 2C, D, TBS-alone 125.52 ± 10.68% vs. 10µM C21 + TBS 167.38 ± 14.94% (p = 0.14), ANOVA test followed by Tukey’s post hoc test). Together, these data underscore the importance of carefully monitoring the working concentrations of DREADD agonists in chemogenetic studies.

In contrast, the same stimulation protocol in 5xFAD slices revealed no difference in fEPSP amplitude (Fig. 2E, F) or slope (Supplementary Fig.S3 C, D) between the C21 + TBS and TBS groups (amplitude: TBS-alone 175.17 [130.87; 256.37] vs. 1µM C21 + TBS 207.21 [153.58; 230.55], vs. 10µM C21 + TBS 207.51 [194.68; 365.29] median [Q1;Q3] % of baseline, p = 0.43, Kruskal-Wallis test; slope: TBS-alone 150.67 [110.59; 302.50] vs. 1µM C21 + TBS 191.30 [142.06; 273.19] median [Q1;Q3] % of baseline, p = 0.87, Mann-Whitney U test). This is likely explained by the fact that the fEPSPs amplitude was already significantly increased following TBS stimulation alone (Supplementary Fig.S4 B).

To characterize short-term plasticity in the TBS and C21 + TBS groups, we also calculated the paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) ratio, which indicates presynaptic changes and the probability of neurotransmitter release61. Our analysis revealed no significant differences between the groups at any of the examined intervals, in both WT and 5xFAD slices (Supplementary Fig.S5, p = 0.83, Kruskal-Wallis test).

Together, our results from chemogenetic experiments likely reflect a disruption of neuroglial regulatory networks due to the AD progression, which could not be rescued by selective activation of Gq-coupled cascades in astrocytes.

Stimulation of astrocytes with low concentration of Compound-21 under resting conditions increased c-fos mRNA expression in WT, but not in the 5xFAD brain slices

Growing evidence indicates that astrocytes expressing DREADD receptors can respond to chemogenetic stimulation with downstream activation of Ca2+-dependent signaling by changing expression of distinct transcription factors, including various immediate early genes (IEGs). However, the transcriptional outcome depends on the specific GPCR signaling cascade activated62–64. Consequently, these molecular rearrangements may shape astrocytic responses to different stimuli. The molecular consequences of astrocyte’s activation via various DREADD receptors remain incompletely characterized.

To better characterize transcriptional changes in brain slices of WT and 5xFAD mice elicited by astrocytic Gq signaling itself under resting conditions or during LTP induction, we analyzed total mRNA expression of several immediate early genes Fos, Egr1, and Arc (Fig. 3), often used as markers of cellular activity. Under resting conditions (no TBS stimulation), chemogenetic stimulation of astrocytic Gq signaling with 1 µM C21 significantly increased the c-fos mRNA expression in WT slices (Fig. 3A). However, this effect was absent, when a higher concentration (10 µM C21) with evident off-target effects was used (Supplementary Fig.S6A).

Given that c-fos expression is not neuron-specific and can be detected in glial cells65, and has been used to verify the DREADD receptor activation in astrocytes43,62, the question remains whether c-fos induction occurs in astrocytes, neurons, or both. We speculate that activation of hM3Dq-expressing astrocytes in healthy mice may underlie the observed increase in c-fos mRNA expression in brain slices, though it requires further verification with cell-type-specific resolution.

In contrast, application of 1 µM C21 under resting conditions (no TBS stimulation) did not increase the c-fos mRNA expression in 5xFAD slices (Fig. 3A). We observed an elevated baseline of c-fos mRNA expression in 5xFAD slices, which may be linked to ongoing inflammatory processes65 described for this AD model66, although this elevation did not reach statistical significance compared to WT, likely due to high dispersion between samples. We hypothesized that this elevated baseline may be part of a complex pathological phenotype, and that the absence of C21-induced c-fos elevation in 5xFAD slices may result from impaired downstream signal transduction – either in the chemogenetically stimulated astrocytes themselves, or in the neurons they activate, or both.

TBS induced IEGs expression in slices from WT and 5xFAD mice, which was not further enhanced by chemogenetic astrocyte stimulation

Long-term plastic processes depend on de-novo transcription67–70, through which specific genes and transcriptional programs are activated to maintain the changes. These changes involve gradual shifts in neuronal gene expression profiles, so-called waves of transcription that are triggered by distinct durations of neuronal activity71. The first wave of transcription is characterized by rapidly inducible transcription factors, such as immediate early genes (IEG)69,70,72. Depending on the duration of neuronal activity, these factors may or may not subsequently promote second and third waves of expression of downstream plasticity-related genes71. Recent evidence indicates that, similar to neurons, astrocytes also exhibit sustained, long-lasting transcriptional changes in response to various stimuli62,73–75.

Given that plastic changes are accompanied by transcriptomic changes in activated brain cells (neurons, astrocytes), we tested whether total mRNA expression of several plasticity-related IEGs (Fos, Egr1, and Arc) would change in WT and 5xFAD hippocampal slices after LTP induction (Fig. 3A-C). In these experiments, control (unstimulated) slices were treated similarly and processed in parallel with the stimulated slices.

C-fos mRNA expression

Fos gene is one of the crucial components in the plasticity-related processes. We found that LTP induction using TBS stimulation protocol triggered the expected upregulation of plasticity-related gene Fos in the WT slices (Fig. 3A). However, combining the TBS stimulation with C21 treatment (1 µM or 10 µM) did not further enhance the genes’ expression (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig.S6 A), which may indicate that the effect is already maximal. A second plausible interpretation is an overlapping transcriptional pathways activated both during the LTP induction and astrocytic Gq stimulation. As a result, stronger activation of transcription does not necessarily lead to a proportional increase in the c-fos mRNA expression. More generally, c-fos is an indirect and context-dependent marker of cellular activation rather than a linear readout of plasticity magnitude, its expression depends on stimulus timing, signaling history, and cell type76,77.

In contrast, the LTP induction was not sufficient to activate Fos expression in the 5xFAD slices (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig.S6 A). Notably, some 5xFAD slices exhibited substantially higher baseline of the c-fos mRNA expression compared to the WT slices. As we mentioned above, the baseline c-fos mRNA expression may already be elevated in the 5xFAD mice due to ongoing inflammatory processes, leaving little dynamic range for further induction by TBS or chemogenetic stimulation. Consequently, the additional stimuli fail to produce a statistically significant increase not because the pathway is unresponsive, but probably because the system is already operating at its transcriptional ceiling.

Egr1 mRNA expression

Along with Fos, the Egr1 gene (also known as Zif268) is expressed in an activity-dependent manner and is essential for long-term plasticity and memory. It is responsible for the transition from short-term to long-term synaptic plasticity (i.e., LTP maintenance) and for regulating the strength of newly encoded memories78,79. Intriguingly, under our experimental conditions, Egr1 mRNA expression did not change in TBS-stimulated WT slices but significantly increased in TBS-stimulated 5xFAD brain slices (Fig. 3B). Given the regulatory role of Egr1 in LTP maintenance, we assume that this discrepancy could be explained by significantly elevated amplitude of fEPSP responses in 5xFAD slices compared to WT (Supplementary Fig.S4 B). These findings support the idea that synaptic plasticity changes can be coupled with molecular rearrangements. Combining TBS stimulation with C21 treatment (1 µM or 10 µM) failed to enhance Egr1 mRNA expression in both WT and 5xFAD slices (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig.S6 B).

Arc mRNA expression

Arc (also known as Arg3.1) is another important plasticity-related gene whose expression correlates with long-term synaptic processes69. However, in contrast to Fos and Egr1 genes, whose expression is associated with NMDA-receptor signaling80–82, Arc expression has been shown to be activated in an NMDA-independent, but mGluR-dependent manner83. In our experimental conditions, Arc mRNA expression did not change in either the TBS, or the C21 + TBS groups, in both WT and 5xFAD slices (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig.S6 C).

Overall, our data suggest that under normal conditions, stimulation of astrocytes can enhance the synaptic plasticity and plasticity-related gene expression; however, unlike previous reports43, this stimulation appears unable to induce de-novo potentiation in our experimental conditions. While these discrepancies could arise from methodological differences (e.g., mouse strains, viral constructs, or chemogenetic activators), they may also reflect off-target effects of CNO. In AD pathology, neuroglial communication likely undergoes reorganization, such that astrocytic Gq signaling no longer effectively regulates specific synaptic and molecular processes. This may be attributable to functional dysregulation (e.g., receptor decoupling from downstream cascades, pathological elevation of gene expression, or competing pathological pathways that override or occlude the chemogenetic effects) or structural changes (e.g., retraction of astrocytes from synapses and subsequent synaptic loss).

Discussion

Astrocytes communicate bidirectionally with neurons by releasing various signaling molecules that modulate plasticity, metabolism and transcription8,84–86. This astrocyte-neuron “dialogue” is fundamental to long-term processes in the nervous system, and its disruption is implicated in various neuropathologies, including Alzheimer’s disease8. Consequently, neurogenetic technologies for selectively controlling neuroglial interactions, such as chemogenetics, are now positioning astrocytes as promising targets for therapeutic modulation of neural circuits in both normal and pathological states.

Stimulus-dependent regulation of neural circuits by astrocytic Gq signaling in health and Alzheimer’s disease

In recent years, neurogenetic approaches (optogenetics, chemogenetics) have been increasingly used to control astrocytes activity and modulate their secretory phenotypes87, revealing how these cells regulate long-term plastic processes in the brain. Studies have successfully employed hM3Dq chemoreceptors and Opto-α1AR opsins to activate the Gq-coupled signaling cascade in astrocytes, demonstrating its critical role in regulating synaptic plasticity and memory42,43,49,50,88. This targeted activation triggers phospholipase C-dependent calcium release from intracellular stores and its accumulation in the cytoplasm43,50. The consequences of activating this pathway include the induction of de-novo potentiation in resting-state brain slices, likely mediated by D-serine43,50, enhancement of long-term synaptic plasticity37,38,42, and strengthening of hippocampus-dependent memory43. Based on this understanding of astrocytic regulatory potential, neurogenetic technologies are now being used to ameliorate various neuropathological conditions, with promising applications in Alzheimer’s disease models18,37,38,48,89.

The primary goal of this study was to elucidate the role of astrocytes in regulating synaptic and molecular processes under normal conditions and in AD pathology. In this context, the 5xFAD mouse model of AD was essential for testing whether hippocampal astrocytes lose their ability to modulate neuronal functions and whether pathologically remodeled astrocyte-neuron communications can be compensated using a chemogenetic approach. One plausible prediction was a partial rescue, as astrocytic Gq-coupled stimulation normalizes neuronal hyperactivity and behavior, and improves plasticity in AD models18,37. An alternative prediction was a reduced astrocyte’s efficacy to modulate neuronal functions, if downstream signaling is already chronically engaged or pathologically remodeled in AD.

Stimulation of the astrocytic Gq pathway with low concentration of the DREADD agonist C21 (1 µM) did not alter the baseline synaptic transmission in brain slices from either wild-type (WT) (Fig. 1D, F) or 5xFAD transgenic mice (Fig. 1E, G). This result contrasts with findings in C57BL/6 mice, where optogenetic activation of the same pathway increased fEPSP slopes in resting-state brain slices42,44. The discrepancy suggests that spatiotemporal characteristics of astrocyte modulation can have different functional outcomes (i.e. stimulus-specific).

Despite the absence of electrophysiological effects, chemogenetic astrocyte stimulation significantly increased total c-fos mRNA expression in WT slices under resting conditions (Fig. 3A), although we cannot verify whether c-fos induction occurs in astrocytes, neurons, or both. This finding aligns with prior study reporting elevated c-fos mRNA levels in mouse brain samples following optogenetic astrocyte stimulation42. However, this c-fos induction was absent in the 5xFAD model (Fig. 3A), pointing to a disease-associated impairment of stimulation-driven transcriptional responses. We conclude that while chemogenetic stimulation of astrocytes per se triggers the initial molecular responses, it can be insufficient to trigger the downstream cascades necessary for long-term modulation of synaptic plasticity. The well-established role of c-fos as a marker of cellular activity extends beyond the plasticity-related processes and includes its activation during inflammatory responses in pathological conditions65. This can be relevant for the 5xFAD model, which is characterized by chronic neuroinflammation66. Given that baseline c-fos expression may already be elevated due to ongoing inflammatory processes in 5xFAD mice, an additional chemogenetic stimulation in our experiments may not produce a further detectable increase. Conversely, the lack of C21-induced c-fos elevation in 5xFAD slices may also point to deficit in downstream signal transduction either in the chemogenetically stimulated astrocytes themselves, or in the neurons activated by chemogenetically stimulated astrocytes, or both.

Recent studies using chemogenetic and optogenetic approaches have established that astrocytes can modulate long-term synaptic plasticity37,38,42. Consistent with this, we found that activation of the Gq-coupled pathway in hippocampal astrocytes enhanced the TBS-induced long-term synaptic potentiation in healthy WT mice (Fig. 2C, D). However, the efficacy of targeted astrocytic Gq-mediated modulation appeared limited in advanced-stage AD (Fig. 2E, F), a finding that contrasts with other studies37,38, probably because the fEPSPs amplitude was already significantly enhanced after the TBS stimulation.

Although our results, showing preserved LTP in 5xFAD mice, appear to contradict the widespread reports of impaired LTP90–92, this is consistent with findings from other mouse AD models and older animals that report normal or even enhanced synaptic plasticity93–96. Several studies91,97 have also demonstrated the presence of LTP at later stages of disease. This apparent discrepancy may arise from differences in stimulation protocols, as well as the nonlinear, lineage-dependent progression of the pathological changes in AD models. In our AD model, 7–8-month-old 5xFAD mice represent an established yet still dynamically remodeling stage, in which plaque load, astrocytic Ca²⁺ dysregulation, neuronal excitability, behavioral impairment, and LTP deficits may not progress in parallel18,22,90,98. This asynchrony allows preserved or even enhanced potentiation before later-stage synaptic failure becomes dominant.

AD-related remodeling undergoes stage- and context-dependent dysregulation. Recent evidence indicates that astrocytic calcium signaling shifts from hypoactivity at early AD stages (3 month), which can be rescued by hM3Dq-mediated Ca2+ enhancement, to hyperactivity at later AD stages (6–8 months)18,22,99, which may prevent additional hM3Dq-mediated Ca2+ enhancement. Several studies suggested that β-amyloid induces pathological astrocytes’ remodeling100–102. In 5–9 months old animals, this included calcium hyperactivity in reactive astrocytes surrounding amyloid plaques, mediated by metabotropic Gq-coupled receptors22,103. At the molecular level, this hyperactive phenotype was associated with the upregulation of the key Gq-coupled signaling cascade components abundant in astrocytes, including metabotropic glutamate receptors mGluR5, purinergic receptors P2Y1, and their downstream IP3 receptors100–104. Blockade of these signaling pathways completely normalized astrocytic hyperactivity100,103. Given these stage-specific alterations in astrocytic conditions, future studies should directly compare astrocytic responses to chemogenetic stimulation in 5xFAD slices from mice of different ages to determine whether Gq-pathway activation has the potential to prevent or slow down the disease progression.

We speculate that in healthy neural circuits, the ability of astrocytic hM3Dq signaling to rapidly facilitate TBS-induced synaptic plasticity (starting from 15 min post-TBS, Supplementary Fig.S4A) is likely mediated by fast processes such as gliotransmitter release or K+ uptake from the extracellular space4,5. However, this regulatory function is limited in AD pathology. Although hM3Dq bypasses endogenous receptor activation, it converges on the same intracellular Gq→PLC→IP3→Ca²⁺ machinery used by endogenous astrocytic receptors. In 5xFAD mice, mGluR5 availability dynamically changed with disease progression, increasing at 6 months105, and declining at 9 months of age106. Thus, in advanced AD, chronic enhancement of astrocytic mGluR5 or P2Y1-mediated signaling may drive endogenous Gq signaling in a state of tonic hyperactivation, in which downstream effectors (e.g., IP3-mediated Ca2+ release from ER) operate near maximal capacity and become unresponsive to further external stimuli. A compelling future direction would be to apply selective inhibitors of mGluR5 or P2Y1 receptors at different stages of pathology to determine (1) which signaling cascade is crucial for driving the observed rearrangements, and (2) whether its blockade can reduce or eliminate the pathological phenotype.

Therefore, our results support the hypothesis that astrocyte’s efficacy to modulate neuronal functions was reduced in AD. We therefore conclude that AD progression disrupted astrocyte-neuron communications, resulting in functional alterations in neural circuits at both synaptic and molecular levels, as detailed in the following section.

DREADD agonists can elicit off-target effects

The most popular DREADD agonist clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) was considered inert to endogenous receptors and was therefore extensively used in numerous studies, often at concentrations of 10 µM and higher, to selectively stimulate various signaling cascades in the DREADD-expressing astrocytes43,50,55,56. However, growing evidences showed that CNO at high doses (including 10 µM) has significant off-target effects both in vitro and in vivo58–60,107.

To address specificity concerns, more selective DREADD agonists, including Compound 21 (C21), have been developed57,58,108. In this study, we used C21 to stimulate Gq pathway in hippocampal astrocytes expressing hM3Dq and found that at high concentration (10 µM), it induced significant enhancement of fEPSP amplitudes in resting-state brain slices in both WT (Fig. 1D, H) and 5xFAD mice (Fig. 1E, I), repeating the phenomenon of de-novo potentiation43. The observed effect was rather non-specific, as the same potentiation was elicited by high micromolar concentrations of C21 (2–10 µM) in non-transduced brain slices that disappeared when the C21 concentration was reduced to 1 µM (Fig. 1B, C). These findings suggest that the phenomenon of de-novo potentiation may require re-evaluation, as it may not be a specific astrocyte-mediated process, but rather a consequence of non-specific activation of brain cells.

Therefore, our results reinforce growing concerns regarding the specificity of DREADD agonists used at high doses. This is evident from two key observations: first, at high concentrations, C21 produced effects independently of the hM3Dq expression109, and second, 10 µM C21 masked any further potentiation in TBS-stimulated slices compared to the enhanced potentiation observed with 1 µM C21 (Fig. 2C, D). Despite these caveats, C21 remains a valuable DREADD agonist at lower concentrations. However, our study highlights the need for dose-dependent validation of agonist selectivity in chemogenetic experiments to minimize off-target effects and ensure accurate interpretation of astrocyte-mediated changes.

DREADD-based approaches for correction of pathological phenotypes

The potential of Gq-coupled pathway stimulation for the treatment of neuropathologies remains uncertain, as outcomes of its activation are highly variable depending on experimental parameters such as age, effector cell type, duration of activation, etc. Thus, direct stimulation of the neuronal Gq-coupled signaling cascade can impair memory in healthy mice in acute experiments43 or enhance amyloid deposition worsening the progression of AD in chronic experiments in 3–4 months-old 5xFAD mice34.

Indirect modulation of neuronal function by stimulation of Gq-coupled signaling cascade in astrocytes produced different effects. Acute selective stimulation of Gq-coupled signaling in astrocytes enhanced synaptic plasticity and memory performance in healthy mice38,42,43. In several AD models (3 months AppNL−F mice; 6–7 months 5xFAD mice), such stimulation normalized the disrupted neuronal connectivity that preceded amyloid depositions18 and can restore the synaptic plasticity and cognitive functions37,38. Chronic astrocyte stimulation can impair synaptic plasticity, causing LTP deficits in neural networks of healthy mice110, but improved memory performance in 5xFAD mice38. Our results add to this complex picture by demonstrating that acute chemogenetic stimulation of the Gq-coupled signaling cascade in hippocampal astrocytes enhanced LTP in brain slices of healthy mice (Fig. 2C, D), but not in an advanced-stage AD model mice (5xFAD, 7–8 months) (Fig. 2E, F). The absence of any modulatory effect in 5xFAD animals probably evidences a progressive pathological remodeling of the astrocyte-neuron communications with age. Growing evidence supports this explanation, demonstrating aberrant changes in neuronal activity and astrocytic calcium signaling throughout the AD progression that likely contribute to bidirectional dysregulation of neuroglial communications16,18,22,99.

Recent studies indicate that calcium signaling in astrocytes is dynamic over the course of AD, changing from hypoactivity in the early stages to hyperactivity in the late stages18,22,99, which is likely associated with their progressive reactive phenotype111. We hypothesized that the lack of effect from chemogenetic astrocyte stimulation in 5xFAD slices (Fig. 2C, D) is due to the initial state of hyperactivation. This pre-existing astrocyte hyperactivation may limit or alter the release of gliotransmitters, or any other metabolic or homeostatic changes, consecuently preventing any further LTP enhancement. This assumption was supported by the qPCR analysis of c-fos expression, the indicator of cell activity, which showed that a single C21 (1 µM) stimulation of Gq pathway in astrocytes failed to elevate c-fos mRNA in the 5xFAD brain slices in contrast to WT slices (Fig. 3A).

Several studies suggest that astrocytic calcium signaling regulates neuronal activity and its hyperactivation can contribute to dysregulation of neural circuits in the AD mouse models30,112,113. Our experimental data indirectly confirmed this assumption showing an altered neural network activity in the advanced-stage AD model. This manifested in the increased amplitudes of evoked synaptic responses in 5xFAD slices compared to WT (TBS groups), reaching levels of chemogenetically stimulated slices of both genotypes (C21 + TBS group) (Supplementary Fig. S4B). These results suggest that synaptic responses in 5xFAD slices are close to maximal levels (Supplementary Fig.S4B). At the molecular level, this synaptic hyperexcitability was accompanied by an altered mRNA expression of cell activity marker c-fos, showing no differences between unstimulated (control) and stimulated groups (TBS, C21 + TBS) (Fig. 3A). Collectively, our results likely reflect a pathological excitation/inhibition imbalance in the 5xFAD neural circuits. Our findings align with existing evidence of neural circuits hyperactivation in both AD models and in patients16,18,19 that can be accompanied by an aberrant c-fos expression114,115.

We conclude that the mechanisms of neuroglial interactions were significantly altered in the familial Alzheimer’s disease model. Our data demonstrated that astrocytic Gq signaling can modulate hippocampal synaptic plasticity in a concentration- and disease-dependent manner, highlighting the need for precise spatiotemporal control of astrocytic signaling. Although astrocytes themselves do not induce de-novo potentiation in our experimental conditions, under certain conditions they can enhance activity-dependent plasticity. However, these capabilities were limited in AD model, likely due to pathological remodeling of astrocyte-neuron communications.

Therefore, the obtained results underscore both the potential and limitations of targeting astrocyte Gq signaling to correct pathological phenotypes in neurodegenerative disorders. Future studies should explore the perspectives of using other DREADD receptors to modulate different signaling pathways in astrocytes during disease progression, focusing on the analysis of specific signaling molecules involved in neuroglial communications and the resulting consequences for synaptic plasticity. These findings may redefine the role of astrocytes in plasticity and cognitive processes in health and disease.

Materials and methods

Animals

The experiments were carried out in 7- to 8-month-old male B6SJL-Tg (5xFAD) mice, a transgenic mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) that carries five familial mutations in the APP/PSEN1 genes, and their age-matched wild-type littermates B6SJL (WT), used as a control (Stock# 034840; The Jackson Lab; USA). The colony was maintained by crossing hemizygous transgenic males with healthy females. Genotyping of the animals was performed at the age of 1 month by isolating genomic DNA from tail tip using phenol-chloroform extraction and subsequent PCR with specific primers according to the recommended protocol (Supplementary Table 1).

All mice were kept under identical conditions, with free access to food and water, and at a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive of 24 November 1986 (86/609/EEC) on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Higher Nervous Activity and Neurophysiology of RAS (ethical approval №04, 29 May 2024).

Plasmids

For selective expression of the hM3Dq receptor in astrocytes we used the commercially available adeno-associated virus (AAV) plasmid pZac2.1 GfaABC1D_hM3D-mCherry SV40 (Addgene, #92284), which expresses the Gq-coupled DREADD receptor hM3Dq under the control of the glial GfaABC1D promoter. This plasmid served as the payload for generating recombinant AAV2/5 viruses. However, initial validation revealed that the expressed in astrocytes hM3Dq receptors formed large clusters in cell bodies and major processes (Supplementary Fig.S1A) that may impair their membrane distribution and compromise their functionality. To address this, we replaced red fluorescent protein mCherry (which we observed to form clusters) with yellow fluorescent protein EYFP from the available donor plasmid GfaABC1D_Opto-a1AR-EYFP using MfeI/NcoI restriction sites. The resulting construct GfaABC1D_hM3Dq-EYFP was verified by sequencing and used for AAV production.

For viral packaging, we used the commercially available AAV5 serotype plasmid (Addgene, #104964) to generate recombinant AAV2/5 viruses. All procedures were performed using the equipment at the Research Resource Center of IHNA and NPh RAS for functional brain mapping.

AAV production and purification

Virus production was performed according to previously described protocol with a minor modification42. Briefly, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with a payload plasmid, serotype 5 plasmid, and a helper plasmid using PEI reagent, then incubated for 72 h (5% CO2, 37 °C). Both culture media and the cells were collected and processed separately. Cells were harvested and subjected to freeze/thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen, while the culture media was concentrated using a Centricon Plus-70 centrifugal filter unit (Merck Millipore). The concentrated media was combined with cell lysates, treated with benzonase nuclease, and purified via iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation. The fraction enriched with viral particles was collected, filtered, and further concentrated using a 100 kDa Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit to a final volume of 100 µl. Viral titer for AAV2/5_GfaABC1D_hM3Dq-EYFP was determined by qPCR using primer pair targeting AAV2 ITR sequence (Forward: GGAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT; Reverse: CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA) and was equal to 3.7 × 1012 vg/mL.

Stereotaxic virus injection

The mice were anaesthetized with 2–5% isoflurane at a flow rate of 1 L/min in oxygen (at a maintenance level of 1.5%) during the operation. Then, 2% lidocaine was injected subcutaneously into the area of the skull above the incision site. A bilateral craniotomy was performed to access the target regions. After cleaning the skull, burr holes were drilled at the following coordinates: Anterior-posterior (AP): -2.2 mm from bregma; lateral (ML): +1.7 mm for the right hemisphere and − 1.7 mm for the left. After installing the syringe in one of the hemispheres at the desired coordinates, 1 µl of an adeno-associated virus (AAV) suspension containing GfaABC1D-hM3Dq-EYFP sequence was injected at a rate of 0.1 µl/min. The syringe was then left in place for a further 10 min after administration to minimize diffusion of the solution along the administration route. Brain slices from this hemisphere were used as the experimental group. Then, a clean, virus-free syringe was placed in the other hemisphere and left in the target area for 10 min. The brain slices obtained from sham-operated hemisphere were used as a control, non-transduced group.

Immunohistochemistry and microscopy

Animals were perfused transcardially with 4% buffered formaldehyde (Biovitrum, Russia). Brain samples were removed and post-fixed for 24 h and transferred to PBS. Coronal sections (50 μm) were cut on a vibrating blade microtome (Leica VT1000S; Leica, Germany) and mounted on PCL-coated glass slides (Biovitrum, Russia). For IHC staining, sections were permeabilized and blocked for 1.5 h at room temperature in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.01% sodium azide, 5% NGS, and 1% BSA. Primary rabbit anti-GFAP (D1F4Q) antibodies (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) were applied overnight at 4 °C in blocking solution. On the next day, sections were washed with 0.05% Tween20 in PBS (PBST) four times and then incubated with secondary goat anti-rabbit antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 546 (1:500, Invitrogen) for 3 h at room temperature. Following four final washes in PBST, slides were coverslipped with self-made Mowiol mounting medium (AppliChem, Germany) supplemented with 8% DABCO.

Microscopy was carried out using equipment of the Research Resource Center of IHNA and NPh RAS for functional brain mapping. The slices were examined using laser confocal Cerna-based microscope (Thorlabs, USA) equipped with a Nikon Plan Apo 20x/0.50 NA Ph1 DLL objective. The acquired Z-stack images were processed using ImageJ (version 1.53t, NIH).

Brain slices preparation

The mice were anaesthetized with sevoflurane (Sevorane) and decapitated at least four weeks after the virus injection. The brain was then removed and 350 μm transverse slices were cut using a vibroslicer (Leica VT1000S, Germany) in ice-cold ACSF containing (in mM): NaCl (124); KCl (3); NaH₂PO₄ (1.25); MgCl₂ (7); CaCl₂ (0.5); NaHCO₃ (26); and glucose (25), at a pH equilibrated with 95% O₂ – 5% CO₂. The slices were then placed in a chamber containing ACSF (in mM): NaCl (124), KCl (3), NaH₂PO₄ (1.25), MgCl₂ (1.3), CaCl₂ (2.5), NaHCO₃ (26) and glucose (25), at a pH equilibrated with 95% O₂ − 5% CO₂, where they were incubated at 34 °C for one hour. Slices from the transduced (AAV) and contralateral sham-operated (non-transduced, NoAAV) hemispheres were kept separately in chamber. The chamber containing the slices was then stored at room temperature. At the beginning of the experiment, hM3Dq expression in astrocytes in the CA1 field of each transduced brain slice was examined using a Keyence BZ-9000 fluorescence microscope (Keyence Corporation, Japan). Only slices with a sufficient fluorescence in the target area were used in the experiment. Slices from the NoAAV group were also checked for fluorescence and used only if it was absent.

Electrophysiological recordings

The SliceMaster system (Scientifica, UK) was used to record field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) in the hippocampus. This system was designed for the simultaneous recording of responses from several slices. The stimulator and amplifier were integrated into the SliceMaster system’s Scientifica StAmp. Multi-channel continuous data acquisition was performed using the Cambridge Electronic Design (CED) Power1401-3 A and the Spike2 analysis software. The fEPSP were filtered at a low frequency of 10 Hz, digitized and processed using a digital converter with a frequency of 5 kHz. In this system, recordings were made in parallel and under the same conditions using warm (34 °C), oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF). After recovery, the slices were transferred to individual recording chambers. A twisted nichrome bipolar stimulating electrode with a diameter of 50 μm was placed rostral to the recording electrode, close to the border between the stratum radiatum of the CA3 and CA2 regions, to stimulate the Schaffer collaterals. Field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were recorded using glass microelectrodes filled with ACSF (0.5–4 MΩ) placed in the stratum radiatum of the CA1 region. fEPSPs were evoked by stimulation every 30 s using paired stimuli of 50 µs duration with an inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of 50 ms. Initially, the stimulus intensity was adjusted to evoke fEPSPs with an amplitude of approximately 50% of the maximum amplitude, which was at least 0.2 mV. During each experiment, fEPSPs were recorded at least for 15 min under basal stimulation conditions. The slices were divided into three experimental groups: the theta-burst (TBS) group, the C21 group, and the C21 + TBS group. In the TBS group, fEPSPs were recorded under basal stimulation conditions for 30 min. After this, the slices were subjected to the theta-burst stimulation (TBS), which induced long-term potentiation. The TBS induction protocol consisted of four trains, each containing four bursts of eight stimuli at 100 Hz, delivered at 5 Hz with 30 s between each train. The standard test stimulation protocol was then resumed and fEPSPs were recorded for 90 min. In the C21 group, 1 µM of the chemogenetic activator Compound-21 (C21) was added to the ACSF after the basal stimulation conditions were applied. fEPSPs were then recorded for 120 min. In the C21 + TBS group, 1 µM of Compound-21 (C21) was added to the ACSF after the basal stimulation conditions were applied, and recordings were made for 30 min. The slices were then subjected to TBS and further fEPSPs were recorded for 90 min under the standard stimulation protocol. In the TBS and C21 + TBS groups, paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) was calculated by sequentially changing the interstimulus interval (30, 50, 100, 200, 300 and 400 ms) after the basal stimulation conditions, before TBS was applied, and 90 min after TBS. After the experiments, the slices were frozen in liquid nitrogen at -80 °C and used for mRNA extraction.

mRNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from brain slices using Qiazol (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, 800 ng of total RNA was treated with DNaseI (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Then half of the sample was taken for the first strand cDNA synthesis in reverse transcription reaction (RT+) using MMLV RT kit (Evrogen, Moscow, Russia), a mixture of random and oligo(dT) primers, and an RNase inhibitor (New England Biolabs). The remaining half of the RNA served as a no reverse transcriptase (NRT) control. Relative mRNA expression of the immediate early genes Fos, Arc, and Egr1 was assessed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using Gapdh as a reference gene as previously described42. Reactions were performed in triplicate on a CFX384 Touch Real Time System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR-green mastermix reagent (Evrogen) and a primer pairs42 at the following settings: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 42 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 61 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Data were analyzed with Bio-Rad CFX Manager software, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

The Spike2 data were processed using the Python programming language to identify the synaptic responses in each brain slice. All recording segments were initially normalized to an average value of 5 ms prior to stimulus was applied. The amplitudes of the synaptic responses to each stimulus were then determined throughout the recording. Only recordings with a baseline change of no more than 10% and paired-pulse facilitation between 1 and 2.5 were included in the analysis. Averaged data from a representative three minute period recording are shown in boxplots.

The temporal dynamics of the synaptic response amplitude were presented as mean ± s.e.m., with each point normalized to the average value of the ten-minute baseline recording before tetanization. Averaged fEPSP amplitudes, slopes, and relative mRNA expression were presented as boxplots with the median, interquartile range, and bars. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test the normality of distribution for all samples. To compare two independent groups with normally distributed data, the Student’s t-test was applied. For multiple comparisons, analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. In cases of non-normal data distribution or too small sample size, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two independent groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Significance was set at p < 0.05.
