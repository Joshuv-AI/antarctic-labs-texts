Arrested development and increased incidence of sandprawn embryonic aberrations along an intertidal human recreation gradient
Authors: K A Madell, U M Scharler, C Savage, A M L Karlson, D Pillay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthropogenic pressures are increasing in coastal ecosystems globally, yet identifying robust indicators of change and managing coastal resources can be complicated by phenotypic plasticity and differential life-history responses of key organisms. We illustrate this using biogeochemical and sandprawn (Kraussillichirus kraussi) response metrics along a human recreation gradient (trampling, sandprawn harvesting) in a South African lagoonal ecosystem. Benthic compaction, oxygen depletion and high porewater ammonia concentrations were associated with greatest recreation intensity. Sandprawn abundance was similar across the recreation gradient and body condition was counter-intuitively greater in areas with maximum recreation, but with higher frequencies of embryonic aberrations and arrested development. These findings suggest different vulnerabilities of life-history stages of sandprawns to recreation, with embryonic stages being highly susceptible. We suggest that embryonic aberrations and developmental changes in endobenthic crustaceans may be sensitive bioindicators of recreation-induced changes in sedimentary systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-78149-0.Subject terms: Ecosystem ecology, Ecosystem services, Urban ecology

Introduction

Coastal biotopes are amongst the most productive and ecologically important ecosystems globally, with the provision of feeding grounds, nurseries and refugia being critical for marine species1–4. From a human perspective, coastal ecosystems are of great financial, subsistence, recreational and cultural value5. These features have historically attracted people to coastal areas and contributed to localised population growth6, to the extent that coasts are currently the most densely populated regions of the world7. However, the potential for further urbanisation, industrialisation, and resource exploitation to amplify coastal ecosystem stress is concerning8. Specifically, synergisms between local-scale stressors and broader global change processes typically compound coastal degradation and erode resilience rapidly7,8 and thus pose significant ecological and management challenges. It is unsurprising that some of the greatest and most rapid ecosystem declines have occurred in coastal zones globally9.

Sedimentary ecosystems such as beaches typify the points raised thus far. These systems are key sites for trade, tourism and recreation but also provide essential ecosystem services such as water filtration and urban infrastructure protection10. Beaches dominate global coastlines, comprising roughly 70% of coasts11, within which recreation is central to tourism and local economies. Beaches are arguably the most utilised of ecosystems for recreation, with few other ecosystems experiencing comparable levels12. However, the limited research conducted on sandy beaches relative to more iconic coastal systems (e.g. coral reefs, mangroves, kelp forests), together with a lack of appreciation of the ecological significance of beaches, can impede beach management10,11,13,14. This point is highly relevant in dissipative, ultra-dissipative or tidal flat coastal ecosystems, given their typically high biological diversity and biomass, and the critical role of intrinsic biological interactions in driving their ecological functionality, relative to reflective beach ecosystems15. Due to their sheltered natures, and typical high biomass of infauna15, dissipative-to-tidal flat beaches are attractive as recreational areas, including for bait harvesting of benthic bioturbating species. Bioturbation is a key biotic driver of ecosystem functionality in sheltered beaches such as tidal flats16, mediating diverse processes such as bentho-pelagic coupling, nutrient fluxes, productivity and benthic biogeochemical cycles17. Endobenthic axiid and gebiid crustaceans are notable bioturbators in such beaches, due to exceptional sediment manipulation rates, occupation of large expanses of habitat, burrow construction to great depths and occurrence at high densities16,17. These crustaceans are, however, also used as bait organisms globally, and are also subjected to recreational trampling, leading to studies raising concerns about population- and ecosystem-level repercussions17–19.

As with most studies involving coastal resource management, the central challenge in identifying consequences of recreational activity for endobenthic crustaceans is the timely detection of changes prior to population and ecosystem declines manifesting20. Developing early warning systems is thus key, but previous studies have not always been unequivocal in detecting responses of endobenthic crustaceans to recreational activities. While some research has shown population and size-structure changes in targeted endobenthic crustaceans under intense harvesting and trampling regimes21–23, others have not detected change24. Biological condition- and growth indices have also been used in the past to understand consequences of recreational activities20,25, but again, outcomes have not always been clear20. The use of individual-level metrics in stressor detection can be complicated by organismal phenotypic plasticity and life-history trade-offs (e.g. between survival, maintenance and reproduction) that have evolved to maximise fitness in changing or stressful environmental conditions26–28. Importantly, plasticity in resource allocation under changing conditions may generate counter-intuitive responses20,29. This is illustrated by research on water striders for example, in which stable environmental conditions were associated with reduced longevity of females relative to unpredictable conditions, due to the prioritisation of reproduction in the former scenario29. Thus, an understanding of organismal trade-offs and resource allocation shifts between survival, maintenance and reproduction is likely to facilitate detection and interpretation of endobenthic crustacean responses to recreational activity. Identifying appropriate life-history stages that robustly signal environmental changes can similarly improve detection of recreation effects on endobenthic crustaceans.

The consequences of recreation for endobenthic crustacean reproduction have been explored to a limited extent, with studies using changes in female fecundity as a biological indicator25. However, embryological changes in endobenthic crustaceans hold significant potential to also signal consequences of recreational harvesting and trampling. This is due the significance of embryogenesis in biological life cycles30,31 and the hypersensitivity of embryos to environmental changes, making them highly susceptible to stressors during development31,32. Under unfavourable conditions, embryos may develop malformations and atypical features31,33,34 that can be used to signal environmental change. Similarly, under stressful conditions, rates of embryological development may decelerate (i.e. arrested development) signalling deteriorating environmental conditions35. The expression of aberrations and arrested development in embryos of endobenthic crustaceans may thus hold potential to be sensitive indicators of recreational harvesting and trampling. This is because harvesting and trampling during bait collection have been shown to induce changes in benthic biogeochemistry, with oxygen depletion and sulphide production being reported21,22. These biogeochemical changes may lead to impaired development of eggs and embryos of endobenthic crustaceans, as has been shown for other invertebrates36–38.

We made use of a putative recreational gradient in Langebaan Lagoon on the west coast of South Africa to assess spatial changes in benthic biogeochemical conditions, and sandprawn (Kraussillichirus kraussi) population, growth, and reproductive metrics over a one-year period in tidal flat beach ecosystems. Our objectives were to (1) understand benthic ecosystem and sandprawn responses to recreational activity and (2) the relative sensitivity of sandprawn metrics to recreational harvesting and bait collecting. Within southern Africa, sandprawns are widely distributed from Mozambique (east coast) to Namibia (west coast), where they form a major constituent of benthic ecosystems, including tidal flats in estuaries, embayments, and lagoons39. Various studies have highlighted the significance of sandprawn ecosystem engineering activities in modulating pelagic and benthic resource flows and assemblage structure17,40,41, but sandprawns are also harvested as bait along the South African coast42. Langebaan Lagoon is an ideal model system in southern Africa to advance understanding of recreational effects on sandprawns (and other endobenthic crustaceans) and benthic ecosystems. Unlike the majority of estuarine systems in the country, where sandprawns are abundant39, confounding effects of spatial changes in environmental conditions (e.g. salinity) are negligible in Langebaan Lagoon, due to the absence of riverine inputs in the system43. This feature, together with diurnal tides, result in salinity in the lagoon being predominantly marine (≈ 35). We hypothesized that benthic oxygen depletion (measured using image analysis of photographs of colour changes in sediment cores among sites), would be greatest at sites experiencing high recreation levels due to a reduction in bioturbation by sandprawns. We also hypothesised that sandprawn abundance and body condition would be lower, and embryonic aberrations higher, under intense recreational activity. Lastly, we expected embryonic changes to be better indicators of recreational activity than sandprawn abundance and body condition.

Materials and methods

Study site

Langebaan Lagoon (33°10’S, 18°05’E) is a permanently open, marine-dominated embayment located within the West Coast National Park, South Africa. It is approximately 15 km long and 4 km wide (Fig. 1) and is a priority conservation area that supports diverse marine and estuarine assemblages22,23,42,44.In 1973, the Sea Fisheries Act 58 divided Langebaan Lagoon into three spatially exclusive zones that permitted contrasting degrees of human recreational activities (Fig. 1). Zone A is a multipurpose recreational area that permits human presence and bait collection42. Zone B permits limited human presence; however, bait collecting is prohibited42. Zone C is a sanctuary that does not permit human presence or bait collection42. Sand- (Kraussillichirus kraussi) and mudprawns (Upogebia africana) are gravimetrically dominant benthic organisms in Langebaan Lagoon, accounting for approximately 48% of the total benthic biomass22,45. In Zone A, the focal area of this research, sandprawns are gravimetrically dominant (likely due to the sandy sediment), and the main benthic organism collected for bait.

Sampling design

Sampling took place over a year for two seasons (winter: July/August 2022; austral summer: January 2023), within Zone A at Klein Oesterwal (Fig. 1). We utilised a putative gradient (based on our past observations) in human numbers in Zone A to quantify repercussions of trampling and bait harvesting for benthic ecosystem- and sandprawn metrics. Five mid-intertidal sites, were sampled, starting at Site 1 (33°06’47” S, 18°02’37” E) and ending ± 1 km from the start of Zone B (33°07’02” S, 18°02’57” E; Site 5, Fig. 1). Sites were approximately 200 m apart and were divided into 10 equidistant plots (20 m apart), from which replicate samples were collected. Sampling began at low spring tide (for benthic microalgae, sediment particle size and organic matter content, redox conditions), progressed over incoming tide (for all sandprawn data) and concluded at high tide (for water column variables and phytoplankton biomass). Sediment penetrability and porewater nutrient concentrations were determined per site in summer of 2024 when more funding was allocated to the project. All response data were collected over two days. Our permitting conditions precluded sampling in Zones B and C; we therefore focussed our sampling to Zone A. While this restricted the spatial scale of data collection, it did minimise spatial co-variance associated with sampling at larger scales. Sediment particle size for example, changes across zones in the lagoon, which can influence sandprawn populations and potentially confound the interpretation of recreational effects46.

Data collection

Number of people per site were determined using a scan-sampling method47, which entailed the number of visitors per site being counted at 30-min intervals for approximately 6 h (from low- to high tide) for two days during sample collection.

Sediment cores (diameter = 2 cm, depth = 1 cm) were collected at three (of ten) plots per site for organic matter content (n = 3 per site) and sediment particle size (n = 3 per site) determinations per season. For particle size analyses, sediment cores were dried (100 °C; 48 h), followed by granulometric analysis using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 SM Auto-analyser (grain size range: 0.02–2000 μm). For sediment organic matter content determination, cores were dried (100 °C; 48 h) and weighed (A&D GR-202 balance; precision: 0.00000 g), followed by combustion in a muffle furnace (600 °C; 3 h). Organic matter content was determined as the change in mass between pre- and post-combusted sediment and expressed as a percentage of total sediment dry weight21. Due to funding limitations, sediment redox conditions were assessed using sediment colour as a proxy48. Sediment cores (diameter = 3.2 cm, depth = ± 20 cm) were collected at five plots per site per season using a clear Perspex corer. Each sediment core was photographed (iPhone 13 mini; 2340 × 1080-pixel resolution) immediately after extraction (while in the Perspex corer), with care being taken to ensure that cores were held vertically and photographed from a standard distance of 1 m. Photographs were thereafter analysed using image processing software (GNU Image Manipulation Program; GIMP). Images were auto-colour corrected and pixel numbers quantified on a grayscale range from 0 (black) to 255 (white). Pixel frequency histograms were thereafter created from which a modal peak per sediment core was calculated in GIMP49.

A penetrometer (adapted from Siebert and Branch50) was used to measure sediment penetrability at 10 plots per site. The instrument comprised a steel spike (length = 1 m, diameter = 1 cm) that was raised 20 cm above the sediment surface, and then released to penetrate the sediment. The depth (in cm) that the spike penetrated the sediment was used as an indication of sediment penetrability (compactness). The steel spike was held in place by a wooden frame with guide rings that allowed the spike to always be released perpendicularly to the sediment surface.

A 50 ml syringe with a 63 μm filter fitted over the inhalant opening was pushed into the sediment (15 cm deep) to collect porewater samples from three plots per site. Concentrations of porewater ammonia, phosphate, nitrate, and nitrite were determined by the accredited laboratory facilities of the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), using a nutrient autoanalyzer.

Sandprawn abundance, estimated as catch per unit effort (CPUE), was quantified during incoming tides (50 cm water depth) from ten sediment cores collected from 5 plots per site (n = 50 cores per site per season). Cores were collected using standard prawn pumps (total length = 90 cm; diameter = 5 cm; as used by recreational sandprawn harvesters) in areas devoid of obvious signs of recent sediment disturbance caused by bait collecting, and thereafter sieved (mesh size = 2 mm). Retained sandprawns with a carapace length (CL) < 5 mm were classified as juveniles, while the rest were sexed (examination of the first pleopods). Sandprawns with a CL greater than 6.5 mm were selected for sexual dimorphism analysis, since sexual dimorphism is not evident in smaller individuals51. Major chela length was used to assess whether sexual dimorphism varied among sampling sites, since chela length is known to be sexually dimorphic in sandprawns51. Males were preserved in jars containing 70% ethanol (per site) while females were individually stored in Ziploc bags (+ 70% ethanol) per site to ensure that all eggs or embryos were preserved with the corresponding female. In the laboratory, sandprawn CL, and major chela length (MCL) were measured with a digital calliper (0.1 mm, Mitutoyo 500 Absolute Digimatic Calliper). MCL was determined by laterally flattening the appendage and measuring from the start of the merus to the tip of the dactyl. For body condition index determination, sandprawns were dried (60 °C; 48 h) and weighed (A&D GR-202 balance, precision = 0.00000 g). Body condition was determined by dividing sandprawn dry weight by corresponding CL values20.

All embryos were removed from female sandprawns using blunt-nosed forceps and pipettes (3 ml), with care taken to prevent damaging embryos, following Jackson30. Fecundity was determined by quantifying the number of embryos per female. Embryonic developmental stages and aberration types were quantified using a stereomicroscope, following the criteria outlined by Jackson30 (summarised in Table 1; Fig. 2).

Water temperature, dissolved oxygen concentration, pH and salinity were measured per site (n = 1) using a CTD profiler (YSI 6600). Phytoplankton biomass (as chlorophyll-a) was determined from a 100 ml water sample collected per site (n = 1), using a fluorometer (Turner Designs Trilogy). Benthic microalgal biomass (n = 10) was determined using a BenthoTorch (BBE Moldaenke), which is an in situ optical tool developed for benthic microalgal quantification. The instrument provided area-specific biomass measurements for diatoms, cyanobacteria, and green algae, which were summed, to provide total microalgal biomass values per measurement.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the data analysis platform R52. Prior to analysis, data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots, Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests. Two-factor Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed using the ‘anova’ function with the ‘car’ package to determine whether abiotic (sediment particle size & organic matter content, mean pixel values for sediment redox conditions) and biotic (sandprawn body condition, sandprawn CPUE, human numbers) response variables differed among sites, seasons, and their interaction. A three-factor Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA; covariate = sandprawn carapace length) was used to determine whether major chela length (as an indicator of sexual dimorphism) varied among sexes, sites, seasons, and their interactions. Since no gravid female sandprawns were recorded in summer, a one-factor ANCOVA (covariate = carapace length) was performed to determine whether fecundity differed among the sites. One-factor ANOVAs were performed to determine whether types of aberrations varied among sites. A Kruskal-Wallis Rank Sum Test was used to quantify whether the proportion of embryos per development stages (stages 1–5; using the ‘coin’ package) and sediment penetrability differed among the five sites, since the data were non-parametric, even after the application of data transformations. Post-hoc (Tukey HSD test for parametric data; Dunn test for non-parametric data) tests were used to identify significant within-site differences.

In terms of benthic environmental conditions, sediment organic matter content did not vary among sites or seasons (p > 0.1; Table 2). However, among-site variance in sediment particle size was recorded (p = 0.0017; Table 2), with grain sizes in Sites 1 and 2 being smaller than at other sites. Mean pixel values of photographs of sediment cores differed among sites (p = 0.039), with a significant site-by-season interaction (p = 0.012; Table 2). Pixel histograms indicated darker (lower near pixel values) modal grayscale intensities at Site 1 and lighter intensities towards Site 5 (higher near pixel values; Table 3; Supplementary Figs. 1 & 2). Sediment pixel frequencies also appeared to be greater in summer than winter, especially in Site 1 (Table 3; Supplementary Figs. 1 & 2). Water column conditions and microphytobenthic biomass varied minimally among the sampling sites, but water temperature increases in summer were evident (Table 3). Sediment penetrability differed among sites (χ24,45 = 23.32, p = 0.0001), with values generally increasing from Site 1 to 5. Porewater ammonia levels similarly varied significantly among sites (F3,8 = 5.52, p = 0.023), with Site 1 values being roughly 4 times higher than the site furthest away (Table 3). Porewater phosphate levels did not differ among sites (F3,8 = 0.075, p = 0.971), while nitrate (< 5 µg/L) and nitrite levels (< 1 µg/L) were below detection limits at all sites.

Sandprawn abundance (as CPUE) did not vary statistically across the five sampling sites (Table 2) but was greater in summer than winter (p < 0.01; Table 3). Total, male, female, and juvenile abundance did not vary between sites. However, seasonal variation in total, female and juvenile CPUE was significant, with greater values recorded in summer (p < 0.01 for both; Table 3). ANCOVA indicated that neither site nor season influenced variance in sandprawn major chela length (MCL; sandprawn carapace length = covariate; Table 4). Sandprawn sex did however explain variance in MCL, with greater values recorded for males than females (p < 0.0001; Table 4, Supplementary Figs. 3 & 4). Interestingly, adjusted means (from ANCOVA analysis) indicated that for both seasons, sex differences in MCL were smallest at Site 1, with sexual dimorphism increasing towards Site 5 (Table 5). A reduction in MCL differences between sexes at Site 1 was visually evident when MCL was standardised by projecting to a consistent lower and upper limit per season (Supplementary Figs. 3 & 4). Generally, adjusted mean female MCL was greatest in Site 1 and decreased to Site 5 for both seasons, with the opposite trend occurring for males (Table 5).

Discussion

Our data for human visitation quantitatively supported in situ observations of a recreational gradient in Zone A of Langebaan Lagoon, with Site 1 being the focus of most recreation, especially in summer, and sites 3 to 5 being virtually unvisited (Table 2). This non-linear gradient in human visitation was likely maintained by proximity to human conveniences, given that Site 1 was closest to the public parking area, restrooms, and the restaurant. Our data for pelagic abiotic (water temperature, dissolved oxygen concentration, salinity) and trophic conditions (microphytobenthic- and phytoplankton biomass, sediment organic matter content) indicated similar values across the recreational activity gradient, suggesting limited co-variance of these variables with recreation. Spatial covariance of environmental conditions in parallel with recreational activity can impede understanding of recreation consequences for beach ecosystems12,53. Yet, the limited covariance in our study suggests that spatial changes in sandprawn and benthic ecosystem metrics that we detected were likely linked to human recreational activity. However, given the correlative nature of our study, we cannot discount the potential for covariance of variables outside of those measured to also explain sandprawn and benthic biogeochemical trends that we recorded.

We did, however, record a general increase in sediment particle size from Site 1 to 5, with sediment ranging between fine- (Sites 1 and 2) to medium sand54 (Sites 3 and 5). While this granulometric shift may be coincidental, it is probably a response to spatial differences in the intensity of recreational trampling and sandprawn harvesting across our sampling sites. Prior research in Langebaan Lagoon and Coronet Bay, Australia, documented similar sedimentological shifts to finer particle sizes following trampling and harvesting of sandprawns and ghost shrimp21,42. This granulometric shift was suggested to arise directly from benthic habitats becoming depressed due to trampling-induced compaction and/or indirectly, through a reduction in sandprawn bioturbation21,42. Consistent with these findings, our penetrometer data indicated that the benthos of Site 1 was the most compacted of sampling sites and Site 5 the least. At Site 1, field observations indicated limited mound formation by sandprawns, despite their burrow openings being visible, suggesting bioturbation impairment (Supplementary Fig. S5). This benthic state contrasts with that of Site 5, which apart from being more porous, had irregular benthic topography that is typical of mound deposition and bioturbation activity of sandprawns (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Contrary to our hypothesis, sandprawn CPUE data showed that sandprawn abundance was not significantly different between Sites 1 and 5, suggesting that reductions in mound formation at Site 1 may be behaviourally mediated (not density-mediated), probably due to greater sediment compaction and associated secondary consequences such as oxygen depletion and high ammonia levels (Table 3). In support of this mechanism, mesocosm experiments by Wiesebron et al.55 have shown that sediments with high bulk density (compact sediment) were associated with slower burrowing rates of macrofauna, reduced luminophore transport (reduced bioturbation) and shallower burrow depth55. Research in an intertidal New Zealand estuary56 demonstrated that in response to variance in sediment grain size, an infaunal crustacean (Austrohelice crassa) constructed burrows with different structural attributes and displayed different sediment reworking rates. This research highlights the context dependence of bioturbation in soft sediments.

Sediment colouration has previously been used to indicate redox state48,57, based on the premise that darker sediment reflects reduced conditions (associated with hypoxia/anoxia) and the presence of sulphide compounds such as iron- (FeS) and hydrogen sulphide (H2S), which are products of anaerobic metabolism16. In our study, changes in sediment colouration generally corresponded with the recreational gradient across the sampling sites, with mean colouration shifting from darker at Site 1 to lighter at Site 5, as hypothesised. Additionally, sediment cores shifted to darker coloration (especially at Site 1) in summer under increased recreational activity (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. S6). The darker sediment coloration at Site 1 aligns with our penetrometer data, suggesting that recreational activity decreases sediment oxygenation through sediment compaction. Benthic oxygen depletion has been reported previously in response to trampling and bait harvesting. Wynberg and Branch22 for example, reported the appearance of a dark sulphide layer 3 cm below the sediment surface following experimental removal of sandprawns, whereas control sediment was uniformly light to a depth of 50–60 cm. Direct measurements of redox potential confirmed localised oxygen depletion following removal of ghost shrimp (Trypaea australiensis) in Australia21. Apart from oxygen and compaction-related changes, our porewater data indicated maximum ammonia levels in Site 1 (4 times greater than site furthest away), perhaps indicating localised impairment of nitrogen cycling. Overall, our abiotic data coupled with previous research21–23,42 suggest that sandflat beaches subjected to intense trampling and bait collecting may shift to a state characterised by sediment compaction, oxygen depletion, reduced bioturbation and high ammonia levels.

Our finding of similar sandprawn abundance (CPUE) along the recreational gradient contrasts with that of Wynberg and Branch42, who reported a reduction in sandprawn densities at sites permitting bait harvesting, based on burrow opening counts. Declines in densities of Callichirus major58,59 in Brazil have also been reported using direct ghost-shrimp counts. However, Skilleter et al.24 did not find any effect of harvesting on Trypaea australiensis using in situ comparative and experimental approaches (direct ghost-shrimp counts). The statistically insignificant spatial differences in sandprawn-abundance in our study may suggest that sandprawn harvesting and benthic trampling negligibly influences Site 1 sandprawn abundance. This is in spite of previous research in Langebaan Lagoon reporting that 31% of bait collectors (n = 115) that were interviewed as part of a survey exceeded the legal harvesting limit of 50 sandprawns/day/harvestor60. The lack of sandprawn abundance declines at Site 1, despite it experiencing highest visitor numbers, especially in summer (Table 3), may relate to recreational activity generally being localised to a linear distance of roughly 200 m (distance between sites 1 and 2). It is therefore plausible that colonisation from surrounding sites may replenish Site 1 sandprawn stocks and offset harvesting/trampling losses. In relation to Site 1, sites 2 (to a lesser degree), 3, 4 and 5, were virtually absent of visitors during our sampling, implying a corresponding near absence of harvesting/trampling. Sites 2 to 5 occupy roughly 80% of our sampling area, suggesting that these sites may provide viable sandprawn source populations for colonisation of Site 1, at least at a local scale.

We also found counter-intuitively that sandprawn body condition was greatest at Site 1 relative to other sites, despite this site experiencing greatest visitor numbers. However, this finding is similar to that reported by Normark et al.20, who recorded greater sandprawn body condition in Zone A of Langebaan Lagoon compared with Zone C. Greater sandprawn body condition in Site 1 might be an adaptive response to benthic condition at this site, where greater biomass per unit length may aid in burrowing in more compacted sediment. Greater body condition could result from a shift in resource/effort allocation, in which body survival/maintenance is prioritised at the expense of reproduction, as has been suggested in prior research to be a general evolutionary response to stress and/or resource limitation61–63. It is also possible that trampling and bait harvesting at Site 1 may indirectly benefit sandprawns. Greater levels of sedimentary anaerobic bacteria at Site 1 for example, may increase trophic resource available to sandprawns for consumption.

Spatial trends in sandprawn reproductive metrics that we recorded are indicative of reproductive impairment with increasing visitor numbers. Predicted maximum differences and adjusted means of major chela length (MCL) indicated a reduction in sexual dimorphism at Site 1 (Supplementary Figs. 3 & 4), with female (adjusted mean) MCLs decreasing from Sites 1 to 5 and the opposite occurring for males, in both summer and winter (Table 5). Since the expression of sexually selected traits can be resource-intensive, involving substantial production and maintenance costs62,64,65, the reduction in sandprawn sexual dimorphism at Site 1 may indicate a trade-off, in which investment in reproduction is reduced while maintenance/survival is prioritised. The greater physical condition of sandprawn at Site 1 but weaker sexual dimorphism is suggestive of the latter. While spatial changes in sexual dimorphism were not statistically supported, trends are worth highlighting given that they align with those for sandprawn embryological changes.

Two other patterns in our data support the suggestion of maintenance/survival being prioritised at Site 1 (greatest visitor numbers) at the expense of reproduction in sandprawns. Firstly, the frequency of aberrations in sandprawn embryos were greatest at Site 1 but declined to Site 5. Secondly, embryos were at earlier developmental stages at Site 1 relative to Site 5, suggesting arrested development33–35,66. These findings are consistent with the idea that background stressors prevalent during prenatal development profoundly impact embryo and offspring development61, either through maternal transmission of stress hormones (glucocorticoids and catecholamines)61 or environment contaminants directly impairing egg/embryo physiology61. Studies quantifying stressor effects on sandprawn (K. kraussi) embryos are rare, but two studies have reported impairment of embryonic development by crude oils and metal contaminants30,32. To the best of our knowledge, recreation consequences for embryonic developmental changes in sandprawns or other endobenthic crustacean have not been researched previously. Overall, our findings for sandprawn embryo traits in relation to recreation effects align with considerations that (1) reproductive markers are direct indicators of environmental change67 and that (2) reproductive propagules (especially of external fertilisers) are more sensitive to environmental stressors and contaminants than adults68.

The greater incidence of embryo aberrations and arrested development in sandprawns under high recreation activity could be a product of localised benthic oxygen depletion (inferred from greater sediment core darkness) and high ammonia levels. Oxygen depletion has been reported to impair embryonic development in copepods36, the American horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus)69, brachyuran crabs (Cancer setosus and Homalaspis plana)70, oriental river prawn (Macrobrachium nipponense)71 and amphipods (Monoporeia affinis)31. In a non-crustacean example, greater levels of malformations, decreased oxygen consumption and active metabolic suppression were recorded in embryos of European squid (Loligo vulgaris) in response to temperature-induced hypoxia35. It would seem that under low oxygen conditions, metabolic demands associated with embryonic development are suppressed to conserve resources and prioritise survival35,36. Furthermore, invertebrate eggs under hypoxic stress may swell to maximise oxygen diffusion by increasing surface area, but the consequence is that egg membrane thickness is reduced72, thereby risking membrane rupture. As an example of parental transmission of stress, Sun et al.71 reported that the first filial generation of offspring of M. nipponense exposed to hypoxia exhibited abnormal embryonic development, decreased hatching success and larval development, despite that generation not directly being exposed to hypoxia. Sulphide production - an indirect outcome of hypoxia/anoxia, can also induce oxidative stress and cause malformations in embryo-larval stages73. Lastly, high ammonia concentrations are toxic to decapod crustaceans74, and a cause of developmental impairment in marine invertebrates75,76. Interestingly, tolerance to ammonia has been shown to increase from post-larval, to juvenile and adult stages in Macrobrachium amazonicum77, suggesting that ammonia intolerance may intensify during early stages of development.

The discolouration of sandprawn egg membranes that we recorded at sites with greatest recreational activity may indicate impairment of membrane functionality. Typically, egg membranes function to protect embryos from environmental stressors, but are also essential for ionic regulation78. Toxins such as hydrogen sulphide are known to be highly permeable to cell membranes79, with toxicity potentially leading to impaired membrane function and causing discolouration. Jackson30 showed that sandprawn larvae that hatched from opaque egg membranes did not survive past the first moult, with larvae being less mobile and remaining “curled up”, with their tails bent and their heads appearing rounded30.

Concluding perspectives and study implications

Our data show that in Zone A of Langebaan Lagoon, sediment compaction, oxygen depletion and high ammonia levels characterise sediments affected by recreational trampling and bait harvesting, with evidence of bioturbation impairment. These benthic changes are not reflected in abundance and condition metrics for adult sandprawns but are strongly tracked by spatial changes in sandprawn embryological development. Based on these findings, we suggest that variance in embryo developmental traits in endobenthic crustaceans may be more sensitive bioindicators of recreational activity than population or condition metrics, as we hypothesised. While embryo malformations have been used as early warning systems in some research fields (e.g. environmental contamination80), they have rarely been used in ecological assessments of physical disturbance such as recreational trampling and harvesting of endobenthic crustaceans.

The sensitivity that we report for sandprawn embryos suggests a susceptibility of this life history component to recreational trampling and harvesting. In systems where recreation is unmanaged, reproductive impairment arising from trampling and harvesting may generate population-level consequences over time, especially when fecundity is low and larval mortality high30. In Langebaan Lagoon, ecosystem-scale population declines do not appear to be the case, given the localised nature of recreational activity in relation to the extent of protected area, based on our findings (Table 3) and past research42,46. However, in unmanaged systems, reproductive impairment due to recreational activity may indirectly lead to key ecosystem engineering functions provided by sandprawns (and other endobenthic crustaceans), such as benthic oxygenation, being compromised through population-level declines. Also at risk is the recently identified water filtration function provided by sandprawns, which has been shown experimentally to counter eutrophication-induced phytoplankton proliferation and shifts to nanophytoplankton dominance40,41. We suggest that with further development and refinement over greater spatial and temporal scales, embryonic indicators can assist authorities to detect and manage local consequences of trampling and bait collecting. Such interventions can buffer effects of broader-scale global change processes by limiting multi-stressor synergisms and protecting key functional components of biodiversity that increase ecosystem resilience7,8.
