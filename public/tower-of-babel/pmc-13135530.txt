Sympathetic and Vagal Distribution in Hepatic Alveolar Echinococcosis and Its Clinical Correlation
Authors: Rongdong He, Zongding Wang, Zhihao Song, Lijiao Li, Li Sun, Hao Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo explore the distribution characteristics of sympathetic and vagal nerves in hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (AE). By analyzing pathological specimens from patients, we focused on the differential distribution of nerves in lesions, perilesional zones, and normal liver tissues and revealed their correlation with clinical local staging, hepatic fibrosis and regenerative processes.MethodsPathological specimens from 74 patients diagnosed with hepatic AE were analyzed. The differential distribution of sympathetic and vagal nerves was examined in three distinct tissue regions: the central lesion, the perilesional zone, and distal normal liver tissue. The correlation between the percentage of sympathetic nerve positivity and clinical local stage, fibrosis, and cell proliferation was statistically analyzed.ResultsThe analysis revealed a marked hyperplasia of sympathetic nerves specifically within the lesion and the perilesional zone, whereas no significant changes were observed in the normal liver tissues. The distribution of vagal nerves showed no significant variation across all regions. The density of sympathetic nerves demonstrated a positive correlation with the clinical local staging of the disease. Histopathological examination confirmed multiple round lesions with fibrous encapsulation and prominent inflammatory cell infiltration in the perilesional zone. Furthermore, sympathetic nerve hyperplasia was significantly correlated with the degree of fibrosis in both the lesion and perilesional zone. The density of sympathetic nerves in the perilesional zone was also positively associated with local inflammatory cell proliferation.ConclusionSympathetic nerves, but not vagal nerves, exhibit significant hyperplasia within and around hepatic AE lesions. The density of these sympathetic nerves is strongly correlated with disease progression, the extent of fibrosis, and local cellular proliferation. These findings suggest that sympathetic nerves may play a key role in the pathological remodeling and progression of hepatic AE.

Introduction

Echinococcosis is a severe zoonotic parasitic disease caused by infections with Echinococcus granulosus and Echinococcus multilocularis, leading to cystic echinococcosis (CE) and alveolar echinococcosis (AE), respectively [1, 2]. This disease is primarily prevalent in livestock-intensive regions such as Western China, Central Asia and South Asia. It is mainly transmitted through the fecal–oral route, poses a significant threat to human health, and remains a major public health issue [3–5].

After completing its life cycle in the intestines of the definitive host, Echinococcus multilocularis gravid proglottids continuously release infective eggs into the environment through feces. When an intermediate host inadvertently ingests contaminated eggs, the oncospheres hatch in the duodenum and penetrate the intestinal mucosal barrier, preferentially localizing to the liver parenchyma via the portal venous system. Some larvae can disseminate through the bloodstream, affecting extrahepatic organs such as the brain, lungs, and skeletal system [6–8]. The liver is the primary target organ in AE and is characterized by the accumulation of vesicles of varying sizes, with a hard texture, significant fibrosis, and aggressive growth that poorly delineates the boundary with surrounding tissues [9]. AE often metastasizes distantly via the lymphatic or vascular routes, and its growth pattern resembles that of malignant tumors, earning it the term “worm cancer” [10].

The liver is an organ with rich neural innervation [11], and hepatic malignancies are often associated with sympathetic nerve proliferation in both the tumor and surrounding tissues [12, 13]. AE lesions exhibit local invasiveness and distant metastasis similar to malignant tumors; however, there is currently no research on the distribution characteristics of vagal and sympathetic nerve fibers in liver AE lesions. Therefore, this study systematically investigates the distribution patterns of autonomic nerves in surgically resected liver AE specimens diagnosed pathologically, and explores the correlation between these patterns and local staging, fibrosis, and liver regeneration through histological analysis.

The study design adhereds to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [14]. This retrospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (No. 20200116-04), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians.

Collection of AE Liver Tissue Samples

Liver tissue specimens obtained during surgery were defined as follows: liver tissue collected within a 0.5 ± 0.5 cm range from the lesion was classified as perilesional zone liver tissue, while tissue obtained at a distance of 3.0 ± 0.5 cm from the lesion was considered normal liver tissue [16, 17], characterized by preserved lobular architecture, absence of significant inflammatory cell infiltration, and no fibrotic expansion of portal tracts or bridging fibrosis. For each sample, areas containing major blood vessels and main bile ducts were avoided. The collected specimens were thoroughly fixed [18], and a portion wae used for immunofluorescence staining and rapidly frozen sectioning. The remaining tissue was processed for paraffin embedding and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin, Sirius red, Masso, and immunohistochemical staining.

Haematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and embedded in paraffin. Routine sectioning (5 μm) was performed, and the sections were baked at 60 °C for 40 min. After gradient deparaffinization and hydration, hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed. The sections were then dehydrated using an alcohol gradient and mounted with neutral balsam. Images were captured using a digital imaging system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemical Staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to gradient deparaffinization followed by hydration. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer using a microwave oven (defrost mode) for 15 min, followed by blocking with goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-rabbit PGP9.5, 1:500, ab8189, Abcam; anti-rabbit GAP43, 1:3000, ab75810, Abcam; anti-rabbit TH, 1:50, bs-0016R, Bioss; anti-rabbit VAChT, 1:1000, ab235201, Abcam; and anti-rabbit PCNA, 1:500,ab29, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit F(ab’)2-HRP, Abcam, Cambridge, USA) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by washing and DAB staining (Abcam, Cambridge, USA) for color development [19]. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining and the sections were dehydrated through a gradient and mounted with neutral balsam. Slides were examined and photographed under a microscope. All images were acquired at × 200 magnification and analyzed using ImageJ. The images were calibrated in micrometers (µm), processed with the H-DAB color deconvolution plugin, and DAB-positive areas were segmented using automatic thresholding (Otsu method) with manual verification.

Negative controls were processed without primary antibodies, and no specific signal was observed. For each anatomical region, 3–5 non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs) were selected and analyzed in a blinded manner, following the approach described by Marcello Trucas et al. [20]. Nerve density was defined and quantified as the percentage of PGP9.5- or TH-positive area relative to the total tissue area within each ROI (% positive area) [21, 22]. The mean nerve density across all ROIs within each region was used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence Assay

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GAP43 (1:100, ab75810, Abcam, Cambridge, USA) and TH (1:50, bs-0016R, Bioss, Beijing, China). After washing, appropriate secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG and CoraLite 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, Proteintech, USA) were applied and incubated for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Confocal microscopy imaging and colocalization analysis were performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Oskar-Barnack-Straße).

Sirius Red Staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were routinely deparaffinized in water, and stained with 0.1% Sirius Red (MM1004, Maokangbio, Shanghai, China) for cellular staining, followed by washing with tap water for 1 min. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin (C0107, Beyotime) for 1 min and washed again with tap water for 1 min. After air drying, the sections were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Masson Staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were routinely deparaffinized in water and stained using a Masson trichrome staining kit (G1340, Solarbio). The sections were first treated with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin solution for 10 min, followed by Masson’s blue staining solution for 3 min and rinsinged with distilled water. The sections were then stained with Ponceau red for 5 min, washed, and placed in an aniline blue staining solution. After gradient dehydration with ethanol and clearing with xylene, sections were air-dried and mounted with neutral balsam. Slides were observed and photographed under a microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Data were statistically analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Measurement data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent-samples t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. When the data did not conform to a normal distribution or showed heterogeneity of variance, nonparametric tests were applied for statistical analysis. For correlation analysis, Pearson’s correlation was used for normally distributed data, while Spearman’s correlation was used for non-normally distributed data. The thresholds for statistical significance were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Result

Autonomic Nervous Distribution Characteristics of Hepatic AE Lesions

The liver is innervated by both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves, which are involved in liver regeneration, glucose and lipid metabolism, and immune function. To investigate the changes in autonomic nerve distribution in different regions, such as the lesion, perilesional zone and normal liver tissue in patients with hepatic AE, immunohistochemical staining with PGP9.5 antibody was used to label the autonomic nerves. The distribution characteristics of autonomic nerves in these regions were analyzed. The results showed that a large number of nerve fibers were distributed around the perilesional zone, whereas fewer nerve fibers were found in the normal liver tissue and the lesion. Identification of Autonomic Nerves in Patients with Hepatic AE

In patients with hepatic AE, the specific type of autonomic nerve innervation in the liver is unknown. To clarify the type of autonomic nerve innervation, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence co-localization were used for identification. Paraffin sections of the liver tissue were stained using the sympathetic nerve marker TH antibody (Fig. 2A) and the parasympathetic nerve marker VAChT antibody (Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemical results showed that, in the liver of AE patients, the sympathetic nerve innervation predominated, while parasympathetic nerve innervation was minimal. To further confirm these findings, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis was performed, which demonstrated complete overlap of the TH antibody-labeled regions with the PGP9.5 antibody-labeled regions (Fig. 2C), indicating that these nerves were sympathetic. Hepatic AE lesions exhibit a characteristic affinity for blood vessels, and a local staging system based on the extent of vascular involvement has been established [23]: P0 indicates no lesions within the liver; P1 refers to peripheral lesions without vascular involvement; P2 represents central lesions within one liver lobe accompanied by local vascular invasion; P3 denotes lesions crossing the hepatic lobes with portal vascular involvement; and P4 signifies extensive vascular involvement with distal dilation. Histological analysis revealed a significant increase in sympathetic nerve fibers in the perilesional area. Spearman’s correlation analysis confirmed a significant positive correlation between the density of sympathetic nerve fibers in the lesion and local staging (n = 74, ρ = 0.60, 95%CI [0.42–0.72], P < 0.001), as well as a significant positive correlation between nerve density at the perilesional zone and local staging (n = 74, ρ = 0.86, 95%CI [0.78–0.91], P < 0.001). However, no statistical association was observed between the distribution of nerves in normal liver tissue and staging (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Pathological Tissue Alterations and Hepatic Cell Proliferation in Hepatic AE

Gross specimens from patients with hepatic AE mainly consist of lesions encapsulated by dense fibrous tissue, which may present as either solitary or multiple lesions. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed that the lesion areas exhibited a vesicular-like structure with destruction of the lobular architecture and dense fibrous stroma proliferation. At the perilesional zone, significant inflammatory infiltration was observed, along with focal fibrosis, while the basic lobular structure was retained. As hepatic AE lesions progressively enlarge, changes in cell proliferation levels occur in the lesion area, surrounding liver tissue, and hepatic tissue to meet the body’s compensatory and adaptive needs. Quantitative analysis of PCNA immunohistochemistry revealed significant suppression of proliferative activity in the lesion area. In the perilesional zone, inflammatory cell nodular proliferation was observed, while in normal liver tissue, the predominant pattern of proliferation was in the form of hepatocyte bands. The proliferative density in both the perilesional zone and normal liver tissue was significantly higher than that in the lesion area (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B-C).

Chronic inflammation drives fibrotic progression of hepatic AE, and the distribution of sympathetic nerves is significantly correlated with the degree of fibrosis. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between sympathetic nerve density and PCNA positivity in the perilesional zone (r = 0.84, P < 0.001), but no statistical correlation between the lesion area and normal liver area (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3D). HE staining of PCNA-positive cell clusters in the perilesional zone confirmed that these cells were predominantly inflammatory, suggesting that sympathetic nerves may participate in the pathological process by regulating the inflammatory microenvironment. The specific mechanism of this involvement requires further functional studies for validation.

Hepatic Fibrotic Remodeling in AE

Hepatic AE patients exhibit significant fibrosis in both the lesion and surrounding areas. To perform further quantitative analysis under a microscope, this section employs immunohistochemistry, Sirius Red staining, and Masson’s trichrome staining. α-SMA is a commonly used marker for the activation of hepatic stellate cells into myofibroblasts. Immunohistochemical analysis with α-SMA antibody staining revealed that the expression of α-SMA in the lesion and perilesional zone was significantly higher than in normal liver tissue (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). To further investigate whether sympathetic nerves are associated with the extent of fibrosis, Pearson correlation analysis showed that α-SMA expression was positively correlated with sympathetic nerve density in the lesion area (r = 0.5168, P < 0.001). Additionally, α-SMA expression was positively correlated with sympathetic nerve density in the perilesional zone (r = 0.8144, P < 0.001). No correlation was observed between sympathetic nerve density and α-SMA expression in normal liver tissue (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4D). The extent of sympathetic nerve proliferation showed a weak positive correlation with fibrosis in the lesion area as indicated by Sirius Red staining (r = 0.3563, P < 0.01). In the perilesional zone, the expression of sympathetic nerves was highly positively correlated with Sirius Red-positive staining (r = 0.8156, P < 0.001), while no correlation was found between sympathetic nerve density and Sirius Red expression in normal liver tissue (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the extent of sympathetic nerve proliferation demonstrated a weak positive correlation with fibrosis in the lesion area as assessed by Masson’s staining (r = 0.3446, P < 0.01). At the perilesional zone, sympathetic nerve expression was highly positively correlated with Masson-positive staining (r = 0.8235, P < 0.001), while no correlation was observed between sympathetic nerve density and Masson expression in normal liver tissue (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4F). Taken together, the results from α-SMA, Sirius Red, and Masson staining all suggest a positive correlation between sympathetic nerves and hepatic fibrosis in both the lesion and perilesional zone, particularly a strong correlation between sympathetic nerves and fibrosis in the perilesional zone.

Discussion

Hepatic AE is a rare zoonotic disease primarily distributed in the Northern Hemisphere, with canids and rodents serving as definitive and intermediate hosts, respectively [24]. Clinically, hepatic AE often mimics malignant tumors, characterized by extensive tissue destruction, ill-defined borders with surrounding organs, and potential lymphatic and hematogenous metastases. These features differ significantly from those of typical encapsulated cystic lesions observed in CE. Statistically, the 10-year mortality rate for untreated AE patients can reach up to 90%, and the main treatment strategy currently involves radical surgical resection combined with long-term albendazole therapy [25]. Histologically, HE staining revealed characteristic changes, with areas of the lesion showing clusters of vesicles of varying sizes surrounded by significant deposition of fibrin and inflammatory cell infiltration. The hepatic lobular structure demonstrates a gradient of changes, ranging from destruction to partial retention, whereases the portal areas exhibit significant inflammatory cell aggregation [26, 27]. Notably, sympathetic nerve hyperplasia is often observed in pathological specimens, a phenomenon also supported by PET-CT findings, where the lesion periphery shows characteristic high metabolic regions, suggesting a potential link between sympathetic nerve activity and lesion progression [28, 29]. Although existing evidence suggests that the sympathetic nervous system may be involved in the pathological process of AE, its specific mechanisms of action still require further elucidation through basic research.

The liver, being the largest organ in the human body, plays a pivotal role in various physiological processes, including digestion, metabolism, immunity, and regeneration. Current research indicates that the autonomic nervous system (ANS) innervates the liver, regulating these essential functions [30, 31]. Significant sympathetic nerve proliferation is observed in patients with AE, especially in lesions and perilesional zones, with the most prominent proliferation occurring at the perilesional zone. Meanwhile, using anti-VAChT antibody staining to label the vagus nerve, it was found that the vagal nerve distribution was sparse in the lesions, perilesional zone, and normal liver tissue, with no significant macroscopic differences. As the disease progresses, the liver lesions tend to advance to later stages, with sympathetic nerves being closely related to the staging of the lesions, except in normal liver tissue. Pathologically, the lesions and perilesional zones in AE patients are often accompanied by significant inflammatory cell infiltration and show marked fibrous proliferation, exhibiting characteristics of chronic inflammation [32–34]. To investigate the distribution and role of the autonomic nervous system in AE patients, particularly its correlation with factors such as lesion staging, stellate cell activation, liver fibrosis, and cellular proliferation, we conducted a comprehensive quantitative analysis. The results revealed that sympathetic nerve proliferation was significantly increased in the lesions and perilesional zones, and was in an active growth state. Furthermore, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence colocalization techniques further confirmed the proliferation of sympathetic nerves in local lesions and perilesional zones, while no significant proliferation was observed in normal liver tissue.

The surrounding lesions of hepatic AE infection are characterized by significant fibrinoid proliferation, with hepatic stellate cells playing an important role in the fibrosis process. α-SMA, a marker for the activation of hepatic stellate cells, is highly expressed in the lesions and its surrounding areas, indicating that stellate cells contribute to the progression of fibrosis by transforming into a fibrogenic phenotype during the pathological process [35–37]. Sirius Red and Masson’s trichrome staining revealeds marked proliferation and deposition of collagen fibers in the lesions and perilesional zones. Excessive collagen fiber production disrupts the structure of the host’s liver tissue, resulting in varying degrees of pathological damage and even liver dysfunction. Fibrosis around the lesions acts as a restrictive barrier to lesion expansion, yet it also impairs the structural integrity and functionality of normal hepatic tissue [38]. The sympathetic nervous system plays a crucial role in immune regulation, and studies have shown that the sympathetic nervous system is key in tumorigenesis and development [39–42]. Correlation analysis revealed a close relationship between the sympathetic nervous system and AE fibrosis, suggesting that the sympathetic nervous system is involved in the regulation of fibrosis. In AE patients, fibrosis predominantly affects the lesions and perilesional zones, with no significant fibrosis observed in the surrounding normal liver parenchyma. This localized and concentrated fibrosis surrounding the lesions in AE contrasts with the diffuse fibrosis typically observed in liver cirrhosis induced by chronic viral hepatitis, such as hepatitis B [43]. Regarding sympathetic nerve distribution, in hepatic AE, sympathetic nerves are mainly concentrated in the lesions and perilesional zones, and significant nerve proliferation has been observed in the pathological studies of cirrhotic patients. Further correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between the distribution of sympathetic nerves in AE and collagen fibers in Sirius Red and Masson staining results, suggesting that sympathetic nerves may regulate the fibrosis process in hepatic alveolar echinococcosis.

Based on the HE staining results of hepatic AE and previous studies, a distinct inflammatory response zone was observed around the lesion, with infiltration of numerous inflammatory cells such as macrophages, NK cells, and lymphocytes in this area [44]. Furthermore, PCNA staining revealed that inflammatory cells in the perilesional zone were in a markedly proliferative state, while cell proliferation in the lesion and normal liver tissue was relatively low. Notably, there was prominent sympathetic nerve proliferation in the perilesional zone, suggesting that the sympathetic nervous system may play an important role in the regulation of inflammation, which was supported by the correlation analysis. As the lesion continued to expand, the volume of normal liver tissue gradually decreased. To compensate for the loss of tissue, hepatocyte proliferation became a critical pathological process for maintaining liver function and facilitating the immune response [45]. There were differences in the cell proliferation rates between regions: the least proliferation occurred within the lesion, while the most significant proliferation was observed in the perilesional zone, where inflammatory cells were highly active. Simultaneously, there was a positive correlation between sympathetic nerve and PCNA-positive cells in the perilesional zone with cell proliferation, indicating that the sympathetic nervous system may be involved in the regulation of inflammatory cells. However, no such correlation was observed between the lesion and normal liver tissue. In the context of chronic inflammation, the sympathetic nervous system may play an active role in the inflammatory process. Therefore, in the chronic inflammation induced by Echinococcus multilocularis infection, hepatocyte proliferation may be partially suppressed. Animal studies have shown that the vagus nerve-acrophage axis participates in regulating liver regeneration following injury [46]. In the present study, vagal nerve fibers were relatively sparse in hepatic AE lesions, suggesting that insufficient vagal signaling may be unfavorable for establishing a microenvironment biased toward regenerative repair; however, this hypothesis requires further validation in prospective animal models and cell-based experiments. GAP-43 is a classical marker of axonal growth, and its expression changes in a time-dependent manner during injury and repair [47]. As our study is based on surgically resected specimens and thus provides histological information at only a single time point, it cannot capture the dynamic expression profile of GAP-43. Therefore, our current findings are associative, and longitudinal functional studies are needed to clarify the causal impact of vagal innervation and GAP-43 dynamics on human liver regeneration.

Conclusion

In patients with hepatic AE, significant proliferation of hepatic sympathetic nerves was observed both at the lesion site and at the perilesional zone. The degree of sympathetic nerve proliferation was significantly positively correlated with the clinical staging and degree of hepatic fibrosis. Additionally, the proliferation of sympathetic nerves around the perilesional zone is positively correlated with the proliferation of inflammatory cells. In contrast, no significant alterations were noted in the vagus nerve within the lesions, perilesional zones, or normal liver tissue.
