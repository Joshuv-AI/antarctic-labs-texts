Community health providers’ perceptions on the preference of caesarean section deliveries: A qualitative study in the state of Andhra Pradesh, India
Authors: Nagendra Babu Gavvala, Janmejaya Samal, M. Benson Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:While Caesarean Sections (C-sections) can be life-saving in complicated deliveries, their overuse is linked to significant maternal and infant morbidity and mortality. This highlights the importance of understanding the factors that drive preferences for C-section deliveries. States like Andhra Pradesh exhibit an alarming trend of rising rates of C-section deliveries in India. This study aimed to explore community health providers’ perceptions regarding the preference for caesarean delivery in Andhra Pradesh and to identify the major determinants of C-section preferences.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The study utilized a qualitative research design, with focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted among community health providers from four districts of Andhra Pradesh. Participants were purposively sampled to gather a variety of viewpoints. A validated semi-structured questionnaire was employed to collect data. Data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed thematically to extract key themes and sub-themes related to preferences for C-section deliveries.RESULTS:The study identified a combination of medical, cultural, and systemic factors influencing C-section preferences. Community health providers underscored the advantages of normal deliveries but acknowledged the challenges they face due to family pressures and socio-cultural practices. Systemic issues, such as financial incentives in private healthcare and the increasing popularity of convenience-type elective C-sections or those that align with auspicious dates, also contributed to the preference for C-section deliveries.CONCLUSION:This study underscores the necessity for balanced, evidence-based practices to tackle the non-medical factors driving the rise in C-section deliveries. Promoting antenatal counselling, addressing maternal and familial concerns, and modifying institutional practices are critical for ensuring safe and well-informed delivery choices.

Introduction

Childbirth is a momentous event in the life of a woman[1] and can be through different modalities of delivery, which is divided into vaginal delivery and C-sections. Internationally, vaginal delivery is the principal and general method of delivery, being a natural process, shorter recovery, and limiting long-term complications.[2] Nevertheless, rates of C-sections are climbing dramatically around the world,[345] well above the interval reported by the World Health Organization (WHO) (<10-15%), with some areas reporting figures above 30%.[6] In India, 88.6% are institutional deliveries, according to the NFHS-5 report. In Andhra Pradesh, the institutional deliveries are 96.5%. In India, there was an increase in the prevalence of C-sections from 17.2% in 2005-2006 to 28.1% in 2015-2016, with wide regional variations. Andhra Pradesh has some of the highest C-section rates in the country, with 42.4% of the deliveries by C-sections and 57.6% by normal delivery. In the private health facilities, 63% of the births were delivered by C-sections versus 26.6% in the public health facilities. The number of C-section deliveries has increased from 40.1% to 42.4% between the two consecutive rounds of the National Family Health Survey. A sharp increase in C-section deliveries has been observed in private facilities, from 57% in NFHS-4 to 63% in NFHS-5.[3] According to the NFHS-5 survey, the prevalence of C-section deliveries in India is far from the normal range of 10-15% in many states.[7] Vaginal deliveries, though preferred for their lower risk of complications and faster postpartum recovery, are often perceived as challenging due to prolonged labour and associated pain.[8]

C-section delivery is a lifesaving surgical intervention to overcome any serious delivery complications. It has resulted in saving the lives of many pregnant women in India and has been instrumental in reducing the Maternal Mortality Ratio.[9] C-section in conditions such as placenta previa, foetal distress or abnormal positioning saves the lives of women and babies, and there is an imperative need for access to safe C-sections. However, there is no evidence of a benefit at the population level of C-sections for women and babies who do not require the procedure.[10] While increasing access to C-sections and Comprehensive and Emergency Obstetric and Neonatal Care (CEmONC) services was once a primary concern in the country, it has now shifted to monitoring the overuse of C-sections in many states.[11] C-section rates are relatively higher in the South Indian states than in other parts of the country.[12] Lack of robust monitoring and poor adherence to quality protocols for caesarean deliveries can lead to surgical complications, longer hospital stays, risk for infection and greater risk of postpartum haemorrhage and deep vein thrombosis in future pregnancies and may lead to risk of mortality and poor health outcomes for both mother and infants.[1314]

In India, particularly in states like Andhra Pradesh, C-section rates are notably higher than the recommended levels, often influenced by a combination of medical, cultural, and systemic factors.[15] Studies have shown that mothers and caregivers often perceive C-sections as a safer and more convenient option. At the same time, community health providers may face institutional or economic pressures that affect their decision-making.[616] Research suggests that the interplay of sociocultural beliefs, health system practices, and provider recommendations significantly shapes delivery choices.[10] Given the fact that the state of Andhra Pradesh reports higher rates of C-section deliveries, which is substantially higher compared to other Indian states, a study was carried out to unravel the factors associated with the perception of the community health providers regarding the preference for C-section deliveries. The study aimed to bring about the fresh perspectives of these providers as they act as a bridge between the community and the secondary level of health service delivery and are involved in rendering counselling services to pregnant mother and their family members and facilitating their decision-making in preferring a particular mode of delivery.

Objectives

The main objectives of this study were to understand the perceptions of the community health providers regarding the preference for C-section deliveries for childbirth and to assess the key contributing factors to high C-section rates in Andhra Pradesh.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The study adopted a qualitative descriptive research design. The study was carried out in four districts of Andhra Pradesh—Visakhapatnam, Krishna, Anantapur, and Chittoor. Figure 1 depicts the study settings.

Study participants and sampling

Purposive sampling was used to select the community health providers across four districts of Andhra Pradesh. Community health providers, including ANMs and ASHAs working in the study districts, were included in the study. Community health providers who didn’t consent to participate in the Focus Group Discussion were excluded from the study [Table 1].

Data collection tool and technique

A validated semi-structured questionnaire was used as the FGD guide. Internal content validation was conducted to ensure the relevance and clarity of the guide. The discussions were facilitated in the local language (Telugu) to ensure effective communication. A total of eight FGDs were conducted, each consisting of four participants. Separate FGDs were facilitated for each stakeholder group—ANMs and ASHAs. Before conducting the FGDs, the moderators discussed the terms and conditions and sought informed consent from all participants for recording the discussions. The first author moderated and audio-recorded all FGDs. The recordings were transcribed and translated into English for analysis. FGDs were organized at Anganwadi centres and Health and Wellness Centres to ensure the convenience of the study participants. The period of data collection was August-October 2024.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of SRMIST on 29th May 2024 (Ref No: 00111/IEC/2024). Permission to carry out the FGDs in all the selected districts was obtained from the Additional Director [MCH], O/o CH and FW, Andhra Pradesh. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the participants’ confidentiality and anonymity were guaranteed. Each FGD lasted approximately 50 minutes.

Analysis method

The data was analyzed using thematic analysis. Data were coded manually. Key themes were identified, highlighting the perceptions of the community health providers regarding preferences for the mode of delivery.

These factors could be largely categorized as (i) Medical factors, (ii) Financial motives of private hospitals, (iii) Government policies and regulations, (iv) Socio-cultural factors, (v) Pregnant women and caregivers’ preferences, and (vi) Other factors.

Results

The study participants constituted the ANMs and ASHAs who were working in the selected districts. There were 16 ANMs and 16 ASHAs among the participants. Focus group discussions were conducted at Anganwadi centres and Health and Wellness Centres in which two homogenous groups were formed according to the two categories of respondents: ANMs and ASHAs. The FGD data were transcribed verbatim, followed by thematic analysis. For the thematic analysis, the transcribed data were first coded, and the codes led to the development of sub-themes and the sub-themes led to the formation of themes. The different factors affecting decision-making are reflected in the themes and sub-themes [Figure 3]. Across all groups, there was a preference for normal delivery from community health providers; however, requests from families due to societal expectations and cultural beliefs are on the rise in addition to the medical necessity and institutional factors. Since the preferences for C-sections cannot be justified without comparing them to normal deliveries, these themes offer a juxtaposing comprehension with normal deliveries.

Preference for normal delivery

Advocacy for normal delivery

Normal delivery is considered by many community health providers to be the most natural and physiological method of birth, and thus, they advocate for normal delivery by stressing the benefits for both mother and child, such as quicker recovery, fewer surgical risks, and a low chance of complications in future pregnancies.

“A normal delivery is the natural way a baby can come to life. It enables the mother’s body to heal quicker, lowers complications of surgery, and has long-term advantages for the mother and child. (C4_ANM)

Whenever it’s safe and possible, it’s the preferred choice, and so I always encourage women to undergo normal delivery. (A1_ANM).

“We educate them for the normal delivery as there won’t be any complications like back pain, and she can take care of her baby easily without someone’s assistance, unlike cesarean section women. In addition, she need not stay in the hospital for a longer duration.” (K2_ASHA)

Healthy and safe for women

Community health providers believe that normal delivery has faster recovery, which enables mothers to resume their daily activities and care for their families without prolonged dependence on others. Additionally, they emphasize that normal delivery promotes better overall health outcomes and reduces the physical and emotional challenges associated with surgical procedures. These advantages make normal delivery a preferred option whenever it is safe and feasible.

“Normal delivery is natural and best because women can do all work by themselves at home and need not take help from anybody.” (A1_ANM)

“If it is normal delivery, within 3 days they will be fine but for C-section, there will be a pain at Stitches, many doubts and fear to move.” (C4_ANM)

“If it is a normal birth mother gets discharged within 3 days, in case of a C-section the mother gets hospitalised for 9 days, even after discharge follow-up care is required for observing stitches and for any complications, we have to take her to PHC or CHC. In case of normal birth, we feel confident that both mother and baby will be safe.” (K4_ANM)

“(Normal delivery) cures faster, will be healthy and strong. There is a lot of difference between cesarean and normal delivery; in normal delivery, healing is fast, and they can do their work on their own. If it’s a cesarean, they need to take rest for about 6 months or so. So, we are telling them to get normal delivery.” (C3_ASHA)

“Based on the training we attended; we know that normal delivery leads to fewer complications and better outcomes for the baby. It’s always our first recommendation when there are no medical reasons for a C-section because the benefits for both mother and child are clear. (V1_ANM).”

Need for reassurance

First-time pregnant women tend to have more concerns and questions about their delivery mode, usually with a preference for normal delivery. In contrast, mothers with second pregnancies are less anxious. While first-time mothers often inquire about the likelihood of a normal delivery, community health providers reassure them that normal delivery is the goal if there are no complications. Generally, women do not request a caesarean unless necessary.

“Usually, they ask about which mode of delivery should they prefer, especially primi cases are more worried than the second pregnancy mothers.” (A2_ASHA)

“Only the women with first pregnancy fear a lot, but those who conceive for the second time have no fear. Women with first pregnancy ask us whether they will deliver normally or not. We tell them they will deliver normally if there is no complication, and there will be no need to worry as doctors try to do normal for you.” (C1_ASHA).

“No one asks for operation.” (C_All)

Preference for caesarean delivery

Increasing C-section preferences

Family requests for C-sections

Community health providers noted that C-sections are often requested by family members, not the pregnant women themselves, as they cannot tolerate seeing the women in labor pain. This pressure from family members leads to an increase in C-sections.

“Usually, it’s the family members who ask for a C-section; the patients are fine. Because of the attendants, we ended up doing more C-sections. They just can’t handle seeing the women in labor pain. “They prefer that only (Caesarean). They are not at all considering the consequences.“ (K1_ANM)

Fear of labour pains

Additionally, some younger women, fearing the pain, ask for a C-section. Providers try to encourage them to endure labor and push for normal delivery whenever possible.

“Some women just can’t handle the pain—especially younger ones. They get scared and ask for a C-section themselves, saying they can’t deal with the pain. We tell them to hang in there for a little while longer, that it’ll be over soon. We try to encourage them and push for a normal delivery.” (A1_ANM)

Perceived safety of baby with caesarean surgeries

Community health providers noted that many women prioritize their baby’s immediate safety over long-term health risks, choosing C-sections to avoid the perceived dangers of prolonged labor. Despite the additional costs and future health implications, such as needing C-sections for future pregnancies, their main concern is the baby’s well-being.

“Not tolerating pains and thinking that C-section will be best for them. They think that in normal delivery, if the baby is inside for more time, then it will affect the baby. We are spending more money on pregnancy, and why will we get into risk by normal delivery? But they are not bothered about their future health problems. If a C-section is performed, then in future deliveries also they will be undergoing a C-section. They are just bothered about their baby; the baby will be deprived of breath, and the baby will get into risk.” (K4_ASHA)

Medical necessities for C-sections

Clinically indicated C-sections

While many women initially aim for normal delivery, complications such as amniotic fluid leakage, meconium staining, fetal distress (like low heartbeat), or issues like big babies, pelvic problems, or previous pregnancy complications may necessitate a caesarean section. In some cases, such as with low fetal heart rate or meconium presence, a C-section is considered the safest option for both the baby and mother, and community health providers prioritize immediate action to prevent risks.

“They come for normal. But either due to leakage of amniotic fluid, or due to meconium stain, suddenly they need an operation.” (V1_ANM)

“Big baby, fluid loss” “Pelvic problems” “If there’s been any abortions or severe blood loss.” (K3_ANM)

“In some complicated cases, like when the baby’s heartbeat is low, a C-section is the only safe option for delivery—it’s better for the baby too. Meconium cases are risky, and families blame us if we don’t go for a C-section. So, in those situations, it’s safer to do a section. Even with big babies or low fetal sounds, we treat them as emergencies and go straight for a C-section. We don’t wait for labor pains in these kinds of conditions.” (C2_ANM)

Preferring C-section for tubectomy

The observations from community health providers highlight that many women opt for caesarean sections due to the concurrent desire for tubectomy (sterilization) after having the desired number of children, typically two. Healthcare workers noted that some women specifically request C-sections for this dual purpose, often unaware of alternative options. Additionally, certain women, especially from rural areas, choose C-sections even when their first delivery is normal, sometimes influenced by family preferences or lack of awareness about other procedures.

“There are few who come decided to go for C-section, even when the first delivery was normal, they ask us to do C-section and family planning also. These are village people, so they don’t know what these procedures are.” (K3_ANM)

“Nowadays, after having two children, many women choose to undergo tubectomy. To get it done, they opt for a C-section so that both the delivery and the tubectomy can be completed at the same time. That’s why they prefer C-sections.” (V4_ASHA)

Previous caesarean section

FGDs revealed a strong preference for elective caesarean sections in women with a history of previous caesarean deliveries. Providers expressed concerns about women presenting late in labour after previous caesarean sections, increasing the risk of complications. To mitigate this, providers advocated for proactive management, including early admission and scheduled elective caesareans. This often resulted in a direct recommendation for caesarean delivery without extensive discussion of alternative options.

“Women with a previous caesarean, wait for pains and come at last moment. So immediately C-section will be done to them. Otherwise, the previous scar will bleed.” (K4_ANM)

“If already she had a caesarean, then according to her EDD, we ask her to go prior and admit in hospital for operation.” (V3_ANM)

“If there is a previous caesarean section case (they will) compulsory have elective caesarean.” (A1, A4_ANM)

“Most cases with us are previous C-sections. First, they get caesarean delivery at a private hospital, and they come here for the second one.” (K1_ANM)

Sociocultural factors influence

Auspicious timing requests

It is observed that in some families, cultural beliefs significantly impact childbirth decisions, sometimes leading to preferences for elective caesareans based on astrological or other culturally significant timings, resulting in community-driven demand for C-sections without any significant medical indications.

“Sometimes family members insist on scheduling the C-section at certain timings because that was the most auspicious time for the baby to be born. They keep on requesting to arrange for surgery without knowing its effects. We usually advise them to go for a normal delivery because it’s better for both the baby’s and the mother’s health. Then we try to explain that it’s best to schedule surgeries based on medical needs; they wouldn’t understand all these.” (K3_ANM)

Educated women prefer C-section

Community health providers observed that a growing number of educated women are opting for C-sections due to the convenience it offers, preferring to avoid prolonged labour pains.

“Many educated women ask for caesarean section operation as they feel that is convenient and not to endure prolonged labour pains.” (K1_ANM)

Women with precious pregnancies

Additionally, they noted a trend where women who marry later in life and may require medical assistance to conceive are more likely to choose C-sections to ensure the safe delivery of their baby. These women tend to prioritize the health of the baby over concerns such as cost or recovery, reflecting a strong focus on a safe delivery experience.

“Nowadays, many women are getting married later in life. Some of them don’t conceive immediately and need medical assistance to get pregnant. In such cases, they often choose a C-section to ensure the baby’s safe delivery. Their focus is entirely on the baby’s health, and they don’t worry about the cost or even their health.” (V4_ANM)

Social prestige of C-section

Some women, especially for their first delivery, prefer to go to private hospitals, often due to the perceived social prestige associated with C-sections. This is especially true when they visit their mother’s place, where there is pressure to have private corporate hospitals, sometimes arranging the necessary funds with difficulty. Community health providers share that many families often see C-sections as a more prestigious option.

“Women usually go to their mom’s place for their first delivery. Over there, they often end up at private hospitals, as many of them see that as a prestige issue and arrange amounts with great difficulty. In private hospitals, they don’t try for normal deliveries.” (A3_ANM)

Institutional factors

Higher C-section rate in private hospitals

FGD findings revealed that, as per community health providers, normal deliveries are more common in government hospitals, while private hospitals tend to have a higher number of caesarean sections. Many mothers working in the private sector prefer to use their insurance benefits at private hospitals, where caesarean delivery is more commonly performed.

“Normal deliveries are more in our government hospitals, not in the outside (outside the government hospitals). Caesareans are more in number outside the government hospitals.” (A3_ASHA).

“C-section deliveries are most common in private hospitals.” (V2_ANM)

“Most of the mothers who work in the private sector usually avail insurance in private hospitals where most definitely the mode of delivery is caesarean section only.” (K1_ANM)

Financial motives in private practice

Private hospitals perform more C-sections, often driven by financial motives. Some providers in private hospitals may even cite fabricated reasons to justify the procedure.

“Mostly caesarean (in a private hospital) is done for money, they are available everywhere and easily reachable at nearby places only” “Don’t know what they would say but do operation. They may say transverse lie or aspiration or anything and do caesarean.” (K3 and K4_ANM)

Discussion

This study explores community health providers’ perceptions of the mode of delivery, especially C-sections, and the factors driving the high prevalence of caesarean deliveries in Andhra Pradesh. The findings highlight the complex interplay of medical, social, psychological, and cultural factors that influence preferences for normal delivery or C-sections. The findings underscore distinct motivations, concerns, and contextual elements shaping these decisions, aligning with the broader literature on maternal health and childbirth practices.

NFHS-5 reports that over 42.4% of all births in Andhra Pradesh were conducted via C-section, an alarmingly high percentage compared to the generally accepted rates for medical indications, suggesting the influence of other factors.[17] Community health providers in our study always proposed normal delivery as the most natural physiological pathway in childbirth. This is consistent with the advantages described in the literature, including faster maternal recovery, less surgical risk, and better outcomes for future pregnancies.[18] In contrast, community responses show increasing elective C-sections due to concerns regarding the perceived risks of vaginal delivery.[19] Community health providers stressed the health benefits of normal delivery, particularly the faster resumption of daily activities and reduced postpartum care needs, consistent with findings of earlier studies.[20] Additionally, first-time mothers in our study exhibited heightened anxiety about a delivery, frequently seeking reassurance, a finding consistent with observations from a study that noted similar concerns among primigravida women.[21] Although our findings underscore community health providers’ advocacy for normal deliveries, they also highlight systemic challenges, including socio-cultural influences and healthcare disparities. For example, previous research has documented how cultural practices, like timing childbirth for auspicious moments, contribute to the prevalence of elective C-sections.[2223] Furthermore, the decision to opt for a C-section delivery doesn’t necessarily lie with the mother; rather, healthcare workers, mostly the treating gynaecologist, decide as revealed in one of the studies in a similar setting in a Southern Indian state.[24]

The study reveals a rising preference for C-sections, often driven by family requests, fear of labour pain, or perceived safety for the baby that are consistent with similar findings such as fears and family-driven decisions in one of the studies in Thailand.[2125] However, providers in this study reported challenges in countering deeply ingrained socio-cultural beliefs and familial pressures, particularly in rural contexts. Some participants mentioned medical indications for C-sections, such as high blood pressure and delayed labour. Previous studies have also revealed the increased trend of medically indicated C-sections due to health risks in India.[26] A study highlighted that these complications usually require surgical procedures to maintain the safety of the mother and neonate.[27] Moreover, studies reveal that Caesarean deliveries were found to be impacted by several circumstances and relative indications, such as primigravida with breech presentation, social or familial influences, and caesarean delivery on maternal request (CDMR), rather than being performed only based on absolute medical indications. The proliferation of private clinics and the presence of family or friends were shown to be the most important variables contributing to the rise. The increase in caesarean deliveries was largely caused by social or familial considerations, especially in private obstetrics and gynaecologist specialist clinics, as found in one of the studies in Ethiopia.[28] This is consistent with findings from earlier research that showed that depending only on the mother’s demand, elders employed in government and private clinics frequently conducted caesarean deliveries for their clients without a medical cause.[2930]

The study also observed that the growing cultural beliefs in preference for delivering on auspicious days also influenced the decision to undergo C-sections, which are also reported by studies regarding the rising trend of “muhurat C-sections”,[1822] signifying the role of astrology in scheduling elective caesareans in India and similar evidence from other middle-income countries underscores the significance of such preferences.[2131] In addition, this study’s findings highlight the preference for C-sections among educated women and those with precious pregnancies like pregnancies in advanced maternal age or from In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) treatment. These findings are consistent with earlier research, which reported higher rates of elective C-sections among urban, educated women in Kerala.[18] Social prestige and family expectation are, however, strongly observed factors in the current study, particularly in first deliveries, but are less explored on a comprehensive scale and not extensively documented in the existing literature.

According to the NFHS-5 fact sheet, C-section deliveries accounted for 68.5% of all births in private hospitals compared to 31.5% of all births in public hospitals of Andhra Pradesh.[17] The study found that the community health providers found a stark contrast between delivery practices in government and private hospitals, that private hospitals do more C-sections than government hospitals. This is consistent with prior findings noting financial incentives and insurance-based decisions as two of the major drivers behind the rising trend of C-sections among private facilities.[262932]

Providers in this study further criticized the perceived misuse of clinical indications in private settings to justify unnecessary surgical interventions, a concern similarly noted in earlier studies.[32] According to different research, mothers who gave birth in private settings were more than three times as likely to have a C-section than mothers who gave birth in public settings.[25] The study’s findings about the impact of insurance on delivery mode choices are consistent with other studies that reported that among insured women, the percentage of C-sections is substantially higher, standing at 63.9%, compared to the same in public health facilities, which stands at 11.7%.[33]

Strengths and limitations of the study

Given the large percentage of C-section births that are reported from the state, this study shed more light on how community health providers view the preference for C-sections over vaginal births. This shapes the notion on the supply side of the health system about the preference for deliveries by caesarean section. The primary limitation of this study, however, was its inability to ascertain how end users of the services—such as women and their family members—perceived the preference for caesarean deliveries. This might have offered the societal factors that led to the preference for a caesarean section over vaginal deliveries.

Conclusion

This study uncovers a group of medical, behavioural, cultural, and systemic factors impacting aspects of childbirth decisions. A tendency towards normal delivery is strong, with perceived benefits from health to personal motivations. The results of this study are supported by existing quantitative evidence such as NFHS surveys, which indicate that non-medical factors underpin the high C-section prevalence in Andhra Pradesh. Despite community health providers actively advocating for normal delivery, in practice, the prevalence of C-sections is increasing due to socio-cultural factors, medical indications, family demand, and institution-level factors. This study demonstrates the importance of targeted interventions that respond to family concerns and facilitate evidence-informed providers’ practices to provide safe and informed childbirth options. The study extends a few policy recommendations, such as: (1) antenatal counselling and health education interventions, especially in the area of risk communication, to mitigate the fear associated with the childbirth process, (2) address the influence of cultural practices in the community such as Muhurat C-Section, and decision timing of the delivery based on astrology, and (3) health system strengthening in the areas of promoting normal deliveries and financial transparency in conducting C-Section deliveries in private hospitals. The study further suggests large-scale observational studies among secondary care and direct services providers, such as Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, Paediatricians and neonatologists and the anaesthesiologists about their perception of C-Section delivery preferences across diverse social and cultural settings.
