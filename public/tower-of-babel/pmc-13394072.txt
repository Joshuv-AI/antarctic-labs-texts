Comprehensive Assessment of Arterial, Tissue, and Venous Collaterals for Evaluating the Infarct Growth Rate: The Multimodal Collateral Score
Authors: Giorgio Busto, Umberto Pensato, Ilaria Casetta, Giorgio U. Marcello, Angelo Barra, Anna Poggesi, Edoardo Carlesi, Sara Mancini, Andrea Ginestroni, Vittorio Miele, Alessandro Padovani, Andrea Morotti, Enrico Fainardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCollaterals are crucial factors that influence the infarct growth rate (IGR). We aimed to determine whether a comprehensive multimodal collateral score (MCS), incorporating collateral assessment at the arterial, tissue, and venous levels, is associated with functional independence and provides incremental prognostic value over individual IGR metrics.MethodsArterial collaterals were assessed using multiphase CT‐angiography (mCTA), ranging from 0 to 5; tissue‐level collaterals were evaluated on CT‐perfusion by the hypoperfusion intensity ratio (HIR), ranging from 0 to 1; and venous outflow was measured on mCTA with the comprehensive venous outflow (CVO) score, ranging from 0 to 8. These metrics were used to develop the multimodal collateral score (MCS), which encompasses good mCTA collaterals (score 4–5), HIR (< 0.4), and CVO (range 4–8). The MCS ranges from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates no favorable collaterals, 1 means only one good collateral metric among the used ones, 2 signifies just two favorable collateral metrics differently combined among the used ones, and 3 represents the sum of all good collateral metrics. Multivariable logistic and ordinal regression analyses identified clinical and radiological factors independently associated with clinical outcomes (modified Rankin scale 0–2 at 90 days, ordinal mRS at 90 days).ResultsOverall, 615 acute ischemic stroke patients treated with thrombectomy within 24 h from symptom onset between January 2017 and December 2023 were included: the median age was 77 [IQR = 67–84], 320 (52%) were females, and 307 (50%) achieved functional independence. The MCS grades of 2 (aOR 2.92 [95% CI = 1.70–5.03]) and 3 (aOR 5.35 [95% CI = 3.03–9.44]) were independently associated with functional independence. The MCS was also independently associated with 90‐day ordinal mRS (adjusted common odds ratio [acOR] 1.73 [95% CI = 1.51–2.01] per 1‐point increase). The AUC of the MCS for predicting functional independence was 0.71 (95% CI = 0.66–0.75), which was superior to the other IGR metrics (p < 0.001).ConclusionMCS was a strong, independent predictor of favorable functional outcomes and demonstrated significantly superior prognostic performance compared with individual IGR metrics.

Introduction

Endovascular treatment (EVT) is the standard of care in acute ischemic stroke patients suffering from large vessel occlusion (LVO) [1]. Nonetheless, nearly half of patients fail to achieve functional independence despite successful reperfusion [2]. EVT is most effective when performed in patients with a large volume of salvageable brain tissue (the ischemic penumbra) and a small infarct, which corresponds to irreversibly damaged cerebral tissue [3]. As the infarct expands progressively into the penumbra until reperfusion is achieved, timely EVT is critical; however, the infarct growth rate (IGR) varies significantly among patients [4]. Accurate estimation of IGR may aid prognostication and guide transfer decisions to EVT‐capable stroke centers [5, 6]. Several imaging biomarkers have been proposed to estimate IGR, including arterial collaterals on CT‐angiography [7, 8], tissue‐level collaterals from CT‐perfusion (CTP) [9, 10], and venous outflow on CTA [11, 12]. These biomarkers appear to reflect different components of the neurovascular collateral system, including pial major arteries, intraparenchymal arterioles and capillaries, and superficial and deep veins, which together sustain cerebral blood flow in ischemic tissue after vessel occlusion [13]. Emerging evidence suggests that a combined assessment of collateral network across these three levels may more accurately predict final infarct volume [14], cerebral edema growth [14], and functional outcomes [14, 15]. In this study, we aimed to determine whether a comprehensive multimodal collateral score (MCS), incorporating collateral assessment at the arterial, tissue, and venous levels, is associated with functional independence and provides incremental prognostic value over individual IGR metrics in ischemic stroke patients undergoing EVT within 24 h of symptom onset.

Methods

Data Availability, Standard Protocol Approval, and Patient Consent

The data analyzed in this study will be made available and shared by the corresponding author upon reasonable request from any qualified researcher, subject to clearance by the local ethics committee. This cohort study was approved by the local ethics boards, and clinical information was recorded during routine clinical activity (PN 26299). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or their legally authorized representatives at admission.

Patient Selection

In this retrospective observational study, we analyzed a prospectively collected cohort of 615 consecutive AIS patients with anterior circulation LVO treated with EVT and admitted to our hospital (Careggi University Hospital in Florence, Italy) from January 2017 to December 2023, as shown in Figure 1. All patients underwent non‐contrast computed tomography (NCCT), multiphase CT‐angiography (mCTA) of the cervical and intracranial vessels, and CTP at admission within 24 h of symptom onset. Patients were included if they presented to the emergency department with the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke within 24 h from witnessed symptom onset or time last seen well; (2) evidence of middle cerebral artery (MCA) M1 or M2 segment occlusion (considered as distal vessel occlusion only when localized to the region near or above the insula's mid‐height) or internal cerebral artery (ICA) occlusion on CTA; (3) selected for receiving EVT; (4) available baseline mCTA and CTP at admission and (5) follow‐up NCCT imaging performed at 24 ± 12 h. Exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 18 years; (2) pregnancy; (3) severe pre‐stroke disability defined as modified Rankin Scale (mRS) ≥ 3; (4) detection of intracerebral hemorrhage on admission NCCT; (5) contraindications to iodinated contrast agent; (6) poor quality of CT acquisition due to motion artifacts; (7) inability to complete the multi‐modal CT protocol or the conventional digital subtraction angiography study at baseline (due to poor image quality, complications after anesthesia administration, or technical issues) and/or 24‐h follow‐up NCCT (due to poor image quality or early transfer to spoke center from our thrombectomy‐capable center).

Clinical Assessment

Clinical, demographic, and technical data were collected by trained investigators, who were blinded to the outcomes of interest, from a prospectively maintained institutional stroke database. Clinical outcomes at 90 days were evaluated with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) and assessed through follow‐up telephone calls or outpatient service evaluations.

Imaging Analysis

Details on the imaging acquisition are available in the Supporting Information. The extent of early ischemic changes was evaluated on baseline NCCT using the ASPECTS methodology [16]. Occlusion sites were identified on mCTA and classified as terminal ICA, MCA‐M1 segment, or MCA‐M2 segment occlusions. As recommended by the vendor (Olea Medical software), total hypoperfused tissue and ischemic core volumes were defined as ischemic brain regions with time‐to‐maximum (Tmax) threshold values > 6 s (Tmax > 6 s) and relative cerebral blood flow (rCBF) threshold values less than 40% of normally perfused tissue (rCBF < 40%), respectively. The difference between the extent of Tmax > 6 s lesions and rCBF < 40% lesions was considered the ischemic penumbra. Mismatch ratio was defined as the volume with Tmax > 6 s divided by the volume with rCBF < 40%. All these parameters were automatically segmented and calculated by the software. Hemorrhagic transformation (HT) was classified on NCCT at 24 h from symptom onset/last known well according to the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study (ECASS)‐II criteria [17]. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) was considered any intracranial hemorrhage associated with a ≥ 4‐point increase in NIHSS [18]. The recanalization rate was assessed on DSA at the end of the EVT procedure using the modified treatment in cerebral ischemia (mTICI) scale. Patients with an mTICI score of 2b‐3 were considered successfully recanalized, whereas patients with an mTICI score of 0–2a were classified as not [19]. Final infarct volumes were manually segmented in ITK‐SNAP from the 24‐h follow‐up NCCT by readers blinded to EVT results and clinical outcomes. Final infarct volumes were automatically calculated by this software using a multislice planimetric method, which involved summing the hypodense areas manually traced on each slice where detectable, then multiplying by the slice thickness [20].

Multimodal Collateral Score

Arterial collaterals and venous outflow were graded on mCTA by two certified diagnostic neuroradiologists (G.B., with over 10 years of experience; E.F., with more than 20 years of experience), both blinded to clinical information. Inter‐reader agreement between these two neuroradiologists in assessing acute stroke imaging features using multimodal imaging in this cohort was previously evaluated and found to be excellent, with a Cohen's kappa coefficient > 0.8 [21]. Pial arterial collateral supply was graded on a 6‐point scale based on a previously published scoring system [22], with grades 0–3 considered poor and 4–5 indicating good collaterals: the lower the mCTA collateral status, the faster the IGR. Tissue‐level perfusion was assessed using the hypoperfusion intensity ratio (HIR), which is the ratio of Tmax > 10s to Tmax > 6 s lesion volumes. HIR values of 0.4 or less predicted good tissue‐related collaterals: the higher the HIR, the faster the IGR [23]. Venous outflow profiles were assessed using the Comprehensive Venous Outflow (CVO) score, which evaluates contrast filling as absent (0), partial (1), or full (2). This assessment compared the affected side to the contralateral healthy side, focusing on the sphenoparietal sinus, vein of Labbè, superficial middle cerebral vein, and internal cerebral vein [24]. Grades 0–3 were considered poor, and grades 4–8 were considered favorable venous outflow: the lower the CVO, the faster the IGR. Multimodal collateral score was derived from the combination of robust pial arterial collaterals (mCTA grades 4–5), tissue‐level collaterals (HIR ≤ 0.4), and venous outflow (CVO scores 4–8). The MCS ranged from 0 to 3, where 0 indicated no favorable collaterals, 1 meant only one good collateral metric among the used ones, 2 signified just two favorable collateral metrics differently combined among the used ones, and 3 represented the total of all good collaterals. Therefore, each component of the MCS, indicating a favorable collateral measure, was considered equal to the others in its pathophysiological implication. Outcomes

The primary outcome was functional independence (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0–2 at 90 days) after EVT. The secondary outcome was ordinal mRS at 90 days.

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographics, clinical variables, and neuroimaging data were compared between two or three MCS groups using chi‐square tests, Student t‐test, the Mann–Whitney U test, the Kruskal‐Wallis test, ANOVA, or trend statistics (for ordered alternatives) as appropriate. The distinct dichotomization thresholds for the three components of the MCS (pial arterial collaterals on mCTA, tissue‐level collaterals defined by the HIR, and VO on mCTA determined by the CVO score) were prespecified. Categorical data were presented as absolute numbers (%), whereas continuous variables were summarized as mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) if normally distributed or median and interquartile ranges (IQR) in case of non‐normal distribution. Baseline characteristics, treatment time metrics, and clinical outcomes were compared between patients with and without functional independence. Associations between IGR metrics and outcomes were assessed with binary or ordinal logistic regression, as appropriate. The secondary outcome measure was analyzed using an ordinal method based on the proportional odds model. If the model was rejected, that would indicate a significant difference in the mRS distribution, and results would be reported descriptively. We conducted both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. All adjusted models included the following prespecified variables: age, sex, mRS before stroke, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), intravenous thrombolysis, CT‐to‐reperfusion time, and reperfusion status (mTICI). Multicollinearity among covariates in the fully adjusted models was evaluated with the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF), with a threshold of 5 indicating potential collinearity. Effect size estimates from logistic regression were reported as unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for binary outcomes and common odds ratios (cORs) for ordinal outcomes, along with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). For HIR, the ORs indicate the odds of the outcome for a 0.1‐point increase. The prognostic performance for predicting the primary outcome of the MCS and its components (mCTA arterial collaterals, HIR, and CVO) was measured using the area under the curve (AUC) and ROC curves, with comparisons made using the DeLong test [20]. To adequately assess the prognostic value of MCS, an additional performance model was also developed using the ROC curve. In this analysis, a composite variable including age, NIHSS at onset, and successful reperfusion status was compared with another variable that included MCS along with these factors. Because only 40% of our patients were treated in the late treatment window, we conducted a subgroup analysis to assess the association between MCS and functional outcomes among late presenters. Since data could not be assumed to be missing at random, no imputation was performed for missing data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐sided). All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 and MedCalc.

Regarding demographic characteristics, patients who achieved functional independence, compared to those who did not, were younger (71 [IQR = 61–81] vs. 77 [IQR = 72–85], p < 0.001), less frequently females (47.5% vs. 56.4%, p = 0.027), and showed lower pre‐stroke disability (mRS 0 [IQR = 0–0] vs. 0 [IQR = 0–1], p < 0.001).

Regarding clinical and radiological factors at baseline, patients who achieved functional independence, compared to those who did not, showed lower NIHSS at admission (15 [IQR = 10–19] vs. 19 [15–24], p < 0.001), more frequent M2‐MCA occlusion (24% vs. 13%, p < 0.001), higher ASPECTS (8 [IQR = 7–9] vs. 7 [IQR = 6–9], p < 0.001), smaller total hypoperfused lesion volumes (84.7 mL [IQR = 45.9–131.1] vs. 105.6 mL [IQR = 65.0–150], p < 0.001), and smaller CTP‐estimated ischemic core volumes (19.1 mL [IQR = 8.3–36.1] vs. 34.0 mL [IQR = 15.7–55.1], p < 0.001).

Regarding IGR measures, patients who achieved functional independence, compared to those who did not, showed a lower IGR across all the mCTA and CTP methods assessed: mCTA collateral status (median [IQR] 4 [4] vs. 3 [3, 4], p < 0.001), HIR (0.29 [IQR = 0.14–0.40] vs. 0.47 [IQR = 0.30–0.53], p < 0.001), and CVO (median [IQR] 4 [4–6] vs. 3 [2–4], p < 0.001).

Regarding treatment workflow, patients who achieved functional independence, compared to those who did not, showed a higher successful reperfusion rate (91.2% vs. 75.9%, p < 0.001), faster reperfusion, that is, within 90 min (31% vs. 24%, p = 0.004), shorter CT‐to‐reperfusion time (110 [IQR = 85–140] vs. 128 [IQR = 95–160], p = 0.001, per each 10 min increase), a smaller follow‐up infarct volume (18.2 mL [IQR = 6.3–31.7] vs. 46.5 mL [IQR = 20–133], p < 0.001), lower hemorrhagic transformation rate (25.7% vs. 32.2%, p = 0.003), and symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage rate (1.4% vs. 10.7%, p < 0.001).

Association Between IGR Measure and Outcomes

Arterial collaterals evaluated with mCTA (aOR 0.67 [95% CI = 0.50–0.90] per 1‐point increase), tissue‐level collaterals assessed with HIR (aOR 0.31 [95% CI = 0.10–0.93] per 0.1‐unit increase), and venous outflow analyzed with the CVO score (aOR 0.86 [95% CI = 0.76–0.98] per 1‐point increase) were all independently associated with functional independence.

The combination of these individual collateral assessments (the MCS) showed a stepwise greater association with functional independence: MCS 1 (aOR 1.08 [95% CI = 0.60–1.95]), MCS 2 (aOR 2.92 [95% CI = 1.70–5.03]), and MCS 3 (aOR 5.35 [95% CI = 3.03–9.44]) (Table 2).

Prognostic Performance of IGR Measures for Functional Independence

The MCS demonstrated higher prognostic performance than each individual IGR metric: MCS (AUC 0.71 [95% CI = 0.66–0.75], p < 0.001), mCTA collateral status (AUC 0.65 [95% CI = 0.60–0.69], p < 0.001), HIR (AUC 0.64 [95% CI = 0.59–0.68], p < 0.001), and CVO (AUC 0.63 [95% CI = 0.58–0.67], p < 0.001), as shown in Figure 3. A composite variable encompassing the MCS, age, NIHSS at onset, and successful reperfusion showed better prognostic performance (AUC 0.73 [95% CI = 0.67–0.79], p < 0.001) than the other model that evaluated all these variables without incorporating the MCS (AUC 0.69 [95% CI = 0.62–0.76], p < 0.001), as shown in Supporting Figure.

Subgroup Analysis for Patients in the Late Treatment Window (6–24 h From Onset)

When the analysis was restricted to the 40% (241/615) of our patients treated in the late treatment window, multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that MCS showed, again, a stepwise greater association with functional independence: MCS 1 (aOR 1.83 [95% CI = 0.58–5.72]), MCS 2 (aOR 4.46 [95% CI = 1.62–12.29]), and MCS 3 (aOR 11.80 [95% CI = 4.15–33.50]). Furthermore, ordinal logistic regression analysis confirmed that MCS was independently associated with 90‐day ordinal mRS (adjusted common odds ratio [acOR] 0.56 [95% CI = 0.41–0.76] per 1‐point increase).

Discussion

Our study has three main findings: (i) a combined multimodal collateral score (MCS) that includes all favorable IGR measures was associated with functional outcomes, (ii) MCS outperformed each individual IGR measure in predicting functional independence, and (iii) patients with at least two favorable collateral measures had higher odds of functional independence.

The optimal method to evaluate IGR remains to be determined. Our findings suggest that cerebral blood flow supplied by pial arterioles, via microvascular tissue‐level collaterals, into deep and cortical veins, is crucial for sustaining the salvageable penumbra during brain ischemia and is directly associated with reduced infarct core growth over time. Moreover, a composite prediction model that included age, NIHSS at onset, and successful reperfusion demonstrated better prognostic performance for functional independence when it encompassed the MCS. This finding further underscores the reliability of the MCS as a key imaging parameter before treatment to aid in prognosis. A recent paper reported a strong correlation across all collaterals at arterial, tissue, and venous levels (i.e., the cerebral collateral cascade) and IGR, defined as ischemic core volume on admission CTP divided by the time from stroke onset [25]. However, this result is unsurprising, as all collateral metrics included in the cerebral collateral cascade and CTP core/onset time are well‐established measures of IGR. Indeed, our results could have important clinical implications for AIS patients eligible for EVT, as those with an unfavorable MCS profile (i.e., grades 0 and 1) are at increased risk of rapid infarct growth, which may lead to poorer functional outcomes after EVT [26]. In addition, these patients, appropriately identified, could benefit from neuroprotective agents designed to slow down cerebral infarct growth. A major strength of our research is the more accurate evaluation of arterial inflow and venous filling achieved by adopting the mCTA approach, as well as the assessment of both superficial and deep venous drainage using the newly described CVO score [24]. In fact, it has been demonstrated that, as cerebral blood flow to the viable penumbra tends to disperse over time, collaterals are often best evaluated with multiple time points by analyzing delayed pial collateral filling, which is often underestimated with a single phase [27]. Moreover, it was recently shown that VO efficiency is best captured in the second peak of the venous phase on mCTA, which is considered the optimal time point for assessing venous opacities [28]. In this regard, CVO demonstrated the ability to outperform other VO measures in predicting functional independence, owing to its combined evaluation of specific superficial and deep venous systems, with a stronger prognostic association [24]. Future research will clarify whether CVO can be considered a reliable parameter, also compared with angiographic assessment of venous outflow on DSA, which is widely considered the gold standard for collateral evaluation [29]. A clear advantage of adopting MCS profiles is that they help address the discrepancy between CTA and CTP in assessing core progression, as patients classified as fast progressors on perfusion imaging may not be similarly categorized on CTA, and vice versa [30]. In addition, a patient may exhibit both fast and slow core progression patterns simultaneously in different regions, resulting from significant differences in the efficiency of macro‐ and microperfusion [31] or venous drainage [11] within the ischemic area. Interestingly, MCS profiles identify infarct core progression independently of the time of symptom onset, which is often unknown, whereas infarct growth rate is affected by the variable pattern of infarct growth [32]. Several imaging modalities have been proposed to characterize core progression [4], but none have demonstrated a clear advantage over the others. MCS demonstrates the utility of HIR as a measure of infarct growth regardless of time since onset [33, 34] by incorporating assessments of macrovascular inflow and outflow around the ischemic tissue. Consequently, MCS was shown to be an independent predictor of functional independence. Although the association between comprehensive collateral evaluation and clinical outcome was already known [13, 14], our analysis relied on mCTA to assess the predictive ability of leptomeningeal arteries and venous outflow, thus making this relationship more reliable than that established with single‐phase CTA in those studies. Different from these reports [13, 14], we found that infarct core volume also independently predicted a good functional outcome. This result indirectly suggests that limiting core progression over time is a key factor in achieving improved clinical outcomes after successful recanalization, supporting the adoption of MCS profiles as a useful tool to identify eligible candidates who could benefit from EVT. Interestingly, ischemic penumbra volumes were similar between patients who achieved functional independence and those who did not; although this observation may be unexpected, it is possible that the ischemic core volume had a greater clinical influence in our cohort, or that a simple binary imaging categorization (core‐penumbra) did not fully capture the nuanced severity of ischemic injury. Taken together, our findings suggest that combining multiple modalities for collateral assessment is more effective than using a single technique to measure IGR and is a strong predictor of clinical outcome. Future research is warranted to establish whether MCS can be as reliable as, or even outperform, other well‐recognized prognostic baseline neuroimaging models that combine evaluation of collaterals at multiple levels [25, 35, 36], and to compare it with other novel imaging parameters that predict futile recanalization [37]. This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size from a single institution may compromise the consistency of our data. Second, because this study used a retrospective design, our findings require prospective validation. Third, during the long data‐collection period, EVT effectiveness may have been influenced by technological advancements and increased operator experience, which could reduce the generalizability of our prognostic findings. Fourth, we cannot exclude the influence of unmeasured confounders, which could introduce selection bias given the non‐randomized design of our study; for instance, devices, passes, groin‐to‐reperfusion time, use of general anesthesia or conscious sedation, and blood pressure after EVT—all variables potentially affecting functional outcomes—were not systematically measured or collected. Fifth, imaging selection criteria probably excluded individuals who were faster progressors, that is, those with an extensive ischemic core (or low ASPECTS) or poor collateral status at presentation. As a result, our data do not encompass the entire range of IGR, limiting the generalizability of our results. Sixth, as recommended by the vendor, the admission infarct core volume was calculated using rCBF < 40% as the threshold, rather than the widely adopted rCBF < 30% threshold. Finally, a single CTP software was used; confirmation of our findings on other platforms is needed.

Conclusions

The multimodal collateral score (MCS), which combines arterial, tissue, and venous collaterals, was associated with functional outcomes and had significantly superior prognostic performance compared with individual IGR metrics. These findings suggest that arterial, tissue‐level, and venous collaterals provide complementary information, and their combination improves prognostication.
