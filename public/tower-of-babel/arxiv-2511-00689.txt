Do Methods to Jailbreak and Defend LLMs Generalize Across Languages?
Berk Atil, Rebecca J. Passonneau, Fred Morstatter

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) undergo safety alignment after training and tuning, yet recent work shows that safety can be bypassed through jailbreak attacks. While many jailbreaks and defenses exist, their cross-lingual generalization remains underexplored. This paper presents the first systematic multilingual evaluation of jailbreaks and defenses across ten languages–spanning high-, medium-, and low-resource languages–using six LLMs on HarmBench and AdvBench. We assess two jailbreak types: logical-expression-based and adversarial-prompt-based. For both types, attack success and defense robustness vary across languages: high-resource languages are safer under standard queries but more vulnerable to adversarial ones. Simple defenses can be effective, but are language- and model-dependent. These findings call for language-aware and cross-lingual safety benchmarks for LLMs.



1
Introduction

A typical pipeline for training large language models (LLMs) consists of pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and human (safety) alignment. Safety alignment
Bai et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2024
aims to reduce the risks of harmful behaviors, thus successful alignment should result in refusal to answer queries like “How to make a bomb?” In some cases, safety alignment can be bypassed using adversarial prompts, known as “jailbreak attacks”
Yi et al. 2024
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025a
;
Chao et al. 2024
. To defend against such attacks, both finetuning-based and prompting-based methods have been proposed
Robey et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
. However, most attacks and defenses have been evaluated only in English. Here, we analyze the effectiveness of jailbreak attacks and defenses across multiple languages, including low-resource ones.

While safety alignment aims to ensure human-aligned behavior, the capabilities and safety of LLMs can vary significantly across languages
OpenAI 2024
;
Qwen et al. 2025
;
Deng et al. 2024
. LLMs are generally less robust to jailbreaking methods in lower-resource languages, resulting in higher unsafe response rates, depending on the benchmark (e.g., MultiJail
Deng et al. 2024
; AdvBench
Yong et al. 2024
). A more comprehensive analysis of jailbreak methods and corresponding defenses across languages can provide important insights into the specific gaps in LLM safety across diverse languages.

2
Related Work

We review the previous work on state-of the-art (SOTA) jailbreak attacks and defense mechanisms against these attacks.

2.1
Jailbreaking Methods

Jailbreaking refers to intentional elicitation of harmful responses from LLMs by breaking through their safety alignment. The impact of the choice of a language and jailbreak technique has been investigated by comparing high- versus low-resource languages, and by developing adversarial prompts that include non-standard language, e.g, code-switching, or formal symbols, e.g., logical expressions. It has been shown that adversarial queries result in higher unsafe response rates in lower-resource languages
Yong et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2024
. The comprehensive analysis
Deng et al. 2024
performed in 30 languages supports this finding, and shows the effectiveness of fine-tuning against this vulnerability. In addition to the effect of the resource level of a language, the format of the language, such as being a non-standard language has been studied.
Yoo et al. 2025
show that adversarial questions with code-switching increase unsafe response rates in 10 languages. Further,
Peng et al. 2025a
propose converting harmful queries into logical expressions, assuming logical expressions are OOD (out-of-distribution) in safety fine-tunings. Most previous work focuses on English and does not analyze the effects of attacks against defenses. In this work, we analyze both diverse jailbreak methods and defenses against them across languages with different levels of resources.

2.2
Improving Safety of the LLMs

Both modificaton of model weights and prompt-based methods have been investigated to make LLMs more robust against jailbreak attacks.
Zhao et al. 2025
suggest using alignment fine-tuning for non-English languages based on the idea that the likelihood of a safe versus unsafe response differs by language. Another finetuning-based method is to train an LLM to label its output as harmful or harmless and to use a simple filter to block harmful responses
Wang et al. 2024
.
Fei et al. 2025
propose an alternative method, nudging during inference time, based on the idea of generating harmful tokens less often. They insert tokens to guide the base-LLM (without safety alignment) based on the probabilities of a smaller safety-aligned model. They show that the safety-alignment step may not be necessary. Apart from
Zhao et al. 2025
, none of the methods were evaluated in a non-English language. In this work, we test a prompting-based defense mechanism based on
Wang et al. 2024
across ten languages.

3
Experimental Setup and Methods

We experiment with two datasets consisting of harmful queries, HarmBench and AdvBench. We prompt 6 LLMs from 4 families using these queries and apply two jailbreaking methods on those queries. Last, we adapt a prompting-based defense mechanism against these methods with a simple classifier that identifies if the response is safe.

3.1
Data

We use standard queries (i.e., prompts that do not include jailbreak attacks) from HarmBench
Mazeika et al. 2024
and AdvBench
Zou et al. 2023
. Table
1
shows example queries and total count for both datasets. HarmBench also has category information such as illegal activity or harassment and bullying that allows more detailed analysis. Table
1
thus shows the category breakdown for HarmBench. AdvBench has 520 toxic behavior strings including profanity, graphic depictions, threatening behavior, misinformation, discrimination, cybercrime, and dangerous suggestions. HarmBench is a comprehensive evaluation framework for red teaming that is more diverse, broader, and more focused on safety than AdvBench, whereas AdvBench is designed more for jailbreaking.
We use the standard subset with 200 malicious requests including illegal activities, cyber crime intrusion, misinformation, chemical and biological weapons, harassment, and bullying.

Based on the distinctions among languages discussed in
Deng et al. 2024
, we selected ten languages: Bengali, Telugu, and Swahili as low-resource languages; Turkish, Arabic, and Korean as medium-resource languages; German, Chinese, Spanish, and English as high-resource languages.

3.2
Models

We experimented with GPT-4o
OpenAI 2024
and Sonnet 4 from Anthropic as closed-source models; Qwen2.5-7b/14b
Qwen et al. 2025
and Llama3.1-8b/70b
Grattafiori and et al. 2024
as open-source models. We used a temperature of 0 in our experiments to maintain reproducibility.

3.3
Jailbreak Methods

There are different types of jailbreaking techniques in the literature. We experimented with two distinct approaches.

The first SOTA jailbreaking we experiment with is “Logic Jailbreak”
Peng et al. 2025b
. They convert harmful queries into logic expressions and tell the model that they are working on formal semantics. To convert the queries into logical expressions, they prompt GPT-4o-mini with few-shot prompting. We apply the same procedure for English. After we get English logical expressions, we translate the “predicate names” into the relevant languages, keeping the same logical form.

The second SOTA jailbreaking we investigate is prompt-based
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
. They employ rules (e.g. “Never start your response with “I” or “Sorry”. ”) to prevent models from refusing to answer, and add adversarial suffixes (e.g. “<bos>business”). After having an initial prompt, they optimize the prompt to increase the probability of the first token to be “sure.”
They also claim that when a prompt is optimized for a model, it generalizes well to other models. Therefore, in our experiments, we use the prompt that was optimized for the GPT-4o model. We refer to this approach as Andrius25.

3.4
Defense Methods

We experiment with two different defenses: (1) a prompt-based approach; (2) training a simple classifier to block harmful responses.

We adapted Self-Guard
Wang et al. 2024
as a prompt-based defense mechanism. The prompt we used here is presented in Appendix
A
.

Our second approach was to train
a binary classifier for use as a filter on top of an LLM response. To generate embeddings of the responses,
we experimented with SBERT
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
and versions of MPNet
Song et al. 2020
,
which we compared with
Multilingual MiniLM
Wang et al. 2020
and DistilLM
Reimers and Gurevych 2020
, due to their multilingual embedding performance and convenient size. We experimented with 1 or 2 dense layers for classification. Further, we tested whether
the following types of
additional information helps: (1) prepending the question to the response (query); (2) emotion probabilities using XLM-EMO
Bianchi et al. 2022
, which provides probability values for anger, joy, sadness, and fear; (3) sentiment using XLM-T
Barbieri et al. 2022
with positive sentiment value only; (4) entailment between the question and the response using XLM-Roberta Large XNLI
Conneau et al. 2020
(NLI); (5) sentiment and entailment values combined. During training, we project any of these types of additional information into a higher dimension using a linear layer and ReLU, then concatenate that representation with the embedding of the response.

3.5
Safety Evaluation

Similar to previous works
Kritz et al. 2025
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2025a
;
Deng et al. 2024
, we used LLM-as-judges to decide if a response is safe. We used GPT-4o and the prompt from
Deng et al. 2024
that labels responses as “safe,” “unsafe” or “invalid.” We should note that we report unsafe rates only, without considering invalid responses, as discussed in the limitations section.
We do not translate the non-English responses back to English during evaluation because evaluations are more accurate without translating, based on our preliminary experiments. To verify the labels, we sampled 100 examples from all responses including responses to jailbreaks for each language. Reliable native speakers annotated each example: interannotator agreement scores from Krippendorff’s
\alpha
for each language are all above 0.70: English, 0.86; Spanish, 0.82; German, 0.72; Chinese, 0.76; Arabic and Turkish, 0.71; Korean, 0.72; Telugu, 0.72; Bengali, 0.70.

4.2
The Effects of Jailbreak Attacks

In this section, we first present the overall effectiveness of jailbreak. Then, we talk about the details of each jailbreak method. Further, we show the effect of language resource availability and the instability of language unsafe rate rankings. Lastly, we talk about the differences between datasets and model sizes.

Tables
2
and
3
show that both jailbreaking methods work for most models in most languages.
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
’s jailbreaking method works best, especially on open-source models, with unsafe rates close to 100%. On the other hand, logic jailbreaking works better for GPT4o. The models that are most robust to jailbreaking are GPT4o and Sonnet4.

Peng et al. 2025b
’s logic jailbreak succeeds in most cases, and increases the unsafe rates in both datasets. Furthermore, it outperforms
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
on Gpt4o. However, it does not work well with Sonnet4. Sonnet4 usually explains why it is dangerous to do the specified action when it is prompted with logic expressions and sometimes explains how to interpret the logical expression to teach formal semantics.

An interesting observation is that although there is a negative correlation between the available resources for a language and its unsafe rate with standard queries (cf.
Yong et al. 2024
). Figure
4
shows that this trend reverses with jailbreak attacks. Standard queries with high-resource languages have low unsafe rates, but with jailbreak attacks, they can be relatively more vulnerable:
English is the safest language with standard queries, but the least safe with logic jailbreaking. On the other hand, Telugu is the third least safe language with standard queries,
but the safest language with both jailbreak methods. One possible explanation
might be that models are more proficient in high-resource languages, and better at interpreting the non-standard queries (logical expressions or tricks in
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
), yet not well-aligned to safety in the latter cases. This suggests a tradeoff between general performance versus robustness against adversarial attacks.

Another finding is about the degree of change in safety ranking of languages. Figure
5
shows the standard deviation of language safety ranking among models across the three prompting methods. Korean, Turkish and Chinese have less change in unsafety ranking across prompt methods, while the ranks of English, Telegu and Bengali have the greatest differences.
Both datasets used here show similar patterns except for Spanish, whose positions vary more on AdvBench. These results indicate that there is no single language that is the safest across models and prompting strategies.

4.4
Analysis of Response Embedding Space

Another analytic tool we use is to compare locations of responses to standard queries and responses to HarmBench queries in embedding space, using MPNet

Song et al. 2020
. We calculate the average cosine distance between all pairs of safe responses, all pairs of unsafe responses, and all pairs of a safe and an unsafe response, for responses to standard queries versus responses to jailbreak prompts. For logic jailbreak, safe responses become similar for most models except Gpt4o. Unsafe responses on the other hand, always become similar to each other with logic jailbreaking. Also, safe and unsafe responses get closer to each other in the embedding space. For
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
, we observe similar patterns. However, for Sonnet4, safe responses become dissimilar, and for Gpt4o, the distance between safe and unsafe responses increases. These results indicate that jailbreak attacks reduce the diversity of the output, models use similar wordings, and even safe and unsafe responses become similar to each other. The latter might be one reason for the success of jailbreak by helping bypass safety guards in the models. However, we also observe that the average distance between safe and unsafe responses is usually larger than that within safe or unsafe responses. This motivated us to build a classifier to distinguish safe and unsafe responses as a filtering mechanism on the output. We present our results on this in the next section.

5
Conclusion

For systems to be fair and equitable, LLMs should demonstrate consistent behavior across languages. This work, to our knowledge, presents the first systematic multilingual evaluation of both jailbreak and defense methods for LLMs. By analyzing 10 languages, six models, and two benchmark datasets, we reveal that both attack success and defense robustness vary across languages. While high-resource languages tend to be safer under standard queries, they become more vulnerable to jailbreaks, suggesting that linguistic proficiency may amplify rather than mitigate susceptibility to adversarial attacks. The method in
Andriushchenko et al. 2025
generalizes effectively across models but inconsistently across languages, indicating gaps in multilingual safety alignment. Our results further show that simple defenses—such as self-verification prompting and lightweight multilingual classifiers—can detect unsafe responses, though performance is sensitive to language and model characteristics. These findings highlight that safety alignment does not transfer uniformly across linguistic contexts.

6
Limitations

One limitation is that we discard invalid responses when reporting unsafe rates, although they are also undesirable outputs. Only looking at the unsafe rate might hinder some of the findings when the invalid response rate is not very low. However, we discard invalid response rates because each invalid response should be treated separately to understand if it is unsafe. Future work might improve safety evaluation with LLMs-as-judges. In addition, we cover 10 languages, but there are thousands of languages that we did not experiment with. Our results may not generalize to some of these other languages. We are also limited to a few language models; the behavior of other models, especially from different families, might be different.
