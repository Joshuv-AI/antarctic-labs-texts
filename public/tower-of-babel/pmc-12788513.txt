Prevalence of Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease in Obese Patients and its Association with Insulin Resistance Markers
Authors: Janak Nayak, Neena Elizebeth Luke, Amit Vasant Deshpande, Sangeeta Gupta, Huma Saiyad, Samreen Farooqui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is increasingly recognized as a major cause of chronic liver disease, especially in obese individuals. Insulin resistance is a key pathophysiological mechanism linking obesity to NAFLD.Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted in 240 obese adults (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) attending a tertiary care center. NAFLD was diagnosed using abdominal ultrasonography. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting insulin, and lipid profiles were measured. Insulin resistance was calculated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Statistical analysis included independent t-tests, chi-square tests, and Pearson correlation, with P < 0.05 considered significant.Results:The mean age was 44.2 ± 10.6 years, and 58.3% were women. NAFLD was diagnosed in 152 patients (63.3%). Patients with NAFLD had significantly higher BMI (34.7 ± 3.8 kg/m² vs. 32.5 ± 3.1 kg/m², P < 0.001), waist circumference (108.2 ± 9.5 cm vs. 102.6 ± 8.8 cm, P < 0.001), fasting insulin (18.6 ± 6.2 µIU/mL vs. 12.9 ± 4.7 µIU/mL, P < 0.001), and HOMA-IR (4.2 ± 1.6 vs. 2.8 ± 1.2, P < 0.001). A positive correlation was observed between HOMA-IR and hepatic steatosis grade (r = 0.61, P < 0.001).Conclusion:NAFLD prevalence in obese patients was high and strongly associated with insulin resistance markers. Early screening and targeted lifestyle or pharmacological interventions are warranted to reduce metabolic and hepatic complications.

INTRODUCTION

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) encompasses a spectrum ranging from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, and cirrhosis.[1] It is now the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, with a reported prevalence of 25–30% in the general population and exceeding 50% among obese individuals.[23] NAFLD is closely linked to the global obesity epidemic and the rising incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).[4]

The pathogenesis of NAFLD is multifactorial, with insulin resistance (IR) considered central in the “multiple-hit” hypothesis.[5] IR promotes hepatic fat accumulation through increased free fatty acid influx, de novo lipogenesis, and impaired lipid export.[6] Several studies have demonstrated a significant association between NAFLD and surrogate IR markers such as fasting insulin and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).[78] However, the magnitude of this association in specific high-risk subgroups such as obese adults in developing countries remains underexplored.

Early detection of NAFLD in obese individuals is crucial because progression to advanced liver disease can occur silently, and lifestyle modifications at early stages can reverse steatosis.[9] Despite the increasing burden, data from South Asian populations, where genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors may alter the prevalence and severity of NAFLD, are limited.[10]

A HOMA-IR value > 2.5 was considered indicative of insulin resistance.

NAFLD diagnosis

Abdominal ultrasonography was performed by an experienced radiologist blinded to laboratory results, using a 3.5 MHz convex transducer. Hepatic steatosis was graded (Grade 0–3) based on echogenicity and visualization of intrahepatic structures.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.

DISCUSSION

In this cross-sectional study of obese adults, NAFLD prevalence was 63.3%, consistent with reports from similar high-risk cohorts.[1112] This underscores the strong relationship between obesity and NAFLD, mediated largely by insulin resistance.[13] Our findings that NAFLD patients had significantly higher fasting insulin and HOMA-IR values align with the pathophysiological role of IR in hepatic steatosis.[45]

The observed positive correlation between NAFLD severity and HOMA-IR mirrors findings from previous studies in both Western and Asian populations.[67] Central obesity, reflected by higher waist circumference, likely contributes to IR via adipose tissue inflammation and altered adipokine secretion.[8]

Interestingly, HDL-C levels were significantly lower in the NAFLD group, supporting its role as part of the dyslipidemic triad associated with metabolic syndrome.[9] Elevated triglyceride levels in NAFLD patients further reinforce the link between hepatic lipid metabolism and systemic insulin resistance.[10]

Our study’s strengths include a well-defined obese cohort, standardized ultrasonographic grading, and assessment of multiple IR markers. Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, and the lack of histological confirmation, although ultrasound remains a practical screening tool in clinical settings.[2]

Future research should focus on longitudinal evaluation of IR progression in NAFLD patients and the effect of targeted interventions on both hepatic and metabolic outcomes.

CONCLUSION

NAFLD is highly prevalent among obese individuals and strongly associated with markers of insulin resistance, including fasting insulin and HOMA-IR. The severity of hepatic steatosis correlates positively with insulin resistance. Early identification and management of IR in obese patients may be pivotal in preventing NAFLD progression and associated metabolic complications.
