Collaborative Working to Address Inappropriate ED Attendances by Nursing Home Residents
Authors: Mary McCarthy, Patricia Sheahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Increase in life expectancy in Ireland and social isolation has led to an increasing number of people living in residential care facilities (RCFs). Residents are frequently transferred to emergency departments (ED) for a variety of reasons. Studies found that up to 40% of these hospital admissions were deemed inappropriate. An inappropriate admission in previous studies has been defined as a situation in which care in lower cost settings would be as safe and less disruptive than care in higher cost hospital settings.MethodsA review of a convenience sample of ED attendances to University Hospital Kerry (UHK) found that 50% were inappropriate. A SMART aim in a quality improvement project (QIP) is an aim which is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time based. The SMART aim of this QIP was to reduce the number of persons residing in RCFs, being inappropriately referred to UHK, from 50% to 30% from March 2024 to May 2025.Quality improvement (QI) measures included the standardisation of terminology through the workings of the palliative frailty multidisciplinary team, development of a communication document on resuscitation status and treatment escalation preferences, and the implementation of an advanced nurse practitioner (ANP) palliative care service for RCFs supported by a palliative medicine physician. Education was integral in this QIP.ResultsQI measures resulted in a reduction in monthly ED attendances of RCF residents from a median of 82 to 50. Inappropriate attendances reduced from 50% to 31%. Stakeholders’ feedback on the new service was overwhelmingly positive. The project resulted in financial savings for the health service.ConclusionIntegration of the geriatric and palliative medicine services with staff of RCFs allowed for sharing of knowledge, standardisation of terminology and development of alternative models of care and pathways to access specialties. The introduction of a designated ANP palliative care service for RCFs has been essential in helping residents to receive the right care, in the right place, at the right time.Keywords: Palliative Care, Geriatrics, Emergency department, Nursing homes, Quality improvement

Problem

A SMART aim for a QIP is an aim which is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time based. Our SMART aim was to reduce the number of persons residing in residential care facilities (RCFs), being inappropriately referred to University Hospital Kerry (UHK) Emergency Department (ED), from 50% to 30% from March 2024 to May 2025.

Saliba et al defined an inappropriate admission as ‘a situation in which care in lower cost settings would be as safe and less disruptive than care in higher cost hospital settings’.1 This definition was adopted for the quality improvement project (QIP).

The project was supported by the Kerry Specialist Palliative Care Service (KSPCS) and the Geriatric Medicine Department of Kerry and involved the frontline staff working across the microsystem that cares for older people living with frailty in RCFs. The Department of Geriatric Medicine has had an increase in workforce numbers which enabled the development of a Geriatric Emergency Multidisciplinary Service and Kerry Integrated Care Programme for Older Person, which includes a nursing home outreach and early supported discharge service.

The 2022 Census found that Kerry had a population of 156 458, of whom 29 566 (19%) were aged 65 years or older.2 KSPCS provides an integrated service for its catchment population of 185 000. KSPCS provides care across the specialist palliative care hub, acute hospital and community setting. The service was developed based on the Report of the National Advisory Committee on Palliative Care 2001 and continues to develop in line with the current National Adult Palliative Care Policy (2024).3 4

The geriatric and palliative medicine multidisciplinary teams (MDT) felt many older people living with frailty in RCFs were being referred to ED inappropriately. The teams believed the issues could have been addressed by alternative pathways, allowing patients to remain in their residences. However, the teams did not have data to support this. “Without data, you are just another person with an opinion.”5

To verify this assertion, a retrospective review of RCF residents referred to UHK ED was undertaken. The review of a convenience sample of ED attendances found that 50% were inappropriate. The problems identified were in line with international findings, for example, lack of advance care planning (ACP), inadequate symptom control and low-acuity medical issues, which could have been managed by an alternative pathway with access to specialist services and allied healthcare professionals (AHP).

The QIP is important for patients to preserve their dignity and autonomy and optimise their comfort. Exposing frail and vulnerable people to a disorientating ED has a negative impact on patients when an alternative option is available.6 7 It is important to families so that their loved ones receive care that is accessible, appropriate and not burdensome. It is important for families to be involved in decision-making processes.8 9 Agreement on ACP is important as it reduces the decision burden on families and staff.

This project is important for staff across all care settings, as providing the right care, in the right place, at the right time, in line with a person’s wishes, improves job satisfaction and morale.10 Inappropriate ED attendances affect the efficiency and culture of each department and organisation, leading to staff burnout and reduced work performance with associated financial implications.11

Background

Life expectancy in Ireland has increased due to innovations in medicine, technology and advancements in healthcare. Along with social isolation, this has led to an increase in the number of people living in RCFs. Currently, over 31 000 older adults are living in RCFs in Ireland. By 2050, it is estimated that 73, 000+ nursing home beds will be needed.12

The COVID-19 Nursing Homes Expert Panel highlighted the need for reform of nursing home care in Ireland. Key priorities included the need for their integration into a continuum of care, supported by community teams and enhanced clinical roles.13 The lottery of your address defining the care received needs to cease, and RCF residents should have access to the same services as community-based residents.

Frail residents have unique complex needs and require access to specialist services in a timely manner to reduce hospital attendance. International research indicates that RCF residents often face significant barriers in accessing these specialist services, leading to unmet healthcare needs.14 15

A review of UK surveys of services within the National Health Service (NHS) found that a common problem encountered by nursing home managers on behalf of their residents was difficulties in accessing specialist services and teams, including palliative care teams.16 Specialist palliative care services were found to play a key role in reducing symptom burden and improving the quality of end-of-life (EOL) care.17 International evidence suggests that 35–67% of admissions to acute hospitals of residents from RCFs are potentially preventable.18

Research shows that the main drivers of inappropriate referrals can be divided into four categories: lack of clinical support for RCF staff, particularly outside normal working hours; lack of ACP; lack of alternative pathways for low-acuity medical issues; and lack of information on the patient’s functional status and medical issues, making it difficult for out-of-hours medical staff (OOHMS) to determine the most appropriate treatment.19

A Cochrane review comparing the effect of alternative models of care with usual care on the number of ED attendances, hospital admissions, quality of life, mortality and costs found that alternative models of care may reduce unplanned hospital admissions by 27%.20 International studies of quality improvement (QI) initiatives suggest education and training of RCF staff can lead to a reduction in acute hospital admissions. The Interventions to Reduce Acute Care Transfers (INTERACT) programme is an example of a QI initiative, which has demonstrated the ability to reduce potentially avoidable admissions by 18%.21

Measurement

The number of RCF residents attending the ED monthly was compiled by a data analyst using the RCF addresses. Baseline data were obtained by two senior clinicians reviewing a convenience sample of attendances and categorising them as either appropriate or inappropriate based on Saliba et al’s definition. The medical charts of 40 patients who attended ED on the first week of March, April and May 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. 20 patients were deemed to be inappropriately referred. Reasons were lack of ACP, low-acuity medical issues that could have been managed by alternative pathways and poor symptom control as outlined in the Pareto Chart (online supplemental appendix 1).

Four focus groups involving staff from all care settings were formed. The palliative frailty MDT focus group was composed of doctors, advanced nurse practitioners (ANPs), Clinical Nurse Managers (CNMs) and Clinical Nurse Specialists (CNSs) from the Departments of Geriatric and Palliative Medicine.

The remaining focus groups included staff of the National Ambulance Service, RCFs in Kerry and UHK ED. The project team collated information from the groups and designed a fishbone diagram which helped illustrate the problems.

A measurement plan was devised which included the following measures.

Outcome measure

The outcome measure will be obtained by two senior clinicians reviewing a convenience sample of ED attendances by RCF residents from March to May 2025 and determining the percentage that is inappropriate based on Saliba et al’s definition.

Process measure

Two process measures will be evaluated. First, the reduction in the number of monthly ED attendances of RCF residents after the introduction of the ANP palliative care service in June 2024 (these data were collected from the hospital integrated Patient Management System (iPMS)). Second, the number of referrals to KSPCS from RCFs for 11 months prior to introduction of ANP service compared with the following 11 months (this was sourced from the KSPCS key performance indicators (KPIs)).

Balancing measure

It was hypothesised that the number of referrals to KSPCS would increase following the introduction of the ANP service to RCFs. The plan was to determine if this had any unintentional consequences including KPI breaches.

Qualitative measure

Qualitative interviews with senior nursing staff and managers of RCFs, general practitioners (GPs) and ED staff will be conducted.

Economic measures

Financial savings are expected despite the cost of the ANP salaries. These savings will be due to a reduction in the number of ambulance transfers between RCFs and ED and from the reduction in the number of patients being reviewed in ED and admitted to the hospital.

Ethics

The QIP involves first order change. The aim is to improve the standard of current practices for the benefit of patients. It involves thinking differently to improve resource utilisation, so it does not require ethical approval.

Data protection and General Data Protection Regulation

The project team complies with the Health Service Executive (HSE) Data Protection Policy (2020), anonymising data in line with the principles of General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Irish legislation.

Funding

This QIP did not receive any external funding. The Aspire Fellowship post was funded by the Royal College of Physicians Ireland.

Design

A stakeholder map and communication plan were created to identify those who could influence the project (online supplemental appendix 2). To get stakeholders to engage, ‘the influencer model’ was used. A driver diagram was developed which visually mapped out a coordinated approach to achieve the aim. The primary drivers were education, documentation, equipment, process measures, policy and communication. During the project, the secondary drivers evolved due to outcomes from Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles and ongoing interaction with the focus groups (online supplemental appendix 3).

Two palliative care ANPs for RCFs, funded by Kerry Hospice Foundation (KHF) and supported by palliative medicine physicians, met with RCF staff regularly. Each interaction was used as a learning opportunity to improve knowledge and confidence of RCF staff in managing complex cases. The ANPs provided formal and informal education. Informal education involved case-based discussions, bedside teaching and reflective practice. The ANPs also created a palliative care resource folder for RCFs.

The palliative frailty MDT developed a communication document for patients being discharged to RCFs, outlining discussions that occurred in UHK between a senior physician and the patient or their designated healthcare representative, on resuscitation status and treatment escalation plan (TEP). This document will accompany patients on discharge. The geriatric and palliative medicine teams, medical staff of RCFs, OOHMS and RCF nursing and management staff were involved in the development of the document.

The palliative frailty MDT organised a GP education evening on ACP, the communication document, anticipatory prescribing at EOL and the role of the ANPs in geriatric and palliative medicine (online supplemental appendices 4 and 5).

Strategy

PDSA cycles

Multiple PDSA cycles were completed over the course of the project addressing different tasks (online supplemental appendix 6).

PDSA cycles on communication document

PDSA cycle 1

A communication document was created by the palliative frailty MDT. Its objective was to improve the communication between the acute hospital and RCF teams about ACP discussions that occurred on resuscitation status and TEP while the person was in UHK (online supplemental appendix 7).

It was introduced across all wards in UHK in July 2024 after education of administrative, nursing and medical staff. The number of patients discharged to an RCF with a communication document was collected in September and 50% were discharged without an accompanying document.

Causes included:

Patients discharged to RCFs at the weekend when administrative staff were not available.

Patients discharged to RCFs at the weekend when administrative staff were not available.

Nurses reported difficulty contacting the nurse in charge of the RCF.

Nurses reported difficulty contacting the nurse in charge of the RCF.

Frequent rostering of agency nurses.

Frequent rostering of agency nurses.

Non- Consultant Hospital Doctors (NCHDs) were hesitant to complete the medical rationale section, had difficulty contacting GPs and felt the document duplicated part of the discharge letter, increasing their workload.

Non- Consultant Hospital Doctors (NCHDs) were hesitant to complete the medical rationale section, had difficulty contacting GPs and felt the document duplicated part of the discharge letter, increasing their workload.

Action from this cycle was to create a version 2 of the document. It would be a single page, without the medical rationale section and incorporated into a triplicate book creating copies for the patient’s notes, RCF and GP.

PDSA cycle 2

Version 2 of the document was introduced in only five wards in January 2025, learning from PDSA 1 that small tests of change are easier to implement (online supplemental appendix 8). Over 8 weeks, 41 forms were completed, but the number of patients discharged unfortunately was not captured.

All completed documents were studied. Certain sections were repeatedly left blank. The action from the PDSA cycle 2 was to seek opinions from both hospital and community healthcare staff on version 2 of the document, in particular the areas left blank.

PDSA cycle 3

The document was reviewed at a GP Continuing Medical Education (CME) evening. Some GPs thought it was a legally binding document as opposed to a communication document. They also stated that being contacted by an NCHD each time a patient is discharged is burdensome to a busy practice. GPs requested the document be emailed so they receive it in a timely manner.

They found it useful to know the outcome of ACP discussions including terminology used, especially if they did not know the patient. The action from this PDSA was to create version 3.

PDSA cycle on GP education evening

A GP CME evening organised by the palliative frailty MDT provided education on the role of ANPs in RCFs, ACP and the role of the resuscitation status and TEP communication document, as well as anticipatory prescribing at EOL. Evaluation forms highlighted their satisfaction with the evening and further areas for improvement.

The actions from this PDSA were to:

1. Update the one-page anticipatory prescribing guideline.

2. Create version 3 of the document.

3. Organise further education on ACP.

Summary of PDSA cycles on electronic prescribing of anticipatory medications in an RCF

Anticipatory medications were inappropriately prescribed electronically in one RCF. The ANP used this as a learning opportunity. The correct method of prescribing was agreed with the GP. The expectation was that the medication would then be electronically prescribed correctly. The outcome was not achieved. The GP confirmed that they had completed the prescription as agreed. The next step in the PDSA involved the ANP collaborating with the palliative care pharmacist and dispensing pharmacist. A root cause analysis identified that a technician had inputted all the details of each medication into one electronic data cell, meaning that other sections were incomplete. The technician was educated on how to correctly input the prescribed dose, route and frequency of each drug electronically. At the next review, the anticipatory medications were correctly prescribed. This learning has been shared with other pharmacies.

Results

Outcome measures

The convenience sampling process used to attain baseline data was repeated to acquire data for the outcome measure. ED attendances from RCFs for the first week of March, April and May 2025 were reviewed by two senior clinicians and categorised as either appropriate or inappropriate based on Saliba et al’s definition. There were 35 ED attendances, with 32 reviewed as three charts could not be located. 10 were deemed inappropriate; six were due to low-acuity medical issues and/or lack of alternative pathway and four were due to lack of ACP. This demonstrates a 19% reduction in inappropriate ED attendances.

The SMART aim was achieved, but there is more work to be done on ACP and alternative pathways. Symptom control was not a reason for inappropriate ED attendances, which we attribute to the education across the microsystem by the geriatric and palliative medicine services.

Process measures: monthly ED attendances from RCFs

The run chart demonstrates the changes in ED attendances from residents of RCFs from January 2023 to April 2025 (figure 1). Prior to the commencement of the ANP service, the median monthly attendances were 82. This median was calculated using 12 data points. During the months of March to May, where there was a phased introduction of the ANP service, the number of attendances reduced.

The median was initially calculated with 8 data points (July 2023–February 2024) and was 10. The median was then recalculated at data point 15, where 6 consecutive points are above the baseline median, signifying a process shift. The new median was 19.

Balancing measures

It was hypothesised that the number of referrals to KSPCS would increase with the introduction of the ANP service. Concerns were expressed about the services capacity to meet the need. Since the introduction of the ANP service, there was one KPI breach in August and December 2024 coinciding with annual leave. Another breach occurred in April 2025, coinciding with the ANPs completing their examinations and a peak in referrals to the service (n=26). This needs to be monitored moving forward to ensure that there is continued capacity within KSPCS (online supplemental appendix 9).

Qualitative measures

Six qualitative interviews with RCF managers, directors of nursing, GPs and RCF medical officers were completed. Evaluation showed a positive response to the ANP service, identifying easy access, the personal touch, improved communication and continuity of care as being beneficial. Bedside education and the palliative care resource folder outlining best evidence-based practice were seen as ‘game changers’. The RCF staff felt empowered and reassured when caring for residents at EOL.

Reasons identified for ED referrals included a lack of access to anticipatory medications and equipment out of hours, difficulty accessing medical and ANP review and diagnostics, as well as staff culture and beliefs. RCF staff felt insufficient staffing increased their likelihood of referring someone dying out of hours to ED. They also believed the designated liaison person for RCFs during COVID was an excellent resource. Unfortunately, this role no longer exists.

Three ED staff members were interviewed about their perceptions on why RCF residents are referred inappropriately. They voiced issues relating to poor communication, lack of TEP documentation and variability of clinical leadership in RCFs (online supplemental appendix 10).

Lessons and limitations

What seemed to be a simple project proved not to be. Our project included an ‘in reach’ programme which sought to enhance the skill set of RCF staff in improving symptom control and providing EOL care. It was a QI initiative that united all those caring for older people living with frailty to work in an integrated way towards a common goal.

Essential to the success of the project was the ANP service, funded by KHF to improve the quality of living and dying of older people living with frailty in RCFs. The agreement is the HSE will take over its funding after 5 years. The ANPs report professionally and operationally to the director of nursing of KSPCS and are supported clinically by palliative medicine physicians. The benefits of the service reflect international research, showing that experienced nurses supporting RCF staff can reduce inappropriate ED attendances.

The palliative frailty MDT allowed standardisation of terminology on ACP and improved communication, promoting collaborative working across the geriatric and palliative care services. Reflective practice and case discussions at the MDT meetings allow for a greater sharing of knowledge and facilitate complex decision-making.

Reasons for inappropriate ED attendances were multiple, complicated by the fact that RCFs are either publicly or privately funded. They have different governance structures, staffing ratios and variability in accessing AHPs, equipment and anticipatory medications out of hours.

Medical input to RCFs is variable, provided by different GP practices and medical officers, but a single OOHMS. RCF care plans are based on either the social or medical care model. This makes care difficult to standardise. These factors may impact the threshold for ED attendance within and across RCFs.

There were a number of limitations to this project. First, obtaining accurate data on the number of residents attending ED was confounded by patients choosing to keep their home address as opposed to their RCF address. Discussion took place with senior administration staff in order to improve accuracy of registered address on ED attendance.

A palliative medicine trainee was undertaking a fellowship year in frailty and geriatric medicine, and a requirement of the curriculum was to complete a QIP during this time period. This required convenience sampling being used to obtain baseline data over a 3-month period with a small sample size. Using the same 3-month period 1 year later to obtain the outcome measure avoided seasonal variation influencing the results. The process measure monitoring ED attendances between January 2023 and April 2025 showed the median monthly ED attendances reduced by 32 following the introduction of the ANP service. This was sustained over the next 9 months.

Some RCF managers felt their current palliative care skill set was sufficient to clients’ needs and were slow to engage with the ANP service. Regular communication with each RCF identifying specific needs has supported integrated working.

It is difficult for OOHMS to make decisions regarding TEP when unfamiliar with patients. This is compounded by the absence of a national standard stating how long documentation of resuscitation status and advance care plans are valid in RCFs.

It is hoped that the new communication document on ACP and TEP will increase the number of discussions and documentation of ACP in RCFs, enabling OOHMS to make informed clinical decisions, reducing inappropriate ED attendances.

GP shortages and their busy workload impact on timely and effective review of RCF residents. The ANPs’ ability to provide a complete episode of patient care reduces this burden on GP services.

Conclusion

The project involved the geriatric and palliative care services working collaboratively with the aim of improving care for RCF residents and avoiding inappropriate ED attendances. This entailed working with and building on pre-existing relationships with GPs and RCF staff. It involved education across the microsystem on ACP, TEP, alternative pathways for low-acuity medical issues, symptom control and EOL care. Education was provided formally and informally by palliative medicine physicians and ANPs.

The project highlighted the inequity for RCF residents compared with their community counterparts in their access to AHPs and alternative pathways for low-acuity medical issues. There is a need for alternative models of care to support those with complex needs. ED attendances increase the risk of complications for older people living with frailty, leading to physical and mental decline while generating significant financial cost. Financial figures for our project were calculated by the finance department of UHK (online supplemental appendix 11). The figures were calculated by balancing the cost of the two ANP posts against the cost of an ED attendance multiplied by the number of attendances avoided over the year in addition to the number of ambulance journeys avoided. This generated a net saving of €68 062.

In 2023, 24% of ED attendances to UHK from RCFs required inpatient admission. The average length of stay was 8 days, with an average daily cost of €1190. This QIP found 384 attendances were avoided, resulting in 90 fewer inpatient admissions, equating to a total of 747 inpatient days which would have cost €889 000. Therefore, the total net savings was €957 142.

Sustainability of this project will be ensured through review of ED attendances of RCF residents being a standing item on the monthly palliative frailty MDT, with reflective practice as part of the process. The palliative medicine consultants will continue to meet with the ANPs weekly and will discuss patients from RCFs referred to the acute hospital palliative care team. The learning is also being sustained through KSPCS’s involvement with the CARU Project ECHO (Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes), which is a learning programme developed by the Irish Hospice Foundation (IHF) in partnership with All Ireland Institute of Hospice and Palliatve Care (AIIHPC) and the HSE which recently commenced in the health region. Its aim is to improve the delivery to residents of compassionate and person-centred palliative, end-of-life and bereavement care.

Educational programmes within the sector such as the CARU online learning programme for nursing homes, the HSE Palliative Care Needs Assessment eLearning Programme launched in 2024 and the Final Journeys training workshop for hospitals will help improve awareness of the palliative care needs of this cohort of patients. This will empower social and healthcare professionals across all disciplines to provide holistic care. These learning programmes, supported by the specialist palliative care services of a region and a designated palliative care ANP for RCFs, will support the spread of this project.

The project’s aim of reducing inappropriate ED attendances of RCF residents was achieved. It also highlights issues that need to be addressed locally, regionally and nationally to improve the quality of living and dying of RCF residents in Ireland.
