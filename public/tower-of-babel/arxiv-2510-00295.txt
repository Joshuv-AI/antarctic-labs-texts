Minimal Mahler Measure in Quartic Galois Number Fields Thanks: The second author was supported by an AMS-Simons PUI Grant
Bishnu Paudel, Kathleen Petersen and Haiyang Wang

Abstract
. We explore the dependence of the minimal integral Mahler measure of Galois quartic fields on the discriminant of the field. We obtain density results which are conditional on the ABC conjecture as well as several unconditional results.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
11G50, 11R06, 11C08

1.
Introduction

For a number field
K
we let
D_{K}
be the absolute discriminant of
K
and
\mathcal{O}_{K}
the ring of integers of
K
.
The Mahler measure of a non-constant polynomial
f(x)=c\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{C}[x]
is

M(f)=|c|\prod_{|\alpha_{i}|\geq 1}|\alpha_{i}|,

and for an algebraic number
\alpha
, we define
M(\alpha)
to be the Mahler measure of a minimal polynomial for
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Z}
(with content 1).
The minimal Mahler measure of a number field
K
is the minimal Mahler measure of a generator

M(K)=\min\{M(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=K\},

and
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is the minimal Mahler measure of an integral generator.
We study the dependence of
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
on the discriminant
D_{K}
for Galois quartic number fields
K
, focusing on the exponent of the discriminant in this dependence.
The main tools we use are Liouville’s theorem in Diophantine approximation and Granville’s work on square-free values of polynomials. Granville’s work depends on the ABC conjecture in many cases and we use these square-free values to construct explicit number fields. The known bounds for
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
specialize to the following for Galois quartics (see Section
1.1
). If
K
is a totally imaginary quartic and
\text{Tor}(K^{\times})\neq\{\pm 1\}
then

(
*
)

2^{-\frac{12}{5}}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{5}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq\big(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\big)^{2}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}

and if
\text{Tor}(K^{\times})=\{\pm 1\}
then

(
**
)

2^{-\frac{12}{5}}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{5}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq D_{K}.

If
K
is a totally real quartic field then

(
***
)

2^{-\frac{4}{3}}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{6}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq D_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now summarize our main results, which differ depending on the Galois group of
K
and whether
K
is totally imaginary or totally real.
For totally real biquadratic fields, assuming the ABC conjecture, we show that all possible rational exponents are realized for infinitely many fields.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\tfrac{1}{6}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
be a rational number. There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that assuming the ABC conjecture there are infinitely many totally real biquadratic fields
K
for which

c_{1}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{2}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

In Corollaries
3.3
,
3.4
, and
3.5
we produce explicit unconditional examples for exponents
\tfrac{p}{q}=\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{1}{4}
and
\tfrac{1}{2}
.

In Theorem
4.3
we improve the theoretical lower bound for totally imaginary biquadratics by increasing the exponent from
\tfrac{1}{5}
to
\tfrac{1}{4}
. Assuming the ABC conjecture, we show that all possible rational exponents are realized for infinitely many fields.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\tfrac{1}{4}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}\leq 1
be a rational number. There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that assuming the ABC conjecture there are infinitely totally imaginary biquadratic fields
K
for which

c_{1}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{2}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

In Corollaries
4.5
,
4.6
,
4.7
, and
4.8
we produce explicit unconditional examples for exponents
\tfrac{p}{q}=\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{2}{3}
and
1
.

For totally real cyclic quartic fields, in Theorem
5.2
, we show that the theoretical lower bound exponent of
\tfrac{1}{6}
is sharp.
The range of exponents in the theoretical bounds is
[\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{1}{2}]
. Assuming the ABC conjecture, we show that all rational exponents in the range
[\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{2})
are realized by infinitely many fields.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\tfrac{1}{4}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}
be a rational number. There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that assuming the ABC conjecture there are infinitely many totally real cyclic quartic fields
K
for which

c_{1}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{2}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

In Theorem
5.3
we produce an infinite family of examples realizing the exponent of
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Our work leaves open the question of whether there is a similar density property for real cyclic quartics and exponents in the range
(\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{1}{4})
. We also calculate the minimal Mahler measure of all totally real cyclic quartic fields of discriminant at most
2\cdot 10^{7}
, and present these results in Figure
1
and Figure
2
.

In Theorem
6.1
we improve the theoretical lower bound for totally imaginary cyclic quartic fields by increasing the exponent
\tfrac{1}{5}
to
\tfrac{1}{3}
. Assuming the ABC conjecture, we show that all possible rational exponents are realized for infinitely many fields.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\tfrac{1}{3}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}\leq 1
be a rational number. There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that assuming the ABC conjecture there are infinitely many totally imaginary cyclic quartic fields
K
for which

c_{1}\,D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\;\leq\;M\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{K}\bigr)\;\leq\;c_{2}\,D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

In Corollaries
6.4
,
6.5
, and
6.6
we produce explicit unconditional examples for exponents
\tfrac{p}{q}=\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{2}
and
1
.

1.1.
Background

In 1964, Mahler
[
14
]
related what we now call the Mahler measure to the discriminant, and this work implies that for
d=[K:\mathbb{Q}]\geq 2
,

d^{-\frac{d}{2d-2}}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

In 1984, Silverman
[
19
, Theorem 2]
extended this result to the non-integral setting, proving

d^{-\frac{d}{2d-1}}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}\leq M(K).

Ruppert
[
18
, page 18]
and Masser
[
17
, Proposition 1]
showed that the lower bound is sharp by providing a family of fields for which this lower bound cannot be improved by a larger exponent on the discriminant. (See also
[
21
]
.) Vaaler and Widmer
[
21
]
showed that for composite
d
there are number fields
K
for which no constant
c_{d}
satisfies

M(K)\leq c_{d}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2(d-1)}}

demonstrating that there are fields whose minimal Mahler measure grows faster than Silverman’s lower bound.
Child and Widmer
[
3
, Corollary 1]
improved the lower bound for totally imaginary number fields in the integral case to

2^{\frac{d(1-d)}{2d-3}}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2d-3}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

The best general upper bound for
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
is

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq|D_{K}|

which follows from Minkowski’s convex body theorem (see
[
15
, Lemma 7.1]
).
Ruppert
[
18
, Proposition 3]
proved
that if
K
is totally real of prime degree then
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(This argument can be extended to all number fields of prime degree using an extension of Minkowski’s linear forms theorem.)
Vaaler and Widmer
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
proved that if
K
is not totally imaginary, and
r_{2}
denotes the number of complex places of
K
, with
c_{K}=\big(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\big)^{r_{2}}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}
, then

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{K}.

(The theorem does not state explicitly that the primitive element is integral, and the integrality follows from the proof. See also the commentary in
[
1
]
.)
Vaaler and Widmer show
[
20
, Theorem 1.3]
that if
K
is totally imaginary there is a constant
B=B(d)
such that

M(K)\leq B|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}

under the assumption of the truth of the generalized Riemann hypothesis for the Galois closure of
K
. This result produces a non-integral small height generator. Akhtari, Vaaler and Widmer
[
1
, Corollary 1.1]
have shown that

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{K}

for
K
with
\text{Tor}(K^{\times})\neq\{\pm 1\}
. Conversely, they show
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
that if
c_{K}<M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
then
K
is totally imaginary and has a totally real subfield
F
such that
K/F
is Galois and
\mathrm{Tor}(K^{\times})=\{\pm 1\}
. Further, if
E\subset K
is totally real, then
E\subset F
. They provide the example (in Section 1) of
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\sqrt{3}-2})
with these features but where the inequality
c_{K}<M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
holds. For any even degree
d
(at least 4) they also construct infinitely many CM fields of degree
d
that satisfy
c_{K}<M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.
In Section
4.4
, for all Galois quartics
K
containing non-trivial roots of unity (roots of unity other than
\pm 1
), we determine explicit elements in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
with Mahler measure smaller than
c_{K}
. Theorem
1.2
and
1.4
demonstrate that there are infinitely many totally imaginary biquadratic fields and infinitely many totally imaginary cyclic quartic fields
K
, all of which are CM, such that
c_{K}<M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
as well as infinitely many of both types of fields for which
\text{Tor}(K^{\times})=\{\pm 1\}
such that
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{K}.

Many of the results above stated using a different height. Our work could equivalently be stated, for example, in terms of the absolute multiplicative Weil height of an algebraic number
\alpha
,
H(\alpha)
, using the fact that if the degree of
\alpha
is
d
then
H(\alpha)=M(\alpha)^{\frac{1}{d}}
. We will discuss number fields
K
in terms of explicit embeddings into
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
as the Mahler measure of two conjugate elements is equal.

1.2.
Results in small degree

For quadratic number fields, the upper and lower bounds have the same exponent on the discriminant. Cochrane et al
[
4
]
showed that for real quadratics

\tfrac{1}{2}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq M(K)\leq|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For cubic number fields the aforementioned bounds specialize to

3^{-\frac{3}{4}}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Eldredge and Petersen
[
5
]
, showed that the exponent on the lower bound is sharp and that there are infinitely many Kummerian cubic fields with exponent
\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Galois quartic number fields are either totally real or totally imaginary and have positive discriminants. The corresponding
c_{K}
values are
D_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\big(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\big)^{2}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, respectively. This gives us the bounds
(*)
,
(**)
, and
(***)
.
Totally imaginary Galois quartic fields are CM fields. The only roots of unity other than
\pm 1
that can be contained in a biquadratic are powers of
\sqrt{-1}
or
e^{\frac{2\pi i}{6}}
, which occur only when
\sqrt{-1}
or
\sqrt{-3}
are contained in
K
. We note that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1},\sqrt{-3})
is a biquadratic field and is the splitting field of the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{12}(x)=x^{4}-x^{2}+1
. The splitting field of
\Phi_{8}(x)=x^{4}+1
,
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1},\sqrt{2})
is the only other cyclotomic biquadratic field. For totally imaginary cyclic quartic fields, only the splitting fields of the cyclotomic polynomials
\Phi_{5}=x^{4}+x^{2}+x+1
and
\Phi_{10}=x^{4}-x^{3}+x^{2}-x+1
contain non-trivial roots of unity.

1.3.
Liouville’s Theorem and Square-Free Values of Polynomials

Many of our results use Liouville’s theorem in Diophantine approximation, which we state with an explicit approximation constant, see
[
2
, Chap. 6, Sec.1]
.

Theorem 1.5

(Liouville)
.

Let
\alpha
be a real algebraic number of degree
d\geq 2
. There is a constant
\mu=\mu(\alpha)>0
such that for all rational numbers
\tfrac{p}{q}
, we have

\left|\frac{p}{q}-\alpha\right|\geq\frac{\mu}{q^{d}}.

We can take

\mu(\alpha)=\frac{1}{a_{d}\prod_{i=2}^{d}(1+|\alpha|+|\alpha_{i}|)},

where
\alpha_{i}
are the conjugates of
\alpha_{1}:=\alpha
and
a_{d}>0
is the leading coefficient of a minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Z}
.

We will also rely upon Granville’s work
[
7
, Theorem 1]
which guarantees square-free values of polynomial functions.

Theorem 1.6

(Granville)
.

Assume that
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
has no repeated roots. Let
B
be the largest integer that divides
f(n)
for all
n
,
B^{\prime}
be the smallest divisor of
B
such that
B/B^{\prime}
is square-free,
q_{p}
be the largest power of
p
dividing
B^{\prime}
and
\omega_{f}(p)
be the number of integers a with
1\leq a\leq p^{2+q_{p}}
satisfying
f(a)/B^{\prime}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{2}}
. Define

c_{f}=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\Big(1-\frac{\omega_{f}(p)}{p^{2-q_{p}}}\big).

Assuming the truth of the ABC conjecture then
c_{f}>0
and

\#\{n\leq N:f(n)/B^{\prime}\text{ is square-free }\}\sim c_{f}N.

As Granville notes, this result can be proven unconditionally if
f
has degree
\leq 2
using the sieve of
Eratosthenes. The result was proven unconditionally by Hooley
[
9
]
for
f
of degree three.

2.
Discriminants and Integers of Quartic fields

Quartic fields can be distinguished by the number of their real and imaginary embeddings as well as their Galois groups. These Galois groups are either
S_{4}
,
A_{4}
,
D_{8}
(the dihedral group with 8 elements),
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
. In this manuscript, we focus on quartic Galois number fields, those with Galois groups
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
.

The theoretical lower bounds differ depending on whether
K
has a real embedding or not. The upper bounds are more subtle, notably by
[
1
, Theorem 1.3]
, in the quartic case, if
c_{K}=\big(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\big)^{2}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
then
K
is a CM field with no roots of unity other than
\pm 1
. A number field
K
is a CM-field if
K
has only complex embeddings and there exists a totally real subfield
F\subset K
such that
K/F
is a quadratic extension. Of note, all totally imaginary cyclic and biquadratic quartics are CM.

Let
\alpha\in K
. For later use, we define

(1)

M^{\prime}(\alpha)=|c(\alpha)|^{-1}M(\alpha),

where
c(\alpha)
denotes the leading coefficient of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Q}
with content 1.
Note that
M^{\prime}(\alpha)=M(\alpha)
when
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

We now collect information about the discriminants and integer rings of Galois quartic number fields.

2.1.
Biquadratic Fields

A biquadratic number field is a quartic Galois number field with Galois group isomorphic to the Klein 4-group,
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. These fields can be written as
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{nl})
, where
m
,
n
, and
l
are pairwise coprime square-free integers. We can reduce to considering the cases when
(ml,nl)\equiv(1,1),(1,2),(2,3)
, or
(3,3)\pmod{4}
since

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{nl})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{nl},\sqrt{ml})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{mn})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{nl},\sqrt{mn}).

Because of these different representations of
K
, we will often assume that
|l|\leq|m|\leq|n|
.
From
[
22
, Theorem 2]
the discriminant of
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{nl})
satisfies
D_{K}=c(lmn)^{2}
where
c
is given by the following congruence modulo 4

c=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1&\ \text{ if }\ (ml,nl)\equiv(1,1)&\pmod{4},\\
16&\ \text{ if }\ (ml,nl)\equiv(1,2)\text{ or }(3,3)&\pmod{4},\\
64&\ \text{ if }\ (ml,nl)\equiv(2,3)&\pmod{4}.\end{aligned}\right.

The ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of
K
is also computed in
[
22
, Theorem 1]
. Elements in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
have the form

f(x_{0}+x_{1}\sqrt{ml}+x_{2}\sqrt{nl}+x_{3}\sqrt{mn}),

where
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are rational integers and

\circ

if
(ml,nl)\equiv(m,n)\equiv(1,1)\pmod{4}
then
f=\tfrac{1}{4}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\equiv x_{3}\pmod{2}
and
x_{0}-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,

\circ

if
(ml,nl)\equiv(1,1),(m,n)\equiv(3,3)\pmod{4}
then
f=\tfrac{1}{4}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\equiv x_{3}\pmod{2}
and
x_{0}-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,

\circ

if
(ml,nl)\equiv(1,2)\pmod{4}
then
f=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{1},x_{2}\equiv x_{3}\pmod{2}
,

\circ

if
(ml,nl)\equiv(2,3)\pmod{4}
then
f=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{2}\equiv 0,x_{1}\equiv x_{3}\pmod{2}
,

\circ

if
(ml,nl)\equiv(3,3)\pmod{4}
then
f=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
x_{0}\equiv x_{3},x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\pmod{2}
.

2.2.
Cyclic Quartic Fields

A cyclic quartic field is a quartic Galois number field with Galois group isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.
For such a
K
, there exist unique integers
A,B,C,D
such that

K\;=\;\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt{A\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}\bigr),

where
A
is odd and square-free,
D=B^{2}+C^{2}
is square-free with
B,C>0
, and
\gcd(A,D)=1
(see
[
8
, Theorem 1]
).
Any field satisfying these properties is a cyclic quartic extension, and it is totally real if and only if
A>0
.

The discriminant of
K
(see
[
8
]
) is

(2)

D_{K}=c\,A^{2}\,D^{3},

where

c=\begin{cases}256&\text{if }D\equiv 0\pmod{2},\\
64&\text{if }D\equiv 1\pmod{2},\;B\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\
16&\text{if }D\equiv 1\pmod{2},\;B\equiv 0\pmod{2},\;A+B\equiv 3\pmod{4},\\
1&\text{if }D\equiv 1\pmod{2},\;B\equiv 0\pmod{2},\;A+B\equiv 1\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Set

(3)

\rho=\sqrt{A\,(D+B\sqrt{D})},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{A\,(D-B\sqrt{D})}.

Then an integral basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is given by one of the following, according to congruence conditions on
A
,
B
, and
D
(see
[
10
, Theorem on page 146]
):

\circ

If
D\equiv 0\pmod{2}
then is
\{\,1,\;\sqrt{D},\;\rho,\;\sigma\}
an integral basis.

\circ

If
D\equiv B\equiv 1\pmod{2}
then

\bigl\{\,1,\;\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{D}),\;\rho,\;\sigma\bigr\}
is an integral basis.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and
A+B\equiv 3\pmod{4}
then

\bigl\{\,1,\;\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{D}),\;\tfrac{1}{2}(\rho+\sigma),\;\tfrac{1}{2}(\rho-\sigma)\bigr\}

is an integral basis.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
,
A+B\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and
A\equiv C\pmod{4}
then

\bigl\{\,1,\;\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{D}),\;\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(1+\sqrt{D}+\rho+\sigma\bigr),\;\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(1-\sqrt{D}+\rho-\sigma\bigr)\bigr\}

is an integral basis.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
,
A+B\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and
A\equiv-C\pmod{4}
then

\bigl\{\,1,\;\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{D}),\;\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(1+\sqrt{D}+\rho-\sigma\bigr),\;\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(1-\sqrt{D}+\rho+\sigma\bigr)\bigr\}

is an integral basis.

The proposition below follows directly from this statement.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{Z}
and the field
K
be as above, and let
\rho,\sigma
be as defined in equation (
3
).
Then the integers of the field
K
are precisely the elements of the following forms where
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}
. :

\circ

If
D\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, the integers are

x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma.

\circ

If
D\equiv B\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, the integers are

\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma),

where
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}
,
x_{3}\equiv x_{4}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and
A+B\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the integers are

\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma),

where
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}
,
x_{3}\equiv x_{4}\pmod{2}
.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
,
A+B\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and
A\equiv C\pmod{4}
, the integers are

\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma),

where
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\equiv x_{3}\equiv x_{4}\pmod{2}
,
x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}+x_{4}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

\circ

If
D\equiv 1\pmod{2}
,
B\equiv 0\pmod{2}
,
A+B\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and
A\equiv-C\pmod{4}
, the integers are

\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma),

where
x_{1}\equiv x_{2}\equiv x_{3}\equiv x_{4}\pmod{2}
,
x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-x_{4}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

3.
Real Biquadratics

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{nl})
as described in Section
2.1
. We assume that
l<m<n
.
The discriminant satisfies
l^{2}m^{2}n^{2}\leq D_{K}\leq 64l^{2}m^{2}n^{2}
. The theoretical bounds are given in
(***)
and the exponents on the discriminant are
\tfrac{1}{6}
and
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
We first determine a lower bound for
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
that is a constant times
n
. We use this to show that many real biquadratics satisfy a lower bound with exponent on the discriminant of
\tfrac{1}{4}
, improving the known bound of
\tfrac{1}{6}
.
We then show that assuming the ABC conjecture, for any
\frac{p}{q}
satisfying
\tfrac{1}{6}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
, there are infinitely many real quadratics with
M(\mathcal{O}_{K})
a constant times
D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}
.

From Section
2.1
, an element
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
can be expressed as

\alpha_{1}=\tfrac{1}{4}(a+b\sqrt{ml}+c\sqrt{nl}+d\sqrt{mn})

for some

3.1.
Real Biquadratics Lower Bounds

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
0<l<m<n
are pairwise coprime square-free integers. For
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{ml},\sqrt{nl})
, we have

\tfrac{1}{48}n\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
generates
K
with the notation above. We will consider the cases
d=0
and
d\neq 0
separately. First, assume that
d\neq 0
. By choosing a suitable conjugate of
\alpha_{1}
, we may assume that
d>0
and
c\geq 0
. Additionally, we can assume that
\alpha_{1}\geq\frac{d}{4}\sqrt{mn}
. To see this, if
\alpha_{1}<\frac{d}{4}\sqrt{mn}
, then
a+b\sqrt{ml}+c\sqrt{nl}<0
and

-\alpha_{3}=\tfrac{1}{4}(-a-b\sqrt{ml}+c\sqrt{nl}+d\sqrt{mn})>\tfrac{1}{4}(2c\sqrt{nl}+d\sqrt{mn})\geq\tfrac{d}{4}\sqrt{mn},

and we can consider the primitive element
-\alpha_{3}
, which will satisfy the assumptions and have the same Mahler measure as
\alpha_{1}
. Similarly, if
d=0
then,
c\neq 0
, and, as above, we may assume that
c>0
and
\alpha_{1}\geq\frac{c}{4}\sqrt{nl}
.

Let
\lambda
be such that
n^{\lambda}=\operatorname{max}\{|\alpha_{2}|,|\alpha_{4}|\}
. Then,
-n^{\lambda}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq n^{\lambda}
for
i=2,4
, and their difference implies that

|c\sqrt{nl}-d\sqrt{mn}|\leq 4n^{\lambda}.

When
d=0
this implies that
c\sqrt{nl}\leq 4n^{\lambda}
. It follows that

M(\alpha_{1})\geq|\alpha_{1}|n^{\lambda}\geq\left(\frac{c}{4}\sqrt{nl}\right)^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{16}nl\geq\tfrac{1}{48}n

as required.

We will now assume
d\neq 0
. Dividing by

, we get

\left|\frac{c}{d}-\sqrt{\frac{m}{l}}\right|\leq\frac{4n^{\lambda}}{d\sqrt{nl}}=\frac{4}{d\sqrt{l}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\lambda}}.

We are now in a position to use Liouville’s Theorem (Theorem
1.5
).
The value
\mu=\mu\left(\sqrt{\frac{m}{l}}\right)
is

\mu=\frac{1}{l+2\sqrt{ml}}\geq\frac{1}{3\sqrt{ml}}.

If

, then the above inequality implies that

\left|\frac{c}{d}-\sqrt{\frac{m}{l}}\right|<\frac{\mu}{d^{2}},

which contradicts the inequality of Theorem
1.5
. So, we must have
d\geq\frac{\sqrt{l}}{4}\mu n^{\frac{1}{2}-\lambda}
. Since
\alpha_{1}\geq\tfrac{d}{4}\sqrt{mn}
, we have

|\alpha_{1}|\geq\frac{\sqrt{ml}}{16}\mu n^{1-\lambda}=\tfrac{1}{48}n^{1-\lambda},

and consequently

M(\alpha_{1})\geq|\alpha_{1}|\max\{|\alpha_{2}|,|\alpha_{4}|\}=|\alpha_{1}|n^{\lambda}\geq\tfrac{1}{48}n.

∎

\frac{1}{6}
of the discriminant on the lower bound to

.

Corollary 3.2

If

In this case,

5.
Real Cyclic Quartics

As in Section
2.2
we write a real cyclic quartic number field as
K=(\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})})
where
A,B,C,
and
D
are rational integers which satisfy
A>0
and
D=B^{2}+C^{2}
with
B>0
.
The theoretical bounds for real cyclic quartics are

4^{-\frac{2}{3}}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{6}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq D_{K}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

From Section
2.2
, an element
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
can be written as

\alpha_{1}=\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma),\quad x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},

where

\rho=\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{A(D-B\sqrt{D})}.

The other conjugates of
\alpha_{1}
are

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}-x_{2}\sqrt{D}-x_{4}\rho+x_{3}\sigma),

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}-x_{3}\rho-x_{4}\sigma),

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{1}-x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{4}\rho-x_{3}\sigma).

First, we establish a lower bound for the integral Mahler measure which depends on
A
and
D
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})})
be a real cyclic quartic field, where
A,B,C,
and
D
with
A>0
satisfy the conditions Section
2.2
. Then

\tfrac{1}{48}A\sqrt{D}\leq M\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{K}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
\rho
,
\sigma
be as in (
3
), and assume that
\alpha_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
generates
K
. Then there exist
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\alpha_{1}=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(x_{1}+x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma\right),

where at least one of
x_{3}
,
x_{4}
is nonzero. Let
\alpha_{2}
,
\alpha_{3}
,
\alpha_{4}
be the other conjugates of
\alpha_{1}
as defined earlier. By selecting an appropriate conjugate of
\alpha_{1}
or
-\alpha_{1}
, we may assume, without loss of generality, that
x_{3}>0
and
x_{4}\geq 0
. Moreover, we can impose the condition

(7)

\alpha_{1}\geq\tfrac{1}{4}x_{3}\rho,

for the following reason. Suppose instead that
\alpha_{1}<\frac{1}{4}x_{3}\rho
. Then

\tfrac{1}{4}(-x_{1}-x_{2}\sqrt{D}-x_{4}\sigma)>0,

from which it follows that

-\alpha_{3}=\tfrac{1}{4}(-x_{1}-x_{2}\sqrt{D}+x_{3}\rho+x_{4}\sigma)>\tfrac{1}{4}(x_{3}\rho+2x_{4}\sigma)\geq\tfrac{1}{4}x_{3}\rho.

We may then work with
-\alpha_{3}
, which satisfies our assumptions and has the same Mahler measure as
\alpha_{1}
.

Set
\lambda=\max\{|\alpha_{2}|,|\alpha_{4}|\}
. Then for
i=2,4
we have
-\lambda\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\lambda
, so taking the difference yields

|x_{4}\rho-x_{3}\sigma|\leq 4\lambda.

Dividing both sides by
x_{3}\rho
, we obtain

\left|\frac{x_{4}}{x_{3}}-\frac{\sqrt{D}-B}{C}\right|\leq\frac{4\lambda}{x_{3}\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})}}.

We use Liouville’s Theorem, Theorem
1.5
, with
\mu=\mu\left(\frac{\sqrt{D}-B}{C}\right)
. Because
\frac{\sqrt{D}-B}{C}
is a root of the equation
Cx^{2}+2Bx-C=0
, we derive

\mu\geq\frac{1}{C+2\sqrt{D}}.

Theorem
1.5
then gives

x_{3}\geq\frac{\mu}{4\lambda}\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})}.

When combined with (
7
), this establishes

|\alpha_{1}|\geq\frac{\mu}{16\lambda}A(D+B\sqrt{D})\geq\frac{A\sqrt{D}(B+\sqrt{D})}{16\lambda(C+2\sqrt{D})}.

Hence,

M(\alpha_{1})\geq|\alpha_{1}|\lambda\geq\frac{A\sqrt{D}(B+\sqrt{D})}{16(C+2\sqrt{D})}\geq\tfrac{1}{48}A\sqrt{D},

where the last inequality follows by taking
B=0
and
C=\sqrt{D}
. Since
\alpha_{1}
was arbitrary, the desired bound follows.
∎

We now show that the exponent of
\tfrac{1}{6}
on the lower bound is sharp.

Theorem 5.2
.

There are infinitely many integers
k>0
so that
k^{2}+1
is square-free. For large enough such
k
, the fields
K_{k}=\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{k^{2}+1+\sqrt{k^{2}+1}}\big)
satisfy

4^{-\frac{2}{3}}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{6}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq 60D_{K}^{\frac{1}{6}}.

Proof.

Let

D(x)=x^{2}+1.

There exist infinitely many positive integers
k
for which
D(k)
is square-free (see
[
6
]
). For any such
k
, we set
D:=D(k)
and consider the field

K:=\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{D+\sqrt{D}}\right).

From (
2
), the discriminant
D_{K}
satisfies

k^{6}\leq D_{K}\leq 257k^{6}.

The lower bound follows from
(***)
. To obtain an upper bound, consider

\alpha_{1}=\lfloor\rho\rfloor+\sqrt{D}+\rho+\sigma,

where
\rho=\sqrt{D+\sqrt{D}}
and
\sigma=\sqrt{D-\sqrt{D}}
. Let
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}
be the other conjugates of
\alpha_{1}
as given earlier. It is clear that

\alpha_{1}\leq 5k.

Direct computations give that

|\sqrt{D}-\sigma|\leq 1,\quad|\sqrt{D}-\rho|\leq 1,\quad|\rho-\sigma|\leq 1.

From these, we deduce

|\alpha_{2}|\leq|\lfloor\rho\rfloor-\rho|+|\sqrt{D}-\sigma|\leq 2,

|\alpha_{3}|\leq|\lfloor\rho\rfloor-\rho|+|\sqrt{D}-\sigma|\leq 2,

|\alpha_{4}|\leq|\lfloor\rho\rfloor-\rho|+|\rho-\sqrt{D}|+|\rho-\sigma|\leq 3.

Therefore

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq M(\alpha_{1})\leq 60k\leq 60\>D_{K}^{\frac{1}{6}}.

∎

We also show that the exponent of

Theorem 5.3
.

There are infinitely many square-free integers

,

The theoretical bounds for these real cyclic quartics have exponents in the range
[\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{1}{2}]
, and in Theorem
5.2
we have shown that the lower bound exponent of
\tfrac{1}{6}
is sharp.

We now prove Theorem
1.3
in two parts. First in Proposition
5.4
, assuming the ABC conjecture, we will obtain all rational exponents in the range
[\tfrac{3}{10},\tfrac{1}{2})
. In Proposition
5.6
, assuming the ABC conjecture, we will obtain all rational exponents in the range
(\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{10})
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\tfrac{3}{10}\leq\tfrac{p}{q}<\tfrac{1}{2}
be a rational number. There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that assuming the ABC conjecture there are infinitely many real cyclic quartic fields
K

c_{1}\>D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\leq c_{2}\>D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

Proof.

Represent
\frac{p}{q}
in the form
\frac{2s+t}{4s+6t}
, where
s,t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. For example, we can take
s=6p-q
and
t=2q-4p
. Define the polynomials:

B(x)=2,\quad C(x)=x^{t},\quad D(x)=B(x)^{2}+C(x)^{2}=4+x^{2t}.

If
t
is odd, then
D(x)
is irreducible by
[
13
, Ch. VI, Theorem 9.1]
. If
t
is even, then

x^{2t}+4=(x^{t}-2x^{\frac{t}{2}}+2)(x^{t}+2x^{\frac{t}{2}}+2),

with both factors Eisenstein and thus irreducible.

Let
T(k)
be the
k
-th Catalan number, i.e.,
T(k)=\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Construct the polynomial:

A(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{2s}a(i)x^{i},

where the coefficients
a(i)
are given by:

a(i)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=2s,\\
2&\text{if }i=2s-t\text{ or }i=0,\\
4&\text{if }i=2s-2t\text{ and }i\neq 0,\\
(-1)^{(j+1)/2}4T\left(\frac{j-3}{2}\right)&\text{if }i=2s-jt\text{ for some odd }j\geq 3,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

so that

A(x)=x^{2s}+2x^{2s-t}+4x^{2s-2t}+4x^{2s-3t}-4x^{2s-5t}+8x^{2s-7t}+\cdots+2.

See Remark
5.5
for another description of
A(x)
. The polynomial
A(x)
is Eisenstein and hence irreducible.
It follows from the factorization of
D(x)
that
A(x)
and
D(x)
have no common roots. Observe that

A(0)D(0)=8,\quad A(1)D(1)\text{ is odd},

so there is no prime that divides
A(k)D(k)
for all integers
k
. By Granville’s work, stated as Theorem
1.6
above,
assuming the ABC conjecture, there are infinitely many positive integers
k
such that
A(k)
and
D(k)
are square-free and coprime.
Moreover,
A(k)
must be odd, since otherwise
k
would be even, implying
4\mid D(k)
and contradicting the square-freeness of
D(k)
. Thus,
A(k),B(k),C(k)
, and
D(k)
satisfy the conditions in Section
2.2
.

Let
k
be such an integer and define

K:=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt{A\,(D+2\sqrt{D})}\bigr),

where
A=A(k)
and
D=D(k)
. By Section
2.2
, we have

D_{K}=c\,A^{2}D^{3},\quad\text{where }c\in\{1,16,64,256\}.

Hence,

A^{2}D^{3}\;\leq\;D_{K}\;\leq\;256A^{2}D^{3}.

The leading term of
A(x)
is
x^{2s}
. Thus, for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,

(1-\epsilon)\,k^{2s}\;\leq\;A\;\leq\;(1+\epsilon)\,k^{2s}.

Here and for the remainder of the proof, any inequality involving
k
is assumed to hold for sufficiently large
k
. Similarly, since the leading term of
D(x)
is
x^{2t}
, for any fixed
\delta>0
,

(1-\delta)\,k^{2t}\;\leq\;D\;\leq\;(1+\delta)\,k^{2t}.

By choosing
\epsilon
and
\delta
sufficiently small, it follows that

\tfrac{1}{2}\,k^{4s+6t}\;\leq\;D_{K}\;\leq\;257\,k^{4s+6t}.

Applying Proposition
5.1
, we obtain the lower bound

\tfrac{1}{12593}D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}\leq\tfrac{1}{49}k^{2s+t}\leq M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

To derive an upper bound, consider

\alpha_{1}=\lfloor\rho\rfloor+k^{s}\sqrt{D}+\rho+\sigma,

where

\rho=\sqrt{A(D+2\sqrt{D})}
and
\sigma=\sqrt{A(D-2\sqrt{D})}
. Let
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}
be the other conjugates of
\alpha_{1}
as given earlier.
Then

\alpha_{1}\leq 5k^{s+t}.

We now show that
|k^{s}\sqrt{D}-\sigma|\leq 9.

Let
A_{0}(x)=A(x)-x^{2s}
and
A_{0}=A_{0}(k)
.
Then

over the three families:

(
\dagger
)

(x_{1},x_{2},1,0),\quad(x_{1},x_{2},0,1),\quad(0,0,x_{3},x_{4}),

with
x_{3},x_{4}>0
.

We now improve the exponent
\tfrac{1}{5}
on the left-hand side of
(*)
and
(**)
to
\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
K
be an imaginary cyclic quartic field. Then

\tfrac{1}{128}D_{K}^{\frac{1}{3}}\;\leq\;M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

Proof.

Examining the three families in (

†

‣
6
), one sees that in each case

M^{\prime}(\alpha)\;\geq\;|\alpha_{i}|^{2}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{16}|A|\,D

for
i=1
or
2
. Hence

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\geq\;\tfrac{1}{16}|A|\,D\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{128}|D_{K}|^{\frac{1}{3}}.

∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
A,B,C,D
be integers satisfying the conditions in Section
2.2
with
A<0
, and set

K=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt{A\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}\bigr).

Then

\tfrac{1}{2304}A^{2}\,D\;\leq\;M\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{K}\bigr).

Proof.

It suffices to consider the three cases described in (

†

‣
6
). If
\alpha_{1}
is represented by the tuple
(x_{1},x_{2},1,0)
or
(x_{1},x_{2},0,1)
, then

M^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})=\frac{\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})\;\lvert A\rvert\,(D-B\sqrt{D})}{256}=\frac{A^{2}\,D\,C^{2}}{256}\geq\frac{A^{2}\,D}{256}.

Next, suppose
\alpha_{1}=(0,0,x_{3},x_{4})
with
x_{3},x_{4}>0
. Set

\lambda:=\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl\lvert x_{4}\sqrt{\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}-x_{3}\sqrt{\lvert A\rvert\,(D-B\sqrt{D})}\Bigr\rvert.

Then

\biggl\lvert\frac{x_{4}}{x_{3}}-\frac{\sqrt{D}-B}{C}\biggr\rvert=\frac{4\lambda}{x_{3}\sqrt{\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}}.

Let

\mu=\mu\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{D}-B}{C}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{C+2\sqrt{D}}

as in Theorem
1.5
. We have

x_{3}\geq\frac{\mu\sqrt{\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}}{4\lambda}\geq\frac{\sqrt{\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}}{4\lambda\,(C+2\sqrt{D})}.

Hence

\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert^{2}\geq\frac{x_{3}^{2}\,\lvert A\rvert\,(D+B\sqrt{D})}{16}\geq\frac{A^{2}\,(D+B\sqrt{D})^{2}}{256\,\lambda^{2}\,(C+2\sqrt{D})^{2}}.

Since
M^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})\geq\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert^{2}\lvert\alpha_{2}\rvert^{2}=\lvert\alpha_{1}\rvert^{2}\lambda^{2}
, we deduce

M^{\prime}(\alpha_{1})\geq\frac{A^{2}\,(D+B\sqrt{D})^{2}}{256\,(C+2\sqrt{D})^{2}}.

Therefore, we conclude

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\geq\frac{A^{2}\,D\,(B+\sqrt{D})^{2}}{256\,(C+2\sqrt{D})^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2304}A^{2}\,D,

where the last inequality follows from
B\geq 0
and
C\leq\sqrt{D}
.

∎

We now improve the exponent
\tfrac{1}{5}
on the left-hand side of
(*)
and
(**)
to
\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{A(D+B\sqrt{D})})
be an imaginary cyclic quartic field, where
A,B,C,
and
D
with
A<0
satisfy the conditions given in Section
2.2
. Then

\tfrac{1}{14630}|A|^{\frac{4}{3}}\,D_{K}^{\frac{1}{3}}\;\leq\;M(\mathcal{O}_{K}).

Proof.

By Proposition
6.2
,

M(\mathcal{O}_{K})\geq\tfrac{1}{2304}A^{2}\,D\geq\tfrac{1}{14630}|A|^{\frac{4}{3}}(256A^{2}D^{3})^{\frac{1}{3}}.

∎

We now prove Theorem
1.4
by showing that assuming the ABC conjecture, all rational exponents in
[\tfrac{1}{3},1]
are realized by infinitely many imaginary cyclic quartic fields.

proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Write

\frac{p}{q}\;=\;\frac{s+t}{s+3t},\qquad s,t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

for example by taking
s=3p-q
and
t=q-p
. Define the polynomials

A(x)=-(x^{s}+2),\qquad D(x)=x^{2t}+1.

Since
A(x)D(x)
has no repeated roots and
A(1)D(1)=-6
is square-free, Theorem
1.6
implies, under the ABC conjecture, that there are infinitely many positive integers
k
for which
A(k)
and
D(k)
are both square-free and coprime.
For such an
k
, set

A=A(k),\quad B=k^{t},\quad C=1,\quad D=D(k),

and let

\rho=\sqrt{\,A\bigl(D+B\sqrt{D}\bigr)\,}\quad\text{and}\quad K=\mathbb{Q}(\rho).

If
k
is odd, then
A,B,C,D
satisfy the conditions in Section
2.2
. If
k
is even, then, from
[
8
, Eq. (2.16)]

K=\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt{\frac{A}{2}\,(D+C\sqrt{D})}\bigr),

and
\frac{A}{2},C,B,D
satisfy the conditions in Section
2.2
. In either case, by (
2
), the discriminant of
K
satisfies

\tfrac{1}{4}k^{2s+6t}\;\leq\;D_{K}\;\leq\;257\,k^{2s+6t}.

Here and for the remainder of the proof, any inequality involving
k
is assumed to hold for sufficiently large
k
.
By Proposition
6.2
we obtain

M\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{K}\bigr)\;\geq\;\frac{A^{2}D}{9216}\;\geq\;\frac{k^{2(s+t)}}{9216}\;\geq\;\frac{D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}}{2368512},

while

M\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{K}\bigr)\;\leq\;M(\rho)\;=\;A^{2}D\;\leq\;2\,k^{2(s+t)}\;\leq\;8\,D_{K}^{\frac{p}{q}}.

This completes the proof.
∎
