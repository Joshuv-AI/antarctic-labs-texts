Radiative local density of states in three-dimensional photonic band-gap crystals to interpret time-resolved emission
Timon J. Vreman, Ad Lagendijk, Willem L. Vos

Abstract
We investigate the spontaneous emission of light in three-dimensional (3D) photonic crystals through theoretical calculations and simulations. It is well known that spontaneous emission depends on the radiative local density of states (RLDOS). Photonic band-gap crystals radically modulate the RLDOS, thereby controlling spontaneous emission. We compare two different methods to calculate the RLDOS: the plane-wave expansion (PWE) method and the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method. The PWE method directly calculates the RLDOS of an infinite photonic crystal, whereas the FDTD method simulates the RLDOS through the power emitted by a dipole in a finite photonic crystal. We demonstrate that the methods yield similar frequency-dependent trends in the RLDOS, with relative differences of less than 12% that originate from the different boundary conditions. We employ the plane-wave expansion method to compute distributions of emission rates that are relevant to many optical experiments where quantum emitters are distributed within a crystal. Such distributions of emission rates enable us to compute and directly interpret the time-resolved decay as observed in experiments. We expect that our results promote the RLDOS to the realm of optical design and products.

Introduction

Spontaneous emission is the fundamental process behind almost all light sources

;
Hewitt 2015

;

;

;

.
Photonic crystals are periodic dielectric materials, where the periodicity is on the scale of the wavelength of light

.
The rate of spontaneous emission, i.e., the radiative decay rate, of a rotating dipole located at a position
\rm{\mathbf{r}}_{0}
emitting at a frequency
\omega
is given by Fermi’s golden rule

that is reformulated in nanophotonics as

;

;

\Gamma_{\mathrm{rad}}\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)=\frac{\pi\omega}{3\hbar\varepsilon_{\rm 0}}\left|\mathcal{P}\right|^{2}N\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right),

(1)

where
\varepsilon_{\rm 0}
is the permittivity of free space,
\mathcal{P}
the transition dipole moment, and

the
radiative
local density of states (RLDOS)

Photonic crystals enhance or inhibit spontaneous emission because their states, Bloch modes, are different from the modes in free space, thus modifying

Multiple numerical methods are used to calculate the decay rate.

;

;

;

;
Hermann and Hess 2002
;
Ishizaki et al. 2009
;
Mavidis et al. 2020
, obtaining the decay rate through the emitted power
Xu et al. 2000
;
Taflove et al. 2013
.
Remarkably, to the best of our knowledge, these popular methods have not yet been compared to date, especially in the case of 3D photonic crystals.

Furthermore, current emission calculations in photonic crystals cannot be directly compared to many emission experiments.
Emission measurements often probe the time-resolved decay curve
f(t)
of a large ensemble of emitters distributed at many positions in a 3D photonic crystal

Yoshino et al. 1998

The RLDOS has been calculated before in photonic crystals with various structures with the goal to interpret spontaneous emission experiments in photonic crystals

;

;
Hermann and Hess 2002
;
Wang et al. 2003
;

While Wang

Here, we perform new calculations on the RLDOS with the goal of interpreting spontaneous emission experiments.
In the first part, we compute the RLDOS at a single position in (A) two different types of 3D photonic crystal structures, and (B) using the PWE and FDTD methods.
We demonstrate that the two methods yield RLDOSs in both photonic crystals of which the trends agree well, but also that the methods differ in details due to the presence or absence of finite-size effects.
In the second part, we calculate the RLDOS at many specific emitter positions using the PWE method, effectively in the same computational time as RLDOS calculations at a single position, similar to Wang
et al.

We use this ensemble of RLDOSs relative to a reference RLDOS to calculate the decay curve of the whole ensemble of emitters present at these many positions.

II.2
Finite-difference time-domain method

With the FDTD method, we simulate a dipole emitter at position
\mathbf{r}_{0}
with a dipole moment oriented in direction
\mathbf{e}_{d}
to calculate the emitted power from which the
partial
RLDOS,
N_{P}(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{d})
, is derived.
The partial RLDOS is equal to the RLDOS in the direction of the dipole moment
\mathbf{e}_{d}

Novotny and Hecht 2012
, and is given by
Taflove et al. 2013

N_{P}(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{d})=\frac{4}{\pi}P(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{d},\mathbf{e}_{d}),

(2)

where
P(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{d})
is the emitted power of the dipole.
To calculate the RLDOS, we use the open-source FDTD software package MEEP
Oskooi et al. 2010
.
The FDTD method time steps the electromagnetic fields in real space,
(t,\mathbf{r})
.
The fields are absorbed by a perfectly matched layer of
1.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
around the photonic crystal, mimicking free space.
The output power is recorded and the partial RLDOS is calculated by internal functions in MEEP.
The RLDOS is found by summing the partial RLDOS obtained from simulations along three orthogonal directions (
\mathbf{e}_{x}
,
\mathbf{e}_{y}
,
\mathbf{e}_{z}
)
Vos et al. 2009
, i.e.,
N(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega)=N_{P}(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{x})+N_{P}(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{y})+N_{P}(\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega,\mathbf{e}_{z})
.

Similar to experiments, the crystals under study are finite; thus, crystal surfaces are intrinsically present.
While unavoidable defects may be considered, this is not typically done as it places a large burden on computational efforts.

II.3
Plane-wave expansion method

The PWE method computes the modes and frequencies of a photonic crystal in the frequency domain by expanding the periodic dielectric function (the scattering potential) and the Bloch modes in a sum of reciprocal lattice vectors in wave vector space
(\omega,\mathbf{k})
.
Inherently, the PWE method considers an infinite photonic crystal.
Thus, neither external crystal surfaces are considered, nor any unavoidable deviations from perfect periodicity, such as point defects.

We use the open-source PWE package MIT Photonic Bands
Johnson and Joannopoulos 2001
to calculate the electric fields
\rm{\mathbf{E}}_{\it{i}}\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega_{\it i}\right)
and frequencies
\omega_{i}
of the Bloch modes in the photonic crystals.
Due to the periodicity, all modes are obtained by sampling the modes at
\mathbf{k}
-vectors in the top half of the first Brillouin zone
Wang et al. 2003
;
Nikolaev et al. 2009
.
The RLDOS is calculated by a weighted counting of the available modes
Snoeks et al. 1995
;
Busch and John 1998
;
Nikolaev 2006
;
Novotny and Hecht 2012
:

N\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}_{0}},\omega\right)=\sum_{i}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i}\right)\left|\rm{\mathbf{E}}_{\it{i}}\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega_{\it i}\right)\right|^{2},

(3)

where the weights are the electric fields normalized according to

\int_{V}{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})\rm{\mathbf{E}}_{\it{i}}\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}},\omega_{i}\right)\cdot\rm{\mathbf{E}}_{\it{j}}\left(\rm{\mathbf{r}},\omega_{j}\right)}d^{3}\rm{\mathbf{r}}=\delta_{\it{i}\rm{,}\it{j}},

(4)

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta function and
V
the volume of the unit cell.

II.4
RLDOS at many positions

So far, we have considered the emission of a single emitter at a single position
\mathbf{r}_{0}
.
However, multiple emitters are often probed simultaneously in experiments, and these emitters may be distributed inhomogeneously throughout the crystal at positions
\{\mathbf{r}\}
within the unit cell.
The PWE method allows us to calculate the RLDOS at these positions,
N(\{\mathbf{r}\},\omega)
, using Eq.
3
.
The calculation takes hardly any extra time because
\mathbf{E}_{i}(\mathbf{r},\omega_{i})
is already obtained when calculating the RLDOS at
\mathbf{r}_{0}
.
The RLDOS at each
\mathbf{r}
is converted to a decay rate through Eq.
1
.

II.5
Analytical reference RLDOS in a homogeneous medium

The RLDOS in a homogeneous medium does not depend on position and can be calculated analytically.
Therefore, homogeneous media serve as an excellent reference.
The radiative local density of states in a homogeneous medium with refractive index
n
is equal to
Loudon 2000
;
Novotny and Hecht 2012

N_{\rm{hom},\it{n}}(\omega)\,\rm{d}\omega=\frac{\it{n}}{\pi^{2}\it{c}_{\rm{0}^{\rm{3}}}}\omega^{2}\,\rm{d}\omega,

(5)

where
\omega
is the angular frequency and
c_{0}
the speed of light in vacuum.
We rewrite the RLDOS as a function of various frequency measures as

\displaystyle N_{\rm{hom},\it{n}}\left(\frac{a}{\lambda}\right)\,\rm{d}\omega=\left(\frac{4\it{n}}{\it{a}^{\rm{2}}c_{\rm{0}}}\right)\frac{\it{a}^{\rm{2}}}{\lambda^{2}}\,\rm{d}\omega

(6)

\displaystyle N_{\rm{hom},\it{n}}\left(\frac{a}{\lambda}\right)\,\rm{d}\!\left(\frac{\it{a}}{\lambda}\right)=\left(\frac{8\pi\it{n}}{\it{a}^{\rm{2}}}\right)\frac{\it{a}^{\rm{2}}}{\lambda^{2}}\,\rm{d}\!\left(\frac{\it{a}}{\lambda}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle N_{\rm{hom},\it{n}}(\tilde{\nu})\,\rm{d}\tilde{\nu}=8\pi\it{n}\tilde{\nu}^{\rm{2}}\,\rm{d}\tilde{\nu},

(8)

where
a
is the (fictitious) pitch,
\lambda
the wavelength of light in vacuum, and
\tilde{\nu}=\frac{\omega}{2\pi c_{0}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}
the wavenumber.
The local density of states is sometimes reported in arbitrary units, see e.g. Refs.
Sprik et al. 1996
;
Busch and John 1998
;
Wang et al. 2002
, which unfortunately does not provide much information to use or reproduce the data.
In other cases, e.g. Ref.
Nikolaev et al. 2009
, Eq.
6
is used, which is not practical because it mixes two different frequency scales.
Equation
7
gives the RLDOS in purely reduced frequencies, which is convenient for theoretical studies.
We prefer the experimental scale wavenumber
\tilde{\nu}

Wikipedia contributors 2024
, which yields the simple Eq.
8
for the RLDOS with units
\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
.
Both computational methods in this paper have been tested with various homogeneous media, and they always agree very well with Eq.
8
.

Because the RLDOS does not depend on position, there is just a single decay rate
\Gamma_{\rm{ref}}
according to Eq.
1
—assuming all emitters are the same.
The single-exponential reference decay curve is equal to

{}f_{\rm{ref}}(t)=A_{\rm{ref}}\Gamma_{\rm{ref}}e^{-\Gamma_{\rm{ref}}t},

(9)

where
A_{\rm{ref}}
is the amplitude, experimentally the number of counts at arrival time
t=0
.

II.6
RLDOS distributions to calculate the decay curve

Experiments probe the decay curve
f(t)
through time-correlated single-photon counting, where
f(t)
is a histogram of photon arrival times that are collected upon repeated excitation in a time-correlated single-photon counting experiment
Lakowicz 2006
.
The decay curve of emitters in the photonic crystal
f_{\rm{phc}}(t)\equiv f(t)
at a wavenumber
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
is compared to a reference decay curve
f_{\rm{ref}}(t)
at
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
to calculate the enhancement.
Our computational approach is similar:
We calculate the decay curve
f(t)
at
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
of many emitters inhomogeneously distributed at positions
\{\mathbf{r}\}
in a photonic crystal, relative to a decay curve in a homogeneous reference medium
f_{\rm{ref}}(t)
at
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
.

From calculations, we know the RLDOSs at the positions of the emitters
\{\mathbf{r}\}
in the photonic crystal
N_{\rm{phc}}(\{\mathbf{r}\},\tilde{\nu})\equiv N(\{\mathbf{r}\},\tilde{\nu})
(Sec.
II.4
) and the RLDOS in the reference medium
N_{\rm{ref}}(\tilde{\nu})
(Sec.
II.5
).
The relative RLDOSs are obtained by dividing
N(\{\mathbf{r}\},\tilde{\nu})
by
N_{\rm{ref}}(\tilde{\nu})
.
We construct a normalized histogram of the relative RLDOSs in a narrow wavenumber regime
\Delta\tilde{\nu}
centered around
\tilde{\nu}_{c}
:

\rho\left(\frac{N(\{\mathbf{r}\},\tilde{\nu}_{c}\pm(\Delta\tilde{\nu}/2))}{N_{\rm{ref}}(\tilde{\nu}_{c}\pm(\Delta\tilde{\nu}/2))}\right)\equiv\rho\left(\frac{N}{N_{\rm{ref}}}\right).

(10)

For simplicity assuming all emitters are equal and have a quantum efficiency of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
,
\rho\left(\frac{N}{N_{\rm{ref}}}\right)
is equal to the distribution of relative decay rates
\rho\left(\frac{\Gamma}{\Gamma_{\rm{ref}}}\right)
using Eq.
1
.
Using the fact that
\Gamma_{\rm{ref}}
is known from the decay curve
f_{\rm{ref}}(t)
(see Eq.
9
), we calculate the distribution of decay rates in the photonic crystal,
\rho(\Gamma)
.
Finally, we compute the distribution

\zeta(\Gamma)=A\Gamma\rho(\Gamma),

(11)

which relates to the decay curve
f(t)
via the Laplace transform
Van Driel et al. 2007

f(t)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\zeta(\Gamma)\exp{-\Gamma t}\,\rm{d}\Gamma.

(12)

When the number of excited emitters is the same in the photonic crystal as in the reference, the amplitude
A
equals
A_{\rm{ref}}
.

IV
Conclusion

We have calculated the radiative decay rate in 3D photonic crystals using two different methods: weighted counting of Bloch modes via plane-wave expansion (PWE) and finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations of the power emitted by a dipole.
The methods agree well.
The slight differences are attributed to the finite-size and surface effects that are present in FDTD, but absent in PWE.
Furthermore, we used the PWE method to calculate the decay curve of an ensemble of emitters distributed inhomogeneously in a 3D photonic band-gap crystal.

Our computations provide new insights into what to expect in experiments.
For example, we have provided an upper bound to the enhancement in decay rate: the enhancement is not much larger than one would obtain using a homogeneous medium of the high refractive index material present in the photonic crystal, except at frequencies just above a 3D band gap.

The results for stationary rotating dipoles can be extended to stationary non-rotating dipoles by also considering the dipole orientation in Eq.
3
.
Furthermore, the speckle in FDTD simulations can be reduced by using a sphere-shaped structure instead of the box-shaped one we used here.
Lastly, while our results focus on the decay curve, the enhancement and inhibition of the emitted power of an ensemble of emitters in a photonic crystal can also be obtained by considering non-radiative decay.
