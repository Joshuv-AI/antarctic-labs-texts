Platelet count has a U-shaped association with mortality in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Xinju Zhao, Angelo Karaboyas, Liangying Gan, Fan Fan Hou, Xinling Liang, Xiaonong Chen, Yuqing Chen, Zhaohui Ni, Roberto Pecoits-Filho, Li Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur previous manuscript showed that thrombocytopenia predicts all-cause mortality in Chinese hemodialysis (HD) patients. Based on the role of platelets in coagulation, clot formation, and systemic inflammation, we speculate that high platelets increase risk of thrombo-embolic events, hence the mortality. However, research evidence is currently lacking. Therefore, we utilized data from a very large international cohort study to explore the association of platelet counts with mortality and cardiovascular (CV) death in hemodialysis (HD) patients. International data from 396 facilities enrolled in the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) phase 5 (2012–2015) were analyzed. Participants were divided into 3 groups according to their platelet counts (low: <100, normal: 100–300, high: >300*109). Associations between platelet counts and all-cause and CV mortality were analyzed using Cox regression, adjusted for confounders. There were 13,631 patients with median age of 65 years old. Males accounted for 61.2%. Mean platelet count was 205*109/L overall and ranged from 173 *109/L in China to 227 *109/L in Sweden. Overall, 2,348 (17.2%) patients died and 1017 (7.5%) died from CV disease. Both low (HR:1.48, 95% CI 1.21–1.80, p < 0.001) and high (HR:1.17, 95% CI 1.00- 1.35, p = 0.044) platelet counts were associated with higher all-cause mortality after adjustment for covariates; results for CV death were consistent. Platelet count has a U-shaped association with all-cause and CV mortality in HD patients, and thus may be used as an outcome predictor that is readily available among HD patients.Keywords: Platelet, Mortality, Hemodialysis, Chronic kidney disease, End-stage kidney diseaseSubject terms: Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

The risk of mortality is several folds higher in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) than in the general population1,2. The number of ESKD patients in China is growing rapidly. It is estimated that the number of dialysis patients was approximately 578,000 in 20163. According to data from the Chinese National Renal Data System (CNRDS), as of December 2021, there were 749,573 patients receiving hemodialysis (HD). Uncovering risk factors, especially modifiable risk factors, is essential to help improve survival for this patient population.

Platelet reactivity plays a central role in the genesis of thrombosis and thromboembolic events, especially in cardiovascular disease4,5—the leading cause of death in HD patients. The immune potential of platelets has been immensely studied in recent years6,7. Platelets have been shown to directly recognize, sequester and kill pathogens, to activate and recruit leukocytes to the sites of infection and inflammation, and to modulate leukocyte6,7. Prior studies also demonstrated that platelets can regulate host immunities and complement responses for initial intrinsic defense against infections and explored different mechanisms of how virus infection can interfere with platelet production and might trigger platelet destruction8–10.

Low platelet counts, also known as thrombocytopenia, can occur as a result of leukemia, immune system disorders, or liver disease. Thrombocytopenia can be fatal, particularly if the bleeding is severe or occurs in the brain. Our previous study had shown that the presence of thrombocytopenia was associated with a poor prognosis for HD patients11. High platelet counts, also known as thrombocytosis, can occur as a result of infection, bone marrow disorder, autoimmune disorders, and malignancies12. Thrombocytosis is associated with increased risk of acute coronary syndromes and acute ischemic stroke. Studies showed the association between high platelet counts and adverse clinical outcomes in several diseased population13,14. However, the association of thrombocytosis and adverse outcomes in HD population is unknown.

Existing studies vary in the populations studied as well as in their design and methods of analysis, and their results are inconsistent. In addition, some of the studies are limited by the small sample size. Limitations of previous studies also include coarse categorization of platelet count, the failure to take into account common comorbidities, and practice patten variables that may confound the association.

To address these issues, we examined the association of platelet count with total and cause-specific mortality in the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS), a global cohort of HD patients with a standardized collection of information on patient characteristics (including detailed comorbidity), lab values, practice patterns and clinical outcomes for HD population.

Methods

Study design

The DOPPS (www.dopps.org) is an international prospective cohort study of patients 18 years or older treated with in-center hemodialysis15,16. Details on study design and methods have been published before15–17. Briefly, the study population was composed of randomly selected patients from a random sample of dialysis facilities within each country. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) receiving chronic in-center HD; (2) patients’ age ≥ 18 years-old; (3) expectation that patient will continue to receive care at the dialysis clinic. The exclusion criteria were: (1) treated with a home-based dialysis modality; (2) Receiving HD for acute kidney injury; (3) expectation that patient will receive dialysis transiently (< 6 weeks) at the study site (e.g., anticipated transfer to another dialysis facility). (4) Receiving concomitant peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis (hybrid therapy utilized in Japan only). Data for demographics, comorbid conditions, laboratory values, and dialysis prescriptions were abstracted from medical records using uniform and standardized data collection tools (DOPPS-link). This analysis was limited to data from patients participated in DOPPS phase 5 (2012–2015). Participants were recruited through 396 unique facilities from the United States, Canada, Japan, Australia and New Zealand, Russia, Turkey, China, Europe (Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom), and the Gulf Cooperation Council (Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and United Arab Emirates).

Patients were enrolled at baseline with inclusion criteria of treatment with regular HD for > 3 months. Patients with the absence of platelet information at baseline were excluded.

The DOPPS program maintains institutional review board or ethical committee approvals in all participating countries. Additional study approvals and patient consent were obtained as required by national and local ethics committee regulations. DOPPS China program was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (ethical approval number: 2018PHB028-01). The authors confirm that all the methods used in this study comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration.

Patient grouping

Participants were divided into 3 groups according to their baseline platelet counts as follows: low (< 100*109), normal (100–300*109), and high (> 300*109).

Data collection and outcomes

Information including demographics (age, sex, dialysis vintage, and body mass index [BMI]), primary kidney diseases, pre-dialysis laboratory values (hemoglobin, albumin, white blood cells, and creatine), dialysis prescription, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery diseases, congestive heart failure, other cardiovascular diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, hepatitis, peripheral vascular diseases, lung diseases, cancer [non-skin], gastrointestinal bleeding, and liver cirrhosis) were collected. The primary outcome of this study was all-cause death, and the secondary outcome was cardiovascular death. The date, place, and the primary and secondary causes of death were recorded. Causes of death were categorized as cardiovascular, infectious, metabolic, hepatic, gastrointestinal, other, or unknown. Death resulting from any reason was defined as all-cause death. Death with the following primary causes were defined as cardiovascular death: atherosclerotic heart disease, cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy, cerebrovascular accident (including intracranial hemorrhage), congestive heart failure, hemorrhage from ruptured vascular aneurysm, ischemic brain damage or anoxic encephalopathy, acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, stroke, and heart valve diseases. Survival/cardiovascular survival was defined as time from study entry to all-cause death/cardiovascular death or censored at the end of study. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

After conducting the Shapiro-Wilk test, continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD). For continuous variables with skewed distributions, median (interquartile range [IQR]) was used. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For variables with missing data of > 5%, multiple imputation was used (variables imputed in this study including standardized Kt/V [stdKtV], albumin, BMI, and intradialytic weight loss). Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were utilized to determine the factors associated with low or high platelet counts. The variables analyzed included demographics, ESKD causes, pre-dialysis laboratory values, dialysis prescription, and comorbidities. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using forward regression, and included variables with P < 0.10 in the univariate analysis. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the log-rank test was used to compare the three groups. One unadjusted (model 1) and four adjusted Cox regression models were used to analyze factors associated with all-cause death and cardiovascular death. All Cox models accounted for facility clustering effects using the robust sandwich covariance estimate. The adjusted covariates included in model 2 were age, sex, BMI, and dialysis vintage; model 3: model 2 plus comorbidities (diabetes, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, other cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, hepatitis, cancer (non-skin), peripheral vascular disease, lung disease, hypertension, psychiatric disorder, gastrointestinal bleeding, recurrent cellulitis, fracture, and neurologic disease); model 4: model 3 plus hemoglobin, albumin, white blood cells, and serum creatinine; model 5: model 4 plus intradialytic weight loss, fistula use, ESKD causes, standardized kt/v, and urine output < 200 ml/day. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of low and high platelet counts in the five Cox models were calculated and plotted. We calculated the efficacy of sample size for this cohort study in obtaining reliable results. The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and SAS 9.4 (SAS institute, Cary, NC; USA), and the figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc., San Diego, CA) and R 4.1.2 (RStudio Inc., Boston, MA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 13,631 HD patients from 396 facilities from 21 countries in the global DOPPS phase 5 study were included. Australia and New Zealand and the Gulf Cooperation Council (Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and United Arab Emirates) were considered together as 2 special areas. Further details of the study sample are shown in Fig. 1. In the study cohort, male patients accounted for 61.2% of the patients. The median age was 65 (IQR 53, 75) years old, and median dialysis vintage was 1.9 (IQR 0.4, 5.3) years. The median follow-up period was 1.5 (IQR 0.7, 2.4) years. There were 652 (5%), 11,625 (85%), and 1354 (10%) patients in the low (< 100*109), normal (100–300*109), and high (> 300*109) platelet groups respectively. Mortality

Among the 13,631 enrolled patients, 2348 (17.2%) died; 1017 (7.5%) of these died from CVD. CV death constituted 43.3% of the patients’ mortality. The total mortality risk was higher in both the groups with low and high platelet counts compared with patients without normal platelet counts (24.8%, and 20.9% vs. 16.4%). The crude mortality was 10.9/100 patient-years for all, and 16.2, 10.2, and 14.8/100 patient-years in low, normal and high platelet groups respectively. Associations between platelet and outcomes

According to the results of the Kaplan-Meier analysis, patients with low or high platelet counts had higher risk of all-cause mortality and CV related deaths (log-rank test; P < 0.01; Fig. 2A and B). In the fully-adjusted Cox model, low and high platelet counts were associated with higher all-cause mortality after adjusting for covariates (HR:1.48, 95% CI 1.21–1.80, p < 0.001; and HR:1.17, 95% CI 1.00- 1.35, p = 0.044 respectively, Fig. 3). The association with CV death was of similar magnitude (HR: 1.47, 95% CI 1.05–2.05, p = 0.025; and HR:1.36, 95% CI 1.07–1.72, p = 0.011 respectively, Fig. 3).

Discussion

Both low- and high-platelet counts were associated with increased risk of total mortality, CV mortality in this multinational cohort of HD patients, even after controlling for comorbidities, demographic factors, anemia and other lab indices in HD population. We also identified some influencing conditions for abnormal platelet counts and some of them might be modifiable, which may offer opportunities to apply our findings in future studies aiming to identify practices that may be associated with best outcomes.

Our results are consistent with those of general population, women, the elderly, and patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, in which a U-shaped association between platelet count and increased mortality has also been recognized18–21. Platelets are not only responsible for initiation of the hemostatic mechanisms that repair injury to the vascular endothelium, but also have been proved to have the ability to recruit leukocytes to sites of inflammation, hence to be involved in immunity and host defense6–10. Evidences also suggest that activated platelets secrete and express many pro- and anti-inflammatory molecules, and hence initiate a process called thromboinflammation6,22. In addition, platelets also contribute to other biologic processes, such as liver regeneration, cancer metastasis, and inflammatory arthritis. These functions are partly mediated by platelet granules, which contain cytokines and growth factors22,23. Moreover, platelet counts are usually stable over time in one individual although may vary between individuals. These characteristics enable platelets to serve as a useful prognostic marker.

Previous studies have shown the decrease of platelet counts can predict morbidity or mortality of disease24,25. Platelet procoagulant mechanisms are very important to achieving hemostasis26. Therefore, bleeding is a concern in patients with severe thrombocytopenia. However, the correlation between platelet counts and bleeding risk is relatively weak. Studies showed that the platelet count does not correlate with bleeding risk at higher platelet counts27,28. The balance between the physiological pro- and anticoagulant pathway plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of bleeding and coagulation. In fact, platelet dysfunction and coagulation abnormalities play an important role in bleeding risk.

It is not surprising that patients with liver cirrhosis and hepatitis tend to have more chances of thrombocytopenia. Thrombopoietin (TPO) is predominantly produced by the liver and expressed by hepatocytes29,30. Severe liver disease with impaired liver synthetic function can cause thrombocytopenia due to reduced TPO levels. Liver disease can accompany compensatory splenomegaly and hypersplenism, which also can cause thrombocytopenia29,30. Males have a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease, and thus more likely receive antiplatelet drugs. Patients taking antiplatelet drugs may suffer more from thrombocytopenia31. The reason may be the same for patients with other CVD. Why patients whose ESKD causes as glomerulonephritis has higher risk for thrombocytopenia is not totally understood. Higher hemoglobulin and white blood cells may imply a good condition of bone marrow status; it is reasonable for these indices change in the same direction. Higher BMI correlates with better nutritional status, which might be a protective factor from thrombocytopenia. One study suggests that the severity of thrombocytopenia induced by parasite has been affected by the nutritional status32. The association of residual renal function with thrombocytopenia is not well established yet, and needs further investigation.

The present study adds new knowledge that thrombocytosis is associated with adverse clinical outcomes in HD patients. The number of patients with thrombocytosis is considerably higher than that of patients with thrombocytopenia in HD population. However, it might be neglected in clinical settings. Patients with higher platelet count may have more chances of thrombosis events, hence worse prognosis. Higher platelet counts can act as an indicator for inflammation as well. Risk factor analysis in our study showed that patients with recurrent cellulitis were at higher risk for thrombocytosis. This was in line with previous findings33. Nutritional indicators, such as creatine level and albumin were reversely correlated with thrombocytosis. Why gastrointestinal bleeding was positively associated with thrombocytosis is tricky. Interestingly, it showed that increasing platelet count, assessed several years before and at venous thromboembolism diagnosis, was also associated with a higher risk of major bleeding events34. This phenomenon warrants further studies.

Notably, patients with uremia often exhibit intrinsic platelet abnormalities and disrupted interactions with endothelial cells. Research has identified that platelet dysfunction in these patients stems from diminished aggregation and compromised adhesion capabilities. This impaired adhesion may be due, at least in part, to the malfunctioning of glycoproteins IIb/IIIa, which are crucial for platelet function35,36. Studies also suggests that alterations in the release of adenosine diphosphate and 5-hydroxytryptamine from platelet alpha granules, may contribute to the platelet dysfunction observed in uremic patients37,38. These findings underscore the complexity of platelet dysfunction in the context of uremia and highlight the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

This study has many strengths. The large sample size allowed us to finely categorize platelet count to identify low and high concentrations associated with increased risk. Other strengths include the availability of information on health status, comorbid conditions, and a wide range of risk factors and clinical practice indices. Our study also has several limitations. Firstly, this observational study might have inherent shortcomings such as selection bias and confounding factors. Secondly, additional measurements of platelet activity and function were not available. Thirdly, the absence of information on anticoagulants may indeed introduce bias into the analysis. To address this, future study that will encompass the necessary parameters is needed. This approach will ensure a comprehensive understanding of the impact of anticoagulants on platelet function and help minimize potential bias. Nevertheless, we believe these factors will not undermine our main findings.

In this large cohort study, both low- and high-platelet counts were associated with increased risk of total mortality and CV mortality. The associations were not affected by adjustment for a number of potential confounding factors, including hemoglobin levels and white blood cell count. The fact that we found similar U-shaped associations with both total mortality and cause-specific mortality suggest platelet count has little to do with the CV pathway, and may instead serve as an indicator of increased risk of death.

Conclusion

In this large prospective cohort study capturing standardized and detailed information on HD patients across the world, both low- and high-platelet counts were associated with increased risk of total mortality and CV mortality. The associations were not affected by adjustment for a number of potential confounding factors. Platelet may be used as an outcome predictor that is readily available among HD patients. Since some factors associated with abnormal platelet counts are potentially modifiable, our findings can be explored in future studies aiming to identify practices that may be associated with best outcomes.
