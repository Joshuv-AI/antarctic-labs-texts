Data as Commodity: a Game-Theoretic Principle for Information Pricing Preprint: APS/123-QED
Pasquale Casaburi, Giovanni Piccioli, Pierpaolo Vivo

Abstract
Data is the central commodity of the digital economy. Unlike physical goods, it is non-rival, replicable at near-zero cost, and traded under heterogeneous licensing rules. These properties defy standard supply–demand theory and call for new pricing principles. We propose a game-theoretic approach in which the value of a data string emerges from strategic competition among N N players betting on an underlying stochastic process, each holding partial information about past outcomes. A better-informed player faces a choice: exploit their informational advantage, or sell part of their dataset to less-informed competitors. By analytically computing the Nash equilibrium of the game, we determine the price range where the trade is beneficial to both buyer and seller. We uncover a rich landscape of market effects that diverge from textbook economics: first, prospective sellers and buyers can compete or jointly exploit the less informed competitors depending on the quality of data they hold. In a symbiotic regime, the seller can even share data for free while still improving her payoffs, showing that losing exclusivity does not necessarily reduce profit. Moreover, rivalry between well-informed players can paradoxically benefit uninformed ones, demonstrating that information abundance does not always translate to higher payoffs. We also show that the number of players influences the competition between informed parties: trades impossible in small markets become feasible in larger ones. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for the pricing of intangible goods in dynamically interacting digital markets, which are in need of robust valuation principles.



I
Introduction

While the pricing of physical goods is well understood through the supply-demand equilibrium
Back 2010
;
Skiadas 2009
,
information
in the form of data – modern societies’ most valuable digital commodity – defies traditional economic frameworks
Laney 2017
. Data presents peculiar properties that fundamentally challenge the conventional market theory
Linde 2009
;
Gu and Lev 2011
: it is non-rival (the same dataset can be sold to multiple buyers without degradation), exhibits near-zero marginal reproduction costs, and can be licensed under different terms restricting lawful use. This creates a pricing dynamics that traditional supply-demand models – built on the assumption that
scarcity
is the main driver of price – cannot capture. This economic paradox has created a trillion-dollar market
Henke et al. 2016
;
Andrés Azcoitia et al. 2022
built on very shaky foundations. From textual data used to train large language models
Common Crawl 2024
to weather data for predicting renewable energy output
Benth and Saltyte-Benth 2012
, information concretely creates economic value, yet first-principles pricing methods remain elusive. The result is systematic market inefficiency: tech giants acquire datasets for billions of dollars without rigorous valuation frameworks, while individuals surrender valuable personal data in exchange for free services.
With business interest expanding rapidly, data valuation has become both a commercial offering and a policy priority for many governments, yet in the absence of established standards, these rely mostly on heuristics or proprietary models narrowly tailored to specific use cases.

Here, we instead develop a game-theoretic pricing principle, where information value emerges from strategic competition rather than ad hoc methods. We introduce a flexible and general model where
N\geq 3
market players bet on the outcomes of an underlying stochastic process, whose generating mechanism is unknown to them and about which they possess only partial information: this may represent, for instance, a group of traders that have to figure out whether the value of an asset will increase or decrease tomorrow. A more informed player may either exploit their informational advantage exclusively (to make a better model of the underlying process and bet in a more favorable way) or sell portions of their data to competitors in exchange for monetary compensation. This trade-off induces an intriguing pricing principle: comparing expected payoffs before and after the data transfer identifies natural price bounds within which transactions become mutually beneficial for buyer and seller. Our framework also reveals a rich landscape of the game as a function of the quality of information held by different players, with many counterintuitive and unconventional market dynamics effects. For instance, while economic intuition suggests that sharing exclusive information should generally harm the seller by
strengthening competition, we discover unexpected “symbiotic” regimes where data sharing benefit both parties even in the absence of monetary compensation. Moreover, we uncover a paradoxical “blessing of ignorance” phenomenon: fierce competition between highly informed players may generate positive payoffs for less-informed participants despite their strategic disadvantage, while the most informed player suffers negative returns despite their superior knowledge.
This demonstrates that information abundance does not guarantee competitive advantage, as strategic market structure can favor less-informed parties through complex interaction effects.
We also find that as the number of players
N
increases, the competition effects between buyer and seller weaken: identical information trades that prove mutually beneficial in large markets become economically unviable in small ones.

II
Related Literature

Several approaches have been explored to assign information a price; for an overview of this field see
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Pei 2020
;
Majumdar et al. 2025
. These can be roughly grouped into different categories:

•

Cost-based pricing
. The price is determined by the cost of acquiring the data. In
Arrow et al. 1949
;
Wald 1992
and later
Bloedel and Zhong 2020
, a single agent can perform experiments in the form of statistical tests in order to get valuable information about the state of the world. A trade-off ensues between the cost of the experiment and the value of the information each experiment yields.

•

Privacy based pricing
. Following this approach, one aims to price the loss of privacy that the seller experiences when selling the data
Jaisingh et al. 2008
;
Ghosh and Roth 2011
;
Majumdar et al. 2025
;
Shen et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2014
.

•

Data value based pricing
: under this method, the price is determined by the utility of the dataset for the buyer. For example, in
Yu and Zhang 2017
prices account for the data’s quality and in
Wang and Strong 1996
several additional attributes are considered, such as data completeness and seller trustworthiness.

•

Seller profit maximization

Admati and Pfleiderer 1986
;
Mehta et al. 2021
. In this case the seller attempts to maximize the revenue from selling the data (possibly to several buyers).

•

Game theory based pricing
, where the price of data emerges from the strategic competition within a market
Fernandez et al. 2020
.

In isolation, each approach inevitably misses some nuances of the problem.
Therefore, our framework is designed to blend together elements from all approaches except for cost-based pricing. In short, we price according to both the buyer’s utility and the seller’s losses, in a game theoretic setting.
Several market architectures for the exchange of data have been considered. Our basic setting borrows the idea of a monopolistic seller from
Admati and Pfleiderer 1986
. In that case, a single data owner could sell potentially corrupted information to several players. However, in stark contrast with our setting, the data owner was unable to exploit the data herself.
Our setting is inspired by the statistical mechanics framework introduced in
Gamberi et al. 2024
, but departs from it in the
use of a Bayesian game-theoretic approach to compute utilities.
Transactions under information asymmetry between the buyer and the seller have been studied in
Gradwohl and Tennenholtz 2023
;
Bergemann et al. 2018
.
In our study, the information asymmetry is modeled as an information hierarchy, where players can be ordered by the amount of information they possess about the game and about the actions of other players. An analogous concept, the cognitive hierarchy, was studied in
Stahl and Wilson 1995
;
Camerer et al. 2004
;
Keynes 1936
.
Finally, our study makes heavy use of the Bayesian games framework,
Harsanyi 1967
;
Harsanyi 1968a
;
Harsanyi 1968b
;
Zamir 2013
, which is particularly suited to model games with incomplete information.

III
The Pricing Game

We consider
N\geq 3
players who engage in a game of chance based on the outcomes of an underlying stochastic process. Although this process may be arbitrarily complex and correlated, its structure is unknown to the players, who only observe a binary signal
w\in\{0,1\}
derived from it – for instance, whether the value of an asset has gone up or down.
Players pay a unitary entry fee to cast their bets. Once all bets are placed, the outcome

u_{i}(\vec{a}\mid w)=\begin{cases}0\ ,&\text{if }\sum_{j}\delta_{w,a_{j}}=0\ ,\\[10.00002pt]
N\dfrac{\delta_{w,a_{i}}}{\sum_{j}\delta_{w,a_{j}}}-1\ ,&\text{otherwise}\ ,\end{cases}

(1)

where

is the Kronecker delta, equal to 1 if player

guesses correctly (i.e.,

), and 0 otherwise. With this payoff form, the game is zero sum, meaning that the sum of all players’ payoffs vanishes.

We assume that each player has access to a string

\displaystyle P_{i}(\rho|\vec{s}_{i})=\frac{P(\vec{s}_{i}|\rho)\pi_{i}(\rho)}{\int P(\vec{s}_{i}|\rho^{\prime})\pi_{i}(\rho^{\prime})d\rho^{\prime}}=\frac{\rho^{H_{i}}(1-\rho)^{R_{i}-H_{i}}}{Z(R_{i},H_{i})}\ ,

(2)

where

is the prior distribution assumed to be uniform. The posterior in Eq. (
2

\mathcal{B}(H_{i}+1,R_{i}-H_{i}+1)

(3)

A further ingredient in the theory is the mixed strategy profile

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E_{\vec{a}}[\E_{w}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w)]]

\displaystyle=\hrho_{i}\E_{\vec{a}}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=1)]+(1-\hrho_{i})\E_{\vec{a}}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=0)]\ ,

III.1
Nash equilibrium

The
Nash equilibrium
is defined as a strategy profile such that no player can improve their expected payoff by unilaterally deviating from their strategy, given the strategies of the others. A key point is that strategies and payoffs adopted by players are not common knowledge since private information may lead each player to form a different estimate of the bias
\rho

III.2
Trading of Information

We now make a further set of assumptions about the information held by each player (see Appendix
C
for a rigorous definition of the information structure of the game).
(A1)
All players use the same method to estimate the coin bias from the information string
\vec{s}_{i}
they hold, based on the Beta posterior in Eq. (
3
), and use the same functional form of Eq. (
4
) for the expected payoff. This assumption is common knowledge.

(A2)
All players have access to a common string
\vec{s}_{0}
of
R
consecutive past outcomes, immediately preceding the one on which they are about to bet. Let
H
denote the number of heads in this shared string; players then estimate the coin bias using the corresponding Beta distribution
\mathcal{B}(H+1,R-H+1)
, leading to a common estimate
\hat{\rho}\coloneq(H+1)/(R+2)
.
(A3)
Player 1 (P1), privately holds additional data – a longer consecutive string with parameters
(H_{1},R_{1})
, with
R_{1}>R
– extending further back in the past, which yields a potentially different estimate
\hat{\rho}_{1}=(H_{1}+1)/(R_{1}+2)
.
(A4)
Other players are unaware that privileged players with access to extra information exist, and instead believe that P1 – like all the others – has access to only
(H,R)
. This set of assumptions induces the aforementioned informational asymmetry: P1 can infer all others’ payoffs from the public data, while the remaining
N-1
players cannot compute hers.

P1 therefore faces a strategic choice on how to leverage her private information: she can either
(i)
keep her data private and exploit her informational advantage to maximize her payoff against the less informed players, or
(ii)
choose to sell part of her private data to another player, conventionally denoted as player 2 (P2). The fundamental principle that sets information apart from tangible goods is indeed that the seller still retains full possession of her dataset even after a possible transaction, leading to competition with the buyer. In scenario
(ii)
the transaction gives P2 an ordered
substring of P1’s extra data, contiguous to the public string, yielding an overall string
\vec{s}_{2}
with parameters
(H_{2},R_{2})
(
R_{1}\geq R_{2}>R
), while P1 still retains her entire dataset which she uses to devise a new optimal strategy against her new competitor.

a competitor (P2) stronger than any of the other
N-1

How should P1 choose between scenarios
(i)
and
(ii)
, and how much should she charge P2 in the latter case? To answer these questions, we have to first compute the expected change in wealth for both P1 and P2 between the two scenarios.

III.3
The fair price of data

In scenario
(i)
, P1 – holding a long (size
R_{1}
) string
\vec{s}_{1}
of data – will compete against
N-1
equally informed players, who all share the same shorter string
\vec{s}_{0}
of
R

\vec{p}^{\star}

9

\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}=((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})

(ii)
, both P1 and P2 will play the game based on different information from the
N-2

\vec{p}^{\star}
in this case will be of the form
\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}}=((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}},q_{\text{eq}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, where ‘post’ refers to a scenario where a transaction has occurred. The probability
p_{\text{eq}}
that less informed participants play Heads is identical in the two scenarios by virtue of
(A4)
. In both scenarios, P1 has complete knowledge of other players’ strategies, therefore
\vec{p}^{\star}
in Eq. (
9

\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}
and

The main technical task will therefore be to compute the probabilities
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}
,
p_{\text{eq}}
,
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}
and
q_{\text{eq}}

Armed with the equilibrium strategies in the two scenarios, we can compute the fair price of the extra information held by P1. The act of selling part of her data to P2 may reduce P1’s expected earnings due to increased competition, and this potential loss must be offset by the monetary payment received from P2. This defines the minimal acceptable price of the sold information, denoted
\Psi_{\text{min}}
, that P1 should demand from P2. At the same time, the extra information purchased by P2 may enhance her ability to make accurate predictions and improve her expected payoff. However, the strategic value of this extra information is bounded: there exists a maximal price
\Psi_{\text{max}}
beyond which the cost of the data exceeds its expected utility for the buyer.
The corresponding price bounds can therefore be most naturally defined as

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{min}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hrho_{1})-\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hrho_{1})\ ,

(10)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{max}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hrho_{1})-\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hrho_{1})\ ,

(11)

where the payoffs
\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i})
are defined in Eq. (
4
). Eq. (
10
) precisely defines the minimum price by the reduction in utility experienced by P1 due to the transaction, while Eq. (
11
) sets the maximum price as the increase in utility gained by P2 after the transaction. A transaction between P1 and P2 can only happen if
\Psi_{\text{max}}>0
and
\Psi_{\text{min}}\leq\Psi_{\text{max}}
, ensuring that any non-negative price
\Psi\geq 0
within the interval
[\Psi_{\text{min}},\Psi_{\text{max}}]
constitutes a mutually beneficial agreement. We emphasize that these prices are defined from the perspective of the seller. More precisely, the terms
\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hrho_{1}),\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hrho_{1})
represent P1’s estimate of P2’s payoff, as indicated by the use of P1’s belief
\hrho_{1}
. While other choices are possible, defining the price ranges from the point of view of P1 is very natural, as P1 has the most complete information set, enabling her to compute the most accurate estimates of other players’ payoffs.

III.4
Volatility aversion

In the setting we just described, the payoffs
\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i})
from Eq. (
4
) only depend on the mean
\hrho_{i}
of the posterior in Eq. (
3
). However, since this estimate
\hat{\rho}_{i}
is obtained from a finite data sample, it is inherently subject to statistical fluctuations.
Consequently, even though
\hat{\rho}_{i}
serves as a point estimate, it may significantly deviate from the true bias, especially for short strings.

It is therefore natural to assume that rational players might take this uncertainty into account when evaluating their strategies. Specifically, they may consider the variance of their payoff with respect to the randomness in the posterior
P_{i}(\rho|\vec{s}_{i})
as a measure of the uncertainty associated with their estimate
\hat{\rho}_{i}
. Depending on their preferences, players may be more or less tolerant to it.

We incorporate this idea by considering
volatility-averse
players who adjust their effective payoff by penalizing large variance in their subjective estimates. This leads to a modified utility function that reflects not only the expected gain but also the confidence the player has in their estimate of the gain. We denote the payoff of a volatility-averse player as
\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i},R_{i};\lambda)
, and define it as

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i},R_{i};\lambda)\coloneqq\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i})-\lambda\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\U_{i}(\vec{p},\rho_{i})]\ ,

(12)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a parameter that quantifies players’ aversion to volatility (assumed identical across all players for simplicity), and
\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\U_{i}(\vec{p},\rho_{i})]
denotes the variance of Eq. (
4
) where
\rho_{i}
is a random variable following the posterior distribution in Eq. (
2
). The choice of a variance penalty is fairly standard in portfolio optimization
Markowitz 1952
;
Markowitz and Todd 2000
;
Phelps 2024
.
Since
\U_{i}(\vec{p},\rho_{i})
is linear in
\rho_{i}
(see Eq. (
4
)), this variance term can be computed analytically:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\U_{i}(\vec{p},\rho_{i})]

\displaystyle=(\E_{\vec{a}}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=1)-u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=0)])^{2}\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\rho_{i}]

\displaystyle=(\E_{\vec{a}}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=1)-u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=0)])^{2}\frac{\hrho_{i}(1-\hrho_{i})}{R_{i}+3}\ ,

(13)

where we have used the fact that
\rho_{i}
follows a Beta distribution
\mathcal{B}(H_{i}+1,R_{i}-H_{1}+1)
. We remark that the entire dependence on
\hrho_{i}
and
R_{i}
is captured in the last factor, thus the variance decreases as
R_{i}
increases.
Higher values of
\lambda
correspond to stronger aversion to uncertainty. When
\lambda=0
, the player is volatility-neutral, and the payoff reduces to the expected value in Eq. (
4
). In the case of volatility-averse players, the admissible price range for the transaction can be defined analogously to the volatility-neutral setting, using the updated form of the payoff given in Eq. (
12
). The definition of the Nash equilibrium also remains formally identical, with the modified payoff replacing the original one in all expressions.

IV.1.1
Calculation of

i
’s corresponding best response
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)
. The symmetric equilibrium strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
is then found self-consistently by solving the fixed-point condition
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p_{\text{eq}})=p_{\text{eq}}
; that is, the best response to others playing
p_{\text{eq}}
is to play
p_{\text{eq}}
as well.
Accordingly, we evaluate player
i
’s expected payoff under the strategy profile
\vec{p}=(p,\dots,p,q_{i},p,\dots,p)
, where
q_{i}
appears in the
i
-th position, using Eq. (
4
). A complete derivation, along with a detailed analysis of the equilibrium strategies, is provided in Appendix
A.1
and leads to the following expression

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho)=(q_{i}-p)\alpha(\hrho,p,N)\ ,

(14)

where the payoff depends linearly on player
i
’s strategy
q_{i}
, with slope

\displaystyle\alpha(\hrho,p,N):=\left[\hrho-\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}\right]\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{p(1-p)}\ .

(15)

The sign of
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)
determines the best response of player
i
, which maximizes her expected payoff:

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)>0
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Heads with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)\equiv 1
).

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)<0
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Tails with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)\equiv 0
).

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)=0
, the payoff is independent of
q_{i}
, hence any
q_{i}\in[0,1]
is a best response.

Figure 2:
Equilibrium strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
as a function of
\hat{\rho}
, for different values of
N
, in the case of equally informed players.

For
\hat{\rho}\in(1-\frac{1}{N},1]
, it holds that
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)>0
for all
p\in[0,1]
. This implies that the best response is always
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)\equiv 1
, regardless of the strategy adopted by the remaining players. Consequently, the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium in this regime is
p_{\text{eq}}=1
, where all players play the pure strategy Heads, with no incentive to deviate. Similarly, for
\hat{\rho}\in[0,\frac{1}{N})
,
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)<0
for all
p\in[0,1]
, so the best response is always to play Tails, leading to the symmetric equilibrium
p_{\text{eq}}=0
. For
\hat{\rho}\in\left[\frac{1}{N},1-\frac{1}{N}\right]
, the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
is determined as the solution of the following equation

\hat{\rho}=\frac{p_{\text{eq}}-p_{\text{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\text{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\text{eq}})^{N}}\ ,

(16)

which corresponds to the condition
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p_{\text{eq}},N)=0
.
In Figure
2
, this corresponds to the central region where the strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
varies smoothly with
\hat{\rho}
. At the boundaries, Eq. (
16
) implies pure strategies:
p_{\text{eq}}=0
when
\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N}
and
p_{\text{eq}}=1
when
\hat{\rho}=1-\frac{1}{N}
, meaning that all players always bet on Tails or Heads, respectively.
By symmetry and the zero-sum structure of the game, it follows that
\U_{i}(\vec{p}_{\text{eq}},\hat{\rho})=0
for all
i=1,\dots,N
, as also confirmed by Eq. (
14
) evaluated at
q_{i}=p=p_{\text{eq}}
.
As seen in Figure
2
, as
N\to\infty
, the equilibrium strategy converges to
p_{\text{eq}}\to\hat{\rho}
, consistent with the limiting form of Eq. (
16
).

IV.1.2
Calculation of
(q_{1})_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{pre}}

Within the group of
N
players sharing the same public information, the prospective seller has access to a longer sequence of past coin outcomes. This informational advantage allows her to compute an alternative estimate of the coin’s bias based on a larger sample. We denote this player as P1 and let
\hat{\rho}_{1}
represent her estimate of the bias, which generally differs from the common estimate
\hat{\rho}
used by the other
N-1
players.

Since
\hat{\rho}
is common knowledge, P1 knows the strategy that the others will adopt. Players
2,\dots,N
continue to follow the same equilibrium strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
derived in Section
IV.1.1
, as they are unaware of P1’s additional information and assume she uses the same estimate
\hat{\rho}
.

P1 can therefore evaluate her expected payoff under the strategy profile
\vec{p}=(q_{1},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, which again leads to the expression in Eq. (
14
)

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p},\hrho_{1})=(q_{1}-p_{\text{eq}})\alpha(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}},N)\ .

(17)

\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hrho_{1})
. In the squares delimited by the white lines, P1’s payoff is zero, since she is playing the same strategy as other players. Above the magenta dashed line, P1 plays according to the pure strategy Heads, below the line she plays with pure strategy Tails.

In Appendix
A.2
, we derive that P1’s best response, maximizing her expected payoff given her belief
\hat{\rho}_{1}
, is

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}[\hrho_{1}>\hrho]&\text{if }\hrho_{1}\in[1/N,1-1/N]\ ,\\
0&\text{if }\hrho_{1}<1/N\ ,\\
1&\text{if }\hrho_{1}>1-1/N\ ,\end{cases}

(18)

where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function such that:
\mathbbm{1}(P)=1
if
P
is true and zero otherwise. As shown in Figure
3
, P1’s strategy is almost always pure. In the central band
\hat{\rho}_{1}\in[1/N,1-1/N]
, P1 compares her belief
\hat{\rho}_{1}
with the public belief
\hat{\rho}
: if she believes others underestimate the probability of Heads (
\hat{\rho}_{1}>\hat{\rho}
), she bets Heads; if they overestimate it (
\hat{\rho}_{1}<\hat{\rho}
), she bets Tails. This threshold behavior is marked in the figure by the dashed magenta line: below the line,
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}=0
(pure Tails); above,
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}=1
(pure Heads); on the magenta line instead, P1’s payoff is zero, independently of
q_{1}
. Outside the interval
[1/N,1-1/N]
, P1’s strategy no longer depends on
\hat{\rho}
. If
\hat{\rho}_{1}<1/N
, she always plays Tails; if
\hat{\rho}_{1}>1-1/N
, she always plays Heads—regardless of others’ beliefs.

P1’s expected payoff
\mathcal{U}_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hat{\rho}_{1})
, with
\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}=((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, is shown as a heatmap in Figure
3
. We find that
\U_{1}\geq 0
everywhere, since P1 exploits private information. The payoff increases with the difference
|\hat{\rho}_{1}-\hat{\rho}|
, and vanishes when
\hat{\rho}_{1}=\hat{\rho}
or when P1 plays the same strategy as the other players, i.e., when both
\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{1}<1/N
or both are larger than
1-1/N
. These regions of zero payoff are enclosed by the white squares in the figure.

IV.1.3
Calculation of
q_{\mathrm{eq}}

We next characterize the strategy adopted by P2 when P1 sells her a portion of private data.
P2 now holds a longer consecutive string
(H_{2},R_{2})
than the less-informed players, which she can use to make a different estimate
\hat{\rho}_{2}
of the bias. Furthermore, the transaction reveals to P2 that P1 possesses a string of length
at least

R_{2}
; hence, the most reasonable assumption for P2 is that P1 has the same information as hers and therefore shares the same estimate
\hat{\rho}_{2}
.
Accordingly, P2 believes P1 to be identical to herself and to adopt the same strategy.
The
N-2
players not involved in the transaction, being unaware that “special” players exist, continue to play the strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
derived in Sec.
IV.1.1
. We then look for a subjective equilibrium symmetric between P1 and P2, i.e.,
\vec{p}^{\,\star}(2)=(q_{\text{eq}},q_{\text{eq}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, where
q_{\text{eq}}
should be determined.
To find the equilibrium, we first evaluate P2’s payoff on a strategy profile of the form
\vec{p}=(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, yielding

\displaystyle\U_{2}(\vec{p},\hrho_{2})=

\displaystyle q_{2}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\text{eq}})q_{1}+B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\text{eq}})\right]+

(19)

\displaystyle+C_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\text{eq}})q_{1}+D_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\text{eq}})\ .

Then we use this expression to compute P2’s best response
q_{2}^{\text{br}}(q_{1},p_{\text{eq}})
to any strategy
q_{1}
played by P1. Finally, using the symmetry assumption, we impose that at equilibrium
q_{2}^{\text{br}}(q_{\text{eq}},p_{\text{eq}})=q_{\text{eq}}
, giving the desired equilibrium.
The detailed derivation of Eq. (
19
), along with the explicit expressions for the coefficients
A_{N}
,
B_{N}
,
C_{N}
, and
D_{N}
, is provided in Appendix
A.3
, together with the explicit computation of the equilibrium.

Here we describe the main steps to find
q_{\text{eq}}
.
One attempts to find the equilibrium by solving for the
q_{1}
that makes the square bracket term in Eq. (
19
) vanish: in this case, the payoff becomes independent of
q_{2}
, hence any
q_{2}
is a best response. This in turn implies that the value we found for
q_{1}
is the equilibrium.
The bracket vanishes for
q_{1}=-B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\text{eq}})/A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\text{eq}})
. However this value can often fall outside the permitted range, i.e.,
q_{1}\in[0,1]
. We prove that the true equilibrium is found by clipping the solution to
[0,1]
. In formulas we have:

\displaystyle q_{1}=q_{2}=q_{\mathrm{eq}}\coloneqq\max\left(0,\min\left(1,-\frac{B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\text{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\text{eq}})}\right)\right).

(20)

This equilibrium encompasses both pure and mixed strategies, depending on the value of
\hat{\rho}_{2}
. In particular, in Appendix
A.3
we prove that, for every value of
p_{\text{eq}}
, there exist two threshold values
\hat{\rho}_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\text{eq}},N)
and
\hat{\rho}_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\text{eq}},N)
, such that:

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0

\displaystyle\iff\hat{\rho}_{2}\leq\hat{\rho}_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\text{eq}},N)\ ,

(21)

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1

\displaystyle\iff\hat{\rho}_{2}\geq\hat{\rho}_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\text{eq}},N)\ .

(22)

For intermediate values of
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, the buyer adopts a genuinely mixed strategy, with
q_{\mathrm{eq}}
being an increasing function of
\hrho_{2}
.

IV.1.4
Calculation of
(q_{1})_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{post}}

In the previous section, we analyzed the behavior of the buyer (P2) under the assumption that she believes the seller (P1) evaluates payoffs using the same coin bias estimate,
\hat{\rho}_{2}
. This reflects P2’s belief, not the actual state of the game, though. In reality, unless the full dataset is transferred, P1 retains additional private information and continues to base her strategy on her own estimate
\hat{\rho}_{1}
, which generally differs from
\hat{\rho}_{2}
.

However, P1 – who has complete knowledge of the information held by other players – knows that P2 will play with
q_{\text{eq}}
computed using Eq. (
20
). Using this information, we can compute P1’s expected payoff as a function of her strategy
q_{1}

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p},\hrho_{1})=

\displaystyle q_{1}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})q_{\text{eq}}+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})\right]+

(23)

\displaystyle+C_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})q_{\text{eq}}+D_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})\ ,

where
\vec{p}=(q_{1},q_{\text{eq}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})

and the functions
A_{N},B_{N},C_{N},D_{N}
are identical to those of Eq. (
19
).

P1’s strategy is then obtained by maximizing Eq. (
23
) with respect to
q_{1}\in[0,1]
. Since this expression is linear in

, the maximum will always be located at one of the boundaries of the interval, and therefore P1 will always play a pure strategy (except for a measure zero set, described in Appendix
A.4
, where P1’s payoff is indipendent of her strategy).
In formulas, P1’s post-transaction equilibrium strategy is given by

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}=\mathbbm{1}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})q_{\text{eq}}+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})>0\right]\ .

(24)

Although
\hat{\rho}_{2}
does not appear explicitly in Eq. (
23
) and Eq. (
24
), it affects P1’s payoff and strategy indirectly via the term
q_{\text{eq}}
, which depends on
\hat{\rho}_{2}
.

IV.2
Payoffs and fair price of information

Sections
IV.1.1
–
IV.1.4
were devoted to determining the strategies played by all players, both before and after a potential data transaction. These analyses yielded the equilibrium strategy profiles
\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}=((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
and
\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}}=((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}},q_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\text{eq}},\dots,p_{\text{eq}})
, which we now use to compute the corresponding payoffs and to evaluate the price bounds for the transaction, defined in Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
11
).
We begin by examining the payoffs prior to the transaction. The equilibrium payoff of P1,
\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{,\text{pre}},\hat{\rho}_{1})
, is given by Eq. (
17
) evaluated at the equilibrium strategy
\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}
, and is shown in Figure
3
.
As for P2, in the absence of any information exchange, she belongs to the group of less-informed players and adopts the common strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
. Due to the zero-sum nature of the game and the symmetry among the less-informed players, P1 can compute P2’s pre-transaction payoff as

\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hat{\rho}_{1})=-\frac{1}{N-1}\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}},\hat{\rho}_{1})\ .

(25)

As expected, if P1 earns a positive payoff by exploiting her additional information, the less-informed players, including P2, incur symmetric losses.

We now examine the payoffs after the transaction. Given the post-transaction strategy profile
\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}}
, the payoff of P1 can be computed using Eq. (
23
)

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hrho_{1})=

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})q_{\text{eq}}+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})\right]+

(26)

\displaystyle+C_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})q_{\text{eq}}+D_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\text{eq}})\ .

She can also compute the payoff
\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hrho_{1})
of the buyer after the transaction using the very same expression in Eq. (
26
), with
q_{\text{eq}}
and
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}
swapped. Finally, the payoff of each of the remaining
N-2
players, those relying solely on the public information, can be computed by P1 using symmetry and the zero-sum condition of the game. Specifically, it is given by

\U_{i}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hat{\rho}_{1})=-\frac{\left[\U_{1}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hat{\rho}_{1})+\U_{2}(\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}},\hat{\rho}_{1})\right]}{N-2},\quad\forall i\geq 3\ .

(27)

\Psi_{\min}

can be divided into six regions:

1.

Both price bounds are positive and satisfy
\Psi_{\text{max}}>\Psi_{\text{min}}>0
, so the transaction is viable provided the seller charges a price above
\Psi_{\text{min}}
to offset her expected loss. This defines a
cooperative
regime, where both players benefit from the exchange at a strictly positive price.

2.

In contrast, in the second region,
\Psi_{\text{min}}>\Psi_{\text{max}}>0
, meaning the seller’s expected loss exceeds the buyer’s potential gain, making the transaction unviable. This defines a
competitive
regime, where the buyer’s gain is too small to justify the price the seller would ask to cover her loss.

3.

At the boundary between the second and third region, a discontinuity in
\Psi_{\text{max}}
arises due to a shift in P1’s post-transaction strategy, from playing Tails to Heads, as shown in Figure
4
(d)
. This strategic switch increases the buyer’s expected benefit, restoring the possibility of a transaction. The third region is thus again a cooperative regime, analogous to the first.

4.

Entering the fourth region,
\Psi_{\text{min}}
becomes negative while
\Psi_{\text{max}}
remains positive, indicating that both players benefit from the transaction even if no payment is made. This defines a
symbiotic
regime, where sharing the data alone is sufficient to increase both players’ payoffs at the expense of the others. The existence of a symbiotic regime is another paradoxical effect: one would normally expect (and indeed previous literature for data pricing hinged on this
Majumdar et al. 2025
;
Jaisingh et al. 2008
;
Ghosh and Roth 2011
) that sharing data necessarily leads to a loss of privacy and hence reduces profits for the sharer. In our case instead, the strategic nature of the game enables both P1 and P2 to profit from the data exchange. This is due to a combination of effects: (i) P2 changes strategy from mixed to pure Tails after the exchange (ii) P1 in response adopts the opposite pure strategy (Heads). Since they play opposite strategies, the two players will never share the pot with each other, in case of victory. This leads to an increase in payoff for both. (iii) On the other hand, P2 underestimates the probability of the outcome Heads, so she will win less and less frequently as
\hrho_{1}
increases. This last effect offsets the gains when reaching the boundary with region 5.

5.

Moving into the fifth region,
\Psi_{\text{max}}
also becomes negative, indicating that the buyer would incur a loss from the transaction. Interestingly, the minimum price remains negative and lies below the maximum price. This creates a hypothetically favorable setting in which player P1 could give the data to P2 at no cost and offer additional compensation, making the transaction mutually beneficial despite the fact that the information would otherwise mislead the buyer and reduce her payoff. This defines an
exploitative cooperation
regime, where a transaction remains possible only if the
seller
is willing to pay the buyer to offset the misleading effect of the data sold.

6.

Finally, in the sixth region, both price bounds are negative and satisfy
\Psi_{\text{max}}<\Psi_{\text{min}}<0
, confirming that no transaction is beneficial under any price. This defines the
no-deal
regime, where the buyer’s loss cannot be offset by what the seller gains, ruling out any possible agreement.

These regimes illustrate the complex and diverse landscape of outcomes that our model uncovers, highlighting how strategic information trading can generate counterintuitive effects.

IV.3
Volatility averse players (

)

We now extend the analysis to volatility-averse players, modeled by a common parameter
\lambda>0
. The underlying game structure remains unchanged, but payoffs are now computed using Eq. (
12
). As in the volatility-neutral setting, we determine the equilibrium strategies of all players both before and after the transaction:

\vec{p}^{\,\text{pre}}=\left((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}},\,p_{\text{eq}},\,\dots,\,p_{\text{eq}}\right)
and

\vec{p}^{\,\text{post}}=\left((q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}},\,q_{\text{eq}},\,p_{\text{eq}},\,\dots,\,p_{\text{eq}}\right)
.
The derivations follow the same steps as in sections
IV.1.1
–
IV.1.4
, with the only change being the use of the volatility-adjusted payoff function (see Appendix
B
for full details).

The first result to highlight is that, although players are now volatility-averse, the Nash equilibrium among equally informed players remains unchanged: the equilibrium strategy
p_{\text{eq}}
as a function of
\hat{\rho}
coincides with that of the volatility-neutral setting described in section
IV.1.1
. In other words, introducing a common volatility penalty does not alter the equilibrium configuration when all players possess the same information. Instead, for the seller before the transaction, the situation changes significantly. Due to volatility aversion, her payoff is no longer linear in her own strategy but becomes a concave quadratic function. As a result, the seller’s optimal choice before the transaction may now be a mixed strategy. This is a novel feature, since in the volatility-neutral setting only pure strategies emerged at equilibrium. Also after the transaction, for both buyer and seller, the payoffs become concave quadratic functions of their strategies,
generally leading to equilibrium configurations that differ from those of the volatility-neutral case. An additional key distinction
is that, under volatility aversion, a player’s equilibrium strategy depends not only on the posterior mean
\hat{\rho}_{i}
, but also explicitly on the length
R_{i}
of the data string they hold. Players with the same estimate
\hat{\rho}_{i}
but different
R_{i}
may behave differently, as longer strings reduce uncertainty and can lead to higher expected payoffs. A direct consequence of the dependence of equilibrium strategies on data length is that the transaction price bounds also become sensitive to the length of the string involved in the exchange. These bounds can still be computed using Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
11
), with the only difference that the payoffs are now evaluated using the risk-averse payoff in Eq. (
12
).

V
Conclusion

In this work we proposed a game-theoretic principle to define and compute the price of information in a competitive setting, where players bet on the outcome of an underlying stochastic process on the basis of asymmetric information about past outcomes. By modeling the potential exchange of data between an informed seller and a less informed buyer, we showed that the value of information naturally emerges from the trade-off between the seller’s expected loss of exclusivity and the buyer’s potential gain in predictive accuracy. Our analysis identified explicit price bounds,
\Psi_{\mathrm{\min}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{\max}}
, which determine whether a mutually beneficial transaction can take place. We characterized different strategic regimes, depending on the beliefs the players hold (or may acquire) about the stochastic process before and after the transaction.

In some cases, the most informed player (P1) may
cooperate
with a prospective buyer (P2) by sharing valuable information to jointly exploit the less informed competitors. In a
competitive
regime, instead, the potential revenue from selling the data is not sufficient to cover the seller’s losses.
In a
symbiotic
regime, the seller can share her data with the buyer at no cost and still improve the payoffs of both players involved in the exchange, contradicting the usual intuition that giving up exclusivity necessarily reduces the seller’s profit. Moreover, our analysis shows that for certain combinations of parameters, the most informed player can paradoxically lose money to the advantage of the least informed players, who instead experience a positive payoff. This is due to the “destructive interference” in the respective winning chances that can arise between buyer and seller after the transaction has occurred, and further highlights the rich and unusual landscape of interesting and even exotic market effects displayed by intangible assets and foreign to classical textbook economics.

In addition, we found that the feasibility and profitability of information transactions are strongly influenced by market size: as the number of players
N
increases, competition between the informed parties weakens, and transactions that are unviable in “small” games can become mutually beneficial in “large” ones.

Extending the model to volatility-averse players, we showed that while equilibrium strategies among equally informed players remain unchanged, new features arise:
mixed strategies may appear and equilibrium strategies now explicitly depend on the length of the data strings. This dependence on sample size also makes the transaction price bounds sensitive to the
amount
of data exchanged – not just its
bias – a feature absent in the volatility-neutral case.

The simple Bayesian game-theoretic framework developed here opens up a wealth of opportunities for implementing even more realistic scenarios by relaxing or extending any of the foundational assumptions without compromising the analytical tractability of the model. First, it would be natural to consider a multi-round setting, where the game is played repeatedly rather than in a single round, so that beliefs, strategies, and transaction prices evolve and are updated over time. Including more sophisticated modelling assumptions on the underlying stochastic process would also be an interesting direction to explore: upon including temporal correlations, for instance through a Markovian process, the arrangement of outcomes within each string would become relevant, potentially increasing the informational value of the data and altering the resulting price bounds. Embedding the game into a complex network of sellers and buyers is likely to generate further feedback loops and even more interesting market effects – as well as going beyond binary outcomes, or allowing for different volatility aversion levels between players. The versatility of this framework and its analytical tractability makes it an excellent candidate to explore
in silico
foundational questions about data valuation in interactive markets characterized by asymmetric information.

This Appendix provides the full derivation of the equilibrium strategies discussed in Section
IV.1
for the case of volatility-neutral players.

A.1
Calculation of
p_{\mathrm{eq}}

In this section, we determine the Nash equilibrium strategies when all players possess identical information. We assume that all players have access to a common string of past toss outcomes, representing the ”coin flips” immediately preceding the one they are about to bet on. Each player will use that string to give a first estimate of the coin bias using Eq. (
2
) of the main text; this in turn implies that at the start of the game each player will assume the same bias
\hrho
for the coin, making beliefs and payoffs common knowledge and identical across all players.

Given the symmetry of this setting, it is sufficient to focus on a single representative player, say player
i
, as the same analysis applies identically to all others. To find the symmetric Nash equilibrium, we assume that every player except
i
adopts strategy
p
, and compute the best response
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)
by evaluating player
i
’s expected payoff under the strategy profile
\vec{p}=(p,\dots,p,q_{i},p,\dots,p)
, where
q_{i}
appears in the
i
-th position.
Given the action profile
\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, which represents all players’ bets, and the toss outcome
w
, let
u_{i}(\vec{a}\mid w)
denote the payoff of player
i
conditional on
w
. Her expected payoff is then the average over all possible outcomes, weighted by the probabilities she assigns to them:

\E_{w}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w)]=p_{i}(w=1)u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=1)+p_{i}(w=0)u_{i}(\vec{a}|w=0)\ ,

(28)

where
p_{i}(w=1)=\hrho
and is the same for all players. Using Eq. (
1
) of the main text we get:

\displaystyle\E_{w}[u_{i}(a_{i}=1,\vec{a}_{-i}|w)]=\hrho\left[\frac{N}{1+\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{1a_{j}}}-1\right]+(1-\hrho)\left[-\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{0a_{j}}>0)\right]\ ,

(29)

\displaystyle\E_{w}[u_{i}(a_{i}=0,\vec{a}_{-i}|w)]=\hrho\left[-\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{1a_{j}}>0)\right]+(1-\hrho)\left[\frac{N}{1+\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{0a_{j}}}-1\right]\ ,

(30)

where we have used the binary indicator function
\mathbbm{1}(P)=1
if
P
is true, and zero otherwise. In Eq. (
29
), player
i
bets on Heads (
a_{i}=1
). She wins with probability
\hat{\rho}
, in which case the pot is shared among all players who also guessed correctly (a total of
\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{1a_{j}}
opponents). With probability
(1-\hat{\rho})
, her prediction is wrong, and she loses her entry fee unless no other player predicted the correct outcome (i.e., when
\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{0a_{j}}>0)=0
). Analogous considerations apply to the case where she bets on Tails, as given in Eq. (
30
). To compute player
i
’s expected payoff, we must also average over possible actions of all other players. Since in the considered strategy profile
\vec{p}
each of the remaining
N-1
players bets on Heads with probability
p
, we can write:

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho)\coloneqq\E_{\vec{a}}\E_{w}[u_{i}(\vec{a}|w)]=

\displaystyle q_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k-1}\E_{w}[u_{i}(a_{i}=1,a_{-i}=[1^{k}0^{N-k-1}]|w)]+

(31)

\displaystyle(1-q_{i})\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k-1}\E_{w}[u_{i}(a_{i}=0,a_{-i}=[1^{k}0^{N-k-1}]|w)]\ ,

where
(a_{i}=1,a_{-i}=[1^{k}0^{N-k-1}])
denotes the case where player
i
bets on Heads,
k
of the remaining players also bet on Heads, and the remaining
N-k-1
players bet on Tails. The analogous notation applies when
a_{i}=0
. Using the following identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k-1}=1\ ,

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(N-1)!}{k!(N-1-k)!}\frac{p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k-1}}{1+k}=\frac{1-(1-p)^{N}}{Np}\ ,

(33)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\binom{N-1}{k}\frac{p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k-1}}{N-k}=\frac{1-p^{N}}{N(1-p)}\ ,

(34)

we can compute the expected payoff explicitly:

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho)=

\displaystyle q_{i}\left\{\hat{\rho}[1-(1-p)^{N}]-\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}(1-p^{N-1})-(1-p^{N-1})\right\}+

(35)

\displaystyle+(1-q_{i})\left\{\hat{\rho}-\frac{\hat{\rho}[1-p^{N}]}{1-p}-\hat{\rho}[1-(1-p)^{N-1}]+\frac{1-p^{N}}{1-p}-1\right\}

\displaystyle=q_{i}\left\{\hrho\left[\frac{1-(1-p)^{N-1}}{p}\right]+(1-\hrho)\left[\frac{p^{N-1}-1}{1-p}\right]\right\}+\frac{1-\hrho}{1-p}(1-p^{N})+\hrho(1-p)^{N-1}-1

\displaystyle=q_{i}\left\{\left[\hrho-\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}\right]\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{p(1-p)}\right\}+\left[\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}-\hrho\right]\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{1-p}\ .

Since the payoff is linear in
q_{i}
, we define its slope
\alpha
as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha(\hrho,p,N)\coloneqq\left[\hrho-\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}\right]\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{p(1-p)}\ .

(36)

The sign of
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)
determines the best response of player
i
, which maximizes her expected payoff:

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)>0
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Heads with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)\equiv 1
).

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)<0
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Tails with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)\equiv 0
).

•

If
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)=0
, the payoff is independent of
q_{i}
, hence any
q_{i}\in[0,1]
is a best response.

To analyze the sign of
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)
, we rewrite it as:

\displaystyle\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)=[\hrho-K(p,N)]G(p,N)\ ,

(37)

where we define the auxiliary functions:

\displaystyle K(p,N)\coloneqq\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}\ ,

(38)

\displaystyle G(p,N)\coloneqq\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{p(1-p)}\ .

(39)

For all
N\geq 3
and
p\in(0,1)
, the function
K(p,N)
is strictly increasing and positive, with limits:

\lim_{p\to 0}K(p,N)=\frac{1}{N}\ ,\qquad\lim_{p\to 1}K(p,N)=1-\frac{1}{N}\ .

(40)

Similarly,
G(p,N)
is strictly positive for
p\in(0,1)
and satisfies:

\lim_{p\to 0}G(p,N)=\lim_{p\to 1}G(p,N)=N\ .

(41)

These properties imply that the sign of
\alpha(\hat{\rho},p,N)
is determined by whether
\hat{\rho}
is larger or smaller than
K(p,N)
. If the public string is such that
\hrho\in[\frac{1}{N},1-\frac{1}{N}]
, there exists a unique symmetric Nash equilibrium where all players follow the same strategy
p=q_{i}=p_{\mathrm{eq}}
solution of:

\displaystyle\hrho=\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}=K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\ ,

(42)

since in this case
\alpha(\hrho,p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=0
and every player payoff is fixed by the strategy of the others. This Nash equilibrium corresponds to a pure strategy in the limiting cases. Specifically, when
\hat{\rho}=1-\frac{1}{N}
, the unique equilibrium is
p_{\mathrm{eq}}=1
, while for
\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N}
, it is
p_{\mathrm{eq}}=0
. Equation
42
is in general not analytically invertible for
N>3
, but can be easily solved numerically to find the equilibrium strategy given
\hrho
.

When
\hat{\rho}>1-\frac{1}{N}
, the slope
\alpha
can only be positive, implying that every player’s best response is always to bet on Heads, regardless of what other players are doing. This means that a pure strategy symmetric Nash equilibrium exists with
p=q_{i}=p_{\mathrm{eq}}=1
. Conversely, when
\hat{\rho}<\frac{1}{N}
, for the same reasons a pure strategy symmetric Nash equilibrium exists with
p=q_{i}=p_{\mathrm{eq}}=0
.

The expected payoff in Eq. (
35
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho)=(q_{i}-p)\alpha(\hrho,p,N)\ ,

(43)

from which it directly follows that, in all symmetric equilibrium configurations, the expected payoff of each player is zero.

A.2
Calculation of
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}

Assume now that among the
N
players who share the same information, one of them, the prospective seller, has access to a longer history of past outcomes of the process. This enables the seller to derive a different estimate of the coin’s bias based on a larger data sample.

Denoting this privileged player as player 1, we let
\hat{\rho}_{1}
represent her estimated bias, which in general differs from the common estimate
\hat{\rho}
used by the remaining
N-1
players.

Since
\hat{\rho}
is common knowledge among all players, player 1 can anticipate the strategy adopted by the remaining
N-1
players. Specifically, players
2,\ldots,N
follow the symmetric equilibrium
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
derived in section
A.1
, as they are unaware that player 1 possesses additional information. Believing that all players, including player 1, rely on the same estimate
\hat{\rho}
, they also expect her to act according to this common strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
Therefore, player 1 can evaluate her payoff assuming the strategy profile
\vec{p}=(q_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\mathrm{eq}},\dots,p_{\mathrm{eq}})
, obtaining the same functional form as in Eq. (
35
):

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p},\hrho_{1})=

\displaystyle q_{1}\left\{\left[\hrho_{1}-\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}\right]\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}{p_{\mathrm{eq}}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\right\}+\left[\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}-\hrho_{1}\right]\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}}\ .

(44)

Since the payoff remains linear in
q_{1}
, and the functional form is the same as in (
35
), player 1’s best response is again determined by the sign of the slope:

•

If
\hrho_{1}>K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Heads with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{1}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})\equiv 1
).

•

If
\hrho_{1}<K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
, the best response is the pure strategy where the player bets Tails with certainty (
q^{\text{br}}_{1}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})\equiv 0
).

•

If
\hrho_{1}=K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
, the payoff is independent of
q_{1}
, hence any
q_{1}\in[0,1]
is a best response,

where the function
K(p,N)
is defined in Eq. (
38
). Since the remaining
N-1
players follow the symmetric Nash equilibrium strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
determined by the common belief
\hat{\rho}
, player 1 can compute
K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
accordingly and compare it with her own estimate
\hat{\rho}_{1}
:

\displaystyle K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{N}&\text{ if }\hrho<\frac{1}{N}\\
1-\frac{1}{N}&\text{ if }\hrho>1-\frac{1}{N}\\
\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}=\hrho&\text{ if }\hrho\in[\frac{1}{N},1-\frac{1}{N}]\end{cases}

(45)

It follows that player 1’s equilibrium strategy before the transaction, denoted as
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}[\hrho_{1}>\hrho]&\text{if }\hrho_{1}\in[1/N,1-1/N]\ ,\\
0&\text{if }\hrho_{1}<1/N\ ,\\
1&\text{if }\hrho_{1}>1-1/N\ .\end{cases}

(46)

Note that when
\hat{\rho}_{1}=\hat{\rho}
and both lie within the interval

A.3
Calculation of
q_{\mathrm{eq}}

We now analyze the new Nash equilibrium that emerges when a transaction occurs in which the seller (still denoted as player 1) shares a portion of her private data with another player (denoted as player 2). As a result of the transaction, player 1 retains her original estimate
\hat{\rho}_{1}
, which remains unknown to all other players, including the buyer. Player 2, having acquired partial information from the seller, forms a new estimate
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, which generally may differ from both
\hat{\rho}_{1}
and the public estimate
\hat{\rho}
used by the remaining
N-2
players.
In this setting, the following assumptions are made:

•

Player 1 has complete information: she knows
\hat{\rho}_{1}
,
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, and
\hat{\rho}
.

•

Player 2 knows
\hat{\rho}_{2}
and
\hat{\rho}
, and assumes that player 1 acts based on
\hat{\rho}_{2}
. This is the most reasonable assumption because the transaction reveals to player 2 that player 1 holds a dataset of length
at least
equal to her newly acquired one. Lacking evidence of further private information, player 2 therefore assumes that player 1 possesses the same data and hence the same estimate
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, even though in reality player 1 may still have access to additional private information.

•

Players
3,\dots,N
are unaware of the information asymmetry and believe that all players, including players 1 and 2, are using the common estimate
\hat{\rho}
. Accordingly, they play the symmetric equilibrium strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
derived in section
A.1
, which is known to both the buyer and the seller.

These assumptions imply that we can compute the payoff of player 2 after the transaction using a strategy profile of the form
\vec{p}=(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},...,p_{\mathrm{eq}})
where
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
is the strategy used by the remaining
N-2
players derived in section
A.1
; known both to P1 and P2 since they know
\hat{\rho}
. This payoff can be written in the compact form:

\displaystyle\U_{2}(\vec{p},\hrho_{2})=

\displaystyle q_{1}q_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(1,1)]\right]+(1-q_{1})q_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(0,1)]\right]+

(47)

\displaystyle+q_{1}(1-q_{2})\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(1,0)]\right]+(1-q_{1})(1-q_{2})\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(0,0)]\right]\ ,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(a_{1},a_{2})]\right]
denotes the expected payoff of player 2 when player 1 plays
a_{1}
and player 2 plays
a_{2}
, while all other players follow the strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
. These expectations values can be computed explicitly using identities in Eqs.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(0,0)]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{k}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{k}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-2-k}\left\{(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})\left[\frac{N}{2+N-2-k}-1\right]-\hat{\rho}_{2}\mathbbm{1}\left(\sum_{j>3}\delta_{1a_{j}}>0\right)\right\}

(48)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})}{(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{2}(N-1)}\left[N(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})-1+p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}\right]+(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-2}-1\ ,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(0,1)]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{k}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{k}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-2-k}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left[\frac{N}{1+k}-1\right]\right\}-(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}N}{(N-1)p_{\mathrm{eq}}}\left[1-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-1}\right]-1\ ,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(1,0)]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{k}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{k}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-k-2}\left\{(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})\left[\frac{N}{1+N-k-2}-1\right]\right\}-\hat{\rho}_{2}

(50)

\displaystyle=\frac{N(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})}{(N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\left[1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N-1}\right]-1\ ,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\vec{a}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w}[u_{2}(1,1)]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\binom{N-2}{k}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{k}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N-k-2}\left\{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left[\frac{N}{2+k}-1\right]-(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})\mathbbm{1}\left[\sum_{j>3}\delta_{0a_{j}}>0\right]\right\}

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}}{(N-1)p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}}\left[Np-1+(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}-(N-1)p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}\right]+p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N-2}-1\ .

Since the payoff remains linear in player 2’s strategy
q_{2}
, it is convenient to express it in the following form:

\displaystyle\U_{2}(\vec{p},\hrho_{2})=q_{2}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\right]+C_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+D_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ ,

(52)

where the general forms of the coefficients are given by:

\displaystyle A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\coloneqq\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left(p^{N}(N(p-1)-2p+1)+(1-p)^{N}+p\left((N-2)(1-p)^{N}+2\right)-1\right)}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}p^{2}}+\frac{p^{N}(N(-p)+N+2p-1)-p^{2}}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}p^{2}}\ ,

(53)

\displaystyle B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\coloneqq\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left(p\left(p^{N}+(1-p)^{N}-1\right)-N\left((1-p)^{N}+p-1\right)\right)}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}p}+\frac{-p^{N}+N(p-1)+1}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}}\ ,

(54)

\displaystyle C_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\coloneqq\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left((N(-p)+N+p)p^{N}+p\left(-\left((N-1)(1-p)^{N}\right)-1\right)\right)}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}p}+\frac{(N(p-1)-p)p^{N}+p}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}p}\ ,

(55)

\displaystyle D_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\coloneqq\frac{p^{N}-(N-1)p^{2}+(N-2)p}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}}-\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}\left(p^{N}-\left((N-1)(1-p)^{N}\right)-Np+N-1\right)}{(N-1)(p-1)^{2}}\ ,

(56)

and the payoff slope can be written as:

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\coloneqq A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ .

(57)

From linearity it follows that, to maximize her payoff, player 2’s best response will be:

\displaystyle q_{2}^{(br)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)>0\\
0&\text{ if }\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)<0\\
\text{any $q_{2}\in[0,1]$}&\text{ if }\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=0\end{cases}

(58)

Since player 2 assumes that player 1 will use the same bias estimate
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, she also assumes player 1 will play the same strategy as hers, i.e., with (
q_{1}=q_{2}
).

This symmetry implies that a strategy with
q_{1}=q_{2}=q_{\mathrm{eq}}
, where
q_{\mathrm{eq}}
solves
\alpha_{2}(q_{\mathrm{eq}},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=0
, will be a symmetric Nash equilibrium for the two players (at least from the point of view of player 2), since this would correspond to a fixed payoff for both, independent of their individual strategy. Therefore, the symmetric equilibrium condition is

\alpha_{2}(q_{\mathrm{eq}},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=0\iff q_{\mathrm{eq}}=-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\ .

(59)

As long as this equation admits a solution in the interval
[0,1]
, i.e. when the ratio
-B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})/A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\in[0,1]
, player 2 will find a symmetric equilibrium in which the two players’ payoffs are independent of their respective strategies.
We will now prove that
even when the ratio
-B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})/A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})
is outside the interval
[0,1]
, a subjective symmetric equilibrium still exists (with
q_{1}=q_{2}=q_{\mathrm{eq}}\in\{0,1\}
).
We first need a preliminary lemma:

Lemma A.1
.

\forall N\geq 3,\forall\hrho_{2},p\in[0,1]
, we have
A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)<0
, where the coefficient
A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)
is defined in Eq. (
53
).

Proof.

We first prove it in the case
p\in(0,1)
. Rewriting
A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)
as:

\displaystyle A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=\frac{[1+N(p-1)-2p](\hrho_{2}-1)p^{N}+\hrho_{2}[(1-p)^{N}-1]+p[-p+(2+(N-2)(1-p)^{N})\hrho_{2}]}{p^{2}(1-p)^{2}(N-1)}\ ,

(60)

since
p\in(0,1)
and
N\geq 3
the denominator is always positive and we only need to prove that the numerator

\displaystyle Q_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\coloneqq[1+N(p-1)-2p](\hrho_{2}-1)p^{N}+\hrho_{2}[(1-p)^{N}-1]+p[-p+(2+(N-2)(1-p)^{N})\hrho_{2}],

(61)

is always negative. To do so we first notice by direct substitution that
Q_{3}(\hrho_{2},p)=-p^{2}(1-p)^{2}<0
and we will now prove that this function is decreasing in
N
, i.e.
Q_{N+1}(\hrho_{2},p)\leq Q_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)\ \ \ \forall N\geq 3
:

\displaystyle Q_{N+1}(\hrho_{2},p)-Q_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=-(N-1)[(1-\hrho_{2})(p-1)^{2}p^{N}+\hrho_{2}p^{2}(1-p)^{N}]\leq 0\ .

(62)

This concludes the proof for
p\in(0,1)
. To complete the proof we compute the limit:

\displaystyle A_{N}(\hrho_{2},0)\coloneqq\lim_{p\rightarrow 0}A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=-\frac{N(N-3)}{2(N-1)}\hrho_{2}-\frac{1}{N-1}\ ,

(63)

which is clearly negative
\forall N\geq 3,\forall\hrho_{2}\in[0,1]
; and the last case:

\displaystyle A_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)\coloneqq\lim_{p\rightarrow 1}A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=\frac{(N-3)N}{2(N-1)}\hat{\rho}_{2}-\frac{N}{2}+1\ ,

(64)

that is also negative since
\forall N\geq 3
this coefficient is an increasing function of
\hrho_{2}
and
A_{N}(1,1)=-\frac{1}{N-1}<0
, implying that it is negative
\forall\hrho_{2}\in[0,1]
.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
A.1
we can now prove that (since player 2 assumes that player 1 is using the same coin bias estimate
\hrho_{2}
) from her point of view there exists a unique symmetric Nash equilibrium for any value of
N,p_{\mathrm{eq}}
and
\hrho_{2}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider player 2’s payoff as defined in Eq. (
52
). If player 2 assumes that player 1 has the same coin bias estimate
\hrho_{2}
(i.e. she assumes the same functional form for player 1’s payoff), then from the point of view of player 2 there exists a unique subjective symmetric equilibrium
\forall N\geq 3;\forall\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}}\in[0,1]
with:

q_{1}=q_{2}=q_{\mathrm{eq}}=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\right)\right)\ .

(65)

Proof.

Considering that all less-informed players are following the strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
computed in section
A.1
, the slope of player 2’s payoff is given by Eq. (
57
):

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ .

(66)

First, notice that
\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{-B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N\right)=0
always holds. Moreover, by Lemma
A.1
,
\alpha_{2}
is a strictly decreasing function of
q_{1}
. We now proceed case by case.

•

Suppose
-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\in[0,1]
. In this case the equilibrium is
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}
, since from player’s 2 perspective this value would corresponds to a flat payoff (zero slope) for both informed players, meaning their expected payoff is independent of their individual strategies.

•

Now suppose that
\frac{-B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}<0
. Since
\alpha_{2}
is strictly decreasing in
q_{1}
, it follows that:

0=\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{-B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N\right)>\alpha_{2}(0,\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)>\alpha_{2}(q_{1},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\quad\forall q_{1}\in[0,1].

These inequalities imply that, in this case, player 2’s best response is to always bet on Tails, regardless of other players’ strategies. Therefore, from player 2’s point of view, there exists a symmetric equilibrium given by
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0
.

•

Now suppose that
-\frac{B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}>1
. Since
\alpha_{2}
is strictly decreasing in
q_{1}
, it follows that:

0=\alpha_{2}\left(\frac{-B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N\right)<\alpha_{2}(1,\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)<\alpha(q_{1},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\;\;\;\forall q_{1}\in[0,1].

These inequalities imply that, from player 2’s perspective, the best response for both herself and player 1 is to always bet on Heads, regardless of the other players’ actions. Therefore, there exists a symmetric equilibrium with
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1
from the point of view of player 2.

∎

A.3.1
Mixed and pure strategies

In this subsection, we analyze for which values of
\hat{\rho}_{2}
the equilibrium strategy identified in Lemma
A.2
is a pure strategy. To this end, we first prove the following lemma:

Lemma A.3
.

The function
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)
, defined in Eq. (
54
), is a strictly increasing function of
\hrho_{2}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)}{\partial\hrho_{2}}>0\;\;\;\;\forall p\in[0,1]\;\;\;\;\forall N\geq 3\ .

(67)

Proof.

The proof is trivial for the two limit values of
p
:

\displaystyle B_{N}(\hrho_{2},0)\coloneqq\lim_{p\rightarrow 0}B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=N\hrho_{2}-1\ ,

(68)

\displaystyle B_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)\coloneqq\lim_{p\rightarrow 1}B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)=\frac{N(N+1)}{2(N-1)}\hrho_{2}-\frac{N}{2}\ .

(69)

We are only left to consider the case
p\in(0,1)
in which:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p)}{\partial\hrho_{2}}=\frac{1}{p(1-p)^{2}(N-1)}\left\{p^{N+1}+(N-p)[1-(1-p)^{N}]-pN\right\}\ .

(70)

Since the denominator is always positive we only need to prove that the function:

\displaystyle W_{N}(p)\coloneqq p^{N+1}+(N-p)[1-(1-p)^{N}]-pN

(71)

is also always positive
\forall p\in(0,1),\forall N\geq 3
. To this end we notice that
W_{3}(p)=6(1-p)^{2}p>0
and we prove that this function is also increasing in
N
, i.e.
W_{N+1}(p)-W_{N}(p)>0\;\;\;\forall N\geq 3
. Writing this difference explicitly:

\displaystyle W_{N+1}(p)-W_{N}(p)=(1-p)[1-(1-p)^{N}-p^{N+1}]+p(1-p)^{N}(N-p)

(72)

this term is always positive since the function

\displaystyle f_{N}(p)\coloneqq[1-(1-p)^{N}-p^{N+1}]

(73)

is always positive. To prove this we compute again
f_{3}(p)=(1-p)p(3+p^{2})>0
and
f_{N+1}(p)-f_{N}(p)=p[(1-p)^{N}+(1-p)p^{N}]>0\;\;\;\forall p\in(0,1)
.
∎

By virtue of Lemma
A.3
, we can identify the threshold values of
\hat{\rho}_{2}
at which player 2 switches from a mixed to a pure equilibrium strategy. Indeed, from Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
, we know that for player 2 at equilibrium:

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0\iff-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}=\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|}\leq 0\iff B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\leq 0\ ,

(74)

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1\iff-\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}=\frac{B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|}\geq 1\iff B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\geq|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|=-A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ .

(75)

Fixing a value of
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\in(0,1)
, we define
\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
as the largest value of
\hrho_{2}
such that
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\leq 0
. Due to Lemma
A.3
to find this threshold we can solve the equation
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})=0
which yields:

\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=-\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\left(-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}+N(p_{\mathrm{eq}}-1)+1\right)}{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\left(p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}+(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}-1\right)-N\left((1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}+p_{\mathrm{eq}}-1\right)}\ ,

(76)

therefore, by Lemma
A.3
we can conclude that

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0\iff\hrho_{2}\leq\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\ .

(77)

Although the coefficient
A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p)
does not exhibit the same monotonicity for all values of
p
, it is still linear in
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, just like
B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p)
(see Eqs. (
53
),(
54
)). For this reason we can similarly define
\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)
as the smallest value of
\hrho_{2}
such that
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\geq-A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})
, and this value will be the solution of
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})=-A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})
, which yields:

\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{(p_{\mathrm{eq}}-1)\left((1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}+Np_{\mathrm{eq}}-1\right)}{Np_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}(N+p_{\mathrm{eq}}-1)}}\ .

(78)

Since we proved that
B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})
is an increasing function of
\hrho_{2}
we have that for player 2:

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1\iff\hrho_{2}\geq\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\ .

(79)

To determine these thresholds for
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\in\{0,1\}
we can either recompute them directly using the limiting values for the coefficients
A_{N},B_{N}
(given in Eqs.(
63
),(
64
),(
68
),(
69
)), or evaluate the limits of the general expressions as
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 0
and
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 1

\displaystyle\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(0,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 0}\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\frac{2}{N+1}\ ,

(80)

\displaystyle\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(1,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 1}\hrho_{2}^{\uparrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=1-\frac{1}{N}\ ,

(81)

\displaystyle\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(0,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 0}\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\frac{1}{N}\ ,

(82)

\displaystyle\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(1,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 1}\hrho_{2}^{\downarrow}(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\frac{N-1}{N+1}\ .

(83)

A.4
Calculation of
(q_{1})_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{post}}

In section
A.3
, we fully characterized the behavior of the buyer (player 2) after the transaction, under the assumption that she believes the seller (player 1) will evaluate payoffs using the same coin bias
\hat{\rho}_{2}
as hers.
This reflects P2’s belief, not the actual state of the game, though. In reality, unless the full dataset is transferred, P1 retains additional private information and continues to base her strategy on her own estimate
\hat{\rho}_{1}
, which generally differs from
\hat{\rho}_{2}
. However, since player 1 retains access to the data she sold, she also knows the value
\hat{\rho}_{2}
that player 2 will estimate from it.
This implies that player 1 knows both her own and player 2’s payoff functions.
In fact, the expected payoff computation from player 1’s perspective is identical to the one already presented in section
A.3
, with the same strategy profile
\vec{p}=(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},...,p_{\mathrm{eq}})
and roles of the two players simply interchanged. In formulas, the payoffs are

\displaystyle\U_{2}(\vec{p},\hrho_{2})

\displaystyle=q_{2}\left(A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\right)+C_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{1}+D_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ ,

(84)

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p},\hrho_{1})

\displaystyle=q_{1}\left(A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{2}+B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\right)+C_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{2}+D_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ ,

(85)

where the coefficients are given in Eqs.(
53
)-(
56
). To compute these expressions at the boundaries
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 0
or
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 1
, the appropriate limits should be taken according to Eqs. (
63
),(
64
),(
68
),(
69
). The value of
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
is uniquely determined by the common estimate
\hat{\rho}
, and is given by the equilibrium analysis in section
A.1
. Moreover, since player 1 knows
\hat{\rho}_{2}
, she can anticipate that player 2 will choose her strategy according to Eq. (
65
):

\displaystyle q_{2}=q_{\mathrm{eq}}=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,-\frac{B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\right)\right).

(86)

Since player 1’s expected payoff in Eq.
85
is linear in her own strategy
q_{1}
, her best response is fully determined by the sign of the slope evaluated at
q_{2}=q_{\text{eq}}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{1},q_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\coloneqq A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{\mathrm{eq}}+B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ .

(87)

Accordingly, player 1’s best response and equilibrium strategy after the transaction will be:

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{1},q_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\mathrm{eq}})>0\ ,\\
0&\text{if }\alpha_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{1},q_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\mathrm{eq}})<0\ ,\\
\text{any }q_{1}\in[0,1]&\text{if }\alpha_{1}(\hat{\rho}_{1},q_{\mathrm{eq}},p_{\mathrm{eq}})=0\ .\end{cases}

(88)

As in the pre-transaction case discussed in section
A.2
, when
\alpha_{1}=0
and any strategy is a best response, player 1’s expected payoff is entirely determined by the strategies of the other players and remains fixed. In this case, we again adopt the convention of setting
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}=0
. With this convention, the equilibrium strategy can be compactly written as:

\displaystyle(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{post}}=\mathbbm{1}\left[A_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})q_{\mathrm{eq}}+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})>0\right].

(89)

We distinguish three cases for
\alpha_{1}
depending on the strategy played by the buyer
q_{\mathrm{eq}}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(\hrho_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})=\begin{cases}B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})&\text{if }q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0\ ,\\[10.00002pt]
A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})+B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})&\text{if }q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1\ ,\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})-A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{\left|A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})\right|}&\text{if }q_{\mathrm{eq}}\in\,(0,1)\ .\end{cases}

(90)

Note that since the coefficients
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
have the same functional form for both players, Lemmas
A.1
and
A.3
continue to apply. These lemmas can therefore be used to analyze the sign of
\alpha_{1}
in each case, enabling a complete characterization of player 1’s best response strategy.

A.4.1
Case1:
\hrho\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}\iff p_{\mathrm{eq}}=1

Using the results from subsection
A.3.1
and the coefficients
A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},1)
and
B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},1)
given in Eqs.
64
,
69
, we distinguish three cases:

a.

If
\hrho_{2}\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}
then
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1
and:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=A_{N}(\hrho_{1},1)+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},1)=1+N(\hrho_{1}-1)\ ,

(91)

meaning that:

•

\alpha_{1}=0\iff\hrho_{1}=1-\frac{1}{N}
,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\iff\hrho_{1}>1-\frac{1}{N}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\iff\hrho_{1}<1-\frac{1}{N}
.

b.

If
\hrho_{2}\leq\frac{N-1}{N+1}
then
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=0
and:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=B_{N}(\hrho_{1},1)=\frac{N}{2(N-1)}[1-N+(1+N)\hrho_{1}]\ ,

(92)

meaning that:

•

\alpha_{1}=0\iff\hrho_{1}=\frac{N-1}{N+1}
,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\iff\hrho_{1}>\frac{N-1}{N+1}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\iff\hrho_{1}<\frac{N-1}{N+1}
.

c.

If
\hrho_{2}\in(\frac{N-1}{N+1},1-\frac{1}{N})
then
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=-B_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)/A_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)\in(0,1)
and:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\frac{A_{N}(\hrho_{1},1)B_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)-A_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)B_{N}(\hrho_{1},1)}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)|}=\frac{N}{2|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},1)|}(\hrho_{1}-\hrho_{2})\ ,

(93)

meaning that:

•

\alpha_{1}=0\iff\hrho_{1}=\hrho_{2}
,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\iff\hrho_{1}>\hrho_{2}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\iff\hrho_{1}<\hrho_{2}
.

A.4.2
Case2:
\hrho\leq\frac{1}{N}\iff p_{\mathrm{eq}}=0

Using the results from subsection
A.3.1
and the coefficients
A_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},0)
and
B_{N}(\hat{\rho}_{1},0)
given in Eqs.(
63
),(
68
), we distinguish three cases:

a.

If
\hrho_{2}\geq\frac{2}{N+1}
then
q_{\mathrm{eq}}=1
and:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=A_{N}(\hrho_{1},0)+B_{N}(\hrho_{1},0)=\frac{N[\hrho_{1}(N+1)-2]}{2(N-1)}\ ,

(94)

meaning that:

,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}>\frac{1}{N}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}<\frac{1}{N}
.

,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}>\hrho_{2}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}<\hrho_{2}
.

A.4.3
Case 3:

Using the results from subsection

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\frac{A_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})B_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})-A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})B_{N}(\hrho_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}})}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|}\ ,

(100)

\displaystyle\frac{(1-2N)p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N+1}+(N-1)p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N+2}+Np_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}+p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}\left((N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}+1\right)+p_{\mathrm{eq}}\left((1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}-1\right)-N(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{(N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{3}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}}(\hrho_{2}-\hrho_{1})

(102)

\displaystyle Z_{N+1}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})-Z_{N}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-N(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}[p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}]-N(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{3}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N+1}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N+1}(N+1)<0\ ,

(105)

proving that
Z_{N}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})<0
for all
N\geq 3
and
p_{\mathrm{eq}}\in(0,1)
. We can then rewrite the slope:

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=-\frac{|Z_{N}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})|}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|(N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{3}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}}(\hrho_{2}-\hrho_{1})=\frac{|Z_{N}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})|}{|A_{N}(\hrho_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}})|(N-1)(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{3}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{3}}(\hrho_{1}-\hrho_{2})\ ,

which finally allows us to conclude that:

•

\alpha_{1}=0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}=\hrho_{2}
,

•

\alpha_{1}>0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}>\hrho_{2}
,

•

\alpha_{1}<0\text{ if }\hrho_{1}<\hrho_{2}
.

Appendix B
Volatility averse players,
\lambda>0

In this Appendix, we consider the case of volatility-averse players who, instead of maximizing expected payoff alone, penalize uncertainty in their belief. In the main text, we denoted the payoff of a volatility-averse player as
\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i},R_{i};\lambda)
, and defined it as:

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i},R_{i};\lambda)\coloneqq\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho_{i})-\lambda\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\U_{i}(\vec{p},\rho_{i})]\ ,

(107)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a parameter that quantifies players’ aversion to volatility, and
Var_{\rho_{i}}[\cdot]
denotes the variance computed with respect to the subjective posterior distribution each player has for the coin bias. This posterior is a Beta distribution
\mathcal{B}(H_{i}+1,R_{i}-H_{i}+1)
, where
R_{i}
is the length of the string observed by player
i
, and
H_{i}
is the number of heads it contains. For computational convenience, we introduce an equivalent parametrization by defining
b_{i}\coloneqq H_{i}+1
and
c_{i}\coloneqq R_{i}+2
, so that the Beta distribution becomes
\mathcal{B}(b_{i},c_{i}-b_{i})
with first two moments:

\displaystyle\hrho_{i}=\frac{H_{i}+1}{R_{i}+2}=\frac{b_{i}}{c_{i}}\ ,

(108)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\rho_{i}}[\rho_{i}]=\frac{(H_{1}+1)(R_{i}-H_{i}+1)}{(R_{i}+2)^{2}(R_{i}+3)}=\frac{b_{i}(c_{i}-b_{i})}{c_{i}^{2}(c_{i}+1)}=\frac{\hrho_{i}(1-\hrho_{i})}{c_{i}+1}\ .

(109)

This alternative parametrization is fully equivalent to the one used in the main text, and is adopted here solely for notational convenience.

B.1
Calculation of
p_{\mathrm{eq}}

In this section, we determine the Nash equilibrium strategies for volatility averse players who all share the same information, namely the public string of past toss outcomes. The setting follows that of section
A.1
, with the key difference that players now exhibit volatility aversion, altering their payoff computations. As in section
A.1
, the setting is symmetric, so we focus without loss of generality on a generic player, denoted as player
i
. Specifically, the expected payoff for volatility-neutral players derived in section
A.1
is linear in
\hat{\rho}
(see Eq. (
35
)) and can thus be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho)=S(q_{i},p,N)\hat{\rho}+P(q_{i},p,N)\ ,

(110)

where the strategy profile is
\vec{p}=(p,\dots,p,q_{i},p,\dots,p)
, where
q_{i}
appears in the
i
-th position, and:

\displaystyle S(q_{i},p,N)\coloneqq\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{(1-p)p}(q_{i}-p)\ ,

(111)

\displaystyle P(q_{i},p,N)\coloneqq\frac{p^{N}-p}{(1-p)p}(q_{i}-p)\ .

(112)

For volatility-averse players, the payoff is adjusted by subtracting a penalty proportional to the variance of the posterior distribution, yielding (see Eq. (
13
) of the main text):

\displaystyle\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho,c;\lambda)

\displaystyle=S(q_{i},p,N)\hat{\rho}+P(q_{i},p,N)-\lambda\frac{\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})}{c+1}S^{2}(q_{i},p,N)

(113)

\displaystyle=Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)(q_{i}-p)^{2}+X(p,\hat{\rho},N)(q_{i}-p)\ ,

where,

\displaystyle Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)\coloneqq-\frac{\lambda}{c+1}\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})\left[\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{(1-p)p}\right]^{2}\ ,

(114)

\displaystyle X(p,\hat{\rho},N)\coloneqq\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{(1-p)p}\hat{\rho}+\frac{p^{N}-p}{(1-p)p}\ .

(115)

From Eq. (
113
), it is clear that the cases
\hat{\rho}\in\{0,1\}
are equivalent to
\lambda=0
, meaning the volatility aversion correction vanishes and the results of section
A.1
are recovered. For this reason, we restrict our analysis to the case
\hat{\rho}\in(0,1)
. It follows that the coefficient
Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)
in Eq. (
114
) is strictly negative, and the payoff in Eq. (
113
), as a function of
q
, is a concave parabola whose vertex corresponds to the best responses of the player:

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)=-\frac{X(p,\hat{\rho},N)}{2Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)}+p\ .

(116)

The condition for a symmetric Nash equilibrium is therefore

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p)=p\iff-\frac{X(p,\hat{\rho},N)}{2Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)}=0\iff X(p,\hat{\rho},N)=0\ .

(117)

This condition gives

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=\frac{p-p^{N}}{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}=K(p,N)\ ,

(118)

where
K(p,N)
is given in Eq. (
38
), implying that we recover the same symmetric Nash equilibrium found in section
A.1
for volatility neutral players (see Eq. (
42
)).
Since
K(p,N)
, for fixed
N
, lies within the interval
\left[\frac{1}{N},1-\frac{1}{N}\right]
, this equilibrium condition can only hold with
\hrho
within this range. To investigate potential Nash equilibria outside this interval, we examine the limiting behavior of Eq. (
113
) as
p\to 0
and
p\to 1
.

B.1.1
Limit
p\to 0

The limit for
p\to 0
of Eq. (
113
) yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0}\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho,c;\lambda)=Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)q_{i}^{2}+X(0,\hat{\rho},N)q_{i}=-\frac{\lambda}{c+1}\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})N^{2}q_{i}^{2}+(N\hat{\rho}-1)q_{i}\ ,

(119)

where:

\displaystyle Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p\to 0}Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)=-\frac{\lambda}{c+1}\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})N^{2}\ ,

(120)

\displaystyle X(0,\hat{\rho},N)\coloneqq\lim_{p\to 0}X(p,\hat{\rho},N)=(N\hat{\rho}-1)\ .

(121)

Since
Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)
is strictly negative, the payoff, as a function of
q
, is again a concave parabola whose vertex corresponds to the players’ best response.

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=0)=-\frac{X(0,\hat{\rho},N)}{2Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)}\ ,

(122)

In this case, for a symmetric Nash equilibrium, we should require that
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=0)=0
. However, because the parabola is concave and
q_{i}
must represent a probability, it is sufficient that the vertex lies to the left of the interval
[0,1]
. In fact, since the parabola is concave, if the vertex lies outside the interval
[0,1]
, the best response will be attained at the nearest boundary.

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=0)=0\iff-\frac{X(0,\hat{\rho},N)}{2Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)}\leq 0\iff\frac{X(0,\hat{\rho},N)}{2|Y(0,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)|}\leq 0\iff X(0,\hat{\rho},N)\leq 0\iff\hat{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{N}\ .

(123)

Again, we recover the results of section
A.1
, finding that if
\hat{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{N}
, the only possible equilibrium is achieved when all players bet on Tails with certainty.

B.1.2
Limit
p\to 1

The limit for
p\to 1
of Eq. (
113
) yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 1}\U_{i}(\vec{p},\hrho,c;\lambda)

\displaystyle=Y(1,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)(q-1)^{2}+X(1,\hat{\rho},N)(q-1)

(124)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{c+1}\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})N^{2}(q-1)^{2}+[N\hat{\rho}+(1-N)](q-1)\ ,

where:

\displaystyle Y(1,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)\coloneqq\lim_{p\to 1}Y(p,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)=-\frac{\lambda}{c+1}\hat{\rho}(1-\hat{\rho})N^{2}\ ,

(125)

\displaystyle X(1,\hat{\rho},N)\coloneqq\lim_{p\to 1}X(p,\hat{\rho},N)=N\hat{\rho}+(1-N)\ .

(126)

Since
Y(1,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)
is strictly negative, the payoff, as a function of
q
, is again a concave parabola whose vertex corresponds to the players’ best response.

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=1)=-\frac{X(1,\hat{\rho},N)}{2Y(1,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)}+1\ .

(127)

By the same reasoning of previous subsection, due to the payoff concavity, we will have a symmetric Nash equilibrium where all players always bet on Heads when
q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=1)\geq 1
:

\displaystyle q^{\text{br}}_{i}(p=1)=1\iff\frac{X(1,\hat{\rho},N)}{2|Y(1,\hat{\rho},c,\lambda,N)|}\geq 0\iff X(1,\hat{\rho},N)\geq 0\iff\hat{\rho}\geq 1-\frac{1}{N}\ .

(128)

We can thus conclude that in all cases, even in the presence of volatility aversion, the symmetric equilibrium strategy of the less-informed players remains the same as in the volatility-neutral case, namely
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
computed in section
A.1
.

B.2
Calculation of
(q_{1})_{\text{eq}}^{\text{pre}}

In this section, we compute the strategy that maximizes the seller’s payoff before any transaction occurs. The seller faces players who rely solely on the public dataset, so she knows their payoffs and strategies, which follow the equilibrium conditions derived in section
B.1
. The setting is the same as in section
A.2
, with the only difference being that the players are volatility averse.
In particular, in the volatility averse case, the functional form of player 1’s payoff remains the same as that of the uninformed players (given in Eq. (
113
)). The only difference from the uninformed players lies in the parameters used, reflecting that player 1 has a longer string and additional data. Therefore we can again write:

\displaystyle\U_{1}(\vec{p},\hrho_{1},c_{1};\lambda)=Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)(q_{1}-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{2}+X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)(q_{1}-p_{\mathrm{eq}})\ ,

(129)

where the strategy profile is
\vec{p}=(q_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}},\dots,p_{\mathrm{eq}})
and
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
denotes the symmetric equilibrium strategy of the less informed players, as derived in section
B.1
, which is known to P1. The coefficients take the following geneform:

\displaystyle Y(p,\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)=-\frac{\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\hat{\rho}_{1}(1-\hat{\rho}_{1})\left[\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{(1-p)p}\right]^{2}\ ,

(130)

\displaystyle X(p,\hat{\rho}_{1},N)=\frac{1-p^{N}-(1-p)^{N}}{(1-p)p}\hat{\rho}_{1}+\frac{p^{N}-p}{(1-p)p}\ .

(131)

Note that the functional forms of
Y
and
X
are the same as those appearing in the analysis of less informed players in section
B.1
, with the only difference being the use of player 1’s personalized parameters
(\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1})
.
Again, we restrict our analysis to the case
\hat{\rho}_{1}\in(0,1)
, since at the boundaries the volatility correction vanishes, recovering the results from section
A.2
. Therefore
Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)
is strictly negative and the payoff remains a concave parabola in
q_{1}
. Consequently, player 1’s optimal strategy corresponds either to the vertex of this parabola or to one of the boundaries of the interval
[0,1]
if the vertex falls outside it:

\displaystyle q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})=\min\{\max\{0,-\frac{X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)}{2Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)}+p_{\mathrm{eq}}\},1\}\ .

(132)

We solve this equation by distinguishing three cases, based on whether the remaining
N-1
players are playing a pure or mixed strategy.

B.2.1
\hrho\in(\frac{1}{N},1-\frac{1}{N})\iff p_{\mathrm{eq}}\in(0,1)

In this case, we first recall that the symmetric equilibrium condition for the uninformed players is given by Eq. (
118
):

\displaystyle K(p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}=\hat{\rho}\ ,

(133)

that can be solved numerically to find their equilibrium strategy
p_{\mathrm{eq}}
. We now examine the conditions under which player 1’s best response is a mixed strategy, i.e.
q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})
lies in
(0,1)
. We have

\displaystyle q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})>0

\displaystyle\iff X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)+2p|Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)|>0\iff X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)>-2p|Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)|

(134)

\displaystyle\iff\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}{(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})p_{\mathrm{eq}}}\hat{\rho}_{1}+\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}}{(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})p_{\mathrm{eq}}}>-\frac{2p\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\hat{\rho}_{1}(1-\hat{\rho}_{1})\left[\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}{(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})p_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\iff\hat{\rho}_{1}-\hat{\rho}>-\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\hat{\rho}_{1}(1-\hat{\rho}_{1})\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}-(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}}{(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}

\displaystyle\iff\hat{\rho}_{1}-\hat{\rho}>\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{\hat{\rho}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}-\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{\hat{\rho}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\hat{\rho}_{1}

\displaystyle\iff\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{\hat{\rho}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}-\left[1+\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{\hat{\rho}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}\right]\hat{\rho}_{1}+\hat{\rho}<0

\displaystyle\iff a\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}-\left[1+a\right]\hat{\rho}_{1}+\hat{\rho}<0\ ,

where we have used Eq. (
133
) and, for clarity of exposition, we have defined the auxiliary parameter

\displaystyle a\coloneqq\frac{2\lambda}{c_{1}+1}\frac{p_{\mathrm{eq}}-p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}}{\hat{\rho}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})}>0\ .

(135)

Since
a>0
, condition Eq. (
134
) is satisfied when
\hat{\rho}_{1}
lies between the two roots of the convex parabola
a\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}-\left[1+a\right]\hat{\rho}_{1}+\hat{\rho}
, which are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{1}=\frac{1+a\pm\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}}{2a}=\frac{1+a\pm\sqrt{1+a^{2}+2a-4a\hat{\rho}}}{2a}\ .

(136)

It is straightforward to prove that the larger root is greater than one

\displaystyle\frac{1+a+\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}}{2a}

\displaystyle>1\iff\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}>a-1\ .

(137)

This last expression always holds since

\displaystyle a<1\Rightarrow\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}>0>a-1

(138)

\displaystyle a\geq 1\Rightarrow\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}>a-1\iff 1+a^{2}+2a-4a\hat{\rho}>a^{2}+1-2a\iff\hat{\rho}<1\quad.

We have thus proved that:

\displaystyle q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})>0\iff\hat{\rho}_{1}\in\left(\frac{1+a-\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}}{2a},1\right]\ ,

(139)

\displaystyle q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})=0\iff\hat{\rho}_{1}\in\left[0,\frac{1+a-\sqrt{(1+a)^{2}-4a\hat{\rho}}}{2a}\right]\ .

(140)

In a similar fashion, we can now characterize the condition under which
q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})<1
:

\displaystyle q_{1}^{br}(p_{\mathrm{eq}})<1

\displaystyle\iff\frac{X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)+2p|Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)|}{2|Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)|}<1\iff X(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},N)<2|Y(p_{\mathrm{eq}},\hat{\rho}_{1},c_{1},\lambda,N)|(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})

(141)

In this section, we compute the symmetric Nash equilibrium that emerges between the seller and the buyer, assuming the transaction takes place. The setup is the same as in section

\displaystyle=S^{(2)}(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\hat{\rho}_{2}+P^{(2)}(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)-\lambda\frac{\hat{\rho}_{2}(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})}{c_{2}+1}[S^{(2)}(q_{1},q_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)]^{2}

\displaystyle Y^{(2)}(q_{1},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},\lambda,c_{2},N)\coloneqq-\dfrac{\lambda(1-\hat{\rho}_{2})\hat{\rho}_{2}}{(1+c_{2})(N-1)^{2}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{4}p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{4}}\gamma^{2}(q_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\ ,

\displaystyle\gamma(q_{1},p_{\mathrm{eq}},N)\coloneqq(-1+(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}+p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N})(p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+q_{1}-2p_{\mathrm{eq}}q_{1})+N(p_{\mathrm{eq}}(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{eq}})^{N}(-1+q_{1}))+(-1+p_{\mathrm{eq}})p_{\mathrm{eq}}^{N}q_{1})\ .

\displaystyle\lim_{p_{\mathrm{eq}}\to 0}Y^{(2)}(q_{1},\hat{\rho}_{2},p_{\mathrm{eq}},\lambda,c_{2},N)=-\frac{\lambda N^{2}(1-\hrho_{2})\hrho_{2}}{4(1+c_{2})(N-1)^{2}}(N(q_{1}-2)-3q_{1}+2)^{2}\ ,

Appendix C
Information hierarchies

In this section, we seek to give a rigorous definition of the hierarchical information structure we encounter in our Bayesian game. In our case, player one (P1) knows the values of
\hrho
used by each other player. After the transaction, player 2 (P2) knows the value of
\hrho
used by everyone except for P1, and the other players are unaware of the values of the existence of P1 and P2. To model this, we first recall the basics of representing players’ beliefs in Bayesian games.

To give a complete specification of a player’s knowledge, one must specify the player’s belief about
\rho
, the player’s belief about the other players’ beliefs, the player’s belief about the
others’ beliefs about the others’ beliefs, and so on. This forms an infinite hierarchy which we aim to represent more compactly.
In particular, we wish to do so in the case of a hierarchical (or nested) information structure, as introduced in
Jacobovic et al. 2024
.
5
5

5

The hierarchy of beliefs should not be confused with the hierarchical information structure. The first represents what each layer believes, what each player believes other players believe,… This is a common feature of all Bayesian games. The second one is just a way to say that players can be ordered from most to least knowledgeable, with the first player having the most complete knowledge.

Consider a game with
N
players. In a setting with hierarchical information, one can order players from most to least knowledgeable (with possibly several players with the same level of knowledge). We suppose that if
i<j
, then player
i
knows everything player
j
knows, plus potentially something else. This implies that player
1
is the most knowledgeable, while player
N
, the least. In addition, we suppose that player
i
believes that all players
j
with
j<i
(so more knowledgeable than
i
), hold the same information as him. For example, P10 believes that players P1 through P9 are all identical to himself.
To model this structure we first assign to each player
i
a belief
\hrho_{i}
.
At this point we must find a way to collapse the infinite hierarchy of beliefs based on the hierarchical information. We define the
r-
th order belief tensors
T^{(r)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\overbrace{N\times N\times\dots\times N}^{r\text{ times}}}
as

i_{1}\text{ believes }i_{2}\text{ believes}\dots\;i_{r-1}\text{ believes }i_{r}\text{ believes }T^{(r)}_{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{r}}

(192)

Let’s see a few examples:

•

T^{(1)}_{i}=\hrho_{i}
, since
\hrho_{i}
is the belief of player
i
.

•

T^{(1)}_{ij}
is the
\hrho
that player
i
believes player
j
believes. Taking the hierarchy of information into account we have
T^{(1)}_{i}j=\hrho_{\max(i,j)}
.

The sequence of tensors
\{T^{(r)}\}_{r\in\N}
uniquely specifies the whole belief hierarchy. We will now show how to collapse it making use of the hierarchical information assumption.
Consider the index sequence
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
and suppose we want to know the value of
T^{(r)}_{I}
. Then the hierarchical information assumption implies that

T^{(r)}_{I}=\hrho_{\max_{a=1\dots,r}I_{a}}

(193)

In words, the belief is determined by the most ignorant player in the sequence.
We can show this equality using the principle that player
i
believes player
j
has the same information as him, whenever
j<i
(
j
is more knowledgeable than
i
).
Consider for example the sequence
I=(3,1,2)
; in this case we’re looking at the beliefs of player
3
about player
1
, but since
3
perceives
1
as a copy of himself, the result is equivalent to that with the sequence
I^{\prime}=(3,3,2)
, which in turn is equivalent to what one obtains with
I^{\prime\prime}=(3,3,3)
(by the same principle).
It is then clear that given a generic sequence
I
, one can transform it into an equivalent sequence
I^{\prime}
(in the sense that
T^{(r)}_{I}=T^{(r)}_{I^{\prime}}
), where
I^{\prime}_{a}=\max(I_{a},I^{\prime}_{a-1})
. Iterating over
a=1,\dots,r
this gives the result in Eq. (
193
).
To conclude, we have seen that under the assumption of hierarchical information, the belief hierarchy is specified by the ordering of the players, plus the ’first-order’ beliefs of each player (i.e.,
(\hrho_{1},\dots,\hrho_{N})
).
