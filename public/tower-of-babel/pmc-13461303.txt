Effect of Regional COVID‐19 Lockdowns on Alcohol Use and Smoking in Two Chilean University Communities: A Difference‐In‐Difference Analysis
Authors: Sebastián Peña, Daniela Palet, Camila Salazar‐Fernández, Francisca Román Mella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEffects of lockdowns on alcohol use and smoking remain unclear. By exploiting the variation in regional COVID‐19 lockdowns in two Chilean regions, we examined their effect on alcohol use and smoking in two university communities.MethodsWe conducted a quasi‐experimental study in university communities in two Chilean regions, Coquimbo and Araucanía. Participants were a longitudinal cohort (n = 896) assessed seven times from July 2020 (t1) to April 2021 (t7). Outcomes were weekly grams of alcohol use, heavy episodic drinking (HED) and cigarettes smoked per day.ResultsLockdown between t1 and t3 was associated with an increase of 15.6 g of alcohol used per week (95% confidence interval 1.8; 29.3) and an increase of 0.3 daily cigarettes smoked (95% confidence interval 0.003; 0.5) at t3 (September 2020), compared to the absence of lockdown. When lockdown reversed at t5, we found weak evidence of a reduction in alcohol use and smoking in the region under lockdown. We did not observe changes in HED during the study period.Discussion and ConclusionsCOVID‐19 lockdown appears to result in an initial relative increase in weekly volume of alcohol use and cigarette smoking compared to a region affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic but without lockdown. We did not find evidence of changes in HED.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier NCT05069090

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic led to substantial societal changes in most high and middle‐income countries, including changes in alcohol and tobacco use [1]. Governments introduced social distancing policies to restrict people's mobility and reduce the risk of COVID‐19. These ranged from stay‐at‐home advice and remote‐work guidance to statutory closures of schools and universities, bars and restaurants, and restrictions for public gatherings [2, 3]. The most restrictive policies included lockdowns (with various levels of stringency) and curfews. Such policies disrupted routines and reshaped social interactions, as well as acting as an acute stressful event, which could have, in turn, affected alcohol use and smoking [4].

Lockdowns, especially those including statutory restrictions for people's mobility, had potentially the largest potential effect on alcohol use and smoking. Previous studies on the impact of COVID‐19 lockdowns on alcohol use have reported a mixed picture, with some studies reporting an increase in high‐risk drinking (either volume or heavy episodic drinking) [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], volume of alcohol use [10] and frequency of alcohol use [11], whereas other studies have reported reductions in alcohol use [12, 13]. Mixed changes in smoking have also been reported, with a relatively equal proportion of respondents describing an increase or reduction in their smoking [5, 8, 14, 15, 16, 17].

A common limitation of such studies is that they compare a pre‐pandemic period with a lockdown period and are thus not able to disentangle the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic with those of lockdowns. Two repeated cross‐sectional studies have examined lockdowns effects. One study compared the effect of easing restrictions and re‐introducing them in the UK [18] and another has compared populations in lockdown versus those without in different periods of time in the US [19]. However, changes over time might be due to changes in the composition of sample participants (as both are based on convenience samples) or calendar effects. Longitudinal studies measure change at the individual level and can potentially provide more robust estimations of lockdown effects.

Chile introduced subnational lockdowns to reduce the viral spread and prevent health system overburdening. By exploiting the variation in the timing of lockdowns in two regions in Chile, we were able to disentangle the effects of the lockdown with those of the COVID‐19 pandemic in the absence of a lockdown. We also provide evidence from a country in Latin America, one of the worst‐hit regions with the pandemic worldwide. Latin America accounted for 8.4% of the global population, but 24.9% of the total COVID‐19 cases and 43.1% of COVID‐19 deaths [20].

We conducted a longitudinal study to examine the effect of regional lockdowns on alcohol use and smoking in two university communities in Chile.

Methods

We registered the study in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05069090). We reported results in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [21]. We made minor changes to the analyses compared to the pre‐registration protocol: (i) we excluded from the analyses participants living in other regions than Coquimbo and Araucanía; (ii) we modelled the presence or absence of lockdown as a binary variable instead of an intensity variable (see Table S1 for the five‐restriction steps) to simplify the analyses and reporting of the results; and (iii) we did not carry out sensitivity analyses using Callaway‐Sant'Anna's method due to being computationally intensive and not relevant for the study design. More details can be found in the Supporting Information.

Study Design

The study was a quasi‐experimental study of COVID‐19 policies in two Chilean regions.

Participants

The study population was university communities from two regions in Chile (Coquimbo, northern Chile and Araucanía, southern Chile). Both regions are located far from the capital city and less affected by migration flows, whereas both universities had a similar multidimensional profile involving teaching, research and community engagement, as well as a large proportion of students residing in the respective region. Eligibility criteria included: (i) those with study rights either at undergraduate or postgraduate levels by 27 July 2020; or (ii) workers with a full‐time or part‐time contract with the University, including academics and assistant personnel; (iii) with an email registered in the Human Resource Office of each university; and (iv) residing in either Coquimbo or Araucanía during the baseline assessment.

Data Sources

We collected the data using an online survey sent by email to all eligible participants. The first round of data collection included questions regarding the period before the COVID‐19 pandemic (pre‐pandemic baseline, t0) and during the pandemic (t1). Data were collected during seven rounds: from 27 July to 13 August 2020 (t0 and t1), 15 to 25 August 2020 (t2), 7 to 22 September 2020 (t3), 5 to 16 October 2020 (t4), 9 to 20 November 2020 (t5), 9 to 21 December 2020 (t6) and 5 to 23 April 2021 (t7). In all survey rounds, we used an online survey tool (QuestionPro) which creates a database automatically. We did not allow participants to leave questions blank but participants could stop answering at any moment. To ensure participation, we sent a reminder every 3 days: twice via email and then twice via text message to those who had provided a mobile phone number. No incentive was provided in exchange for their participation in any of the survey rounds.

Ethical Approval

The Scientific Ethics Committee from the Universidad de La Frontera in La Araucanía (decision 086/20) and Universidad Católica del Norte in Coquimbo (decision 011/2020) approved the study. We obtained electronic written consent from all participants. To avoid stigmatisation, we hereafter refer to the Coquimbo region university and Araucanía region university.

Intervention

We examined the effect of regional lockdowns in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic. During the study period, the Chilean Ministry of Health established a five‐step process for social distancing measures depending on the number of COVID‐19 cases and saturation of hospital services. These ranged from full lockdown (Step 1) to almost no restrictions (Step 5) (Table S1 for details). Even under full lockdown, citizens were able to obtain up to two permits per week for essential activities. Table S2 describes the different types of permits people were allowed to use. It must be noted that, due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, the Chilean government issued a nationwide curfew with a total prohibition to leave home between 22.00 and 05.00 for the whole country from 22 March 2020 throughout the study period. Figure 1 describes the stages of COVID‐19 mobility restrictions over time in both regions at the time of each data collection period.

Social distancing policies in Coquimbo and Araucanía for the study period. There was no Step 5 in the study period.

Comparator

The comparator was the absence of lockdown at a given time. During most of the study period, the whole country had in place restrictions for public gatherings, school closures and advice for remote work. Both universities instructed remote work from 16 March 2020, and teaching was done remotely throughout the study period.

Outcomes

We examined three primary outcomes: weekly grams of alcohol use, heavy episodic drinking (HED) and the number of cigarettes smoked per day. Weekly grams of alcohol use was measured at baseline with a question of how many drinks of beer (in portions of 330 cc), wine and similar (chicha and pipeño, two types of unfiltered wines, in portions of 140 cc) and spirits (pisco, rum, whisky, tequila, vodka, gin or other strong liquors, portions not specified) in a typical week. These questions were accompanied with pictures of reference portions. We asked the same questions for the pre‐pandemic period and the during‐the‐pandemic period. The follow‐up on times 2–6 included a similar question but for each day of the week (‘how many drinks did you have each day of the week’).

We converted the number of drinks into grams assuming 15.52 g per standard drink, based on the results of the Chilean National Health Survey 2009–2011. This amount of ethanol included a correction for the underreporting of the size of each standard drink unit in the Chilean population [22]. This data was available for all survey rounds. Data collected at times 0, 1, 5 and 6 included separate questions for beer, wine and spirits.

We measured HED with a question on how often the respondent drank 5 or more drinks on a single occasion (considering a 1‐month period). The options were never, once, twice, 3–5 times, 6–9 times and 10 or more. We converted this information into a continuous variable. We transformed the options ‘3–5’ and ‘6–9’ times to their arithmetic midpoint and the ‘10 or more’ option into the lower boundary (i.e., 10). This data was collected at times 0, 1, 4, 5 and 6.

We measured the number of cigarettes smoked per day with a question on how many cigarettes the participant smoked daily on average during the past week. We transformed this information into a continuous variable. This data were available for all survey rounds.

Covariates

We adjusted for covariates that are not influenced by the intervention but correlate with the outcome to increase the statistical power [23]. We adjusted for gender (male, female, other and I'd rather not say), education (complete secondary education, incomplete undergraduate education, complete undergraduate education and postgraduate education degree), living arrangements (living alone or not), role at the university (student, service or administration personnel, and academic or leadership position) and existing medical conditions. Existing medical conditions were categorised into a dichotomous variable on whether participants reported ever being diagnosed by a doctor with: high blood pressure; diabetes; cardiac problems (cardiac insufficiency, myocardial infarction or others); respiratory conditions (asthma, respiratory insufficiency); depression; anxiety; other mental health problem; cancer (any malignancy including leukaemia or lymphoma; or fibromyalgia).

Statistical Analysis

We exploited the variation in regional lockdown policies in the two regions to measure the effect of lockdown on alcohol use, HED and smoking. For this purpose, we used a difference‐in‐difference (DiD) design to compare the effect of lockdown in multiple treatment periods. DiD is a quasi‐experimental approach that allows using longitudinal data to obtain causal effects in the absence of randomisation [24]. DiD is a well‐established method in public health research and was first used by John Snow in his seminal study on the transmission of cholera in 1855 [25].

The canonical DiD consists of two time periods and two groups. One computes the difference in the mean outcomes of the treatment and control groups after the treatment and subtracts the difference that existed before treatment had any effect [26]. (1) δDD=Y−posttreat−Y−pretreat−Y−postcontrol−Y−precontrol

As depicted in Figure 1, our case is more complex, as there were multiple time periods (seven in total) and two groups that were both treated and not‐treated in different time periods. For τ periods and a particular time period denoted by t where t = 1, …, τ, t0 represented the pre‐pandemic baseline measurement captured retrospectively at t1. In time periods t1, t2 and t3, the Coquimbo group was exposed to lockdown while the Araucanía group was exposed to less restrictive social distancing policies. At t4 both groups were exposed to Step 2 social distancing policies and, at t5 and t6, this situation reversed, Araucanía was exposed to lockdown and Coquimbo moved to less restrictive social policies (see Table S1 for details). At t7, both regions were in lockdown.

In this setting, we used a generalised difference‐in‐difference that allows for multiple treatment periods [27, 28]. The regression of a DiD model can be expressed in more general terms as follows: (2) Yist=α+βiRegion+γtTime+δDDRegion*Time+eist where Yist is the outcome for an individual s at time t and i is the region (Coquimbo or Araucanía), βi is a region fixed effect, γt is a set of time fixed effects, δDD is the DiD estimate and represents the effect at a given time compared to baseline t0, and Xist is a vector of control variables (e.g., age, sex). We considered La Araucanía as the control group from t1 and t4 and Coquimbo as the control group from t5 to t7. All DiD estimates consider the pre‐pandemic period t0 as the baseline reference.

The identifying assumption, in this case, relies on a common trend assumption that both treatment groups would develop equally over time under at least one of the treatments. It assumes effect homogeneity conditional on observables Xist. More details and a practical example on this method can be found in the Supporting Information.

We used linear mixed‐effects models that consider the correlated nature of the data (repeated measures nested within individuals) and provide mathematically equivalent estimates to fixed‐effects linear regression. An additional advantage of linear mixed‐effects models is that they use full information maximum likelihood, which allows varying the number of observations within each participant, handling missing data more efficiently than other analytical approaches [29]. Following our peers, we conducted additional analyses modelling daily smoking as count data, for which we used multilevel negative binomial models.

We used the lme4 and glmmTMB functions to run the DiD model and R version 4.5.2 for all analyses. The statistical code can be found in the Supporting Information.

Results

A total of 1038 persons participated in the study (5.4% of the total target population). After excluding 142 participants living in different regions, we analysed 896 participants with at least one measurement point. More than 40% of participants were lost to follow‐up after the baseline measurement; participation remained relatively stable after that (Figures 2 and S2).

Participants from both universities were primarily students (62.7% overall), followed by academics and managers in Araucanía and service and administrative personnel in Coquimbo (Table 1). The majority were females in both groups. The age distribution and the proportion living alone were similar among both study groups. Araucanía participants had a larger proportion of participants with postgraduate education. Participants from Araucanía reported more often having a chronic disease than Coquimbo participants.

For heavy episodic drinking, we did not find evidence of relevant changes either at t1 when Coquimbo was exposed to lockdown nor at t5 or t6 when Araucanía was exposed to lockdown.

For smoking, we observed an increase in smoking as a result of lockdown in the Coquimbo region between t1 and t3, which persisted after easing restrictions in Coquimbo and tightening restrictions in Araucanía at t4. When the exposure contrast is reversed at t5 and Araucanía is exposed to lockdown, our results are weakly compatible with a smaller reduction of smoking in Araucanía, but confidence intervals are compatible with a large range of associations. Additional analyses modelling smoking as count data were consistent with the primary results (Table S3).

Sensitivity analyses running DiD models without adjusting for covariates were consistent with the main analyses (Table S4).

Discussion

We examined the effect of regional lockdowns on alcohol use and smoking in two university communities in Araucanía and Coquimbo regions in Chile. Our results were moderately informative that the lockdown in Coquimbo Region in times t1 to t3 was associated with a higher increase in weekly alcohol use and smoking at times t1 to t3 compared to participants from Araucanía Region, whereas we did not find evidence of changes in HED. The increases in weekly alcohol use and smoking persisted even after mobility restrictions were relaxed at t4. When the exposure reversed and Araucanía was exposed to lockdown at t4, our results are weakly compatible with a reduction in alcohol use and smoking in Araucanía as a result of the lockdown. Given that assumptions to establish causality cannot be tested with our data and the varying effective implementation of the mobility restrictions, a causal interpretation of our findings should be taken cautiously.

We found that weekly alcohol use and HED initially decreased compared to the pre‐pandemic period in both regions. Over time, alcohol use increased, but HED remained stable and lower than the pre‐pandemic period in both groups. Our results are consistent with several studies reporting reductions in alcohol use and HED during the early months of the COVID‐19 pandemic [30, 31, 32, 33]. An increase after an initial reduction has also been described by longitudinal studies in the Netherlands, Belgium, France and Australia [31, 34, 35, 36].

We observed an overall reduction in smoking in both groups, with some fluctuations over time. This is consistent with previous research showing reductions in smoking prevalence and mean number of cigarettes smoked in adult populations [8, 37] and adolescents and young adults [38, 39].

These findings of changes in alcohol use and smoking can be explained in the Chilean context by the implementation of a nationwide curfew and the introduction of mobility restrictions affecting the whole Chilean territory, including restrictions in public gatherings, closure of educational institutions and orders for remote work [40]. These resulted in disruptions of social gatherings, where alcohol is primarily consumed in Chile, especially among younger populations, thus reducing the opportunity to consume alcohol. Mobility restrictions progressively lost adherence in the second half of 2020 and especially after the introduction of COVID‐19 vaccinations in late‐December 2020. These changes, together with seasonal effects (i.e., early summer and Christmas holidays), might explain the uptick in alcohol use. In addition, the COVID‐19 pandemic caused the largest contraction in GDP since 1982 (5.8% in 2020) [41], likely hampering the affordability of alcohol and tobacco in the longer term, which might partially explain why levels of HED and smoking remained lower than pre‐pandemic levels.

Our difference‐in‐difference analysis suggests that exposure to lockdown earlier during the COVID‐19 pandemic (i.e., between August and September 2020) resulted in an increase in alcohol use and smoking compared to those not exposed to lockdown during the same time period. The effect on volume of alcohol use became clearer at t3, suggesting a lagged behavioural response. Comparison with previous studies was limited as, to our knowledge, few studies had been able to separate the effects of lockdown from those of the COVID‐19 pandemic. Our findings were in line with a US study showing a sharp increase in mean Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test scores from June to September 2020 among those exposed to lockdown but not those among lockdown [19]. Another study in Australia suggested that those in lockdown had lower alcohol use than those not in lockdown. However, baseline values for the region under lockdown (Victoria) were not shown and therefore it is not possible to unveil a lockdown effect [36]. A UK study did not find evidence of changes after either introducing a lockdown or easing restrictions on mean units per week, the proportion of individuals drinking more than 14 units per week, weekly heavy drinking occasions or drinking days [18]. We were not aware of similar studies examining the effect of lockdown on smoking.

When lockdowns were reversed at t5, our results are weakly informative of a reduction of alcohol use and smoking in Araucanía at t5 and t6. Although this may sound paradoxical, it should be noted that Chilean studies, using data from highway traffic or mobile phone records, have shown that lockdown effects on mobility weaken their impact over time [42, 43]. We could hypothesise that the initial lockdown resulted in more persistent behavioural changes, which were not totally offset by the effect of the reversed lockdown.

Strengths of our study included its longitudinal nature with an extended follow‐up of seven rounds of data collection and the use of a comparable questionnaire. We adjusted for several confounders, improving the efficiency of our difference‐in‐difference estimates. Some limitations, however, are noted. First, we collected baseline data retrospectively. Our pre‐pandemic baseline estimates are, therefore, subject to memory bias. In addition, the lockdown experienced by the Araucanía Region in April 2020 before data collection could have differentially affected the recall of pre‐pandemic behaviours. Second, our study is at high risk of selection bias both due to low study participation and attrition during follow‐up. Selection bias limits the external validity of our results; as our participants are primarily young, female, who do not live alone, our results might only generalise to similar population groups in university communities in Latin America and elsewhere. However, we believe this bias has a more limited impact on the internal validity, as we used full information maximum likelihood to account for missing data due to attrition and the DiD design and adjusting for covariates ameliorates the effect of between‐group differences. Third, we contacted participants using the institutional email, which might have prompted social desirability bias. This could have influenced the results if this bias differed by university community, which we believe is not the case. Fourth, portions were not specified for measuring spirits consumption, which might have resulted in some measurement error. However, the questionnaire includes a picture of the reference portion, and hence we consider this error likely to be small.

Our study has public health implications for this and future pandemics. Lockdowns are effective in reducing viral spread, yet the unintended consequences on alcohol use and smoking warrant a more comprehensive policy design. Specifically, our results call for policy measures that reduce the alcohol and tobacco availability and marketing during lockdowns. Policies might exclude alcohol and tobacco as essential items [44], introduce restrictions on temporal availability of on and off‐trade alcohol outlets, regulate the home delivery of alcohol, and strengthen restrictions on alcohol and tobacco marketing in social media.

Conclusions

Regional lockdowns were associated with an initial relative increase in the weekly volume of alcohol use and cigarette smoking compared to a region affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic but without a lockdown. We did not find evidence of changes in HED. Future research should expand these analyses to other settings and populations and explore further effects of lockdowns on other outcomes, including illicit drug use, gambling, and the consumption of unhealthy food.
