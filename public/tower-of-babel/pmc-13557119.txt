Multiomic single-nucleus profiling reveals cell-type-specific epigenetic and transcriptional dysregulation in major depressive disorder brain
Authors: Aleena Francis, Yogesh Dwivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading global cause of disability, marked by persistent mood disturbances, cognitive deficits, and changes in prefrontal cortex neural circuitry. In this study, we aimed to define cell-type-specific molecular and regulatory mechanisms underlying MDD by mapping gene-expression and chromatin-accessibility changes in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) (dlPFC).MethodsPostmortem dlPFC (BA9) tissue from 7 MDD and 8 well-matched controls was analyzed using 10× Genomics snRNA-seq and paired ATAC+RNA multiome sequencing. Sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger pipelines, nuclei were filtered for quality and doublets/debris, and datasets were integrated and clustered using Seurat/Signac packages. Differential gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and transcription factor motif activity were tested between MDD and controls within each cell type, followed by peak-to-gene linkage and Gene Ontology (GO)/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and PsyGeNET enrichment to interpret dysregulated regulatory mechanisms.ResultsA total of 20 distinct clusters encompassing major neuronal and non-neuronal populations were identified. Differential analyses uncovered extensive cell type-specific changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression, particularly within excitatory layer 5/6 and inhibitory Pvalb neurons, as well as glial and vascular populations. Functional enrichment indicated dysregulation of synaptic organization, neurotransmission, myelination, stress-response, and immune-regulatory pathways across neuronal and non-neuronal cells. Notably, glucocorticoid-responsive transcription factors NR3C1/NR3C2 exhibited conserved regulatory networks implicating stress signaling in MDD pathophysiology.ConclusionsTogether, these findings provide a comprehensive single-nucleus atlas of gene regulation in the MDD PFC, highlighting coordinated dysfunction across neurons, glia, and vascular cells.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a severe psychiatric illness with a lifetime risk estimated between 10% and 20% in the general population, affecting approximately 300 million people worldwide and serving as a major cause of disability globally.1 MDD features ongoing sadness, hopelessness, loss of interest, cognitive issues, and symptoms like fatigue, sleep problems, and appetite changes, creating significant social and medical challenges. The condition can have severe implications, including a heightened risk of suicide, and affected individuals may also exhibit vulnerability to alexithymia.2,3 Notably, psychosocial stressors, including those observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, may further exacerbate fatigue, sleep disturbances, and overall symptom burden.4 Therefore, understanding the pathophysiology of MDD at the molecular level is crucial for developing better treatment options, as traditional therapies often fail to address its complex and heterogeneous nature.5

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) has consistently been identified as a brain region significantly affected in MDD, showing both structural and functional changes.6–8 Functional imaging often demonstrates prefrontal lobe hypometabolism in MDD patients, with the severity of depression frequently correlating with the degree of frontal inactivity.9,10 Specifically, MDD is associated with reduced gray matter volume and cortical thinning in various cortical areas.11 These structural changes may affect the functional connection between fronto-cingulate regions, contributing significantly to the symptoms of clinical depression by affecting emotion regulation and cognitive control. In this context, the brain’s intricate cellular composition, especially within the prefrontal cortex, presents a significant challenge for understanding complex psychiatric conditions such as MDD. It is now well established that gene expression changes, often regulated by epigenetic modifications, can greatly influence an individual’s lifetime susceptibility or resilience to depression.12 Both epigenetic and gene expression patterns differ greatly across cell types. This heterogeneity complicates interpreting differential gene expression patterns from bulk tissue, as observed molecular differences may result from cellular composition variations, not disease state. Understanding the specific roles and dysregulations of individual cell types is paramount for elucidating the etiopathogenesis of MDD and developing targeted therapeutic interventions.13

The advent of single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) has revolutionized the study of cellular heterogeneity, offering an unprecedented opportunity to dissect gene expression profiles at the individual cell level within heterogeneous brain tissues. This high-throughput technology allows researchers to overcome the limitations of traditional bulk tissue analysis by capturing transcriptomic information from tens of thousands of individual nuclei, revealing previously undetectable changes in specific cell types. Complementing this, single-nucleus Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (snATAC-seq) provides insights into chromatin accessibility, a key indicator of active epigenetic regulation at single-cell resolution. By identifying regions of open and closed chromatin, snATAC-seq can help reveal cell-type-specific gene regulatory mechanisms that contribute to MDD predisposition and pathogenesis, providing a comprehensive view of how genetic variations influence gene expression in a cell-type-specific manner.14

In this study, we investigated whether MDD is characterized by cell-type-specific transcriptional and epigenetic dysregulation in the dlPFC driven by altered chromatin accessibility and coordinated regulatory networks. To address this, we integrated single-nucleus RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets from the dlPFC of a modest but demographically and clinically well-matched cohort of non-psychiatric control and MDD subjects. This integrative multiomics approach uncovers key gene regulatory networks and transcriptional programs altered in MDD, providing critical insights into the chromatin regulation and gene expression dynamics underlying the disorder and offering a framework for developing precise molecular targets and more effective therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Human Postmortem Brain Samples

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Samples from dlPFC (Brodmann area [BA] 9) were obtained from the Maryland Brain Collection at the Maryland Psychiatric Research Center, Baltimore. The cohort comprised 15 individuals, including 7 MDD and 8 nonpsychiatric controls (hereafter referred to as controls). The psychiatric diagnosis was determined by psychological autopsy as described in the Supplemental section. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohorts are presented in Table S1. The subjects in the two groups were well-matched with respect to age, sex, race, postmortem interval (PMI), and brain pH. None of the subjects had features of Alzheimer’s disease, infarctions, demyelinating diseases, or atrophy (or clinical history of these disorders). Also, the subjects had negative antidepressant toxicity, alcohol, or substance abuse. Additionally, the subjects were not taking antidepressants for at least 2 months before death or had a history of alcohol or substance use.

Single-Nucleus RNA Sequencing

snRNASeq was conducted by Singulomics Corporation, USA In summary, frozen human dlPFC tissues were homogenized and lysed with Triton X-100 in RNase-free water for nuclei isolation, which were purified, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS with BSA and RNase Inhibitor. The nuclei isolated were loaded into the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller to encapsulate single nuclei into droplet emulsions following the manufacturer’s recommendations (10× Genomics, CA). Library preparation was performed according to Chromium Next GEM 3’ Single Cell Reagent kits v3.1. Amplified cDNAs and the libraries were measured by Qubit dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quality assessed by TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, USA). Libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq (Illumina, USA), and reads were subsequently processed using the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger analytical pipeline and the human reference genome GRCh38 (2020-A) with introns included in the analysis. Dataset aggregation was performed using the cell ranger aggr function, normalizing for the total number of confidently mapped reads across libraries.

Multiome Analysis

Single nuclei gene expression libraries and single nuclei ATAC libraries were constructed from nuclei isolated from dlPFC samples on the 10× Genomics Chromium System using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression Reagent Bundle. Samples were homogenized, lysed, and permeabilized with Digitonin and Tween-20. The isolated nuclei were purified, centrifuged, and resuspended in diluted nuclei buffer, and then loaded into the Chromium X (10× Genomics, USA) to encapsulate single nuclei into droplet emulsions following the manufacturer’s recommendations. ​About 5000 nuclei were targeted to be captured/sample. Library preparation was performed according to the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression User Guide. Amplified cDNAs and the libraries were measured by Qubit dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quality assessed by TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, USA). Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus instrument (Illumina, USA), and reads were subsequently processed using 10× Genomics Cell Ranger ARC (v 2.0.2) analytical pipeline and human GRCh38 (2020-A) reference. Dataset aggregation was performed using the cellranger-arc aggr function, normalizing for the total number of confidently mapped reads across libraries.

SnRNAseq Analysis

After sequencing, all libraries are processed through the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline to process the sequenced data. Debris-contaminated droplets were removed using DIEM15 and clean barcodes were selected based on debris score < 0.5, Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) count >500, and feature count >250. Data were normalized using SCTransform and integrated across samples in Seurat.16 RunUMAP was performed with the parameter settings reduction = “harmony”, dims =1:20 to create a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) representation based on the Harmony embeddings. Subsequently, Louvain clustering was conducted utilizing the FindNeighbors function reduction = “harmony”, dims =1:20, and the FindClusters function with a resolution of 0.5. To identify cell types, the Azimuth application and Seurat’s anchor-based label transfer method were applied.16,17 These methods leveraged a single-cell RNA-seq reference dataset obtained from a similar tissue, specifically the human motor cortex. This reference dataset, compiled from data and annotations provided by the Allen Institute, comprises 76 533 nuclei integrated across two individual donors.18 In this report, cell annotations are provided at the subclass level, and additional annotation levels can be found in the output files. Cell typing analysis was performed on each sample individually, utilizing these cell type annotations for the integrated sample to facilitate differential expression and pathway analysis. By using cell type annotations from individual samples for the integrated sample, we ensured the use of the maximal sequencing data available for cell typing before undergoing the normalization process required to integrate the samples. High-confidence annotations (prediction score > 0.75) were supported by consistent anchors. The mapping score reflects how well the cell’s local neighborhood structure is preserved during the reference mapping process. Marker analysis for each cluster and cell type was carried out using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test, implemented through the Seurat FindAllMarkers() function. The differential expression analysis of the genes between control and MDD in each cell type was performed with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and log2 foldchange >+/-0.25 cutoff.

Multiome ATAC-Seq Data Analysis

All data were generated using the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression platform. For each sample, the input for custom analysis was the output from Cell Ranger ARC. Analysis was performed using Seurat v5.1.016 and Signac v1.13.019 to create multiomic Seurat objects integrating paired gene expression (RNA) and chromatin accessibility (ATAC) profiles.

Quality Control and Preprocessing

Datasets were preprocessed with Seurat to remove low-quality and outlier nuclei based on the following criteria: total RNA-seq UMI counts >500, RNA-seq features >200, mitochondrial percentage < 5%, nucleosome signal <3, and transcription start site enrichment score > 1. Outliers were further filtered using median absolute deviation (MAD), calculated as MAD = median(|X_i - median(X)|), where X_i represents log10(total RNA counts) or log10(total ATAC counts) per cell. Cells differing by >5 MADs from the median were removed, following a permissive strategy.

Debris-contaminated droplets were removed from snRNA data using DIEM with a minimum UMI count cutoff of 500 and a minimum gene count of 250 (default parameters otherwise). Potential doublets were identified and removed using Scrublet with default parameters.

Per-Sample Analysis

For each sample, quality control was followed by dimensionality reduction, clustering (resolution = 0.8), and UMAP visualization for RNA, ATAC, and a joint weighted nearest neighbor (WNN) graph integrating both modalities. Peak calling was performed using MACS2.20 Cell type annotations were assigned based on marker gene expression. Gene activity scores were inferred from ATAC data using Signac’s GeneActivity function, aggregating chromatin accessibility signals near gene bodies and promoters to estimate transcriptional activity.

Multi-Sample Integration

Samples were integrated using Seurat for downstream analysis, including identification of differentially accessible (DA) candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs), differentially active motifs, and key regulators across cell types. Integration encompassed joint dimensionality reduction, clustering, and UMAP visualization at RNA, ATAC, and WNN (Weighted-Nearest Neighbor) levels, with cells colored by cluster, condition (MDD vs. control), sample, or cell type.

DA cCREs were identified at the ATAC level for each cell type using FindMarkers in Seurat, with no initial cutoffs. Marker genes were calculated at the RNA level, and gene activity matrices were generated from ATAC data. Heatmaps visualized row-normalized chromatin accessibility of cell type-specific cCREs (|avg_log2FC| ≥ 0.5 and adjusted p ≤ .1). Transcription factor motif activities were inferred using chromVAR, with position weight matrices from the JASPAR2020 database. Differentially active motifs across cell types were identified.

Differential Analysis between MDD and Controls

DA cCREs, motifs, and key regulators were analyzed between MDD and control groups in the integrated dataset, both globally and per cell type or cluster. DA cCREs were identified using FindMarkers, annotated with the closest gene, peak type, genomic region, and distance. Marker peaks were filtered (p < 0.05 and |avg_log2FC| >1). Condition-specific chromVAR motif activities were computed. Key regulators were identified by integrating RNA expression and ATAC motif accessibility using Presto21 to compute area under the curve (AUC) statistics for genes and motifs. TFs were ranked by average AUC across assays, with UMAP visualizations of TF expression (RNA), gene activity (ATAC), and motif activity (chromVAR) normalized as log-fold changes. Analyses are repeated per cell type.

Functional Analysis

Peak-to-Gene Linkages

The peak-to-gene linkages were identified using the LinkPeaks() function within a distance of 5 × 105 bases. The peak-linked genes were filtered for P-value≤0.05 and Pearson’s r > 0.1.22

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Enriched Gene Ontology terms and pathways (P < 0.05) were identified for DAR-linked genes (r > 0.1) in each cell type using enrichGO() and enrichKEGG() from clusterProfiler (v. 4.10.1) package with org.Hs.eg.db (v. 3.18.0).

PsyGeNET Analysis

To examine the association of DAR-linked genes (r > 0.1) with psychiatric phenotypes, the psygenet2r (v.1.28.0) package was utilized with the database set to “ALL”.23

Results

SnRNAseq Analysis

Across control (C1-8) and MDD (MDD1-7) groups, nuclei showed median gene counts of ~2000-6000, with subsets exceeding 10 000, reflecting high transcriptional activity. Total RNA counts were consistent across samples, and mitochondrial expression remained below 5%, confirming the high quality of the nuclei suitable for downstream analyses (Figure S1, Table S2). Harmony-integrated UMAP analysis identified 26 transcriptionally distinct clusters that were preserved across control and MDD groups. Comparable distributions of nuclei across clusters in both groups were observed (Fig. S2A). Visualization by condition confirmed that nuclei from control and MDD groups were interspersed across clusters without batch-driven segregation (Figure S2B-D, Table S3), and reference mapping scores supported high confidence of cell type annotations (Figure S2E). A total of 20 distinct transcriptional clusters, encompassing 65371nuclei, were identified within the snRNA dataset. These clusters correspond to major neuronal and non-neuronal populations, including excitatory and inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), microglia/macrophages, endothelial cells, and vascular leptomeningeal cells (VLMCs) (Figure 1A). Known marker gene expression validated the identity of each cluster, showing expected enrichment patterns (Figure 1B), and canonical marker genes VIP in Vip, MOBP in oligodendrocyte, and PDGFRA in OPC were also identified as cell-type marker genes (Figure S3). Hierarchical clustering and expression heatmaps further confirmed the lineage-specific clustering of cell types (Figure 1C). The distribution of cell type proportion was broadly consistent across control and MDD samples, though subtle shifts were observed in specific neuronal and glial subpopulations (Figure 1D). Layer-specific and subtype-specific marker genes for excitatory and inhibitory neurons demonstrated robust cell-type-specific expression (Figure 1E-F), and comparative analyses indicated contributions of excitatory, inhibitory, and non-neuronal classes were preserved across groups (Figure 1G). In both cohorts, oligodendrocytes constituted the largest cell population (Fig. 1H).

Multiomic Characterization of dlPFC

Across all control and MDD samples, the QC metrics demonstrated consistent RNA and ATAC library quality (Figure S4, Table S4). UMAP embeddings of gene expression (RNA) and chromatin accessibility (ATAC) demonstrated the consistent clustering of 54 036 nuclei into shared, cell-type-specific populations (Figure 2A-B). Integration across modalities preserved distinct neuronal and non-neuronal identities, with excitatory, inhibitory, glial, and vascular-associated clusters aligning robustly (Figure 2C). Gene activity tracks further confirmed the concordance between RNA expression and chromatin accessibility, with markers such as SLC17A7, GAD2, AQP4, POGFRA, MAG, and FLT1 showing cell-type-specific accessibility peaks that matched RNA expression patterns (Figure 2D-F, Figure S5A-C). Additionally, the known marker genes exhibited high expression in the nuclei of their respective cell types (Figure S6). We identified a total of 103 040 unique differentially accessible regions (DARs) across cell types (Table S5). Notably, transcription factors (TFs) such as NEUROG1 and RORC demonstrated marked enrichment within neuronal populations. Conversely, SOX13 and MAZ showed high expression levels in OPCs and oligodendrocytes. Additionally, glial-associated gene expression was observed for NFIC and EHF. Furthermore, FOSL2::JUND and Foxf1 were predominantly enriched in non-neuronal cell types, including vascular and mesenchymal lineages. These TF represent key regulators of cell identity within the prefrontal cortex (Figure. S7, Table S6). Additionally, Atoh1, NEUROG1, EOMES, and NEUROD2 were identified as lineage-specific regulators for the excitatory neurons, as they were expressed across all excitatory subpopulations (Figure 2G-J).

Chromatin Accessibility Changes Associated with MDD

Analysis of chromatin accessibility across all identified cell types revealed substantial numbers of DARs (Table S7). Both open and closed chromatin regions were observed in each cell type, with L5 ET of excitatory neurons, Sncg of inhibitory neurons, and astrocytes from non-neuronal cell types having the largest numbers of DARs. Pie chart distributions indicated variability in the balance of open versus closed peaks across cell types, reflecting cell type-specific regulatory landscapes that may underlie transcriptional differences between control and MDD (Figure S8). Most DARs were located within intronic and promoter regions of the linked genes, underscoring the importance of CREs in controlling cell type-specific transcriptional programs (Figure S9A-B). The majority of the linked genes contained between 1 and 5 distinct DARs, either intronic or within the promoter regions (Figure S9C). Our analysis also showed a significant number of differentially expressed genes in each cell type, with oligodendrocytes having the most differentially expressed genes (DEGs) while having the least DARs (Figure S9D-G). Similarly, excitatory neurons from L5 ET had the highest number of both open and closed chromatin regions; however, the differentially expressed genes did not reflect this regulation.

Multiomic Profiling of Excitatory Neurons

The excitatory neuronal population was stratified into 8 distinct layers according to their marker gene expression profiles (Figure 3A). For instance, CUX2 is expressed in Layer 2/3 IT neurons, SULF2 in L5 ET neurons, RORB in L5 IT neurons, and HTR2C in neurons spanning L5/6 NP cells as inferred from the snRNA dataset (Figure 1E). A substantial increase in the proportion of L5 IT (P = 0.02) and L6 IT neurons (P = 0.04) nuclei relative to all cell classes was observed in the MDD group (Figure 3B and C). Conversely, the proportions of excitatory neurons in other cortical layers did not exhibit significant changes between control and MDD (Figure S10). In L5 ET neurons, the majority of peaks displayed reduced chromatin accessibility under MDD (Figure 3D). Notably, these neurons, which accounted for most of the identified DARs, showed an overlap of 20 genes between genes linked to increased chromatin accessibility and up-regulated genes, and 24 genes overlapped between genes linked to decreased chromatin accessibility and down-regulated genes (Figure 3E and F). In contrast, L6 IT Car3 neurons showed 16 overlapping genes for both open/up-regulated and closed/down-regulated comparisons, while L5 IT neurons showed the fewest overlaps (Figure S11). HTR2C, identified as a potential biomarker gene,24 was downregulated in L5 ET and L6 IT Car3 neurons. Conversely, RELN expression was upregulated in L6b neurons and downregulated in L5 IT neurons (Figure 3G, Figure S12). Functional enrichment of genes linked to open DARs showed significant overrepresentation of pathways related to synaptic organization, modulation of chemical synaptic transmission, regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, calcium signaling, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction. These pathways reflect enhanced chromatin accessibility in genes regulating neuronal communication, excitability, and signal transduction, suggesting activation of excitatory circuit components in MDD (Figure 3H, Figure S13). Notably, many of these functional categories were also enriched among closed DAR-linked genes, indicating shared regulatory processes that may undergo bidirectional modulation depending on cellular context (Figure S14). Both upregulated and downregulated genes showed marked cell type specificity, with only a small subset overlapping between different cell types. Among the downregulated genes, APOD, KCNF1, SBNO2, and MT-ND3 were common to L5 IT, L6 IT, and L6b neurons (Figure 3I), while in upregulated genes, OR2L5, OR2L2, and ARHGAP10 were shared among L6 IT, L6 CT, and L6b (Figure S15).

Multiomic Profiling of Inhibitory Neurons

Inhibitory neuronal populations were subdivided into distinct subtypes, including Sst Chodl, Sst, Pvalb, Vip, Sncg, and Lamp5 neurons, based on characteristic marker gene expression profiles (Figures 1F and 4A). Among these, Pvalb neurons exhibited a significant increase in proportion under MDD (P = 0.036) (Figure 4B), while other inhibitory subtypes showed no major shifts (Figure S16). A total of 1464 DARs were identified in the Pvalb subtype, predominantly consisting of closed chromatin peaks (Figure 4C). Differential accessibility and expression analyses demonstrated small but meaningful overlap between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes, as well as closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes, across subtypes (Figure 4D and E, Figure S17). Volcano plot highlighted both coding and non-coding genes associated with MDD, including key genes such as DENND2D and SLC39A2, which were downregulated, and STON1, RORB-AS1, and LINC00508, which were upregulated in Pvalb neurons under MDD (Figure 4F). Among other inhibitory neuronal subtypes, EBF1, GAL, IKZF1, and CAPN8 were downregulated while TAC3, LINC02263, and CTXN3 were upregulated in Lamp5, Sncg, Sst, and Vip, respectively (Figure S18). Functional enrichment analyses of open DAR-linked genes demonstrated a significant association with neuroactive ligand signaling pathways. In particular, GNAL, GRIN1, OPRD1, and SLC32A1 genes were implicated in Pvalb neurons (Figure 4G), GABRA1, GABRA5, GABRG2, GNB5, GNG2, GRIK4, and PRKCA were associated with Lamp5 neurons, and GABRA1, GABRA5, and GRIK3 genes were linked to Sst neurons, respectively (Figure S19). In comparison, the closed DAR-linked genes showed enrichment for GABAergic synapse, MAPK signaling, glutamatergic synapse, and neuroactive ligand signaling pathways in Lamp5, Pvalb, Sncg, and Sst neurons, respectively (Figure S20). Similar to excitatory neurons, inhibitory neuronal subtypes also showed cell-type-specific regulation for most differentially expressed genes. We identified the downregulated genes ARL17B, RPGRIP1, and LINC01291 as common across all inhibitory cell types, while MUC20 and ZNF100 were commonly upregulated (Figure 4H, Figure S21).

Multiomic Profiling of Non-Neuronal Cell Types

Analysis of non-neuronal populations, including oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, OPCs, endothelial cells, microglia, and VLMC, revealed distinct transcriptional and epigenetic alterations associated with MDD. Oligodendrocytes, the most abundant non-neuronal population, showed a mild but non-significant decrease in MDD (P = 0.076). Integration of RNA and ATAC data identified two overlapping genes between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes, as well as between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes. Differential expression analysis revealed upregulation of AL359208.1 and MAP3K14-AS1, whereas genes such as DACT2 and ZNF716 were downregulated. Functional enrichment of open DAR-linked genes highlighted pathways involved in stress response to copper and metal ions, cellular response to zinc ions, and N-glycan biosynthesis, indicating activation of stress-adaptive and metabolic processes in MDD. Closed DAR genes were enriched for oligodendrocyte development, myelination, axon ensheathment, and sphingolipid biosynthetic processes, suggesting impaired oligodendrocyte development and myelin maintenance (Figure S22). Together, these results point to disrupted glial homeostasis and compromised oligodendrocyte function in MDD, which may contribute to altered neuronal connectivity and reduced synaptic efficiency.

Analysis of OPCs revealed no significant differences in cell proportions between control and MDD groups (P = 0.232). Combined RNA and ATAC data identified limited overlap between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes, consistent with subtle regulatory alterations in this population. Differential expression analysis indicated upregulation of MCTP2, LINC00326, and ADGRL4, along with downregulation of NPY, IER3, and LINC01446, reflecting transcriptional shifts linked to inflammatory environment and neural protection.25,26 Functional enrichment analyses of open DAR genes highlighted pathways associated with response to hypoxia, mitochondrial organization, and cortisol synthesis and secretion, suggesting activation of stress-adaptive and hormonal response mechanisms. Closed DAR-linked genes were enriched for gliogenesis, regulation of synaptic transmission, oligodendrocyte differentiation, and MAPK signaling, pointing to impaired maturation and reduced glial-neuronal support in MDD (Figure S23).

Astrocytes displayed MDD-associated transcriptional and epigenetic alterations, although no significant difference in overall proportion was observed (P = 0.232). Integration of ATAC and RNA data identified 1725 DARs, with 101 overlapping genes between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes and six between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes. Differential expression analysis indicated upregulation of LINC00326, VWDE, and LIN28B, alongside downregulation of BARX2, AC004870.4, and AL138762.1, reflecting altered transcriptional regulation within astrocytic populations. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that open DAR-linked genes were enriched for axon ensheathment, myelination, and gliogenesis, suggesting a potential compensatory response aimed at maintaining neuronal and synaptic integrity in MDD. Conversely, closed DAR genes were enriched for pathways such as positive regulation of neurogenesis, cell development, glutamatergic synapse, and actin cytoskeleton regulation, suggesting diminished neuronal communication and impaired cytoskeletal dynamics (Figure S24).

In endothelial cells, a significant increase in cell proportion (P = 0.0361) was observed. Integrated analysis found 10 genes overlapping between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes and 27 between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes. Upregulated genes included UTY, USP9Y, SERPINE1, and NLGN4Y, while downregulated genes were LINC02398, CD81-AS1, and CNTNAP5, reflecting transcriptomic changes in the endothelial cells. Functional enrichment of open DAR-linked genes indicated roles in axon ensheathment, gliogenesis, Hippo and phosphatidylinositol signaling, and calcium signaling, suggesting potential functions in neurovascular coupling. Closed DAR-linked genes were enriched for synaptic transmission, trans-synaptic signaling, focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and Rap1 signaling, indicating changes affecting barrier stability. These findings support altered vascular signaling and compromised neurovascular support in MDD, likely disrupting the cortical microenvironment (Figure S25).

Microglia and perivascular macrophages exhibited transcriptional and chromatin accessibility changes indicative of immune and structural remodeling in MDD, with a small but non-significant reduction in cell proportion (P = 0.076). We identified 23 overlapping genes between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes and seven between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes. Differential expression analysis showed the upregulation of CLEC12A, FCN1, and AC011586.1, and the downregulation of NOSTRIN, FOXQ1, and LINC02211, indicating alterations in immune and inflammatory signaling.27,28 Functional enrichment of open DAR-linked genes highlighted pathways such as axon ensheathment, glial cell differentiation, cholesterol metabolism, and lysosome function, suggesting a potential shift toward enhanced phagocytic and metabolic activity. Closed DAR-linked genes were enriched in positive regulation of cell activation, cytokine production, mononuclear cell differentiation, and immune response, suggesting attenuated inflammatory control and immune modulation under MDD conditions (Figure S26).

Analysis of VLMCs showed no significant difference in cell proportion (P = 0.389) in MDD. The integration of RNA and ATAC data revealed 7 overlapping genes between open DAR-linked and upregulated genes, and eleven between closed DAR-linked and downregulated genes. Differential expression analysis showed upregulation of HBB, RBBP8, and IGFN1, alongside downregulation of PWRN1, suggesting shifts in transcriptional regulation related to vascular structure and signaling. Enrichment analysis implicated open DAR-linked genes in pathways associated with axon ensheathment, myelin assembly, cell adhesion, ether lipid metabolism, and locomotor behavior, indicating a potential role for VLMCs in maintaining neuronal insulation and extracellular support functions. Closed DAR-linked genes were enriched in forebrain and telencephalon development, small GTPase-mediated signal transduction, gliogenesis, and PI3K-Akt signaling, pointing to impaired vascular-glial communication and cytoskeletal signaling (Figure S27).

Key Regulator TFs and Gene Regulatory Network

Integrative chromVAR and motif enrichment analyses identified key TFs that regulate cell-type-specific transcriptional programs across neuronal and non-neuronal populations in MDD (Tables S8-S9). In excitatory neurons, FOXP1, PBX1, THRB, NR3C1, and NR3C2 emerged as the top regulators, displaying high AUC values with enriched motifs across subtypes. Additional members of the forkhead/winged helix and nuclear receptor families, including FOXP2, FOXP4, and RORA, also exhibited strong regulatory activity (Figure 5A-E). ChromVAR deviation scores revealed increased FOXP1 activity in MDD, implicating these TFs in stress hormone-related and synaptic gene regulation (Figure 5F). Motif sequence analysis revealed a consensus GTAAACA motif characteristic of FOXP TFs (Figure 5G). Gene regulatory network reconstruction revealed FOXP1 as a pivotal regulatory transcription factor, targeting depression-associated genes such as SLC6A1, BDNF, HLF, and GRIK3, and also several of the upregulated genes, like COL6A1, SNTG2, and ANKRD62 (Figure 5H, Table S10).

Among non-neuronal cells, TF analysis by integrating motif activity and RNA expression identified TCF4, ZEB1, MEF2C, THRB, NR3C2, and RFX3 as major regulators. These TFs displayed high AUC values and motif enrichment across glial and vascular populations. Motif enrichment revealed the consensus CACCTG motif for ZEB1, typical of homeo domain factors, highlighting their roles in promoting astro-glial and preventing neuronal cell fate.29 Gene regulatory network analysis of ZEB1 revealed that several depression-associated genes, including NTM, GFAP, VGF, and APP, were among its targets. Moreover, a total of 57 upregulated genes were also identified as target genes of this transcription factor (Figure S28). Notably, NR3C1 and NR3C2 were consistently detected as key regulators across all major cell types, suggesting that glucocorticoid signaling-related transcriptional control is a unifying mechanism underlying MDD-associated changes in both neuronal and glial compartments. All identified target genes of NR3C1 were shared with NR3C2, whereas the latter possesses several unique targets, including GRIK3, IMPA2, and GNAL (Figure S29, Table S10).

Among the downregulated TFs, key regulators were predominantly identified in non-neuronal cell populations, whereas no major regulatory factors were detected within neuronal subtypes. In non-neuronal cell types, TF analysis integrating chromVAR and RNA expression data identified NFATC4 in astrocytes, IKZF1 in Micro-PVM, and SOX8 in oligodendrocytes. NFATC4 targeted several depression-associated genes, including NTRK2, SLC1A3, and LSAMP, while IKZF1 targeted MDD-associated genes such as DISC1, IL6R, TNF, HSPA1B, and HSPA1A (Figure S30, Table S11).

Discussion

Our integrative single-nucleus RNA and ATAC profiling of the human prefrontal cortex provides a comprehensive cellular and regulatory atlas in the dlPFC of control and MDD subjects. The identification of 20 distinct transcriptional clusters highlights the cellular diversity within this region. It also confirms that both excitatory and inhibitory neuronal subtypes, as well as major glial and vascular cell populations, are preserved across groups. The broadly consistent distribution of cell types between the control and MDD groups suggests that large-scale shifts in cellular composition are not the predominant driver of disease-related changes, although subtle alterations in specific neuronal and glial subtypes may contribute to MDD pathophysiology. Consistent with this, while we observed significant increases in the proportions of several cell types, including L5 IT and L6 IT neuronal nuclei, these differences likely reflect variation in the relative representation of captured nuclei rather than true changes in cell number, and may be influenced by transcriptional activity, cell size, or technical biases during nuclei isolation.

​The strong concordance between gene expression and chromatin accessibility reinforces the validity of our cell type annotations and underscores the importance of epigenetic regulation in maintaining cell identity and function within the MDD brain. ​We identified numerous DARs and DEGs across various cell types, with DARs predominantly located in intronic and promoter regions. ​This emphasizes that altered gene regulation at the cis-regulatory level is a crucial mechanism contributing to the transcriptional changes observed in MDD.14 ​The functional enrichment analyses further elucidated MDD-associated alterations in pathways critical for neuronal communication, stress responses, and glial support, collectively painting a picture of a brain experiencing widespread molecular distress.30,31

In excitatory neurons, we observed significant increases in the proportions of L5 IT and L6 IT neurons relative to other cell types, suggesting a potential shift in cortical architecture or neuronal excitability. ​The widespread reduction in chromatin accessibility in L5 ET neurons, coupled with overlaps between DARs and DEGs, indicates altered gene regulatory programs governing neuronal function. The downregulation of HTR2C, a known biomarker candidate for MDD, in L5 ET and L6 IT Car3 neurons, directly implicates serotonergic signaling disruption in specific excitatory subtypes.24 ​The differential expression of RELN, a gene crucial for neuronal migration and synaptic plasticity, further underscores subtype-specific vulnerabilities and potential impairments in neural circuit development or maintenance in depression.32 The fact that many functional categories, including synaptic organization, chemical synaptic transmission, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, were enriched among both open and closed DAR-linked genes suggests a complex, context-dependent regulation of regulatory processes affecting synaptic function in MDD.33

Similarly, in inhibitory neurons, the increased proportion of Pvalb neurons in MDD is particularly relevant, as these neurons are critical for gamma oscillations and precise circuit timing, which are often disrupted in MDD.34 The extensive number of DARs in Pvalb neurons, predominantly closed chromatin peaks, suggests altered epigenetic regulation leading to impaired inhibitory function. Downregulated genes like DENND2D and SLC39A2, and upregulated genes such as STON1, RORB-AS1, and LINC00508, offer specific molecular targets that could contribute to an imbalance in inhibitory tone.35,36 Genes encoding GABA-A receptor subunits (GABRA1, GABRA5, GABRG2) are enriched among DAR-linked genes in inhibitory neuron subtypes in MDD. The GABAergic deficit hypothesis of MDD is supported by evidence of reduced GABA levels and altered expression of GABA-A receptor subunits, changes that are partially reversed by effective antidepressant treatment.37

Beyond neurons, our study emphasizes the critical role of non-neuronal cells in MDD pathology. Microglia and perivascular macrophages exhibit transcriptional shifts indicating immune dysregulation, with altered cholesterol metabolism and lysosome function suggesting heightened phagocytic activity. Concurrently, glial cells, including oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and OPCs, demonstrate widespread dyshomeostasis. Oligodendrocytes show compromised myelin maintenance and cellular stress, which can disrupt neuronal communication efficiency. OPCs display impaired maturation, suggesting a reduced capacity for myelin repair and regeneration. Furthermore, astrocytes exhibit both compensatory responses and diminished neurogenesis support, impacting brain plasticity and mood regulation. ​These glial abnormalities, such as reduced glial cell density and altered morphology, have been consistently reported in post-mortem brain studies of MDD patients.38​

Integrative motif activity and expression analyses revealed a set of master TFs orchestrating these cell-type-specific programs. In neurons, FOXP1 was identified as a key regulator. FOXP1, a forkhead transcription factor essential for synaptic development and cortical connectivity, was previously shown to mediate stress responses and neuronal plasticity.39 Its increased activity in MDD suggests maladaptive engagement of stress-related transcriptional control. Of particular note, NR3C1 (glucocorticoid receptor) and NR3C2 (mineralocorticoid receptor) were consistently identified as key regulators across all cell types. Dysregulation of glucocorticoid receptor signaling is a well-established hallmark of MDD, mediating chronic stress-induced synaptic and glial dysfunction.40–42 The shared target network of NR3C1/NR3C2 includes depression-associated genes such as BDNF, SLC6A1, GRIK3, IMPA2, and GNAL, linking glucocorticoid-driven transcriptional modulation to synaptic signaling and mood regulation. Among downregulated TFs, NFATC4 in astrocytes and IKZF1 in microglia stand out. NFATC4 mediates calcium-dependent transcription involved in neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory responses,43,44 while IKZF1 regulates immune activation and cytokine signaling; their suppression in MDD may exacerbate neuroinflammation and neuronal stress vulnerability.44,45

Conclusion

The findings reveal that MDD involves convergent dysregulation of neuronal excitability, glial support, and vascular homeostasis orchestrated by key transcriptional regulators and stress-responsive pathways. The integration of RNA and ATAC modalities highlights how chromatin accessibility dynamically shapes these gene expression changes, providing mechanistic insight into cell-type-specific regulatory dysfunction in the prefrontal cortex. Importantly, our results indicate that these alterations are not confined to a single cell population but instead reflect coordinated, system-level disruption across neuronal, glial, and vascular compartments. This integrated regulatory landscape suggests that MDD arises from the breakdown of cellular communication and homeostatic balance within cortical circuits rather than isolated molecular defects. Our data support a model in which chronic stress and altered glucocorticoid signaling reprogram gene regulatory networks in specific cell types, contributing to the structural and functional deficits observed in the PFC of MDD patients. These results provide a foundation for targeted interventions that restore cell-type-specific regulatory balance, potentially enabling new therapeutic avenues for depression. While the present study yields significant insights, its modest sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, findings identified at nominal significance thresholds (P < .05) should be considered preliminary and warrant validation in larger, independent cohorts. A limitation of this study is the lack of independent experimental validation for key findings, although concordant RNA-seq and ATAC-seq signals provide strong supporting evidence. These constraints should be acknowledged when interpreting disease-associated patterns. Additionally, detailed information regarding time, season, and latitude of death was not available for the current cohort. These factors are known to influence circadian regulation and gene expression patterns in the human brain and may contribute to variability in transcriptional and epigenetic profiles. Therefore, the inability to account for these variables represents an additional limitation of the present study. Given established sex differences in MDD prevalence and symptomatology, as well as age-related brain changes, future research should employ larger and more diverse cohorts. Moreover, incorporating clinical covariates such as treatment history, symptom severity, and psychiatric comorbidities will be essential for stratifying patient populations and identifying molecular signatures specific to distinct MDD phenotypes.
