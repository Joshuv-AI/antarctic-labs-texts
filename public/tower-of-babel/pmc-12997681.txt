‘Dad, I’m not even doing anything!’: a qualitative inquiry into father‒child play dynamics
Authors: Erhan Alabay, Yüksel Kantekin Atabay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this research is to investigate the dynamics of play between fathers and their preschool-aged children. The study was conducted with ten fathers residing in Istanbul and their children aged 36–72 months via a basic qualitative research design. Data were collected through the "Father‒Child Play Process Observation Form," developed by researchers, along with video recordings of each father‒child dyad. In a natural home environment, fathers and children were provided with playdough as unstructured play material, and their interactions were recorded. The data were analyzed via both descriptive and content analysis techniques. The findings indicated that fathers often directed the play process themselves, used imperative language, exhibited perfectionist tendencies, and frequently intervened both verbally and physically. The study suggests that fathers tend to adopt central and authoritative roles during play, which may limit children’s opportunities for active engagement and self-directed play. In addition, it was observed that many fathers exhibited encouraging language or behaviors during play. This attitude was categorized in the study as motivational support, alongside three additional categories of positive attitudes and behaviors: responsiveness to help requests, openness to the child’s lead, and asking guiding questions. It was noted, however, that only a small number of participating fathers demonstrated these positive attitudes and behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03886-4.Keywords: Play, Father, Child, Father‒child play interaction

Introduction

In Türkiye, paternal responsibilities have long been shaped by the identities of authority figures, providers, and managers [1]. This traditional framework limits fathers’ direct involvement in their children’s care, development, and educational processes, many fathers restrict their interactions with their children primarily to play activities. However, the contribution of such play-based engagement to child development and the quality of these interactions often remain unexplored.

In recent years, fatherhood in Türkiye has been gradually transforming from an authority-oriented and distant role toward a more emotionally attuned and participatory form of involvement. Nevertheless, recent research shows that many fathers continue to display restrictive and authoritarian attitudes in their parenting, maintaining minimal levels of direct engagement in their children’s daily lives and developmental experiences [2–4]). Particularly in the preschool period, the scarcity of primary, data-based studies directly observing father–child play interactions highlights a significant research gap. By examining fathers’ behaviors, guidance styles, and communication approaches during play, this study aims to contribute to a deeper understanding of the evolving roles of fatherhood and to identify interaction patterns that effectively support children’s development.

Father involvement

Father involvement is a multifaceted concept that pertains to the degree and nature of a father’s participation in the care, development, and daily life of the child. This engagement goes beyond physical presence and includes active participation at emotional, cognitive, and social levels [5–7]. Consequently, father involvement should be assessed not only by the amount of time spent with the child but also by the quality of that time, the nature of the relationship, and the strategies through which the father facilitates the child’s development. UNICEF [8] defines father involvement as the father’s active engagement in promoting the child’s development and well-being across physical, emotional, social, and cognitive domains. In line with this perspective, father involvement can be conceptualized as the father’s active, responsible, and supportive participation in caregiving, education, play, emotional nurturing, and decision-making processes concerning the child. Jones et al. [9] conducted a study examining the relationship between parenting quality, fathers’ psychological well-being, and children’s adjustment in families where fathers assumed the role of primary caregivers. The findings challenge the assumption that women are inherently or socially better suited to primary caregiving roles due to biological or societal factors. The study demonstrates that fathers can be equally effective as primary caregivers and emphasizes that caregiving responsibilities and parenting competence should not be confined to traditional gender roles.

Historically, father involvement has received limited attention within attachment theories, which have focused primarily on the mother’s role. Bowlby [10] and Ainsworth et al. [11] emphasized the mother–child relationship as central to the development of secure attachment. However, recent studies have demonstrated that fathers can also serve as secure attachment figures, particularly through playful interactions [12, 13]. Father–child play fosters a sense of security by providing emotional responsiveness, attention, and engagement, which support the child’s confident exploration of the social world and facilitate the development of healthy relationships [14]. As a key context for emotional and social bonding, play is regarded as one of the most effective avenues for strengthening the father–child relationship [15, 16].

Michael E. Lamb’s “Father Involvement Model” stands out as a foundational theoretical framework that systematically explains the multifaceted nature of father involvement. The model asserts that fatherhood goes beyond mere physical presence and requires active engagement in emotional and organizational domains. Lamb defines father involvement through three core dimensions: accessibility, engagement, and responsibility. Accessibility refers to the father’s consistent physical and psychological availability to the child, which does not necessarily imply constant interaction but underscores the importance of being present. Engagement encompasses direct involvement in the child’s daily care, shared activities, and especially play. Responsibility involves the father’s recognition of the child’s needs, planning to meet those needs, and consciously assuming a role in promoting the child’s development. Within this framework, play-based interactions are particularly central to father involvement, as they strongly reflect both the engagement and responsibility dimensions [17]. Pleck [18] extended Lamb’s foundational model by offering a more nuanced, qualitative, and functional account of father involvement, identifying three primary components: positive engagement, warmth and responsiveness, and control. Positive engagement refers to interactions that directly promote the child’s development, whereas warmth and responsiveness encompass the father’s expressions of affection, empathy, and emotional sensitivity. Control is defined as the father’s role in monitoring behavior, providing guidance, and participating in decision-making processes concerning the child. In addition, Pleck elaborated on Lamb’s original dimension of responsibility by distinguishing between indirect care and process responsibility. This conceptual expansion emphasizes that fathers are actively involved not only in visible caregiving tasks but also in less observable yet critical activities such as planning, supervising, and sustaining developmental processes [19].

Father involvement has been increasingly recognized as beneficial for both children and fathers. Strengthening the father–child relationship plays a vital role not only in supporting children's emotional, social, and cognitive development, but also in enhancing fathers’ psychological well-being, parenting competence, and sense of familial connectedness. Recent research indicates that fathers who engage sensitively and consistently with their children significantly contribute to the development of children's self-regulation, empathy, and academic adjustment [20–23]. In addition, active and nurturing paternal involvement has been associated with improved mental health in fathers, reduced parenting stress, and reinforced paternal identity and efficacy within the family [24–26]. A responsive and supportive father–child relationship thus creates reciprocal benefits, promoting children’s resilience and long-term well-being while simultaneously enhancing fathers’ life satisfaction and emotional bonding within the family [14, 27].

Empirical data from Türkiye significantly reinforces and expand upon theoretical models of father involvement. The report “Involved Fatherhood and Its Determinants in Türkiye” published by the Mother Child Education Foundation [28] reveals that the most frequently observed forms of father‒child interaction involve physical and high-energy activities, such as wrestling and rough‒tumble play. Many fathers emphasized the importance of allowing children to release energy and maintain a connection with nature, often encouraging outdoor activities such as running and playing with soil. Notably, these patterns were reported across both sons and daughters, suggesting a relatively gender-neutral approach to physical play. However, the same report highlights a striking gap in caregiving responsibilities: 91% of fathers identified mothers as the primary caregivers, and 51% stated that they had no intention of taking on such responsibilities. Notably, only 0.1% of fathers reported being actively involved in their children's daily caregiving routines. In addition, the study conducted by Kılıç and Şenol [29] examined fathers’ involvement in childcare. The findings indicate that the concept of fatherhood in Turkey has been historically shaped by patriarchal foundations, yet it is currently undergoing a transformation influenced by modern dynamics. Fathers’ caregiving responsibilities and levels of participation appear to fluctuate between traditional expectations and emerging forms of new fatherhood. Despite the observed increase in paternal involvement, traditional fatherhood norms continue to exert a strong influence on fathers’ roles and practices. A study examining fathers’ frequency of play with their children and their views on play within the framework of demographic characteristics suggests that fathers’ levels of participation and perspectives on play vary according to these demographic factors. Low income, extended family structure, and full-time employment emerged as key factors that restrict fathers’ involvement in play activities [30].

When evaluated collectively, both theoretical frameworks and empirical findings underscore the critical role of father involvement in promoting child development across both quantitative and qualitative dimensions. An increased quality of paternal engagement has been consistently associated with improvements in children’s cognitive abilities [31], socioemotional competencies [15], secure attachment formation [32], academic achievement [33], and overall well-being [34]. Furthermore, empirical evidence has demonstrated that active paternal involvement contributes positively to children’s mental health [35], mitigates depressive symptoms, and facilitates social adjustment [36]. Notably, it has also been linked to better sleep regulation and higher levels of self-esteem [37, 38]. Accordingly, a father's warm, consistent, and developmentally supportive involvement—particularly through play-based interactions—holds profound significance not only for children's developmental trajectories but also for strengthening familial relationships and advancing gender equity within the broader sociocultural context.

The significance of fatherhood in Türkiye and its impact on father‒child dynamics

In Türkiye, the concept of fatherhood differs on the basis of social structure, cultural conventions, and family typology. In rural regions and among extended family systems, fatherhood continues to play an authoritative role, with the father's responsibilities being influenced by conventional norms [39]. Despite the emergence of fractures in this conventional framework due to urbanization, rising educational attainment, and the prevalence of nuclear family units, the psychological power ascribed to fathers persists. The study conducted by Özdemir et al. [40] examined co-parenting experiences and the division of childcare responsibilities among parents living in both urban and rural areas of Turkey. The findings indicate that, in urban settings, fathers were more actively involved in the primary care of their children and collaborated with their partners in managing childcare processes. In contrast, in rural areas, the responsibility for childcare was largely assumed by mothers, with fathers’ participation remaining limited. The study highlights that geographical and cultural contexts play a significant role in shaping the ways in which parents collaborate and engage in childcare.

The conventional paternal archetype is frequently regarded as an authoritative provider and disciplinarian; however, among educated and younger generations of fathers, there is a growing inclination to assume a more engaged role in child-rearing, addressing emotional needs, and offering developmental assistance [41]. In the study conducted by Başay and Aksoy [42], when inquiring about the concept of fatherhood, participants predominantly emphasized functional roles such as protection, economic provision, and family management,conversely, contemporary attributes of fatherhood, including direct engagement with the child's care and developmental requirements, were largely overlooked. The Mother Child Education Foundation [28], in its report Understanding Fatherhood in Türkiye, identifies five distinct categories of fatherhood roles in Turkish society. The most common and foundational level is characterized by the perception of the father as traditional, patriarchal, and unquestionable. In this setting, the practice of fatherhood in Türkiye remains predominantly influenced by the concept of "hegemonic masculinity" [43].

The dominance of the patriarchal fatherhood model directly influences the father‒child relationship. In conventional frameworks, fathers are frequently linked to surface tasks, such as engaging in physical activities or play with the child, rather than participating in caregiving, education, or developmental assistance. This scenario diminishes the father's position in the family to solely provide financial support and engage through entertainment rather than addressing the child's needs. Extensive research indicates that in parent‒child interactions, mothers are predominantly engaged with children's fundamental developmental and educational requirements, whereas fathers are more inclined to partake in recreational activities and play-oriented interactions [44–48]. The findings suggest that the role of paternity is evolving in Türkiye; nevertheless, this evolution has not yet culminated in an involvement that prioritizes the child's developmental needs.

The present study

This study is grounded in the conceptual framework of father involvement, which emphasizes the multidimensional nature of paternal engagement, including emotional closeness, responsiveness, and guidance within daily interactions [17, 49]. Within this framework, play is considered a primary context through which fathers express warmth, sensitivity, and support, promoting children’s emotional and social development.

In Türkiye, paternal roles have traditionally been defined through constructs such as authority figures, economic providers, and family heads—roles that have historically limited fathers’ direct participation in their children’s caregiving, developmental, and educational processes. As a result, many fathers tend to restrict their involvement in play-based activities. A substantial body of national research consistently indicates that while mothers predominantly engage in caregiving and educational responsibilities, fathers are more frequently involved in play interactions with their children [46, 50–53].

Cabrera and Roggman [54] similarly noted that scholarly work on father–child play remains significantly underrepresented compared with studies focused on mother–child play. However, the qualitative nature of these interactions—particularly the communicative patterns and their developmental implications—has often been overlooked. Despite widespread acknowledgment of play’s critical role in early childhood development, there is a notable scarcity of empirical, observational studies examining father–child play dynamics in naturalistic contexts within the Turkish setting. Moreover, existing research has primarily measured paternal involvement through quantitative indicators such as time spent, largely neglecting the structural and relational dimensions of these interactions.

To address this gap, the present study investigates the positioning, guidance styles, and relational approaches of fathers during play, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the evolving construct of fatherhood and offering practical insights for supporting more developmentally attuned, child-centered paternal engagement. The primary aim of this study is to examine how the evolving notion of fatherhood in Türkiye is reflected in play-based interactions and to provide an in-depth analysis of the quality of these engagements. The findings are expected to inform the design of early childhood education programs tailored for fathers and to offer meaningful guidance for policymakers and practitioners working in the fields of family studies and child development.

Method

Research design

This research was conducted using a qualitative research method. The basic qualitative research design, one of the qualitative research designs, was adopted. Basic qualitative research is an approach that aims to examine a phenomenon, experience, or situation in depth through the ways participants make sense of it [55]. This study employed a basic qualitative research design due to the spontaneous nature of father–child play interactions, which involve contextually grounded and emotionally nuanced behaviors. This design allows for a comprehensive interpretation and examination of behavioral patterns exhibited during play. The qualitative approach was deemed most suitable for uncovering how fathers communicate, guide, and respond to their children within these interactions. Accordingly, father–child play was conceptualized not as a measurable variable but as an experiential and relational phenomenon. Therefore, the basic qualitative research design was considered the most appropriate approach for achieving the purpose of the study. Data were collected through video-recorded observations of father–child play interactions in home settings. The study was structured in accordance with the core principles of qualitative inquiry, utilizing observational methods to capture authentic behaviors [55].

Study group

This study employed maximum variation sampling, a purposeful sampling strategy aimed at capturing a broad spectrum of perspectives related to the central research phenomenon [56]. To ensure diversity, participant selection considered fathers’ educational backgrounds, age groups, and the gender of their children. Accordingly, the study sample consisted of 10 fathers and their 10 preschool-aged children.

Fathers residing in Istanbul who had children aged 36–72 months with typical development, provided voluntary informed consent, and agreed to participate in the study within their home environment were included in the research. Fathers who did not provide consent, whose children did not give verbal assent, whose children showed atypical development or had a diagnosed developmental condition, or who resided outside Istanbul were excluded from the study.

The ages of the participating fathers ranged from 30 to 45 years. Specifically, 50% were aged 30–34, 30% were aged 35–39, and 20% were aged 40–45. In terms of educational background, five fathers held a university degree, three had a high school diploma, and two had completed primary education. All fathers were employed in either the public or private sector. The participants come from nuclear families with an urban family background. The fathers self-reported their socioeconomic status as “middle.” Four participants had one child, while six had two. Among the children, there were six girls and four boys, with a mean age of 54.68 months.

Data collection tools

Father–child demographic information form

The demographic information form was developed by researchers and consisted of six closed-ended questions. The form collected data on the father's age, education level, employment status, number and gender of children, and child’s date of birth.

The father–child play process observation form

The Father–Child Play Process Observation Form was developed by the researcher specifically for this study. The existing instruments in the literature were found to be limited in capturing the naturalistic and spontaneous aspects of father–child play interactions. Therefore, this form was designed to systematically evaluate both verbal and behavioral components of fathers’ engagement during play without interfering with the natural flow of interaction. The development process was guided by a comprehensive literature review on paternal play and father involvement (e.g., [12, 54]), ensuring theoretical and contextual consistency with the study’s objectives. Additionally, the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form was selected because it provides a structured yet flexible framework for capturing the quality and nature of father–child interactions during play.

The Father–Child Play Process Observation Form was developed on the basis of a comprehensive review of the literature and consists of 19 closed-ended items. Each item is designed to capture observable and objectively recordable behaviors throughout the play interaction. Expert evaluations did not necessitate any content modifications; only minor linguistic refinements were proposed. The finalized version of the form is structured into three sections: Initiating the Play Process, Play Process, and Concluding the Play Process. Items presented in Table 1 include observations such as “father responds to child’s verbal or non-verbal cues,” and “father encourages the child to take initiative during play,” which reflect dimensions of responsiveness and autonomy support during different phases of the play process, including initiating, maintaining, and concluding play.

Video recordings

Father–child play sessions were video-recorded from the moment the father initiated play until the conclusion of the interaction. All the recordings were conducted in the participants’ home environments to ensure that the interactions took place within a natural and familiar context. During the data collection process, a single video recording was made for each father–child play session. Once a mutually convenient time was determined for each dyad, a stationary camera was positioned in the designated play area with the father's consent. The camera captured the session without any external interference, focusing solely on the play interaction. The length of the recordings ranged from 4 min and 34 s to 24 min and 12 s. All videos were thoroughly reviewed by the researchers, and both verbal and behavioral exchanges between the father and child were transcribed in detail. Nonverbal cues—such as gestures, facial expressions, and behavioral signals—were also noted to preserve the contextual integrity of the interactions.

Data collection and analysis process

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the University of Health Sciences Hamidiye Scientific Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all fathers and for their children’s participation. Following this approval, the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form was developed and reviewed by two experts in child development and one in early childhood education to ensure content validity. Expert feedback was incorporated to refine item clarity, relevance, and consistency with the study’s objectives. To establish interrater reliability, 20% of the video recordings were independently coded by two researchers, yielding a Cohen’s κ coefficient of 0.84, indicating a high level of agreement between coders. Any discrepancies between coders were discussed until full consensus was reached. During the development of the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form, the theoretical foundations of father–child interactions and play processes were reviewed to ensure construct validity. In addition to expert evaluation, the content validity of the form was supported by the researchers’ academic expertise in father–child interactions and play-based observational studies. Their prior research experience contributed to ensuring that the behavioral indicators were theoretically grounded and contextually appropriate. Furthermore, a pilot study was conducted with one father and one child prior to the main data collection to assess the clarity of the observation items, evaluate the practicality of the data collection process, clarify the researcher’s role, and identify potential ethical issues in advance. The 6-min and 43-s pilot video recording was independently examined by the researchers to assess the observability and clarity of the form’s items. Upon discussion, the researchers reached a consensus on the form’s applicability within the study context. The pilot participants were not included in the main research sample. To ensure the reliability of video-based observations, the recordings were carefully reviewed using pause and replay functions during the coding process, and coders received targeted training based on standardized criteria. The pilot video was jointly examined and discussed to establish interrater consensus, and the camera setup was assessed to ensure optimal visual and auditory clarity of the father–child interactions.

Fathers of preschool-aged children residing in Istanbul were invited to participate in the study. Volunteers were fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the research; written consent was obtained from the fathers, and verbal assent was obtained from their children. Three fathers refused to participate due to work-related commitments. Fathers or children who chose not to participate were excluded from the study. After conducting interviews with 10 participants, recurring themes began to emerge, and no new information was obtained. At this point, data saturation was considered to have been achieved, and the data collection process was concluded.

Data collection took place between June and November 2024. Each observation was conducted during scheduled home visits, in which every father–child dyad participated in a semi-structured play session. To ensure a standardized starting point, the researcher provided a new set of four-colored playdoughs and briefly instructed fathers to “play with your child as you normally would.” Beyond this initial instruction and the provision of materials, the play process was entirely unstructured, allowing natural and spontaneous interactions to unfold between the father and the child.

The play took place in the area where the child typically plays, with a stationary camera discreetly positioned to record both verbal and nonverbal interactions without distraction. Once the recording began, the researcher exited the room, and the father stopped the recording when the play ended. All ten father–child pairs participated voluntarily and completed the study process without any interruptions. The video recordings obtained during the research were used solely for data analysis purposes, and participant confidentiality was strictly maintained. Upon completion of the study and publication of the findings, all video recordings were permanently and securely deleted in accordance with the ethics committee’s approval.

Following data collection, qualitative content analysis was conducted, supported by descriptive statistics for frequency reporting. The quantitative data obtained from the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form were analyzed via descriptive statistics, with the frequency of each behavioral item reported as percentages and counts. This allowed for a numerical representation of behavioral patterns during the play sessions. Concurrently, qualitative data derived from video recordings were subjected to content analysis, systematically examining verbal interactions, emotional responses, and behavioral dynamics. The resulting findings were organized into overarching themes and subthemes. This dual approach enables a comprehensive understanding of father–child play interactions by integrating numerical data with in-depth interpretive insights.

To ensure the reliability of the study, interrater agreement was calculated on the basis of data from the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form. The consistency rate was calculated by dividing the number of consistent codes by the total number of items and multiplying the result by 100 [57]. In this study, the consistency rate was found to be 96.34%. To further enhance reliability, multiple researchers were involved in the coding process. For external dependability, verbatim excerpts from father and child dialogs were included to substantiate the identified subthemes.

Lastly, for the observation item in the Father–Child Play Process Observation Form — “Did the father and child engage in verbal communication at least every 25 s during the play process?” — each video was segmented into fixed 25-s intervals. A positive mark was recorded for each interval in which verbal communication occurred between the father and the child. If more than 50% of the intervals contained silence exceeding 25 s, the interaction was coded as “No”; conversely, if verbal exchanges occurred in more than 50% of the intervals, it was coded as “Yes.” Two researchers independently analyzed the recordings using manual timestamp checks based on the video player’s time indicators and compared their codings to ensure consistency.

Observations revealed that none of the fathers exhibited motivational verbal or behavioral cues at the beginning of the play process. While half of the participants asked their children about their preferred play activity, the remaining fathers initiated the session on the basis of their own preferences. Notably, even among those who sought their children's input, most did not follow the children's suggestions and instead directed the play according to their own plans. As presented in Table 2, although five fathers inquired about their children's preferences, only two initiated play accordingly, whereas three proceeded with their own preferred play scenarios.

Observations revealed that several fathers interfered with the flow of play through both verbal and behavioral means, impeding their children's active engagement. Notably, seven fathers demonstrated dual forms of interference, including disrupting the child’s constructed play materials, criticizing the child’s intended creations or narratives, and setting verbal constraints that limited the child’s play autonomy.

The observation data revealed that fathers exhibited varied interaction patterns during the play process. Six fathers posed questions to sustain engagement. Among the fathers who asked questions, four provided time or space for their children to respond. Four allowed the child to guide the play, and four engaged in cooperative play with their child. In six out of ten cases, fathers were observed to take a more active role than their children during the play process. Only three fathers encouraged their child to describe the product they had created. In eight cases, fathers maintained verbal communication at intervals of 25 s or less, whereas two remained silent for extended periods. With respect to motivational behaviors, seven fathers offered supportive input—six used only verbal praise (e.g., “well done,” “that looks great”), whereas one combined both verbal and behavioral strategies (e.g., clapping, stroking the child’s hair) to encourage the child.

The observational findings revealed that fathers frequently occupied a central and directive role during the play process. In six out of ten recorded sessions, the father maintained a predominant presence, actively steering the course of interaction. In three cases, the child assumed the primary role, with minimal paternal engagement. Only one session demonstrated a balanced dyadic interaction, characterized by mutual and equal participation between the father and child.

Observations related to the termination of the play process revealed that the majority of fathers did not inform their children in advance about the forthcoming end of the activity. Specifically, 80% of fathers concluded the sessions without providing prior notice. Moreover, most fathers did not display behaviors indicating emotional awareness or sensitivity to their children's affective responses regarding the play outcome or the process as a whole. For example, in several cases, children attempted to show their play through creations, yet fathers ended the play without offering any verbal or behavioral acknowledgment. Emotional attunement was similarly limited at the end of the sessions; only three fathers provided verbal praise in response to their child’s apparent joy, and just one of them expressed affection through a physical gesture, such as a hug. Despite these limitations, most fathers concluded the sessions with a generally positive affective tone. These findings suggest that paternal awareness and responsiveness during the closing phase of play interactions remain limited, particularly with respect to managing closure and attending to children's emotional cues.

Findings from the Father–Child Video Recordings

The video recordings of father–child play sessions were systematically analyzed through content analysis. The findings were structured under two main themes: “Positive Attitudes and Behaviors” and “Negative Attitudes and Behaviors.” The positive theme encompassed supportive and constructive interactions that fostered the child’s development and enriched the play experience, whereas the negative theme included restrictive, critical, disengaged, or intrusive paternal behaviors. Themes and Categories of the Father–Child Play Interaction Process

Positive attitudes and behaviors

The first theme identified in the study is “positive attitudes and behaviors,” comprising four distinct categories: (1) motivational support, (2) being responsive to help requests, (3) being open to the child’s lead, and (4) asking guiding questions.

Motivational support

This category comprises verbal and behavioral responses by fathers intended to enhance the child's engagement, sustain their interest, and acknowledge their efforts throughout the play interaction. The findings revealed that such motivational responses were predominantly delivered through verbal expressions. Seven out of the ten fathers were observed to employ encouraging language or behaviors toward their children. These responses represented the most frequently observed form of positive parental involvement during father–child play. Representative examples related to this category are provided below.

During the play session with the first father, his statement, “You did a perfect job, my girl. Wow!” in response to the child’s completion of a clay eye figure was identified as a clear instance of verbal praise. Similarly, the second father’s comment, “What beautiful shapes you’re making!”, reflected his recognition of the child’s effort and provided encouraging feedback. In another case, the ninth father responded to his child’s confident remark, “I’m doing truly well!”, by saying, “You always do great,” and followed this with a kiss on the child’s head—demonstrating both verbal and physical forms of motivation.

Being responsive to help requests

This category encompasses instances in which fathers responded sensitively and supportively to their children's requests for help during play. Rather than remaining passive, these fathers actively engaged in the play process, responding to both verbal and behavioral cues. The observations revealed that 4 out of 10 fathers demonstrated such responsiveness. Illustrative examples are provided below.

During a playdough activity, one child expressed difficulty by saying, “I can’t make my sibling,” which prompted the father to offer immediate and direct assistance. In another session, a child asked, “Dad, how can I make a carrot?” The father responded with clear instructions: “You’ll roll it like this with your hand and shape it like a carrot.” These examples illustrate how some fathers adopted a supportive role by recognizing and responding to their children’s emerging needs during the play process.

Openness to the child’s lead

This category refers to instances in which fathers allow their children to lead play without interference, respecting the child's ideas, preferences, and initiative. Such behavior is recognized as a valuable interaction style that supports children's active involvement, creativity, and self-confidence. However, this pattern was observed in only 3 out of the 10 participating fathers. Illustrative examples are presented below.

In one session, a child asked, “Which one do you want, dad? The purple, the pink, the brown, or the green?” The father replied, “I want the brown one,” thus engaging in the child-initiated dialog. He followed up with questions such as “What shall we do with these?” and “What should we do now with these, my girl?” allowing the child to determine the direction of the play. Similarly, another father asked, “What should we make?”, which signals his willingness to let the child take control. When the child suggested, “Shall I make a ring for you and mommy, so you can give it to her, and she can give it back to you?”, the father accepted the idea and joined in accordingly, demonstrating a positive interaction where the child led the play process.

Asking guiding questions

This category includes inquiries posed by fathers during play to sustain engagement, enhance the child’s involvement, and prevent the interaction from becoming one-sided. These questions stimulate the child’s cognitive engagement, encourage active participation, and reflect the father’s supportive and facilitative role within the play context.

The findings revealed that only one of the ten participating fathers demonstrated this behavior. The remaining fathers did not utilize questions that would support the child’s initiative or ensure the continuity of the play. During a playdough activity in which the seventh father and his child crafted dumplings and carrots, the father asked open-ended, content-related questions such as “Should it have minced meat inside?”, “What are you making?”, “What should we use to cut this?”, and “Do you know how to make cookies, my girl?”. These inquiries enabled the child to guide the play process and demonstrated the father’s active yet collaborative engagement.

Negative attitudes and behaviors

Eight subthemes related to the negative attitudes and behaviors observed during the father–child play process were identified. These subthemes, listed in order of frequency, are as follows: (1) use of imperative language, (2) physical and verbal obstruction, (3) the father-centered play process, (4) perfectionism expectations, (5) ignoring the child’s requests, (6) rejecting the child’s suggestions, (7) criticizing the child’s play product, and (8) reflecting gender stereotypes in play. These subthemes systematically illustrate the negative approaches exhibited by fathers throughout the play process.

Use of imperative language

The most frequently observed negative behavior in father–child play interactions was the use of imperative language by fathers when addressing their children. In 8 out of 10 recorded sessions, fathers communicated via direct commands. For example, one father repeatedly instructed his child with statements such as “Take this, roll the dough” and “Push your hair back” during a playdough activity. Similarly, another father guided the play by saying, “Put it on your finger” and “Roll the dough.” Such directive language may limit the child’s ability to make autonomous decisions and hinder opportunities for creative thinking during play.

Physical and verbal obstruction

The second negative category identified in the father–child play process concerns physical and/or verbal intrusions into the child’s self-directed play narrative. This category encompasses situations in which the father directs, modifies, or interrupts behaviors initiated independently by the child during play. These intervention behaviors were observed in 7 of the 10 participating fathers. For example, in the interaction between the ninth father and his child, the child was shaping round pieces from playdough for a meal when the father intervened, stating, “We’ll place these on the plate here and put them in the oven.” In response to the child’s remark, “However, I decorated them,” the father persisted, asking, “Aren’t we going to send them for baking? Those are bagels,” thereby sustaining his intervention.

Similarly, both physical and verbal interventions were documented in the play session of the fourth father. While the child was creating family members from playdough and requested the father’s assistance in forming the arm of a human figure, the father examined the creation and suggested, “Shall we fix this part?” before dismantling the arm and reshaping the entire figure into a ball. When the child stated, “I did it, Dad,” the father countered, “You didn’t do it, son,” continuing the intervention. The child’s protest, “It was nice the way I made it,” received no acknowledgment from the father.

Father-centered play process

This category encompasses situations in father–child interactions where the father assumes the central role in play, relegating the child to a passive position. A father-centered play process occurs when the father independently dictates the play scenario, issues commands, and allows minimal opportunity for the child’s input or participation. In 6 of the 10 observed cases, the play session was predominantly initiated and sustained by the father. For example, in the interaction with the eighth father, immediately after the playdough was introduced, he began directing the activity with phrases such as “Let’s do it this way” and “Press the dough like this,” thereby constructing the entire play narrative without soliciting the child’s ideas and limiting opportunities for the child to express original play concepts.

Similarly, in interaction with the first father, he manipulated the playdough on his own while the child merely observed it. When the child remarked, “Dad, but I’m not doing anything,” the father responded, “Okay, sweetheart, wait. You will learn, and then you will do it.” This response effectively marginalized the child from active engagement. The child’s protest, “However, I have already learned,” reflects a clear eagerness to participate actively; however, this intention was constrained by the father’s dominance over the play process.

Perfectionism expectations

This category pertains to paternal directives aimed at ensuring that their children’s play products are more orderly, aesthetically pleasing, or aligned with an “ideal” standard. The expectation of perfectionism was evident in situations where the child, rather than engaging in spontaneous and creative play, was subjected to external evaluation, thereby disrupting the natural flow of the activity. Such tendencies were identified in 5 of the 10 fathers observed. For example, in the interaction with the tenth father, he prepared a playdough-based lahmacun and instructed his child to place pieces of dough “meat” on top. As the child placed them randomly, the father advised, “Place them a bit more scattered, don’t put them in the same spots,” encouraging a more structured arrangement.

A similar pattern was observed in the interaction with the fifth father. While making a playdough ring, the father’s child expressed a desire to make a cake. When the child’s creations were examined, the father remarked, “What did you make? A marble? Make it a bit nicer,” indicating that he considered the product inadequate and in need of improvement. Such remarks illustrate fathers’ tendency to uphold specific aesthetic expectations for their children’s play outcomes and to exhibit a perfectionist approach during the play process.

Ignoring the child’s requests

This category pertains to instances in the father–child play interaction where the father disregards or fails to acknowledge the child’s requests, suggestions, or creations. Such behavior reflects an interaction pattern in which the father exhibits indifference to the child’s desire to engage, potentially undermining the child’s motivation for play. This tendency was observed in 4 of the 10 fathers in the present study. For example, in the interaction with the sixth father, the child made a train from playdough and said, “Dad, look, I made a train,” but received no response. When the child tried again, saying, “Look, Dad,” the attempt was likewise ignored, followed only by a terse and indifferent reply: “Wait, son.” This example clearly illustrates an absence of responsiveness to the child’s expressed desire for active participation in the play process.

Rejecting the child’s suggestions

This category encompasses situations in which the father rejects the child’s expressed ideas or preferences during play. Such behavior constrains the child’s willingness to participate and reflects an interaction dynamic in which the father exerts dominance over the play process. This pattern was observed in 3 of the 10 fathers in the study. For example, in the interaction with the fourth father, the child proposed, “We will draw three people in blue,” yet the father rejected this suggestion and imposed his own character and color choices, directing the play according to his preferences. Similarly, in the interaction with the tenth father, after asking, “What topping do you want on your pizza?”, the child replied, “Potato,” to which the father responded, “Potato on a pizza? That’s not possible!” and proceeded to guide the play on the basis of his own choice.

Criticizing the child’s play product

This category encompasses verbal and behavioral expressions through which the father conveys dissatisfaction with the child’s play creation. This behavior was observed in 3 of the 10 fathers in the study. For example, in the interaction with the sixth father, the child made a star from playdough and said, “Dad, look, I made a star.” The father replied, “Yours is a bit flawed; let’s fix it properly—this one isn’t right,” thereby expressing disapproval. Similarly, in the interaction with the first father, as the child attached an eyebrow to a playdough face, the father asked, “What is that?” When the child responded, “An eyebrow,” the father remarked, “Look, I make it small like this—you should make it smaller,” again reiterating dissatisfaction with the child’s creation.

Reflecting gender stereotypes at play

This category encompasses instances in which fathers convey messages consistent with socially constructed gender norms and stereotypes during play. Such expressions were observed in 3 of the 10 fathers in the study. For example, in the interaction with the sixth father, when the child had playdough smeared on his face, the father remarked, “That paint hasn’t come off your face. The girls will laugh at you tomorrow,” followed by “The girls will say it looks very ugly,” thereby underscoring appearance-related social standards and the perceived need for gendered social approval. In the interaction with the fourth father, while sculpting family figures from playdough, he assigned the color red to females and blue to males, stating, “These are your mother and sister, and these are you and me,” thus reinforcing gender stereotypes through color symbolism.

Discussion

This study examines the structural and content characteristics of father–child play interactions. The findings indicate that negative attitudes and behavioral patterns were significantly more prevalent during the play process. Positive attitudes were limited to the codes “motivational support,” “responsiveness to help requests,” “openness to the child’s lead,” and “asking guiding questions,” whereas negative attitudes were more diverse, including “use of imperative language,” “physical and verbal obstruction,” “father-centered play process,” “perfectionism expectation,” “ignoring the child’s requests,” “rejecting the child’s suggestions,” “criticizing the child’s play product,” and “reflecting gender stereotypes in play.” It was observed that many fathers exhibited encouraging language or behaviors during play. This attitude was categorized in the study as motivational support, alongside three additional categories of positive attitudes and behaviors: responsiveness to help requests, openness to the child’s lead, and asking guiding questions. It was noted, however, that only a small number of participating fathers demonstrated these positive attitudes and behaviors. The themes identified in this study—motivational support and openness to the child’s guidance—suggest that fathers actively engaged in play interactions in ways that supported their children’s autonomy. These findings are consistent with Meuwissen and Carlson’s [58] research, which demonstrated that fathers’ autonomy-supportive behaviors during joint activities were positively associated with preschoolers’ executive function development. In their study, fathers who allowed children to take initiative and respected their choices without exerting excessive control fostered greater self-regulation, attentional control, and problem-solving skills in their children. Similarly, the fathers in the present study appeared to promote children’s independent thinking and cognitive flexibility by remaining open to their guidance during play. Furthermore, the themes of responsiveness to children’s help requests and asking guiding questions align with the principles of guided play outlined by Weisberg et al. [59]. Guided play emphasizes adult participation that follows the child’s interests while providing subtle scaffolding through prompts, questions, and suggestions that stimulate exploration and learning. In this context, fathers’ responsiveness and use of guiding questions observed in the current study can be viewed as a form of sensitive and facilitative interaction that encourages children’s active engagement and cognitive development. Thus, the findings highlight the significance of paternal behaviors that balance guidance and sensitivity, creating a play context that is both supportive and instructive for young children. Overall, most fathers did not initiate play with encouraging expressions or supportive behaviors, inadequately incorporated their children’s ideas into the process, and often imposed verbal or behavioral barriers that limited the child’s active participation and creative expression. In general, fathers adopted a directive, controlling, and authoritarian play style rather than a collaborative style, with a father-centered structure dominating most sessions. These patterns may stem from fathers’ internalization of traditional gender norms that position men primarily as authority figures rather than emotionally responsive caregivers. As a result, their play behaviors may inadvertently reflect societal expectations rather than being attuned to the individual needs of their children.

These findings are consistent with previous research. These qualitative findings are consistent with quantitative evidence reported by Uyanık et al. [60], who found that fathers of preschool-aged children from low socioeconomic backgrounds in Türkiye exhibited relationship patterns influenced by child gender and family size, while education level did not significantly improve the quality of father–child interactions. Their results, showing that paternal involvement often remained limited and shaped by traditional gender expectations, support the present study’s observation that fathers frequently maintained a directive rather than collaborative role during play. Taken together, these findings suggest that paternal behaviors during play are not only shaped by conscious intentions but also by embedded cultural scripts regarding masculinity, authority, and parenting roles. Teufl and Ahnert [12], in their comparison of father–child and mother–child play interactions, reported that fathers generally adopt more restrictive attitudes during play. Robinson et al. [61], in a systematic review, highlighted that controlling, intrusive, and directive paternal behaviors can negatively affect children’s cognitive, emotional, and social development. Similarly, Ryan et al. [62] reported that fathers were more inclined to assume leadership and regulate play during unstructured sessions. Laplacette and Raznoszczik [63] and Chiarello et al. [64] noted that mothers often assume more relational and supportive roles, whereas fathers tend to direct play activities. Ahnert, Teufl, and Zeller [65] emphasized that high levels of paternal direction restricted children’s initiative and reduced play quality, whereas Menashe-Grinberg and Atzaba-Poria [66] reported that fathers often controlled the games they initiated, thereby limiting children’s participation in free play.

The evidence suggests that paternal play behaviors are shaped not only by individual tendencies but also by social context and cultural norms. In Türkiye, Işıkoğlu and İvrendi (2010) reported that fathers typically engage in play initiated by the child or mother, rarely taking the lead themselves. This reflects the influence of patriarchal family structures and traditional perceptions of the paternal role on play interactions. Similarly, Cabrera and Roggman [54] and Roopnarine et al. [67] emphasized that paternal behaviors are shaped by cultural norms, family structures, and societal expectations, whereas Cabrera et al. [68] reported that nonsupportive paternal play attitudes may hinder children’s emotional and social development. In light of the present findings, it is plausible that prevailing sociocultural norms in Türkiye continue to shape fathers’ limited engagement during play, not merely due to lack of awareness, but because such behaviors are not strongly reinforced or modeled within their social environments.

Research in Türkiye indicates that fathers do spend time playing with their children; however, most studies have focused on the duration of play [69–71]. The quality of play is as important as its duration. Liu et al. [14] reported that in China, paternal engagement is meaningful only when accompanied by high-quality interactions, whereas inconsistent or negative parenting styles can adversely affect children’s behavioral and emotional development. This underscores that in father–child interactions, the quality of time spent is more critical than the quantity.

The findings of this study indicate that father–child play interactions provide a meaningful context in which fathers demonstrate emotional attunement, guidance, and responsiveness. These results suggest that paternal involvement in play should be evaluated not solely by the amount of time spent but by the quality of interactions that foster children’s emotional, social, and cognitive development. The spontaneous and reciprocal nature of play allows fathers to strengthen their relational bonds with their children and to cultivate positive paternal identities through shared enjoyment and collaboration.

In conclusion, both the present findings and previous research highlight that fathers who adopt the role of a co-player—rather than that of a manager or controller—make more substantial contributions to their children’s development. Therefore, intervention programs aiming to enhance father–child play should prioritize not only increasing fathers’ playtime but also improving the quality of their engagement. Approaches that emphasize sensitivity, reciprocity, and emotional attunement in play are more likely to meet children’s developmental and relational needs effectively. These insights can guide early childhood educators and policymakers in developing evidence-based initiatives that encourage fathers’ active, sensitive, and child-centered participation in play.

This study has certain limitations. The sample consisted of only 10 fathers living in Istanbul with preschool-aged children, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Although the qualitative design and use of video observations provided in-depth data, fathers may have consciously or unconsciously modified their behavior in front of the camera. Furthermore, the exclusive use of playdough activities limited the observation of alternative interaction patterns that might occur in different types of play. The cross-sectional nature of the data also precludes insights into how father–child interactions may vary across contexts and over time. Future research should include larger, more socioeconomically and culturally diverse samples and employ longitudinal observations.

Conclusion and Implications

This study sought to explore the dynamics of father–child interactions during play, aiming to deepen the understanding of paternal roles within this everyday relational context. The findings revealed that most fathers tended to remain unresponsive to their children’s play ideas, often taking control of the play process and directing activities according to their own preferences. Fathers frequently introduced verbal and behavioral constraints that limited children’s creativity, autonomy, and active participation in play.

Although several fathers displayed supportive and responsive behaviors—acknowledging children’s play initiatives and participating collaboratively—these instances were relatively rare. Overall, the play process was dominated by a father-centered and control-oriented structure, suggesting that traditional notions of authority, masculinity, and parental power continue to shape paternal behavior within the play context.

The findings underscore the critical role of fathers’ attitudes and behaviors in children’s socioemotional development during early childhood. When fathers dominate or restrict play, they may inadvertently suppress children’s problem-solving abilities, self-regulation, empathy, and cooperative skills. In contrast, responsive and emotionally attuned paternal involvement enhances children’s confidence, emotional expression, and overall play experience.

From a practical standpoint, these results highlight the importance of designing and implementing father-inclusive parent education programs. Intervention models should focus on helping fathers recognize the developmental value of play, support children’s play ideas, share leadership during play, and respond empathetically to children’s emotional cues. Early childhood education settings should also encourage father participation in play-based activities, enabling fathers to engage meaningfully with their children and strengthen emotional bonds through play.

From a theoretical perspective, this study emphasizes that fatherhood should be conceptualized not only through the lenses of protection or provision but also through interactive and relational practices such as play. Examining paternal involvement in play offers a nuanced understanding of family dynamics, emotional attunement, and the construction of gendered parental roles. Future studies could expand upon these findings by exploring father–child play across diverse socioeconomic and cultural groups, as well as by integrating mixed-method designs that combine observational and quantitative data to provide a more holistic account of how paternal attitudes influence children’s cognitive and emotional growth.

In conclusion, the findings point to the need for developing more balanced, sensitive, and child-centered father–child play interactions. The ways in which fathers engage with their children through play are not merely reflections of individual parenting styles but are also shaped by broader cultural expectations and social norms. Therefore, both policy initiatives and family support programs should adopt a comprehensive approach that promotes active, responsive, and developmentally supportive paternal involvement in play.
