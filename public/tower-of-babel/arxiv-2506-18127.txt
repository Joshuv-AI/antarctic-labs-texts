An Extended Model of Fractional-Dimensional Space for Anisotropic Solids with Deformed Derivatives
José Weberszpil

Abstract
In this work, we extend a fractional-dimensional space model for anisotropic solids by incorporating a q q -deformed derivative operator, inspired by Tsallis’ nonadditive entropy framework. This generalization provides an analytical framework for exploring anisotropic thermal properties, within a unified and flexible mathematical formalism. We derive modified expressions for the phonon density of states and specific heat capacity, highlighting the impact of the deformation parameters on thermodynamic behavior. We apply the model to various solid-state materials, achieving excellent agreement with experimental data, across a wide temperature range and demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing anisotropic and subextensive effects in real systems.

Derivation of the Cumulative Number of States in Fractional-Dimensional
Space

To determine the cumulative number of quantum states
G(E)
up to
a given energy
E
in a fractional-dimensional space of Hausdorff

\alpha

The number of quantum states with wavevector magnitude less than
k

G(E)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\int_{|\vec{k}|\leq k(E)}d^{\alpha}k,

(3)

where
d^{\alpha}k
denotes the infinitesimal volume element in momentum
space with Hausdorff dimension
\alpha

The volume of a hypersphere of radius
k
in
\alpha

is:

\int_{|\vec{k}|\leq k}d^{\alpha}k=\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\int_{0}^{k}k^{\prime\alpha-1}dk^{\prime}=\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\frac{k^{\alpha}}{\alpha}.

(4)

Therefore, the cumulative number of states becomes:

G(E)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot k(E)^{\alpha},

(5)

where the factor of
2
accounts for spin degeneracy.

To express

E=E_{0}+\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}\quad\Rightarrow\quad k(E)=\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(E-E_{0})},

(6)

where
E_{0}

k^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2}(E-E_{0})^{\alpha/2}.

(7)

Substituting into the expression for

G(E)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2}\cdot(E-E_{0})^{\alpha/2}.

(8)

C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\alpha/2},

(9)

so that:

G(E)=C_{\alpha}(E-E_{0})^{\alpha/2}.

3.1
Specific Heat: Derivation of Equation’s Standard Debye Approach

We evaluate the internal energy associated with phonon modes in a
medium of fractional Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
:

U(T)=\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\hbar\omega\cdot g_{p}(\omega)\cdot n_{B}(\omega,T)\,d\omega,

(21)

where
g_{p}(\omega)
is the phonon density of states,
n_{B}(\omega,T)=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}-1)

is the Bose-Einstein distribution, and
\omega_{D}
is the (temperature-independent)
Debye cutoff frequency.

From the previous section, the phonon density of states in fractional-dimensional
space is:

g_{p}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\omega^{\alpha-1},

(22)

where
v_{s}
is the speed of sound.

We change variables to
x=\hbar\omega/k_{B}T
, so
\omega=\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}x

and
d\omega=\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}dx
. The energy becomes:

\displaystyle U(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\frac{\hbar\omega^{\alpha}}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}-1}\,d\omega

(23)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\right)^{\alpha+1}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\hbar\cdot\int_{0}^{\theta_{D}/T}\frac{x^{\alpha}}{e^{x}-1}dx,

(24)

where
\theta_{D}=\hbar\omega_{D}/k_{B}
is the Debye temperature.

In the low-temperature limit
T\ll\theta_{D}
, the upper limit of
the integral tends to infinity and the integral becomes:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha}}{e^{x}-1}dx=\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha+1),

(25)

where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function.

Therefore, the internal energy scales as:

U(T)\propto T^{\alpha+1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C_{v}(T)=\frac{dU}{dT}\propto T^{\alpha}.

(26)

This confirms that in a space with Hausdorff dimension
\alpha
,
the specific heat scales with temperature as
T^{\alpha}
at low
temperatures.

3.2
Specific Heat with Deformed Phonon Density of States

To include non-linear effects in the phonon statistics, we apply the

q
-deformed derivative operator, Eq.(
1
),
to the cumulative phonon mode distribution
G_{p}(\omega)
(the undeformed
expression) derived in Eq. (
19
).

The
q
-deformed phonon density of states becomes:

g_{p,q}(\omega):=\mathcal{D}_{q}[G_{p}](\omega)=\left[1+(1-q)\omega\right]\cdot\frac{dG_{p}}{d\omega}.

(27)

Computing the derivative:

\frac{dG_{p}}{d\omega}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\omega^{\alpha-1},

(28)

we obtain:

g_{p,q}(\omega)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\omega^{\alpha-1}\cdot\left[1+(1-q)\omega\right].

(29)

Now, the internal energy becomes:

U_{q}(T)=\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\hbar\omega\cdot g_{p,q}(\omega)\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}-1}d\omega.

(30)

Using the change of variables
x=\hbar\omega/k_{B}T
and
\omega=\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}x
,
we obtain:

\displaystyle U_{q}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\right)^{\alpha+1}\cdot\hbar

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int_{0}^{\theta_{D}/T}\frac{x^{\alpha}\left[1+(1-q)\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}x\right]}{e^{x}-1}dx,

(31)

where
\theta_{D}=\hbar\omega_{D}/k_{B}
is the Debye temperature.

Separating terms inside the integral:

U_{q}(T)=AT^{\alpha+1}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha}}{e^{x}-1}dx+(1-q)\cdot\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+1}}{e^{x}-1}dx\right],

(32)

where the prefactor
A
is:

A=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\hbar\cdot\left(\frac{k_{B}}{\hbar}\right)^{\alpha+1}.

(33)

Using standard integrals:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\mu}}{e^{x}-1}dx=\Gamma(\mu+1)\zeta(\mu+1),

we obtain for the internal energy:

U_{q}(T)=AT^{\alpha+1}\left[\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha+1)+(1-q)\cdot\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar}\cdot\Gamma(\alpha+2)\zeta(\alpha+2)\right].

(34)

Differentiating with respect to
T
, the specific heat becomes:

\displaystyle C_{V,q}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{dU_{q}}{dT}

\displaystyle=A(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha+1)T^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad+A(1-q)\cdot\frac{k_{B}}{\hbar}\left[(\alpha+2)\Gamma(\alpha+2)\zeta(\alpha+2)T^{\alpha+1}\right].

(35)

This expression reduces to the classical Debye result in the limit

q\to 1
and introduces a temperature-dependent correction governed
by
(1-q)T^{\alpha+1}
.

3.3
Factorization of the Deformed Specific Heat Expression

From the full derivation of the deformed phonon specific heat, we
obtained:

C_{V,q}(T)=A\left[(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha+1)T^{\alpha}+(1-q)\cdot\frac{k_{B}}{\hbar}\cdot(\alpha+2)\Gamma(\alpha+2)\zeta(\alpha+2)T^{\alpha+1}\right],

(36)

where the prefactor
A
is given by:

A=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{\alpha/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{v_{s}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\hbar\cdot\left(\frac{k_{B}}{\hbar}\right)^{\alpha+1}.

(37)

Let us define the constants:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=A(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)\zeta(\alpha+1),

(38)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=A\cdot\frac{k_{B}}{\hbar}(\alpha+2)\Gamma(\alpha+2)\zeta(\alpha+2).

(39)

Then the specific heat becomes:

C_{V,q}(T)=A_{1}T^{\alpha}+A_{2}(1-q)T^{\alpha+1},

(40)

which can also be factorized as:

C_{V,q}(T)=T^{\alpha}\left[A_{1}+A_{2}(1-q)T\right].

(41)

It is important to note that this expression has physically meaningful
prefactors, derived from the deformed Debye model in fractional-dimensional
space.

Debye Saturation Behavior and the Role of the Cutoff Term

In real crystalline solids, the specific heat exhibits a well-known
saturation behavior as the temperature approaches or exceeds the Debye
temperature
T_{D}
. This arises from the complete thermal excitation
of all phonon modes in the system.

At high temperatures,
T\gg T_{D}
, all vibrational modes are populated,
and the heat capacity asymptotically approaches the classical Dulong-Petit
limit:

C_{V}(T)\rightarrow 3R

for monatomic solids.

However, the original entropic model,

C_{V,q}(T)=T^{\alpha}\left[A_{1}+A_{2}(1-q)\,T\right]

can grow unboundedly for
\alpha>1
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, potentially
diverging or overshooting the physical behavior.

To incorporate the observed saturation of heat capacity at high temperatures,
we include a cutoff factor in our deformed model, of the form

\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}},

(42)

This factor mimics the asymptotic behavior predicted by Debye theory,
where the specific heat approaches a finite value as all phonon modes
are excited. For
T\ll T_{D}
, the correction is negligible, preserving
the low-temperature power-law scaling, while for
T\gg T_{D}
, the
term suppresses divergence and reflects the physical saturation of

C_{V}(T)
. The exponent
n
controls the sharpness of this transition
and can be adjusted to match the observed thermal behavior of the
material.

Inclusion of a Debye-Like Saturation Term.

To mimic the physical saturation and avoid divergence, we include
a cutoff factor of the form:

\left(1+\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1}.

This multiplicative term suppresses high-temperature growth and ensures
that the model remains bounded. The behavior of this term is as follows:

•

For
T\ll T_{D}
:

\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\ll 1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left(1+\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1}\approx 1,

meaning the factor has negligible effect in the low-temperature regime.

•

For
T\gg T_{D}
:

\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\gg 1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left(1+\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1}\rightarrow 0,

meaning the specific heat growth is suppressed and saturates.

Phenomenological Flexibility.

The parameter
n
controls the sharpness of the saturation transition:

•

n=2
: smooth decay, similar to a Gaussian-type cutoff,

•

n=4
or
n=6
: sharper transition near
T_{D}
,

•

n\rightarrow\infty
: idealized step-function cutoff at
T=T_{D}

(nonphysical, but useful for contrast).

This flexibility allows one to tailor the high-temperature behavior
of the model based on empirical data, ensuring a smooth interpolation
between the low-temperature scaling and the physically correct asymptotic
saturation.

The final expression, which we refer to as the
\emph{entropicmodel}
,
is given by:

C_{V,q}(T)=T^{\alpha}\left[A_{1}+A_{2}(1-q)\,T\right]\left(1+\left(\frac{T}{T_{D}}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1}.

(43)

The term
\emph{entropicmodel}
is employed here to emphasize the
role of the nonextensive entropy parameter
q
, which modulates the
system’s deviation from classical extensive thermodynamics.
The presence of the deformation encodes thermodynamic anomalies often
observed in complex or anisotropic materials.

Justification for Using

Although the entropic model developed here is formally structured

Therefore, throughout this work, we adopt the experimentally measured

5.1
Discussion

The optimized values
q<1
obtained from experimental fitting, across
several materials (see Table
1
and
the literature sources at Table
2
),
suggest subextensive statistical behavior, often associated with underlying
structural disorder, phonon confinement, or heterogeneous connectivity.
These features are particularly relevant in nanostructured or anisotropic
media. This regime, as understood within the framework of nonadditive
statistical mechanics
[
tsallis1988possible
]
, typically reflects
the presence of long-range interactions, fractal-like structures,
or topological constraints in the system. In particular, for nanostructured
or amorphous solids such as cobalt nanowires and bismuth silicate,

q<1
is indicative of restricted phase space accessibility due to
disorder or anisotropy.

The entropic dimensional parameter
\alpha<3
, when interpreted in
analogy with the low-temperature Debye law
C_{V}\sim T^{3}
for
three-dimensional phonon systems, provides insight into effective
vibrational dimensionality. Lower values of
\alpha
suggest phonon
confinement, surface-dominated dynamics, or a deviation from the ideal
three-dimensional phonon density of states. For instance, in nanostructured
or layered materials, phonon dispersion is altered due to boundary
conditions and quantum confinement effects, recovering the classical
derivative in the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, thereby ensuring consistency
with conventional thermodynamic descriptions.

The saturation factor introduced in the entropic model,

\left(1+\left(\frac{T_{D}}{T}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1},

provides a phenomenological but thermodynamically consistent mechanism
to interpolate between the low-temperature power-law behavior and
the high-temperature saturation expected from the Dulong-Petit law.
This factor allows the model to respect both phonon freezing at low

T
and full mode activation as
T\gg T_{D}
, without overestimating

C_{p}
in the mid-to-high temperature regime as the classical Debye
model may.

Quantitatively, the model demonstrates excellent agreement with experimental
data across the entire measured temperature range, with typical relative
errors remaining below 2%. This is a significant improvement over
the classical Debye approach in materials with strong anharmonicity,
structural anisotropy, or disordered phases. The flexibility of the
entropic formalism, combined with a minimal number of adjustable parameters,
positions it as a viable alternative for modeling real solids with
complex thermal behaviors.

These findings support the broader interpretation that the modified
entropic saturation model not only fits data well but also encodes
physically meaningful deviations from ideal crystalline behavior.
Future extensions may incorporate explicit microscopic justifications
for the
q
-deformation in terms of phonon scattering, fractal geometry,
or Tsallis-type partition functions.

6
Conclusions

We presented a generalized formalism incorporating deformed derivatives
into the fractional-dimensional space model for anisotropic solids.
Detailed derivations show how the
q
-deformation modifies density
of states and specific heat. This enhances the physical interpretability
and flexibility of fractional models, with relevance to modern quantum
and thermal systems.

In this work, we have proposed and tested a generalized entropic model
for the specific heat capacity of solids, given by

C_{V,q}^{\text{mod}}(T)=T^{\alpha}\left[A_{1}+A_{2}(1-q)T\right]\left[1+\left(\frac{T_{D}}{T}\right)^{n}\right]^{-1},

(45)

which effectively blends low-temperature power-law behavior with high-temperature
saturation, in analogy with Debye-like phonon models. The model introduces
tunable parameters
\alpha
,
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
,
q
,
T_{D}
,
and
n
, allowing for flexible but physically interpretable fitting
of experimental specific heat data.

We have validated the model against high-resolution experimental data
for a range of materials, including:

•

Synthetic sapphire (Al
2
O
3
), using tabulated DSC reference
data,

•

Quartz (SiO
2
),

•

Germanium (Ge),

•

Bismuth (Bi),

•

Antimony (Sb), and

•

Bismuth silicate compounds.

The model demonstrates excellent agreement with experimental results
across a broad temperature range, showing residuals within or below
experimental uncertainty. The success of the fit is attributable to:

1.

The
T^{\alpha}
prefactor, which models low-temperature phonon activation.

2.

The linear
(1-q)T
deformation, which introduces entropy-based corrections.

3.

The high-temperature saturation term, which prevents divergence and
simulates Dulong–Petit behavior.

We have also tested a Tsallis-type saturation variant,

C_{V,q}^{\text{Tsallis}}(T)=T^{\alpha}\left[A_{1}+A_{2}(1-q)T\right]\left[1+(1-q_{s})\left(\frac{T_{D}}{T}\right)^{n}\right]^{-\frac{1}{1-q_{s}}},

(46)

but found that the simpler Debye-like saturation yields more stable
and accurate fits across the tested materials.

Summarizing, the proposed entropic model offers a powerful yet simple
framework to describe specific heat capacity over wide temperature
ranges, surpassing classical models in flexibility while maintaining
physical plausibility. Its performance suggests applicability in both
theoretical modeling and practical data fitting, and warrants consideration
for broader adoption in thermophysical studies.

Future work, we will explore its predictive capacity for disordered
systems, low-dimensional materials, and thermal transport phenomena
linked to entropy production.
