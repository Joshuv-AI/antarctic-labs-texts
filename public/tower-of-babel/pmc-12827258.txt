Impact of spatial lesion symmetry on the structural response of hydroxylated B-DNA
Authors: Bahare Noohi, Mansour Zahedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oxidative/nitrosative stresses, common characters of inflamed/cancer tissues, produce reactive oxygen/nitrogen species that alter guanine, such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-hydroxy-dG)—a lesion highly involved in carcinogenesis. Although its biological relevance is established, structural impact of such lesions remains partially clarified. Here, reactive molecular dynamics (RMD) simulations have been used for examining spatial distribution of the hydroxylated guanines dictates canonical B-DNA’s 3D structure towards canonical B-DNA structure. Through the Dickerson–Drew dodecamer, a standard, five schemes of hydroxylation have been modeled, such as global (MtOH), site-visited (MdOH, MlOH), and orientation-dependent terminal schemes (Ml2r14OH, Ml2r24OH). Five helical deformation descriptors—center-of-base-pair distance (COBP), propeller twist angle (PTA), global/local twist angle (GTA/LTA), and end-to-end DNA length—were obtained from equilibrium trajectory for encoding local/global disturbances structure quantitatively. The results display a clear-cut priority of deformation: fully hydroxylated duplexes display isotropic elongation and wide unwinding, while vertically assembled terminal lesions retain almost native helical structure, oppositely cross-axis lesions, which impose collaborative end-fraying with wide alterations. The results illustrate that more than lesion occurrence, positional symmetry decisively dictates the stability of DNA, giving mechanistic information relevant for mutagenesis, genome instability, and pathogenetic processes under oxidative stress.

Introduction

DNA continuously experiences a wide range of endogenous as well as exogenous reactive species that compromise its structural stability and genetic integrity. Reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) have special prominence, particularly in facilitating base alterations that initiate cascades leading to mutagenesis. Four major nitrosating/nitrating species—nitric oxide (NO·), dinitrogen trioxide (N2O3), nitrogen dioxide (NO2·), and peroxynitrite (ONOO−)—are formed during inflammation reactions and cellular signaling, respectively, and have been shown, individually, to react with nucleobases with specific yet mutually complementing modalities. NO· is autooxidized to yield N2O3, a very reactive nitrosating species that attacks the primary amines of DNA bases through deamination reactions. NO2·, formed on oxidation of NO• or on homolysis of ONOO−, attacks, almost exclusively, the guanine C8 position, giving rise to nitrated lesions like 8-nitroguanine (8-nitroG). Concurrently, ONOO− breaks down into NO2· and ·OH, adding nitrative, yet oxidative, vulnerability, respectively. These routes yield diagnostic lesions, such as 8-nitroG and the 5-guanidino-4-nitroimidazole, which impede local electronic environment and hydrogen bond donation/ acceptance patterns of guanine, giving rise, respectively, to mispairing events, as well as inflammation-related mutagenesis, Fig. 11–9.

In comparison, ROS are continuously generated in cells, mainly by mitochondrial respiration, UV irradiation, and other oxidative stresses, and they cause a wider and chemically more variegated range of guanine lesions (Fig. 2). The major four oxidants—hydroxyl radicals (·OH), superoxide anions (O2·−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and singlet oxygen (1O2)—attack guanine predominantly for its lowest oxidation potential amongst the nucleobases. ·OH radicals target the C8 position, forming 8-hydroxy-2′-deguanosine (8-hydroxy-dG) and 8-oxo-guanine (8-oxoG), which are signature premutagenic lesions10,11. Further oxidation conversions yield the secondaries such as oxazolone and nox-dG, formed via ring opening and advanced redox alterations. These lesions are structurally variegated and generally more destabilizing than nitrated analogues, because they disturb both π-system alteration, and hydrogen-bonding networking of guanine. Importantly, the oxidative pathways are continuously active in vivo, whereas nitrative pathways are generally activated during inflammatory conditions. This disparity of frequency, persistence, and structural effects makes oxidative lesions, much more common, robust, and oncogenically significant. This biological domination compellingly justifies the current study’s centrality towards considering the oxidative guanine lesions over nitrated analogues, Fig. 112–17.

The three-dimensional structure of canonical B-DNA allows for a specific structure reference for assessing the impact on the double helix resulting from modifications at the bases. The Dickerson–Drew dodecamer (DDD) sequence, d(CGCGAATTCGCG)2, is amongst the most highly characterized B-DNA geometries and has established a standard for DNA deformation, both experimentally and theoretically, Fig. 2. Modification at the guanine at the position-C8, either through nitration or oxidation, is likely to create local, and at times, a textural, distortion of DNA geometry. The distortion, in turn, manifests through shifts of pivotal structure parameters such as distance between inter-base, propeller twist, and local/global helical twist, all of which prove important for base stacking, hydrogen bonding, and overall stability, alongside helix biological readability18–23.

In biological molecules, the oxidation damage does not homogeneously permeate the double helix. Other factors like steric accessibility, sites for hydrogen-bonding, and stability for base-pairing stacking stacking vary between terminal ends and internal DNA regions. Terminal guanines, for instance, are more stereically crowded and thus more prone open for the attack with the hydroxyl radical, allowing their oxidative modification a greater likelihood at physiologic stress. This positional variations for 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-hydroxy-dG) formation gives rise to a primary structure question: does the effects of the oxidative lesions for guanine on DNA geometry rely on their spatial arrangement spatially along the helix24–28? This question is of deep biological relevance. Lysis of 8-hydroxy-dG and corresponding oxidative lesions has a close correlation with carcinogenesis, especially for the initiation of G→T transversion lesions at the oxidative hotspots of lung, colon, breast, and hepatocarcinomas cancers. Rearing the positional dependence of the structure distortion caused by lesions is then mandatory for detailing how DNA damage is being conveyed into lesional effects29–33.

According to the importance of this issue, in the present study, reactive molecular dynamics simulations were employed to address how varying the spatial distribution of 8-hydroxy-dG affects both local and global structures of B-DNA. Detailed information on model construction, lesion patterns, solvation box setup, and structural analysis protocols is provided in the following sections.

Materials and methods

Lesion structures

As the structural reference, DDD sequence, was used to represent B-form DNA containing a complete right-handed helical turn. To examine positional heterogeneity of oxidative damage, six systems were constructed: a lesion-free control (NDNA), a fully hydroxylated duplex with all guanines modified at the C8 position (MtOH), a duplex modified only in the lower half of the helix (MdOH), one modified in the left half (MlOH), in order to investigate the effect of structural symmetry relative to horizontal and vertical axes of B-DNA, and two terminal lesion distributions (Ml2r14OH and Ml2r24OH) corresponding to different terminal placements across or along the duplex axes. These arrangements emulate the reduced steric crowding at helix termini, allowing systematic comparison of lesion positions. Schematics of these patterns are provided in Fig. 3.

Simulation box and solvation

Based on anisotropic experimental structure18,19, a duplex was held at the center of a rectangular simulation box (Lx = 35.3 Å, Ly = 42.0 Å, Lz = 50.0 Å), with the helical axis along z alignment. The volume around was populated with randomly oriented waters using PACKMOL34 to produce a bulk density of 1.010 ± 0.002 g cm−335, which provided at least three solvent shells surrounding the DNA along the x and y axes. The x, y dimensions had periodic boundary functions, and reflector walls were set al.ong z for eliminating spurious interactions between periodic images along the helix axis without a rise in system size36 (Fig. 4).

Reactive force field and molecular dynamics simulation

All molecular dynamics simulations employed the ReaxFF reactive force field, that currently provides the most reliable description of DNA structural dynamics in pure aqueous environments37–46 within the LAMMPS package46,47. ReaxFF provides dynamic partial charge equilibration and accurately describes bond polarization and local rearrangements induced by lesions, even in the absence of net chemical reactions49. This capability is crucial for modeling subtle structural perturbations in DNA under oxidative modification.

Simulations were carried out within the NVT ensemble according to a four-stage protocol. Energy minimization was carried out for a conjugate gradient algorithm, which had convergence tolerances of 10−4 for energy and 10−6 kcal mol−1 Å−1 for force. Thermalization from 0 K to 300 K was carried out over 80 ps using a Nose–Hoover thermostat, which had a damping constant of 10 fs. Equilibration for 100 ps at 300 K then occurred. Production runs then ensued. A 0.2 fs integration time step was employed throughout50.

To increase statistical reliability, the last 4 ps of each equilibration were sampled at every 400 fs to produce 10 different starting points. Each snapshot was used for a standalone 200 ps production trajectory, giving ten replicas per system. The observables were first averaged over time for each trajectory, then over the replicas, to decrease statistical fluctuations. For a typical representative system, the production was carried out for 800 ps for one instance to confirm stability of temperature energy over longer time-scales50.

Results and discussion

In a quantitative assessment of how deformations resulting from hydroxylation lesions change DNA structure, five geometric descriptors mutually supplemental were accessed: center-of-base-pair distance (COBP), propeller twist angle (PTA), global/local twist angle (GTA/LTA), and end-to-end DNA length. These descriptors, when used together, provide a holistic view of deformations at lesion-induced deformations at numerous structure length scales. COBP traces base-pair long-range spacings along the axis tractably tracking regularities of base-lying stacks along the helix axis. PTA traces intra-coplanarity of base-pair constituents and hydrogen bond geometries. The global twist angle (GTA) measures relative helical rotation on adjacent base pairs along the duplex DNA strand. Specifically, GTA is given as the angle between the helical axis between the C′1α– C′1β, vectors for each base pair, its immediate successor, and that for its successor. On the other hand, the local twist angle (LTA), measures twist between neighbor nucleobases on a given duplex strand. Here, LTA is given as angle between the C′1α– C′1α/C′1β– C′1β vectors on α/β, track, corresponding one on successor base along same track. Further, duplex end-to-end length was computed for capturing global stretching or compaction effects on the duplex. Here, duplex length was given for distance between midpoints on first, (C′1α– C′1β at base pair 1–24), and last (C′1α– C′1β at base pair 12–13), base pair mid points along duplex helix axis. The C′1α– C′1β segment is the bond connecting the α and β strands’ C1′ carbon atoms on the deoxyribose sugars within a specific base pair. Likewise, the N′1α– N′1β segment links the pairing nitrogen atoms on the two base molecules at a given base pair, which are attached on α and β strands respectively on their own C1′ atoms, that all depicted in Fig. 3. These descriptors were selected from equilibrium MD simulation traces, time-averaged, and averaged across replicas, then analyzed per se, so both overall structure-piercing, globally distributed structure disturbances introduced on specific lesion distributions18,19,50:

Across all four descriptors, the lesion patterns display qualitatively consistent trends in structural disturbance, although the exact rank ordering differs somewhat between parameters. The duplex that is fully hydroxylated (MtOH) exhibits largest departures from native NDNA structure, with upward-shifted of COBP, propeller twist angle, globally, locally twist angle values, in most base pairs. These are symptoms of a helix-wide unwinding, elongation due to cumulative π–π stacking agitation disturbances, weakened base-pair coplanarity disturbances. In contrast, lesion-free NDNA exhibits canonical B-DNA structure, with COBP near 5.4 Å, propeller twist angle, globally, locally twist angle values bunched at near 21°, 34° and 37° respectively, with the correct regular pitch for a helix, Fig. 5a, and Table 1a, with robust stack interactions.

Local variations cause spatially nonsymmetric distortions reflecting their location along the duplex. The MdOH structure, localized on the nether half, causes extensive movements of COBP and twist parameters on the nether half, while MlOH operates more diffused on the center, causing moderate, but diffuse deformations. Notably, local effects are differently perceived by the four measures: PTA exhibits spottier, local indexes of intra-pair distortion, while GTA and LTA report how local such disturbances diffuse along the axis of the helix, oftentimes growing around terminal regions, Fig. 5; Table 1. The two terminal lesion schemes, Ml2r14OH, then Ml2r24OH, then have highest contrast. While both have duplex terminal lesions, their effects on structural parameters go strongly in opposite directions. Ml2r14OH, with lesions on perpendicular axes, generates large end-fraying signatures: COBP opens noticeably but not severely towards ends, PTA exhibits terminal flattening that is strong, while both GTA and LTA exhibit extreme unwinding that propagates inwardly. There exists a strong contrast for Ml2r24OH, with vertical axis lesions, which maintains the profiles closest to native duplex on all four descriptors. COBP maintains almost-regular separations, PTA maintains signature-negative values, while GTA/LTA exhibit small devolutions from 36°, characteristic of vertical alignment disrupting the helical framework much less than cross-axis schemes, Fig. 5; Table 1.

This reciprocal bilaterality of terminal dispensations betrays a flagship structure rule: terminal lesion alignment controls terminal damage redeployment of mechanical stress across the duplex. Trans-axis locations compromise both longitudinally-arranged pileup, intra-pair coplanarity, respectively, with mutual deflections, while vertical-axis lesions compromise almost entirely on a geometrical modality, thus holding overall helix order more forcefully. These findings are replicated incessantly along COBP, PTA, GTA, and LTA, independently of differential sensitivities—locally highest at PTA, while LTA identifies fine strand-level aberrancies despite overall twist being relatively preserved.

Overall-structural analysis, gives a clear suggestion towards a hierarchy for amongst the hydroxylation patterns. Here, MtOH provides the least disrupting configuration, which led overall stretching, loss of base-pair planarity, large-scale unwinding, spread across whole duplex. Ml2r24OH, however, has almost native-structural features for all the descriptors, even terminal lesions—to suggest stabilization due to vertical axial alignment.

Of the three intermediate conformations (MdOH, MlOH, and Ml2r14OH), a finer-scale ordering is evident. Ml2r14OH has highest departures of the group, which are defined predominantly by large end-fraying and inner-inward-extended cooperative distortions developed about the ends. MdOH drops centrally, being defined by highly localized spatial perturbations corresponding to its lower-half lesion site localizations. MlOH has least extensive departures of the trio, which have moderate, more centrally-extended distortions occurring about midsection areas of the helix, which shows that the disruption of the vertical axis of the DNA structure causes less distortion.

In general, these findings emphasize that lesion orientation and positional symmetry, rather than lesion number per se, determine extent, extent of, and severity on structural disorders of structurally suppressed duplexes of hydroxylated DNA. Cross-axis terminal conformations destabilize the duplex cooperatively, while increasing terminal effects. Vertical alignment conserves regularity of the helix best.

The end-to-end length of duplex is a main world-wide descriptor that complements angular parameters through registering deformations like stretching or squeezing on the helix axis. For this work, the length of the DNA was defined as the distance between terminal phosphate mass centers on the two complement strands, after translation and rotation overall had been subtracted out. This definition, based on previously calibrate protocols, is a measure of macroscopic cohesion of duplex and is particularly responsive to end-fraying, unwinding of helix, or compaction effects that may not be entirely reproduced within local or world-wide twist parameters. Length values were extracted from equilibrated replicas production trajectory and averaged temporally and over replicas for statistical rigidity.

As per the results are tabulated in Table 2, NDNA exhibits a length of 36.22 Å on average, in great accord with the experimental landmark (36.62 Å)18,19, with a very modest standard deviation (0.10 Å), affirming the stability and reliability of the simulation protocol. When all guanines are hydroxylated (MtOH), the duplex length reduces substantially to 35.26 Å, nearly 1 Å less than NDNA, showing a marked axial contraction. This reduction probably results from the cumulative π–π stacking interactions deformation across the duplex, giving rise, despite unwinding seen from the angular descriptors, to a modest contraction of the helix. A similar, although somewhat less moderate, reduction is seen for the MdOH and MlOH conformations (35.37 Å and 35.36 Å, respectively), both of which epimerize just one half of the duplex. Their values almost identical suggest that placing the lesions at the vertical or horizontal half of the helix has a similar effect at a whole length, indicating localized disarrays propagating along the axis just partially.

The two terminal lesion patterns have particularly intriguing and opposite trends. Ml2r14OH, with lesions at both axis termini, gives a mean length of 36.03 Å, which is ridiculously close to NDNA (difference ≈ 0.19 Å), demonstrating that this terminal configuration preserves duplex length virtually entirely. Ml2r24OH, which locates lesions at horizontally oriented terminal lesions, however, has the highest contraction of all of the modified systems, with a mean of 35.18 Å—about 1.04 Å short of NDNA and 1.44 Å short of experiment—and a relatively high standard deviation (0.08 Å). This is particularly remarkable because, for the angular descriptors (COBP, PTA, GTA, LTA), Ml2r24OH consistently had the closest match of native structure trend, yet for length it has the coolest trend. This indicates that horizontally oriented terminal lesions have the ability of causing axial compaction without dramatically altering helical twist, likely due to subtle end-closing effects or less end-fraying, resulting in tighter end stacks that correspondingly compact the molecule.

Sorting duplexes with mutant duplexes according to degree of deviation from native length provides: Ml2r24OH (strongest contraction) > MtOH > MdOH ~ MlOH > Ml2r14OH (most deviant). Outgrowing alterations are outgrowing the combined SDs, which thus indicate alterations are statistically significant. Generally, length analysis resolves two vivid schemes for axial deformation: general contraction being imposed for the fully hydroxylated duplex imposed by homogeneous stacking perturbation, and terminal end-driven contraction for Ml2r24OH based on terminal lesion lining being structurally distinct. These effects illustrate that angular distortions and axial compaction are mutually entangled, and ends have determining influence on defining overarching geometry of damaged hairpin duplex. Ironically, the Ml2r24OH arrangement is highly structurally deceiving: preserving near-native twist profiles, yet unleashing strongest axial shortening, which backs compelling argument for study of length, and complementing twist, for complete lesion-induced structural perturbation characterization.

Conclusions

Elucidating how base oxide alterations impact DNA structure is essential for clarifying the molecular origins of mutagenesis and illness. Here, reactive molecular dynamic (RMD) simulations were carried out on native B-DNA and five different hydroxylation motifs to explore systematically how the spatial relocation of the oxidative guanine lesion impacts the 3D structure of the double helix. Oxidative and nitrosative stresses, such as those created under inflamed or tumorous tissues, such as reactive oxygen/nitrogen species, are understood to comprise guanine modification. One of the derived lesions from mentioned motifs, is 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-hydroxy-dG)—a lesion highly implicated in G→T transversions and initiation of cancers.

These simulations revealed positional symmetry of lesions was determinative for structurally determinate outcomes, regardless of simple presence of oxidative alterations. Full hydroxylation (MtOH), formed global elongation, unwinding, and large-scale Geometry disturbances of intra- and inter-base-pair. Local changes (MdOH and MlOH), formed directionally asymmetric local distortions for their spatial regions, whereas terminal lesions demonstrated impressive orientation-dependent effects: cross-axis assemblies (Ml2r14OH), led on cooperative end-fraying, large-scale deviations which spread inward, whereas vertical-axis assemblies (Ml2r24OH), preserved nearly native helical geometry, despite axal squeeze.

This paper describes the potential of RMD in unveiling thin, position-print structure outcomes of oxidative DNA damage. Through analysis of certain helical deformation parameters—center-of-base-pair distance (COBP), propeller twist angle (PTA), worldwide and neighborhood twist (GTA/LTA), and end-to-end DNA extent—it demonstrated a robust structure ordering between lesion templates. These findings broker a mechanistic interpretation for interjecting the role of oxidative damage into genomic instability, mutational hotspot formation, and, consequently, causality of malignancy and degenerative diseases. This structure-level information could guide further study on damage recognition, repair potential, and ordering of restorative events on pathways of oxidative stress.
