Risk prevention and control evaluation and optimization strategy for digital transformation in oil and gas production management areas
Authors: Xing Chen, Dong Jiang, Jiahao Wu, Yongning Huang, Dongxu Gao, Rui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the deep integration of digital technology and oil and gas production, digital transformation has become an important pathway to enhance efficiency and safety. This paper takes Oil and Gas Production Management Area A as the research object. Following the national standard GB/T 46,712 − 2025, we apply its five-dimensional evaluation framework with dimension weights from Table A.6 and tertiary indicator weights from Appendix of the standard. Quantitative assessments were conducted on three stations and individual wells. Results show that the maturity levels of various stations range from Level 2 to Level 3, indicating an overall intermediate level; the emergency management dimension scores relatively high, but foundational capabilities and risk prevention and control capabilities are generally low, becoming the main shortcomings of the transformation. The study identifies five core problems: prominent human-machine coordination contradictions, insufficient emergency response effectiveness, risks associated with single-worker operations, lack of personnel competency, and institutional lag. Five optimization measures are proposed: optimizing human-machine coordination, strengthening digital emergency response capabilities, improving health protection systems, enhancing talent cultivation, and perfecting institutional systems. This paper provides systematic evaluation methods and practical pathways for risk prevention and control in the digital transformation of oil and gas fields, offering significant theoretical value and practical guidance significance.Keywords: Digital transformation, Unattended stations, Risk prevention and control, Systematic evaluationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Global energy transformation and digital integration have made digital transformation a core pathway for the oil and gas industry to enhance efficiency and safety1,2. Unattended stations, centralized monitoring, and intelligent equipment are becoming mainstream, promoting production optimization and labor reduction3,4. However, digital transformation is a complex system involving technology, management, personnel, and institutions, and often creates new safety risks5–7.

Literature review

International research has extensively explored Oil and Gas 4.02, IoT8, big data analytics9, and AI applications3 in upstream operations. In safety science, systems thinking5,6, dynamic risk assessment10,11, and digital twin-based monitoring4,12 have been widely adopted.

Domestic scholars have made significant contributions. Studies have examined safety management of unattended stations13, construction practices of digital unattended gas field stations14, and how digital technology enhances intrinsic safety15. Case studies from the Sulige Gas Field have reported intelligent oilfield construction16, safety risks of unattended gathering stations17, and intelligent technology applications18. Liu et al.19 proposed design principles for smart oilfield construction.

Research gap and contribution

Despite these advances, two gaps remain. First, the national standard GB/T 46,712 − 2025 provides a five-dimensional evaluation framework for unattended stations, but empirical applications to complex, mixed manned/unattended production management areas are lacking. Second, the specific implementation gaps in such settings—human-machine coordination, digital emergency effectiveness, single-worker risks, personnel competency, and institutional lag—remain underexplored.

This paper takes Production Management Area A as a case study, applies GB/T 46,712 − 2025 with its pre-defined weights, quantitatively assesses three stations, identifies five core risk problems, and proposes optimization measures. The study provides empirical validation of the standard and practical guidance for similar digital transformation efforts.

Research questions

To guide the empirical analysis, this study addresses three research questions: (RQ1) What are the current risk prevention and control maturity levels across different station types? (RQ2) Why do score variations exist across dimensions and stations? (RQ3) What targeted strategies can address the identified gaps?

The national standard GB/T 46,712 − 2025 was selected as the evaluation framework because it is specifically designed for unattended stations in China’s oil/gas sector, provides a five-dimensional structure for systematic comparison, and includes quantifiable scoring rubrics with example weights for oil/gas fields (Appendices E and F).

The scope is limited to one management area (five station categories, 2023–2025). Upfront limitations include site-specific results, documentary bias in the emergency dimension, equal-weighting for a subset of indicators, and the exclusion of dynamic upgrade effects. These limitations are revisited in Sect. 4.3 and 6.

Research context

The studied management area is a gas production region containing multiple processing stations and off‑station single wells. It has undertaken significant digital upgrading: Distributed Control Systems (DCS), Safety Instrumented Systems (SIS), and Gas Detection Systems (FGS) have been installed; some stations have been converted to unattended operation with remote SCADA (Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition) monitoring; and a “1 + N” integrated control center has been established, wherein one central command center (“1”) provides consolidated remote monitoring, scheduling, and emergency coordination for multiple distributed stations and wells (“N”). The operational model is a mix of manned core stations and unattended well sites, with optimized shift patterns and risk‑based inspection frequencies. This setting—characterized by centralized monitoring and control over multiple distributed assets—is representative of many mature fields undergoing phased digital transformation, making it suitable for extracting generic insights.

Data collection and scoring process

Time period and scope

The evaluation was conducted using data from January 2023 to December 2025 (36 months). All system logs, interview records, and archival documents used for scoring fell within this window.

Data sources

Four types of data sources were used:

System logs: DCS, SIS, and SCADA alarm records, remote control operation logs, and video inspection logs were extracted from the management area’s digital platform (2023–2025).

Operator interviews: Semi‑structured interviews were conducted with 28 frontline personnel (12 operators, 8 maintenance technicians, 5 shift supervisors, and 3 safety officers) in February 2025. Each interview lasted 30–45 min and covered topics such as remote control usability, alarm response, and perceived risks of unattended operation.

Archival records: Emergency drill reports (2023–2025), preventive maintenance logs, near‑miss incident reports, training records, shift rosters, and the Hazard Identification and “Hidden danger inspection” System (HIS) database were reviewed.

On‑site walkthroughs: Physical equipment inspections, remote control functionality tests, and safety interlock spot checks were performed at each station in March 2025 to Octorber 2025.

Assessment team and scoring procedure

Five independent assessment teams (one per evaluation dimension) were formed. Each team consisted of at least three members: a project manager (oversight), an engineer (technical assessment), and an on‑site operator (practical feasibility). For Dimension A (Basic Capability), the team was expanded to five members due to the larger number of indicators. All members had ≥ 3 years of relevant experience and received a one‑day training session on GB/T 46,712‑2025.

Each team member independently scored each station against the rubric derived from GB/T 46,712‑2025 Appendices E and F. After independent scoring, the team convened to discuss discrepancies; any indicator with a score difference exceeding 5 points was resolved by consensus. The final scores reported in Table 2 represent consensus scores.

Inter‑rater reliability

Pre‑consensus scores were used to calculate Fleiss’ kappa. The average kappa across all five dimensions was 0.83 (range: 0.78–0.89), indicating substantial agreement.

Evaluation dimensions and indicators

Focusing on the entire production process of the stations, the evaluation established five core dimensions: foundational capability, risk prevention and control capability, capability maintenance, consequence mitigation, emergency management and disposal. Each dimension includes specific evaluation elements, forming a three-level evaluation system of “dimension-element-indicator” (Fig. 1).

Risk evaluation results

Cross-dimensional analysis: why do dimensions diverge?

As shown in Table 2, the five evaluation dimensions exhibit substantial score variation. Emergency Management and Disposal (Dimension E) consistently scores highest (90.20–95.50), while Basic Capability (Dimension A) and Risk Prevention and Control (Dimension B) score lowest (67.98–76.13).

Why Dimension E scores highest: The management area has devoted significant resources to emergency preparedness, driven by regulatory requirements (e.g., mandatory quarterly drills and documented emergency plans). All stations maintain up-to-date emergency response documents and conduct regular drills. However, high scores do not guarantee operational readiness. Most indicators in Dimension E are documentary (existence of plans, drill records) rather than performance-based. A station with well-documented plans may still have slow actual response times—as evidenced by the 32-minute average response time at Testing and Production Station C reported in Sect. 5.2. Thus, the high Dimension E scores may reflect calibration leniency rather than genuine emergency capability.

Why Dimensions A and B score lowest: These dimensions contain technically demanding indicators: automation rate, remote control completeness, SIL verification, cybersecurity measures, and physical security. The low scores reflect real implementation gaps: aging equipment at Processing Stations A and B, incomplete remote valve control (only 70–80% coverage), and insufficient cybersecurity hardening (e.g., no formal SIL verification for some safety loops). Unlike documentary indicators, these require capital investment and technical upgrades, which lag behind the pace of digital transformation.

Calibration differences across dimensions: Comparing the example rubrics in GB/T 46,712 − 2025 Appendices E and F reveals that Dimension E indicators are weighted toward document existence (easily achieved), whereas Dimensions A and B indicators require demonstrable technical performance (harder to achieve). This inherent weighting in the standard partially explains the observed score disparities. We revisit this limitation in Sect. 4.3.

Cross-station comparative analysis: two drivers of score differences

Table 2 presents scores for five station categories, classified as manned (Inside Stations of A and B) and unattended with periodic inspection (Single Wells of A and B, Testing and Production Station C). The results show that A Single Wells score highest (81.63, Level 3), followed by C Station (80.06, Level 3), B Inside (79.67, Level 2), A Inside (78.93, Level 2), and B Single Wells lowest (78.91, Level 2). Two primary drivers explain this pattern.

Driver 1–Digital retrofit progress and intelligent equipment deployment

Processing Station A completed a comprehensive digital retrofit in 2024, achieving high remote control coverage and extensive deployment of smart sensors and actuators. Processing Station B, due to budget constraints and operational priorities, has seen slower retrofit progress. This directly explains why A’s single wells outperform B’s single wells on Dimensions A and B.

Driver 2–Production complexity and temporary process modifications

This is the critical differentiator between Stations A and B. Station B faces a significantly more severe water production situation (higher water-to-gas ratio, frequent liquid loading events). To cope, the station has implemented numerous temporary process modifications — including additional separators, bypass lines, and manual intervention points — to manage liquid unloading and prevent well shutdowns. Critically, these temporary modifications have not been fully integrated into the automation system. Many modified process configurations lack corresponding control logic in the DCS/SIS, meaning operators must manually control valves and monitor parameters even where remote control hardware exists. Consequently, Station B’s automation system cannot keep pace with the rapidly changing process conditions, depressing its Dimension A (Basic Capability) and Dimension B (Risk Prevention) scores.

Validity and limitations of the evaluation results

Face validity: The scoring results align with independent operational records. Station A’s higher scores correspond with its documented retrofit completion in 2024.

Construct validity: Inter-correlations among the five dimension scores average r = 0.34, with Dimensions A and B showing moderate correlation (r = 0.51) and Dimension E showing near-zero correlation with others (r = 0.12), confirming that the five dimensions measure relatively distinct constructs.

Core risk issues in digital transformation

Through comprehensive evaluation and on-site investigation, five core problems existing in the digital transformation process of the management area were systematically identified. These problems are interrelated and mutually constraining, forming multiple obstacles to the deepening of the transformation, as detailed below13,20:

Insufficient focus on low-efficiency links, prominent human-machine coordination contradictions

The core goal of digital transformation—“replacing humans with intelligence”—has not been fully realized, with some links still relying on manual operation, resulting in low human-machine coordination efficiency. First, video inspection Insufficient efficiency, with station cameras having blind spots, low resolution, and poor light adaptation. According to the management area’s Hazard Identification and “Hidden danger inspection” System (HIS) database (2023–2025), manual inspections detected 1,200–1,600 leakage/seepage events per year, while video inspections detected only 110–130 events per year—representing less than one‑tenth of manual detection volume. This significant discrepancy indicates substantial blind spots in the current video inspection system (e.g., camera blind zones, low resolution, poor light adaptation). Consequently, employees have low confidence in the supplementary capability of video inspection and tend to revert to manual rounds. (Source: System (HIS) database, 2023–2025; anonymized extract available from corresponding author)5. Second, intelligent technology implementation is constrained by costs. Tasks such as gas field water hauling at Testing and Production Station C, produced water oil collection and sludge discharge at Processing Station A, and water and oil unloading at Processing Station B still require manual operation6. Third, equipment remote control is incomplete. Single-well centrifugal pumps can start and stop automatically, but outlet valves cannot be remotely controlled. The “intelligent start-stop + manual valve operation” model not only fails to improve efficiency but increases operational risks such as pump damage and pipeline.

Prominent emergency response risks, insufficient digital emergency effectiveness

The emergency response system has not been upgraded synchronously with digital transformation, with personnel configuration and technical support unable to adapt to the needs of the unattended model. As part of the digital transformation strategy (“fewer personnel, higher efficiency”), the management area has reduced night shift on‑site personnel to 2 (from the previous 5–6). However, according to the management area’s Standard Operating Card (SOC), incidents such as valve leakage or separator flow path switching require 4–5 personnel to complete the necessary on‑site operations safely and efficiently. This staffing‑to‑demand mismatch creates a critical safety gap: in the event of a major nighttime incident, the available 2‑person team is insufficient for rapid and safe resolution, potentially leading to delayed response or escalated consequences; Based on quarterly emergency drill records from 2023 to 2025 (12 drills total at Testing and Production Station C), the average emergency rescue arrival time (from alarm confirmation to first responder arrival) was 32 min, with a minimum of 28 min (range: 28–41 min). This exceeds the 30‑minute threshold and is significantly longer than the 15–20 min benchmark for similar stations in the region. While this finding is station‑specific due to its remote location and road conditions, it reflects a broader risk pattern for remote gathering stations in mature gas fields. Existing automation systems only achieve status monitoring and abnormal alarms and cannot replace manual personnel in conducting complex on-site assessments and process adjustments, making it difficult to provide support under abnormal circumstances.

Inadequate personnel health management, prominent safety risks in single-worker operations

The “fewer people, higher efficiency” model driven by digital transformation has led to a significant increase in single-worker operation scenarios. The traditional “people watching people” safety mechanism has failed, and personnel health management has blind spots. Some employees with advanced age and underlying diseases have become high-risk groups for independent operations. If sudden health incidents such as cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events occur, they are difficult to detect and rescue in a timely manner; the site has not established health monitoring or emergency mechanisms for single-worker operations, revealing obvious shortcomings in operational safety guarantees.

Insufficient personnel digital literacy, shortage of compound talents

Under the traditional operation and maintenance model, personnel skills are singular, making them unable to adapt to the cross-position and cross-system work requirements following digital transformation. First, there is a shortage of compound talents—individuals who understand processes, automation, and data analysis—making it difficult for employees to simultaneously handle monitoring screen data analysis and on-site fault disposal. Second, professional software application capabilities are weak. For software such as process simulation and oil and gas reservoir modeling, most personnel only master parameter modification and basic modeling, with few proficient users, failing to utilize the software to solve practical production problems. Third, the training system is imperfect. Training content such as digital applications and advanced software functions is lacking, and the pertinence and effectiveness of training are insufficient, with the pace of employee skill improvement lagging behind the needs of the transformation.

Imperfect supporting systems, lack of support for long-term transformation promotion

After digital transformation and organizational model optimization, related systems and standards have not been updated in a timely manner, leading to a lack of long-term mechanism guarantees for the transformation. There is no clear responsibility division or process standards for standardized platform applications and data maintenance, resulting in outdated and inaccurate information; the coordination process and responsibility division between digital inspection and manual inspection have not been standardized, leading to low inspection coordination efficiency; the assessment and incentive mechanism for digital applications is not well-established, and employees’ initiative to actively use intelligent tools and explore platform functions is insufficient; emergency plans and post operation procedures have not been optimized according to transformation needs, resulting in disconnection between systems and on-site realities.

Optimization strategies for digital transformation risk prevention and control

Addressing the risk prevention and control shortcomings exposed during the digital transformation of Oil and Gas Production Management Area A, this paper constructs a “technology-management-human factors” trinity collaborative optimization framework from an industry-wide perspective and systematically proposes the following risk prevention and control optimization strategies, aiming to provide theoretical basis and practical guidance for the digital transformation of similar oil and gas fields .

Constructing a human-machine collaborative hybrid enhanced intelligence operation paradigm

Addressing the industry-wide problems of low human-machine coordination efficiency and difficulty in implementing intelligent technologies, we should break through the single path of “completely replacing humans with machines” and shift towards a “human-machine complementary advantages” hybrid enhanced intelligence model. Specifically, the oil and gas industry needs to systematically evaluate the adaptation boundaries between humans and machines in various operational scenarios, deploy intelligent equipment with environmental perception and autonomous decision-making capabilities in inspection, operation, emergency response, and other links, and promote the upgrading of video monitoring systems to “edge computing + AI recognition” to achieve active warning of leaks, spills, and abnormal conditions. At the same time, a dynamic human-machine task allocation mechanism should be established, assigning low-value repetitive labor to machines and freeing up human resources to focus on complex assessment and emergency response, fundamentally solving the dilemma of “intelligence without efficiency.”

Establishing a resilient emergency management system adapted to unmanned scenarios

The unattended mode poses fundamental challenges to traditional emergency response systems, making it urgent to build a resilient emergency management framework centered on “prevention-warning-response-recovery”. At the industry level, digital modeling of emergency resources and intelligent dispatching platforms should be promoted to achieve real-time optimization of emergency supplies, personnel, and routes. Additionally, a “redundant response” mechanism matching the reduced personnel should be established, compensating for single-point manpower shortages through cross-station coordination and regional collaboration to ensure the timeliness and effectiveness of emergency response .

Improving the active health risk prevention and control mechanism for operating personnel throughout the lifecycle

The normalization of single-worker operations brought about by digital transformation has made personnel health risks a blind spot in industry safety management. The priority is to build a full-cycle health protection system of “pre-job assessment - on-duty monitoring - emergency intervention.” Promote the application of wearable physiological monitoring devices and personnel positioning systems to achieve real-time monitoring of vital signs and automatic abnormal alarms in single-worker operation scenarios; at the same time, establish a job adaptation model based on health big data to dynamically adjust the operational tasks of high-risk personnel, forming an active protection mechanism that combines technological empowerment and institutional guarantees.

Building a compound talent cultivation and knowledge inheritance system for digital transformation

The lag in personnel digital literacy is a key bottleneck restricting the deepening of transformation. The industry should establish the goal of cultivating specialized talents with “technology + management + data” capabilities, promote the establishment of digital talent training bases jointly by universities, research institutes, and enterprises, and carry out interdisciplinary training integrating processes, automation, and data analysis. Meanwhile, to address the shortage of specialized talent, a structured personnel development and training program should be established, focusing on three core competency areas: automation systems, monitoring data analysis, and on-site operational response. The practical measures include: (1) selective cultivation of cross‑functional personnel—identifying and developing employees who can integrate these three skill sets, thereby directly addressing the “screen analysis + field response” capability gap identified in Sect. 5.4; and (2) optimizing the training curriculum by strengthening modules on digital monitoring applications, automation system diagnostics, and emergency response procedures. Training effectiveness should be reinforced through scenario‑based drills, on‑the‑job assessment, and performance‑linked incentives, ensuring that personnel capabilities keep pace with the demands of digital transformation.

Improving the institutional system and long-term promotion mechanism adapted to digital transformation

Institutional lag is the deep-rooted cause of the “superficial implementation” of digital transformation. The industry needs to promote the dynamic update of standard systems, accelerate the introduction of supporting systems such as unattended station operation specifications, data governance standards, and human-machine collaborative operating procedures, and clarify the rights, responsibilities, and coordination processes of all participating entities. Regarding incentive mechanisms, the application of digital tools, intelligent hazard identification, and contributions to system optimization should be incorporated into performance appraisals and promotion systems to stimulate employees’ intrinsic motivation.

Conclusion and prospects

Summary of findings

This study applied the GB/T 46,712 − 2025 framework to evaluate digital transformation risks in a mixed manned/unattended gas production management area. The quantitative assessment revealed that overall maturity levels range from Level 2 to Level 3, indicating intermediate capability with substantial room for improvement. The emergency management dimension scored highest (reflecting strong documentation compliance), while foundational capability and risk prevention scored lowest (reflecting technical implementation gaps).

Five core problems were systematically identified: (1) human-machine coordination contradictions, driven by incomplete automation integration of temporary process modifications; (2) emergency response risks, exacerbated by reduced night shift staffing and long dispatch distances; (3) single-worker health and safety risks under the “fewer personnel” model; (4) insufficient personnel digital competency, particularly at stations with less reliable automation; and (5) institutional lag, including outdated procedures and weak incentives for digital adoption.

Interplay among problems

These problems are not isolated. The lack of automation integration (Problem 1) directly undermines operator confidence, which worsens digital competency deficits (Problem 4). Together, they reinforce a negative cycle of manual reverting. Institutional lag (Problem 5) enables this cycle by failing to update procedures or reward digital engagement.

Broader implications

For the oil and gas industry, this study demonstrates that digital transformation success depends not only on technology deployment but also on the alignment of automation reliability, personnel capability, and institutional support. Stations with severe production challenges (e.g., high water cut) require targeted retrofit strategies that integrate temporary modifications into automation logic — a gap not currently addressed by GB/T 46,712 − 2025. Future revisions of the standard should consider adding indicators for “automation adaptability to process changes.”.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the example weights from GB/T 46,712 − 2025’s appendices were adopted without customization; different oil/gas fields may require adjustments to reflect local risk priorities. Second, despite using a consensus-based scoring procedure, some indicators (e.g., personnel digital literacy) inevitably involve subjective judgment. Third, the evaluation was conducted over a specific time window (January 2023–December 2025); results may change after significant system upgrades or organizational changes. Future research could employ the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to refine indicator weights based on local expert input, and incorporate objective performance metrics (e.g., automated user action logs) to reduce subjectivity.

Some quantitative findings are derived from drill records (response time) or internal reporting systems (inspection discrepancy) rather than actual emergency events or independent audits. Drills may not fully replicate real‑emergency conditions, and internal databases may have reporting biases. Independent validation in future studies would strengthen confidence.
