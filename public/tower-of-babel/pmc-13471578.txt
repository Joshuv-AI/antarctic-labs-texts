Sex-dependent central amygdala physiology after supradural CGRP exposure is associated with migraine-like behaviors
Authors: Nico Antenucci, Sambantham Shanmugam, Mariacristina Mazzitelli, Kenichi Tanaka, Caroline Kopruszinski, Igor Ponomarev, Edita Navratilova, Frank Porreca, Volker Neugebauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a key mediator of migraine, but the mechanisms through which meningeal CGRP engages central circuits involved in the sensory and affective dimensions of migraine remain incompletely understood. Here, we tested if supradural CGRP is sufficient to induce migraine-like sensory and affective behaviors and if these effects are associated with sex-dependent changes in parabrachial-central amygdala (PB-CeA) signaling.MethodsAdult male and female C57BL/6J mice received supradural CGRP or vehicle. Behavioral testing, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, and qPCR analyses were performed 3 h later.ResultsCGRP induced robust periorbital mechanical allodynia selectively in females, while hindpaw mechanical thresholds were reduced in both sexes, with a greater effect in females. CGRP also increased avoidance-like behavior in both sexes, reflected by decreased open-arm exploration in the elevated plus maze and decreased time spent in the light compartment of the light-dark box. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from neurons in the right latero-capsular central amygdala in CGRP-exposed mice revealed higher excitability and larger PB-evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents in females than males. Ex vivo bath application of the CGRP receptor antagonist CGRP₈₋₃₇ was used to test local CGRP receptor sensitivity and selectively decreased excitability and PB-evoked excitatory transmission in females, while paired-pulse ratio remained unchanged in both sexes. In addition, qPCR analysis identified region- and sex-dependent transcriptional changes after supradural CGRP, with the most consistent molecular effects observed in the right PBN and more limited changes in the right CeA.ConclusionsTogether, these findings show that supradural CGRP is sufficient to produce sex-differentiated mechanical hypersensitivity and avoidance-like behavior and is associated with sex-dependent physiological and transcriptional changes, including a right-sided pattern of transcriptional effects. These results identify the PB-CeA pathway as a candidate substrate linking meningeal CGRP signaling to central mechanisms relevant to hypersensitivity and amygdala plasticity that is more pronounced in females.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02417-9.

Background

Migraine is one of the most prevalent neurological disorders worldwide and a leading cause of disability [1, 2]. Its pathophysiology is complex and involves multiple phases and symptoms that extend beyond the actual headache, including cutaneous allodynia, photophobia, and negative affective states [3, 4]. Currently available acute and preventive treatments remain inadequate for many patients, including a substantial subset treated with calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeting therapies [4, 5]. These observations suggest that migraine reflects distributed dysfunction across multiple neural systems and that important CGRP-sensitive mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Among the factors implicated in migraine, CGRP has emerged as a key signaling molecule. CGRP is expressed in trigeminal ganglion neurons, including afferents that innervate the dura, and acts through a receptor complex composed of calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CALCRL), receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1), and receptor component protein (CRCP). CGRP released from trigeminal afferents can act at the meninges to promote migraine-like responses, and preclinical studies have shown that direct dural application of CGRP produces female-selective hypersensitivity in rodents [6, 7]. These studies support a critical role for peripheral CGRP signaling but how meningeal CGRP engages central circuits that contribute to the sensory and affective dimensions of migraine-like states remains unknown.

Migraine is also associated with hypersensitivity to light and altered emotional processing. Experimental studies have shown that CGRP can induce light-aversive behavior in mice through both peripheral and central mechanisms, supporting the idea that CGRP influences not only nociception, but also aversive and sensory-affective behaviors relevant to migraine [8, 9]. In parallel, human imaging studies have implicated both brainstem and limbic structures in migraine, including increased activation in the amygdala and dorsolateral pons [4, 10, 11]. These findings suggest that migraine-related symptoms may depend on interactions between peripheral trigeminovascular signaling and central circuits involved in salience, threat, and affect.

A particularly strong candidate for this integration is the parabrachial-central amygdala (PB-CeA) pathway. The parabrachial nucleus (PBN) serves as a major hub for nociceptive and interoceptive processing and relays aversive signals to the amygdala [12–14]. Importantly, PBN CGRP neurons provide a dense projection to the CeA and have been linked to threat and affective pain processing [14, 15]. The CeA, and particularly the laterocapsular region, is a key site for the emotional-affective dimension of pain and for pain-related neuroplasticity [16, 17]. In addition, CGRP receptor activation in the CeA contributes to pain-related synaptic and behavioral plasticity [15, 18]. Together, these observations make the PB-CeA axis a plausible circuit through which meningeal CGRP could influence both nociceptive hypersensitivity and affective/avoidance-like behavior. This view is also consistent with broader models emphasizing the importance of limbic circuits in the emotional and motivational dimensions of pain. This issue is particularly important because CGRP-targeting therapies are not effective in all patients. In addition, although monoclonal antibodies against CGRP or its receptor are generally thought to have limited access to the brain, small-molecule gepants may have somewhat greater CNS penetration, which should be considered when interpreting the possible contribution of central CGRP-sensitive mechanisms to migraine pathophysiology [19, 20]. Moreover, although migraine is more prevalent in females, sex-dependent mechanisms along the pain neuraxis, particularly within central affective circuits, remain incompletely understood [21].

Here, we tested the hypothesis that supradural CGRP is sufficient to produce both mechanical hypersensitivity and avoidance-like behavior and that these effects are accompanied by sex-dependent changes in central amygdala function. To address this, we combined behavioral testing with whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from neurons in the CeA, stimulation of parabrachial afferents, and qPCR analysis in the CeA and PBN. Our results identify sex-dependent physiological and transcriptional changes after supradural CGRP and highlight the PB-CeA pathway as a candidate substrate linking meningeal CGRP signaling to hypersensitivity and affective-like behavioral changes.

Methods

Animals

Experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center and were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) for the ethical use of animals in research. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and the number of animals used. Adult male and female C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g body weight) were used for behavioral assays and ex vivo electrophysiological recordings and quantitative real-time PCR analyses. Estrous cycle stage was determined in female mice used for electrophysiological recordings by vaginal lavage followed by crystal violet staining and cytological evaluation prior to slice preparation, as previously described [22]. All females included in these experiments were in metestrus or diestrus. Estrous cycle stage was not monitored for the behavioral experiments. Wild-type C57BL/6J breeders were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Animals were housed in groups of 4–5 in standard cages with ad libitum access to food and water, maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:30 AM), at 22 °C and 66% humidity under standard laboratory conditions. Sample sizes were selected based on our previous experience with similar behavioral, electrophysiological, and molecular experiments, together with group sizes commonly used in related studies.

Drugs

α-CGRP (Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide; Product No. 4025897; CAS: 83651-90-5) and its receptor antagonist, CGRP₈₋₃₇ (Product No. 4034544; CAS: 129121-73-9), were purchased from Bachem (Torrance, CA, USA). Upon arrival, both peptides were reconstituted in synthetic interstitial fluid (sIF), aliquoted into single-use volumes to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, and stored at -80 °C according to manufacturer recommendations until use. For supradural injections, α-CGRP was diluted in sIF to a final dose of 3.8ng in 5µL; vehicle injections consisted of sIF alone. sIF consisted of 135 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 10 mM glucose, with pH adjusted to 7.4 using NaOH. For slice electrophysiology, CGRP₈₋₃₇ was diluted in aCSF (in mM: 117 NaCl, 4.7 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 2.5 CaCl2, 1.2 MgCl2, 25 NaHCO3 and 11 glucose) to a final bath concentration of 1 µM for gravity-driven superfusion of the brain slice.

Supradural injection

To model migraine-like responses, mice received supradural injections of CGRP (3.8 ng in 5 µL) dissolved in synthetic interstitial fluid (sIF). The procedure followed the method of Burgos-Vega et al. (2019). Under brief isoflurane anesthesia (1.5-2% in O₂), mice were placed in a stereotaxic frame and a midline scalp incision was made. A custom injector, adapted from commercially available cannulas (Plastic One/Invivo1, part no. C313I/SPC) by inserting a stopper to project a length of 0.65–0.7 mm, was used to avoid dural penetration. The injector was connected to Tygon tubing (Cole Parmer) and a 25 µL Hamilton syringe. The injector was inserted through the junction of the sagittal and lambdoid sutures to deliver 5 µL of CGRP solution onto the dura. Control mice received sIF alone. Following injection, the incision was closed, and animals were allowed to recover in their home cages. Behavioral testing and slice preparation for electrophysiology were performed 3 h after supradural injection of CGRP or vehicle (sIF).

Behavioral tests

Hind Paw Mechanosensitivity. Mechanical withdrawal thresholds were measured using a plantar electronic von Frey anesthesiometer (IITC Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA, USA). The probe tip was applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface at the base of the third or fourth toe of the hind paw, with gradually increasing force until a withdrawal reflex was elicited. The applied force at the time of withdrawal was automatically recorded in grams. Three trials, spaced at least 5 min apart, were conducted for each paw, and the average was used as the final threshold for each mouse.

Periorbital Allodynia. Mechanical sensitivity in the periorbital region was assessed using calibrated von Frey filaments. Mice were placed in a custom-built elevated tube apparatus that allowed controlled access to the periorbital region during testing. The tube was wide enough to avoid full restraint, while a small movable stopper at the rear prevented the animal from moving too far backward and helped maintain a consistent position for filament application. Animals were habituated to the apparatus 1 day prior to testing. On the day of the experiment, mice were placed in the chamber 2.5 h after supradural CGRP or vehicle injection and allowed to habituate for 20 min before testing, so that testing began 3 h post-injection. A series of von Frey filaments in logarithmic force increments (0.008, 0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, and 0.6 g) was applied to the periorbital region with just enough force to produce a slight arch in the filament. Stimulation began with the 0.16 g filament. If the mouse did not respond within 5 s, the next stronger filament was applied. If a positive response occurred (vigorous forepaw stroking of the face, head withdrawal, or head shaking), a lighter filament was applied next. Six trials were conducted per mouse or until four consecutive positive or negative responses were recorded. If no response occurred at the highest force (0.6 g), this value was considered the cut-off threshold.

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM). To assess anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity, mice were tested in the elevated plus maze, consisting of two open arms (30 × 5 cm) and two closed arms (30 × 5 cm, enclosed by 15-cm-high walls) arranged in a plus configuration and elevated 60 cm above the floor. Mice were placed in the central square (5 × 5 cm) facing an open arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Time spent in the open arms and total distance traveled during the 5-min session were recorded using a computerized video tracking and analysis system (EthoVision XT 15 software, Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA). These parameters were used as measures of anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity.

Open Field Test (OFT). To assess anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity, mice were placed in the center of a white acrylic open-field arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The arena was divided into a central zone (20 × 20 cm) and a peripheral zone. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using EthoVision XT 15 software (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA). Total distance traveled, time spent in the center zone, and number of entries into the center zone during the 5-min session were quantified. These parameters were used as measures of anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity.

Light-Dark Box (LDB). To assess avoidance-like/anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity, mice were tested in a light-dark box consisting of two compartments connected by a 5 × 5 cm opening. The dark compartment measured 17.5 × 40 cm and the light compartment measured 25 × 40 cm, with a height of 35 cm. The light compartment was illuminated to approximately 400 lx, whereas the dark compartment remained unlit. Mice were placed in the light compartment at the start of the 5-min session and allowed to explore freely. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using EthoVision XT 15 software (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, VA, USA). The following parameters were measured during the 5-min session: latency to first entry into the dark compartment, time spent in the dark compartment, and total distance traveled. These parameters were used as measures of avoidance-like/anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity.

Patch-clamp recording of CeA neurons in the right amygdala

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed in acute coronal brain slices prepared from mice 3 h after supradural CGRP injection. Recordings were obtained from neurons in the laterocapsular division of the central amygdala (CeL/C) in the right hemisphere using differential interference contrast infrared (DIC-IR) microscopy (Olympus BX51). Patch pipettes (6–9 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass and filled with an intracellular solution containing (in mM): 122 K-gluconate, 5 NaCl, 0.3 CaCl₂, 2 MgCl₂, 1 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 5 Na₂ATP, and 0.4 Na₃GTP. The pH was adjusted to 7.2–7.3 with KOH, and osmolarity was adjusted to 280 mOsm/kg with sucrose. Electrophysiological signals were acquired using a dual four-pole Bessel filter (Warner Instruments), an Axoclamp-2B amplifier (Axon Instruments), and a Digidata 1322 A digitizer, and analyzed using pClamp 10 software (Molecular Devices).

Current-clamp protocols were used to assess intrinsic excitability. Depolarizing current steps (500 ms duration, 20 pA increments) were injected from resting membrane potential to generate frequency-current (F-I) curves. Voltage sag was assessed during hyperpolarizing current injections (500 ms, 20 pA increments from 0 to -200 pA). Sag amplitude was calculated as the difference between the initial peak hyperpolarized membrane potential and the final steady-state membrane potential during each negative current step. In voltage-clamp mode, neurons were held at -70 mV to record excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and paired-pulse ratios (PPRs). PPR was assessed by delivering two consecutive synaptic stimuli separated by an interstimulus interval of 100 ms and was calculated as the ratio of the second EPSC peak amplitude to the first (EPSC2/EPSC1). Synaptic responses were evoked by focal electrical stimulation (150 µs square-wave pulses) delivered through a concentric bipolar electrode (David Kopf Instruments) positioned on the fiber tract dorsomedial to the CeA and ventral to the caudate-putamen where the parabrachial (PB) input has been shown to travel on its way to the CeA [23, 24].

To assess CGRP receptor-dependent effects, a CGRP receptor antagonist (CGRP₈₋₃₇ 1 µM; Bachem) was applied via gravity-driven superfusion (~ 2 mL/min) of the brain slice. CGRP₈₋₃₇ was selected as a well-established CGRP receptor antagonist previously used to evaluate CGRP receptor-dependent neuronal effects in the amygdala. The antagonist was applied for at least 15 min before post-drug recordings to allow tissue equilibration. aCSF alone served as vehicle control. For all electrophysiological experiments, only one neuron was recorded per animal.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

The left and right central amygdala (CeA) and parabrachial nucleus (PB) were microdissected from freshly collected brain slices obtained 3 h after supradural injection of CGRP or sIF. Total RNA was extracted using the MagMAX™-96 Total RNA Isolation Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). For cDNA synthesis, 2 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in a final reaction volume of 40 µL. The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:2 with nuclease-free water prior to quantitative PCR analysis. Real-time PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX384 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Each 10 µL qPCR reaction contained 2 µL of diluted cDNA, 5 µL of TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Bedford, MA, USA), and 20 pmol of the respective TaqMan primer/probe set (Table 1). Cycling conditions consisted of 50 °C for 2 min for UNG activation, followed by an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, and then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Raw cycle threshold (Ct) values for each target gene were normalized to the geometric mean of the reference genes Hprt and Malat1 to obtain ΔCt values. Relative gene expression for graphical presentation was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method [25]. Target genes analyzed included Calca, Calcrl, Crcp, Itgam, Ramp1, and Tnf. Target genes were selected to assess both CGRP pathway-related signaling components (Calca, Calcrl, Ramp1, and Crcp) and inflammatory/immune-related markers potentially relevant to migraine-associated tissue responses (Itgam and Tnf). qPCR analyses were performed at 3 h after supradural injection to match the timing of the behavioral and electrophysiological experiments and to capture early molecular changes associated with the acute response to CGRP.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with sex and treatment as independent factors, followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparisons test when appropriate. Electrophysiological data were analyzed using unpaired t-tests, one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, or two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests, as indicated in the figure legends. For periorbital von Frey testing, threshold values were derived from discrete filament categories; therefore, although these data were analyzed by ANOVA for consistency with the overall study design, the results should be interpreted with caution. For qPCR, statistical analyses were performed on ΔCt values, as these retain the appropriate distributional properties for parametric testing. ΔCt values were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with sex (male vs. female) and treatment (vehicle vs. CGRP) as independent factors, including the interaction term (sex × treatment). When appropriate, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test to control the family-wise error rate across pairwise group comparisons within each gene analysis. In addition, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across qPCR targets within each brain region for treatment- and interaction-related comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. For data visualization, relative expression values were calculated as 2^-ΔΔCt and are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Experiments and analyses were not performed under blinded conditions.

Results

Supradural CGRP induces cephalic and extra-cephalic mechanical allodynia in a sex-dependent manner

Male and female mice received a supradural injection of CGRP (3.8 ng in 5 µL) or vehicle (sIF) and were tested for changes in mechanosensitivity 3 h later (Fig. 1A). CGRP produced a marked reduction in periorbital mechanical von Frey thresholds in females compared with vehicle-treated females, while males did not show a significant change (Fig. 1B; interaction: F(1,36) = 32.66, p < 0.0001; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p < 0.0001). Hindpaw mechanical thresholds assessed using electronic von Frey were reduced in both the left and right hindpaws in male and female mice following CGRP administration (Fig. 1C–D; left: interaction, F(1,36) = 12.10, p = 0.0013; male vehicle vs. male CGRP, p = 0.0057; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p < 0.0001; right: treatment effect, F(1,36) = 78.02, p < 0.0001; male vehicle vs. male CGRP, p < 0.0001; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p < 0.0001). These findings suggest that supradural CGRP promotes sex-dependent mechanical hypersensitivity, with females showing greater sensitivity, particularly in the periorbital region (two-way ANOVA with Sidak post hoc test; n = 10 mice per group).

Supradural CGRP elicits anxiety- and light-avoidance behavior in both sexes

To evaluate affective behavioral effects of supradural CGRP, mice were tested in the elevated plus maze (EPM), open field test (OFT), and light-dark box (LDB) 3 h post-injection of CGRP or sIF. In the EPM, CGRP-treated mice of both sexes spent less time in the open arms and showed reduced total distance traveled compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2A-B; treatment effects: open-arm time, F(1,36) = 23.00, p < 0.0001; total distance, F(1,36) = 32.64, p < 0.0001). In the OFT, CGRP-treated mice showed a reduction in total distance traveled (Fig. 2E; treatment effect, F(1,36) = 13.49, p = 0.0008), whereas center entries and time spent in the center were unchanged (Fig. 2C-D). In the LDB, CGRP-treated mice of both sexes spent more time in the dark compartment, while latency to first transition to the dark compartment was unchanged (Fig. 2F-G). Total distance traveled was also reduced following CGRP in both sexes (Fig. 2H; treatment effect, F(1,36) = 14.37, p = 0.0006; male vehicle vs. male CGRP, p = 0.0159; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p = 0.0300). These findings suggest that supradural CGRP elicits migraine-like sensory and affective (avoidance-like) behaviors in both sexes (two-way ANOVA with Sidak post hoc test; n = 10 mice per group).

Supradural CGRP exposure reveals greater CeA excitability and PB-evoked excitatory transmission in females than in males

To assess sex differences in CeA neuronal physiology after supradural CGRP exposure, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were obtained from neurons in the right latero-capsular CeA 3 h after supradural CGRP injection. All recordings were obtained from CGRP-treated mice, and CGRP receptor signaling was manipulated ex vivo via bath application of the CGRP receptor antagonist CGRP₈₋₃₇ (1 µM) (Fig. 3A). In current clamp, female CeA neurons exhibited higher pre-drug firing rates than male neurons in response to depolarizing current injections, consistent with greater intrinsic excitability. Representative voltage responses to a depolarizing current step are shown in Fig. 3B. Analysis of the frequency-current (F-I) relationship showed that bath application of CGRP₈₋₃₇ significantly reduced firing in females, eliminating the difference between sexes, whereas no significant effect was observed in males (Fig. 3C; F(21.82,214.3) = 6.514, p < 0.0001; n = 10 neurons per sex from 10 mice per sex). In response to hyperpolarizing current steps, baseline voltage sag amplitude was greater in males than females (Fig. 3E; t(18) = 3.488, p = 0.0026; n = 10 neurons per sex from 10 mice per sex). Following CGRP₈₋₃₇ application, sag amplitude increased in females (Fig. 3F; sex effect: F(1,18) = 13.76, p = 0.0016; treatment effect: F(1,18) = 12.67, p = 0.0022; n = 10 neurons per sex from 10 mice per sex). Although increased Ih/sag can support rebound depolarization and promote return toward resting membrane potential, it can also act as a membrane stabilizer depending on its interaction with input resistance and spike threshold [26]. To probe synaptic effects, we recorded EPSCs evoked by stimulation of parabrachial (PB) afferents. Baseline EPSC peak amplitudes were larger in females than males (Fig. 3H; t(15) = 2.368, p = 0.0317; n = 9 neurons from 9 male mice and 8 neurons from 8 female mice), and CGRP₈₋₃₇ reduced EPSC amplitude in females but not males (Fig. 3I; interaction: F(1,15) = 18.27, p = 0.0007; treatment effect: F(1,15) = 19.77, p = 0.0005; n = 9 neurons from 9 mice and 8 neurons from 8 female mice), suggesting greater synaptic drive from PBN in females. Paired-pulse ratio was not significantly altered by CGRP₈₋₃₇ in either sex (Fig. 3J-K; interaction: F(1,14) = 0.09867, p = 0.7581; sex effect: F(1,14) = 0.1599, p = 0.6953; treatment effect: F(1,14) = 2.759, p = 0.1189; n = 8 neurons from 8 mice per sex), arguing against presynaptic changes.

Supradural CGRP is associated with region- and sex-dependent gene expression changes in the right CeA and right PBN

To determine whether supradural CGRP was associated with early molecular changes in brain regions linked to the electrophysiological findings, qPCR was performed in the right and left CeA and PBN 3 h after supradural CGRP or vehicle injection. In the right CeA, Ramp1 expression showed a significant interaction between sex and CGRP treatment (Fig. 4A; F(1,27) = 5.386, p = 0.0281; n = 7–8 per group). Post hoc analysis revealed a trend toward reduced Ramp1 expression in female CGRP-treated mice compared with female vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4A; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p = 0.0539). No significant effects were detected for Calca, Calcrl, Crcp, Itgam, or Tnf in the right CeA (Fig. 4B-D; Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). In the left CeA, no significant effects of sex, CGRP treatment, or interaction were detected for Calca, Calcrl, Crcp, Itgam, Ramp1, or Tnf (Supplementary Fig. 1F-K; n = 7–8 per group). In the right PBN, several genes showed significant transcriptional changes. Calcrl expression showed a significant main effect of CGRP treatment (Fig. 4E; F(1,18) = 7.707, p = 0.0125; n = 5–6 per group), while Itgam (Fig. 4G; F(1,18) = 8.959, p = 0.0078) and Ramp1 (Fig. 4F; F(1,18) = 8.224, p = 0.0102) also showed significant treatment effects. Post hoc analyses demonstrated reduced Itgam (Fig. 4G; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p = 0.0148) and Ramp1 (Fig. 4F; female vehicle vs. female CGRP, p = 0.0202) expression in female CGRP-treated mice compared with female vehicle-treated controls. In addition, Calca expression in the right PBN showed a significant main effect of sex (Supplementary Fig. 1C; F(1,18) = 22.33, p = 0.0002), with lower expression in females than males across treatment conditions. Significant differences were detected between male vehicle vs. female vehicle (p = 0.018), male vehicle vs. female CGRP (p = 0.0468), male CGRP vs. female vehicle (p = 0.0062), and male CGRP vs. female CGRP (p = 0.0166). No significant differences were observed for Crcp or Tnf in the right PBN (Supplementary Fig. 1D-E). No significant differences were detected in the left PBN for Calca, Calcrl, Crcp, Itgam, Ramp1, or Tnf (Supplementary Fig. 1L-Q; n = 5–6 per group). Together, these findings indicate region- and sex-dependent transcriptional responses after supradural CGRP, with the most consistent molecular effects observed in the right PBN and more limited changes in the right CeA. Under the present experimental conditions, these results are best interpreted as a right-sided pattern of molecular change rather than definitive evidence of hemispheric lateralization, although they remain consistent with prior work suggesting hemispheric specialization in amygdala pain processing [26–28].

Discussion

This study shows that the dose of supradural CGRP used in the present study is sufficient to induce both sensory and affective behavioral changes in mice and to engage amygdala-related circuit mechanisms in a sex-dependent manner. Supradural CGRP produced periorbital mechanical hypersensitivity selectively in females, with hindpaw mechanical thresholds decreased in both sexes, but more so in females (Fig. 1). In parallel, CGRP increased avoidance-like behavior in both males and females in the elevated plus maze and light-dark box (Fig. 2). At the circuit level, recordings from right CeL/C neurons revealed greater excitability and larger PB-evoked EPSCs in females than males after supradural CGRP, and these effects were reduced by CGRP receptor blockade (Fig. 3). qPCR further identified transcriptional changes in the right CeA and right PBN that were more pronounced in females (Fig. 4). Together, these findings support the idea that meningeal CGRP engages sex-dependent central sensory-affective circuitry involving the PB-CeA axis.

Females developed significant periorbital allodynia after supradural CGRP, consistent with previous reports of female-biased migraine-like responses to dural CGRP [6, 7]. In contrast, hindpaw thresholds were reduced in both sexes, although the effect remained stronger in females. This pattern suggests that cephalic sensitivity is more strongly sex-dependent, while extra-cephalic sensitization may reflect broader central amplification of nociceptive processing, consistent with migraine-associated cutaneous allodynia and central sensitization [29]. It is also possible that the stronger responses observed in females reflect a lower functional threshold for CGRP-driven migraine-like effects rather than an exclusively female-specific mechanism. These findings should not be interpreted as indicating that CGRP-sensitive migraine mechanisms are exclusive to females. Instead, they suggest that the dose and conditions used here revealed more pronounced behavioral and central effects in females, potentially reflecting sex-dependent differences in sensitivity or threshold rather than the absence of CGRP-sensitive mechanisms in males. Although migraine pain is often unilateral, the associated allodynia can become more diffuse, consistent with the bilateral hindpaw hypersensitivity observed here. This is consistent with a greater contribution of peripheral trigeminomeningeal CGRP mechanisms to cephalic sensitivity, whereas extra-cephalic sensitization may more strongly reflect central recruitment of pain-related circuits, potentially including the PB-CeA system. Recent human provocation data also suggest sex-dependent differences in cGMP-mediated migraine susceptibility, with greater migraine induction in women than in men, which may be relevant to the female-predominant responses observed here [30]. CGRP-treated mice spent less time in the open arms of the elevated plus maze and more time in the dark compartment of the light-dark box, while open-field center measures were unchanged. This pattern is consistent with enhanced avoidance-like behavior rather than a generalized anxiogenic effect across assays. One possible interpretation is that the elevated plus maze is more sensitive than the open field to certain anxiety-like or avoidance-related effects, whereas open-field behavior is more strongly influenced by general exploratory drive and locomotion [31]. The increase in dark preference also aligns with light-avoidance behaviors commonly interpreted as photophobia-like responses in rodent migraine models [8, 9]. Recent work in a CGRP-based rat model has also highlighted the importance of optimizing light/dark box paradigms to better distinguish light aversion from anxiety-related behavior, which may help refine future studies of migraine-like photophobia [32]. Although locomotion was decreased in some assays, the preserved open-field center measures and unchanged latency to first dark entry argue against a nonspecific motor effect.

The electrophysiological data identify sex-dependent, CGRP-sensitive physiological differences in CeA neurons after supradural CGRP exposure. In the right CeA neurons, females displayed higher firing rates than males after supradural CGRP, which indicates the induction of neural changes that are preserved in a reduced slice preparation independent of the peripheral site of pain induction. CGRP₈₋₃₇ selectively decreased firing in females, suggesting greater functional CGRP sensitivity in CeA neurons recorded from female CGRP-exposed mice. In slices from mice exposed to supradural CGRP, neurons recorded from female mice showed larger PB-evoked EPSC amplitudes than those recorded from males, and these responses were preferentially decreased by ex vivo CGRP receptor blockade. It should be noted that the present data do not establish a direct causal link between supradural CGRP administration and CeA/PBN-CeA physiological changes, because the observed central responses may also reflect downstream consequences of trigeminal afferent activation or sensitization. The selective effect observed in females is consistent with prior electrophysiological evidence showing stronger CGRP-dependent migraine-like responses in females [6, 33]; studies in the amygdala have shown that CGRP enhances PB-CeA excitatory transmission and contributes to pain-related synaptic plasticity through a predominantly postsynaptic mechanism [15, 24]. These findings indicate that CGRP receptor signaling enhances both intrinsic excitability and PB-driven excitatory transmission in the CeA in females. Because paired-pulse ratio was unchanged, the decrease in PB-evoked EPSC amplitude is unlikely to involve changes in the presynaptic release probability, consistent with a primarily postsynaptic mechanism in CeA neurons as previously reported [33]. Given the established role of the CeA and PB-CeA pathway in the affective dimension of pain [14, 16, 19, 34], these results identify a plausible circuit substrate linking meningeal CGRP signaling to both sensory and affective migraine-like behaviors. The voltage sag analysis further suggests that supradural CGRP influences intrinsic membrane properties. Neurons recorded from male mice exhibited greater baseline sag amplitude than neurons recorded from female mice, consistent with greater baseline membrane stabilization or resilience to hyperexcitability, and CGRP₈₋₃₇ increased sag amplitude in females. Because sag is commonly associated with the hyperpolarization-activated current (Ih), these data suggest that CGRP signaling may influence Ih in female CeA neurons and thereby alter intrinsic membrane properties. Hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) channels have been implicated in migraine-related pain mechanisms, supporting the relevance of this possibility in a migraine-like context [35]. The higher baseline sag observed in neurons recorded from male mice may also have contributed to the smaller apparent effect of CGRP₈₋₃₇ in males, although this possibility was not directly tested here. Because baseline sag was already elevated in males, the absence of a significant additional effect should be interpreted cautiously and does not necessarily exclude CGRP-sensitive regulation.

The qPCR data, derived from bulk tissue at a single acute time point, provide additional support for sex-dependent molecular changes after supradural CGRP. The most consistent transcriptional effects were observed in the right PBN, where Ramp1 and Itgam were reduced in female CGRP-treated mice and Calca differed by sex across treatment conditions. In contrast, molecular changes in the right CeA were more limited, with Ramp1 showing a significant interaction and a trend toward reduced expression in female CGRP-treated mice. The qPCR data showed transcriptional changes in the right CeA and right PBN, whereas corresponding left-sided regions did not show significant effects under the present conditions. However, because electrophysiological recordings were obtained only from the right CeA and no direct right-versus-left statistical comparison was performed for the qPCR dataset, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not establish hemispheric lateralization. Rather, they identify a right-sided pattern of molecular change that is consistent with prior work suggesting hemispheric specialization in amygdala pain processing [26–28, 36, 37]. The most relevant aspect of this profile is the female-specific decrease in CGRP-related genes in the right CeA, particularly Ramp1 and Crcp. Previous work has shown sex- and pain state-dependent changes in central amygdala CGRP signaling, with CGRP mRNA increased in males at an acute stage and CGRP/CGRP receptor-related expression higher in females at a chronic stage [38]. These transcript changes may reflect acute regulation of CGRP pathway genes during a state of enhanced functional CGRP sensitivity, rather than a simple reduction in pathway engagement. Future studies will need to determine whether inhibition of central CGRP-sensitive mechanisms, including within the CeA/PBN-CeA circuit, can modify behavioral responses to supradural CGRP.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study include that electrophysiological recordings were obtained from unidentified CeL/C neurons, only in the right hemisphere, and only in brain slices from CGRP-treated animals. The present study therefore does not yet distinguish whether the observed sex differences were induced by supradural CGRP or reflect baseline differences in CeA physiology. Experiments and analyses were not performed under blinded conditions. Periorbital von Frey thresholds were derived from discrete filament categories rather than a truly continuous measure, which should be considered when interpreting the statistical analysis of these data. Future studies may also benefit from alternative analytical approaches for von Frey threshold estimation that improve the accuracy of 50% threshold calculations from discrete filament responses [39]. In addition, electrophysiological recordings were obtained only from the right CeA, and no direct statistical comparison between hemispheres was performed for the qPCR data; therefore, the present study does not establish hemispheric lateralization. The estrous cycle stage was monitored only for electrophysiological but not behavioral experiments, which should be considered when interpreting sex-dependent behavioral effects. qPCR was performed in bulk tissue, and all measurements were made at a single post-injection time point. Because qPCR was performed at 3 h after supradural CGRP, the observed transcriptional changes should be interpreted as an early molecular snapshot, and future studies will be needed to define their temporal evolution. Future studies will define the relevant CeA cell types, determine the temporal evolution of these changes, and clarify the molecular mechanisms linking CGRP signaling to PB-CeA circuit plasticity. It will also be important to determine whether similar right-sided physiological and transcriptional changes are enhanced in models of migraine chronification, particularly because CGRP-targeting therapies are often intended for patients with frequent episodic or chronic migraine.

Conclusions

In conclusion, supradural CGRP produced sex-differentiated mechanical hypersensitivity, increased avoidance behavior, and was associated with sex-dependent physiological changes in the right CeA and a right-sided pattern of transcriptional changes. Female mice showed stronger cephalic sensitivity, greater CeA excitability, greater PB-CeA synaptic transmission, and more pronounced transcriptional changes in the right PBN, with more limited molecular changes in the right CeA. These findings identify PB-CeA circuitry as a candidate substrate linking meningeal CGRP signaling to sensory hypersensitivity and female-biased central amygdala plasticity that was more pronounced in females, while suggesting that affective alterations may also involve additional circuits beyond the PB-CeA pathway.
