Antimicrobial peptide-induced inner membrane hyperpolarization is associated with antibiotic sensitization and attenuated MIC escalation in multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens
Authors: Jih-Chao Yeh, Prakash Kishore Hazam, You-Ying Lin, Yuan-Chih Chang, Sanjay Prasad Selvaraj, Chao-Chin Li, Po-Yen Lin, Yi-Ping Huang, Chi-Fon Chang, Ming-Feng You, Jyh-Yih Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance and dysregulated inflammation drive mortality in multidrug-resistant (MDR) sepsis. We evaluated the cationic peptide TP2-5 as a low-dose antibiotic adjuvant. At sub-MIC concentrations, TP2-5 enhanced antibiotic susceptibility of MDR E. coli in broth and 50% human serum, and in combination with antibiotics was associated with attenuated MIC escalation during 21-day serial passage. Membrane potential assays and cryo-electron tomography showed envelope perturbation characterized by inner-membrane hyperpolarization. This biophysical state was temporally associated with preferential interactions with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and anionic phospholipids rather than nonspecific permeabilization. TP2-5 neutralized LPS and reduced TLR4-dependent cytokine production. In our murine polymicrobial CLP sepsis model, TP2-5 alone or with meropenem achieved 100% survival, accompanied by reduced bacterial burden and systemic inflammatory cytokines, consistent with combined antibacterial and host-directed effects, supporting a multifunctional adjuvant profile. This study did not measure bacterial membrane potential in vivo, and the causal role of hyperpolarization in protection or attenuated MIC escalation remains to be determined. Subject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria are major causes of pneumonia and sepsis and are associated with high mortality despite the use of last-line antibiotics1–3. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is projected to cause up to 10 million deaths annually by 20501. When infections are caused by MDR pathogens, failure to clear bacteria is often accompanied by an uncontrolled inflammatory response that can lead to life-threatening sepsis. Available therapeutic options are increasingly limited, so there is a clear need for agents that can be combined with existing antibiotics to treat MDR infections while at the same time mitigating excessive inflammation.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising options due to their broad-spectrum bactericidal activity and lower propensity to induce resistance4–6. However, using AMPs in the clinic has been limited by toxicity and instability7. As a result, using AMPs as antibiotic adjuvants has emerged as an alternative strategy8–10. In combination, AMPs can lower the effective MIC of conventional antibiotics against MDR pathogens and may permit dose reduction of toxic drugs such as colistin11–13. However, most studies describe AMP adjuvants in general terms such as “increasing membrane permeability”14–16 and rarely investigate how specific membrane effects influence antibiotic entry, PMF or resistance evolution over time.

Hyperpolarization of the bacterial membrane at sub-inhibitory peptide concentrations (sub-MIC) has been reported for several membrane-active agents17–19, and a recent study suggested this phenomenon represents a reproducible biophysical response arising from selective cross-membrane ion transport and diffusion potential20. These studies identify hyperpolarization as a characteristic response to certain peptides but do not systematically examine how this state interacts with conventional antibiotics or how long it persists in MDR Gram-negative pathogens. In particular, the relationship between inner-membrane hyperpolarization and antibiotic potentiation remains poorly defined. It is also unclear whether this hyperpolarized state is associated with attenuated MIC escalation during serial passage in clinically relevant MDR strains.

Tilapia piscidin 2 (TP2) is a natural AMP with negligible antimicrobial activity and low toxicity. Through rational sequence modification, TP2-5 was developed as a cationic derivative with improved antibacterial potency and favorable stability and safety profiles in vitro and in vivo21,22. However, the potential of TP2-5 to act as an antibiotic adjuvant to resensitize MDR Gram-negative pathogens, how its capacity to remodel membrane potential relates to its adjuvant activity, and its impact on resistance evolution and sepsis-associated inflammation have not been clarified.

In this study, we investigated the cationic peptide TP2-5 as an antibiotic adjuvant against MDR Gram-negative infections. We used MDR E. coli BAA 2452 as a primary model to test whether TP2-5 can synergize with meropenem, colistin, ciprofloxacin, and vancomycin at sub-MIC concentrations, including in 50% human serum, and whether these combinations attenuate antibiotic MIC escalation during 21 days of serial passage. Real-time NPN, DiBAC₄(3), and PI assays and cryo-electron tomography were used to define how TP2-5 remodels the outer and inner membranes and to determine whether inner-membrane hyperpolarization is temporally associated with antibiotic potentiation. Lipid and cation competition assays, LPS binding, and docking analyses were applied to assess interactions with LPS and peptidoglycan-associated anionic phospholipids as molecular targets. Finally, we assessed the immunomodulatory effects, safety, and therapeutic efficacy of TP2-5 in combination with antibiotics in A. baumannii pneumonia and cecal ligation and puncture-induced polymicrobial sepsis models. These experiments were designed to examine whether TP2-5 remodels the bacterial envelope in a manner associated with antibiotic potentiation, and whether its combined antibacterial and immunomodulatory activities are consistent with improved outcomes in MDR Gram-negative infection.

Results

TP2-5 synergizes with multiple antibiotics and attenuates MIC escalation during serial passage

We first investigated whether TP2-5 can act as an antibiotic adjuvant against MDR E. coli BAA 2452. Checkerboard assays showed strong synergistic interaction between TP2-5 and meropenem (MEM), colistin (COL), vancomycin (VA) and ciprofloxacin (CIP). In the presence of 1/4× MIC of TP2-5, the MIC of MEM was reduced 4-fold (from 1 µg/mL to 0.25 µg/mL), while the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of COL and CIP were each reduced 4-fold. VA exhibited a high MIC of 64 µg/mL but also showed an 8-fold MIC reduction in combination with TP2-5 (Fig. 1a–d and Table S2). Because vancomycin is normally excluded by the Gram-negative outer membrane, the observed MIC reduction is interpreted as evidence of envelope barrier permeabilization rather than a clinically intended treatment combination. Synergy was defined as a fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) < 0.5 and was observed for TP2-5 in combination with MEM (FICI = 0.31), COL (FICI = 0.38), CIP (FICI = 0.38), and VA (FICI = 0.38) against MDR E. coli in Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB). This synergistic activity was retained when tested in 50% human serum (Fig. 1a–d). To broaden our assessment of TP2-5 as an adjuvant, we determined FIC index values for combinations of TP2-5 with vancomycin, ciprofloxacin, meropenem and colistin across multiple wild-type and MDR pathogens (Fig. 1e–h). All combinations exhibited synergistic activity when used to treat different pathogens, except for the combination of TP2-5 with vancomycin to treat S. epidermidis 32015, P. aeruginosa 19660, and MRSA 50015, and the combination of TP2-5 with ciprofloxacin against P. aeruginosa 19660. In these strains, the interaction with vancomycin or ciprofloxacin was generally additive (0.5 < FICI < 1) rather than synergistic (FICI ≤ 0.5), consistent with strain dependent intrinsic determinants of antibiotic entry and target access.

Growth inhibition assays (Fig. S1a–d) revealed that neither TP2-5 nor antibiotics at 1/4× MIC alone could significantly inhibit bacterial growth during a 24-h period. However, their combination at the same concentrations (1/4× MIC) led to marked growth suppression. Bactericidal kinetics assays further showed that the combined treatment achieved complete bacterial clearance within 4–8 h (Fig. S1e–h), whereas either agent alone allowed only partial killing. These results confirm that the combined treatment achieves real synergistic bactericidal activity at sub-MIC levels that are individually non-lethal.

We next evaluated the potential of TP2-5 to enhance antibiotic-mediated inhibition and elimination of MDR E. coli BAA 2452 biofilms using crystal violet staining assays. In the presence of sub-MIC TP2-5, the inhibitory effects of sub-MIC antibiotics were greater than those of antibiotics alone (Fig. S2a–e). Additionally, treating mature biofilms with the combination of sub-MIC TP2-5 and sub-MIC antibiotics caused the biofilms to disperse (Fig. S2f–h).

Finally, we evaluated the effect of TP2-5 on antibiotic MIC escalation during a 21-day serial passage experiment (Fig. 1i–l). When MDR E. coli BAA 2452 was cultured with antibiotics alone, high-level resistance emerged rapidly, with MICs increasing 8-fold for MEM, 16-fold for COL, 128-fold for VA, and 64-fold for CIP. For the vancomycin monotherapy lineage, the experiment was concluded in 9 passages because the MIC reached 4096 μg/mL. At this extreme concentration, further testing was discontinued as the MIC had reached the upper limit of the assay, and the magnitude of resistance was considered biologically definitive. In contrast, when antibiotics were serially passaged in the presence of TP2-5 fixed at 1/4× MIC (day 0), the magnitude of antibiotic MIC escalation was markedly attenuated throughout the 21-day experiment. In parallel, the TP2-5 alone group was conducted at 1× MIC to assess whether bacteria could develop resistance to the peptide under inhibitory conditions, and the minimal change in TP2-5 MIC observed over the same period is consistent with the intrinsic resistance-evasive property of membrane-active AMPs (Fig. 1i–l). As TP2-5 was passaged at 1× MIC in the monotherapy lineage, whereas it was maintained at a fixed sub-MIC concentration in the combination lineage, these conditions are not directly equivalent, and cross-condition comparison should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Sub-MIC TP2-5 remodels the bacterial envelope and is associated with inner-membrane hyperpolarization in Gram-negative bacteria

To understand how TP2-5 is associated with antibiotic potentiation and attenuated MIC escalation, we next examined its effects on bacterial envelope structure and membrane potential. 2D-NMR spectroscopy and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that TP2-5 exhibits an amphipathic α-helical conformation (Fig. S3b) with hydrophobic (light brown), hydrophilic (blue) moieties (Fig. S3c), and a positively charged surface. This characteristic is expected to be essential for antimicrobial function, as it facilitates peptide interaction with the bacterial membrane. In support of this mechanism, we also observed TP2-5 interaction with a model bacterial membrane in a 2000-ns in silico study (Fig. S3d). This intramolecular interaction was further supported by enhanced hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions as a function of time (Fig. S3e, f), consistent with preferential interaction with anionic bacterial lipids.

After demonstrating that TP2-5 interacts with bacterial membranes, we examined its effects on MDR E. coli BAA 2452. N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN) assays revealed that TP2-5 significantly increased outer membrane permeability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2a). We then monitored cytoplasmic membrane potential using the anionic dye DiBAC₄(3). At high bactericidal concentrations (1×, 5× and 10× MIC), TP2-5 induced strong depolarization, indicated by a marked increase in DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence (Fig. 2b) with physical membrane disruption consistent with loss of membrane potential integrity. Notably, at 1/4× MIC, DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence significantly decreased below baseline, consistent with inner-membrane hyperpolarization under sub-lethal peptide exposure (Fig. 2b). At 1/2× MIC, the signal returned close to baseline. These data suggest that, at sublethal doses, TP2-5 does not immediately collapse membrane potential; rather, sub-MIC TP2-5 is associated with a hyperpolarized membrane state, whereas higher doses trigger classical depolarization. The propidium iodide (PI) assay, which detects complete inner membrane disruption, also showed a sharp increase in fluorescence intensity with increasing concentrations of TP2-5, suggesting that TP2-5 disrupts inner membrane integrity (Fig. 2c).

We visualized E. coli treated with TP2-5 using cryo-electron tomography to investigate TP2-5-induced membrane disruption. Without treatment, the outer and inner membranes of E. coli minicells remained intact, and the distance between the outer and inner membranes was fixed (Fig. 2d–f). After treatment with TP2-5, dose-dependent morphological changes were observed. At lower concentrations (1/4× MIC, Figs. 2g and 1/2× MIC, Fig. 2h), membrane thinning with irregular holes on the outer membrane were observed. The minicells also showed blister formations protruding from the outer membrane, likely due to a membrane-thinning effect caused by TP2-5. In addition, small lipid clusters were present in close proximity to the outer and inner membranes, suggesting lipids had been removed from the membrane and collapsed into lipid clusters.

At higher TP2-5 concentrations (1× MIC, Fig. 2i, j), lipid clusters, holes and blisters on the outer membrane were apparent, along with shrunken minicells with double membranes. When the concentration of TP2-5 was increased to 5× or 10× MIC (Fig. 2k, l), some residual minicells were found to be trapped in the lipid clusters. Five hundred tomograms were collected and analyzed to understand these morphological changes (Fig. S4a, b).

The results showed that pore formation, low-density inner membranes and lipid clusters were frequently observed at a low TP2-5 concentration (1/4× MIC, 4 μg/mL). Although monotherapy with sub-MIC TP2-5 was ineffective at eliminating bacteria, the membrane remodeling observed at this dose was temporally associated with the synergistic activity observed in combination treatment. At higher TP2-5 concentrations (1× MIC to 10× MIC), shrunken minicells, lipid clusters with or without residual minicells were observed. Three-dimensional reconstructions of tomograms from TP2-5-treated minicells were generated using the back-projection method (Fig. S5, Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). The resulting images clearly showed membrane disruption at multiple sites on cells after TP2-5 treatment.

TP2-5-induced inner-membrane hyperpolarization is temporally associated with antibiotic sensitization

We performed real-time kinetic assays using sequential addition of 1/4× MIC TP2-5 and 1/4× MIC antibiotics to understand how TP2-5 and antibiotics interact dynamically at the outer and inner membranes (Fig. 3a, b). First, outer membrane permeability was assessed using NPN dye (Fig. 3c–f) with TP2-5 or antibiotics added at t = 20 and t = 40 min. At t = 20 min, TP2-5 alone (red line) induced a slight increase in NPN fluorescence, whereas MEM (Fig. 3c), VA (Fig. 3e) and CIP (Fig. 3f) added alone at t = 20 min (blue curves) failed to alter NPN signal. By contrast, COL alone (blue line) caused a dramatic and immediate increase in fluorescence (Fig. 3d). For the TP2-5 + Antibiotic group, addition of MEM, VA, or CIP at t = 40 min did not cause any further increase in NPN signal, resulting in a similar result to TP2-5 alone. Thus, under these conditions, outer membrane permeabilization is primarily attributable to TP2-5 or colistin, not to MEM, VA, or CIP. For inner-membrane potential, we used DiBAC₄(3) with the same t = 20 and t = 40 sequential design (Fig. 3a). When TP2-5 was added first at t = 20 min, DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence immediately decreased, indicating hyperpolarization (Fig. 3g–j, red curves). Subsequent addition of MEM, COL, VA, or CIP at t = 40 min caused a rapid increase in DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence from hyperpolarization, consistent with depolarization. Conversely, when MEM, COL, VA or CIP were added at t = 20 min, DiBAC₄(3) signal remained essentially unchanged relative to the PBS control (Fig. 3g–j, blue curves). Only after addition of TP2-5 at t = 40 min did fluorescence change, reflecting the intrinsic ability of TP2-5, rather than the antibiotics, to cause membrane potential change. These kinetics suggest that MDR E. coli treated with sub-MIC TP2-5 enters a relatively stable hyperpolarized state that is temporally associated with rapid membrane collapse upon subsequent antibiotic exposure. Whether this hyperpolarized state is mechanistically required for observed synergy or represents a correlated biophysical response remains to be established by functional perturbation experiments.

We then monitored inner membrane permeability using propidium iodide (PI). In the TP2-5 + antibiotic group (red line), addition of TP2-5 at t = 0 min caused PI fluorescence to rise rapidly and remain elevated, indicating that 1/4× TP2-5 alone is sufficient to create permeable lesions in the inner membrane (Fig. 3k-n, red curves). Subsequent addition of MEM, or VA at t = 20 min did not appreciably increase PI signal, whereas COL and CIP caused a further modest rise. Conversely, in the antibiotic + TP2-5 group (blue line), when antibiotics were added first at t = 0 min, none of the four drugs produced any signal change relative to the PBS control (green line) from 0 to 20 min (Fig. 3k–n). Only upon addition of TP2-5 at t = 20 min did PI fluorescence abruptly increase. Under the sub-MIC (1/4× MIC) used for synergy, only addition of TP2-5 induced PI uptake, whereas antibiotics alone caused little change, indicating that TP2-5 is the primary contributor to inner-membrane permeabilization.

TP2-5 targets LPS and peptidoglycan-associated anionic phospholipids

To identify the molecular targets underlying TP2-5-induced envelope remodeling, we examined different cell wall and membrane components on TP2-5 activity. The addition of exogenous LPS (LPS of E. coli O111:B4) caused a dose-dependent increase in the MIC of TP2-5 against MDR E. coli, indicating that LPS can bind to the peptide (Fig. 4a). Among phospholipids, pre-incubation with phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) markedly increased the MIC of TP2-5, whereas phosphatidylethanolamine (POPE) and phosphatidylcholine (POPC) showed negligible effects (Fig. 4b). These data indicate that TP2-5 preferentially targets anionic moieties enriched in both the outer (LPS) and inner (POPG) membrane.

Cation competition assays showed that high concentrations (480 µg/mL) of divalent cations (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) suppressed TP2-5 activity, whereas monovalent cations (Na⁺, K⁺) had no significant effect (Fig. 4c). Given that divalent cations bridge lipid A phosphate groups and stabilize the LPS leaflet, these findings are consistent with TP2-5 interfering with this divalent-cation-mediated stabilization through electrostatic competition (Fig. 4c). The LPS interaction was further confirmed by BODIPY-TR-cadaverine displacement assay, in which TP2-5 efficiently displaced the fluorescent probe from LPS, although with a distinct binding profile compared to polymyxin B (Fig. 4d). Molecular docking simulations visualized and predicted TP2-5 along the lipid A–core region of LPS via forming multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, with a calculated binding energy of -3.3 kcal/mol (Fig. 4e, f).

Finally, to understand the functional implications of TP2-5–LPS binding, we performed a cell-free neutralization assay. FITC-labeled LPS was co-incubated with TP2-5 and analyzed by flow cytometry. TP2-5 caused a concentration-dependent leftward shift of the FITC–LPS fluorescence together with a significant reduction in mean fluorescence intensity, indicating that TP2-5 directly binds and neutralizes LPS, thereby reducing the accessible FITC-LPS signal (Fig. 4f, g). Confocal microscopy further showed co-localization of TP2-5–Cy5.5 with FITC–LPS at the macrophage surface (Fig. 4h), suggesting that TP2-5 associates with LPS within receptor complexes. Collectively, these data support a dual-target model wherein TP2-5 targets both LPS in the outer membrane and peptidoglycan-associated anionic phospholipids in the inner membrane, thereby disrupting both the structural integrity and bioenergetics of the MDR Gram-negative bacteria.

TP2-5 modulates LPS-induced inflammatory responses in macrophages

Beyond its direct effects on the bacterial membrane, we investigated whether TP2-5 modulates LPS-induced inflammatory signaling. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a direct interaction on the surface of LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells, showing that TP2-5 dose-dependently inhibited the binding of FITC-conjugated LPS to macrophages and also interacted with pre-bound LPS on the cell surface (Fig. 5a, b). This surface-level neutralization of LPS translated into a potent suppression of downstream inflammatory signaling. TP2-5 significantly attenuated LPS-induced nitrite accumulation at 8 and 16 μg/mL; however, a slight relative increase was observed at higher concentration (32 and 64 μg/mL), which may reflect sublethal cellular stress at these doses rather than direct cytotoxicity, as these concentrations remain well below the IC₅₀ for RAW 264.7 cells (309 μg/mL) (Fig. 5c). Western blot showed that TP2-5 attenuated LPS-induced phosphorylation of p38 and c-Jun, and suppressed the expression of key inflammatory proteins, including iNOS, COX-2, IL-6, and TNF-α. Notably, the expression of the upstream adaptor protein MyD88 was unaffected, suggesting that TP2-5 acts downstream of this initial signaling event (Fig. 5d-k).

To assess the global impact of TP2-5 on the inflammatory transcriptome, we performed RNA-seq analysis on LPS-activated macrophages with or without TP2-5 treatment. This revealed a broad suppression of LPS-induced proinflammatory genes, such as Rsad2[27], Mx1 [28], Cmpk2 [20], CXCl10 [29], CXCl11 [30], and Fcgr1 [31]. Interestingly, we also noted the upregulation of Mmp9 [34], a gene with complex roles in LPS-mediated inflammation (Fig. S6a, b). Despite this, the overall transcriptomic profile was clearly anti-inflammatory. Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses confirmed that TP2-5 significantly downregulated biological processes and molecular pathways related to defense response, leukocyte migration, and cytokine activity (Fig. S6c, d, Fig. S7). Collectively, these findings indicate that TP2-5 not only neutralizes LPS at the membrane but also inhibits the downstream inflammatory transcriptional program, which may contribute to its protective effects in vivo.

Safety and toxicological evaluation of TP2-5 in vitro and in vivo

TP2-5 exhibited limited activity against Gram-positive wild-type and drug-resistant strains (Table 1), with MICs of 128 μg/mL for all tested strains except for Staphylococcus epidermidis. In contrast, TP2-5 demonstrated greater efficacy against Gram-negative bacteria, with MIC values ranging from 4 to 16 μg/mL for most tested strains, and 32 μg/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates. To assess the safety and selectivity of TP2-5, the selectivity index (SI; ratio of IC₅₀ to MIC) was calculated for four mammalian cell lines (Fig. 6a–d, Table 1).

Human keratinocytes (HaCaT, Fig. 6a), mouse macrophages (RAW 264.7, Fig. 6b), A549 cells (Fig. 6c) and HepG2 cells (Fig. 6d) were treated with TP2-5. The concentrations causing 50% cytotoxicity (IC50) were as follows for each cell line: HaCaT, 267 µg/mL; RAW 264.7, 309 µg/mL; A549, 194 µg/mL; and HepG2, 590 µg/mL. Based on these data, the SI was calculated. TP2-5 showed highly favorable SI values against Gram-negative MDR strains, ranging from 6.1 to 147.5 (Table 1), while values against Gram-positive strains were lower (1.5–18.4). These results indicate a preferable biocompatibility profile, particularly for treating Gram-negative bacterial infections.

To evaluate the in vivo safety of TP2-5, female C57BL/6 mice received a single intravenous administration of TP2-5 up to 12.5 mg/kg. Over a 7-day observation period, no significant changes in body weight or survival were noted (Fig. 6e, f). Furthermore, analysis of blood samples revealed that biomarkers for hepatic function (GOT, GPT) in TP2-5-treated mice were comparable to those of the saline-treated control group (Fig. 6i, j). Regarding renal function, while a minor statistically significant variation in CRE was observed at low doses in healthy mice (Fig. 6h), all values including BUN (Fig. 6g) remained within the established physiological baseline for this strain. Notably, this transient change was not observed in the subsequent CLP sepsis model (Fig. 7m) and did not correlate with any signs of systemic toxicity, suggesting that the observed CRE levels reflect individual animal differences rather than functional renal impairment.

To determine the in vivo biodistribution of TP2-5, the peptide was conjugated with Cy5.5 and administered intravenously to mice. In vivo fluorescence imaging (IVIS) revealed that the peptide’s signal peaked within 6 h post-administration and subsequently declined over a 72-h period (Fig. S8a). Quantitative analysis of regions of interest (ROIs) showed that TP2-5 accumulated most significantly in the liver, with detectable but lower levels observed in the kidney, heart, lungs, and spleen (Fig. S8). These data support the use of TP2-5 at doses sufficient for adjuvant activity without unacceptable toxicity.

TP2-5 demonstrates in vivo efficacy and antibiotic synergy in a Galleria mellonella infection model

The in vivo efficacy of TP2-5 was evaluated in a Galleria mellonella model of MDR E. coli BAA 2452 infection. In the infection model, larvae challenged with the pathogen alone showed fewer than 20% survival over 5 days (Fig. S9). While sub-MIC concentrations of antibiotics alone provided limited or partial protection, the addition of sub-MIC TP2-5 consistently and significantly improved survival outcomes across all four antibiotic classes tested. Specifically, combining TP2-5 with either colistin or vancomycin resulted in 100% survival of the infected larvae (Fig. S9c, d). Moreover, TP2-5 restored the efficacy of ciprofloxacin, increasing survival from 40% to 80% (Fig. S9d), and markedly enhanced the protective effect of meropenem, boosting survival to 90% in the combination group (1/2× MIC TP2-5 + 1/4× MIC MEM) (Fig. S9b).

Combination of TP2-5 and antibiotics improves outcomes in MDR pneumonia and polymicrobial sepsis

The therapeutic efficacy of TP2-5 was first assessed in a murine model of pneumonia induced by MDR Acinetobacter baumannii 2088. A single 8 mg/kg dose of TP2-5 administered post-infection resulted in a significant, approximately 3-log reduction in the pulmonary bacterial burden compared to the untreated group (Fig. 7c). While bacterial loads in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and liver showed a non-significant trend towards reduction (Fig. 7b, d), the potent local effect in the lungs was clear. Consistent with the reduction in bacterial load, a trend toward lower nitrite levels, an indicator of inflammation, was also observed (p = 0.08, Fig. 7e).

To evaluate TP2-5 in a more severe systemic infection, we used the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of polymicrobial sepsis. TP2-5 monotherapy (2 mg/kg) achieved 100% survival over the 7-day study, compared to 62.5% for meropenem and 75% for colistin (Fig. 7f). This result suggests that the in vivo efficacy of TP2-5 is multifunctional, likely reflecting contributions from both direct antibacterial activity and potent host-directed immunomodulatory effects, including LPS neutralization and cytokine reduction, rather than antibacterial activity alone. Co-administration of TP2-5 with meropenem also resulted in 100% survival over the same period (Fig. 7f), consistent with synergistic in vivo activity. Besides, co-administration of TP2-5 with colistin resulted in 90% survival (Fig. 7f), which was numerically lower than TP2-5 monotherapy but higher than colistin alone (75%) and did not reach statistical significance compared to the TP2-5 alone group, suggesting that this difference reflects individual animal variability rather than a true antagonistic interaction.

This survival benefit was underpinned by robust bacterial clearance. TP2-5, both alone and in combination with antibiotics, significantly reduced the bacterial load in the peritoneal fluid and systemically cleared bacteria from multiple vital organs, including the lungs, liver, kidneys, and spleen (Fig. 7g–k). This effective infection control led to significant organ protection, as evidenced by the normalization of biomarkers for renal function (BUN, CRE) and hepatic function (GOT, GPT) in TP2-5-treated groups (Fig. 7l–o). Furthermore, TP2-5 treatment profoundly suppressed the systemic levels of key proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, confirming its potent immunomodulatory activity in vivo (Fig. 7p–q).

Discussion

MDR Gram-negative infections remain difficult to treat, even with last-line antibiotics1–3. Treatment often fails because drugs cannot cross the bacterial envelope or resistance emerges rapidly. In this study, the cationic peptide TP2-5 functioned as an antibiotic adjuvant that addressed both problems. At sub-MIC levels, TP2-5 restored the activity of MEM, COL, VA, and CIP against MDR E. coli BAA 2452, even in 50% human serum. While the synergy with vancomycin is consistent with envelope remodeling, its clinical application against Gram-negative pathogens requires further validation regarding dosing and local drug concentrations. The combination of TP2-5 with vancomycin did not meet the strict threshold for synergy (FICI ≤ 0.5) against S. epidermidis 32015, P. aeruginosa 19660, and MRSA 50015, nor did the combination with ciprofloxacin against P. aeruginosa 19660. These observed FICI values fell within the additive range (0.5 < FICI ≤ 1.0), indicating that TP2-5 still enhanced the susceptibility of these strains to vancomycin or ciprofloxacin. This finding suggests that the degree of envelope sensitization by TP2-5 may vary depending on the intrinsic membrane composition and resistance profile of individual strains.

During 21 days of serial passage, combinations of TP2-5 with antibiotics substantially attenuated antibiotic MIC escalation compared with antibiotic monotherapy, consistent with TP2-5 functioning as an adjuvant that can enhance antibiotic susceptibility in MDR pathogens and limit the magnitude of MIC escalation under selection. The TP2-5 alone group in the serial passage experiment was conducted at 1× MIC to monitor whether bacteria could develop resistance to the peptide itself over time. The minimal change in TP2-5 MIC observed over 21 days is consistent with the well-established property of membrane-active AMPs to resist the development of resistance, as their rapid physical disruption of the bacterial membrane at inhibitory concentrations imposes a high fitness barrier that is difficult for bacteria to overcome through conventional mutational mechanisms7,12,13. This condition is therefore not directly comparable to the fixed sub-MIC TP2-5 used in the combination lineage. Furthermore, as a sub-MIC TP2-5 monotherapy serial passage control was not included, we cannot exclude the possibility that sub-MIC TP2-5 alone contributes to the attenuation of MIC escalation independently of the antibiotic. The resistance-attenuating effect observed in the combination lineage is therefore best interpreted as consistent with TP2-5 adjuvant activity, without implying that this effect is uniquely attributable to the combination treatment. AMPs have been reported to synergize with antibiotics, but the mechanism is often described broadly as “increasing membrane permeability”7,12,13. In contrast, our data are consistent with a model in which TP2-5 acts as a membrane sensitizer, generating inner-membrane changes temporally associated with antibiotic action, rather than behaving as a purely membranolytic peptide.

NMR and molecular dynamics show that TP2-5 forms an amphipathic α-helix at the membrane interface, consistent with preferential binding to bacterial membranes and with the high SI seen in cytotoxicity assays. In real-time NPN, DiBAC₄(3) and PI assays, low concentrations (0.25× MIC) of TP2-5 increased outer-membrane permeability, induced inner-membrane hyperpolarization, and compromised membrane integrity. At the same concentrations (0.25× MIC), MEM, VA and CIP exhibited negligible effects on membrane potential. Colistin disrupted the outer membrane23,24, as expected, but did not fully reproduce the inner-membrane effect of TP2-5. Therefore, under the sub-MIC conditions used for combination treatment, TP2-5 appears to be the primary contributor to envelope destabilization.

The sequential addition experiments reveal a temporal association between inner membrane hyperpolarization and antibiotic activity. When TP2-5 was added first at sub-MIC, DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence dropped and remained low for at least 20–40 min, indicating that the inner membrane entered a hyperpolarized state. None of the four antibiotics, when added first at the same sub-MIC concentrations, changed DiBAC₄(3) or PI fluorescence signals. Only when TP2-5 was added into antibiotic-treated cells did we observe the characteristic decrease in DiBAC₄(3) and the rise in PI. Conversely, when TP2-5 was added first and antibiotics were added later, the system flipped in the opposite direction, wherein DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence jumped upward from the hyperpolarized baseline and PI increased sharply, indicating rapid loss of membrane potential and integrity. Cryo-electron tomography provides a structural correlate for these changes: at sub-MIC, TP2-5 produced membrane thinning, pore-like defects, blister-like protrusions and lipid clustering, whereas at bactericidal concentrations it generated shrunken cells surrounded by large lipid aggregates.

While AMP-induced bacterial membrane hyperpolarization at sub-MIC levels has been reported previously17,20,25, those studies established hyperpolarization as a possible response to membrane-active agents rather than examining its interaction with antibiotics or its impact on resistance evolution. Notably, recent work20 has demonstrated that chemically diverse AMPs with membrane-inducible amphipathic structures trigger cytoplasmic membrane hyperpolarization in E. coli at sublethal concentrations via selective ion transport and diffusion potential without gross membrane rupture. Consistent with diffusion-potential models proposed in recent AMP studies, the observed DiBAC₄(3) decrease may reflect ion-selective flux rather than nonspecific membrane rupture; however, specific ion fluxes were not directly measured in the present study. Our observations are consistent with this phenomenon, wherein the rapid, sustained drop in DiBAC₄(3) fluorescence at 0.25× MIC is consistent with a diffusion potential-driven hyperpolarized state, as proposed by Bhaumik et al.20, rather than non-selective membrane rupture. These observations are consistent with TP2-5-induced hyperpolarization as a biophysical response to peptide-membrane interaction. The structural basis for this activity is consistent with the criteria defined by Bhaumik et al.17,20,25, who showed that AMPs capable of inducing bacterial hyperpolarization share three structural features: (i) a membrane-induced amphipathic secondary structure, (ii) a side-chain arrangement that spatially segregates a hydrophobic face from a cationic face, and (iii) the absence of disulfide bonds that would restrict conformational flexibility during cell-wall traversal. Our 2D-NMR spectroscopy and 2000 ns molecular dynamics simulations showed that TP2-5 forms a well-defined amphipathic α-helix upon membrane association, with hydrophobic residues on one face and positively charged lysine residues on the opposite face, a geometry consistent with the structural criteria associated with diffusion potential-generating AMPs as described by Bhaumik et al.20.

In contrast to disulfide-constrained β-sheet peptides such as tachyplesin II, which are conformationally locked and ineffective at inducing hyperpolarization at sub-MIC concentrations in that study17,20,25, TP2-5 lacks disulfide bonds and adopts an inducible amphipathic α-helical architecture upon membrane association. This property provides a structural basis consistent with inner-membrane hyperpolarization at sublethal concentrations. However, while our data demonstrate a strong temporal association between hyperpolarization and antibiotic potentiation, hyperpolarization is best interpreted as a sensitizing biophysical state temporally associated with antibiotic action, rather than a singular causal determinant of synergy. The hyperpolarized state associated with sub-MIC TP2-5 is hypothesized to represent a sensitizing biophysical state that coincides with altered bacterial capacity to sustain the proton motive force (PMF). Under this condition, subsequent antibiotic treatment is associated with rapid depolarization, structural collapse and bactericidal activity. Whether the inner membrane hyperpolarization is mechanistically required for synergistic effect or represents a correlated membrane stress response remains to be clarified in future studies employing membrane potential-clamping approaches.

In lipid and cation competition experiments, exogenous addition of E. coli O111:B4 LPS and the anionic phospholipid POPG significantly increased the MIC of TP2-5 in a dose-dependent manner, whereas POPE and POPC showed negligible effects. This indicates a preferential interaction with anionic headgroups enriched in LPS and peptidoglycan-associated inner membrane domains. High concentrations of Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, which normally bridge LPS phosphate groups and stabilize the outer membrane26, reduced TP2-5 activity more strongly than Na⁺ and K⁺, consistent with electrostatic competition for negatively charged headgroups. BODIPY–TR–cadaverine displacement, molecular docking and cell-free FITC–LPS neutralization assays collectively support direct binding of TP2-5 to the lipid A in the core region of LPS. These results support a dual-target model in which TP2-5 engages LPS in the outer membrane and PG-associated anionic phospholipids in the inner membrane, leading to defined changes in envelope structure and membrane potential.

Safety is an important consideration for any peptide development. The SI is defined as the ratio between the IC₅₀ in mammalian cells and the MIC against bacteria. In early-stage screening, compounds with SI values of 10 or higher are often regarded as having acceptable in vitro selectivity or low toxicity27,28. In our study, TP2-5 exhibited high SI (ranging from 6.1 to 147.5) across multiple MDR Gram-negative strains, indicating that antibacterial activity is achieved at concentrations well below those that impair mammalian cell viability. In mice, intravenous and intratracheal administration of TP2-5 at the tested doses did not cause obvious changes in body weight, survival, serum markers of liver or kidney injury. The combination of a high SI and a low adjuvant dose suggests that TP2-5 can enhance antibiotic efficacy without imposing excessive additional toxicity, which is particularly relevant when combined with last-line agents such as colistin. In MDR A. baumannii pneumonia and in CLP-induced polymicrobial sepsis models, combinations of TP2-5 with antibiotics significantly reduced bacterial loads, nitrite, and systemic cytokines, improved markers of organ injury, and increased survival. The observation that TP2-5 monotherapy achieved 100% survival in the CLP sepsis model, whereas conventional antibiotics provided only partial protection, suggests that host-directed effects, including LPS neutralization and attenuation of systemic cytokine responses, play a substantial role in vivo potentially independently of direct antibacterial activity. Accordingly, the in vivo benefits of TP2-5 are likely multifactorial, and a direct mechanistic link between the in vitro membrane hyperpolarization phenotype and in vivo survival outcomes has not been established in this study. These data support the idea that TP2-5 can be used at low doses to enhance antibiotic efficacy and mitigate LPS-driven inflammatory damage at the same time.

The framework developed here generates several experimentally testable predictions. First, if inner-membrane hyperpolarization is mechanistically required for antibiotic potentiation, then collapsing the membrane potential before TP2-5 addition using ionophores such as carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) or valinomycin should abolish or reduce synergy in checkerboard assays. Second, bacterial mutants with altered inner-membrane phospholipid composition, such as strains with reduced phosphatidylglycerol content, would be predicted to show diminished hyperpolarization responses and attenuated synergy with TP2-5. Finally, if the hyperpolarization state enhances antibiotic accumulation, direct measurement of intracellular antibiotic concentrations during the hyperpolarized window should reveal increased drug uptake relative to non-hyperpolarized controls. These predictions provide a roadmap for establishing whether hyperpolarization is causally required, sufficient, or merely correlative with respect to the adjuvant activity of TP2-5.

In summary, TP2-5 is a membrane-sensitizing peptide adjuvant that targets LPS and PG-associated anionic phospholipids. At concentrations below the MIC, TP2-5 is associated with an inner-membrane hyperpolarization phenotype and a sensitizing biophysical state preceding antibiotic exposure. These properties are associated with synergy with multiple antibiotic classes, attenuated MIC escalation during serial passage in vitro, and improved outcomes in experimental pneumonia and sepsis while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. The in vivo efficacy likely reflects contributions from both direct membrane-targeting activity and host-directed immunomodulatory effects. These results provide a conceptual and experimental framework for using inner-membrane-targeting peptides as adjuvants to help extend the useful lifespan of existing antibiotics against MDR Gram-negative infections.

Methods

Antimicrobial activity assay

The MICs of TP2-5 and other antibiotics were measured by the broth microdilution method. Briefly, the treatment (TP2-5 or antibiotic) was diluted in two-fold dilution series in MHB. Each dilution was then mixed with an equal volume of bacterial suspension in MHB (1 × 106 CFU/mL) and incubated at 37 °C under static conditions for 18 h. The wells with the lowest concentration showing no visible growth were identified, and the treatment concentration in these wells was considered the MIC10.

Fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index

The FIC index was measured using a checkerboard assay. One hundred microliters of MHB was added to each well of a 96-well plate. Antibiotics were then diluted along the abscissa, and TP2-5 was diluted along the ordinate. For some assays, human serum, LPS (E. coli O111:B4, Sigma-Aldrich), PG, PE, PC (Avanti), Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, or K+ (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the MHB in order to test the effects of these substances on the synergistic activity of TP2-5 and antibiotics. The bacterial suspension was mixed to achieve a cell density of 5 × 105 CFU/mL, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C under static conditions for 18 h. The wells with no growth and the lowest combined test candidate concentrations were identified, and corresponding concentrations were used to calculate the FIC index9,10. Synergy was defined as FICI ≤ 0.5; additivity 0.5 < FICI ≤ 1.0; indifference 1.0 < FICI < 4.0; antagonism ≥ 4.0.

Growth curve and killing kinetics assay

Different concentrations of TP2-5, antibiotics, or combinations of TP2-5 with antibiotics were added to different wells of a 96-well microplate, and each treatment was mixed with an equal volume of suspended bacteria in MHB (1 × 106 CFU/mL). The growth curves were measured by monitoring absorbance at 600 nm over time at 37 °C using a microplate reader (Agilent). Plates were maintained at 37 °C under static conditions (no shaking) during kinetic reads.

For the killing kinetics assay, different concentrations of TP2-5, antibiotics, or combinations of TP2-5 with antibiotics were mixed with equal volumes of suspended bacteria in MHB (1 × 106 CFU/mL) and incubated at 37 °C under static conditions for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. The mixtures were then serially diluted and spread onto MH agar plates for culturing at 37 °C under static conditions for 24 h. The killing kinetics curves were plotted after calculating the colony-forming units (CFUs) for each treatment10.

Cryo-tomography electron microscopy

E. coli (MC4100 malTc DmalE malG501 DminCDE::kan) was kindly provided by Prof. Rita P. Y. Chen and cultured in 200 mL of LB medium with Kanamycin (40 μg/mL) at 37 °C overnight. To collect the E. coli minicells, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C. Finally, the supernatant was collected and concentrated by ultrafiltration at 800 × g for 50 min at 4 °C. E. coli minicells treated with TP2-5 (4–640 μg/mL) were loaded onto a glow-discharged Quantifoil R 2/2 holey carbon grid (Quantifoil GmbH). The samples were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Imaging was performed using a 200 kV Talos Arctica transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Falcon III direct electron detector operated in linear mode. Tilt series images were captured at a nominal magnification of 28,000×, corresponding to a pixel size of 3.6 Å, and a defocus value of 15 µm. Data acquisition was conducted using Tomography software version 4.10.0. The tilt series images were collected over an angular range from +51° to −51° in 3° increments, with an electron dose of 3 e-/ Å2 per tilt. Image recording was further processed using Tomography software (version 5.2.0). Intra-layer distance and pixel correction were performed according to a published protocol29.

Membrane permeability and potential assay

Bacterial cells were washed and resuspended in 10 mM PBS, pH 7.4, and adjusted to 108 CFU/mL. Glucose (25 mM) was present in the buffer, and up to 10 µM N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN) dye was added. Ninety microliters of cells were added to a black plate, and the fluorescence signal was allowed to stabilize for 40 min (λ ex/ λ em: 350/420 nm). Then, 10 µL of test drug was added, and signals were recorded as a function of time. For membrane potential measurements, bacterial cells were washed and resuspended in 10 mM PBS, pH 7.4, and adjusted to 108 CFU/mL. Glucose (25 mM) was present in the buffer, and 50 µM (Bis-(1,3-Dibutylbarbituric Acid)Trimethine Oxonol) (DiBAC4(3)) dye was added. 90 µL bacterial suspension was added to a black plate and the fluorescence signal was allowed to equilibrate for 30 min to ensure a stable baseline. Then, 10 µL of test candidate at indicated concentrations was added to initiate the reaction. Fluorescence signals were recorded for excitation and emission wavelengths of 490 nm and 516 nm, respectively. PI (20 µg/mL) was added to mid-log phase bacterial cultures with cell density of 108 CFU/mL. Ninety microliters of bacterial suspension was added to a black plate, followed by addition of 10 µL of test candidate at the test concentrations. Fluorescence signals were recorded for excitation and emission wavelengths of 584 nm and 620 nm, respectively. During baseline equilibration and kinetic acquisition, plates were maintained under static conditions (no shaking), and wells were gentle mixed by pipetting immediately after addition of test candidates9.

Biofilm inhibition and eradication assays

The activities of TP2-5 and antibiotic combinations were determined for inhibition of biofilm formation and eradication of mature biofilms using a checkerboard assay. A total of 50 µL of cell suspension (2 × 105 CFU/mL) was mixed with 50 µL of treatments, followed by incubation at 37 °C under static conditions for 24 h. Afterward, the wells were washed and treated with methanol for 15 min. Then, the wells were washed with PBS and treated with 100 µL of 0.02% crystal violet solution for 30 min. The plates were dried, and the stain was dissolved with 33% acetic acid. Stain intensity (biomass) was measured by absorbance at 595 nm. For the biofilm eradication assay, bacterial cells without test agents were incubated for 24 h. Then, different concentrations of TP2-5 alone, antibiotics alone, or combined TP2-5 and antibiotics were added, followed by incubation overnight at 37 °C. Wells were washed with PBS, followed by methanol treatment for 15 min. Methanol was removed, and the wells were dried, followed by the addition of 0.02% crystal violet and incubation for 30 min. Residual solution was discarded, and the wells were dried, followed by dissolution of biomass in 33% acetic acid. Biomass was quantified by absorbance at 595 nm10,30.

Induced resistance assay

To assess induced resistance, resistance evolution was monitored by daily serial passage over 21 days. For each passage, two-fold serial dilutions were prepared in MHB for (i) TP2-5 alone, (ii) antibiotic alone, or (iii) antibiotic in the presence of TP2-5 fixed at 0.25× MIC (day 0) to assess its role as a stable adjuvant. A cell suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was then added and incubated at 37 °C under static conditions for 24 h. MICs were determined as described above and defined as the lowest concentration with no visible growth. For the next passage, bacteria were collected from the well containing the highest concentration that still permitted visible growth and resuspended in fresh MHB, used to inoculate a newly prepared dilution series. For the combination lineage, TP2-5 was maintained at the fixed concentration described above to prioritize its adjuvant-driven effects, while the antibiotic dilution series was updated daily according to the newly determined MIC. The procedure was repeated for 21 days9,10.

Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy

Binding of TP2-5 to FITC-conjugated LPS was observed using flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter). RAW 264.7 cells were treated with different concentrations of TP2-5 for 1 h at 4 °C, then washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 25 ng/mL FITC-LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. For imaging experiments, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with 25 ng/mL FITC-LPS for 30 min, then washed with PBS and incubated with Cy5.5-conjugated TP2-5 for 2 h at 4 °C. Images were acquired using confocal microscopy. The inhibitory effect of TP2-5 on LPS binding to TLR4 was analyzed with flow cytometry and confocal microscopy. RAW 264.7 cells were treated with 25 ng/mL LPS and with or without TP2-5 for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with FITC-TLR4 at 4 °C for 1 h, using flow cytometry to measure the fluorescence intensity31.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from RAW 264.7 macrophage cells using radio immunoprecipitation buffer (RIPA, Sigma-Aldrich). Cell lysates were collected and immunoblotted with primary antibodies against MyD88 (Abcam, 1:1000), p-p38 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), p-c-jun (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), total-p38 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), total c-jun (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), IL6 (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:500), TNF-α (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:500), and α-tubulin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:1000)31

Transcriptomic analysis

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 25 ng/mL) was used to stimulate RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cells for 30 min, followed by TP2-5 treatment for 24 h. The cells were harvested, and Trizol® Reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to isolate RNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A library was generated using SureSelect XT HS2 mRNA Library Preparation kit (Agilent), and size selection was performed using AMPure XP beads. Sequencing was performed, and BCL files were converted to FASTQC reads, followed by standardization and comparative analysis with mouse reference genome. Differential expression analysis was performed using the genome bias detection/correction method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (cluster Profiler v3.6) based on the following criteria: p < 0.05 and fold-change > 2.0. DEGs were then analyzed according to Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG tool32.

NMR study

NMR spectra were collected using a Bruker AV800 spectrometer equipped with 5-mm triple resonance cryoprobe and Z-axis pulsed field gradient. NMR data were obtained from Shigemi tubes with 2 mM TP2-5 sample in 1× phosphate buffer (PB) at pH 6.92 with 40% TFE-d3 and 10% D2O. Two-dimensional homo-nuclear NMR experiments were performed at 293 K or 288 K. TOCSY was performed with 3-9-19 solvent suppression sequence at mixing time of 80 or 60 ms. NOESY was conducted at pre-saturation with mixing time of 300 or 500 ms. COSY was performed at pre-saturation. Spectral analysis was accomplished with a Bruker Top Spin 3.5, followed by corresponding analysis using the Sparky program suite33.

Competitive LPS interaction assay: displacement of BODIPY-TR-cadaverine dye

LPS (10 µg/mL) was mixed with 5 mM BODIPY-TR-cadaverine (BC) for 4 h in the dark at room temperature. Later, the test peptide was added, and free dye was quantified spectroscopically (λ ex, 580 nm and λ em, 620 nm)33.

Cytotoxicity assay

Human hepatoblastoma cell line (HepG2), human lung carcinoma cells (A549), human keratinocyte cell (HaCaT), and mouse RAW 264.7 macrophage cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Viability of mammalian cells was measured using the AlamarBlue assay (Sigma-Aldrich) with absorbance at 570 nm9, and cytotoxicity was calculated by comparison to controls. TP2-5 was added in 96-well plates containing 1 × 104 cells in DMEM and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by the AlamarBlue assay.

In vivo toxicity

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Academia Sinica (Protocol No. 21-12-1771) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Female C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old and approximately 20 g) were obtained from BioLasco. Mice were treated with different concentrations of TP2-5 by intravenous injections and were kept under observation for 7 days. Mouse survival, body weight, serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT), serum glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (GPT), creatinine (CRE), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were monitored9.

Biodistribution assay

The biological distribution of TP2-5 was assessed using IVIS imaging (Revvity). A dose of 10 mg/kg Cy5.5-conjugated TP2-5 was injected intravenously. Then, 3D quantitative imaging fluorescence images were acquired, and fluorescence intensity was measured in ROIs at different time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 26, 48 and 72 h)34.

In vivo Galleria mellonella infection model

G. mellonella larvae (weight: 250 to 300 mg, length: 2 to 3 cm) were purchased from Taiwan Green Propolis Biotech Ltd. A total of 2 × 106 CFU MDR E. coli BAA 2452 was injected into Galleria mellonella in the left posterior proleg. One hour after injection, each infected G. mellonella was injected with the designated treatment in the right posterior proleg (n = 10 per group for survival analysis). The survival rates for each treatment group were recorded over 5 days. The experiment was performed with two independent biological replicates10. For microbial burden analysis, a separate experiment of five infected larvae per group was collected at 24 h post-treatment and frozen at −20 °C for 30 min to ensure humane euthanasia and to quench microbial metabolism, thereby preventing further bacterial proliferation prior to homogenization. The frozen larvae were washed with ethanol and homogenized with a tissue homogenizer for 5 min. The homogenate was serially diluted and inoculated onto chromogenic base (CHROMagar, Saint-Denis, France) plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under static conditions for 48 h. The number of CFUs was calculated to quantify the microbial burden.

Molecular docking analysis

The PDB file for TP2-5 (PDB ID:9IO2) (Table S1) was obtained from the NMR analysis. The file for LPS (PDB ID: 1QFG) was obtained from the E. coli ferric hydroxamate receptor complex. Docking analysis was performed using PyRx software and AutoDock Vina program with an exhaustiveness value set to 20. Binding energies were calculated, and the docking conformations were obtained and analyzed (BIOVIA Discovery Studio)35.

In vivo intratracheal infection study

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Academia Sinica (Approval No. 21-12-1771). Healthy female C57BL/6 mice (n = 4) were acclimatized and treated with MDR A. baumannii 2088 at 107 CFU/animal through the nasal route. Approximately 30 min after infection, 8 mg/kg TP2-5 was administered, and animals were kept under observation for the next 24 h. The animals then were sacrificed and the BALF, lungs, liver and blood were collected. Parts of the samples (except blood) were plated to test bacterial load. Blood samples were used to estimate the nitrite levels as a measure of inflammation in test animals. The study was conducted as approved and according to ethical guidelines (IACUC no: 21-12-1771)36.

In vivo polymicrobial sepsis model using cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) method

The experiment was performed as reported elsewhere9. Healthy female C57BL/6 mice (n = 8) were acclimatized to the laboratory, and surgery was performed on anesthetized mice (1.5–2% isoflurane). Female mice were selected for this study as they demonstrate higher and more consistent survival rates in the CLP sepsis model due to the protective effects of estrogen, whereas male mice often exhibit high variability in their response. In this study, female mice were chosen in accordance with established protocols to minimize experimental variance and allow for a focused evaluation of therapeutic mechanisms37.

In the surgery, the cecum was punctured with a 23G needle to induce sepsis. 60 minutes post-surgery, treatments (i.e., TP2-5, antibiotics, and combinations) were administered via intraperitoneal injection. Percent survival was monitored for each group over the next 7 days. In a separate experiment, lung, liver, kidney, and spleen were collected from animals (n = 5) for bacterial load analysis after 24 h treatment. Blood samples from test and control groups were collected and used to assess kidney function (BUN and CRE), liver function (GOT and GPT), and inflammation (IL6 and TNF-α).

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as mean ± standard deviation from experiments performed in at least triplicate. Statistical significance was analyzed by Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and survival data were plotted in Kaplan-Meier curves using Prism 10 software (GraphPad). Significant differences are indicated by asterisks (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001) in the figures.
