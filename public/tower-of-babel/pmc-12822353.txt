Additive value of combining triglyceride-glucose index and estimated glucose disposal rate for incident stroke prediction across glycaemic-regulation subgroups: a prospective cohort of 5,789 Chinese adults
Authors: Xuelun Zou, Feng Zhang, Cailin He, Wuyang Zhang, Rongmei Tang, Juan Zhu, Junyi Ma, Ye Li, Li Wang, Chaoyang Cai, Qiaoling Tang, Yexiang Yi, Ran Liu, Kexian Zhou, Xiangbin Zhang, Yi Zeng, Le Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading contributor to the high disease burden in low- and middle-income countries, making early prevention essential for risk reduction. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) are surrogate markers of insulin resistance, and both have been associated with cardiometabolic risk. However, the predictive utility of their combined use for stroke risk across different glucose metabolism states remains unclear.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 5789 stroke-free participants aged ≥ 45 years, including 3490 with abnormal glucose regulation(AGR) and 2296 with normal glucose regulation(NGR). The TyG-eGDR index was dichotomised as TyG × eGDR. The TyG + eGDR combination was further partitioned into 12 strata defined by TyG tertiles crossed with eGDR quartiles. We evaluated the relationship between theseTyG, eGDR, TyG-eGDR and TyG + eGDR categories and incident stroke using cox proportional-hazards models, mediation analysis, restricted cubic splines, receiver-operating-characteristic curves, and subgroup analyses Query.ResultsAmong 5789 participants followed for incident stroke, 460 new events were documented, corresponding to an overall incidence of 9.93 per 1000 person-years. We observed a negative association between lower eGDR index (OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.62–0.79) and lower TyG-eGDR index (OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.63–0.81) with stroke risk across all populations, while in the AGR subgroup, a higher TyG index (OR = 1.24, 95% CI 1.11–1.37) was associated with a higher risk of stroke. Compared to the reference group (low TyG and high eGDR), the combination of high TyG (≥ 8.90) and low eGDR (< 7.09) conferred the highest stroke hazard (HR = 3.75, 95% CI 2.29–6.15, p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁷), and this pattern was consistent in both AGR and NGR sub-cohorts. However, the TyG + eGDR combination exerted a stronger influence on stroke risk within the AGR group. RCS showed non-linear dose–response for TyG and TyG-eGDR with stroke (p < 0.05), but linear for eGDR. The TyG + eGDR composite model achieved superior discrimination (area under the ROC curve: 0.71). Mediation analysis indicated that eGDR accounted for 29.99% of the association between TyG and stroke risk, while TyG explained 5.81% of the association between eGDR and stroke risk; comparable mediation patterns were observed in the AGR population but not in the NGR population.ConclusionsLow eGDR and a low TyG-eGDR product independently predict a higher stroke risk, whereas elevated TyG predominantly increases stroke risk in individuals with AGR. Combining TyG and eGDR markedly improves stroke discrimination beyond either metric alone. Routine integration of these two inexpensive indices permits earlier, more precise risk stratification and can inform targeted interventions that substantially reduce stroke incidence.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02991-3.Keywords: Stroke, Triglyceride-glucose index, Estimated glucose disposal rate, Diabetes, Glycaemic regulation

Introduction

Stroke is the third greatest contributor to global disease burden, trailing only ischaemic heart disease and COVID-19, and ranks fourth worldwide in disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) [1, 2]. Each year it erases 160.5 million DALYs—5.6% of the global total—inflicting disproportionate harm on public health. Although thrombolysis and other acute therapies can limit immediate damage, the metabolic and vascular injury that underlies stroke is largely irreversible [1, 2]. For this reason, reliable early warning is the most effective strategy for reducing its impact [3]. Biomarkers offer the combined advantages of high sensitivity, operational simplicity, and biological reversibility; translating them into practical predictive tools would therefore enable precise risk stratification and timely, targeted interventions that can substantially curb stroke incidence and its sequelae [4–6].

The triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) is a well-validated, non-invasive marker of insulin resistance and a key predictor of adverse outcomes across a spectrum of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [7–10]. It is thought to shape stroke prognosis by promoting atherosclerosis, impairing endothelial function, and exacerbating cerebral ischaemia–reperfusion injury [8, 11]. Nevertheless, studies that have sought insulin-resistance metrics independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors have documented instances in which TyG remains low despite high cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk. Prior work has compared the predictive value of different insulin-resistance indices for stroke, but whether TyG confers additional prognostic information when combined with other markers—or whether this metabolic pattern differs across glucose-regulation categories—remains unexplored.

Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) is a validated, convenient proxy for insulin resistance [12, 13]. Derived from waist circumference, hypertension status and glycated haemoglobin, it offers a novel, non-invasive gauge of insulin sensitivity [12, 13]. Studies show eGDR is independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and predicts functional outcome after ischaemic stroke [13]. Low eGDR raises both stroke and mortality risk in type 2 diabetes, with hypertension contributing the largest attributable fraction [12]. eGDR inversely correlates with stroke (OR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.22–0.73, p = 0.003) and death (HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.52–0.98, p = 0.037), underscoring its value as a stroke biomarker [14]. Both TyG and eGDR reflect insulin resistance and are linked to incident stroke.

Previous work has combined TyG and eGDR to examine their joint influence on long-term mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic adults, identifying a possible additive effect in the high-TyG/low-eGDR stratum (TyG > 9.04 and eGDR < 4) [15]. Whether the same combination—expressed as the TyG-eGDR or TyG + eGDR index—improves stroke prediction remains untested, and it is unknown whether any such benefit differs between individuals with abnormal glucose regulation (AGR, diabetes or prediabetes) and those with normal glucose regulation(NGR). We therefore propose a prospective cohort study to quantify the combined predictive value of TyG and eGDR for incident stroke across distinct glucose-regulation categories, and to dissect their bidirectional mediating pathways. The findings will inform tailored metabolic interventions and refine risk-stratification strategies for populations at high risk of stroke.

Method

Study design and data sources

Data were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of adults aged ≥ 45 years that is refreshed every two years [16]. Using multistage, probability-proportional-to-size sampling, CHARLS recruited residents from 150 rural and urban counties/districts in 28 provinces, achieving an 80% response rate and capturing the socioeconomic and health profile of China’s middle-aged and older population [16]. Trained interviewers administered standardised questionnaires face-to-face, collecting sociodemographic details, medical history and biological measurements [16]. The analytic baseline was set at the 2011–2012 wave; four follow-up waves (2013–2014, 2015–2016, 2017–2018 and 2019–2020) were completed, each dynamically updated to maintain cohort freshness and representativeness [16].

We used the 2011–2012 survey wave as baseline and the 2017–2018 wave as the censoring point. Respondents were excluded if they were aged < 45 years, had self-reported or recorded stroke at baseline, or lacked any of the following: fasting triglycerides, fasting plasma glucose, waist circumference, blood pressure or HbA1c. We further required at least one valid glucose measure (fasting, 2-h OGTT or random) and ≤ 20% missing covariate or follow-up data. Of 17,705 initial participants, 5789 remained eligible. These individuals were classified by glycaemic status: 3,490 were assigned to the AGR group if they either (1) reported physician-diagnosed diabetes and were taking glucose-lowering drugs or (2) met any laboratory criterion for diabetes (fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or 2-h/random glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L) or prediabetes (fasting glucose 6.1–6.9 mmol/L, 2-h OGTT 7.8–11.0 mmol/L, or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%). The remaining 2,296 participants with fasting glucose < 6.1 mmol/L, 2-h glucose < 7.8 mmol/L and HbA1c < 5.7% were classified as NGR group (Fig. 1).

Calculation of TyG, eGDR, TyG-eGDR and TyG + eGDR combinations

Fasting triglycerides, blood glucose, waist circumference, hypertension, and HbA1c data were obtained from clinical information collected during the baseline period or through blood sample testing. Waist circumference was measured using a tape measure at the level of the iliac crest. Any waist circumference measurement deviating by more than three standard deviations (above or below) from the overall mean waist circumference measurement was defined as an abnormal waist circumference measurement. Hypertension diagnosis was determined through a comprehensive assessment based on the following criteria: participants were first asked, “Have you been diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor?” Additionally, one of the following conditions had to be met: systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Stroke event confirmation

The primary endpoint was incident stroke, defined as the first physician-diagnosed stroke occurring after baseline among participants free of prior stroke history. Ascertainment required both an affirmative answer to the question “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a stroke?” and either a documented date of diagnosis or current stroke-related treatment; individuals who subsequently denied the diagnosis or reported no use of stroke medication were adjudicated as non-events and censored. These criteria mirror those used in the CHARLS study [19]. Event time was calculated from baseline to the date of first diagnosis, while participants without stroke were followed from baseline to the final survey date.

Data collection

Data for this study were drawn from questionnaire-based interviews conducted in an ongoing cohort. Sociodemographic variables were age (45–60 years vs. > 60 years), sex (male vs. female), education (< secondary vs. ≥ secondary), marital status (married vs. single), and household registration (rural vs. urban). Health behaviours and anthropometry were current smoking (yes/no), current alcohol drinking (yes/no), and BMI (weight in kg/height2 in m2) classified as normal or abnormal. Self-reported physician diagnoses provided the history of diabetes, cancer, heart disease, and dyslipidaemia. Regular use of anti-diabetic, anti-cancer, cardiovascular, anti-hypertensive, or lipid-lowering medication was ascertained by the affirmative answer to: “Do you regularly take any of these medications as prescribed by your doctor?” Resting blood pressure was measured twice on the right arm after ≥ 10 min of rest with a standard sphygmomanometer; the average of the two readings yielded systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) values. Laboratory measures comprised fasting plasma glucose, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-cholesterol, and LDL-cholesterol. Dyslipidaemia was defined as triglycerides ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, total cholesterol ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, LDL-cholesterol ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, HDL-cholesterol < 1.0 mmol/L, prior diagnosis, or current lipid-lowering therapy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarised as mean (SD) or median (IQR) and categorical variables as n (%). To limit bias from missing data we used multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE, Python 3.12.4): each incomplete variable is regressed on all others in an iterative cycle that creates several complete data sets. Before imputation, values > 1.5 × IQR above or below the quartiles for height, weight and waist circumference were removed to avoid distorting the imputations. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant; analyses were run in R 4.4.3.

We evaluated the associations of four insulin-resistance-related indices—TyG, eGDR, TyG-eGDR and TyG + eGDR—with incident stroke using univariable and multivariable Cox regression, reporting hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three nested models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 added smoking, alcohol use, BMI, marital status, education and household registration; Model 3 further included cancer, cardiac disease, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, antidiabetic and cardiac medications, systolic/diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose and HbA1c. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for the key exposure variables (TyG, eGDR, TyG-eGDR, and TyG + eGDR) using Schoenfeld residuals. The test results indicated that the assumption was satisfied for all these variables (all p-values > 0.05). Restricted cubic splines, adjusted for the same covariates, were used to visualise dose–response curves for TyG, eGDR and TyG-eGDR. Discrimination was assessed with ROC curves and DeLong’s test for pairwise comparisons. Finally, we stratified the cohort by age, sex, marital status, education, hukou, BMI, smoking, alcohol intake, dyslipidaemia, diabetes and heart disease to examine whether the TyG-eGDR and TyG + eGDR indices exerted differential effects across clinically relevant subgroups.

Baseline characteristics according to the combination of the TyG index and eGDR

During follow-up, 460 individuals (7.95%) developed stroke and 5329 (92.05%) did not. Table 2 contrasts the two groups. The stroke cohort was older, contained more single participants, had higher BMI, and showed a greater prevalence of heart disease, hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia. Use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic and lipid-lowering medication also differed significantly between the groups. Quantitative measures were higher in the stroke group for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, HbA1c, HDL and triglycerides (p < 0.002). The TyG index was markedly elevated in stroke patients (8.9 vs 8.7), whereas eGDR (8.0 vs 9.3) and TyG-eGDR (70.6 vs 80.1) were correspondingly lower.

ROC analysis showed that adding TyG and eGDR together to the baseline covariates yielded the highest predictive value for stroke (AUC: 0.71), outperforming the baseline model alone (AUC: 0.67), as well as models augmented with TyG only, eGDR only, or TyG-eGDR (each AUC: 0.69). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that the combined use of the TyG index and the eGDR provides a robust and reliable prediction of incident stroke. Elevated TyG and reduced eGDR—or low TyG-eGDR values—were each associated with higher stroke risk, with distinct dose–response patterns across glycaemic strata. Among all combinations, participants with high TyG (≥ 8.90) and low eGDR (< 7.09) exhibited the greatest hazard, particularly among those with accelerated glycation rate, suggesting a synergistic effect. ROC analysis confirmed that incorporating the TyG + eGDR composite improved discriminative performance beyond the baseline model. Mediation analyses revealed that eGDR explained a significant portion of the TyG–stroke association, while TyG partially mediated the eGDR–stroke link, underscoring their bidirectional interplay.

The TyG index is a convenient and validated surrogate of insulin resistance and has been linked to metabolic syndrome and to adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes across multiple populations [7–10, 20–24]. However, its predictive utility is not universal. Because TyG is derived solely from fasting triglyceride and glucose levels, it fails to account for major determinants of vascular risk such as hypertension, central adiposity, and systemic inflammation. Consequently, in certain high-risk subgroups—particularly those with obesity or hypertension—stroke risk may remain elevated despite low TyG levels. This limited sensitivity reflects the fact that TyG captures only the lipid–glucose axis of insulin resistance, without considering the hemodynamic and adiposity-related components that also contribute to vascular injury.

To overcome these limitations, eGDR integrates waist circumference, hypertension status, and glycated haemoglobin, thereby providing a more comprehensive representation of insulin sensitivity [12, 13]. Unlike TyG, eGDR reflects both metabolic and hemodynamic influences on vascular health. Prior research has consistently shown that low eGDR predicts incident stroke and mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals [12, 25–28]. These findings underscore eGDR’s broader applicability across glycaemic categories and its independence from conventional risk factors. By combining TyG and eGDR, our study extends this evidence, showing that their joint application captures complementary physiological dimensions—metabolic (TyG) and vascular-hemodynamic (eGDR)—yielding superior predictive performance.

Consistent with earlier work, we found that TyG’s predictive value was significant only in individuals with AGR and absent in those with NGR [29, 30]. This pattern parallels prior studies in coronary and cerebrovascular disease, where TyG strongly predicted major adverse cardiovascular events among diabetic patients but showed limited predictive value in non-diabetic populations. In contrast, eGDR demonstrated consistent associations with stroke risk across both glycaemic strata, suggesting its robustness and broader physiological relevance. These findings indicate that TyG mainly captures hyperglycaemia-driven lipid dysfunction, whereas eGDR reflects vascular and metabolic disturbances extending beyond glycaemic control.

The observed synergy between high TyG and low eGDR likely reflects overlapping yet distinct biological pathways of insulin resistance. Persistent insulin resistance promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, enhancing platelet activation and accelerating atherosclerotic plaque formation [31–36]. Low eGDR, reflecting obesity, hypertension, and poor glycaemic control, captures additional mechanisms of vascular remodeling and arterial stiffness [37, 38]. The co-occurrence of high TyG and low eGDR therefore identifies individuals with multidimensional metabolic stress, translating into a cumulative burden of vascular injury. The consistency of this combined effect across subgroups—including age, sex, BMI, smoking, and lipid levels—further highlights its generalizability, with heart disease being the only notable modifier of effect (P-interaction < 0.05) [39].

From a mechanistic perspective, TyG and eGDR contribute complementary insights. Elevated TyG primarily reflects hepatic and peripheral insulin resistance, leading to dyslipidaemia, endothelial dysfunction, and increased oxidative burden. Low eGDR, conversely, highlights the impact of hypertension and adiposity on microvascular remodeling, impaired nitric oxide signaling, and sympathetic overactivation [34, 40]. Integrating both measures allows for multidimensional quantification of insulin resistance, bridging metabolic and hemodynamic pathways. This broader metabolic capture likely explains the superior predictive power of the TyG + eGDR composite observed in our analyses. The dual-index approach aligns with current understanding that multifactorial metabolic dysfunction, rather than isolated biomarkers, drives the transition from insulin resistance to overt cerebrovascular disease.

Clinically, the combined TyG + eGDR index offers a simple, inexpensive, and accessible biomarker system that could facilitate individualized stroke risk assessment, especially in diabetic or prediabetic populations. Its integration into predictive algorithms could improve early identification of high-risk individuals, guide preventive interventions targeting insulin resistance, and enhance public health strategies aimed at reducing the global stroke burden. Because both indices can be derived from routine clinical parameters, they are well suited for implementation in large-scale epidemiological screening and primary prevention settings.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-centre retrospective design introduces potential bias, and multi-centre replication is warranted to confirm external validity. Second, TyG and eGDR were measured only once at baseline, precluding assessment of dynamic changes over time. Besides, one limitation of our study is the reliance on self-reported stroke events, which were not objectively validated through medical records or imaging studies. This may have led to some misclassification or recall bias. Future studies could benefit from using clinically confirmed stroke cases to improve the accuracy and reliability of the results. Fourth, unmeasured or unknown confounders cannot be excluded despite extensive covariate adjustment. Another important limitation of this study is that TyG and eGDR were measured only once at baseline, and dynamic changes during follow-up were not captured. In addition, potential alterations in the use of glucose-lowering or weight-management agents (such as metformin, thiazolidinediones, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists), lipid-lowering medications, antihypertensive therapies, and body weight could have affected the associations observed. These factors should be carefully considered in future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated biomarker assessments and treatment updates. Finally, the mediation analysis assumes a directional pathway from TyG through eGDR to stroke, yet reverse causality remains possible; therefore, the results should be interpreted as statistical rather than biological mediation. Future prospective studies incorporating repeated biomarker assessments and medication updates (e.g., glucose- or lipid-lowering agents, antihypertensives) are needed to clarify temporal stability and causal inference.

In summary, our study provides novel evidence that combining TyG and eGDR enhances stroke prediction beyond either index alone, particularly among individuals with abnormal glucose regulation. By integrating lipid–glucose metabolism and hemodynamic components, the TyG + eGDR composite offers a multidimensional and practical approach for identifying individuals at elevated risk of stroke. Given its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and wide applicability, this dual-index strategy has strong potential to be incorporated into future risk assessment and preventive frameworks aimed at mitigating stroke burden worldwide.
