Bochner-type Theorems for Distributional Category
Ekansh Jauhari, John Oprea

Abstract
. We show that in the presence of a geometric condition such as non-negative Ricci curvature, the distributional category of a manifold may be used to bound invariants, such as the first Betti number and macroscopic dimension, from above. Moreover, à la Bochner, when the bound is an equality, special constraints are imposed on the manifold. We show that the distributional category of a space also bounds the rank of the Gottlieb group, with equality imposing constraints on the fundamental group. These bounds are refined in the setting of cohomologically symplectic manifolds, enabling us to get specific computations for the distributional category and LS-category.

1.
Introduction

While Lusternik–Schnirelmann (LS) category was originally invented to provide an estimate for the number of
critical points of a smooth function on a manifold, it soon became apparent that the concept connected
in non-trivial ways with other topological invariants (see
[
CLOT03
]
). The more recent development of the idea of
topological complexity and its relation to the motion planning problem (see
[
Far08
]
) spawned a re-evaluation of
LS-category in that context, and this resulted in a new “categorical” notion called
distributional category

(see
[
DJ24
]
and
[
KW24
]
, where the term
analog category
is used in the latter). LS-category and distributional category are
most certainly different. For instance, the LS-category of any real projective space
{{\mathbb{R}}P^{n}}
is
n
while the distributional
category is
1
for all
n
. Yet, intriguingly, sometimes
\dcat(X)=\cat(X)
(where we have used the obvious notations).
In particular, from
[
KW24
]
(also see
[
Dra
]
), we know that for a torsion-free discrete group
\pi
with finite cohomological
(and homotopy) dimension
k
, we have
\dcat(\pi)=\cat(\pi)=k
. In
[
Jau25
]
, the first author gave some conditions under which
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)
holds when
M
is a closed (not necessarily aspherical) manifold. In this paper, we would
like to explore other situations where the above equality holds, or at least where natural topological or geometric conditions imply
something interesting about
\dcat
. We refer to these conditions as
Bochner-type conditions
for reasons
that will become apparent below.

The results of our paper suggest close relationships between distributional category and various classical invariants of interest,
such as the first Betti number, the Gottlieb group, LS-category, macroscopic dimension, etc. Furthermore,
\dcat
affords us an opportunity to improve
upon earlier results in the literature that connect these classical invariants (see, for example,
[
Opr02a
]
,
[
Opr02b
]
,
[
OS10
]
, and
[
DCDJ
]
). Our results are strongest in the setting of
c
-symplectic manifolds and non-negatively curved manifolds, where they also give us
various new estimates and computations of
\dcat
. In particular, unlike the results of
[
DJ24
]
and
[
Jau25
]
, we obtain first
examples of
inessential manifolds

M
(in the sense of
[
Gro83
]
) for which
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)
holds due to various
topological, group-theoretical, and geometric conditions rather than purely cohomological ones.

Just as LS-category has found applications in many areas of topology, geometry, and
critical point theory, we hope that the results of our paper will hint at similar interconnectedness of distributional category, and at least give some intuition about its behavior
on spaces with extra structures. We also mention several directions for further study that the reader may find interesting (see, for instance, Remark
5.6
).

In this paper, all topological spaces are assumed to be path-connected.

2.
Preliminaries

In order to make this paper somewhat self-contained, we must review some foundational topics.

2.A.
Topological basics

Let
R
be a commutative ring with unity.
Then the
cup-length
of the cohomology ring
H^{*}(X;R)
, denoted
c\ell_{R}(X)
, is defined to be the largest integer
k

for which there exist
\alpha_{i}\in H^{s_{i}}(X;R)
,
1\leq i\leq k
,
s_{i}>0
,
such that
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{k}\neq 0
. In this paper, we reserve the notation
\cu(X)
for the rational cup-length
c\ell_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
. Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group. Then its
cohomological dimension
, denoted
\cd(\Gamma)
, is defined to be the largest integer
k

such that
H^{k}(\Gamma,A)\neq 0
for some
{\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma
-module
A
.
The Eilenberg–Ganea theorem and the Stallings–Swan theorem (see
[
Bro82
]
) together say that for
\cd(\Gamma)=k\neq 2
, there is an
Eilenberg–Mac Lane space
K(\Gamma,1)
of dimension
k
, and this is the smallest such dimension.

2.B.
LS-category

The
Lusternik–Schnirelmann category
(LS-category) of a space
X
, denoted
\cat(X)
, is defined to be the smallest integer

n
for which there is a covering
\{U_{i}\}
of
X
by
n+1
open sets that are contractible in
X
. In particular,

\cat(X)=0
if and only if
X
is contractible. The following are some well-known facts about Lusternik–Schnirelmann
category, whose proofs can be found in
[
CLOT03
]
.

2.1 Theorem
.

Let
X
be CW a complex.

(1)

If
Y
is homotopy equivalent to
X
(i.e.,
Y\simeq X
), then
\cat(Y)=\cat(X).

(2)

If
X
is
r
-connected for
r\geq 0
, then
\cat(X)\leq\tfrac{\dim(X)}{r+1}
.

(3)

For any CW complex
Y
,
\cat(X\times Y)\leq\cat(X)+\cat(Y)
.

(4)

If
X^{\prime}
is a covering space of
X
, then
\cat(X^{\prime})\leq\cat(X)
.

(5)

For any ring
R
,
c\ell_{R}(X)\leq\cat(X)
. In particular,
\cu(X)\leq\cat(X)
.

These properties of
\cat
will be quite useful for our purpose. In particular, Properties (2) and (5) tell us immediately that
for each
n
,
\cat({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})=n=\cat({\mathbb{C}}P^{n})
. A harder fact is that
\cat(X)=1
if and only if
X
is a co-
H
-space, see
[
CLOT03
]
.

2.C.
Distributional category

Recently, a probabilistic variant of LS-category was introduced in
[
DJ24
]
and
[
KW24
]
. This is the
distributional category

(or
analog category
in
[
KW24
]
). We refer to
[
KW24
]
and
[
Jau25
]
for motivations behind this new invariant.
Given a pointed metric space
(X,x_{0})
, the
distributional category
of
X
, denoted
\dcat(X)
, is defined to be the smallest integer

n
for which there exists a map
H:X\to{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}(P_{0}(X))
such that
H(x)(0)\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}(P(x,x_{0}))
for each
x\in X
.
Here,
P_{0}(X)=\{\phi\in X^{[0,1]}\mid\phi(1)=x_{0}\}
and
P(x,x_{0})=\{\phi\in P_{0}(X)\mid\phi(0)=x\}
have the (metrizable) subspace
topology coming from the compact-open topology of
X^{[0,1]}
, and
{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}(Z)
is the space of probability measures on

Z
supported by at most
n+1
points, equipped with the Lévy–Prokhorov metric,
[
Pro56
]
. We note that like
\cat(X)
(see
[
CLOT03
, Lemma 1.25]
),
\dcat(X)
is also independent of the choice of the basepoint
x_{0}\in X
, see also
[
DJ24
, Definition 3.1]
.

For a proof of the following, we refer to
[
DJ24
]
.

2.2 Theorem

(Dranishnikov–Jauhari)
.

Let
X
be a CW complex.

(1)

\dcat(X)\leq\cat(X)
.

(2)

If
Y\simeq X
, then
\dcat(Y)=\dcat(X)
.

(3)

If
X^{\prime}
is a covering space of
X
, then
\dcat(X^{\prime})\leq\dcat(X)
.

(4)

If
X
is a finite CW complex, then
\cu(X)\leq\dcat(X)
.

2.3 Example
.

For each
n
,
\dcat(T^{n})=\cat(T^{n})=n
.
If
X
is a closed surface, then
\dcat(X)=1
if
X\simeq S^{2}
or
X\simeq{\mathbb{R}}P^{2}
, and
\dcat(X)=\cat(X)=2
otherwise.

In general, the distributional category and the LS-category of a space may differ. Indeed,
\dcat({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})=1<n=\cat({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})
for
each
n\geq 2
, and for each finite group
\Gamma
, we have that
\dcat(\Gamma)<|\Gamma|<\infty=\cat(\Gamma)
, see
[
KW24
]
(and also
[
DJ24
]
).
The following is the main result of
[
KW24
]
(see also
[
Dra
]
).

2.4 Theorem

(Knudsen–Weinberger)
.

If
\Gamma
is a torsion-free discrete group, then
\dcat(\Gamma)=\cat(\Gamma)=\cd(\Gamma)
.

In particular,
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=\dim(M)
for closed aspherical manifolds (hence, nilmanifolds). This result has an analog for non-aspherical spaces. A closed manifold
M
with
\cat(M)=\dim(M)
is said to be

essential

[
Gro83
]
(see also
[
KR06
]
). In
[
Jau25
]
, it was shown that
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=\dim(M)
for an essential manifold
M
if
\pi_{1}(M)
is
torsion-free and
M
obeys an extra homological condition called the
cap property
. This result may be extended to
(generalized) connected sums under certain “cap property” conditions (see
[
Jau25
]
).

2.D.
c
-symplectic manifolds

A
2n
-dimensional manifold
M
is called
c
-symplectic
if there exists a cohomology class
\omega\in H^{2}(M;{\mathbb{Q}})
such that

\omega^{n}\neq 0
. The appellation
c
-symplectic is used because these spaces “mimic” symplectic manifolds cohomologically,
[
LO95
]
.
While each symplectic manifold is
c
-symplectic, the converse is not true. Indeed,
{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}\#{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}
is
c
-symplectic but not symplectic.
Some other examples of
c
-symplectic manifolds include the Calabi–Yau manifolds
[
Yau77
]
(which, of course, are Kähler)
and symmetric products of closed orientable surfaces
[
Mac62
]
(see also
[
DCDJ
]
).
If
M
is a closed
2n
-symplectic manifold and
M
is simply connected, then
\cu(M)=\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=n
holds due to Theorems
2.1

and
2.2
. If
M
is not simply connected, then both
\cat
and
\dcat
can attain any integer value between
n
and
2n
.
Indeed, for any
0\leq k\leq n
, one can start with a closed simply connected symplectic
(2n-2k
)-manifold
N
and create
M=N\times T^{2k}
with

\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=n+k
. For some special
c
-symplectic manifolds
M
, namely the
symplectically aspherical manifolds
,
it was shown in
[
RO99
]
that
\cat(M)=\dim(M)
. For a symplectically aspherical manifold
M
satisfying the “cap property”,

\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=\dim(M)
was shown in
[
Jau25
]
.

2.E.
Gottlieb groups and Hurewicz rank

The
Gottlieb group
of a space
X
, denoted
G(X)
, is defined to be the subgroup of
\pi_{1}(X)
consisting of the
elements
\alpha\in\pi_{1}(X)
that have associated maps
A:S^{1}\times X\to X
with
A|_{S^{1}}\simeq\alpha
and
A|_{X}\simeq 1_{X}
.
This is equivalent to
G(X)
being the image of the evaluation map
\text{ev}_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(X^{X},1_{X})\to\pi_{1}(X)
, where
\text{ev}(f)=f(x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in X
. Since
\text{ev}_{*}
is the connecting map for the universal fibration
X\to\text{Baut}_{*}(X)\to\text{Baut}(X)
(see
[
Got69
]
), we see that

G(X)\subset\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(X)
, where
\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(X)
is the center of
\pi_{1}(X)
. In the above setting for
G(X)
, if we replace
S^{1}
by
S^{n}
and
\pi_{1}

by
\pi_{n}
, then we get the
n
-th Gottlieb group of
X
, denoted
G_{n}(X)
. The following are some basic properties of
G(X)
, see
[
Got69
]
.

(1)

G(X)
is equal to the center of
\pi_{1}(X)
if
X
is an aspherical space, and it is equal to the full fundamental group if
X
is an
H
-space.

(2)

Let
p:X^{\prime}\to X
be a covering of
X
and
p_{*}:\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})\to\pi_{1}(X)
be the induced homomorphism. If
\alpha\in\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
and

p_{*}(\alpha)\in G(X)
, then
\alpha\in G(X^{\prime})
.

(3)

Let
K
be a compact Lie group acting on
(X,x_{0})
and let
\mathcal{O}:K\to X
be the orbit map defined as
g\mapsto g\cdot x_{0}
.
Then
\text{Im}(\mathcal{O}_{*})\subset G(X)
.

(4)

For any
X
and
Y
,
G(X\times Y)=G(X)\oplus G(Y)
.

(5)

If
X\to E\to B
is a fibration, then there is a map to the universal fibration (as above) which induces the commutative diagram

\textstyle{\pi_{2}(B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\partial_{*}}
\textstyle{\pi_{2}(\mathrm{Baut}(X))\cong\pi_{1}(X^{X},1_{X})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\rm{ev}_{*}}
\textstyle{\pi_{1}(X)}

that shows that
\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{*})\subset\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{ev}_{*})\subset G(X)
.

Here is an example of the influence of the Gottlieb group on the structure of a space. Suppose
X
is a topological space such that
H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
is finitely generated. Let
h:\pi_{1}(X)\to H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
be the Hurewicz homomorphism.
Then the
Hurewicz rank
of
X
is defined to be the number of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-summands of
H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
which are contained in
h(G(X))
.
We refer to
[
Got89
]
,
[
Opr02b
]
for a proof of the following.

2.5 Theorem

(Gottlieb)
.

Let
X
be a space such that
H_{*}(X;{\mathbb{Z}})
is finitely generated. If the Hurewicz rank of
X
is
s
, then
X\simeq T^{s}\times Y
for some space
Y
.

3.
Related results

In this section, we recall some results from the literature that we aim to improve and/or extend in this paper. The most basic result for us
is the classical result of S. Bochner that a closed
n
-manifold
M
with non-negative Ricci curvature has
b_{1}(M)\leq\dim(M)

and, perhaps most importantly, if
b_{1}(M)=\dim(M)
, then
M
must be (diffeomorphic to) a flat torus. The proof given by Bochner was
analytic in nature, and a modern proof involving the Dirac operator may be found in
[
LM89
]
. In fact, a simpler proof follows from the
powerful splitting theorem of Cheeger and Gromoll
[
CG71
]
, which is stated as follows.

3.1 Theorem

(Cheeger–Gromoll)
.

If
M
is a closed
n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature, then there exists a finite covering space
M^{\prime}
of
M
and a
diffeomorphism
M^{\prime}\cong T^{r}\times W
, where
W
is a closed simply connected manifold.

We will refer to such a finite cover
M^{\prime}
as a
Cheeger–Gromoll splitting
of
M
.

3.2 Remark
.

If
\pi_{1}(M)
is finite, then
M^{\prime}
is the universal cover of
M
. So, Theorem
3.1
is not interesting in this case. Hence, we will
consider only those closed manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature that have infinite fundamental groups.

Using the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting, Bochner’s theorem was improved by the second author in
[
Opr02a
]
as follows.

3.3 Theorem

(Oprea)
.

Let
M
be a closed
n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is infinite. If
M^{\prime}\cong T^{r}\times W
is a
Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of
M
, then

b_{1}(M)\leq\cat(M)-\cu(W)
.
Moreover,
b_{1}(M)=\cat(M)
if and only if
M
is a torus. Furthermore, if
M
is also a closed
c
-symplectic
2n
-manifold,
then the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting takes the form
M^{\prime}\cong T^{2k}\times W
, where
W
is a closed simply connected
c
-symplectic
manifold, and

b_{1}(M)\leq 2\cat(M)-\dim(M)
.

3.4 Remark
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
uses the fact that the homomorphism
p^{*}:H^{*}(M;{\mathbb{Q}})\to H^{*}(M^{\prime};{\mathbb{Q}})
induced by the finite covering map
p:M^{\prime}\to M
is a split monomorphism due to the corresponding transfer map in cohomology, see
[
Bro82
, Sections III.9 & III.10]
. As a result,
b_{1}(M)\leq b_{1}(M^{\prime})
.

In the case when
M
is a closed
c
-symplectic manifold, Theorem
3.3
uses the following lemma from
[
Opr02a
]
, which we shall use below.

3.5 Lemma
.

If
p:M^{\prime}\to M
is a finite covering map and
M
is a
c
-symplectic
2n
-manifold with a cohomology class
\omega\in H^{2}(M;{\mathbb{Q}})
,
then
M^{\prime}
is also
c
-symplectic with
\omega\in H^{2}(M^{\prime};{\mathbb{Q}})
. Moreover, if
M^{\prime}\cong T^{r}\times W
for some closed simply
connected manifold
W
, then
r=2k
, and
w=w_{1}\oplus w_{2}
for cohomology classes
w_{1}\in H^{2}(T^{2k};{\mathbb{Q}})
and
w_{2}\in H^{2}(W;{\mathbb{Q}})
satisfying
w_{1}^{k}\neq 0
and
w_{2}^{n-k}\neq 0
.

3.6 Remark
.

The simplest examples of closed
c
-symplectic (actually symplectic) manifolds having non-negative Ricci (actually sectional) curvature are the products
M^{2n}=T^{2k}\times N^{2n-2k}
for closed simply connected symplectic
(2n-2k)
-manifolds
N
. Here, we have
b_{1}(M)=2k<n+k=\cat(M)
.
For such a product manifold
M
, the
second estimate of Theorem
3.3
is sharp, but
M
is
not
a torus. Besides these examples, there are the Calabi–Yau manifolds with infinite fundamental groups
[
Yau77
]
(although most physicists take their fundamental groups to be finite).

The paper
[
FH82
]
, which formulated a minimal model approach to rational LS-category, essentially re-invigorated the subject.
One of the main results of
[
FH82
]
relates the “size” of (all) the Gottlieb groups of a space with the LS-category of its rationalization
(in the simply connected case) as follows: if
X
is a simply connected topological space, then
\dim(G_{*}(X)\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}})\leq\cat(X_{{\mathbb{Q}}})
,
where
X_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
is the rationalization of
X
. For non-simply connected spaces, an integral analog of the above result for the
first Gottlieb group was obtained in
[
Opr02b
]
using Theorem
2.5
.

3.7 Theorem

(Oprea)
.

If
A
is a finitely generated free abelian subgroup of
G(X)
, then
\rank(A)\leq\cat(X)
.
In particular, if
G(X)
is finitely generated free abelian, then
\rank(G(X))\leq\cat(X)
.
Moreover, if
X
is a closed
n
-manifold, then equality holds if and only if
X\simeq T^{n}
.

4.
Extensions for distributional category

In this section, we will see that
\dcat
allows us to improve the results of Section
3
.
As motivation, recall that a compact Riemannian manifold
M^{n}
is
flat
if the sectional curvature at each
point is zero. It is known that
M^{n}
is flat if and only if
M\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/G
, where
G
is a
Bieberbach group
(see below) acting freely on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\cong
denotes “is isometric to”. Hence,
M
is
aspherical with fundamental group
\pi_{1}(M)\cong G
. Bieberbach’s Theorem characterizes a Bieberbach group

G
as one having a maximal normal free abelian subgroup
N\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
such that
G/N=Q
is finite.
In
[
Vas70
]
, Vasquez removed the maximal condition by showing that any free abelian
N
with
G/N

finite has a normal maximal free abelian centralizer of the same rank. Furthermore, using the fact that a finite index subgroup contains a normal finite index subgroup (namely the normal core), it can be shown that
G
is the fundamental group of a compact flat Riemannian
n
-manifold if and only if

(1)

G
is torsion-free,

(2)

G
contains a subgroup
N
which is free abelian of rank
n
, and

(3)

the index
[G:N]
is finite.

Hence, if
M
is flat, then
\pi_{1}(M)=G
obeys these conditions. The converse was shown in

[
FH83
]
; that is, a closed (connected) manifold
M^{n}
(
n\not=3,4
)
1
1

1

For the case
n=3
, see
[
Wol67
]
, and for the case
n=4
, see
[
Lam07
]
.
supports a flat
Riemannian structure if and only if
M^{n}
is aspherical (with
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=n
) and
\pi_{1}(M)
contains an abelian
subgroup of finite index. From Section
2.E
, we see that a flat manifold
M
has the Gottlieb group given by the center of the fundamental group, i.e.,

G(M)=\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(M)
. Moreover, by Theorems
2.4
and
3.7
, we have

\rank(\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(M))=\rank(G(M))\leq\cat(M)=\dcat(M).

In
[
HS86
]
, it was shown that
\rank(\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(M))=b_{1}(\pi_{1}(M))=b_{1}(M)
, so we see that we also have

b_{1}(M)\leq\dcat(M)
,
an analog to Theorem
3.3
. In this case, the condition

b_{1}(M)=\cat(M)=\dcat(M)=\dim(M)=n

then says that
\pi_{1}(M)
contains a
central
free abelian subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. This can only happen if
\pi_{1}(M)

is itself free abelian of rank
n
(essentially by the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
(3) given below). Hence,
M\cong T^{n}
.

Since flat manifolds have non-negative Ricci curvature, they provide non-trivial examples of such manifolds with non-trivial Gottlieb groups, where we can also determine
\dcat
. Also, in
[
DHS09
]

many flat manifolds were described that are Kähler (hence symplectic), so we have such non-trivial examples of
c
-symplectic nature too. Finally, we note that many of these flat Kähler manifolds have non-trivial centers (determined by their non-zero first Betti numbers), so they have non-trivial Gottlieb groups as well.

Now, let us go beyond flat manifolds to consider manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature. Our first result is a direct analog of Theorem
3.3
above for
\dcat
.

4.1 Theorem
.

Let
M
be a closed
n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is infinite.
If
M^{\prime}\cong T^{r}\times W
is a Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of
M
, then

b_{1}(M)\leq\dcat(M)-\cu(W).

Moreover,
b_{1}(M)=\dcat(M)
if and only if
M
is a torus.

Proof.

In view of Remark
3.4
, we have that
b_{1}(M)\leq b_{1}(M^{\prime})
. Since
W
is simply connected,
b_{1}(M^{\prime})=b_{1}(T^{r})=r=\cu(T^{r})
.
Now, Theorem
2.2
gives us the following series of inequalities:

\dcat(M)\geq\dcat(M^{\prime})\geq\cu(M^{\prime})=r+\cu(W)\geq b_{1}(M)+\cu(W).

If
M
is a torus, then
b_{1}(M)=\cat(M)=\dcat(M)
by Theorems
3.3
and
2.4
. Conversely,
if
b_{1}(M)=\dcat(M)
, then the above inequalities give
\cu(W)=0
. Since
W
is closed and simply connected (hence orientable),
this implies
W\simeq\ast
. Hence,
M^{\prime}\cong T^{n}
. This means
M
is a closed aspherical
n
-manifold. Therefore,

b_{1}(M)=\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=n
by Theorem
2.4
. But this is the situation of Bochner’s theorem, so
we obtain
M\cong T^{n}
.
∎

We now obtain an analog of Theorem
3.7
for
\dcat
. Notice, however, that we do not get the full analog of Theorem
3.7

because we only have a lower bound for
\dcat
given by the rational cup-length instead of the integral cup-length.

Recall that in geometric
group theory (see
[
BB97
]
), a discrete group
\Gamma
is said to be in
FH(R)
(written
\Gamma\in FH(R)
) for a commutative ring
R
if

\Gamma
acts freely, properly discontinuously, cellularly, and cocompactly on an
R
-acyclic space (i.e., a CW complex
Y
with
H^{j}(Y;R)=0

for
j>0
). This is one step in the ladder of finiteness conditions for a group (also see
[
Bro82
]
).

4.2 Theorem
.

If
A\subset G(X)
is a finitely generated free abelian group, then

\rank(A)\leq\dcat(X).

In particular, if
G(X)
is finitely generated free abelian, then
\rank(G(X))\leq\dcat(X)
.
Moreover,

(1)

if
X
is compact and
\rank(G(X))=\dcat(X)
, then
\pi_{1}(X)\in FH({\mathbb{Q}})
and, hence,
{\mathbb{Q}}
has a finite
resolution by finitely generated
free

{\mathbb{Q}}\pi_{1}(X)
-modules;

(2)

if
X
is a nilpotent space and
\rank(G(X))=\dcat(X)
, then
X\simeq_{\mathbb{Q}}K(\pi_{1}(X),1)
;

(3)

if
X
is a closed
n
-manifold and
G(X)
has finite index in
\pi_{1}(X)
, then
\rank(G(X))=\dcat(X)
implies
X\simeq T^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
p:X^{\prime}\to X
be a regular covering map corresponding to
A\subset\pi_{1}(X)
, so that
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})\cong A
. In fact, by Property (2) in
Section
2.E
, we have
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})=G(X^{\prime})
since
p_{*}(A)\subset G(X)
. Because
A
is free abelian, we have
A\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
for some
k

so that the Hurewicz rank of
A
is
k
. Thus, by Theorem
2.5
, we have
X^{\prime}\simeq T^{k}\times Y
, where
Y
is simply connected. Clearly,
Theorem
2.2
gives us the following series of inequalities (where recall that
\cu
denotes the rational cup-length):

\dcat(X)\geq\dcat(X^{\prime})\geq\cu(X^{\prime})=k+\cu(Y)\geq k=\rank(A).

Now, suppose
\rank(G(X))=\dcat(X)
. Then we have
\cu(Y)=0
so that
Y
is rationally acyclic. But since
X^{\prime}\simeq T^{k}\times Y
,
we have for the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
of
X
that
\widetilde{X}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times Y
. Hence,
\widetilde{X}
is also
{\mathbb{Q}}
-acyclic.
Since the fundamental group of a space always acts properly discontinuously on the universal cover and
X
is compact, we conclude
that
\pi_{1}(X)\in FH({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Note that in the previous argument, we only needed
X
to be compact to fit the definition of
FH({\mathbb{Q}})
. The fact that
Y
, and
hence
\widetilde{X}
, is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-acyclic does not depend on the compactness of
X
. Now,
if
X
is a nilpotent space (i.e.,
\pi_{1}(X)
is nilpotent and acts nilpotently on the homology of the universal cover), we can rationalize
it to get
X_{\mathbb{Q}}
with the property that
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathbb{Q}})
is the Malcev
{\mathbb{Q}}
-completion
\pi_{1}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
of the nilpotent group
\pi_{1}(X)
, and
\pi_{j}(X_{\mathbb{Q}})\cong\pi_{j}(X)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}\cong\pi_{j}(\widetilde{X})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}\cong\{1\}
for each
j\geq 2
because
\widetilde{X}
is simply connected and

{\mathbb{Q}}
-acyclic. Therefore,
X_{\mathbb{Q}}\cong K(\pi_{1}(X_{\mathbb{Q}}),1)\cong K(\pi_{1}(X)_{{\mathbb{Q}}},1)
is an Eilenberg–Mac Lane space and
X
itself is rationally homotopy
equivalent to
K(\pi_{1}(X),1)
. This is often written as
X\simeq_{\mathbb{Q}}K(\pi_{1}(X),1)
.

Now, suppose that
X
is a closed
n
-manifold and
G(X)
has finite index in
\pi_{1}(X)
. Then we see that
A\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}

(as above) also has a finite index in
\pi_{1}(X)
. Therefore,
X^{\prime}\simeq T^{k}\times Y\to X
is a finite covering and
X^{\prime}
is then
a closed
n
-manifold as well. Thus,
Y
is also a closed manifold of dimension
n-k
. Just as before, since
\rank(G(X))=k=\dcat(X)
, we have
\cu(Y)=0
. Hence,
Y
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-acyclic and simply connected. This means that
X^{\prime}
is an orientable closed

n
-manifold. So, we must have
H_{n}(X^{\prime};{\mathbb{Q}})\cong{\mathbb{Q}}
. But since
Y
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-acyclic, this can only happen if
k=n
,
Y\simeq*
,
and
X^{\prime}\simeq T^{n}
. This then means that
X\simeq K(\pi_{1}(X),1)
, so that
\pi_{1}(X)
is torsion-free. But then we
have a short exact sequence

{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to\pi_{1}(X)\to F,

where
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is central in
\pi_{1}(X)
and
F
is finite. Consider the associated
5
-term exact sequence (see

[
Bro82
, Corollary VII.6.4]
)

H_{2}(\pi_{1}(X))\to H_{2}(F)\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}/[{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},\pi_{1}(X)]\to H_{1}(\pi_{1}(X))\to H_{1}(F).

Because
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is central, the middle term reduces to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
itself. The quotient
F
is finite, so
H_{2}(F)
is finite as well and
therefore, the image of
H_{2}(F)\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is zero and
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to H_{1}(\pi_{1}(X))
is an injection. This translates to an injection
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to\pi_{1}(X)/[\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{1}(X)]
so that
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\cap[\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{1}(X)]=\{1\}
. But then we have an injection

[\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{1}(X)]\to\pi_{1}(X)/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, where
\pi_{1}(X)/{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is finite by our hypothesis. Since as a subgroup of
\pi_{1}(X)
,

[\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{1}(X)]
is torsion-free, we must have
[\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{1}(X)]=\{1\}
. Therefore,
\pi_{1}(X)
is a finitely generated
torsion-free abelian group. This implies that
\pi_{1}(X)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
X\simeq T^{n}
.
∎

4.3 Remark
.

If
M
is a closed nilmanifold, then
\dcat(M)=\dim(M)
by Theorem
2.4
. Note that
\rank(G(M))\leq\rank(\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(M))<\dim(M)
, except when
M
is a torus, see
[
OS12
, Appendix B]
.
Therefore, being a compact Eilenberg–Mac Lane space
is not sufficient to obtain
\rank(G(X))=\dcat(X)
.

4.4 Corollary
.

Suppose
M=L\times Y
for a closed manifold
Y
such that
\pi_{1}(Y)
is finite with a trivial center
and a closed manifold
L
with an H-space structure (for example, a closed Lie Group). Then

\dcat(M)>\rank(\pi_{1}(L))
.

Proof.

First, note that such a
Y
always exists since any finitely presented group can be realized as the fundamental group of a closed
4
-manifold.
For such an
L
, we have
G(L)=\pi_{1}(L)
. Because
\mathcal{Z}\pi_{1}(Y)=\{1\}
,
G(M)=G(L)=\pi_{1}(L)
has finite index in
\pi_{1}(M)
.
By Theorem
4.2
(3), since
M
is not a torus, we must have
\rank(\pi_{1}(L))=\rank(G(M))<\dcat(M)
.
∎

The shortness of the proof of Corollary
4.4
belies the fact that there does not seem to be another way of seeing
\dcat(M)>\rank(\pi_{1}(L))
. Note that, in particular, if
L=T^{k}
above, we get
\dcat(M)>k
. Moreover, the closed Lie group

L
can be chosen such that
\pi_{1}(L)
is any given finitely generated abelian group using tori and the fact that the projective
special unitary group has
\pi_{1}(PSU(n))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
.

4.5 Remark
.

If a torus
T^{k}
acts on a closed
n
-manifold
M
such that the map
{\mathcal{O}}_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{k})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}\to\pi_{1}(M)
induced by the
orbit map
{\mathcal{O}}
is a monomorphism, the action is called an
injective toral action
. These were studied extensively in
[
CR71
]
. By (3) of Section
2.E
, any such action has
\text{Im}(\mathcal{O}_{*})\subset G(M)
. Thus,
G(M)
contains a copy of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
.
So, we have the situation of Theorem
4.2
and there is an associated covering
M^{\prime}\simeq T^{k}\times N
with
N
simply connected. It was shown in
[
Opr02b
]

that
\cat(M)\geq k+\cu(N)
, and
\cat(M)=k
holds if and only if
k=n
and

M\simeq T^{n}
. Any flat manifold
M
has an injective toral action by
T^{k}
, where
k=b_{1}(M)
. For
\dcat
, however, we only
have conclusions as in Theorem
4.2
for injective total actions.

4.6 Example
.

In
[
OS10
]
, it was observed that any finitely generated abelian group can arise as the Gottlieb group of a closed
manifold. The construction is very simple. If
A={\mathbb{Z}}^{k}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
, then let

M=T^{k}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})

where
{\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}\subset S^{1}\subset S^{3}
gives the factor space
S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})
. For this type of factor space, the Gottlieb group is the center of
{\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
by
[
Lan72
]
,
[
Opr91
]
, so we see that
G(S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}))\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
itself. By Property (4) in Section
2.E
, we then have
G(M)\cong A
. But now we notice the following two things about
M
.

First, since each quotient
S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})
is a closed orientable
3
-manifold, we have
H^{3}(S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}});{\mathbb{Q}})\cong{\mathbb{Q}}
and

H^{k}(S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}});{\mathbb{Q}})=\{1\}
in degrees
1
and
2
(this follows from the transfer in Remark
3.4
). Thus,
\cu(M)=k+n
.
Therefore, we have that

\rank(G(M))=k<k+n=\cu(M)\leq\dcat(M).

In particular, the difference between
\rank(G(M))
and
\dcat(M)
can be arbitrarily large. Also, here we see an explicit example
where
\rank(G(M))=\dcat(M)
holds exactly when
n=0
and
M=T^{k}
as in (3) of Theorem
4.2
.

The second observation is that
M
has non-negative Ricci curvature if
T^{k}
is taken to be flat and each
S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})
has the
associated spherical metric. Then Theorem
4.1
gives the estimate

k=b_{1}(M)\leq\dcat(M)-\cu\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}S^{3}\right)=\dcat(M)-n,

so we see the inequality
k+n\leq\dcat(M)
from this viewpoint as well.

4.7 Example
.

Suppose
L
is a compact connected Lie group and
H
is a connected closed subgroup. Then there is a Barratt–Puppe sequence

\Omega(BH)\simeq H\to\Omega(BL)\simeq L\to L/H\to BH\to BL,

so that the connecting homomorphism of the fibration
L/H\to BH\to BL
may be taken to be
\partial_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(L)\cong\pi_{1}(\Omega(BL))\cong\pi_{2}(BL)\to\pi_{1}(L/H)
.
This implies that
\text{Im}(\partial_{*})\subset G(L/H)
— see Property (5) in Section
2.E
. Since
H
is connected,
\partial_{*}
is an epimorphism. Thus,
G(L/H)\cong\pi_{1}(L/H)
.

As an explicit example, take
L=T^{k}\times K
for a compact connected Lie group
K
, and let
H=S^{1}\times C
, where
S^{1}
is a factor of

T^{k}
and
C
is the maximal torus of
K
. Then

L/H\cong T^{k-1}\times K/C

and
K/C
is a closed simply connected Kähler manifold. Therefore, we have that
\cu(L/H)=\cu(T^{k-1})+\cu(K/C)=k-1+\tfrac{\dim(K)-\rank(K)}{2}\leq\dcat(L/H)
, while

\dcat(L/H)\leq\cat(L/H)\leq\cat(T^{k-1})+\cat(K/C)=k-1+\frac{\dim(K)-\rank(K)}{2}.

Hence, we have the inequality

\rank(G(L/H))=k-1<k-1+\frac{\dim(K)-\rank(K)}{2}=\dcat(L/H).

Here, we again have a construction where the difference between
\rank(G(L/H))
and
\dcat(L/H)
is arbitrary.

In the following section, we will refer to the cover
X^{\prime}\simeq T^{k}\times Y
mentioned in the proof of Theorem
4.2
as a

Hurewicz splitting
of
X
corresponding to
A\subset G(X)
.

5.
Extensions for
c
-symplectic manifolds

Our results from Section
4
illustrate that the techniques used to lower bound LS-category (see
[
Opr02a
]
,
[
Opr02b
]
) by
b_{1}(M)
and
\rank(G(X))
remain useful in lower bounding distributional category as well. So, it is natural to ask whether
\dcat=\cat
in the settings of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
. In this section, we address this interesting question by explaining how our results from Section
4
can be refined in the setting of closed
c
-symplectic manifolds and
\dcat=\cat
can be obtained.

5.A.
Manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature

5.1 Proposition
.

Let
M
be a closed
c
-symplectic Riemannian
2n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is infinite.
If
M^{\prime}\cong T^{2k}\times W
is a Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of
M
, then

b_{1}(M)\leq 2k\leq 2\dcat(M)-\dim(M).

Moreover,
\dcat(M^{\prime})=\cat(M^{\prime})=n+k
.

Proof.

First, we note that such a splitting exists, see Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
. Since
W
is a closed simply-connected

c
-symplectic
(2n-2k)
-dimensional manifold, we have
\cu(W)=\cat(W)=n-k
,
see Section
2.D
. Thus, we get

2\dcat(M)\geq 2\dcat(M^{\prime})\geq 2\cu(M^{\prime})=4k+2(n-k)=2k+\dim(M)

in view of Theorem
2.2
.

To show the second part, since
n+k=\cu(M^{\prime})\leq\dcat(M^{\prime})\leq\cat(M^{\prime})
, it suffices to prove that
\cat(M^{\prime})\leq n+k
.
Of course, Theorem
2.1
gives us

\cat(M^{\prime})\leq\cat(T^{2k})+\cat(W)=2k+(n-k)=n+k.

∎

5.2 Remark
.

We note that without the non-negative Ricci curvature hypothesis on
c
-symplectic manifolds
M
having infinite fundamental group, even the (relatively) weaker inequality
b_{1}(M)\leq\dcat(M)
from Theorem
4.1
fails.
To see this, consider the
n
-th symmetric product
SP^{n}(M_{g})
of the closed orientable surface
M_{g}
of genus
g>n\geq 2
, which is
c
-symplectic but cannot support a Riemannian metric of non-negative Ricci curvature,
[
DCDJ
, Section 5]
. It follows from
[
DCDJ
, Section 6]
that

b_{1}(SP^{n}(M_{g}))=2g>2n=\dcat(SP^{n}(M_{g}))=\dim(SP^{n}(M_{g})).

For a proof of the following classical result, we refer to
[
Bro82
, Theorem VIII.3.1]
.

5.3 Theorem

(Serre)
.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
. If
\Gamma
is torsion-free, then
\cd(\Gamma)=\cd(\Gamma^{\prime})
.

In the case when
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free, we can say more than Proposition
5.1
by using Serre’s theorem.
This is a prime example of the type of geometric condition leading to the equality
\dcat=\cat
.

5.4 Theorem
.

Let
M
be a closed
c
-symplectic Riemannian
2n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free.
If
M^{\prime}\cong T^{2k}\times W
is a Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of
M
, then

\dcat(M)=\cat(M)=n+k.

Proof.

Because of Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
2.1
above, we have the inequality

n+k=\dcat(M^{\prime})=\cat(M^{\prime})\leq\cat(M)
.
The main result of
[
Dra19
]
says that

\cat(M)\leq\frac{\dim(M)+\cd(\pi_{1}(M))}{2}=\frac{2n+\cd(\pi_{1}(M))}{2}.

Since
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free and
M^{\prime}
is a finite cover of
M
(see Theorem
3.1
),
Serre’s Theorem
5.3
implies that
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=\cd(\pi_{1}(M^{\prime}))
. Since
W
is simply connected,

\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})\cong\pi_{1}(T^{2k})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{2k}
. Hence,
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=2k
. Therefore, we get from the above two inequalities that

\cat(M)=n+k
. By Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
2.2
, we have

n+k=\dcat(M^{\prime})\leq\dcat(M)\leq\cat(M)=n+k.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following is an immediate consequence of the argument in Theorem
5.4
.

5.5 Corollary
.

Let
M
be a closed
c
-symplectic
2n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free.
Then
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))
is even and is the rank of the torus in the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of

.

5.6 Remark
.

By our earlier discussion of flat manifolds in Section

5.B.

We begin by noting that from the proof of Lemma
3.5
, if

is a closed

-symplectic
2n
-manifold,

is a finitely generated free abelian

5.7 Proposition
.

Let
M
be a closed

-symplectic
2n
-manifold. If

is a finitely generated, free abelian finite index subgroup of

5.8 Corollary
.

If

5.9 Theorem
.

Let
M
be a closed
c
-symplectic
2n
-manifold such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free. If

Note that in

manifold
M
with non-negative Ricci curvature obeys

\cat(M)\geq b_{1}(M)+e_{0}(\widetilde{M}),

where
e_{0}
is a rational homotopy invariant best described with the machinery of minimal models. Suffice it to say that it is always the case that
\cu(X)\leq e_{0}(X)
for simply connected
X
, and for an
X
that is
formal
in the sense of rational homotopy theory,
\cu(X)=e_{0}(X)
. Therefore, our bound

\dcat(M)\geq b_{1}(M)+\cu(W)

in Theorem
4.1
is tantalizingly close to saying that
\cat(M)=\dcat(M)
for all such
M
(since the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting

T^{k}\times W
implies
W\simeq\widetilde{M}
). In both these results, if
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free, then
b_{1}(M)
may be replaced
by
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))
. We leave it to the reader to ponder whether
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)
for such manifolds.

Finally, note that there is a minimal model formulation of the rational category
\cat_{0}(X)
, see
[
CLOT03
]
. We pose the problem
of finding such a formulation for
\dcat_{0}(X)=\dcat(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
for
X
a simply connected space.

6.
Distributional category and macroscopic dimension

Before we end, we would like to relate the distributional category to another very geometric type of invariant in the Bochner fashion.
The notion of the macroscopic dimension of Riemannian manifolds was introduced by Gromov in
[
Gro96
]
, in part to study
the problem of the (non-)existence of Riemannian metrics of positive scalar curvature (see also
[
Dra13
]
,
[
DCDJ
]
). For a Riemannian manifold
X
,
its
macroscopic dimension
, denoted
\dim_{mc}X
, is the smallest integer
n
for which there exists a continuous proper map

f:X\to K
to an
n
-dimensional simplicial complex
K
such that for some
c>0
,
\text{diam}(f^{-1}(y))<c
for all
y\in K
.

6.1 Remark
.

By definition,
\dim_{mc}X\leq\dim(X)
, and equality holds if
X={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. If
X
is a compact manifold, then
\dim_{mc}X=0
.
So, for a compact manifold
M
, we will talk about the macroscopic dimension of its universal Riemannian cover
\widetilde{M}
.

For closed manifolds
M
, a strict improvement to the above inequality was recently obtained in
[
DCDJ
, Theorem 7.18]

using LS-category as follows:

\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}\leq\cat(M)\leq\dim(M).

We further improve this inequality in the case of manifolds having non-negative Ricci curvature
2
2

2

There is a different notion of macroscopic dimension due to Dranishnikov (see
[
Dra13
]
), which is defined using Lipschitz maps instead of continuous maps. For closed manifolds
M
having non-negative Ricci curvature, these two notions coincide on
\widetilde{M}
— see
[
DCDJ
, Proposition 7.10]
.
using distributional category.

6.2 Theorem
.

Let
M
be a closed Riemannian
n
-manifold with non-negative Ricci curvature such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is infinite. If
M^{\prime}\cong T^{r}\times W
is a
Cheeger–Gromoll splitting of
M
, then

\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}\leq\dcat(M^{\prime})\leq\dcat(M).

Moreover,
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dcat(M)
if and only if
M
is flat.

Proof.

The proof of
[
DCDJ
, Proposition 7.10]
implies that
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=r
. But we have from the proof of Theorem
4.1

that
r+\cu(W)\leq\dcat(M^{\prime})
. Therefore,

\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=r\leq r+\cu(W)\leq\dcat(M^{\prime})\leq\dcat(M).

If
M
is flat, then
M
is aspherical and
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dim(M)=\dcat(M)
by
[
DCDJ
, Corollary 7.7]
and Theorem
2.4
, respectively. Conversely, if
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dcat(M)
, then we get from the above series of inequalities that
\cu(W)=0
for the closed simply connected manifold
W
. This means
W\simeq\ast
and hence,
M^{\prime}\cong T^{n}
(as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
). Therefore,
M
is a closed aspherical
n
-manifold and
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free. Since
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
has finite index in
\pi_{1}(M)
, the generalization of Vasquez’s characterization
[
Vas70
]
mentioned in Section
4
implies that there exists a closed flat
n
-manifold
N=K(\pi_{1}(M),1)
. Since
M=K(\pi_{1}(M),1)
, we must have
M\simeq N
. In the cases
n\neq 3,4
, the results of Farrell–Hsiang
[
FH83
]
imply that the corresponding
surgery structure set
is zero and so
M\cong N
(see Section
4
). For
n=3,4
,
[
KL09
, Theorems 0.7 & 0.11]
imply that the flat manifold
N
is
strongly Borel
, which means the homotopy equivalence
N\to M
above is homotopic to a homeomorphism. Therefore, in any case,
M
is flat.
∎

6.3 Remark
.

The fact that
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dim(M)
implies the flatness of
M
was proved in
[
DCDJ
, Proposition 7.10]
in a completely different way, so once we showed in the proof above that
M
is aspherical, we could have simply applied that result along with Theorem
2.4
. Nevertheless, it seems worthwhile to see how a more classical approach leads to the flatness conclusion.

6.4 Corollary
.

Suppose
M=N\times Y
has non-negative Ricci curvature for a closed manifold
Y
such that
\pi_{1}(Y)
is finite and a closed aspherical manifold
N
. Then

\dcat(M)>\dim(N).

Proof.

Observe that
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}\leq\dim_{mc}\widetilde{N}+\dim_{mc}\widetilde{Y}=\dim(N)
because
N
is aspherical and
\widetilde{Y}
is compact. Then by the definition of macroscopic dimension, we must have
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dim(N)
. But because
M
is not flat, Theorem
6.2
implies
\dim(N)=\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}<\dcat(M)
.
∎

6.5 Remark
.

The hypothesis of non-negative Ricci curvature in Theorem
6.2
is necessary.
To see this, consider the
g
-th symmetric product
SP^{g}(M_{g})
of the closed orientable surface
M_{g}
of genus
g\geq 2
and note its following properties from
[
DCDJ
]
:

SP^{g}(M_{g})
cannot support a metric of non-negative Ricci curvature,
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{SP^{g}(M_{g})}=2g=\dcat(SP^{g}(M_{g}))
, and
\pi_{2}(SP^{g}(M_{g}))
is infinite.

6.6 Example
.

We note that for the non-negatively curved manifolds of the type

M=T^{k}\times\prod_{i=1}^{n}S^{3}/({\mathbb{Z}}/s_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})

from Example
4.6
, we have
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}\leq k+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dim_{mc}S^{3}=k<k+n\leq\dcat(M)
as in Corollary
6.4
. Therefore, the difference between
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}
and
\dcat(M)
can be arbitrary. In fact, here we see an explicit example where
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=\dcat(M)
holds exactly when
n=0
and
M
is a (flat) torus as in Theorem
6.2
.

6.7 Remark
.

We conclude by pointing out that the arguments of Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
6.2
give us the improved bound
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}\leq 2\dcat(M)-\dim(M)
for any closed
c
-symplectic manifold
M
having non-negative Ricci curvature and infinite fundamental group. However, the equality
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=2\dcat(M)-\dim(M)
here does not imply that
M
is flat (or even aspherical). To see this, consider
M=T^{2k}\times N
for a closed simply connected symplectic
(2n-2k)
manifold
N
with non-negative Ricci curvature. Then,
\dim_{mc}\widetilde{M}=2k
and
\dcat(M)=n+k
, but
M
is not aspherical. The same example also shows that in the setting of Proposition
5.1
, the equality
b_{1}(M)=2\dcat(M)-\dim(M)
does not imply that
M
is a torus.
