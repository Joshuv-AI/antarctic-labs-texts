Solution-processed organic infrared photodetectors operating beyond 1.4 μm for sensitive environmental monitoring
Authors: Yifei Geng, Tengfei Li, Zhenzhen Zhang, Huiqing Hou, Yuze Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photon energies in the short-wave infrared (SWIR) beyond 1.4 μm enable access to vibrational-overtone signatures of O–H, C–H, and N–H bonds in pollutants and waste materials, offering an imaging-based nondestructive approach for environmental monitoring. However, current SWIR imaging technologies rely on costly epitaxial semiconductors incompatible with monolithic integration, limiting consumer-grade deployment. Here we report a cost-effective organic SWIR imager with response beyond 1.4 μm, integrating high-detectivity organic photodetectors (OPDs) with amorphous silicon thin-film transistors via solution processing. The developed highly-ordered SWIR organic semiconductors with (halogenated) thiophene-fused quinoid terminals exhibit ultra-narrow optical bandgaps of 0.68–0.94 eV and Urbach energies down to 23 meV. The SWIR OPDs achieve spectral response from 0.3 μm to 1.5–1.7 μm with responsivities up to ∼0.1 A W−1 and specific detectivities of 2.03–4.60 × 1010 Jones at 1.4 μm. The active-matrix SWIR imagers achieve accurate oil-leak monitoring in simulated seawater (R2 = 0.993–0.997) and reliable waste-textile classification (silhouette score = 0.824), demonstrating their capabilities in environmental monitoring.

INTRODUCTION

Short-wave infrared (SWIR) photodetection beyond 1.4 μm enables precise, rapid, and nondestructive monitoring of environmental pollutants by exploiting the moderate first-overtone absorptions of O–H, N–H, and C–H bonds common in pollutants and waste materials (1, 2), offering deeper penetration depths than the strong mid-infrared fundamental absorptions while providing stronger signals than the weak second-overtones below 1.4 μm (Fig. 1A) (3), and surpassing traditional time-consuming and destructive methods reliant on complex sample pre-treatment or manual sorting (Fig. 1B) (4). However, current inorganic SWIR sensors, like InGaAs and Ge, rely on costly epitaxial growth and complex flip-chip bonding for integration with readout circuits (5, 6), limiting their low-cost consumer-grade applications, particularly in less-developed regions.

Solution-processed emerging semiconductors offer a cost-effective route to SWIR sensor systems via facile fabrication (7–12), among which organic semiconductors outperform quantum dots in free of heavy metal toxicity, structural diversity and tunable optoelectronic properties (13, 14). However, due to the lack of organic semiconductors comprising both ultra-narrow optical bandgap (Egopt < 0.9–1.0 eV) (15, 16) and long-range ordered packing (Fig. 1C) (17, 18), the reported organic photodetectors (OPDs) operating beyond 1.4 μm are scarce and generally exhibit limited responsivity (R ≤ 0.05 A W−1) and/or specific detectivity (D* ≤ 108–109 Jones) (19–21), lagging far behind those (D* ≥ 1010 Jones) inorganic photodetectors. Therefore, highly ordered organic photoactive materials with ultra-narrow bandgaps are desired for consumer-grade sensitive SWIR imagers with extended spectral response for wide-scale applications (22, 23), such as efficient nondestructive pollution and waste monitoring.

Here, we demonstrate an active-matrix organic SWIR imager operating beyond 1.4 μm by monolithically integrating solution-processed OPDs based on the developed SWIR materials, onto amorphous silicon (a-Si) thin-film transistor (TFT) backplane, and thereby enable sensitive environmental monitoring. Highly ordered SWIR organic semiconductors employing (halogenated) thiophene-fused quinoid terminals are rationally designed and synthesized. These terminal blocks possess high electronegativity and uneven electrostatic potential, leading to Egopt as narrow as 0.68–0.94 eV and Urbach energy (EU) down to ∼23 meV of organic semiconductors. The optimized SWIR OPDs response to 1.5–1.7 μm with the high R up to ∼0.1 A W−1 and D* of 2.03–4.60 × 1010 Jones at 1.4 μm. Additionally, our devices achieve a linear dynamic range (LDR) of 149 dB, which exceeds that of InGaAs photodetector (132 dB) and those reported for SWIR OPDs with response beyond 1.4 μm. The resulting active-matrix SWIR imagers enable accurate oil-leak monitoring, achieving reliable and robust monitoring across the full concentration range (R2 > 0.99) while attaining single-pixel spatial resolution with detecting oil droplets as small as ∼1 mm in diameter (signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) = 4.59). For textile-waste identification, the system delivers robust clustering between natural and synthetic fibers, yielding a silhouette score of 0.824 (range −1 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating optimal quality), while maintaining reliable detection across a broad range of fabric thicknesses, underscoring the suitability of this approach for practical textile-waste sorting.

RESULTS

Design of SWIR organic semiconductors

Sulfur atoms exhibit a much higher polarizability (∼3.4 Å3) than carbon atoms (∼1.2 Å3), owing to their larger atomic radii and more spatially extended p orbitals (24, 25) (Fig. 2A and fig. S1), which is expected to facilitate more effective delocalization of excess negative charges within the π-conjugated system and further enhance the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) effect. Additionally, the electron clouds of highly polarizable sulfur atoms tend to deform asymmetrically under nearby charge influence, which is likely to induce uneven local charge distributions that potentially enhance non-covalent interactions between sulfur and surrounding atoms, thereby promoting more ordered molecular packing (26).

Therefore, we replaced the benzene ring in the strongly electron-withdrawing 2-[4-oxonaphthalen-1(4H)-ylidene]malononitrile (QC) terminal with thiophene to design three terminals (TCx, x = 1, 2, 3, Fig. 2B), whose Mulliken electronegativities (5.61, 5.72, and 5.53 eV) obtained from Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm electron-withdrawing strengths comparable to QC (5.60 eV) (15). Additionally, as reflected by similar negative electrostatic potential (ESP) minima (−30.08 kcal mol−1 for QC; −29.31 to −30.65 kcal mol−1 for TCx) but notably higher positive ESP maxima (26.91 kcal mol−1 for QC; 32.19 to 33.01 kcal mol−1 for TCx) (Fig. 2B and fig. S2), compared to the QC unit, the TCx end groups exhibit more uneven local charge distributions around the sulfur atoms as expected.

Based on TCx as acceptor (A) units, A-D-A type SWIR sensing materials LTCx are designed using dithienothiophen[3,2-b]pyrrolobenzothiadiazole (BTP) as the donor (D) unit, and DFT calculations reveal that LTC1 and LTC2 possess similar HOMO levels (−5.35 and −5.34 eV) but deeper LUMO levels (both −4.02 eV) than the QC analogue L2-eC9 (−5.33 eV/−3.91 eV), resulting in smaller transport gaps (1.32–1.33 eV vs. 1.42 eV) (fig. S3). This can be attributed to enhanced ICT arising from the high polarizability of sulfur, which improves charge accommodation and electron delocalization along the conjugated backbone. The net transferred charges (QCT) from the D fragment to the A fragment during electronic excitation are 0.628 electrons for both LTC1 and LTC2, higher than that of L2-eC9 (0.618 electrons) (27) (Fig. 2C), confirming the enhanced ICT effect in LTC1 and LTC2. Interestingly, LTC3 differs from LTC1 and LTC2 because the distinct thiophene orientation weakens the ICT effect (28) (detailed analysis can be found in figs. S3 to S5 and note S1). Furthermore, LTCx dimers exhibit more negative BSSE-corrected binding energies (29, 30) (−24.6 to −28.4 kcal mol−1) than L2-eC9 (−23.0 kcal mol−1) (Fig. 2D and fig. S6), indicating stronger intermolecular stacking that favors enhanced molecular crystallinity and charge transport.

Properties of SWIR organic semiconductors

According to the synthetic routes outlined in fig. S7, LTC1 and LTC2 featuring narrower bandgap than LTC3 were synthesized with terminal group of TC1 and TC2, respectively. The ultraviolet-visible-near infrared (UV-vis-NIR) absorption spectra show that in solution (∼10−6 M CHCl3), LTC1 and LTC2 exhibit nearly identical long-wavelength absorption peak (λmax) values at 940 nm and 947 nm, redshifted by 72 and 79 nm compared with L2-eC9 (868 nm), consistent with the enhanced ICT predicted by DFT, while in thin films their λmax values further shift to 1080 and 1060 nm versus 958 nm for L2-eC9 (fig. S8). Consequently, the films of TCx-based materials exhibit significantly larger bathochromic shifts compared to their dilute solutions, featuring Δλmax and corresponding energy differences of 140 nm and 0.171 eV for LTC1, and 113 nm and 0.139 eV for LTC2, respectively, exceeding those of L2-eC9 (90 nm and 0.134 eV). Among them, LTC1 exhibits the largest Δλmax and corresponding energy differences, indicating stronger intermolecular interactions and enhanced molecular packing in the LTC1 solid state (discussed further below), which is consistent with its highest dimer binding energy observed in theoretical calculations. As illustrated in Fig. 2E, the Egopts of LTC1 and LTC2 are estimated to be 0.94 and 0.97 eV, respectively, which are smaller than L2-eC9 (1.03 eV). Meanwhile, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy and low-energy inverse photoemission spectroscopy measurements further show that deeper LUMO levels of LTCx than L2-eC9 (−4.29/−4.30 eV vs. −4.18 eV) with similar HOMOs (−5.52 eV vs. −5.48 eV) (fig. S9) account for the reduced Egopt. Grazing-incidence wide-angle x-ray scattering (GIWAXS) analysis of L2-eC9, LTC1, and LTC2 films (Fig. 2F, fig. S10, and table S1) shows that all adopt a primarily face-on orientation. Compared with L2-eC9 (19.0%), LTC1 and LTC2 films exhibit reduced amorphous content in the π-π stacking direction (11.9% and 12.7%), demonstrating that sulfur incorporation promotes more ordered π-π stacking. In addition, their lamellar (100) diffraction peaks at 0.367–0.375 Å−1 with longer crystalline coherence lengths (CCLs) (43.2 and 36.0 Å vs. 32.3 Å for L2-eC9) indicate improved lamellar ordering, which is attributed to stronger sulfur-mediated intermolecular interactions (26) as supported by single-crystal analysis and correlates with the larger Δλmax observed in LTC1 and LTC2 films.

High-quality single crystals of LTC1 were obtained via solvent diffusion, and characterized by single-crystal x-ray diffraction (table S2). In contrast, single crystals of LTC2 could not be obtained, which is likely attributed to its higher degree of structural disorder compared to LTC1, consistent with its relatively smaller CCL observed in GIWAXS analysis. LTC1 crystallizes in a monoclinic system (space group P21/c) with two distinct molecular conformations in the unit cell. As shown in Fig. 2G, the molecule adopts a nearly planar conformation, stabilized by intramolecular S⋯O interactions between the BTP core and the terminal group, with an average interaction distance of 2.67 Å, forming interlocking structural features. Along the molecular backbone direction, adjacent molecules exhibit non-covalent N⋯S and S⋯S interactions, with distances of 3.233 Å and 3.338 Å, respectively, both shorter than the corresponding van der Waals radii sums (3.35 Å for N⋯S and 3.60 Å for S⋯S), leading to a wavelike arrangement within the planar layer. LTC1 exhibits multiple dimer configurations featuring head-to-tail and head-to-core π-π stacking modes with interlayer distances of 3.325–3.384 Å, accompanied by non-covalent S⋯S and S⋯C interactions (3.528 Å and 3.467 Å, respectively) mediated by the terminal sulfur atoms. These interactions yield a three-dimensional intermolecular network with ordered molecular stacking, which is advantageous for facilitating efficient multidirectional charge transport (31), consistent with the superior crystallinity observed in LTC1 films. A packing coefficient of 68.8% was calculated for the unit cell of LTC1, representing the ratio of the sum of molecular volumes to the cell volume and reflecting the tightness of intermolecular packing (32), which is one of the highest values reported among high-performance organic photovoltaic materials to date (33), indicating that LTC1 exhibits dense molecular packing and a compact crystal structure, potentially promoting higher electron mobility. Accordingly, LTC1 exhibits the highest electron mobility (μe = 5.6 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1), followed by LTC2 (μe = 3.5 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1), with both outperforming L2-eC9 (μe = 2.5 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1), as determined by space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements (fig. S11). The EU values derived from highly sensitive external quantum efficiency (sEQE) measurements were 23.0 meV for LTC1 and 24.1 meV for LTC2, both lower than that of L2-eC9 (26.1 meV) (Fig. 2H), with the lowest value of LTC1 arising from enhanced intermolecular packing, consistent with previous analyses and ranking among the smallest reported for organic semiconductors (34).

Device performance of SWIR OPDs

Photodiode-type SWIR OPDs with an inverted structure were fabricated, using bulk heterojunction active layers of PTB7-Th (HOMO: −5.05 eV, LUMO: −3.40 eV; fig. S12) (35) blended with LTC1, LTC2, or L2-eC9 to ensure efficient exciton dissociation via well-aligned energy levels (36). As shown in Fig. 3A, PTB7-Th: LTC1 and PTB7-Th: LTC2 devices exhibit broader spectral responses (0.3–1.5 μm) than PTB7-Th: L2-eC9 (0.3–1.3 μm), with PTB7-Th: LTC1 achieving the highest R values with a peak of 0.097 A W−1 at 1.06 μm, while PTB7-Th: LTC2 and PTB7-Th: L2-eC9 reach maximum R values of 0.087 A W−1 at 1.06 μm and 0.093 A W−1 at 1.04 μm, respectively.

The D* is a critical figure of merit for OPD devices, quantifying their ability to detect faint light signals (14), and is calculated from the R and the noise current (in) using the following equationD∗=RABin=ANEP(1)where A is the device area (0.04 cm2 in this work), B is the electrical measurement bandwidth, and the NEP is the noise-equivalent power. As depicted in Fig. 3B, the noise current at zero bias exhibits frequency independence across the tested frequency range, and the average in values of LTC1- and LTC2-based OPDs are measured as 4.29 × 10−14 A Hz−1/2 and 6.37 × 10−14 A Hz−1/2, respectively, lower than those reported for OPDs with comparable response ranges (19, 37). Previous studies have demonstrated that the in of OPDs in photovoltaic mode strongly correlates with the effective bandgap (Eeff, defined as the difference between the LUMO of acceptor and the HOMO of donor) and the bandtail disorder spread (38). Notably, the deeper Fermi levels of LTC1 (−4.56 eV), LTC2 (−4.79 eV), and L2-eC9 (−4.71 eV) relative to that of PTB7-Th (−4.46 eV) lead to vacuum level shifts at the donor–acceptor interfaces upon contact (35, 39). As a result, the actual Eeff in the PTB7-Th: LTC1 is derived as 0.86 eV, smaller than that of PTB7-Th: LTC2 films (1.08 eV) (fig. S13). Meanwhile, the PTB7-Th: LTC1-based OPD exhibits an obviously lower EU value of 23.5 meV compared to 26.3 meV for PTB7-Th: L2-eC9, indicating reduced bandtail disorder. In contrast, PTB7-Th: LTC2 -based OPD shows a higher EU of 26.2 meV relative to PTB7-Th: LTC1 (fig. S14), consistent with its elevated noise current. Consequently, the D* values for LTC1- and LTC2-based devices, determined by R and in, exceed 1011 Jones across the 0.3 to ∼1.4 μm range. Notably, LTC1-based devices achieve a peak D* of 4.53 × 1011 Jones at 1.06 μm, significantly surpassing commercial Ge detectors at wavelengths below 1.3 μm and representing one of the highest reported values among OPDs responsive beyond 1.4 μm (Fig. 3C and table S3). Considering that OPDs generally operate under reverse bias in practical applications, we further evaluated the performance of the LTC1- and LTC2-based devices under different reverse biases (figs. S15 and S16). Although the enhancement in responsivity under reverse bias, the concomitant increase in in values results in an approximately 1 ∼ 2 orders reduction in detectivity for the OPDs relative to those operating at zero bias.

The PTB7-Th: LTC1-based OPDs show extrapolated detection limits of 16.2 and 26.3 pW cm−2 under 1.05 and 1.3 μm illumination at 0 V, slightly lower than those of LTC2-based devices (27.9 and 37.8 pW cm−2) and in good agreement with calculated values (11.2 and 21.4 pW cm−2), with the detection limit at 1.3 μm shifting to 191 pW cm−2 (calculated: 186 pW cm−2) under −0.5 V bias (Fig. 3D and figs. S17 to S19). The LDR reaches 149 dB at 0 V and remains 130 dB under −0.5 V bias, both comparable to or exceeding that of commercial InGaAs detectors (132 dB) (Fig. 3E and fig. S20) (40). Moreover, the devices deliver fast photoresponse with trise/tfall of 2.34/2.55 μs and a − 3 dB cut-off frequency of 379 kHz at 0 V, outperforming LTC2- and L2-eC9-based devices, and further accelerate to 1.37/1.39 μs and 838 kHz under −0.5 V bias (Fig. 3F and figs. S21 and S22).

To elucidate the origin of the superior performance, the morphology of the blended films and the carrier dynamics of the devices were further investigated. GIWAXS reveals face-on orientation for all blended films, with PTB7-Th: LTC1 and PTB7-Th: LTC2 displaying longer lamellar CCLs and markedly lower amorphous fractions along the π-π direction than L2-eC9 (figs. S23, S24, and table S4), indicating that sulfur incorporation facilitates stronger intermolecular interactions and more ordered π-π stacking (note S2). Correspondingly, PTB7-Th: LTC1 devices exhibit the lowest trap densities and more balanced, faster charge transport (note S3 and figs. S25 and S26), which suppress recombination and enhance charge extraction, as evidenced by the lowest bimolecular recombination rate coefficient and the shortest carrier extraction times from transient photovoltage and transient photocurrent measurements (note S4 and fig. S27). Collectively, these morphological ordering and charge dynamics underpin the highest responsivity of PTB7-Th: LTC1 devices.

Extended-response OPD-based SWIR imager

Building on the thiophene-fused terminals, we employed a halogenation strategy to further strengthen the electron-withdrawing character of the terminal groups (16, 41) and extend the photoresponse range of organic semiconductors over 1.5 μm. The α-position of the thiophene ring in the TC1 terminal was brominated and chlorinated to yield BrTC1 and ClTC1 (fig. S7), which were used to construct electron acceptors LTC1-Br and LTC1-Cl (Fig. 3G). DFT calculations show QCT values of 0.657 and 0.658 e for LTC1-Br and LTC1-Cl, respectively, both higher than LTC1 (0.628 e), confirming enhanced ICT. Consistently, the solution absorption of LTC1-Br and LTC1-Cl are both redshifted to 974 nm (vs. 940 nm for LTC1, fig. S28A), while thin-film absorption maxima reach 1.16 μm (LTC1-Br) and 1.17 μm (LTC1-Cl) (vs. 1.08 μm for LTC1, fig. S28B), with narrower optical bandgaps of 0.89 (LTC1-Br) and 0.87 eV (LTC1-Cl) (vs. 0.94 eV for LTC1, fig. S28C) and extended absorption edges to ∼1.55 μm, attributed to enhanced intermolecular charge-transfer-coupled aggregation. Similar to LTC1 film, LTC1-Cl also exhibits good photostability and thermal stability (fig. S29).

Subsequently, SWIR OPDs based on PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl and PTB7-Th: LTC1-Br were fabricated and characterized (fig. S30). Both devices exhibited markedly enhanced R across a broad spectral range of 0.3–1.6 μm, achieving peak R values of 0.111 A W−1 and 0.104 A W−1 at 1.18 μm for PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl and PTB7-Th: LTC1-Br, respectively (Fig. 3H). Compared to PTB7-Th: LTC1-Br-based OPDs, PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl-based OPDs demonstrate consistently higher responsivity, with notable R value up to ∼0.1 A W−1 at ∼1.4 μm, which is also markedly higher than that of LTC1-based OPDs around 1.4 μm. Furthermore, the average in values for SWIR OPDs based on PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl and PTB7-Th: LTC1-Br were measured at 5.17 × 10−13 A Hz−1/2 and 3.23 × 10−13 A Hz−1/2, respectively (fig. S31). Consequently, SWIR OPDs based on PTB7-Th: LTC1-X exhibit D* values of 2.95 × 1010 Jones for LTC1-Cl and 2.03 × 1010 Jones for LTC1-Br at 1.4 μm (Fig. 3I). Under reverse bias, the performance variation trends of the PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl and PTB7-Th: LTC1-Br based OPDs are similar with those of the LTC1-based devices (figs. S32 and S33).

Given their good operational stability similar to that observed for LTC1-based devices, high R and D* at 1.4 μm, sufficient LDR of 89 dB at −0.5 V, and fast response times (trise = 1.23 μs and tfall = 1.31 μs at −0.5 V) (figs. S34–S36), the solution-processed LTC1-Cl-based OPDs are directly deposited onto a commercial 64 by 64 a-Si TFT backplane with a 500 μm by 500 μm pixel pitch without additional photolithographic patterning to form the active-matrix imager. The a-Si TFT backplane is built on a glass substrate with patterned Mo: Al gate electrodes and scan lines, a SiNx gate insulator, an a-Si semiconductor layer, Mo: Al source and drain electrodes forming bottom-gate staggered TFTs, a SiNx passivation layer, and ITO pixel electrodes contacted to the TFTs through vias in the dielectric. Owing to the scalability of a-Si TFT backplanes and the solution-processable organic OPD layer, the same architecture can be straightforwardly scaled to larger areas and higher pixel densities at much lower cost than complementary-metal-oxide-semiconductor circuits, and it offers a practical solution for wide-scale consumer-grade application of SWIR imaging (42). Integrated with a commercial readout system, the resulting 64 by 64 sensor array delivers an average photocurrent of ∼200 nA per pixel under 1.4 μm light-emitting diode (LED) illumination (2 mW cm−2) in the absence of ambient light (fig. S37), providing sufficient SWIR signal levels for the following environmental monitoring demonstrations.

Proof of concept for environmental sensing

Oil spill simulation detection

Timely and accurate pollution monitoring is a prerequisite for environmental sustainability, particularly in aquatic and marine systems where oil spills pose severe ecological risks and require rapid source identification to mitigate environmental damage (43). The SWIR OPD-based sensor array leverages the strong first-overtone absorption of O–H at ∼1.4 μm and the comparatively weak hydrocarbon absorption at the same wavelength to rapid discriminate of oil-water mixtures. Compared with conventional methods (Fig. 4A), the OPD-based array provides several advantages: relative to satellite or airborne remote sensing, it enables real-time on-site monitoring that avoids the hours-to-days delay in data collection and processing (44); compared with acoustic techniques such as multibeam sonar, it offers higher spatial resolution, allowing reliable detection of small oil droplets and thin films that acoustics often under-resolves (45); and unlike thermal infrared imaging, which is limited to sea-surface slicks due to water’s strong absorption (44, 46), the SWIR OPD-based arrays enable subsurface and underwater detection.

To evaluate the SWIR OPD-based sensor array for detecting oil spills in seawater, we selected commercial No. 0 diesel from a local gas station, which is a common component of marine oil spills, and seawater from the Bohai Sea to simulate oil spill scenarios (fig. S38). Under 1.4 μm LED illumination, the net photocurrent exhibited an exponential dependence on oil fraction (Fig. 4, B and C and fig. S39), which can be described byJP(ϕoil)=J0ekϕoil(2)where JP(ϕoil) is the net photocurrent at oil fraction ϕoil, J0 is the extrapolated zero-content photocurrent, and k is the exponential growth factor reflecting the sensitivity to oil concentration. Fitting yielded k = 2.10 (per v/v) and J0 = 7.73 × 10−9 A, with a deviation of only 1.5% from the measured blank of 7.85 × 10−9 A and an excellent coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.997), demonstrating the high measurement accuracy of the sensor array for oil-leak detection across the full concentration range (0–100%) in seawater. In real seawater environments, oil slicks inevitably undergo emulsification over time, driven primarily by wave-induced mechanical agitation together with the surface-active components naturally present in crude oil. To mimic this process, simulated oil-water emulsification was conducted for 1 h, after which the net photocurrents at identical oil fractions were consistently lower than those of the non-emulsified mixtures (Fig. 4, B and C and fig. S40), owing to enhanced light scattering and turbidity from the dispersed oil droplets that reduced effective light transmission. At oil fractions above 5%, the photocurrent-concentration relationship can be well fitted by the exponential model with an exponential growth factor of k = 2.29 and an extrapolated baseline photocurrent of J0 = 5.34 × 10−9 A, whereas at very low oil fractions (ϕoil = 0.01) the measured photocurrent deviates from the fitted trend. These results demonstrate that the SWIR OPD-based sensor array maintains high accuracy and sensitivity for oil-leak detection even under realistic emulsified seawater conditions, as confirmed by the excellent goodness of fit (R2 = 0.993). In addition, oil droplets of different sizes ranging from ∼1 mm to ∼12 mm in diameter were detected (Fig. 4D and fig. S41), and even for the smallest droplets (∼1 mm), a SNR of 4.59 was obtained, corresponding to an effective spatial resolution of ∼1 mm.

To further emulate realistic flow-state oil spill scenarios, we established a controlled water-flow channel with continuous introduction of oil-water mixtures (Fig. 4, E and F), as captured in movie S1, which highlights the system’s potential for real-time marine monitoring. Under 1.4 μm LED illumination, the array distinguishes oil, water, and air bubbles at the single-pixel spatial scale (Fig. 4G): water strongly absorbs 1.4 μm light and appears darker in grayscale images, oil shows weaker absorption and appears brighter, and bubbles are identified by characteristic scattering and refraction features, enabling precise discrimination of millimetre-scale droplets and thin films. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the SWIR OPD-based sensor array achieves high accuracy, sensitivity, and spatial resolution for oil-leak detection under realistic and complex seawater conditions, highlighting its potential for practical marine environmental monitoring.

Identification of textile fabrics

According to official European Union data, approximately 5 million tons of clothing are discarded annually within the European Union, which is equivalent to an average of 12 kg per person. Yet only about 1% is recycled into new garments, while the remainder is incinerated or landfilled, thereby intensifying environmental degradation (47). Accurate, rapid, and nondestructive fiber identification is essential for effective waste textile recycling. Traditional classification methods, such as manual sorting, burn tests, microscopic examination, and chemical analysis, face serious drawbacks (Fig. 5A). Manual sorting is labor-intensive and prone to errors. Burn tests and chemical analysis are destructive and may create secondary pollutants. Microscopic analysis is time-consuming and difficult to scale. These limitations highlight the urgent need for innovative classification techniques that provide speed, accuracy, and nondestructive performance.

Natural fibers such as cotton, wool, and silk are rich in O–H or N–H bonds, originating from cellulose in plant-based fibers and peptide linkages in protein-based fibers. In contrast, most synthetic fibers, such as polyester and acrylic, contain few or no O–H or N–H bonds, while polyamide-based fibers such as nylon contain N–H bonds but lack the strong O–H absorption characteristic of natural cellulose (48). As we mentioned above, these functional groups exhibit distinct first-overtone absorptions around 1.4 μm in the SWIR, so illumination near this wavelength produces stronger attenuation in fibers rich in cellulose or protein than in polyester or nylon. The resulting intensity contrasts can be quantified with a SWIR OPD-based sensor array to enable rapid, nondestructive classification of natural and synthetic textiles.

Given the variability in textile thickness, it is essential to establish its influence on OPD response. Under 1.4 μm illumination, the net photocurrent (dark-subtracted) was found to decrease exponentially with thickness (Fig. 5, B and C and fig. S42) according toJP(t)=J0e−μefft(3)where JP(t) is the net pixel photocurrent at thickness t (mm), J0 is the extrapolated zero-thickness photocurrent (A), and μeff is the effective attenuation coefficient (mm−1) that reflects absorption and scattering. Fitting yielded μeff = 1.89 mm−1 and J0 = 113.67 nA. The SNR decreased systematically from ∼10 at 0.16 mm to ∼3.6 at 1.12 mm, and according to the Rose criterion (49), which regards SNR ≥ 3 as the minimum threshold for reliable detection (Fig. 5D), signals observed up to 1.12 mm can be assigned to genuine peaks rather than random noise with a confidence above 99.7%, thereby establishing the detection limit beyond 1.12 mm under the present conditions.

All textile samples used for classification were prepared with thicknesses in the 0.3–0.4 mm range, which lies within the high-SNR regime established above and thereby avoids confounding effects from excessive optical attenuation. Consequently, we selected twelve pure natural or synthetic fabrics, dried to minimize moisture interference, and imaged them under 1.4 μm LED illumination using an OPD-based sensor array, converting colored swatches to grayscale according to the measured photocurrent (Fig. 5, E and F). Statistical analysis of identical regions from each grayscale image yielded average pixel currents in the range of 113–141 nA with standard deviations of 0.74–1.11 nA, corresponding to less than 1% of the mean values and reflecting the high precision and reproducibility of the measurements. K-means clustering (K = 2, Euclidean distance) classified the twelve averaged currents into two well-separated groups. Cluster 0 (synthetic fibers) comprised five samples with currents of 135.04–140.44 nA with a mean of 137.53 nA. Cluster 1 (natural fibers) comprised seven samples with currents of 113.29–120.84 nA with a mean of 118.21 nA (Fig. 5G). The inter-cluster difference of approximately 19.3 nA was over 20 times greater than the average measurement uncertainty of 0.84 nA, providing a SNR of 7.3 relative to error bars, ensuring robust, error-resilient classification precision. Cluster validity indices confirmed high reliability, with a silhouette score of 0.824 (ranging from −1 to 1, with unity representing optimal quality), indicating strong intra-cluster cohesion and excellent separation between natural and synthetic fibers, with no negative values suggestive of misclassification. Collectively, these results demonstrate that OPD-based sensor arrays enable precise and reliable molecular-level classification of textiles, achieving rapid, nondestructive discrimination between natural and synthetic fibers and supporting sustainable recycling within a circular economy framework.

These application demonstrations show that SWIR OPD-based sensor arrays, by identifying O–H, and N–H absorptions, enable pollution monitoring as well as waste identification and sorting. Extending device response beyond ∼1.6 μm enables direct detection of C–H overtones characteristic of hydrocarbons and polymer backbones, while enhanced sensitivity near 1.5–1.6 μm facilitates the detection of N–H features and their reliable discrimination from the stronger O–H bands of water. Accordingly, the strong electron-donating unit 2,6-bis(3-alkoxythiophen-2-yl)cyclopentadithiophene (COT) was coupled with the TC1 end group to synthesize L4TC1 (fig. S7) (15), which exhibits an ultra-narrow Egopt of 0.68 eV and an absorption onset beyond 2 μm (fig. S43), surpassing the ∼1.7 μm photoresponse limit of Ge and common InGaAs. Furthermore, the PDPPDTP (50): L4TC1-based SWIR OPDs exhibit a spectral response extending to >1.7 μm (detection limit of the instrument) with a peak responsivity of 0.012 A W−1 at 1.18 μm as well as an average in of 2.18 × 10−12 A Hz−1/2 (fig. S44), corresponding to D* values exceeding 108 Jones over the 0.3–1.7 μm range and reaching a maximum of 1.06 × 109 Jones at 1.18 μm (Fig. 5H). The broad response range covers O–H, N–H, and C–H overtones, and thus holds promise for imaging applications like waste plastic sorting and microplastic identification.

DISCUSSION

In summary, this work couples terminal-guided SWIR molecular engineering with solution-processed active-matrix integration to realize a high-performance OPD-based SWIR imager with photoresponse beyond 1.4 μm, enabling efficient and nondestructive monitoring of environmental pollution and waste. The developed SWIR semiconductors featuring (halogenated) thiophene-fused quinoid terminals possess both ultra-narrow bandgaps and low energetic disorder, enabling the corresponding SWIR OPDs to exhibit a broad spectral response of 0.3–1.6 μm, simultaneously high R and D* values beyond 1.4 μm, as well as a large LDR. Finally, the resulting SWIR imager leverages vibrational-overtone molecular signatures to achieve pixel-level detection of simulated seawater oil spills with accurate quantification across the full concentration range and a high SNR of 4.59, as well as reliable differentiation between natural and synthetic fibers with a silhouette score of 0.824 and an SNR of 7.3, underscoring its potential for practical marine monitoring and textile recycling. This work provides a feasible route to low-cost, consumer-grade, high-performance SWIR sensing systems with broadband photoresponse extending beyond 1.4–1.7 μm, supporting progress toward wide-scale smart resource management and sustainable environmental monitoring.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of materials

The derivatives TCx-Br (x = 1, 2) and XTC1-Br (X = Cl, Br) of TCx and XTC1 were synthesized from 6,7-dihydrobenzo[b]thiophen-4(5H)-one via distinct multi-step routes. TC1-Br was obtained through bromination, cyclization, aromatization, and Knoevenagel condensation, while TC2-Br involved a Wolff-Kishner reduction and oxidation before the same sequence. XTC1-Br were prepared by halogen substitution, followed by the same synthetic procedures as for TC1-Br. Then, LTCx and LTC1-X were obtained by Stille coupling reaction between the bis-stannylated BTP unit (BTP-2SnMe3) and TCx-Br/XTC1-Br, respectively. The detailed synthetic routes are shown in Supplementary.

Device fabrication and characterization

Indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates were sequentially cleaned and treated by UV–ozone (20 min). Two OPD architectures were fabricated.

Inverted architecture (ITO/ZnO/active layer/MoO3/Ag). A ∼ 30 nm ZnO electron-transport layer was deposited from a precursor solution by spin-coating (4000 rpm, 30 s) and annealed at 200°C for 30 min. Active layers were processed in nitrogen: for PTB7-Th:L2-eC9/LTC1/LTC2, chlorobenzene solutions (1:1, w/w; 24 mg mL−1 total) containing 4 vol% 1-chloronaphthalene (1-CN) were spin-coated at 1400 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 80°C for 5 min; for L4TC1 devices, PDPPDTP:L4TC1 (1:1.2, w/w) was dissolved in chlorobenzene with 0.2 vol% 1-CN (8 mg mL−1 total) and spin-coated at 1400 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, MoO3 (∼5 nm) and Ag (∼80 nm) were thermally evaporated under high vacuum (∼1 × 10−5 Pa).

Conventional architecture (ITO/PEDOT: PSS/active layer/PDINN/Ag). A ∼ 30 nm PEDOT: PSS layer (Heraeus Clevios P VP AI 4083; 0.45 μm filtered) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s and baked at 150°C for 15 min. PTB7-Th: LTC1-X (X = Cl, Br) blends (1:1, w/w) were prepared in chlorobenzene with 3 vol% 1-CN and spin-coated at 1200 rpm for 30 s in a nitrogen glovebox, followed by thermal annealing at 80°C for 5 min to yield ∼130 ± 5 nm active layers. A PDINN layer (1 mg mL−1 in methanol) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s. Finally, Ag (∼80 nm) was thermally evaporated under high vacuum (∼1 × 10−5 Pa).

EQE spectra and R were measured using a solar-cell spectral-response system (QE-R3011, Enli Technology Co., Ltd.). The in was recorded using an SR570 current preamplifier connected to an Agilent 35670A FFT analyzer. The reverse bias was applied using the Bias Voltage function of the SR570, and the voltage magnitude was monitored using a multimeter. Current density-voltage characteristics under AM 1.5G (100 mW cm−2) were obtained with an AAA-grade solar simulator (XES-70S1, SAN-EI Electric Co., Ltd.), calibrated by a KG5-filtered silicon reference cell (National Institute of Metrology). The active device area for all OPDs was ∼0.04 cm2, verified by optical microscopy.

Active-matrix SWIR imaging

The a-Si TFT array substrate with patterned ITO pixel electrodes (LinkZill Technology Co., Ltd.) underwent sequential cleaning in deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol (10 min each), followed by ultraviolet-ozone treatment for 20 min. As the purchased readout systems are limited to collecting holes, the hole transport layer of OPDs needed to be deposited directly on the patterned ITO pixel electrodes of the TFT array substrate. Therefore, OPDs with the conventional structure of ITO/PEDOT: PSS/PTB7-Th: LTC1-Cl/PDINN/Ag were employed to integrate with TFT array under the same preparation conditions as those described above for the OPD devices. Finally, device encapsulation employed UV-curable adhesive and a piece of glass to ensure environmental stability.

The SWIR imaging system consisted of a light source with four 1.4 μm LEDs (the light intensity varied slightly across different positions of the sample), imaging targets (waste textile samples or oil-water mixtures), the OPD-integrated TFT array, a commercial matrix readout system (Zill Box, LinkZill) with a built-in flip option for output images, and a mobile terminal for data visualization. Electrical interfacing between the TFT array and readout circuitry was achieved through flexible printed cables. During operation, row-wise scanning applied a 10 V gate voltage for pixel activation. Pixel currents were digitized through 8-bit analog-to-digital conversion and transmitted row-sequentially to the mobile interface in both raw data and reconstructed image modes via wireless protocols. The observed slight inhomogeneity of grayscale across the array mainly stemmed from subtle differences in the light intensity received by the pixel points at different positions and spatial variations in the thickness of the spin-coated active layer. A small fraction of pixels exhibited malfunction (“dead pixels”) in the acquired images, primarily located near the edges of the array. These dead pixels arose from contact imperfections during the wiring/interconnection process, rather than from intrinsic defects of the photoactive material, and they were excluded from analysis. For quantitative evaluation of the pixel current in the imaging results, the mean value within each region was used as the representative signal, while the standard deviation was taken as the uncertainty.

Oil spill detection

Commercial No. 0 diesel obtained from a local gas station and seawater collected from the Bohai Sea were used to simulate marine oil-spill conditions. The oil fraction ϕoil was defined on a volumetric basis according toϕoil=VoilVoil+Vwater(4)where Voil and Vwater are the volumes of diesel oil and seawater, respectively. The total solution volume was fixed at 10 mL. To emulate emulsification in real seawater, 100 ppm sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added to the oil–water mixtures, followed by mechanical stirring at 1000 rpm for 1 h to produce stable emulsions before measurement. An active-matrix sensor array based on SWIR OPDs was used to record the photocurrent under 1.4 μm LED illumination. The net photocurrent was defined asJP=Jon−Jdark(5)where Jon and Jdark are the currents measured under illumination and in the dark, respectively. The photocurrent–concentration relationship was modeled using Eq. 2, and linear fitting was performed by plotting lnJP versus ϕoil. Baseline deviation was quantified by comparing the extrapolated zero-content photocurrent J0 with the measured blank Jblank according toDeviation(%)=∣J0−Jblank∣Jblank×100%(6)

The SNR was defined relative to the water baseline asSNR=JPoil−JPwaterσ(JPwater)(7)where JPoil and JPwater denote the mean net photocurrents of oil and water, respectively, and σ(JPwater) is the standard deviation of the water photocurrent. Reliable detection was judged according to the Rose criterion (SNR ≥ 3). Droplet-size tests were performed with diameters in the millimeter range to evaluate the spatial resolution limit. Note: In the experiments for detecting oil droplets of different sizes, the petri dish was suspended above the detector using an iron ring wrapped with aluminum foil as a supporting frame, as shown in Fig. 4D. This setup avoided direct mechanical pressure on the detector during long-term measurements, thereby ensuring device stability.

Clustering analysis

Average pixel currents obtained from OPD-based images were clustered using the K-means algorithm (k = 2, Euclidean distance). Each data point was assigned to the nearest cluster centroid, and the classification threshold was defined as the midpoint between the two centroids. For this dataset, the centroids were 1.1821 × 10−7 A and 1.3753 × 10−7 A, giving a threshold ofT=μL+μH2=1.2787×10−7 A(≈127.9 nA)(8)

The quality of clustering was evaluated using the silhouette coefficient, calculated ass=b−amax(a,b)(9)where a is the mean intra-cluster distance of a sample and b is the mean nearest-cluster distance. The silhouette coefficient ranges from −1 to 1. Values close to 1 indicate well-separated and compact clusters. Values near 0 indicate overlapping clusters or little structure. To assess the robustness of the classification against measurement uncertainty, a SNR was introduced and defined for each sample asri=∣xi−T∣ei(10)where xi is the sample value, ei is the associated error bar (standard deviation), and T is the classification threshold. The SNR across all samples was 7.3, quantifying the worst-case margin of classification confidence.
