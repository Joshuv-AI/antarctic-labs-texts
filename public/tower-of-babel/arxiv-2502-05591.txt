Round and Resilience-Optimal Approximate Agreement on Trees and Block Graphs
Marc Fuchs, Diana Ghinea, Zahra Parsaeian, Joel Rybicki

Abstract
Approximate Agreement ( 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} ) is a fundamental primitive that, even in the presence of Byzantine faults, allows honest parties to obtain close (but not necessarily identical) outputs that lie within the range of their inputs. While the optimal round complexity of synchronous 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} on real values is well understood, its extension to other input spaces has remained open, with fundamental questions regarding achievable resilience and round efficiency still unresolved. In this work, we investigate the optimal round complexity of synchronous 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} on trees under Byzantine failures. In this setting, parties hold as inputs vertices of a publicly known labeled tree T T and must output 1 1 -close vertices lying in the convex hull of the honest inputs. We present a synchronous protocol with round complexity O ⁡ ( log ⁡ D ​ ( T ) log ⁡ log ​ D ​ ( T ) ) O\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(T)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(T)}\right) , where D ​ ( T ) \textsf{D}(T) denotes the diameter of the input space tree. Our protocol is obtained via a reduction to real-valued 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} , and maintains the resilience guarantees of the underlying real-valued 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} protocol: t = c ⋅ n t=c\cdot n , for any constant c < 1 / 3 c<1/3 in settings with no cryptographic assumptions, and for any constant c ∈ [ 1 / 3 , 1 / 2 ) c\in[1/3,1/2) assuming digital signatures. Complementing this result, we extend impossibility results for real-valued 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} to any graph G G by proving a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ D ​ ( G ) log ⁡ log ​ D ​ ( G ) + log ⁡ n + t t ) \Omega\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(G)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(G)+\log\frac{n+t}{t}}\right) rounds, where n n is the number of parties and t t the number of Byzantine faults. Together, these results establish the asymptotic optimality of our protocol whenever t ∈ Θ ⁡ ( n ) t\in\Theta(n) . We further extend our techniques to block graphs by leveraging their clique tree structure. This yields protocols for 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} on block graphs with optimal resilience in both the synchronous and asynchronous models; in the synchronous setting, the resulting protocols achieve asymptotically optimal round complexity. In doing so, we make progress on a broader open problem: beyond trees, the optimal resilience thresholds for 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} on graph input spaces were largely unresolved, and our results pin down these thresholds for the class of block graphs. For the exact-agreement variant of the problem, the resilience threshold has been shown to be t < n / ω t<n/\omega on any graph with clique number ω \omega , where t t denotes the number of Byzantine faults. Our work shows that a similar lower bound does not hold in general for 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} . Finally, in contrast to prior protocols for 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} beyond the real line, which typically rely on safe-area constructions and can incur exponential local computation, our approach in the synchronous model reduces the problem to real-valued 𝒜 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{AA} and therefore admits efficient local computation.

Introduction

Ensuring consistency among parties in a distributed system is essential, yet it is a difficult task in the face of potential failures or malicious behavior. Agreement protocols serve as indispensable tools for achieving consensus in such environments. One such fundamental primitive is Approximate Agreement (
\mathcal{AA}
)
[
14
]
. The Byzantine variant of this problem considers a setting of
n
parties in a network, where each party holds a real value as input. Even when
t
out of the
n
parties involved are Byzantine (i.e., malicious),
\mathcal{AA}
enables the honest parties to obtain
\varepsilon
-close
values (for any predefined error
\varepsilon>0
) that lie within the range spanned by the honest inputs. This relaxed form of agreement has proven useful in scenarios where exact consensus is either unnecessary or infeasible, such as clock synchronization
[
30
]
, blockchain oracles
[
6
]
, distributed machine learning
[
40
,
18
,
17
]
, aviation control systems
[
39
,
32
]
, and robot gathering
[
38
]
.

The
\mathcal{AA}
problem is not limited to real-valued inputs. Several variants have been explored, including multidimensional real inputs
[
41
,
34
,
33
]
and discrete domains such as various classes of (connected) graphs
[
37
,
13
,
31
,
2
,
5
]
. These generalizations naturally extend the original requirements: the honest outputs still need to be close and they must be in the
convex hull
of the honest inputs (defined according to the convexity notion associated with the input space). Intuitively, this means that honest outputs must lie “inside the region” spanned by the honest
inputs; formal definitions will be provided later.

Regardless of the input space considered, the
\mathcal{AA}
problem admits solutions that follow a common iteration-based outline.
In each iteration, the parties use a mechanism to distribute their current values to all parties.
Based on the values received, each party computes a
safe area
: this is a set guaranteed to lie within the convex hull of the values distributed by the honest parties. The honest parties’ safe areas are not necessarily identical, but they have some overlap. Each party then computes its new value essentially as a midpoint of the safe area it has obtained. The parties’ new values remain within the honest inputs’ convex hull, and they become closer with each iteration. This way, after a sufficient number of iterations,
\mathcal{AA}
is achieved.

While this outline has been a powerful tool in establishing sufficient conditions for various input spaces, multiple questions remain open: both in terms of resilience and efficiency (e.g., rounds, communication, local computation). Except for the communication complexity of short inputs,
\mathcal{AA}
on real values is well understood in the synchronous model
[
28
,
20
,
19
,
7
,
27
,
36
]
. Moreover, while optimal resilience thresholds are known for spaces such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
33
]
and trees
[
37
,
13
]
, a unified understanding of optimal efficiency tradeoffs, as well as resilience in more general graph classes, remains elusive.

This paper makes a step forward by exploring optimal round complexity for
\mathcal{AA}
under Byzantine faults beyond the real line, namely on trees, in the synchronous model. In this variant
[
37
]
, the input space is a publicly-known labeled tree
T
.
Each party holds a vertex of
T
as input, and every honest party must output a vertex of
T
such that the following conditions hold:
(i) honest outputs must be within distance one of each other, and (ii) honest outputs must lie in the honest inputs’ convex hull (in the smallest subtree of
T
containing all honest input vertices).

1.1
Our Contribution

Results for trees.

We establish tight asymptotic round-complexity bounds for synchronous
\mathcal{AA}
on trees under Byzantine faults.
We first show that

\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(G)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(G)+\log\frac{n+t}{t}}\right)
rounds are necessary for
\mathcal{AA}
on any graph
G
, where
\textsf{D}(G)
denotes the diameter of
G
. This result is obtained by adapting Fekete’s bound
[
20
]
from
\mathcal{AA}
on real numbers. Afterwards, we show that this lower bound is tight for
\mathcal{AA}
on a tree
whenever
t\in\Theta(n)
by reducing the problem to real-valued
\mathcal{AA}

\mathcal{AA}
on any tree

O\!\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(T)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(T)}\right)
by using a round-optimal real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
protocol as a building block. At the same time, our protocol achieves optimal resilience: depending on the real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
protocol used, our protocol is resilient up to
t<n/3
Byzantine parties without cryptographic assumptions, and up to
t=c\cdot n
Byzantine parties, for any constant
c\in[1/3,1/2)
, given a public-key infrastructure and a secure digital signature scheme.

Extension beyond trees: block graphs.

We show that our techniques extend also to a more general class of graphs, namely block graphs. Block graphs admit a clique tree in which adjacent cliques intersect in exactly one vertex, which enables us to reduce
\mathcal{AA}
on any connected block graph

to
\mathcal{AA}
on a tree
T

t<n
crashes) with weaker validity conditions
[
2
]
.
Using our protocol for trees, we get a synchronous protocol
with asymptotically optimal round complexity
O\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}{(G)}}{\log\log\textsf{D}(G)}\right)
whenever
t\in\Theta(n)
.
The resilience guarantees are inherited from the underlying real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
protocol:
t<n/3

Byzantine parties, where

In comparison to prior work, we observe the following:

•

Our results improve over previously known sufficient conditions for achieving
\mathcal{AA}
on block graphs of maximum clique size
\omega

t<n/\omega
in the synchronous model, and
t<n/(\omega+1)
in the asynchronous model
[
37
,
13
]
. These conditions were only known to be tight for the exact-agreement variant of the problem, known as Convex Agreement (
\mathcal{CA}
). As
\mathcal{CA}
and
\mathcal{AA}
have matching optimal resilience thresholds for
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d}
and trees, it may be natural to assume that the optimal resilience thresholds match for other input spaces as well. Our result for block graphs shows that this is not the case, and leaves the following question open: characterizing the boundary at which
\mathcal{AA}
and
\mathcal{CA}

•

Previous protocols achieving
\mathcal{AA}
on spaces beyond the real line involve high local computation complexity due to the safe-area approach, which requires each party to intersect up to
\binom{n}{n-t}
convex sets in each round

,
41
,
33
,

,
13

. Our synchronous protocol for block graphs avoids this overhead – without relaxing the requirements on the honest outputs, by reducing the problem to real-valued
\mathcal{AA}

n
real values, discarding the outliers (i.e., the lowest
t
and the highest

Real values.

\mathcal{AA}

The problem of approximately agreeing on vertices in a graph was first considered by Alcántara et al.

Preliminaries

We describe the model and key concepts and definitions.

Model and Adversary.

We consider
n
parties
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}
running a protocol in a fully-connected network where links model authenticated channels. We assume that the network is synchronous: the parties’ clocks are synchronized and every message is delivered within a publicly known amount of time
\Delta
.
Our protocols assume a (computationally unbounded) adversary that can permanently corrupt up to
t<n/3
parties. The adversary is adaptive – it may choose which parties to corrupt at any point in the protocol’s execution. Corrupted parties become Byzantine: they may deviate arbitrarily (maliciously) from the protocol. Our lower bound, however, holds even against a static adversary that has to choose which parties to corrupt at the beginning of the protocol’s execution.

Approximate Agreement on
\mathbb{R}
.

We recall the definition of
\mathcal{AA}
on real values, as presented in
[
14
]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\Pi(\varepsilon)
be an
n
-party protocol in which every party holds a value in
\mathbb{R}
as input and
\varepsilon
is a publicly known parameter.
We say that
\Pi(\varepsilon)
achieves
\mathcal{AA}
if the following properties hold for any predefined
\varepsilon>0
even when up to
t
of the
n
parties are corrupted:

(Termination)
Every honest party produces an output in
\mathbb{R}
and terminates;
(Validity)
Honest parties’ outputs lie within the range of the honest inputs;
(
\varepsilon
-Agreement)
If two honest parties output
v
and
v^{\prime}
, then
\big\lvert v-v^{\prime}\big\rvert\leq\varepsilon
.

We use the term
valid value
to refer to a value within the range of the honest inputs.

Notations for graphs.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, we use
\textsf{V}(G)
to denote its set of vertices. We denote the shortest path between two vertices
u,v\in\textsf{V}(G)
by
P(u,v)
: as we are concerned with trees and block graphs, these are unique.
We define the distance
\textsf{d}(u,v)
as the length of
P(u,v)
.
We use
\textsf{D}(G)
to denote the diameter of
G
, i.e., the maximum distance between any two vertices in
\textsf{V}(G)
.
The
(shortest path) convex hull

\langle{S}\rangle
of a set of vertices

is the union of all shortest paths between any pair of vertices in
S
. Note that for trees, this is equivalent to the set of vertices in the smallest connected subtree containing
S
.

\mathcal{AA}
on a graph
G
.

We now define the problem of
\mathcal{AA}
on graph
G

[
37
]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
G

\Pi(G)
be an
n

G

We say that
\Pi(G)

(Termination)

As in the case of real values, we refer to a vertex in the honest inputs’ convex hull as a

Round complexity.

In a synchronous communication round, every party may send messages to the other parties, receive all messages sent to it during that round, and perform local computations. The round complexity of a protocol is the maximum number of such rounds until every honest party terminates, over all admissible executions.

3
Lower Bound

In this section, we analyze the round complexity required to achieve
\mathcal{AA}
on a graph. We begin by revisiting Fekete’s lower bound for real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
regarding
how close the honest values may get
after a fixed number of rounds. Fekete
[
20
]
used chain arguments to prove lower bounds for the convergence rate of real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
protocols. However, upon closer inspection, his proof only relies on a weaker validity condition than
Definition

1
called
Strong Unanimity
: if all honest parties hold input
v
, no honest party outputs
v^{\prime}\neq v
.

Fekete constructed for the
R
-round full-information protocol a chain of

s\leq\frac{(n+t)^{R}}{t_{1}\cdot t_{2}\cdot\cdots\cdot t_{R}}
views,
where
\sum_{i=1}^{R}t_{i}\leq t
, and in the first view, all honest parties start with input
a
, and in the last view, all honest parties start with input
b
. For these views,
t_{i}
roughly represents the number of Byzantine parties that deviate from the protocol for the first time in round
i
. For any pair of views that are consecutive in the chain, there exists an execution of the full-information protocol in which two honest parties obtain those views.

For any protocol satisfying strong unanimity, the first view leads to all honest parties outputting
a
and the last view leads to all honest parties outputting
b
. In the case of real values, this implies the existence of two consecutive views in the chain that yield two honest outputs
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\big\lvert v-v^{\prime}\big\rvert\geq\big\lvert a-b\big\rvert/s
. However, the proof works as-is on any set
V
of values with a distance metric
d
.
Following the exact same steps as in Fekete’s proof
[
20
, Theorem 15]
,
we get the next lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Pi
be any deterministic
R
-round protocol with inputs and outputs from the set
V
that satisfies strong unanimity even when up to
t
of the
n
parties involved are Byzantine.
Then for any
a,b\in V
, there exists an execution of
\Pi
in which two honest parties output
v
and
v^{\prime}
such that
\textsf{d}(v,v^{\prime})\geq\textsf{d}(a,b)\cdot\left(\frac{t}{R(n+t)}\right)^{R}.

The lower bound is an immediate corollary of the above lemma. By Fekete’s results
[
20
, Theorem 18]
, the same asymptotic lower bound holds for crash faults by replacing
t/(n+t)
with
t/(2n+3t)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n>t>0
be fixed. Any deterministic
n
-party protocol that achieves
\mathcal{AA}
on a graph
G
even when up to
t
of the
n
parties are Byzantine requires
\Omega\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(G)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(G)+\log\frac{n+t}{t}}\right)
rounds to terminate.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=(n+t)/t
.
Choose
a,b\in V
such that
\textsf{d}(a,b)=\textsf{D}(G)
.
Then by 1-Agreement and
Lemma

1
, any honest parties’ output values
v
and
v^{\prime}
satisfy

1\geq\textsf{d}(v,v^{\prime})\geq\textsf{D}(G)\cdot\left(\frac{t}{R(n+t)}\right)^{R}=\textsf{D}(G)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{R\delta}\right)^{R}.

By rearranging the above, the claim now follows for any fixed
\delta
, as

\displaystyle\left(R\delta\right)^{R}\geq\textsf{D}(G)

\displaystyle\iff R\log(R\delta)\geq\log\textsf{D}(G)

\displaystyle\iff R\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(G)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(G)+\log\delta}\right).

∎

4.3
Weakening the Path Assumption: Initial Attempt

In Section
4.2
, we have discussed a round-optimal approach for
\mathcal{AA}
on trees given that the honest parties
know
a path in
T
that intersects the honest inputs’ convex hull. This section makes a first step towards removing this assumption, leading to a solution with round complexity
O\left(\frac{\log\big\lvert\textsf{V}(T)\big\rvert}{\log\log\big\lvert\textsf{V}(T)\big\rvert}\right)
. This is the protocol we have described in
[
22
]
, and represents a stepping stone towards our final solution for trees, presented in
Section

4.4
.

Intuitively, it may seem that finding a path

that passes through the honest inputs’ convex hull comes down to solving
Byzantine Agreement
. This would require
t+1=O(n)
communication rounds
[
15
]
, which generally prevents us from achieving our round complexity goal.
Instead, we implement a subprotocol
\textsf{PathsFinder}^{0}
that enables the honest parties to
approximately
agree on such a path. Concretely, each honest party obtains a subpath
P
of
T
such that:
(i)
P
intersects the convex hull of the honest inputs; and
(ii) if two honest parties obtain different paths
P
and
P^{\prime}
, then either
P
extends
P^{\prime}
by one edge, or
P^{\prime}
extends
P
by one edge.
Formally, if
P=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
and
P^{\prime}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k^{\prime}})
, then either
P=P^{\prime}
, or
P^{\prime}=P\oplus(v_{k},u_{k^{\prime}})
, or
P=P^{\prime}\oplus(u_{k^{\prime}},v_{k})
.
In Section
4.3.2
, we show that this suffices to apply the approach from Section
4.2
.

4.5
Finding These Paths

We now present our protocol
\textsf{PathsFinder}(T)
, providing the parties with paths satisfying the preconditions of
TreeAAFromPaths
(described in
Lemma

6
) within
O(1)
rounds.

Each party joins
\textsf{PathsFinder}(T)
with its input vertex
v_{\textsc{in}}
. Then, we fix some vertex
v_{\textsf{start}}
(e.g., the vertex in
T
with the lowest label in lexicographic order) and each party communicates the path from
v_{\textsf{start}}
to its input vertex
P(v_{\textsf{start}},v_{\textsc{in}})
. Each party will try to define its path
P
roughly as the
longest common prefix
of the paths
P(v_{\textsf{start}},v_{\textsc{in}})
it has received: note that the honest parties might not have an identical view over the paths
P(v_{\textsf{start}},v_{\textsc{in}})
due to the Byzantine parties. Then, each party will define its path
Q
roughly as what it
believes
other honest parties might have identified as a longest common prefix.
We formalize this idea with the help of a mechanism called Gradecast (
\mathcal{GC}
), which ensures that parties distribute their paths in a consistent manner, i.e., Byzantine parties are unable to send different messages to different parties, but they can still cause inconsistencies by making sure that only a subset of the honest parties receive their messages. We note that
\mathcal{GC}
delivers each message with a grade in
\{0,1,2\}
. Honest parties’ messages are always delivered with grade
2
. In addition, if two parties receive messages with grades
g
and
g^{\prime}
from a party
p
, then
\big\lvert g-g^{\prime}\big\rvert\leq 1
. Moreover, if both
g
and
g^{\prime}
are greater than zero, then the two parties have received the same message from
p
.
We state the formal definition of
\mathcal{GC}
below, as presented in
[
7
]
.

Definition 4
.

(Gradecast)
Let
\Pi
be a protocol where a designated party
S
(the sender) holds a value
v_{S}
. We say that
\Pi
achieves
\mathcal{GC}
if the following properties hold even when
t
out of the
n
parties involved are Byzantine:
(Termination)
Every honest party eventually outputs a pair
(v,g)
, where
v
is a message and
g\in\{0,1,2\}
;
(Integrity)
If
S
is honest, then every honest party outputs
(v_{S},2)
;
(Consistency)
If two honest parties output
(v,g)
and
(v^{\prime},g^{\prime})
then
\big\lvert g-g^{\prime}\big\rvert\leq 1
. In addition, if
g,g^{\prime}>0
, then
v=v^{\prime}
.

We then make use of the
\mathcal{GC}
protocol of
[
7
]
(presented in the full version
[
8
]
), described below.

Theorem 4

(Theorem 1 of
[
8
]
)

There is a protocol

\mathcal{GC}
even when up to
t<n/3
of the
n
parties involved are Byzantine, with round complexity

.

Hence, each party will distribute its path

via

. Every party obtains at least
n-t
paths with grade
2
(from honest parties), plus up to
t
paths with various grades. Then, each party defines its path

as a path prefix

by

parties with grade
2
, as defined below.

We say that a path

Extension to Block Graphs

In this section, we extend our algorithm from trees to connected block graphs. Our approach utilizes the clique tree representation to reduce the problem on a block graph to a problem on a tree, enabling us to achieve optimal round complexity for block graphs

Block Graphs.

A graph
G=(V,E)
is a

if every maximal
2
-connected subgraph (block) of
G
is a clique. Equivalently,

is a chordal graph in which every two maximal cliques intersect in at most one vertex.

Clique trees.

For any chordal graph

, hence also for any block graph

, its maximal cliques can be arranged as the vertices of a tree

, called the

[
10
]
, as shown in

\textsf{V}_{G}(c)
to denote the set of vertices in the clique vertex

. If clique vertices

are adjacent in

\textsf{V}_{G}(c)\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c^{\prime})\neq\varnothing

represent a subset of the non-empty intersection relation between maximal cliques in

, selected such that the

holds: for any two maximal cliques

, the intersection
\textsf{V}_{G}(c)\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c^{\prime})
is contained in every clique vertex on the unique path connecting them in
T
. Such a clique tree of minimum diameter can be computed deterministically and efficiently in
O(|V|+|E|)
time for any chordal graph
[
11
]
.

The following lemma states an outcome of the running intersection property on block graphs: every vertex in the intersection of at least two maximal cliques of

is a cut vertex in graph

Subdivided Clique Tree.

Lemma 13

Lemma 14

By the

\textsf{d}_{T_{S}}(c_{\textsc{out}},c^{\prime}_{\textsc{out}})\leq 1.

Case 1:

The lemma below will enable us to prove the round complexity of
\textsf{BlockGraphAA}(G)
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
T
be a minimum-diameter clique tree of a block graph
G
. Then
\textsf{D}(T)\leq 2\cdot\textsf{D}(G)
.

Proof.

If
G
consists of a single maximal clique, then its clique tree
T
consists of a single vertex, and hence
\textsf{D}(T)=0\leq 2\cdot\textsf{D}(G)
. If
G
contains exactly two maximal cliques, then
T
consists of a single edge and thus
\textsf{D}(T)=1\leq 2\cdot\textsf{D}(G)
. For the remainder of the proof, we assume that
G
contains at least three maximal cliques. In particular,
\textsf{D}(G)\geq 2
.

Let
P:=(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
be a longest path in
T
, so that
k=\textsf{D}(T)
. If
k\leq 2
, then
\textsf{D}(T)\leq 2\leq 2\cdot\textsf{D}(G)
, and the claim follows. Hence, we assume
k\geq 3
.

For every
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, since
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}
are adjacent in
T
, we have
\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})\neq\varnothing
. As
G
is a block graph, any two distinct maximal cliques intersect in at most one vertex. Therefore, for each
i
, there exists a unique vertex
v_{i}\in\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})
. We then define the vertex sequence
S:=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1})
.
We claim that none of the following cases can occur in the sequence
S
:

1.

There exists an index
i
such that
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\notin E(G)
and
\{v_{i+1},v_{i+2}\}\notin E(G)
.

2.

There exist indices
0<i+1<j\leq k-1
such that
v_{i}=v_{j}
.

We first rule out case (1).
Since both
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
belong to the clique
c_{i+1}
, that is,
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\subseteq\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})
, we have
v_{i}=v_{i+1}
. This is because, in a clique, every two distinct vertices are adjacent, and if
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\notin E(G)
, this implies that
v_{i}=v_{i+1}
. Similarly, since both
v_{i+1}
and
v_{i+2}
belong to the clique
c_{i+2}
, i.e.,
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\subseteq\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+2})
, we have
v_{i+1}=v_{i+2}
. As a result, we obtain
v=v_{i}=v_{i+1}=v_{i+2}
.

Consequently, the cliques
c_{i}
,
c_{i+1}
,
c_{i+2}
, and
c_{i+3}
all intersect in the same vertex
v
, forming a subpath of length three in
T
whose cliques share a common intersection
\{v\}
. More generally, let
c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{i+\ell}
with
\ell>2
be a maximal subpath of
T
such that
\cap_{r=i}^{r=i+\ell}\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{r})=\{v\}
. Since
k\geq 3
, such a subpath exists with
\ell>2
.
We then replace this subpath by a star centered at
c_{i}
, where
c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{i+\ell}
become adjacent to
c_{i}
, while preserving their original neighbors in
T
outside the set
\{c_{i+1},\ldots,c_{i+\ell}\}
. The running intersection property is preserved because all these cliques contain the common vertex
v
, and for any two cliques among
c_{i},\ldots,c_{i+\ell}
the unique clique on the path between them in the modified tree is
c_{i}
, which also contains
v
. Hence, every clique on the path between any two cliques containing
v
continues to contain
v
. This transformation
strictly decreases the diameter of
T
, contradicting the assumption that
T
is a minimum-diameter clique tree of
G
. Hence,
v_{i}\neq v_{i+1}
for all
i
for which
v_{i+1}=v_{i+2}
. Thus, case (1) cannot occur.
This implies that in the sequence
S
no three consecutive vertices can be identical.

We now rule out case (2). Suppose that there exist indices
i
and
j
with
i+1<j
such that
v_{i}=v_{j}=v
. Then
v\in\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})
and
v\in\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{j})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{j+1})
. The running intersection property of clique trees implies that
v
belongs to every clique on the path from
c_{i}
to
c_{j+1}
in
T
. Let us first consider the case where
v_{i}\neq v_{i+1}
. Since both
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
belong to
c_{i+1}
and
c_{i+2}
, we have that
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\subseteq\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+2})
.
This implies that
|\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+1})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+2})|\geq 2
, which contradicts the assumption that
G
is a block graph, as in a block graph any two distinct maximal cliques intersect in at most one vertex.

It remains to consider the case where
v_{i}=v_{i+1}
. If
v_{i+1}\neq v_{i+2}
, then we may follow the same argument as above: replacing
v_{i}
by
v_{i+1}
, we obtain that both
v_{i+1}
and
v_{i+2}
belong to
c_{i+2}
and
c_{i+3}
, and hence
\{v_{i+1},v_{i+2}\}\subseteq\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+2})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+3})
, which again implies that
|\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+2})\cap\textsf{V}_{G}(c_{i+3})|\geq 2
. This contradicts the assumption that
G
is a block graph. On the other hand, if
v_{i+1}=v_{i+2}
, then this is exactly case (1), which we have already shown cannot occur. Therefore, there exist no indices
0<i+1<j\leq k-1
such that
v_{i}=v_{j}
.

Let us construct a sequence
P_{G}
in
G
using
S
. For every
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, if
v_{i}\neq v_{i+1}
, we add
v_{i}
to
P_{G}
, and otherwise we skip it. Note that if
v=v_{i}=v_{i+1}
and we skip
v_{i}
, then by the conclusion of case (1)
v_{i+1}\neq v_{i+2}
, and therefore
v_{i+1}=v
will be added to
P_{G}
. We claim that
P_{G}
is a path in
G
. This is because for every index

Conclusions

In this work, we have investigated the optimal round complexity for achieving
\mathcal{AA}
on trees in the synchronous model.
Our results extend previous findings from real-valued domains to tree-structured input spaces, providing novel insights for achieving efficient
\mathcal{AA}
in discrete input spaces.
We presented a protocol with round complexity
O\left(\frac{\log\textsf{D}(T)}{\log\log\textsf{D}(T)}\right)
, improving upon the previously best-known
O(\log\textsf{D}(T))
-round protocol of
[
37
]
. Our construction achieves optimal resilience and relies on a reduction from
\mathcal{AA}
on trees to its real-valued counterpart.
Moreover, we have provided an asymptotically matching lower bound
by adapting the lower bound of
[
20
]
from real values to graphs, demonstrating that our protocol achieves asymptotically optimal round complexity whenever
t\in\Theta(n)
.

We further extend our techniques to block graphs by exploiting their clique tree structure. By reducing
\mathcal{AA}
on a block graph to
\mathcal{AA}
on a suitably constructed tree, we obtain efficient synchronous and asynchronous protocols with optimal resilience, with asymptotically optimal round complexity in the synchronous model whenever
t\in\Theta(n)
.

In contrast to prior work on
\mathcal{AA}
outside the real line, our protocols rely only on efficient local computation and avoid the exponential-time safe-area constructions.

Our results suggest several directions for further investigation. On the practical side, improving the constants in the round complexity (particularly those inherited from real-valued
\mathcal{AA}
) would directly improve the efficiency of our protocols for trees and block graphs. On the theoretical side, it remains open whether similarly optimal-resilience efficient protocols can be obtained for broader classes of graphs, and where the precise structural boundary lies between
\mathcal{AA}
and
\mathcal{CA}
in terms of resilience. More broadly, we view reduction-based techniques such as ours as a promising approach for efficient
\mathcal{AA}
across diverse input spaces.

Appendix E
Analysis of
ListConstruction

We present the proof of Lemma
3
, describing the guarantees of algorithm
ListConstruction
. The parties run this algorithm locally to convert the input tree
T
into a list representation that provides a few special properties.

3

Proof.

First, we prove that
\textsf{ListConstruction}(T,v_{\textsf{root}})
terminates in a finite amount of time. The algorithm follows a depth-first search (
dfs
) traversal, which has a time complexity of
O(|V|+|E|)
for a graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. Since
T
is a tree, it has
|E|=|V|-1
edges, leading to an overall complexity of
O(|V|)
. Thus, the algorithm terminates within a finite number of steps. Then, we prove each property in order.

Property 1.

This follows directly from the definition of
dfs
. At each step of the
dfs
traversal, if a vertex has an unvisited child, we move to that child. Otherwise, we backtrack to the parent. This ensures that each successive pair of vertices in
L
represents either a move to a child or a return to a parent, both of which are adjacent in
T
.

Property 2.

We first show that
\big\lvert L\big\rvert\leq 2\cdot\big\lvert\textsf{V}(T)\big\rvert
holds. We proceed by induction on the number of vertices
m:=|\textsf{V}(T)|
.

Base case:
If
T
consists of a single vertex, then
L=[v_{\textsf{root}}]
. Since
\big\lvert L\big\rvert=1\leq 2\cdot|\textsf{V}(T)|=2
, the claim holds.

Inductive step:
Suppose the claim holds for all trees with at most
m-1
vertices. Consider a tree
T
with
m
vertices. Let
T^{\prime}
be the tree obtained by removing a leaf
v
and its incident edge
(u,v)
.

By the induction hypothesis, the
dfs
traversal on
T^{\prime}
results in a list
L^{\prime}
of length at most
2(m-1)
. When we add
v
back, the
dfs
first visits
v
once and then backtracks to
u
, adding two elements to
L^{\prime}
. Therefore,
|L|=|L^{\prime}|+2\leq 2(m-1)+2=2m
, which completes the induction.

To show that every vertex appears in
L
, note that the
dfs
visits each vertex at least once upon first exploration, ensuring that every vertex is included.

Property 3.

During
dfs
, the first occurrence of
v
in
L
happens before visiting any of its descendants. The last occurrence happens after all descendants have been visited. Hence, the indices corresponding to
v
enclose all its descendants in
L
. If
u
is in the subtree of
v
, all its occurrences in
L
appear between
i_{\min}
and
i_{\max}
.
Conversely, if
L(u)\subseteq[i_{\min},i_{\max}]
, then
dfs
must have discovered
u
while visiting
v
’s subtree, confirming that
u
is in the subtree rooted at
v
.

Property 4.

If one of
v
or
v^{\prime}
is an ancestor of the other, then the lowest common ancestor (
lca
) of two nodes
v
and
v^{\prime}
is trivially in this range.

Otherwise, let
w:=\textsf{lca}(v,v^{\prime})
. If
w\notin{L_{k}:\min(i,i^{\prime})\leq k\leq\max(i,i^{\prime})}
, then

would have visited
v
’s subtree and then
v^{\prime}
’s subtree without encountering

must appear in the interval, completing the proof.
