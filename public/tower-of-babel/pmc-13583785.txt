Are study design characteristics in single-arm trials comparable with those of their objective performance criteria or performance goals? A scoping review
Authors: Handong Wang, Qianyun Chai, Fanhui Jin, Yuting Feng, Rui Cao, Zhihan Liu, Xiang Li, Minjing Luo, Liyuan Tao, Yutong Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSingle-arm trials (SATs) with objective performance criteria (OPCs) or performance goals (PGs) are increasingly used for regulatory approval of medical devices and other interventions. However, the comparability of study design characteristics between SAT and their external comparator sources remains unclear. This scoping review aimed to evaluate the comparability of study design characteristics between SATs and their matched OPC/PG sources.DesignScoping review.Data sourcesPubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library and four Chinese databases—China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, CQVIP and SinoMed—were searched from inception to 30 April 2026.Eligibility criteriaWe included SATs that used one or more OPCs or PGs as external comparators to evaluate safety and/or effectiveness endpoints and reported specific numerical values of OPCs or PGs.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened the retrieved records and extracted data using a standardised form. For each included SAT, we retrieved the cited OPC/PG sources and extracted study design characteristics data (age, sex, health conditions, outcome definitions and measurement time points) for comparison. Age and sex were compared using summary t-tests and χ² tests; health conditions were assessed by two clinicians based on eligibility criteria and baseline characteristics. Results were stratified by OPC versus PG.ResultsA total of 1243 records were identified, and 133 SATs were included. Most studies used PGs (84, 63.2%); 34 (25.6%) claimed to use OPCs, and 15 (11.3%) could not be classified. Of the 60 age comparisons available from 41 studies, 30 showed statistically significant differences; of the 82 sex comparisons available from 59 studies, 60 showed significant differences. Health conditions, outcome definitions and time points were assessed descriptively in a subset of studies, and discrepancies were also observed.ConclusionSuboptimal comparability of study design characteristics was observed between SATs and their OPC/PG sources, which might influence the treatment effect estimates. Greater attention to the comparability of study design characteristics in SATs with OPC/PGs may improve the validity of evidence.

Introduction

Single-arm trial (SAT) with objective performance criterion (OPC) or performance goal (PG) has become a common approach for evaluating the effectiveness and safety of clinical interventions. In 2013, the US FDA (Food and Drug Administration) published a guidance on approvals of medical devices, which classified SAT with OPC/PG as one of the medical device pivotal studies for pre-market approval applications.1 As real-world studies continue to develop,2 3 the scope of applications for SAT with OPC/PG has been expanded to drugs and other interventions, especially when it is not ethical or practical to establish a control group.

OPC is a numerical value derived from substantial clinical data and could be treated as an external comparator in SAT. It requires a mature territory, sufficient patient-level data from multiple sources and appropriate statistical analysis methods to develop an OPC and needs to be updated over time.4 Since it is challenging to develop an OPC, there are very few validated OPCs available. PG could also be used as an external comparator in SAT, but PG provides an inferior level of evidence to OPC. PGs are typically developed in less mature fields with data based on a few other published literatures but also need to be updated over time. For the territory that has not developed a confirmed OPC, investigators tend to choose PG as the comparator in their SAT since it is easier to develop.

The selection of OPC/PG is a critical aspect in the design of SAT. The baseline clinical characteristics are comparable between different groups in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) since those groups are drawn from the same source, randomly allocated and follow an identical study design. However, those internal controls are absent in SATs.5 The validity of the result strengthens if study design characteristics are similar between the single-arm group and their matched OPC/PG.6 7 If the prognostic characteristics of the population from which the OPC/PG is derived are more likely to lead to a worse treatment effect than that of the single-arm, such OPC/PG as external control raises the risk of a false positive error of the study result, vice versa.8 Although the critical importance of comparability has been emphasised, no well-acknowledged and accepted method to develop OPC/PG has been identified,9 and the extent to which comparability is actually assessed and reported in published SATs remains unclear. A comprehensive review of the current practice is therefore needed.

Given the scattered and heterogeneous evidence, a scoping review was conducted to summarise the characteristics of OPCs/PGs in published SATs (Population) and to evaluate the comparability of study design characteristics between the single-arm group and the external data source (Concept) across all disease areas and intervention types (Context).

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in seven electronic databases from their inception to 30 April 2026. The initial search was performed on 30 April 2024, and an updated search was carried out on 30 April 2026. The databases searched were PubMed (via National Center for Biotechnology Information), Embase (via Embase.com), the Cochrane Library (via Wiley) and four Chinese databases—China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, China Science and Technology Journal Database (CQVIP) and Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed) (all accessed via their respective official platforms).

The search was structured around two main concepts: (1) ‘single-arm’ trial design and (2) ‘objective performance criteria’ or ‘performance goal’. The search was limited to articles published in English or Chinese. No other restrictions or filters were applied during the search process. The full search strategies for all databases used are provided in online supplemental file S1.

The reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify primary source articles from which the OPCs/PGs were derived (if such sources were reported in the included studies). These primary articles, when identified, were retrieved for subsequent data charting and analysis.

Selection process

All retrieved records were imported into NoteExpress for deduplication. Four researchers were separated into two groups (Wang and Chai, Cao and Liu) and independently conducted a two-stage screening: first by titles and abstracts, then by full texts for those not excluded. Any disagreements about selection were resolved by consultation with a third researcher (Fei).

Data charting

Six researchers (Chai, Jin, Feng, Cao, Liu and Luo) independently charted data from the included studies using a standardised form for consistency; then, all charted data were verified by another two researchers (Wang and Fei) to ensure accuracy.

Following information was charted: title, author, year of publication, age and sex of the participants, disease category, diagnostic and eligible criteria, comorbidities, type of intervention, outcomes related to OPC/PG (definition, time point, result) and detail of OPC/PG (classification, amount, value, method of development and adjustment). In cases where multiple OPC/PGs were involved in a single study, the first three OPC/PGs were selected. A previous study9 found that most SATs used three or fewer OPC/PGs, and selecting the first three avoids potential distortion caused by a small number of studies reporting a large number of OPC/PGs. This approach may omit some secondary endpoints but was considered adequate for describing the overall landscape. The full analysis set was chosen if there were multiple analysis sets.

For studies that reported accessible sources for developing the OPCs/PGs, the reference lists were checked to identify the primary articles and corresponding data—including age, sex, health conditions, intervention and outcomes—were charted. For OPCs/PGs pooled from multiple sources, the three studies with the highest weights were selected for age and sex comparison; if weights were not reported, the three most recently published studies were selected. For health conditions, a single study with the highest weight, or if not available, the most recent publication, was selected for descriptive purposes only, as heterogeneity in disease definitions and reporting standards precluded formal comparison.

Data that were not reported in the included studies or their OPC/PG source documents were recorded as ‘not reported’ without imputation or author contact, because incomplete reporting was considered a finding of interest in this scoping review.

Data analysis

All included studies were first described in terms of their general characteristics. The types, numerical values and sources of OPC/PGs were summarised separately. Results were stratified by OPC versus PG.

Clinical characteristics of the SATs were compared with their related OPC/PG source groups, where such data were available. Age, presented as mean±SD, was compared using summary t-test. Sex, presented as the proportion of males, was compared using the χ² test. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Because the included studies covered heterogeneous diseases and interventions, no unified quantitative standard could be applied to baseline health condition. Two clinicians (Jin and Li) independently reviewed the eligibility criteria and baseline characteristics of each study and made a qualitative judgement as to whether the SAT was similar with the related OPC/PG source. Given the subjective nature of this assessment, health condition comparisons are presented descriptively and interpreted with caution.

In addition to demographic and health condition comparisons, the outcome definitions and measurement time points used in the SATs were descriptively compared with those in the OPC or PG source documents. It was assessed whether the definition of the primary outcome was consistent between the SAT and the OPC/PG source and whether the timing of outcome assessment matched. Any discrepancies were recorded and summarised.

All statistical analyses were conducted with R 4.4.2.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

The initial search, conducted on 30 April 2024, identified 940 records from seven databases, and 97 studies met the eligibility criteria. An updated search was performed on 30 April 2026 to capture recently published studies, yielding 303 records, and 36 studies were included after screening. Details of objective performance criterion/performance goals

Of the 133 included studies, 84 (63.2%) studies used PGs, and 34 (25.6%) studies claimed that they used OPCs. Due to translation, 15 (11.3%) studies could not be classified as either PG or OPC.

There were 102 (76.7%) studies that used a single OPC/PG for the evaluation of their outcomes, while the rest used multiple OPC/PGs for different outcomes. Totally 169 outcomes in 133 SATs were compared with OPC/PGs, 153 of them were categorical variables and 16 were continuous variables. In addition, 43 (32.3%) articles established target values, which are the expectations of the interventions’ efficacy.

Only 93 studies provided the sources of OPC/PGs through references or supplementary appendices, while the remaining studies presented numerical values that were unable to be traced. Almost all the studies (89/93) made adjustments while developing their OPC/PGs. However, only 41 studies provided methods to develop or adjust their OPC/PGs and explained the reasons, while others just gave a numeric value different from the primary sources with no explanation.

Comparability of health conditions

Almost all the disease categories were similar between SATs and the sources that developed OPC/PGs. However, studies differed in comorbidities, subtypes or stages, such as different location of lesion in coronary artery disease,10 ischaemic stroke in recovery phase versus acute ischaemic stroke11 12 or coronary artery disease with diabetes vs coronary artery disease.13 Some SATs use different diagnostic criteria from their OPC/PG sources due to different time periods.14 Besides, most of the interventions were medical devices, and some studies focused on device application scenarios and parameters rather than a single disease diagnosis.10

Not all SATs reported eligibility criteria and health conditions in detail, and some of the OPC/PGs sources were not clinical articles or could not provide available data. Only 57 SATs could be compared for health conditions with their OPC/PGs sources. Based on the eligibility criteria and baseline characteristics data, five SATs chose more severe health conditions, and 22 chose the opposite. These assessments relied on qualitative judgement by two clinicians and are inherently subjective; they are therefore presented as a descriptive overview only.

Comparability of outcome measurements

Outcome definitions and measurement time points were assessed in 62 SATs for which both the SAT and its matched OPC/PG sources reported relevant details, yielding 76 comparisons. Outcome definitions were consistent in 56 comparisons and inconsistent in the remainder. In some cases, the SAT used a composite outcome whose definition differed from that in the OPC/PG source. For instance, one study15 defined major adverse events (MAEs) as a composite of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, renal/respiratory failure, paralysis, bowel ischaemia and procedural blood loss exceeding 1 L requiring transfusion, whereas the OPC/PG source defined MAEs as stroke, paralysis/paraplegia, myocardial infarction, procedural bleeding, respiratory failure, renal failure, wound healing complications and aneurysm-related mortality. Measurement time points were consistent in 50 comparisons. Among the inconsistent comparisons, 12 SATs had longer observation periods, 12 had shorter periods and two set multiple observation points, none of which matched the OPC/PG source.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This scoping review analysed 133 SATs that used OPC/PGs as external comparators. Of these, 84 (63.2%) used PGs, 34 (25.6%) claimed to use OPCs and 15 (11.3%) could not be reliably classified. More than 60% of the studies focused on cardiovascular diseases, and medical devices were the predominant intervention type.

Among the subset of studies for which comparisons were possible, differences were observed in multiple factors: approximately half of the age comparisons and over 70% of the sex comparisons showed statistically significant differences between the single-arm group and the OPC/PG source. SATs and their OPC/PG sources were also frequently not comparable with respect to health conditions, outcome definitions and measurement time points. These findings indicate that the comparability of study design characteristics between SATs and their OPC or PG sources is suboptimal.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review provides a systematic description of the use of OPCs and PGs in published SATs and quantitatively assessed the comparability of age and sex between single-arm groups and the OPC/PG source populations. The search was comprehensive, encompassing both English and Chinese databases, and the screening and data charting processes followed rigorous procedures with independent verification.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the diversity of disease categories and study purposes, only age and sex could be quantitatively compared; other prognostic factors, such as comorbidities or disease severity, could not be uniformly assessed. Moreover, a substantial proportion of studies did not report the OPC/PG source documents or the sources could not be accessed, and the demographic and clinical data required for comparison were frequently unavailable. These missing data were recorded as ‘not reported’ without imputation or author contact, as documenting incomplete reporting was itself an objective of this scoping review. Consequently, the quantitative comparisons may not fully reflect the actual degree of comparability, because studies with well-reported source data may also be those with better overall methodological rigour. Health condition comparability, outcome definitions and measurement time points were assessed qualitatively and based on subjective judgement; these findings are therefore presented as descriptive only. For studies with multiple OPCs or PGs, only the first three were selected; while this decision was based on prior evidence about reporting practices, some secondary endpoints may have been omitted. In addition, many SATs adjusted their OPCs or PGs, but the impact of these adjustments on comparability could not be assessed because the methods and justifications were rarely reported. Finally, the review was restricted to English and Chinese language publications. Although no study was excluded on the basis of language during screening, potential language bias cannot be ruled out.

Objective performance criterion and performance goal

A notable finding of this review is that most SATs used PGs rather than OPCs, and some studies that claimed to use OPCs did not provide evidence that met the established criteria for an OPC. OPCs and PGs differ in their evidentiary basis: an OPC is derived from substantial clinical data with rigorous statistical methods, whereas a PG is typically based on a smaller number of published studies and provides a lower level of evidence.2 The predominance of PGs therefore suggests that the external comparators in most published SATs are supported by relatively limited evidence.16 Moreover, even among studies that explicitly reported using PGs, the benchmark was often derived from a single published study, which further limits its representativeness as an external comparator. The mislabelling of PGs as OPCs obscures the evidentiary distinction between the two types of comparators. This finding is consistent with broader concerns raised in the studies about the transparency of externally controlled trials.

Previous studies have documented the rapid increase in the use of SATs for regulatory decisions and have called for greater rigour in the selection and reporting of external benchmarks.16 The present results extend shows that the study design characteristics of SAT and their OPC/PG source populations are often not comparable and that the reporting of OPC/PG derivation is insufficient.

Comparability of study design characteristics

In RCTs, random allocation balances both measured and unmeasured factors between groups, thereby minimising systematic differences. SATs lack this mechanism; therefore, any imbalance between the trial group and the external comparator source may undermine the validity. Age and sex are consistently associated with the prognosis of most diseases, and differences in these characteristics between the single-arm group and the OPC/PG source population could bias the evaluation of the intervention effect, although the current data cannot quantify the direction of such bias.

Beyond age and sex, inconsistencies were also observed in disease subtypes or stages, the definition of outcomes and the timing of endpoint measurements. These discrepancies could be addressed at the study design stage by aligning the eligibility criteria, outcome definitions and follow-up schedules with those used in the source studies. Although the current data cannot quantify the impact of these inconsistencies on conclusions, conservative practice would warrant optimising comparability.

Implications for future research

SATs with OPC/PGs are becoming more and more widely conducted, extending from medical device evaluation to drug development.17 18 The most essential and most challenging procedure is developing an appropriate OPC/PG.19 The design of SATs should be as consistent as possible with the sources of OPC/PG, especially the eligibility criteria, the definition of related outcomes and their time points. And after the completion of enrolment, it should be evaluated whether there is a difference of the important prognostic characteristics between the actual enrolled participants in the single-arm groups and the sources of their OPC/PGs.20 This requires the accessibility of the individual patient data or detailed enough aggregated data that developed the OPC/PG to facilitate statistical evaluation of the actual comparability.4 However, the present review found that the sources, derivation methods and adjustments of OPCs and PGs were frequently unreported or inadequately described. This lack of transparency undermines the credibility of the evidence generated by SATs. Standardised instruments for developing, selecting and reporting OPCs/PGs are therefore needed to improve the transparency and credibility.

SATs with OPCs or PGs have been increasingly used beyond medical device evaluation, and the present review confirmed that the majority of published studies in this area produced positive results. However, SATs with OPCs or PGs should be regarded as a design option when a concurrent control group is not feasible, rather than as a substitute for RCTs. This design is best suited for exploratory or preliminary investigations rather than for confirmatory purposes. Investigators should clearly state the rationale for adopting this design, acknowledge its inherent limitations and avoid drawing definitive causal conclusions from SATs with external benchmarks alone.21

Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates that the comparability of study design characteristics between SATs and their OPC/PG sources is frequently suboptimal. Statistically significant differences in demographic characteristics were common, and inconsistencies in other design characteristics were observed. Most published SATs relied on PGs, which provide a lower level of evidence than OPCs, and the reporting of sources, derivation methods and adjustments was often insufficient to allow independent assessment of their appropriateness.

These findings indicate that the inadequate development and reporting of OPC/PGs, combined with the lack of comparability between the SATs and the external comparators, undermine the strength of the conclusions from such studies. Standardised instruments for the development, selection and reporting of OPC/PGs are needed to improve transparency and credibility. SATs with OPCs or PGs should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory, and their inherent limitations should be explicitly acknowledged when they are used to support clinical or regulatory decisions.
