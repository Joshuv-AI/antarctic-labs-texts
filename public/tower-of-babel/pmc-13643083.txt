Diagnostic pitfalls of computed tomography angiography in high-energy knee trauma: two case reports on complete popliteal artery transection despite preserved distal flow
Authors: Shinseok Kim, Joosuk Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report describes two rare cases of complete popliteal artery transection in young, previously healthy men following high-energy trauma. In both cases, computed tomography (CT) angiography demonstrated a segmental arterial defect with preserved distal flow and no contrast extravasation, resulting in a delayed or uncertain diagnosis. These cases underscore the importance of incorporating the mechanism of injury into clinical decision-making. Both patients exhibited neurologic deficits in the affected limbs. Physical examination revealed signs of ischemia and sensorimotor dysfunction. Despite discordant CT findings, surgical exploration confirmed complete arterial transection in both cases, necessitating vascular reconstruction. In cases of high-energy knee trauma, surgical exploration should be considered when clinical findings suggest neurovascular compromise, even if CT angiography demonstrates preserved distal flow.Keywords: Popliteal artery, Computed tomography angiography, High-energy trauma, Vascular surgical procedures, Case reports

INTRODUCTION

Computed tomography (CT) angiography is widely used in trauma centers as a primary imaging modality for evaluating suspected vascular injuries. It is recognized for providing detailed and reliable vascular anatomy [1,2]. When a segmental defect is observed with preserved distal flow and no contrast extravasation, the finding is often interpreted as a nonocclusive vascular injury, such as thrombosis or an intimal flap [3,4].

However, in the setting of high-energy trauma, this conventional interpretation of CT findings may delay recognition and management of severe vascular injury [5]. Although CT angiography may depict an apparently benign vascular profile, the underlying pathology may be significantly more severe. This discrepancy becomes particularly concerning when clinical findings such as signs of ischemia or neurologic deficits suggest vascular compromise.

This report describes two cases of high-energy knee trauma demonstrating such discrepancies. Despite imaging findings suggestive of nonocclusive injury, both patients exhibited clear ischemic signs and were found to have complete arterial transection at surgical exploration.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

A 29-year-old previously healthy man presented to a local emergency department after sustaining blunt trauma to the posteromedial aspect of the distal femur. The injury occurred when a recoiling mooring rope struck his leg during maritime work, indicating a high-energy mechanism. The impact site was adjacent to the knee joint. Despite the high-energy nature of the trauma, the initial physical examination, documented at the outside hospital prior to transfer, revealed no abnormal findings. Distal pulses were palpable, and no signs of ischemia or neurologic deficits were recorded at that time.

CT angiography was performed at the outside hospital. The scan demonstrated a segmental defect in the popliteal artery with preserved distal flow and no contrast extravasation (Fig. 1). These findings, together with the initially benign physical examination and equivocal angiographic appearance, contributed to a delay in transfer. Consequently, the patient was transferred to our trauma center approximately 26 hours after the injury.

Initial computed tomography angiography from the outside hospital showing (A) a segmental defect of the popliteal artery with (B, C) preserved distal flow and no contrast extravasation. Distal flow is visible at the level of the proximal fibula, where the popliteal artery bifurcates into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries (arrows).

Upon arrival at our institution, the patient’s condition had markedly deteriorated. He reported progressive numbness and weakness in the affected limb. Physical examination revealed decreased motor strength and sensation, absent distal pulses, and severe tense swelling with overlying bullae, indicating compromised soft-tissue integrity and raising concern for impending acute compartment syndrome (Fig. 2). Repeat CT angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the popliteal artery (Fig. 3). Laboratory testing showed a markedly elevated creatine kinase level of 7,766 IU/L, which, in conjunction with the clinical findings, strongly suggested evolving compartment syndrome. Given the deterioration in neurovascular status, the worsening CT angiographic findings, and the documented high-energy mechanism, emergency surgical exploration was performed. Surgery was conducted in a negative-pressure operating room, with the surgical team wearing full protective equipment because the patient had COVID-19.

Clinical photographs of the injured extremity showing (A) severe swelling and (B) bruising with bullae (arrow), consistent with impending acute compartment syndrome.

Upon arrival at our institution, the patient exhibited clinical signs of ischemia. Repeat computed tomography angiography demonstrated complete occlusion of the popliteal artery. In contrast to the initial image (Fig. 1), distal flow was no longer visualized (arrow).

Intraoperatively, complete transection of the popliteal artery was identified, consistent with high-energy trauma. No intimal flap or partial tear was observed, definitively refuting the initial radiologic interpretation. A femoral-to-tibial bypass was performed (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, the patient’s respiratory status deteriorated secondary to COVID-19 infection, precluding further limb salvage efforts. An above-knee amputation was therefore performed.

Postoperative computed tomography angiography following femoral-to-tibial bypass, demonstrating restoration of distal flow (blue arrow) distal to the previously identified segmental defect (yellow arrow).

Case 2

A 27-year-old man sustained a traumatic posterior dislocation of the right knee in a high-speed motorcycle accident. Upon presentation to the emergency department, he reported severe knee pain and acute numbness in the lower leg. Clinical examination revealed absent distal pulses, diminished sensation, and significant motor weakness in the affected limb.

Emergent CT angiography demonstrated a segmental filling defect in the popliteal artery. Despite this defect, contrast enhancement was preserved distal to the lesion, and no contrast extravasation was observed (Fig. 5). The radiologic interpretation described total occlusion of the right popliteal artery with distal reconstitution via collateral circulation. These findings suggested a partial vascular injury, such as thrombosis or intimal dissection, given the apparent distal perfusion. However, the discrepancy between imaging findings and the severe clinical signs raised strong suspicion for more extensive vascular injury.

Initial computed tomography angiography demonstrating a segmental defect (yellow arrow) with preserved distal flow and no contrast extravasation (blue arrow). Distal flow is observed at the level of the bifurcation into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries.

Emergency surgery was performed immediately, approximately 8 hours after injury. Closed reduction and external fixation of the knee joint were performed, followed by surgical exploration. Operative findings revealed complete transection of the popliteal artery at the level of the knee joint (Fig. 6). The proximal and distal arterial ends were completely separated, with no residual continuity or lumen, directly contradicting the preserved distal flow observed on CT angiography. An interposition vein graft was used for arterial reconstruction. Despite multiple attempts at soft-tissue reconstruction, progressive and extensive necrosis of the skin and muscle developed, ultimately necessitating above-knee amputation (Fig. 7).

Intraoperative photograph demonstrating complete transection of the popliteal artery (arrow). The distal arterial segment is held with forceps. Vascular reconstruction was subsequently performed.

Postoperative clinical photographs. (A) One month after surgery, progressive necrosis extending from the toes is observed. (B, C) Two months after surgery, extensive necrosis involving muscle and soft tissue was evident, for which surgical debridement was performed.

DISCUSSION

High-energy trauma around the knee is a well-recognized cause of vascular injury, particularly involving the popliteal artery. Lee et al. [6] reported that approximately 1.6% to 64% of knee dislocations are associated with popliteal artery injury, underscoring the importance of prompt CT angiography for vascular evaluation. Similarly, Machado et al. [7] reported an incidence of up to 60% following knee dislocation. This vulnerability reflects the anatomic fixation of the popliteal artery proximally at the adductor hiatus and distally at the soleal arch, rendering it susceptible to stretch and shear forces during trauma [8].

Consistent with these reports, both patients in our series were young men who sustained high-energy knee trauma and were ultimately found to have complete popliteal artery transection. These cases reinforce the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for vascular injury and of performing CT angiography promptly in such settings [1,2].

Traditional interpretations of CT angiography often regard segmental filling defects with preserved distal flow and no extravasation as indicative of nonocclusive injuries, such as intimal flaps or mural thrombi [3,4]. However, Tamburrini et al. [5] emphasized that the absence of extravasation does not exclude complete arterial disruption. In high-energy knee trauma, preserved distal flow and lack of contrast extravasation on CT angiography do not reliably rule out complete arterial transection. Such discrepancies may result from collateral circulation masking the true extent of vascular disruption [6].

Our two cases support this concern. Despite CT angiography demonstrating a segmental defect with preserved distal flow and no extravasation, both patients exhibited clear ischemic signs and were found to have complete arterial transection at surgery. When clinical findings such as pulselessness, pallor, neurologic deficits, or progressive pain and swelling are present, early surgical exploration should be strongly considered, even if CT angiography appears reassuring. Therefore, in high-energy knee trauma, the presence of ischemic signs should raise strong suspicion for complete arterial transection and prompt timely surgical evaluation to minimize the risk of irreversible limb loss (Fig. 8).

In the first case, clinical decision-making was further complicated by deterioration in systemic status, particularly respiratory decline, which made perioperative and wound management challenging. In the second case, progressive soft-tissue necrosis represented the primary obstacle to limb salvage.

In both cases, determining whether to pursue limb salvage or amputation posed a significant challenge. Although both patients presented more than 6 hours after injury, limb salvage was initially attempted because of their young age, overall good health, and strong preference for preservation.

To our knowledge, prior studies specifically addressing discrepancies between CT angiography and intraoperative findings in high-energy knee trauma are lacking. Our observations suggest that a segmental defect with preserved distal flow may represent complete vessel transection rather than a nonocclusive injury. Accordingly, early surgical exploration should be strongly considered when this imaging pattern is encountered, as timely intervention is essential.
