On Generation in Metric Spaces
Jiaxun Li, Vinod Raman, Ambuj Tewari

Abstract
We study generation in separable metric instance spaces. We extend the language generation framework from 13 beyond countable domains by defining novelty through metric separation and allowing asymmetric novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator. We introduce the ( ε , ε ′ ) (\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}) -closure dimension, a scale-sensitive analogue of closure dimension, which yields characterizations of uniform and non-uniform generatability and a sufficient condition for generation in the limit. Along the way, we identify a sharp geometric contrast. Namely, in doubling spaces, including all finite-dimensional normed spaces, generatability is stable across novelty scales and invariant under equivalent metrics. In general metric spaces, however, generatability can be highly scale-sensitive and metric-dependent; even in the natural infinite-dimensional Hilbert space ℓ 2 \ell^{2} , all notions of generation may fail abruptly as the novelty parameters vary.



1
Introduction

LLMs are trained on massive amounts of data from which they learn to generate new, previously unseen, grammatically valid strings in a natural language such as English.
13
recently provided an elegant framework that captures the essence of generation. In their model, the learner receives a sequence of strings from an adversary drawn from a language in a known language collection. The learner has to generate novel valid strings from the true language that is initially unknown to the learner. This framework is inspired by the seminal work of
7
on language identification in the limit. In
7
’s model, an adversary enumerates strings from a language in a collection. The learner’s task is to identify the true language from only the positive examples shown by the adversary.
13
changed the identification requirement to generation: the learner has to generate novel, unseen strings from the true language.

This simple change, from identification to generation, completely changes the nature of provable guarantees. On the one hand, since the seminal characterization by
3
of language collections identifiable in the
7
model, we know that very few natural collections are identifiable in the limit. On the other hand,
13
showed that
any
countable collection is generatable in the limit. They also connected their theoretical findings to empirical phenomena such as under-generation (mode collapse) and over-generation (hallucination). Their elegant framework and surprising results have led to a flurry of work on extending their basic framework in various interesting directions.

In particular, a recent work
(
21
)
examined characterizations of various variants of the language generation model, including generation in the limit, non-uniform generation, and uniform generation. However, like much of the work on the language generation model, this work also assumed that the example space is
countable
. Because no additional structure is assumed, the only formalization of “novelty” is that the generated object be distinct from previously seen objects. There is no way to ensure that the object is “different enough.” However, generative AI is now being used to generate objects in spaces with rich structure. In such spaces, the assumption of countability becomes restrictive. For example, a neural surrogate for an SDE solver might generate random functions over a spatial domain
(
16
)
. A generative model for proteins has to not only model their 3D structure in a continuous space but also respect translational and rotational symmetries
(
9
)
. Inspired by applications in spaces with richer structure, in this paper we initiate the study of generation in
metric spaces
.

1.1
Main Contributions

This work initiates a theoretical study of
generation in metric spaces
, extending prior learning-theoretic frameworks that were restricted to countable example spaces. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We extend the language generation model to separable metric instance spaces
\mathcal{X}
, while retaining binary hypothesis classes
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. We introduce a notion of novelty based on metric separation rather than mere distinctness, and define two independent novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator. This leads to a strictly richer and nontrivial generalization of the countable setting.

•

We introduce the
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-closure dimension, a metric analogue of closure dimension that explicitly depends on scale. Using this notion, we obtain complete characterizations of uniform and non-uniform generatability, as well as a sufficient condition for generation in the limit. Moreover, we generalize the algorithmic framework from the countable case to the metric space setting under appropriate oracle access.

•

In doubling spaces, including all finite-dimensional normed spaces, we show that generatability behaves in a well-controlled manner: uniform and non-uniform generatability is invariant to the choice of novelty scales and preserved under equivalent metrics.

•

Beyond doubling spaces, we demonstrate sharp and sometimes non-intuitive behaviors. Generatability can be very sensitive to the novelty parameters and to choices of metric. Our constructions show that all notions of generation can fail abruptly as scales change even in one of the simplest infinite-dimensional space
\ell^{2}
, highlighting a qualitative gap between finite and infinite-dimensional spaces.

Extending generation to metric spaces presents several challenges.
First, unlike in the countable setting, novelty can no longer be defined by distinctness alone and must instead reflect geometric separation.
This necessitates introducing asymmetric novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator.
Second, several characterization results in the countable example space setting rely explicitly on countability, for example through enumeration arguments, which do not extend to general metric spaces.
Finally, due to the asymmetry between the adversary’s and the generator’s novelty parameters, constructing hypothesis classes and counterexamples requires careful consideration of their relative scales.

1.2
Related Work

Our work builds on the framework of language generation in the limit, introduced by
13
. Since then, subsequent work has investigated a range of questions surrounding this language generation model. Here, we discuss the most relevant prior works.

Uniform and Non-Uniform Generation

21
introduced stronger notions of generation: uniform and non-uniform generation, and provided characterization for uniform and non-uniform generation.
5
also, independently studied non-uniform generation and showed that all countable collections can be non-uniformly generated.
6
proposed an almost Pareto-optimal algorithm for non-uniform language generation.
8
studied unions of generatable classes and solved two open problems posed by
21
.

Extensions of Language Generation

Several extensions of language generation have been proposed.
20
,
18
and
4
investigated language generation with contamination, in which the adversary is allowed to omit instances or generate instances outside the language.
19
studied representative generation, in which the generator needs to output a distribution that is representative of the seen data.
2
introduced and investigated safe language generation, in which there exists a harmful language that the generator needs to avoid.

Other Related Work

Several follow-up studies have proposed and studied different definitions of breadth in the language generation model, and showed that requiring the generator to generate diverse instances from the target language makes generation significantly harder
(
11
;
12
;
14
;
15
)
.

1
study language identification in a metric space setting by revisiting the classical identification in the limit framework and extending it to scenarios where distances between languages are measured by a metric. In contrast, our work adopts a fundamentally different perspective: we study
generation
rather than identification, and the metric structure is imposed on the
instance space
rather than on the space of languages.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Example Space and Hypothesis Class

Throughout the paper, we let
\mathcal{X}
, the example space, be a
separable metric space
, i.e., a metric space with a countable
dense
subset. Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a binary hypothesis class. For a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let
\text{supp}(h):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:h(x)=1\}
, that is, its collection of positive examples.
For any class
\mathcal{H}
and a finite sequence of examples
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, define the version space as:

\mathcal{H}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)\}.

For any class
\mathcal{H}
, define its induced closure operator
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
as:

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=\begin{cases}\bigcap_{h\in\mathcal{H}(x_{1:n})}\operatorname{supp}(h),&\text{if $|\mathcal{H}(x_{1:n})|\geq 1$}\\
\bot,&\text{if $|\mathcal{H}(x_{1:n})|=0$}\end{cases}.

Intuitively,
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
captures the set of points that must belong to the target hypothesis given consistency with the observed examples. We will also need several other definitions about metric spaces.

Definition 2.1

(Closed Ball)
.

Assume
\mathcal{X}
is a separable metric space equipped with metric
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
. For a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
or a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
B(x,\varepsilon)
or
B(A,\varepsilon)
the closed
\varepsilon
-balls centered at
x
or
A
, respectively, defined as

B(x,\varepsilon):=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\rho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\},\qquad B(A,\varepsilon):=\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\inf_{x\in A}\rho(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\}.

Definition 2.2

(Covering Number)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be a metric space and
\varepsilon>0
.
An
\varepsilon
-covering of a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a finite set
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
such that

A\subset B(\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M},\varepsilon)
. The
\varepsilon
-covering number
N(\varepsilon;A,\rho)
is defined as

N(\varepsilon;A,\rho):=\min\left\{M\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\ \varepsilon\text{-covering of }A\text{ with }M\text{ points}\right\}.

If no such finite covering exists, we write
N(\varepsilon;A,\rho)=\infty
.

To prevent the adversary from exhausting all positive examples and thus making the task of generating unseen positive examples impossible, we make the following assumption for the hypothesis set. This assumption extends a similar condition used in previous work on generation in the countable example space setting (see Definition
A.1
).

Definition 2.3

(
r
-Uniformly Unbounded Support (
r
-UUS))
.

For some
r>0
, a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the

r
-Uniformly Unbounded Support (
r
-UUS)
property if for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
,

N(r;\operatorname{supp}(h),\rho)=\infty.

The
r
-UUS property satisfies the following monotonicity result, which we prove in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any class satisfying the
r
-UUS property for an
r>0
. Then for any positive
\delta<r
,
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the
\delta
-UUS property.

2.2
Generatability

Consider the following two-player game for a hypothesis set
\mathcal{H}
. At the start, the adversary picks a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
and does not reveal it to the learner. The game then proceeds over rounds
t=1,2,\dots
. In each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the adversary reveals a
new
example
x_{t}\in\operatorname{supp}(h)
. The generator, after observing enough examples of
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
, must output some
new
example inside
\operatorname{supp}(h)
. Crucially, the generator
never
observes its loss as it does not know
h
. The goal of the generator is to eventually perfectly generate new, positive examples
x\in\operatorname{supp}(h)
.

To make this formal, we first define a generator as a mapping from a finite sequence of examples to a new example.

Definition 2.4

(Generator)
.

A generator is a map
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}^{\star}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
that takes a finite sequence of examples
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
and outputs a new example
x
.

The challenge of extending the framework to metric spaces lies in the definition of
novelty
. In a countable example space, novelty can simply be defined as
distinct instances
. In a metric space, however, distinctness is not enough: one does not expect an instance that is very close to the previous ones to be a
new
instance.

As a result, we define a
new
instance to be
\varepsilon
far away from the previous instances. To make the definition general, the
\varepsilon
governing novelty is allowed to be different on the adversary and the generator sides. That is, we define two novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator, denoted as
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, respectively.

Definition 2.5

(Generatability in the Limit)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. If there exists a generator
\mathcal{G}
such that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, and any sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
such that
\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\varepsilon)
(i.e., the adversary eventually reveals an
\varepsilon
-cover of the support), there exists a
t^{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{G}(x_{1:s})\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s},\varepsilon^{\prime})
for all
s\geq t^{\star}
, then we say
\mathcal{H}
is
(
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}
)-generatable in the limit
.

Remark 2.1
.

The assumption
\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\varepsilon)
is an analogous to the enumeration assumption in the countable example space setting (see Definition
A.2
). It requires the adversary to eventually show the whole structure of the hypothesis set to the generator.

For the rest of the paper,
\varepsilon
governs the adversary’s revelation scale, while
\varepsilon^{\prime}
governs the generator’s novelty requirement. Our definition above is intentionally general and does not specify the relative magnitudes of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. This is because the relative magnitudes of novelty on the adversary and generator sides are application dependent. For example, consider authors preparing a paper submission to a conference.
An experienced author may read several papers that are closely related in topics (corresponding to a small
\varepsilon
for adversary) yet generate a paper that is substantially different, for example, by introducing a new technique or applying known methods to a new problem. In this case the generator’s
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is larger than than the adversary’s
\varepsilon
.
Alternatively, a beginning researcher may read a very diverse set of papers spanning many topics (a large
\varepsilon
), yet generate a paper closely aligned with one of them, such as by extending an existing line of work. Here, the generator’s
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is smaller than the adversary’s
\varepsilon
. Our definition of generatability in the limit is designed to encompass both behaviors.

Next, we extend two strengthenings of the
notion of generation in the limit introduced by
21
. The first notion, called uniform generation, is
the strongest. It requires the number
t^{\star}
in Definition
2.5
(which is the number of samples required by the generator before it starts generating consistently) to be independent of
both
the target hypothesis set
and the sequence of instances.

Definition 2.6

(Uniform Generatability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. If there exists a generator
\mathcal{G}
and
d^{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and any sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
, if there exists
t
such that
N(\varepsilon;\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\rho)\geq d^{\star}
, then
\mathcal{G}(x_{1:s})\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s},\varepsilon^{\prime})
for all
s\geq t
, then we say
\mathcal{H}
is
(
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}
)-uniformly generatable
.

The second is called non-uniform generation, which requires the number
t^{\star}
in Definition
2.5
to only be independent of the sequence of instances (i.e., it can depend on the target hypothesis).

Definition 2.7

(Non-Uniform Generatability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. If there exists a generator
\mathcal{G}
such that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
there exists a
d_{h}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
, if there exists
t
such that
N(\varepsilon;\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\rho)\geq d_{h}
, then
\mathcal{G}(x_{1:s})\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s},\varepsilon^{\prime})
for all
s\geq t
, then we say
\mathcal{H}
is
(
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}
)-non-uniformly generatable
.

It is clear that our framework strictly generalizes the countable example space setting considered in
21
. This is achieved by equipping a countable example space with the discrete
\{0,1\}
-metric. We include a formal proposition in Appendix
D
for completeness sake.

3
Characterizations of Generatability

In this section, we characterize hypothesis classes that are uniformly and non-uniformly generatable, and we provide a sufficient condition for generatability in the limit. We show that, despite the additional metric structure, the resulting characterizations closely parallel those obtained in the countable example space setting (see Appendix
A
for more details). Consequently, we state the main results in this section and defer the proofs to Appendix
B
. In Appendix
E
, we also have a discussion about the computability of generation. Although these characterizations are straightforward, generation in metric spaces exhibits several counterintuitive phenomena that do not arise in the countable example space setting. We explore these phenomena in more detail in Section
4
.

We first extend the techniques from
21
to account for metric space structure. As a result, we first define a metric analog of the Closure dimension originally defined for countable example space (see Definition
A.5
).

Definition 3.1

(
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-Closure dimension)
.

The
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-Closure dimension
of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted

\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})
, is the largest
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
there exist
h\in\mathcal{H}
and a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)

satisfying

N(\varepsilon;\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\rho)=d\quad\text{and}\quad N(\varepsilon^{\prime};\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\rho)<\infty.

If the above holds for arbitrarily large
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})=\infty
.
If no such sequence exists for
d=1
, we set

\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})=0
.

Unlike the Closure dimension, the
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-Closure dimension is
scale-sensitive
. In Section
4
we will further explore its property for different scales of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Our first theorem uses this dimension to provide a complete characterization of which classes are uniform generatable.

Theorem 3.1

(Characterization of Uniform Generatability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. Then,
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly generatable if and only if
\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
.

Similarly to the countable example space setting, for every finite hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
satisfying the
r
-UUS property with
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, we have
\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
. In contrast, when
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
, there exists a finite hypothesis class that is not non-uniformly generatable. Intuitively, if the generator is required to produce outputs that are more novel than those revealed by the adversary, the generation task may become impossible. Further discussion is provided in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 3.2

(Finite Classes are Uniformly Generatable)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any finite hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
such that
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
. Then,
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly generatable.

We next move to characterize non-uniform generatability. We show that our characterization of uniform generatability leads to a characterization of non-uniform generatability.

Theorem 3.3

(Characterization of Non-uniform Generatability)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-non-uniformly generatable.

(ii)

There exists a
non-decreasing
sequence of classes
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}\subseteq\dots
such that
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{n})<\infty
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}.

An immediate consequence of combing Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
is the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4

(Countable Classes are Non-uniformly Generatable)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any countable hypothesis class satisfying the
r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
such that
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
. Then,
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-non-uniformly generatable.

We finally give a sufficient condition for generatability in the limit by showing the finite union of uniform generatable classes are generatable in the limit.

Theorem 3.5

(Sufficient Condition for Generatability in the Limit)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any class satisfying the
r
-
UUS
property for some
r>0
and let
0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. If there exists a finite sequence of classes
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{n}
such that
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H}_{i})<\infty
for all
i\in[n]
, then
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generatable in the limit.

It is worth noting that characterizing generation in the limit is already subtle even in the absence of any metric structure
(
21
)
. Nevertheless, we show that our sufficient condition is in an “almost necessary” sense, as suggested by the counterintuitive behavior of generatability under unions. The proof is also shown in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be any metric space. For any
r>0,0\leq\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r/2
such that
N(r;\mathcal{X},\rho)=\infty
, there exists
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly generatable,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-non-uniformly generatable, but
\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}
is not
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generatable in the limit.

4
Properties of Generatability

Beyond the structure of the hypothesis class itself, generatability depends on two additional parameters: the novelty thresholds with respect to the adversary and the generator, denoted by
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, respectively. In this section, we study how generatability varies as these novelty parameters change. We also investigate generatability under different metrics defined on the same underlying space.

4.1
Generatability is Well Behaved in Doubling Spaces

We first establish a robustness result for generatability in doubling spaces. A doubling space is a metric space in which balls at one scale can be covered by finitely many balls at any smaller scale. In such spaces, we show that, both uniform and non-uniform generatability are invariant across all choices of scale parameters
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and are preserved under any change to an equivalent metric.

Definition 4.1
.

A metric space
\mathcal{X}
with metric
\rho
is said to be
doubling
if there exists a constant
M>0
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and any
r>0
, the ball
B(x,r)

can be covered by at most
M
balls of radius
r/2
.

We first show a stronger result than Lemma
2.1
: in doubling spaces, the
r
-UUS property is invariant to the choice of the scale parameter
r
. The proof is deferred to Appendix
D
for completeness.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a doubling metric space with metric
\rho
. Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
is a class satisfying
r
-UUS property for some
r>0
. Then it also satisfies the
r^{\prime}
-UUS property for any
r^{\prime}>0
. In this case, we simply say that
\mathcal{H}
has the UUS property.

We are now ready to present robustness results for uniform and non-uniform generatability in doubling spaces.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a doubling metric space with metric
\rho
.
Let
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
satisfy
0<\delta<\varepsilon
and
0<\delta^{\prime}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the UUS property.
Then
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly (resp. non-uniformly) generatable if and only if it is
(\delta,\delta^{\prime})
-uniformly (resp. non-uniformly) generatable.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
and
3.3
, we only need to show that
\operatorname{C}_{\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
if and only if
\operatorname{C}_{\delta^{\prime}}^{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is a doubling space, there exists
C
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
B(x,\varepsilon)
can be covered by the union of at most
C
balls of radius
\delta
and
B(x,\varepsilon^{\prime})
can be covered by the union of at most
C
balls of radius
\delta^{\prime}
. Then under our assumption, for any subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

N(\varepsilon;A,\rho)=\infty\iff N(\delta;A,\rho)=\infty.

And if
N(\varepsilon;A,\rho)<\infty
, we have

N(\varepsilon;A,\rho)\leq N(\delta;A,\rho)\leq CN(\varepsilon;A,\rho).

Comparing with the definition of the closure dimension completes the proof.
∎

It is well known that Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a doubling metric space. As a result, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 4.3
.

In any finite-dimensional normed vector space
\mathcal{X}
, if
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the UUS property, then for any
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
,
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly (resp. non-uniformly) generatable if and only if it is
(\delta,\delta^{\prime})
-uniformly (resp. non-uniformly) generatable.

We next study how the generatability behave under changes of the underlying metric. The proof is similar to that of Theorem
4.2
so we defer it to Appendix
D
.

4.2
Generatability is Scale-Sensitive

So far we have seen that generatability behaves stable in a doubling space. When moving forward to general, potentially infinite-dimensional, metric spaces, however, such robust properties do not hold any more. Generatability becomes depending on novelty parameters
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and the underlying metric of the space.

Informally, consider the generation game as follows: The adversary reveals instances from a hypothesis set
h
that are mutually
\varepsilon
-separated, while the generator must eventually produce instances in
h
that are at least
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-far from all previously observed ones. Intuitively, the game becomes easier when
\varepsilon
is larger, since the adversary is forced to reveal more widely separated instances, allowing the generator to observe new regions of the hypothesis support. Similarly, the game also becomes easier when
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is smaller, as this permits the generator to generate instances with less novelty relative to those already observed.

In this section, we establish monotonicity results with respect to both
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. For
uniform
and
non-uniform
generation, the results align with this intuition: if a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly (resp. non-uniformly) generatable, then it remains so when
\varepsilon
is increased or
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is decreased. We illustrate this behavior with an example in the
\ell^{2}
space as Example
4.9
, where increasing
\varepsilon
or decreasing
\varepsilon^{\prime}
makes generation easier. Notably, this example also shows a separation between all finite dimensional metric spaces, and the infinite-dimensional space
\ell^{2}
. Depending on the choice of scale parameters
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, the same hypothesis class can exhibit all possible regimes of generatability, ranging from not generatable in the limit to uniformly generatable. This phenomenon cannot occur in a doubling space.

The situation for
generation in the limit
is more subtle, as already illustrated by Example
4.5
. When considering generation in the limit, monotonicity holds only when
\varepsilon
is decreased. In contrast, increasing
\varepsilon
can cause generatability to fail: a hypothesis class may be
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generatable in the limit for small
\varepsilon
, yet lose this property once
\varepsilon
exceeds a threshold. Example
4.8
demonstrates that, in
\ell^{2}
, generatability in the limit can disappear when
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
exceed such thresholds. Moreover, in contrast to Example
4.5
in the doubling-space setting, Example
4.8
is more general: the threshold values of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
at which generatability fails can be chosen arbitrarily.

4.3
Generatability is Metric-Dependent

In Section
4.1
, we showed that uniform generatability is invariant under changes to equivalent metrics. In contrast, in general metric spaces, generatability can depend on the underlying metric: even equivalent metrics on the same instance space may induce different generatability properties.

We begin by establishing a stability result. Theorem
4.10
shows that if one metric is bounded by another, then both the UUS property and generatability in the limit transfer in a controlled manner, with the relevant parameters scaling linearly with the bounding constant. In this sense, generatability remains stable under sufficiently mild distortions of the metric. We present the proof in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a metric space with metric
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. Assume there exists
M>0
such that for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\rho_{2}(x,y)\leq M\rho_{1}(x,y)
. Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any class satisfying the
r
-UUS property for an
r>0
with respect to
\rho_{2}
, and
0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r
. Then
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the
r/M
-UUS property with respect to
\rho_{1}
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{H}
is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generatable in the limit with respect to
\rho_{2}
, then
\mathcal{H}
is also
(\varepsilon/M,\varepsilon^{\prime}/M)
-generatable in the limit with respect to
\rho_{1}
.

Our next example highlights a sharp contrast. Even when two metrics are equivalent in the topological sense, generatability need not be preserved. In particular, we construct an example in
\ell^{2}
showing that a hypothesis class can be uniformly generatable under the standard metric, yet fail to be non-uniformly generatable under an equivalent metric. We state the example and leave the full proof in Appendix
C
.

Example 4.11
.

In
\ell^{2}
-space, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies the
\varepsilon
-UUS property and a metric
\rho^{\prime}
that is equivalent with the ordinary metric
\rho
such that
\mathcal{H}
is
(\tau,\tau)
-uniform generatable with respect to
\rho
for any
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon)
, but
\mathcal{H}
is not
(\tau,\tau)
-non-uniformly generatable with respect to
\rho^{\prime}
for any
\tau\in(0,\varepsilon)
.

5
Discussion

We initiated a theoretical study of generation in metric spaces, extending prior frameworks that were restricted to countable domains. Our results show that introducing geometric structure leads to new scale-dependent and metric-dependent phenomena, while preserving several older characterizations in modified form. In particular, we identified doubling metric spaces as a broad regime in which generatability behaves in a stable and robust manner, and demonstrated that outside this regime, generatability can be highly sensitive to both the novelty parameters and the choice of metric.

Although our framework is theoretical, it aligns naturally with how novelty is operationalized in several continuous-domain generative applications. For example, in protein structure generation, a generated structure is often classified as novel only if its maximum structural similarity to all training structures falls below a fixed threshold, corresponding to a distance-based exclusion relative to the training set
(
17
)
. More broadly, empirical studies of generative model generalization have observed that generated samples may lie closer to the training set than independently held-out test samples in a feature space, reflecting a separation between the scale at which training data are revealed and the scale at which novelty is required of generated outputs
(
10
)
. Our framework makes this distinction explicit by allowing separate novelty parameters for the adversary and the generator.

Several directions remain open, including studying generation when outputs are considered equivalent under natural symmetries, identifying geometric conditions beyond doubling that ensure stable behavior, and strengthening our understanding of generation in the limit under additional structural assumptions.

Remark B.1
.

The inference

N\!\left(\varepsilon^{\prime};\ \langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t^{\star}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},\rho\right)=\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N\!\left(\varepsilon^{\prime};\ \{z_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{\infty},\rho\right)=\infty

where

is a dense subset of
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t^{\star}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}
and

Lemma B.6

(Embedding
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
into
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
be a metric space and let
r>0
. Assume that the covering number

N(r;\mathcal{X},\rho)=\infty
. Let
0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq r/2
.
Then there exists an injective map (embedding)

\iota:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}

such that for every
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly generatable
(resp., non-uniformly generatable, generatable in the limit) in the sense of
Definitions
A.3
,
A.4
,

A.2
if and only if

\iota(\mathcal{H}):=\{\iota(h):h\in\mathcal{H}\}

is
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-uniformly generatable (resp., non-uniformly generatable,
generatable in the limit).

Proof.

Since
N(r;\mathcal{X},\rho)=\infty
, there exists an infinite
r
-packing in
\mathcal{X}
.
In particular, we may choose a countably infinite subset and relabel it as

\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that

\rho(x_{i},x_{j})>r\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j.

Define
\iota:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
by

\iota(h)(x_{j})=h(j)\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\iota(h)(x)=0\quad\forall x\notin\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Then
\iota
is injective and satisfies

\operatorname{supp}(\iota(h))=\{x_{j}:j\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\}.

We claim that for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\bigl|B(x,\varepsilon)\cap\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigr|\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl|B(x,\varepsilon^{\prime})\cap\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigr|\leq 1.

Indeed, if
x_{i},x_{j}\in B(x,\varepsilon)
with
i\neq j
, then by the triangle inequality,

\rho(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\rho(x_{i},x)+\rho(x,x_{j})\leq 2\varepsilon\leq r,

contradicting
\rho(x_{i},x_{j})>r
. The argument for
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is identical.

The above claim implies that, restricted to hypotheses supported on
\{x_{j}\}
,
membership in an
\varepsilon
-ball (resp.
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-ball) identifies at most one index

j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently, under the identification
j\leftrightarrow x_{j}
,
the
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-novelty constraints in
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
coincide with the
distinctness-based novelty constraints in the countable space
\mathbb{Z}
used in
Definitions
A.3
,
A.4
,

A.2
.

Formally, any adversary strategy on
\mathbb{Z}
can be simulated on
\mathcal{X}
by replacing each
integer
j
it plays with the point
x_{j}
; likewise, any generator strategy on
\mathbb{Z}

can be simulated on
\mathcal{X}
by outputting
x_{j}
whenever it would output
j
.
Conversely, any play in
\mathcal{X}
against
\iota(\mathcal{H})
can be projected back to
\mathbb{Z}

by mapping each encountered point in
\{x_{j}\}
to its unique index. (Without loss of generality, we assume the generator’s outputs lie in

\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
, since outputs outside

\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

cannot certify membership in any
\operatorname{supp}(\iota(h))
.)

Therefore, there exists a winning (uniform / non-uniform / limit) strategy for
\mathcal{H}
in the
countable game if and only if there exists a winning
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-strategy for

\iota(\mathcal{H})
in the metric game. This proves the stated equivalences.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be any hypothesis class satisfying the

r
-
\operatorname{UUS}
property for some
r>0
, and let

0<\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}<r
. Suppose there exists a sequence of instances
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and an integer

such that for every subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying
i_{k}=k
for all
k\leq m
and
i_{k+1}>i_{k}>m
for all
k>m
, there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq B\left(\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\,\min\{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\}\right),

then
\mathcal{H}
is not
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generatable in the limit.

Intuitively, the lemma says that if the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is rich enough such that an adversary can fix a finite prefix after which any continuation is consistent with uncountably many
h
, then the generator can be permanently trapped and forced to fail.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a generator. We show that there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
and a subsequence
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, both depending on
\mathcal{G}
, such that

\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B(x_{i_{k}},\varepsilon),

and
\mathcal{G}
fails to generate
h
in the limit.

We construct the subsequence inductively. Let
i_{1}=1,i_{2}=2,\dots,i_{m}=m
. Define

i_{m+1}=\min\left\{k>i_{m}\mid x_{k}\notin B\left(\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{m}},\mathcal{G}(x_{i_{1}}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{m}})\},\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\right\}.

Assuming
i_{1},\dots,i_{n-1}
have been defined where
n>m
, we set

i_{n}=\min\left\{k>i_{n-1}\mid x_{k}\notin B\left(\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n-1}},\mathcal{G}(x_{i_{1}}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n-1}})\},\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\right\}.

By the assumption that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-UUS property, we have
N(\varepsilon^{\prime},\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\rho)=\infty
, so this inductive process continues indefinitely. Thus,
\{x_{i_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a well-defined subsequence of
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

By assumption, there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B(x_{i_{k}},\varepsilon)\quad\text{ and}\quad\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B(x_{i_{k}},\varepsilon^{\prime})

Finally, we claim that for every
n>m
, the generator
\mathcal{G}
either outputs a point not in
\operatorname{supp}(h)
, or a point lying in

. To establish this, it suffices to show that

\mathcal{G}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n}})\notin\bigcup_{k=n+1}^{\infty}B(x_{i_{k}},\varepsilon^{\prime}).

This follows directly from the inductive construction: for any

Hence,
\mathcal{G}
fails to generate
h
in the limit.

D.1
Proofs Related to the UUS Property

This subsection collects short proofs establishing a basic monotonicity result for the UUS property in general metric spaces, as well as a stronger scale-invariance result in doubling spaces. We first show that the UUS property is monotone in the scale parameter.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

If

∎

The previous lemma shows monotonicity of the UUS property in the scale parameter. Under the additional assumption that

Proof of Lemma

satisfies the

D.2

This subsection shows that our metric-space formulation strictly generalizes the countable example space model studied in
21

In particular, when the instance space is equipped with the discrete metric at a sufficiently small scale, our notions of novelty, coverage, and generatability reduce exactly to their counterparts in the countable setting. Thus, all generatability notions considered in this paper are consistent with the classical framework.

Proposition D.1
.

Let

h\in\mathcal{H}
, if

N(\rho_{2};\operatorname{supp}(h),r)=\infty,

then

N(\rho_{1};\operatorname{supp}(h),r/M)=\infty.

Thus,
\mathcal{H}
satisfies the
(r/M)
-UUS property with respect to
\rho_{1}
.

Now suppose
\mathcal{G}
is an
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-generator for
\mathcal{H}
with respect to
\rho_{2}
.
We show that
\mathcal{G}
is also an
(\varepsilon/M,\varepsilon^{\prime}/M)
-generator with respect to
\rho_{1}
.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
be a sequence such that

\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq B_{1}\!\left(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\varepsilon/M\right).

By the inclusion above, this implies

\operatorname{supp}(h)\subseteq B_{2}\!\left(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty},\varepsilon\right).

Therefore, there exists
s
such that for all
t\geq s
,

\mathcal{G}(x_{1:t})\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\setminus B_{2}\!\left(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime}\right).

Since

B_{1}\!\left(x,\varepsilon^{\prime}/M\right)\subseteq B_{2}(x,\varepsilon^{\prime}),

it follows that

\mathcal{G}(x_{1:t})\in\operatorname{supp}(h)\setminus B_{1}\!\left(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime}/M\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix E
Remarks on Computability

There are few remarks on the algorithms on generation in the limit in the countable example space setting
[
13
,
21
,
6
]
.
We point out that the algorithm by
13
for generation in the limit which only uses the oracle “is
x\in\operatorname{supp}(h)
” is not directly extendable to the metric space setting. Because it explicitly uses the countable structure of the example space, as well as the countable structure of the hypothesis class. We leave the extension as an open problem. However, the algorithm in Appendix I by
21
which uses an ERM oracle can be extended with some modification.

From our definition,
\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is the set of positive examples common to all hypothesis in the version space of
\mathcal{H}
consistent with the sample
(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{n},1).
From this perspective, one can check closure membership, i.e. given an example
x
and a generatable sequence
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, return
\mathds{1}\{x\in\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\}
, using access to an Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) oracle. Formally, an ERM oracle is a mapping
\mathcal{O}:2^{\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{\star}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, which given a class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and a labeled sample
S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{\star}
, outputs
\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{(x,y)\in S}\mathds{1}\{h(x)\neq y\}.
Then, given a class
\mathcal{H}
, a generatable sequence
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, one can compute
\mathds{1}\{x\in\mathds{1}\{\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\}
using the following procedure: Define the sample
S_{x}=\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{n},1),(x,0)\}.
Query
\mathcal{O}
on
S_{x}
and
\mathcal{H}
and let
r_{x}
be its output. Output
r_{x}
. To see why this works, suppose
r_{x}=0
. Then, that means there exists a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\{x_{1},\dots.x_{n}\}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
but
x\notin\operatorname{supp}(h)
. Thus, it cannot be the case that
x\in\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.
On the other hand, if
r_{x}=1
, then it must mean that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(h)
, we have that
h(x)=1
. Accordingly,
x\in\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
by definition.

The generator described in Lemma
B.2
can be efficiently implemented given access to a max–min oracle

\mathcal{O}_{\emph{max-min}}:2^{\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}\times\mathcal{X}^{\star}\to\mathcal{X},

together with oracle access to the metric. Given a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
and a finite sequence of examples
x_{1},\dots,x_{t}
, the oracle
\mathcal{O}_{\emph{max-min}}
returns

\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})}\;\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{h(x_{i})\neq 1\}+\mathds{1}\{h(x)\neq 0\}.

The inner minimization coincides with the output of an empirical risk minimization (ERM) oracle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}
as described above. Consequently, the max–min oracle can be equivalently written as

\mathcal{O}_{\emph{max-min}}(\mathcal{H},x_{1:t})=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})}\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\!\big(\mathcal{H},\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{t},1),(x,0)\}\big).

The generator in Lemma
B.2
can therefore be implemented using a single call to the max–min oracle at each round. To see this, suppose that

t\geq\operatorname{C}(\mathcal{H})+1
. Since

N(\varepsilon^{\prime};\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\rho)=\infty
, it follows that

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})\neq\emptyset,

and moreover, for every

x\in\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})
,

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\!\big(\mathcal{H},\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{t},1),(x,0)\}\big)\geq 1.

As a result,

\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})}\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\!\big(\mathcal{H},\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{t},1),(x,0)\}\big)\geq 1,

and thus the oracle
\mathcal{O}_{\emph{max-min}}(\mathcal{H},x_{1:t})
returns a point

\hat{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus B(\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})
such that

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\!\big(\mathcal{H},\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{t},1),(\hat{x}_{t},0)\}\big)\geq 1.

Such a point
\hat{x}_{t}
must therefore belong to

\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, completing the proof.

If we insist on using only an ERM oracle, then the max–min oracle is unattractive, as it may require uncountably many ERM calls per round. Instead, fix a countable dense subset
(y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of
\mathcal{X}
. When
t\geq\operatorname{C}(\mathcal{H})+1
, we can search over
(y_{i})
and invoke only finitely many ERM calls before returning:

1

if

y_{i}\notin B(\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{t},\varepsilon^{\prime})

then

2

if

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{ERM}}\!\big(\mathcal{H},\{(x_{1},1),\dots,(x_{t},1),(y_{i},0)\}\big)\geq 1

then

3

y_{i}

end if

4

t\geq\operatorname{C}(\mathcal{H})+1

When
