Comparison of model demonstration and video-based education methods on oral hygiene practices and caries incidence in 3–5-year-old children: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Elif Ayşe Tamtekin Erdoğan, Hazal Özcan, Aliye Tuğçe Gürcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to evaluate the oral and dental health status of children aged 3–5, to examine their behaviours regarding dental health, and to improve their existing oral care habits using model and video education methods and compare these methods.Materials and methodsThis study was carried out on 282 children aged 3–5, randomized into three groups, attending six private kindergartens affiliated with the Kartal, Pendik, Tuzla District Directorate of National Education in Istanbul. Firstly, a survey was administered to parents to learn their knowledge about their children's oral and dental health. Intraoral examinations were performed on all children, and their dental and gingival health status was examined by using ICDAS II, plaque and gingival indices. The first group received model-based educaiton, the second group received video-based education, and the third group served as the control group and was not trained. Six months later, parent surveys and intraoral examinations were conducted again and changes children's oral and dental health knowledge and behaviours were evaluated.ResultsSignificant positive changes were observed in the oral and dental health-related behaviour of the children in the groups that received model- or video-based education. Additionally, there was a statistically significant positive change after the training regarding the importance of regular and proper toothbrushing, using dental floss and fluoride-containing children's toothpaste, tongue cleaning, reducing unhealthy snack consumption and visit the dentist regularly, with parental support (p < 0.05). While a statistically significant change was detected in plaque index scores of the trained groups (p < 0.05), there was no statistically significant change in the gingival index (p > 0.05). While there was no significant difference between the model method and video methods in oral and dental health education, model-based education was found to be more effective when ICDAS II and plaque indexes were evaluated in terms of its effect on caries incidence.ConclusionAs a result, both model and video education methods increase children's oral and dental health and knowledge level, and model education is more effective in reducing the incidence of caries.Trial registrationISRCTN66028008Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08787-5.Keywords: Kindergarten Children, Oral Hygiene Education, Model Education, Video Education, Preventive Dentistry

Introduction

Oral and dental health is critical for overall body health [1]. Primary dentition and nutrition play a significant role in the healthy development of children [2]. Dental caries observed in primary teeth during the preschool period is referred to as Early Childhood Caries (ECC), which can lead to issues such as pain, sleep disturbances, and eating disorders [3]. ECC is a chronic, infectious, and multifactorial disease observed in children younger than 71 months [4].

Dental caries occurs when acids, produced by the fermentation of sugary foods by oral microorganisms (particularly Streptococcus mutans), damages the tooth structure [5]. Early colonization of S. mutans increases the risk of caries [6]. Caries in primary teeth can also increase the risk of caries in permanent dentition [7].

Although dental caries is a preventable issue, insufficient emphasis is placed on oral and dental health education [8]. The preschool period is a critical time for children to develop healthy habits. Kindergartens provide an ideal environment for tooth brushing and oral hygiene education [2]. Educational programs initiated with the eruption of the first tooth can provide long-term economic benefits [9].

Educational programs should be age-appropriate, tailored to socioeconomic status, understandable, supported by visual aids, and memorable. Children are influenced by engaging, colourful visuals and storytelling methods; thus, musical video presentations can be an effective approach [9].

However, there is limited evidence directly comparing interactive model-based education with video-based educational approaches in preschool children. The aim of this study is to assess the oral and dental health status of 3–5-year-old children, examine their behaviours, and compare the effectiveness of model and video-based education methods in improving oral hygiene practices.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a randomized controlled, single-blind trial to evaluate the effectiveness of a dental model and an animated video in oral health education among children aged 3–5 years. The study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for randomized controlled trials. This randomized controlled trial was conducted prospectively and was retrospectively registered in the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN66028008), an ICMJE- and WHO-approved clinical trial registry, on April 13, 2026, after participant recruitment had begun on November 4, 2022. Registration was completed during the publication process in accordance with journal requirements. Importantly, ethical approval had been obtained on September 1, 2022, prior to study initiation, and no changes were made to the study protocol, outcomes, or methodology after registration.The study was conducted in six private kindergartens located in the Kartal, Pendik, and Tuzla districts of Istanbul, operating full-time between 08:30 and 18:00. To prevent interaction among children, those within the same classroom were assigned to the same group. Therefore, cluster randomization was applied at the school/classroom level to minimize interaction between groups. Schools were randomized into model, video, and control groups using a sealed-envelope method by its own teacher in each class. A total of 282 children (n = 94 per group) were included. This was a single-blind study. The participating children were not informed about their group allocation and were therefore blinded to the intervention they received. However, the same investigator (EATE) who delivered the education sessions (model demonstration or video education) also performed all intraoral clinical examinations and was therefore not blinded to group allocation.

This study was approved by the Altınbaş University Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision no: 2022/140). The researcher visited kindergartens to inform school administrations and parents about the study. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians of all participating children, including both in the control group and other groups, as oral examinations were performed.

The preparatory phase (topic selection, literature review, ethics committee application, and preparation of parental consent and data collection forms) was completed between March and October 2022. Kindergarten visits began in November 2022 and were completed in June 2023.

For the video group, the selected 3-min-21-s animated video titled “Kıpırdak ile Şıpırdak (Pearly Teeth)” was selected by a specialist pedagogue from publicly accessible content on YouTube following a comprehensive literature and content review to ensure its suitability for early childhood. Particular attention was paid to ensuring that the video content provided information equivalent to that delivered to the model group and was appropriate for children’s attention span and to ensure the standardization of educational content across the intervention groups. The video was obtained from an open-access platform and was used solely for educational and research purposes without any commercial intent; therefore, no additional written permission was required. Permission for the use of a toothbrushing model and toothbrush for the model group was obtained from Colgate.

Data were collected at baseline and at 6-month follow-up using oral examinations (ICDAS II, plaque and gingival indices) and parental questionnaires. Data collection was carried out in two rounds: at baseline (before the oral examination) and at 6-month follow-up. Parents of children in the control group also completed the same questionnaire at baseline and follow-up; however, no educational intervention was provided to the children in this group. All intraoral examinations were performed in a classroom setting using dental mirrors, probes, and dental tweezers.

Statistical analysis

Power analyses were performed using GPower 3.1.9.4 and PASS 2022, assuming 80% power, a 5% significance level, and a Cohen’s w effect size of 0.30. The sample size was determined as 193 for independent groups and 216 for dependent groups; 72 participants per group (total 216) were deemed sufficient.

The study was planned with 303 children from six kindergartens, but 5 were excluded for various reasons, starting with 298 children. During the second screening, 16 more children were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 282 children (94 per group), exceeding the minimum required sample size of 216 (Consort Diagram on Supplementary File 3).

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25. Chi-square tests (Pearson or Maximum Likelihood Ratio) were used for univariate analyses, and significant results were further analysed with multivariate logistic regression models. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni-corrected Z-tests. Pre- and post-education comparisons used McNemar-Bowker (3 + groups) and McNemar (2 groups) tests, while inter-group comparisons used Chi-square tests (Pearson, Fisher, or Maximum Likelihood Ratio). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Findings

This randomized controlled study evaluated the impact of oral and dental health education using model and video methods on dental habits and caries incidence in 282 children aged 3–5 years (mean age: 4.19 ± 0.766). Participants were divided into three groups (n = 94 each): model education, video education, and control (no education). The education focused on brushing techniques, dental floss use, toothpaste use, and nutrition. Assessments included pre- and post-education (6 months later) parental surveys and clinical indices including ICDAS II and plaque and gingival indices [10–12] . Statistical analyses included Chi-square, McNemar-Bowker, multinomial logistic regression, and Bonferroni-corrected Z-tests (p < 0.05).

Baseline demographic characteristics were similar across groups (Table 1). Parental education and income generally showed no significant effect on habits (education: χ2 = 96.010, p = 1.000; income: χ2 = 45.293, p = 0.164); however, unhealthy snacking frequency varied by education level (χ2 = 33.312, p = 0.007), though not significant in regression. Caries groups (yes/no/unknown), cleaning methods, floss use, snacking, dentist visits, and reasons for visits showed significant differences (p < 0.05); multinomial regression confirmed model significance (χ2 = 147.507, p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis showed higher dentist visits due to caries/pain in the caries group, but no difference in snacking.

Post-education, the model and video groups showed significant improvements in most habits compared to the control group (Table 2). The model group showed greater improvement in brushing frequency, assistance, cleaning tools, floss use, toothpaste amount/type/wetting, fluoride use, brushing time monitoring, tongue brushing, and brush replacement frequency. The video group was also effective but showed less improvement in some areas (e.g., floss use increase: model ~ 22.6 times, video ~ 12 times). The control group showed minimal/no change. Inter-group comparisons (Table 3) confirmed the superiority of model and video over the control group (p < 0.05); the model outperformed the video in fluoride use and brushing frequency.

Clinical indices improved to varying degrees (Table 3). ICDAS worsened more in the control group (negative change 31.9%) compared to the model (3.2%) and video (12.8%; p < 0.001). The plaque index showed the highest positive change in the model group (27.7%).

Discussion and conclusion

The prevalence of dental caries in preschool children ranges from 1 to 85% globally and 11% to 84.9% in Turkey [4, 13]. Oral and dental diseases, particularly prevalent in developing countries, are largely preventable. In Turkey, caries prevalence in 5-year-olds is 69.8%, compared to 39.4% in the United Kingdom [14, 9]. Early education using visual and auditory methods effectively improves oral and dental health habits [14, 9]. In this study, model and video education significantly improved brushing frequency, floss use, fluoride toothpaste use, tongue cleaning, and reduced unhealthy snacking compared to the control group [14]. Consistent with previous research, oral health education using visual and auditory methods has been shown to significantly improve children’s oral hygiene behaviours. Studies by Nakre and Harikiran and other researchers demonstrated that educational interventions increase toothbrushing frequency and improve oral hygiene practices. Similarly, school-based oral health programs have been reported to be effective in improving children’s knowledge and behaviours [15, 16, 17, 29].

In line with these findings, the present study demonstrated that both model-based and video-based education significantly improved oral health behaviours, including toothbrushing frequency, use of dental floss, fluoride toothpaste use, tongue cleaning, and reduction in unhealthy snacking habits. Notably, previous studies evaluating oral hygiene education in children have often overlooked or insufficiently emphasized the importance of tongue brushing. In contrast, the present study demonstrated a significant increase in tongue cleaning practices in both groups following the educational interventions. This finding highlights the importance of including comprehensive oral hygiene instructions, such as tongue cleaning, in pediatric oral health education programs. These results are consistent with studies conducted in different populations, where audiovisual and interactive educational methods resulted in improved plaque control and oral hygiene [18–20].

Notably, model-based education was more effective than video-based education in improving toothbrushing frequency and fluoride toothpaste use. This finding may be explained by the interactive and tactile nature of model education, which allows children to actively participate in the learning process. Previous studies have also highlighted that hands-on and participatory learning approaches are more effective in young children due to their developmental characteristics and limited attention span.

On the other hand, video-based education was more effective in reducing unhealthy snacking habits. This may be attributed to the use of engaging visual elements and storytelling techniques, which enhance children’s attention and emotional engagement. Similar findings have been reported in studies using animated or story-based educational materials, which were found to positively influence dietary behaviours in children [21, 22].

Parental involvement plays a critical role in shaping children’s oral health behaviours. Previous studies have shown that children primarily acquire oral hygiene habits through their parents, and parental education significantly influences these behaviours. In the present study, although parental education level was not significantly associated with caries prevalence, this may be due to the relatively homogeneous and highly educated parent population. However, no significant relationship was found between parental education level and caries prevalence, possibly due to easy access to information [23, 24].

Interestingly, this study found that higher socioeconomic status was associated with an increased risk of caries. This could be explained by greater access to sugary foods and snacks in high-income families. Similar trends were reported suggesting that dietary habits may mediate the relationship between socioeconomic status and caries risk [25].

As highlighted in previous studies, school-based education supported by collaboration between teachers and parents has proven to be more effective for children [16, 26–28] . Moreover similar educational approaches have been previously investigated in preschool children [24, 25, 27]. The effectiveness of school-based interventions observed in this study is consistent with previous literature emphasizing the importance of integrating oral health education into early childhood education programs. Schools provide an ideal environment for reaching children and indirectly influencing parents and teachers.

In terms of clinical outcomes, improvements in plaque index and ICDAS II scores were more pronounced in the model education group. Model education was more successful likely due to the tactile learning component, while video education was effective in reducing unhealthy snacking. These findings are consistent with studies reporting that practical demonstrations and supervised toothbrushing training result in greater reductions in plaque levels compared to passive educational methods. These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of interactive and multifaceted oral health education strategies in early childhood.

One of the most important strengths of this study is its randomized controlled design, relatively large sample size, and the inclusion of both behavioural and clinical outcome measures. However, this study has several important limitations. Due to its single-blind design, both the educational interventions and clinical examinations were conducted by the same researcher (E.A.T.E.), who was aware of group allocation, introducing a potential risk of detection bias. To mitigate this, standardized examination forms and calibrated indices (Silness & Löe plaque and gingival indices, and ICDAS II) were employed. Additionally, the limitations of the study include the limited clinical examination conditions due to being conducted in a classroom setting, and lack of long-term follow-up. Notwithstanding these limitations, model-based education appeared more effective due to its tactile learning component, whereas video-based education was more effective in reducing unhealthy snacking behaviours. Future programs with longer follow-up periods and enhanced parent-teacher collaboration are recommended.

In conclusion, both model-based and video-based education methods were effective in improving oral health behaviours in preschool children. However, model-based education was more effective in improving toothbrushing habits and plaque control, likely due to its interactive and hands-on nature. These results have important implications for developing effective early childhood oral health promotion strategies. Furthermore, these findings support the implementation of structured, school-based oral health education programs that incorporate interactive teaching methods.
