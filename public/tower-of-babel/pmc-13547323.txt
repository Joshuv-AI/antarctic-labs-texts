Vehicle CO₂ emission prediction based on firefly with ant colony optimization tuned long short-term memory model
Authors: Safia abbas, Yasser Fouad, Sarah M Alhammad, Ahmed M Osman, Hazem M El-Bakry, Ahmed M Elshewey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate vehicle-level CO₂ emission prediction is essential for regulatory planning and greener powertrain design. This study presents an integrated FA–ACO–LSTM framework for estimating vehicle-level tailpipe CO₂ emissions from standard vehicle descriptors. The contribution lies in combining leakage-aware Binary Firefly feature selection, mixed-variable FA–ACO hyperparameter tuning, repeated-run evaluation, and post hoc interpretability into a unified prediction pipeline. Using a public fuel-consumption dataset sourced from the official Canadian vehicle fuel-consumption and emissions registry (open government portal) (22,556 vehicles), we adopt a 70/20/10 train/validation/test split and perform wrapper-based feature selection with Binary Firefly Algorithm (BFA). The FA–ACO scheduler jointly tunes hyperparameters for five candidate learners (LSTM, CNN, GRU, MLP, TabNet) and selects the best. FA–ACO-LSTM attains MSE = 0.0099, MAE = 0.0791, MedAE = 0.0664, MAPE = 0.84%, and R² = 98.71% on the test set, surpassing FA–ACO-CNN (R² = 93.55%), FA–ACO-GRU (90.03%), FA–ACO-MLP (87.60%), and FA–ACO-TabNet (84.88%). Compared with untuned baselines, FA–ACO improves LSTM from R² = 96.53% to 98.71% and reduces MSE by 45%. BFA yields the lowest average error among feature selectors versus BMWO, BFO, and BGWO, and an ANOVA across models confirms significance (F = 30.2, p < 0.0001). These results represent a fuel-consumption-informed prediction scenario because the selected feature set includes COMB (L/100 km), HWY (L/100 km), and COMB (mpg), which are strongly associated with certified CO₂ emissions. When all fuel-consumption indicators were excluded before feature selection and training, model performance decreased from to . The ablation confirms that fuel-consumption proxies account for a substantial part of the main predictive performance, while non-consumption vehicle attributes retain meaningful predictive value, and indicates that, under the adopted fixed feature-ordering representation and FA–ACO tuning protocol, LSTM achieved the best performance among the evaluated neural and tabular baselines. Introduction

Road transportation remains a major and growing source of energy-related greenhouse gas emissions worldwide, making accurate, fine-grained prediction of vehicle-level CO₂ essential for regulation, eco-design, and fleet analytics. Recent synthesis in high-impact venues underscores that transport decarbonization hinges on both technology shifts and data-driven operational improvements; for example, life-cycle analysis shows large, policy-relevant differences between electrified and petroleum pathways, reinforcing the need for reliable, localized emissions estimation to inform transitions and targeting1. At the same time, transport emissions have shifted from static factors to learning-based models that ingest vehicle descriptors, consumption metrics, and real-world operating signals. Deep-learning and ML pipelines trained on PEMS/trajectory data have achieved state-of-the-art on-road CO₂ rate estimation for light-duty fleets, demonstrating substantial accuracy gains over traditional formulations2. Complementing this, end-to-end deep models (with SHAP-based explanations) that predict vehicle CO₂ from official open-data attributes show that compact feature sets can attain very high out-of-sample R² and an interpretable ranking of drivers (engine size, fuel consumption, vehicle class)3.

Despite substantial progress in predicting vehicle CO₂ emissions, several practical challenges remain. First, many studies rely on highly correlated fuel-consumption indicators, which can improve predictive accuracy but may also serve as strong proxies for certified CO₂ emissions. Second, model performance is sensitive to preprocessing, feature selection, and hyperparameter configuration, making fair and reproducible comparison essential. Third, although recurrent neural networks and metaheuristic optimization have been studied previously4, fewer works provide a complete leakage-aware evaluation pipeline that jointly reports selected features, optimizer settings, repeated-run uncertainty, proxy-feature ablation, and interpretability under a controlled experimental protocol. These issues motivate the present study, which focuses on transparent pipeline construction and empirical evaluation rather than proposing a fundamentally new optimization algorithm.

This paper aims to develop a lightweight predictive framework to estimate vehicle tailpipe CO₂ emissions (g/km). The approach incorporates metaheuristic-driven optimization to simultaneously perform wrapper-based feature selection for dimensionality reduction and to tune the hyperparameters of candidate learning models. In addition, the LSTM networks are evaluated against other deep learning and tabular baselines under a consistent experimental protocol. Finally, the study emphasizes model interpretability through feature attribution methods and assesses the practical implications of the proposed framework for real-world deployment and policy-oriented decision-making.

The primary contribution of this study is an integrated FA–ACO–LSTM prediction framework rather than a fundamentally new standalone optimization algorithm. Within this framework, the Binary Firefly Algorithm is used for wrapper-based feature selection, while a coordinated FA–ACO scheduler tunes the mixed continuous, discrete, and categorical hyperparameters of the LSTM model. The Firefly component searches continuous variables, including learning rate, dropout, and regularization, whereas the Ant Colony component samples discrete choices, including optimizer type, batch size, and network depth.

The study makes three specific contributions. First, it develops a leakage-aware end-to-end pipeline that combines feature selection, mixed-variable hyperparameter optimization, repeated-run evaluation, and SHAP-based interpretation. Second, it evaluates the proposed FA–ACO–LSTM framework against untuned neural models, conventional search strategies, competitive tabular learners and established metaheuristic–LSTM hybrids, including PSO-LSTM, WOA-LSTM, GWO-LSTM and MSPSO-LSTM, under matched experimental conditions. Third, it investigates the dependence of predictive performance on fuel-consumption proxy variables via a dedicated feature ablation experiment. Because the dataset is cross-sectional, the LSTM is interpreted as a nonlinear feature-interaction learner rather than as a temporal forecasting model.

Section 2 reviews recent learning-based vehicle-emission prediction and metaheuristic optimization for environmental forecasting. Section 3 details the dataset, preprocessing, feature selection, model architectures, and FA–ACO tuning , as well as the experimental settings and metrics. Section 4 reports results, ablations, and interpretability, and discusses implications and limitations. Section 5 concludes and outlines future work.

Related works

Several studies have explored the prediction of vehicular and sectoral CO₂ emissions using machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and metaheuristic optimization, emphasizing the importance of data-driven methods for environmental sustainability. Initial studies used conventional regression or standalone neural networks but struggled to capture complex nonlinearities in emissions data. Recent advancements include the use of temporal models such as LSTM, BiLSTM, and GRU, as well as ensemble learning and hybrid optimization strategies involving algorithms such as PSO, GWO, and Firefly for improved feature selection and hyperparameter tuning. Furthermore, explainable AI (XAI), spatio-temporal modeling, and lightweight deployment in IoT environments have become essential to address the need for interpretability and practical application.

Al-Nefaie and Aldhyani5 addressed the pressing issue of transportation-related carbon emissions by developing an artificial intelligence–driven framework for vehicle CO₂ prediction. Using a Kaggle dataset that included detailed technical attributes such as vehicle class, engine size, cylinders, transmission, fuel type, and consumption metrics, they implemented deep learning models based on LSTM and BiLSTM architectures. The study demonstrated that BiLSTM achieved superior accuracy (R² = 93.78) with low error values across multiple statistical indicators (MSE, RMSE, and correlation analysis), thereby confirming its robustness in capturing nonlinear dependencies between vehicle attributes and emission outcomes. Beyond predictive performance, the work contributed an original methodological approach for emissions forecasting, highlighting the potential of recurrent neural networks to support environmental decision-making.

Haghshenas et al.6 investigated the impact of driver behavior on fuel consumption and CO₂ emissions, highlighting the influence of driving style, speed, and acceleration. They collected 370 data samples through mobile sensing and OBD interfaces, assessing variables such as fuel flow, engine RPM, vehicle speed, and road grade. The study compared a conventional artificial neural network (ANN) with a hybrid deep learning model (LSTM-MVO). Results showed that the LSTM-MVO model significantly surpassed ANN in predictive capability for fuel consumption and emissions, contributing to a novel integration of metaheuristic optimization and temporal modeling to better understand driver behavior and support environmental pollution mitigation.

Liu et al.7 highlighted the transportation sector’s significant and growing share of greenhouse gas emissions, which currently account for around 25% of energy-related emissions and are increasing faster than industrial and power emissions, thereby hindering Paris Agreement targets. They introduced the X-MARL model, a predictive framework utilizing a Stacking approach to improve CO₂ emission forecasting accuracy. Trained on a Canadian dataset of 7,385 samples across 12 vehicle attributes, the model was assessed using MSE, RMSE, and R² metrics. Results indicated that X-MARL outperformed baseline methods, effectively capturing complex nonlinear relationships between vehicle characteristics and emissions.

Xie et al.8 proposed the MSCL-Attention architecture to improve the forecasting of automotive CO₂ emissions by combining multi-scale convolutional neural networks, long short-term memory networks, and multi-head self-attention mechanisms. This hybrid approach enhances feature extraction, models temporal dependencies, and improves representation learning, resulting in higher accuracy and robustness compared to state-of-the-art methods. The model’s benchmarks show superior performance, suggesting it is an effective tool for predicting vehicle CO₂ emissions, aiding emission-reduction strategies, and contributing to global climate change mitigation efforts.

Tena-Gago et al.9 tackled the challenge of predicting CO₂ emissions in hybrid vehicles, where fluctuating powertrain dynamics complicate accurate estimation. They proposed a novel LSTM-based framework, termed UWS-LSTM, supported by extensive optimization of input features across more than 20 parameters. The model achieved 97.5% accuracy, outperforming both conventional machine learning and ANN-based approaches. A key contribution lies in its practical deployment: the predictor was successfully embedded in a low-power IoT device installed in a commercial hybrid vehicle, delivering near-real-time predictions with an average latency of just 21.64 ms.

Liu et al.10 evaluated the U.S.-based Motor Vehicle Emission Simulator (MOVES) for use in China, where light-duty gasoline vehicles contribute to elevated air pollution. Their research, using data from portable emission measurement systems, revealed that the original MOVES model was ineffective in China. To improve this, they integrated MOVES with machine learning techniques and the Boruta algorithm for feature selection, significantly enhancing prediction accuracy. The hybrid model showed an R² improvement of 0.132 for CO₂ and 0.261 for NO compared to the baseline. This work illustrates how machine learning can adapt existing regulatory tools for local scenarios, yielding more dependable emission forecasts with minimal field data, and serves as a guide for enhancing MOVES applications outside the U.S. context.

Wei et al.11 addressed the simulation of transient vehicular CO₂ and NOx emissions, varying across vehicle types and exhaust systems. They created a Super-learner ensemble model using real driving data from nine China VI N2 vehicles, achieving R2 values of 0.71 for CO₂ and 0.82 for NOx, outperforming the MOVES-based method and reducing RMSE. SHAP-based analysis was utilized to interpret input feature contributions, highlighting pollutant-specific sensitivities.

Yang et al.12 developed a high-resolution framework for estimating on-road CO₂ emissions in urban areas, focusing on spatio-temporal variability at the road-segment level. Using a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model, the study simulated traffic volumes and predicted emissions with R2 values above 0.6 for most road types and an RMSE of 88 vehicles/h. The approach was applied to Chengdu’s Jinniu District, where the results revealed clear diurnal emission peaks during morning and evening commutes, with evening peaks dominating in magnitude, while morning peaks showed faster growth. The analysis also highlighted increased emissions on weekends and holidays under moderate weather conditions.

Kabir et al.13 investigated the influence of congestion at signalized intersections on vehicular fuel consumption, emphasizing the role of congestion in traffic management and energy efficiency. Leveraging cost-effective sensing technologies such as onboard diagnostics (OBD) and crowdsourced probe vehicle trajectory data, the study applied an LSTM neural network to model fuel consumption under diverse conditions, including free flow, stopping, acceleration, deceleration, and idling. The findings highlighted a clear association between total fuel consumption and intersection-level traffic operation parameters, particularly vehicle delay. By demonstrating that LSTM can accurately predict fuel consumption from trajectory data, the study provides a scalable approach to understanding fuel-economy impacts at intersections, with implications for the design of intelligent transportation systems and optimized signal control strategies.

Blades et al.14 examined the impact of route-level parameters on the prediction of energy consumption for next-generation battery-electric buses, with the goal of supporting more efficient zero-emission transport planning. The authors analyzed 24 characteristic parameters, grouped into scenarios that reflect varying levels of operational information available to bus operators. Using multiple feature selection techniques, they identified the parameters that most strongly influence energy consumption and developed regression-based predictive models validated on both standardized and real-world drive cycles. The models achieved mean absolute percentage differences ranging from 2.10% to 10.67%, underscoring their accuracy and robustness. The study contributes a novel data-driven methodology for estimating the energy use of electric buses, offering practical guidance for operators, policymakers, and energy providers in the pursuit of public transport decarbonization.

Mądziel et al.15 investigated CO₂ emissions and vehicle energy consumption at high-traffic urban intersections, where conventional macro-, meso-, and micro-scale emission models often fail to capture the complexity of stop-and-go driving. To address this gap, they developed predictive models using a range of machine learning techniques, including linear regression, LASSO, Ridge regression, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), with XGBoost achieving the highest accuracy. The density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) algorithm was applied to segment intersection-specific data, enabling more targeted analysis. Real-world driving data were collected via portable emissions measurement systems and a Hioki 3390 power analyzer, and the models were further validated through Vissim-based simulations. This study contributes a refined methodology for accurately representing emissions and energy consumption in intersection environments, supporting more effective road infrastructure planning, traffic management, and strategies for emission reduction in urban transport networks.

Hussain et al.16 investigated advanced machine learning methods for predicting electric vehicle (EV) energy consumption, a critical task for effective grid management as EV adoption accelerates. Using historical driving data from Colorado, the study compared eleven models, including linear regressors, tree-based ensembles, and non-linear approaches such as MLP, XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM. Among these, the Extra Trees Regressor achieved the best performance, with an R2 of 0.9592, outperforming other models on the MAE, MSE, and RMSE metrics. Gradient Boosting and KNN also performed competitively, although with slightly greater variability. The results showed that while non-linear and ensemble methods capture intricate consumption patterns better than linear models, they still have limitations in accurately modeling extreme values. This contribution lies in systematically benchmarking a wide range of machine learning algorithms for EV energy prediction, highlighting the superiority of ensemble methods and identifying challenges that need to be addressed to enhance robustness in real-world applications.

Hussain et al.17 proposed a novel data-driven framework for predicting electric vehicle (EV) energy consumption, addressing the complexity introduced by heterogeneous driving conditions, vehicle specifications, and environmental factors. Using an extensive real-world dataset from Colorado, the study introduced three key innovations: the systematic comparison of four hyperparameter optimization techniques (GridSearchCV, RandomizedSearchCV, Optuna, and Particle Swarm Optimization) for KNN regression, the development of a stacking hybrid ensemble that combines KNN with tree-based learners, and the application of temporal feature engineering for performance enhancement. Results showed that the stacking ensemble achieved the highest predictive accuracy, with an R2 of 0.9601 and the lowest error metrics (MAE = 0.646, RMSE = 1.789). Among the optimization methods, Optuna proved to be the most effective for tuning KNN. This work advances EV energy consumption modeling by demonstrating the complementary value of ensemble learning, temporal features, and advanced optimization methods, offering a deployable tool for manufacturers and policymakers to support sustainable energy management and infrastructure planning.

Viji et al.18 introduced STO-IWGAN, a novel hybrid optimization framework designed to enhance energy management and operational efficiency in fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs). The method integrates the Siberian Tiger Optimization (STO) algorithm for parameter tuning of the fuel cell arrangement with an improved Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network (IWGAN) to predict vehicle power demand. Implemented and tested in MATLAB, the STO-IWGAN framework was benchmarked against other optimization methods, including heap-based optimization (HBO), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and wild horse optimization (WHO). Results demonstrated a superior efficiency of 95%, significantly outperforming HBO (85%), PSO (75%), and WHO (65%), thereby extending fuel cell lifespan, reducing fuel consumption, and improving hybrid system utilization.

Hu et al.19 addressed the challenge of accurately forecasting CO₂ emissions in the building industry, where robust predictive tools are needed to support sustainable development. They proposed a hybrid framework that integrates a multi-strategy improved particle swarm optimization (MSPSO) algorithm with an LSTM model. The MSPSO enhances standard PSO through tent chaotic mapping, mutation of the least-fit particles, and random perturbation strategies, and its robustness was verified on 23 benchmark test functions. Applied to CO₂ emission data from the Yangtze River Delta region, the MSPSO-LSTM achieved an R2 of 0.9677, 10% higher than conventional BP, LSTM, and CNN models, and also surpassed other hybrid metaheuristic-LSTM combinations such as PSO-LSTM, GWO-LSTM, and WOA-LSTM. With significantly reduced MAE and MSE, the model demonstrates superior accuracy and generalization.

Wang et al.20 addressed the challenge of accurately predicting exhaust emissions from heavy-duty diesel vehicles under China VI regulatory standards. Using portable emissions measurement system (PEMS) data collected from an N2-category vehicle, the study applied grey relational analysis and principal component analysis to refine and reduce the input parameters, thereby improving input quality. The authors developed a compact yet powerful Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) neural network and further optimized it using a hybrid strategy that combined Bayesian optimization with k-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning. To improve training stability and search efficiency, a dual learning-rate adjustment mechanism that integrates cosine annealing and exponential decay was employed. The resulting BO-GRU model achieved strong predictive performance, with an R2 of 0.9569 and an RMSE of 1.2352 mg s-1, while maintaining a small-scale architecture with good generalization. This work presents a novel, resource-efficient approach to real-time monitoring of NOx emissions in heavy-duty diesel vehicles, advancing the prospects for online, precise emissions management.

Chen et al.21 investigated carbon emissions in China’s logistics industry, a major contributor to the tertiary sector’s environmental footprint and an essential focus for achieving the country’s “Dual Carbon” targets. To identify the most influential factors, the study first employed gray relational analysis (GRA) and then developed a hybrid predictive framework by combining support vector regression (SVR) with particle swarm optimization (PSO) for hyperparameter tuning. Using sectoral data spanning 2000 to 2021, the PSO-SVR model achieved a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 0.82%, demonstrating superior predictive accuracy compared with conventional methods. In addition to methodological innovation, the study provided practical recommendations for restructuring the logistics industry and reducing emissions.

Previous studies indicate a shift in vehicle CO₂ and fuel consumption prediction from conventional regression and tree-based models to advanced deep learning and hybrid optimization frameworks. Traditional machine-learning methods such as Random Forest, XGBoost, SVR, and ensemble regressors perform well with structured vehicle variables but suffer from reduced accuracy due to nonlinear relationships among descriptors, fuel consumption, and emissions.

In contrast, some studies emphasize deep learning models such as LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and CNN-LSTM to capture complex nonlinear and temporal dependencies in vehicle energy consumption data. These models enhance predictive accuracy, particularly when emission behaviors correlate with sequential driving patterns and traffic conditions. Nonetheless, many rely on fixed feature sets and manually selected hyperparameters, which may compromise robustness and reproducibility. Others explore metaheuristic and hybrid optimization methods such as PSO, GWO, and Bayesian optimization for feature selection and hyperparameter tuning to navigate complex search spaces. However, existing studies often treat these processes separately, limiting their integration into the prediction pipeline. The present study innovatively combines wrapper-based Binary Firefly Algorithm feature selection with a hybrid Firefly–Ant Colony Optimization tuning strategy. This integrated approach focuses on predictive accuracy, compact feature representation, and robust evaluation methods, marking a departure from the traditional single-model evaluation. The novelty lies in the framework’s ability to enable fair comparisons across various architectures, including LSTM-based models, while ensuring statistical validation and post hoc interpretability for vehicle-level emission modeling.

Methodology

Dataset

This study uses the official Canadian vehicle fuel-consumption and emissions registry (open government portal)22, which reports certified test results for light-duty vehicles sold in Canada. The working corpus comprises 22,556 vehicle records spanning multiple model years and manufacturers. Each record includes catalog attributes (e.g., make, model, class, engine, transmission, fuel) and laboratory-measured fuel consumption under standardized city/highway cycles, with the corresponding certified tailpipe CO₂ emissions (g/km) used as the regression target.

Table 1 summarizes the descriptive statistics of the dataset used in this study. The corpus consists of 22,556 vehicle configurations, with engine sizes ranging from 0.8 to 8.4 L (mean: 3.36 L) and cylinder counts ranging from 2 to 16 (mean: 5.85). Transmission codes are diverse (mean category count ≈ 13.7), reflecting the broad coverage of manual, automatic, and CVT systems. Fuel consumption varies widely: city cycle consumption averages 12.76 L/100 km (min 3.5, max 30.6), highway averages 8.91 L/100 km, and combined consumption averages 11.03 L/100 km. The corresponding combined fuel economy (mpg) ranges from 11 to 78 mpg. These values highlight the dataset’s heterogeneity in vehicle specifications and operational efficiency, providing a robust basis for predictive modeling of CO₂ emissions.

Data preparation

All preprocessing was performed under a train-only fit-and-test-time-transform paradigm to prevent information leakage; random seeds were fixed to ensure reproducibility. We began with schema validation to enforce column names, data types, and measurement units (e.g., L/100 km, mpg, g/km), followed by unit-consistency checks, including the inverse relationship between combined fuel consumption in L/100 km and its mpg counterpart. Rows violating the tolerance for this identity were flagged for inspection. Exact duplicates across the tuple (MAKE, MODEL, VEHICLE CLASS, ENGINE SIZE, CYLINDERS, TRANSMISSION, FUEL, CITY L/100, HWY L/100, COMB L/100) were removed to avoid over-representation of repeated configurations.

The target variable was certified CO₂ emissions measured in g/km. Before feature selection, the target itself and any variables directly derived from it were excluded from the predictor set. In the principal fuel-consumption-informed experiment, however, the recorded fuel-consumption variables were retained in the BFA candidate pool. These variables included city fuel consumption, HWY (L/100 km), COMB (L/100 km), and COMB (mpg). COMB (mpg) is an inverse unit representation of COMB (L/100 km) and therefore does not constitute an independent physical measurement. Its retention in the main candidate pool is explicitly disclosed and should not be interpreted as removal of deterministic redundancy. A separate fuel-consumption-purged experiment excluded all four consumption indicators before feature selection and model training.

Although the registry was largely complete, occasional missing values were handled using statistics estimated exclusively from the training data. Continuous variables were imputed using the training median and categorical variables using the training mode. The fitted transformations were then applied unchanged to the validation and test subsets.

Outliers were diagnosed using robust (MAD-based) z-scores and visually inspected using distribution, boxplot, and pairplot figures. Our policy was to retain physically plausible extremes to preserve real-world variability while capping clear measurement artifacts via two-sided winsorization at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles (thresholds learned on the training split). Because neural models are sensitive to heavy-tailed inputs, continuous variables were transformed using a RobustScaler (median/IQR) fit on the training data only; tree-based models consumed raw-scale features without normalization.

The 0.5th and 99.5th percentile winsorization thresholds were selected as a conservative, robust preprocessing strategy. Rather than deleting extreme observations, winsorization caps only the most extreme tails of the distribution, thereby reducing the influence of potential measurement artifacts while retaining physically plausible high- and low-emission vehicle configurations. The thresholds were estimated from the training split only and then applied unchanged to the validation and test sets to avoid information leakage23,24.

For high-cardinality categorical identifiers such as MAKE and MODEL, out-of-fold target encoding was used because direct one-hot encoding would produce sparse high-dimensional representations. Target encoding has been widely recommended for high-cardinality categorical attributes, but it may introduce target leakage if category-level target statistics are computed from the same samples used for encoding. Therefore, the encoding values in this study were estimated in an out-of-fold manner within the training data: for each fold, category statistics were computed only from the remaining folds and then applied to the held-out fold. The final encoders were subsequently refitted on the full training set and applied to validation and test data without using their target values25,26.

Categorical variables were encoded according to their cardinality and fitted strictly within the training workflow to avoid information leakage. Low- and medium-cardinality variables, including VEHICLE CLASS, FUEL, and the parsed TRANSMISSION type, were one-hot encoded using categories observed only in the training data. Categories that appeared in validation or test data but were not observed during training were mapped to an “unknown” category. High-cardinality categorical identifiers, particularly MAKE and MODEL, were encoded using smoothed out-of-fold target/count encoding rather than direct one-hot encoding, because direct one-hot encoding would produce sparse high-dimensional representations and unstable category effects.

The 70/20/10 train/validation/test partition was adopted to provide a sufficiently large training subset for model fitting, a separate validation subset for early stopping and hyperparameter selection, and an untouched test subset for final generalization assessment. This split follows common supervised-learning validation practice, where model selection and final performance reporting are separated to reduce optimistic bias27,28. Stratification was performed with respect to VEHICLE CLASS and FUEL to preserve the dataset’s main distributional structure across the three subsets. Moreover, splitting was performed at the vehicle-configuration level to reduce the likelihood that duplicate or near-duplicate configurations appear simultaneously in the training and test partitions, which could otherwise lead to data leakage and an overestimated performance estimate29. Therefore, all preprocessing, feature selection, and model tuning operations were fitted only within the training/validation workflow and were not allowed to access the test labels.

Model hyperparameters for the candidate learners (LSTM, CNN/GRU, MLP, TabNet, and tree ensembles) were tuned via a population-based scheduler within the training+validation data, with early stopping on validation MAE. To ensure reproducibility and control leakage, the complete preprocessing pipeline was implemented as fold-aware. In each cross-validation iteration, imputation statistics, winsorization thresholds, scaling parameters, one-hot encoding categories, target/count encoding statistics, redundancy filtering decisions, and feature-selection masks were estimated only from the fold-specific training subset. The fitted transformations were then applied to the fold-specific validation subset. The independent test set was not used during preprocessing design, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or early stopping. After the final model configuration was selected, the preprocessing pipeline was refitted using the training data and applied once to the untouched test set for final evaluation.

Hyperparameter optimization (Firefly–Ant Colony Optimization)

Hyperparameter optimization is a valuable step in ensuring the robustness and efficiency of machine learning models, particularly for complex, non-convex problems such as vehicle CO₂ emission prediction. Traditional optimization methods, like grid search or random search, often fail to effectively explore the vast and mixed-type hyperparameter space, resulting in suboptimal models or high computational cost. To overcome these challenges, the hybrid Firefly–Ant Colony Optimization (FA–ACO) strategy has emerged as a powerful metaheuristic approach. Firefly Algorithm (FA) is used to tune continuous hyperparameters, such as learning rate, weight decay, and dropout probability, by exploiting the concept of attractiveness and light intensity to guide candidate solutions toward promising regions. At the same time, Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) efficiently handles discrete and categorical hyperparameters, such as optimizer selection, batch size, and network depth, by probabilistically constructing solutions based on pheromone trails and heuristic information. This coupling enables a synergistic exploration of both continuous and discrete subspaces, maintaining a balance between global search and local refinement.

The FA–ACO design was adopted because the hyperparameter space contains both continuous variables and discrete/categorical variables. The Firefly Algorithm is suitable for continuous search variables such as learning rate, dropout, recurrent dropout, and L2 regularization because candidate solutions can move through a bounded continuous space via attractiveness and randomized exploration31. In contrast, Ant Colony Optimization is naturally suited to discrete and combinatorial decisions, such as optimizer type, batch size, number of layers, and bidirectionality, through pheromone-guided probabilistic sampling33,34. Combining these two mechanisms enables coordinated exploration of mixed hyperparameter spaces, which is preferable to manual tuning or exhaustive grid search when only a limited computational budget is available35. The optimization objective was computed through cross-validation and validation MAE with early stopping, ensuring that the selected configuration reflected generalization performance rather than training-set fit.

The FA–ACO hybridization not only improves convergence speed but also enhances the likelihood of identifying hyperparameter configurations that optimize accuracy, efficiency, and generalization simultaneously. By incorporating cross-validation and early stopping into the optimization loop, the method ensures that models are not only well-tuned but also robust to overfitting. Moreover, constraints such as maximum parameter count, computational time, and memory usage can be integrated directly into the optimization process, providing practical relevance for deployment. The proposed methodology

The dataset used in this study is cross-sectional and tabular; therefore, it does not contain a natural chronological sequence among vehicle records. The LSTM component was not intended to model temporal dependencies across vehicles or model years. Instead, after preprocessing and feature selection, each vehicle record was represented as a fixed-length ordered feature vector and reshaped into a sequence-like input for the LSTM. If features were retained after BFA feature selection, each sample was represented as , where the sequence length equals the number of selected features, and each timestep corresponds to one selected feature. The feature order was fixed across all samples according to the preprocessing pipeline and the selected feature list. Thus, the LSTM was used as a nonlinear feature-interaction learner over a fixed, ordered representation, rather than as a temporal forecasting model.

From there, the process proceeds to feature selection using the BFA, which leverages wrapper-based cross-validation to identify compact, informative subsets of attributes. This step enhances both parsimony and generalizability. The workflow then incorporates hyperparameter optimization via a coupled FA–ACO scheduler, which jointly optimizes both continuous and categorical hyperparameters in a coordinated search to identify well-balanced configurations.

Experimental setup

To rigorously evaluate the proposed FA–ACO–LSTM methodology for vehicle CO2 emission prediction, the dataset was preprocessed by imputing missing values, one-hot encoding categorical variables, and scaling continuous features. Following preprocessing, the BFA was applied to select a compact yet informative subset of features. Subsequently, the FA–ACO hybrid was employed to optimize LSTM hyperparameters, including learning rate, dropout rate, hidden layer size, and batch size. The optimized models were trained on the training set, validated via cross-validation to mitigate overfitting, and finally assessed on a held-out test set.

All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of VRAM, an Intel Xeon Silver 4214R CPU with 24 cores, and 128 GB of RAM, running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS and Python 3.10. The models were implemented in PyTorch 2.0, with CUDA 11.8 acceleration. Early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs was employed to avoid overfitting. To ensure statistical robustness, each experiment was repeated five times with different random seeds, and the results were averaged. Comparative baselines included CNN, GRU, MLP, and TabNet models, which were evaluated under identical data splits and preprocessing protocols to ensure fairness.

All candidate models were assessed under identical experimental conditions, with a uniform 70/20/10 train/validation/test split applied across LSTM, CNN, GRU, MLP, and TabNet. A consistent leakage-safe preprocessing pipeline was used, including missing-value imputation, categorical encoding, winsorization, and scaling as needed. The test set remained untouched during preprocessing, feature selection, early stopping, and hyperparameter tuning. For models requiring normalization, the same scaler fitted on the training set was applied to the validation and test sets, while TabNet used identical feature values and encodings without any additional test-set information.

The hyperparameter optimization process maintained consistency across models by assigning each FA–ACO-tuned learner the same maximum candidate evaluations, cross-validation folds, validation metric, and early-stopping patience. While the search space was tailored to architecture-specific parameters, the optimization budget was uniform to prevent bias towards the LSTM model. Tuning included parameters such as learning rate and dropout for all neural models, while architecture-specific aspects like filter counts for CNNs and units for GRUs/LSTMs were adjusted within similar ranges. This approach ensured that comparisons focused on model suitability rather than variations in preprocessing or tuning efforts.

To isolate the contribution of the hyperparameter optimizer, four established metaheuristic–LSTM baselines were implemented: PSO-LSTM, WOA-LSTM, GWO-LSTM and MSPSO-LSTM. In all five hybrid models, including FA–ACO–LSTM, the LSTM implementation, input features, preprocessing transformations, train/validation/test indices, hyperparameter search space, validation objective, early-stopping criterion and maximum number of candidate evaluations were held constant. Each optimizer was allocated 100 objective-function evaluations and was executed using the same five random seeds. Hyperparameters were selected exclusively from the training and validation data, and the held-out test set was used only once for final evaluation.

The comparison therefore examines optimizer performance rather than differences in model architecture, feature information, or computational budget. Results are reported as the mean and standard deviation across the five matched runs. Pairwise comparisons against FA–ACO–LSTM were conducted using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, followed by Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Adjusted p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Optimization runtime and the number of objective evaluations were also recorded to compare predictive performance with computational cost.

Nevertheless, the result is technically plausible because the retained catalog and technical attributes encode several physical and operational determinants of emissions. ENGINE SIZE and CYLINDERS characterize engine capacity and are associated with fuel demand; VEHICLE CLASS provides indirect information about vehicle mass, body configuration, and operating requirements; TRANSMISSION influences drivetrain efficiency and engine operating conditions; and FUEL identifies differences in fuel properties and carbon intensity. The nonlinear relationships among these attributes allow the model to explain a substantial proportion of the variation in CO₂ emissions even when direct fuel-consumption measurements are unavailable.

From an application perspective, the model may be useful as a preliminary screening tool when only catalog or design-stage specifications are available, for example, during early vehicle development or before standardized fuel-consumption test results are obtained. In such a setting, the model could assist with initial emissions ranking, identifying potentially high-emitting configurations, or prioritizing vehicles for further testing. This potential use should, however, be interpreted cautiously. The present experiment does not validate a real pre-certification deployment workflow, and an of 88.08% does not by itself establish regulatory suitability, calibration at decision thresholds, or reliability for individual vehicles. The model should therefore be considered a complementary screening instrument rather than a substitute for standardized emissions certification.

Limitations

Although the proposed FA–ACO–LSTM framework achieved strong predictive performance on the Canadian vehicle fuel-consumption and CO₂ emissions registry, its external validity should be interpreted with caution. The dataset reflects a specific national vehicle market, certification procedure, fuel composition, regulatory context, and fleet distribution. Therefore, the learned relationships between vehicle descriptors, fuel-consumption indicators, and certified CO₂ emissions may not transfer directly to other countries or regions where vehicle classes, engine technologies, emissions standards, testing cycles, driving conditions, and fuel properties differ. For example, fleets dominated by hybrid vehicles, electric vehicles, diesel vehicles, heavy-duty vehicles, or region-specific powertrain technologies may exhibit emission patterns that differ from those represented in the Canadian dataset. Consequently, the present results demonstrate strong internal validity under a controlled experimental protocol, but they should not be interpreted as universal evidence of performance across all vehicle markets. Future work should therefore evaluate the proposed framework on independent external datasets from different countries, regulatory agencies, and vehicle fleets. Such validation would allow direct assessment of cross-domain robustness under different emissions standards, certification cycles, and market distributions. In addition, domain adaptation or transfer-learning strategies could be investigated to adapt the trained model to new regions with limited labeled data. External validation across multiple datasets would provide stronger evidence that the proposed optimization and prediction framework can support broader policy, fleet-management, and regulatory applications beyond the Canadian context.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study proposed an FA–ACO–LSTM framework for vehicle-level CO₂ emission prediction, using data from the Canadian fuel-consumption and emissions registry. Under the adopted leakage-safe preprocessing protocol, BFA-based feature selection, FA–ACO hyperparameter tuning, and repeated-run evaluation, the proposed model achieved the best performance among the evaluated models, with MSE = 0.0099, MAE = 0.0791, MedAE = 0.0664, MAPE = 0.84%, and on the held-out test set. Statistical testing further supported the significance of the observed differences among optimized models. The highest reported performance, , was obtained under a fuel-consumption-informed prediction scenario and should not be interpreted as proxy-free prediction based solely on independent vehicle specifications. When all fuel-consumption indicators were removed, the model achieved , confirming that the consumption variables explain a substantial proportion of the original predictive accuracy. At the same time, the purged experiment demonstrates that non-consumption technical and categorical vehicle attributes retain meaningful predictive value. Based on the findings, several recommendations can be drawn for both research and practice. First, policymakers and automotive stakeholders should adopt metaheuristic-optimized deep learning models, such as FA–ACO–LSTM, to support evidence-based decision-making in the design of emission standards and fleet management strategies. Second, researchers are encouraged to expand the scope of this work by integrating real-time vehicular IoT data and multi-pollutant emissions analysis to enhance environmental monitoring frameworks. Additionally, extending the FA–ACO optimization to hybrid or ensemble models could further improve predictive performance and generalization. Finally, deploying lightweight versions of the proposed models on smart vehicles and edge devices can provide real-time, interpretable predictions that directly reduce transport sector emissions, aligning with global sustainability and climate change mitigation goals. Although the proposed model demonstrated high predictive accuracy on the Canadian vehicle emissions dataset, future work will extend the evaluation to independent international datasets and heterogeneous vehicle fleets to verify external validity under different emissions standards, fleet compositions, and regulatory testing protocols. Future work should focus on extensions that arise directly from the limitations of the present study. First, the framework should be evaluated on independent vehicle datasets from different countries, regulatory testing cycles, fuel compositions, and fleet distributions to assess external validity. Second, a redundancy-controlled feature-selection experiment should restrict deterministic alternative representations, such as COMB (L/100 km) and COMB (mpg), from being selected simultaneously. Third, the fuel-consumption-purged model should be evaluated prospectively as a preliminary screening tool using only vehicle catalog and technical specifications available before formal fuel-consumption testing. Further work should also examine prediction uncertainty, calibration across vehicle classes and fuel types, and robustness under changes in vehicle technology and regulatory standards.
