Critical point search and linear response theory for computing electronic excitation energies of molecular systems. Part I: General framework, application to Hartree-Fock and DFT
Laura Grazioli, Yukuan Hu, Eric Cancès

Abstract
Computing excited states of many-body quantum Hamiltonians is a fundamental challenge in computational physics and chemistry, with state-of-the-art methods broadly classified into variational (critical point search) and linear response approaches. This article introduces a unified framework based on the Kähler manifold formalism, which naturally accommodates both strategies for a wide range of variational models, including Hartree-Fock, CASSCF, Full CI, and adiabatic TDDFT. In particular, this formalism leads to a systematic and straightforward way to obtain the final equations of linear response theory for nonlinear models, which provides, in the case of mean-field models (Hartree-Fock and DFT), a simple alternative to Casida’s derivation. We detail the mathematical structure of Hamiltonian dynamics on Kähler manifolds, establish connections to standard quantum chemistry equations, and provide theoretical and numerical comparisons of excitation energy computation schemes at the Hartree-Fock level.

Hamiltonian dynamics on Kähler manifolds

In this section, we intend to provide a brief introduction to Kähler manifolds and associated Hamiltonian dynamics. We refer interested readers to e.g., the monographs
45
,
46
,
47
.

A Kähler manifold is a complex manifold
\mathcal{M}
of complex dimension
n={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{M})
, endowed with a positive definite Hermitian form
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle_{\bullet}
, which allows one to endow
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
(the manifold
\mathcal{M}
seen this time as a real
2n
-dimensional manifold) with

1.

a Riemannian structure with metric defined by

\forall u,v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad g_{x}(u,v):=\mbox{Re}(\langle u,v\rangle_{x});

2.

a symplectic structure with symplectic form defined by

\forall u,v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\omega_{x}(u,v):=\mbox{Im}(\langle u,v\rangle_{x}).

The symplectic operator
J_{x}:T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
is defined by

\forall u,v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad g_{x}(u,v)=\omega_{x}(u,J_{x}v).

It is easy to check that, for any
u
,
v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
,

g_{x}(J_{x}u,J_{x}v)=g_{x}(u,v),\quad\omega_{x}(J_{x}u,J_{x}v)=\omega_{x}(u,v),\quad\omega_{x}(u,v)=-g_{x}(u,J_{x}v).

(7)

Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth real-valued function on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote respectively by

d\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T^{*}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}

the differential of
\mathcal{E}
and its Riemannian gradient. For each
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
,
d\mathcal{E}(x)
is the unique
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear form on
T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that for any smooth path
c:[-1,1]\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
drawn on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
c(0)=x
and
\dot{c}(0)=v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathcal{E}(c(t))=\mathcal{E}(x)+t\,d\mathcal{E}(x)(v)+o(t),

and
{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}E(x)
the unique vector of
T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\forall v\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad d\mathcal{E}(x)(v)=g_{x}\left({\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x),v\right).

The Hamiltonian dynamics on the symplectic manifold
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},J_{\bullet})
is defined by

{\frac{dx}{dt}(t)=J_{x(t)}{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x(t))}.

(8)

The equilibrium points (steady states) satisfy

{{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})=0}

(9)

and the LR around an equilibrium point
x_{\star}
consists in studying the linearized dynamics around
x_{\star}
given by

{\frac{dy}{dt}(t)=J_{x_{\star}}{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})y(t)},

(10)

where
{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star}):T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
is the Riemannian Hessian of
\mathcal{E}
at
x_{\star}
. Recall that
{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})
is the unique symmetric
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear operator on
T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all smooth paths
c:[-1,1]\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
c(0)=x_{\star}
and
\dot{c}(0)=v\in T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathcal{E}(c(t))=\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\,g_{x_{\star}}(v,{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})v)+o(t^{2}),

and that symmetric means

\forall u,v\in T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad g_{x_{\star}}(u,{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})v)=g_{x_{\star}}({\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})u,v).

Eq. (
10
) is a Hamiltonian dynamics on the symplectic space
(T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},J_{x_{\star}})
with quadratic Hamiltonian

\mathfrak{h}_{x_{\star}}(y):=\frac{1}{2}g_{x_{\star}}(y,{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})y).

Let
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}
for
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle_{x_{\star}}
, i.e., a family of
n
vectors of
T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}
such that

\langle u_{j},u_{j^{\prime}}\rangle_{x_{\star}}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}},

and
\mathcal{X}:={\rm Span}_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
the
n
-dimensional real-vector space spanned by the
u_{j}
’s. We have

T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathcal{X}\oplus_{\mathbb{R}}(J_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{X}),

and we infer from Eq. (
7
) that in the basis
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},{J_{x_{\star}}u_{1},\ldots,J_{x_{\star}}u_{n}})
of
T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
,
g_{x_{\star}}
is represented by the identity matrix
I_{2n}
,
J_{x_{\star}}
by the skew symmetric matrix

J:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The Hessian matrix
{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})
is represented by the real symmetric matrix

\mathfrak{H}_{\star}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H^{\rm rr}&H^{\rm ri}\\
H^{\rm ir}&H^{\rm ii}\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}_{\rm sym}

(11)

with entries

\displaystyle[H^{\rm rr}]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=g_{x_{\star}}(u_{j},{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})u_{j^{\prime}}),

(12)

\displaystyle[H^{\rm ri}]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=g_{x_{\star}}(u_{j},{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})(J_{x_{\star}}u_{j^{\prime}})),

(13)

\displaystyle[H^{\rm ir}]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=g_{x_{\star}}(J_{x_{\star}}u_{j},{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})u_{j^{\prime}}),

(14)

\displaystyle[H^{\rm ii}]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=g_{x_{\star}}(J_{x_{\star}}u_{j},{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})(J_{x_{\star}}u_{j^{\prime}})).

(15)

If
x_{\star}
is a nondegenerate local minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
, then the matrix
\mathfrak{H}_{\star}
is real symmetric positive definite (SPD). According to Williamson’s theorem
48
, there exists a symplectic matrix
S\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
(i.e., a matrix in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
such that
S^{T}JS=J
) such that

S^{T}\mathfrak{H}_{\star}S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Omega&0\\
0&\Omega\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\Omega:=\mbox{diag}(\omega_{1}^{\rm LR},\ldots,\omega_{n}^{\rm LR}),\quad 0<\omega_{1}^{\rm LR}\leq\cdots\leq\omega_{n}^{\rm LR}.

(16)

The
\omega_{j}^{\rm LR}
’s are called the symplectic eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{H}_{\star}
. It is easy to show that these quantities are independent of the chosen orthonormal basis
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
of
T_{x_{\star}}\mathcal{M}
. They are therefore intrinsic and are called the symplectic eigenvalues of
{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(x_{\star})
.

Denoting by
Y(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
the column vector containing the coefficients of
y(t)
in the basis
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},J_{x_{\star}}u_{1},\ldots,J_{x_{\star}}u_{n})
and by

Z(t):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Omega^{-1/2}&0\\
0&\Omega^{1/2}\end{array}\right)S^{-1}Y(t)=:\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q(t)\\
p(t)\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad q(t),p(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

we have

\frac{dZ}{dt}(t)=J\nabla\mathfrak{H}^{\rm HO}_{\star}(Z(t))\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathfrak{H}^{\rm HO}_{\star}(Z)=\mathfrak{H}^{\rm HO}_{\star}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q\\
p\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}q^{T}\Omega^{2}q.

The angular frequencies
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
therefore represent the excitation frequencies of the system in the linear response regime.

Let us finally consider the special case of separable Hamiltonians of the form

\mathfrak{H}_{\star}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H^{\rm rr}&0\\
0&H^{\rm ii}\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times 2n}_{\rm sym}.

(17)

Introducing the symplectic matrix

S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R&0\\
0&R^{-T}\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad R=(H^{\rm rr})^{-1/2}\left((H^{\rm rr})^{1/2}H^{\rm ii}(H^{\rm rr})^{1/2}\right)^{1/4},

we get

S^{T}\mathfrak{H}_{\star}S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\Omega}&0\\
0&\widetilde{\Omega}\end{array}\right),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widetilde{\Omega}=\left((H^{\rm rr})^{1/2}H^{\rm ii}(H^{\rm rr})^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}.

(18)

Diagonalizing
\widetilde{\Omega}
readily provides the symplectic eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{H}_{\star}
.

3
Dynamics on Grassmann manifolds (FCI, TDHF, TDDFT)

In this section, we restrict ourselves to the case when the Kähler manifold is the complex Grassmann manifold

\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(r,n)=\left\{\gamma\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\;|\;\gamma^{2}=\gamma,\;{\rm Tr\,}(\gamma)=r\right\},

(19)

where
r,n
are positive integers such that
1\leq r\leq n-1
(the trivial cases
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,n)=\{0\}
and
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(n,n)=\{I_{n}\}
are uninteresting). Such manifolds appear in quantum dynamics in at least two settings:

1.

pure-state dynamics of generic isolated finite-dimensional quantum systems described by the linear Schrödinger equation, or equivalently the linear quantum Liouville equation (see Section
3.3
). In computational quantum chemistry, this corresponds to the FCI level of theory;

2.

mean-field models for many-body fermionic systems. This framework encompasses the cases of both TDHF and adiabatic TDDFT, for which the dynamics is governed by a system of coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations, or equivalently a nonlinear quantum Liouville equation (see Section
3.4
).

Before elaborating on these two fundamental examples, the geometry and Kähler structure of the complex Grassmann manifold
{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(r,n)
are recalled in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, and the abstract Eqs. (
8
)-(
10
) are rewritten in a more concrete form in the special case when
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(r,n)
in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
. More details about Grassmann manifold can be found in the review articles in Refs.
49
,
50
and the monographs in Refs.
51
,
52

3.1
Geometry of complex Grassmann manifolds

The tangent space to the complex Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(r,n)
at some
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}
is given by

T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}=\{Q\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\;|\;\gamma Q+Q\gamma=Q\}.

Any
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}
can be decomposed as

\gamma=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)U^{*},\quad\mbox{with}\quad UU^{*}=U^{*}U=I_{n},

and we then have

T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}=\left\{\left.Q=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&X\\
X^{*}&0\end{array}\right)U^{*}\right|X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times m}\right\}\quad\mbox{with}\quad m:=n-r.

(20)

The Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}
can be endowed with the following Kähler structure:

•

in view of Eq. (
20
), the tangent space
T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}
can be identified with the complex vector space
{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times m}
, endowed with the Hermitian inner product

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M},\quad\forall Q_{1},Q_{2}\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M},\quad\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle_{\gamma}:=2{\rm Tr\,}(X_{1}^{*}X_{2})=2{\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{1}\gamma Q_{2}(1-\gamma)\right),

where
X_{j}
is the matrix of
{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times m}
such that

Q_{j}=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&X_{j}\\
X_{j}^{*}&0\end{array}\right)U^{*},\quad j=1,2;

(21)

•

Riemannian metric: using the properties

\gamma^{2}=\gamma,\quad(1-\gamma)^{2}=1-\gamma,\quad\forall Q\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\;\gamma Q+Q\gamma=Q\mbox{ i.e., }\gamma Q=Q(1-\gamma),

(22)

we get, for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
,

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}(Q_{1},Q_{2}):

\displaystyle={\rm Re}(\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle_{\gamma})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}(X_{1}^{*}X_{2})+{\rm Tr\,}(X_{1}X_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{1}\gamma Q_{2}(1-\gamma)\right)+{\rm Tr\,}\left((1-\gamma)Q_{2}\gamma Q_{1}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{1}\gamma Q_{2}\right)+{\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{1}(1-\gamma)Q_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}(Q_{1}Q_{2});

•

Symplectic form: we infer from Eq. (
22
) that, for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
,

\displaystyle\omega_{\gamma}(Q_{1},Q_{2}):

\displaystyle={\rm Im}(\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=-i{\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{1}(2\gamma-1)Q_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-i{\rm Tr\,}\left(Q_{2}(1-2\gamma)Q_{1}\right).

Note that, still using Eq. (
22
), we have

\displaystyle\overline{{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{1}(2\gamma-1)Q_{2})}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}(Q_{2}(2\gamma-1)Q_{1})=2{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{2}\gamma Q_{1})-{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{2}Q_{1})

\displaystyle=2{\rm Tr\,}((1-\gamma)Q_{2}Q_{1}(1-\gamma))-{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{2}Q_{1})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr\,}(Q_{1}(1-2\gamma)Q_{2})=-{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{1}(2\gamma-1)Q_{2}),

so that
{\rm Tr\,}(Q_{1}(2\gamma-1)Q_{2})
is an imaginary number.

A simple calculation shows that the symplectic operator
J_{\gamma}:T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\forall Q\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad J_{\gamma}Q:=-i[Q,\gamma].

(23)

In the representation (
21
), it reads

\forall Q=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&X\\
X^{*}&0\end{array}\right)U^{*}\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\qquad J_{\gamma}Q=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&iX\\
-iX^{*}&0\end{array}\right)U^{*}.

Recall that the orthogonal projector
P_{\gamma}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
(for the Frobenius inner product) has a simple expression:

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\forall M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm},\quad P_{\gamma}M:=\gamma M(1-\gamma)+(1-\gamma)M\gamma=[\gamma,[\gamma,M]],

(24)

and that in the representation (
21
),

\forall M=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
B^{*}&C\end{array}\right)U^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm},\quad P_{\gamma}M=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B\\
B^{*}&0\end{array}\right)U^{*}.

It follows by direct calculations that

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\forall M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm},\quad

\displaystyle i[\gamma,M]\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad[\gamma,[\gamma,[\gamma,M]]]=[\gamma,M].

(25)

Finally, the exponential map is given by

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad\forall Q\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad{\rm Exp}_{\gamma}(Q)=e^{[\gamma,Q]}\gamma e^{-[\gamma,Q]},

which in essence coincides with the exponential parametrization of molecular coefficients widely used in quantum chemistry
53
.

3.2
Hamiltonian dynamics on Grassmann manifolds

Let
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(r,n)
be a complex Grassmann manifold,
E:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to{\mathbb{R}}
an energy functional, smooth in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
, and
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
the restriction of
E
to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Denoting by
H_{\gamma}:=\nabla E(\gamma)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}
the gradient of
E
at
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
(for the Frobenius inner product), we have

{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma)=P_{\gamma}(\nabla E(\gamma))=P_{\gamma}(H_{\gamma})=[\gamma,[\gamma,H_{\gamma}]].

(26)

Using Eqs. (
23
) and (
25
), we get

\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad J_{\gamma}{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma)=-i[{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma),\gamma]=-i[[\gamma,[\gamma,H_{\gamma}]],\gamma]=-i[H_{\gamma},\gamma].

The symplectic dynamics (
8
) generated by the energy functional
\mathcal{E}
is thus given by

{\frac{d\gamma}{dt}(t)=-i[H_{\gamma(t)},\gamma(t)]\quad\mbox{(K\"{a}hler dynamics on Grassmann manifolds)}.}

(27)

Stationary points
\gamma_{\star}
are characterized by the equation

{[H_{\gamma_{\star}},\gamma_{\star}]=0}\quad\mbox{(critical points on Grassmann manifolds)}.

(28)

This equilibrium condition implies that there exists a unitary matrix
U_{\star}\in U(n)
such that

\gamma_{\star}=U_{\star}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{r}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)U_{\star}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad H_{\gamma_{\star}}=U_{\star}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}E^{r}&0\\
0&E^{m}\end{array}\right)U_{\star}^{*},

(29)

where
E^{r}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r\times r}_{\rm herm}
and
E^{m}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times m}_{\rm herm}
are real diagonal matrices.

As
\mathcal{M}
is a Riemannian submanifold of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}
, the Riemannian Hessian on
\mathcal{E}
is given by

\forall Q\in T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{\star})(Q)=P_{\gamma_{\star}}([D\mathcal{G}(\gamma_{\star})](Q)),

where
\mathcal{G}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}
is any smooth extension of
{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma)
in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
. In view of (
26
), an obvious extension is

\mathcal{G}(M):=[M,[M,\nabla E(M)]],

for which

[D\mathcal{G}(\gamma_{\star})](Q)=\underbrace{[Q,[\gamma_{\star},H_{\gamma_{\star}}]]}_{=0~\text{by Eq. \eqref{eq:critical_point_Grassmann}}}+\underbrace{[\gamma_{\star},[Q,H_{\gamma_{\star}}]]}_{\in T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\;\mbox{\rm if}\;Q\in T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}}+\underbrace{[\gamma_{\star},[\gamma_{\star},D^{2}E(\gamma_{\star})(Q)]]}_{=P_{\gamma_{\star}}(D^{2}E(\gamma_{\star})(Q))},

where
D^{2}E(\gamma_{\star}):{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}
is the second derivative of
E
at
\gamma_{\star}
. The Riemannian Hessian
{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{\star}):T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\to T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
is thus given by

{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{E}(\gamma_{\star})(Q)=[\gamma_{\star},[Q,H_{\gamma_{\star}}]]+P_{\gamma_{\star}}\left(D^{2}E(\gamma_{\star})(Q)\right).

(30)

Using Eqs. (
25
) and (
28
), we finally obtain that the linearized dynamics is given by

{\frac{dQ}{dt}(t)=-i[H_{\gamma_{\star}},Q]-i[D^{2}E(\gamma_{\star})Q,\gamma_{\star}]\quad\mbox{(linear response on Grassmann manifolds)}}

(31)

where we used the fact that Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) imply

\forall Q\in T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}},\quad J_{\gamma_{\star}}\left([\gamma_{\star},[Q,H_{\gamma_{\star}}]]\right)=-i[[\gamma_{\star},[Q,H_{\gamma_{\star}}]],\gamma_{\star}]=-i[H_{\gamma_{\star}},Q].

3.3
Linear energy functionals on projective spaces (FCI)

Consider an isolated quantum system with finite-dimensional state space
\mathcal{H}
. In the quantum chemistry applications we have in mind,
{\mathcal{H}}
is the FCI
N
-electron state-space generated by the
N_{\rm b}
atomic spin-orbitals of the discretization basis, and is therefore of dimension
n=\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N}
.

The space
\mathcal{H}
can be identified with
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by the choice of an orthonormal basis. The postulates of quantum mechanics imply that the set of FCI pure states of the system can be identified with the projective space
P(\mathcal{H})\cong{\rm CP}^{n-1}\cong{\rm Gr}(1,n)
. Recall that an element of
{\rm CP}^{n-1}
can be represented by

1.

a normalized vector
\psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, it being understood that
\psi
and
e^{i\theta}\psi
, with any
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, represent the same element of
{\rm CP}^{n-1}
. This representation is the usual one in quantum mechanics (wavefunction formalism);

2.

a non-zero vector
\widetilde{\psi}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, it being understood that
\widetilde{\psi}
and
\lambda\widetilde{\psi}
, with any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, represent the same element of
{\rm CP}^{n-1}
;

3.

a unique element
\Gamma\in{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,n)
. This representation is used in the density-matrix formulation of quantum mechanics.

The connection between these three representations is

\Gamma=\psi\psi^{*}=\frac{\widetilde{\psi}\widetilde{\psi}^{*}}{|\widetilde{\psi}|^{2}}\quad\mbox{or, using Dirac's bra-ket notation,}\quad\Gamma=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\frac{|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle\langle\widetilde{\psi}|}{\langle\widetilde{\psi}|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle}.

Let
H\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}
be the Hamiltonian matrix of the system in the chosen orthonormal basis of the FCI
N
-electron state-space. Let
E:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the linear energy functional defined by
E(\Gamma)={\rm Tr\,}(H\Gamma)
. The FCI energy functional is the restriction
\mathcal{E}
of
E
to
{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,n)
:

\mathcal{E}(\Gamma)={\rm Tr\,}(H\Gamma)=\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle=\frac{\langle\widetilde{\psi}|H|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle}{\langle\widetilde{\psi}|\widetilde{\psi}\rangle}.

The FCI approximation is thus a special case of the general framework described in Section
3.2
, corresponding to
r=1
,
n=\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N}
, and
E:{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}_{\rm herm}\to{\mathbb{R}}
linear. The dynamics (
27
) then reads

\frac{d\Gamma}{dt}(t)=-i[H,\Gamma(t)],\quad i\frac{d\psi}{dt}(t)=H\psi(t)-\beta(t)\psi(t),\quad i\frac{d\widetilde{\psi}}{dt}(t)=H\widetilde{\psi}(t)-\mu(t)\widetilde{\psi}(t),

(32)

where
\beta(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mu(t)\in{\mathbb{C}}
are gauge parameters.
Steady states (see Eq. (
28
)) are characterized by

[H,\Gamma_{\star}]=0,\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}H\psi_{\star}=E\psi_{\star},\\
\|\psi_{\star}\|=1,\\
\psi_{\theta}(t)=e^{i\theta(t)}\psi_{\star},\end{array}\right.,\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}H\widetilde{\psi}_{\star}=E\widetilde{\psi}_{\star},\\
\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(t)=e^{\lambda(t)}\psi_{\star}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, the local minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
are global and are the ground-states of
H
and the assumption that the ground-state
\Gamma_{\star}
is nondegenerate is equivalent to the assumption that the ground-state eigenvalue
E_{0}
of
H
is simple. Other critical points of
\mathcal{E}
are then exactly the excited states of
H
, corresponding to the energies
E_{1}\leq E_{2}\leq\cdots\leq E_{n-1}
, and the excitation energies determined by CP are
\omega^{\rm CP-FCI}_{k}=E_{k}-E_{0}
(
k=1,\ldots,n-1
).

Let us turn to LR. Due to the fact that the energy functional
E(\Gamma)={\rm Tr\,}(H\Gamma)
is linear in
\Gamma
, we have
H_{\Gamma_{\star}}=\nabla E(\Gamma_{\star})=H
and
D^{2}E(\Gamma_{\star})=0
. It follows that the linear response Eq. (
31
) around a steady state
\Gamma_{\star}=|\Psi_{\star}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\star}|
on
{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,n)
(with
H\Psi_{\star}=E_{0}\Psi_{\star}
) reads

\frac{dQ}{dt}(t)=-i[H,Q(t)]\quad\mbox{on}\quad T_{\Gamma_{\star}}{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(1,n)_{\mathbb{R}}.

(33)

Using an orthonormal basis of the FCI Hamiltonian, in which the first basis vector is
\Psi_{\star}
, to identify the discretized
N
-particle state space with
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
n=\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N}
, the representation in Eq. (
29
) for
r=1
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\star}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\Psi^{*}\\
\Psi&0\end{array}\right),\quad\Psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n-1},

\displaystyle-i[H,Q]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&((H|_{\Psi_{\star}^{\perp}}-E_{0})\Psi)^{*}\\
(H|_{\Psi_{\star}^{\perp}}-E_{0})\Psi&0\end{array}\right),

where
H|_{\Psi_{\star}^{\perp}}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{\rm herm}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
denotes the projection of
H
onto
\Psi_{\star}^{\perp}
. Consequently, Eq. (
33
) is equivalent to

i\frac{d\Psi}{dt}(t)=(H|_{\Psi_{\star}^{\perp}}-E_{0})\Psi(t)\quad\mbox{on}\quad\Psi_{\star}^{\perp},

which shows that the LR-FCI excitation energies are given as expected by
\omega^{\rm LR-FCI}_{k}=E_{k}-E_{0}
(
k=1,\ldots,n-1
).

In summary, we recover the well-known fact that both CP and LR are exact for linear quantum Hamiltonians (FCI level of theory in quantum chemistry).

3.4
Mean-field models (TDHF and adiabatic TDDFT)

Let us now consider the case of mean-field models. We will focus on general HF (GHF) models, in which non-collinear spin states are allowed. However, the formalism presented here can be easily adapted to restricted and unrestricted HF models, more widely used in applications, as well as restricted/unrestricted adiabatic TDDFT.

We still denote by
N_{\rm b}
the number of atomic spin-orbitals of the chosen discretization basis, and assume that these functions are real-valued, which is the case for standard atomic orbital basis sets. We also assume that the system is not subjected to an external magnetic field and that spin-orbit coupling terms are neglected. In this case, it makes sense to seek HF or Kohn-Sham ground states satisfying time-reversal symmetry, leading to real-valued HF or Kohn-Sham occupied orbitals, and real-symmetric Fock or Kohn-Sham matrices at the ground state. In the rest of the section, we work in an orthonormal basis of the one-particle state-space
\mathcal{H}
consisting of
N_{\rm b}
real occupied/virtual molecular spin-orbitals
(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N_{b}})
at the GHF ground state
\gamma_{\star}
with energies ranked in nondecreasing order. We denote by
h\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{\rm b}\times N_{\rm b}}_{\rm herm}
the one-electron Hamiltonian matrix with

h_{pq}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\{\alpha,\beta\}}\varphi_{p}(\bm{x})\widehat{h}\varphi_{q}(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}\bm{x},\quad\text{where}~\widehat{h}~\text{is the one-body Hamiltonian,}

and by

\displaystyle(pq|rs):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\{\alpha,\beta\}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\{\alpha,\beta\}}\frac{\overline{\varphi_{p}(\bm{x})}\varphi_{q}(\bm{x})\overline{\varphi_{r}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}\varphi_{s}(\bm{x}^{\prime})}{|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|}\,{\rm d}\bm{x}\,{\rm d}\bm{x}^{\prime}

the two-electron integrals in this basis. Here,
\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)
denotes the spatial and spin coordinates. Note that for the chosen basis, the complex conjugation in the above formula can be omitted as the basis functions are real. We also use the standard notation

(pq||rs):=(pq|rs)-(ps|rq).

The GHF energy functional
E^{\rm GHF}
is given by

E^{\rm GHF}(\gamma)={\rm Tr}(h\gamma)+\dfrac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(G(\gamma)\gamma),

where
G:{\mathbb{C}}_{\rm herm}^{N_{\rm b}\times N_{\rm b}}\to{\mathbb{C}}_{\rm herm}^{N_{\rm b}\times N_{\rm b}}
is defined as
G(\gamma)=J(\gamma)-K(\gamma)
, with
J,K:{\mathbb{C}}_{\rm herm}^{N_{\rm b}\times N_{\rm b}}\to{\mathbb{C}}_{\rm herm}^{N_{\rm b}\times N_{\rm b}}
the Coulomb and exchange functionals, respectively:

[J(\gamma)]_{pq}:=\sum_{r,s=1}^{N_{\rm b}}(pq|rs)\gamma_{sr},\quad[K(\gamma)]_{pq}:=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{N_{\rm b}}(ps|rq)\gamma_{sr}.

(34)

It follows that
H_{\gamma}:=\nabla E^{\rm GHF}(\gamma)=h+G(\gamma)
is the Fock matrix, that (
27
) coincides with the usual TDHF equations, and (
28
) with the McWeeny formulation of the HF equations.
In GHF theory, the no-unfilled shell property proved in Ref.
54
in the continuous setting also holds in the discretized setting. We therefore have that in the chosen basis of occupied/virtual real molecular spin-orbitals

\displaystyle\gamma_{\star}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{N}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad H_{\gamma_{\star}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})&0\\
0&{\rm diag}(\varepsilon_{N+1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N_{\rm b}})\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mbox{with}\quad\varepsilon_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{N}<\varepsilon_{N+1}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{N_{\rm b}}.

(39)

Let us now show that the Kähler formalism provide a simple alternative to Casida’s derivation of LR excitation energies for mean-field models. Following the systematic strategy described in Section
2
, it indeed suffices to select a basis

\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N(N_{\rm b}-N)},J_{\gamma_{\star}}u_{1},\ldots,J_{\gamma_{\star}}u_{N(N_{\rm b}-N)}\right)

of
T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
, build the matrix
\mathfrak{H}_{\star}
defined in Eq. (
11
) with entries given by Eqs. (
12
)-(
15
), and compute the symplectic eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{H}_{\star}
. Choosing the canonical basis of
T_{\gamma_{\star}}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
, that is

u_{[ia]}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&e_{[ia]}\\
e_{[ia]}^{T}&0\end{array}\right),\quad J_{\gamma_{\star}}u_{[ia]}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&ie_{[ia]}\\
-ie_{[ia]}^{T}&0\end{array}\right),\quad\mbox{with}\quad[e_{[ia]}]_{jb}=\delta_{(ia),(jb)},

for
1\leq i\leq N
and
N+1\leq j\leq N_{\rm b}
, we deduce from Eq. (
30
), the fact that
D^{2}E^{\rm GHF}(\gamma)(Q)=G(Q)
, and the real character of the spin-orbitals that

\mathfrak{H}_{\star}^{\rm GHF}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A+B&0\\
0&A-B\end{array}\right),

(40)

where the entries of the matrices
A
and
B
are given respectively by

A_{ai,bj}:=(\varepsilon_{a}-\varepsilon_{i})\delta_{ij}\delta_{ab}+(ai||jb),\quad B_{ai,bj}:=(ai||bj).

These matrices are the GHF analogues of the
A
and
B
matrices in LR-TDDFT Casida’s equations. The linearized dynamics Hamiltonian is therefore separable and the matrix
\widetilde{\Omega}
in Eq. (
18
) then reads in this specific case

\widetilde{\Omega}=\left((A+B)^{1/2}(A-B)(A+B)^{1/2}\right)^{1/2},

an expression in agreement with Ref.
55
Eqs. (II.78)-(II.79)
.

4
Theoretical analysis in the weakly-interacting regime

In this part, we aim to compare theoretically, in the weakly-interacting regime, the CP and LR formalisms for computing the electronic excitation energies at the FCI and HF levels of theory. We consider a family of Hamiltonians parametrized by an additional coupling parameter
\lambda
that modulates the degree of interaction within the energy functional. In the non-interacting limit (
\lambda=0
), HF and FCI lead to the same ground and excited state energies, so that CP-HF provides the same sequence of excitation energies as FCI, while LR-HF returns the subsequence of excitation energies corresponding to single excitations. Under a nondegeneracy assumption, we are able to compute the FCI (Section
4.1
), as well as CP-HF and LR-HF (Section
4.2
) excitation energies up to second-order errors in
\lambda
in the weakly-interacting regime (
|\lambda|\ll 1
). To our knowledge, this is the first study aiming at comparing the CP and LR methodologies from a theoretical point of view. These theoretical findings are substantiated by numerical results in Section
5
. We shall remark that although the cases of
\lambda\neq 1
are non-physical, our analysis could offer potential insights into the study of more complicated real molecules.

In this section, we consider a system with
N=2N_{\rm p}
electrons (
N_{\rm p}
is the number of electron pairs) in
N_{\rm b}
atomic orbitals (hence
2N_{\rm b}
atomic spin-orbitals) and the family of second-quantized Hamiltonians on the Fock space
{\rm Fock}({\mathbb{C}}^{2N_{\rm b}})
defined by

\widehat{H}_{\lambda}:=\widehat{h}+\lambda\widehat{V}=\sum_{\kappa,\lambda=1}^{N_{\rm b}}h_{\kappa\lambda}\sum_{\sigma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}}\widehat{a}_{\kappa\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda\sigma}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\kappa,\lambda,\mu,\nu=1}^{N_{\rm b}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\{\alpha,\beta\}}(\kappa\sigma,\mu\sigma|\lambda\sigma^{\prime},\nu\sigma^{\prime})\widehat{a}_{\kappa\sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\lambda\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\widehat{a}_{\nu\sigma^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{\mu\sigma},

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a coupling parameter. Let
(\phi_{n}^{0})_{1\leq n\leq N_{\rm b}}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{\rm b}}
diagonalizing the one-electron Hermitian matrix
h
, such that

h\phi_{n}^{0}=\varepsilon_{n}^{0}\phi_{n}^{0}\quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon_{1}^{0}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{N_{\rm b}}^{0}.

To avoid technicalities, we assume that

(H)

the eigenvalues of
h
are nondegenerate and the
\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}
real numbers obtained by summing up
N_{\rm p}
of the
N_{\rm b}
eigenvalues of
h
are all different.

4.1
Analysis at the FCI level of theory

It follows from Assumption (H) that the only degeneracies in the eigenstates of
\widehat{H}_{0}
in the
N
-particle sector are due to degenerate spin states. Restricting our analysis to the
S_{z}=0
spin sector, each state has
N_{\rm p}
spin-up electrons and
N_{\rm p}
spin-down electrons, the
N
-electron ground state of
\widehat{H}_{0}
is a nondegenerate singlet state given by

\Phi_{0}^{(0)}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\;(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\beta)\wedge\cdots\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}}^{0}\otimes\beta),

and the first excited state is two-fold degenerate, spanned by

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\;(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\beta)\wedge\cdots\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}-1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}-1}^{0}\otimes\beta)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}+1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}}^{0}\otimes\beta),

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}_{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\;(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{1}^{0}\otimes\beta)\wedge\cdots\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}-1}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}-1}^{0}\otimes\beta)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}}^{0}\otimes\alpha)\wedge(\phi_{N_{\rm p}+1}^{0}\otimes\beta).

Note that
\Phi^{(0)}_{\alpha}
and
\Phi^{(0)}_{\beta}
are not eigenfunctions of
\bm{S}^{2}
, but that

\Psi_{\rm S}^{(0)}:=(\Phi^{(0)}_{\alpha}-\Phi^{(0)}_{\beta})/\sqrt{2}
is a singlet state (the first-excited state in the
\bm{S}^{2}=0
sector) and
\Psi_{\rm T}^{(0)}:=(\Phi^{(0)}_{\alpha}+\Phi^{(0)}_{\beta})/\sqrt{2}
the
S_{z}=0
component of a triplet state (the ground state in the
\bm{S}^{2}=2
sector). The energies of the ground state
\Phi^{(0)}_{0}
and the two-fold degenerate first excited state are respectively

E^{(0)}_{0}=2\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}}\varepsilon_{i}^{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad E^{(0)}_{\alpha}=E^{(0)}_{\beta}=E^{(0)}_{\rm S}=E^{(0)}_{\rm T}=E^{(0)}_{0}+\varepsilon_{N_{\rm p}+1}-\varepsilon_{N_{\rm p}}.

It follows from standard nondegenerate perturbation theory that for small
\lambda
, the ground state of
\widehat{H}_{\lambda}
is nondegenerate and its energy
E_{0}(\lambda)
is a real-analytic function of
\lambda
. Likewise the first excited state in the
\bm{S}^{2}=0
sector as well as the ground state in the
\bm{S}^{2}=2
sector are nondegenerate, and their energies
E_{\rm S}(\lambda)
and
E_{\rm T}(\lambda)
are real-analytic functions of
\lambda
for
|\lambda|\ll 1
. Consequently, so are the FCI excitation energies
\omega_{\rm S}^{\rm FCI}(\lambda):=E_{\rm S}(\lambda)-E_{0}(\lambda)
and
\omega_{\rm T}^{\rm FCI}(\lambda):=E_{\rm T}(\lambda)-E_{0}(\lambda)
:

\omega_{\rm S}^{\rm FCI}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{k}\omega^{{\rm FCI},(k)}_{\rm S},\quad\omega_{\rm T}^{\rm FCI}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{k}\omega^{{\rm FCI}(k)}_{\rm T},

where

\omega^{{\rm FCI},(0)}_{\rm S}=\omega^{{\rm FCI},(0)}_{\rm T}=\omega^{(0)}_{\rm HOMO-LUMO}:=\varepsilon_{N_{\rm p}+1}-\varepsilon_{N_{\rm p}}.

The coefficients
\omega^{(1)}_{\rm S}
and
\omega^{(1)}_{\rm T}
can be easily obtained by first-order perturbation theory:

\displaystyle\omega^{{\rm FCI},(1)}_{\rm S/T}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}((N_{\rm p}+1)\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta||j\beta,j\beta)-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}(N_{\rm p}\beta,N_{\rm p}\beta||j\beta,j\beta)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}((N_{\rm p}+1)\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta|j\alpha,j\alpha)-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}(N_{\rm p}\beta,N_{\rm p}\beta|j\alpha,j\alpha)

\displaystyle\pm(N_{\rm p}\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta|(N_{\rm p}+1)\alpha,N_{\rm p}\alpha).

(41)

4.2
Analysis at the UHF level of theory

Let us now consider the UHF approximation, for which the underlying manifold is
\mathcal{M}={\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(N_{\rm p},N_{\rm b})\times{\rm Gr}_{\mathbb{C}}(N_{\rm p},N_{\rm b})
. The UHF approximation of
\widehat{H}_{\lambda}
gives rise to a parametrized family of energy functionals of the form

\displaystyle E^{\rm UHF}(\lambda,\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta})=

\displaystyle{\rm Tr\,}(h\gamma_{\alpha})+{\rm Tr\,}(h\gamma_{\beta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\rm Tr\,}(J(\gamma_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\beta})(\gamma_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\beta}))

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\rm Tr\,}(K(\gamma_{\alpha})\gamma_{\alpha})-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\rm Tr\,}(K(\gamma_{\beta})\gamma_{\beta}),

(42)

where
J
and
K
are defined in Eq. (
34
).

CP-UHF.
The critical points of
\mathcal{M}\ni(\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta})\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{\rm UHF}(0,\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta})={\rm Tr\,}(h\gamma_{\alpha})+{\rm Tr\,}(h\gamma_{\beta})\in{\mathbb{R}}
are the
(\gamma_{\alpha\star},\gamma_{\beta\star})\in\mathcal{M}
satisfying
[h,\gamma_{\alpha\star}]=[h,\gamma_{\beta\star}]=0
. Under the nondegeneracy assumption (H), these critical points are isolated and there are exactly
\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}\times\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}
of them. The ground state is

\left(\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}^{(0)},\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}^{(0)}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}^{(0)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}\phi_{j}^{0}{\phi_{j}^{0}}^{T},

(43)

and the excited states are

\left(\gamma_{\alpha,[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}]}^{(0)},\gamma_{\beta,[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{(0)}\right)\quad\mbox{where}\quad\gamma_{\sigma,[I_{\sigma},A_{\sigma}]}:=\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}^{0}-\sum_{i\in I_{\sigma}}\phi_{i}^{0}{\phi_{i}^{0}}^{T}+\sum_{a\in A_{\sigma}}\phi_{a}^{0}{\phi_{a}^{0}}^{T},

(44)

for
I_{\sigma}\subset\{1,\ldots,N_{\rm p}\}
,
A_{\sigma}\subset\{N_{\rm p}+1,\ldots,N_{\rm b}\}
,
|I|=|A|\geq 1
.
It follows from a simple application of the analytic implicit function theorem that under the nondegeneracy assumption (H), there exist
\lambda_{\rm c}>0
and
\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}\times\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}
real-analytic functions

\left(\gamma_{\alpha,[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}]}(\bullet),\gamma_{\beta,[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}(\bullet)\right):(-\lambda_{\rm c},\lambda_{\rm c})\to\mathcal{M},

I_{\alpha},I_{\beta}\subset\{1,\ldots,N_{\rm p}\}
,
A_{\alpha},A_{\beta}\subset\{N_{\rm p}+1,\ldots,N_{\rm b}\}
,
|I_{\alpha}|=|A_{\alpha}|
,
|I_{\beta}|=|A_{\beta}|
,
such that for any
\lambda\in(-\lambda_{\rm c},\lambda_{\rm c})
, the function
\mathcal{M}\ni(\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta})\mapsto\mathcal{E}(\lambda,\gamma_{\alpha},\gamma_{\beta})\in{\mathbb{R}}
has exactly
\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}\times\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}
critical points, all isolated, nondegenerate, and given by
\left(\gamma_{\alpha,[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}]}(\lambda),\gamma_{\beta,[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}(\lambda)\right)
. Note that, by symmetry,
\gamma_{\alpha,[\emptyset,\emptyset]}(\lambda)=\gamma_{\beta,[\emptyset,\emptyset]}(\lambda)=:\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}(\lambda)
, i.e., the UHF ground state coincides with the RHF ground state.

For
\lambda\in(-\lambda_{\rm c},\lambda_{\rm c})
, the excitation energies obtained by CP-UHF are thus the
\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}\times\binom{N_{\rm b}}{N_{\rm p}}-1
positive numbers

\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF}}(\lambda):=E^{\rm UHF}\left(\lambda,\gamma_{\alpha,[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}]}(\lambda),\gamma_{\beta,[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}(\lambda)\right)-E^{\rm UHF}\left(\lambda,\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}(\lambda),\gamma_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}(\lambda)\right)

(45)

with
I_{\sigma}\subset\{1,\ldots,N_{\rm p}\}
,
A_{\sigma}\subset\{N_{\rm p}+1,\ldots,N_{\rm b}\}
,
|I_{\sigma}|=|A_{\sigma}|
,
|I_{\alpha}|+|I_{\beta}|=|A_{\alpha}|+|A_{\beta}|\geq 1
.
In addition, the functions
\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF}}:(-\lambda_{\rm c},\lambda_{\rm c})\to{\mathbb{R}}
are real-analytic and it holds

\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF}}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{k}\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF},(k)},

with

\displaystyle\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF},(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A_{\alpha}\cup A_{\beta}}\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}\cup I_{\beta}}\varepsilon_{i}^{0},

\displaystyle\omega_{[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}],[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{{\rm CP-UHF},(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial E^{\rm UHF}}{\partial\lambda}\left(0,\gamma_{\alpha,[I_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}]}^{(0)},\gamma_{\beta,[I_{\beta},A_{\beta}]}^{(0)}\right)-\frac{\partial E^{\rm UHF}}{\partial\lambda}\left(0,\gamma^{(0)}_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]},\gamma^{(0)}_{[\emptyset,\emptyset]}\right).

The first-order variation of the excitation energy for the first excited state, given by the HOMO-LUMO excitation is

\displaystyle\omega^{\text{CP-UHF},(1)}_{\rm HOMO-LUMO}:=

\displaystyle\omega^{\text{CP-UHF},(1)}_{[\emptyset,\emptyset],[N_{\rm p},N_{\rm p}+1]}=\omega^{\text{CP-UHF},(1)}_{[N_{\rm p},N_{\rm p}+1],[\emptyset,\emptyset]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}((N_{\rm p}+1)\sigma,(N_{\rm p}+1)\sigma||i\sigma,i\sigma)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}}((N_{\rm p}+1)\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta|i\alpha,i\alpha)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}(N_{\rm p}\sigma,N_{\rm p}\sigma||i\sigma,i\sigma)-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}}(N_{\rm p}\beta,N_{\rm p}\beta|i\alpha,i\alpha)

(46)

for
\sigma=\alpha
or
\beta
.

LR-UHF.
Let us now turn to the LR-UHF approximation of excitation energies. First, as the complex dimension of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is
2N_{\rm p}(N_{\rm b}-N_{\rm p})
, there are at most
2N_{\rm p}(N_{\rm b}-N_{\rm p})
of them. For
\lambda=0
, they can be interpreted as single excitations:

\omega_{[i,a]}^{{\rm LR-UHF},(0)}:=\omega_{[i,a],[\emptyset,\emptyset]}^{{\rm LR-UHF},(0)}=\omega_{[\emptyset,\emptyset],[i,a]}^{{\rm LR-UHF},(0)}=\varepsilon_{a}^{0}-\varepsilon_{i}^{0},\quad 1\leq i\leq N_{\rm p},\quad N_{\rm p}+1\leq a\leq N_{\rm b}.

Using again the analytic implicit functional theorem, it can be shown that for
\lambda
small enough, the excitation energies computed by linear response are given by real-analytic functions
(-\lambda_{\rm c},\lambda_{\rm c})\ni\lambda\mapsto\omega_{[i,a]}^{{\rm LR-UHF}}(\lambda)\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\omega_{[i,a]}^{{\rm LR-UHF}}(0)=\omega_{[i,a]}^{{\rm LR-UHF},(0)}
. In particular, the first excited state corresponds to the HOMO-LUMO transition and the first-order variation is given by

\displaystyle\omega^{\rm LR-UHF,(1)}_{\rm HOMO-LUMO}:=\omega^{\rm LR-UHF,(1)}_{[N_{\rm p},N_{\rm p}+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}((N_{\rm p}+1)\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta||j\beta,j\beta)-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}-1}(N_{\rm p}\beta,N_{\rm p}\beta||j\beta,j\beta)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}((N_{\rm p}+1)\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta|j\alpha,j\alpha)-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm p}}(N_{\rm p}\beta,N_{\rm p}\beta|j\alpha,j\alpha)

\displaystyle-(N_{\rm p}\beta,(N_{\rm p}+1)\beta|(N_{\rm p}+1)\alpha,N_{\rm p}\alpha).

(47)

Notice that this expression coincides with the one found in the FCI case, in Eq. (
41
). Therefore, in the non-interacting limit, FCI and UHF-LR are equivalent in computing single excitation energies, while they differ in the interacting case.
