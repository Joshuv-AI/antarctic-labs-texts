Computational aspects of the trace norm contraction coefficient
Idris Delsol, Omar Fawzi, Jan Kochanowski, Akshay Ramachandran

Abstract
We show that approximating the trace norm contraction coefficient of a quantum channel within a constant factor is NP -hard. Equivalently, this shows that determining the optimal success probability for encoding a bit in a quantum system undergoing noise is NP -hard. This contrasts with the classical analogue of this problem that can clearly be solved efficiently. Our hardness results also hold for deciding if the contraction coefficient is equal to 1 1 . As a consequence, we show that deciding if a non-commutative graph has an independence number of at least 2 2 is NP -hard. In addition, we establish a converging hierarchy of semidefinite programming upper bounds on the contraction coefficient.

Introduction

A central question in information theory is how to optimally encode information for a given noise model. The objective of this paper is to study this question from an algorithmic point of view: given as input an explicit classical description of a quantum channel
\Phi
that represents the noise and an integer
k
, what is the maximum success probability that can be achieved for sending
k
messages using
\Phi
? When
\Phi
is a classical channel, Barman and Fawzi
[
1
]
showed that this problem is
NP
-hard to approximate within a factor greater than
1-e^{-1}
and that efficient algorithms matching this hardness exist. When
\Phi
is a quantum channel, much less is known about the complexity of the problem. When
\Phi
is given implicitly as a quantum circuit on
n
qubits, this problem is known to be
QMA
-hard
[
2
,
6
]
. Approximation algorithms have been proposed for some variants of the problem
[
3
,
10
,
17
]
but the guarantees are in general significantly weaker than the case of classical channels. In fact, the complexity of finding the optimal encoding of a single bit (
k=2
), which has a trivial efficient algorithm in the classical case, remains unknown.

1.1
Main results

The main focus of this paper is to study the maximal success probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,2)
for transmitting a bit over the quantum channel
\Phi
. It is simple to connect this expression to the trace norm contraction coefficient (see Proposition
4
for details) of the channel
\Phi
:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,2)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)

where the contraction coefficient is defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

The
trace norm contraction coefficient
of a channel
\Phi
is

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\sigma\in\D\\
\rho\not=\sigma\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\Phi(\rho)-\Phi(\sigma)\|_{1}}{\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}},

(1)

where
\D
is the set of density operators on the input space of
\Phi
.

As such, computing the trace norm contraction coefficient is equivalent to computing the optimal success probability and we focus in the following on the contraction coefficient. We start by establishing hardness results. The first result establishes hardness of approximation within a constant factor.

Theorem 2

(General hardness)
.

Given a channel
\Phi
, its contraction coefficient
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
is
NP
-hard to approximate to a factor
1/\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Even in the restricted setting where
\Phi
is promised to be a quantum-classical channel (i.e., a channel with quantum input and classical output), the contraction coefficient is
NP
-hard to approximate to a factor
\sqrt{2/\pi}+\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

This result is proven in Section
3
and is based on a reduction from the Little Grothendieck Problem. Our second result shows that the question of deciding whether
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
or
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
is also
NP
-hard.

Theorem 3

(Hardness for the complete case)
.

Given a quantum channel
\Phi
, it is
NP
-hard to distinguish between
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
and
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq 1-\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
, where
n
is the dimension of the input Hilbert space.

In terms of channel coding, this corresponds to deciding the existence of a perfect, i.e., zero-error, encoding of a bit. The existence of a zero-error code of size
k
for a quantum channel
\Phi
is equivalent to the independence number of the corresponding non-commutative confusability graph of
\Phi
being greater or equal to
k

[
9
]
. A non-commutative graph is a subspace
S
of matrices containing the identity and satisfying
S=S^{*}
, which is more commonly also known as an operator system.
1
1

1

The way to see an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
in this setting is by choosing
S=\operatorname{Span}\{E_{i,j}:i,j\in V,i=j\text{ or }(i,j)\in E\}
, where
E_{i,j}
is the matrix containing
1
in position
(i,j)
and zero everywhere else.
Theorem
3
shows that deciding whether a non-commutative graph has independent number at least
2
is
NP
-hard. In the model where the channel is described implicitly by a circuit, the hardness of this problem was studied in depth in
[
6
]
.

A consequence of this work is that known efficiently computable bounds on the contraction coefficient such as the quantum Doeblin coefficient
[
13
,
15
]
cannot be always tight. In fact, we give in
Proposition

23
an explicit example of channel
\Phi
where the Doeblin coefficient fails to capture
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
.

Our final result is applying the methodology of
[
3
]
for constrained bilinear problems to obtain a converging hierarchy of semidefinite programming upper bounds on the contraction coefficient (Theorem
25
). We illustrate this hierarchy by applying it to some simple channels (
Fig.

1
,
Fig.

2
). On all the examples we tried, the first level of our hierarchy gives bounds that are tighter than the quantum Doeblin coefficient introduced in
[
15
]
. We note that
[
13

considered a semidefinite programming hierarchy for a variant of the Doeblin coefficient called the induced Doeblin coefficient. This hierarchy is different from ours as it cannot converge to the contraction coefficient. In fact, as shown in the discussion following
Proposition

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
.

Notation and basic statements

In this paper, all Hilbert spaces
\cH
are finite dimensional. We write
\B(\cH)
for the set of linear operators from
\cH
to itself. The state of a quantum system modeled by
\cH
is described by a density operator, i.e., a positive and unit trace operator in
\B(\cH)
. We denote by
\D(\cH)
the set of such density operators. Physically possible transformations are mathematically represented by completely positive trace-preserving maps, also called quantum channels. For every quantum channel
\Phi:\B(\cH)\to\B(\cH^{\prime})
, we can find a set of operators
\{K_{i}:K_{i}\in\B(\cH^{\prime},\cH)\}
satisfying
\sum_{i}K_{i}^{*}K_{i}=I
such that for all
x\in\B(\cH)
:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle(x)=\sum_{i}K_{i}xK^{*}_{i}.

(2)

These operators are called Kraus operators of the channel
\Phi
and there is a Kraus representation of
\Phi
using at most
\dim(\cH)\dim(\cH^{\prime})
such operators. The Choi state of
\Phi
is denoted as

\displaystyle J(\Phi)=(\text{Id}_{\cH}\otimes\Phi)(\ket{\psi^{+}}\bra{\psi^{+}}),

(3)

where
\ket{\psi^{+}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\dim(\cH)}}\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(\cH)}\ket{i}\ket{i}
for some fixed orthonormal basis
\ket{i}
of
\cH
. Given
\Phi
a channel, we note
A
its input system and
B
its output system, so that
J(\Phi)
is a bipartite state on the compound system
(A,B)
. We will therefore write
J(\Phi)_{AB}
the Choi state of
\Phi
, whenever the systems on which
J(\Phi)
acts may be ambiguous, especially in
Section

5
. Given
S
a physical system, we use the shorthand notation
d_{S}:=\dim(S)
. We denote
\Phi^{*}
for the adjoint (also called the dual) of the map
\Phi:\B(\cH)\to\B(\cH^{\prime})
, i.e., we have
\langle\Phi(X),Y\rangle=\langle X,\Phi^{*}(Y)\rangle
for all
X\in\B(\cH),Y\in\B(\cH^{\prime})
. Here,
\langle.,.\rangle
is the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(A^{*}B)
. Note that
\Phi
is a quantum channel if and only if
\Phi^{*}
is completely positive and unital, i.e.,
\Phi^{*}(I)=I
.

We now give a precise definition for the optimal success probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
for transmitting
k
messages over the channel
\Phi
. A Positive Operator Valued Measure, or POVM, on a space
\B(\cH)
is a collection of positive operators
\{M_{i}:i\}
such that
\sum_{i}M_{i}=I
. We define

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)=\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(M_{i}\Phi(\rho_{i})).

(4)

We now relate
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,2)
to
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
using the Holevo-Helström theorem.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\Phi
be a channel, then

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,2)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\tr}(\Phi).

(5)

Proof.

For any
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\D(\cH)
and any 2-outcome POVM
\{M_{1},I-M_{1}\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\tr(M_{1}\Phi(\rho_{1})+(I-M_{1})\Phi(\rho_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(M_{1}(\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})))

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})\|_{1}}{\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{1}}

where we used the Holevo-Helström theorem
[
14
]
for
(a)
. For the other direction, we first use
Lemma

5
below and choose
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
orthogonal, i.e.,
\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{1}=2
, such that
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})\|_{1}
. Then
M_{1}
is the positive part of
\Phi(\rho_{1})-\Phi(\rho_{2})
.
∎

In this section, we prove
Theorem

2
by reducing the Hermitian non-commutative (resp. real commutative) Little Grothendieck Problem to the problem of computing
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
(resp. computing
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
, for
\Phi
a quantum classical channel). The complexities of various Grothendieck problems are studied in depth in
[
5
]
and
[

]
.

Before introducing the Hermitian non-commutative Little Grothendieck Problem let us first reduce the two-parameter optimisation problem which defines
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
into a single-parameter optimisation. Ruskai showed in
[
18
]
the following lemma.

Lemma 5

(
[
18
]
, Theorem 2; see also Lemma 8.3 of
[
22
]
)
.

For a quantum channel
\Phi:\B(\cH)\rightarrow\B(\cH^{\prime})
,

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=\max_{\rho,\sigma\in\D(\cH),\rho\perp\sigma}\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi(\rho)-\Phi(\sigma)\|_{1},

(6)

where
\rho\perp\sigma
means
\rho
and
\sigma
have orthogonal supports. In addition, the states in the maximum can be taken pure.

From this lemma, we can reduce the problem of computing the contraction coefficient to the following optimisation problem over Hermitian operators in the unit ball for the Schatten
\infty
-norm:

Lemma 6
.

For a quantum channel
\Phi:\B(\cH)\rightarrow\B(\cH^{\prime})
,

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\max_{X^{*}=X,\|X\|_{\infty}\leq 1}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi^{*}(X)),

(7)

where, for any Hermitian operator
O
,
\lambda_{\max}(O)
, respectively
\lambda_{\min}(O)
, is the maximal, respectively minimal, eigenvalue of
O
.

Proof.

In the following equations, the vectors
u
,
v
are taken of unit length, i.e.,
\|u\|_{\ell_{2}}=\|v\|_{\ell_{2}}=1
. By
Lemma

5
,

\displaystyle\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{u,v\in\cH,u\perp v}\|\Phi(uu^{*})-\Phi(vv^{*})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{1}{2}\max_{u,v\in\cH,u\perp v}\max_{X^{*}=X,\|X\|_{\infty}\leq 1}|\langle\Phi(uu^{*}-vv^{*}),X\rangle|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{u,v\in\cH,u\perp v}\max_{X^{*}=X,\|X\|_{\infty}\leq 1}|\langle uu^{*}-vv^{*},\Phi^{*}(X)\rangle|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\max_{X^{*}=X,\|X\|_{\infty}\leq 1}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi^{*}(X)),

where
(a)
follows from the Hölder duality between the trace and operator norm as well as the fact that
\Phi
is Hermitian preserving and the last step follows directly from writing an eigendecomposition of
\Phi^{*}(X)
and using the fact that
u
and
v
have norm
1
.
∎

Hence as claimed the optimisation problem
Lemma

6
is only over a single parameter.

We now switch our focus to the Little Grothendieck Problems, which we will reduce to the computation of the contraction coefficient. A Banach space
X=(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{X})
is a linear space
\mathcal{X}
endowed with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
with respect to which
\mathcal{X}
is complete. For any linear map
\F:X\rightarrow Y
between two Banach spaces, its operator norm is defined as

\|\F\|_{X\to Y}=\sup_{\|a\|_{X}\leq 1}\|\F(a)\|_{Y},

(8)

i.e., the supremum of
\|\F(a)\|_{Y}
when
a
ranges in the unit ball of
X
.
The Little Grothendieck Problems are all stated as problems concerning computations of such operator norms of linear maps
\F
between certain Banach spaces. By abuse of notation, we write
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the Banach space obtained when we endow
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
\ell_{2}
norm.
We write
\ell_{1}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
the Banach space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the
\ell_{1}
norm and analogously
\ell_{\infty}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
for the Banach space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{\ell_{\infty}})
. Furthermore, we write
S_{1}^{n}=(\B(\C^{n}),\|\cdot\|_{1})
and
S_{\infty}^{n}=(\B(\C^{n}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
the Banach spaces of complex
n\times n
matrices endowed with the Schatten
1
- and
\infty
-norm. Moreover, we write
S_{1}^{H,n}=(\text{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\|\cdot\|_{1})
and
S_{\infty}^{H,n}=(\text{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
the
(real)
Banach spaces of
n\times n
Hermitian complex matrices endowed with, respectively, the Schatten 1- and
\infty
-norm (the former being also called the trace norm and the latter the operator norm).
The real commutative Little Grothendieck Problem consists in computing
\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})}
for
\F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})
a linear operator. This problem can be generalized to a non-commutative setting by taking
\F
operator-valued. The variant we use for our proof is the Hermitian non-commutative Little Grothendieck Problem, which can be stated as computing
\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,d}}
, given a linear operator
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow S_{1}^{H,d}
with output space endowed with the Schatten
1
-norm.
Briët et al. proved the following hardness theorems for the commutative and Hermitian non-commutative Little Grothendieck Problem in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 1.3 in
[
5
]
)
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, it is
NP
-hard to approximate the
real commutative
Little Grothendieck Problem to within a factor greater than
\sqrt{2/\pi}+\varepsilon
.

Theorem 8

(Theorem 1.2 and Sec. 5.1 in
[
5
]
)
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, it is
NP
-hard to approximate the
Hermitian non-commutative
Little Grothendieck Problem to within a factor greater than
1/\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon
.

Finally note that we can express the Little Grothendieck Problems in terms of the dual of
\F
as per the argument given in Section 6 of
[
5
]
. Indeed, for any Banach spaces
X
,
Y
and any linear map
\F:X\rightarrow Y
, it is a standard fact that the operator norm of
\F
is equal to that of its dual
\F^{*}:Y^{*}\rightarrow X^{*}
, i.e.
\|\F\|_{X\to Y}=\|\F^{*}\|_{Y^{*}\to X^{*}}
. For the Hermitian non-commutative Little Grothendieck Problem, we have
\F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow S_{1}^{H,d}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Hilbert space and thus self-dual and the dual of
S_{1}^{H,d}
is
S_{\infty}^{H,d}
, therefore
\F^{*}:S_{\infty}^{H,d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,

\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,d}}=\|\F^{*}\|_{S_{\infty}^{H,d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}=\sup_{\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1,~Y^{*}=Y}\|\F^{*}(Y)\|_{\ell_{2}}.

(9)

Since the action of a linear map
\F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow S_{1}^{H,d}
is fully determined by where it maps a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can write:

\F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}F_{i},

(10)

with, for all
i\in[n]
,
F_{i}=\F(e_{i})\in S_{1}^{H,d}
, with
\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}
the canonical basis of
\R^{n}
. Then we can express
\F^{*}
as:

\forall Y\in S_{\infty}^{H,d}:\quad\F^{*}(Y)=(\langle F_{i},Y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}.

(11)

Therefore,

\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,d}}=\|\F^{*}\|_{S_{\infty}^{H,d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}=\sup_{\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1,~Y^{*}=Y}\|(\langle F_{i},Y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}\|_{\ell_{2}}.

(12)

The same line of argument also hold
mutatis mutandis
for the real commutative Little Grothendieck Problem. In this case, we have
\F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we write for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{i},

with
\{f_{i}=\F(e_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:1\leq i\leq n\}
. Then, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\F^{*}(y)=(\langle f_{i},y\rangle)_{i\in[n]},

and

\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\F^{*}\|_{\ell_{\infty}^{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}=\sup_{\|y\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1}\|\F^{*}(y)\|_{\ell_{2}}.

We now have all the tools to prove
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We start with the first point, i.e., the hardness result for general channels.
Let
\F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow S_{1}^{H,d}
be a linear map, and let
\{F_{i}=\F(e_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
, with
\{e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:i\in[n]\}
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}F_{i}.

(13)

The proof then proceeds in two steps. First, we construct a channel
\Phi_{\alpha}
out of
\F
, then, we relate the difference
(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}(Y))
to
\|\F^{*}(Y)\|_{\ell_{2}}
. Finally, we use on the one hand
Lemma

5
to deduce
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha})
from the difference
(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}(Y))
and, on the other, that, from
Theorem

8
, it is
NP
-hard to maximise
\|\F^{*}(Y)\|_{\ell_{2}}
on the operators
Y\in S^{H,d}_{\infty}
,
\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, to then conclude that it is also
NP
-hard to compute
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha})
.

Thus, let us perform some changes to
\F
so as to construct a valid quantum channel. Let
\widetilde{\F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow S_{1}^{H,2d}
be the linear map defined as

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\widetilde{\F}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}F_{i}\oplus(-F_{i})=\begin{pmatrix}\F(x)&0\\
0&-\F(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

Then,
\widetilde{\F}^{*}(I_{2d})=(\langle F_{i}\oplus(-F_{i}),I_{2d}\rangle)_{i\in[n]}=(\langle F_{i},I_{d}\rangle-\langle F_{i},I_{d}\rangle)_{i\in[n]}=0
. Furthermore, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|\widetilde{\F}(x)\|_{1}=2\|\F(x)\|_{1}
, so that

\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,d}}=\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{\F}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,2d}}.

(15)

We write
\{\widetilde{F}_{i}=\widetilde{\F}(e_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
.

\Psi(Y)=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widetilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle e_{i}\bigg)e_{n+1}^{T}+e_{n+1}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widetilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle e_{i}\bigg)^{T}.

(16)

Note that
\Psi
is linear over
\mathbb{C}
, so that we can turn it into the dual of a channel
\Phi
by shifting and renormalizing.
Let
\Phi^{*}_{\alpha}
be defined as:

\forall Y\in\B(\mathbb{C}^{2d}),\quad\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}(Y):=\alpha\Psi(Y)+\frac{\tr(Y)}{2d}I_{n+1},

(17)

with
\alpha>0
. For
\alpha
small enough,
\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}
is completely positive (cf.
[
21
]
). Furthermore, as
\widetilde{\F}^{*}(I_{2d})=0
,
\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}
is unital, so that, for
\alpha
small enough,
\Phi_{\alpha}
is a channel.
Finally, remark that, for
Y\in\text{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2d})
,

\displaystyle(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi_{\alpha}^{*}(Y))

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\alpha\Psi(Y))

(18)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}2\alpha\|(\langle\widetilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}\|_{\ell_{2}}

(19)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\|\widetilde{\F}^{*}(Y)\|_{\ell_{2}}

(20)

\displaystyle=4\alpha\|\F^{*}(Y)\|_{\ell_{2}},

(21)

where
(a)
follows from the fact that any operator
A=be^{*}+eb^{*}
with
e\perp b
satisfies
\lambda_{\max}(A)=-\lambda_{\min}(A)=2\|b\|_{\ell_{2}}\|e\|_{\ell_{2}}
. Note that because
F_{i}
and
Y
are Hermitian, the vector
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widetilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle e_{i}
is real so its transpose and complex conjugate are the same.
Thus, combining the last system of equations with both
Eq.

9
and
Lemma

6
, we have that:

\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S_{1}^{H,d}}=2\alpha\cdot\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha}),

which ends the reduction and shows by
Theorem

8
that it is
NP
-hard to approximate
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha})
to within a factor
1/\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
.

Let us now move to the second point of Theorem
2
about quantum-classical channels. The proof uses exactly the same techniques as the proof of the first point.
In fact, given
\F:\R^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\R)
, we write as before
\F(e_{i})=f_{i}\in\R^{d}
for
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
the canonical basis of
\R^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{i},\qquad\F^{*}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle f_{i},y\rangle e_{i},

(22)

with
\F^{*}:\ell_{\infty}^{d}(\R)\rightarrow\R^{n}
the dual of
\F
. As in the general quantum case, we set
\tilde{f_{i}}=f_{i}\oplus(-f_{i})\in\R^{2d}
,
\tilde{F_{i}}=\operatorname{diag}(\tilde{f_{i}})
the diagonal
2d\times 2d
matrix whose diagonal entries are the entries of
\tilde{f_{i}}
, and we define the map
\Psi:\B(\C^{2d})\rightarrow\B(\C^{n+1})
:

\forall Y\in S^{2d}_{\infty},\quad\Psi(Y)=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\tilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle e_{i}\bigg)e_{n+1}^{T}+e_{n+1}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\tilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle e_{i}\bigg)^{T}.

(23)

Then, for
\alpha>0
small enough, the map
\Phi^{*}_{\alpha}
defined as

\forall Y\in\B(\C^{2d}),\quad\Phi^{*}_{\alpha}(Y)=\alpha\Psi(Y)+\frac{\tr(Y)}{2d}I_{n+1},

(24)

is unital completely positive, so that
\Phi_{\alpha}
is a channel. Note that, as the
\tilde{F}_{i}
are diagonal,
\Phi_{\alpha}
is a quantum-classical channel, i.e., its output is diagonal for all inputs. Finally, using
Lemma

6
,

\displaystyle\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{Y^{*}=Y,\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Phi^{*}_{\alpha}(Y))

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\sup_{Y^{*}=Y,\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})(\Psi(Y))

(26)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\alpha\sup_{Y^{*}=Y,\|Y\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\|(\langle\tilde{F}_{i},Y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}\|_{\ell_{2}}

(27)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\alpha\sup_{y\in\R^{2d},\|y\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1}\|(\langle\tilde{f}_{i},y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}\|_{\ell_{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\sup_{y\in\R^{d},\|y\|_{\ell_{\infty}}\leq 1}\|(\langle f_{i},y\rangle)_{i\in[n]}\|_{\ell_{2}}

(29)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\|\F^{*}\|_{\ell_{\infty}^{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}

(30)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})},

(31)

Finally by
Theorem

7
it is
NP
-hard to approximate
\|\F\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\ell_{1}^{d}(\mathbb{R})}
to a factor greater than
\sqrt{2/\pi}+\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon>0
, from which we conclude that it is
NP
-hard to approximate
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi_{\alpha})
to the same factors.
∎

4
NP
-hardness of the complete case

In the previous section, we showed that it is
NP
-hard to approximate the contraction coefficient of a channel. In this section we strengthen this result, showing it is
NP
-hard even in the ‘complete’ case where we want to test whether a channel has contraction coefficient
1
.

For the proof, we use several characterizations of channels with
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
: one in terms of optimisation over separable states and the second one in terms of the confusability graph of
\Phi
. We start by defining the set of separable states.

Definition 9

(Separable states)
.

\mathrm{SEP}(\cH\otimes\cH^{\prime}):=\mathrm{conv}\{\rho\otimes\sigma\mid\rho\in\D(\cH),\sigma\in\D(\cH^{\prime})\}=\mathrm{conv}\{uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*}\mid u\in\cH,v\in\cH^{\prime}\}.

(32)

For
A\in\B(\cH\otimes\cH^{\prime})
,

\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(A):=\min_{X\in\mathrm{SEP}(\cH\otimes\cH^{\prime})}\langle A,X\rangle=\min_{u\in\cH,v\in\cH^{\prime}}\langle A,uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*}\rangle.

(33)

Now, we introduce the confusability graph of a channel.
The
confusability graph
of a classical channel was introduced by Shannon in
[
19
]
to study the zero-error capacity of the channel.
This graph has the set of inputs of the channel as its vertex set and two inputs are connected by an edge if and only if there is a non-zero probability that the channel maps them to the same output. This structure was generalized to quantum channels in
[
8
]
.

Definition 10

(Quantum confusability graph, Eq. 3 in
[
8
]
)
.

For
\Phi:\B(\cH)\rightarrow\B(\cH^{\prime})
defined by
\Phi(x)=\sum_{i}K_{i}xK_{i}^{*}
, for all
x\in\B(\cH)
, its
quantum confusability graph
is

G_{\Phi}=\operatorname{Span}\{K_{i}^{*}K_{j}:i,j\}.

(34)

It is simple to see that
G_{\Phi}
does not depend on the specific choice of Kraus operators and forms an operator system, i.e., a unital self-adjoint subset of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. In fact, any operator system is the confusability graph of some channel
\Phi
:

Lemma 11

(Lemma 2 in
[
8
]
)
.

For a subspace
S\subseteq\B(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, there is a channel
\Phi
such that
G_{\Phi}=S
if and only if
S
is self-adjoint (i.e., if
x\in S
then
x^{*}\in S
) and contains the identity.

Furthermore, if
S
is self-adjoint and contains the identity, we can choose a Hermitian basis
\{M_{1},\dots,M_{d}\}
for
S
and construct a channel
\Phi:\B(\C^{n})\to\B(\C^{n^{\prime}})
with
n^{\prime}
polynomial in
n
with Kraus operators
\{K_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
, satisfying
G_{\Phi}=S
and, for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
K_{i}^{*}K_{j}=\delta_{i,j}M_{i}
. In addition, given such a basis of
S
, the Kraus operators of
\Phi
can be computed in polynomial time in
n
.

Remark 12
.

The second paragraph of the previous Lemma is not in the statement of Lemma 2 of
[
8
]
but is explicitly shown in its proof in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\Phi:\B(\cH)\to\B(\cH^{\prime})
be a quantum channel, then the following are equivalent

1.

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
;

2.

there exists a rank-one matrix
x
such that
x\in G_{\Phi}^{\perp}
;

3.

\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(J(\Phi^{*}\circ\Phi))=0
.

This result follows from
[
8
]
; see
[
20
, Theorem SI.11]
for a synthesis of related characterizations.

We now introduce the
NP
-hard problem that we will embed into a channel
\Phi
such that “yes” instances are mapped to channels satisfying the equivalent properties in
Theorem

13
and “no” instances are mapped to channels that do not. The problem we use is Graph
2
-CSP.

Definition 14

(Graph 2-CSP, see Definition 3 in
[
12
]
)
.

A
constraint graph

G=(V,E)
is an undirected graph (possibly with self-loops) along with a set
\Sigma
of “colours” and a mapping
R_{e}:\Sigma\times\Sigma\rightarrow\{0,1\}
for each edge
e=(v,u)\in E
(called the constraint to
e
). A mapping
\tau:V\rightarrow\Sigma
(called a
colouring
) satisfies the constraint
R_{e}
if
R_{e}(\tau(v),\tau(u))=1
for an edge
e=(v,u)
in
E
. The graph
G
is said to be
satisfiable
if there is a colouring
\tau
that satisfies all the constraints, while
G
is said to be
(1-\eta)
-unsatisfiable if for all colourings
\tau
, the fraction of constraints satisfied by
\tau
is at most
1-\eta
.

Theorem 15
.

There is a universal constant
\gamma>0
such that, given a Graph 2-CSP instance promised to be either (1) satisfiable, or (2)
(1-\gamma)
-unsatisfiable, it is
NP
-hard to distinguish between the two cases.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem 3 of
[
12
]
.
∎

In order to embed Graph
2
-CSP into the confusability graph of a quantum channel, we will follow the ideas of
[
4
]
and
[
12
]
relating to languages with short
quantum proofs
.

Definition 16
.

A promise problem
(L,\overline{L})
(i.e.,
L,\bar{L}
disjoint subsets of
\{0,1\}^{*}
) is in
\texttt{QMA}_{\log}(2,a,b)
if there is a constant
c
and a polynomial-time classical verification algorithm
\mathcal{V}
that on input
x
of length
n
prepares a quantum circuit
\cV(x)
acting on
O(\log n)
qubits such that for any
n
and any instance of size
n
, we have

•

If
x\in L
, there are
u,v\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes c\log n}
unit vectors such that

\tr(\Pi_{\cV(x)}uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*})\geq a

where
\Pi_{\cV(x)}
is the acceptance projector of the verification circuit
\cV(x)
.

•

If
x\in\overline{L}
, for any unit vectors
u,v\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes c\log n}
, we have

\tr(\Pi_{\cV(x)}uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*})\leq b.

The main result of
[
12
]
relates Graph
2
-CSP to the above class.

Theorem 17
.

For the constant
\gamma>0
given in
Theorem

15
,
let
L
be the set of satisfiable Graph
2
-CSP instances, and
\overline{L}
be the set of
(1-\gamma)
-unsatisfiable Graph
2
-CSP instances. Then
(L,\overline{L})\in\texttt{QMA}_{\log}(2,1,1-\Omega(1/n))
.

We now describe the steps of the reduction from Graph 2-CSP to the contraction coefficient problem. For improved readability, we state a lemma for each step of the reduction. The first step is given a Graph 2-CSP instance
G
to construct a projector
\Pi
.

Lemma 18

(From
G
to
\Pi
)
.

Given a Graph 2-CSP instance
G
, we can construct in polynomial time a projector
\Pi\in\B(\cH\otimes\cH)
with
\dim\cH=n
such that if
G
is satisfiable then
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=0
and if
G
is
(1-\gamma)
-unsatisfiable
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
.

Proof.

Let
\cV
be the verifier coming from
Theorem

17
. Consider an instance
G
of Graph 2-CSP of size
m
, we let
\Pi=I-\Pi_{\cV(G)}
, where
\Pi_{\cV(G)}\in\B((\C^{2})^{\otimes c\log m}\otimes(\C^{2})^{\otimes c\log m})
is the acceptance projector on input
G
. Let
\cH=(\C^{2})^{\otimes c\log m}
and
n:=\dim\cH=m^{c}
. Then we know that if
G
is satisfiable, there exists
u,v\in\cH
such that
\tr(\Pi_{\cV(G)}uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*})=1
so
\tr(\Pi uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*})=0
. As a result
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=0
. If
G
is
(1-\gamma)
-unsatisfiable, then for any
u,v\in\cH
,
\tr(\Pi_{\cV(G)}uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*})\leq 1-\Omega(\frac{1}{m})
which can be rewritten as
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=\Omega(\frac{1}{m})
. In addition, note that as
\cV
is a polynomial-time classical algorithm and the circuit
\cV(G)
acts on
O(\log m)
qubits,
\Pi
can be computed in polynomial time.
∎

The next step is to construct a subspace
S
from
\Pi
such that
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)
is related to the distance between rank-one matrices and
S
.
For that, it is useful to introduce a correspondence between
\C^{p\times q}
and
\C^{p}\otimes\C^{q}
. Let
\{\ket{i}:i\in[p]\}
and
\{\ket{j}:j\in[q]\}
be fixed orthonormal bases of
\C^{p}
and
\C^{q}
, then we define the linear map
\Wvec:\C^{p\times q}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{q}
as

\Wvec(\ket{i}\bra{j}):=\ket{i}\otimes\ket{j}.

(35)

This map is an isometry in the sense that
\langle x,y\rangle=\langle\Wvec(x),\Wvec(y)\rangle
.
Note that an operator
x\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
is rank one if and only if
x=uv^{*}
with
u\in\mathbb{C}^{p}\backslash\{0\}
,
v\in\mathbb{C}^{q}\backslash\{0\}
and thus if and only if
\Wvec(x)=u\otimes\bar{v}
with
\bar{v}
the complex conjugate of
v
in the basis
\{\ket{j}:j\in[q]\}
.

Lemma 19

(From
\Pi
to
S
)
.

Let
\Pi\in\B(\cH\otimes\cH)
be an orthonormal projection and
K
be the kernel of
\Pi
. We construct the subspace
S
of
\B(\cH)
as
S:=\Wvec^{-1}(K)
. We have

\langle\Pi,uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*}\rangle=\operatorname{dist}(u\otimes v,K)^{2}=\operatorname{dist}(uv^{T},S)^{2},

with

\operatorname{dist}(x,K):=\min_{y\in K}\|x-y\|_{\ell_{2}},\quad\operatorname{dist}(x,S):=\min_{y\in S}\|x-y\|_{2}.

Proof.

Let
\{a_{i}\in\cH\otimes\cH:i\}
be an orthonormal basis of the support of
\Pi
so that
\Pi=\sum_{i}a_{i}a_{i}^{*}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\Pi,uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle a_{i}a_{i}^{*},uu^{*}\otimes vv^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}|\langle a_{i},u\otimes v\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dist(u\otimes v,K)^{2}

\displaystyle=\dist(\Wvec^{-1}(u\otimes v),\Wvec^{-1}(K))^{2},

which gives the desired result.
∎

The constructed subspace
S
has an orthogonal complement that is not necessarily the confusability graph of a quantum channel. But we can construct a subspace
\widehat{S}
that has this property and that behaves in the same way as
S
.

Lemma 20

(From
S
to
\widehat{S}=G_{\Phi}^{\perp}
)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
be a subspace, then

\widehat{S}:=E_{01}\otimes S+E_{10}\otimes S^{*}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
B^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}:A,B\in S\right\}\subseteq\B(\mathbb{C}^{n+m})

(36)

satisfies
(a)~\widehat{S}^{\perp}=\widehat{S}^{\perp*}
,
(b)~I_{n+m}\in\widehat{S}^{\perp}
and
(c)~
there is a constant
C>0
such that

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\B(\C^{n+m})\\
\rank(x)=1,\|x\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\dist(x,\widehat{S})\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\C^{n\times m}\\
\rank(x)=1,\|x\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\dist(x,S)\leq C\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\B(\C^{n+m})\\
\rank(x)=1,\|x\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\dist(x,\widehat{S}).

(37)

Furthermore, let
(s^{\perp}_{1},\dots,s^{\perp}_{d})
be an orthonormal basis of
S^{\perp}
, with
d=\dim(S^{\perp})
,
let
(h^{(1)}_{1},\dots,h_{n^{2}}^{(1)})
and
(h^{(2)}_{1},\dots,h^{(2)}_{m^{2}})
be, respectively, a Hermitian orthonormal basis of
\B(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\B(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, let

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}h^{(1)}_{p}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:1\leq p\leq n^{2}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&h_{p}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}:1\leq p\leq m^{2}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&s^{\perp}_{p}\\
s^{\perp*}_{p}&0\end{pmatrix}:1\leq p\leq d\Bigr\}

\displaystyle B_{4}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&is_{p}^{\perp}\\
-is^{\perp*}_{p}&0\end{pmatrix}:1\leq p\leq d\Bigr\}.

Then,

B_{\widehat{S}^{\perp}}=B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup B_{3}\cup B_{4}

is a Hermitian orthonormal basis of
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Points
(a)
and
(b)
are easy. For
(c)
, we have

\dist\bigl(\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\widehat{S}\bigr)=\dist(x,S),

(38)

and this proves that the first inequality in (
37
) holds.
For the second one, let

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\B(\mathbb{C}^{n+m})\text{ with }\Bigl\|\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\|_{2}=1,

(39)

and let

\delta^{2}:=\dist\Bigl(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\widehat{S}\Bigr)^{2}=\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|d\|_{2}^{2}+\dist(b,S)^{2}+\dist(c,S^{*})^{2}.

We will show that there exists
x\in\C^{n\times m}
with
\|x\|_{2}=1
such that
\rank(x)\leq\rank\Big(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\Big)
and
\dist(x,S)\leq 2\delta
.
If
\delta\geq 1/2
, then the property clearly holds by taking an arbitrary
x
of rank
1
and using the fact that
0\in S
. Now assume
\delta<1/2
.
Note that the normalization condition
can be written as

\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}+\|c\|_{2}^{2}+\|d\|^{2}_{2}=1.

Therefore,

1-(\|b\|_{2}^{2}+\|c\|_{2}^{2})+\dist(b,S)^{2}+\dist(c,S^{*})^{2}=\delta^{2}.

As the distances between
b
,
c
and
S
,
S^{*}
are non-negative, we end up with:

\max\{\|b\|_{2}^{2},\|c\|_{2}^{2}\}\geq\frac{\|b\|_{2}^{2}+\|c\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{2}.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\|b\|_{2}\geq\|c\|_{2}
(otherwise we exchange the role of
b
and
c
). Using additionally
\dist\left(b,S\right)\leq\delta
we get

\dist\left(\frac{b}{\|b\|_{2}},S\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{2}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}\delta\leq 2\delta

and we have that
\rank(b)\leq\rank\Big(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\Big)
. This proves the claimed statement and by taking the infimum over
a,b,c,d
leads to the second inequality in (
37
).

We now move on to the explicit construction of a orthonormal basis of
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
. Observe that

\widehat{S}^{\perp}=E_{00}\otimes\B(\mathbb{C}^{n})+E_{01}\otimes S^{\perp}+E_{10}\otimes S^{\perp*}+E_{11}\otimes\B(\mathbb{C}^{m}).

(40)

In fact, let
x\in\B(\mathbb{C}^{n+m})
be written as
x=E_{00}\otimes x_{00}+E_{01}\otimes x_{01}+E_{10}\otimes x_{10}+E_{11}\otimes x_{11}
,

\displaystyle x\in\widehat{S}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\iff\forall y\in\widehat{S},\langle x,y\rangle=0

\displaystyle\iff\forall y,z\in S,\langle E_{00}\otimes x_{00}+E_{01}\otimes x_{01}+E_{10}\otimes x_{10}+E_{11}\otimes x_{11},E_{01}\otimes y+E_{10}\otimes z^{*}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\iff\forall y,z\in S,\langle x_{00},0\rangle+\langle x_{11},0\rangle+\langle x_{01},y\rangle+\langle x_{10},z^{*}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\iff x\in E_{00}\otimes\B(\mathbb{C}^{n})+E_{01}\otimes S^{\perp}+E_{10}\otimes S^{\perp*}+E_{11}\otimes\B(\mathbb{C}^{m}),

where, in the last equivalence, we used the fact that we could choose
y=0
(resp.
z=0
) to conclude that, necessarily, for all
z\in S
,
\langle x_{10},z^{*}\rangle=0
(resp. for all
y\in S
,
\langle x_{01},y\rangle=0
).
Now to construct a basis of this subspace, note first that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can construct an orthonormal Hermitian basis for
\B(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. It suffices to take

\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{p,q}+E_{q,p}),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}i(E_{p,q}-E_{q,p}):1\leq p<q\leq n\right\}\cup\{E_{p,p}:1\leq p\leq n\}.

Then, we can easily check that
B_{\widehat{S}^{\perp}}
is a Hermitian orthonormal basis of
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
, i.e. that
\operatorname{Span}(B_{\widehat{S}^{\perp}})=\widehat{S}^{\perp}
, all
x\in B_{\widehat{S}^{\perp}}
are Hermitian and for all
x,y\in B_{\widehat{S}^{\perp}}
,
\langle x,y\rangle=\delta_{x=y}
.
∎

Now
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
is an operator system and so by
Lemma

11
, there exists a quantum channel
\Phi
such that
G_{\Phi}=\widehat{S}^{\perp}
. By
Theorem

13
, the property
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
exactly captures the property that
\widehat{S}
contains a rank-one element. To establish
Theorem

3
, it only remains to quantitatively control some of the steps in the reduction.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

We start with an instance
G
from Graph 2-CSP. As in
Lemma

18
, we construct
\Pi\in\B(\C^{n}\otimes\C^{n})
and then a subspace
S
of
\B(\C^{n})
as in
Lemma

19
. Then, we construct
\widehat{S}
a subspace of
\B(\C^{2n})
as in
Lemma

20
and then let
\Phi:\B(\C^{2n})\to\B(\C^{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
be such that
G_{\Phi}=\widehat{S}^{\perp}
as per
Lemma

11
. Note that each step of the construction can be done in polynomial time.

For
G
satisfiable, we have
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=0
, so
S
contains a rank-one matrix, so
\widehat{S}=G_{\Phi}^{\perp}
contains a rank-one matrix and so
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
.

For
G

(1-\gamma)
-unsatisfiable, we have
\ell_{\mathrm{SEP}}(\Pi)=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
(
Lemma

18
). Then by
Lemma

19
, we have
\inf_{x,\rank(x)=1,\|x\|_{2}=1}\dist(x,S)^{2}=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
. Using
Lemma

20
, we get
\inf_{x,\rank(x)=1,\|x\|_{2}=1}\dist(x,G_{\Phi}^{\perp})^{2}=\Omega(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)})
. By
Theorem

13
, this implies
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
, but we want a quantitative bound.

By
Lemma

20
,
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
admits a Hermitian orthonormal basis. Let
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{l}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\widehat{S}
and
\{s^{\perp}_{1},\dots,s^{\perp}_{l^{\prime}}\}
be a Hermitian orthonormal basis of
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
. Then, every rank one operator
uv^{*}
can be decomposed as:

uv^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\langle s_{i},uv^{*}\rangle s_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{l^{\prime}}\langle s^{\perp}_{j},uv^{*}\rangle s_{j}^{\perp}.

As
\dist(uv^{*},\widehat{S})=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|uv^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{l}\langle s_{i},uv^{*}\rangle s_{i}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{l^{\prime}}\langle s^{\perp}_{j},uv^{*}\rangle s_{j}^{\perp}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{l^{\prime}}|\langle s^{\perp}_{j},uv^{*}\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{l^{\prime}}\max_{j}|\langle s_{j}^{\perp},uv^{*}\rangle|.

Thus there exists
\tilde{j}
such that
|\langle s^{\perp}_{\tilde{j}},uv^{*}\rangle|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{l^{\prime}}}\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\right)
. As
l^{\prime}\leq\dim(\widehat{S}^{\perp})\leq\dim(\B(\mathbb{C}^{2n}))=4n^{2}
and we have
|\langle s^{\perp}_{\tilde{j}},uv^{*}\rangle|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\right)
.

As we explained in the previous paragraph,
\widehat{S}^{\perp}
is the quantum confusability graph of some channel
\Phi
with Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}_{i}
. By the second paragraph of
Lemma

11
, we can choose the Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}_{i}
so that, for all
i,j
,
K_{i}^{*}K_{j}=\delta_{i,j}s^{\perp}_{i}
. In particular, we have

|\langle K^{*}_{\tilde{j}}K_{\tilde{j}},uv^{*}\rangle|=|\langle s^{\perp}_{\tilde{j}},uv^{*}\rangle|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{\mathrm{poly}(n)}}\right).

(41)

Consider
u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
such that

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi(uu^{*})-\Phi(vv^{*})\|_{1},

which exist as per
Lemma

5
. We now use the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequality
[
11
]
to upper-bound the trace distance between
\Phi(uu^{*})
and
\Phi(vv^{*})
. For two states
\rho,\sigma
, we write
F(\rho,\sigma)=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}^{2}
the fidelity between
\rho
and
\sigma
, the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequality then states that:

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}.

Therefore, we have:

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq\sqrt{1-F(\Phi(uu^{*}),\Phi(vv^{*}))}.

Then,

\displaystyle F(\Phi(uu^{*}),\Phi(vv^{*}))

\displaystyle=\|\sqrt{\Phi(uu^{*})}\sqrt{\Phi(vv^{*})}\|_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\sqrt{\Phi(uu^{*})}\sqrt{\Phi(vv^{*})}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sqrt{\Phi(uu^{*})}\sqrt{\Phi(vv^{*})},\sqrt{\Phi(uu^{*})}\sqrt{\Phi(vv^{*})}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Phi(uu^{*}),\Phi(vv^{*})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{i}K_{i}uu^{*}K_{i}^{*},\sum_{j}K_{j}vv^{*}K_{j}^{*}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}|\langle K_{i}^{*}K_{j},uv^{*}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|\langle K_{\tilde{j}}^{*}K_{\tilde{j}},uv^{*}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\mathrm{poly}(n)}\right),

where the last inequality follows from
Eq.

41
. Finally,

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq\sqrt{1-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\mathrm{poly}(n)}\right)}\leq 1-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\mathrm{poly}(n)}\right).

∎

4.1
Quantum Doeblin Coefficients

The trace norm (or total-variation) contraction coefficient of a classical channel is upper bounded by the Doeblin coefficient
[
7
]
. In
[
22
, Theorem 8.17]
, a quantum version of Doeblin’s theorem was proposed. Quantum Doeblin coefficients were defined and studied in a systematic way more recently in
[
15
,
13
]
.

Recall that for any channel
\Phi
, we denote
A
its input system,
B
its output system,
d_{A}
, and
d_{B}
their respective dimension and
J(\Phi)
the Choi state of
\Phi
.

Definition 21

(Corollary 3.7 in
[
15
]
)
.

Given a channel
\Phi
, the quantum Doeblin coefficient of
\Phi
is defined as

\alpha(\Phi):=\sup_{X_{B}\in\textup{Herm}(B)}\bigl\{\tr[X_{B}]:I_{A}\otimes X_{B}\preceq J(\Phi)\bigr\},

(42)

where, for matrices
X
,
Y
,
X\preceq Y
if and only if
Y-X
is positive semidefinite.

This quantity yields an upper bound on the trace norm contraction coefficient.

Proposition 22

(Lemma 3.2 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a channel, then

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq 1-\alpha(\Phi).

(43)

Therefore, for any channel
\Phi
, whenever
\alpha(\Phi)>0
, we have
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
. The authors of
[
13
,
15
]
asked whether for any
\Phi
such that
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
it was always the case that
\alpha(\Phi)>0
. If that were the case, then deciding whether
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)=1
or
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
would have been in
P
as
\alpha(\Phi)
is computable from a semidefinite program whose size is polynomial in
d_{A}d_{B}

[
15
]
.
Therefore, our result
Theorem

3
answers this question by the negative. We further present here an explicit example of a channel
\Phi
such that
\alpha(\Phi)=0
while
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
.

Proposition 23
.

Let
\cH
be a Hilbert space of dimension
d>2
and
\{\ket{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
an orthonormal basis of
\cH
. The channel
\Phi:\B(\cH)\rightarrow\B(\cH)
with Kraus operators
\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\ket{i}\bra{j}:i\not=j,1\leq i,j\leq d\right\}
is such that:

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1\text{ but }\alpha(\Phi)=0.

(44)

Proof.

We first show that
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
. We have

G_{\Phi}=\operatorname{Span}\{\ket{j}\bra{i}\ket{k}\bra{l}:(i,j,k,l)\in[d]^{4},i\not=j,k\not=l\}.

As
d\geq 3
, for all
(j,l)\in[d]^{2}
, there is an integer
k\in[d]
such that
i\not=k
,
k\not=l
. Thus, for every
(j,l)\in[d]^{2}
,
\ket{j}\bra{l}\in G_{\Phi}
.
Therefore,
G_{\Phi}=\B(\cH)
and thus
G_{\Phi}^{\perp}=\{0\}
. Hence, there is no rank one element in
G_{\Phi}^{\perp}
and by
Theorem

13
,
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
.

On the other hand,
J(\Phi)=\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{i\not=j}\ket{i}\bra{i}\otimes\ket{j}\bra{j}
and thus

\displaystyle J(\Phi)-I_{A}\otimes X_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{j\neq i}\ket{j}\bra{j}-X_{B}\right).

(45)

Positivity of this operator means that for any
i
,
0\preceq\left(\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{j\neq i}\ket{j}\bra{j}-X_{B}\right)
, which implies that
\bra{i}X_{B}\ket{i}\leq 0
and so
\tr(X_{B})\leq 0
. This means
\alpha(\Phi)=0
.
∎

Furthermore, a relaxation of the quantum Doeblin coefficient, called the
induced Doeblin coefficient
, was also proposed in
[
13
]
.

Definition 24

(Induced Doeblin coefficient, Proposition 7 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\alpha_{I}(\Phi)
, the
induced Doeblin coefficient
, be defined as

\alpha_{I}(\Phi)=\max_{\begin{aligned} J(\Phi)-&I_{A}\otimes X_{B}\in\textup{Sep}^{*}(A:B),\\
&X_{B}\in\textup{Herm}(B)\end{aligned}}\tr(X_{B}),

(46)

where
\textup{Sep}^{*}(A:B)
is the dual of the cone of separable operators on the compound system
(A,B)
, which corresponds to the cone of block-positive operators.

The induced Doeblin coefficient is
not
easy to compute but gives an upper bound on
\alpha(\Phi)
by Proposition 5 of
[
13
]
.
Yet, by equation (6.34) in
[
13
]
, we still have

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq 1-\alpha_{I}(\Phi),

for any channel
\Phi
. It is natural to ask whether the induced Doeblin coefficient captures the property
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)<1
. It turns out this is not the case. In fact, the same example
\Phi
of
Proposition

23
also has
\alpha_{I}(\Phi)=0
. This follows since the operator in (
45
) is block-diagonal and hence positivity and block-positivity are equivalent.

5
Converging hierarchy of efficiently computable upper bounds

In this section, we use
[
3
]
to propose a hierarchy of semidefinite programming upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
, which in the special case
k=2
gives bounds on
\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)
.
Recall that for every channel
\Phi
and input operator
X_{A}
, we can express
\Phi(X_{A})
as:

\Phi(X_{A})=d_{A}\tr_{A}(J(\Phi)(X_{A}^{T}\otimes I_{B})).

(47)

We will introduce copies of the system
B
that we will denote
B_{1}\dots B_{m}
. As a shorthand, we write
B_{1}^{m}=B_{1}\dots B_{m}

An operator on
AB_{1}^{m}
will be denoted
W_{AB_{1}^{m}}
and we write
W_{B_{1}^{m}}=\tr_{A}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}})
and
W_{AB_{1}^{l}}=\tr_{B_{l+1}^{m}}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}})
with
B_{l+1}^{m}=B_{l+1},\dots,B_{m}
.
Furthermore, let
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
be the symmetric group on
m
elements. For
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
, let
\mathcal{U}^{\pi}_{B_{1}^{m}}
be the unitary which permutes the systems
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
according to the permutation
\pi
. Its action on product operators over
B_{1}^{m}
is as follows:

\mathcal{U}^{\pi}_{B_{1}^{m}}(W_{B_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes W_{B_{m}}):=W_{B_{\pi(1)}}\otimes\dots\otimes W_{B_{\pi(m)}}.

A multipartite operator
W_{AB_{1}^{m}}
on
AB_{1}^{m}
is called
symmetric with respect to
A
if

(\text{Id}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{B^{n}_{1}}^{\pi})(W_{AB_{1}^{m}})=W_{AB_{1}^{m}},~\forall\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}.

We use the shorthand
j_{1}^{m}
for
j_{1},\dots,j_{m}
.

The following
Theorem

25
gives a hierarchy of semidefinite programs which forms a non-increasing sequence of upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
. We will show in
Theorem

27
that this hierarchy corresponds to the hierarchy proposed in
[
3
]
for general constrained bilinear optimisation and thus we can use their convergence result.

Theorem 25
.

For
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
, the semidefinite programs

\displaystyle\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k)=\max

\displaystyle\quad\begin{cases}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)W_{AB_{1}}^{(i,i)})&\textup{ if }m=1,\\
\frac{d_{A}}{kd_{B}^{m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j_{2}^{m}\in[k]^{m-1}}\tr\big(J(\Phi)W_{AB_{1}}^{(i,ij_{2}^{m})}\big)&\textup{ else,}\\
\end{cases}

(48)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\forall(i,j_{1}^{m})\in[k]^{m+1},~W^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}_{AB_{1}^{m}}\succeq 0,

(49)

\displaystyle\tr(\sum_{(ij_{1}^{m})\in[k]^{m+1}}W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})})=kd_{B}^{m},

(50)

\displaystyle\quad\forall\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{m},W^{(i,j_{\pi(1)},\dots,j_{\pi(m)})}_{AB_{1}^{m}}=\left(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{B_{1}^{m}}^{\pi}\right)\left(W^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}_{AB_{1}^{m}}\right),

(51)

\displaystyle\quad\forall(i,j_{1}^{m-1})\in[k]^{m},~\sum_{j=1}^{k}W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m-1}j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}W_{AB_{1}^{m-1}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m-1}j)}\otimes\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}},

(52)

\displaystyle\quad\forall(i,j_{1}^{m})\in[k]^{m+1},~W_{B_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{m}W_{B_{1}^{m}}^{(l,j_{1}^{m})},

(53)

form a non-increasing sequence of upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
. We write
\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi)=2\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,2)-1
for the corresponding upper bound on the contraction coefficient.

Before proving this theorem, we express the success probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
defined in
Eq.

4
using the Choi state of
\Phi
.

Lemma 26
.

We have:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)=\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}).

(54)

Proof.

We have the following equalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(M_{i}\Phi(\rho_{i}))

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(M_{i}\tr_{A}(J(\Phi)(\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes I_{B})))

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}),

where
(a)
follows from
Eq.

47
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

25
.

We just have to show that the semidefinite programs defined in the theorem are relaxations of the optimisation problem in the right-hand side of
Eq.

54
.

It suffices to take, at the level
m\in\mathbb{N}
of the hierarchy, the variables
W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}
to be equal to the product operator
\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{j_{p}}
, with
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i}
,
\{M_{j}\}_{j}
being the states and POVMs on which the optimisation in
Eq.

54
is done. Then, the positivity and symmetry constrains
Eq.

49
and
Eq.

51
are trivially satisfied. Furthermore, we have:

\tr\bigg(\sum_{(i,j_{1}^{m})\in[k]^{m+1}}\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{j_{p}}\bigg)=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tr(M_{j})\Big)^{m}\bigg)=kd_{B}^{m},

so
Eq.

50
is satisfied. Then, let
(i,j_{1}^{m-1})\in[k]^{m}
be fixed, we have on the one hand:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}}\otimes M_{j}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}}\otimes\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}M_{j}\Big)=\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}}\otimes I_{B};

and, on the other hand:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tr_{B_{m}}\Big(\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}}\otimes M_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tr(M_{j})\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}}=d_{B}\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m-1}M_{j_{p}},

so these variables satisfy
Eq.

52
. Finally, for
(i,j_{1}^{m})\in[k]^{m+1}
fixed, we have:

\displaystyle\tr_{A}(\rho_{i}^{T}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{j_{p}})

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{i_{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{i_{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tr_{A}\Big(\rho_{l}^{T}\otimes\bigotimes_{p=1}^{m}M_{i_{p}}\Big),

so that this choice of variables also satisfies
Eq.

53
.
Then, we have to show that, for this choice of variables, the objective function gives the same value as the objective function in the right-hand side of
Eq.

54
. We present the case
m\geq 2
as the case
m=1
is trivial.

\displaystyle\frac{d_{A}}{kd_{B}^{m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j_{2}^{m}\in[k]^{m-1}}\tr\big(J(\Phi)W_{AB_{1}}^{(i,ij_{2}^{m})}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{kd_{B}^{m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)\sum_{j_{2}^{m}\in[k]^{m-1}}\tr_{B_{2}^{m}}\Big(\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}\bigotimes_{p=2}^{m}M_{j_{p}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{kd_{B}^{m-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)(\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}))\tr\Big(\sum_{j_{2}^{m}\in[k]^{m-1}}\bigotimes_{p=2}^{m}M_{j_{p}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{kd_{B}^{m-1}}\tr(I_{B})^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)(\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)(\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i})).

∎

Note that it is clear from the proof that we can add positive partial transpose (PPT) constraints to the SDP. Indeed, for all
(i,j_{1},\dots,j_{m})\in[k]^{m+1}
, we can additionnaly ask the variables
W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}
to satisfy

T_{A}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})})\succeq 0,~T_{B_{1}}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})})\succeq 0,T_{B_{1}^{2}}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})})\succeq 0,\dots,~T_{B_{1}^{m-1}}(W_{AB_{1}^{m}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})})\succeq 0,

(55)

where
T_{S}
denote the partial transpose on system
S
. Note that products of positive operators satisfy the PPT constraints of
Eq.

55
, therefore the SDP we obtain by adding
Eq.

55
is still a relaxation of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
(
Eq.

4
). We denote
\mathrm{SDP}_{m}^{\mathrm{PPT}}
the value of the corresponding SDP when adding the PPT constraints.

In the remainder of this section, we show that the hierarchy of semidefinite programs defined in
Theorem

25
actually converges to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
. Note that, although semidefinite programs are efficiently computable, the number of variables of
\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k)
grows exponentially in
m
, while we prove only a convergence speed in
\text{poly}(d)/\sqrt{m}
, therefore, the convergence of this hierarchy to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
does neither contradict
Theorem

2
nor
Theorem

3
.

Theorem 27

(Convergence of the SDP of
Theorem

25
)
.

For all
\Phi
,
k
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

0\leq\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k)-\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)\leq\frac{\mathrm{poly}(d)}{\sqrt{m}},

(56)

with
d=\max\{d_{A},d_{B}\}
with
A
the input system of
\Phi
and
B
its output system, so that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k).

(57)

In order to prove the convergence, we express
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
as a constrained bilinear program
[
3
]
and show that the SDP hierarchy we propose in
Theorem

25
is the same as the general converging one derived in
[
3
]
. We write
\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ket{i}\bra{i}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}
, with
k=\dim(\bar{A})=\dim(\bar{B})
the (unnormalized) maximally correlated operator between systems
\bar{A}
and
\bar{B}
.

Lemma 28
.

We can express
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
as the following constrained bilinear program:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)=d_{A}d_{B}

\displaystyle\max\tr\Big((J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}})(W_{A\bar{A}}\otimes W_{B\bar{B}})\Big),

(58)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t.}~W_{A\bar{A}}\succeq 0,~W_{B\bar{B}}\succeq 0,

(59)

\displaystyle\qquad\tr(W_{A\bar{A}})=1,~\tr(W_{B\bar{B}})=1,

(60)

\displaystyle\qquad\tr_{A}(W_{A\bar{A}})=\frac{I_{\bar{A}}}{k},

(61)

\displaystyle\qquad\tr_{\bar{B}}(W_{B\bar{B}})=\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}}.

(62)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in two steps, first we show that we can suppose without loss of generality that the variables
W_{A\bar{A}}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}
are block-diagonal with respect to the systems
\bar{A}
and
\bar{B}
. Then, we show that for such block-diagonal variables, the program of the theorem is equivalent to the optimisation problem of
Lemma

26
.

For the first part of the proof, recall that
k=\dim(\bar{A})=\dim(\bar{B})
. Then, we can write

W_{A\bar{A}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}W_{A}^{(i,j)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j}_{\bar{A}},\quad W_{B\bar{B}}=\frac{1}{d_{B}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}W_{B}^{(i,j)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j}_{\bar{B}}.

(63)

From these variables we construct the block-diagonal operators
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
,
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
obtained from
W_{A\bar{A}}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}
by keeping only the diagonal blocks in the decomposition of
Eq.

63
, i.e. write

W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{A}^{(i,i)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}},\quad W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}=\frac{1}{d_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{B}^{(i,i)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{B}}.

(64)

The objective function of the program of
Lemma

28
evaluated on the two variables
W_{A\bar{A}}
,
W_{B\bar{B}}
can be written as:

\displaystyle d_{A}d_{B}\tr\Big((J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}})(W_{A\bar{A}}\otimes W_{B\bar{B}})\Big)

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\bigg(\Big(J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{B}}\Big]\Big)\Big(\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}W_{A}^{(i,j)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j}_{\bar{A}}\Big]\otimes\Big[\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}W_{B}^{(i,j)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j}_{\bar{B}}\Big]\Big)\bigg)

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\Big(\sum_{i,j,j^{\prime}=1}^{k}J(\Phi)_{AB}(W_{A}^{(i,j)}\otimes W_{B}^{(i,j^{\prime})})\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j}_{\bar{A}}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{j^{\prime}}_{\bar{B}}\Big)

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}J(\Phi)_{AB}(W_{A}^{(i,i)}\otimes W_{B}^{(i,i)})\Big)

(68)

\displaystyle=d_{A}d_{B}\tr\Big(\bigl(J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}}\bigr)\bigl(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}\otimes W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}\bigr)\Big).

(69)

Furthermore, if
W_{A\bar{A}}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}
satisfy the constraints of the bilinear optimisation program of the lemma, then so do
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
,
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
. If
W_{A\bar{A}}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}
are positive semidefinite, then so are all their diagonal blocks
W_{A}^{(i,i)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}}
and
W_{B}^{(i,i)}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{B}}
, thus
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
are constrained to be positive semidefinite. The three other conditions involve traces or partial traces and are also easily satisfied by
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
and
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
as we have the equalities
\tr(W_{A\bar{A}})=\tr(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D})
,
\tr(W_{B\bar{B}})=\tr(W_{B\bar{B}}^{D})
,
\tr_{A}(W_{A\bar{A}})=\tr_{A}(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D})
and
\tr_{\bar{B}}(W_{B\bar{B}})=\tr_{B}(W_{B\bar{B}}^{D})
.

Therefore we can suppose without loss of generality that we optimise the SDP on the block-diagonal variables
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
,
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
of the form given in
Eq.

63
.

Now, by
Lemma

26
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)=\max_{\begin{aligned} &\{M_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}\text{ POVM},\\
&\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}\end{aligned}}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(J(\Phi)\rho_{i}^{T}\otimes M_{i}).

For
\{\rho_{i}\in\D(\cH):1\leq i\leq k\}
any set of states and
\{M_{i}\succeq 0:1\leq i\leq k\}
any POVM, we associate the block-diagonal variables:

W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rho_{i,A}^{T}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}},\quad W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}=\frac{1}{d_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i,B}\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{B}}.

It is easy to check that these variables satisfy the constraints of the program of
Lemma

28
and, furthermore,

\displaystyle d_{A}d_{B}\tr\Big[(J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}})(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}\otimes W_{B\bar{B}}^{D})\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{k}J(\Phi)_{AB}(W_{A}^{(i,i)}\otimes W_{B}^{(i,i)})\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{k}J(\Phi)_{AB}(\rho_{i,A}^{T}\otimes M_{i,B})\Big],

where
(a)
follows from
Eq.

68
. Therefore, the bilinear optimisation program proposed in this lemma is an upper bound on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
.

To show the inequality in the other direction, consider two block diagonal variables
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}
,
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}
as in
Eq.

63
which satisfy the constraints of the optimisation program. By the previous system of equations, we have:

d_{A}d_{B}\tr\bigl[(J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}})(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}\otimes W_{B\bar{B}}^{D})\bigr]=\frac{d_{A}}{k}\tr\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{k}J(\Phi)_{AB}(W_{A}^{(i,i)}\otimes W_{B}^{(i,i)})\Big].

As
W_{A\bar{A}}^{D}\succeq 0
and
W_{B\bar{B}}^{D}\succeq 0
, we have that each of their diagonal blocks
W_{A}^{(i,i)}
and
W_{B}^{(i,i)}
are positive semidefinite. Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{I_{\bar{A}}}{k}=\tr_{A}(W_{A\bar{A}}^{D})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tr(W_{A}^{(i,i)})\otimes\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}}.

Therefore, for each
i\in[k]
, we have
\tr(W_{A}^{(i,i)})=\bra{i}I_{\bar{A}}\ket{i}=1
, so that all operators
W_{A}^{(i,i)}
are states (or, equivalently, transposes of states). Analogously

\displaystyle\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}}=\tr_{\bar{B}}(W_{B\bar{B}}^{D})=\frac{1}{d_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{B}^{(i,i)}\tr(\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{B}})=\frac{1}{d_{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_{B}^{(i,i)}.

So that

\{W_{B}^{(i,i)}:1\leq i\leq k\}
forms a POVM.
∎

Now, from the bilinear optimisation program of
Lemma

28
, we can construct a hierarchy of semidefinite programs of the form of Eq. 5 in
[
3
]
that will automatically converge to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{succ}}(\Phi,k)
by the results of
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
. This hierarchy can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{SDP}}_{m}(\Phi,k)=d_{A}d_{B}

\displaystyle\max\tr\bigl[(J(\Phi)_{AB}\otimes\Psi^{(k)}_{\bar{A}\bar{B}})(W_{A\bar{A}B\bar{B}})\bigr],

(70)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}}\succeq 0,~\tr(W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}})=1,

(71)

\displaystyle\quad\forall\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},~(\text{Id}_{A\bar{A}}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}}^{\pi})(W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}})=W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}},

(72)

\displaystyle\quad\tr_{A}(W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}})=\frac{I_{\bar{A}}}{k}\otimes W_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}},

(73)

\displaystyle\quad\tr_{\bar{B}_{m}}(W_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}})=W_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m-1}}\otimes\frac{I_{B_{m}}}{d_{B}}.

(74)

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k)=\widetilde{\mathrm{SDP}}_{m}(\Phi,k).

In fact, given a feasible solution
W_{AB_{1}^{n}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}
for
\mathrm{SDP}_{m}(\Phi,k)
, we define

W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}}=\frac{1}{kd_{B}^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j_{1}^{m}\in[k]^{m}}\ket{i}\bra{i}_{\bar{A}}\otimes\ket{j_{1}^{m}}\bra{j_{1}^{m}}_{\bar{B}_{1}^{m}}\otimes W_{AB_{1}^{n}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}.

We then check that it achieves the same value for the objective function and it satisfies the constraints. In the other direction, given a feasible solution
W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}}
for
\widetilde{\mathrm{SDP}}_{m}(\Phi,k)
, we define

W_{AB_{1}^{n}}^{(i,j_{1}^{m})}=\bra{i}_{\bar{A}}\bra{j_{1}^{m}}_{\bar{B}_{1}^{m}}W_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{m}}\ket{i}_{\bar{A}}\ket{j_{1}^{m}}_{\bar{B}_{1}^{m}},

and also check that it is feasible and achieves the same value for the objective function.
Theorem

27
then follows.

5.1
Numerical illustration

We now briefly illustrate this hierarchy on some simple channels.
We tested the first level of the hierarchy of
Theorem

25

on the channel
\Phi
of
Proposition

23
, i.e., the one with vanishing quantum Doeblin and obtained a bound of

\eta_{\tr}(\Phi)\leq\frac{1}{2}<1.

(75)

Thus, this hierarchy already detects at the first level that this channel has a contraction coefficient
<1
.

We now consider (multiple copies of) amplitude damping channels
\mathcal{A}_{p,\eta}
as in
[
15
]
. Amplitude damping channels are defined via their Kraus operators:

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{\eta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt{1-\eta}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\eta}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-p}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\sqrt{1-\eta}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
p,\eta\in[0,1]
. We obtain the upper bounds given in Fig
1
. Note that this improves on the bounds obtained in
[
15
, Fig. 2]
. In fact, it is simple to see that

\eta_{\tr}(\mathcal{A}_{p,\eta})\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}_{p,\eta}(\ket{+}\bra{+})-\mathcal{A}_{p,\eta}(\ket{-}\bra{-})\|_{1}=\sqrt{\eta},

(76)

with
\ket{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
,
\ket{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}-\ket{1})
so our upper bounds for
\eta_{\tr}(\mathcal{A}_{p,\eta})
are numerically tight.
