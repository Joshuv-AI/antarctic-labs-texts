Anticoagulation in Advanced Chronic Kidney Disease: Navigating Uncertainty
Authors: Arzu Velioğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by a complex hemostatic state in which thrombotic and bleeding risks coexist. As kidney function declines, balancing thrombotic protection against bleeding risk becomes increasingly challenging. Although atrial fibrillation is common and associated with a high stroke risk in advanced CKD, the net clinical benefit of anticoagulation remains uncertain because this population has been largely excluded from pivotal randomized trials. Existing risk scores incompletely capture CKD-specific and dynamic determinants of both thrombosis and bleeding. Observational data suggest potential advantages of apixaban over warfarin, whereas evidence for other agents remains limited. Recent randomized evidence has reinforced the difficulty of extrapolating antithrombotic strategies from the general cardiovascular population to advanced CKD. Future research should prioritize CKD-specific risk prediction, inclusion of advanced CKD populations in randomized trials, and safer anticoagulant strategies, including factor XI/XIa inhibitors.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with a complex and often paradoxical hemostatic profile. Patients are simultaneously predisposed to thrombotic events, such as venous thromboembolism (VTE), atrial fibrillation (AF), stroke, and vascular access thrombosis, and to bleeding complications [1]. As kidney function declines, these abnormalities become more pronounced, particularly in patients with advanced CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) and those on dialysis. This does not merely represent an amplification of traditional cardiovascular risk factors; rather, it constitutes a distinct biological state characterized by uremia-related platelet dysfunction, endothelial injury, chronic inflammation, and altered coagulation pathways [2]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies developed in the general population cannot be directly extrapolated to patients with significant renal impairment. Indeed, most randomized controlled trials evaluating anticoagulants have excluded patients with advanced CKD or end-stage kidney disease. As a result, current practice for these patients relies heavily on post hoc analyses and observational studies, most of which have focused on AF.

AF is highly prevalent in advanced CKD and dialysis populations, and the risk of stroke is substantially increased [3]. At first glance, anticoagulation appears to be an obvious therapeutic solution. However, the reality is considerably more complex. Observational studies indicate that anticoagulation may not consistently reduce stroke risk while increasing bleeding risk in patients with AF and advanced CKD.

Risk Stratification Tools

Clinical decision-making regarding initiation of anticoagulation traditionally relies on risk stratification tools such as CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores. Although useful in the general population, these models have important limitations in CKD [4,5]. Kidney dysfunction is a powerful determinant of both thrombotic and bleeding risk, yet it is incompletely incorporated into existing prediction models. Furthermore, dynamic factors unique to advanced CKD, including fluctuating renal function, dialysis-related hemodynamic changes, vascular calcification, and inflammation, are not adequately captured. Although alternative models such as ATRIA and GARFIELD-AF incorporate some renal variables, they have not been specifically validated in advanced CKD and have not demonstrated better discrimination than CHA2DS2-VASc [5]. Consequently, risk scores may provide an illusion of precision in a population for which uncertainty remains substantial.

Oral Anticoagulant Agents in Advanced CKD

For decades, vitamin K antagonists represented the cornerstone of anticoagulation therapy. Warfarin remains familiar to clinicians and offers the theoretical advantage of laboratory monitoring. However, achieving stable anticoagulation in advanced CKD is notoriously difficult. Time within therapeutic range is often suboptimal, and warfarin management algorithms are inconsistently applied in nephrology practice [6]. In addition, concerns regarding accelerated vascular calcification and calciphylaxis have further reduced enthusiasm for its use in patients with advanced CKD [6].

The introduction of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) was therefore welcomed as a potential solution. In pivotal AF trials, including ARISTOTLE and ROCKET-AF, DOACs demonstrated efficacy at least comparable to that of warfarin for stroke prevention, with favorable bleeding profiles [2]. Subgroup analyses also suggested that the relative benefits of DOACs were generally preserved across declining levels of kidney function. Among available agents, apixaban subsequently emerged as the most extensively studied DOAC in advanced CKD. Multiple observational studies have consistently reported lower rates of major bleeding compared to warfarin while maintaining similar effectiveness for stroke prevention in patients with AF [7]. Observational data in patients with advanced CKD and end-stage kidney disease treated for VTE also suggest that apixaban provides effectiveness and safety comparable to warfarin, with some large database studies reporting lower risks of recurrent VTE and major bleeding [8,9]. Collectively, these findings support apixaban as a reasonable alternative to warfarin in this high-risk population [6]. Rivaroxaban has also attracted interest, particularly in dialysis populations, where the randomized controlled VALKYRIE trial suggested potential reductions in cardiovascular events and bleeding compared to vitamin K antagonists [10]. However, that trial was limited by its small sample size, and the broader evidence base remains insufficient to establish a clear net clinical benefit of rivaroxaban in advanced CKD. The recently reported TRACK trial provides another important perspective. In this randomized placebo-controlled study, low-dose rivaroxaban failed to reduce major cardiovascular events in patients with stage 4-5 CKD, including those receiving dialysis, while increasing major bleeding risk [11]. However, because TRACK largely evaluated primary prevention and included relatively few atherothrombotic events, the question of whether rivaroxaban could provide benefit in secondary prevention remains unresolved. All these findings highlight how the balance between thrombotic benefit and bleeding harm may differ substantially in advanced CKD.

This uncertainty is particularly consequential in kidney transplantation candidates, where antithrombotic treatment may increase peritransplant bleeding risk or complicate timely access to transplantation [12]. The question may therefore not be “Which anticoagulant should we use?” but rather “Which patients truly benefit from anticoagulation?”

Despite the need for individualized decision-making, current guidelines offer limited granularity, with recommendations largely based on CKD stage. Cardiology clinical practice guidelines strongly recommend warfarin or, preferably, DOACs in patients with CKD stage 3 and AF at elevated risk of stroke. In stage 4 CKD, warfarin or DOACs may be considered, although the recommendation is less definitive (Class 2a). For patients with stage 5 CKD or those receiving dialysis, evidence supporting DOAC use remains limited, and warfarin or apixaban may be considered only with greater caution (Class 2b) [13]. The 2024 update of the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes CKD guidelines is more directive and recommends the use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants in preference to vitamin K antagonists (e.g., warfarin) for thromboprophylaxis in AF for patients with CKD of stages 1-4 (level of evidence: 1C). However, data remain insufficient for patients receiving dialysis, and careful dose adjustment according to kidney function is emphasized in CKD of stages 4-5 [14].

Ultimately, future research should move beyond comparisons between current anticoagulant agents alone. The central challenge is identifying which patients with advanced CKD derive a meaningful net benefit from anticoagulation and which patients are more likely to be harmed. More sophisticated risk prediction tools incorporating CKD-specific clinical and biological factors will likely be required (Figure 1). Randomized trials including patients with advanced CKD must also become a priority rather than an exception.

Future Directions

Another emerging area that may reshape anticoagulation in advanced CKD is the development of factor XI/XIa inhibitors, including agents such as abelacimab, osocimab, and asundexian. By targeting a component of the intrinsic coagulation pathway that appears to contribute more to thrombosis than to physiological hemostasis, these agents aim to reduce thrombotic risk while preserving hemostatic function. Early clinical studies have shown promising antithrombotic efficacy with substantially lower bleeding rates compared to conventional anticoagulants [15]. Beyond efficacy alone, future anticoagulant strategies in CKD will likely be judged by their safety and reversibility. The availability of specific reversal agents, such as andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors, underscores the increasing emphasis on safety considerations when selecting anticoagulant therapies. Furthermore, artificial intelligence and machine-learning models may enable more precise and dynamic prediction of thrombotic and bleeding risks in advanced CKD [16].

Conclusion

Perhaps the greatest lesson from the studies to date on anticoagulation in advanced CKD is that the goal is not to eliminate thrombosis at any cost, nor to avoid bleeding at all costs. These risks coexist and neither can be considered in isolation. The challenge is not to choose one side of the paradox, but rather to identify the patients who can safely navigate between them.
