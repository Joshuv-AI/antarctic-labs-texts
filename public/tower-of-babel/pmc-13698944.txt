Wild-Type Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy Mimicking Cardiac Sarcoidosis on 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose-Positron Emission Tomography/Computed Tomography

An 85-year-old man with arrhythmias and nonobstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) presented with fatigue and dyspnea. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) demonstrated myocardial FDG uptake, initially attributed to cardiac sarcoidosis, and methotrexate was initiated. Over time, progressive left ventricular (LV) thickening and worsening strain abnormalities prompted reevaluation. Resolution of FDG uptake, a high amyloid probability on artificial intelligence (AI)-enhanced electrocardiogram (ECG), and grade 3 uptake on technetium-99m pyrophosphate imaging confirmed wild-type transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy. Methotrexate was discontinued and tafamidis initiated, with subsequent clinical improvement. Case Presentation

History of presentation

We report a case of wild-type transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (wtATTR-CM) presenting with 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG)/13N-ammonia positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) findings suggestive of myocardial inflammation, initially interpreted as isolated cardiac sarcoidosis.

An 85-year-old man initially presented with progressive exertional fatigue, intermittent palpitations, fatigue, and occasional lightheadedness, but denied syncope, chest pain, lower extremity edema, or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea.

Initial evaluation showed high-sensitivity troponin T of 25 ng/L (reference range: ≤15 ng/L) and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide of 268 pg/mL (reference range: ≤540 pg/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed increased concentric left ventricular (LV) wall thickness of 14 mm, reduced medial e’ velocity (0.04 m/s), and normal global longitudinal strain (GLS) with a relative apical sparing pattern (Figure 1A).

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated patchy mid-myocardial and transmural delayed enhancement predominantly involving basal segments (Figure 2). Initial 18F-FDG/13N-ammonia PET/CT revealed basal lateral, inferior, and septal perfusion defects with corresponding FDG uptake (Figure 3A), consistent with active myocardial inflammation. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with presumed isolated cardiac sarcoidosis and started on prednisone and methotrexate.

Over the following 3 years, he continued to experience persistent fatigue and dyspnea despite immunosuppressive therapy, with intermittent adjustments in methotrexate dosing.

Past medical history

Supraventricular tachycardia with prior ablation for atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (5 years before his initial presentation with presumed cardiac sarcoidosis), nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, hyperlipidemia, and nonobstructive coronary artery disease.

Differential diagnosis

Progressive cardiac sarcoidosis, myocarditis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, cardiac amyloidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (including sigmoid septal variant), and other infiltrative cardiomyopathies.

Investigations

High-sensitivity troponin T increased to 39 ng/L and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide to 1,279 pg/mL. Repeat TTE showed LV wall thickness progression to 18 mm and worsening GLS with more pronounced apical sparing (Figure 1B). Follow-up 18F-FDG/13N-ammonia PET/CT demonstrated persistent perfusion defects but complete resolution of FDG uptake (Figure 3B), suggesting absence of active myocardial inflammation. CMR findings remained notable for patchy mid-myocardial and transmural delayed enhancement involving basal segments (Figure 2).

In light of these findings, an alternative infiltrative cardiomyopathy was considered. Artificial intelligence–enhanced electrocardiography revealed a high amyloid probability score of 0.98 (threshold >0.485; sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.85), compared with 0.34 previously. Cardiac amyloid radionuclide imaging demonstrated grade 3 myocardial tracer uptake on single-photon emission computed tomography combined with computed tomography (Figures 3C to 3E). Plasma cell dyscrasia evaluation and transthyretin gene testing were negative, confirming the diagnosis of wtATTR-CM.

Management

Methotrexate was discontinued and tafamidis was initiated.

Outcome and follow-up

At 2-year follow-up, the patient reported improvement in his functional status.

Discussion

Although historically underdiagnosed, wtATTR-CM is now increasingly recognized with the availability of noninvasive diagnostic tools and disease-modifying therapies. It accounts for up to 10% to 15% of older adults with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and increased LV wall thickness, particularly in men older than 60 years.1

Multimodality imaging is central to raising suspicion and establishing the diagnosis of wtATTR-CM. TTE may demonstrate increased LV wall thickness, diastolic dysfunction, and abnormal GLS with relative apical sparing.2 CMR typically shows diffuse subendocardial or transmural late gadolinium enhancement with elevated native T1 and extracellular volume fraction.2 However, neither TTE nor CMR is diagnostic and these findings may overlap with other infiltrative or inflammatory cardiomyopathies.1 Noninvasive diagnosis requires abnormal cardiac amyloid radionuclide imaging in the absence of plasma cell dyscrasia.1 On the other hand, 18F-FDG/13N-ammonia PET/CT is routinely used to evaluate inflammatory cardiomyopathies such as cardiac sarcoidosis by detecting myocardial inflammation; however, its role in cardiac amyloidosis remains poorly defined.

This case highlights a diagnostic challenge in which early findings were suggestive of an inflammatory cardiomyopathy, leading to treatment for presumed isolated cardiac sarcoidosis. In retrospect, several features at initial presentation, including mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin T, increased concentric LV wall thickness, reduced medial tissue Doppler velocity, and relative apical sparing strain pattern, were suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis. However, the presence of focal FDG uptake on PET/CT coupled with history of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia was interpreted as more consistent with an inflammatory etiology such as cardiac sarcoidosis. In the absence of extracardiac FDG uptake, diagnosis was presumed isolated cardiac sarcoidosis, which is a recognized clinical entity.3 Unfortunately, extracellular volume quantification was not available from the initial CMR, which may have provided further discriminatory value between amyloidosis and sarcoidosis.4

The overall clinical course supports wtATTR-CM as the primary underlying process. Despite immunosuppressive therapy, the patient's symptoms persisted and he demonstrated progressive increase in LV wall thickness and worsening global strain with a pattern of relative apical sparing, whereas follow-up PET/CT showed complete resolution of FDG uptake. This discordance between resolution of metabolic activity and progression of clinical and structural disease favors a noninflammatory infiltrative process, such as ATTR-CM, as the primary driver of cardiomyopathy.

Emerging data suggest that myocardial inflammation may be present in a subset of patients with cardiac amyloidosis, potentially related to amyloid fibril deposition and local immune activation.5,6 Proposed mechanisms include macrophage infiltration, cytokine-mediated myocardial injury, and altered myocardial metabolism leading to increased glucose uptake. However, the extent to which FDG uptake represents true inflammation vs nonspecific metabolic activity remains unclear.7 Importantly, FDG uptake in amyloidosis has been variably reported and is not consistently observed, limiting its diagnostic utility.

In this context, the resolution of FDG uptake with methotrexate requires careful interpretation. Methotrexate is not expected to affect amyloid fibril deposition. The observed change in FDG signal likely reflects suppression of a transient or nonspecific inflammatory or metabolic signal rather than treatment of an underlying inflammatory cardiomyopathy. An alternative explanation is that the initial FDG uptake represented a coincidental inflammatory process such as myocarditis; however, the lack of sustained response to immunosuppression and the discordant imaging trajectory make this less likely.

Ultimately, this case highlights the importance of integrating serial multimodality imaging with longitudinal clinical assessment. In patients with increased LV wall thickness and evolving imaging features, apparent inflammatory findings on PET/CT should be interpreted with caution. Recognition of wtATTR-CM is critical, as accurate diagnosis allows avoidance of unnecessary immunosuppression and initiation of disease-modifying therapy such as tafamidis, which has been shown to reduce morbidity and mortality in ATTR-CM.8

Conclusions

wtATTR-CM can present with FDG uptake on PET/CT, mimicking inflammatory cardiomyopathy and leading to potential misdiagnosis. Resolution of FDG uptake despite progressive structural disease should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and evaluation for amyloidosis. Serial multimodality imaging and longitudinal reassessment are essential to distinguish inflammatory from infiltrative cardiomyopathies and to ensure timely initiation of appropriate therapy.
