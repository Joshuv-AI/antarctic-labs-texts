Beyond PaO2/FiO2 at altitude: are we correcting the ratio in the wrong direction?
Authors: Pablo Álvarez-Maldonado, Lucero A Torres-Alarcón, Ulises W Cerón-Díaz
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the review by Al-Husinat and colleagues, which offers a thoughtful physiological reappraisal of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and advocates for a broader, multidimensional interpretation of hypoxaemia [1]. We agree with the authors not only that oxygenation indices should not be interpreted in isolation, but also that the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, despite its well-recognised limitations, should remain the initial step in the assessment of patients with ARDS due to its simplicity. However, one issue warrants further discussion: the physiological interpretation of PaO2/FiO2 at altitude.

For decades, ARDS definitions, clinical trials, and guidance documents have recommended correcting PaO2/FiO2 for altitude by multiplying the observed ratio by the local barometric pressure divided by 760 mmHg, that is, [PaO2/FiO2 × (barometric pressure/760)]. This correction is intuitively appealing because it acknowledges that arterial oxygen tension decreases as barometric pressure falls. Yet its physiological basis may be problematic. At altitude, lower barometric pressure reduces inspired oxygen pressure and, consequently, alveolar oxygen tension. Thus, the measured PaO2 is already lower, at least in part, because of reduced environmental oxygen availability rather than exclusively because of more severe pulmonary gas-exchange failure. Applying a factor below 1 to the observed PaO2/FiO2 reduces the ratio even further and may therefore impose a double penalty for the same barometric effect.

A simple bedside example illustrates the problem. In Mexico City, where barometric pressure is approximately 580 mmHg, the conventional correction factor is about 0.76. A patient with a PaO2 of 90 mmHg while receiving an FiO2 of 0.50 has an observed PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 180. Under the conventional formula, this becomes 137, suggesting substantially more severe oxygenation failure. However, because baseline alveolar oxygen tension is intrinsically lower at this altitude, the observed PaO2 has already been downwardly displaced by hypobaria. From this perspective, lowering the ratio again appears physiologically incongruent. An alternative interpretation is that the difference from sea-level oxygen pressure should be added back to contextualise the observed ratio rather than subtracted from it: [PaO2/FiO2 × (760/barometric pressure)]. By that logic, the same patient would have a ratio closer to 224, shifting the interpretation from apparent severe hypoxaemia to a less advanced category.

Expanding this physiological explanation, a numerical example based on the recent review of the oxygen cascade by Wongtirawit et al. [2] is presented in Table 1. In individuals without primary respiratory pathology, the reduction in PaO2 at altitude begins before pulmonary gas exchange occurs. Beyond barometric pressure alone, humidification, alveolar PCO₂, diffusion gradients, and tissue oxygen consumption all contribute to the interpretation of PaO₂ at altitude [3]. At a barometric pressure of 580 mmHg, atmospheric PO2, humidified inspired PO2, and estimated alveolar PO2 are all substantially lower than at sea level, even considering that the lower expected alveolar PCO₂ in acclimatised individuals partially attenuates the fall in alveolar oxygen tension. Consequently, if the observed PaO2/FiO2 ratio is already displaced downward by hypobaria, applying the conventional correction then lowers it again, potentially exaggerating the severity of gas-exchange impairment.

This is not merely a mathematical nuance. If the conventional barometric correction systematically lowers PaO2/FiO2 in acclimatised patients, ARDS severity may be overstated at altitude. Such misclassification could influence bedside decisions, distort comparisons across populations, and affect the apparent prognostic performance of oxygenation-based severity strata. Importantly, contemporary evidence has already described the traditional correction as imprecise in acclimatised mechanically ventilated patients [4]. In parallel, recent high-altitude perspectives have suggested that patients treated at altitude may have better outcomes, partly attributed to chronic hypoxia adaptation and distinct pulmonary biology [5]. While these mechanisms are plausible, a competing methodological explanation should also be considered: if patients at altitude are assigned to lower PaO2/FiO2 categories because of a physiologically questionable correction, they may appear to survive better than sea-level patients within the same severity stratum simply because those strata are not truly equivalent.

We therefore suggest that the discussion should move beyond whether PaO2/FiO2 ought to be adjusted at altitude and instead address whether it has been adjusted in the correct direction. The review by Al-Husinat et al. rightly emphasizes that oxygenation alone incompletely reflects the burden of lung injury. At altitude, this limitation may be compounded by a barometric correction that obscures rather than clarifies physiological meaning. Reconsidering how PaO2/FiO2 is interpreted in hypobaric environments may help avoid severity inflation, improve comparability across studies, and refine both the clinical and epidemiological understanding of ARDS in high-altitude settings.
