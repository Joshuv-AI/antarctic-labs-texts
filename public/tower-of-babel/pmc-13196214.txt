Impact of multimorbidity on change in quality of life in a multi-ethnic Asian population: a longitudinal study
Authors: Fatima Zahoor, Hamimatunnisa Johar, Joanne Ryan, Devi Mohan, Sophia Zoungas, Tin Tin Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing burden of multimorbidity is associated with a risk of poorer health outcomes and mortality; however, evidence on its long-term impact on quality of life (QoL) remains limited. This study aims to explore the association of multimorbidity and multimorbidity clusters with QoL over a five-year period in a multiethnic, semi-rural cohort across different age groups.MethodsThis study utilized two-wave data from 8280 participants aged 18 years and above, collected as part of health surveys in 2013 and 2018 at the South East Asia Community Observatory (SEACO) Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) site in Malaysia. Multimorbidity was defined as having two or more of the following eight self-reported chronic conditions: diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease (chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal failure), arthritis, asthma, and obesity. QoL was assessed using the WHOQOL-BREF, a 26-item questionnaire comprising four domains (physical, psychological, social relationships, and environmental health). Multivariable linear regression models were used for both count and clusters of chronic conditions to evaluate the association between multimorbidity and change in QoL over 5 years.ResultsAt baseline, multimorbidity was significantly associated with better QoL in the social relationships domain only. Multimorbidity at baseline was not significantly associated with the change in QoL over 5 years across any age group. Two clusters of multimorbidity were identified: [1] cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal conditions and [2] cardiorespiratory and renal conditions. Among younger adults (18–34 years), cluster 1 was associated with improvement in the psychological domain (ß: 4.54, SE: 2.25, p-value = 0.03), whereas among adults (35–59 years), cluster 2 was associated with a decline in social relationships (ß: -6.57, SE: 2.87, p-value = 0.01).ConclusionsThe study findings highlight that clinicians and policymakers need to focus on developing age-tailored interventions. Moreover, a simple count of chronic conditions fails to fully capture whether a certain group of conditions is resulting in a decline in QoL. Therefore, a cluster-based approach could provide a more useful insight.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02528-6.

Introduction

Due to advances in healthcare and prolonged life expectancy, the global population is ageing. This demographic shift contributes to a higher number of older people living with multiple chronic conditions, also known as multimorbidity [1]. The prevalence of multimorbidity is steadily increasing worldwide, particularly in the low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The socioeconomic disadvantages create additional health system challenges and modern lifestyle changes, which increase the risk of non-communicable diseases and thus multimorbidity [2]. Systematic reviews in LMICs found that the overall pooled prevalence of multimorbidity ranged from 29% to 36% [3, 4]. Although multimorbidity risk increases with age, over half of the individuals with multimorbidity are in younger and mid-life age groups [5], and over four-fifths of multimorbidity incidence cases occur earlier in the life course [6]. This emphasizes the important impact it also has on younger age groups.

Multimorbidity is associated with increased mortality risk, poorer quality of life (QoL), and higher healthcare costs [7, 8]. QoL has become a crucial patient-focused factor, and it has been associated with health and social outcomes [9]. It is therefore important to examine QoL in the context of multimorbidity as it may help identify those more at risk of worse disease outcomes and can benefit most from treatments and targeted interventions [10]. Understanding the relative impact of multimorbidity on QoL is essential for better allocation of resources for research and health care.

QoL is a multidimensional construct, measured across the physical, mental, social, and environmental domains, which influence the overall functionality of individuals [11, 12]. Previous studies show that multimorbidity is inversely associated with QoL [9, 13], which worsens with increasing numbers of chronic conditions [14–16]. A meta-analysis found that each additional condition reduced the WHOQoL-BREF physical health domain score by 4.37% (95% CI: -7.13%, -1.61%) [9]. Moreover, specific chronic disease patterns distinctly affect physical and psychological functioning [17, 18], yet the impact of multimorbidity clusters on QoL remains under-researched.

Cross-sectional studies, mostly involving older individuals and conducted mainly in high-income countries, did not differentiate among specific QoL domains. The QoL may change over time, and a cross-sectional design with a single measurement may be insufficient to establish causality [19]. The aim of this study is to examine the association between the number and clusters of chronic conditions and QoL in a multi-ethnic, semi-rural cohort over a 5-year period.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This study utilised the dataset from the South East Asia Community Observatory (SEACO) which is a health and demographic surveillance system (HDSS) located in Segamat district, Johor state in Malaysia. The SEACO was established by Monash University in 2011 [20]. The SEACO surveillance area is largely semirural and comprises an ethnic mix of Malays, Chinese, Indians, and Orang-Asli. More details of SEACO are provided elsewhere [20]. 18,686 participants aged 18 years and above responded at baseline health survey in 2013. A follow-up survey was performed among 24,710 participants in 2018. After excluding participants who did not participate in both baseline and follow-up surveys (n = 9821) and had missing data on chronic conditions at baseline (n = 585), the final study sample included in this study consisted of 8280 participants (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of study participants

All study participants received a briefing about the health survey’s objectives from trained enumerators. Individual face-to-face interviews were conducted regarding sociodemographic characteristics and health-related issues using standardised health assessments. The health screening and anthropometric measurements were conducted according to the World Health Organisation STEPwise survey approach to Surveillance of non-communicable diseases (STEPS) [21]. The data were recorded directly into Android mobile devices and tablets using survey forms designed in Open Data Kit in the baseline (2013) and SurveyCTO in the follow-up (2018), with the language matched to the participant’s native language (i.e., Malay, Mandarin, or Tamil).

Multimorbidity

Multimorbidity was defined as having two or more [22] of the following eight physical conditions: diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease (chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal failure), arthritis, asthma, and obesity. The selection criteria for chronic conditions were based on their high prevalence in the country and study population and their potential to cause disability and mortality, consistent with commonly used definitions of multimorbidity in population-based studies [23, 24]. Mental health conditions were not included in the multimorbidity definition due to the unavailability of data on diagnosed mental health conditions. Chronic conditions were self-reported based on the previous clinical diagnosis, and participants were asked, “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you had….? (yes/no)”. Obesity was identified based on actual measurements of height (meters) and weight (kg) measured with portable electronic weighing scales with electronic height sensors (Patient Focus, model GBS-721). WHO body mass index (BMI) classification was used to measure obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) [25, 26].

Quality of life

The primary outcome measure was QoL, measured using WHOQOL-BREF, which is the shorter version of the World Health Organization QoL questionnaire [11]. The WHOQOL-BREF is a 26-item scale that contains two independent items from the overall QoL (“How would you rate your quality of life?”) and general health (“How satisfied are you with your health?”). The rest of the 24 items were grouped into four domains: physical health (7 items), psychological health (6 items), social relationships (3 items) and environmental health (8 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The mean score of items within each domain was used to calculate the domain score. Raw domain scores were transformed by multiplying by four to obtain a score comparable to the WHOQOL-100 [27]. Items 3, 4 and 26 were reverse-coded, and higher scores mean a higher QoL. The score was not calculated for the domain with more than one missing value. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.72 for the physical domain, 0.71 for the psychological domain, 0.81 for social relationships and 0.83 for the environmental domain.

Covariates

Age was classified into three categories: 18–34 (younger adults), 35–59 (adults), and ≥ 60 (older adults), adapted from the WHO’s age distribution [28]. Other covariates included sex (male or female), ethnicity (Malay, Chinese, Indian, and others), marital status (never married, married, widow or divorced or separated), living alone (yes vs. no), education level (less than high school vs. completion of high school), employment status (unemployed, pensioner or retiree, housewife, working) and household income (bottom 40% (B40), middle 40% (M40), top 20% (T20)).

Lifestyle factors consisted of smoking status (currently smoking vs. no smoking) and physical activity. Physical activity was assessed using the validated Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [29]. The data were transformed according to the WHO protocol to derive estimates of the average number of minutes per week engaged in physical activity across three domains (occupational, transport, and leisure), and total physical activity at each time point was calculated. Participants were classified as physically active if they engaged in > 600 MET minutes of physical activity per week [29]. The Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated using the height (meters) and weight (kg) of individuals measured by portable electronic weighing scales with electronic height sensors (Patient Focus, model GBS-721), and categorized based on the WHO BMI classification. Additional psychological covariates included depressive symptoms, anxiety and perceived stress assessed by the validated Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale–21 (DASS-21) [30].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. The multimorbidity vs. no multimorbidity groups were compared using an independent t-test (means) and a chi-square test (percentages). The change scores of participants with and without multimorbidity were also compared by an independent t-test. The change scores were defined as the difference between QoL scores at follow-up and baseline. A positive value represents an improvement in the QoL score, and a negative value represents a worsening of the QoL score. P-values for pairwise comparisons were obtained from estimated marginal means using the emmeans package in R.

Multimorbidity clusters were identified using agglomerative hierarchical clustering with the R package “hclust” to identify the chronic conditions that clustered together. The Jaccard coefficient was used to measure the similarity matrix for binary disease variables. This coefficient only considers the diseases that at least one of the two cases has and ignores diseases that neither case has [31]. The Ward method was used for dendrogram analysis because it minimizes the variance within clusters, resulting in more compact clusters [31], unlike single and average linkage methods that tend to group observations into a single cluster [32]. The number of final clusters was determined by the dendrogram visualisation (cut-off threshold), prior research and clinical significance [31].

Linear regression analysis was used to investigate the relationship between multimorbidity and both baseline QoL scores and changes in QoL scores. Moreover, the association of multimorbidity clusters with change in QoL scores across all domains was also identified. Multimorbidity was entered into the model as a binary variable (0 = no multimorbidity, 1 = multimorbidity). The scores on each QoL domain were considered dependent variables. The model was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, education, employment, living status, smoking, physical activity and depressive symptoms.

As a sensitivity analysis, we assessed whether age, sex, education or living status modifies the association between multimorbidity and QoL. The interaction terms (age*multimorbidity, sex*multimorbidity, education*multimorbidity and living status*multimorbidity) were added separately into the regression models using a forward selection approach. Additionally, we explored the association between change in QoL and chronic conditions, stratified as 0 (no condition), 1 condition, and > 2 conditions, to assess whether a single condition affects QoL or a dose-response relationship exists.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess the multicollinearity of the explanatory variables. VIF values among the variables entered into multivariate analysis were below 5, indicating an acceptable level of multicollinearity; therefore, no variables were removed [33]. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.3 [34]; the models were fitted using the built-in lm-function.

Results

Compared to participants retained in the study, excluded participants (n = 10,406) were significantly younger (mean age 45 vs. 51 years, p < 0.0001), less likely to be female (51.3% vs. 60.3%, p < 0.0001), and had slightly lower BMI (25 vs. 27, p< 0.0001), as given in Supplementary Table 1.

Baseline characteristics of study participants

The final sample consisted of 8,280 participants aged ≥ 18 years with a mean age (SD) of 51.3 (14.6). Most of the participants in the study sample were female (60.3%). 54.4% of the participants have higher education levels, 44% were working, and the majority (87.9%) have personal income < MYR1500. The prevalence of current smoking and alcohol use is very low, and only 13.8% of participants reported physical activity > 600 METs/week.

Table 1 outlines the baseline demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, cardiometabolic, and psychological characteristics of the study participants by multimorbidity status. Participants in the multimorbidity group are significantly older, less educated and have a slightly higher BMI than the participants without multimorbidity. There was no significant difference in smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity among the two groups (p = 0.06, p = 0.97, and p = 0.67, respectively). Moreover, anxiety (p = 0.05) was borderline significant, while depressive symptoms (p = 0.54) and perceived stress (p = 0.86) were not significant.

Over the five-year follow-up, 77.5% of participants reported the same multimorbidity status (either with or without multimorbidity) at both time points. Incident multimorbidity occurred in 20.3% of those without multimorbidity at baseline, while 30.6% of those with multimorbidity at baseline reported fewer than two conditions at follow-up.

Prevalence of chronic conditions

Among 8280 participants, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes were the most common conditions, with prevalence of 25.3%, 22.8% and 12.7% respectively. All chronic conditions were more prevalent in older adults, except obesity, which was more common in the adults (35–59 years) age group (29.9%). Overall, 51.3% of participants had no chronic condition, 28% had one chronic condition, and 20.7% had multimorbidity. Participants aged 60 years and above had a higher prevalence of multimorbidity (31.1%) compared to younger age groups (19.8% and 3.08%, respectively), as depicted in Supplementary Table 2.

Mean QoL scores by multimorbidity status

Participants show improvement in all QoL domains from baseline to follow-up. However, there was no significant difference in QoL scores among participants with and without multimorbidity at baseline and follow-up, as depicted in Supplementary Table 3.

The change scores (QoL score at follow-up - QoL score at baseline) for each QoL domain were lower in participants with multimorbidity compared to those without multimorbidity. However, the change in mean scores was statistically significant (p-value < 0.05) for both psychological and social relationship domains, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Association between multimorbidity and QoL

The results of linear regression models for the association between multimorbidity and QoL domains are shown in Table 2. At baseline, multimorbidity was significantly associated with better QoL in the social relationships domain (ß: 0.94, SE: 0.39, p-value = 0.02). No significant associations between multimorbidity and other QoL domains (physical, psychological and environmental) were found. In longitudinal analysis, the association between multimorbidity and change in QoL score for the psychological and social domain was significant in the unadjusted analysis (p-value = 0.04). However, after adjusting for covariates and baseline QoL, no significant associations were observed between multimorbidity and change in any QoL domains over the 5-year follow-up.

Supplementary Table 4 shows the model adjusted for covariates only, without adjusting baseline QoL, in which multimorbidity was associated with a decline in social relationships over a 5-year follow-up (ß: -1.10, SE: 0.51, p-value = 0.03), highlighting that the apparent longitudinal association was largely explained by differences in baseline QoL.

We further investigated the modifying role of sociodemographic factors (age, sex, education, and living alone) on the association between multimorbidity and QoL domains. The interaction term of multimorbidity*age was significant for the psychological domain (ß: -0.09, SE: 0.04, p-value = 0.03). The change QoL scores for the psychological domain declines significantly over time in older adults with multimorbidity, as depicted in Fig. 3. This suggests that psychological QoL with multimorbidity gets worse over time with increasing age. However, the interaction terms for multimorbidity*sex, multimorbidity*education and multimorbidity*living alone were not significant for change in QoL scores in all domains (Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, comparing participants with 0, 1, and ≥ 2 chronic conditions revealed no significant differences in change QoL scores across all domains (Supplementary Table 6).

Association between multimorbidity clusters and change in QoL scores

Two multimorbidity clusters identified by the cluster analysis are shown in Fig. 4. The first cluster consists of hypertension, diabetes, obesity and arthritis, and has a prevalence of 18% (n = 1491). The second cluster consists of asthma, stroke, heart and kidney disease, with a prevalence of 0.51% (n = 42). Due to small sample size in this cluster, regression estimates should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, there were 2.21% (n = 183) participants within both clusters. Participants with both multimorbidity clusters were excluded from the regression analysis due to the possibility of interactions between the clusters.

Table 3 shows that cluster 2 was associated with a decline in QoL score for the social relationships domain (ßpattern2: -4.88, SE: 1.98, p = 0.03), compared to no multimorbidity. Moreover, when stratified by age group, in younger adults (18–34 years), cluster 1 was significantly associated with an improvement in QoL score for the psychological domain (ß: 4.54, SE: 2.25, p-value = 0.03). However, in adults (35–59 years), cluster 2 was significantly associated with a decline in social relationships (ß: -6.57, SE: 2.87, p-value = 0.01). No significant association was found between multimorbidity clusters and QoL domains among the older adults (≥ 60 years). The results of the association between multimorbidity clusters and changes in QoL, without adjusting for baseline QoL, are presented in Supplementary Table 7.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the impact of multimorbidity and clusters of chronic conditions on QoL domains in a relatively large, multi-ethnic longitudinal cohort in Malaysia. The prevalence of multimorbidity in the study cohort, across eight self-reported chronic conditions, was 20.7%. Overall, multimorbidity was not significantly associated with a change in QoL over a 5-year period. However, the multimorbidity cluster of cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal conditions was significantly associated with better QoL in the psychological domain among younger adults (18–34 years), whereas the cluster of cardiorespiratory and renal conditions was associated with a decline in social relationships among adults (35–59 years).

In this multiethnic semirural cohort, mean QoL scores improved from baseline to 5-year follow-up across all domains (physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environmental health). Self-care management is often regarded as a theoretical approach to improving QoL in chronic conditions [35]. However, these improvements may also reflect psychological adaptation or response shift over time rather than true improvement in underlying health status. Response shift refers to changes in individuals’ values or perceptions of health and QoL as they adapt to aging or chronic diseases over time [13]. Moreover, improved access to healthcare and increased health awareness over time may also have contributed to the observed improvements across QoL domains [36]. Participants with multimorbidity had significantly lower change in psychological and social relationships QoL scores (follow-up - baseline) than those without multimorbidity.

In our study, multimorbidity was not associated with a change in QoL in any domain over a 5-year follow-up period, which contrasts with the widely reported negative association of multimorbidity with QoL [9, 10]. For example, a systematic review of 74 studies showed a significant decline in the physical and psychological QoL with each added chronic condition [9]. The discrepancy may partly be explained by differences in population characteristics and the prevalence of chronic conditions among different studies. While the most prevalent conditions in previous studies were diabetes, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal diseases, the most common conditions in our study were obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. These conditions may have a smaller impact on functional status and hence QoL [37]. This is likely due to their high prevalence and frequent detection through routine screening in Malaysia, facilitating early management, reduced stigma and a lower disease burden compared with more severe conditions such as COPD, cancer, or renal failure [38]. Previous studies also showed that participants with hypertension and diabetes tend to have comparatively higher QoL scores among other chronic conditions [37, 39].

The association between multimorbidity and QoL did not vary by age, sex, education or living status. However, a significant interaction between multimorbidity and age was observed, with multimorbidity associated with a decline in psychological QoL among older adults over the 5-year follow-up. These results are consistent with findings of previous studies [17, 18]. The modifying role of age on the association between multimorbidity and psychological QoL may reflect higher cumulative disease burden, functional decline, and reduced social support in older adults, which may further amplify the psychological impact of multimorbidity. However, no significant interaction was found with sex, education and living status.

Due to the complex relationship between multimorbidity and QoL, a cluster-based approach might provide more useful insight than a simple count of the number of diseases [40]. Previous studies have used different approaches to explore the multimorbidity patterns, such as factor analysis [41, 42], latent class analysis [43, 44], and network analysis [45]. However, in this study, we used hierarchical clustering, considering the binary nature of the chronic conditions, easy interpretation and visualisation of results using dendrograms [46, 47].

The impact of multimorbidity clusters on QoL varies by age groups [40, 48]. In our study, cluster 1 of cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal conditions was significantly associated with improved psychological QoL among younger adults (18–34 years). This may be explained by more resilience, effective coping strategies and stronger support networks among younger adults, which can mitigate the psychological impact of chronic conditions [49]. Younger adults may also be more likely to adopt lifestyle modifications and health-promoting activities following diagnosis, contributing to better psychological well-being.

Cluster 2 of cardiorespiratory and renal conditions was significantly associated with a decline in QoL related to social relationships among adults. Adults aged 35–59 years are often in a life stage that typically involves active employment, caregiving, and social responsibilities, which can contribute to functional and lifestyle challenges and result in a more pronounced impact on QoL compared to younger or older adults [50]. As noted, the small sample size (n = 42) may have limited the stability and precision of these findings, and they should be interpreted cautiously. Multimorbidity clusters were not associated with QoL domains among the older adults (≥ 60 years). This might be explained by the lower expectations in terms of health with progressing age, known as response shift and better adaptability to lifestyle changes due to certain health conditions [13]. Previous studies have also reported that HRQoL declines more sharply in younger populations with multimorbidity than in older adults [9, 51, 52]. However, despite the increasing burden of multimorbidity in younger and mid-life age groups, most interventions have primarily focused on older adults with multimorbidity [53, 54]. Our findings highlighted the need for age-tailored interventions, particularly addressing the needs of younger adults with multimorbidity.

A key methodological consideration is our adjustment for baseline QoL. Our primary assumption was that baseline QoL functions as a confounder, and we adjusted for baseline QoL to isolate change over follow-up. However, we acknowledge an alternative interpretation: baseline QoL may lie on the causal pathway between multimorbidity and subsequent QoL. Because multimorbidity and QoL were measured concurrently, we cannot determine whether baseline QoL represents a confounder or lies on the causal pathway. We therefore present both adjusted and unadjusted models (Supplementary Table 4) as a sensitivity analysis. The substantial attenuation after adjustment suggests baseline differences largely explain the apparent association. Readers should interpret findings with this ambiguity in mind.

Strengths and limitations

The study, conducted in large multiethnic population aged 18 years and above has a number of strengths. Firstly, the analysis of two waves of data allowed us to determine whether multimorbidity is associated with a change in the QoL over five years. Secondly, we covered all the QoL domains in our study. Multimorbidity can potentially impact all aspects of QoL but most previous studies only focused on the physical and psychological domains [9], with the impact of multimorbidity on the social and environmental domain unclear [55, 56]. Additionally, we evaluated the impact of multimorbidity on QoL domains based on both the number and clusters of chronic conditions.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be considered. Chronic conditions were self-reported based on the previous diagnosis by a doctor, and recall bias may have led to an over- or underestimation of prevalence. We also did not consider the severity or duration of the chronic conditions. Although the status of multimorbidity as defined in our study is unlikely to be changed, the severity and duration of chronic conditions may have affected the QoL scores. Additionally, our multimorbidity definition did not include mental health conditions due to data availability. While we adjusted for the baseline depressive symptoms in all regression models, excluding mental health conditions from the multimorbidity definition may have influenced associations with QoL, particularly for the psychological well-being domain.

Participants excluded were differed from those retained in the study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, only 13.8% of participants met the recommended physical activity threshold. This limited variability may have reduced our ability to fully account for confounding by physical activity in the relationship between multimorbidity and QoL.

We acknowledge that multimorbidity is dynamic, and our approach of modelling it only at baseline does not capture changes that may have occurred during follow-up. This could potentially bias our findings toward the null. However, when we examined the stability of multimorbidity, we found that 77.5% participants remained in the same multimorbidity category throughout follow-up, suggesting that baseline multimorbidity status is a reasonably reliable indicator of exposure for most participants. Nevertheless, future studies with clinically verified diagnoses and repeated measures of multimorbidity are needed to capture its dynamic nature and examine how transitions between states affect QoL trajectories over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, multimorbidity was not significantly associated with the overall change in QoL over 5 years. However, a key contribution of this study is the identification of distinct multimorbidity clusters with differential associations with QoL. These findings highlight the age-specific associations, with younger adults with cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal conditions were associated with better psychological QoL, whereas adults with cardiorespiratory and renal conditions experienced greater declines in social relationships-related QoL. These findings underscore the need to develop age-tailored interventions. Such strategies have relevant clinical implications for the risk assessment and management of these conditions, particularly among younger and middle-aged adults. Moreover, a simple count fails to fully capture whether a certain group of conditions are resulting in a decline in QoL and a cluster-based approach could provide a more useful insight. Therefore, instead of focusing on single health conditions, a multidisciplinary targeted approach would be more effective to improve the QoL in multimorbidity patients.
