Inflammasomes in glioblastoma
Authors: Vaidya Govindarajan, Quinn Jackson, Christian K Ramsoomair, Manuela Aramburu, Juan Pablo de Rivero Vaccari, Robert W Keane, Ashish H Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignancy of the brain with a poor prognosis. Despite advances in therapeutics, the gold standard of treatment remains maximal safe resection followed by temozolomide and radiation therapy, with the inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity of GBM making effective management challenging. As a result, the search for mechanisms underlying this heterogeneity has been paramount. The immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory microenvironment of GBM has been of particular interest in recent years. Inflammasomes are protein complexes responsible for initiation of pro-inflammatory cascades and a specialized form of regulated cell death known as pyroptosis in response to noxious cellular stimuli. Inflammasomes play a critical role in several disease processes, including multiple sclerosis and traumatic brain injury. However, their role in GBM pathogenesis and regulation in the tumor microenvironment remains poorly understood. Here, we describe the current status of investigations into inflammasomes, their role in GBM pathogenesis, and their potential as therapeutic targets.Keywords: Apoptosis, Pyroptosis, Glioblastoma, Inflammasome

Background

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary brain malignancy with a dismal prognosis [1]. The current front-line treatment for GBM is tumor resection with a combination of radiotherapy and adjuvant chemotherapy with temozolomide [1], with only a modest increase in overall survival, the median overall survival of primary GBM remains 14 months, with recurrence considered inevitable within 9 months postoperatively [1, 2]. Molecular heterogeneity is a key feature in GBM, conferring significant survival differences amongst GBM patients, and demonstrate significant differences in rates of chemotherapy and radiotherapy responses.

Inflammasomes are key regulators of the inflammatory response in multiple disease processes, including multiple sclerosis and traumatic brain injury [3, 4]; however, their role in GBM pathogenesis remains poorly understood.

Inflammasomes drive pyroptosis, a key modulator of the host inflammatory response. Unlike, apoptosis, which typically does not involve overt violation of the plasma membrane, pyroptosis has been long-considered a form of regulated necrosis, as it is a form of lytic cell death executed by gasdermins as opposed to caspases and serves a direct immunomodulatory role by driving inflammatory cascades [5]. Inflamamsome-driven pyroptosis has been well-described as particularly effective in innate antimicrobial activity, as specific pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are key initiators of inflammasome activity, and organisms lacking inflammasomes are particularly susceptible to bacterial infections [6]. Recent scholarship has focused on the role of inflammasomes in cancer. However, the precise role of inflammasomes remains unclear, as they can both act as oncogenic drivers and curtail disease progression [7, 8]. Overall, the role of inflammasomes in cancer is highly dependent on signaling pathways unique to cancer type and cellular origin, thus understanding the role these complexes play in the context of the GBM microenvironment would be of tremendous benefit [9].

Understanding the tumor microenvironment (TME) of GBM remains a point of emphasis in modern GBM research, particularly with respect to host-tumor immune response [10, 11]. The brain parenchyma is a largely immunoprivileged environment due to the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), with immunocyte infiltration largely restricted to pro-inflammatory states that compromise BBB integrity. However, despite these innate mechanisms, GBM is characterized by poor immunocyte infiltration [11]. Intercellular communication between the myeloid cell compartment and GBM cells appears to be a critical feature of maintaining an immunosuppressive TME [12].

However, despite the emphasis on myeloid lineage cells and lymphocytes, key inflammatory and immunological mechanisms, such as the inflammasome complex have largely been unexplored in GBM. For example, IL-1β is a key product of inflammasome activation and is required for initiating systemic inflammatory responses and differentiation of memory CD4 + T-cells [13]. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that inflammasomes play a critical role in myeloid cell activation in other disease states, such as in response to viral infection [14]. However, there is a lack of similar research detailing the role of inflammasomes in GBM-associated myeloid cells (tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid derived stem cells (MDSCs), etc.) and the effect of inflammasome inhibition on these cell types.

Moreover, the differential effect of inflammasomes across cell types and disease states further emphasizes the need for proper understanding of their role in GBM pathogenesis and progression. For example, in a systematic cell-type analysis in epithelial skin carcinomas, Drexler et al. identified a proinflammatory and oncogenic role in associated myeloid cells, but acted as a tumor suppressor in myeloid cells [15]. Similarly, other studies have identified MDSC mobilization and subsequent gastric cancer progression in response to inflammasome mediated IL-1β overexpression [16]. Therefore, there are likely similar mechanisms employed by both GBM cells and associated myeloid cell compartments, and warrant investigation. Furthermore, understanding these mechanisms can potentially have therapeutic applications, likely through synergistic or modulatory effects with existing immunotherapeutic strategies and could be applied to GBM [17].

Inflammasomes: description and mechanism

The inflammasome is a multiprotein complex present in the cytoplasm of immune cells like macrophages and dendritic cells that help play a key role in innate immune responses, mainly by releasing the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 [18]. There are two pathways associated with inflammasome activation: a canonical activation pathway, activated by intracellular components like calcium (Ca2+), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and potassium (K+) [18], and a non-canonical activation pathway that is activated by gram-negative bacteria or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [19]. Inflammasomes are composed of three components: an inflammasome sensory protein such as a NOD-like receptor (NLR), the adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase-recruitment domain (ASC), and caspase-1 [20]. The toll-like receptor (TLR) and NLR proteins are families of pattern recognition receptors (PRR) necessary for the recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), markers of pathogens and cell damage, respectively [21, 22]. PRR receptor binding activates a priming of the inflammasome (often referred to as signal 1), leading to activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) [21]. The adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) protein is particularly critical, as it serves to link the sensory protein (NLR) to the cysteine aspartase caspase-1 [22, 23].

In the canonical pathway, inflammasome activation results in production of mature IL-1β and IL-18 in response to infection or tissue damage or a variety of metabolic disturbances, including lysosomal disruption [24], mitochondrial dysfunction [25, 24], release of mitochondrial DNA (mt DNA), reactive oxygen species (ROS) [24], and high intracellular calcium (Ca2+) [26]. PAMP or DAMP activate NLRs within the inflammasome to lead to the oligomerization of NLR proteins via homotypic pyrin (PYD)-PYD interactions through or caspase-recruitment domain (CARD) interactions [26]. Based on these interactions, inflammasome sensors assemble into complexes with or without the adaptor protein ASC. However, efficient cytokine processing requires ASC recruitment and assembly into the inflammasome [27].

When clustered procaspases undergo autoactivation to mature caspase-1, the inflammasome is considered active and can convert pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 to their mature forms [26]. Cleavage of Gasdermin-D (GSDMD) by either caspase-1 or caspase-11 (in rodents), caspases-4/5 (in humans) is critical for the induction of inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis [26]. GSDMD is composed of carboxy (C) and amino (N) terminus fragments, with the C-terminus autoinhibiting the pyroptotic and inflammatory actions of the N-terminus fragment, with the latter being recruited to the cell membrane to form a pore (GSDMD-N) [26]. Caspase-11 cleaves GSDMD in response to intracellular LPS from Gram-negative bacteria. Upon cleavage by caspase-11, the amino fragment of GSDMD-N initiates pyroptosis and is also associated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation, leading to upregulation of IL-1β [28]. In addition, GSDMD knockout mice are protected from lethal doses of LPS and release lower levels of IL-1β [28]. Similarly, Ninjurin-1monomoers assemble and form a pore on insertion into the cell-membrane, and is similarly thought to drive cell lysis and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [29].

The non-canonical pathway is activated by rodent caspase-11 and its human orthologs, caspase-4 and caspase-5. Interestingly, caspase-11 is less efficient in IL-1β and IL-18 processing than it is in GSDMD cleavage, and the caspases of the non-canonical pathway [30]. This dual action also requires strict control to prevent pyroptosis. One such control mechanism involves the transient receptor protein channel 1 (TRPC1), a non-selective ion channel, which is itself a substrate for caspase-11 [30]. Potassium efflux through pannexin-1 channels is necessary for NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis, and TRPC1 knockouts have demonstrated stronger inflammatory responses [30]. Inflammasomes in central nervous system disease

Inflammatory responses are a critical aspect of CNS physiology, acting as a protective mechanism against infection, toxins, and other stressors [31]. Microglia are the key cellular regulators of neuroinflammation. In response to noxious stimuli and markers of active cytotoxic processes (glutamate, nucleotides from damaged cells, etc.), microglia release pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species to stimulate a spreading inflammatory cascade, leading to blood brain barrier disruption and ultimately, leukocyte infiltration [31]. However, aberrant neuroinflammation has been linked to overactivation of microglia in response to immunogenic molecules, which in turn has been implicated in disruption of neuronal function (Fig. 2). For example, in 2017, Liddelow and colleagues demonstrated that overactivation of microglia resulted in induction of a neurotoxic phenotype in neighboring astrocytes, suggesting an interplay between multiple cell types within the physiologic microenvironment in the nervous system and how dysregulation of these processes contributes to pathogenesis [32].

Microglia activate in response, leading to a spreading inflammatory cascade, furthering cell death and ultimate blood-brain barrier disruption

Neuroinflammation plays a key role in the pathogenesis of multiple central nervous system disease states. As such, inflammasomes are key regulators of CNS disease processes. In normal physiology, inflammasomes play a critical role in promoting tissue repair and clearance of dead or damaged cells [33, 34]. Recently, Yoon and colleagues demonstrated that microglial NLRP3 inflammasome activation is a key driver of blood brain barrier (BBB) disruption [35]. Furthermore, they noted that loss of microglial GSDMD resulted in attenuation of BBB disruption. Interestingly, this process was independent of IL-1β processing, leading the authors to suggest the existence of a unique microglial-NLRP3 axis that drives neuroinflammation, relying on the recruitment of local neutrophils and release of matrix metalloproteinases to drive BBB disruption and further activation of the neuroinflammatory cascade [35]. However, this could also indicate that the effects of the inflammasome on the BBB are related to pyroptosis and not to the processing of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β since these could be independent events mediated by the inflammasome.

The distribution of the various inflammasome complexes appears to be cell-type dependent in the central nervous system, with NLRP3 being largely found in microglia and astrocytes, though this may vary based on disease states [36]. For example, inflammasome activation occurs primarily in microglia and astrocytes and may contribute to reactive gliosis in response to neurological insult [33]. However, it is also well established that prolonged inflammasome activation in these same cell types can induce cell death in healthy neurons and aberrant glial activation, which in turn is thought to drive neurodegeneration [37, 38]. Recent scholarship has also identified key inflammatory and immunological roles for neurons as well, given their expression of NOD-like receptors (NLRs) and Toll-like receptors (TLRs), allowing them to detect markers of cellular stress and alter the microenvironment accordingly via cytokine release [39]. Similarly, neurons appears to express inflammasome proteins as well, namely NLRP1, NLRP3, AIM2, and NLRC4, and are activated by similar DAMPs and PAMPs as described in other cell types [40]. Interestingly, inflammasome complexes have also been identified in CSF-derived exosomes in patients with traumatic brain or spinal cord injuries [40]. Given that inflammasomes have been identified in macrophage-derived exosomes as well, there may potentially be a secretory mechanism in both CNS myeloid cells and neurons with respect to inflammasome release [41]. Understanding the distribution of inflammasome activity in complex cellular compartments has been investigated in other disease processes by leveraging spatially resolved and multiomic strategies. Dasdemir et al. recently employed integrative spatial transcriptomics to identify inflammatory niches in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and demonstrated increased inflammasome expression in regions of high leukemic activity and were associated with tumor progression [42]. Similar strategies can similarly be applied to GBM, which has clear spatial and transcriptomic distinctions between cell compartments and heterogeneous molecular subtypes [43–45].

Recently, NLRP3 has been demonstrated to play a neuroprotective role in the early phases of neuroinflammation. Namely, its activation in turn promotes microglial activity, which in turn promotes entry of leukocytes as mentioned previously. However, prolonged activation of NLRP3 has been implicated as a possible mechanism for autoimmune and other aberrant inflammatory processes seen in CNS disease states [46]. While much of the existing literature describes the role of inflammasomes in autoimmune and neurodegenerative conditions, it nevertheless highlights the central role inflammasomes have within CNS disease processes and suggest their potential in the pathogenesis of other hitherto unexplored disease processes [47, 48]. An overview of inflammasomes in CNS disease states is discussed below.

Inflammasomes in Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Inflammasomes have been well-described in several autoimmune conditions, and with respect to CNS disease, their mechanism with respect to MS pathogenesis and progression has been extensively studied [4]. Both ASC and NLRP3 have been identified in Th17 cells in MS, and further, Th17 cells have been shown to promote neuroinflammation through the release of IL-1β [49, 50]. Th17-driven IL-1β release in particular may actively disrupt Treg mediated suppression of autoimmunity and inflammasome activation, which may be a key aspect of MS pathogenesis [51, 52]. Indeed, excess exposure of Treg cells to IL-1β has been associated with suppressed autoimmunity [53].

Inflammasomes in CNS trauma

Perhaps the most common source of primary neuroinflammation, inflammasomes have been extensively researched in neurotrauma. Neurotrauma follows a biphasic pattern, with an initial inflammatory phase occurring immediately after injury, and a secondary inflammatory phase that occurs chronically [54]. However, pyroptosis-driven neuroinflammation largely occurs in the secondary phase, and has been implicated in neurodegeneration, plasticity, and synaptic reorganization that are common sequelae to neurotrauma [55, 56]. Inflammasomes play a key role in modulating neuroinflammation secondary to trauma. The release of DAMPs from necrotic cells (primarily reactive oxygen species) largely in the form of extruded potassium, stimulate pannexin-1 channels, which in turn activate the NLRP1 inflammasome [57]. NLRP1 in turn induces caspase 1 and caspase 3 activation through cleavage of X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), leading to IL-1β release (via caspase 1) and initiation of apoptosis (via caspase 3) [58].

Given NLRP3 can be activated by reactive oxygen species (ROS), its role in TBI has been heavily investigated, as ROS are key mediators of secondary injury in TBI [59]. NADPH oxidases (NOX) have been implicated as key drivers of NLRP3 activation in TBI secondary to their role in producing ROS following injury [60, 61]. Particularly, NOX2 may play a role in chronic injury following initial insult, as prior studies have demonstrated NOX2 activity even after a year following initial injury, suggesting that NLRP3 activation may similarly continue to occur well after an injury has taken place [62, 63]. While there is a lack of commensurate long-term data tracking NLRP3 expression and activity post-TBI, multiple rodent models have demonstrated prolonged NLRP3 activity after 48 h after initial insult [64, 65]. Similarly, there have been small-scale studies that have demonstrated persistently elevated NLRP3 expression in the CSF of adult and pediatric TBI patients up to several days post-injury [66–68].

Inflammasomes in neurodegenerative disease

Neurodegeneration is another subset of CNS pathology where inflammasomes have been heavily investigated. While neuron dysfunction in the setting of these diseases is often attributed solely to the accumulation of malformed proteins, there is growing scholarship suggesting neuroimmune dysfunction plays a key role in driving pathogenesis [69–71]. For example, one landmark study identified amyloid beta as a DAMP unique to Alzheimer disease, which induced NLRP3 assembly in microglia [72]. Correspondingly, postmortem expression analysis of Alzheimer Disease brains revealed overexpression of NLRP3 in both hippocampal and cortical samples [73]. Correspondingly, Dempsey et al. trialed inflammasome inhibition (using NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950) and demonstrated mitigated amyloid beta induced caspase-1 activation, in addition to reduced overall amyloid beta accumulation, again asserting the role of a neuroinflammatory milieu mediated by NLRP3 [74]. There have been several early studies documenting the effects of NLRP3 inhibition in murine models of Alzheimer Disease. Auger et al. recently demonstrated that post-symptom onset NLRP3 inhibition was associated with cognitive rescue in mice along with reduced neuronal loss [75].

In Parkinson Disease (PD), alpha-synuclein plays an analogous role to amyloid beta, acting as a DAMP to promote NLRP3 activation in microglia in a murine model [76]. Interestingly, dopamine supplementation appears to inhibit NLRP3 activation, suggesting that loss of dopaminergic neurons promotes NLRP3 activation, which is further amplified by the accumulation of alpha-synuclein, inducing a neuroinflammatory cascade that serves to promote neurodegeneration [77, 78].

GBM microenvironment

Understanding the immunosuppressive milieu of the GBM microenvironment remains one of the most actively researched aspects of GBM biology. Loosely, immunosuppression in GBM can be categorized as tripartite, divided amongst features contributing to reduced tumor immunogenicity, active immunosuppression, and violation of physical barriers [79]. In healthy brain tissue, the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) restricts immunocyte infiltration and mitigates unnecessary inflammatory responses, while the immune compartment is largely composed of resident microglia and border-associated macrophages (BAMs) [80]. In GBM, BBB disruption leads to infiltration of circulating T, B, and natural killer cells in addition to monocytes. However, the immune landscape of GBM is largely driven by several predominant cell types within the myeloid compartment of GBM as will be discussed below.

Tumor-associated Macrophages (TAMs)

The myeloid cell compartment dominates the immune landscape of GBM, particularly with respect to tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) that arise from peripheral microglia [81]. These TAMs typically are associated with tumor regions characterized by high cellular burden and invasion; in contrast, monocyte-derived TAMs are typically seen in necrotic portions of GBM, which are typically associated with immunosuppressive microenvironments [82, 83].

The myeloid cell compartment has been of particular interest in recent years, particularly with respect to intercellular communication between GBM cells and myeloid-lineage cells. Most recent data suggests that myeloid-lineage cells preferentially differentiate into immunosuppressive phenotypes, primarily tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [84]. TAMs specifically have been implicated in GBM pathogenesis and progression. TAMs exhibit two distinct phenotypes, pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory, with the anti-inflammatory phenotype being associated with tumorigenesis and disease progression via cytokine release, namely TGF-β1 [85]. Native microglia also play a significant role in maintaining the GBM microenvironment, and similarly to TAMs, native microglia differentiate into pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes in response to differing stimuli, nearly identical to those of TAMs [86].

TAMs overall appear to be inextricably linked to GBM pathogenesis and progression. Release of VEGFA by TAMs in conjunction with GBM cells promotes angiogenesis while simultaneously weakening the blood brain barrier, which appears to differentially occur in hypoxic regions of the GBM microenvironment [87]. Paradoxically, despite blood brain barrier violation leading to increased immunocyte infiltration, TAMs mitigate this effect via TGFβ-1 and TGFβ-2 secretion, which in turn downregulates vascular cell adhesion molecules 1 and 2 (VCAM1 and VCAM2) [88]. The few T-cells that manage to successfully infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME) face several obstacles mediated by TAMs. CCL2 derived from TAMs in conjunction with GBM cells drives differentiation of CD4 T-cells into Treg cells [89].

Furthermore, Treg cells further limit T-cell activation through cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4) expression, which competes with CD28 binding to pro-inflammatory TAMs, thus inducing anergy [90]. This mechanism in particular is effective in maintaining an immunosuppressive TME, as the anergic response to antigen presentation in targeted T-cells induces exhaustion through repetitive presentations [91]. Immunosuppressive TAMs, Treg cells, MDSCs, and GBM cells further enhance this effect via IL-10 secretion, as has been previously shown in CD8 T-cells in GBM [92]. TAMs have long been demonstrated to induce apoptosis in infiltrating T-cells via direct Fas-Fas ligand interactions [93].

However, recent publications have demonstrated varied immunomodulatory functions of TAMs in GBM, and suggest that there may be further nuance in their function than previously demonstrated. Miller et al. recently performed a detailed multiomic assessment of the myeloid cell compartment in GBM organoid explants and identified four unique immunomodulatory programs: microglial pro-inflammatory, scavenger immunosuppressive, systemic inflammatory, and complement immunosuppressive [94]. Interestingly, this work also suggests that functional differentiation of TAMs into these programs is dependent on microenvironmental factors (namely hypoxia, TGFβ, interleukin-1β) as opposed to TAM origin, cellular lineage, or tumor mutational state as has been previously suggested [94]. This is further supported by more recent spatial transcriptomic and trajectory analyses that demonstrate conversion of native CNS microglia and monocytes into TAMs bearing immunosuppressive transcriptional programs [95–97]. Inflammasomes may be implicated in this conversion process. Hofbauer et al. demonstrated that NLRP3 activation in myeloma TAMs in response to β2-microglobulin, and consequently drove multiple myeloma progression [98]. Other studies have demonstrated that high NLRP3 expression in TAMs found in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas are similarly associated with worse overall survival [99]. Therefore, there may similarly be an oncogenic effect in GBM TAMs in response to increased inflammasome expression and activation, and further, this effect may be localized to TAMs with immunosuppressive transcriptional programs.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells are a native part of the healthy brain microenvironment, typically differentiating into macrophages and other myeloid lineage cells [84]. However, in GBM, MDSCs fail to differentiate, remaining in their precursor state and play a key role in maintaining and developing the immunosuppressive microenvironment in GBM [100]. Notably, MDSC lineage cells fail to express MHC II, which in turn prevents activation of helper CD4 + T-cells, limiting the adaptive immune response to GBM [101, 102]. Furthermore, MDSCs secrete large quantities of nitric oxide synthase (NOS) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) which serve to suppress T-cell activity [103, 104]. Interestingly, ROS are key DAMPs that drive inflammasome activation, however the link between MDSCs and inflammasomes in GBM has not been fully investigated. However, there is some preliminary murine data in studies involving lung cancer and melanoma demonstrating reduced MDSC accumulation and tumor regression in response to NLRP3 knockout [105]. More recent scholarship suggests that an ATP-NLRP3 axis within MDSC cells themselves may drive their immunosuppressive effect [106]. Therefore, there may exist a mechanism within GBM-associated MDSCs such that inflammasomes activation is triggered, leading to MDSC activation and accumulation, which in turn leads to T-cell suppression.

As stated in the aforementioned discussion regarding TAMs, MDSCs similarly employ a cytokine-mediated mechanism of T-cell suppression and Treg recruitment via TL-10 and TGFβ as well as via direct inhibition via PD-1 [107, 108]. MDSCs have also been implicated in fostering a systemic immunosuppressive effect, as they accumulate in the peripheral circulation, fostering T-cell sequestration to the bone marrow, which appears to be sustained even after therapy in recurrent GBM patients [109]. Additionally, MDSCs are unique in that they have direct effects on the TME that amplify both their own and overall immunosuppressive mechanisms in GBM. Pyroptosis in native and GBM states

The complexity of the immunosuppressive GBM TME demonstrates the significant hurdles to developing novel treatment strategems and immunotherapies. Thus, understanding native apoptotic mechanisms in both healthy CNS and GBM states is necessary. Primarily, there appear to be multiple differences in apoptotic and necrotic mechanisms between GBM cells and healthy CNS cells, particularly with respect to Inhibitor of Apoptosis proteins [111]. Neuronal apoptosis inhibitory protein (NAIP) is a key regulator of apoptosis in healthy neurons by directly binding to caspases 3, 7, and 9 (CASP3, CASP7, CASP9) [112]. Correspondingly, NAIP has been found to be overexpressed in GBM cells relative to normal human astrocytes [113]. Clinically, recent studies have demonstrated worse overall survival (OS) in patients with NAIP overexpression, and there may be some evidence to suggest increased resistance to radiation therapy [114, 115].

XIAP (BIRC4), is described as the most potent member of the Inhibitor of Apoptosis family, and is overexpressed in GBM tissue relative to normal human astrocytes [116]. AKT, well-established as promoting GBM progression through the mTOR pathway, similarly aids in suppressing apoptosis by phosphorylating XIAP, increasing stability and further enhancing its anti-apoptotic effect [117].

There are comparatively few studies that have demonstrated key differences in pyroptotic mechanisms between GBM cells and healthy CNS tissue. However, there is some preliminary scholarship demonstrating key differences in gene expression along the pyroptotic pathway. Lin and colleagues identified a 29-member geneset of pyroptosis associated genes (PAG), of which 18 were overexpressed in GBM cell relative to healthy CNS cells [118].

Inflammasomes in GBM

While inflammasomes themselves have not been fully investigated in GBM, there is some preliminary data that suggests a role for pyroptotic mechanisms in affecting GBM outcomes. Zheng et al. demonstrated unique gene signatures associated with pyroptotic mechanisms that conferred a survival detriment in GBM patients [119]. Other studies have found increased expression of pyroptosis-associated genes in response to inhibition of the STAT3 pathway [120]. Although NLRP3 has been the most commonly investigated inflammasome in GBM, the regulation of the NLRP3 complex in GBM remains poorly explored. Initial studies aimed to investigate the expression of inflammasome complex end-products, such as IL-1, in patient-derived GBM cultures [121]. While these studies demonstrated overexpression of IL-1 in GBM cell lines relative to astrocyte cultures, it is unclear whether this cytokine expression was a result of inflammasome activation, or if inflammasome knockdown/inhibition attenuated expression.

Balcom et al. similarly aimed to compare the expression of inflammasome complex proteins in GBM relative to controls [122]. Despite demonstrating overexpression of inflammasome complex proteins in GBM, they also noted overexpression in the tumor periphery relative to the core, suggesting a heterogeneity in inflammasome expression within the GBM microenvironment. Similarly, Lim et al. demonstrated that upregulation of NLRC4 in GBM patients was associated with worse prognosis [114] (Fig. 4). However, once again, expression of NLRC4, as was previously seen in NLRP3, was localized outside of GBM cells and enriched in the microenvironment. Inflammasomes have similarly been identified in normal human astrocytes, specifically NRLP2 [123]. Minkiewicz et al. previously reported the presence of NLRP2 in human astrocytes and their subsequent activation in response to neurotrauma, suggesting that astrocyte-based inflammasomes play a role in neuroinflammation [123].

In a murine model of GBM, tumor treating fields (TTFs) have been observed to correlate with increased AIM2 and STING activation, which in turn demonstrated downstream activation of T-cells. NLRP12 is of particular interest in GBM given its documented role in reducing host immune response activation, while its knockdown is associated with reduced GBM proliferation

Other inflammasomes have also been investigated for their role in adaptive immunity as well. Chen et al. recently reported AIM2 and STING activation in response to tumor treating fields (TTF) [124]. The authors reported induction of downstream T-cell activation as a direct effect of AIM2/STING activation in response to TTFs in a murine model [124].

NLRP12 has also been investigated for its role in GBM pathogenesis. Similar to other inflammasomes, NLRP12 is largely restricted to myeloid lineage cells [125]. However, unlike its counterparts, NLRP12 demonstrates variable activation in response to stimuli. Early investigations into NLRP12 signaling suggested that it may inhibit T-cell activation via an anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 dependent mechanism [125]. NRLP12 activation has also been associated with reduction in IL-2 and CD-25 expression, which in turn reduces expression and activation of NF-κB, while also reducing the host inflammatory response [126]. However, NLRP12 in bone derived macrophages induce a pro-inflammatory cascade in response to DAMPs [126]. Specifically, NLRP12 activation results in TLR2 and TLR4 association with Myd88/IRF1, ultimately leading to ROS production and apoptosis [127]. Specifically with respect to GBM, preliminary in vitro studies have demonstrated that knockdown of NLRP12 resulted in reduced proliferation and cell migration in GBM cell lines [128] (Fig. 4).

Role of inflammasomes in adaptive GBM immunity

Adaptive immunity plays a key role in the GBM microenvironment, and similarly, while inflammasomes have classically been considered a component of innate immunity, they have also been shown to assemble in B and T cells, suggesting that they contribute to adaptive immune responses as well [129]. Stoecklein et al. showed that irradiated B and T lymphocytes presented inflammasome activation independent of NLRP3, suggesting a DNA-damage mediated pathway via the AIM2 inflammasome [130].

Adaptive immunity has been heavily investigated in GBM pathogenesis and for its potential therapeutic targets. Studies have focused on the role of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), which are recognized by circulating surveillance T-cells [131]. However, the tumor microenvironment of GBM is inherently immunosuppressive, largely through production of immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, etc.), and co-mediated by tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), leading to inactivation and propagation of pro-apoptotic signaling within T-cells [132]. Importantly, the direct action of circulating immunosuppressive interleukins within the tumor microenvironment (TME) has been implicated in converting TAMs into an immunosuppressive phenotype [133] (Fig. 5).

Brief overview of adaptive immunity in GBM. GBM cells release both anti-inflammatory cytokines and tumor associated antigens (TAAs). Circulating tumor associated T-cells respond to local TAAs, leading to clonal proliferation, skewing of TAMs to an M1 phenotype, induction of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Simultaneously, release of anti-inflammatory cytokines skews TAMs towards an M2 phenotype, leading to GBM immune escape and tumor progression

While there is little explicit research demonstrating the role of inflammasomes in the GBM microenvironment, the key mechanisms behind TAM physiology in GBM has remarkable overlap with key features of inflammasome activation. There are two key TAM phenotypes, proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory subpopulations [134]. Proinflammatory phenotype TAMs differentiate from their immature precursors in response to DAMPs and PAMPs, suggesting that proinflammatory differentiation may be an inflammasome-dependent process, and its phenotype is characterized by upregulation of pro-inflammatory and tumor-suppressive pathways [134, 135]. Conversely, differentiation into the anti-inflammatory subtype is triggered by release of anti-inflammatory cytokines, namely IL-6 and IL-10, and it serves to maintain an immunosuppressive phenotype in the GBM microenvironment [136, 137].

Recent research suggests that polarization of TAMs to an anti-inflammatory phenotype is potentially induced by local GBM cells, potentially via an exosome-mediated mechanism. Undifferentiated TAMs are first recruited to the TME by chemokines released from GBM cells (CX3CL1, CCL2, MIC1, and CSF1) [138, 139]. The migration of TAMs is further amplified by hypoxic conditions similarly driven by release of HIF by GBM cells [140].

Once recruited to the microenvironment, GBM cells release a host of factors that contribute to polarization of TAMs to the anti-inflammatory phenotype and suppress pro-inflammatory phenotype TAMs. For example, Il-4, IL-10, and IL-13 induce anti-inflammatory polarization by recruiting regulatory T-cells and suppressing cytotoxic T-cells [141–143]. Conversely, polarization towards the proinflammatory phenotype is governed by the release of GM-CSF, interferon gamma, and lipopolysaccharides, a mechanism very similar to inflammasome activation [144, 145].

Role of inflammasomes as therapeutic targets

Given the currently poorly understood role of inflammasomes in GBM, there is a similar paucity of research into their role as therapeutic targets in GBM management. Resveratrol has been previously explored as a possible novel therapeutic [146], and in GBM-derived cell lines, exposure to resveratrol did demonstrate reduced proliferation through inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway [146]. However, while there was associated reduction in NLRP3 expression, there was no direct evidence to suggest that these findings were a direct result of NLRP3 inhibition.

Further directions in the development of novel therapeutics targeted against inflammasomes may leverage downstream effectors of the inflammasome. For example, interferon (IFN)-β has been explored as a therapeutic against GBM and has been shown to attenuate the stem-cell compartment of GBM [147]. IFN-β has been shown to downregulate the expression of NLRP3 in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS). Thus, it is possible that similar effects may be seen with respect to GBM.

If more robust in vitro and in vivo analyses are indeed able to establish that NLRP3 and other inflammasomes play roles in GBM pathogenesis, then existing trials into novel inflammasome inhibitors may be adapted to use in GBM. There are currently several small molecule inhibitors of NLRP3 currently under investigation in animal models [148]. IC100 is a well-documented putative inflammasome inhibitor [3, 149–151]. It is a humanized antibody against the ASC adaptor protein of the inflammasome. Since ASC is present in multiple inflammasomes, targeting ASC has the potential to have a much broader effect than therapeutics targeting individual NLRs such as NLRP1, NLRP3, AIM2 or NLRC4 [150].

Few studies have specifically focused on manipulating inflammasome-related pathways in GBM. Yu et al. recently demonstrated that utilizing a small-molecule inhibitor of STAT3 led to increased expression of pyroptotic genes, including NLRP3 and GSDMD [120]. Furthermore, given that M1 TAMs appear to be activated by inflammasome-related pathways, targeting TAM polarization may be an indirect way to therapeutically modify the inflammasome pathway in GBM. PD-L1 blockade has been well-investigated in recent years as a possible immunotherapy in GBM; however, its true mechanism remains poorly understood [152, 153]. Gordon et al. has recently demonstrated that PD-1 positive TAMs demonstrate M2 polarization and inferior phagocytic activity, but it can be reversed by PD-L1 blockade [154]. Similarly, PD-L1 knockdown has demonstrated polarization of GBM TAMs to an M1 phenotype, reducing tumor invasion and migration [155, 156].

As a key downstream effector of inflammasome activation and potent modulator of inflammation in its own right, the IL-1 family has also been investigated as therapeutic target in other solid organ malignancies. For example, canakinumab, an IL-1β inhibitor, has been shown to improve overall survival in lung cancer [157].

The recent surge in immunotherapeutics in other solid organ malignancies, which inherently leverage native and dysfunctional inflammatory processes in various disease states, offers multiple avenues to investigate possible synergies with pyroptotic mechanisms. PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has been heavily investigated as novel immunotherapy in multiple solid organ malignancies, largely due to its ability to mediate T-cell exhaustion [158]. Pembrolizumab, for example, was the index case of effective anti-PD-L1 antibodies in treatment of melanoma [159]. Recently, the interaction between inflammasomes and PD-1/PD-L1 has been investigated to optimize immunotherapeutic strategies. For example, Ding et al. demonstrated reduced PD-L1 expression in response to NLRP3 inhibition in an in vitro model of hepatocellular carcinoma [160]. In melanoma, MCC950, a known NLRP3 inhibitor, has been shown to synergize with anti-PD1 immunotherapy to mitigate MDSC infiltration in pancreatic cancer [148]. NLRP3 inhibition has also been investigated in breast cancer, where both inhibition with MCC950 and siRNA-mediated silencing have been shown to attenuate the chronic inflammatory response associated with paclitaxel administration, thereby enhancing the overall effectiveness of chemotherapy [161]. Given the synergistic effect between NLRP3 inhibition and anti-PD1 therapies, this presents a unique opportunity to translate these findings to GBM, particularly given the increasing interest in anti-PD1 therapies in GBM [162–164].

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy is also of relevance given its predominant role in most modern immunotherapy trials. While there is limited literature investigating the interaction between inflammasomes and CAR T-cell therapy, recent data suggest that CAR T-cells may depend on NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis. Huang et al. recently showed that NLRP3 inhibition in a murine model of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) resulted in reduced sensitivity to CAR T-cell therapy [165]. Given the increasing research interest in CAR T-cell therapy for GBM, understanding the role of inflammasomes could potentially aid in translating findings from other malignancies into GBM management [162].

Discussion

The advent of high fidelity multiomics, particularly with respect to spatial transcriptomics, has sparked a revolution in GBM research, sparking a bevy of high-quality studies into the GBM microenvironment [43–45]. Multiple recent publications identify the myeloid cell compartment and other features that promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [94, 166]. However, the role of inflammasomes in GBM remains poorly understood. Particularly, the relative expression of inflammasome complex genes in GBM relative to healthy tissue remains inconclusive based on our review of the literature, and further, there appear to be differences in activity and expression even across the various cells in the healthy CNS [39, 40]. Thus, primarily determining the expression of these genes in GBM relative to healthy brain tissue, and further, determining if there is differential expression of these genes between different GBM cell states or in the various compartments of the tumor microenvironment (e.g. TAM vs. tumor body) should be the primary goal of future studies investigating the role of inflammasomes in GBM. This goal can be easily accomplished through basic bioinformatic analyses leveraging a multiomics approach that includes single cell RNA sequencing (scRNASeq), spatial transcriptomics and proteomics data, as has been shown in other malignancies like AML [42].

Developing insights into inflammasome expression and spatial localization within GBM will ultimately drive in vitro studies that will determine the precise role of inflammasomes in GBM pathogenesis. For example, knockdown or knockout of key inflammasome complex genes would be an elegant and direct way of determining possible roles of inflammasomes in GBM progression in murine or cell culture models, or utilizing known inhibitors such as MCC950 [74, 161]. Though unconfirmed, it appears that TAMs and MDSCs may be the key mediators of inflammasome expression in GBM, as opposed to tumor cells themselves. More importantly, the distinct feature of M1 TAM polarization being very similar to key steps in the inflammasome activation suggest that the anti-tumor and pro-inflammatory mechanism of M1 phenotype TAMs may be inflammasome-mediated.

With respect to potential experimental design, preliminary studies may include direct exposure of inflammasome inhibitors, predominantly MCC950 (an established NLRP3 inhibitor) to patient derived GBM cell lines and isolated GBM TAMs and MDSCs [167]. Conditioned medium experiments with co-cultured glioma cells can be used to induce differentiation of macrophages into TAM-like phenotypes [168, 169]. Polarizing macrophages to adopt M1 phenotypes with and without exposure to inflammasome inhibition would offer unique data on whether this process occurs in an inflammasome-dependent fashion. Similar co-culture methodologies may be employed to analyze MDSCs in vitro [170]. These studies can be subsequently expanded into murine xenograft models, in order to observe the effect of inflammasome inhibition on survival, disease progression, and microenvironmental analyses including spatial transcriptomics to identify the effect of inflammasome inhibition on both the myeloid cell compartment and overall distribution of molecular programs within the tumor core itself.

Despite extensive literature investigating the role of inflammasomes in neurotrauma [171–173] or in autoimmune diseases [4, 150], there is very little literature investigating their role as targets in treatment of solid organ malignancies, including GBM. As such, exposure of GBM to known inflammasome inhibitors, such as IC100 or NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors may similarly offer additional insight into how inflammasomes may contribute to GBM progression, or if any adjuncts to the inflammasome pathway may similarly be targeted as a means of rational drug design. In addition, manipulation of known inflammatory pathways in GBM may similarly offer alternative therapies, such as PD-L1 blockade, which drives M1 TAM phenotype differentiation and may as well serve as a pro-inflammasome therapeutic. Data from research in other malignancies suggest that there may be differential effects of inflammasome inhibition depending on treatment modality, as shown in the differential impacts in CAR T-cell therapy versus PD-1 blockade [148, 165]. While these findings must first be translated to GBM, they suggest that inflammasome inhibition must be carefully considered against the potential of reducing the effects of concurrent therapies.

Conclusion

Exploration of the tumor microenvironment has become the new frontier in GBM bioinformatics and translational research and while the myeloid-tumor interaction has been heavily investigated, there are several key features that remain poorly understood in the context of GBMInflammasomes have been well-explored in a variety of disease processes, and their role in myeloid cells and inflammatory modulation in other disease states and malignancies has been well-described. However, their role in GBM remains poorly understood. Preliminary mechanistic and pharmaceutical studies have been largely inconclusive and limited in scope, suggesting that there are ample opportunities for investigation. However, the limited available data suggest that as in other pathologies, inflammasomes may not be intrinsic to GBM, but involved in complex intercommunication between GBM cells and myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment that contributes to GBM immune escape and increased proliferation. Translating existing investigative models and therapeutic strategies in combination with multiomic methods to GBM can elucidate the role of inflammasomes in sustaining an immunosuppressive milieu and produce novel synergistic treatment regimens.
