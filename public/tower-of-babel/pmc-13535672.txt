ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPneumothorax with persistent air leak (PAL) is a debilitating condition. Patients with PAL often require prolonged chest drainage, with associated complications, such as, hospital‐acquired infections, thromboembolism, and deconditioning. Endobronchial valves (EBVs) are an approved bronchoscopic treatment for PAL and may allow earlier chest tube removal and hospital discharge. Current evidence supporting the use of EBV is limited to case series and uncontrolled studies. EBVs are usually used as a last resort when other treatments failed. Whether early referral for EBV placement is feasible and can reduce hospital length of stay in patients with pneumothorax and PAL has not been tested in a randomised controlled trial (RCT).MethodsEVAPoRATe is a prospective, open‐labelled, pilot, multi‐centre RCT. Forty patients with pneumothorax of any cause and PAL who are unsuitable for or unwilling to undergo surgical management will be randomised 1:1 to the Early EBV Referral arm or the Standard Care arm. Patients in the Early EBV Referral arm will be assessed by an interventional clinician for EBV placement at the earliest feasible time from Day 5 of pneumothorax. Patients allocated to the Standard Care arm will receive conventional management by the attending team; EBV is allowed if clinically appropriate when air leak persists after 12 days. Participants will be followed for 6 months after discharge.DiscussionThe primary outcome is the feasibility of the trial protocol, assessed by recruitment rate, adherence to the intervention arm, and retention at follow‐up. Secondary outcomes include total hospital length of stay, PAL‐related length of stay, interventions for PAL and EBV complications, time to air leak cessation, time to chest drain removal, pneumothorax recurrence, adverse events and serious adverse events, removal of EBVs, performance status, and mortality. Introduction

Background and Rationale

Pneumothorax can be spontaneous, traumatic, or iatrogenic in aetiology.

Spontaneous pneumothorax affects around 140 people per million population [1] and can be classified as primary or secondary. Primary pneumothorax presents without a precipitating external event and in the absence of clinical lung disease. Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) complicates patients with any underlying lung diseases and is significantly more common than primary ones (by about four‐fold in those aged 50 or more). SSPs are more common in older patients (usually aged 55 or above) and the peak incidence occurs around 80 years of age [2]. Data from the United Kingdom (from 1968) showed an ongoing rise in incidences of SSP, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (61% of cases) and/or asthma documented as the leading cause, followed by interstitial lung diseases and malignancy [3].

Traumatic pneumothorax can result from trauma or injury to the thorax and may occur following certain invasive procedures. Thoracic injuries account for approximately 25% of trauma‐related deaths, with 40%–50% of these cases involving a pneumothorax [4]. A pneumothorax that develops secondary to an invasive procedure is termed an iatrogenic pneumothorax. Common procedures that can lead to pneumothorax include lung biopsy (both transbronchial and transthoracic), central venous line placements, and positive pressure ventilation. In a study from the United States, 30% of cases of iatrogenic pneumothorax followed central line placements [5]. The incidence of pneumothorax following transthoracic needle lung biopsy averages 20% [6].

Treatment for pneumothorax depends on the size of the pneumothorax and the hemodynamic stability of the patients. Many patients, especially those with SSP, often have distressing breathlessness and, if left untreated, can be fatal, especially in patients with compromised lung reserves from their lung diseases. Many patients need chest drain insertion to remove the accumulated air (and pressure) and to alleviate symptoms. Persistent air leak (PAL) and lengthy hospitalisation [7] with a chest drain in situ (often from days to weeks) are common. This is associated with sizeable healthcare costs as well as morbidity and mortality from complications such as hospital‐acquired infections, venous thrombosis, and deconditioning.

In the minority of patients fit for surgery, video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) plus pleurodesis can be considered but must be balanced against the risks of general anaesthesia and surgical complications [8]. Patients with PAL who are unwilling or unfit to undergo surgery have limited options, including bedside chest tube pleurodesis, one‐way flutter valves, and intrapleural blood patches. None of these are supported by high‐level evidence nor have high success rates; their applications are most often wishful attempts to stop the PAL in the absence of any known effective therapy [9, 10].

Endobronchial valve (EBV) is a new and Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA)‐approved treatment for PAL. These valves are inserted, via a flexible bronchoscope, into the relevant airway(s) of the lung affected by the PAL. Functioning as a unidirectional (one‐way) valve, EBV facilitates the closure of air leaks by preventing air entry to the airways of the lung (and thus also the pleural space). It contributes to sealing the leak, or a significant reduction in its size to enhance natural healing and promote lung expansion. EBV placement is usually performed under general anaesthesia but can also be inserted under mild to moderate sedation if required.

When successful, EBV therapy can stop the air leak and allow the removal of the chest drain to facilitate hospital discharge. EBVs can be removed if no longer needed or deemed contributing to adverse effects. The benefits of EBV placement for PAL are supported by retrospective case series [11, 12, 13, 14] without comparison groups. The British Thoracic Society Guidelines on Pneumothorax found insufficient quality evidence to support or deny its use [15]. A systematic review of clinical evidence [16], including 99 SSP patients from three retrospective case series and 25 case reports, found EBVs a promising minimally invasive option for PALs, especially in high‐risk patients. Air leaks ceased within 24 h following valve insertion in 66% of patients, with few complications and no reported deaths attributed to EBV placement.

EBV is expensive, requires specialist expertise, and is not always available in many centres. Most of the published series to date only applied EBVs as a last resort, after failed attempts including chemical pleurodesis, and only when PAL had persisted for up to weeks (median 12.5 days in the systematic review) [17, 18]. The potential of early referral for EBV therapy to facilitate the removal of the chest drain without lengthy hospital stays is an attractive option worth exploring.

Objective

The Endobronchial VAlves for Pneumothorax to Reduce Admission Time (EVAPoRATe) study is a pilot feasibility multi‐centre randomised clinical trial (RCT) that will enrol patients with a pneumothorax and PAL. It aims to determine the feasibility of the trial protocol to establish if early referral for EBVs in these patients is feasible, can reduce the hospital length of stay (LoS) and/or improve key healthcare outcomes (including the need for re‐intervention and pneumothorax recurrence).

Trial Design

Forty patients with pneumothorax with PAL will be randomised 1:1 to either early EBV placement or to standard care. This pilot feasibility study will assess whether a future full‐scale RCT of EBV for PAL is practical and will provide data to guide protocol refinement and sample size calculation.

PAL is defined as an air leak that persists for at least 96 h since the first radiographic evidence of pneumothorax [18]. Standard care is defined as the usual practice that the physicians advise based on the guidelines [15] and local practice.

Methods

Participants, Interventions and Outcomes

Study Setting

Study sites involve interventional pulmonary centres with experience and expertise in EBV therapy and will include Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital and Fiona Stanley Hospital in Western Australia, Royal Adelaide Hospital in South Australia, Northern Hospital in Victoria, Liverpool Hospital, Westmead Hospital and Prince of Wales Hospital in New South Wales, and Middlemore Hospital in Auckland, New Zealand.

Eligibility Criteria

Patients with pneumothorax of any cause with ongoing air leak at Day 4 or Day 5 of pneumothorax and who are not suitable or unwilling to receive surgical management are eligible for the study. Ongoing air leak is defined as continuous or intermittent bubbling in the patient's chest drain water seal chamber.

Participant exclusion criteria are as follows:

Age < 18 years;

Age < 18 years;

Inability to consent or to comply with the protocol; and

Inability to consent or to comply with the protocol; and

Hemodynamic instability.

Hemodynamic instability.

Hemodynamic instability is defined as requiring vasopressors/inotropes to maintain mean arterial pressure > 60 mmHg, respiratory support including non‐invasive ventilation or invasive mechanical ventilation with high oxygen (> 60% FiO2), and haemoptysis necessitating blood transfusion.

Who Will Take Informed Consent?

The site primary investigator (PI) or designated site research staff (as per delegation log) will screen patients with ongoing air leaks on Day 3 or Day 4 of pneumothorax (Day 0 is the day when radiographic evidence of pneumothorax is first observed) and who have had a chest drain inserted.

Potential participants will be approached about the study when the patients are admitted to the clinical team. Potential participants will be provided with the participant information and consent form (PICF) to read and given time to ask questions to the study team. They will also be given time to discuss the study with family and carers and their general practitioner.

Consecutive eligible participants will be enrolled after providing informed consent. The site PI will be aware of their dual role as the primary physician of the participant and as a clinical researcher.

Additional Consent Provisions for Collection and Use of Participant Data and Biological Specimens

Baseline data will be collected once informed consent has been obtained. Data collected will include demographics including age and sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status and clinical assessment for Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for shortness of breath and pain, comorbidities, baseline chest X‐ray findings, and current treatment offered including the chest drain. No extra biological specimens will be obtained.

Interventions

Patients will be offered trial entry if the air leak persists on Day 4, with a view that treatment (e.g., EBV placement) can be delivered on/after Day 5 from the onset of pneumothorax. Eligible patients, who meet inclusion criteria and do not meet any exclusion criteria, will be informed about the trial. The patients will receive standard care from their attending team, and all treatment options suitable will be considered, including surgery. In cases where patients are not suitable candidates for surgery or if they opt out of the surgical procedure, enrolment to EVAPoRATe will be offered to the patient.

If they consent to participate, patients will be randomly assigned to either the Early EBV Referral arm or the Standard Care arm. Both groups will be monitored daily for resolution of air leaks and lung expansion. Management of chest drains and decisions for their removal and hospital discharge are the responsibility of the patient's attending team.

Early EBV Referral Arm

Patients with PAL will be assessed by an interventional clinician for EBV placement at the earliest feasible time from Day 5 of pneumothorax. The risks and benefits of the procedure will be explained to the patients by the clinical team performing the procedure. The decision to proceed to EBV insertion will be that of the interventionalist. Should there be clinical reasons not to proceed to EBV insertion (e.g., inconsistent air leak to guide valve placement, unable to tolerate sedation/anaesthesia), the patient will be assessed regularly for suitability of EBV treatment until the air leak heals and the interventional clinician must provide a reason that will be documented in the patient file and research notes.

Standard Care Arm

Patients randomised to this group will be managed conventionally by the attending team. Standard care will usually involve conventional care of chest drain until the air leak heals. Additional procedures, such as bedside chest tube pleurodesis and blood patch treatment, can be undertaken if the attending team considers them appropriate. EBV is allowed if considered appropriate by the attending team if air leak persists after 12 days. This time is chosen based on the median time of EBV insertion (as a rescue procedure) in an audit of Western Australian hospital practice and is consistent with literature data; thus, patients enrolled in the study will not be disadvantaged.

Participants in both arms will be managed by their clinical teams and receive all other medical treatments as deemed clinically appropriate. Patients' medical care, including drain care and other medical management, will be directed by their attending physicians, as per standard practice in the treatment hospital, regardless of study group allocation. All participants and carers will have access to the trial research staff via a direct phone line should any concerns arise. All clinical decisions, such as the need for additional procedures, removal of chest drain and subsequent removal of EBV, are the responsibility of the attending clinicians according to the clinical need of the patient.

Participants will be able to take their usual medications during the study.

Participant Timeline

Potential participants, as part of the informed consent process, will have the study procedures and follow‐up plan discussed in detail. All patients will be followed up regularly by the research team for their clinical outcomes, the effect of the EBV on PAL, or any adverse events at the pleural clinic after hospital discharge, with scheduled appointments at between 7 and 10 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months. These follow‐ups can be conducted either through phone calls or face‐to‐face appointments, depending on what is most suitable for the patient. Where participants do not answer follow‐up calls/attend planned study visits, the research staff will contact them again or book an additional visit if required. If the participant misses a visit due to an admission to the site hospital, the visit will be carried out in the hospital if appropriate. Sample Size

Although a pilot feasibility study typically does not require sample size calculations, as it must account for factors such as significance level, power, difference between groups, and SD [22], it is essential to include 15–20 participants per group to ensure the validity of such a study [23]. Therefore, our pilot feasibility study will aim to recruit 20 patients per group. The sample size is set for practical reasons and is not driven by hypothesis testing or estimating effect sizes.

Recruitment

Enrolment and screening logs will be maintained, and sites will send de‐identified logs to the lead site at the beginning of every month. Consecutive eligible participants will be enrolled after providing informed consent.

Participant Withdrawal Criteria

Participants can withdraw at any time from the study and do not need to provide the research staff with a reason. We will retain all participants' data up until the time of withdrawal as outlined in the PICF. There may be reasons for the site PI to decide to withdraw a participant from the study. This could be due to an inability to comply with the study protocol, such as availability for follow‐ups or other compliance issues. A participant may also be withdrawn in their best interests.

In all cases, the study withdrawal form will be completed, and a copy submitted to the lead site. Withdrawn participants will not be replaced. Where considered clinically necessary, withdrawn participants will be asked to return to the clinic for a safety follow‐up appointment.

Implementation

The site PI or designated site research staff will screen patients and approach potential participants. Consecutive eligible participants will be enrolled after providing informed consent. Participants will be randomised 1:1 to either the Early EBV Referral arm or Standard Care arm using the Griffith Randomisation Service automated web portal.

Blinding (Masking/Anonymising)

This is a prospective, open‐labelled, pilot multi‐centre RCT. Due to the nature of the intervention, staff and trial participants will not be blinded to treatment group allocation. All records of hospitalisation will be reviewed by an independent investigator to assess that the length of stay was appropriate to clinical need.

Data Collection, Management and Analysis

Data Collection Methods

Case Report Forms (CRFs) will be used for study data collection. Data recorded directly on the CRFs, with no prior written or electronic record, will be considered source data. A summary of parameters that will be recorded in the CRFs, visit details and follow up outcomes are summarised in Figure 2.

Schedule of treatment for each visit and follow up procedures.

For the Early EBV Referral arm, follow‐up will include daily assessment of chest drain for ongoing or cessation of air leak and procedure‐related complications. Post‐procedure assessment will include assessment for cessation of air leak and drain removal.

For the Standard Care arm, follow‐up will include daily assessment of the chest drain for ongoing air leak and recording of any additional therapeutic procedures such as chemical pleurodesis and/or autologous blood patch.

Follow‐ups for both arms will be scheduled between 7 and 10 days after discharge and at 1, 3 and 6 months post‐discharge to collect reports of recurrence, adverse events, unscheduled visits and/or hospital admissions related to the trial or for unrelated reasons.

Data Management

All procedures for the handling and analysis of data will be conducted using Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2023), and local policies and procedures for the handling and analysis of data.

Data collected will be stored at the site on password‐protected computers accessible only by the site research staff and will be held in the department where the PI is based. All physical documentation will be stored in a secure, locked filing cabinet with restricted access in private offices within the PI's department in line with the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research for clinical trials and local policy guidelines for research data archiving. All data associated with this trial will be archived for 15 years after the completion of the project.

Confidentiality

Patient privacy and confidentiality will be maintained, as any information that identifies participants will be available only at the enrolment study site and only to designated study investigators, all of whom will either have signed a confidentiality agreement or be employees of the hospital.

Statistical Methods

The primary outcome on the feasibility of randomising participants to the study will be provided as descriptive data including the recruitment rate, the retention rate, and the proportion of participants screened who consented to be randomised. Estimates of these rates will be provided per treatment arm and overall.

The endpoints and outcomes listed in the secondary aims will be analysed first by generating descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations; medians and interquartile ranges, and proportions as appropriate) and second by estimating the differences and associated variability between treatment arms. For exploratory purposes, t‐test or Mann–Whitney test (for continuous measures), paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, and chi‐squared tests will be used wherever appropriate to compare treatment arms. Adverse and serious adverse events will be reported in descriptive figures.

Data will be analysed on an intention‐to‐treat basis. All randomised participants will be included in the analysis. No interim analysis is planned for this pilot feasibility study. Any deviation(s) from the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) will be reported initially to the Trial Steering Committee (TSC) and described and justified in the final report as appropriate.

In common with many clinical studies, missing data may exist either in the form of total non‐response (e.g., attrition due to death or patient withdrawal) or item non‐response (when some but not all the required information is collected from the patient). We will attempt to minimise the missing data due to item non‐response. Throughout the duration of the trial, participants will have regular contact with the respiratory department and the study team. This will maximise proper and complete data collection. The research team will document as accurately as possible the reasons for any non‐completion or missing data, thereby minimising truly absent data. The details of the statistical analysis will be set out in the SAP.

Monitoring

Data Monitoring

The remit of the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) is to ensure the safety of study participants through study procedures, reviewing adverse events and serious adverse events, and considering new data (recently published studies) that may determine the validity of study continuation. All deaths anticipated or unanticipated will be discussed with the DSMB. The committee determines whether significant benefits or risks have been uncovered that may have an impact on the feasibility and/or ethical conduct of the study.

The DSMB will also help to ensure the scientific integrity of the study by reviewing the quality of the data it uses to make its decisions. The DSMB provides recommendations to the TSC, which oversees the study and determines whether the study should continue or be suspended or terminated.

The TSC will be responsible for the supervision of the trial in all its aspects. It will be responsible for ensuring the completion of the trial to clinical and ethical standards. It will monitor site recruitment and review any recommendations received from the DSMB.

Harms

The participants will be monitored as per the standard of care and during follow‐ups throughout the study. All adverse events relating to the study, serious and non‐serious, will be fully documented on the appropriate CRFs. For each AE, the investigator will provide the onset and end dates, intensity, treatment required, outcome, seriousness, and action taken. The investigator will determine the relationship of the experimental procedure to all AEs as defined in the Adverse Event Reporting Section of the Investigator Site File.

An AE is defined as any untoward medical occurrence, including an exacerbation of a pre‐existing condition in a patient in a clinical investigation who received an experimental procedure. The event does not necessarily have to have a causal relationship with this treatment. All adverse events relating to, and occurring during, the clinical study (i.e., from signing the informed consent until death or the end of the study follow‐up period, whichever comes first) will be collected and documented by the investigator in individual participant AE logs. Events will also be reported if a causal link (relatedness) between the AE and the study is suspected but not confirmed.

Uncommon but potential complications in the Early EBV Referral arm may include coughing up or migration of the valve; procedure and anaesthesia‐related complications; reaction to the EBVs; infection distal to the EBV blockage; and respiratory failure requiring removal of EBVs.

A SAE is defined as any AE which results in death, is immediately life‐threatening, results in persistent or significant disability/incapacity, requires or prolongs patient hospitalisation, or is deemed serious for any other reason if it is an important medical event when based upon appropriate medical judgement, which may jeopardise the patient and may require medical or surgical intervention to prevent one of the other outcomes listed in the definitions. AE logs will be followed up until resolution. Where AEs are not resolved at study completion, this will be noted on the AE log. This participant group is under the ongoing care of the respective respiratory physicians unrelated to the study, so adverse events will have ongoing management.

This may include but is not limited to review by external sponsors, Human Research Ethics Committees, and institutional Governance review bodies.

Discussion

Various single‐ or multi‐centre case series have reported successful use of EBV for patients with PAL. A systematic review [16] of 99 patients who underwent EBVs for SSP found the procedure safe with no mortality directly associated with EBV placement. In our audit of EBV placement for PAL in Western Australia (between 2017 and 2023), no serious adverse events were encountered. The recurrence rate of pneumothorax was low (6%).

Current published literature on the role of EBV in PAL has limitations. First, data published are mostly retrospective (with a few prospective) series without a control group or randomisation. Selection and publication biases limit the interpretation of the findings. Second, placement of EBVs is often limited in specialist centres. This, together with its costs, means that EBVs are generally used as a last resort after a prolonged period of air leak and hospitalisation, often when patients are deconditioned and/or suffer from hospital‐acquired complications.

If EBVs are beneficial in stopping air leaks then referral for their early use will provide the best benefits and avoid prolonged hospitalisation, multiple pleural interventions, and associated adverse events. The EVAPoRATe study aims to determine the feasibility of a future full‐scale RCT that will investigate whether an early referral for an EBV in patients with PAL can reduce the length of hospital stay.
