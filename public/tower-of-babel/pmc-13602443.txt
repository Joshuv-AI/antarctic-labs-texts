Myringoplasty Outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Children Treated Via the Deadly Ears Program: A 10‐Year Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Lara Gahan, James Nightingale, Trent Lyon, Dennis Conlon, Christopher Perry, Claire Frauenfelder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAustralian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children experience some of the highest rates of otitis media with tympanic membrane (TM) perforation in the world. Deadly Ears is a statewide, Indigenous‐led outreach service providing on‐Country ear health care across Queensland. We evaluated myringoplasty surgery outcomes delivered through this program.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children undergoing myringoplasty surgery by Deadly Ears between January 2015 and February 2025 was performed. Demographic, clinical, surgical, and audiological data were extracted from medical records. Primary outcomes were TM closure and improvement between pre‐ and post‐operative audiological categories. Associations between primary outcomes and patient, ear, and surgical variables were analysed.ResultsOne hundred and four children underwent 135 myringoplasties (median age 10 years, IQR 8–12) across nine communities. Surgical and audiological follow‐up was complete for 123 (91%) cases (median follow‐up 707 days, IQR 358–1156). Complete TM closure achieved in 60.2% of cases and perforation size improved in 74.8%. Hearing improved by at least one category in 64.2% of ears. The proportion of children with normal hearing increased from 3.7% to 44.7%. Cartilage‐based grafts, consultant surgeon operating, and shorter time to initial follow‐up were associated with improved TM closure rates. Hearing improvement was associated with age < 12 years.ConclusionStrong community engagement facilitated by Deadly Ears was reflected by high and complete follow‐up rates. Surgical and audiological outcomes were consistent with other Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander outreach programs but remain lower than metropolitan centres. Ongoing investment in culturally responsive, community‐designed outreach models is essential.Keywords: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health, chronic suppurative otitis media, myringoplasty, tympanic membrane perforation, tympanoplasty

Introduction

Deadly Ears is Child Health Queensland's ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgery outreach initiative aimed at reducing the significant burden of middle ear disease and hearing loss experienced by children of remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities [1, 2]. The program delivers comprehensive paediatric ear healthcare, led by identified Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander practitioners, incorporating ENT subspecialty and audiological services plus wide‐ranging allied health input. Both opportunistic and referral‐based appointments are available during outreach visits.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children experience some of the highest rates of middle ear disease in the world [2]. Their middle ear disease is more severe, persistent and presents younger than in other populations [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Middle ear disease and the resultant conductive hearing loss significantly impacts language development, literacy skills and academic performance, adversely affecting the trajectory of children's lives [3, 8, 9]. Additionally, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultural knowledge and history is traditionally shared verbally, so endemic hearing loss not only affects person, but Community [3].

At 3 months of age over 90% of remote‐living Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children have otitis media with effusion [10], and by 2 years of age 25%–66% will have developed tympanic membrane (TM) perforation [5, 11, 12]. While many of these perforations will close spontaneously, access to an ENT surgeon for surgical repair with myringoplasty is needed for some to achieve a clean, dry, and better hearing ear.

Myringoplasty is a well‐established surgical procedure to repair TM perforations. Myringoplasty technique varies according to surgeon and institute, and according to disease and patient factors [13]. Regardless of chosen operative approach, metropolitan TM closure rates of approximately 88.5%–90% are routinely reported [13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast, myringoplasty outcomes in remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities are less successful and more variable, with closure rates between 39% and 82% reported in small, heterogeneous series with modest follow‐up [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Robust data pertaining to functional hearing outcomes is scarce.

The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare and recent large systematic review called for prioritised reporting and evaluation of remote Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander outreach programs, highlighting a significant gap in outcome data, and a need for community‐designed models of care to address persistent inequities in ear health outcomes [25, 26]. This study presents the first description of myringoplasty outcomes from the Deadly Ears program. We aimed to: (1) Describe myringoplasty TM closure and audiological outcomes; (2) Identify patient, ear, and surgical factors associated with successful myringoplasty surgery; and (3) Contextualise our results within existing literature to inform future service delivery.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A retrospective cohort study evaluated patients undergoing myringoplasty surgery via the Deadly Ears program between January 2015 and February 2025. Deadly Ears services nine communities across Queensland including Cherbourg, Mt. Isa, Thursday Island, the Northern Peninsula Area, Doomadgee, Palm Island, Normanton, Woorabinda, and Mornington Island. These communities are classified as remote or very remote according to the Australian Statistical Geography Standard. These settings are characterised by geographic isolation and reduced access to healthcare services.

The study was approved by the Children's Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (EX/2025/QCHQ/119753). Consent was waived according to National Health and Medical Research Council guidelines.

This research was co‐designed with Aboriginal stakeholders, including members of the Deadly Ears leadership team and author group who identify as Aboriginal (Lyon, Conlon). Their lived experience and cultural knowledge inform the work of Deadly Ears daily and our study design, methodology and interpretation of findings.

Participants

Eligible participants were all Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander children who had chronic TM perforation and underwent myringoplasty via Deadly Ears during the study period, including bilateral and revision procedures. Children referred to tertiary centres for myringoplasty were excluded.

Deadly Ears offers transcanal myringoplasty for children aged 7–16 years with American Society of Anaesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA) 1 or 2. Historically, some centres offered small numbers of post‐auricular approach procedures until policy changed in 2021; thereafter, patients requiring post‐auricular approach were usually referred on to tertiary centres to allow for appropriate post‐operative care.

Statistical Analysis

Data were stored securely on Queensland Health servers, re‐identifiable with a separate key. Statistical analysis was performed using PRISM software (v11.0.0) with support from Queensland Cyber Infrastructure Foundation Ltd. (QCIF).

Associations between categorical variables and outcome were explored using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact analyses. Continuous predictors were compared using Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated where appropriate.

Previous ENT Surgery

Only 7 myringoplasty patients (5.2%) had previous ipsilateral grommet insertion, suggesting > 90% of perforations were due to chronic suppurative otitis media.

Seventy one myringoplasty patients (53%) previously had adenoidectomy and this was not significantly associated with TM closure (p = 0.52) or audiological outcome (p = 0.71, OR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.41–1.80).

Pre‐Operative Ear Characteristics

17% (n = 23) of ears were wet at the time of surgery and central perforations were most common (46%). Smaller grade 1 perforations were most frequent (52%), and frequency decreased with higher grades of perforation.

Contralateral ears were aerated with intact TMs in most patients (64%) while dry perforation (16%) and suppurative otitis media (7%) were also seen in contralateral ears.

Pre‐operatively, most ears (58%) had a moderate conductive hearing loss. Mild hearing loss was present in 36% of ears and severe in 3%. Five patients with TM perforations had normal hearing.

Primary, Revision and Bilateral Myringoplasty

Primary myringoplasty was performed for 112 of 135 (83%) patients. Twenty three revision procedures were performed by Deadly Ears. Two patients required more than one revision: one went on to have successful bilateral revision myringoplasties. One patient had a successful primary surgery on one side and five unsuccessful contralateral myringoplasties, and was then referred to tertiary centre. Documentation process errors were identified contributing to multiple revision procedures, which were subsequently addressed. Seventeen children required tertiary referral for revision after graft failure.

Sequential bilateral myringoplasty was performed for 22 children over the audit period. Twelve children (55%) had both surgeries performed by Deadly Ears. No child underwent concurrent myringoplasties.

Surgical Technique

The most common approaches were transcanal with tympanomeatal flap (55.5%) and push‐through myringoplasty (30.4%). Composite (32%) and perichondrium (24%) graft material were preferred. Temporalis fascia, Biodesign, and fat plug grafts were each used for 11% of cases. Underlay (79%) or inlay (21%) methods were used. No surgeon used an overlay technique.

Consultants operated alone for 44% of myringoplasties, while consultants and registrars were both scrubbed for 41% of cases. Registrars performed 15% of myringoplasties with unscrubbed consultant supervision.

Post‐Operative Follow‐Up

Follow‐up was complete for 123/135 patients (91.1%). 36% of ears were reviewed by our service within 3 months of surgery. The median time to first post‐operative review with Deadly Ears was 153 days (IQR 62–215).

87.6% of cases had at least 6 months follow‐up. Median total review duration was 707 days (358–1156 days). This total review duration included initial post‐operative review, ongoing monitoring and follow‐up, plus any walk‐in consultations requested by patient or family.

Primary Outcomes

Tympanic Membrane Closure

Among the 123 ears with follow‐up data, complete closure of the tympanic membrane was successful in 60.2% (74/123), with a further 14.6% (18/123) achieving partial TM closure (reduction in size of TM perforation). Overall, improved perforation size was observed in 74.8% of operated ears. 25.2% (31 ears) had the same size or larger perforations post‐operatively.

Factors associated with successful perforation closure were graft material, consultant surgeon operating status, and time to initial post‐operative review.

Cartilage‐based grafts (cartilage, composite or perichondrium) were positively associated with perforation improvement compared to non‐cartilage grafts (temporalis fascia, fat plug, Biodesign) (p = 0.03) (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.11–5.92) (Figure 2). There was no association between graft material and audiological outcome (p = 0.75).

Graft material and relationship to tympanic membrane closure. Cartilage‐based grafts were associated with improved perforation closure compared to non‐cartilage grafts (p = 0.03).

An association between better closure outcomes when a consultant operated or assisted was identified (Figure 3). This was significant (p = 0.02) when compared to registrar‐only operating, and was independent of perforation size, graft material, and revision status. Outcomes did not significantly differ between consultant‐only and combined registrar‐consultant cases.

Surgeon seniority and TM closure Successful TM closure was more likely when a consultant surgeon scrubbed during myringoplasty, as lead or assistant operator (p = 0.02).

Earlier initial follow‐up was associated with higher rates of partial TM closure (p = 0.02), but when combined with complete closure cases, this pattern demonstrated a trend only (p = 0.07) (Figure 4).

TM closure and time to first post‐operative review. Earlier post‐operative review had a trend of association with improved perforation closure (p = 0.07).

Audiological Outcome

Hearing improved by at least one category in 64.2% of cases (79/123) (Figure 5). The average improvement was 1.4 categories, and 33% improved by two or more categories (26/79). No patients had a severe hearing loss following myringoplasty.

Hearing status pre‐ and post‐myringoplasty (by ear). Overall hearing status improved following myringoplasty for all groups.

The proportion of ears with normal hearing improved from 3.7% (5 ears) to 45% (55 ears). Four ears dropped audiological categories post‐operatively; two of these had discharging ears at the time of review.

Younger age at time of surgery was associated with improved post‐operative hearing outcomes (Figure 6). When cases were dichotomised at age 12, this relationship was highly significant (p < 0.01) with a large effect size (OR = 3.34, 95% CI 1.46–7.64). There was no association between age and TM closure.

Age and audiological outcomes: Younger age was associated with improved hearing outcomes (p = 0.02).

TM closure did not have to be successful for functional audiological improvement to be observed. 33% of TM failure cases improved by at least one audiological category. Perforation improvement was associated with high odds of audiological improvement (OR = 5.95, 95% CI 2.46–14.42).

No single factor was associated with both tympanic membrane perforation closure and audiological improvement.

Discussion

There is significant disparity in prevalence, severity, and chronicity of middle ear disease between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander and other Australians [3, 27]. Up to two thirds of remote living Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children will have had TM perforation by 2 years of age [12, 28] where non‐Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children only have an incidence of 0.1% [29].

Access to myringoplasty for a better hearing, clean, dry and safe ear remains challenging in remote communities. Surgery should be provided in a culturally informed manner with patient‐tailored technique and graft material and community experience. Few studies have reported myringoplasty outcomes in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations, with limitations of retrospective design, small sample sizes, and high attrition rates [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].

Over the past 10 years, Deadly Ears has cared for the ear health of Queensland's Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children. For currently, for the first time, Deadly Ears has an entirely Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander leadership group. Community uptake and confidence is high and, as a result, we can report 91% follow‐up rates, reflecting some of the lowest attrition rates in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander ENT outreach literature [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 30, 31].

Myringoplasty Outcome: Tympanic Membrane Closure

Over 60% of myringoplasties resulted in complete perforation closure. This is in‐keeping with previously reported Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander outreach programs: 39% [20], 51% [17], 51.3% [24], 57.5% [23], 64.7% [18], 64.2% [19], 70% [22], and 82.7% [21] across Australian communities.

TM perforations were improved in 74.8% of myringoplasties (combined complete and partial TM closure). Perforation improvement was significantly related to graft material, consultant surgeon operating status, and time to follow‐up.

Graft Material

Recent survey of Australian otolaryngologists reports temporalis fascia as the preferred myringoplasty graft material (> 51% of respondents) with 90% of surgeons not altering preferred graft for rural or Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients [13]. Deadly Ears surgeons used temporalis fascia in only 11% of myringoplasties, preferring composite material (32%). This preference proved efficacious as only 50% of myringoplasty procedures using temporalis fascia resulted in complete TM closure, in comparison to cartilage (80%), perichondrium (68%), and composite material (64%). We found a significant association between the use of cartilage‐based grafts and overall perforation improvement, with a high effect size (OR = 2.57).

Anecdotal preference for cartilage‐based grafts for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients supports these findings, although literature remains scarce [21]. Most Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander myringoplasty outreach studies report temporalis fascia outcomes [19, 20, 22, 23]. Loh et al. exclusively used cartilage grafts and report the highest published myringoplasty success rate for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients (82.7%) [21].

Consultant Surgeon Operating Status

TM closure was significantly more likely when a consultant surgeon was scrubbed as either lead or assistant operator. This highlights the need for senior surgical guidance and underlines the need for further registrar experience operating with this specialised patient group. Involvement of registrars in rural/regional health service delivery is a vital part of training and is required to sustain the future of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander outreach services.

Community Engagement and Follow‐Up

Close post‐operative follow‐up is a significant predictor of myringoplasty success for both metropolitan and remote patients [12]. Challenges with post‐operative follow‐up have frequently been associated with less favourable outcomes [18, 20, 23, 24]. Deadly Ears reports a follow‐up rate of over 91%, higher than most published services (21.5% [20], 32% [18], 46.8% [19], 50.6% [21], 53% [23], 66.1% [24], 78% [17], 93% [22]). Median time to post‐operative review was 5 months (IQR 2–7), with earlier time to follow‐up trending towards improved closure outcomes.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander leadership, partnership with local services, and access to concurrent audiology and allied health services contribute to performance and community trust in outreach services [32]. Early and regular post‐operative follow‐up remains difficult as specialist outreach can be irregular, expensive, and brief [33]. Teleotology with local health workers and remote specialist input may support earlier detection of complications and merits further investigation [33, 34]. The Deadly Ears model aims to balance accessibility and overall service expenditure.

Myringoplasty Outcome: Improvement in Hearing

Hearing loss is a leading cause of disability in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and contributes significantly to health and wellbeing inequity [6]. Our cohort's hearing improved by at least one audiological category in 64% of cases. The proportion of ears with normal hearing increased more than 10‐fold post myringoplasty.

Hearing outcomes in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children following outreach myringoplasty are inconsistently reported. Studies present varied measures of audiological outcome, making contextualisation of results difficult. Studies reporting a comparable collated hearing improvement include Soumya et al. (29%) and Mak et al. 2004 (51%) [17, 20].

Additional studies report a return to normal hearing as the benchmark. In our cohort, normal hearing ears improved from 3.7% to 44.7%, demonstrating clear functional audiological improvement. MacKendrick et al. report normal post‐operative hearing in 56% of patients [23], Mak et al. 61.8% [18], and Loh et al. 85% [21]. Overall, hearing outcomes correlate with previous literature. However, our use of a broad audiological grading system rather than more specific measures likely resulted in underestimation of functional hearing improvement.

Consistent with previous findings, TM closure did not have to be successful for myringoplasty to have a positive effect on a child's hearing [19, 20]. Audiological improvement is posited to be related to enhanced ossicular chain mobility and decreased middle ear inflammation rather than TM closure alone [35, 36].

Traditionally, myringoplasty was reserved for older children to allow for maturation of eustachian function and improved tolerance of post‐operative cares [37]. However, in our cohort, younger children had better odds of audiological improvement, and age had no negative effect on surgical closure. Dichotomising at age 12, younger age was highly significant (p < 0.01) with a large effect size (OR = 3.34). Worse outcomes for older patients may reflect a cumulative detrimental effect of chronic otitis media on middle ear mucosa and ossicular chain. Hearing loss impacts speech, language, communication and learning. Myringoplasty facilitates functional hearing improvement in young children [38]. Our study supports the growing consensus in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander ENT outreach literature to address symptomatic TM perforations sooner rather than later [17, 19].

No Effect of Wet Middle Ear

This study corroborates outreach community literature reporting that wet operative middle ear mucosa has no negative prognostic effect on perforation closure or audiological outcome [17, 18, 19]. This has significant implications for outreach services in a patient population where chronic ear disease is endemic and fluctuating middle ear status is common.

Limitations

This retrospective study comes with inherent risk of misclassification and measurement variability. Small sample sizes across variables with many subgroups limit power. Clustered observations with some children contributing more than one ear open underestimation of variance. However, including children undergoing multiple procedures helps to reflect complexities of practice in our patient cohort, and outweighs possible bias. Study strengths include cohort follow‐up, diversity of surgeons, and inclusion of patients from geographically diverse Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. These factors improve the generalisability of results.

Conclusions and Future Directions

We report strong post‐operative myringoplasty outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in Community. This study contributes robust outcome data on a critical health issue affecting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children. Rates of TM closure and hearing improvement were appropriate and consistent with previous studies. Strong community engagement with the Deadly Ears program was demonstrated by excellent post‐operative follow‐up and duration in the context of a holistic patient‐focused ear health program.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people bear 2.3× the burden of disease compared to other Australians but receive only 1.2–1.4× per capita health spend [25]. Both spending and overall burden of disease have not changed significantly over a 30‐year period [25]. A lack of population‐level data regarding ear disease and outcome data from existing outreach programs has been a barrier to changing healthcare spending and changing policy [26].

Our study provides insight into a successful, Indigenous‐led and culturally responsive model of care which facilitates care on Country and makes progress towards better ear health for young Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.
