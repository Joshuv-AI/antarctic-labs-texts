Large Rabi Splitting Driven by Plasmon‐Enhanced Hybrid Anapole States in Si–Au–WSe2 Heterostructures
Authors: Yanhui Deng, Mingyang Yu, Xizhen Xing, Chenxi Zhao, Tianyu Yang, Yujie Ma, Zhang‐Kai Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExciton–polaritons arising from strong light–matter coupling are key enablers of quantum coherent phenomena, whereas large Rabi splitting is critical for ensuring their robustness and stability. However, existing microcavity photonic modes rarely combine low radiative dissipation with strong field confinement, thereby limiting large splitting strong coupling, particularly in the few‐exciton regime. Here, we design a metal–dielectric hybrid cavity in which coherent hybridization among plasmon resonances, magnetic quadrupole modes, and anapole states gives rise to a plasmon‐enhanced hybrid anapole state (HAS) featuring narrow linewidths (54 meV) and strong field localization (180‐fold). Based on the plasmon‐enhanced HAS, we realize both HAS–exciton and HAS–plasmon polaritons and further demonstrate HAS–plasmon–exciton triplex polaritons enabled by multichannel radiative coupling. The ternary system achieves a Rabi splitting of 330 meV, six times larger than the binary case, with a reduced effective exciton number of 6205 based on a characteristic excitonic volume, significantly lower than values reported for the dielectric‐exciton systems under the same volume‐based estimation procedure. A three‐coupled oscillator model elucidates the light–matter interaction mechanism, providing a new pathway to efficient room‐temperature strong coupling and a design guide for high‐performance dielectric‐cavity‐based polariton devices.Keywords: exciton−polariton, hybrid anapole state, metal‐dielectric cavity, plasmon resonance, Rabi splitting

Introduction

Strong light–matter coupling is central to many modern research and applications, including quantum electrodynamics (QED), ultrafast optics, and topological optics [1, 2, 3]. This regime is when the coherent energy exchange rate between the photonic mode and material excitation (exciton) exceeds their respective decay rates, forming hybrid light–matter quasiparticles (polaritons) [4, 5, 6]. Such couplings hold great potential for quantum manipulation, Bose–Einstein condensation [7, 8], ultrafast optical switching [9, 10, 11], non‐Hermitian photonics [12, 13], and topological polariton devices [14, 15].

In the strong coupling regime, the collective coupling strength for an ensemble of N excitons is given by g = N g 0, where g 0 = μ·E is the coupling strength between a single‐exciton and the cavity mode, μ the exciton transition dipole moment, and E the electric field of the cavity mode [16, 17]. Enhancing g thus requires increasing and aligning μ and E . Two‐dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are ideal candidates due to their sharp excitonic resonances and large binding energies that support strongly confined energetic excitons (∼1 nm Bohr radius) [18, 19, 20]. Embedding TMDs layers within optical microcavities enables the realization of strong light–matter coupling, giving rise to polariton eigenstates. This creates opportunities for engineering the exciton–photon interactions.

Plasmonic nanocavities possess strong electric field confinement capabilities and have become representative systems for early room‐temperature strong coupling. Extensive studies have investigated TMDs–plasmonic hybrid systems; however, the intrinsically large dissipation of plasmonic resonances imposes stringent conditions for achieving strong coupling in the visible regime [21, 22, 23, 24, 25], requiring sufficiently large coupling strengths g to overcome the coupling threshold g > γ2+γex2/8, where γ and γ ex denote the dissipation rates of the cavity and excitonic modes, respectively. Recently, Liang et al. proposed a local‐to‐nonlocal transition (LNT) strategy to realize high‐quality (high‐Q) plasmonic resonances from the near‐to mid‐infrared regime [26], offering a potential route to reduce the strong coupling threshold. Nevertheless, because the maximum achievable Q factor is strongly wavelength‐dependent, realizing low‐loss plasmonic modes in the visible range remains challenging. Moreover, purely metallic systems suffer from low damage thresholds and high fabrication costs. Consequently, all‐dielectric and metal–dielectric hybrid platforms have increasingly emerged as promising candidates for realizing room‐temperature strong coupling quantum states. Owing to their high refractive indices, dielectric cavities can support high‐Q photonic states with suppressed radiative dissipation through various mechanisms, including surface lattice resonances [27], quasi‐bound states in the continuum (q‐BICs) [7, 28, 29], anapole states [30, 31, 32, 33, 34], and plasmonic–dielectric hybrid states [35, 36, 37, 38], thereby offering opportunities to relax the critical conditions for strong coupling and enhance cavity–exciton interactions. Notably, anapole states are uniquely suited for this application as their far‐field radiation is suppressed by destructive interference between electric dipole (ED) and toroidal dipole (TD) radiation channels, whereas intense electromagnetic energy remains tightly confined inside dielectric nanostructures. Representative investigations on anapole–exciton coupling, higher‐order anapole–exciton coupling, and cavity‐assisted anapole–exciton coupling have demonstrated that spatial and spectral feature of anapole states can effectively tailor exciton–photon interactions [34, 39, 40]. However, achieving both low dissipation and strong electric field confinement within existing photonic modes remains challenging. In particular, the electric field enhancement of high‐Q modes is typically limited to below two orders of magnitude, which generally necessitates higher exciton densities and restricts the achievable coupling strengths to only several tens of meV [41, 42, 43, 44].

To address the above challenges, we propose a novel metal–dielectric nanocavity in which the plasmonic resonance hybridizes with the anapole‐associated photonic state, whose optical‐field characteristics originate from the coupled magnetic quadrupole (MQ) and anapole responses, thereby forming a plasmon‐enhanced hybrid anapole state (HAS), achieving an exceptional balance between low dissipation (54 meV) and strong electric field enhancement (180‐fold). The structure can achieve room‐temperature Rabi splittings of 50 and 80 meV for HAS–exciton and HAS–plasmon couplings, respectively. When a plasmonic coupling channel is further introduced, the HAS, plasmon, and exciton form a tripartite coupled system. The resulting UP–LP splitting reaches approximately 330 meV, nearly six times that of the corresponding HAS–exciton system. The enhancement originates from the cooperative contribution of the additional plasmonic coupling channel and the enhanced local field of the hybrid cavity. The effective exciton numbers participating in the HAS–exciton and tripartite HAS–plasmon‐exciton systems are estimated to be only ∼125 and ∼6200, respectively, based on their characteristic excitonic volumes and the TMDs exciton Bohr radii. This mechanism provides an alternative route for achieving large light–matter interaction strengths without relying solely on increasing the exciton population, which is significantly superior to the results reported recently [35, 37, 45] under the same volume‐based estimation procedure. It thus presents a novel platform for designing ultracompact quantum photonic devices.

Results and Discussions

Design Principal: The Plasmon‐Enhanced HASs in Metal–Dielectric Nanocavities

According to the theoretical expression of the Rabi splitting energy under zero‐detuning conditions, that is, Ω=4g2−γ−γex2/4 achieving large Rabi splitting requires simultaneously enhancing the coupling strength (g ∝ E) and suppressing the radiative dissipation of the cavity mode [46]. To this end, we employ the three‐step strategy illustrated in Figure 1a to simultaneously engineer E and γ in a metal–dielectric nanostructure, including resonance coupling, field pulling, and field amplification. In the first step, under normal incidence of linearly polarized light, a magnetic quadrupole (MQ) and an anapole state are simultaneously excited in the slotted silicon disk–ring structure (System I). Their coherent interference gives rise to a MQ‐anapole state (MAS), which can be compatible with both small γ and large E to a certain extent. The air slot's length and width are L 1 = 260 nm and d = 10 nm, respectively. The MAS enables spatially uniform E enhancement (> 60 fold), distributed in the air slot of the all‐dielectric SDR (see Figure 1b‐I). However, the enhanced E remains predominantly confined inside the cavity and is difficult to efficiently extend into the free‐space region near the cavity surface.

In the second step, we introduced plasmonic resonances by symmetrically embedding a pair of thin (of 20 nm width) gold (Au) slabs along the long edges of the air slot (the Au‐SDR or System II; as in Figure 1a‐II inset). This configuration enhances the uniform E by approximately 70‐fold (Figure 1b‐II), approaching the enhancement level achievable in purely plasmonic resonances [16], while effectively pulling the strong E toward the structure surface. However, the large mode volume of dielectric cavities tends to decrease the average field intensity, thereby requiring high exciton densities for strong coupling, which limits the realization of room‐temperature single‐qubit quantum states. In the third step, we integrate short gold slabs (with L 2 = h = 10 nm and width 20 nm) atop the Au‐SDR structure (the hybrid cavity or System III, see the inset of Figure 1a‐III). This structure confines the fields to within a subwavelength volume, resulting in extreme local enhancement (Figure 1b‐III). Figure 1c illustrates the spatial evolution of the resonant field enhancement (with the coordinate origin at the SDR center) for three cases, that is, a uniform intracavity field, a surface‐enhanced field, and a strongly localized ultrahigh field, corresponding to systems I, II, and III, respectively. The progressive evolution of the near‐field distribution not only broadens the integration schemes between excitonic materials and cavities but also provides an effective route toward enhancing the coupling strength. The peak enhancement factors show remarkable increase, that is, E I = 66, E II = 74, and E III = 180‐fold (Figure 1d, lower panel). Notably, the hybrid cavity supporting a hybrid anapole state (HAS) can efficiently enhance the E as well as suppress γ (32, 46, and 54 meV for the systems I, II, and III, respectively. Figure 1d, upper panel). Although metal dissipation still causes damping in the visible region, it is much weaker than in pure metal systems. Therefore, this hybrid cavity can be utilized to lower the threshold conditions for realizing room‐temperature strong coupling and to enhance the Rabi splitting.

We focus on the hybrid cavity with R D = 230 nm (red circle in Figure 2a). Electric field enhancement distributions in the x–y plane (z = 0) cross‐section demonstrate counter‐rotating circular displacement currents at the up‐ and down‐sides of the disk‐ring (left panel, Figure 2b). These generate two magnetic dipoles (MD1 and MD2) oriented perpendicular to the disk‐ring surface (right panel, Figure 2b), forming parallel toroidal dipole moments (TD1 and TD2) aligned with the incident polarization direction, indicating the emergence of dual subradiant anapole states. Critically, the superposition of dual anapole states enhances the cavity's field confinement capability, amplifying local electric fields. Simultaneously, the antiparallel MD configuration excites a narrow‐linewidth magnetic quadrupole (MQ) mode that ensures low radiative damping of the HAS.

The HAS–Plasmon Bimodal Strong Coupling Systems

Although strong coupling between HAS‐supported hybrid cavities and excitons has been demonstrated, the coupling strength remains limited. We further propose an enhanced metal‐dielectric hybrid cavity (Figure 3a) comprising an Au‐SDR integrated with 13 pairs of gold slabs to amplify coupling via plasmonic mode hybridization. Compared with the hybrid cavity in Figure 1, the enhanced‐hybrid cavity increases the proportion of the plasmon modes and broadens the area of electric field enhancement, achieving strong coupling between the HAS and plasmon at room temperature. To probe the HAS–plasmon interactions, we systematically tuned the resonance detuning (ΔAP=ωHA−ωpl) while monitoring scattering spectra (Figure 3b). The hybrid UP (red solid curve) and LP (blue solid curve) results in pronounced anticrossing behavior, confirming the formation of HAS–plasmon polaritons. Similarly, the simulated spectrum under the zero‐detuning condition (R D = 212 nm) was analyzed using the coupled oscillator model, finding g AP = 44.1 meV for the coupling strength between the HAS and the plasmon and 85 meV for the Rabi splitting. The plasmon resonance frequency and radiation damping are ωpl = 1.66 meV and γpl = 101 meV, respectively. The ωpl is independently determined from the absorption spectrum of the uncoupled 13‐pair Au‐slab subsystem and then used as the plasmonic reference energy in the SCS and coupled‐oscillator analysis. The strong coupling splitting between the HAS and the plasmon mode is not vanishing and should be experimentally observable. The physical origins of the enhanced coherent coupling in HAS–plasmon systems are subsequently examined. We quantify the far‐ and near‐field properties of HAS–plasmon polaritons. Figure 3e presents the center point electric field enhancement of the enhanced‐hybrid cavity at the z = 0 (blue) and z = 36 nm (orange) planes as well as SCS (red) and ACS (olive) at zero detuning. As emphasized in literature [35], concurrent anticrossing in scattering and absorption (or PL) spectra are essential for confirming strong coupling. We confirm the coupling of HAS–plasmon enter the strong coupling regime using the ACS with Rabi splitting and its anticrossing behavior (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S3a). Furthermore, the Rabi splitting behavior in the near‐field spectra at different positions also provides strong evidence for the strong coupling effect of the HAS–plasmon. The corresponding behavior of the electric field enhancement in the z = 0 (blue) and z = 36 nm (orange) planes is due to the transition of the polarization state of the HAS–plasmon from HAS‐dominant to plasmon‐dominant. Compared to the HAS–exciton system, the polarization state of the HAS–plasmon not only broadens the bandwidth of the local field enhancement but also expands the spatial coverage of high‐field regions. As demonstrated in Figure 3f, the near‐field distributions provide ∼80‐fold enhancement across a 50 × 260 nm2 area in the x–y (z = 36 nm) and y–z planes. This configuration dramatically increases the possibility of cavity‐exciton alignment, enabling substantial increase of the coupling strength.

Introducing plasmon–dielectric cavity coupling into the hybrid nanocavity, namely the formation of a plasmon‐enhanced HAS, can significantly strengthen the cavity photon–exciton interaction. Notably, the Rabi splitting observed in the three‐oscillator system (plasmon–HAS–exciton) is substantially larger than that in either of the corresponding two‐oscillator systems, namely HAS–exciton (56 meV) and HAS–plasmon (85 meV). This enhancement arises from the combined effects of increased local field intensity and the additional coupling channel provided by the plasmonic mode. Based on the optically participating WSe2 volume of approximately 1.3 × 104 nm3, the corresponding effective number of coherently participating excitons N eff is estimated to be ∼3.1 × 103.

Finally, to further enhance the spatial overlap between excitons and localized fields (a critical factor governing coupling strength), we designed a strengthened Si−Au−WSe2 heterostructure comprising of an enhanced‐hybrid cavity and a 10 × 260 × 10 nm3 WSe2 bulk placed in the gap region of 13 pairs of gold slabs. As shown in Figure 4d, enhanced interactions among HAS, plasmon, and exciton resonances generate triply hybridized states (UP, MP, and LP) exhibiting a giant Rabi splitting (ℏΩUP−LP ∼330 meV). Additionally, the anticrossing behavior in the ACS spectrum is also demonstrated (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S3b). With g AP = 44.1 meV fixed, the coupling strengths of HAS–plasmon and HAS–exciton were fitted to be g AX = 145 meV and g PX = 40 meV, respectively. Zero‐detuning serves as a critical reference condition for identifying the anticrossing characteristics of conventional two‐resonance strong coupling systems [55, 56]. Different from the two‐resonance strong coupling framework, the enhanced tripartite structure is designed such that the HAS remains at finite detuning from the plasmonic (ΔAP = ω HA–ω pl = 130 meV) and excitonic resonances (ΔAX = ω HA–ω ex = 160 meV) over the specific parameter range (R D = 160–250 nm), which in turn reduces the visual prominence of the anticrossing behavior. Nevertheless, the coupling strengths are sufficiently large to produce pronounced three‐resonance hybridization. As a result, the three basis states are strongly hybridized, giving rise to nearly symmetric triple splitting rather than weak perturbative shifts (See the inset in Figure 4d). The SCS map provides the spectral signature of the hybridized resonances, whereas the anticrossing behavior is identified from the polaritonic branches dispersion and further supported by the Hopfield coefficients.

The fitted dispersion curves (solid curve) of the three hybrid states of the current system and the simulation data (UP, red cubes, MP, black spheres, and LP, blue triangles) are presented in Figure 4e, and they show consistency. The detuning‐dependent calculation provides the polaritonic branches evolution of the 3 × 3 Hamiltonian as the bare HAS resonance energy is varied. Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrate that the combination of plasmon resonances and HASs can significantly enhance the electric field confinement capability of optical microcavities while preserving low radiative dissipation of HASs, thereby boosting photon–exciton coupling strength and lowering the strong coupling threshold. Specifically, the proposed HAS–plasmon–exciton hybrid system achieves a giant Rabi splitting of 330 meV. The metal–dielectric hybrid cavity supports up to 180‐fold local electric field enhancement while maintaining a low dissipative damping of 54 meV through plasmon‐enhanced HASs. In addition, the system enables the realization of dual‐branch polaritonic states, including HAS–exciton and HAS–plasmon polaritons as well as ternary HAS–plasmon–exciton polaritons. Based on the characteristic Bohr radius of WSe2 excitons, the effective numbers of excitons participating in the coupling are estimated to be only 6205 for the HAS–plasmon–exciton system and 125 for the HAS–exciton system, both significantly lower than those reported in previous dielectric cavity–exciton strong coupling studies under the same volume‐based estimation procedure. These results establish the proposed coupled architecture as a promising platform for exploring strong light–matter interactions and provide a new route toward cavity–multiexciton polaritonic systems in quantum photonics.

Methods

Finite‐difference time‐domain (FDTD) simulations are performed for far‐field and near‐field calculations. Material parameters for Si, Au, and WSe2 are obtained from published datasets. The mesh volumes are configured as follows: 5 × 5 × 5 nm3 for the Au‐SDR cavity, 2 × 2 × 2 nm3 for the air slot, 1 × 1 × 1 nm3 for the WSe2 nanosphere/cube, and 4 × 2 × 0.1 nm3 for the WSe2 monolayer with a simulation domain mesh volume of 4 × 4 × 2 μm3. The scattering cross‐sections, absorption cross‐sections, and electric field enhancements are calculated using a total‐field/scattered‐field (TFSF) source spanning 400–1200 nm.
