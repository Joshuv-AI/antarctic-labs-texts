The Prevalence and Risk Factors for at‐Risk MASH and Advanced Liver Fibrosis in People With Metabolic Risk Factors in Primary Care
Authors: Leon A Adams, Shaoke Lei, Luis Calzadilla‐Bertot, Silja Schrader, Sophie Frear, Chian Zhou, Sarah E Heywood, Jon Emery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe majority of the burden of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) exists in primary care; however, the prevalence of severe disease in patients with metabolic dysfunction remains poorly described.MethodsA cross‐sectional study of 84 general practices located in Victoria, Australia identified patients with metabolic dysfunction over a 12 month period from the PATRON data repository. At‐risk MASH was defined using the Fibrotic NASH Index (FNI) based on laboratory data. Patients needing further liver assessment were defined by FIB‐4 ≥ 1.3 (≥ 2.0 if 65+ years) and = < 2.67. Advanced liver fibrosis was defined as a FIB‐4 of > 2.67.Results110,938 individuals were identified with metabolic dysfunction. Of those with a FNI available (n = 16,586), the prevalence of At‐risk MASH was 24.3%. Among those with a FIB‐4 available (n = 64,948), 16.3% required further liver assessment, while the prevalence of advanced fibrosis was 4.3%. On multivariate analysis, male sex, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension were associated with At‐risk MASH, whereas male sex and type 2 diabetes were associated with advanced liver fibrosis. Socio‐economic disadvantage was a risk factor for both At‐risk MASH and advanced liver fibrosis.ConclusionsSignificant liver disease was observed in a substantial proportion of patients with metabolic dysfunction within primary care, particularly among patients with type 2 diabetes, supporting the rationale for risk stratification. Associations with socio‐economic disadvantage highlight the need for societal and public health policy change to improve liver outcomes.Keywords: epidemiology, fatty liver, risk stratification, screening, socio‐economic disadvantage, type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disease (CLD) globally affecting approximately one in three adults [1]. Significantly, MASLD has emerged as a leading cause of cirrhosis and its complications of liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2]. In addition, patients with MASLD have an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes and future cardiovascular disease [3, 4]. The risk of future complications increases with the severity of the underlying liver disease, highlighting a need to identify the subset of MASLD patients at most risk [3, 4, 5]. Specifically, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is the inflammatory form of MASLD, portending a higher risk of disease progression and future cardio‐metabolic disease. Liver fibrosis is the dominant factor that predicts outcomes in patients with MASLD, with the onset of advanced (F3‐4) liver fibrosis signifying a significantly increased risk for the development of HCC and decompensation [5, 6]. Patients with MASH and significant (F2+) fibrosis (termed ‘At‐risk MASH’) are also at increased risk of liver‐related outcomes and represent the target population for clinical trials in MASLD and for approved pharmacotherapy [7, 8].

The majority of patients with At‐risk MASH and/or advanced liver fibrosis only encounter medical care in general practice, but the rates of identification remain low and patients are commonly diagnosed when they present with advanced disease [9]. Despite calls to increase the diagnostic rate of At‐risk MASH in the community, the prevalence and risk factor profile of patients with At‐risk MASH in primary care remains unknown [10, 11]. Surprisingly, the prevalence of advanced liver fibrosis related to metabolic dysfunction in primary care is also uncertain, with estimated prevalence rates varying from 4.8% to 19% [11].

Determining the prevalence of At‐risk MASH and advanced fibrosis in primary care populations is the key initial step to raise awareness and thus facilitate the identification of At‐risk patients and instigation of management to reduce future disease‐related morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, understanding the real‐world disease burden has clear implications for assessing health inequalities, guiding health policy and resource allocation including the funding of pharmacotherapeutic interventions.

This study aimed to provide real‐world insights into the prevalence of At‐risk MASH and advanced liver fibrosis in a large primary care population with metabolic risk factors. In doing so, we sought to address critical gaps in the understanding of the epidemiology and clinical recognition of MASLD and MASH in general practice.

Methods

Study Design and Population

A cross‐sectional study of patients attending primary care practices within the state of Victoria, Australia during the year 2019 was undertaken. This time period was chosen to avoid the effect of prolonged lock‐down episodes related to COVID‐19 that occurred in the state of Victoria from March 2020 to October 2021 and impacted patient health‐care utilization [12]. Patients were identified from the general practice electronic medical records (EMR) obtained from the University of Melbourne Primary Care Audit, Teaching and Research Open Network (PATRON) repository [13], and the Victorian Admitted Episodes Dataset (VAED) [14]. PATRON contains routinely collected de‐identified EMR data on 1.8 million unique patients covering more than 130 general practice clinics, which is approximately 6% of all general practice clinics in Victoria. Practices within the PATRON dataset cover metropolitan, regional and rural areas and patients are broadly representative of the general Victorian population [15]. Eighty‐four general practices from PATRON who consented for their data to be used for collaborative research between industry and academic institutions were included in this study. PATRON data includes a variety of data fields; this study primarily used data relating to patients' clinic visits and laboratory data including medical history, observations and measurements taken, medication prescribed, and pathology results. VAED contains data on all admitted patient episodes in public and private hospitals in Victoria. Linkage with datasets from the PATRON and VAED was facilitated through the Centre for Victorian Data Linkage (CVDL) [16]. Ethics approval was provided by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (Ref: 2025–23,718–71,849‐19).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

A 1 year sample of patients who had any encounter record in PATRON was ascertained between January and December 2019. Within this cohort, adult patients (> 18 years of age) with metabolic dysfunction were identified by any of the following criteria documented within the 1 year period [17].

Recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D) or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥ 11.0 mmol/L or haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% or use of diabetic medication (Table S1).

Recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D) or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥ 11.0 mmol/L or haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% or use of diabetic medication (Table S1).

Recorded diagnosis of non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), or metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).

Recorded diagnosis of non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), metabolic dysfunction‐associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), or metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).

Recorded diagnosis of overweight or obesity or body mass index (BMI) > 25 kg/m2 (Caucasian) or > 23 kg/m2 (Asian).

Recorded diagnosis of overweight or obesity or body mass index (BMI) > 25 kg/m2 (Caucasian) or > 23 kg/m2 (Asian).

Any two of the following: waist circumference ≥ 102/88 cm in Caucasian men and women (or ≥ 90/80 cm in Asian men and women); systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg or a recorded diagnosis of hypertension; Plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L or recorded diagnosis of dyslipidemia; recorded diagnosis of prediabetes or fasting glucose levels 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L, or 2 h post‐load glucose levels 7.8 to 11.0 mmol or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%.

Any two of the following: waist circumference ≥ 102/88 cm in Caucasian men and women (or ≥ 90/80 cm in Asian men and women); systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg or a recorded diagnosis of hypertension; Plasma triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L or recorded diagnosis of dyslipidemia; recorded diagnosis of prediabetes or fasting glucose levels 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L, or 2 h post‐load glucose levels 7.8 to 11.0 mmol or HbA1c 5.7%–6.4%.

Diagnoses of MASLD were identified through ICD‐10‐AM codes (Table S2) recorded in patients' admitted episodes in VAED.

Exclusion criteria consisted of concomitant causes of liver disease and alcohol‐related diagnoses (Table S2) determined by VAED diagnosis records within 2019.

Determination of At‐Risk MASH

Within the group of patients with metabolic dysfunction, the prevalence of At‐risk MASH, defined as the presence of F2+ fibrosis and steatohepatitis, was determined using the Fibrotic NASH Index (FNI). The FNI is an algorithm consisting of serum AST, HbA1c, and HDL‐cholesterol levels which has been independently validated to predict biopsy‐proven MASH and is predictive of the development of severe liver disease (cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver transplantation or liver related death) [18, 19]. Blood test results for AST, HbA1c, and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) obtained within 30 days of each other were used to calculate FNI. In the case of multiple FNI scores during the one‐year ascertainment period, the result closest to the earliest date of recorded metabolic dysfunction was used. Result values above 300 IU/L for AST were not included to avoid false‐positive results from confounding disease [18].

Determination of Advanced Fibrosis

Within the group of patients with metabolic dysfunction, the prevalence of advanced liver fibrosis was determined using the Fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4) index. FIB‐4 has good accuracy for the determination of advanced fibrosis in MASLD with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.76–0.84 [20, 21, 22]. Blood test results on the same day for aspartate transferase (AST), platelet count (PLT), and alanine transaminase (ALT) were used for calculating FIB‐4 score. In cases where multiple FIB‐4 scores were obtained for the same patient during the one‐year ascertainment period, the result closest to the patient's earliest date of recorded metabolic dysfunction was used. Result values above 300 IU/L for AST and ALT, and below 20 or above 400 for PLT were not included in FIB‐4 calculations to avoid the results being skewed by clinical outliers and confounding medical conditions [23].

Determination of Prevalence of At‐Risk MASH and Advanced Fibrosis

The prevalence of At‐risk MASH was determined in adults with metabolic dysfunction with an available FNI score, using an FNI cut‐off of ≥ 0.30. A sensitivity analysis was performed using two cut‐offs: > 0.10 (90% sensitive for At‐risk MASH) and ≥ 0.33 (90% specific for At‐risk MASH) [18].

The prevalence of advanced fibrosis was determined in adults with metabolic dysfunction with an available FIB‐4 value as the denominator, using a FIB‐4 cut‐off of > 2.67. The prevalence of patients with metabolic dysfunction requiring further evaluation for liver disease according to international guidelines was defined as those with a FIB‐4 ≥ 1.3 in those less than 65 years of age and ≥ 2.0 in those ≥ 65 years of age [24, 25, 26].

Patient Cohort

Figure 1 outlines the derivation of the cohort. Of 329,716 patients initially identified within the PATRON cohort with at least one encounter recorded in 2019, 110,693 remained after exclusion of those without any metabolic dysfunction and cases with concomitant causes of liver disease and alcohol‐related diagnosis. Of these 110,693 patients who met our inclusion criteria, 64,948 and 16,586 had FIB‐4 and FNI scores available. Demographic data were obtained from the PATRON database at the time of the encounter with recorded metabolic dysfunction conditions. They included patient age, sex, geographical remoteness classification based on location of residence [27] and Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage (IRSD) [28]. The IRSD index uses census data including information on income, education, employment, occupation, housing and family structure to summarize the socio‐economic characteristics of an area and is a broad measure of disadvantage. People with a low IRSD score have relatively greater disadvantage compared to those with a high IRSD score.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were described and compared between those with and without advanced fibrosis or At‐risk MASH. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared tests, and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. To examine factors associated with each outcome, two separate multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted. For each analysis, variable selection was performed using backward elimination guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). A p < 0.05 was considered significant. All datasets were cleaned, managed, and analysed using R version 4.3.1b (R Core Team, 2023), within the secure virtual environment of the Victorian Data Access Linkage Trust (VALT).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The prevalence of metabolic dysfunction among the PATRON cohort was 33.6% (110,693/329,716). Among the 64,948 patients with metabolic dysfunction and a FIB‐4 score available, the median (IQR) age was 61 (48, 72) years with a slight female predominance (53%), as outlined in Table 1. Among the 16,586 patients with metabolic dysfunction and an available FNI score, the median (IQR) age was 63 (52, 72) years with an even sex distribution. Approximately two‐thirds of patients in both cohorts resided in major cities, and 22% of both cohorts were classified as being socio‐economically disadvantaged defined as the 1st quintile of the IRSD. The prevalence of having overweight or obesity was 39%–41% within the cohorts. The prevalence of type 2 diabetes was higher in the FNI cohort (39%) compared with the FIB‐4 cohort (19%).

Prevalence of At‐Risk MASH and Advanced Fibrosis

Among patients with metabolic dysfunction, the prevalence of At‐risk MASH (FNI > 0.30) was 24.3% (4038/16,586) shown in Figure 2. Sensitivity analyses using a FNI threshold of > 0.10 (90% sensitivity) demonstrated a prevalence of At‐risk MASH of 60.8%, whereas a threshold of ≥ 0.33 (90% specificity) demonstrated a prevalence of 22.0%.

A and B. Prevalence of At‐Risk MASH and Advanced Fibrosis in Patients with Metabolic Dysfunction in Primary Care.

The prevalence of advanced fibrosis (FIB‐4 > 2.67) in the cohort was 4.3% (2821/64,948). One in six patients (16.3%) had an intermediate FIB‐4 score (≥ 1.3 if < 65 years, ≥ 2.0 if 65+ years, but < 2.67) which would require further evaluation to exclude advanced liver fibrosis.

Among patients who had both FNI and FIB‐4 performed (n = 15,396), the prevalence of At‐Risk MASH (FNI > 0.30) was 24.1%, the prevalence of an intermediate FIB‐4 score was 17.8%, and the prevalence of advanced fibrosis (FIB‐4 > 2.67) was 4.5%.

Characteristics of At‐Risk MASH Patients

The prevalence of At‐risk MASH was greater in males than females (30.3% v 18.3%, p < 0.001) and peaked in the 50–64 year age category (outlined in Table 2). Patients who were most socially disadvantaged had the highest prevalence of At‐risk MASH (25%) compared with those who were least disadvantaged (IRSD quintile 5) who had a prevalence of 15.5%. The prevalence was also significantly higher in patients who had overweight/obesity (28.7%), or those with type 2 diabetes (50.2%) or hypertension (25.0%, p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Sensitivity analyses using different FNI thresholds illustrated similar relationships between those with and without At‐risk MASH.

Discussion

In a large cohort of patients in primary care, we show that 24% of individuals with metabolic dysfunction have At‐risk MASH, 16% meet the FIB‐4 threshold for further liver assessment, and 4% have advanced liver fibrosis. Patient factors including male sex and age were independently associated with At‐risk MASH and advanced fibrosis, whereas type 2 diabetes was the strongest metabolic factor that was associated with these outcomes. Notably, socio‐economic disadvantage was associated with both At‐risk MASH and advanced liver fibrosis, highlighting the impact of social determinants of health on the severity of MASLD.

Whilst MASLD is common in primary care and has significant health implications including the potential development of cirrhosis and HCC, there is a significant under‐recognition of the more severe spectrum of disease in general practice [9, 29, 30]. Using validated non‐invasive scores based on the results of readily available blood tests, we have quantified the At‐risk populations in primary care whose identification has direct management implications. The importance of identifying patients with At‐risk MASH is highlighted by the recent approval of resmetirom and semaglutide in the United States as effective treatment options for this population [7, 8, 31, 32]. In our study, we observed nearly one in twenty patients as having advanced liver fibrosis defined by a high (> 2.67) FIB‐4, which is associated with a 17‐fold increased risk of incident severe liver disease in the general population [33]. Consequently, these patients are recommended for referral for specialist review and management [24, 25, 26, 34]. Sixteen percent of patients in our study had an intermediate (> 1.3 if < 65 years, > 2.0 if 65+ years, but < 2.67) FIB‐4 score, which signifies a modestly increased risk of adverse liver outcomes, and are recommended for further assessment with liver stiffness measurement in order to refine prognosis and need for specialist review [25, 34].

The prevalence of advanced fibrosis (FIB‐4 > 2.67) in our cohort was slightly higher than that found in a recent multi‐centre European liverscreen cohort based on a liver stiffness of > = 10 kPa (4% vs. 2.5% respectively) [35] Similarly, a meta‐analysis of 46 studies found the global prevalence of advanced fibrosis to be 3.3%, which is slightly lower than in our cohort [36] This reflects that our population was derived from patients attending primary care who were selected on the basis of metabolic risk factors, rather than an unselected ‘healthy’ population‐based cohort. In contrast, prospective cohort studies have demonstrated a higher prevalence of advanced fibrosis in patients with MASLD who were overweight/obese or had type 2 diabetes at 10.8%–14%, respectively [37, 38] These differences may relate to the different methods used to determine advanced fibrosis (FIB‐4 versus elastography) with differing specificities at the thresholds used.

The prevalence of At‐risk MASH in our population (24.4%) is higher than that found in the NHANES population (5.8%) and the UK Biobank (2.2%) [39, 40]. Notably, the prevalence of At‐Risk MASH increased to 22.5% in the NHANES population with type 2 diabetes and 16% in a prospective evaluation of 530 patients with type 2 diabetes, affirming our findings that it is an important risk factor for the occurrence of more severe liver disease [41]. It is important to note that the requirement for the routine ordering of the components of the FNI and FIB‐4 is likely to introduce selection bias and enrich the population towards those with more significant and recognized metabolic disease. Nonetheless, the high prevalence of At‐risk MASH in our cohort of patients in primary care supports a rationale to screen those with metabolic dysfunction.

Our findings have highlighted the association between At‐risk MASH, advanced liver fibrosis and socio‐economic disadvantage. Upstream determinants of health outcomes which include income, education, housing and residential environment, are established predictive factors of alcohol and obesity related disease [42]. Socio‐economic disadvantage is associated with higher rates of cirrhosis diagnosis and social determinants of health are predictive of all‐cause mortality among people with MASLD [43, 44]. Living outside of metropolitan areas is associated with reduced access to health care, lower likelihood of liver transplantation and higher liver‐related and HCC mortality rates [45, 46]. Highlighting the increased prevalence of At‐risk MASH and advanced liver fibrosis in patients who are socio‐economically disadvantaged is the first step in addressing the complex determinants of poor liver health. At a patient level, clinicians should be aware of the increased risk of more advanced liver disease and be attuned to the barriers to implementing health care in these populations [47].

The prevalence of advanced liver fibrosis in our population was based on a FIB‐4 of > 2.67 which has similar specificity when compared to a Fibroscan liver stiffness cut‐off of 15 kPa [48]. The Liver Risk Score is more accurate than FIB‐4 in identifying elevated liver stiffness; however, it has not been as extensively validated as FIB‐4 for the prediction of biopsy proven advanced fibrosis [49]. Interestingly, we found age to be positively associated with advanced fibrosis but inversely associated with At‐Risk MASH, which mirrors observations in the UK Biobank [40]. This may relate to survival bias in this cross‐sectional cohort, or reflect the reduction in hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and ballooning which tends to occur as liver fibrosis progresses with age [50, 51].

This study has several strengths, including its inclusion of a large number of patients across the socio‐economic spectrum within primary care. The limitations of this study must also be acknowledged. Case finding of patients with metabolic dysfunction was reliant on documentation within the electronic medical record. It is likely that there was under‐coding of metabolic dysfunction, and thus the target population may be larger. It is also possible that patients with moderate alcohol consumption (MetALD) were included within the cohort. In addition, not all individuals had covariates for FIB‐4 or FNI available, leading to selection bias. In particular, the requirement for HbA1c as a component of the FNI led to the cohort population being enriched with type 2 diabetes, which is likely to have increased the prevalence of at‐risk MASH. Nonetheless, this represents real‐world practice, and the cohort epitomizes the population which is currently identifiable and being managed in primary care who are at risk and warrant liver‐directed assessment.

The burden of MASLD resides predominantly in primary care and is largely unrecognized. Among patients who have metabolic risk factors including overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia and hypertension, the prevalence of significant liver disease is high; more than one in four patients have At‐risk MASH, one in six require further evaluation for significant liver disease, and nearly one in 20 have advanced liver fibrosis. Recognition of these patients is valuable to support guideline‐based screening and referral recommendations, identify those who are eligible for pharmacotherapy, as well as those who require further assessment or surveillance for future liver complications. The identification of socio‐economic disadvantage as a risk factor for advanced liver disease in patients with metabolic dysfunction needs to stimulate societal and health policy change to address health inequalities and improve liver outcomes.
