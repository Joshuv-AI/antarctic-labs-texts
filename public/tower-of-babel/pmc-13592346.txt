Genetic Sequencing in Saudi Patients with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Ibrahim A Al-Homood, Sarah Binhassan, Khalid AlMatham, Lena M Hassen, Manar Samman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify novel candidate gene variants and investigate their role in a cohort of Saudi patients with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE). In addition, evaluate their distribution relative to common risk loci identified in the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog.MethodsWhole Exome Sequencing (WES) was performed on the extracted genomic DNA for 29 patients with confirmed SLE. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize the patients’ characteristics. Variant prioritization was initiated through a manual clinical interpretation according to ACMG guidelines, followed by a multi-step filtering pipeline to identify rare, high-impact candidates (gnomAD MAF < 1%, CADD > 20). The statistical analysis was conducted using R software R version 4.4.2 and several specialised packages.ResultsThe analysis revealed an exploratory “multi-hit” genetic landscape. Notable candidate variants were identified in genes related to DNA damage response (ATM, BRCA1) and membrane integrity (DYSF, USH2A). NPFFR2 emerged as a recurrently variant candidate gene (24% of the cohort). Phenotypic partitioning suggested that NPFFR2 variant may be associated with a distinct neuro-musculoskeletal profile; 85.7% of carriers exhibited musculoskeletal involvement. Conversely, carriers showed a marginal inverse trend with oral ulcers (P = 0.093), and NPFFR2 variant showed a potential trend toward mutual exclusivity with haematological involvement (P = 0.066), suggesting carriers may be less likely to present with either mucocutaneous or haematological manifestations. These findings suggest that SLE clinical heterogeneity may be influenced by specific genetic endotypes. Our observations open the door for a preliminary roadmap for precision medicine and the identification of potentially treatment-refractory patients.Keywords: systemic lupus erythematosus, SLE, whole exome sequencing, WES, NPFFR2 variant, drug resistance

Introduction

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a debilitating, chronic autoimmune disease characterized by profound clinical heterogeneity, ranging from mild mucocutaneous symptoms to severe, life-threatening organ involvement, such as lupus nephritis and neuropsychiatric SLE.1–3 This complexity suggests that SLE is not a single disease but a spectrum of endotypes driven by distinct molecular mechanisms.4 While environmental factors play a role, geneticsare paramount, with heritability estimates ranging between 44% and 66%.5

Over the past decade, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified over 100 common genetic susceptibility loci for SLE.6 These common variants, however, typically possess small effect sizes and collectively explain only a fraction of the total heritability, leaving a significant gap known as missing heritability.7 This limitation highlights the necessity of exploring alternative genetic architectures, specifically focusing on rare variants which are often under strong purifying selection and carry high penetrance. Whole exome sequencing (WES) provides a powerful, unbiased approach to identifying these rare variants.8,9 By focusing on germline variants, WES can uncover primary genetic drivers that may define unique, potentially treatable subgroups of SLE patients.

In this study, we employed WES coupled with a rigorous variant prioritization strategy based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines10 to analyse a cohort of 29 SLE patients. Our primary aims were to: 1) characterize the landscape of rare germline variants (Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, and Variants of Unknown significance VUS) in this SLE cohort; 2) statistically validate our methodology by assessing the overlap with known GWAS loci; and 3) correlate the identified variants with key clinical features, including organ involvement and therapeutic response, to explore novel genotype-phenotype relationships. Our results highlight the potential role of a rare variant architecture in SLE heterogeneity and hypothesise the role of NPFFR2 as novel candidate connecting the neuro-immune axis to specific clinical outcomes.

Methodology

Study Participants and Inclusion Criteria

This study utilized a retrospective cohort of N=29 patients diagnosed with SLE recruited from King Fahd Medical City (KFMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All patients met the revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR) or Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) classification criteria for SLE at the time of enrolment. Exclusion criteria included active lupus nephritis; known secondary autoimmune disorders and a prior history of organ transplantation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical Data Collection

The clinical data and specimens were collected under appropriate Institutional review board (IRB) approvals from KFMC institutional ethical committee, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Comprehensive clinical data were systematically collected from electronic health records and patient interviews, focusing on disease features at the time of enrolment and over the disease course. Key clinical variables included: demographic information (age, sex), residency, treatment, and major organ system involvement (renal, neuropsychiatric, haematological, musculoskeletal, and cutaneous) as defined by SLICC criteria. Therapeutic response was classified, with drug resistance defined as failure to achieve or maintain disease control (eg, SLEDAI ≤ 4) despite at least six months of standard care including immunosuppressive therapy (eg, high-dose glucocorticoids, azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, or mycophenolate mofetil).

Sample Collection and DNA Extraction

Peripheral blood samples (approximately 5 mL) were collected from each participant in EDTA tubes following standard phlebotomy protocols. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was subsequently extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the (Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit), (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration was measured by spectrophotometry using the Nanodrop ND 1000 (Nano Drop, UNISCIENCE, USA) to ensure sufficient material and integrity for subsequent WES library preparation.

Whole Exome Sequencing (WES)

WES was performed on the extracted gDNA samples by an external sequencing institution. Testing was performed according to the institution’s established laboratory standard protocol. Briefly, genomic DNA was fragmented and prepared for sequencing using a library construction protocol consistent with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Target enrichment was performed using the Roche KAPA Hyper Exome probe suite (Roche Sequencing Solutions, Pleasanton, CA, USA), targeting all protein-coding exons and flanking intronic splice-site boundaries. Following hybridization and capture, the enriched libraries were sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China), utilizing a high-throughput paired-end sequencing strategy. The quality control index of sequencing data was achieved with average sequencing depth of the target area of 200X, and the proportion of > 95% of the sites with the average depth of the target area > 20X.

Bioinformatics Analyses

Variant calling raw sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA; Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute, Cambridge, UK) to remove duplicates. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (INDELs) and genotype discovery, as well as base quality refinement, were conducted using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK; Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA). Copy Number Variation (CNV) analysis was conducted at the exon level using the Exome Depth R package (University College London, London, UK).

Variant interpretation followed the standards established by the Human Genome Organization Gene Naming Committee (HGNC; European Bioinformatics Institute, Hinxton, UK) for gene symbols and the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) for variant names.

As WES was primarily conducted within a hospital setting for clinical diagnostic purposes, The pathogenicity of variants was classified using Qiagen Clinical Insight (QCI) Variants (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the Standards and Guidelines for the Interpretation of Sequence Variants of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) Genomics (ACMG; Bethesda, MD, USA). According to these guidelines, sequence variants are categorised into five classes: i) Pathogenic, ii) Likely Pathogenic, iii) Variant of Unknown Significance (VUS), iv) Likely benign, and v) Benign. In the context of this study, these classifications refer to the strength of the evidence scores within the ACMG framework; for novel candidate genes, these labels denote research interest.

Variant Prioritization and Filtering

To identify high-priority candidates, a multi-step filtering cascade was implemented, beginning with the application of the ACMG guidelines to categorize variants according to established clinical pathogenicity criteria. This primary assessment by a multidisciplinary team of molecular scientists and clinicians ensured that the interpretation was grounded in recognized medical standards. Subsequently, rare variants were isolated by excluding common polymorphisms based on a Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) threshold of <1% in the gnomAD database. To ensure the relevance of findings to our specific study group, we prioritized variants appearing with a frequency of ≥ 7% within our 29-patient cohort, framing these as exploratory candidates for this regional population. Finally, protein-altering variants were filtered based on predicted deleteriousness using a CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion) scaled score cutoff of >20 (Table S1). The HLA-DRB1 variant was retained as an exception to the <1% gnomAD MAF threshold (gnomAD AF = 10.5%) because of its well-established status as a canonical SLE risk locus, and it is presented separately as a positive methodological control rather than as a novel rare-variant candidate; all other variants reported met the pre-specified rarity criteria.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of the 29 patients with SLE. Continuous variables, including age and disease duration, were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. The genomic analysis was conducted using R software R version 4.4.2 and several specialised packages. Data processing and manipulation were performed using the tidyverse and dplyr packages. The variation landscape of the cohort was characterised and visualised using the maftools package. A heatmap plot was generated to display the overall variant frequency and the distribution of specific gene variants across individual patients.

To identify associations between gene variations and clinical features, a clinical enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test. This test was applied to compare the frequency of variations in patient groups stratified by clinical characteristics (eg, presence or absence of ulcers, drug resistance. etc). Odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to estimate the strength of these associations. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction method.

The statistical significance of co-occurring and mutually exclusive gene variants was also evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Furthermore, the set of variant genes identified in this cohort was cross-referenced with the GWAS Catalog database to determine the overlap with previously established SLE-associated genes, a process facilitated by the gwasrapidd package. Data were retrieved on September 14, 2025, ensuring the inclusion of the most recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) findings. For all statistical comparisons and enrichment tests, a p-value or False Discovery Rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The investigation into family history revealed that 32.1% (n = 9/28) of the patients had a first-degree relative affected by an autoimmune disorder, while 25.0% (n = 7/28) specifically reported a family history of SLE. The geographical distribution showed that the highest proportion of subjects resided in the Central region 41.4% (n= 12/29), followed by the Southern region 27.6% (n= 8/29). Lesser proportions were observed in the Northern 13.8%, Western 10.3%, and Eastern 6.9% regions.

Most of the patients 79.3% (n= 23/29) were receiving Hydroxychloroquine at the time of assessment. Other immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory treatments included Prednisolone 27.6% (n= 8/29) and Azathioprine 17.2% (n= 5/29). The biological agent Rituximab was administered to 3.4% (n= 1/29) of the patients. Importantly, drug resistance was noted in 10.3% (n= 3/29) of the subjects.

Analysis of the disease characteristics demonstrated that musculoskeletal involvement was the most common clinical phenotype, affecting 75.9% (n= 22/29) of the patients. This was closely followed by cutaneous involvement, observed in 69.0% (n= 20/29) of the cohort. Other frequent manifestations included ulcers 44.8% (n=13/29) and fever 44.8% (n= 13/29). In contrast, more severe organ system involvement, such as renal involvement (n= 1/29), pericarditis (n= 1/29), and cardiovascular involvement (n= 1/29), occurred at a lower frequency of 3.4% each.

Correlation with Clinical Characteristics

The bottom annotation panel (Figure 1) details the clinical characteristics for each patient, including neuropsychiatric, haematological, renal, musculoskeletal, and cutaneous involvement, drug resistance, and age group (≤30, 31–45, > 45).

Analysis of the variant landscape against patient phenotypes suggest several potential correlations. Drug resistance occurred in patients P05 and P25 ≤30 years old with VUS. It was also found in an older patient P 31–45 years old P14 who exhibited two variants in NPFFR2 and BUB1. Cutaneous involvement was observed in the majority of patients, where four of them had NPFFR2 variant P11 with KMT2C and P13 with IGSF3 additional variants.

Musculoskeletal involvement was reported in several patients, including 6/7 with NPFFR2 P13, P20, P38, P14, P21 and P11. Although ulcers were seen in 13 patients, only one P21 harboured NPFFR2 variant. One patient P41 had renal involvement with RECQL4 variant. Haematological involvement was reported in both patients P29 and P41 with Likely Pathogenic variants, in addition to other patients who had VUS or no variation. Two patients with neuropsychiatric involvement P8 with no variants and P21 with NPFFR2, the other two had only VUS of HLA-DRB1.

Genotype-Phenotype Correlation Enrichment

We performed a quantitative enrichment analysis using Fisher’s exact test on the two most recurrently variant genes HLA-DRB1 and NPFFR2 to statistically assess their association with clinical features (Table 2). These two genes were selected because they were the only candidates with sufficient within-cohort recurrence (HLA-DRB1, 41%; NPFFR2, 24%) to populate a 2×2 contingency table; the remaining 20 candidate genes were each identified in only one or two patients, which precluded meaningful Fisher’s exact testing for these genes. The analysis compared the variation status (variant vs wild-type) against the presence or absence of each clinical feature. Due to the exploratory small sample size (N=29), the 95% confidence intervals for the Odds Ratios (ORs) were necessarily broad, and no association reached the False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjusted significance threshold (P < 0.05).

Overlap with Known SLE-Associated Loci

We finally compared the set of 22 genes identified as variants in our cohort against a set of 550 genes previously associated with SLE in the GWAS Catalog (Figure 3).11 The analysis revealed a low overall overlap (4.5%). The single overlapping gene was HLA-DRB1 which is a canonical SLE risk. This minimal overlap underscores the distinction between common, low-effect variants captured by GWAS and the rare, candidate variants identified here via WES. These findings highlight the potential of WES to uncover novel, non-HLA related candidate genes that may contribute to the disease landscape in the Saudi population.

Discussion

This study utilized WES combined with a rigorous, clinical-grade variant filtering pipeline based on ACMG guidelines to investigate the role of rare, high-penetrance germline variants in a small cohort of 29 SLE patients. Because these analyses were conducted in a hospital-based diagnostic setting, we applied the ACMG guidlines to prioritize variants for research purposes. It should be noted that the terms “Pathogenic”, “Likely Pathogenic”, and “VUS” are used herein to describe the bioinformatic and functional prediction profile (eg, rarity and predicted protein impact) according to ACMG criteria. We acknowledge that for genes without a currently established Mendelian association in OMIM, such as NPFFR2, these labels represent prioritized candidate status based on deleterious potential, rather than a confirmed clinical diagnosis or established phenotypic impact. Our findings emphasise the heterogeneous genetic landscape of SLE and highlight novel candidate genes with potential clinical relevance. In alignment with the complex, polygenic nature of SLE, we interpret the variants identified here as potential contributors to specific clinical manifestations rather than as definitive diagnostic markers or primary phenotype drivers.

Methodological Validation and Genetic Architecture

The identification of variants in HLA-DRB1 in 41% of our cohort provides methodological validation, as this is a canonical SLE risk locus. The minimal overlap between our WES findings and the 550 common loci reported in the GWAS Catalog (Figure 3) underscores a distinct genetic architecture. While GWAS captures common variants with weak effect sizes, our WES approach selectively identifies variants that may represent contributors to “missing heritability”.7 These variants likely intersect with a polygenic background that may influence disease expression in our cohort.

This distinction between common and rare-variant architecture is further supported by large-scale transancestral genetic mapping of SLE. Langefeld et al genotyped over 27,000 individuals of European, African American, and Hispanic ancestry and identified 58, 9, and 16 distinct non-HLA risk regions in these groups respectively, including 24 novel loci, with clear evidence of both ancestry-dependent and ancestry-independent contributions to SLE risk.12 That study also proposed a cumulative genetic-load model in which SLE risk increases non-linearly as the number of common risk alleles rises, consistent with a multi-hit genetic architecture. These findings reinforce two points relevant to our study: first, that common-variant genetic architecture in SLE is substantially ancestry-dependent, underscoring the need for population-specific, including Middle Eastern, reference data when interpreting rare-variant findings such as ours; and second, that a cumulative multi-hit model of genetic risk, similar in spirit to the pattern we observe among rare coding variants, may be a shared feature of SLE genetic architecture across both common and rare-variant classes.

NPFFR2 as a Candidate for Phenotypic Partitioning

A key finding is the recurrence of a candidate variant in NPFFR2, identified in 24% of our cohort. While NPFFR2 is traditionally characterized in the context of the anti-opioid system and pain modulation,13 its frequency here suggests a potential role in the “neuro-immune” manifestations of SLE.

Our data suggests a possible clinical linkage between NPFFR2 and the “pain-fatigue” axis, with 85.7% of patients harbouring this variant exhibiting musculoskeletal involvement. Given the NPFFR2 receptor’s role in regulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and hippocampal function,14 we hypothesize that this variant may lower the physiological threshold for chronic pain and inflammation. However, this hypothesis, along with the potential inverse trends observed for oral ulcers (P = 0.093) and haematological involvement (P = 0.066)—both suggesting lower prevalence of these features among NPFFR2 carriers—are preliminary and require validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Synergistic Architectures and Pathway Convergence

The co-occurrence of different variants suggests that the SLE phenotype in this cohort is likely driven by a “multi-hit” genetic architecture rather than isolated defects. We observed a convergence of variants on the DNA damage response (DDR) axis (ATM, RECQL4, BRCA1)15–17 and membrane-structural integrity (DYSF, USH2A, JPH2).18–20 These variations may cause a cumulative disruption of cellular homeostasis. This suggests that DNA damage in this cohort may serve as a potent driver of the inflammatory cascade, triggering an autoimmune response directed at self-antigens.21 Further investigation into the synergistic impact of these co-occurring variants is essential to validate their role in compromising cellular homeostasis and promoting the release of immunostimulatory self-antigens.

Clinical Implications for Drug Resistance and Management

The presence of multiple variants in patients with drug resistance (eg, P14) highlights the potential for a “variant burden” to influence therapeutic outcomes. We noted variants in genes such as BUB1 and RECQL4; however, we acknowledge that these genes are primarily associated with unrelated recessive syndromes (eg, microcephaly or Rothmund-Thomson syndrome). In the context of SLE, these are interpreted only as exploratory contributors to a complex cellular environment rather than primary disease drivers. The identification of pathogenic NPFFR2 and BUB1 variations in patient P14 who exhibited clinical drug resistance highlights the potential for rare variants to influence therapeutic outcomes. BUB1 is essential for the spindle assembly checkpoint.22 We hypothesise that its disruption combined with altered neuro-immune signalling via NPFFR2, may create a refractory state where cellular instability and systemic inflammatory thresholds are simultaneously compromised.

If NPFFR2 signalling is fundamentally disrupted, standard immunosuppressive therapies may not adequately address the neuropeptide-driven components of pain and fatigue. Consequently, NPFFR2 stands as a candidate for functional studies. Understanding how this receptor bridges the gap between the nervous system and immune homeostasis may open new avenues for personalized treatment in SLE, particularly for patients whose disease is defined by refractory pain, oral ulcers, and musculoskeletal distress rather than classical renal or haematological markers. It should be noted that, at present, no NPFFR2-targeted therapies exist in rheumatology practice, and identifying an NPFFR2 variant does not currently alter management under existing ACR/EULAR treatment protocols; the clinical implications discussed here are therefore prospective and contingent on future functional validation and therapeutic development, rather than immediately actionable. Further exploration of the correlations between these multi-hit profiles and therapeutic response offers a preliminary roadmap for precision medicine in SLE.

Study Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is the small sample size (N=29), for which no a priori sample size or power calculation was performed given the exploratory nature of this pilot study. This severely limits the statistical power of the genotype-phenotype correlation tests, resulting in broad Odds Ratio confidence intervals and p-values that do not survive strict FDR correction (as seen in Table 2). Additionally, associations were assessed using univariate Fisher’s exact tests only; this approach does not adjust for potential confounders such as age, sex, disease duration, or concurrent treatment, and future studies with larger cohorts should employ multivariate regression models to account for these factors.

While the reported potential trends in NPFFR2 are biologically compelling, they require validation in a larger, independent, and ethnically diverse SLE cohort. It is crucial to note that none of the observed associations met the criteria for statistical significance after FDR adjustment (FDR ≥ 0.132). Therefore, these results must be interpreted as hypotheses-generating rather than definitive conclusions. Specifically, the potential involvement of NPFFR2 in SLE pathogenesis remains a bioinformatic postulate; in the absence of direct biological or functional validation, these findings should not be interpreted as confirmed mechanistic drivers of the disease.

The small sample sizes in the groups with the clinical features (eg, 9 patients with haematological involvement and 13 with oral ulcers) also limit the power of the analysis. Future work should focus on replicating these findings in larger, independent cohorts and conducting functional studies to determine the precise role of the NPFFR2 variant in the pathology of these two distinct clinical features in addition to other features.

Furthermore, this study is limited to WES, meaning causal variants residing in non-coding regulatory elements were not captured. Our rarity filtering also relied on the gnomAD database, which remains underrepresented for Arab and broader Middle Eastern populations; population-specific allele frequency differences could affect the classification of variants as rare in this cohort, and future studies would benefit from validation against a locally derived allele frequency reference and an ethnicity-matched control cohort. Finally, the ACMG classification used for prioritization is predictive; functional studies (eg, cell-based assays, CRISPR-Cas9 models) are required to definitively prove the pathogenicity of the identified Pathogenic and Likely Pathogenic variants.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that the clinical heterogeneity of SLE in this Saudi cohort may be influenced by a “multi-hit” genetic architecture, where rare candidate variants act as potential phenotypic determinants. By integrating WES with clinical data, we identified NPFFR2 as a prioritized candidate variant associated with a distinct neuro-musculoskeletal profile. This variant was observed in 24% of our cohort and showed an exploratory trend toward co-occurrence with musculoskeletal involvement, while notably partitioning away from oral ulcers and haematological complications. These findings point toward a proposed neuro-immune axis of pathogenesis that warrants further investigation to explore if it operates independently of classical inflammatory pathways.

Furthermore, our findings involve genes traditionally associated with diverse cellular functions, in the context of SLE, they may contribute to a state of sustained inflammatory signalling. Finally, these results shift the view of SLE from a monolithic autoimmune disease toward a collection of potentially genetically defined endotypes. While our findings are exploratory and require functional validation, they advocate for the continued investigation of rare variants to guide future precision medicine and targeted therapeutic interventions in refractory SLE.
