Foundations of Simplicial Complexes:From Geometric Independence to Realizations
Sanjay Mishra

Abstract
. This paper develops a complete foundational treatment of simplicial complexes from Euclidean spaces through geometric realizations, emphasizing concrete computations, examples, and practical verification methods. Beginning with finite point sets in finite and infinite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, geometric independence is established via linear independence of relative vectors, with explicit matrix rank tests. n n -simplices arise as convex hulls of such independent points, proven convex, compact, uniquely spanned, and homeomorphic to unit balls, with detailed barycentric coordinate. Simplicial complexes form through collections closed under faces and with simplex intersections either empty or common faces, verified by necessary and sufficient disjoint interior conditions, illustrated across dimensions from lines to tetrahedra plus non-examples. Derived structures including subcomplexes, p p -skeletons, vertices, stars, and links lead to geometric realizations as continuous spaces with weak topology, proven Hausdorff and locally compact, alongside ray characterizations of convexity and continuity via simplicial maps.

1.
Introduction

Simplicial complexes provide a powerful combinatorial framework for studying the geometry and topology of spaces using finite building blocks called simplices. A simplex is the convex hull of a finite set of geometrically independent points in Euclidean space, such as a line segment in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
, a triangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or a tetrahedron in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.[web:228] By gluing simplices together along their faces in a controlled way, one obtains a simplicial complex, which encodes both the combinatorial structure (how simplices intersect) and the geometric shape of the underlying space.

From a geometric and topological point of view, simplicial complexes serve as discrete models of continuous spaces. Their geometric realizations are built by viewing each abstract simplex as a genuine Euclidean simplex and then identifying faces according to the combinatorial data. This construction yields a topological space, often called the polytope or underlying space of the complex, on which one may study fundamental notions such as continuity, connectedness, compactness, and local finiteness.

Simplicial complexes also play a central role in modern applications. In computational topology and topological data analysis, finite subsets of Euclidean space are used as vertex sets from which one constructs complexes that approximate the shape of data. In numerical analysis and the finite element method, triangulations of domains are modeled as simplicial complexes to support piecewise linear approximations of functions and solutions of differential equations. These examples illustrate how the abstract theory of simplicial complexes connects discrete structures with continuous phenomena.

The goal of this article is to develop an expository treatment of simplicial complexes starting from Euclidean spaces and finite data sets, and then progressing to geometric independence, simplices, simplicial complexes, and their geometric realizations. Along the way, emphasis is placed on explicit examples, computational viewpoints, and structural results such as necessary and sufficient conditions for a collection of simplices to form a simplicial complex, the relationship between geometric and linear independence, and the topological properties of the underlying space.

The article is structured as follows. Section (
2
) provides a comprehensive foundation on Euclidean spaces, covering both finite-dimensional spaces like the familiar plane and three-dimensional room, and infinite-dimensional spaces, with detailed definitions of finite point sets as the basic building blocks for geometric constructions. It proves these spaces form vector spaces for addition and scaling, normed spaces with length measurements, metric spaces for distances between points, and topological spaces with notions of openness and continuity, illustrated through examples. Section (
3
) introduces simplices as the convex hulls formed by geometrically independent points, rigorously proving their key properties: convexity showing line segments between any two points stay inside, compactness ensuring every sequence has a convergent subsequence within the simplex, uniqueness of the spanning vertex set up to ordering, properties of the interior as open and convex within its affine plane, and a homeomorphism to the unit ball that maps the boundary precisely to the unit sphere. Section (
4
) defines simplicial complexes as collections of simplices closed under taking faces where any two simplices intersect either emptily or along a common face, establishing necessary and sufficient conditions through disjoint interiors of distinct simplices, defining complex dimension as the highest simplex dimension present, and providing concrete examples from one-dimensional line segments through two-dimensional filled triangles to three-dimensional tetrahedra, alongside counterexamples of invalid collections.

2.
Euclidean Space

Let us begin our study with some essential preliminaries. Euclidean space is a fundamental concept in mathematics, originating from Euclidean geometry. It refers to a space in which familiar notions such as distance, angle, and geometric shapes are well-defined.

In computational topology, data often originates as a finite collection of points in Euclidean space. These points may represent physical locations, pixel coordinates, measurements, or abstract features in the space. From a topological perspective, such a finite subset of Euclidean space serves as the foundational input for constructing simplices and, ultimately, simplicial complexes. This section introduces the interpretation of a finite subset of Euclidean space as a vertex set, which forms the basis for building higher-dimensional geometric objects. We discuss how to treat such point sets as discrete data, describe the conditions under which they may generate simplices, and prepare this data for computational processing in later sections.

(Finite-Dimensional Euclidean Space as Data Set)
.

The finite-dimensional Euclidean space (denoted by
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
) of dimension
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is defined as

\mathbb{R}^{m}=\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})\colon x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\text{ for }i=1,2,\ldots,m\}

(1)

Definition 2.2

(Infinite-Dimensional Euclidean Space)
.

\mathbb{R}^{{\mathbb{N}}}=\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\colon x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},\,i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

(2)

.

Euclidean spaces

Before presenting fundamental theorems about Euclidean space, it is important to understand that Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
serves as a foundational example connecting various mathematical structures. It is not only a vector space, supporting operations of addition and scalar multiplication, but also naturally equipped with a norm, metric, and topology derived from its algebraic and geometric properties. These layers of structure enable rigorous definitions and proofs across diverse areas such as linear algebra, analysis, and topology. The following theorems summarize these central viewpoints on Euclidean space, laying the groundwork for advanced study and applications in mathematics.

Theorem 2.8

(Euclidean Space as Vector Space)
.

The Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a vector space over the field
{\mathbb{R}}
with vector addition and scalar multiplication

\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}=(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+y_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+y_{n}),\,\alpha\mathbf{x}=(\alpha x_{1},\alpha x_{2},\ldots,\alpha x_{n})

for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 2.9

(Euclidean Space as Norm Space)
.

The Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is norm space over the field
{\mathbb{R}}
with norm

\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}}

for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.10

(Euclidean Space as Metric Space)
.

The Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is metric space with metric

d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|=\sqrt{(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}+\cdots+(x_{n}-y_{n})^{2}}

for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.11

(Euclidean Space as Topological Space)
.

The Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is topological space with standard topology that is the collection of open sets. The set
U\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is open in this standard topology, if for every point
\mathbf{x}\in U
there exists a radius
\epsilon>0
such that open ball

B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})<\epsilon\}

is entirely contained in
U
.

2.1.
Analytic geometry of Euclidean space

Analytic geometry, combines algebra and geometry to study geometric objects and their relationships using coordinate systems and algebraic equations. In the context of Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it provides a structured way to represent points, lines, planes, and curves in spaces of any dimension using ordered tuples of real numbers. Now we describe some elementary subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
from an analytic geometric perspective. We begin with the concept of a geometrically (or affinely
1
1

1

Geometric independence and affine independence refer to the same concept; the former is used in topology and geometry, while the latter is used in linear algebra.
) independent sets of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, which plays a central role in the construction of simplices and simplicial complexes.

Definition 2.12

(Geometrically Independent Set)
.

A set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is said to be geometrically independent if, for any real scalars
t_{i}
, holds following implication:

\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=0\,\text{and}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow t_{i}=0,\,\forall i

(5)

As we saw from the standard result that Euclidean space is also a vector space, so let us see in the next result how geometrically independence is related to linear independence which will help us in verifying geometrically independence of subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Geometric independence and linear independence are related but distinct concepts, especially in the context of affine geometry and linear algebra. To better understand first we recall linear independent set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Definition 2.13

(Linearly Independent Set)
.

A set of vectors
V=\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is said to be linearly independent if, for any real scalars
\lambda_{i}
holds following implication:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow\lambda_{i}=0,\,\forall i

(6)

Let us look at some examples that clarifies the difference between geometric and linear independence.

Example 2.3
.

Is the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(2,0)\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
geometrically independent or linearly independent?

Solution.

A set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(2,0)\}
is geometrically independent if, for any real scalars
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}
, the following implication holds:

\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}=0.

Assume
t_{0}=1
,
t_{1}=-2
,
t_{2}=1
; then

\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}=1+(-2)+1=0

and

\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=(1)(0,0)+(-2)(1,0)+(1)(2,0)=(0,0)=\mathbf{0}.

However, not all
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}
are zero; therefore, the set
A
is not geometrically independent.

On the other hand, if the points of
A
are regarded as position vectors, then the set of vectors
V=\{\mathbf{v}_{1}=\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=(1,0),\mathbf{v}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=(2,0)\}
is said to be linearly independent if, for any real scalars
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, the following implication holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0.

Assume
\lambda_{1}=2
,
\lambda_{2}=-1
; then

\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=2(1,0)+(-1)(2,0)=(0,0)=\mathbf{0}.

But not all
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are zero; therefore, the set
A
is not linearly independent. Hence,
A
is neither geometrically independent nor linearly independent.
∎

Example 2.4
.

Is the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(0,1)\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
both geometrically independent and linearly independent?

Solution.

A set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(0,1)\}
is geometrically independent since the only scalars
t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}
satisfying

\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}=0

and

\sum_{i=0}^{2}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=0\cdot(0,0)+0\cdot(1,0)+0\cdot(0,1)=(0,0)=\mathbf{0}

are
t_{0}=t_{1}=t_{2}=0
.

Therefore, the set
A
is geometrically independent.

On the other hand, the set of vectors
V=\{\mathbf{v}_{1}=\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=(1,0),\mathbf{v}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,1)\}
is linearly independent since the only scalars
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=0\cdot(1,0)+0\cdot(0,1)=(0,0)=\mathbf{0}

are
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
.

Therefore, the set
A
is linearly independent. Hence,
A
is both geometrically independent and linearly independent.
∎

By observing the above two Examples (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we can establish a useful connection between geometric and linear independence of sets in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The following Theorem (
2.14
) provides necessary and sufficient conditions for the geometric independence of a set in terms of linear independence. This result will help solve problems related to geometric independence by applying linear algebra techniques, making the process more time-efficient and computationally convenient.

Theorem 2.14

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Geometric Independent Set)
.

A set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is geometrically independent if and only if the set of vectors

V=\{\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\}

is linearly independent in the sense of linear algebra.

Proof.

First we prove that geometric independence implies linear independence. As given set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
of points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is geometrically independent. Now we prove that set of vectors
V=\{\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\}
is linear independence.

By definition, a set of points
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is geometrically independent if the only way to express any point in
A
as an affine combination of the other points in
A
is the trivial case where one of the coefficients is
1
and the rest are
0
. That is, if

\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0},\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=0,

then necessarily
t_{i}=0
for all
i
.

Rewriting the sum in terms of the vectors
\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=-t_{0}\mathbf{a}_{0}.

Since
\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=0
, we substitute
t_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}
, yielding:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\right)\mathbf{a}_{0}=\mathbf{0}.

This simplifies to:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=\mathbf{0}.

Since
A
is geometrically independent, the only solution to this equation is
t_{1}=t_{2}=\dots=t_{n}=0
.
Thus, the vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},,\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
are linearly independent.

Conversely, we prove that linear independence implies geometric independence. Now, suppose that the set
V=\{\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\}
is linearly independent. We need to prove that the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent. Suppose we have an affine dependence relation among the points in
A

\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0},\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=0.

Rewriting,

t_{0}\mathbf{a}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0}.

Rearranging in terms of the vectors,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=-t_{0}\mathbf{a}_{0}.

Since
\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=0
, we get
t_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}
, giving

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\right)\mathbf{a}_{0}=\mathbf{0}.

On simplifying, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=\mathbf{0}.

Since the vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
are linearly independent, the only solution is
t_{1}=t_{2}=\dots=t_{n}=0
. From
t_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}
, it follows that
t_{0}=0
as well. Thus, the only possible affine combination satisfying the condition is the trivial one, proving that
A
is geometrically independent.
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

One point set of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is always geometrically independent.

Corollary 2.16
.

In
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
two distinct points, three non-collinear points, four non-coplanar points and so on form a geometrically independent set.

Using the theory of geometric independence of sets as we discussed earlier, we now present a systematic working rule for determining whether a given finite set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is geometrically independent.

Working Rule 2.1

(Determining the Geometric Independence of a Finite Set)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
be the set of
(n+1)
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. To determine geometrically independence of
A
follow these steps:

(1)

Formulate the position vectors:
Suppose the elements
\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
of
A
as position vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

(2)

Define the set of relative vectors:
Define the set
V
of relative vectors with respect to
\mathbf{a}_{0}
as

V=\{\textbf{v}_{i}\colon\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\,\text{for}\,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}

The total
n
vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}
will be used to determine independence.

(3)

Form a matrix of relative vectors:
Construct a matrix
M_{V}
whose columns are the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}

M_{V}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{11}&v_{12}&\cdots&v_{1n}\\
v_{21}&v_{22}&\cdots&v_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
v_{n1}&v_{n2}&\cdots&v_{mn}\end{bmatrix}_{m\times n}

(4)

Determine the rank of
M_{V}
:
Calculate the rank of the matrix
M_{V}
by any suitable method such as the Echelon method or the normal method.

(5)

Apply the Geometric Independence Criterion:

(a)

If
\operatorname{rank}(M_{V})=n
, then
A
is geometrically independent.

(b)

If
\operatorname{rank}(M_{V})<n
, then
A
is not geometrically independent.

.

Is the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,1)\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
geometrically independent?

Solution.

Since
A
has distinct elements, it is geometrically independent by Corollary (
2.16
).

Example 2.6
.

Is the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(0,1)\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
geometrically independent?

Solution.
The set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0}=(0,0),\mathbf{a}_{1}=(1,0),\mathbf{a}_{2}=(0,1)\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

(1)

Suppose the elements
\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2}
of

2.2.
An
n
-Dimensional Plane Spanned by a Geometrically Independent Set

To further understand the geometric structure of Euclidean spaces, it is useful to consider the concept of an
n
-plane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. These are the natural generalizations of lines and planes to higher dimensions, and they provide the foundational geometric setting for defining simplices and simplicial complexes. An
n
-plane (denoted by
P_{n}
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is the affine subspace of dimension
n
determined by
n+1
geometrically independent points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Definition 2.17

(
n
-plane spanned by a geometrically independent set)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
be a geometrically independent set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The
n
-plane
P_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
spanned by
A
is the set

P_{n}=\left\{\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\ \text{for some scalars}\ t_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\ \text{such that}\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\right\}.

(7)

Remark 2.18
.

It is observed that, since the points
\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
are geometrically independent, the scalars
t_{i}
are uniquely determined by the point
\mathbf{a}
. That is, for each
\mathbf{a}\in P_{n}
, the coefficients
t_{i}
are unique.

Definition 2.19

(
n
-plane passing through a point)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
be a geometrically independent set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Then the
n
-plane, denoted by
P_{n}(\mathbf{a}_{0})
, that passes through the point
\mathbf{a}_{0}
and is parallel to the vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, is the set

P_{n}(\mathbf{a}_{0})=\left\{\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}),\ \text{for some scalars }t_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

(8)

Remark 2.20
.

In case of finite Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
instead of the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, both definitions (
2.17
) and (
2.19
) of an
n
-plane whether it is spanned by a geometrically independent set or passes through a point the integers
n
and
m
must satisfy the condition
1\leq n\leq m
. This ensures that the set
A
of
(n+1)
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
can be geometrically independent. Geometric independence requires that the
n
relative vectors formed from these points be linearly independent, which is only possible when
n\leq m
in the
m
-dimensional Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Theorem 2.21

(Properties of an
n
-Plane)
.

Let an
n
-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
be defined as an affine subspace spanned by
n+1
geometrically independent points. Then the following properties hold:

(1)

Dimension:
The
n
-plane has dimension
n
, meaning it is parameterized by
n
independent variables (the coefficients
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
).

(2)

Affine Subspace:
The
n
-plane is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, not necessarily passing through the origin unless
\mathbf{a}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.

(3)

Uniqueness:
For
n+1
geometrically independent points, there exists a unique
n
-plane passing through them.

(4)

Linear Independence:
The relative vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
for
i=1,2,\dots,n
must be linearly independent for the points to span an
n
-plane.

Working Rule 2.2

(Determining whether a point lies on an
n
-plane and finding its equation)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
be a geometrically independent set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and let
\mathbf{w}
be a point in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. To check whether the point
\mathbf{w}
lies on the
n
-plane
P_{n}
spanned by
A
and further find the equation of
P_{n}
, apply the following steps:

(1)

Choose reference vector:
Let us choose
\mathbf{a}_{0}\in A
as a fixed reference vector.

(2)

Compute the set of relative vectors:
Compute the set of relative vectors with respect to
\mathbf{a}_{0}
as follows:

V=\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\colon\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\;i=1,2,\ldots,n\}.

(9)

(3)

Compute displacement vector:
Compute displacement vector of given point
\mathbf{w}
with respect to
\mathbf{a}_{0}
as follows:

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0}

(10)

(4)

Form system of linear equations:
Write displacement vector
\mathbf{u}
as a linear combination of the relative vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in V
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
that represent the system of linear equations

\mathbf{u}=s_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+s_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}+\cdots+s_{n}\mathbf{v}_{n}

(11)

where
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
is the unknown column vector and
s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(5)

Construct the matrix form of the system of linear equations:
The matrix form of above system of linear equations (
11
) is

\mathbf{u}=M_{V}\mathbf{s}

(12)

where

\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\,M_{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}&\mathbf{v}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{v}_{n}\end{bmatrix}_{m\times n}\,\text{and}\,\mathbf{s}=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}&\cdots&s_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(13)

(6)

Solution of system of linear equations:
Solve the system of equations (
12
) by suitable method.

(7)

Check whether
\mathbf{w}
lies on
P_{n}
:
Apply following:

(a)

If system of equations (
12
) is consistent and have unique solution
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
, then
\mathbf{w}
lies on
P_{n}
.

(b)

If the system of equations (
12
) is inconsistent, then
\mathbf{w}
does not lie on
P_{n}
.

(8)

Compute affine coefficients:
If
\mathbf{w}
lies on
P_{n}
, then compute the affine coefficients as follows:

t_{i}=s_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,\quad t_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}

(14)

(9)

Verify affine condition:

To verify that
\mathbf{w}
is an affine combination of points in
A
, check

\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1

(15)

(10)

Find the equation of
P_{n}
on which
\mathbf{w}
lies:

If
\mathbf{w}
lies on
P_{n}
, therefore,
P_{n}
is given by

P_{n}=\left\{\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},\;s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}

(16)

Example 2.10
.

Consider the concept of
n
-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

(1)

A
0-plane

P_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a single point, i.e., a set containing only one element.

(2)

A
1-plane

in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a one-dimensional affine subspace, i.e., a straight line parameterized by one parameter.

(3)

A
2-plane

in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a two-dimensional affine subspace, commonly referred to as a flat surface (for example, a plane embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
).

(4)

A
3-plane

in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
or higher dimensions generalizes the notion of a plane to an affine subspace of dimension three.

, and find the equation of that plane if it does.

as a fixed reference vector.

(2)

Compute the set of relative vectors:

\mathbf{a}_{0}
is

, where

(3)

Theorem 2.22

(Extending a Geometrically Independent Set)
.

If
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is arbitrary point lies outside the
n
-plane
P_{n}
which is spanned by the points of
A
, then set
B=\{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is also geometrically independent.

Proof.

As given
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and a arbitrary point
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that
\mathbf{w}\notin P_{n}
, where
P_{n}
the
n
-plane spanned by set
A
. Now our aim to show that
B=\{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is also geometrically independent.

Since
A
is geometrically independent, then by Theorem (
2.14
) the vector
\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
(for
i=1,\ldots,n
) are linear independent i.e. there exists scalars
t_{i}
such

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\Rightarrow t_{0}=\ldots=t_{n}

Now, we consider the set
B=\{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
. We check whether the vectors

\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0}

are linearly independent. Suppose there exist scalars
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n},s
such that

t_{1}(\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0})+t_{2}(\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0})+\dots+t_{n}(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0})+s(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=\mathbf{0}

(26)

Rewriting, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=-s(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0})

(27)

The left-hand side of Equation (
27
) is a linear combination of the vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
, which lie in the
n
-plane
P_{n}
. The right-hand side of Equation (
27
) is a scalar multiple of
\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
, which does not belong to
P_{n}
since
\mathbf{w}\notin P_{n}
.

Since
P_{n}
is an
n
-dimensional affine plane, the only way the above equation can hold is if

t_{1}=t_{2}=\dots=t_{n}=s=0

This proves that the vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
are linearly independent. Hence, the set
B
is geometrically independent.
∎

(Extending a Geometrically Independent Set)
.

Let

be a geometrically independent set of points in

, and let

be a point not lying in the

-plane

spanned by

. To check whether the extended set

Choose a reference point:

\mathbf{a}_{0}\in A
as a fixed reference point.

(2)

Compute the set of relative vectors:

as follows:

(28)

(3)

Compute the relative vector for
\mathbf{w}
:

with respect to

as follows:

\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{w}}=\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}_{0}.

(29)

(4)

Construct the matrices:

(b)

(5)

then the set

is geometrically independent.

Let

2.3.
Affine Transformation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}

Affine transformations are a fundamental class of mappings in geometry and linear algebra that generalize linear transformations by including translations. Unlike purely linear maps, which always fix the origin, affine transformations can shift, rotate, scale, or shear objects while preserving the structure of straight lines and parallelism. More formally, an affine transformation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a function that maps points to points in a way that preserves affine combinations—that is, combinations of the form
t\mathbf{x}+(1-t)\mathbf{y}
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. These transformations play a crucial role in various areas such as computer graphics, robotics, topology, and optimization, as they capture geometric transformations of figures without altering their fundamental shape or relative configuration.

Definition 2.23

(Matrix as a Linear Transformation)
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
be a real square matrix. Then
A
defines a linear transformation

T_{A}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x},

where the output
A\mathbf{x}
is obtained by standard matrix-vector multiplication. The transformation
T_{A}
is called a linear transformation because it satisfies the following properties for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and scalars
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
:

T_{A}(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y})=\alpha T_{A}(\mathbf{x})+\beta T_{A}(\mathbf{y}),\ \text{and}\ T_{A}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}

(32)

The above Definition (
2.23
) tells us that any real
m\times m
matrix
A
defines a function
T_{A}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
via matrix multiplication. This function is called a linear transformation because it preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication. That is, the transformation
T_{A}
respects the algebraic structure of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Linear transformations map the origin to the origin and preserve the straightness of lines, although they may change lengths and angles. Examples include rotations, scalings, reflections, and shear transformations. To illustrate this, we now consider a concrete example in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Example 2.13

(Linear Transformation Induced by a Matrix)
.

Let
A
be the
2\times 2
matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
0&3\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the function
T_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
T_{A}(\mathbf{x})=A\mathbf{x}
.

Is
T_{A}
a linear transformation, and what are its algebraic and geometric effects on vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
?

Solution.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then the image of
\mathbf{x}
under
T_{A}
is:

T_{A}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
0&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_{1}+x_{2}\\
3x_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

•

Linearity check:
Let
\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then:

T_{A}(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y})=A(\alpha\mathbf{x}+\beta\mathbf{y})=\alpha A\mathbf{x}+\beta A\mathbf{y}=\alpha T_{A}(\mathbf{x})+\beta T_{A}(\mathbf{y}),

confirming that
T_{A}
preserves linear combinations.

•

Zero vector check:
Let
\mathbf{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}
. Then:

T_{A}(\mathbf{0})=A\mathbf{0}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
0&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{0}.

So,
T_{A}
maps the zero vector to itself, as required by linearity.

•

Geometric interpretation:

The transformation
T_{A}
scales the
y
-coordinate by a factor of 3, and applies a shear in the
x
-direction by adding
x_{2}
to
2x_{1}
. The origin remains fixed, and straight lines and parallelism are preserved, as expected from a linear transformation.

∎

Definition 2.24

(Affine Transformation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
)
.

A function
T\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
define by
T(\mathbf{a})=A\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}
is called affine transformation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, where
A
is
N\times N
is a real matrix (representing a linear transformation),
\mathbf{b}
is a fixed a vector (representing a translation vector).

Theorem 2.25

(Properties of Affine Transformation)
.

Affine transformation preserves collinearity, it preserves ratio i.e. it maintains the ratios of distances along a straight line. Unlike linear transformations, affine transformations do not necessarily fix the origin unless a fix vector
\mathbf{b}
.

Example 2.14

(Affine Transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
)
.

Is the function
T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by

T(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-2\end{bmatrix}

an affine transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
?

How does this transformation, consisting of a linear transformation by the matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
1&3\end{bmatrix},

which scales, rotates, and shears the space, combined with a translation by the vector
\mathbf{b}=(1,-2)
, affect specific points such as the origin and the unit vectors along the axes?

Solution.

The function
T
is indeed an affine transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as it can be expressed as a linear transformation followed by a translation. For example, the points transform as follows:

(1)

The origin
(0,0)
is mapped to
(1,-2)
:

T(0,0)=A(0,0)+(1,-2)=(1,-2).

(2)

The unit vector
(1,0)
along the x-axis is mapped to
(3,-1)
, showing scaling and shearing:

T(1,0)=A(1,0)+(1,-2)=\begin{bmatrix}2\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\

-1\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

The unit vector
(0,1)
along the y-axis is mapped to
(2,1)

T(0,1)=A(0,1)+(1,-2)=\begin{bmatrix}1\\

∎

(Examples of Affine Transformations)

, where

Theorem 2.26

(Preservation of Geometrically Independency during Affine Transformation)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
T

is any affine transformation on
A
, then
T(A)
is also geometrically independent. That is, the Affine transformation preserves geometrically independency of set.

Proof.

By definition a set of points
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is said to be geometrically independent if the vectors

\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}

are linearly independent in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Therefore by Definition of linear independency of vectors

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=0\Rightarrow t_{i}=0,\forall i

(33)

An affine transformation
T\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is given by

T(\mathbf{a})=A^{\prime}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b},

where
A^{\prime}
is an
N\times N
invertible matrix (a linear transformation), and
\mathbf{b}
is a translation vector.
Applying
T
to the set
A
, we obtain the set of transformed points

T(A)=\{T(\mathbf{a}_{i})\}=\{A^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathbf{b}\colon\mathbf{a}_{i}\in A\,\forall i\}

Now our aim is to show that set
T(A)
is geometrically independent for this we prove that it is linearly independent.

The transformed difference vectors are:

T(\mathbf{a}_{i})-T(\mathbf{a}_{0})=(A^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathbf{b})-(A^{\prime}\mathbf{a}_{0}+\mathbf{b})=A^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})

Now, if the original vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
are linearly independent, then the equation

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}A^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=0

implies

A^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)=0.

Since
A^{\prime}
is invertible, the only solution is:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})=0,

then by Equation (
33
) implies
t_{i}=0,\,\forall i
. Therefore, vectors
T(\mathbf{a}_{1})-T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),\ldots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})-T(\mathbf{a}_{0})
are linear independent. Thus, the transformed set
T(A)=\{T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),T(\mathbf{a}_{1}),\dots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})\}
remains geometrically independent.

Since an affine transformation is a composition of a linear transformation and a translation, and we have shown that it preserves linear independence of difference vectors, it follows that an affine transformation preserves the geometrical independence of a set.
∎

Theorem 2.27

(Preservation of Plane during Affine Transformation)
.

Let
P_{n}
is the
n
-plane spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
. Let
T\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is affine transformation
T
carries the plane
P_{n}
spanned by
\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
onto the plane spanned by
T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),\ldots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
P_{n}
be the
n
-plane spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
By definition, every point
\mathbf{a}\in P_{n}
can be written as an affine combination of the points in
A

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}),t_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},\forall i

(34)

Since
T\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is an affine transformation, it has the form:

T(\mathbf{a})=A^{\prime}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}

(35)

where
A^{\prime}
is an
N\times N
invertible matrix (a linear transformation), and
\mathbf{b}
is a translation vector.

By Equation (
34
), applying
T
to both sides of the affine combination, we get

T(\mathbf{a})=T\left(\mathbf{a}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)

Using the property of affine transformations

T(\mathbf{a})=T(\mathbf{a}_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(T(\mathbf{a}_{i})-T(\mathbf{a}_{0}))

This equation shows that every point
T(\mathbf{a})
is an affine combination of the transformed points
T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),T(\mathbf{a}_{1}),\dots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})
, which means the set
T(P_{n})
is precisely the plane spanned by the transformed points. Since
T(P_{n})
consists of all affine combinations of
T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),\dots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})
, we conclude that
T
maps the plane
P_{n}
onto the plane spanned by
T(\mathbf{a}_{0}),\dots,T(\mathbf{a}_{n})
.
∎

3.
Simplices

Having established the essential background in Euclidean geometry and affine transformations, we are now prepared to introduce the fundamental building blocks of simplicial complexes—simplices. The word simplices is used as the plural of simplex. A simplex is the simplest possible polytope in any given dimension: a point in dimension 0, a line segment in dimension 1, a triangle in dimension 2, a tetrahedron in dimension 3, and so on. Simplices generalize the notion of “flat shapes” to arbitrary dimensions and play a central role in the study of both geometric and algebraic topology.

There are two approaches to studying simplex - geometric and abstract (axiom based). In this section we will first study its geometrical approach and later consider the abstract approach. In what follows, we define simplex formally, illustrate them with examples, and explore their basic properties essential for constructing simplicial complexes.

Definition 3.1

(
n
-simplex)
.

Let
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The
n
-simplex (denoted by
\sigma_{n}
) spanned by
\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
is the set of all points
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i

Symbolically,

\sigma_{n}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i\Big\}

(36)

Now we are going to define some important terms related to simplex which will help us understand some other properties of simplex.

Definition 3.2

(Set of Vertices of Simplex)
.

If simplex
\sigma_{n}
is spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, then set
A
is called set of vertices of the simplex
\sigma_{n}
.

Definition 3.3

(Dimension of Simplex)
.

The dimension of the simplex
\sigma_{n}
spanned by
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is defined by
\dim(\sigma_{n})=n
, where
n
is one less than the number of geometrically independent points in
A
.

Definition 3.4

(Barycentric Coordinate of Point in Simplex)
.

The simplex
\sigma_{n}
spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is

\sigma_{n}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i\Big\}

The real numbers
t_{i}
that is uniquely determined by
\mathbf{x}
is called
barycentric coordinate
of the point
\mathbf{x}
of the simplex
\sigma_{n}
w.r.t. vertices
\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
.

After understanding the definition of an
n
-simplex, the set of its vertices, and the concept of barycentric coordinates, it is natural to examine the topological structure of a simplex. Beyond its geometric description, a simplex can be viewed as a topological space with specific properties that make it a fundamental object in topology. In particular, we are interested in how a simplex behaves as a subset of Euclidean space, its topological dimension, its boundary and interior, and its role in constructing more complex topological spaces. This perspective is essential for developing simplicial complexes and understanding their use in algebraic topology.

Theorem 3.5

(Simplex as Topological Space)
.

If
\sigma_{n}
is a
n
-simplex spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, then
\sigma_{n}
is topological space with subspace topology.

Proof.

If
\sigma_{n}
is a
n
-simplex spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, then by Definition

\sigma_{n}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i\Big\}

Since
\sigma_{n}
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, it inherits the subspace topology from the standard topology on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The subspace topology on
\sigma_{n}
is defined as follows

\mathcal{T}_{\sigma_{n}}=\{U\cap\sigma_{n}\colon U\,\text{is open in}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\}

This means that a set
V\subset\sigma_{n}
is open in
\sigma_{n}
if and only if there exists an open set
U
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that
V=U\cap\sigma_{n}
. To show that
(\sigma_{n},\mathcal{T}_{\sigma_{n}})
is a topological space, we check the axioms of a topology:

(1)

Empty Set and Full Space
:

(a)

The empty set
\emptyset
is open because
\emptyset=\emptyset\cap\sigma_{n}
.

(b)

The entire simplex
\sigma_{n}
is open because
\sigma_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{N}\cap\sigma_{n}
, where
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is open in itself.

(2)

Arbitrary Unions:
If
\{V_{\alpha}\}
is a collection of open sets in
\sigma_{n}
, then each
V_{\alpha}=U_{\alpha}\cap\sigma_{n}
for some open set
U_{\alpha}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Their union is

\bigcup_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\cap\sigma_{n})=\left(\bigcup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}\right)\cap\sigma_{n}

Since
\bigcup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the resulting set is open in
\sigma_{n}
.

(3)

Finite Intersections:
If
V_{1},V_{2}
are open in
\sigma_{n}
, then
V_{1}=U_{1}\cap\sigma_{n}
and
V_{2}=U_{2}\cap\sigma_{n}
for some open sets
U_{1},U_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Their intersection is

V_{1}\cap V_{2}=(U_{1}\cap\sigma_{n})\cap(U_{2}\cap\sigma_{n})=(U_{1}\cap U_{2})\cap\sigma_{n}

Since
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the resulting set is open in
\sigma_{n}
.

Since all three conditions hold,
\mathcal{T}_{\sigma_{n}}
is a valid topology, meaning
\sigma_{n}
is a topological space with the
subspace topology
.
∎

To gain a deeper understanding of the structure of a simplex, it is important to explore how its subsets are organized and interpreted within topology and geometry. Concepts such as faces, boundaries, interiors, and closures of a simplex provide crucial insights into how simplices connect and combine to form more complex geometric structures like simplicial complexes. These notions not only describe the geometric composition of a simplex but also play a central role in defining chains, boundaries, and homology groups in algebraic topology. We now formally define and illustrate each of these concepts in order to establish a solid foundation for the study of simplicial complexes.

Definition 3.6

(Face of Simplex)
.

Let
n
-simplex
\sigma_{n}
is spanned by the geometrically independent set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Any
k
-simplex
\sigma_{k}
(
0\leq k<n
) spanned by the subset
B

\sigma_{n}
. Symbolically,

\sigma_{k}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}t_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{j=0}^{k}t_{j}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{j}\geq 0,\,\forall j\Big\}

(37)

In particular, the face
\sigma_{k}
of
\sigma_{n}
spanned by the set
B=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}\subset A
is called
face opposite to
\mathbf{a}_{0}
. The faces different from
\sigma_{n}
itself are called
proper faces
of
\sigma_{n}
.

Definition 3.7

(Boundary of Simplex)
.

The boundary of
\sigma_{n}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Bd}(\sigma_{n})
(or
\operatorname{Bd}\sigma_{n}
), is the union of all its
(n-1)
-faces. Symbolically,

\operatorname{Bd}(\sigma_{n})=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n}\sigma_{n-1}^{(i)}

(38)

where
\sigma_{n-1}^{(i)}
is the
(n-1)
-face obtained by omitting the vertex
\mathbf{a}_{i}
from
A
.

Definition 3.8

(Interior of Simplex)
.

The interior of
\sigma_{n}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{n})
, consists of all points
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
whose barycentric coordinates are strictly positive. Symbolically,

\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{n})=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}>0,\,\forall i\Big\}

(39)

Interior of the simplex can also be defined by the equation

\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{n})=\sigma_{n}-\operatorname{Bd}(\sigma_{n})

(40)

Geometrically, the interior excludes the boundary of the simplex. The set
\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{n})
is also called
open simplex
.

Definition 3.9

(Closure of Simplex)
.

The closure of
\sigma_{n}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Cl}(\sigma_{n})
, is the smallest closed set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
that contains
\sigma_{n}
.

Remark 3.10
.

(1)

The set
\operatorname{Bd}(\sigma_{n})
contains all point
\mathbf{x}
such that at least one of the barycentric coordinates
t_{i}
of
\mathbf{x}
is zero.

(2)

The set
\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{n})
contains all point
\mathbf{x}
such that the barycentric coordinates
t_{i}(x)>0
for all
i
.

(3)

From above two observation, we can say that given
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
, there is exactly one face
\sigma_{k}
(
0\leq k<n
) of
\sigma_{n}
such that
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{k})
, for
\sigma_{k}
must be the face of
\sigma_{n}
spanned by the those
\mathbf{a}_{i}
, for which
t_{i}
is positive.

Now we are going to discuss the important properties of simplifications which are very useful for the study of simplification complexes.

For convenience, let us recall some important notations related to simplices. Consider a geometrically independent set of points

A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which determines an
n
-plane
P_{n}
. The simplex spanned by these points is denoted as
\sigma_{n}
. For an arbitrary point
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
, there exist unique barycentric coordinates
\{t_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
satisfying

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\quad\text{where}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\quad\text{and}\quad t_{i}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }i.

The barycentric coordinates not only describe the position of
\mathbf{x}
within the simplex, but also vary continuously as
\mathbf{x}
moves within the simplex. This continuity plays a fundamental role in topology and geometry, ensuring that simplicial maps and constructions behave well under limits and preserve structure.

Theorem 3.11

(Continuity of Barycentric Coordinates)
.

Let
n
-simplex
\sigma_{n}
is spanned by the set
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. The barycentric coordinate
t_{i}(\mathbf{x})
of
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
w.r.t. set
A
are continuous functions of
\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

By definition of
n
-simplex, any point
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
can be expressed uniquely as a convex combination of the points in
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
, so

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{a}_{i},\quad\text{where}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1

(53)

where scalars
t_{i}(\mathbf{x})
are called the barycentric coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
with respect to the set
A
. The uniqueness of the barycentric coordinates follows from the geometric independence of
A
, which ensures that the affine hull of
A
is
n
-dimensional.

Let us define the matrix
M
whose columns are
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}

M=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&\cdots&|\\
\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\\
|&|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}

The matrix
M
has size
N\times n
, and its rank is
n
due to the geometric independence of
A
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
, rewrite
\mathbf{x}
in terms of
t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}

\mathbf{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0})

In matrix form

\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}_{0}=M\cdot\mathbf{t},

where
\mathbf{t}=[t_{1}(x),t_{2}(x),\ldots,t_{n}(x)]^{\top}
.

Now solve for
\mathbf{t}
, we get

\mathbf{t}=M^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}_{0}),

where
M^{\dagger}
is the pseudoinverse of
M
(or the inverse if
n=N
). The coordinate
t_{0}(\mathbf{x})
is determined using the condition

t_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})

Since
M
is constant and non-singular, the entries of
M^{\dagger}
depend continuously on
M
(which is fixed) and linearly on
\mathbf{x}
. Thus, the mapping:

\mathbf{x}\mapsto M^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}_{0})

is a continuous function of
\mathbf{x}
. The barycentric coordinates
(t_{1}(\mathbf{x}),t_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,t_{n}(\mathbf{x}))
are linear transformations of
\mathbf{x}
and hence are continuous functions. Finally,
t_{0}(\mathbf{x})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is also a continuous function since it is a linear combination of continuous functions.
∎

Working Rule 3.1

(Determining the Barycentric Coordinates of a Point in a Simplex)
.

Let
\sigma_{n}
be an
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with vertex set
V(\sigma_{n})=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
. To determine the barycentric coordinates
(t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
of a point
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
, follow these steps:

(1)

Formulate position vector:
Suppose the vertices of simplex
\sigma_{n}
are position vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

(2)

Define relative vectors:
Form the relative vectors w.r.t.
\mathbf{a}_{0}

\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\ldots,n

Also define
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}_{0}
.

(3)

Construct the matrix equation:
Express vector
\mathbf{v}
as a linear combination of the
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, then write

\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}

This leads to the matrix equation

\mathbf{v}=M\cdot\mathbf{t^{\prime}},

where
M
is the
m\times n
matrix with columns

The structure of an

The concept of convexity plays a foundational role in the geometry and topology of simplices. A set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is said to be convex if, for any two points in the set, the entire line segment joining them also lies within the set. This property ensures that the shape has no “dents” or “holes” and is geometrically well-behaved. The
n
-simplex, defined as the set of all convex combinations of its
n+1
geometrically independent vertices, naturally inherits this convex structure. Understanding the convexity of simplices not only confirms their geometric coherence but also establishes a crucial bridge to more advanced constructions in simplicial complexes, convex polytopes, and topological spaces.

Definition 3.13

(Convex set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
)
.

A subset
A
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is called convex if for each pair
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
of points
A
, the line segments joining them lies in
A
. Symbolically,
A
is convex if

(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}\in A,\forall\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in A\,\text{and}\,\forall\,t\in[0,1]

(56)

where the expression
(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}
represents a point on the line segment between points
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
of
A
. The parameter
t
determines how far along the line segment the point lies. If
t=0
, the point is at
\mathbf{x}
, if
t=1
, the point is at
\mathbf{y}
and if
t\in(0,1)
, the point lies somewhere between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

Example 3.8

(Examples of Convex Sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
)
.

The set
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, line segments, triangle (including its interior), a circle or sphere (including the interior) and convex polygon or polyhedron are convex sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Example 3.9

(Non-Examples of Convex Sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
)
.

A crescent or “moon-shaped” region, a set with a “hole” or gap (e.g., a circle with its interior removed) and two disjoint sets.

Theorem 3.15

(Compactness of
n
-simplex)
.

The
n
-simplex
\sigma_{n}
spanned by the points of the
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is compact in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Proof.

(1)

\sigma_{n}
:
Since
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{n}
is a convex combination of the points
\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
, we can write

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1,\;t_{i}\geq 0.

Let

, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Then

\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}M=M\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=M

Thus,

\sigma_{n}

Theorem 3.17
.

\sigma_{n}
, there is one and only one geometrically independent set of points spanning
\sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

An
n
-simplex
\sigma_{n}
is the convex hull of
n+1
geometrically independent points
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. A set of points
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
is geometrically independent if the vectors
\{\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{2}-\mathbf{a}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{0}\}
are linearly independent, meaning they span an
n
-dimensional affine subspace. We aim to show that the
n+1
points of
A
that define
\sigma_{n}
are unique up to relabeling.

(1)

Any
n+1
points spanning
\sigma_{n}
must be geometrically independent:
Assume
\sigma_{n}=\text{conv}(A)
is defined by
A=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
. If
A
were not geometrically independent, at least one point in
A
, say
a_{k}
, could be written as a linear combination of the other points

\mathbf{a}_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}t_{i}=1,\,t_{i}\geq 0

In this case,
\mathbf{a}_{k}
would lie within the convex hull of the remaining points. Hence,
A
would not span the entire simplex
\sigma_{n}
, contradicting the definition of an
n
-simplex. Therefore,
A
must consist of
n+1
geometrically independent points.

(2)

(1)

Mapping

4.
Simplicial Complex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}

A simplicial complex is a fundamental concept in algebraic topology that provides a way to study topological spaces through simpler geometric objects called simplices. A simplex is a generalization of a triangle, and it can exist in any dimension. For example, a 0-simplex is a point, a 1-simplex is a line segment, a 2-simplex is a triangle, and a 3-simplex is a tetrahedron. A simplicial complex is essentially a collection of these simplices that are glued together in a specific way, where every face of a simplex is also included in the complex, and the intersection of any two simplices is itself a simplex (or possibly empty).

Simplicial complexes serve as a powerful tool for approximating and studying the properties of more complex topological spaces. They are used to define and compute various topological invariants, such as homology groups, which help classify spaces based on their shape and connectivity. Through the use of simplicial complexes, it becomes possible to break down and understand intricate topological spaces in terms of simpler, discrete elements.

Definition 4.1

(Simplicial Complex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
)
.

A non-empty collection
\mathcal{K}
of simplices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is called a simplicial complex if the following conditions are met:

(1)

Closure under faces:
If
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
, then every face of
\sigma
is also in
\mathcal{K}
.

(2)

Intersection of simplices:
If
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\sigma\cap\tau
is either empty or a face of both
\sigma
and
\tau
.

Definition 4.2

(Dimension of Simplicial Complex)
.

The dimension of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is largest dimension of its simplices i.e. the highest number
n
such that there exists at least one

n
-simplex in
\mathcal{K}
. The dimension of
\mathcal{K}
is denoted by
\dim(\mathcal{K})
.

Since it is not an easy task to verify whether a given collection is simplicial complex or not, let us look at the next important Lemma (
4.3
) which gives necessary and sufficient conditions for any collection to be simplicial complex which ultimately also helps in verification.

Lemma 4.3
.

A collection
\mathcal{K}
of simplicies is a simplicial complex if and only if following conditions hold:

(1)

Closure under faces:
Every face of a simplex of
\mathcal{K}
is in
\mathcal{K}
.

(2)

Disjoint Interiors for distinct simplicies:
Every pair of distinct simplicies of
\mathcal{K}
have disjoint interiors.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\mathcal{K}
is simplicial complex, then we would like to prove that
\mathcal{K}
satisfies both the given conditions.

The first condition is clearly satisfied by
\mathcal{K}
since it is the same as the definition of the simplicial complex.

Now the second condition, if
\mathcal{K}
is simplicial complex, then we would like to prove that every pair of distinct simplices of
\mathcal{K}
has disjoint interiors i.e. if
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{K}
such that
\sigma\neq\tau
, then
\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)\cap\mathrm{Int}(\tau)=\emptyset
. We will prove its contrapositive statement which is: If
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)\cap\mathrm{Int}(\tau)
is arbitrary, then
\sigma=\tau
. As
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)\cap\mathrm{Int}(\tau)
is given, this implies that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)
i.e.
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma
. Let
\rho=\sigma\cap\tau
. Let
\rho\subset\sigma
be a proper face of
\sigma
, then
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{Bd}\sigma
, but
\mathbf{x}\notin\operatorname{Bd}\sigma
since
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)
. Therefore, it is not possible for
\rho
to be proper face so
\rho=\sigma
. Similarly, we can prove that
\rho=\tau
. Thus,
\rho=\sigma=\tau
.

Conversely, if the collection
\mathcal{K}
of simplices holds both conditions
(1)
and
(2)
, then we would like to prove that
\mathcal{K}
is simplicial complex. This will be proved directly from the definition of simplicial complex. This first condition of the simple complex clearly matches
(1)
because both are the same. We will now prove that the intersection of any two simplexes
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{K}
is either empty of a face of each of them.

Let us suppose
\sigma\cap\tau\neq\emptyset
. Then we claim
\sigma\cap\tau=\rho
is the face of
\sigma
that is spanned by those vertices
\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}
of
\sigma
that lies in
\tau
. Since
\sigma\cap\tau
is convex and contains the vertices
\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}
, which means that
\rho\subset\sigma\cap\tau
. Now let
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma\cap\tau
be arbitrary, this means that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)\cap\mathrm{Int}(\tau)
for some faces
\sigma^{\prime}
of
\sigma
and
\tau^{\prime}
of
\tau
. But according to the given condition
(2)
, this implies that
\sigma^{\prime}=\tau^{\prime}
. Therefore, the vertices of
\sigma^{\prime}
are contained in
\tau^{\prime}
and they are elements of
\{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{m}\}
. Thus,
\sigma^{\prime}
is a face of
\rho
which means
\mathbf{x}\in\rho
and this implies
\sigma\cap\tau\subset\rho
. Therefore,
\sigma\cap\tau=\rho
.
∎

4.2.
Geometric Realization of Simplicial Complex

As we have already studied about simplicial complexs which are combinatorial structures as a collection of vertices and how they are grouped into simplices. Since simplicial complexes are discrete in nature, the question arises: how can we study the continuous properties of such structures? The answer is the geometric realization of simplicial complexes that serve as a bridge between discrete models and continuous shapes. The geometric realization of a simplicial complex gives a continuous topological space corresponding to its combinatorial structure. It bridges the gap between discrete and continuous mathematics, enabling the application of topological, geometric, and analytical techniques. By establishing a concrete space where points are convex combinations of vertices, it allows for the study of homotopy, homology, and topological invariants in algebraic topology. Additionally, it facilitates metric geometry, differential geometry, and computational methods such as finite element analysis and topological data analysis. The realization provides a natural embedding in Euclidean space, making abstract complexes more tangible and useful for approximating smooth manifolds and complex topological spaces. Overall, geometric realization serves as a crucial tool for analyzing and computing properties of spaces that originate from purely combinatorial simplicial structures.

The geometric realization of simplicial complexes has many popular or synonymous names such as underlying space, polyhedron, and simplicial space, but in our study we use only the geometric realization. If
\mathcal{K}
is a simplicial complex then its geometric realization is denoted by
|\mathcal{K}|
.

Definition 4.8

(Geometric Realization of Simplicial Complex)
.

The geometric realization of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
that is union of simplices corresponding to each simplex in
\mathcal{K}
, glued together along their common faces. Symoblically,

|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{K}}\sigma_{n}

(66)

where

\sigma_{n}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\text{where}\,\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}=1\,\text{and}\,t_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i\Big\}

(67)

The geometric realization
|\mathcal{K}|
is the actual geometric realization of
\mathcal{K}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, capturing its shape in a continuous way rather than just as an abstract combinatorial structure.

or

, breaking the continuity of

. If we took a stronger topology (with more open sets), it would still make the inclusions continuous, but we want the smallest such topology. Thus, this topology
\mathcal{T}
is the weakest (coarsest) topology ensuring that every inclusion map
\iota
is continuous.

Question
3
.

Why is continuity of inclusion map
\iota\colon\sigma\to|\mathcal{K}|
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
is important?

Answer
2
.

A simplicial complex is built by gluing simplices together. If the topology is too weak, the gluing might not behave well under continuous mappings.
Ensuring that the inclusion

\iota\colon\sigma\to|\mathcal{K}|
is continuous means that any function defined on individual simplices can be naturally extended to the whole space
|\mathcal{K}|
.

Theorem 4.11
.

In general the topology of the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is finer than the topology
|\mathcal{K}|
inherits as a subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

Proof.

As given, let us denote the topology on
|\mathcal{K}|
by
\mathcal{T}
and the topology is obtained as a subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
i.e. by the subspace topology
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Both topologies are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\{U\subset|\mathcal{K}|\colon U\cap\sigma\,\text{is open in}\,\sigma,\,\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\}

(70)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\{U\subset|\mathcal{K}|\colon U=V\cap|\mathcal{K}|,\,V\,\text{is open in}\,{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\}

(71)

Our aim is to show that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Let
U\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be arbitrary, then there exists open
V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that
U=V\cap|\mathcal{K}|
. Since each simplex
\sigma
is itself a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, then
U\cap\sigma=(V\cap|\mathcal{K}|)\cap\sigma=V\cap\sigma
. Since
V
is open in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, its intersection with
\sigma
(a topological subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
) is open in
\sigma
(by the definition of the subspace topology). Thus, for every simplex
\sigma
,
U\cap\sigma
is open in
\sigma
, meaning that
U\in\mathcal{T}
. Thus,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.
∎

Theorem 4.12
.

In general, the two topologies are different on the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
but the same for finite simplicial spaces
\mathcal{K}
. In other words, the topology
\mathcal{T}
of the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is strictly finer than the subspace topology
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. However, if
\mathcal{K}
is finite, then these two topologies coincide.

Proof.

There are two case arise:

(1)

\mathcal{K}
is an infinite simplicial complex:
In this case we show that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}
i.e. that there exist open set in
\mathcal{T}
that is not open in the subspace topology
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
inherited from
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.

In this proof, we consider the case where the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is infinite and demonstrate that the topology
\mathcal{T}
, which is the weakest topology making all simplex inclusions continuous, is strictly finer than the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We begin by understanding the given simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, which consists of an infinite collection of vertices and edges. Mathematically, we define it as

\mathcal{K}=\{\{a_{n}\}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\{a_{n},a_{n+1}\}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\},

meaning that the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is the union of all these simplices, forming an infinite sequence of edges. Each 1-simplex
[a_{n},a_{n+1}]
is a closed interval in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and together they form an infinite one-dimensional geometric structure.

To prove that
\mathcal{T}
is strictly finer than the subspace topology, we must show that there exists at least one open set in
\mathcal{T}
that is not open in the subspace topology. Consider the set

U=|\mathcal{K}|\setminus\{a_{0}\}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}[a_{n},a_{n+1}],

which consists of all edges except the removal of the vertex
a_{0}
. In the topology
\mathcal{T}
, a set is open if its intersection with each simplex is open in the standard topology of that simplex. Since
U
consists of full edges
[a_{n},a_{n+1}]
for
n\geq 1
, and the part of
[a_{0},a_{1}]
remaining in
U
is
(a_{0},a_{1}]
, the set
U
is open in
\mathcal{T}
.

We now check whether
U
is open in the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In the subspace topology, a set is open if it is the intersection of an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|\mathcal{K}|
, meaning

U=W\cap|\mathcal{K}|

for some open
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. However, this is not possible because the point
a_{0}
is a limit point of
U
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and any Euclidean open set containing
U
must also contain a small ball around
a_{0}
. Consequently,
W\cap|\mathcal{K}|
would necessarily include some neighborhood around
a_{0}
, contradicting
U=W\cap|\mathcal{K}|
. Furthermore, in the Euclidean topology, a single point
a_{0}
cannot be removed without also removing an open neighborhood around it, which
U
does not possess.

Since we have found an example of a set that is open in
\mathcal{T}
but not in the subspace topology, we conclude that

\mathcal{T}\supsetneq\text{(subspace topology from }\mathbb{R}^{N}\text{)}.

Thus, the topology
\mathcal{T}
is strictly finer than the subspace topology from
\mathbb{R}^{N}
when
\mathcal{K}
is infinite. This completes the proof.

(2)

\mathcal{K}
is a finite simplicial complex:
In this case we show that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}
. Now, we consider the case where the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is finite and show that the topology
\mathcal{T}
coincides with the subspace topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Given that
\mathcal{K}
is finite, the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
consists of a finite number of simplices, including vertices, edges, and possibly higher-dimensional simplices. Each simplex in
\mathcal{K}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and their finite union is also compact in the subspace topology.

To prove the equality of the topologies, we need to show that any set open in
\mathcal{T}
is also open in the subspace topology and vice versa. Since
\mathcal{T}
is the weakest topology making all inclusion maps continuous, it follows that any open set in the subspace topology must also be open in
\mathcal{T}
. Conversely, in a finite simplicial complex, the intersection of any open set with a simplex remains open in the Euclidean topology of that simplex. Since there are only finitely many simplices, this ensures that any open set in
\mathcal{T}
is also open in the subspace topology.

Furthermore, compactness plays a crucial role in finite simplicial complexes. Since
|\mathcal{K}|
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the subspace topology on
|\mathcal{K}|
is locally compact and coincides with
\mathcal{T}
. This follows because in finite-dimensional spaces, compact and locally compact topologies coincide with their subspace topologies.

Thus, we conclude that for finite
\mathcal{K}
, the two topologies are identical, i.e.,

\mathcal{T}=\text{(subspace topology from }\mathbb{R}^{N}\text{)}.

∎

Lemma 4.13
.

If
\mathcal{L}
is sub-complex of the simplicial space
\mathcal{K}
, then
|\mathcal{L}|
is closed subspace of the space
\mathcal{K}
. In particular,
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\sigma
is a closed subspace of
|\mathcal{K}|
.

Proof.

The geometric realization of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is given by
|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}}|\sigma|
, and similarly, the realization of a subcomplex
\mathcal{L}
is
|\mathcal{L}|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{L}}|\sigma|
. Each simplex
|\sigma|
is homeomorphic to a standard Euclidean simplex and is therefore a compact subset of some
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The space
|\mathcal{K}|
is equipped with the weak topology induced by its simplices, meaning that a subset
U\subseteq|\mathcal{K}|
is closed if and only if
U\cap|\sigma|
is closed in
|\sigma|
for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
, it follows that
|\mathcal{L}|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{L}}|\sigma|
. Each simplex
|\sigma|
is closed in
|\mathcal{K}|
because simplices in a geometric realization are compact, and compact sets are closed in their ambient space. Since
|\mathcal{L}|
is a union of such closed sets, it follows that
|\mathcal{L}|
is closed in
|\mathcal{K}|
, as unions of closed sets remain closed under the weak topology.

In particular, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
, then
|\sigma|
itself is closed in
|\mathcal{K}|
. This follows from the fact that simplices form compact subspaces in Euclidean topology, and compact sets remain closed in the weak topology. Therefore, we conclude that
|\mathcal{L}|
is a closed subspace of
|\mathcal{K}|
and that each
|\sigma|
is a closed subspace of
|\mathcal{K}|
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

A map
f\colon|\mathcal{K}|\to X
is continuous if and only if
f|_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\to X
is continuous for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
f:|\mathcal{K}|\to X
is continuous.

By definition of continuity, for any open set
U\subseteq X
, the preimage
f^{-1}(U)
is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
. Since
|\mathcal{K}|
is endowed with the weak topology determined by its simplices, a set
A\subseteq|\mathcal{K}|
is open if and only if
A\cap|\sigma|
is open in
|\sigma|
for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
. Applying this to
f^{-1}(U)
, we get:

f^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }|\mathcal{K}|\implies f^{-1}(U)\cap|\sigma|\text{ is open in }|\sigma|,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

Since
f|_{\sigma}=f|_{|\sigma|}
is simply the restriction of
f
to
|\sigma|
, we conclude that:

f^{-1}(U)\cap|\sigma|=(f|_{\sigma})^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }|\sigma|,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

Thus,
f|_{\sigma}
is continuous for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
, since continuity is defined by the preimage of open sets being open.

(
\Leftarrow
)Suppose
f|_{\sigma}:|\sigma|\to X
is continuous for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
.

To show that
f
is continuous, we must show that for any open set
U\subseteq X
, the preimage
f^{-1}(U)
is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
. Since
|\mathcal{K}|
has the weak topology with respect to its simplices, a subset
A\subseteq|\mathcal{K}|
is open if and only if
A\cap|\sigma|
is open in
|\sigma|
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
. Given that
f|_{\sigma}
is continuous, we know that:

(f|_{\sigma})^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(U)\cap|\sigma|\text{ is open in }|\sigma|,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

Since this holds for every simplex
\sigma
, and the weak topology ensures that openness of
f^{-1}(U)\cap|\sigma|
for all
\sigma
implies openness of
f^{-1}(U)
in
|\mathcal{K}|
, we conclude that
f^{-1}(U)
is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
. Therefore,
f
is continuous.
∎

Example 4.11
.

Consider the simplicial complex

\mathcal{K}=\{\{\mathbf{a}_{0}\},\{\mathbf{a}_{1}\},\{\mathbf{a}_{2}\},\sigma_{1}=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1}\},\sigma_{2}=\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2}\},\sigma_{3}=\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2}\}\}.

The geometric realization
|\mathcal{K}|
is a filled-in triangle with edges and vertices included. Define a function
f:|\mathcal{K}|\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(\mathbf{a}_{0})=0,f(\mathbf{a}_{1})=1,f(\mathbf{a}_{2})=2
and extend
f
linearly on each edge and inside the triangle.

Is
f
continuous on
|\mathcal{K}|
if and only if its restriction
f|_{\sigma}
is continuous for each simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
?

Solution.

To verify the theorem, we check continuity both locally (on each simplex) and globally (on
|\mathcal{K}|
).

(1)

Checking Continuity on Each Simplex:
Analyze the function
f
on each simplex separately:

•

On each vertex
\mathbf{a}_{i}
,
f
is trivially continuous.

•

On each edge
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
: Since
f
is linearly interpolated along the edges, it is continuous as a function on a line segment (which is homeomorphic to
[0,1]
).

•

On the triangle
\sigma_{3}
: Since
f
is defined linearly inside the triangle, it is continuous in the Euclidean topology on the triangle.

Thus, for each simplex
\sigma
, the restriction
f|_{\sigma}
is continuous.

(2)

Checking Global Continuity on
|\mathcal{K}|
:

Since
|\mathcal{K}|
has the weak topology, a function is continuous if its restriction to each simplex is continuous. We have already verified that
f|_{\sigma}
is continuous for every simplex
\sigma
. Therefore, by the continuity theorem,
f
must be continuous on
|\mathcal{K}|
.

∎

The main objective of introducing the coherent topology (or final topology) is to construct a topology on a space
X
based on a collection of subspaces
\{X_{i}\}
in a way that ensures the continuity of maps from each
X_{i}
into
X
. The coherent topology allows us to glue together different subspaces while preserving their topological structure. The simplicial complexes use the coherent topology to define their global topology from local simplices or cells. The geometric realization of a simplicial complex is given the coherent topology relative to its simplices.

Definition 4.15

(Coherent Topology)
.

Let
X
be a set and
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of subspaces of
X
with topologies
\tau_{i}
. The coherent topology (or final topology) on
X
with respect to
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the topology
\tau
defined by

\tau=\{U\subseteq X\colon U\cap X_{i}\in\tau_{i}\text{ for all }i\in I\}

Remark 4.16
.

Equivalently,
\tau
is the finest topology on
X
such that each inclusion map
\iota_{i}\colon X_{i}\to X
is continuous. This means that a function
f\colon X\to Y
into a topological space
Y
is continuous if and only if each restriction
f|_{X_{i}}\colon X_{i}\to Y
is continuous for all
i\in I
.

Theorem 4.17

(Coherent Topology on
|\mathcal{K}|
)
.

The topology of
|\mathcal{K}|
is coherent with the collection of subspace
\sigma
, for
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Let
|\mathcal{K}|
be the geometric realization of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, where each simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
is given the subspace topology inherited from
|\mathcal{K}|
. We want to show that the topology of
|\mathcal{K}|
, denoted by
\tau
, is coherent with the collection of subspaces
\{\sigma\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\}
, i.e.

\tau=\{U\subseteq|\mathcal{K}|\colon U\cap\sigma\text{ is open in }\sigma,\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\}.

First, we show that
\tau
is at least as fine as the coherent topology. Suppose
U
is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
, i.e.,
U\in\tau
. By the definition of the subspace topology, we have

U\cap\sigma\text{ is open in }\sigma,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

Since this is precisely the condition for
U
to be in the coherent topology, it follows that
U
belongs to the coherent topology. Hence,

U\in\tau\implies U\in\text{coherent topology}.

Next, we show that the coherent topology is at most as fine as
\tau
. Suppose
U
is open in the coherent topology, meaning that

U\cap\sigma\text{ is open in }\sigma,\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.

By definition, the topology on
|\mathcal{K}|
is the weakest topology that makes all inclusion maps
\iota_{\sigma}:\sigma\to|\mathcal{K}|
continuous. This means that a set is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
if and only if its restriction to each simplex is open in that simplex. Since
U\cap\sigma
is open in
\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
, it follows that
U
is open in
|\mathcal{K}|
, i.e.,

U\in\text{coherent topology}\implies U\in\tau.

Since both inclusions hold, we conclude that
\tau
is precisely the coherent topology, proving that the topology of
|\mathcal{K}|
is coherent with the collection of subspaces
\sigma
, for
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
.
∎

Definition 4.18

(Barycentric Coordinate)
.

Let
\sigma=[\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}]
be an
n
-dimensional simplex in a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
. The barycentric coordinate is function that is associated with a vertex
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\sigma
and defined as:

\lambda_{j}:|\mathcal{K}|\to[0,1]

such that for each
\mathbf{x}\in|\mathcal{K}|
, if
\mathbf{x}
belongs to some simplex
\sigma
, then
\mathbf{x}
can be uniquely expressed as a convex combination:

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{a}_{i},\quad\text{where }\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1\text{ and }\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\text{ for all }i.

The function
\lambda_{j}
assigns to each
\mathbf{x}
its barycentric coordinate with respect to
\mathbf{a}_{j}
, i.e.,

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\text{coefficient of }\mathbf{a}_{j}\text{ in the convex combination of }\mathbf{x}.

This function is continuous on
|\mathcal{K}|
and varies smoothly within each simplex. the barycentric coordinate function

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})
provides a natural way to describe the position of
\mathbf{x}
relative to the vertices of a simplex.

Example 4.12
.

Consider a triangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with vertices:

\mathbf{a}_{0}=(1,1),\quad\mathbf{a}_{1}=(4,2),\quad\mathbf{a}_{2}=(2,5).

Find the barycentric coordinates
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
of the point
\mathbf{x}=(3,3)
.

Solution.

Since
\mathbf{x}
is a convex combination of the vertices:

\mathbf{x}=\lambda_{0}\mathbf{a}_{0}+\lambda_{1}\mathbf{a}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{a}_{2}.

This gives the system:

\displaystyle 3=\lambda_{0}(1)+\lambda_{1}(4)+\lambda_{2}(2),

(72)

\displaystyle 3=\lambda_{0}(1)+\lambda_{1}(2)+\lambda_{2}(5),

(73)

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1.

(74)

Rewriting in matrix form:

\begin{bmatrix}1&4&2\\

Lemma 4.19

(Hausdorffness of Simplicial Complex)
.

The underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is a Hausdorff space.

Proof.

To prove that the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is a Hausdorff space, we must show that for any two distinct points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in|\mathcal{K}|
, there exist disjoint open neighborhoods
U
and
V
such that
\mathbf{x}\in U
and
\mathbf{y}\in V
.

The underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is the union of the simplices in
\mathcal{K}
, each endowed with the subspace topology from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
i.e.
|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}}\sigma
. Each simplex
\sigma
is a subset of some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the topology on
|\mathcal{K}|
is the coherent topology induced by the simplices. The key observation is that each individual simplex is Hausdorff, since it is a subset of Euclidean space, which is Hausdorff.

(1)

When
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
belong to different simplices:
Let
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{1}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\sigma_{2}
, where
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}=\emptyset
. Since each simplex
\sigma_{i}
is an open set in its ambient Euclidean space and simplices are disjoint, we can choose small open neighborhoods
U_{1}\subset\sigma_{1}
and
U_{2}\subset\sigma_{2}
such that
\mathbf{x}\in U_{1}
and
\mathbf{y}\in U_{2}
, with
U_{1}\cap U_{2}=\emptyset
. Thus, the points are separated by disjoint open sets, proving the Hausdorff property in this case.

(2)

When
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
belong to the same simplex:
Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\sigma
for some simplex
\sigma
. Since every simplex is a subset of Euclidean space, it inherits the standard topology of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is Hausdorff. That is, for any two distinct points in
\sigma
, there exist disjoint open sets separating them within
\sigma
, say
U,V\subset\sigma
, such that
U\cap V=\emptyset,\quad\mathbf{x}\in U,\quad\mathbf{y}\in V
. Since the topology of
|\mathcal{K}|
is coherent with the simplices, these sets
U
and
V
are open in
|\mathcal{K}|
as well, ensuring the separation condition.

(3)

When
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
belong to different simplices but share a common face:
Now, let
\mathbf{x}\in\sigma_{1}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\sigma_{2}
, but the simplices share a common face
\tau
, i.e.,
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}=\tau\neq\emptyset
. If either
\mathbf{x}
or
\mathbf{y}
belongs to
\tau
, then this reduces to the previous case of being within a single simplex, which we have already proven. Otherwise, they lie in different relative interiors of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
. Since relative interiors of disjoint simplices do not intersect, we can find small disjoint open neighborhoods
U_{1}\subset\sigma_{1}
and
U_{2}\subset\sigma_{2}
that are open in
|\mathcal{K}|
, ensuring separation.

We have shown that for any two distinct points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in|\mathcal{K}|
, whether they belong to the same simplex, disjoint simplices, or simplices with a shared face, we can always find disjoint open neighborhoods that separate them. Thus, the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
satisfies the Hausdorff separation property.
∎

Lemma 4.20

(Compactness of Simplicial Complex)
.

The underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
of a finite simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is compact. Conversely, if a subset
A
of
|\mathcal{K}|
is compact, then
A\subset|\mathcal{K}_{0}|
for some finite sub-complex
\mathcal{K}_{0}
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a finite simplicial complex. The underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is given by the finite union of its simplices as
|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}
, where each
\sigma_{i}
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Each simplex
\sigma_{i}
is the convex hull of a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, meaning it is a bounded and closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the Heine-Borel theorem, every closed and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact i.e.
\sigma_{i}
is compact for all
i=1,2,\dots,m
. Since compactness is preserved under finite unions, the finite union of compact sets remains compact i.e.
|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}
is compact.
∎

To describe the local properties of the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
, let us look at some special subspaces of
|\mathcal{K}|
which will definitely help us in studying the local properties of
|\mathcal{K}|
.

Definition 4.21

(Star of vertex in Simplicial Complex)
.

\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{a}
in

Remark 4.22
.

(1)

It provides a localized view around
\mathbf{a}

.

(2)

It plays an essential role in topological properties like local connectivity and defining subdivisions in simplicial complexes.

Definition 4.23

(Closure of Star of vertex in Simplicial Complex)
.

The closure of the star of

Lemma 4.26

(Existence of compact subspace in Simplicial Complex)
.

If
A
is subset of the underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is compact, then
A\subset|\mathcal{K}_{0}|
for some finite sub-complex
\mathcal{K}_{0}
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

The realization
|\mathcal{K}|
is covered by the interiors of its simplices i.e.
|\mathcal{K}|=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Int}(\sigma)
. Since
A\subset|\mathcal{K}|
, we can write
A\subset\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{Int}(\sigma)
.

The sets
\operatorname{Int}(\sigma)
form an open cover of
A
. Since
A
is compact, there exists a finite subcover, meaning there exist finitely many simplices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}
such that
A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{i})
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{0}
be the finite subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
generated by the simplices
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}\}
, meaning
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\{\tau\in\mathcal{K}\mid\tau\subseteq\sigma_{i}\text{ for some }i\}
. Since each simplex
\sigma_{i}
belongs to
\mathcal{K}
, all its faces are also in
\mathcal{K}
, ensuring that
\mathcal{K}_{0}
is a valid simplicial complex. The geometric realization of this subcomplex is
|\mathcal{K}_{0}|=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}|\sigma_{i}|
. Since
A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Int}(\sigma_{i})
and each simplex’s closure is contained in
|\sigma_{i}|
, we get
A\subset|\mathcal{K}_{0}|
.
∎

Definition 4.27

(Locally Finite Simplicial Complex)
.

A simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is said to be locally finite if every vertex
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{K}
, the set
\{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\colon\mathbf{a}\in\sigma\}
is finite i.e. each vertex is contained in only finitely many simplices.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite is for each vertex
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{K}
, its star
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K},\mathbf{a}\in\sigma}\sigma
is composed of only finitely may simplices.

Theorem 4.28
.

A simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite if and only if each closed star
\mathrm{st}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
is polytope of a finite sub-complex of
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}
is a locally finite simplicial complex. By definition, this means that for every vertex
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{K}
, the set of simplices containing
\mathbf{a}
is finite i.e.
\{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\colon\mathbf{a}\in\sigma\}
is finite. The closed star of a vertex
\mathbf{a}
is given by
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K},\mathbf{a}\in\sigma}\sigma
. Since
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite, the number of simplices containing
\mathbf{a}
is finite. Thus, the union in the definition of
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
is taken over a finite collection of simplices. Let
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
be the smallest subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
that contains all simplices in
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
. Since this collection is finite,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
is a finite subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
. The geometric realization
|\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})|
is a bounded region in Euclidean space formed by the simplices in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
. Since
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
is a finite subcomplex, its geometric realization forms a polytope. Thus we have shown that
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
is the polytope of a finite subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
.

Conversely, assume that for every vertex
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{K}
, the closed star
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
is the polytope of a finite subcomplex
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}\subset\mathcal{K}
. By assumption,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
is a finite subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}
. This means that it contains only finitely many simplices. Since
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
contains all simplices that contribute to
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
, the number of simplices in
\mathcal{K}
that contain
\mathbf{a}
is at most the number of simplices in
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{a}}
, which is finite. Since we have established that for every vertex
\mathbf{a}
, the set
\{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}\colon\mathbf{a}\in\sigma\}
is finite, it follows that
\mathcal{K}
satisfies the local finiteness condition. Thus,
\mathcal{K}
is a locally finite simplicial complex.

∎

Lemma 4.29
.

The simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite if and only if the their underlying space
|\mathcal{K}|
is locally compact.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{K}
is a locally finite simplicial complex, meaning that every vertex
\mathbf{a}
of
\mathcal{K}
is contained in only finitely many simplices. Our aim is to show that
\mathcal{K}
is locally compact
2
2

2

A topological space
X
is locally compact if every point
x\in X
has a compact neighborhood, i.e., for every
x\in X
, there exists an open set
U
containing
x
such that the closure
\overline{U}
is compact
. We need to show that every point in
|\mathcal{K}|
has a compact neighborhood and for this we construct it as follows. Consider a point
\mathbf{x}\in|\mathcal{K}|
, the geometric realization of the simplicial complex. There are two cases:

•

Case 1:
If
\mathbf{x}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{K}
, say
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{a}
, then the closed star
\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})
is defined as

\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K},\mathbf{a}\in\sigma}\sigma
.
Since
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite, only finitely many simplices contain
\mathbf{a}
. Hence, the realization
|\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})|
is a finite union of compact simplices (each of which is compact in the subspace topology of Euclidean space). A finite union of compact sets is compact, so
|\operatorname{St}(\mathbf{a},\mathcal{K})|
is a compact neighborhood of
\mathbf{a}
.

•

Case 2:
If
\mathbf{x}
is an interior point of a higher-dimensional simplex, it still belongs to finitely many simplices due to local finiteness. A small enough open neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
will be contained in the closed star of some nearby vertex, ensuring compactness.

Since every point in
|\mathcal{K}|
has a compact neighborhood,
|\mathcal{K}|
is locally compact.

Conversely, assume that
|\mathcal{K}|
is locally compact, meaning that for every point
x\in|\mathcal{K}|
, there exists an open neighborhood
U
such that
\overline{U}
is compact.

For any vertex
\mathbf{a}
in
\mathcal{K}
, take a small open neighborhood
U
around
\mathbf{a}
such that
\overline{U}
is compact. Since
|\mathcal{K}|
is a simplicial complex,
\overline{U}
must be covered by finitely many simplices. Since
\overline{U}
is compact and each simplex in
\mathcal{K}
is a compact subset of Euclidean space, only finitely many simplices can intersect
\overline{U}
. But every simplex containing
\mathbf{a}
must be entirely contained in
\overline{U}
, implying that only finitely many simplices contain
\mathbf{a}
. Since this holds for every vertex
\mathbf{a}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{K}
is locally finite.
