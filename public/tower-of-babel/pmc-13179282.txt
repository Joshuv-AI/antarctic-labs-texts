Germline Pathogenic Variants in HER2‐Positive Breast Cancer: Spectrum and Clinical Implications in a High‐Risk Chinese Cohort
Authors: Lijia Zhou, Mengqian Ni, Anli Yang, Qingru Zhou, Daining Wang, Shu Dun, Fei Xu, Jiajia Huang, Xiwen Bi, Wen Xia, Ruoxi Hong, Qiufan Zheng, Meiting Chen, Kuikui Jiang, Jun Tang, Xi Wang, Zhongyu Yuan, Shusen Wang, Yanxia Shi, Fang Wang, Xin An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective Hereditary cancer risk assessment has predominantly focused on HER2‐negative breast cancer (BC), with limited characterization of germline pathogenic variants (GPVs) in HER2‐positive disease. This study aimed to delineate the prevalence, clinicopathological correlates, and clinical implications of GPVs in a cohort of HER2‐positive BC patients.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed BC patients who had undergone genetic testing at the Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center from 2014 to 2024. GPV profiles, clinicopathological features, and survival outcomes were compared between HER2‐positive and HER2‐negative patients, with additional stratified analysis in HER2‐positive GPV carriers.ResultsAmong 1692 bc patients, 1,499 patients met NCCN high‐risk criteria. In multigene panel testing, GPVs were identified in 18.1% (52/288) of HER2‐positive BC and 22.4% (269/1,201) of HER2‐negative BC patients. HER2‐positive GPV carriers exhibited distinct molecular profiles, with higher frequencies of BRCA2 (23/52, 44.2%) and TP53 (14/52, 26.9%) mutations and a lower BRCA1 prevalence (6/52, 11.5%) compared to HER2‐negative carriers (BRCA2: 106/269, 37.9%; TP53: 9/269, 3.3%; BRCA1: 117/269, 43.5%). Clinically, HER2‐positive carriers were diagnosed at a younger median age, had higher hormone receptor (HR) positivity, and less frequently reported a family history of BRCA‐related cancers. GPVs were not associated with increased locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis in HER2‐positive BC but significantly elevated the risk of contralateral BC and other secondary primary cancers—a risk particularly pronounced in TP53 carriers.ConclusionsHER2‐positive BC patients harbored a distinct GPV spectrum compared to HER2‐negative patients. While GPVs did not worsen primary BC‐specific outcomes, they conferred a significantly increased risk of second primary cancers, underscoring the clinical utility of multigene panel testing for comprehensive risk stratification and long‐term management in this population.Study LimitationsThis retrospective, single‐center study comprised a clinically enriched high‐risk cohort, which may limit the generalizability of prevalence estimates. The extended inclusion period and evolving classification standards, despite reannotation, represent inherent methodological constraints.

Introduction

The detection of germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (GPVs) has assumed an increasingly important role in the management of breast cancer (BC), informing strategies for screening, prevention, and treatment [1]. This paradigm shift has been driven largely by the approval of Poly‐(ADP)‐ribose polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) for patients with germline BRCA (gBRCA)‐mutated, HER2‐negative BC, spanning both metastatic and high‐risk early‐stage disease. Consequently, the demand for gBRCA testing in BC patients has risen substantially [2, 3, 4]. Although current guidelines for genetic testing eligibility vary, they collectively reflect a trend toward broader inclusion: Some advocate testing for individuals at high risk and all candidates for PARPi therapy irrespective of traditional risk factors, while a growing body of evidence supports the rationale for universal testing [5].

Notably, despite this expanding emphasis on genetic testing, research to date has predominantly focused on HER2‐negative BC. As a result, the prevalence and full spectrum of GPVs in HER2‐positive disease remain poorly characterized. Prior studies reported widely variable HER2‐positivity rates among gBRCA1/2 mutated BC, ranging from 0% to 10% in BRCA1 carriers and 0% to 13% in BRCA2 carriers [6]. The largest pooled analysis to date, comprising 21,083 gBRCA1/2 mutated BC cases, found that HER2‐positive disease accounted for 9.1% (95% CI, 7.3%–11.2%) [7], indicating that the presence of gBRCA mutations is not as rare in HER2‐positive BC as previously believed. Nevertheless, the clinical implications of these mutations within this subtype are not well defined. Recent reports on their prognostic impact have been conflicting [8, 9], and the efficacy of PARPi in gBRCA‐mutated, HER2‐positive BC remains uncertain.

Compared with BRCA‐only testing, multigene panel testing can improve diagnostic yield by up to 50% and identify additional clinically actionable non‐BRCA GPVs, driving its increasing adoption in clinical practice. For example, PALB2, now recognized as a PARPi‐sensitive gene, has demonstrated clinical response to olaparib treatment [10, 11]. Despite this, few studies have specifically investigated the utility of multigene panel testing in HER2‐positive BCs, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of its utility in this population. Our previous study has shown that among 184 HER2‐positive BC patients undergoing panel testing, 27 (14.7%) harbored GPVs, of which 15 (55.6%) were in BRCA1/2, and 12 (44.4%) in non‐BRCA genes, highlighting the potential importance of comprehensive genetic evaluation in this population [12].

To better characterize GPV profiles in HER2‐positive BC, this study analyzed a larger cohort of BC patients who underwent panel testing, aiming to compare mutation profiles between HER2‐positive and HER2‐negative BC, assess clinical differences between HER2‐positive BC patients with or without GPVs, and evaluate survival outcomes in HER2‐positive BC stratified by GPV status. These findings are intended to help refine genetic testing strategies for HER2‐positive BC patients, thereby guiding more personalized risk assessment and therapeutic decision‐making.

Methods

Study Population

In this retrospective cohort study, we reviewed all patients with pathologically confirmed BC who underwent clinical genetic counseling and germline genetic testing at the Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) between 2014 and 2024. Comprehensive clinical data were extracted from institutional electronic medical records, including demographics, personal and family cancer history, tumor pathological characteristics, genetic testing results, treatment details, and survival outcomes. The study protocol was approved by the SYSUCC Institutional Review Board (IRB) and China's Ministry of Science and Technology for human genetic resource management (Approval No. SL‐B2025‐042‐01).

Pathologic Assessment

Tumor expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 was evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC). ER and PR positivity were defined as nuclear staining in ≥ 1% of tumor cells. HER2 positivity was defined as either IHC 3+ or IHC 2+ with confirmatory fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrating gene amplification, consistent with current ASCO/CAP guidelines.

Genetic Testing

Germline DNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells obtained from all participants. Testing from 2014 to 2016 targeted only BRCA1 and BRCA2. Starting in 2017, we employed an expanded 21‐gene cancer panel (BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, PALB2, STK11, CDH1, PTEN, RAD51C, CHEK2, ATM, BRIP1, RAD50, BARD1, MUTYH, MRE11A, NBN, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS1, and PMS2). All sequencing was carried out on the BGISEQ‐2000 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) at the Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory of SYSUCC.

Genetic Variant Classification and Analysis

To ensure temporal consistency across the extended study period, the entire dataset underwent a comprehensive re‐review. All germline variants were reclassified, adhering to the latest ClinGen and ACMG/AMP guidelines. This process involved consulting authoritative resources, including ClinVar, ClinGen, Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD), BRCA Exchange, and Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) for pathogenicity assessment [13]. Following reclassification, variants were classified into standard categories: Benign variant (class I), likely benign variant (class II), variant of uncertain significance (VUS, class III), likely pathogenic variant (LPV, class IV), and pathogenic variant (PV, class V). Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were aggregated and termed germline pathogenic variants (GPVs).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Clinical endpoints were defined as follows: Locoregional recurrence (LRR) referred to recurrence in the ipsilateral breast/chest wall or regional lymph nodes; distant metastasis (DM) referred to recurrence at any other site; metachronous contralateral breast cancer (MCBC) was defined as a new primary breast cancer diagnosed > 6 months after the initial diagnosis (including in situ and invasive disease); and other new primary cancers. Synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC) was defined as contralateral disease diagnosed within ≤ 6 months of the initial primary. Breast cancer recurrence‐free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of primary diagnosis to the first occurrence of LRR, DM, death from any cause, or the last follow‐up. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of primary diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow‐up. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test. To balance baseline characteristics between GPV carriers and non‐carriers, propensity score matching (PSM) was implemented, with matching covariates including age, disease stage, hormone receptor status, and primary treatment modality. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25), Microsoft Excel 2019, and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).

Results

Distribution of GPVs in HER2‐Positive and HER2‐Negative BC

Figure 1 outlines the patient screening and germline genetic testing workflow. The study enrolled 1692 patients, comprising 311 (18.4%) with HER2‐positive and 1381 (81.6%) with HER2‐negative BC. The majority of patients met the NCCN high‐risk criteria for genetic testing: 92.6% (288/311) in the HER2‐positive group and 87.7% (1211/1381) in the HER2‐negative group. Among HER2‐positive patients, GPVs were identified in 13.0% (3/23), who underwent gBRCA1/2‐only testing, and in 18.1% (52/288) who received multigene panel testing. Among HER2‐negative patients, GPVs were detected in 20.6% (37/180) tested with gBRCA1/2‐only testing, and 22.4% (269/1,201) tested with the multigene panel. Figure 2 details the distribution of GPVs among patients who underwent panel testing. In HER2‐positive BC, the most frequent GPVs occurred in BRCA2 (23/52, 44.2%), TP53 (14/52, 26.9%), and BRCA1 (6/52, 11.5%). In contrast, HER2‐negative tumors predominantly harbored GPVs in BRCA1 (117/269, 43.5%), BRCA2 (102/269, 37.9%), and PALB2 (17/269, 6.3%). VUS accounted for 20.0% and 31.3% in HER2‐negative and HER2‐positive groups, respectively (Figure S1).

Clinicopathological Characteristics of HER2‐Positive BC With GPVs

Clinicopathological features of HER2‐positive BC GPV carriers were compared with those of HER2‐negative GPV carriers (Table 1) and with HER2‐positive non‐carriers (Table 2). Overall, HER2‐positive GPV carriers were diagnosed at a younger median age and presented with a higher frequency of hormone receptor (HR)‐positive disease and lymph node (LN) involvement and synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC), although not all differences reached statistical significance. Tumor size and histological grade did not differ significantly between groups. Moreover, a family history of BRCA‐related cancers (breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic) was observed less frequently among HER2‐positive GPV carriers compared to HER2‐negative GPV carriers. Among HER2‐positive BC patients, distinct clinical characteristics were observed according to the specific gene altered (Table 3). Patients with TP53 GPVs were diagnosed at the youngest age, with all 14 cases diagnosed before the age of 40, and 13 cases diagnosed before the age of 35. TP53 GPV carriers also exhibited higher rates of LN positivity, SBBC, and high histologic grade, and a lower HR‐positive disease rate. Notably, HER2‐positive GPV carriers demonstrated a substantially elevated risk of contralateral breast cancer (both synchronous and metachronous). This risk was particularly pronounced in TP53 GPV carriers, who showed a significantly higher incidence of contralateral cancer compared to other GPV subtypes (Figure 3).

Discussion

The current study delineates the spectrum and clinical implications of GPVs in a selective cohort of HER2‐positive BC. A similar proportion of HER2‐positive (92.6%) and HER2‐negative (87.7%) patients met the NCCN high‐risk criteria for genetic testing. Using a 21‐gene panel, GPVs were identified in 18.1% of HER2‐positive and 22.4% of HER2‐negative BC. Among all patients with BRCA1/2 GPVs, the HER2‐positive subtype accounted for 11.2% (32/286), a proportion slightly higher than the 9.1% reported in a large‐scale study of the Caucasian population [14].

Notably, a significantly higher proportion of GPVs in HER2‐positive BC involved non‐BRCA genes (45.3% vs. 19.7% in HER2‐negative BC). Furthermore, the mutational profiles differed between subtypes: In HER2‐positive BC, BRCA2 and TP53 were the most frequently altered, followed by BRCA1, ATM, PALB2, and RAD51C. Whereas in HER2‐negative BC, BRCA1 and BRCA2 predominated, followed by PALB2, TP53, and ATM. These findings underscore the necessity of panel testing in HER2‐positive BC rather than BRCA‐only testing.

HER2‐positive BC patients with GPVs were significantly younger at diagnosis than both HER2‐negative GPV carriers and HER2‐positive non‐carriers, with TP53 GPV carriers exhibiting the earliest onset. This is consistent with prior reports of elevated HER2 positivity among young TP53 GPV carriers [15, 16]. Although these observations highlight the importance of multigene panel testing in younger HER2‐positive patients, 12.7% (7/55) of HER2‐positive GPV carriers were diagnosed after age 50, indicating that age alone is an insufficient screening criterion. Moreover, a family history of BRCA‐associated cancers was less commonly reported in HER2‐positive GPV carriers than in HER2‐negative carriers, likely reflecting the higher prevalence of BRCA1—a gene strongly linked to familial clustering—in HER2‐negative disease. This finding challenges the utility of conventional family history‐based criteria for genetic testing in HER2‐positive BC. A higher rate of HR‐positivity was also observed in HER2‐positive GPV carriers, a pattern that may be attributable in part to the known association between BRCA2 mutations and HR‐positive breast cancer [17].

Our study identified a significantly elevated incidence of contralateral BC (both synchronous and metachronous) in HER2‐positive GPV carriers compared to HER2‐negative GPV carriers and HER2‐positive non‐carriers. This risk was particularly pronounced in TP53 GPV carriers, among whom 14.2% (2/14) presented with SBBC, and 50.0% (7/14) developed MCBC, consistent with recent data showing a 25.3% 5‐year cumulative incidence of MCBC in early‐stage BC patients with TP53 GPVs [18]. These findings underscore the importance of enhanced contralateral BC surveillance and early discussion of risk‐reducing mastectomy (RRM) options in HER2‐positive breast cancer patients with germline pathogenic variants, particularly those harboring TP53 mutations. Beyond MCBC, other secondary primary cancers, including lung cancer, glioblastoma, gastric cancer, fibrosarcoma, and endometrial cancer, were observed in TP53 carriers, in keeping with the known tumor spectrum of Li‐Fraumeni syndrome.

Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the prognostic impact of gBRCA1/2 mutations in HER2‐positive BC. Viansone et al. reported poor OS in BRCA‐mutated HER2‐positive cases [9], whereas Akkoc Mustafayev et al. found no significant survival difference between BRCA carriers and non‐carriers [8]. These discrepancies are likely due to differences in cohort selection and endpoint definitions. Moreover, the clinical implications of non‐BRCA GPVs in this setting remain poorly characterized. In the present study, we systematically evaluated the impact of both BRCA and non‐BRCA GPVs in HER2‐positive BC using distinct clinical endpoints: LRR and DM from primary BC, as well as second primary cancers, including MCBC and other cancers. Our results indicate that GPVs were not associated with an increased risk of recurrence and metastasis from primary BC. However, GPV carriers exhibited a notably higher incidence of second primary cancers. Importantly, patients carrying germline TP53 mutations had a particularly high risk of developing secondary cancers, including MCBC.

The therapeutic potential of PARPi in HER2‐positive BC remains investigational. The phase II OPHELIA trial, which evaluated olaparib combined with trastuzumab in HER2‐positive metastatic BC, was terminated early due to slow accrual. However, preliminary signals of clinical benefit were observed in 4 of 5 evaluable patients [19], suggesting this combination merits further exploration. Therefore, the current clinical utility of genetic testing in HER2‐positive BC lies primarily in risk stratification, tailored surveillance, prevention of second primary cancers, and cascade testing for at‐risk family members, rather than in guiding immediate, mutation‐specific systemic therapy.

Several limitations of our current study should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design and the inclusion of patients tested over nearly a decade may introduce heterogeneity in variant classification practices, despite efforts to align interpretations with contemporary standards. Furthermore, our cohort is enriched with high‐risk patients referred based on clinical features such as young age, personal or family history of cancer, which may introduce testing indication bias and affect the generalizability of the estimated variant prevalence. Second, as a single‐center study from a tertiary cancer center in East Asia, the genetic spectrum reported may not be fully generalizable to other populations. Third, the sample size of HER2‐positive cases remains moderate, and the median follow‐up of less than five years may be insufficient to capture the late survival outcomes or very long‐term risks of secondary cancers. Larger, prospective, multi‐ethnic studies are warranted for validation.

Conclusion

HER2‐positive BC with GPVs exhibits distinct genetic and clinical features compared to HER2‐negative disease. HER2‐positive GPV carriers presented with a higher prevalence of non‐BRCA GPVs, younger age at onset, more frequent HR‐positive disease, and fewer BRCA‐linked family histories. GPVs were not associated with an increased risk of recurrence or metastasis from primary BC, but were linked to an elevated risk of second primary cancers, particularly contralateral BC. These results underscore the clinical utility of multigene panel testing in guiding comprehensive risk management for HER2‐positive breast cancer.
