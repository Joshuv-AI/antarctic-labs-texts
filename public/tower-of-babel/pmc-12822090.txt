Urban-rural heterogeneity in the association of indoor air pollution exposure with early childhood development among children aged 0–5 years in a Chinese plateau area
Authors: Yajie Li, Bin Yu, Zhuhong Cui, Guoxia Bai, Li Yin, Xianzhi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests indoor air pollution (IAP) from solid fuel use may adversely affect early childhood development (ECD), yet urban-rural disparities in these associations are not well characterized, particularly in high-altitude regions. This study investigated the disparate effects of solid cooking and heating fuel exposure on ECD across urban and rural communities in Tibet, China.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 1,085 children from the Tibetan plateau. IAP exposure was determined through validated questionnaires, while ECD was assessed using the Chinese version of the Age and Stage Questionnaires, Third Edition (ASQ-3). Multivariable logistic regression models evaluated associations between IAP exposure and developmental outcomes, with urban-rural effect heterogeneity examined through interaction tests.ResultsSolid cooking fuel exposure was significantly associated with developmental delays, particularly in fine motor (OR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.42–3.56) and communication skills (OR = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.29–3.76). Whereas solid heating fuels showed no overall effect, they specifically impaired fine motor (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.54–7.69) and problem-solving abilities (OR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.26–3.90). Most notably, combined exposure exhibited a synergistic effect, with the risk of fine motor impairment escalating markedly (OR = 6.27, 95% CI: 2.34–16.84). Furthermore, this association was significantly modified by residential location, being more pronounced in rural (OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.28–2.56) than in urban children (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.32–1.54; P for difference < 0.05).ConclusionsHousehold solid fuel use for cooking and heating is associated with domain-specific developmental delays, with effects most pronounced in rural communities. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions to transition to cleaner household energy sources in high-altitude regions, which is essential to safeguard early child development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26001-z.Keywords: Indoor air pollution, Early childhood development, Solid fuel use, Plateau area, Children

Introduction

Indoor air pollution (IAP) generated from the incomplete combustion of solid fuels, such as wood, coal, and animal dung, represents a major global environmental health risk. It is estimated that approximately 2.8 billion people worldwide, primarily in developing regions, rely on these fuels for daily cooking and heating [1]. This reliance leads to significant indoor concentrations of harmful particulate matter and gases, resulting in an estimated 3.2 million premature deaths annually [2, 3]. Beyond its well-documented links to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [4], there is growing concern regarding the impact of IAP on neurodevelopment, particularly during early childhood.

The early years of life constitute a critical period of rapid brain development and heightened plasticity [5]. During this vulnerable window, exposure to neurotoxicants can disrupt fundamental neurological processes, potentially leading to long-term deficits in cognitive and motor functions [6–8]. Such developmental delays not only affect individual life trajectories but also impose substantial societal and economic burdens [9]. Consequently, identifying and mitigating modifiable environmental risk factors like IAP is crucial for achieving global sustainable development goals related to child health and well-being [10].

Although numerous studies have linked ambient air pollution (e.g., PM2.5, NO2, and CO) to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in children [11–15], the evidence concerning IAP is considerably less extensive. Given the compositional similarities between IAP and outdoor pollution [16], it is biologically plausible that IAP exerts comparable neurotoxic effects. A limited but growing number of epidemiological studies, conducted in settings like Ghana, Bangladesh, and the United States, have begun to substantiate this concern, reporting associations between solid fuel use (SFU) for cooking and an increased risk of developmental delays in young children [17–19]. However, a significant knowledge gap persists, as no such study has been conducted in mainland China, where household energy profiles and cultural contexts differ considerably.

Furthermore, the existing literature exhibits several specific limitations that our study seeks to address. First, the impact of IAP may be exacerbated in unique geographical settings. The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), renowned for its pristine outdoor air, paradoxically faces severe indoor air quality challenges due to the heavy dependence on biomass fuels for heating and cooking in a cold climate, with documented PM2.5 levels far exceeding WHO guidelines [20]. In 2017, it was estimated that IAP caused 1,804.5 disability-adjusted life years and 75.8 age-standardized deaths per 100,000 people in Tibet [21]. However, the potential synergistic effects of IAP and high-altitude hypoxia on the developing brain are entirely unexplored. Second, the distinct and potentially synergistic contributions of solid fuels used for cooking versus heating to developmental outcomes have not been adequately disentangled. Third, although urban-rural disparities in IAP exposure are recognized, it remains unclear whether the association between IAP and early childhood development (ECD) is modified by residential location—a critical question for targeting interventions. Children in rural areas are often more vulnerable due to a confluence of factors, including higher exposure levels, poorer living conditions, and lower socioeconomic status [21, 22].

To address these evidence gaps, we conducted a cross-sectional study among children aged 0–5 years in the Tibetan Plateau, China. The novelty of our work lies in its specific focus on this high-altitude population, its comprehensive assessment of IAP from both cooking and heating sources, and its explicit investigation of urban-rural heterogeneity in the IAP-ECD association. We hypothesized that: (1) exposure to solid fuels for cooking and heating would be associated with an increased risk of developmental delays, with effects varying across specific developmental domains; (2) a dose-response relationship would exist between the level of IAP exposure and the risk of delay; and (3) these associations would be significantly stronger among children living in rural areas compared to their urban counterparts. The findings from this study are expected to provide vital evidence to inform targeted public health strategies and clean energy policies aimed at safeguarding neurodevelopment in children residing in high-altitude and other vulnerable settings.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a community-based cross-sectional survey in Tibet, China, during April and May 2023. A multi-stage stratified random sampling method was employed to recruit participants. In the first stage, seven counties within the region were selected with a probability proportional to their population size. Subsequently, two towns or sub-districts were randomly chosen within each selected county. In the final stage, three villages or communities were randomly selected from each town/sub-district, resulting in a total of 42 sampling units. Within these units, children were deemed eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) were aged 0–5 years; (2) were permanent residents; and (3) had a primary caregiver available who provided informed consent and completed the questionnaire. All eligible children from the selected units were invited to participate, yielding an initial sample of 1,198 children.This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tibet Center for Disease Control and Prevention (2021-001) and all participants’ parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent forms before formal enrollment.

In the current analyses, we excluded children who (1) were aged under 1 month or over 66 months; (2) came from a household that never cooks; and (3) had missing data on ECD outcomes. Ultimately, 1085 children were included (Figure S1).

Exposure assessment

To contextualize the pollution levels in our study area, we note that a recent investigation in the same Tibetan plateau region reported that indoor PM2.5 concentrations in households using solid fuels far exceeded WHO guidelines, with mean levels often surpassing 518 mg/m3 [20], substantiating the significant IAP challenge. Information on household cooking and heating practices was collected via interviewer-administered questionnaires. A primary limitation of our exposure assessment is the lack of direct, real-time measurements of air pollutant concentrations (e.g., PM2.5, CO). Instead, exposure was assessed using a validated questionnaire, a method widely employed in large-scale epidemiological studies [23–25]. Our classification of solid fuels (coal, wood, crop residues, animal dung, charcoal) versus clean fuels (electricity, liquefied petroleum gas, natural gas, biogas) is consistent with established classifications in the household air pollution literature [23–25]. To create a more nuanced exposure variable for solid cooking fuels, we adapted a classification scheme from a previous study by Rana et al. [19], which integrates the primary cooking fuel type, cooking location, and the presence of ventilation—key behavioral determinants of actual personal exposure. Children were classified as ‘highly exposed’ if their households met al.l of the following criteria: primarily used solid fuels for cooking, cooked indoors without a ventilation hood or chimney, and did not regularly open windows during cooking [19]. The ‘unexposed’ group comprised children from households that used clean fuels and cooked outdoors or in a separate kitchen [19]. All other combinations were categorized as ‘moderately exposed’ [19]. This multi-dimensional approach provides a more refined proxy for exposure intensity than fuel type alone.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome, early childhood development (ECD), was assessed using the culturally adapted and standardized Chinese version of the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, Third Edition (ASQ-3) [26, 27]. The Chinese ASQ-3 has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with reported Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.76 to 0.88 across the five domains, and test-retest reliability coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.91, indicating strong internal consistency and stability [26].

This tool comprises 21 age-specific questionnaires (for age intervals of 2, 4, 6, 8, 9,10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24, 27, 30, 33, 36, 42, 48, 54, and 60 months) designed to be completed by parents or primary caregivers to screen developmental progress in children from 1 to 66 months of age. The ASQ-3 evaluates child development across five key domains: communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem-solving, and personal-social skills. Each domain contains six items. For each item, caregivers indicate whether their child can perform the activity ‘always,’ ‘sometimes,’ or ‘not yet,’ which corresponds to scores of 10, 5, and 0 points, respectively. The scores for the six items within a domain are summed, yielding a total domain score ranging from 0 to 60 [28].

A child was considered to have a developmental delay in a specific domain if their total score fell more than two standard deviations (SD) below the mean score of the age-specific Chinese normative sample, as established by Wei et al. [26]. For example, for the 36-month interval, the normative means (and SDs) are as follows: Communication: 48.4 (9.9), Gross Motor: 51.3 (8.7), Fine Motor: 48.9 (10.1), Problem-Solving: 48.7 (9.9), Personal-Social: 50.1 (9.6). A score below 28.6 in the Communication domain at this age would thus indicate a delay.

To ensure data quality, the questionnaires were administered by trained interviewers. The interviewers provided standardized instructions to the primary caregivers, answered clarifying questions, and checked the questionnaires for completeness before collection. The outcome ‘any fail’ was defined as a delay in one or more of the five domains [16].

Covariates assessment

The multivariable models included the following covariates: child’s age (in months, continuous) and sex; ethnicity (Han or Tibetan); singleton status (yes/no); area of residence (urban or rural); and altitude of residence (continuous, in kilometers). Birth history was accounted for by adjusting for premature delivery (defined as gestational age < 37 weeks) [29] and low birth weight (a birth weight below 2500 g) [30]. Socioeconomic and maternal factors included maternal age, maternal education level, and annual household income. Child-rearing environment and behavioral factors were captured by the number of toys owned (categorized as < 4 or ≥ 4 based on the median), average daily outdoor playtime (< 1 or ≥ 1 h/day) [31], exposure to secondhand smoke (yes/no), and whether the home had been renovated in the year preceding the survey [32].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study participants. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and differences between groups (e.g., children with vs. without any developmental delay) were assessed using the Chi-squared test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and group differences were evaluated using Student’s t-tests.

We employed multivariable logistic regression to quantify the associations between IAP exposure variables (e.g., solid cooking fuel use, solid heating fuel use, combined exposure, and the ordinal exposure level) and each ECD outcome (any fail, and domain-specific fails). Potential confounders were selected a priori based on their established biological or sociological plausibility as common causes of both IAP exposure and ECD, drawing from the existing literature [16, 19]. The fully adjusted multivariable models included all selected covariates, encompassing child characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, singleton status), birth history (premature delivery, low birth weight), socioeconomic and maternal factors (area of residence, maternal age and education, annual household income), and child-rearing environment and behavioral factors (number of toys, daily outdoor playtime, secondhand smoke exposure, altitude of residence, and home renovation). We did not perform automated variable selection to ensure our approach was based on subject-matter knowledge rather than statistical algorithms. Continuous covariates (child’s age and altitude of residence) were modeled as continuous linear terms after confirming linearity assumptions with fractional polynomials. Categorical variables were included as factors in the models. The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The goodness-of-fit of the primary logistic regression models was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with a non-significant p-value indicating adequate fit, and model explanatory power was summarized using McFadden’s pseudo R2.

To assess potential dose-response relationships across the three-level cooking fuel exposure classification (unexposed, moderately exposed, highly exposed), we treated this variable as a continuous ordinal term in the regression models and tested for linear trends using the Wald test. To formally test for effect modification by residential location (urban vs. rural), we introduced a multiplicative interaction term between the IAP exposure variable and the residence variable into the fully adjusted logistic regression model. The statistical significance of this interaction term was assessed using the likelihood ratio test.

To assess the robustness of our findings and the impact of confounding, we performed two key sensitivity analyses. First, we evaluated the influence of individual confounders by comparing the fully adjusted model with a series of reduced models that sequentially introduced blocks of covariates (e.g., child’s basic demographics and birth history, then socioeconomic and maternal factors, then detailed child-rearing environment and behavioral factors). The consistency of the odds ratios (ORs) across these models was examined. Second, we further adjusted for the child’s recent history of illness (within two weeks preceding the survey) to assess the potential for reverse causality or confounding by acute health status.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.2). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

IAP exposure and ECD outcomes

The associations of IAP exposure with ECD outcomes are presented in Table 2. Solid cooking fuels exposure increased the risk of failure in any developmental, fine motor, gross motor, and communication domain by 58%, 125%, 48%, 121%, and 71%, respectively. There is no statistical association between solid heating fuels exposure and failure in any developmental domain, but solid heating fuels exposure increased the odds of failing the fine motor and problem-solving domain by 245% and 122%, respectively. Both SFU for cooking and heating was associated with failing any developmental, fine motor, and problem-solving domain, with adjusted odds of 1.80 (95%CI: 1.15, 2.83), 6.27 (95% CI: 2.34, 16.84), and 2.76 (95% CI: 1.48, 5.13), respectively. Sensitivity analyses produced identical results (Table S1 and Table S2).

Figure 1 showed the associations of solid fuels exposure with ECD outcomes stratified by residence groups. We observed significant and positive associations between solid cooking fuels exposure and failure in any domain (OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.28, 2.56), fine motor domain (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.29, 3.48), gross motor domain (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.50), communication domain (OR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.24, 4.10), and problem-solving domain (OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.23, 2.78) among children living in rural areas, while the associations were not significant among children living in urban areas. Discussion

While the effects of IAP on child growth and development have been documented in lowland regions [16, 19], evidence from high-altitude settings remains limited. Our study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between IAP exposure from SFU and ECD on the QTP. Despite the region’s reputation for ambient air purity, it confronts significant challenges related to degraded indoor air quality [20]. Our analysis revealed that SFU adversely affects ECD, with the most marked deficits observed in fine motor and problem-solving abilities. A dose-response pattern was evident, as moderate and high exposure to solid cooking fuels corresponded with progressively elevated risks of developmental impairment. Furthermore, the adverse association between solid cooking fuel use and ECD was significantly stronger in rural communities compared to urban areas.

Our findings indicate that SFU for cooking adversely affects nearly all developmental domains except interpersonal skills, consistent with observations in other low-resource settings [16, 19]. For instance, a study in Bangladesh reported that SFU was positively associated with delays in learning and cognitive domains [19]. Early life represents a critical window of brain development, and young children typically spend substantial time indoors, making them particularly vulnerable to IAP [33]. Mechanistically, solid fuel combustion releases a range of air pollutants, many of which are known to impair neurodevelopment. These pollutants can translocate nanosized particles into the central nervous system [34] or induce systemic inflammation via circulating cytokines, leading to oxidative stress and potential brain injury [35, 36]. Notably, we found that solid fuel use for heating (SFU-heating) exhibited stronger associations with certain developmental deficits than SFU-cooking. Prior studies have demonstrated a noteworthy adverse impact linked to the major space heating fuel on individual exposure when cooking and space heating activities occur simultaneously [37]. Interestingly, SFU for cooking was additionally associated with impairments in communication and gross motor skills. This suggests that regular, long-term exposure to cooking related pollutants may exert distinct effects on these domains, as opposed to the seasonal or intermittent nature of heating-related exposure. Further experimental studies are warranted to clarify the underlying pathways and confirm these observational associations.

Beyond the overall associations, the urban-rural disparity in the association between SFU and ECD warrants further attention. Our results indicate that exposure to solid cooking and heating fuels was significantly associated with developmental delays in rural areas, but not in urban settings. This heterogeneity may be partly attributable to differential fuel usage patterns. Rural households in China predominantly rely on solid fuels, whereas urban households primarily use clean energy sources [38]. Additionally, socioeconomic factors—such as income constraints, limited fuel availability, and disparities in health literacy—likely contribute to this divide, particularly in impeding the transition to cleaner fuels in rural regions [39]. Existing literature has highlighted the role of urban-rural heterogeneity in shaping health inequities related to SFU [17, 40]. For example, a study in Ghana reported that SFU had a more pronounced adverse effect on learning outcomes in rural children than in their urban counterparts [17]. Some studies have suggested that higher background pollution in urban areas may exacerbate the effects of indoor contaminants, strengthening the observed associations in cities [17, 41]. However, this explanation may not apply to our study context. The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau is characterized by minimal industrial and traffic-related pollution. Thus, we speculate that the stronger association observed in rural areas is more likely driven by socioeconomic vulnerabilities and lower health literacy, rather than by concurrent outdoor pollution sources.

Several modifiable factors play a significant role in influencing children’s growth and development. A systematic review indicates that physical activity may contribute positively to behavioral and cognitive outcomes in early childhood, though additional studies are required to confirm these findings [42]. Moreover, research from severely impoverished regions of China demonstrated that insufficient access to play materials was associated with an elevated risk of suspected developmental delays across multiple domains, including gross and fine motor skills, cognition, and personal-social abilities [43]. Addressing such factors could thus support improvements in ECD. Although direct modification of maternal literacy and household income remains challenging, their impact may be mediated through indirect interventions or policy-based approaches. For instance, prior research suggests that maternal education influences ECD both directly and indirectly, particularly through improvements in the nutritional status of children in rural settings [44]. Similarly, income supplementation programs have been proposed as a viable strategy to enhance developmental outcomes among children from lower-income households [45].

In high-altitude regions, low ambient temperatures pose a persistent challenge. In cold climates with limited access to heating resources, ventilation is often considered secondary to heating, despite being a critical factor influencing indoor air quality. As plateau residents typically spend extended periods indoors, the combustion of traditional solid fuels can lead to severe IAP. Consequently, IAP associated with SFU has been recognized as a major environmental health risk [20]. Previous research has highlighted that in colder areas, homes tend to be less ventilated and often feature wall-to-wall carpeting and high levels of insulation [46]. Thus, promoting clean fuels and enhancing ventilation in high-altitude areas remain essential measures for protecting residents’ health. Given the dose-response relationship between IAP exposure and impaired child development, further large-sample studies are warranted to establish safe thresholds for the health impacts of such pollution.

This study possesses several notable strengths. First, it was conducted on the Tibetan Plateau at elevations exceeding 3,500 m, offering a unique environment for investigating the health effects of high altitude. Second, our assessment of both cooking and heating fuels provides a more comprehensive understanding of the health impacts of solid fuel use across different domestic scenarios. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. A primary constraint is the lack of quantitative IAP monitoring; although a well-defined proxy was used to establish associations, future work should incorporate direct measurements of pollutants (e.g., PM2.5, carbon monoxide) to determine precise dose-response relationships and risk thresholds. Second, while our analysis focused on SFU, the actual cumulative exposure duration likely varied among participants. Additionally, other potentially toxic household pollution sources, such as mosquito coils [47], were not assessed and may represent confounding factors. Third, although children in this setting are presumed to spend most of their time indoors, individual differences in time-activity patterns could affect personal exposure and were not accounted for. Fourth, in the stratified analysis, the association between solid cooking fuel and fine motor delay among urban children was imprecise, as indicated by a wide confidence interval. This is likely due to the limited number of urban children exposed to solid fuels and the even smaller number within this group who experienced a fine motor delay, reflecting the lower prevalence of solid fuel use in urban settings and resulting in reduced statistical power for this specific subgroup analysis. Finally, as both household environmental exposures and child development outcomes were collected by caregiver report, the possibility of recall bias cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation into the association between SFU and ECD in high-altitude regions of China. Our results demonstrate a significant link between SFU and compromised ECD in these settings, with pronounced effects observed in the domains of fine motor and problem-solving skills. This association was stronger among children residing in rural areas compared to their urban counterparts, underscoring the heightened health vulnerability of children in rural highland communities. These findings highlight the necessity for further research employing empirical exposure data to precisely quantify the health impact of IAP on this vulnerable pediatric population.
