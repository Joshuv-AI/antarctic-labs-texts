On the Monotonicity and Rate of Convergence of the Markovian Persuasion Value
Dimitry Shaiderman

Abstract
We study a dynamic Bayesian persuasion model called Markovian persuasion. In such a model, the belief of the receiver regarding the current state of a Markov chain ( X n ) n ≥ 1 (X_{n})_{n\geq 1} , over a finite state space K K , is controlled through signals she obtains from a sender, who observes ( X n ) n ≥ 1 (X_{n})_{n\geq 1} in real time. At each stage n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , the receiver takes an action based on his current belief, which together with the realized state of X n X_{n} , determines the n n ’th stage payoff of the sender. The sender’s goal in a Markovian persuasion game is to find a signaling policy that maximizes her expected δ \delta -discounted sum of stage payoffs for a discount factor δ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \delta\in[0,1) . We show that starting from any invariant distribution ( X n ) n ≥ 1 (X_{n})_{n\geq 1} the trajectory of the δ \delta -discounted value is a monotone decreasing in δ \delta . By combining this result with the opposite increasing monotone trajectories found in Lehrer and S. (2025, [ 12 ] ), we are able to derive an upper bound on the rate of convergence of the δ \delta -discounted values (as δ → 1 − \delta\to 1^{-} ) in the case where ( X n ) n ≥ 1 (X_{n})_{n\geq 1} is ergodic. The results for the Markovian persuasion model are then extended to the Markov chain games model of Renault (2006, [ 15 ] ). Keywords: Markovian persuasion, repeated games with incomplete information on one-side, Markov chains, discounted values, monotone trajectories, rate of convergence, mixing times MSC2000 Subject Classification: 91A25, 91A27, 91A28 JEL Codes: D82, D83

Introduction

The Markovian persuasion game.

The study of information provision is central in many economic fields and models. In the field of Bayesian persuasion, one studies the optimal way in which an informed party, the sender, should manipulate the beliefs of a decision maker, the receiver, using her private information. The baseline model in the field, introduced and studied by Gentzkow and Kamenica
[
8
]
, concentrates on a static situation, where the sender transmits to the receiver a stochastic signal, whose correlation with the possible states of nature determines the amount of information the sender wishes to share. By committing to her information provision policy, the sender allows the receiver to update his belief over the possible states upon obtaining the sender’s realized signal. Based on his updated belief, the receiver takes an action, which together with the true state determines the payoffs of both parties. A famous example of such a game, given in
[
8
]
, is that where a prosecutor, who is fully informed about the state of nature, i.e., whether the suspect is guilty or innocent, wishes to persuade a judge, for instance, to convict the suspect of a crime.

A natural extension of the baseline model of Gentzkow and Kamenica, concerns the case where the sender and the receiver engage in a repeated game, in which the true state evolves over time stochastically according to a discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. This dynamic Bayesian persuasion problem was studied by Ely
[
4
]
, Renault, Solan, and Vieille
[
16
]
, Farhadi and Teneketzis
[
5
]
, and Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
11
,
12
]
.
Compared to the static model of Gentzkow and Kamenica
[
8
]
, where the sender acts myopically, in the dynamic model the information provision policy of the sender affects both her current stage payoff as well as her future stage payoffs.

Optimal information provision policies in dynamic Bayesian persuasion games were described by Ely
[
4
]
, Renault, Solan, and Vieille
[
16
]
, Farhadi and Teneketzis
[
5
]
, for stylized problems, involving special families of Markov chains and payoff functions.

As in Renault, Solan, and Vieille
[
16
]
and Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
11
,
12
]
, we assume that the sender discounts her stage payoffs by a discount factor
\delta\in[0,1)
. The optimization problem of the sender is thus to find an information provision policy that maximizes her expected
\delta
-discounted sum of stage payoffs. Under such optimal policy, the sender’s payoff is termed the
\delta
-discounted value
. The dynamic Bayesian persuasion problem at hand will be referred to as a Markovian persuasion game.

The main results.

Our first main result, stated in Theorem
1
, shows that in the Markovian persuasion game, whenever the Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is stationary–i.e., starts from an invariant distribution of the chain, the
\delta
-discounted values decrease as
\delta
grows. Qualitatively, such result states that under stationary dynamics, the sender cannot benefit from patience.

The result of Theorem
1
adds to recent results of Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
regarding the analytic behavior of the
\delta
-discounted value, as
\delta
varies. As shown in
[
12
]
, when
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is irreducible, there exist weighted averages of the
\delta
-discounted values evaluated at various sets of initial distributions for
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, that increase in
\delta
. In particular, there exist such initial distributions, for which the
\delta
-discounted values at those distributions, do not decrease with
\delta
. Such phenomena, together with the result of Theorem
1
, show that patience may have opposite effects on the sender, based on the initial distribution of the Markov chain. The exact details of those opposing effects is described in detail in Subsection
3.2
, dedicated to the survey of known monotone trajectories of the
\delta
-discounted values in
\delta
.

Our second main result, stated in Theorem
3
, utilizes the results on monotone trajectories in
\delta
in the case where
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is ergodic (i.e., irreducible and aperiodic), to provide an upper bound on the rate of convergence of the
\delta
-discounted values as
\delta
tends to
1
. This theorem shows, that regardless of the initial distribution of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, the
\delta
-discounted value converges to a number, termed the ‘asymptotic value’, at a rate which is faster then
(1-\delta)^{\alpha}
for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

The monotonicity through information design technique.

The recent results on monotone trajectories of the
\delta
-discounted values presented in the current work and that of Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
were derived based on a new proof technique, which we term ‘monotonicity through information design’. Such technique consists of generalizing the original Markovian persuasion model, by adding an exogenous independent Bernoulli process, having an effect on the evolution of information in the baseline model.

The first instance of such technique was showcased in Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
, where the true state was revealed to the receiver at each stage with a fixed positive probability, called the revelation rate, independently of all other events in the game. In particular, in such a setup, the sender is no longer the informational monopolist. The monotone trajectories found by Lehrer and Shaiderman (e.g., Theorems 2 and 3 in
[
12
]
) were derived by analyzing the asymptotics of the
\delta
-discounted values of the generalized game as
\delta
approaches
1
, and comparing different revelation rates.

In the current work, the information design used to derive Theorem
1
, is one in which, at any stage, the receiver’s memory of past signals from the sender is erased with a fixed positive probability. The exogenous Bernoulli process in that case, is the one that governs the memory erasures along the game. Such information design is accompanied with an introduction of an auxiliary player, which also has the capability to erase the receiver’s memory. The result of Theorem
1
is then made possible by embedding the generalized model with memory erasures into a zero-sum stochastic game with signals between the sender and the adversary (see Subsection
5.1
). Thus, the combination of zero-sum stochastic games with signals together with the monotonicity through information design technique can be viewed as a further advancement and development of the original technique used in Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
.

Connection to zero-sum repeated games with incomplete information.

As it turns out, the results of Theorems
1
and
3
, hold also for the
\delta
-discounted values of another model of repeated games. Such model, first introduced and studied by Renault
[
15
]
forms a dynamic extension of Aumann and Maschler’s seminal model of repeated games with incomplete information on one-side
[
1
]
. The proof of the parallel results to those of Theorems
1
and
3
, which are summarized in Theorem
5
, are achieved using the above techniques, with appropriate modifications for the current model.

As it pertains to the contribution of Theorem
5
to the respective field, we have two remarks. First, Sorin (see Proposition 3.11 in
[
21
]
) proved that the
\delta
-discounted values of Aumann and Maschler’s model decrease with
\delta
. Thus, Theorem
5
can be viewed as a verification of Sorin’s result to the dynamic setup of Renault
[
15
]
, in the case where the dynamics are stationary. Secondly, Theorem
5
shows that under ergodic dynamics, the rate of convergence of the
\delta
-discounted values admits a ‘faster’ upper bound compared to the bound known for the Aumann and Maschler model, which is of order
\sqrt{1-\delta}
(e.g., Corollary V.2.9. on p. 223 in
[
14
]
).

Lastly, we leave it open for future research, to study whether the methodology and results of the current work can be utilized to obtain similar results for models of zero-sum repeated games with incomplete information on both sides. In particular, does there exist similar monotone trajectories and upper bounds for the rates of convergence in the splitting game model of Laraki
[
9
]
and in the model of Gensbittel and Renault
[
6
]
.

Additional related literature.

In terms of the monotonicity properties of values of repeated games, it is interesting to mention that the phenomena described in the current work need not hold in similar models to the ones considered in the paper. Indeed, under
n
-stage evaluations rather than discounted ones, the
n
-stage values of repeated games with incomplete information on one side with signals need not follow any monotone pattern (see Lehrer
[
10
]
and Yariv
[
24
]
).

In a broader sense, the paper adds to existing results regarding the analytic behavior of the
\delta
-discounted values as a function of
\delta
, for different game-theoretic models. In Shapley’s model of stochastic games (e.g., Shapley
[
18
]
), the
\delta
-discounted values are known to be a semi-algebraic function of
\delta
(e.g., Bewely and Kohlberg
[
2
]
). The latter, in particular, implies that the
\delta
-discounted values converge as the discount factor approaches
1
. Nevertheless, in the more general model of stochastic games with signals (e.g., Chapter 4.3 in Solan and Ziliotto
[
20
]
), the behavior of the
\delta
-discounted values need not be regular in
\delta
. Ziliotto
[
25
]
provided an example of a zero-sum repeated game with public signals and perfect observation of the actions, where the
\delta
-discounted values do not converge as the discount factor approaches
1
. Such phenomenon, i.e., failure of convergence, was also shown to exist under the framework of zero-sum stochastic game with compact action sets as showcased by Vigeral
[
23
]
and Ziliotto
[
25
]
. Moreover, Sorin and Vigeral
[
22
]
study the oscillations of the
\delta
-discounted values in the above setups.

2
The Markovian Persuasion Model

2.1
The Utilities of the Sender and the Receiver

Let
K=\{1,...,k\}
be a finite set of possible states. The
receiver
is a player equipped with a set of actions
B
. We assume that
B
is either finite or
B=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
. The utility of the receiver is given by a mapping
W:K\times B\to\mathbb{R}
. The receiver is assumed to maximize his utility based on his belief’s distribution over
K
. Formally, given the belief
\xi=(\xi^{\ell})_{\ell\in K}\in\Delta(K)
the receiver takes an action

\displaystyle b(\xi)\in\text{arg\, max}_{b\in B}\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot W(\ell,b).

The sender’s utility is described by a mapping
V:K\times B\to\mathbb{R}
. That is, it depends on the true state as well as on the receiver’s action. For simplicity we assume that
V(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-negative. A common assumption in the literature (e.g.
[
8
]
) asserts that the receiver ‘breaks ties’ in favor of the sender. That is, for every belief
\xi\in\Delta(K)
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot V(\ell,b(\xi))\geq\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot V(\ell,b^{*})

for all
b^{*}\in\text{arg\, max}_{b\in B}\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot W(\ell,b)
. The action policy
b(\cdot):\Delta(K)\to\mathbb{R}
of the receiver is assumed to be known by the sender.

2.2
The Dynamics of the True State

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a Markov chain over
K
, described by a prior probability
q\in\Delta(K)
and transition rule given by a stochastic matrix
M
. The receiver knows that the true state of the game evolves stochastically over time according to
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. The receiver has incomplete information regarding the evolution of the true state over time, as he does not observe the realizations of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, but rather is only aware of the distribution of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(i.e., of
q
and
M
).

2.3
The Sender’s Information Provision

The
sender
is an agent who seeks to influence the actions of the receiver by filtering his information regarding
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in real time. At each time period
n\geq 1
, the sender observes the current true state (i.e., the realization of
X_{n}
). Upon obtaining this information the sender sends a signal
s_{n}
to the receiver, from a finite set of signals
S
, where
|S|\geq|K|
.

A
signaling policy

\sigma
of the sender is described by a sequence of stage strategies
(\sigma_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
\sigma_{n}:(K\times S)^{n-1}\times K\to\Delta(S)
. That is, the signal
s_{n}
is chosen according to the lottery
\sigma_{n}
, whose laws may take into account past events, i.e., the realizations of
X_{1},...,X_{n-1}
, previously sent signals
s_{1},...,s_{n-1}
, together with ‘today’s information’, being the realization of
X_{n}
. Denote by
\Sigma
the space of all signaling policies available to the sender.

2.4
The Receiver’s Beliefs Processes

At the outset of the game, the sender commits to a signaling policy
\sigma\in\Sigma
, which is known to the receiver. Such a commitment allows the receiver to assign probabilities to different events along the game. Formally, by Kolmogorov’s Extension Theorem, the strategy
\sigma
together with
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
induce a unique probability measure
P_{q,\sigma}
on the space of infinite histories of states and signals
(K\times S)^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The receiver’s information regarding the evolution of the true state is described by two stochastic processes, termed the
priors
and
posteriors
processes. Formally, for each
n\geq 1
, the
n
’th prior
q_{n}=(q_{n}^{\ell})_{\ell\in K}\in\Delta(K)
, is defined by

q_{n}^{\ell}:=P_{q,\sigma}\left(X_{n}=\ell\,|\,s_{1},...,s_{n-1}\right),

so that
q_{1}=q
, whereas the
n
’th posterior
p_{n}=(p_{n}^{\ell})_{\ell\in K}\in\Delta(K)
is given by

p_{n}^{\ell}:=P_{q,\sigma}\left(X_{n}=\ell\,|\,s_{1},...,s_{n-1},s_{n}\right).

In simple terms,
q_{n}
describes the receiver’s belief on the state of the Markov chain at time
n
before obtaining the
n
’th signal
s_{n}
, whereas
p_{n}
describes the Bayesian update of
q_{n}
after obtaining
s_{n}
.

Two important properties showcasing the connection of the processes
(q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are

E_{q,\sigma}\,(p_{n}\,|\,q_{n})=q_{n},\quad\forall n\geq 1,

(1)

and

q_{n+1}=p_{n}M,\quad\forall n\geq 1.

(2)

Note that (
1
) follows from the martingale property, whereas (
2
) follows from the Markovian dynamics of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

2.5
The Timeline of the Markovian Persuasion Game

At the outset of the game, the sender commits to a signaling policy
\sigma\in\Sigma
. The game is then played in stages, so that at each stage
n\geq 1
the following events take place in chronological order:

•

The sender sends the signal
s_{n}
, which is chosen by the lottery
\sigma_{n}
.

•

The receiver updates his prior belief
q_{n}
to his posterior belief
p_{n}
.

•

The receiver takes the action
b(p_{n})
.

•

The sender’s and the receiver’s obtain their
n
’th stage payoffs, which equal
V(X_{n},b(p_{n}))
and
W(X_{n},b(p_{n}))
, respectively.

•

The receiver computes his next prior
q_{n+1}=p_{n}M
, and the game proceeds to stage
n+1
.

2.6
The Sender’s Optimization Problem

The sender’s goal is to find a policy
\sigma\in\Sigma
that maximizes her expected
\delta
-discounted sum of payoffs, where
\delta\in[0,1)
is a fixed discount factor. That is, her goal is to maximize

\displaystyle\gamma_{\delta}(q,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=E_{q,\sigma}\left[(1-\delta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n-1}\cdot V(X_{n},b(p_{n}))\right]

\displaystyle=E_{q,\sigma}\left[(1-\delta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n-1}\cdot u(p_{n})\right]

over all
\sigma\in\Sigma
, where
u:\Delta(K)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is given by
u(\xi):=\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot V(\ell,b(\xi))
. A key property of the function
u
is that its upper semi-continuous. Such property is guaranteed by the tie-breaking rule of the receiver. Upper semi-continuous functions play a key role in the subsequent analysis due to the fact that they attain their maximum on compact domains.

Let
v_{\delta}(q):=\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\gamma_{\delta}(q,\sigma)
denote the
\delta
-discounted value of the Markovian persuasion game with prior
q\in\Delta(K)
. Note that as
V(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
, we have that
v_{\delta}(\cdot)\geq 0
as well. Note that for
\delta=0
, the optimization problem coincides with the classical Gentzkow and Kamenica
[
8
]
one-shot Bayesian persuasion problem. The solution in that case is described in terms of the concavification of
u
. Formally, set

\displaystyle(\text{Cav}\,u)(\xi):=\inf\{h(\xi)\,:\,h:\Delta(K)\to\mathbb{R}\text{\, is concave},u\leq h\},\quad\forall\xi\in\Delta(K).

That is,
(\text{Cav}\,u)
is the smallest concave function that majorizes
u
. Then, Gentzkow and Kamenica
[
8
]
have shown that
v_{0}(q)=(\text{Cav}\,u)(q)
for every
q\in\Delta(K)
. For the sake of completeness, the steps leading to the former result will be described in detail in Subsection
4.2
.

3
Main Results

3.1
The Effect of Stationary Dynamics

The first main result of the current work establishes a new monotonicity property of the discounted value
v_{\delta}
of a Markovian persuasion game, and reads as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Consider a Markovian persuasion game. Then, for every invariant distribution
\pi
of
M
, the trajectory
\delta\mapsto v_{\delta}(\pi)
is non-increasing on
[0,1)
.

From a dynamics standpoint, Theorem
1
states that whenever the Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is stationary, the
\delta
-discounted value cannot increase when
\delta
grows. In behavioral terms, this implies that increased patience does not benefit the sender in Markovian persuasion games with stationary dynamics.

The result of Theorem
1
may look intuitive for the following reason. When the patience of the sender increases, i.e., when the discount factor
\delta
grows, the relative weight of future payoffs has a higher effect on the total payoff. Thus, in that case, it seems intuitive that the sender does not benefit from the informational spillover across time. Theorem
1
affirms that intuition when the prior is an invariant distribution. However, as we shall see in Theorem
2
in Subsection
3.2
such intuition does not fit all priors.

A Glimpse to the Proof Technique of Theorem
1

The technique we use to prove Theorem
1
can be coined ‘monotonicity through information design’. The main idea behind this technique, which was first showcased in Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
, is to add an information parameter
x\in(0,1)
to the Markovian persuasion model, and study a corresponding generalized strategic model that depends on
x
.

The generalized strategic model suited to prove Theorem
1
is a zero-sum stochastic game with signals (see Subsection
5.1
) between the sender and an auxiliary adversary. In this game, the information parameter
x\in(0,1)
arises by considering a situation in which at each stage
n\geq 1
the receiver’s memory of past observed signals
s_{1},...,s_{n-1}
is erased by chance with probability
x
, independently of all other events along the game. The auxiliary adversary to the sender introduced to the stochastic game is a player who only has recall of his own actions and the current stage period. At each stage, conditional on the receiver’s memory not being erased by chance, the adversary possesses the ability to erase it.

The memory erasures suit the current framework, as the stationary dynamics guarantee that the belief of the receiver after any memory erasure will return to the invariant distribution
\pi
of the Markov chain. Therefore, as from the sender’s view point any memory erasure starts the game anew, she can be strategically myopic in every sub-game occurring between any two successive memory erasures. The monotonicity arises from a ‘coupling’ argument; if the adversary decides to erase the receiver’s memory at each stage with prob.
(y-x)/(1-x)
, for some
x<y<1
, the sender essentially faces a situation where the memory erasures are performed according to an i.i.d. Bernoulli process with mean
y
rather then with mean
x
, as under chance’s process.

The suitability of exogenous i.i.d. Bernoulli processes is useful due to their connection to
\delta
-discounted valuations. Indeed, under both chance’s process, and the adversary’s coupling process, the random duration between any two memory erasures follows a geometric distribution with parameters
x
and
y
, respectively. As it turns out, the expected sum of payoffs along any such random duration admits a formula in terms of the discounted expected payoff (see Subsection
4.4
). Such a fact is rooted in the known probabilistic interpretation of discounted valuations, in which the total reward of a player equals his payoff of the last stage of the game, where the former is drawn at random from a geometric distribution. The formal details of the proof of Theorem
1
can be found in Section
5
.

A Survey of Monotonic
\delta
-Trajectories

The monotonic
\delta
-trajectory
\delta\mapsto v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})
exhibited in Theorem

adds to a family of monotonic

-trajectories for the Markocian persuasion model showcased in Lehrer and Shaiderman

]
for an irreducible

. However, contrary to the decreasing nature of the
\delta
-trajectories described in Theorem
1
,
Theorem 3 in
[
12
]
provides the following family of increasing
\delta
-trajectories.

Theorem 2

(Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
)
.

Consider a Markovian persuasion game where
M
is irreducible and let
\pi_{M}
be the unique invariant distribution of
M
. Assume that the beliefs
\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}\subseteq\Delta(K)
satisfy

(a)

\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
are affinely independent.

(b)

M(\Delta(K))\subseteq conv\,\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}
, i.e., for every
q\in\Delta(K)
,
qM\in conv\,\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}
.

Then, the trajectory

\delta\mapsto\gamma_{1}v_{\delta}(\xi_{1})+...+\gamma_{k}v_{\delta}(\xi_{k}),

is non-decreasing on
[0,1)
, where
\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{k}
are the unique convex weights satisfying

\pi_{M}=\gamma_{1}\xi_{1}+...+\gamma_{k}\xi_{k}.

In simple terms, Theorem
2
states that there exists infinitely many families of priors, such that certain averages of their
\delta
-discounted values exhibit a monotonic non-decreasing behavior as
\delta
grows. This result is weaker than that of Theorem
1
, in the sense that it does not allow one to deduce the existence of a non-decreasing monotonic trajectory of the form
\delta\mapsto v_{\delta}(q)
for some prior
q\in\Delta(K)
. Nevertheless, it implies that there is no range of discount factors in which all the trajectories
\delta\mapsto v_{\delta}(\xi_{1}),...,\delta\mapsto v_{\delta}(\xi_{k})
decrease simultaneously.

The informational parameter
x\in(0,1)
added to Markovian persuasion model in order to prove Theorem
2
serves to embody random information revelations. In further detail, for every
n\geq 2
, some information regarding
X_{n}
is assumed to be revealed to the receiver at the start of the
n
’th stage with probability
x
.
1
1

1

In exact terms, at the start of the
n
’th stage, prior to obtaining the
n
’th sender’s signal
s_{n}
, the receiver’s prior belief
q_{n}
is ‘split’ to the beliefs
\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}
. Such a ‘split’ is well defined as by Eq. (
2
)
q_{n}\in M(\Delta(K))
for every
n\geq 2
. Further details can be found in Section 9 in
[
12
]
.
Thus, in comparison to the proof technique of Theorem
1
, we have a situation where the receiver obtains information as opposed to losing all of his past information.

Without elaborating on the different steps required to prove Theorem
2
, we note that the monotonicity in Theorem
2
builds on a coupling argument as well. The corresponding coupling is done by the sender, who can at each stage reveal to the receiver the corresponding information regarding
X_{n}
with prob.
(y-x)/(1-x)
, where
x<y<1
, whenever the stochastic revelation failed to do so. This ‘coupling’ is intended to mimic the case where the stochastic revelations occur with probability
y
.

3.2.1
On the Analytic Behavior of Two Trajectories

For any irreducible stochastic matrix
M
, a set of priors
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem

3.3
An Upper Bound on the Rate of Convergence to the Asymptotic Value

Assume that in addition to
M
being irreducible it is also aperiodic. Put differently, assume that the Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an ergodic one. In Theorem 1 in Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
11
]
it was shown that there exists a number
v_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\cdot)-v_{\infty}|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\delta\to 1^{-}.

(3)

That is, the discounted valued
v_{\delta}
converge uniformly on
\Delta(K)
to
v_{\infty}
. The limit
v_{\infty}
is referred to as the
asymptotic value
of the Markovian persuasion model.

Our second main result provides a first upper bound on the rate of convergence of
v_{\delta}
to
v_{\infty}
and reads as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a Markovian persuasion game. Then, for
1-(1/2)\cdot\min_{\ell\in K}\pi_{M}^{\ell}<\delta<1
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\cdot)-v_{\infty}|\leq O\left((1-\delta)\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\right).

In particular, Theorem
3
implies that the discounted values
v_{\delta}
converge uniformly to
v_{\infty}
at a rate of at least
(1-\delta)^{\alpha}
for every
\alpha<1
, as
\delta\to 1^{-}
. The initial step for Theorem
3
builds on the following observation:

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})-v_{\infty}\leq\Phi(\delta)-\Psi(\delta),\quad\forall\delta\in[0,1).

Indeed, such observation follows from the definitions on
\Phi
and
\Psi
, Theorems
1
and Theorem
2
, and the identity in (
3
). Thereafter, using an appropriate analysis given in Section
6
, the upper bound in Theorem

is enabled by a classic upper bound on the mixing time of ergodic Markov chains due to Levin and Peres
[
13
]
.

3.4
Application to Zero-Sum Repeated Games with Incomplete Information on One-Side

As it turns out, the proof techniques developed for the main results in the Markovian persuasion model may be modified to obtain new results to a different model of repeated games. The latter, which was first introduced by Renault
[
15
]
, sets a dynamic extension of the classic Aumann and Maschler
[
1
]
model of repeated games with incomplete information on one-side.

The model under consideration is described as follows. The set of states
K=\{1,...,k\}
is associated with a set of zero-sum matrix games, denoted
\{G^{1},...,G^{k}\}
. The matrices
\{G^{\ell}:\ell\in K\}
are assumed to be of equal dimensions. The set of actions of the row player (the maximizer) in those games, is denoted by
I
, and that of the column player (the minimizer) by
J
. The payoff at state
\ell\in K
under the action pair
(i,j)\in I\times J
equals to
G^{\ell}(i,j)
. As in the Markovian persuasion case, we assume that all payoffs are non-negative, i.e.,
G^{\ell}(i,j)\geq 0
for every
\ell\in K
,
(i,j)\in I\times J
.

The row and column players, called Players 1 and 2, respectively, engage in a repeated game, whose state evolves over time according to a Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
over
K
with prior
q\in\Delta(K)
and stochastic transition matrix
M
. The play proceeds by stages; at each stage
n\geq 1
the following events take place in chronological order:

•

Player 1 is told the state at stage
n
, being the realized value
x_{n}
of
X_{n}
, unlike Player 2 who only knows
(q,M)
; Player 1 thus learns the zero-sum matrix game
G^{x_{n}}
to be played at that stage.

•

The players are prescribed to choose simultaneously their
n
’th stage actions
i_{n}\in I
and
j_{n}\in J
.

•

The pair of actions
(i_{n},j_{n})
is publicly announced and becomes common knowledge among the players.

•

Player 1’s payoff for stage
n
equals to
G^{x_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})
. Note that this payoff is known only to Player 1.

•

The game proceeds to stage
n+1
.

The above description is assumed to be common knowledge among the players. Adopting Renault’s terminology
[
15
]
, the above described game is called a
Markov chain game
. The Aumann and Maschler
[
1
]
model is an instance of a Markov chain game where
M=\text{Id}_{k}
. In such case, the matrix game chosen at the first stage is played repeatedly at every subsequent stage.

The statement of the optimization problem faced by both players requires the definition of
behavioral strategies
. A
behavioral strategy
\alpha
of Player 1
consists of a sequence of mappings
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
\alpha_{n}:(K\times I\times J)^{n-1}\times K\to\Delta(I)
. That is, under the behavioral strategy
\alpha
, the
n
’th action
i_{n}
of Player 1 is drawn at random from the lottery
\alpha_{n}(x_{1},i_{1},j_{1},...,x_{n-1},i_{n-1},j_{n-1},x_{n})
. In particular, it may depend on all information available to her at the time. Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of all behavioral strategies of Player 1. Similarly, a
behavioral strategy
\beta
of Player 2
is described by the sequence of mappings
\beta_{n}:(I\times J)^{n-1}\to\Delta(J)
,
n\geq 1
. As is the case for Player 1, Player 2’s
n
’th action
j_{n}
may take into account his available information at the time: the past pairs of chosen actions
(i_{1},j_{1},...,i_{n-1},j_{n-1})
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}
the set of all behavioral strategies of Player 2.

For every pair of behavioral strategies
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
denote by
P^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}
the unique probability measure over the space of infinite histories
(K\times I\times J)^{\mathbb{N}}
along the game.
2
2

2

We equip
(K\times I\times J)^{\mathbb{N}}
with the product topology, and thereafter consider the Borel sigma-field on that space. The probability measure
P^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}
is defined on events in the Borel sigma-field over
(K\times I\times J)^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The optimization problem is to find for every discount factor
\delta\in[0,1)
the
\delta
-discounted value of the Markov chain game, denoted
V_{\delta}(q)
, and defined by:

\displaystyle V_{\delta}(q)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\,\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}\left[(1-\delta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n-1}\cdot G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\,\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}\left[(1-\delta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta^{n-1}\cdot G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right],

(4)

where the second equality is due to Sion’s minimax theorem, and
E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}
stands for the expectation operator with respect to
P^{q}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

A common underlying assumption when dealing with zero-sum repeated games is that of
pre-play communication
. Such assumption asserts that at every stage
n\geq 1
, prior to choosing their respective actions
(i_{n},j_{n})
the players may communicate with each other. This communication is unrestricted, allowing players to send any message they desire. This assumption is not-restrictive in the sense that the value
V_{\delta}
is independent of it. Indeed, the pre-play communication can only add to Player 2’s information, and thereby help him minimize Player 1’s payoff. As common in the literature, the possibility of pre-play communication is not added to the formal definition of behavioral strategies, and thus we keep the former in above simplified version.

Lehrer and Shaiderman
[
12
]
have shown that the result of Theorem
2
may also be achieved for Markov chain games. Formally, the statement reads as follows.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a Markov chain game. Assume that
M
is irreducible and let
\pi_{M}
be the unique invariant distribution of
M
. Assume that the beliefs
\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}\subseteq\Delta(K)
satisfy

(a)

\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}
are affinely independent.

(b)

M(\Delta(K))\subseteq conv\,\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}
, i.e., for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
,
\xi M\in conv\,\{\xi_{1},...,\xi_{k}\}
.

Then, the trajectory

\delta\mapsto\gamma_{1}V_{\delta}(\chi_{1})+...+\gamma_{k}V_{\delta}(\chi_{k}),

is non-decreasing on
[0,1)
, where
\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{k}
are the unique convex weights satisfying

\pi_{M}=\gamma_{1}\chi_{1}+...+\gamma_{k}\chi_{k}.

In the current work, we show that the equivalent results of Theorems
1
and
3
also hold for Markov chain games. Formally, our result may be summarized as follows:

Theorem 5
.

Consider a Markov chain game. We have the following:

1.

For every invariant distribution
\pi
of
M
, the trajectory
\delta\mapsto V_{\delta}(\pi)
is non-increasing on
[0,1)
.

2.

Assume that
M
is ergodic (i.e., irreducible and aperiodic). Then, there exists a number
V_{\infty}\geq 0
such that for
1-(1/2)\cdot\min_{\ell\in K}\pi_{M}^{\ell}<\delta<1
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\Delta(K)}|V_{\delta}(\cdot)-V_{\infty}|\leq O\left((1-\delta)\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\right).

The first item of Theorem
5
can be viewed as an extension of a parallel known result for Aumann and Maschler’s model
[
1
]
. Such model is a private case of the model considered above where
M=\text{Id}_{k}
(i.e., the Markov chain is constant over-time). For such choice of
M
, every belief
\xi\in\Delta(K)
is an invariant distribution of
M=\text{Id}_{k}
. Sorin has shown that in the Aumann and Maschler model
\xi\mapsto V_{\delta}(\xi)
is non-increasing on
[0,1)
for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
(see Proposition 3.11 on p. 34 in Sorin
[
21
]
). Sorin’s proof builds on an algebraic identity, connecting payoffs under any two disjoint discount factors. For the sake of completeness, we present Sorin’s proof in Appendix
B
. Such proof is of particular interest, as it can be directly applied to deduce the first item in Theorem
5
as well.

The second item in Theorem
5
provides a new insight on the effect of the ergodicity of
M
on the rate of convergence of the discounted values
V_{\delta}(\cdot)
. In the classic Aumann and Maschler model it was shown that
V_{\delta}(\cdot)
converges to
(\text{Cav}\,U)(\cdot)
uniformly over
\Delta(K)
at a rate of at least
(1-\delta)^{1/2}
, where
U:\Delta(K)\to\mathbb{R}
is the so called ‘value of the one shot non-revealing game’, defined by:

\displaystyle U(\xi):=\text{val}\left(\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}G^{\ell}\right),\quad\forall\xi\in\Delta(K).

However, by the second item of Theorem
5
we have that under the ergodicity assumption
V_{\delta}(\cdot)
converges uniformly on
\Delta(K)
at a rate of at least
(1-\delta)^{\alpha}
for every
0<\alpha<1
. Such a result suggests that the mixing properties of the stochastic transition matrix
M
may affect the rate of convergence of the discounted valuations. The tightness of the above mentioned bounds on the rate of convergence remains an open problem. It is our hope that the techniques and tools of the current research may advance the work on this problem.

The proof of Theorem
5
which appears in Section
7
, is achieved by surveying the required modifications to the proofs of the main results in regards to the Markovian persuasion model. Before proceeding to such proofs, we move to the development of a suitable toolbox for the Markovian persuasion model. Such toolbox will be necessary for all subsequent proofs in the current work.

The Toolbox

4.1
A Markov Decision Problem Reformulation

As first noted by Ely
[
4
]
and Renault, Solan, and Vieille
[
16
]
, the Markovian persuasion model may be reformulated in terms of a Markov decision process (MDP) having the following attributes:

•

The set of
states
is
\Delta(K)
. The initial state is denoted
q
.

•

At each state
\xi\in\Delta(K)
the
set of available actions at

\xi
, denoted
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\xi}=\Bigg\{(\alpha_{s},\xi_{s})_{s\in S}\,:\,\alpha_{s}\geq 0,\,\xi_{s}\in\Delta(K),~\forall s\in S,~\sum_{s\in S}\alpha_{s}=1,~\sum_{s\in S}\alpha_{s}\xi_{s}=\xi\Bigg\}.

In words,
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
describes all discrete distributions over
|S|
beliefs in
\Delta(K)
whose mean equals
\xi
, also known as the set of
splits
of
\xi
to
|S|
beliefs in
\Delta(K)
.

•

The
transition rule

\rho
associates with each state
\xi\in\Delta(K)
and action
\chi=(\alpha_{s},\xi_{s})_{s\in S}\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
a new state, denoted
\rho(\xi,\chi)
, picked stochastically by the law

\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\chi)=\xi_{s}M~~\text{with probability}~~\alpha_{s}.

•

The
reward function

r
assigns to each state
\xi
and action
\chi=(\alpha_{s},\xi_{s})_{s\in S}\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
, the number
r(\xi,\chi)
defined by

\displaystyle r(\xi,\chi):=\sum_{s\in S}\alpha_{s}\cdot u(\xi_{s}).

For the sake of completeness, let us briefly discuss the validity of the above reformulation, albeit that it was previously addressed in detail in works such as
[
4
]
,
[
16
]
, and
[
11
]
. Such validity is rooted in the Aumann and Maschler Splitting Lemma (e.g., Aumann and Maschler
[
1
]
). Consider a strategy
\sigma=(\sigma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\Sigma
, and note that for each
n\geq 1
,
q_{n}
’s distribution depends on
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n-1}
, but not on
\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n+1},...
. Such Lemma implies that given that
q_{n}=\xi
, for any
\chi\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
the sender can construct the lottery
\sigma_{n}
so that
p_{n}
would be distributed according to
\chi
.

On the other hand, as relation (
1
) implies that the conditional distribution of
p_{n}
given
q_{n}=\xi
lies in
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
, we have that at each stage
n
the sender should choose which ‘split’ of
q_{n}
to employ. Therefore, in essence, the prior
q_{n}
corresponds to the state of MDP at stage
n
, whereas the conditional distribution of the posterior
p_{n}
given
q_{n}
, corresponds to the
n
’th action in the MDP.

Under such a correspondence, the definition of the transition rule
\rho
of the MDP follows immediately from relation (
2
), whereas the reward function
r
was defined so that the payoff at stage
n
of the MDP, corresponds to the conditional expectation of
u(p_{n})
given
q_{n}
.

Abusing notation, a
pure strategy

\sigma
of the sender in the MDP is thus described by a sequence of stage strategies
\sigma_{n}
, so that
\sigma_{n}:(\xi_{1},\chi_{1},...\xi_{n-1},\chi_{n-1},\xi_{n})\mapsto\chi_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi_{n}}
, where
\xi_{m}
and
\chi_{m}
denote the
m
’th state and action, respectively, along the play of the MDP. Thus
\Sigma
described the set of all pure strategies
\sigma
in the MDP. A
mixed strategy
in the MDP is will be defined to be a finite distribution over
\Sigma
, i.e., a random choice of a strategy among a finite set of pure strategies.

The Signal’s Cardinality Invariance Principle

By the above discussion and structure of

4.3

By combining the results of Eqs. (

\displaystyle E_{q,\sigma}(p_{n+1}\,|\,p_{n})=p_{n}M,\quad\forall n\geq 1.

\displaystyle E_{q,\sigma}\,p_{n}=qM^{n-1},\quad\forall\sigma\in\Sigma,~\forall n\geq 1.

.

The distributions of optimal posteriors.
For every

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{n}(\delta,q)(E):=P_{q,\sigma_{\delta}^{*}}\,(p_{n}\in E),\quad\forall~\text{Borel}~E\subseteq\Delta(K),

•

By the definition of

•

4.4
The Random Duration Payoff

Consider a scenario in which the number of stages in the Markovian persuasion model is random. That is, the game between the sender and the receiver lasts only a (random) finite number of stages. Furthermore, under such a scenario, let us assume that the sender evaluates his total payoff as the sum of stage payoffs along the game. If we model the random duration by a geometric random variable
Y
with parameter
x\in(0,1]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle P(Y=n)=(1-x)^{n-1}\cdot x,\quad\forall n\geq 1,

then the sender’s
expected random duration payoff
associated with the signaling policy
\sigma\in\Sigma
, denoted
\gamma^{\text{r-d}}_{x}(q,\sigma)
, is given by the formula

\gamma^{\text{r-d}}_{x}(q,\sigma)=E_{q,\sigma}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u(p_{n})\cdot\textbf{1}\{n\leq Y\}\right).

(8)

In addition let us assume that
Y
is independent of all other events along the game. Denote by
v^{\text{r-d}}_{x}(q):=\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\gamma^{\text{r-d}}_{x}(q,\sigma)
the ‘random duration’ value.

The following claim uncovers the relations between the discounted values and random duration values.

Claim 1
.

For every
q\in\Delta(K)
, and
x\in(0,1]
, we have

v^{\emph{r-d}}_{x}(q)=\frac{v_{1-x}(q)}{x}.

(9)

Moreover, the strategy
\sigma_{1-x}^{*}\in\Sigma
defined in Subsection
4.2
is optimal under the expected random duration payoff, that is,
\gamma^{\emph{r-d}}_{x}(q,\sigma_{1-x}^{*})=v^{\emph{r-d}}_{x}(q)
.

Proof of Claim
1
.

Since
Y
is independent of
(X_{n})
and
(p_{n})
, we may disintegrate it from (
8
), and then change the order of summation to obtain that for every
\sigma\in\Sigma
we have that

\begin{split}\gamma^{\text{r-d}}_{x}(q,\sigma)&=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}(1-x)^{N-1}\cdot x\cdot E_{q,\sigma}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}u(p_{n})\right)\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{N=n}^{\infty}(1-x)^{N-1}\cdot x\right)E_{q,\sigma}\,u(p_{n})\\
&=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x\cdot(1-x)^{n-1}\,\,E_{q,\sigma}\,u(p_{n})=\frac{1}{x}\cdot\gamma_{1-x}(q,\sigma).\end{split}

Maximizing over

we obtain Eq. (
9

implies the optimality of

under the expected random duration payoff, thus completing the proof of the claim.

5.1
A Competitive Stochastic Game with Signals

For the sake of the proof of Theorem

\Gamma_{x}

5.1.1
The Primitives of
\Gamma_{x}

The game
\Gamma_{x}
fits the model of a zero-sum stochastic game with signals (e.g. Subsections 1.a and 1.e in Chapter 4 in Mertens, Sorin, and Zamir
[
14
]
or Chapter 4.3 in Solan and Ziliotto
[
20
]
). The primitives defining
\Gamma_{x}
are the following:

•

States.
The states of
\Gamma_{x}
are elements of
\Omega:=\Delta(K)\cup\{\pi^{*},\pi^{**}\}
, where
\pi^{*}
and
\pi^{**}
are two distinguished copies of
\pi
.

•

Actions.
At each state
\xi\in\Delta(K)
, the available actions of the sender are the splits of
\xi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\xi}=\Bigg\{(\alpha_{i},\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}\,:\,\alpha_{i}\geq 0,\,\xi_{i}\in\Delta(K),~\forall i\geq 1,~\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}=1,~\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}=\xi\Bigg\}.

where we recall that the signal set
S=\mathbb{N}
(see concluding discussion of Subsection
4.2
). For
\xi\in\{\pi^{*},\pi^{**}\}
, the available actions are defined to be
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
.

As for the adversary, the set of available actions at each state
\xi\in\Omega
, is
C=\{0,1\}
. That is, the state of the game has no effect on the actions available to the adversary.

•

Payoff function.
The adversary’s payoff to the sender is described by a function
f:\Omega\times\bigcup_{\xi\in\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\xi}\times C\to\mathbb{R}
. The payoff associated with state
\xi\in\Omega
and actions
\chi=\{(\alpha_{i},\xi_{i})\}\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
,
c\in\{0,1\}
, is given by

\displaystyle f(\xi,\chi,c):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\cdot u(\xi_{i}).

In particular, the payoff that the adversary transfers to the sender depends only on the split of
\xi
that the sender chooses to employ.

•

Transition rule.
The transition rule is described by a lottery
t:\Omega\times\bigcup_{\xi\in\Omega}\mathcal{A}_{\xi}\times C\to\Delta(\Omega\times\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2})
, where
\Lambda_{1}
is termed the
signal set of the sender
and
\Lambda_{2}
the
signal set of the adversary
.
5
5

5

The set of signals
\Lambda_{1}
corresponds to signals that the sender obtains along
\Gamma_{x}
, as opposed to the ones she transmits (chosen from the set
S=\mathbb{N}
) to induce splits in
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
,
\xi\in\Omega
. This would be made clear in the following subsection.
Such a lottery is defined by,

\displaystyle t(\xi,\chi,0)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\pi^{*},\pi^{*},\emptyset),&\hbox{with prob.\,\,}\,\,x\\
(\xi_{i}M,\xi_{i}M,\emptyset),&\hbox{with prob.\,\,}\,\,(1-x)\cdot\alpha_{i}\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle t(\xi,\chi,1)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\pi^{*},\pi^{*},\emptyset),&\hbox{with prob.\,\,}\,\,x\\
(\pi^{**},\pi^{**},\emptyset),&\hbox{with prob.\,\,}\,\,(1-x)\end{array}\right.,

for every
\xi\in\Omega
and every
\chi=\{(\alpha_{i},\xi_{i})\}\in\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
. In particular, we may take
\Lambda_{1}=\Omega
and
\Lambda_{2}=\emptyset
.

5.1.2
The Play Description of
\Gamma_{x}

The game
\Gamma_{x}
is played by stages. For each stage
n\geq 1
denote by
\omega_{n}
the state at the
n
’th stage of
\Gamma_{x}
.
The game starts from state
\omega_{1}=\pi
, which is common knowledge among the players. At the first stage the sender and the adversary are prescribed to choose simultaneously actions
\chi_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
and
c_{1}\in C
.

The game
\Gamma_{x}
then proceeds iteratively as follows. At any stage
n\geq 2
, given
(\omega_{n-1},\chi_{n-1},c_{n-1})
a tuple
(\zeta_{n},\lambda_{n-1}^{1},\lambda_{n-1}^{2})\in\Omega\times\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2}
is chosen according to the lottery
t(\omega_{n-1},\chi_{n-1},c_{n-1})
, i.e.,
\lambda_{n-1}^{1}=\zeta_{n}
and
\lambda_{n-1}^{2}=\emptyset
. Upon this choice, the play at stage
n
proceeds in the following chronological order:

•

The new state
\omega_{n}
is set to equal
\zeta_{n}
.

•

The sender observes the signal
\lambda_{n-1}^{1}=\omega_{n}
, whereas the adversary observes the signal
\lambda_{n-1}^{2}=\emptyset
.

•

The sender and the adversary are prescribed to choose simultaneously their respective actions
\chi_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{\omega_{n}}
and
c_{n}\in C
.

Given
(\omega_{n},\chi_{n},c_{n})
the game
\Gamma_{x}
then proceeds stage
n+1
.

Let us now give some important remarks. By the above description, the sender observes at each stage
n
the current state
\omega_{n}
, unlike the adversary who obtains no information regarding
(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 2}
. Moreover, the adversary obtains no information regarding the sender’s actions. In contrast, if at some stage
n\geq 2
,
\omega_{n}=\pi^{**}
, the sender knows that
c_{n-1}=1
, thus getting some information regarding the adversary’s past actions. However, it may be the case that
c_{n-1}=1
and
\omega_{n}=\pi^{*}
, in which case the sender does not know the choice of
c_{n-1}
.

We assume that the description of the play and primitives of
\Gamma_{x}
is commonly known among the players. In addition, we assume that the players have perfect recall of the signals they observe, as well as of their own past played actions.

5.1.3
The Motivation Behind
\Gamma_{x}

Broadly speaking, the game
\Gamma_{x}
can be viewed as a generalization of the Markovian persuasion model, where the sender has no full control of the receiver’s evolving beliefs. If we view the states
(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\Gamma_{x}
as a generalized version of the prior beliefs
(q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, then at the start of each stage of
\Gamma_{x}
chance may erase the receiver’s memory at random according to a Bernoulli process with parameter
x
. Upon a memory erasure at stage
n
, the stationarity of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
implies that
q_{n}
should return to the invariant distribution
\pi
. For this reason, we model memory erasures of chance in
\Gamma_{x}
via transitions to state
\pi^{*}
.

The adversary in
\Gamma_{x}
can be thought of as a competitive strategic device that is able to erase the receiver’s memory as well. As follows from the definition of
\Gamma_{x}
, the adversary’s memory erasures affect the game only in the case where chance didn’t make a move, i.e., performed a memory erasure. The adversary’s erasures are made distinguishable from those of chance, by introducing the state
\pi^{**}
. Indeed, the visits to
\pi^{**}
may occur only when the adversary chooses to erase the memory of the receiver, i.e., take the action
c=1
. It is worth noting that the adversary in
\Gamma_{x}
plays in the ‘dark’, in the sense that he has recall of his own actions only, unlike the sender who obtains information both on the moves of chance and the adversary.

From a payoff perspective,
\Gamma_{x}
is similar to the Markovian persuasion model in that the sender’s stage payoffs are fully at hers control. Indeed, the payoff function
f
only depends on the splits of the sender, similarly to the stage payoffs
\{u(p_{n})\,:\,n\geq 1\}
in the Markovian persuasion model which are determined by the posterior beliefs
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, being splits of
(q_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. In that view, compared to the Markovian persuasion model, in
\Gamma_{x}
the sender also has full control of the receiver’s posterior beliefs, but by contrast, has only partial control of the receiver’s prior beliefs, which are affected by the moves of chance and the adversary.

5.1.4
Strategies and

-Cesàro Payoffs

As follows from the definition and play description of

As it pertains to the adversary, the sequence

After

\displaystyle P^{x}_{\theta,\tau}(\omega_{1},\chi_{1},c_{1},...,\omega_{N},\chi_{N},c_{N})

\displaystyle=\textbf{1}\{\theta_{1}(\omega_{1})=\chi_{1}\}\times\tau_{1}[c_{1}]\times\prod_{n=2}^{N}t(\omega_{n-1},\chi_{n-1},c_{n})[\omega_{n}]

For every

\displaystyle\geq P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}(F_{N})E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{T_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\geq P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}(F_{N})E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F_{N}\right)

(17)

\displaystyle\geq E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\right)\quad\quad\quad:=I_{N}^{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,(F^{\text{c}}_{N})\,E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right)\quad\quad\quad:=I_{N}^{2}(\tau).

The linearity of the expectation operator and Claim

imply that, for any
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
it holds

\displaystyle I_{N}^{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)=\frac{a_{N}}{N}\cdot\frac{v_{1-x}(\pi)}{x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(N-1)x-N^{0.75}}{N}\cdot\frac{v_{1-x}(\pi)}{x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N-1}{N}\cdot v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{v_{1-x}(\pi)}{xN^{0.25}}\geq v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{xN^{0.25}}

\displaystyle\geq v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{x}\left(\frac{x}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{0.25}}\right)\geq v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{x}\cdot\frac{2}{N^{0.25}}.

Next, let us analyze
I_{N}^{2}(\tau)
. First, we have for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}

E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right)\\
\leq\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}\,E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\kappa_{m+1}\,\Big|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right).

(19)

We now claim that

F^{\text{c}}_{N}=\{\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{a_{N}}>N-1\}.

Indeed, the event
F^{\text{c}}_{N}
occurs when there were less then
a_{N}=\lceil(N-1)x-N^{0.75}\rceil
memory erasures performed by chance in the first
N
stages. As chance moves may occur from the second stage on (
\omega_{1}=\pi\neq\pi^{*}
), this means that along stages
2,...,N
there were less then
a_{N}=\lceil(N-1)x-N^{0.75}\rceil
memory erasures. Moreover,
\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{a_{N}}-a_{N}
follows a negative binomial distribution with parameters
(a_{N},x)
(being the number of stages with no erasures performed by chance until the
a_{N}
’th erasure performed by chance). Therefore, as we have that

E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\kappa_{m+1}\,\Big|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right)=a_{N}\\
+E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\left(\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\kappa_{m+1}\right)-a_{N}\,\Big|\,\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{a_{N}}>N-1\right),

(20)

we may now use the following bound by Geyer (e.g., Eq. (16) in Geyer
[
7
]
) for the lower truncated mean of a negative binomial distribution
W
with parameters
(r,p)
:

\displaystyle E(W\,|\,W>n)=E(W)+\frac{n+1}{p(1+\beta_{n+1}(r,p))},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\beta_{n+1}(r,p):=P(W>n+1)/\,P(W=n+1)
. Applying a relaxed version of the above (using
1+\beta_{n+1}(r,p)\geq 1
) to our setting we obtain that for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
it holds that

E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\left(\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\kappa_{m+1}\right)-a_{N}\,\Big|\,\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{a_{N}}>N-1\right)\leq\\
\frac{a_{N}(1-x)}{x}+\frac{N}{x}.

(21)

Combining Eqs. (
19
), (
20
) and (
21
) we obtain that

\displaystyle E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}\cdot\left(a_{N}+\frac{a_{N}(1-x)}{x}+\frac{N}{x}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}\cdot\left(Nx+1+\frac{(Nx+1)(1-x)}{x}+\frac{N}{x}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}\cdot\left(2N+\frac{2N}{x}+\frac{N}{x}\right)=\|u\|_{\infty}\left(2+\frac{3}{x}\right),

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
a_{N}=\lceil(N-1)x-N^{0.75}\rceil\leq Nx+1
and last inequality uses the relation
Nx+1\leq 2N
. The last step in estimating
I_{N}^{2}(\tau)
consists of estimating
P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,(F^{\text{c}}_{N})
. Using Hoeffding’s inequality for the sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables
T_{N}
(see relation (

\tau\in\mathcal{T}
it holds that

\displaystyle P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,(F^{\text{c}}_{N})

\displaystyle=P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,\left(\{T_{N}<\lceil(N-1)x-N^{0.75}\rceil\}\right)

\displaystyle=P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,\left(\{T_{N}<(N-1)x-N^{0.75}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\{|T_{N}-(N-1)x|\geq N^{0.75}\}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2N^{1.5}}{N-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
T_{N}
is integer-valued.
Thus, combining Eqs. (
5.4
) and (
5.4
) we have that

\displaystyle I_{N}^{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=P^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\,(F^{\text{c}}_{N})\,E^{x}_{\theta^{*},\tau}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{a_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m}}^{\kappa_{0}\oplus\kappa_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,F^{\text{c}}_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)\cdot\|u\|_{\infty}\left(2+\frac{3}{x}\right).

The latter combined with Eqs. (
5.4
) and (
5.4
) implies

\displaystyle A_{N}(x,\theta^{*},\tau)

\displaystyle\geq v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{x}\cdot\frac{2}{N^{0.25}}-2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)\cdot\|u\|_{\infty}\left(2+\frac{3}{x}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{1-x}(\pi)-\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{x}\,\left(\frac{2}{N^{0.25}}+2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)\left(2x+3\right)\right),

which in conjunction with Eq. (

for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
. The validity of Proposition

may now be derived as

\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{x}\,\left(\frac{2}{N^{0.25}}+2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)\left(2x+3\right)+\frac{1-x}{N}\right)\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.

Proof of Proposition

Fix
y\in(x,1]
and denote
\beta_{y}:=(y-x)/(1-x)
. Consider the following strategy
\tau^{y}\in\mathcal{T}
: at any stage
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\tau^{y}_{n}:(c_{1},...,c_{n-1})\mapsto(1-\beta_{y})\cdot\delta_{\{0\}}+\beta_{y}\cdot\delta_{\{1\}},\quad\forall(c_{1},...,c_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}.

In words, in every stage
n\geq 1
, the adversary performs a memory erasure with probability
(y-x)/(1-x)
, independently of his past moves.

For each
n\geq 2
let
Z_{n}:=\textbf{1}\{\omega_{n}\in\{\pi^{*},\pi^{**}\}\}
. By the definition of
\tau_{y}
, for any
\theta\in\Theta

(Z_{n})_{n\geq 2}
forms an i.i.d. Bernoulli process with parameter
y
on the the probability space
(H,\mathcal{H},P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}})
. Indeed, the identical distribution follow from the fact that

\displaystyle P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\{Z_{n}=1\}\right)

\displaystyle=P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\{\omega_{n}=\pi^{*}\}\right)+P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\{\omega_{n}\neq\pi^{*}\}\cap\{\omega_{n}=\pi^{*}\}\right)

\displaystyle=x+(1-x)P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\{\omega_{n}=\pi^{*}\}\,|\,\{\omega_{n}\neq\pi^{*}\}\right)

\displaystyle x+(1-x)\cdot\frac{y-x}{1-x}=x+y-x=y.

The fact that the random variables
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 2}
are independent on
(H,\mathcal{H},P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}})
follows from the definition of
\tau^{y}
, stating that the moves of the adversary are independent of past events.

Let us now generalize the probabilistic framework considered in Subsection
5.3
for a given
\theta\in\Theta
. First, define by iteration the sequence of random variables
(\kappa^{*}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
(H,\mathcal{H},P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}})
according to:

\displaystyle\kappa^{*}_{0}

\displaystyle:=1,

\displaystyle\kappa^{*}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\inf\,\{\,n\geq 1\,:\,Z_{\kappa^{*}_{0}+\kappa^{*}_{1}\cdots+\kappa^{*}_{n-1}+n}=1\,\}\quad

\displaystyle\,\,\,=\inf\,\{\,n\geq 1\,:\,\omega_{\kappa^{*}_{0}+\kappa^{*}_{1}\cdots+\kappa^{*}_{n-1}+n}\in\{\pi^{*},\pi^{**}\}\,\},\quad n\geq 1.

The sequence
(\kappa^{*}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
counts the number of stages between successive memory erasures performed by either chance or the adversary. Since
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 2}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with parameter
y
on
(H,\mathcal{H},P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}})
, we have that under
P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}
,
(\kappa^{*}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are i.i.d. geometric random variables with parameter
y
.

Next, define for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
the random integer
T^{*}_{N}
by

\displaystyle T^{*}_{N}:=\max\,\{\,n\in\{0,1,...,N-1\}\,:\,\kappa^{*}_{0}+\kappa^{*}_{1}+\cdots+\kappa^{*}_{n}\leq N\,\}.

The random variable
T_{N}^{*}
, which also admits the representation
T^{*}_{N}:=Z_{2}+\cdots+Z_{N}
, counts the total number of memory erasures by either chance or the adversary in the first
N
stages of
\Gamma_{x}
. Lastly, let
\ell^{*}_{N}:=\kappa^{*}_{0}+\kappa^{*}_{1}+\cdots+\kappa^{*}_{T^{*}_{N}}
be the last stage within the first
N
stages at which a memory erasure was performed by either chance or the adversary.

In terms of the new probabilistic framework, the payoff
\gamma_{N}^{x}(\theta,\tau^{y})
can now be written as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{N}^{x}(\theta,\tau^{y})

\displaystyle=E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{\ell^{*}_{N}}f(\omega_{n},\chi_{n},c_{n})\right)\quad\quad\quad:=A^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=\ell^{*}_{N}+1}^{N}f(\omega_{n},\chi_{n},c_{n})\right)\quad\quad\quad:=B^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y}).

Using an adapted version of the arguments in the probabilistic analysis that leading to Eq. (
15
), we have that in the current setup it holds

\displaystyle|\gamma_{N}^{x}(\theta,\tau^{y})-A^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y})|\leq\frac{1-y}{y}\cdot\frac{\|u\|_{\infty}}{N}.

A^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y})

. To do so, we consider the events
E_{N}:=\{T^{*}_{N}\leq b_{N}\}
where

for every

, and

is non-negative we have

\displaystyle A^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y})

\displaystyle=E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{T^{*}_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}}^{\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}(E_{N}^{\text{c}})\|u\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}(E_{N})\,E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{b_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}}^{\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\,\Bigg|\,E_{N}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}(E_{N}^{\text{c}})\|u\|_{\infty}

(25)

\displaystyle\leq E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{b_{N}-1}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}}^{\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)\right)+P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}(E_{N}^{\text{c}})\|u\|_{\infty}.

\displaystyle P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}(E_{N}^{\text{c}})

\displaystyle=P^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\{T_{N}^{*}>\lceil(N-1)y+N^{0.75}\rceil\}\right)

Combining Eqs. (
5.5

\displaystyle A^{*}_{N}(x,\theta,\tau^{y})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=0}^{b_{N}-1}E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}}^{\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\right)+2\exp\left(-2\sqrt{N}\right)\cdot\|u\|_{\infty}.

Claim 3

For every
m\geq 0
it holds that

\displaystyle E^{x}_{\theta,\tau^{y}}\left(\sum_{i=\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}}^{\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m+1}-1}f(\omega_{i},\chi_{i},c_{i})\,\Bigg|\,\kappa^{*}_{0}\oplus\kappa^{*}_{m}\right)\leq\frac{v_{1-y}(\pi)}{y}.

Consider the event

\partial B_{\ell_{1}}(\pi_{M},c_{*})

\displaystyle p=\frac{\|z-p\|_{1}}{\|\xi-z\|_{1}}\cdot\xi+\frac{\|\xi-p\|_{1}}{\|\xi-z\|_{1}}\cdot z.

As
v_{\delta}
is concave on
\Delta(K)
for every
\delta\in[0,1)
we obtain that

\displaystyle v_{\delta}(p)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|z-p\|_{1}}{\|\xi-z\|_{1}}\cdot v_{\delta}(\xi)+\frac{\|\xi-p\|_{1}}{\|\xi-z\|_{1}}\cdot v_{\delta}(z)

\displaystyle=v_{\delta}(\xi)+\frac{\|\xi-p\|_{1}}{\|\xi-z\|_{1}}(v_{\delta}(z)-v_{\delta}(\xi))

\displaystyle\geq v_{\delta}(\xi)-\|u\|_{\infty}\frac{\|\xi-p\|_{1}}{c_{*}/2},

where the last inequality follows from
0\leq v_{\delta}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}
and the inequality
\|\xi-z\|_{1}\geq c_{*}/2
which holds due to relation (
31
). Therefore we have obtained that

\displaystyle v_{\delta}(p)-v_{\delta}(\xi)\geq-\frac{2\|u\|_{\infty}}{c_{*}}\|\xi-p\|_{1},\quad\forall\xi,p\in B_{\ell_{1}}(\pi_{M},c_{*}/2).

Changing the roles of
\xi
and

we obtain that Eq. (

) holds, thus proving the Lemma.
∎

The last preliminary result required for the proof of Theorem

is stated in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3
.

For
\delta\in(1-c_{*}/2,1)
and every

it holds that:

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi)-v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})|\leq O\left((1-\delta)\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\right).

Proof of Lemma
3
.

By an upper bound on the mixing time of Markov chains due to Levin and Peres (see Section 4.5 and 4.7 in Levin and Peres
[
13
]
) there exists a constant
C
that depends only on
M
and an integer
N_{\delta}\leq C\lceil\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-\delta}\rceil
such that
\xi M^{N_{\delta}}\in B_{\ell_{1}}(\pi_{M},1-\delta)
for every
\delta\in[0,1)
. As we assumed that
\delta>1-c_{*}/2
, we have that
1-\delta<c_{*}/2
, and thus using Lemma
2
and Corollary
2
we obtain that

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi)-v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi)-v_{\delta}(\xi\,M^{N_{\delta}})|+\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi\,M^{N_{\delta}})-v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N_{\delta}(1-\delta)\|u\|_{\infty}}{\delta}+\frac{2\|u\|_{\infty}}{c_{*}}(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\|u\|_{\infty}}{1-c_{*}/2}\lceil\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-\delta}\rceil(1-\delta)+\frac{2\|u\|_{\infty}}{c_{*}}(1-\delta).

As the function

\delta\to\frac{C\|u\|_{\infty}}{1-c_{*}/2}\lceil\log_{2}\frac{1}{1-\delta}\rceil(1-\delta)+\frac{2\|u\|_{\infty}}{c_{*}}(1-\delta)

we complete the proof of the Lemma.

We are now in position to deduce Theorem

\displaystyle\leq O\left((1-\delta)\log_{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)\right).

Using the latter and Lemma

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi)-v_{\infty}|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\Delta(K)}|v_{\delta}(\xi)-v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})|+|v_{\delta}(\pi_{M})-v_{\infty}|

7.1
Optimal Stationary Strategies via MDP Reduction

Similarly to the Markovian persuasion model, our starting point is to reduce the repeated game model to an MDP. Such a reduction builds on known arguments and observations showcased in Renault
[
15
]
.

The MDP is over the state space
\Delta(K)
. The set of actions
\mathcal{A}_{\xi}
at each state
\xi\in\Delta(K)
is
\Delta(I)^{K}
. Namely, the decision maker in the MDP (Player 1 in this case) may assign to each state in
K
a mixed action over
I
. For each action
\chi\in\Delta(I)^{K}
, let
\chi^{\ell}\in\Delta(I)
denote the
\ell
’th coordinate of
\chi
, where
\ell\in K
. Next, for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
,
\chi\in\Delta(I)^{K}
, denote by
\chi(\xi)\in\Delta(I)
the probability measure defined by
\chi(\xi)[i]:=\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\cdot\chi^{\ell}[i]
for every
i\in I
. In terms of the original model,
\chi(\xi)[i]
describes the total probability of choosing action
i\in I
, given the belief
\xi
and one-stage strategy
\chi
. In the case where
\chi(\xi)[i]>0
define
\xi(\chi,i)\in\Delta(K)
by

\displaystyle\xi(\chi,i)[\ell]:=\frac{\xi^{\ell}\cdot\chi^{\ell}[i]}{\chi(\xi)[i]},\quad\forall\ell\in K.

If
\chi(\xi)[i]=0
define
\xi(\chi,i)\in\Delta(K)
arbitrarily. In terms of the original model, the belief
\xi(\chi,i)\in\Delta(K)
corresponds to the posterior belief of Player 2 over
K
upon observing the action
i
, given that his prior belief was
\xi\in\Delta(K)
, and given that he knew the one-shot strategy
\chi
of Player 1. Thus, just in the Markovian persuasion model, given that the posterior belief equals
\xi(\chi,i)
, the prior belief in the next stage is shifted to
\xi(\chi,i)M
. For this reason we set the stochastic transition rule
\rho
in the current MDP by

\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\chi)=\xi(\chi,i)M~~\text{with probability}~~\chi(\xi)[i].

The reward function
r
assigns to each state
\xi
and action
\chi
, the number
r(\xi,\chi)
, defined by

\displaystyle r(\xi,\chi):=\min_{j\in J}\sum_{\ell\in K}\xi^{\ell}\sum_{i\in I}\chi^{\ell}[i]G^{\ell}(i,j).

(32)

The number
r(\xi,\chi)
describes the expected stage payoff of Player 1 when Player 2 chooses his optimal response to the one-stage strategy
\chi
of Player 1 at the belief
\xi
. Note that by relation (
3.4
) we may assume that Player 2 knows the strategy
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
of Player 1, and therefore he can play his optimal responses at every stage, based on his prior belief at that stage.

To summarize in different terms, the MDP reformulates the repeated game as an optimization problem of Player 1 only, where Player 2 is now substituted with a non-strategic device that counters each action of Player 1 with an optimal response at the given state as reflected in (
32
). The pure strategies and mixed strategies in such MDP are defined in the same fashion as in Subsection
4.1
.

The MDP reformulation allows us to apply the dynamic programming principle, just as in the Markovian persuasion model. Using such principle (e.g., Theorem 1.22 in
[
19
]
) we obtain for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
the following recursive formula for
V_{\delta}(\xi)
:

\displaystyle V_{\delta}(\xi)=\sup_{\chi\in\Delta(I)^{K}}\bigg\{(1-\delta)\cdot r(\xi,\chi)+\delta\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\chi(\xi)[i]\cdot V_{\delta}(\xi(\chi,i)M)\bigg\}.

(33)

The function
V_{\delta}:\Delta(K)\to\mathbb{R}
is known to be concave, and thus continuous. Therefore, by definition, for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
the mapping
\chi\mapsto\chi(\xi)[i]\cdot V_{\delta}(\xi(\chi,i)M)
is also continuous on the domain
\Delta(I)^{K}
. This together with the continuity of the mapping
\chi\mapsto r(\xi,\chi)
on
\Delta(I)^{K}
, implies the supremum in Eq. (
33
) is attained for every
\xi\in\Delta(K)
. Hence, for every
\delta\in[0,1)
there exists an optimal stationary strategy of Player 1, which we’ll denote by
\hat{\chi}_{\delta}
, where
\hat{\chi}_{\delta}:\Delta(K)\to\Delta(I)^{K}
is selected so that

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{\delta}(\xi)\in\underset{\chi\in\Delta(I)^{K}}{\text{arg\, max}}\bigg\{(1-\delta)\cdot r(\xi,\chi)+\delta\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\chi(\xi)[i]\cdot V_{\delta}(\xi(\chi,i)M)\bigg\},\quad\forall\xi\in\Delta(K).

To conclude the current discussion, let us define for every
q\in\Delta(K)
and
n\geq 1
, the random variable
\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)\in\Delta(K)
by

\displaystyle(\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta))[\ell]=P^{q}_{\hat{\chi}_{\delta},\beta}\left(X_{n}=\ell\,|\,i_{1},j_{1},...,i_{n-1},j_{n-1}\right),\quad\forall\ell\in K.

The random variable
\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)
describes the prior belief of Player 2 over
K
at the start of stage
n
, given that Player 1 follows the strategy
\hat{\chi}_{\delta}
. A well-known fact in the literature on repeated games with incomplete information on one-side is that the definition of
\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)
is independent of the choice of
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, by the definition of
\hat{\chi}_{\delta}
, the value of
\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)
is also independent of the previous actions
j_{1},...,j_{n-1}
of Player 2. For every
n\geq 1
, denote by
\hat{\mu}^{*}_{n}(\delta,q)\in\Delta(\Delta(K))
the distribution of
\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{*}_{n}(\delta,q)(E):=P^{q}_{\hat{\chi}_{\delta},\beta}\,(\{\hat{q}_{n}(q,\delta)\in E\}),\quad\forall~\text{Borel}~E\subseteq\Delta(K).

(34)

An important property in regards to such distribution is that
\hat{\mu}^{*}_{n}(\delta,\xi)
has mean
\xi M^{n-1}
, for every
n
and
\delta
. Put differently,
\hat{\mu}^{*}_{n}(\delta,\xi)
is a split of
\xi M^{n-1}
. In particular, for every
n
,
\hat{\mu}^{*}_{n}(\delta,\pi)
is a split of
\pi
, whenever
\pi
is an invariant distribution of
M
.

Appendix B
Sorin’s Proof

The current section provides a detailed version of Sorin’s proof for the Aumann Maschler model, which corresponds to the case where
M=\text{Id}_{k}
in the framework of Subsection
3.4
. Assume that the game starts from the prior
q\in\Delta(K)
.

For any sequence of numbers
\{a_{n}\,:\,n\geq 1\}
and
0<\lambda\leq 1
define:

F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\}):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}a_{m+n}.

Note that
F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\})
equals to the
(1-\lambda)
-discounted valuation of the shifted sequence
\{a_{m+1},a_{m+2},...\}
. The key algebraic property used by Sorin is the following: Assume that
0<\mu<\lambda\leq 1
. Then one has that

\displaystyle F_{\mu}^{0}(\{a_{n}\})=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}F_{\lambda}^{0}(\{a_{n}\})+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}(\lambda-\mu)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(1-\mu)^{m}F_{\lambda}^{m+1}(\{a_{n}\}).

(36)

In particular, the
(1-\mu)
-discounted valuation of
\{a_{n}\}
can be decomposed to a convex combination of the
(1-\lambda)
-discounted valuations of the shifted sequences
\{(a_{m},a_{m+1},...)\,|\,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Next, let
\beta_{\lambda}(q)\in\mathcal{B}
denote an optimal strategy for Player 2 in the game with discount factor
(1-\lambda)
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Define
a_{n}:=E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta_{\lambda}(q)}\left[G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]
for every
n\geq 1
. Then for every
m\geq 0
it holds that:

\displaystyle F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\})\leq V_{1-\lambda}(q).

Proof of Lemma
5
.

For
m=0
the inequality is immediate from the optimality of
\beta_{\lambda}(q)
, and the definition of
F_{\lambda}^{0}
and
\{a_{n}\}
. Next, consider the case where
m\geq 1
and assume by contradiction that
F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\})>V_{1-\lambda}(q)
. Consider the following strategy
\beta^{*}\in\mathcal{B}
of Player 2: along stages
1,...,m
follow
\beta_{\lambda}(q)
; then, starting from stage
m+1
, forget all previous information, and start playing
\beta_{\lambda}(q)
anew.

By the stationarity of the underlying dynamics of the game we have that:

\displaystyle E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{m+n},j_{m+n})\right]\leq V_{1-\lambda}(q)<F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\}).

(37)

On the other hand, as by definition
a_{n}=E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]
for every
n=1,...,m
we obtain that with the help of (
37
) that

\displaystyle E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]=F_{\lambda}^{0}(\{a_{n}\})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}a_{n}+(1-\lambda)^{m}F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\})

\displaystyle\quad>\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(1-\lambda)^{m}E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{m+n},j_{m+n})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right].

As the inequality is strict, and holds for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, it in particular holds for the optimal strategy
\alpha_{1-\lambda}(q)
of Player 1 in the game with discount factor
1-\lambda
. For such a strategy the above inequality suggests that:

\displaystyle V_{1-\lambda}(q)>E^{q}_{\alpha_{1-\lambda}(q),\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right].

However, as by the definition of
\alpha_{1-\lambda}(q)
we have

\displaystyle E^{q}_{\alpha_{1-\lambda}(q),\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]\geq V_{1-\lambda}(q),

thus arriving at a contradiction. Therefore,
F_{\lambda}^{m}(\{a_{n}\})\leq V_{1-\lambda}(q)
for every
m\geq 1
as required.
∎

Next, let us fix some
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and consider once again the sequence
a_{n}:=E^{q}_{\alpha,\beta_{\lambda}(q)}\left[G^{X_{n}}(i_{n},j_{n})\right]
. Using Lemma
5
and relation (
36
) we obtain that

\displaystyle F_{\mu}^{0}(\{a_{n}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}F_{\lambda}^{0}(\{a_{n}\})+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}(\lambda-\mu)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(1-\mu)^{m}F_{\lambda}^{m+1}(\{a_{n}\})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{\lambda}V_{1-\lambda}(q)+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}(\lambda-\mu)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(1-\mu)^{m}V_{1-\lambda}(q)

\displaystyle=V_{1-\lambda}(q).

As the above was true for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, by the definition of
F_{\mu}^{0}
, we obtain that
V_{1-\mu}(q)\leq V_{1-\lambda}(q)
for every
\mu<\lambda
. This suffices for the proof of the monotonicity in the Aumann and Maschler model.

Consider now the case where
M\neq\text{Id}_{k}
and let
\pi
be an invariant distribution of
M
. The proof above can be duplicated, in the same manner with
q=\pi
. Indeed, the argument in the proof of Lemma
5
according to which Player 2 can start following
\beta_{\lambda}(\pi)
starting from stage
m+1
to guarantee that

\displaystyle E^{\pi}_{\alpha,\beta^{*}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda(1-\lambda)^{n-1}G^{X_{m+n}}(i_{m+n},j_{m+n})\right]\leq V_{1-\lambda}(\pi),\quad\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A},

only requires that starting from stage
m+1
, the dynamics of the states are the same as those starting from the first stage. However, this holds, as the Markov chain’s distribution over
K
at stage
m+1
, is just
\pi M^{m}=\pi
. Therefore, the result for the general case follows by the same arguments as before.
