Melatonin Attenuates the Inflammatory Response of Astrocyte Following Ischemia Stroke through SIRT1/NF-κΒ Signaling Pathway
Authors: Hao Zhuang, Wen Lei, Weiyi Huang, Chao Cheng, Jun Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke (IS) remains a significant health burden with high rates of disability and mortality. Rapid reperfusion is critical for treating IS, yet paradoxically, it induces reperfusion injury. Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cells in the CNS, are increasingly recognized for their role in neuroinflammation and tissue repair post-ischemia. Silent information regulator 2 homolog 1 (SIRT1), a NAD + -dependent deacetylase, has emerged as a key regulator of cell survival, metabolism, and neuroprotection. Melatonin, an endogenous hormone with potent anti-inflammatory properties, has demonstrated the ability to mitigate neuroinflammatory processes, including the modulation of microglial activation through upregulation of the expression of SIRT1. However, the role of melatonin in the activation of inflammation in astrocytes after ischemic stroke remains unclear. This study investigates the role of melatonin in modulating astrocyte inflammatory activation following ischemic stroke and explores the potential involvement of SIRT1. Using rat models of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (tMCAO/R) and in vitro oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) injury in primary astrocytes, we demonstrate that melatonin treatment significantly reduces inflammation by enhancing SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of NF-κB, a critical regulator of inflammatory responses. Our findings suggest that the melatonin-SIRT1 axis may represent a novel therapeutic target for the treatment of ischemic stroke, highlighting the potential of pharmacological interventions that harness this pathway to attenuate neuroinflammation and promote neuroprotection.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is the most common type of stroke, characterized by sudden onset, rapid progression, a high rate of disability, and a high mortality rate, making it a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [1]. Treatment focuses on rapid reperfusion to salvage the ischemic penumbra, but this process can also lead to reperfusion injury [2]. While restoring blood and oxygen to the brain is crucial, it can trigger additional damage, including inflammation, oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, neuronal loss, and glial cell activation [3]. Among these, neuroinflammation plays a key role in exacerbating ischemia–reperfusion injury [4].

Glial cells, the largest cell population in the central nervous system (CNS), have garnered significant attention due to their activation in response to CNS injury [5]. Astrocytes are the most abundant type of glial cell in the central nervous system, widely distributed throughout brain tissue and constitute a significant component of the CNS [6]. They play a crucial role in many physiological processes, such as the formation and maintenance of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), synaptogenesis, neurotransmission, and metabolic regulation [7] [8]. Previous research has primarily focused on the inflammatory activation role of microglia in ischemia reperfusion injury [9]. However, recent studies have increasingly highlighted the significant role of astrocytes in this process as well [10]. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms of astrocyte inflammatory activation following cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury and developing interventions targeting these processes could be crucial for mitigating ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is an endogenous hormone secreted by the pineal gland. In recent years, numerous experimental studies have found that melatonin can reduce cerebral infarct size and brain edema after ischemia–reperfusion injury and improve neurological function by regulating oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, apoptosis, autophagy, glutamate excitotoxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction [11]. Additionally, a recent single-center clinical trial has preliminarily confirmed that early administration of melatonin may help improve neurological recovery in patients with ischemic stroke [12]. Current studies have also found that melatonin can exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the proliferation and activation of astrocytes, thereby reducing ischemia–reperfusion injury [13] [14]. However, research on the role of melatonin in the inflammatory activation of astrocytes remains to be further explored. Previous studies have shown that melatonin inhibits the inflammatory activation of microglia after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion [15] [16]. Furthermore, in neurodegenerative diseases and traumatic brain injury, melatonin has been shown to reduce the inflammatory response of microglia and inhibit inflammatory signal transduction [17] [18] [19]. Additionally, some studies have elucidated that SIRT1, possibly acting as a downstream target of melatonin, can be activated to exert its protective effects [20] [21]. However, this phenomenon is not well understood in the context of inflammatory activation in astrocytes during cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Silent information regulator 2 homolog 1 (SIRT1) is a NAD + -dependent deacetylase that regulates cell survival and metabolism. In recent years, increasing research has shown that SIRT1 plays a significant role in ischemic reperfusion injury [22] [23]. SIRT1 can deacetylates various target proteins, including histones, p53, and NF-κB, thereby regulating their activity. The expression level of SIRT1 is regulated by a variety of factors, including transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. As an NAD + -dependent deacetylase, its activity is directly related to the NAD +/NADH ratio. AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) activation can enhance SIRT1 activity, thus playing an important role in energy balance [24]. The activity and stability of SIRT1 can also be regulated by post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and ubiquitination [25]. Stress responses such as oxidative stress and inflammation can affect the expression and activity of SIRT1. Research has shown that melatonin can indirectly regulate the expression and activity of NAD + -dependent SIRT1 by clearing ROS production and adjusting the NAD +/NADH ratio [26]. Whether this regulatory mechanism exists in astrocytes during cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury is not yet clear.

This study aims to investigate the effects of prophylactic melatonin on the inflammatory activation of astrocytes following ischemic stroke and to elucidate its potential mechanisms. We found that melatonin exerts anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects in a rat model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) and in primary astrocyte cultures subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) injury. Compared to previous studies, we have further explored the role of prophylactic use of melatonin in the inflammatory activation of astrocytes, we used co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), molecular docking, and immunofluorescence co-localization techniques to reveal a novel perspective: melatonin confers protection by enhancing SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in astrocytes. Our findings provide a robust scientific basis for drug development in clinical trials targeting cerebral ischemia.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All the experimental animals came from the Animal Experiment Center of Wuxi People’s Hospital, and the contents of the experimental animal research were examined by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Wuxi People’s Hospital, and conformed to the Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals issued by the National Science and Technology Commission, the Implementing Rules for the Management of Medical Experimental Animals issued by the Ministry of Health, and the relevant statutes of the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Wuxi People’s Hospital, with the Approval No. (2024)234.

The rats were housed in a controlled environment with a temperature of 22 °C and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. The animals were randomly divided into four groups: sham-operated group, model group, model + vehicle group and model treatment group. All experiments were conducted according to standard laboratory procedures for randomization and blinding. Rat brain samples were collected at different time points after reperfusion at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively, and subsequent experiments were performed.

tMCAO/R Model and rCBF Monitoring

The tMCAO/R surgery was performed to induce focal cerebral ischemia, as previously reported [27]. Briefly, after anesthetizing the rat with isoflurane, position it in a supine position and maintain the anesthesia. Throughout all surgical procedures, maintain the body temperature at 37 °C ± 1 °C using a heating pad. Perform a midline incision to expose the right common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery (ECA), and internal carotid artery (ICA). Then, ligate the distal end of the ECA and place a temporary clip on the proximal end to block blood flow. Insert the nylon monofilament into the ICA through the stump of the ECA, and then advance it to the origin of the MCA and leave it in place to restrict blood flow. To allow reperfusion, remove the filament after 40 min of occlusion. Perform the same procedure on sham-operated rats without occluding the artery, and keep them on the surgical platform for 40 min. All surgeries were performed during the daylight between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m. The relative cerebral blood flow (rCBF) of rats was monitored from pre-surgery to occlusion and reperfusion using the laser speckle contrast imaging system (RFLSI ZW, RWD Life Science). A successful tMCAO/R animal model is defined when the rCBF decreases to ≤ 20% during occlusion and recovers to over 80% of baseline after reperfusion. A total of 37 rats were included in the experiment, of which 11 were excluded.

Drug Treatment

Melatonin was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and dissolved in DMSO. To exclude the neuroprotective effect of DMSO, we added a model + DMSO group in the design of our experimental groups. In vivo, the melatonin-treated group received intraperitoneal injections of melatonin (50 mg/kg) every two days for one week prior to modeling. The sham and stroke groups and the vehicle group received an equal volume of saline or DMSO. In vitro, melatonin dilutions were prepared in complete medium (DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin), with the final concentration of DMSO being less than 0.01%. In all experiments, astrocytes were pretreated with melatonin for 24 h.

5-Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride (TTC) Staining

2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining was performed 24 h after the tMCAO/R surgery to estimate the extent of cerebral infarction, the rats were decapitated, and their brains were immediately removed. The brains from each group were quickly placed on ice and sliced into approximately 4-mm thick sections. These sections were then placed into the TTC solution (Millipore #17,779), concentration is 10 mg/ml, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. In this process, the colorless TTC is reduced to formazan by succinate dehydrogenase in the mitochondria of living cells, turning viable brain tissue red, while the infarcted tissue remains white. The infarct volume was calculated using ImageJ software.

Neurological Function Score

Neurological function in mice 14 days post-MCAO/R was assessed using the modified neurological severity score (mNSS). The modified neurological severity score (mNSS) was performed to evaluate the sensory, reflex, balance, and motor behaviors of the animals. The maximum mNSS score is 18 points, with higher scores indicating more severe neurological damage [28].

H&E Staining

Sagittal sections of rat brain slices for H&E staining were taken from the infarcted lateral hemisphere. The rat brain tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, gradually dehydrated and embedded in paraffin for sectioning. The microtome cuts the paraffin block into 4-μm sections, which are then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and mounted for observation.

Primary Astrocytes Culture and Identification

The culture of primary astrocytes was conducted as previously described [29]. Briefly, primary astrocytes were prepared from newborn SD rats (1–2 days old). The cerebral cortex was isolated, then washed and minced to remove the white matter and meninges. The washed cerebral cortex was digested with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 15 min. The mixture was dispersed using a pipette until most tissue clumps disappeared, then terminated with complete medium. The mixture was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube and left to stand for 10 min to allow the tissue to settle at the bottom of the tube. The supernatant was then carefully aspirated and centrifuged (300 g, 5 min). After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in the complete medium to the desired density, and then seeded into 25 cm2 culture flasks pre-coated with poly-D-lysine. The cells were cultured in a cell incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air. After 24 h, the culture medium was changed, and subsequently changed every 2 days until confluence was reached (typically 7–10 days). To remove loosely adherent oligodendrocytes and microglia, the flasks were sealed and gently shaken at 220 rpm for 18 h. After that, the cells were passaged once to produce purified astrocyte cultures (secondary cultures), for the identification of astrocytes, primary extracted cells were stained for GFAP/DAPI using immunofluorescence, and three randomly selected fields of view under the microscope were considered to be astrocytes if the GFAP/DAPI ratio was greater than 95%.

Oxygen Glucose Deprivation and Reperfusion (OGD/R) Model

The oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model was established in primary astrocytes, as previously reported [30]. After replacing the medium with glucose free DMEM (Gibco), the cells were placed in an anaerobic chamber filled with a gas mixture of 95% N₂ and 5% CO₂ to replace the air in the chamber. After 6 h, the cells were returned to aerobic environment, and the glucose-free DMEM was replaced with complete medium (DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and 1%). Cells were then collected at different reoxygenation time points (0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h) for subsequent experiments.

Transfection

Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The SIRT1 overexpression plasmid based on the pcDNA3.1/+ vector and SIRT1-specific siRNA were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China). After 24–48 h of transfection, the cells were collected for further analysis. Control siRNA-treated or mock-transfected cells served as negative controls.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay

Briefly, astrocytes were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well. After 24 h of culture, cells were treated with DMSO as a control or with different concentrations of melatonin (5–200 μM). The final concentration of DMSO in the medium was less than 0.01%, which does not affect cell growth. After 24 h of treatment, cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before measuring absorbance at 450 nm with a spectrophotometer, the 96-well plates were shaken for a few seconds to ensure proper mixing of the reagents. The analysis was performed in triplicate. Cell viability was defined as (OD of melatonin-treated samples/OD of control group) × 100.

Detection of Intracellular ROS

Intercellular ROS was detected using Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). DCFH-DA, as an oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe, can be used to evaluate specific intracellular ROS by being oxidized to fluorescent DCF. According to the instructions, after treatment, the astrocytes were removed from the cell culture medium, and an appropriate volume of diluted DCFH-DA (1:1000) and Hoechst dye (1:200) was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in a cell incubator. The cells were then washed three times with serum-free cell culture medium to thoroughly remove any DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells. The staining effect was detected using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence was performed as previously reported. The rat brain was quickly removed and prepared for frozen sections which were taken from the cortical infarct area of the rats. The astrocytes cell slides and brain sagittal sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS. The cell membranes were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Then blocked with PBS containing 10% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the samples were incubated with the secondary antibody (1:1000) and Hoechst (1:400). After mounting, they were observed and photographed using EVOS fluorescence microscope. The antibodies used were shown in the Supplementary Table.

ELISA

The cell culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged twice at 800 g for 5 min to remove suspended cell debris, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm pore filter. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 480 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Immunoblotting

Total protein isolated from astrocytes were lysed using RIPA buffer for 20 min with the addition of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified using BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein samples from the cell lysates were subjected to immunoblot analysis using 8–10% SDS-PAGE. The proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk and 0.05% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h, followed by incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted at 1:5000 at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, antigen–antibody complexes were detected using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control. The density of each band was quantified using ImageJ software, with each experiment performed in triplicate. Antibodies used were given in the Supplementary Table.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Astrocytes were washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min, and the supernatants were incubated overnight with Protein A/G Magnetic Beads conjugated with anti-SIRT1 or anti-P65 antibodies. After overnight incubation, the proteins bound to the beads were separated from the beads, loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to a PVDF membrane for the detection of target proteins. The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (Fujifilm North America Corporation, Valhalla, NY, USA).

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using Trizol Reagent following the instructions, Quantitative real-time PCR was performed by Custom gene qRT-PCR quantitation kit (GenePharma, Suzhou, China). The primers sequences of the detected genes were shown in the Supplementary Table. The relative changes in gene expression were determined using the 2-ΔΔCT cycle threshold (Ct) comparison method. The relative expression levels were normalized to the relative expression level of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which served as an endogenous control. The results were calculated to represent the fold induction (2-ΔΔCT). Three duplicates of each experiment were carried out.

Molecular Docking

The interaction between SIRT1 and P65 was predicted using the GRAMM Web Server for protein docking (https://gramm.compbio.ku.edu/) [31]. The protein structures of SIRT1 (Q96EB6) and P65 (Q04206) were obtained from the AlphaFold database as PDB files. The predicted protein docking results were analyzed using the PyMOL protein visualization software.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Error bars in the figures represent the SEM values of three independent experiments. Comparisons between the two groups were made using independent unpaired Student’s t-tests. Multiple comparisons were made using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with results considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Detailed statistical information for each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Melatonin Mitigates Ischemia Stroke on tMCAO/R Model

Melatonin is a neuroendogenous hormone synthesized and secreted by the pineal gland [32]. To explore the biological role of melatonin in regulating cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury, we used a rat model of stroke and reperfusion (tMCAO/R), administering a certain amount of melatonin to the rats via intraperitoneal injection (50 mg/kg) a week before each surgery. The rCBF images demonstrate the success of the modeling, consistent with what is mentioned in methods (Fig. 1A, B). TTC staining results showed that melatonin can reduce the infarct area after tMCAO/R (Fig. 1C, D). Further assessment using the mNSS neurological scoring system also revealed that melatonin can significantly alleviate neurological functional damage after tMCAO/R (Fig. 1E). In addition, 24 h after reperfusion, rat brain specimens were collected for H&E staining and immunofluorescence, and proteins were extracted for immunoblotting experiments. The H&E staining results revealed that in the sham group, the morphology and structure of the neural cells were normal, with the nuclei exhibiting a regular appearance. In the tMCAO/R group, many vacuoles were observed, and neuronal cells appeared sparse and disorganized, with widened cell gaps and abnormal nuclear morphology. However, in the melatonin treatment group, the neuronal morphology was restored and displayed a relatively orderly arrangement, also showed a significantly lower vacuolation ratio compared with the tMCAO/R group (Fig. 1F, G). Immunofluorescence was used to further evaluate the role of melatonin on inflammation after tMCAO/R in rats. Immunofluorescence results showed that iNOS expression levels in the cortex of the infarcted area of rats were significantly down-regulated in the melatonin group compared with the ischemic group (Fig. 2A, B). The results of the Western blot confirmed that the levels of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the melatonin-treated group, as well as the levels of inflammatory markers iNOS and COX-2, were significantly lower compared to the ischemia group (Fig. 2C–F). SIRT1 Expression Levels in Astrocytes Decreased after Ischemic Stroke in vivo and in vitro

SIRT1 is a deacetylase that plays a crucial role in various biological processes. Melatonin has been shown to upregulate the expression and activity of SIRT1. This regulation contributes to the protective effects of melatonin against oxidative stress and inflammation [33]. By collecting cortical samples from control rats and rats at different reperfusion time points after ischemia, the expression profile of SIRT1 at various reperfusion times post-ischemia was investigated. Western blot results indicate that the protein expression level of SIRT1 is continuously declining in vivo (Fig. 3A, B). In the oxygen–glucose deprivation and reperfusion model of primary cultured astrocytes, western blot results suggest that the expression level of SIRT1 first decreases but then it shows a reactive increase as the duration of reoxygenation extends (Fig. 3C, D). The aforementioned results indicate that ischemic stroke will alter the expression level of SIRT1, and the addition of melatonin will ameliorate this phenomenon (Fig. 3E, F). Neuroinflammation Activated in Astrocytes after OGD/R

To further explore the inflammatory activation of astrocytes after ischemia reperfusion injury in vitro, we established an in vitro oxygen–glucose deprivation and reperfusion (OGD/R) model in primary cultured astrocytes to simulate the in vivo ischemic stroke reperfusion environment. As shown in the figure, primary cultured astrocytes were identified using immunofluorescence, GFAP was used as a marker for astrocytes, and the purity of astrocytes was observed to be more than 99% in three randomly selected fields of view under the microscope, as mentioned in the previous method (Fig. 4A). Western blots were used to detect the expression of inflammatory markers at different acute time points such as 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h to verify the inflammatory activation of astrocytes after OGD/R. Western blot results showed a significant increase in the protein levels of iNOS and COX2 at the 12 h and 24 h time points (Fig. 4B, C). In addition, the PCR results showed the same results, with a significant increase in the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in astrocytes at 12-h time point following reoxygenation (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, double-labeling immunofluorescence for GFAP and iNOS in Fig. 4E, F revealed a significant increase in iNOS protein expression in astrocytes following 6 h of OGD and 12 h of reperfusion. In summary, these findings indicate that astrocyte inflammation is activated after OGD/R treatment, and the activation is most pronounced at the 12-h time point. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that prophylactic use of melatonin significantly mitigates ischemic stroke-induced neuroinflammation through the upregulation of SIRT1 expression, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury. Our results indicate that melatonin not only reduces the infarct area and neurological deficits in a rat model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (tMCAO/R) but also attenuates the inflammatory response in primary cultured astrocytes subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation and reperfusion (OGD/R). More importantly, melatonin upregulates SIRT1 by inhibiting the acetylation and nuclear translocation of the P65 subunit in astrocyte, thereby suppressing the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and exerting a protective effect after ischemic reperfusion injury. These results suggest that SIRT1 in astrocytes may be an attractive therapeutic target for ischemic stroke. The pathological and physiological processes of cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury primarily involve inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, calcium overload, apoptosis, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, and excessive release of excitatory amino acids [36]. These pathological changes can cause severe damage to astrocytes, microglia, neurons, and endothelial cells, which interact through positive feedback loops to further exacerbate brain injury.

Astrocytes play a complex role in ischemic stroke, exerting a dual effect on neuronal regeneration after being activated in the area of cerebral ischemia [37]. On the one hand, astrocytes can protect neurons by regulating axonal regeneration, ions, neurotransmitters, and immune responses [38]. On the other hand, activated astrocytes participate in the inflammatory process, secreting inflammatory cytokines, which may lead to an expansion of the infarct area and have a detrimental impact on neuronal repair [39].

Among these, the inflammatory response is a key link in the development of cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury and is considered a primary target for the treatment of ischemic stroke [40]. Recent research has found that astrocytes, which are the most abundant glial cells in the central nervous system, also play a crucial role in the inflammatory response. After ischemia–reperfusion, the inflammatory response is activated, during which astrocytes are induced to become activated. On the one hand, they secrete inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and upregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators like COX2 and iNOS, further inducing the activation of surrounding astrocytes and microglia, and other neural cells, playing an immunological cascade amplification role and mediating local inflammatory damage [41] [10]. Investigating the mechanisms of astrocyte inflammatory activation after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury and intervening in them may be an important target for alleviating ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Melatonin is a neurohormone synthesized and secreted by the pineal gland, primarily regulating the body’s circadian rhythms and sleep–wake cycles. Melatonin has previously been shown to play a role in the modulation of apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress after ischemic stroke, but the specific mechanisms of astrocyte inflammatory activation remain to be further elucidated [42–44]. Researches have confirmed that melatonin has an inhibitory effect on the inflammatory activation of microglia after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion, and in neurodegenerative diseases and traumatic brain injury, melatonin can attenuate microglial inflammatory responses and inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby reducing the occurrence of neuroinflammation [45] [46]. In addition, melatonin has significant antioxidant properties. It is a potent antioxidant that can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and stimulate the activity of other antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase [47] [48]. Our study confirmed that melatonin could reduce the expression of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) in astrocytes after OGD/R treatment, which indirectly leads to a decrease in ROS levels, potentially providing protective effects through this pathway. Our research results indicate that the addition of melatonin alleviated the inflammatory activation of astrocytes following ischemia–reperfusion, and this effect was mediated by increasing the expression levels of SIRT1.

SIRT1 is a NAD + -dependent deacetylase that plays a crucial regulatory role in metabolism, inflammation, stress resistance, DNA repair, and cell survival by deacetylating key transcription factors, enzymes, and proteins. It is currently unclear whether such protective effects exist in astrocytes after cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury [49]. Our research has found that in the in vivo transient middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (tMCAO/R) model and in primary cultured astrocytes subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) in vitro, the expression of SIRT1 decreases after treatment. Moreover, overexpression of SIRT1 can suppress the activation of inflammation. Subsequently, using siRNA to knock down SIRT1 in primary astrocytes, we observed an exacerbation of the inflammatory response. However, the addition of melatonin reversed this phenomenon. Concurrently, the classic inflammatory activation pathway NF-kB was found to be involved in this process.

NF-κB signaling pathway, as one of the classical signaling pathways, is involved in regulating various physiological and pathological processes, including inflammatory response, apoptosis, immune response, and cell proliferation [50]. In cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury, the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway is closely related to inflammation and apoptosis [51]. The inhibitory protein IκB inhibits the transcriptional activity of the NF-κB complex composed of P65 and p50 in the cytoplasm. Upon ischemia–reperfusion, damage-associated molecular patterns transmit signals through membrane receptors to the IKK kinase, causing phosphorylation of the inhibitory protein IκB. Phosphorylated IκB becomes a ubiquitination target and is degraded. The degradation of IκB exposes the nuclear localization sequence of NF-κB, allowing the NF-κB dimer to enter the nucleus and regulate transcription. P65, as a subunit of the NF-κB dimer, plays a critical role in maintaining the structural stability of the NF-κB pathway’s transcriptional activity [52]. Studies have shown that the acetylation of P65 is a key factor in maintaining NF-κB transcriptional activity [53]. Our research found that SIRT1 is involved in regulating the acetylation process of the P65 subunit. Through Western blot, immunofluorescence, and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments, we verified the interaction between SIRT1 and the P65 subunit. After cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury, the acetylation of the P65 subunit increased, leading to its enhanced nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, which activated the downstream NF-κB signaling pathway and resulted in inflammation activation. Additionally, the regulation of P65 subunit deacetylation by SIRT1 was inhibited following ischemia stroke. The addition of melatonin increased the expression of SIRT1, inhibited the acetylation and nuclear translocation of the P65 subunit, thereby suppressing inflammation activation.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that melatonin exerts significant neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury by upregulating SIRT1 expression. These findings underscore the potential of melatonin as a therapeutic agent for ischemic stroke and highlight the importance of targeting the SIRT1-NF-κB signaling pathway in the development of novel treatments for neuroinflammation. Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. For instance, we did not investigate the protective role of astrocytes in maintaining blood–brain barrier integrity, their involvement in immune regulation, or the potential effects of melatonin on these processes. Future studies will aim to address these aspects to further elucidate the multifaceted roles of astrocytes and the therapeutic mechanisms of melatonin.
