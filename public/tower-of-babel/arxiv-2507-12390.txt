Derivation of the time-dependent Hartree equations for strongly interacting dense fermionic systems
Duc Viet Hoang, David Mitrouskas, Peter Pickl

Abstract
The time-dependent Hartree and Hartree–Fock equations provide effective mean-field descriptions for the dynamics of large fermionic systems and play a fundamental role in many areas of physics. In this work, we rigorously derive the time-dependent Hartree equations as the large- N N limit of the microscopic Schrödinger dynamics of N N fermions confined to a volume of order one and interacting via strong pair potentials. A central step in our analysis is the implementation of time-dependent gauge transformations, which eliminate the dominant contribution from the interaction potential in both the Schrödinger and Hartree evolutions.



1
Introduction and main result

1.1
Introduction

Understanding the dynamics of interacting many-body quantum systems is a central challenge in quantum physics and chemistry. However, direct solutions of the Schrödinger equation—whether exact or numerical—are typically intractable due to the exponential complexity that arises with increasing particle number. Fortunately, in many regimes, effective theories allow for significant simplification of the dynamics. They capture the essential behavior of the system while reducing its complexity, thereby enabling both analytical insight and efficient numerical implementation.

Prominent examples of effective theories are the Hartree and Hartree–Fock equations, which have become indispensable tools for modelling large fermionic systems, from atoms and molecules
[
15
,
41
]
to complex nuclei
[
21
,
4
]
. In recent years, substantial progress has been made in rigorously deriving the Hartree and Hartree–Fock equations from first principles, that is, from the many-body Schrödinger dynamics. A particularly well-studied case is the semiclassical regime, where interactions between the fermions are weak and Planck’s constant is effectively small
[
14
,
6
,
5
,
36
,
18
,
19
,
26
]
. In this regime, the particles typically occupy a volume of order one, and the total density varies on a macroscopic scale. To leading order, the dynamics are effectively governed by the Vlasov equation for a classical phase-space distribution
[
28
,
29
,
37
,
38
,
11
,
12
]
. Hartree and Hartree–Fock theory recover the classical evolution in the appropriate limit, while also incorporating quantum corrections beyond the Vlasov dynamics. This regime captures many relevant physical systems, including electrons in large neutral atoms, degenerate Fermi gases in slowly varying external potentials, and dense astrophysical objects such as white dwarfs.

However, Hartree and Hartree–Fock theory are also widely used beyond the semiclassical regime, in systems where quantum effects are more dominant. This includes, for example, many-body systems in quantum chemistry and models of electronic structure in large molecules
[
24
,
23
,
41
]
, where the density can vary on microscopic length scales. In such settings, the system no longer admits a semiclassical approximation, yet mean-field theory often continues to provide an accurate description. Compared to the semiclassical regime, the mathematical derivation of fermonic mean-field equations in non-semiclassical settings has received somewhat less attention. Existing results have focused on weakly interacting systems, where the interaction is effectively subleading
[
2
,
3
,
20
,
35
,
1
,
34
]
. As discussed, for instance, in
[
1
, Appendix B]
and
[
34
]
, the dynamics in these models are dominated by kinetic effects and are largely governed by free evolution, with only small corrections due to interactions.

The goal of this work is to rigorously derive the fermionic Hartree equations (sometimes called the reduced Hartree–Fock equations) from the
N
-body Schrödinger dynamics in a non-semiclassical regime with strong interactions—that is, when both quantum effects and inter-particle forces contribute at leading order. To this end, we focus on
dense systems of strongly interacting fermions
, a notion recently introduced in
[
17
]
. From a physical perspective, these systems are natural, as they do not rely on any
N
-dependent weak coupling assumption. Instead, the effective dynamics emerge through a rescaling of the time variable. To our knowledge, the present work provides the first derivation of mean-field dynamics in this setting.

The starting point for our analysis is the microscopic description of a fermionic
N
-particle system, whose evolution is governed by the Schrödinger equation (we set
\hbar=1
throughout)

\displaystyle i\partial_{\tau}\Phi_{\tau}=H\Phi_{\tau},

(1.1)

for an antisymmetric wave function
\Phi_{\tau}\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
. Here,

\displaystyle L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})=\left\{\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})\ \middle|\ \Phi(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(N)})=\text{sign}(\pi)\,\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\ \forall\,\pi\in S_{N}\right\},

(1.2)

with
S_{N}
denoting the permutation group of
N
elements. We consider Hamiltonians of the form

H=\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}(-\Delta_{i})\ +\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}v(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(1.3)

where
v
is a sufficiently regular,
N
-independent two-body interaction potential. As usual,
\Delta_{i}
denotes the Laplacian acting on the
i
-th particle. For notational simplicity, we set the mass of the fermions equal to
m=\frac{1}{2}
.

The initial
N
-particle state
\Phi_{0}
is assumed to be close to a Slater determinant
\varphi_{1}^{0}\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_{N}^{0}
for some orthonormal one-particle orbitals
\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Our goal is to show that this Slater structure is approximately preserved over time, i.e., that
\Phi_{\tau}\approx\varphi_{1}^{\tau}\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_{N}^{\tau}
in an appropriate sense, where the orbitals evolve according to the fermionic Hartree equations

i\partial_{\tau}\varphi_{k}^{\tau}=h(\tau)\varphi_{k}^{\tau}\qquad\text{with}\qquad h(\tau)=-\Delta+v\ast\rho_{\tau}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{\tau}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\varphi_{k}^{\tau}|^{2}

(1.4)

for
k=1,\ldots,N
. Here,
v\ast\rho_{\tau}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}dy\,v(x-y)\rho_{\tau}(y)
denotes the mean-field potential, given by the convolution of the interaction potential
v
with the Hartree density
\rho_{\tau}
. We note that the Hartree orbitals remain orthonormal for all
\tau>0
.

Since no mean-field scaling is imposed on the potential, the interaction term in (
1.3
) is much larger compared to the kinetic term. More precisely, for
N
fermions confined to a volume of order one, the kinetic energy scales as
O(N^{5/3})
, whereas the interaction energy scales as
O(N^{2})
. For such strongly interacting systems, the Hartree approximation is not expected to be accurate for times
\tau=O(1)
. Instead, the natural time scale is
\tau=\varepsilon t
with
\varepsilon=N^{-2/3}
and
t=O(1)
. The rescaling of the time variable can be justified by a simple Ehrenfest-type argument: When
N
fermions are confined to a volume of order one, the Pauli exclusion principle forces them into high-momentum states, with typical momenta being of order
O(N^{1/3})
. At the same time, each particle experiences a force from all other particles of order
O(N)
. The change in momentum for each particle is thus heuristically given by

time

\times

force
=
O(\tau)\times O(N)=O(N^{1/3})
, which matches the scale of the initial momenta. This indicates that inter-particle interactions have a nontrivial effect on the motion of each particle. Over such time scales, particles travel distances of order
O(N^{-1/3})
, which—despite being small—can lead to macroscopic changes in the particle density, in particular for initial densities that vary on the same microscopic length scale (see the discussion of initial states in Section
1.3
). As a result, macroscopic observables, such as the spatial density, exhibit nontrivial variations on the rescaled time scale.

The above heuristics are implemented by rescaling
\tau=\varepsilon t
in (
1.1
) and studying

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}=\varepsilon H\Phi_{t}\qquad\text{for}\qquad\varepsilon=N^{-2/3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t=O(1).

(1.5)

We shall compare this with the evolution of the rescaled Hartree equations

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\varphi_{k}^{t}=\varepsilon h(t)\varphi_{k}^{t}\qquad\text{with}\qquad h(t)=-\Delta+v\ast\rho_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\varphi_{k}^{t}|^{2}

(1.6)

for
k=1,\ldots,N
.

It is important to note that even after rescaling the Hamiltonian to
\varepsilon H
, the interaction experienced by each particle is still of order
O(N^{1/3})
, which is significantly stronger than in the typical mean-field regime, where it is of order one. In fact, this presents the central challenge in the derivation of the Hartree equations (
1.6
) from the microscopic evolution (
1.5
). To address this problem, we implement a suitable gauge transformation that removes the large potential term from the wave function, at the cost of introducing new magnetic-type interaction terms. Although these new terms are less regular, they are effectively of order one (per particle) and thus better suited for the derivation of the mean-field equations. To the best of our knowledge, this approach is novel and may also prove useful for other models of strongly interacting systems. A detailed discussion of our strategy is provided in Section
2.3
.

1.2
Main result

We state the assumptions on the pair potential and the initial data for the Hartree equations. These will be taken as our standing assumptions for the remainder of the paper.

Assumption 1.1
.

The potential
v
is real-valued, radial and belongs to
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Under this assumption, the operator
H
is self-adjoint with domain
D(H)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})\cap L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, where
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
denotes the second Sobolev space. Thus, the solution to the Schrödinger equation (
1.5
) is given by
\Phi_{t}=e^{-it\varepsilon H}\Phi_{0}
.

Assumption 1.2
.

We consider a sequence of orthonormal sets
(\varphi_{1}^{0},\dots,\varphi_{N}^{0})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfying

\displaystyle\max\bigg\{\,N^{-5/3}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{0}\|^{2}\,,\,N^{-7/3}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{0}\|^{2}\,\bigg\}\leq C

(1.7)

for some
N
-independent constant
C>0
and all
N\geq 1
.

The second assumption sets the scale of the average particle momenta (and their second moments). It is consistent with a system of
N
fermions confined to a volume of order one, that is, a dense fermionic system. In this setting, the Pauli exclusion principle implies that the typical momentum of a particle is of order
O(N^{1/3})
. In Section
1.3
, we describe two physically relevant classes of initial states that exhibit different spatial structures compatible with Assumption
1.2
.

We note that, under the above assumption, global existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.6
) follow from
[
7
]
or alternatively
[
1
, Theorem A.1]
. In particular
(\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t})\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
; see Lemma
A.2
for explicit estimates.

Next, we introduce the orthogonal projections

\displaystyle p^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|\quad\text{and}\quad q^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\coloneqq\mathds{1}-p^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}},

(1.8)

associated with an orthonormal set
(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Moreover, for a normalized
N
-particle wave function
\Phi\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, we define its one-particle reduced density matrix
\gamma^{\Phi}
as the trace-class operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with kernel

\displaystyle\gamma^{\Phi}(x,y)\coloneqq\int dx_{2}\dots dx_{N},\overline{\Phi(x,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})},\Phi(y,x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}).

(1.9)

In particular,
\text{Tr}(\gamma^{\Phi})=1
and
0\leq\gamma^{\Phi}\leq N^{-1}
, as a consequence of the normalization and antisymmetry of
\Phi
. Also note that for exact Slater determinants,
\gamma^{\varphi_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\varphi_{N}}=N^{-1}p^{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N}}
.

We can now state our main result, which provides an approximation of bounded one-particle multiplication observables evaluated in the microscopic state
\Phi_{t}
by the corresponding observables computed in the simpler Slater state
\varphi_{1}^{t}\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi_{N}^{t}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the interaction potential
v
satisfy Assumption
1.1
, and let
\varphi_{1}^{t},\dots,\varphi_{N}^{t}
be solutions to the Hartree equations (
1.6
) with initial data
\varphi_{1}^{0},\dots,\varphi_{N}^{0}
satisfying Assumption
1.2
. Let
\Phi_{t}=e^{-it\varepsilon H}\Phi_{0}
denote the solution to the rescaled Schrödinger equation (
1.5
) with normalized initial state
\Phi_{0}\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, and let
\gamma^{\Phi_{t}}
denote its one-particle reduced density. Suppose that for some
\delta_{1}>\frac{5}{6}
and
\delta_{2}>\frac{1}{3}
, the initial data satisfy

\displaystyle\sup_{N\geq 1}\left(N^{\delta_{1}}\textnormal{Tr}\left(\gamma^{\Phi_{0}}q^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0}}\right)+N^{\delta_{2}}\left|\textnormal{Tr}\left(\varepsilon(-\Delta)\gamma^{\Phi_{0}}\right)-\frac{1}{N}\textnormal{Tr}\left(\varepsilon(-\Delta)p^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0}}\right)\right|\right)<\infty.

(1.10)

Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
and
N\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\|M\|=1}\left|\textnormal{Tr}\left(M\gamma^{\Phi_{t}}\right)-\frac{1}{N}\textnormal{Tr}\left(Mp^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 110.96556pt\leq\exp\left(Ce^{(1+t)^{2}}\right)\max\left\{N^{\frac{5}{24}-\frac{\delta_{1}}{4}},N^{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\delta_{1}}{8}},N^{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{\delta_{2}}{4}},N^{-\frac{1}{24}}\right\},

(1.11)

where the supremum is taken over all bounded multiplication operators
M
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Remarks
.

a

1.

Since both traces on the left-hand side of (
1.11
) are of order one, the estimate is meaningful for
N\to\infty
with
t=O(1)
, where the right-hand side vanishes.

2.

The quantity
N\textnormal{Tr}(\gamma^{\Phi_{0}}q^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0}})
represents the expected number of particles in
\Phi_{0}
that are not in the Slater determinant
\varphi_{1}^{0}\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi_{N}^{0}
. Thus, Assumption (
1.10
) implies that the number of
bad particles
is initially at most of order
O(N^{1-\delta_{1}})
, in particular, much smaller than the total number of particles. The second term in (
1.10
) quantifies the deviation in kinetic energy per particle between the microscopic state and the Hartree state. Here, the assumption implies that the deviation in kinetic energy is of order
O(\varepsilon^{-1}N^{-\delta_{2}})
, thus much smaller than the typical kinetic energy per particle, which is of order
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
. For exact Slater determinants
\Phi_{0}=\varphi_{1}^{0}\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi_{N}^{0}
, the left-hand side of (
1.10
) vanishes. In this case, the assumption holds for any
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
, and the convergence rate in our main result becomes
N^{-1/24}
.

3.

Neither the convergence rate nor the restrictions
\delta_{1}>\frac{5}{6}
and
\delta_{2}>\frac{1}{3}
are expected to be optimal. In particular, we have not aimed to optimize the proof in this regard.

4.

The convergence of microscopic dynamics to mean-field solutions is typically described in terms of the difference in reduced one-particle densities, measured in the trace norm topology. This topology is closely linked to comparing expectation values of bounded one-body observables. In Theorem
1.1
, we approximate only expectation values of multiplication operators. This is related to the large potential term in the Hamiltonian, which we eliminate from the wave function by using a coordinate-dependent gauge transformation.

5.

With some technical modifications and additional assumptions on the Hartree solutions, our proof can be extended to include singular potentials of the form
v(x)=|x|^{-s}
for
s\in(0,5/8)
. To keep the presentation as concise as possible and to avoid introducing further technical details, we do not pursue this extension here. The singular case, potentially including the Coulomb potential
v(x)=|x|^{-1}
, shall be addressed in future work.

1.3
Initial orbitals: delocalized vs. localized

We discuss two classes of initial data for the Hartree equations, which we call
delocalized
and
localized
orbitals, and then explain the implications of Theorem
1.1
for each class. The terms delocalized and localized refer to the spatial structure on length scales of order
O(N^{-1/3})
.

Delocalized orbitals arise, for example, as ground states of
N
fermions in slowly varying (i.e.,
N
-independent) external trapping potentials
[
16
,
22
,
8
,
9
]
, or as so-called
\pi
-electrons in large molecules
[
13
]
. Such orbitals are extended over a macroscopic volume of order one and do not exhibit variations on smaller scales, a property that is reflected in the spatial density

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\varphi_{k}^{0}|^{2}

(1.12)

satisfying
\|\nabla\rho_{0}\|_{1}={O}(N)
. The orthogonality of the orbitals results from oscillations, similarly to the case of plane waves forming a free Fermi ball on the torus.

Another class of initial data compatible with Assumption
1.2
consists of orbitals that are localized on microscopic scales—for instance,
N
orbitals with pairwise disjoint supports, all confined within a volume of order one. Physically, such orbitals may arise as ground states of non-interacting fermions in external potentials with microscopic structure, or in molecular systems where electrons are tightly bound to different (pairs of) atoms, such as core and
\sigma
-electrons
[
13
]
. For such orbitals, the spatial density varies on microscopic length scales, which is reflected in the larger asymptotics of the density gradient:
\|\nabla\rho_{0}\|_{1}={O}(N^{4/3})
.

Figure 1.1:
Illustration of the mean-field evolution of the spatial density associated with
N
localized Hartree orbitals. Initially, the orbitals have disjoint support (left). Over time they evolve over distances of order
O(N^{-1/3})
, resulting in a macroscopic change in the spatial density (right). The grey region represents the support of a multiplication observable
M
. The illustration is conceptual and does not depict the precise evolution of the orbitals.

The qualitative behavior of the solution to the Hartree equations (
1.6
) differs substantially between the two classes of initial orbitals. To clarify this distinction, recall that on the relevant time scale, each particle effectively travels a distance of order
O(N^{-1/3})
.

In the delocalized case, since the density varies on macroscopic scales of order one, these small displacements do not significantly affect the overall density profile. Consequently, the observable density remains close to its initial configuration, and we expect that to leading order,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(Mp^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}})\approx\operatorname{Tr}(Mp^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0}}).

(1.13)

However, the situation is different for localized orbitals. Here, the initial density varies on the same scale as the typical particle displacement, i.e., on length scale
{O}(N^{-1/3})
. As a result, even small shifts can produce a macroscopic change in the system. The evolution of the orbitals under the Hartree dynamics therefore leads to an observable change in the density profile, and thus in the measured observable
M
. This behavior is illustrated in Figure
1.1
, which depicts the evolution of initially disjoint orbitals.

We emphasize that both cases are covered by Theorem
1.1
. The distinction affects the observable consequences of the dynamics: while the Hartree evolution may be nearly static for delocalized orbitals, it encodes genuine macroscopic quantum transport for localized ones.

Physical context and application.

The distinction between localized and delocalized orbitals is conceptually relevant for the modeling of large molecules, where both types typically coexist. For instance, in large fullerene molecules, electrons can be categorized into core electrons,
\sigma
-bonding electrons localized between nuclei, and delocalized
\pi
-electrons spread over the molecule
[
13
]
. Our model captures aspects of the early out-of-equilibrium dynamics for both localized and delocalized orbitals, while neglecting the presence of the nuclei for simplicity. In molecular physics, the non-equilibrium dynamics are often triggered by external perturbations such as light pulses or high-energy collisions
[
43
,
25
]
. While this highlights the conceptual applicability of our results, we stress that time-dependent mean-field theory is limited to the initial response and does not fully account for long-time processes like bond breaking and bond formation or correlation effects, for which more refined approaches such as time-dependent density functional theory are required
[
24
,
42
,
41
]
.

1.4
Comparison with semiclassical regime

To conclude this section, we compare our model to the well-studied semiclassical regime describing
N
fermions in a combined weak-coupling and semiclassical limit. The corresponding Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle H^{\rm sc}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}(-\Delta_{i})+N^{-1/3}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}v(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(1.14)

with relevant time scales of order
O(N^{-1/3})
. In this model, typical paticle momenta are of order
O(N^{1/3})
and kinetic and potential energy both scale as
O(N^{5/3})
. Similarly as in our model, the short time scale can be justified by noting that the change in momentum per particle, estimated as
time

\times

force

=O(N^{1/3})
, matches the scale of the typical initial momenta. Another important observation is that by multiplying both sides of (
1.14
) by
N^{-2/3}
, the dynamics resemble those of a quantum system with effective Planck constant
\hbar=N^{-1/3}
and interaction strength
\lambda=O(N^{-1})
, for times
t=O(N^{1/3})
. The initial mean-field data in this regime is typically
assumed to satisfy semiclassical conditions, formulated in terms of commutator bounds:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big|[x,p^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\dots,\varphi_{N}^{0}}]\big|=O(N^{2/3}),\qquad\text{Tr}\big|[\nabla,p^{\varphi_{1}^{0},\dots,\varphi_{N}^{0}}]\big|=O(N).

(1.15)

As explained, for instance, in
[
10
]
, the second condition implies that the associated spatial density satisfies
\|\nabla\rho_{0}\|_{1}=O(N)
. Thus, the initial configurations considered in the semiclassical regime fall into the class of delocalized orbitals, as discussed in the previous section, and exclude more localized orbitals such as the ones illustrated in Figure
1.1
. For a detailed discussion of the semiclassical conditions and their interpretation, we refer to
[
6
,
10
]
. Under the above assumptions, it is well known
[
32
,
40
]
that the Wigner transform associated with the many-body quantum dynamics converges to a solution of the classical Vlasov equation, which describes the evolution of a one-body phase-space distribution. Subsequent works
[
14
,
6
,
5
,
36
]
have shown that the fermionic Hartree and Hartree–Fock equations provide refined approximations by incorporating quantum corrections to the Vlasov dynamics.

Our model differs from the semiclassical setting in several important aspects; a side-by-side comparison is provided in Table
1
. While the kinetic term in (
1.3
) also scales as
O(N^{5/3})
, the potential term is larger by a factor of
N^{1/3}
. Accordingly, we consider shorter time scales of order
O(N^{-2/3})
. Moreover, the present model does not feature a small Planck constant, and we do not impose any semiclassical structure on the initial data. Preparations

2.1
Counting functional

In this section, we introduce the so-called counting functional. For an orthonormal set
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we recall Definition (
2.1
). For a one-particle operator
A
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and particle index
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we define its extension to the
N
-particle space
L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
as

\displaystyle A_{i}\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathds{1}_{i-1}\otimes{A}_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{i+1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathds{1}_{N}.

(2.1)

Next, we consider the family of orthogonal projections
P^{(N,k)}
for
k=0,\dots,N
, acting on
L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, defined by

\displaystyle P^{(N,k)}\coloneqq\left(\prod_{m=1}^{k}q_{m}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\prod_{m=k+1}^{N}p_{m}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\right)_{\text{sym}}

(2.2)

where
sym
denotes the symmetrization over all
N
variables. One straightforwardly shows the relations
P^{(N,k)}P^{(N,\ell)}=\delta_{k,\ell}
and
\sum_{k=0}^{N}P^{(N,k)}=\mathds{1}
. For a detailed discussion, we refer to
[
33
,
35
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
be an orthonormal set and
f:\{0,\dots,N\}\to[0,1]
. We call
f
a weight function and define the associated weight operator

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{N}f(k)P^{(N,k)}.

(2.3)

Moreover, for
\Psi\in L_{a}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, we define the counting functional

\displaystyle\alpha_{f}\left(\Psi,\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}\right)\coloneqq\left\langle\Psi,\hat{f}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\Psi\right\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}f(k)\|P^{(N,k)}\Psi\|^{2}

(2.4)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
.
For notational convenience, we write
\hat{f}\equiv\hat{f}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}
whenever the reference states are clear from the context. For integer
|d|\leq N
, we further define the shifted operator

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{d}\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{N}f_{d}(k)P^{(N,k)}\quad\text{with}\quad f_{d}(k)\coloneqq\chi_{[0,N]}(k+d)f(k+d)

(2.5)

where
\chi_{S}
denotes the indicator function of the set
S
.

In the present paper, the relevant choices of weight functions are

\displaystyle n(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{N},\quad\ell(k)=\sqrt{\frac{k}{N}},\quad m^{(\gamma)}(k)=\min\Big\{1,\frac{k}{N^{\gamma}}\Big\},\quad w^{(\gamma)}(k)=1-m^{(\gamma)}(k)

(2.6)

for
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Note that
n=m^{(1)}
. If
\Psi
is normalized, then
\|P^{(N,k)}\Psi\|^{2}
is the probability of finding exactly
k
particles in
\Psi
that are not in one of the orbitals
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}
. Accordingly, the quantity
\alpha_{f}(\Psi,\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N})
has the physical interpretation of an
f
-weighted expectation value of the number of such excitations. For the choice
f=n
, the functional
\alpha_{n}
measures the average fraction of excitations When
f=m^{(\gamma)}
, the weight saturates once the number of excitations exceeds
N^{\gamma}
, thus it distinguishes between states with few and many excitations. Conversely, the choice
f=w^{(\gamma)}
emphasizes configurations with a small number of excitations, assigning zero weight to states with more than
N^{\gamma}
excitations.

We conclude this section by noting the following straightforward but important properties. A discussion of further properties of the counting functional is given in Section
5.1
.

i)

For any weight functions
f
and
g
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{fg}=\hat{f}\hat{g}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{f}\,p_{j}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}=p_{j}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\hat{f}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.

(2.7)

ii)

If
f(k)>0
for all
k\geq 1
, then
\hat{f}^{-1}:=\widehat{f^{-1}}
is defined on
\text{Ran}(\mathds{1}-P^{(N,0)})
and

\displaystyle\hat{f}\hat{f}^{-1}\restriction\text{Ran}(\mathds{1}-P^{(N,0)})\ =\ {\mathds{1}}_{\text{Ran}(\mathds{1}-P^{(N,0)})}.

(2.8)

iii)

For any
\Psi\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, we obtain the sequence of identities

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(\Psi,\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{N})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\Psi,\sum_{m=1}^{N}q_{m}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}P^{(N,k)}\Psi\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi,q_{1}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\Psi\right\rangle.

(2.9)

iv)

The shifted operator arises in commutation relations, such as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{-1}\left(q_{1}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}A_{1}^{\phantom{()}}p_{1}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\right)=\left(q_{1}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}A_{1}^{\phantom{()}}p_{1}^{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N}}\right)\hat{f}

(2.10)

for one-body operators
A_{1}
, and similarly for two- and three-body operators; see Lemma
5.1
.

2.2
Gauge transformation and auxiliary Hamiltonian

Gauged microscopic dynamics.
A key ingredient in our analysis is the removal of the large potential term from the solution of the Schrödinger equation (
1.5
). This is achieved by introducing a suitable gauge factor. Concretely, let
\Phi_{t}=e^{-it\varepsilon H}\Phi_{0}
denote the solution to the Schrödinger equation with initial condition
\Phi_{0}
and Hamiltonian
H
as given in (
1.3
). We then consider the transformed solution

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}:=\exp\bigg(it\varepsilon\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}v(x_{i}-x_{j})\bigg)\Phi_{t}.

(2.11)

The gauged solution
\Psi_{t}
satisfies the Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}\Psi_{t}=\varepsilon H^{g}(t)\Psi_{t}
with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H^{\textnormal{g}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(i\nabla_{i}+t\varepsilon\sum_{j=1:j\neq i}^{N}f_{ij}\bigg)^{2}

(2.12)

where we introduce the notation for the force field

\displaystyle f_{ij}\coloneqq f(x_{i}-x_{j})\qquad\text{with}\qquad f\coloneqq-\nabla v\ .

(2.13)

While the transformed Hamiltonian has a more complicated (magnetic-like) structure compared to the original operator
H
, the important advantage is that the new interaction terms—due to their coupling with the kinetic energy—acquire additional factors of
\varepsilon
. As we explain in detail in Section
2.3
, this plays a crucial role in our analysis.

Gauged Hartree dynamics
. We now introduce the corresponding gauge transformation at the level of the Hartree equations. Given
(\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t})
as the solutions of (
1.6
) with initial data
(\varphi_{1}^{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{0})
, we define the transformed functions

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{t}:=\exp\Big(it\varepsilon(v\ast\rho_{t})\Big)\varphi_{k}^{t},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,

(2.14)

where
\rho_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\varphi_{k}^{t}|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2}
. These satisfy the gauged Hartree equations

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{k}^{t}=\varepsilon h^{\textnormal{g}}(t)\psi_{k}^{t},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,

(2.15)

with generator

\displaystyle h^{\textnormal{g}}(t)=\Big(i\nabla+t\varepsilon\overline{f}\Big)^{2}+t\varepsilon\Big(\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}+\overline{(f\cdot i\nabla)}+2t\varepsilon\overline{f\cdot\overline{f}}\Big).

(2.16)

Here, we have introduced the notation for the mean-field force terms:

\displaystyle\overline{f}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq f\ast\rho_{t}(x),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad

\displaystyle\overline{(f\cdot i\nabla)}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\psi_{j}^{t},f(\cdot-x)\cdot i\nabla\psi_{j}^{t}\right\rangle,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\overline{f\cdot\overline{f}}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\psi_{j}^{t},f(\cdot-x)\cdot\overline{f}(\cdot)\psi_{j}^{t}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle f(\cdot-x)\cdot i\nabla\psi_{j}^{t},\psi_{j}^{t}\right\rangle.

(2.18)

Remark 1
.

The second term in (
2.16
) follows from the identity

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}(v\ast\rho_{t})=t\varepsilon\Big(\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}+\overline{(f\cdot i\nabla)}+2t\varepsilon\overline{f\cdot\overline{f}}\Big)

(2.19)

which is a direct consequence of the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-2\varepsilon\nabla\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\textnormal{Im}\{(\varphi^{t}_{j})^{\ast}\nabla\varphi_{j}^{t}\}
.

Remark 2
.

The mean-field forces are of course time-dependent, but we omit this dependence for ease of notation.

Auxiliary Hamiltonian
. While the gauged operator
H^{g}(t)
exhibits more favorable scaling factors than
\varepsilon H
, its main disadvantage is that the interaction involves gradient operators, making it more difficult to control. In large parts of our analysis, we shall address this difficulty by working with an auxiliary Hamiltonian obtained via a suitable quadratic approximation of
H^{g}(t)
. Specifically, let
\{\psi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
be the (orthonormal) solutions of the gauged Hartree equations (
2.16
). We then consider the (now time-dependent) orthogonal projections

\displaystyle p=p^{\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\psi_{j}^{t}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}^{t}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad q

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{1}-p,

(2.20)

and use these to rewrite
H^{g}(t)
as

\displaystyle H^{\textnormal{g}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_{i}+q_{i})\bigg(i\nabla_{i}+t\varepsilon\sum_{j=1:j\not=i}^{N}(p_{j}+q_{j})f_{ij}(p_{j}+q_{j})\bigg)^{2}(p_{i}+q_{i}).

(2.21)

After expanding the products, we define a new auxiliary operator by discarding all terms that contain three or more
q
operators. This operator has the advantage that we can always move a free gradient in the interaction onto a
p
operator, which in turn can be controlled through the regularity of the (gauged) Hartree solutions. We refer to the resulting auxiliary Hamiltonian as
\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
and denote by
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}
the solution to the corresponding Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}=\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}
with
\widetilde{\Psi}_{0}=\Phi_{0}
. More precisely,
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}=\widetilde{U}(t,0)\Phi_{0}
with
\widetilde{U}(t,s)
the two-parameter evolution generated by
\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
. For a precise definition of the latter and well-posedness of the dynamics, we refer to Section
3.1
.

Remark 3
.

The idea of discarding terms with three or more
q
operators in (
2.21
) is reminiscent of the Bogoliubov approximation for many-body Bose systems; see, e.g.,
[
27
,
31
,
30
]
. In the present setting, it serves as a tool to control otherwise singular terms in the Hamiltonian
H^{g}(t)
.

2.3
Strategy of the proof

The old approach
. A way to quantify the closeness of the microscopic evolution
\Phi_{t}=e^{-i\varepsilon tH}\Phi_{0}
to the mean-field solutions
\{\varphi_{j}^{t}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of the rescaled equations (
1.6
) is to control the counting functional

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(\Phi_{t},\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t})=\Big\langle\Phi_{t},\hat{n}^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}}\Phi_{t}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\Phi_{t},q_{1}^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}}\Phi_{t}\Big\rangle

(2.22)

with weight function
n(k)=\frac{k}{N}
. The quantity
N\alpha_{n}
represents the expected number of particles in
\Phi_{t}
that are not in the Hartree states. Thus, if
\alpha_{n}
remains small over time—compared to one—it indicates that most of the particles follow the mean-field evolution. Mathematically, the smallness of
\alpha_{n}
implies that the reduced one-particle density
\gamma^{\Phi_{t}}
is close to the mean-field projection
N^{-1}p^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}}
in trace norm distance. The usual approach to control
\alpha_{n}
is to estimate its time derivative and then apply Grönwall’s inequality. This approach has been successfully implemented for weakly interacting systems
[
1
,
35
]
.

Why the old approach fails.
For strongly interacting fermions described by the Hamiltonian
\varepsilon H
, this method runs into serious difficulties. The main problem becomes apparent when differentiating
\alpha_{n}
. As shown in
[
35
]
, the derivative is

\displaystyle-i\tfrac{d}{dt}\alpha_{n}(\Phi_{t},\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left\langle\Phi_{t},\left[H-h_{1}(t),q_{1}\right]\Phi_{t}\right\rangle

(2.23a)

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon(N-1)\textnormal{Im}\langle\Phi_{t},q_{1}(p_{2}v_{12}p_{2}-\tfrac{1}{N-1}v\ast\rho_{t}(x_{1}))p_{1}\Phi_{t}\rangle

(2.23b)

\displaystyle\quad+2\varepsilon(N-1)\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Phi_{t},q_{1}q_{2}v_{12}p_{1}p_{2}\Phi_{t}\right\rangle

(2.23c)

\displaystyle\quad+2\varepsilon(N-1)\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Phi_{t},q_{1}p_{2}v_{12}p_{1}p_{2}\Phi_{t}\right\rangle

(2.23d)

where
v_{12}=v(x_{1}-x_{2})
, and
p_{i}=p_{i}^{\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t}}
,
q_{i}=\mathds{1}-p_{i}
denote the projections at time
t
. While each of the inner products on the right-hand side can be controlled in terms of
\|q_{1}\Phi_{t}\|^{2}=\alpha_{n}(\Phi_{t},\varphi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\varphi_{N}^{t})
plus some error of order
O(N^{-1})
, the prefactor scales like
O(N^{1/3})
. This leads to an inequality of the form
|\frac{d}{dt}\alpha_{n}|\leq CN^{1/3}(\alpha_{n}+N^{-1})
, which is clearly too large to permit any useful bound on
\alpha_{n}
over time scales
t=O(1)
. Moreover, there are no cancellations among the various contributions that could mitigate this problem. In summary, this approach breaks down for the present model due to the large size of the interaction term as
N\to\infty
.

In what follows, we present an alternative strategy to overcome this obstacle.

The new approach
. As explained, the main obstacle in the old approach is the large potential contribution in
\Phi_{t}=e^{-i\varepsilon tH}\Phi_{0}
. A second observation is that the above strategy fails to exploit the small prefactor in front of the kinetic terms in
\varepsilon H
and
\varepsilon h(t)
, as they cancel exactly in (
2.23a
). To address both points, we introduce the gauged wave function
\Psi_{t}=\exp(it\varepsilon\sum_{i<j}v(x_{i}-x_{j}))\Phi_{t}
as in (
2.11
), along with (
2.14
) for the mean-field solutions. This transformation eliminates the dominant potential term in exchange for magnetic-type interactions. Since the latter arise from commutators with the kinetic term, they acquire additional factors of
\varepsilon
. In the new generator, given by (
2.12
), the interaction terms scale as
O(N\varepsilon^{1/2}\times\text{gradient})
and
O(N^{3}\varepsilon^{3})=O(N)
, respectively. For the present model, one should heuristically think of
\varepsilon^{1/2}\times\text{gradient}=O(1)
, as the typical momentum of a particle is of order
O(N^{1/3})
. By comparing to the interaction in
\varepsilon H
, which scales as
O(N^{2}\varepsilon)=O(N^{4/3})
, this shows that we effectively gained an additional factor of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
by implementing the gauge transformation.
Hence, proceeding analogously to (
2.23a
) for the gauged evolutions
\Psi_{t}
and
\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}
, we can improve the large prefactor
O(N^{1/3})
to a prefactor of order
O(1)
. This may suggest that the old approach could become applicable if we work with the gauged dynamics instead of the original ones, i.e., for the counting functional
\alpha_{n}(\Psi_{t},\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t})
.

However, this strategy gives rise to a new problem: the two-body interaction in the gauged Hamiltonian contains gradient operators of the form
(f_{12}\cdot i\nabla_{1}+h.c.)
; see (
2.12
). In particular, when estimating the term analogous to (
2.23d
) for such a two-body interaction, this would necessarily lead to a factor
\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla_{1}q_{1}\Psi_{t}\|
, which we are not able to control by a bound that is small compared to one. This term measures the kinetic energy of particles outside the mean-field states and is difficult to estimate, even with the improved scaling factors in the gauged Hamiltonian.

To avoid this problematic term, we introduce an auxiliary Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
, defined as the quadratic approximation of (
2.12
); see Section
3.1
for the precise definition. The advantage of
\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
is that, due to to the presence of two or more
p
projections in each interaction term, the problematic term with three
q
operators and one
p
operator does not appear when differentiating the associated counting functional. In fact, for the auxiliary evolution
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}=\widetilde{U}(t,0)\Psi_{0}
, where
\widetilde{U}(t,s)
denotes the two-parameter evolution associated with
\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
, this allows us to close Grönwall’s inequality. Concretely, we shall establish for weight function
m^{(\gamma)}
defined in (
2.6
) with
\gamma\in(0,1]
that for suitable initial conditions

\displaystyle\alpha_{m^{(\gamma)}}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t})=O(N^{-\gamma}).

(2.24)

The precise statement is given in Section
3.2
. Another way to appreciate the benefit of the auxiliary Hamiltonian is that its interaction terms are more regular than those in
H^{g}(t)
. This improved regularity arises because gradients can always bee regularized by a
p
-projection, effectively giving a factor of order
p\times\text{gradient}=O(N^{1/3})
. This also enables us to derive a bound of the form
\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla_{1}q_{1}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|=O(N^{-1/4})
, which, while not necessary for proving (
2.24
), becomes relevant when relating the auxiliary evolution
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}
to the full (gauged) evolution
\Psi_{t}
in the next step.

Having established the necessary bounds for the auxiliary dynamics
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}
, we turn to controlling the counting functional for the gauged dynamics
\Psi_{t}
. As a first step, we derive a norm approximation of the form

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{t}-\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|=O(N^{-1/24}).

(2.25)

The usual starting point (motivated from the analysis of bosonic mean-field systems) to estimate the norm difference between the full evolution and an approximation in terms of a quadratic generator is the identity

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{t}-\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|^{2}=2\varepsilon\textnormal{Im}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Psi_{s},(H^{g}(s)-\widetilde{H}^{g}(s))\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}\right\rangle ds

(2.26)

together with the idea of exploiting the presence of
q
-projections to control this expression (by definition, each term in the difference of the two Hamiltonians contains at least three
q
-projections). However, for the present model, this approach fails due to two issues: the presence of gradients in the interaction and the lack of control over the
q
-projections when acting on
\Psi_{t}
. We explain the problematic terms in more detail at the beginning of Section
4
. To avoid these problems, we proceed slightly differently by first estimating

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{t}-\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|\leq\|\hat{m}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|+\|\Psi_{t}-\hat{w}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|,

(2.27)

with weight functions
w^{(\gamma)}=1-m^{(\gamma)}
and
m^{(\gamma)}
defined in (
2.6
). The first term satisfies
\|\hat{m}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|^{2}\leq\langle\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\hat{m}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\rangle=\alpha_{m^{(\gamma)}}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t})=O(N^{-\gamma})
, as established in (
2.24
). The second term is controlled via the quantity
\delta(t):=2-2\textnormal{Re}\langle\Psi_{t},\hat{w}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\rangle
whose time derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\delta(t)=2\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Psi_{t},\left(\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)-H^{g}(t)\right)\hat{w}^{(\gamma)}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\right\rangle-2\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Psi_{t},\Bigg[\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{j}^{g}(t),\hat{m}^{(\gamma)}\Bigg]\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\right\rangle.

(2.28)

The commutator term can be bounded by the square root of
\alpha_{m^{(\gamma)}}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t})
. In fact, this works in close analogy to the bound for the time-derivative of the counting functional. The first term, on the other hand, resembles the right-hand side of (
2.26
), but with the advantage that the Hamiltonians are now multiplied by
\hat{w}^{(\gamma)}
. Since
w^{(\gamma)}
assigns zero weight to states with more than
N^{\gamma}
excitations, the presence of this operator allows us to exploit the
q
-projections more effectively, in particular also when acting on
\Psi_{t}
, through bounds of the form
\|q_{1}q_{2}\hat{w}^{(\gamma)}\psi\|\leq CN^{\gamma-1}\|\psi\|
for all
\psi\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
; see Section
5.1
. In this part of the argument, we need to use the previously established bound
\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla_{1}q_{1}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|=O(N^{-1/4})
. Finally, choosing an appropriate
\gamma\in(0,1]
, this enables us to balance the different error terms and establish the desired estimate (
2.25
).

2.4
Estimates on the Hartree solutions

In this section, we introduce notation related to the mean-field evolution and establish important a priori estimates for the solutions of the gauged Hartree equations. We begin by collecting useful estimates for the various terms that appear in the mean-field Hamiltonian.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}
denote the solution to the gauged Hartree equations (
2.15
) with initial data satisfying Assumption
1.2
, and recall the definitions in (
2.17
) and (
2.18
). There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
, we have the bounds:

\displaystyle\|\bar{f}\|_{\infty}\ +\ \|(\nabla\bar{f})\|_{\infty}+N^{-1}\ \|\overline{f\cdot\bar{f}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq CN,

(2.32)

\displaystyle\|\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|\overline{(f\cdot i\nabla)}\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq CN\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\nabla\psi_{j}^{t}\|^{2},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\|Ap^{\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq CN\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|A\psi_{j}^{t}\|^{2}

(2.34)

where
A\in\{\nabla,\Delta\}
.

Proof.

The first bound is straightforward; the others follow from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}^{t}\|\leq N^{1/2}(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|A\psi_{k}^{t}\|^{2})^{1/2}
.
∎

It is convenient to rewrite the gauged mean-field Hamiltonian (
2.16
) in the form:

h^{\textnormal{g}}(t)=-\Delta+t\varepsilon R(t)+t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}W(t)

(2.35)

where the operators
R(t)
and
W(t)
are defined as

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq i\nabla\cdot\overline{f}+\overline{f}\cdot i\nabla+\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}+\overline{(f\cdot i\nabla)}\quad\text{and}\quad W(t)\coloneqq 2\overline{f\cdot\overline{f}}+\overline{f}\cdot\overline{f}

(2.36)

For future use, we define the functions

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\nabla\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2},\quad\rho_{t}^{\Delta}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\Delta\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2},\quad\rho_{t}^{R(t)}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|R(t)\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2},\quad\rho_{t}^{W(t)}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|W(t)\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2}.

(2.37)

To control these, we introduce the quantity

\displaystyle D(t)\coloneqq\max\left\{\,N^{-5/6}\|\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{1}^{1/2}\,,\,N^{-7/6}\|\rho_{t}^{\Delta}\|_{1}^{1/2}\,,\,1\,\right\}.

(2.38)

which will appear frequently in our subsequent proofs.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}
be as in Lemma
2.2
. There is a
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{1}\leq CD(t)^{2}N,\quad\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho_{t}^{\Delta}\|_{1}\leq CD(t)^{2}N,\quad\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho^{R(t)}_{t}\|_{1}+\varepsilon^{4}\|\rho_{t}^{W(t)}\|_{1}\leq CD(t)^{2}\varepsilon N^{3}.

(2.39)

Proof.

The first two bounds hold by definition. To bound
\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho_{t}^{R(t)}\|_{1}
, consider for example

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}\psi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\|\overline{(i\nabla\cdot f)}\|_{\infty}^{2}N\leq C\varepsilon^{2}N^{2}\|\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{1}\leq CD(t)^{2}\varepsilon N^{3}

by means of Lemma
2.2
. The remaining terms in
\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho_{t}^{R(t)}\|_{1}
are estimated similarly. Likewise,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}\|\rho_{t}^{W(t)}\|_{1}=\varepsilon^{4}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|(2\overline{f\cdot\bar{f}}+\bar{f}\cdot\bar{f})\psi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{4}N^{4}\|\rho_{t}\|_{1}=C\varepsilon^{4}N^{5}=C\varepsilon N^{3}.

(2.40)

This completes the proof.
∎

Thanks to Assumption
1.2
, we have
D(0)\leq C
uniformly in
N
. The following lemma (proved in the appendix) shows that
D(t)
remains uniformly bounded for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}
be as in Lemma
2.2
. There exists a
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

D(t)\leq e^{C(1+t)^{2}}D(0).

(2.41)

3
Auxiliary dynamics

In this section, we introduce and analyze the auxiliary dynamics generated by the Hamiltonian obtained from (
2.21
) by neglecting all terms involving three or more
q
operators.

To simplify notation throughout this section, we abbreviate the time-dependent projections as

\displaystyle p_{i}:=p_{i}^{\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{i}:=\mathds{1}-p_{i},

(3.1)

where
\{\psi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
denote the solution to the gauged Hartree equations (
2.14
) with initial data satisfying Assumption
1.2
.

3.1
Definition and well-posedness

For
i,j,k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
we define the following two- and three-particle projection operators. The superscript denotes the number of
q
-projections, while the subscripts indicate the set of particles on which the operator acts:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq p_{i}p_{j},\qquad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ijk}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq p_{i}p_{j}p_{k},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq p_{i}q_{j}+q_{i}p_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ijk}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq p_{i}p_{j}q_{k}+p_{i}q_{j}p_{k}+q_{i}p_{j}p_{k},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq q_{i}q_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{ijk}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq p_{i}q_{j}q_{k}+q_{i}p_{j}q_{k}+q_{i}q_{j}p_{k}.

(3.4)

Next, we introduce the relevant interaction terms:

\displaystyle(w_{\nabla f})_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(i\nabla_{i})\cdot f_{ij}+f_{ij}\cdot(i\nabla_{i})+(i\nabla_{j})\cdot f_{ji}+f_{ji}\cdot(i\nabla_{j}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle(w_{f})_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f_{ij}\cdot f_{ij}+f_{ji}\cdot f_{ji},

(3.6)

\displaystyle(w_{ff})_{ijk}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2f_{ij}\cdot f_{ik}+2f_{ji}\cdot f_{jk}+2f_{ki}\cdot f_{kj},

(3.7)

where we recall the notation introduced in (
2.13
). Note that these operators are symmetric under permutations of particle indices. Then we define for
X\in\{\nabla f,f\}
the projected interaction terms:

\displaystyle(\widetilde{w}_{X})_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{a=0}^{2}(w_{X})_{ij}^{(a)}\quad\text{with}\quad

\displaystyle(w_{X})_{ij}^{(a)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{b=0}^{a}\mathbf{P}^{(b)}_{ij}(w_{X})_{ij}^{\phantom{()}}\mathbf{P}^{(a-b)}_{ij},

(3.8)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{w}_{ff})_{ijk}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{a=0}^{2}(w_{ff})_{ijk}^{(a)}\quad\text{with}\quad

\displaystyle(w_{ff})_{ijk}^{(a)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{b=0}^{a}\mathbf{P}^{(b)}_{ijk}(w_{ff})_{ijk}^{\phantom{()}}\mathbf{P}^{(a-b)}_{ijk},

(3.9)

where the superscript
(a)
indicates the number of involved
q
-projections. We emphasize that through the time-dependence of the
P
projections, these operators are time-dependent as well.

Finally, the auxiliary Hamiltonian, consisting of all terms in (
2.21
) with less than three
q
-projections, can now be written in the form

\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{1\leq i\leq N}(-\Delta_{i})+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\left(t\varepsilon(\widetilde{w}_{\nabla f})_{ij}+t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}(\widetilde{w}_{f})_{ij}\right)+\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq N}t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}(\widetilde{w}_{ff})_{ijk}

(3.10)

The well-posedness of the auxiliary time evolution, generated by the Hamiltonian
\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
is given by the
following statement.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
v
satisfy Assumption
1.1
. Let
\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}
be the solutions to the gauged Hartree equations (
2.15
) with initial data satisfying Assumption
1.2
, and let
\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
be defined as in (
3.10
) with respect to the projections (
3.1
). Then, for every
T>0
, there exists a unique two-parameter family of operators
(\widetilde{U}(t,s))_{t,s\in[0,T]}
acting on
{L}_{a}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
with the following properties:

1.

\widetilde{U}(t,s)D(K)\subset D(K)
for all
t,s\in[0,T]
, where
D(K)=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})\cap L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
. Moreover, the map
t\mapsto\widetilde{U}(t,s)\Psi
is continuously differentiable for
each
s\in[0,T]
and
\Psi\in D(K)
with

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\widetilde{U}(t,s)\Psi=\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)\widetilde{U}(t,s)\Psi.

(3.11)

2.

\widetilde{U}(t,t)=1
and
\widetilde{U}(t,r)\widetilde{U}(r,s)=\widetilde{U}(t,s)
for all
r,s,t\in[0,T]
.

3.

(t,s)\mapsto\widetilde{U}(t,s)\Psi
is continuous for all
\Psi\in L_{a}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
.

4.

The solution map
\Psi_{0}\mapsto\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}:=\widetilde{U}(t,0)\Psi_{0}
is unitary.

Proof.

The proof of the first three items is based on
[
39
, Theorem 2.5]
, which requires that (i) the time-dependent part
B(t):=\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)-\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-\Delta_{i})
is bounded, (ii)
t\mapsto B(t)
is strongly continuous, and (iii)
t\mapsto B(t)\Psi
is Lipschitz for every
\Psi\in D(K)
.

Boundedness of
B(t)
follows from Assumption
1.1
and Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
. For instance, for normalized
\Psi\in L^{2}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, consider the term

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{P}_{ij}^{(0)}(\nabla_{i}\cdot f_{ij})\mathbf{P}^{(2)}_{ij}\Psi\right\|\leq C\sup\left\{|f|,|\nabla f|\right\}(1+\|\nabla p\|)\leq C_{N}(t)<\infty.

(3.12)

Since each gradient in
B(t)
can be regularized via a
p
-projection in this way, and since gradients can be commuted with
f_{ij}
without generating unbounded functions (by Assumption
1.1
), we find
\|B(t)\Psi\|\leq C_{N}(t)
, showing that
B(t)
is indeed bounded.

Properties (ii) and (iii) follow from the stronger observation that
t\mapsto B(t)
is Lipschitz continuous (in operator norm), which in turn follows from Lipschitz continuity of
t\mapsto p=p^{\psi_{1}^{t},\ldots,\psi_{N}^{t}}
. The latter can be established using Duhamel’s formula:

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{t}-\psi_{k}^{s}=\left(e^{-i(t-s)\varepsilon(-\Delta)}-1\right)\psi_{k}^{s}-i\varepsilon\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{d}r\left(h^{g}(r)-(-\Delta)\right)\psi_{k}^{r},

(3.13)

where the first term is bounded by
|t-s|\varepsilon\|\Delta\psi_{k}^{s}\|\leq|t-s|C_{T,N}
for all
t,s\in[0,T]
by Lemma
2.2
. For the second term, the integrand is bounded by
\|\nabla\psi_{k}^{r}\|\leq C_{T,N}
for all
r\in[0,t]
, so overall
\|\psi_{k}^{t}-\psi_{k}^{s}\|\leq C_{T,N}|t-s|
, proving the Lipschitz continuity of
t\mapsto p
. From this it is straightforward to show that
t\mapsto B(t)
is also Lipschitz continuous. This establishes properties
1.
,
2.
and
3.
The last statement is a straightforward computation using self-adjointness of
\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)
.
∎

For later use, we express the operators (
2.36
) as partial traces of the operators (

\displaystyle R_{1}(t)=\textnormal{Tr}_{2}\left(p_{2}(w_{\nabla f})_{12}p_{2}\right),\qquad W_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{Tr}_{2,3}\left(p_{2}p_{3}(w_{ff})_{123}p_{2}p_{3}\right).

(3.14)

Proposition 3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Under the assumptions as in Proposition
3.2

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}{\alpha}_{f}(t)=(\textnormal{Ia})+(\textnormal{Ib})+(\textnormal{Ic})+(\textnormal{IIa})+(\textnormal{IIb})+(\textnormal{IIc})

(3.18)

with

\displaystyle(\textnormal{Ia})

\displaystyle\coloneqq t\varepsilon^{2}N\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\left(\hat{f}-\hat{f}_{-1}\right)\left((N-1)\mathbf{P}^{(1)}_{12}(w_{\nabla f})_{12}^{\phantom{()}}\mathbf{P}^{(0)}_{12}-2q_{1}R_{1}(t)p_{1}\right)\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\right\rangle,

The identity follows from a straightforward computation. We outline the main steps. First note that by Lemma
3.1
and Equation (

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad i\partial_{t}\hat{f}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\varepsilon\left[h^{\textnormal{g}}_{j}(t),\hat{f}\right].

(3.19)

Using the antisymmetry of

\displaystyle-i\frac{d}{dt}{\alpha}_{f}(t)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left\langle\widetilde{\Psi}_{t},\left[\widetilde{H}^{g}(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}h^{\textnormal{g}}_{j}(t),\hat{f}\right]\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=:\frac{1}{2}A(t)+\frac{1}{6}B(t).

(3.20)

From here, we follow essentially the same strategy as in

1q
-terms

5.11

\displaystyle|({\rm Ib})|

4

We establish a norm approximation between the gauged dynamics

Before stating the result, let us briefly explain why a direct application of Duhamel’s formula,

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{t}-\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}\|^{2}=2\varepsilon\left|\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Psi_{s},\left(H^{g}(s)-\widetilde{H}^{g}(s)\right)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}\right\rangle\right|,

is not sufficient to establish smallness of the norm approximation with the bounds obtained so far. One of the resulting terms on the right-hand side is

\displaystyle T:=\varepsilon^{2}N(N-1)\int_{0}^{t}ds\,s\,\textnormal{Im}\left\langle\Psi_{s},q_{1}p_{2}\left(f_{12}\cdot i\nabla_{1}+i\nabla_{1}\cdot f_{12}\right)q_{2}q_{1}\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}\right\rangle.

As explained in Section

, we do not control expressions such as
\|q_{1}\Psi_{s}\|
for the gauged dynamics. Therefore, we would have to apply the two free

-projections to the auxiliary dynamics. This would lead to the estimate

5.2
Diagonalization estimates

The goal of the following sections is to establish the technical estimates used in the proofs of Sections
3
and
4
. We organize the estimates according to the number of
q
operators involved. In the case of zero or one
q
operator, special care is required to exploit cancellations between the microscopic Hamiltonian and the corresponding mean-field term.

The next three lemmas provide the preparations for estimating terms where such cancellations play a crucial role. For instance, terms such as

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,\left((N-1)\mathbf{P}_{12}^{(1)}(w_{\nabla f})_{12}\mathbf{P}_{12}^{(0)}-2q_{1}\textnormal{Tr}_{2}(p_{2}(w_{\nabla f})_{12}p_{2})p_{1}\right)\psi\right\rangle

(5.13)

This term, which appears for instance in the estimate for
|\partial_{t}\alpha_{m^{(\gamma)}}(t)|
, contains only a single
q
operator, which is not sufficient to close the Grönwall argument. To handle such terms, we follow the strategy introduced in
[
33
,
35
]
, which relies on diagonalizing operators of the form
p_{2}(w_{\nabla f})_{12}p_{2}
. After subtracting the mean-field contribution, this diagonalization effectively yields an additional
q_{2}
operator, which is crucial for obtaining the desired bounds.

Due to the magnetic-like structure of the gauged Hamiltonians, we require substantial generalizations of the diagonalization lemmas used in
[
33
,
35
]
. Most importantly, we need to diagonalize not only with respect to
p_{2}
, but also with respect to non-symmetric operators of the form
p_{2}^{\nabla}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\nabla\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|_{2}
, and we need estimates for expressions involving additional gradient operators and three-particle interactions. This is summarized in the following statements, in particular Lemma
5.8
. In Section
5.3
, we apply the results from this section to bound all relevant terms of the form (
5.13
).

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
h_{12}
denote a two-body operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},dx_{1})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},dx_{2})
of the form
h_{12}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{1}\otimes B_{2})g_{12}+\tfrac{1}{2}g_{12}(\mathds{1}_{1}\otimes B_{2})

where
g_{12}=g(x_{1}-x_{2})
is a multiplication operator for some measurable function
g:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
and a self-adjoint operator
B
. Let further denote
A
a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and let
p_{2}^{A}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|A\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{i}|_{2}
for an orthonormal set
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset D(A)
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{1}}|\langle A\varphi_{i},h_{12}A\varphi_{j}\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle(x_{1})|<\infty\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N.

(5.14)

For every
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
there exists an orthonormal
set
\left\{\chi_{i}^{1}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\equiv\left\{\chi_{i}^{x_{1}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\textnormal{span}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N})

such that

\displaystyle\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x_{1})|\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle\langle\chi_{i}^{1}|_{2}\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{i}(x_{1})=\langle A\chi_{i}^{1},h_{12}A\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1}).

(5.15)

Moreover, we have
\langle A\chi_{i}^{1},h_{12}A\chi_{j}^{1}\rangle_{2}=0
for

i\not=j
and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle A\varphi_{i},h_{12}A\varphi_{i}\rangle_{2}(x_{1}).

(5.16)

Remark 4
.

In the special case that
B_{2}=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=g\ast\rho^{A}\quad\text{with}\quad\rho^{A}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}|A\varphi_{i}|^{2}.

(5.17)

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

The proof is a generalization of
[
33
, Lemma 6.9]
where

B=1
,
A=1
and
p_{2}^{A}=p_{2}
is a projection. Abbreviating
1\otimes B_{2}=B_{2}
, we write

\displaystyle\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\langle A\varphi_{i},\left(B_{2}g_{12}+g_{12}B_{2}\right)A\varphi_{j}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})|\varphi_{i}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|_{2}.

(5.18)

Now, for fixed
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the right -hand side defines a symmetric
N\times N
matrix. Hence, it can be diagonalized through a unitary
N\times N
matrix

. More precisely, we can write

\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x_{1})|\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle\langle\chi_{i}^{1}|_{2}

(5.19)

for

(5.20)

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{i}^{1},\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}U_{ij}^{\ast}(x_{1})U_{ik}(x_{1})\langle\varphi_{j}^{1},\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}\varphi_{k}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}U_{ij}^{\ast}(x_{1})U_{ik}(x_{1})\langle A\varphi_{j}^{1},h_{12}A\varphi_{k}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})=\langle A\chi_{i}^{1},h_{12}A\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1}).

(5.21)

Moreover, using

so that

\displaystyle\langle A\chi_{k}^{1},h_{12}A\chi_{l}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{k}^{1},\left(p_{2}^{A}\right)^{\ast}h_{12}p_{2}^{A}\chi_{l}^{1}\rangle_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(x_{1})\langle\chi_{k}^{1},\chi_{i}^{1}\rangle_{2}\langle\chi_{i}^{1},\chi_{l}^{1}\rangle_{2}=\lambda_{k}(x_{1})\delta_{k,l}

(5.22)

for all
k,l=1,2,\ldots,N
. Thus, it holds

(5.23)

where we used
\sum_{i}U_{ij}^{\ast}(x_{1})U_{ik}(x_{1})=\delta_{jk}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\hat{\ell}_{+2}\varphi,\mathbf{P}^{(0)}_{12}(w_{f})_{12}^{\phantom{()}}\mathbf{P}^{(2)}_{12}\hat{\ell}^{-1}\psi\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\ell}_{+2}\varphi\|\|\hat{\ell}^{-1}q_{1}q_{2}\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq C(\|q_{1}\varphi\|^{2}+N^{-1}\|\varphi\|^{2})^{1/2}\|q_{1}\psi\|

(5.71)

where we used
\|\hat{\ell}_{+2}\varphi\|^{2}=\langle\varphi,\hat{n}_{+2}\varphi\rangle\leq C(\|q_{1}\varphi\|^{2}+N^{-1})
and

, see Lemma
5.3
. The third bound follows in the same way. For the first bound, we must ensure that

does not act on
q_{i}
for both

, so we rewrite

. Applying (
5.25
) with constant function

and

, we further obtain

. Using this, the first bound of the lemma then follows using the strategy from (
5.71
).
∎

. In order to have the expression

Lemma 5.16

(2q estimates of kinetic terms, asymmetric terms)

Appendix A
Analysis of the Hartree solutions

The goal of this section is to prove Lemma
2.4
. To this end, we state and prove two lemmas. The first one provides estimates of the energy of the gauged solutions in terms of the non-gauged ones. In the second one, we propagate the energy for the non-gauged solutions. Adding both together then proves Lemma
2.4
.

Recall that
\rho_{t}^{A}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|A\psi_{k}^{t}|^{2}
, where
A
is an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\{\psi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
is a solution to (
1.4
). Similarly, let
\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{A}
denote the corresponding quantity defined with respect to the ungauged solutions
\{\varphi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of the rescaled mean-field equations (
1.6
). In the next statement, we show that any
L^{p}
-norm estimate for
p\in[1,\infty]
on the solutions
\{\varphi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
to the original mean-field equations carries over to the solutions
\{\psi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of the gauged mean-field equations (
2.16
), since the two differ only by a phase factor.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\|i\partial_{t}\psi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(t^{2}+t\varepsilon^{5/2}\|\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{1}\right)\|\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}+\varepsilon\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla\psi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(t\|\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}\right),

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|\Delta\psi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left((1+t)^{2}\|\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}+t\varepsilon^{1/2}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}+\varepsilon\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\|_{p}\right).

Moreover, with
\rho_{\varphi^{t}}^{\nabla}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}|^{2},\rho_{\varphi^{t}}^{\Delta}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}|^{2}
,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varepsilon\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{p}+t^{2}\|\rho_{t}\|_{p}\right),

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon^{2}\rho_{t}^{\Delta}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varepsilon^{2}\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\Delta}\|_{p}+t^{2}\|\varepsilon\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{p}+(t^{2}+t^{4})\|\rho_{t}\|_{p}\right).

Proof.

The first inequality is an immediate consequence of

\psi_{k}^{t}=e^{it\varepsilon(v\ast\rho_{t})}\varphi_{k}^{t}
and (
1.4
), yielding

i\partial_{t}\psi_{k}^{t}=\left(-t\varepsilon\partial_{t}(v\ast\rho_{t})-\varepsilon\Delta\right)\varphi_{k}^{t}.

(A.1)

Moreover, by Cauchy-Schwarz and using Assumption
1.1
, we estimate

\displaystyle\|t\varepsilon\partial_{t}(v\ast\rho_{t})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq Ct\left(\varepsilon^{2}\||f|\ast\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\||f|\ast\rho_{t}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}+t\varepsilon^{3}\||f|\ast\rho_{t}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ct\left(\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\rho_{t}\|_{1}^{1/2}+t\varepsilon^{3}\|\rho_{t}\|_{1}^{2}\right).

(A.2)

Furthermore, it holds

\displaystyle\nabla\psi_{k}^{t}

\displaystyle=e^{it\varepsilon(v\ast\rho_{t})}\left(it\varepsilon\overline{f}\varphi_{k}^{t}+\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\right),

(A.3)

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{k}^{t}

\displaystyle=e^{it\varepsilon(v\ast\rho_{t})}\left(-t^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\overline{f}^{2}\varphi_{k}^{t}+it\varepsilon(\nabla\cdot\overline{f})\varphi_{k}^{t}+it\varepsilon(\overline{f}\cdot\nabla)\varphi_{k}^{t}+\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\right).

(A.4)

We find the desired estimates using (
A.2
) with
\|\nabla\cdot\overline{f}\|_{\infty},\|\overline{f}\|_{\infty}\leq CN

and
\varepsilon\|\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{1}\leq ND(t)^{2}
. In addition,
it holds by direct computation that

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon\rho_{t}^{\nabla}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\varepsilon\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{\infty}+t^{2}\varepsilon^{3}\|\overline{f}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\rho_{t}\|_{p}\right),

(A.5)

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon^{2}\rho_{t}^{\Delta}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\varepsilon^{2}\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\Delta}\|_{\infty}+t^{2}\varepsilon^{3}\|\overline{f}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\varepsilon^{2}\rho_{\varphi_{t}}^{\nabla}\|_{p}+\left(t^{4}\varepsilon^{3}\|\overline{f}\|_{\infty}^{4}+t^{2}\varepsilon^{4}\|\nabla\cdot\overline{f}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)\|\rho_{t}\|_{p}\Big).

(A.6)

∎

Since the solutions
\{\varphi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}

of (
1.4
) satisfy energy conservation in the sense that

E(t)\coloneqq\langle\varphi_{k}^{t},(-\Delta+\tfrac{1}{2}v\ast\rho_{t})\varphi_{k}^{t}\rangle=E(0)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we directly obtain the bound

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\leq E(t)+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\langle\varphi_{k}^{t},(v\ast\rho_{t})\varphi_{k}^{t}\rangle|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{0}\|^{2}+2CN^{\frac{4}{3}}.

(A.7)

By Assumption
1.1
, the first term on the right side is bounded by a constant times
N
. In the next proposition, we use a Grönwall argument to show that the upper bound can in fact be improved to be proportional to
N
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\{\varphi_{k}^{t}\}_{k=1}^{N}
be the solutions to (
1.4
) with initial conditions satisfying Assumption
1.1
. Then, there is a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\leq CNe^{C(t+1)}.

(A.8)

Proof.

We neglect the implicit
t
-dependencies in the notation.
It holds by Cauchy-Schwarz that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\right|=\varepsilon^{2}\left|\langle\varphi_{k}^{t},\left[-\Delta,v\ast\rho_{t}\right]\varphi_{k}^{t}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\left|\langle\varphi_{k}^{t},(\Delta v\ast\rho_{t})\varphi_{k}^{t}+2\left(\nabla v\ast\rho_{t}\right)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}\|\nabla v\ast\rho_{t}\|_{\infty}\|\varepsilon\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|+\varepsilon^{2}\|\Delta v\ast\rho_{t}\|_{\infty}\|\varphi_{k}^{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}N\|\nabla v\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|+\varepsilon^{2}N\|\Delta v\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}+1\right).

(A.9)

Similarly, one shows that

\left|\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}\right)\right|\leq C\left(\varepsilon^{2}\|\Delta\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\varphi_{k}^{t}\|^{2}+1\right).

(A.10)

Thus, with the aid of Grönwall’s inequality and Assumption
1.1
, we obtain the claimed bound.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

The proof is a direct consequence of Lemmas
A.1
and
A.2
.
∎
