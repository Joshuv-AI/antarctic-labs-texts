The effects of oil adjuvant on the degradation of spirotetramat and its metabolites in apple cultivation
Authors: Andrea Lentola, Werner Rizzolli, Thomas Facchini, Andrea Rivelli, Davide Refosco, Urban Spitaler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Spirotetramat is a frequently used insecticide in integrated pest management (IPM) strategies against rosy apple aphid (Dysaphis plantaginea) and woolly apple aphid (Eriosoma lanigerum) in apple cultivation. It is known that paraffin oil adjuvants increase the effect of spirotetramat against aphids. In contrast, there is a knowledge gap regarding the effects of co-applied paraffin (mineral or petroleum) oil on the degradation of spirotetramat and its metabolites (B-enol, B-keto, B-mono, and B-glu), which has not been previously investigated. Spirotetramat combined with formulated paraffin oil was tested against two aphid species and the residues of spirotetramat and its metabolites on leaves and apples were quantified using Liquid Chromatography coupled with tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The results showed that spirotetramat is highly effective against D. plantaginea, decreasing infestation by almost 100%. Furthermore, spirotetramat was shown to be effective against E. lanigerum, the reduction in infestation ranged between 67.9 and 97.7% during the last validation date. The addition of paraffin oil increased the effect of spirotetramat and affected its degradation, indicating that its efficacy could be connected to its metabolites. B-mono and B-glu were the most persistent metabolites in apple fruit overall when paraffin oil was applied.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74831-5.

Introduction

Spirotetramat is an insecticide derived from tetramic acid (Insecticide Resistance Action Committee (IRAC) group 23)1. It is effective against sucking insect pests such as aphids, mealybugs, whiteflies, thrips, and psyllids on many crops, including apple (Malus × domestica Borkh)2–5. Its mechanism of action involves the inhibition of lipid biosynthesis in insects6. During the degradation of spirotetramat four major metabolites are formed: spirotetramat-enol (B-enol), spirotetramat-cis-keto-hydroxy (B-keto), spirotetramat-mono-hydroxy (B-mono) and spirotetramat-enol-glucoside (B-glu) (Fig. 1)7. In the orchard, after leaf application spirotetramat is able to penetrate the plant tissues where it is rapidly converted, through the cleavage of the ethyl carbonate group, into the metabolite B-enol8. B-enol can move in the apical and basipetal directions into the plant vascular system through the xylem and phloem, which represents a unique systemic property2. It can be further oxidize or reduce into B-keto and B-mono metabolites, respectively. Alternatively, the hydroxy group can react with a glucose molecule to form the B-glu metabolite. In lettuce, spirotetramat metabolites mainly accumulate in the cell wall, while a small amount accumulates in soluble fractions and is transported to the shoot via transpiration. Differences in metabolism among different plant tissues were also detected, B-enol was mainly distributed in soluble fractions, whereas B-keto and B-glu were mainly distributed in the cell wall and organelles, respectively7. The decrease of spirotetramat concentration over time under field conditions has already been investigated in herbs, where the half-life of spirotetramat was 0.51–0.83 days9; as well as in pineapples, where the half-life was found to be 2.3–4.6 days10. In addition to the impact of the crop, climatic conditions, and applied amount, it was shown that the combination of spirotetramat with other plant protection products can impact the uptake, translocation and degradation of spirotetramat in tomato and kiwi plants11,12. Only limited data are available on the degradation of spirotetramat in field experiments and none of them in apple cultivation11,13.

Apple is the third most produced fruit crop worldwide14. This cultivation is threatened by several insect pests, such as aphids. Overall, the rosy apple aphid, Dysaphis plantaginea (Passerini) and the woolly apple aphid, Eriosoma lanigerum (Hausmann) are widespread parasites15. Aphids are considered major apple pests in temperate and Mediterranean climates. They can harm fruit production by their detrimental effects on fruit quality, reduce overall tree vigor due to phloem sap intake, sooty mold development on honeydew and aphids can be vectors of virus transmission15. Until 2020, chlorpyriphos-methyl was mainly used against aphids in apple production in the European Union (EU)16, and since then, the insecticide spirotetramat has been used as an alternative due to its high efficacy against these pests5. Spirotetramat is currently routinely applied in apple cultivation because of its high efficacy against aphids and other sucking insect pests17. However, it is worth noting that the approval for the use of spirotetramat is also scheduled to end in 2025 in the EU18. Nevertheless, it is already predictable that spiropidion, another active ingredient derived from tetramic acid such as spirotetramat, could soon be available in the EU for the control of sucking pests as a substitute for spirotetramat19.

Crude paraffin (mineral or petroleum) oils are complex mixtures of hydrocarbons, referred mostly to paraffinic (linear and branched alkenes), naphthenic (alkyl-substituted cyclo-alkanes) or aromatic (including polynuclear aromatic (PAHs)) hydrocarbons. Minor amounts of other compounds may also be included20.

Spirotetramat formulations can be tank-mixed with paraffin oil, to improve spread and penetration properties and therefore to exploit their full biological efficacy when applied to apple, pear, grape or citrus plants (Movento® 48 SC product information). The combined application of paraffin oil as an adjuvant is also used, to obtain a stronger insecticidal effect21. However, no data on how the presence of paraffin oil adjuvants can impact the degradation of spirotetramat are available. In fact, an improved absorption of the active ingredient can also result in a different degradation kinetics and a longer permanence of residue into plant tissues2.

The purpose of our study was to test different application timings, to assess the presence of residues of spirotetramat and its four metabolites in apple fruits at harvest using Liquid Chromatography coupled with tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and to evaluate compliance with the required Maximum Residue Level (MRL). Furthermore, the effects of a paraffin oil adjuvant on both spirotetramat degradation pathway in apple leaves and fruit and its efficacy against aphids were investigated in field trials.

Materials and methods

Experimental fields and design

Orchards were managed according to the guidelines for integrated pest management (IPM) of apple fruits in Italy22. The field trials were performed in apple orchards at two locations in the South Tyrol region (Italy) during the 2021 and 2022 growing seasons. The apple varieties used were Fuji, Gala and Granny Smith on rootstock M9, which were cultivated as slender 3.5 m long spindles. The apple orchards Fuji 41, Fuji 65, Fuji 103, Fuji 108, Gala 26, and Granny 91 were located at the Laimburg Research Centre (46°22’57.4"N 11°17’16.1"E). The apple orchard Gala 307 was located at the Experimental Farm Fragsburg (46°38’18.4"N 11°11’36.8"E). All apple orchards were covered by hail nets. Each plot contained 15 consecutive trees in a single row. The first three and the last three trees of a plot bordering the other plots were not considered for evaluation. The experiments were set up in a randomized block design with four replicates, where a row constituted a block. Between each block, two untreated rows prevented the influence of spray drift.

Insecticide application

All treatments were applied using a plot sprayer (Typ Q/014; Waibl Sprayers, Italy) with four containers (200 L) with axial fan and fan attachment. The spray rate per hectare was 1,500 L, which corresponds to 500 L of spray per meter of canopy height per hectare, and it was applied at a driving speed of 5.0 km h−1 using eight injector nozzles (Albuz CVI 80 03) on each site at 13.5 bar. The treatments were applied from both sides to the row. The controls were not treated.

The spirotetramat treatment contained 2.5 mL of Movento® 48 SC (Bayer CropScience S.r.l., Milan, Italy; 48 g of spirotetramat per L of product) per L of water. The applied amount resulted in 180 g of spirotetramat per hectare or 120 g of spirotetramat per 1,000 L of water.

Paraffin oil products were added as emulsions of oil at a concentration of 4 mL per L of water using Oliocin® Plus (Bayer CropScience S.r.l., Milan, Italy; 696 g of paraffin oil containing 95% unsulfonated residues (CAS 8042-47-5) per L of product). The applied amount of Oliocin® Plus resulted in 4.176 kg of paraffin oil per hectare or 2.784 g per L of water. In the orchard Fuji 41 in 2022, Ultra Fine Oil (UFO®) (CBC Europe S.r.l. - Biogard division, Grassobbio, Italy; 790 g of paraffin oil containing 99% unsulfonated residues (CAS 64742-46-7) per L of product) was used instead of Oliocin® Plus. The applied amount of UFO® resulted in 4.74 kg of paraffin oil per hectare or 3.16 g of paraffin oil per L of water. Bordering rows were not treated.

Validation of aphid infestation

The infestation of D. plantaginea aphids was determined by evaluating one-year-old shoots. This involved visual inspection of 40 shoots (20 per tree side) per replicate and validation date, resulting in 160 shoots per treatment. The infestation intensity (% of infested shoots per tree) was recorded.

The infestation by E. lanigerum was also evaluated on the one-year-old shoots, by counting the number of infested buds. In total 40 shoots (20 per tree side) per replicate were evaluated per validation date, which resulted in 160 shoots per replicate and validation date. On each shoot, 10 buds were validated. The infestation intensity (% of infested buds per shoot) was recorded.

Application timing trials

The impact of application timing on spirotetramat residues was evaluated in the Gala 307 orchard in 2021 and in Gala 26 orchard in 2021 and 2022. Applications were performed one or two times on different dates to assess the persistence of spirotetramat and its metabolites in apples at harvest. Paraffin oil adjuvant trials

The impact of the paraffin oil adjuvant on spirotetramat residues was evaluated on leaves and apples in the Fuji 65 and Fuji 108 orchards in 2021, Fuji 41 in 2021 and 2022, Gala 307 in 2021, Gala 26 in 2021 and 2022, as well as Granny 91 in 2021. The entire orchards, except the control, were treated with 140 g of flonicamid (Teppeki®, Belchim Crop Protection Italia S.p.A., Milan, Italy; 500 g of flonicamid per kg of product) per hectare (corresponding to 0.093 g per L) before the start of the trials in the pre-flowering stage. No flonicamid applications were performed in the orchard Granny 91. This was done to slow the development of the D. plantaginea infestation observed in previous years and to reduce the presence of other aphid species. One application was performed per trial. Reagents and standards

LC-MS grade methanol, acetonitrile and acetone were purchased from Honeywell and deionized water was obtained from the Milli-Q® Advantage A10 system by Millipore. LC-MS grade formic acid and ammonium formate were purchased from Chem-Lab and VWR, respectively, and CHROMABOND QuEChERS Mix I and III were obtained from Macherey-Nagel™. The reference material of spirotetramat was purchased from HPC Standards and the four metabolites and the internal standard acetamiprid-d3 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All of them were solids with purities higher than 97.5%. Stock solutions were prepared dissolving 10 mg of the solid in 10 mL of acetone and working solutions at 10 mg L−1 were prepared in acetonitrile starting from the stock solutions. All these solutions were stored at -20 °C, instead the calibration solutions were prepared daily starting from the working solutions.

Leaf and apple samples collection and analysis

The sampling dates for all trials are reported in Tables 1 and 2. Leaf and apple samples were prepared by mixing two of the four replicates (row 1 with 2 and row 3 with 4), resulting in two replicates per treatment. Approximately 2 kg of apples or 30 leaves were collected per sample.

Leaves and apples were ground to obtain homogenous samples and stored at -20 °C until analysis. Extraction was performed according to the QuEChERS method23. In brief, 10 g of apples or 2 g of leaves were weighed in a 50 mL Falcon tube, 100 µL of the 10 mg L−1 internal standard acetamiprid-d3 working solution and 10 mL of acetonitrile were added. After mixing, a salt mixture containing 4 g of anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄), 1 g of sodium chloride (NaCl), 0.5 g of disodium hydrogen citrate sesquihydrate (Na₂HCit·1.5 H₂O) and 1 g of trisodium citrate dihydrate (Na₃Cit·2 H₂O) was added and the samples were further mixed. After centrifugation, 6 mL of the supernatant were transferred for d-SPE purification in a 15 mL Falcon tube containing 900 mg of MgSO₄ and 150 mg of primary secondary amine (PSA) for apple samples and 900 mg of MgSO₄, 150 mg of PSA and 45 mg of graphitized carbon black (GCB) for leaf samples. After mixing, the purified extract was transferred to a vial for LC-MS analysis.

Analysis was performed with an Acquity I-Class Waters® UPLC, equipped with a C18 HSS T3 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm Waters® column and coupled with a triple quadrupole Quattro Premier XE mass spectrometer from Waters®. The injection volume was 10 µL, mobile phase A was 95% water, 5% methanol with 0,1% formic acid and 5 mM ammonium formate and mobile phase B was methanol with 0,1% formic acid. The column temperature was set at 45 °C and the flow rate was 0.4 mL min−1 starting with 5% of phase B kept for 0.25 min, followed by a gradient to 99% of phase B reached at 20 min and maintained for 2 min. Positive ESI ionization was used, the capillary voltage was set at 1.5 kV, and the source and desolvation temperatures were 120 °C and 450 °C respectively. Nitrogen was used as the desolvation gas at 850 L h−1, cone gas was set at 50 L h−1 and argon was used as the collision gas at 0.12 mL min−1. For all the analytes at least two MRM transitions were acquired and the selected ions are reported in Table 3. Statistical analyses

All the statistical analyses and visualizations were performed with R version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing http://www.R-project.org). The aphid infestations were evaluated independently for each validation date. Data were analyzed with a linear mixed model using the R package lme426. The treatment was entered into the model as a fixed effect, and the replicate was entered into the model as a random effect. Tukey’s pairwise comparisons were performed for the treatments. Models were chosen based on Akaike information criterion values, and residuals were analyzed to verify the distribution of the errors. The residue concentrations were compared with two sided Welch’s t-test. The significance threshold for all analyses was set at p ≥ 0.05. Figures were drawn using the R package tidyverse27. Abbott’s formula was used to calculate the relative mortality data28.

Results

Effect of application timing on D. plantaginea infestation

In the application timing trials Gala 307 in 2021, Gala 26 in 2021 and Gala 26 in 2022 the number of D. plantaginea infested shoots was validated. All treatments with spirotetramat reduced D. plantaginea infestation by 100%, therefore no colonies were detected in the treated plots. The highest percentage of infested shoots in the control treatment group was 8.33 ± 11.05% on June 8 2021 in trial Gala 307, 12.92 ± 22.62% on July 1 2021 in trial Gala 26 and 36.67 ± 7.2% on May 26 2022 in trial Gala 307 (Supplementary Table S1).

Effect of the paraffin oil adjuvant on aphid infestations

The effect of the formulated paraffin oil adjuvant on D. plantaginea infestation was validated in the Gala and Granny varieties. All treatments with spirotetramat, reduced the number of D. plantaginea infested shoots by 100% in the Gala 307 trial in 2021 and the Gala 26 trial in 2021, while the control treatments resulted in high infestation. The same effect was observed in the Granny 91 trial in 2021. Only in the spirotetramat with paraffin oil treatment were observed some colonies in one replicate, resulting in 1.7% infested shoots on June 9. The control showed a maximum of 30% of infested shoots on June 9 (Supplementary Table S2).

The effect of formulated paraffin oil adjuvants on E. lanigerum was validated in the variety Fuji. It can be emphasized that the addition of paraffin oil reduced E. lanigerum infestation in all trials and on all validation dates, even if significant differences were found only for some dates. In Fuji 65 in 2021 spirotetramat and the combination of spirotetramat and paraffin oil significantly affected E. lanigerum infestations on all the validation dates (May 27: df = 1, F = 221.36, P < 0.001; June 11: df = 2, F = 95.425, P < 0.001; June 25: df = 2, F = 118.81, P < 0.001; July 2: df = 2, F = 43.821, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). At the final validation on July 2, spirotetramat reduced the infestation by 74.1% and treatment with paraffin oil resulted in an 84.4% reduction. In Fuji 108 in 2021, E. lanigerum infestation was significantly affected by the treatment (May 28: df = 2, F = 11, P < 0.001; June 10: df = 2, F = 102.85, P < 0.001; June 25: df = 2, F = 86.316, P < 0.001; July 5: df = 2, F = 36.368, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2b). At the final validation (July 5), spirotetramat reduced the infestation by 97.7% and the paraffin oil treatment showed a 99.5% reduction. In Fuji 41 in 2021, spirotetramat and the combination of spirotetramat and paraffin oil significantly affected E. lanigerum infestations on all the validation dates (May 28: df = 2, F = 18.585, P < 0.001; June 11: df = 1, F = 33.203, P = 0.004; July 2: df = 2, F = 57.86, P < 0.001; July 9: df = 2, F = 130.89, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). At the final validation on July 9, spirotetramat reduced the infestation by 90.6% and treatment with paraffin oil resulted in a 95.0% reduction. In the last trial, Fuji 41 in 2022, the different treatments significantly affected E. lanigerum infestations on all validation dates (May 19: df = 2, F = 24.675, P < 0.001; June 23: df = 2, F = 516.36, P < 0.001; July 4: df = 2, F = 267.28, P < 0.001; July 13: df = 2, F = 56.244, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2d). At the final validation on July 13, spirotetramat reduced the infestation by 76.9% and treatment with paraffin oil resulted in a 98.5% reduction (Supplementary Table S3).

The effect of the addition of formulated paraffin oil on the degradation of spirotetramat was also studied through the analysis of apple and leaf samples. Figure 3 shows that the addition of paraffin oil seems to affect the degradation of spirotetramat. The parent compound was less concentrated in leaves 36 days after treatment when paraffin oil was added (t = 6.38, df = 5.82, P < 0.001), but in the subsequent samplings, 49 and 74 days after treatment, no significant differences in concentration were observed (Fig. 3b). Discussion

Spirotetramat was found to be very effective against D. plantaginea, when included in an IPM strategy for apple plants. All applications of spirotetramat and spirotetramat in combination with one of two paraffin oil formulations reduced D. plantaginea infestation by almost 100% in our trials. The threshold level for insecticide application against D. plantaginea is 1–2% of infested shoots29. Therefore, a high efficacy is needed. These results can be explained by the biology of this aphid. Since the fundatrices hatch in spring from winter eggs, no further migration to the apple trees occurs; therefore, the whole infestation relies on the infestation that occurred in autumn30. Spirotetramat has also been shown to have strong effects on other holocyclic aphids of fruit crops such as Myzus persicae in peach and Myzus mumecola in apricot31,32.

The aphid E. lanigerum is believed to be anholocyclic (developing without eggs) on apple in Europe spending the winter either aerially or on roots33. In other countries, such as New Zealand, it has been shown that reproduction of E. lanigerum, can take place parthenogenetically or sexually when both host plants, apple and elm (Ulmus americana L.), are available34. Due to the permanent presence of E. lanigerum in the apple orchard, new infestations to the shoots are caused by root infestations. Therefore, 100% efficacy of insecticide treatments is unlikely. The results showed that spirotetramat significantly reduced infestation by E. lanigerum to a tolerable level in all trials. The reduction in infestation ranged between 67.9 and 97.7% during the last validation date. These results are in line with those of other studies, which reported an efficacy of 63.3–97.7%5. Furthermore, the addition of formulated paraffin oil showed some increased effect in all trials and on all validation dates, compared to that of spirotetramat treatment without a paraffin oil adjuvant.

In the application timing trials, one or two applications of spirotetramat at different time points were studied, to assess the amount of spirotetramat residues in apple fruit at harvest, taking into consideration that the product can be applied two times per growing season and the safety interval for Movento® 48 SC of 21 days in apple. Few residues were detected in the apples at harvest, with a maximum cumulative concentration of 0.0802 mg kg−1 at 28 days post application, which is well below the MRL of 0.7 mg kg−1. Apple producers can use it with a low risk of MRL violation and it is also unlikely for the consumer to come into contact with high levels of residues. In addition, even if only spirotetramat and B-enol are currently included in residue monitoring in the EU25, our results show that they are not the most persistent metabolites under field conditions. The highest residue concentration found for spirotetramat was only 0.0130 mg kg−1 at 25 days post application, and for B-enol was 0.0157 mg kg−1 at 28 days post application. Instead, B-mono and B-gluco were clearly more persistent in apple, because they were found to have a maximum concentration of 0.0291 mg kg−1 and of 0.0221 mg kg−1 respectively, both at 67 days post application. B-keto showed a maximum residue of 0.0105 mg kg−1 67 days after application and was therefore present in the lowest quantities. In fact, spirotetramat and B-enol were found in apple samples only when two applications were applied and when applications were performed close to the safety interval. Since aphid control in apple plants is overall important in spring, standard strategies apply spirotetramat earlier. Therefore, spirotetramat poses a minimal risk of exceeding the MRL.

In the trials including formulated paraffin oil, the final residue concentration of the sum of spirotetramat and its metabolite was always far below the legal MRL, with a maximum detected concentration of 0.0247 mg/kg spirotetramat sum in a paraffin oil treatment measured 105 days after application. Paraffin oil was confirmed to increase spirotetramat efficacy. Therefore, this adjuvant is frequently included in aphid control strategies and is recommended on the label of Movento® 48 SC. A clear effect of paraffin oil on the degradation of spirotetramat was also observed in apple fruits. Overall in the trials, the residues on fruits were below the LOQ of 0.005 mg kg−1; for the positive samples, B-mono, B-gluco and the sum were greater when paraffin oil was added. In leaves, the addition of paraffin oil led to a significant lower cumulative concentration of spirotetramat and metabolites at 36 days after application. This can be explained by the faster degradation of spirotetramat and the lower amount of the main metabolites in leaves B-enol as well as B-keto. At the same time, the paraffin oil adjuvant resulted in higher amounts of B-glu and B-mono in leaves.

The promotion of faster degradation of spirotetramat and the different effects of formulated paraffin oil on single metabolites indicate that the efficacy of spirotetramat could be connected to single metabolites. Interestingly, B-mono and B-enol were also the most persistent metabolites in the apple samples, indicating their persistence also in other tissues of the apple tree in particular when formulated paraffin oil was applied. This may also be related to the faster absorption of spirotetramat, leading to more extensive distribution of the active ingredient and its metabolites within plant tissues. Information on the efficacy of single metabolites in controlling aphids could be useful for understanding the effects of paraffin oil on increasing spirotetramat efficacy against aphids.

In conclusion, based on comprehensive data about residues in apples sampled at harvest, spirotetramat poses a low risk of being present in apples above the LOQ, and if the safety interval is considered, there is minimal risk to overcome the MRL. High efficacy of spirotetramat against D. plantaginea and E. lanigerum was observed, particularly when formulated paraffin oil was used as an adjuvant. The combined application of the paraffin oil affected the degradation of spirotetramat and the concentration of the metabolites in plant tissues, suggesting the relevance of the metabolites for product efficacy. The paraffin oil adjuvant had a great impact when formulated with spirotetramat, influencing its efficacy against aphids, its degradation, and, to some extent, residue levels in apples. Therefore, it is important that validation of active ingredients also consider the use of adjuvants to accurately assess their efficacy and safety.
