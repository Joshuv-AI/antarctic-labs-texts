Sub-Nyquist USF Spectral Estimation: K K Frequencies with 6 ​ K + 4 6K+4 Modulo Samples
Ruiming Guo, Yuliang Zhu, Ayush Bhandari

Abstract
Digital acquisition of high bandwidth signals is particularly challenging when Nyquist rate sampling is impractical. This has led to extensive research in sub-Nyquist sampling methods, primarily for spectral and sinusoidal frequency estimation. However, these methods struggle with high-dynamic-range (HDR) signals that can saturate analog-to-digital converters (ADCs). Addressing this, we introduce a novel sub-Nyquist spectral estimation method, driven by the Unlimited Sensing Framework (USF), utilizing a multi-channel system. The sub-Nyquist USF method aliases samples in both amplitude and frequency domains, rendering the inverse problem particularly challenging. Towards this goal, our exact recovery theorem establishes that K K sinusoids of arbitrary amplitudes and frequencies can be recovered from 6 ​ K + 4 6K+4 modulo samples, remarkably, independent of the sampling rate or folding threshold. In the true spirit of sub-Nyquist sampling, via modulo ADC hardware experiments, we demonstrate successful spectrum estimation of HDR signals in the kHz range using Hz range sampling rates (0.078% Nyquist rate). Our experiments also reveal up to a 33-fold improvement in frequency estimation accuracy using one less bit compared to conventional ADCs. These findings open new avenues in spectral estimation applications, e.g., radars, direction-of-arrival (DoA) estimation, and cognitive radio, showcasing the potential of USF.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\diamond}=\begin{cases}\mathscr{S}^{\sigma}_{\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{0}+\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{1}-2\mathbf{v}_{i},\left(-1\right)^{i}\left(\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{0}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{1}\right)\right),i=0,1\\

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{i}
using (

) via (

), (

) amounts to a constrained minimization on
\{\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{I}_{4}}
. Thus, we reformulate (
III
),

\displaystyle\boxed{\mathsf{P2}}\ \

\displaystyle\mathop{\min}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}_{i}\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\nolimits_{i\in\mathbb{I}_{4}}\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2},\ \mbox{s.t.}\ \mathbf{u}_{i}\in 2\lambda_{i}\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{i+1}-\mathbf{v}_{i+1}}\right\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sigma,\ i=\{0,2\}.

(25)

Consider the above minimization (
III-B
) without the on-grid constraints. Let
\Delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)=\mathbf{u}_{i+1}-\mathbf{u}_{i}
denotes the vector difference. By rewriting the
\infty
-norm constraints, we have,

\displaystyle\mathop{\min}_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{i}\left[n\right]\end{subarray}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in\mathbb{I}_{4}}\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(26)

subject to

\displaystyle-\sigma\mathbf{1}\preccurlyeq\Delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)\preccurlyeq\sigma\mathbf{1},\ \ i=0,2

where
\preccurlyeq
denotes element-wise smaller or equal and
\mathbf{1}=\left[1,\cdots,1\right]^{{\top}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. Notice that, the minimization on
u_{0},u_{1}
is independent of
u_{2},u_{3}
, thus (
26
) can be split into two independent sub-problems. Considering the variable symmetry, let’s consider the minimization on
u_{0},u_{1}
, leading to

\displaystyle\mathop{\min}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}\end{subarray}}\quad

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{1}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(27)

subject to

\displaystyle-\sigma\mathbf{1}\preccurlyeq\Delta_{0}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)\preccurlyeq\sigma\mathbf{1}.

(28)

We follow Lagrange-multiplier approach for the minimization. To this end, we define,

\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left\|{\mathbf{u}_{i}+\mathbf{v}_{i}-\mathbf{\underline{g}}_{i}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{{\top}}(-\Delta_{0}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)-\sigma\mathbf{1})+{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{{\top}}(\Delta_{0}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)-\sigma\mathbf{1}),

and
\Delta_{i}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)=\mathbf{u}_{i+1}-\mathbf{u}_{i}
denotes the vector difference. The minimizer satisfies

\displaystyle{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{{\top}}(-\Delta_{0}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)-\sigma\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{0},\quad{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{{\top}}(\Delta_{0}\left(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\right)-\sigma\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{0}.

Taking the derivative results in

\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{l}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}\right)}{\partial u_{0}\left[n\right]}=\left(u_{0}+v_{0}-{\underline{g}}_{0}+{\bm{\beta}}_{0}+{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\right)\left[n\right]=0\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{u}_{1}\right)}{\partial u_{1}\left[n\right]}=\left(u_{1}+v_{1}-{\underline{g}}_{1}-{\bm{\beta}}_{0}-{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\right)\left[n\right]=0\end{array}}\right..

Notice that, the LHS and RHS of (
28
) cannot hold simultaneously due to its conflicting nature. For instance,
{\bm{\beta}}_{1}[n]=0
if the LHS of (
28
) is active. To simplify expressions, let
\eta_{i}\left[n\right]=({\underline{g}_{0}+\underline{g}_{1}-2v_{i}})\left[n\right]
and
\zeta\left[n\right]=({\underline{g}}_{0}-{\underline{g}}_{1})\left[n\right]
. Thus, the optimal solution can be categorized into three cases:

#1

|\zeta\left[n\right]|<\sigma
. In this situation, both LHS and RHS of (
28
) are loose or inactive, resulting in

u_{i}^{\diamond}\left[n\right]=\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{i}\left[n\right]+(-1)^{i}\zeta\left[n\right]),\ i=\{0,1\}.

(29)

#2

\zeta\left[n\right]\leqslant-\sigma
. In this scenario, the RHS of (
28
) is tight,

u_{i}^{\diamond}\left[n\right]=\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{i}\left[n\right]-(-1)^{i}\sigma),\ i=\{0,1\}.

(30)

#3

\zeta\left[n\right]\geqslant\sigma
. In this scenario, the LHS of (
28
) is tight,

u_{i}^{\diamond}\left[n\right]=\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{i}\left[n\right]+(-1)^{i}\sigma),\ i=\{0,1\}.

(31)

From (
29
)-(
31
), we can characterize the optimizer as (
i=0,1
)

u_{i}^{\diamond}\left[n\right]=\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{i}\left[n\right]+(-1)^{i}\operatorname{sgn}(\zeta\left[n\right])\mathop{\min}(\left|\zeta\left[n\right]\right|,\sigma))

(32)

which leads to a thresholding operator in (
2
). Similarly, the same calculation applies to
u_{i}^{\diamond},i=2,3
. Enforcing
u_{i}^{\diamond}
on the grid
2\mathbb{Z}\lambda_{i}
can be implemented via the quantization operator
\mathscr{Q}_{\lambda_{i}}=2\lambda_{i}\left\lfloor\left(\cdot+\lambda_{i}\right)/\left(2\lambda_{i}\right)\right\rfloor
,
which completes the proof.

The overarching goal of our experiments is to computationally push the frequency range of our hardware by using sub-Nyquist spectral estimation methods. In particular, through a series of

In noiseless scenarios, numerical simulations demonstrate that the method presented in Section

V
Conclusion

Undersampled or sub-Nyquist spectral estimation, aimed at capturing high bandwidth signals where Nyquist rate sampling is impractical or expensive, has been a focus of research for decades. However, most advancements have centered on algorithms and can not handle high-dynamic-range (HDR) signal acquisition. To overcome this fundamental mismatch between theory and practice, our work introduces a multi-channel scheme for sub-Nyquist spectral estimation based on the Unlimited Sensing Framework. Our algorithm utilizes modulo samples, and our recovery theorem guarantees that
K
frequencies can be estimated from just
6K+4
measurements, independent of the sampling rate. Hardware experiments with modulo ADCs, coupled with a novel, robust recovery algorithm, indeed show that kilohertz range signals can be recovered at hertz scale sampling rates, providing a true sense of sub-Nyquist spectral estimation. These results serve as a compelling stepping-stone, catalyzing a whole new range of spectral estimation applications, previously unexplored via the lens of USF. Going forward, tightening recovery guarantees, reducing the number of channels, developing robustness guarantees and integrating our approach with new hardware and applications, all remain interesting future research directions.
