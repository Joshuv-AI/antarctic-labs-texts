Game Theory in Social Media: A Stackelberg Model of Collaboration, Conflict, and Algorithmic Incentives
By Arjan Khadka

Abstract
Social media platforms are ecosystems in which many decisions are constantly made for the benefit of the creators in order to maximize engagement, which leads to a maximization of income. The decisions, ranging from collaboration to public conflict or “beefing,” are heavily influenced by social media algorithms, viewer preferences, and sponsor risk. This paper models this interaction as a Stackelberg game in which the algorithm is the leader, setting exposure and reward rules, and the content creators are the followers, who optimize their content to maximize engagement. It focuses on two influencer strategies of collaborating and beefing. Viewer preferences are modeled indirectly through the algorithm’s utility function, which rewards engagement metrics like click-through rate and watch time. Our simplified game-theoretic model demonstrates how different algorithmic priorities can shift creator strategies and provides insight into the equilibrium dynamics of social media influence.

2.5
Nonlinear Utility Extension

While the linear utility model offers tractability, real-world creator utility often exhibits nonlinear patterns due to diminishing returns and escalating penalties. We propose the following nonlinear utility function:

U_{\text{creator}}(s)=\alpha\cdot\log(1+\text{Click}_{s})+\beta\cdot\sqrt{\text{Watch}_{s}}+\gamma\cdot\text{Shares}_{s}-\delta\cdot(\text{DramaRisk}_{s})^{2}

This formulation captures:

•

Diminishing returns on clicks and watch time:
modeled via the logarithm and square root functions.

•

Nonlinear sponsor penalty:
the quadratic term penalizes high drama risk more heavily, reflecting sponsors’ increasing aversion to controversy.

The nonlinear form allows for richer strategy dynamics and more realistic modeling of creator incentives.

Equilibrium and Implications

In this model, the

Given the algorithm’s choice of weights
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
, each creator selects a strategy
s

or
Beefing
) to maximize their own utility function:

U_{\text{creator}}(s)=\alpha\cdot\text{Click}_{s}+\beta\cdot\text{Watch}_{s}+\gamma\cdot\text{Shares}_{s}-\delta\cdot\text{DramaRisk}_{s}

•

\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma

•

\text{Click}_{s}
,
\text{Watch}_{s}

: the engagement metrics resulting from strategy

•

\delta
: sponsor sensitivity coefficient (how much sponsors penalize drama).

•

\text{DramaRisk}_{s}

The creator’s best response

s^{*}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\arg\max_{s\in\{\text{Collaboration, Beefing}\}}U_{\text{creator}}(s)

Step 2: Algorithm’s Optimization

The algorithm acts as a rational leader that anticipates the creators’ best responses to its chosen weights. This hierarchical decision-making framework captures the interdependence between platform incentives and creator behavior
[
7
,
8
]
. The algorithm anticipates how creators will respond to the weights
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
it chooses. Its goal is to maximize its own utility, which is a combination of engagement metrics:

U_{\text{algorithm}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\alpha\cdot\text{Click}_{s^{*}}+\beta\cdot\text{Watch}_{s^{*}}+\gamma\cdot\text{Shares}_{s^{*}}

Since the algorithm expects creators to choose the best strategy
s^{*}
based on its weights, the algorithm’s optimization problem is:

(\alpha^{*},\beta^{*},\gamma^{*})=\arg\max_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}U_{\text{algorithm}}\big(s^{*}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\big)

where
s^{*}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is the creators’ best response given the weights.

The

The interaction forms a
Stackelberg game
where the leader (algorithm) moves first, and the followers (creators) respond optimally.
The equilibrium is reached when both the algorithm’s weights and creators’ strategies are mutually optimal and stable.

3
Behavioral Evidence

To ground this game-theoretic model in real-world phenomena, we examine behavioral evidence from empirical studies on creator behavior in digital ecosystems. Unlike models that assume fully rational agents, we incorporate the principle of
bounded rationality
, which posits that individuals aim to make satisfactory decisions under constraints of limited information, cognitive processing capacity, and uncertainty. This framework better reflects the decision-making environment of social media creators, who must react dynamically to algorithmic opacity and fluctuating audience preferences.

3.1
Satisficing and Heuristic-Based Decisions

Creators often operate under conditions of incomplete feedback and limited foresight. Instead of globally optimal strategies, they employ satisficing behaviors—seeking ”good enough” outcomes through trial-and-error and local optimizations.

•

Adaptive Behavior:
Empirical studies show that creators frequently use simple heuristics, such as imitating trending formats or replicating previously successful videos
[
10
,
11
]
. This aligns with bounded rationality, where decision rules are derived from experience rather than formal optimization.

•

Limited Lookahead:
In beef scenarios, creators often escalate conflict without anticipating long-term reputation damage. This is consistent with limited-depth strategic reasoning—akin to level-
k
thinking in behavioral game theory
[
12
]
.

3.2
Conflict as a Heuristic for Engagement

Creators often exploit interpersonal conflict (“beefing”) as a cognitively simple yet effective engagement strategy.

•

Availability and Salience Biases:
Conflict is highly salient and cognitively sticky, increasing the likelihood that creators will choose such strategies under uncertainty. Viewers disproportionately attend to conflictual content, making beef a high-reward heuristic.

•

Retaliatory Dynamics:
Behavioral experiments show that humans tend to respond to perceived slights with retaliation even when cooperation is payoff-dominant
[
13
]
. This helps explain why creators may escalate conflict cycles despite long-term costs.

3.3
Collaboration and Satisficing Coalitions

Collaboration serves as a low-risk strategy that creators use when direct competition is costly or ineffective. These alliances are often formed not through exhaustive cost-benefit analysis but through local social cues and mutual interest.

•

Rule-of-Thumb Strategies:
Cross-promotional collaborations often follow heuristics such as “collab with creators of similar size” or “within the same niche”
[
14
]
. These satisficing rules bypass complex optimization and reflect bounded rational decision-making.

•

Reputation Spillover:
The decision to collaborate often considers the qualitative perception of the partner’s brand. Creators act under social bounded rationality, where social proof and prior exposure play a major role.

3.4
Algorithmic and Audience Feedback Loops

Both the platform algorithm and viewers act as environment-level forces, shaping creators’ heuristics and reactive behaviors.

•

Behavioral Adaptation to Algorithms:
Given limited transparency, creators treat the recommendation algorithm as a black box and adapt through experimental learning, A/B testing, and imitation
[
15
]
. This trial-and-error approach is a hallmark of bounded rationality.

•

Audience Influence:
Viewer feedback (likes, comments, shares) informs creators’ content adjustments. Studies show creators are highly reactive to negative feedback, often over-correcting or altering future strategies based on limited data
[
16
]
.

These findings support our use of a game-theoretic model where players are not fully rational but instead behave strategically under cognitive and informational constraints. Bounded rationality offers a more psychologically plausible framework for understanding creator decisions in dynamic social media environments.

The simulations and utility models reveal key leverage points in the social media ecosystem. The following implications are drawn from the Stackelberg structure of the game:

Platform Incentive Design Matters

Since the algorithm is the Stackelberg leader, its choice of weights
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
determines the creators’ strategic responses

,
14
]

Small changes in algorithmic priorities can dramatically shift creator behavior:

•

Emphasizing watch time (
\beta

[

]

•

Overweighting clicks (
\alpha
) and shares (
\gamma
) can incentivize sensationalism or drama to provoke engagement spikes
[

Design Implication:
Platforms can steer content quality by transparently adjusting how they rank and reward engagement metrics.

5.2
Sponsor Pressure Can Be Modeled and Utilized

The sponsor penalty parameter
\delta
captures external pressure against risky content. Higher values of
\delta
penalize drama and make collaboration more appealing.

[
14
]

Design Implication:
Platforms could internalize brand-safety risks into their algorithm, effectively increasing

5.3
Strategic Transparency Reduces Manipulation

If creators clearly understand the platform’s algorithmic values (i.e., know
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
), they can optimize accordingly. While this increases efficiency, it may also encourage gaming the system.
[
6
,
13
]

Design Implication:
Partial transparency—showing general priorities without disclosing exact formulas—can balance healthy strategic behavior with unpredictability to prevent manipulation.

5.4
Viewer Preferences Are Indirect but Powerful

Even though viewers are not modeled as active players, their preferences are embedded in how the algorithm assigns
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. Thus, viewer engagement data acts as an implicit leader influence.
[
16
]

Design Implication:
Platforms must carefully analyze viewer metrics to distinguish between temporary spikes (e.g., viral beefs) and sustainable engagement (e.g., meaningful collaborations).

5.5
Toward Healthier Equilibria

The Stackelberg equilibrium changes as platform incentives and external penalties change. A toxic content environment is not inevitable; it is a function of poorly tuned incentives.
[
12
,
8
]

Design Implication:
Algorithmic responsibility means designing utility functions that lead to socially beneficial equilibria. This may include:

•

Demoting drama-heavy content through dynamic penalty scaling.

•

Boosting cross-collaborative efforts via reward multipliers.

•

Monitoring and responding to emergent negative behavior patterns algorithmically.

7
Limitations and Future Work

While our simplified Stackelberg model provides insight into how algorithmic incentives shape content creator behavior, it is important to acknowledge the model’s limitations and suggest avenues for refinement.

7.1
Model Limitations

•

Binary Strategy Set:
The model reduces creator choices to two discrete strategies: collaboration and beefing
[
19
]
. In practice, content strategies exist on a continuum ranging from wholesome partnerships to subtle shade to overt controversy. This binary simplification limits the model’s ability to capture marginal or hybrid tactics (e.g., humorous parody, indirect responses, or temporary alliances). A continuous or multi-strategy framework could better model the complexity of real-world behavior.

•

Static Environment:
The current model treats the game as a one-shot Stackelberg interaction
[
20
]
. However, social media engagement is inherently dynamic. Viewer preferences evolve, algorithms adapt via machine learning, and creators update their strategies based on past feedback. Thus, a more realistic model would consider a dynamic Stackelberg game or even a reinforcement learning setting where the algorithm updates its weights over time based on observed engagement patterns.

•

Homogeneous Agents:
We assume all creators are identical in their utility functions and engagement outcomes
[
21
]
. In reality, creators differ in follower count, brand image, audience demographics, and risk tolerance. For instance, a controversial influencer may be rewarded for beefing, while a family-friendly creator may suffer severe losses. Modeling a population of heterogeneous creators could allow the emergence of mixed strategies and more nuanced equilibria.

•

Linear Utility and Penalty Functions:
The creator utility and sponsor penalty are modeled linearly
[
22
]
:

U_{\text{creator}}(s)=\alpha\cdot\text{Click}_{s}+\beta\cdot\text{Watch}_{s}+\gamma\cdot\text{Shares}_{s}-\delta\cdot\text{DramaRisk}_{s}

However, sponsor reactions may be nonlinear—tolerating low levels of drama but imposing steep penalties past a certain threshold (e.g., a Heaviside step function). Likewise, engagement metrics may exhibit diminishing returns (e.g., logarithmic growth in utility). Incorporating nonlinear terms or utility curvature would improve realism.

•

No Direct Viewer or Sponsor Strategy:
Both viewers and sponsors are modeled passively
[
23
]
. The algorithm internalizes viewer behavior into weights
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, and sponsors impose a fixed penalty. This omits strategic behavior like viewer backlash, boycott campaigns, or sponsors withdrawing mid-campaign. Modeling viewers and sponsors as active agents in a larger multi-agent game could add important feedback loops.

•

Single-Platform Focus:
The model assumes creators are only optimizing for one algorithm
[
24
]
. In reality, creators often produce content across multiple platforms simultaneously (e.g., reposting TikToks to Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts), each with different reward systems. These cross-platform trade-offs are a major factor in real content strategy.

7.2
Directions for Future Work

To enhance the model’s predictive power and applicability, we propose the following research directions:

•

Multi-Strategy Extension:
Introduce a richer strategy set with continuous variables (e.g., drama level
d\in[0,1]
), or additional tactics like trending content, education, or satire. This allows creators to blend strategies and may lead to interior equilibria rather than strict binaries.

•

Repeated and Dynamic Games:
Extend the Stackelberg model to a repeated framework with time-evolving parameters. The algorithm can adjust weights
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t},\gamma_{t})
based on observed viewer responses, while creators update strategies using best-response dynamics or reinforcement learning. This would better model long-term dynamics such as algorithm updates or changing trends.

•

Agent Heterogeneity and Network Effects:
Model creators as nodes in a network with varying attributes. Include influence graphs to represent who collaborates or beefs with whom. This opens the door to simulating virality and cascading drama, where one conflict triggers a wave of engagement through the network.

•

Empirical Calibration:
Collect engagement data from real platforms (e.g., using public APIs or scraped statistics) to estimate plausible values for weights and strategy payoffs. This would allow empirical testing of model predictions and alignment with real phenomena like algorithm changes or influencer controversies.

•

Cross-Platform Optimization:
Develop a multi-platform Stackelberg model where each algorithm sets different reward rules, and creators must choose strategies that balance competing incentives. This would mirror real-world creator behavior where maximizing engagement requires navigating divergent ecosystems.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a game-theoretic model of social media interaction by framing content creator behavior as a Stackelberg game. We positioned the platform algorithm as the leader that sets engagement-based incentives, while creators are followers who strategically choose between collaboration and beefing based on the resulting payoffs.

The model captures how algorithmic priorities—represented by the weights
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
on clicks, watch time, and shares—determine the strategic landscape. A central insight is that small shifts in these weights can lead to dramatic shifts in equilibrium behavior. For instance, if
\alpha
(clicks) and
\gamma
(shares) are emphasized, creators are incentivized to adopt high-drama strategies like beefing, even at the cost of watch time or sponsor loss. Conversely, a high
\beta
(watch time) or a strong sponsor penalty
\delta
can tip the balance toward more cooperative and brand-safe strategies like collaboration.

We formalized creator utility as:

U_{\text{creator}}(s)=\alpha\cdot\text{Click}_{s}+\beta\cdot\text{Watch}_{s}+\gamma\cdot\text{Shares}_{s}-\delta\cdot\text{DramaRisk}_{s}

and examined how this leads to a Stackelberg equilibrium, where the algorithm indirectly controls creator behavior by tuning these parameters.

Our numerical simulations demonstrated that:

•

Creator strategies are highly sensitive to the algorithm’s reward structure.

•

Sponsor penalties act as a regulatory force that can suppress exploitative or controversial tactics.

•

Viewer preferences, though not modeled as explicit players, are crucially embedded in the algorithm’s optimization objective.

Overall, this work provides a simplified but insightful lens into the strategic nature of social media ecosystems. It also raises important questions about the responsibility of platforms in shaping online discourse through algorithmic design. Future research could expand this model into dynamic, multi-agent, or empirically calibrated directions to better understand real-world creator behavior and platform governance.
