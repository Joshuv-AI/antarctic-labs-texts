Predicting mild cognitive impairment in patients with Parkinson’s disease by integrating striatal MRI radiomics with clinical features
Authors: Haisong Chen, Asta Debora, Hongyan Wang, Jian Xu, Xuemiao Zhao, Jingru Wang, Yunjun Yang, Mengying Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMild cognitive impairment (MCI), a common and impactful non-motor complication in Parkinson’s disease (PD) that often precedes dementia, underscores the urgent need for early predictive tools applicable to routine clinical practice. This study aims to address this issue by investigating whether integrating striatal radiomics features from structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with clinical data can predict MCI in PD patients.MethodsBaseline T1-weighted MRI images and clinical data of 254 PD patients from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) database were retrospectively analyzed. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), with PD patients classified as PD-MCI or cognitively normal (PD-CN). A total of 1,316 radiomics features were extracted from the bilateral caudate nucleus (CN) and putamen (PU). After dimension reduction and feature selection, a radiomics model was constructed. Independent clinical risk factors were identified via univariate and multivariate logistic regression, and further integrated with radiomics features to develop a clinical-radiomics combined model for PD-MCI prediction. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curve, confusion matrix, F1 score, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Correlations between key radiomics features and MoCA scores were also evaluated.ResultsAge and years of education (YOE) were identified as independent clinical risk factors for PD-MCI. The clinical-radiomics combined model outperformed the radiomics-only model in both the training and test sets, with the model incorporating the right PU (PUR) radiomics features achieving the highest AUC: 0.852 (95% CI: 0.787–0.918) in the training set and 0.790 (95% CI: 0.657–0.923) in the test set. The corresponding F1 scores were 0.704 and 0.667, respectively. Additionally, specific radiomics features showed weak but significant correlations with MoCA scores (P < 0.05).ConclusionIntegration of striatal radiomics features derived from structural MRI images with routine clinical factors demonstrates promising predictive performance for PD-MCI. The proposed clinical-radiomics combined model leverages clinically accessible resources, and its predictive value for PD-MCI establishes a preliminary foundation for subsequent related explorations. However, the model’s generalizability remains unconfirmed, further validation on independent datasets is required before any consideration of its clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02014-3.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder that predominantly affects the elderly [1], with characteristic motor symptoms (e.g., bradykinesia, resting tremor, and rigidity) serving as key criteria for PD diagnosis [2]. Beyond these core motor symptoms, cognitive impairment is a crucial non-motor symptom of PD and is much more prevalent in PD patients than in the general population [3]. Such cognitive deficits typically manifest as impairments in executive function, memory, visuospatial ability, and attention in the early stages of PD, even before the onset of obvious motor symptoms. These deficits progress insidiously and severely undermine patients’ daily living functions [4]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents a transitional state between normal cognition and dementia. Notably, approximately 30% of PD patients with MCI (PD-MCI) will progress to PD dementia (PDD), which significantly impairs their quality of life and social functions [5].

Alterations in striatal microstructure, particularly in the caudate nucleus (CN) and putamen (PU), constitute part of PD’s pathophysiological basis and are closely related to movement and cognition [6, 7]. The striatum maintains extensive structural and functional connections with cortical and subcortical brain regions. Abnormal neural communication in this circuit, which arises from striatal microstructural damage, is a major pathological feature of PD. Specifically, microstructural alterations (e.g., protein deposition, synaptic reduction, and neurotransmitter dysfunction) also play a key role in inducing cognitive impairment [8]. Despite significant progress in diagnosis, clinicians still rely on the limited set of diagnostic tests currently available to confirm the clinical diagnosis, resulting in a relatively low diagnostic accuracy rate [9]. Therefore, early detection of PD-MCI is vital for optimizing clinical diagnosis and intervention strategies, potentially improving patient outcomes.

In the field of neuroimaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as a safe and non-invasive modality, is widely used in the diagnosis and staging of nervous system diseases [10]. In daily clinical practice, conventional pulse sequences such as T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) remain the mainstay [11]. In recent years, advanced MRI sequences and molecular imaging techniques have been increasingly applied to the diagnosis and research of PD due to their ability to detect objective neurochemical changes in the brain. Examples include quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), positron emission tomography (PET), and others. However, the complex imaging principles and relatively high costs of these methods limit their clinical applications [12, 13].

Radiomics has emerged as a promising approach to developing decision-support tools by mining high-dimensional data from medical images and quickly extracting numerous advanced features through high-throughput calculation. These features, when combined with other available patient data, could enhance the capabilities of the decision support model [14]. Based on machine learning (ML) methods, radiomics technology can sensitively capture subtle brain changes associated with PD-MCI patients from structural MRI scan data. This provides a powerful tool for deeper exploration of the underlying mechanisms of PD-MCI and helps to promote substantial progress in this field [15, 16].

Several relevant studies have used the radiomics approach to diagnose and research PD on the T1WI sequence. Tupe-Waghmare et al. [17] found that radiomics features extracted from T1WI images could effectively differentiate between PD and atypical parkinsonian syndromes (APS). Park et al. [18] indicated that combining radiomics features of the bilateral CN and PU with clinical data could improve the prediction of the development of dementia in PD patients. Research by Jian et al. [19] has shown that T1WI-derived features of whole-brain gray and white matter can predict cognitive decline in PD patients. However, previous studies have rarely focused on the value of striatal radiomics features derived from T1WI in distinguishing between PD-CN and PD-MCI. Building on these findings, this study hypothesizes that T1WI-derived radiomics features of the bilateral striatum can distinguish PD-MCI from PD-CN.

The aim of this study is to explore the feasibility of identifying PD-MCI patients based on striatal radiomics features from T1WI images, and thereby provide support for early clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. For this retrospective study, all data from PD patients were collected from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI; https://www.ppmi-info.org), a multicenter collaborative open-source database for PD that includes MRI images, neuropsychological scales, and clinical data [20]. The PPMI study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating sites and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before their enrollment in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Early, untreated PD patients with a positive dopamine transporter (DAT) SPECT; (2) Availability of baseline brain MRI, detailed neuropsychological assessments, and complete clinical data; (3) High-quality MRI images free of artifacts that would affect feature extraction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Errors in reading files of automatically segmented results; (2) Obvious deviations in automatic contouring results. The specific screening process can be found in Fig. 1. All imaging data were acquired using a 3 Tesla (T) MRI scanner. The MRI imaging protocol included a high-resolution T1-weighted, 3D volumetric sequence (e.g., Magnetization Prepared Rapid Acquisition with Gradient Echo [MP-RAGE], Inversion Recovery Fast Spoiled Gradient Echo [IR-FSPGR]; field of view [FOV], 256 mm; slice thickness, 1 mm; voxel size, 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; matrix size, 256 × 256; other parameters including repetition time [TR], echo time [TE] and flip angle [FA], defined by the imaging core according to the scanner). For detailed MRI parameters and protocols, please refer to the PPMI MRI Operations Manual, which specifies the equipment, parameters, and imaging protocols. All examinations and assessments are detailed at https://www.ppmi-info.org/study-design. Finally, a total of 254 PD patients were included in this study. Eleven baseline clinical variables were included, encompassing demographic characteristics (gender, age, body mass index [BMI], years of education [YOE]), vascular risk factors (presence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus), and motor symptoms (presence of onset tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and gait abnormalities). The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to assess cognitive function, covering multiple cognitive domains such as visuospatial/executive function, abstract ability, and memory [21]. According to the commonly used MoCA scoring criteria for cognitive assessment, PD patients were divided into two subgroups: PD-CN (26–30 points, n = 185, 72.8%) and PD-MCI (21–25 points, n = 69, 27.2%). We used stratified random sampling to maintain this subgroup ratio, and the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (n = 179: 130 PD-CN [72.6%], 49 PD-MCI [27.4%]) and a test set (n = 75: 55 PD-CN [73.3%], 20 PD-MCI [26.7%]) at a 7:3 ratio. To avoid single-split bias and facilitate robust model selection, this stratified hold-out process was independently repeated 10 times, with unique random splits generated each time while preserving the original class distribution. The specific processes are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Regions of interest (ROIs) segmentation

The bilateral striatal subregions including CN and PU in the T1WI images were automatically segmented via volBrain (https://www.volbrain.net), a brain analysis software pipeline that automatically segment deep gray matter nuclei such as these striatal components, as well as the globus pallidus and thalamus [22]. All T1WI images underwent the following preprocessing steps (integrated in the volBrain pipeline): first, denoising using the Spatially Adaptive Non-Local Means (SANLM) Filter to enhance image quality; then, coarse inhomogeneity correction with the N4 method; subsequent spatial normalization via linear affine registration to the Montreal Neurological Institute 152 (MNI152) space, resulting in 181 × 217 × 181 images with 1 mm3 isotropic resolution, where the transformation matrix estimated from inhomogeneity-corrected images was applied to denoised images without such correction; fine inhomogeneity correction after registration to MNI space; and finally, intensity normalization through a tissue-derived approach [23]. A radiologist with 8 years of experience visually inspected and manually corrected the segmentation results using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2, https://www.slicer.org). Manual correction was performed when (1) the ROI included non-target tissues (e.g., adjacent white matter) or (2) the ROI missed the target striatal region. The radiologist was blinded to the clinical data.

Radiomics feature extraction

The AK software (Artificial Intelligence Kit, version 1.5.1, GE Healthcare) was used to extract radiomics features from ROIs. The T1WI images were preprocessed to minimize the influence of scanning parameters on feature extraction, including resampling to 1 mm isotropic resolution. The extracted features were categorized into two main groups: (1) Original image features, including first-order statistical features and texture features (e.g., gray level co-occurrence matrix [GLCM] features, gray level run-length matrix [GLRLM] features, gray level size zone matrix [GLSZM] features, gray level dependence matrix [GLDM] features, and neighborhood grey tone difference matrix [NGTDM] features); (2) Transformed image features, including wavelet-transformed features (wavelet) and logarithmic scale features (log-sigma). Interpretations for all radiomics features can be found on the pyradiomics’ website (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/). A total of 1,316 radiomics features were extracted from each ROI (bilateral CN, PU in the T1WI sequence) for subsequent analysis. The extracted features were z-score normalized, where the mean and standard deviation required for normalization were calculated exclusively based on the training set, and the same parameters were applied to normalize the test set.

Feature selection and radiomics signature construction

Prior to feature selection, two critical preprocessing steps were performed to optimize the radiomics feature set: First, non-informative feature elimination was implemented to remove features with near-zero variance that contributed little to distinguishing between subgroups; this was determined using two criteria: a frequency ratio (most frequent value to second most frequent value) ≥ 19, and a unique value ratio (proportion of unique values relative to total samples) of less than 10%. Second, to address potential class imbalance and avoid model bias toward the majority subgroup, class balance optimization was applied exclusively to the training set prior to feature selection using the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), which generates synthetic samples for the minority subgroup. Notably, the SMOTE-processed training set was used for subsequent feature selection to ensure the selected features were optimized for the balanced data distribution, while the test set remained unmodified to preserve the original data distribution for unbiased evaluation.

The minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression methods were employed to select the optimal features. First, mRMR was applied to the SMOTE-balanced training set to eliminate redundant and irrelevant features, retaining the 20 most informative ones. Subsequently, LASSO was conducted to further refine the optimal feature subset for radiomics model construction, leveraging L1 regularization to penalize large coefficients and set redundant features’ coefficients to zero, thereby determining the number of features to include. This process included selecting the regularization parameter λ based on 10-fold cross-validation. After establishing the feature count, the most predictive subset was chosen, and the corresponding coefficients were evaluated. Using these LASSO-derived features and coefficients, we constructed a radiomics signature (Rad-score) to integrate these features into a single composite predictor, which was calculated by summing the selected features weighted by their coefficients. The radiomics model was developed by fitting logistic regression with these selected features to predict the target subgroup of PD patients. Scatter plots and box plots were used to visualize the distribution of Rad-score between two subgroups in the training and test sets. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to evaluate the differences in the distribution of Rad-scores between these groups. Additionally, the radiomics model performance was assessed with the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Ten iterations of training-test splits were conducted, and the optimal model was selected from these iterations to ensure the model’s generalization ability and reliability.

Construction and validation of the clinical-radiomics combined model

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed on 11 clinical variables to screen for independent clinical risk factors for predicting PD-MCI. Subsequently, a clinical-radiomics combined model was constructed by integrating Rad-score and the selected clinical features. This combined model used logistic regression as the core classifier, and a visual nomogram was established. The DeLong test was used to compare the AUCs between the radiomics and combined models. The optimal diagnostic threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden Index, and accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated based on this threshold. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was performed to evaluate the goodness of fit, and the calibration curve was used to assess model calibration. The classification performance of the model was assessed using the confusion matrix and F1 score. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the models.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and model development were performed using R software (version 4.4.1, https://www.r-project.org). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the Shapiro-Wilk test were used to assess the normality of the quantitative data. For data conforming to a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation was used to describe group comparisons, and the independent-samples t-test was applied for analysis. For non-normal distribution data, the median (interquartile range, IQR) was used, and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed for analysis. Categorical variables were recorded as frequencies, and their associations were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the correlations between radiomics features and MoCA scores. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants

A total of 254 PD patients were included in this study, comprising 152 males and 102 females. Patient age ranged from 32.3 to 84.4 years. Among them, 185 cases were classified as PD-CN and 69 cases as PD-MCI. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the PD patients are summarized in Table 1. Statistically significant differences were observed between the PD-CN and PD-MCI groups in age (P = 0.009), hypertension (P = 0.013), and hyperlipidemia (P = 0.046). Additionally, a highly significant difference was noted in YOE (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found in gender, BMI, diabetes, and onset motor symptoms (P > 0.05).

Selected features and performances of the radiomics model

Following dimension reduction using mRMR and LASSO, the most predictive radiomics features were retained. The feature selection process and results are shown in Fig. 3 (taking the right putamen [PUR] model as an example; Figure S1 in Additional File 1 for other models). To optimize the penalty term λ in LASSO and avoid overfitting, 10-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set. The Wilcoxon test indicated significant group differences in both the training and test sets (P < 0.05). The radiomics model exhibited good discriminative ability in both sets, with the PUR model achieving the highest AUCs: 0.814 (95% CI: 0.744–0.884) in the training set and 0.737 (95% CI: 0.596–0.878) in the test set.

Construction and evaluation of the clinical-radiomics combined model

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified age (Odds ratio [OR] = 1.06, 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.11, P = 0.005) and YOE (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.87, P < 0.001) as independent clinical risk factors for predicting PD-MCI, with details shown in Table 2. A clinical-radiomics combined model was constructed based on these factors. Given that the PUR model exhibited the highest classification performance, a visual nomogram was established (Fig. 4). The model performance metrics and DeLong test results are displayed in Fig. 5; Table 3. The performance metrics and feature selection results of the 10 repeated experiments for the radiomics model are provided in Additional File 2. Calibration curves (Fig. 6A, B) showed close agreement between bias-corrected predicted probabilities and observed values, reflecting improved calibration. The combined model’s performance was further evaluated using the confusion matrix and F1 score (Fig. 6C, D). Finally, DCA showed that the combined model indicated a significantly higher net benefit than the radiomics model across most threshold probabilities (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study constructed a model based on the striatal radiomics features from conventional structural MRI to predict PD-MCI. The results showed that the performance of the clinical-radiomics combined model was better compared with that of the radiomics model alone. This indicates that the integration of striatal radiomics features and clinical data holds significant capacity for predicting PD-MCI. This approach may provide potentially valuable insights and methods for the clinical screening of high-risk populations with PD-MCI, thereby facilitating early intervention.

Cognitive intervention is considered promising in the treatment and even prevention of cognitive dysfunction in PD patients. Given this, early evaluation of cognitive function followed by timely intervention is necessary, which carries substantial clinical and practical value [24].PD-MCI refers to a condition in which the degree of cognitive decline exceeds the normal range corresponding to age and educational attainment but does not yet impair functional activities [25]. As the commonly used imaging method in clinical neurology, conventional MRI reflects the structural changes in brain tissues. By contrast, radiomics mines high-dimensional quantitative imaging features to capture subtle structural or functional changes. Importantly, radiomics can detect the potential risk of cognitive impairment in the early stage when clinical symptoms are not obvious, providing valuable opportunities for early intervention [11, 14].

Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of the striatum for PD-related pathological processes, while others have investigated multi-modal or region-specific radiomics models for identifying PD-MCI. For instance, Pang et al. [26] demonstrated that integrating multimodal striatal neuromarkers with the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale-III (UPDR-III) scores enhanced the differential diagnosis between idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (IPD) and multiple system atrophy (MSA), with the dorsolateral putamen emerging as the most valuable discriminative neuromarker in this context. Beheshti et al. [27] extracted gray matter (GM) volumetric features from structural T1-weighted MRI data of cognitively unimpaired PD patients at baseline in the PPMI database, and used these to predict PD-MCI onset. Employing a support vector machine (SVM) for classification, their model achieved an AUC of 0.73 and an accuracy of 0.77 in the test set. More directly relevant to our focus on PD-MCI, another study integrated quantitative electroencephalogram (qEEG) power spectrum features and cortical structural MR features based on surface-based morphometry (SBM), and its SVM model achieved an AUC of 0.77 and accuracy of 0.80 in the test set [28]. Consistent with the core insight from the latter study that integrated multi-dimensional features outperform single-modal ones, our work further narrows the focus to striatal subregions and radiomics characteristics. We found that the clinical-radiomics combined model based on the PUR in the T1WI sequence outperformed models of other ROIs. For class-imbalanced scenarios, metrics like balanced accuracy and F1 score are more suitable for evaluation, as they can more objectively reflect the model’s ability to identify the minority class. This model showed competitive performance in PD-MCI prediction on the test set, achieving an AUC of 0.790, a balanced accuracy of 0.762, and an F1 score of 0.667. Previous studies have consistently reported that the prevalence of MCI among PD patients ranges from 25% to 30% [3, 4]. This consistency between our test set distribution and real-world prevalence further supports the potential clinical utility of our model in actual PD-MCI screening scenarios. Notably, aligning with the striatum’s recognized status as a core functional region in PD-related pathological processes, our findings reinforce that targeting striatal subregions (specifically the right putamen) and integrating their radiomics features with clinical data forms a valuable discriminative predictive framework, providing an alternative and complementary approach for PD-MCI assessment. Furthermore, Bernal et al. [29] utilized data from the PPMI to compare the performance of shallow Markov models, deep recurrent models (Long Short-Term Memory [LSTM] networks), and non-recurrent models (Temporal Fusion Transformer [TFT]) in predicting dynamic changes in cognitive status over time. The results demonstrated that the TFT model outperformed the other models, and the ensemble method further enhanced the predictive performance. This also suggests that, in addition to the integration of feature dimensions, the optimization and innovation of model types represent another crucial direction for exploration in the field of PD cognitive status prediction.

Radiomics features hold unique value in evaluating MCI in PD patients. Owing to their capability for high-dimensional information mining, radiomics features can facilitate deeper research into the pathogenesis of PD-MCI [14]. Numerous studies have emphasized that microstructural changes in the striatum, such as protein deposition, synaptic loss, and neurotransmitter dysfunction, contribute to cognitive impairment [8]. In this study, we extracted radiomics features from the bilateral striatum using a T1WI dataset with a sufficient sample size for comprehensive analysis. Following dimensionality reduction, features with the highest predictive value were retained, and their feature coefficients were calculated. Among these, texture features and wavelet-transformed image features played a dominant role. Texture features, which quantify spatial patterns of gray-level intensity in brain tissue, directly reflect microstructural integrity of the striatum. Our retained texture features likely capture these microstructural perturbations. For instance, a reduction in DependenceVariance indicates more stable and uniform local texture gray-level dependence, as well as a more regular structure. Such changes may be associated with pathological processes such as neural tissue atrophy and degeneration, thereby affecting cognitive function. Previous studies have shown that wavelet transformation reflects multi-scale information, enabling the extraction of spatial localization features and time-frequency characteristics, including low- and high-frequency components. Given their ability to capture multi-scale structural and temporal variations in brain tissues, these wavelet-derived features may better characterize subtle pathophysiological changes in the striatum [30, 31]. At the neurobiological level, these multi-scale changes reflect the graded damage of the striatum: high-frequency features capture early microdamage to synapses and neurons, while low-frequency features reflect subsequent volume loss. Both contribute to impaired information processing in the basal ganglia-cortical network, which is a key driver of cognitive decline in PD.

Considering the complexity of ROI delineation in the brain functional regions and the time-consuming manual contouring process, this study utilized an automatic contouring method to enhance both efficiency and accuracy in radiomics feature extraction. Harkey et al. [32] systematically compared three images segmentation tools (volBrain, FreeSurfer, and 3D Slicer) for contouring accuracy in neuroimaging. Results showed that volBrain provided the simplest segmentation workflow. Notably, volBrain achieved higher segmentation accuracy than other tools in cases of suboptimal T1WI image quality. Leveraging these findings, this study selected volBrain for ROI delineation from the T1WI sequence images, capitalizing on its operational simplicity and robustness across imaging conditions.

In terms of clinical features, age and YOE are key factors in evaluating cognitive function in PD patients. The results demonstrated a significant inverse association: older PD patients with fewer YOE exhibited more severe cognitive impairment. The negative correlation between age and cognitive function is unsurprising and aligns with findings from prior studies [33]. Aging is associated with physiological degeneration of brain nerves, including oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which disrupt neurotransmitter homeostasis. For example, PD is characterized by the progressive reduction of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, a hallmark pathological feature that impairs signal transmission and disrupts cognitive function in the brain [34]. The impact of YOE on cognitive function is profound and cannot be overlooked. The association between lower YOE and more severe cognitive impairment may be attributable to neurobiological mechanisms. From a neurobiological perspective, long-term learning processes enhance structural and functional neuroplasticity, strengthen neural connections, and increase synaptic density, thereby forming more complex and efficient neural networks. These adaptations enhance the brain’s resilience to neurodegeneration, potentially explaining why higher education protects cognitive function in PD [35]. Individuals with higher educational attainment may leverage their accumulated knowledge and effective cognitive compensatory strategies to better cope with cognitive decline and delay symptom progression in PD. Moreover, higher educational attainment is often correlated with better socioeconomic status (SES), healthier lifestyle behaviors, and greater healthcare engagement. Collectively, these factors may coordinately support cognitive maintenance [36, 37]. These findings underscore the importance of age and YOE in PD-MCI, prompting clinicians to integrate these factors into cognitive assessments. Such considerations are essential for tailoring individualized interventions and prognostic predictions.

Additionally, we investigated correlations between radiomics features of bilateral ROIs and MoCA scores. Results demonstrated a mild association between T1WI-derived striatal radiomics features and MoCA scores (P < 0.05), with the highest correlation coefficient of r = 0.202 among all analyzed features. Kang et al. [38] previously reported correlations between radiomics features, magnetic susceptibility values (MSVs) and MoCA scores in PD patients, with MSVs demonstrating stronger association with MoCA scores in those with PDD. This may be because the changes in brain iron deposition in patients with PDD are more obvious, and the QSM is sensitive to iron deposition and can provide more quantitative information. Our study further validates the previous points on T1WI, indicating that brain microstructure also altered along with the development of cognitive impairment in PD patients.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it is subject to potential sample selection bias. Prospective studies will be essential to enhance the generalizability of our findings. Second, the model included a limited number of clinical indicators. Incorporating multi-omics data in subsequent research may improve the comprehensiveness and accuracy of risk assessment. Third, our study relied solely on the PPMI dataset without external validation, limiting the model’s direct generalizability beyond this cohort. Future work will prioritize validating it on independent, multi-center datasets to confirm robustness across settings. Finally, this study utilized a traditional radiomics method. Investigations integrating deep learning algorithms to extract richer, more abstract features from MRI images may provide robust technical support for precise evaluation.

Conclusion

Our study established a preliminary proof-of-concept comprehensive model based on striatal radiomics and clinical features. This model demonstrated good discriminative performance, providing a preliminary reference for exploring PD-MCI prediction, and also highlights the potential of striatal radiomics features as candidate biomarkers for PD-MCI. However, the model’s generalizability has not yet been confirmed, and further validation using independent datasets is essential before we can further explore its potential role in clinical scenarios, such as early PD-MCI screening and personalized PD intervention decisions.
