Maternal factors associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis among caesarean-delivered babies at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital, Uganda: a case-control study
Authors: James M. Maisaba, Richard Migisha, Asiphas Owaraganise, Leevan Tibaijuka, David Collins Agaba, Joy Muhumuza, Joseph Ngonzi, Stella Kyoyagala, Musa Kayondo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBabies born via caesarean section in low-income settings face a higher risk of early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS), which has greater mortality than late-onset sepsis. However, maternal factors contributing to EONS among caesarean-delivered babies in these settings, including Uganda, are not well documented. We determined maternal factors associated with EONS among term babies delivered by caesarian section at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), southwestern Uganda.MethodsWe conducted an unmatched case-control study at MRRH from December 2019 to March 2020. Cases were caesarean section-delivered term babies with EONS (within 72 h). Controls were caesarean section-delivered term babies without EONS. We enrolled mother-baby pairs for both groups, obtaining maternal data via structured questionnaires The diagnosis of EONS was made using the WHO Young Infant Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses algorithm. Cases were consecutively recruited while controls were recruited by simple random sampling in a ratio of 1:2. We excluded newborns whose mothers were too ill to consent. We used multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify maternal factors associated with EONS.ResultsWe enrolled 52 cases and 104 controls. The mean age for the mothers was 27 (± 5.5) years. Neonates born to referred mothers had higher odds of EONS than those born to non-referred mothers (AOR = 6.2, 95% CI: 1.8–21). Additionally, decision-to-delivery time > 1 h for emergency caesarean section (AOR = 16, 95% CI: 4.2–65), antepartum hemorrhage (AOR = 8.0, 95% CI: 1.6–40), primiparity (AOR = 4.8, 95% CI: 1.1–21), and > 3 vaginal examinations after membrane rupture (AOR = 4.3, 95% CI: 1.5–12) were associated with EONS.ConclusionsPrime gravidity, antepartum hemorrhage, multiple vaginal examinations after membrane rupture, long decision-to-delivery time, and referral status were associated with EONS among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH. To reduce EONS risk, clinicians should limit post-membrane rupture vaginal exams or consider prophylactic antibiotics if multiple exams are needed. Screening babies born to primiparous women, those referred, those with antepartum hemorrhage, multiple vaginal exams after membranes rupture, and long decision-to-delivery times, could aid prompt recognition of EONS and timely interventions. Implementing standard procedures to reduce caesarean decision-to-delivery time could reduce risk for EONS in this setting.

Introduction

Neonatal sepsis is a clinical syndrome characterized by a bloodstream infection in neonates, accompanied by an inflammatory response [1]. Early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) is when manifestations of sepsis begin within the first 72 h of birth [2]. The incidence of neonatal sepsis is disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) compared to high-income countries, with sub-Saharan Africa experiencing the highest rates [3, 4]. Neonatal sepsis incidence in LMICs was estimated at 2,824 cases per 100,000 live births from 1979 to 2018, with a 17.6% mortality rate. During 2009–2018, the incidence increased to 3,930 per 100,000 live births [3]. This disparity is attributed to the combination of limited access to well-equipped and adequately staffed healthcare facilities and a high prevalence of infectious diseases [5]. Neonatal sepsis contributes to approximately 18–28% of neonatal deaths within LMICs [3]. Of note, the mortality associated with EONS is considerably higher than that of late onset neonatal sepsis [3]. Studies in Africa indicate a 23% mortality rate and 13% long-term sequelae rate for babies who had EONS [6].

Risk factors for EONS include maternal febrile illness two weeks prior to delivery, amniotic fluid with a foul smell and/or meconium staining, prolonged rupture of membranes (beyond 24 h), multiple vaginal examinations during labor after membrane rupture, and mode of delivery [6–8]. Causative organisms in EONS, both in term and preterm neonates, include Group B Streptococci and Escherichia Coli, along with Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus species, Gram-negative enteric bacilli (e.g., Enterobacter species), Hemophilus influenzae (especially non-typeable strains in the Haemophilus influenzae type b [Hib] vaccine era), and Listeria monocytogenes [6, 9–11]. However, the implicated organisms can vary by location.

From 2016 to 2020, the incidence of EOS in Uganda, rose from 11.7 to 13.4 cases per 1,000 live births, with an average annual increase of 3%; the neonatal sepsis surveillance data analysis showed that the majority (74%) of cases were classified as EONS, with Mbarara District having one of the highest incidence rates, exceeding 47 cases per 1,000 live births [12]. In April 2019, an audit of Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital’s (MRRH) Neonatal Unit revealed that sepsis accounted for 54.5% (198 out of 363) of total admissions. Of these cases, 3.8% resulted in mortality, with the majority (61%) being babies delivered by caesarean section. The vast majority (81%) of neonatal sepsis cases at MRRH were classified as EONS. These findings underscored EONS as the predominant cause of morbidity and mortality among newborns admitted at MRRH, a majority of whom were delivered by caesarean section. The high prevalence of group B streptococci at 28.8% among pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) at MRRH [13], combined with a high caesarean section rate ranging from 40 to 45% at MRRH [14, 15], poses a risk for EONS. Despite these facts, maternal factors associated with EONS among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH have not been documented. These data are crucial to guide clinical care teams to identify at-risk mothers and implement timely interventions, including prophylactic treatment. We hypothesized that maternal antepartum, intrapartum, and perinatal factors were associated with EONS among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH and tested this hypothesis using a case-control study. This study aimed to determine maternal factors associated with EONS among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH, southwestern Uganda.

Methods

Study design and study population

This was a hospital-based unmatched case-control study conducted at Mbarara Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH). The study population included both mothers and their newborns delivered by caesarean section at term. Cases were newborns delivered by caesarean section at MRRH who developed EONS (within 72 h). Controls were newborns delivered by caesarean section at the same hospital who did not develop EONS. We excluded newborns whose mothers were critically ill, especially those in a coma, and unable to provide consent for the study. We enrolled mother-baby pairs for both groups.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the postnatal and neonatal units of MRRH from December 23, 2019 to March 21, 2020. The Postnatal Ward is managed by a team of 2 obstetricians, 18 midwives, and 12 residents skilled in deliveries, including caesarean sections. The hospital handles approximately 12,000 deliveries annually, with 40 to 45% delivered through caesarean Sects. [14, 15]. Mothers and their newborns receive care at the Postnatal Ward. If newborns show signs of illness, they are transferred to the nearby neonatal unit for further assessment and management by the pediatrics team. The pediatrics team consists of 8 pediatricians, 13 residents, and 10 nurses. The Pediatric Ward admits approximately 5,000 children each year, with almost 60% being neonates. As of March 2020, the neonatal unit within the Pediatric Ward had an average occupancy of 60 beds, and was staffed with 2 pediatricians and 4 nurses, supplemented by trainee nurses who assisted with clinical duties. Preliminary tests like cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and blood smears are done in the Pediatric Ward side laboratory, while specialized microbiological tests (e.g., blood and CSF cultures) for sepsis are performed at external laboratories.

Sample size calculation and sampling

The sample size was determined using the OpenEpi platform, employing the formula proposed by Kelsey et al. [16]. Assumptions included a two-sided confidence level of 95%, 80% power, and a control-to-case ratio of two. We considered 39.6% exposure in controls, 65% exposure in cases, and aimed to detect a least extreme odds ratio of 2.83. This computation resulted in a total sample size of 138 participants (46 cases and 92 controls). Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final total sample size calculated was 153 participants (51 cases and 102 controls). Our primary exposure of interest was caesarean section mode of delivery. Information on the proportion of exposed cases and controls was extracted from an unmatched case-control study conducted at a Trauma and Specialist Hospital in Ghana [17].

Consecutive sampling was used for cases until reaching the target sample size. Controls were selected through simple random sampling; mothers, at 72 h post-caesarean, chose “yes” or “no” papers, with babies to those selecting “yes” enrolled as controls.

Diagnosis of early onset neonatal sepsis and case definition

We defined a case of EONS as the onset of clinical manifestations of blood stream infection within 72 h of life, as determined by the clinical team (residents and specialists), in a newborn delivered by caesarean section at MRRH from December 23, 2019 to March 21, 2020.

Clinical neonatal sepsis was considered when two or more of the following signs and symptoms were present: difficulty breastfeeding, abdominal distention, convulsions, minimal movement (movement only when stimulated), respiratory distress (respiratory rate > 60 breaths per minute, severe chest indrawing, nasal flaring), axillary temperature > 38.5 °C, or hypothermia (< 36.0 °C), confirmed twice at least one hour apart, following adaptation from the WHO Young Infant Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses algorithm. This tool has demonstrated high sensitivity in identifying severe illnesses and serves as an effective diagnostic tool for a wide range of childhood illnesses in infants [18].

Study variables

The study investigated EONS as the dependent variable, with independent variables including maternal socio-demographic characteristics, neonatal characteristics, and obstetric factors. Maternal socio-demographic characteristics considered in the analysis included age, distance to the health facility, referral status, and education level. Neonatal characteristics included birth weight and sex. Obstetric factors were categorized into antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum factors.

Antepartum factors included the number of antenatal care visits, occurrences of antepartum hemorrhage, and instances of maternal fever. Intrapartum factors encompassed conditions such as prolonged rupture of membranes (> 24 h), the number of vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes (> 3), grading of meconium-stained liquor (I-III), maternal fever (temperature ≥ 38 °C), intrapartum antibiotic use, type of caesarean section (emergency versus elective), decision-to-delivery time for emergency caesarean section, and cervical ripening. Postpartum factors comprised maternal fever, parity, and occurrences of postpartum hemorrhage. Maternal medical conditions assessed included urinary tract infection, Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and diabetes.

Study procedure

Cases, term neonates with EONS delivered by caesarean section MRRH, were identified through daily chart reviews at the neonatal unit. Using the WHO Young Infant Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses (IMCI) algorithm, signs and symptoms were matched for confirmation. Information on weight, age, and sex was recorded, and consent was obtained from mothers at the postnatal ward. Missing data were retrieved from mothers’ files.

For controls, post-caesarian mothers at 72 h post-surgery were identified at the postnatal ward. Simple random sampling was conducted, and infants were assessed to confirm the absence of EONS using WHO IMCI criteria. Mothers without recent HIV tests underwent testing after counseling. The case-to-control ratio was 1:2, and research assistants were trained on early onset neonatal sepsis identification criteria using the WHO Young Infant IMCI checklists before data collection. The research assistants including the two postnatal ward midwives and one pediatric ward nurse, received training on the study protocol, identification of term babies, using WHO IMCI for early onset neonatal sepsis, and data tool use. Clinical criteria charts for early onset neonatal sepsis were provided by the principal investigator. Quality control involved the pediatrician reviewing 10 random case charts to confirm accurate clinical diagnoses. Written informed consent was obtained from mothers of newborns before participation.

Data management and analysis

The data were entered into REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), and exported to STATA software version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages. Hypothetical tests (chi-square test or Fischer’s exact test) for categorical variables, were computed to compare characteristics between cases and controls. To identify maternal socio-demographic, obstetric, and medical factors associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS), we conducted both bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. This approach ensures that potentially relevant factors with weak associations are considered. The level of significance was set at 0.05.

Results

We enrolled a total of 156 mother-baby pairs, comprising 52 cases and 104 controls.

Maternal and neonatal characteristics of study participants

The mean age of mothers was 27 (standard deviation [SD] ± 5.5) years; the majority (83%; n = 129) of the mothers were aged 20–35 years, with no significant difference between cases and controls (p = 0.09). The majority (72%; n = 112) of mothers resided in rural areas, and were married (92%; n = 144). Education levels varied, with the most of the mothers having attained primary education (56%; n = 88). Most mothers were engaged in peasant occupations (60%; n = 94) (Table 1). Overall, the distribution of socio-demographic characteristics was similar between cases and controls.

Slightly more than half (59%; n = 92) of the mothers were prime gravida. Gravidity was different between cases and controls, with primigravida accounting for 50% of cases compared to 63% of controls (p = 0.042). Additionally, a significantly higher proportion cases experienced antepartum hemorrhage (27%) compared to controls (11%) (p = 0.009) (Table 2).

Cases, had a higher proportion of mothers undergoing > 3 vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes (52% vs. 30% in controls, p < 0.001). Meconium staining of amniotic fluid was more prevalent in cases (56%) compared to controls (24%), p < 0.020. Additionally, mothers to cases experienced a longer decision-to-delivery time for emergency caesarean sections, with 82.70% having a delay of more than one hour, compared to 63% in controls (p = 0.001). More mothers of neonates with EONS (81%) were referred from other healthcare facilities compared to mothers of controls (40%) (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Maternal factors associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis

In the multivariable analysis (Table 3), referral from another health facility, a decision-to-delivery time > 1 h, antepartum hemorrhage, primiparity, and > 3 vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes were significantly associated with EONS. Neonates born to mothers referred from other health facilities had six times the odds of early-onset neonatal sepsis compared to neonates whose mothers were not referred (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 6.2, 95% CI: 1.8–21, p = 0.004). Similarly, a decision-to-delivery time of more than an hour (AOR: 16, 95% CI: 4.2–65, p = 0.001), antepartum hemorrhage (AOR: 8.0, 95% CI: 1.6–40, p = 0.012), primiparity (AOR: 4.8, 95% CI: 1.1–21, p = 0.012), and more than three vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes (AOR: 4.3, 95% CI: 1.5–12, p = 0.005) were associated with increased odds of EONS.

Discussion

This study assessed factors associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH, Southwestern Uganda. The case control study found that significant factors associated with EONS included maternal referral from other health facilities, primigravida status, antepartum hemorrhage, multiple vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes, and a decision-to-delivery time exceeding one hour for emergency caesarean sections.

Term neonates born to mothers referred from other health facilities had higher odds of developing EONS than those born to non-referred mothers. This is in agreement with findings from a study conducted at Mulago National Hospital (Central Uganda), and in Eastern Uganda [19, 20]. This heightened risk may be attributed to several factors commonly observed in referral cases, including poor labor progress and pre-referral interventions such as herbal remedies and artificial membrane rupture, which can predispose newborns to infections. Given these findings, clinical care teams should consider heightened vigilance and potentially screening neonates born to referred mothers for signs and symptoms of EONS. Such an approach can aid in the timely detection and management of this condition.

Babies born to mothers with a delayed decision-to-delivery time interval had 16 times increased odds of developing EONS. Similar findings, where longer decision-to-delivery interval has been associated with poor perinatal outcomes, including EONS, were reported in other sub-Saharan African countries, including Rwanda, Kenya, and Nepal [21–23]. This interval is crucial for feto-maternal outcomes in category-1 emergency caesarean sections and can lead to delays in emergency obstetric care, prolonging labor and increasing the risk of neonatal sepsis [24]. Neonates born after a long decision-to-delivery interval in an emergency caesarean section have increased odds of Apgar scores of < 7 at the first and fifth minutes [25]. Babies with birth asphyxia face an increased risk of neonatal infection, likely due to the need for resuscitation, which has been associated with EONS in previous studies [26]. Infections can result from poor practices and non-adherence to guidelines by health professionals during resuscitation, predisposing neonates to infectious microorganisms and increasing the risk of neonatal sepsis [27]. Implementing standard operating procedures to deliberately reduce the decision-to-delivery time for emergency caesarean sections to less than an hour could reduce the burden of EONS and potentially improve neonatal outcomes in this setting.

Babies born to mothers who experienced antepartum hemorrhage had eight times higher odds of developing early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS). This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Ghana, where hemorrhage during pregnancy was associated with 8.8 times higher odds of neonatal sepsis [17]. This may be explained by a prior exposure of the intrauterine environment to ascending infections from the genital tract following a break in the cervical plug, and blood, acting as a culture medium, enhances the growth of offending microorganisms, thereby increasing the risk of early-onset neonatal sepsis. Intrauterine infections can also lead to antepartum hemorrhage, predisposing the fetus to EONS [28].

Babies born to primigravida women had 4.8 times higher odds of developing EONS compared to those born to multigravida women. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have found primiparous women to be at higher risk for neonatal sepsis compared to multiparous women [17, 26, 27, 29, 30]. Our finding may be explained by the complications of labor commonly present in first-time mothers, such as obstructed and prolonged labor. Additionally, the anxiety associated with first-time labor experiences may lead these mothers to frequently request examinations, resulting in multiple vaginal examinations and an increased risk of infection, as observed in a study conducted Nepal [26].Babies born to mothers who underwent more than three vaginal examinations after the rupture of membranes had 4.3 times higher odds of developing early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) compared to those who had three or fewer examinations. Similar findings were reported in a systematic review from sub-Saharan Africa, where multiple vaginal examinations were associated with a 2.2-fold increase in the odds of neonatal sepsis [31]. This is because EONS is primarily caused by ascending organisms from the maternal genital tract. Multiple vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes can facilitate contamination of the intrauterine environment, leading to chorioamnionitis and, subsequently, neonatal sepsis from ascending infection. The micro-organisms may ascend through multiple vaginal examinations when the amniotic membranes rupture or before the onset of labor, causing an intra-amniotic infection that can be transmitted to the fetus [32]. To mitigate the risk of EONS, clinicians should consider restricting the number of vaginal examinations following rupture of membranes. If multiple examinations are deemed necessary, administering intrapartum prophylactic antibiotics could be considered.

This study has some limitations. First and foremost, it is prone to recall bias. However, we mitigated this by cross-referencing self-reported data with mothers’ medical records, specifically verifying key clinical details such as the number of vaginal examinations post-membrane rupture, decision-to-delivery time for caesarean section, and the presence of antepartum hemorrhage. Secondly, our diagnosis of EONS did not include blood culture, potentially leading to differential misclassification of cases (over-ascertainment) and biasing our associations away from the null. We suggest future studies to include microbiological analyses to understand etiological agents of EONS among babies born to mothers delivering by caesarean section in the region. This information will be crucial for informing evidence-based prevention and control strategies.

Conclusion

Maternal factors associated with early-onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) among term babies delivered by caesarean section at MRRH, Southwestern Uganda, included referral from another health facility, antepartum hemorrhage, multiple vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes, a long decision-to-delivery duration for emergency caesarean section, and prime-gravidity. To mitigate the risk of EONS, clinicians should consider limiting vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes and, where necessary, administering intrapartum antibiotics. Screening babies born to primiparous women, those referred, those with antepartum hemorrhage, those with multiple vaginal examinations after rupture of membranes, and those with long decision-to-delivery durations for emergency caesarean sections could facilitate early recognition and interventions. Prophylactic antibiotics may be warranted for these high-risk groups. Implementing standard operating procedures to reduce decision-to-delivery times for emergency caesarean sections could reduce the burden of EONS in this region.
