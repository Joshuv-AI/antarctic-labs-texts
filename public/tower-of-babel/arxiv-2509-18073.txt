Pareto-Optimal Linear Programming
Bart van Rossum, Twan Dollevoet

Abstract
Pareto-optimality plays a central role in evaluating the efficiency of solutions to allocation problems, such as house allocation, school choice, and kidney exchange. We introduce a general linear programming problem subject to Pareto-optimality conditions, which we call Max-Pareto . Using the novel result that Pareto-optimal bipartite matchings are fractionally Pareto-optimal, we prove that Max-Pareto is 𝒩 ​ 𝒫 \mathcal{NP} -complete. We propose a bilinear programming formulation of Max-Pareto , and evaluate its computational performance on the problem of finding Pareto-optimal allocations of highest welfare. Keywords: Pareto-optimality, Allocation problem, Bipartite matching, Linear programming, Complexity



1
Introduction

The concept of Pareto-optimality describes societal outcomes in which no person can be made better off without making at least one person worse off. This well-known efficiency measure is frequently studied in the context of allocation problems, where a set of objects must be assigned to agents. Important applications include house allocation
(
Abraham et al. 2004
)
, school allocation
(
Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez 2003
)
, and kidney exchange
(
Sönmez and Ünver 2014
)
. Since the solutions to allocation problems can be represented as matchings in (bipartite) graphs, it features a close connection with matching theory.

Next to Pareto-optimality, it is common to consider the social welfare of an allocation. This can represent a valuation of the object-agent assignments, reflecting, for example, compatibilities or transportation costs. The social welfare objective typically conflicts with that of Pareto-optimality: an allocation that maximises social welfare is not necessarily Pareto-optimal, and a Pareto-optimal allocation does not necessarily maximise social welfare. As such, it makes sense to maximise welfare among Pareto-optimal allocations.

Biró and Gudmundsson 2021
show that it is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard to determine a Pareto-optimal allocation, among all Pareto-optimal allocations, that maximises social welfare. This holds even under object-based weights and complete preferences.
Aziz et al. 2023
consider the problem of maximising welfare among ‘fair’ allocations, and show that this problem is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard for several fairness measures, including envy-freeness and proportionality. Welfare maximisation has also proven to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard when one considers stable fractional matchings
(
Caragiannis et al. 2019
)
. Omitting the welfare objective, computing a Pareto-optimal and almost proportional allocation can be done in strongly polynomial time
(
Aziz et al. 2020
)
.

In this work, we move beyond allocations and consider Pareto-optimal solutions to general linear programming problems. In particular, we assume that each feasible solution maps to a payoff vector, representing the payoffs that a finite number of agents derive from the solution. We are interested in maximising a linear function, e.g., some social welfare function, over the set of Pareto-optimal payoff vectors. We refer to this problem as
Max-Pareto
. The linear programming model provides a flexible framework to model a wide class of problems. As we will see, this framework can also be used to model discrete allocation problems.

Intuitively, one might suspect that relaxing integrality makes it easier to determine welfare-maximising Pareto-optimal solutions. Perhaps surprisingly, however, our main contribution is to show that the decision version of
Max-Pareto
remains
\mathcal{NP}
-complete. To obtain this result, we show that Pareto-optimal matchings on bipartite graphs always satisfy the stronger notion of fractional Pareto-optimality. Informally, this result enables us to model the allocation problems discussed above, including that of
(
Biró and Gudmundsson 2021
)
, as instances of
Max-Pareto
. The hardness result directly follows. Finally, we propose a bilinear programming formulation of
Max-Pareto
, and evaluate its computational performance on the problem of finding Pareto-optimal allocations of highest welfare. On some instances featuring a large number of objects, our generic method provides improved primal solutions compared to a problem-specific integer linear program proposed by
Biró and Gudmundsson 2021
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We formally describe
Max-Pareto
in Section
2
, and prove several structural properties in Section
3
. We study Pareto-optimal bipartite matchings in Section
4
, showing that any Pareto-optimal matching also satisfies the stronger notion of fractional Pareto-optimality. Leveraging this result, we prove that
Max-Pareto
is
\mathcal{NP}
-complete in Section
5
. Finally. we propose a bilinear programming formulation in Section
6
, and evaluate its computational performance on the problem of finding Pareto-optimal allocations of maximum welfare in Section
7
.

Problem Description

Consider a nonempty, bounded polyhedron
\mathcal{X}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:A\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}
, with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Every solution vector
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
maps linearly to an
n
-dimensional payoff vector
\bm{u}:=U\bm{x}
, where
n
is the number of agents under consideration and
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is a linear mapping from solutions to payoff vectors. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, entry
u_{i}
equals the payoff that agent
i
derives from the solution. Without loss of generality, one can replace the linear mapping
U
by an affine transformation.

Denote by
\mathcal{U}:=\{U\bm{x}:\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\}
the set of attainable payoff vectors. We say
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
Pareto-dominates
\bm{u^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{U}
, denoted by
\bm{u}\succ\bm{u}^{\prime}
, whenever
u_{i}\geq u_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
and
u_{i}>u_{i}^{\prime}
for some
i
. In other words, no agent is worse off and at least one agent is strictly better off. We say
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
is Pareto-optimal when it is not Pareto-dominated by any
\bm{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
. Denote the set of Pareto-optimal payoff vectors by
\mathcal{U}_{P}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, and the corresponding set of Pareto-optimal solutions by
\mathcal{X}_{P}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}:U\bm{x}\in\mathcal{U}_{P}\}
. Note that a solution is Pareto-optimal when it generates a Pareto-optimal payoff vector. The goal is to maximise some linear objective function
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}
over the set of Pareto-optimal solutions
\mathcal{X}_{P}
:

3
Structural Properties

We now present some structural properties of
Max-Pareto
. We will use these results to prove
\mathcal{NP}
-completeness in Section
5
and to develop a bilinear programming formulation in Section
6
.

We start by observing that the set of attainable payoff vectors forms a polyhedron.

Lemma 1
.

The set of attainable payoff vectors
\mathcal{U}
is a polyhedron.

Proof.

Define the auxiliary polyhedron
\mathcal{Y}:=\{(\bm{x},\bm{u}):A\bm{x}\leq b,\bm{u}=U\bm{x}\}
. Since
\mathcal{U}=\{\bm{u}:(\bm{x},\bm{u})\in\mathcal{Y}\}
is a projection of
\mathcal{Y}
, the set
\mathcal{U}
is also a polyhedron.
∎

It is easy to see that the set of non-dominated payoff vectors lies on the boundary of this polyhedron.

Lemma 2
.

The set of Pareto-optimal payoff vectors
\mathcal{U}_{p}
is fully contained in the boundary of
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

By contradiction, assume there exists an
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{p}
in the interior of
\mathcal{U}
. Then, there exists an
\epsilon>0
for which the ball of radius
\epsilon
around
\bm{u}
is fully contained in
\mathcal{U}
. In particular, there exists an
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
for which
\bm{u}^{\prime}:=\bm{u}+\epsilon^{\prime}\bm{1}\in\mathcal{U}
. Clearly,
\bm{u}^{\prime}\succ\bm{u}
. This contradicts the fact that
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{p}
.
∎

This result is rather intuitive, as Pareto-optimal payoffs must be ‘maximal’ in some sense. In welfare economics, this is formalised through the classic result that Pareto-optimal allocations maximimise a weighted sum of agents’ utilities
(
Negishi 1960
)
. We now prove a linear-programming counterpart:

Lemma 3
.

A solution
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is Pareto-optimal if and only if there exists a weight vector
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
such that
U\bm{x}\in\arg\max\limits_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}
.

Proof.

First, let
\bm{w}
satisfying the conditions be given. Clearly,
\bm{x}
is Pareto-optimal: domination by some
\bm{x}^{\prime}
would imply that
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}^{\prime}>\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}
, contradicting optimality of
U\bm{x}
.

Conversely, let
\bm{x}
be Pareto-optimal and let
\bm{u}=U\bm{x}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we construct a supporting vector
\bm{w}
based on a hyperplane separating
\mathcal{U}
from the set of vectors strictly dominating
\bm{u}
. Define
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{u}}:=\{\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\bm{t}\succeq\bm{u}\}=\{\bm{u}+\bm{r}:\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\}
as the set of points that Pareto-dominate
\bm{u}
, and
\bm{u}
itself. It is readily seen that
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{u}}
is a polyhedron.

In the following, we will refer to vectors
\bm{w}
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
3
as supporting weight vectors. We will use Lemma
3
to formulate
Max-Pareto
as a bilinear program in Section
6
. Moreover, we can use it to prove the existence of optimal vertex solutions to
Max-Pareto
.

Lemma 4
.

There always exists an optimal solution to
Max-Pareto
at a vertex of
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{y}
be an optimal solution with objective value
z
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is nonempty and bounded, such a solution always exists. Denote the set of vertices of
\mathcal{X}
by
\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})
. If
\bm{y}\in\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})
, we are done. Hence, assume the optimal solution is a convex combination of vertices:
\bm{y}=\sum\limits_{\bm{x}\in\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})}\alpha_{\bm{x}}\bm{x}
for
\alpha_{\bm{x}}\geq 0,\sum\limits_{\bm{x}\in\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})}\alpha_{\bm{x}}=1
. As
\bm{y}
is Pareto-optimal, by Lemma
3
there exist a weight vector
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
and scalar
c
such that
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{y}=c
and
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}\leq c
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. Consider any vertex
\bm{x}
for which
\alpha_{\bm{x}}>0
. Since
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}\leq c
and
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{y}=\sum\limits_{\bm{x}\in\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})}\alpha_{\bm{x}}\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}
, it must be that
\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}=c
. It follows from Lemma
3
that
\bm{x}
is Pareto-optimal.

By optimality of
\bm{y}
, it also holds that
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\leq z
. If not,
\bm{x}
would be a Pareto-optimal solution with strictly higher objective value, contradicting optimality of
\bm{y}
. By the same reasoning as before, it must hold that
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}=z
. Hence, any
\bm{x}
for which
\alpha_{\bm{x}}>0
is an optimal vertex solution. As
\sum\limits_{\bm{x}\in\text{vert}(\mathcal{X})}\alpha_{\bm{x}}=1
, at least one such vertex exists.
∎

To conclude this section, we identify an interesting case where zero welfare loss is incurred when imposing Pareto-optimality.
Biró and Gudmundsson 2021
prove a similar statement in the context of Pareto-optimal allocations and refer to this situation as ‘aligned interests’.

Proposition 5
.

Whenever
\bm{c}=U^{\top}\bm{w}
for some
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, any optimal solution to
\max\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\}
is an optimal solution to
Max-Pareto
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{c}=U^{\top}\bm{w}
for some
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\arg\max\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bm{x}\in\arg\max\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{(U^{\top}\bm{w})^{\top}\bm{x}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bm{x}\in\arg\max\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow U\bm{x}\in\arg\max\limits_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}\}.

This shows that
\bm{x}
is supported by
\bm{w}
. It follows from Lemma
3
that
\bm{x}
is Pareto-optimal.
∎

A simple example is when
\bm{c}
is a strictly positive vector and all agents have uniform preferences over the set of feasible solutions, i.e., every row in
U
contains
n
identical entries. All instances satisfying the conditions of Proposition
5
can be solved in polynomial time using standard linear-programming algorithms.

4
Pareto-Optimal Bipartite Matchings

In this section, we prove the somewhat surprising result that Pareto-optimal matchings in bipartite graphs always satisfy the stronger notion of fractional Pareto-optimality. This result allows us to model discrete allocation problems as special cases of
Max-Pareto
and thereby prove
\mathcal{NP}
-completeness of
Max-Pareto
in Section
5
. We introduce preliminary notation and definitions in Section
4.1
. We prove several lemmas on Pareto-optimal matchings in Section
4.2
, and turn our attention to fractionally Pareto-optimal matchings in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Preliminaries

Throughout the following, we use
G=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E,\bm{w})
to denote a weighted bipartite graph from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
with edge set
E

\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}

is denoted by
N(R)
. For notational convenience, we will use
N(i)
to indicate
N(\{i\})
. A matching

V_{1}(M)

)

Bipartite graph

for all

.

To simplify notation, we use

to denote the vertex in

matched to

in matching

. Similarly, for each subset

, we define

. Finally, for any subset

4.2
Pareto-Optimal Matchings

We now study Pareto-optimal matchings on weighted bipartite graphs. To this end, assume that each matching
M
maps to a payoff vector
\bm{u}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V_{1}|}
, whose entries are defined as
u_{i}^{M}=w_{i,M(i)}
if
i\in V_{1}(M)
and
u_{i}^{M}:=0
otherwise. In other words, edge weight
w_{i,j}
represents the payoff to vertex
i\in V_{1}
when matched to vertex
j\in V_{2}
. A matching is Pareto-optimal when there exists no other matching in which at least one vertex receives a strictly higher payoff and no vertex receives a strictly lower payoff.

Definition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of matchings in
G
. We say
M\in\mathcal{M}
is Pareto-optimal (PO) when there does not exist an
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
for which
\bm{u}^{M^{\prime}}\succ\bm{u}^{M}
.

The following simple lemma will prove useful.

Lemma 8
.

Let matching
M
in
G
be PO. For any nonempty
R\subset V_{1}(M)
, the matching
M^{\prime}:=\{(i,M(i)):i\in V_{1}(M)\setminus R\}
in the reduced graph
G_{R}
is PO.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that matching
M^{\prime}
in
G_{R}
is dominated by matching
M^{\prime\prime}
. Augment
M^{\prime\prime}
to a matching in
G
by adding all edges in
M\setminus M^{\prime}
. This augmented matching dominates
M
, contradicting Pareto-optimality of
M
.
∎

The proof above is illustrated in Figure
2
. Assume
M
is Pareto-optimal. The matching
M^{\prime}
is given by the solid lines in Figure
2(b)
. If this matching
M^{\prime}
in
G_{R}
would be dominated by a matching
M^{\prime\prime}
in
G_{R}
, then
M^{\prime\prime}
can be augmented to find a matching in
G
that dominates
M
.

Definition 9
.

Let
M
be a matching in
G
. We say that nonempty subset
B\subseteq V_{1}(M)
is a blocking set with respect to
M
when it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

w_{i,j}\leq w_{i,M(i)}
for all
i\in B,j\in N(i)
,

(ii)

w_{i,j}<w_{i,M(i)}
for all
i\in B,j\in N(i)\setminus M(B)
.

Informally, a blocking set is a set of vertices
B\subseteq V_{1}(M)
that all (i) receive their highest possible payoff and (ii) are strictly worse off when matched to any node
j\notin M(B)
. As a consequence, if another matching
M^{\prime}
matches any node
i\in V_{1}\setminus B
to a node
j
in
M(B)
, then at least one node in
B
will be strictly worse off in
M^{\prime}
than in
M
. We provide an example below.

It is easy to see that the union of blocking sets is again a blocking set. Moreover, a blocking set always exists for Pareto-optimal matchings in which at least one vertex is not assigned its highest possible payoff.

Lemma 11
.

Let
M
in
G
be PO. If
w_{i,j}>u_{i}^{M}
for some
i\in V_{1}
and
j\in N(i)
, then there exists a blocking set with respect to
M
.

Proof.

We use induction on
|V_{1}|
. First, we show that the statement holds for
|V_{1}|=2
. In that case,
V_{1}=\{i,i^{\prime}\}
for some node
i^{\prime}\in V_{1}
and
j\in V_{2}
. We know that
G
contains edge
(i,j)
, for which it holds that
w_{i,j}>u_{i}^{M}
. Moreover, both the graph
G
and
M
must contain edge
(i^{\prime},j)
with weight
w_{i^{\prime},j}>0
. If not, the matching
M^{\prime}=\{(i,j)\}
dominates
M
. Suppose first that there are no other edges incident to
i^{\prime}
. Then we immediately find that
\{i^{\prime}\}
is a blocking set. Then suppose that
(i^{\prime},l)
is included in
G
for some
l\in V_{2}\setminus\{j\}
. To show that
\{i^{\prime}\}
still forms a blocking set, we must show that
w_{i^{\prime},l}<w_{i^{\prime},j}
. To the contrary, suppose that
w_{i^{\prime},l}\geq w_{i^{\prime},j}
. Then matching
M^{\prime}:=\{(i,j),(i^{\prime},l)\}
would Pareto-dominate
M
. We conclude again that
\{i^{\prime}\}
is a blocking set, and hence the statement holds for
|V_{1}|=2
.

Next, assume the statement holds for all
|V_{1}|\leq k
and consider a graph
G
with
|V_{1}|=k+1
satisfying the conditions. Then
M
is a PO matching and
i\in V_{1}
,
j\in V_{2}
are such that
w_{i,j}>u_{i}^{M}
.

In order to prove the existence of a blocking set, consider the auxiliary graph

4.3
Fractionally Pareto-Optimal Matchings

We now turn our attention to the stronger notion of fractional Pareto-optimality. To illustrate the concept of fractional Pareto-optimality, consider three Pareto-optimal payoff vectors
(5,1)
,
(2,2)
, and
(1,5)
. It holds that
(2,2)
is Pareto-dominated by the convex combination
\frac{1}{2}(5,1)+\frac{1}{2}(1,5)=(3,3)
. Similarly, a matching is fractionally Pareto-optimal when it is not Pareto-dominated by any convex combination of matchings.

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of matchings of
G
. We say
M\in\mathcal{M}
is fractionally Pareto-optimal (fPO) when there does not exist a vector
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{M}|}_{+}
satisfying
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}=1
and
\sum\limits_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M^{\prime}}\bm{u}^{M^{\prime}}\succ\bm{u}^{M}
.

Clearly, every fPO matching is PO. Perhaps surprisingly, every PO matching is also fPO.

Theorem 13
.

If matching
M
in
G
is PO, then
M
is fPO.

Proof.

By contradiction, let
G^{\prime}:=(V_{1}^{\prime}\cup V_{2}^{\prime},E^{\prime},\bm{w}^{\prime})
be a graph that contains a matching
M
that is PO but not fPO with the smallest possible set
|V_{1}|
. Then, there exist a set
\mathcal{N}
of matchings in
G^{\prime}
and vector
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{N}|}_{+}
satisfying
\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{1}=1
and
\sum\limits_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\alpha_{M^{\prime}}\bm{u}^{N}\succ\bm{u}^{M}
. Let

be a vertex for which
\sum\limits_{M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}\alpha_{M^{\prime}}u_{i}^{M^{\prime}}>u_{i}^{M}
.

This implies the existence of a matching
M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and vertex

satisfying

and

. By Lemma
11
, there exists a blocking set

. By definition, for every

for all

and

for every

5
Complexity

We prove
\mathcal{NP}
-hardness of
Max-Pareto
through a reduction from
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
, which was shown to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard by
Saban and Sethuraman 2015
. The goal of this problem is to determine whether a bipartite graph admits a Pareto-optimal matching in which a specific subset of objects is matched. To reduce this problem to
Max-Pareto
, we exploit that (i) bipartite matching can be formulated as a linear program with a totally unimodular constraint matrix and (ii) PO=fPO for bipartite matchings by Theorem
13
.

We introduce some additional notation to formally define
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
. Given a set of
n
agents
\mathcal{A}
and objects
\mathcal{O}
, a preference profile
P:=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
is a collection of strict orderings of objects. In particular, object
a
preceding object
b
in ordering
P_{i}
, denoted by
a\succ_{P_{i}}b
, indicates that agent
i
prefers object
a
over
b
. Profile
P_{i}
need not be a complete ordering. In that case, objects not included in the ordering are said to be inadmissible to agent
i
.

Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
can now be stated as follows: Given a preference profile
P
from agents
\mathcal{A}
over objects
\mathcal{O}
, in which agents may have inadmissible objects, and a subset of objects
Q\subseteq\mathcal{O}
, does there exist a Pareto-optimal matching in which all objects in
Q
are matched?

A reduction from the above problem allows us to prove the following hardness result.

Theorem 14
.

The decision version of
Max-Pareto
is
\mathcal{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

It is readily seen that
Max-Pareto
is in
\mathcal{NP}
, as membership of
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
can be easily confirmed, and Pareto-optimality can be checked in polynomial time by solving the following linear program:

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\bm{e}^{\top}U\bm{y}

(2a)

s.t.

\displaystyle U\bm{y}\geq U\bm{x}

(2b)

\displaystyle\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}.

(2c)

We now reduce
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
to an instance of the decision version of
Max-Pareto
: Does there exist an
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{P}
for which
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\geq k
? We construct this instance of
Max-Pareto
as follows.

•

Feasible region:
Construct a bipartite graph
G:=(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O},E,\bm{w})
as follows. Edge
e=(i,a)
is included in
E
if agent
i\in\mathcal{A}
admits item
a\in\mathcal{O}
, and has weight
w_{(i,a)}:=1+|\{o\in\mathcal{O}:a\succ_{P_{i}}o\}|
. In other words, we choose weights that assign more value to objects higher up in the agent’s preference profile. The bipartite graph is not necessarily complete. We now define
\mathcal{X}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}_{+}:\sum\limits_{e\in\delta(v)}x_{e}\leq 1,\forall v\in V_{1}\cup V_{2}\}
as the matching polytope of
G
, where
\delta(v)
is the set of edges incident to
v
. Since
G
is bipartite, the corresponding constraint matrix is totally unimodular and each vertex of the matching polytope corresponds to an integral matching.

•

Payoff mapping:
There are
|\mathcal{A}|
agents. For any
e=(i,a)\in E
, we set
U_{(i,e)}:=w_{e}
. This payoff function ensures that an integral matching is Pareto-optimal in
Max-Pareto
if and only if it is Pareto-optimal in
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
.

•

Objective function:
Choose
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
by setting
c_{(i,a)}:=1
if
a\in Q
and
c_{(i,a)}:=0
otherwise. The objective is thus to maximise the number of matched objects in
Q
.

•

Objective threshold:
The objective threshold equals
k:=|Q|
.

This instance of
Max-Pareto
is constructed in time polynomial in the size of the input. We now show that it is a yes-instance if and only if
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
is a yes-instance.

\Rightarrow
First, suppose that
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
is a yes-instance. Let
M
be a Pareto-optimal matching containing all items in
Q
. Choose
\bm{x}
by setting
x_{e}:=1
if
e\in M
, and
x_{e}:=0
otherwise. It is clear that
\bm{x}
is feasible in
\mathcal{X}
and meets the objective threshold.

It remains to show that
\bm{x}
satisfies Pareto-optimality in
Max-Pareto
. By construction, this is equivalent to showing that
M
is fPO with respect to all matchings in
G
. This follows directly from Theorem
13
and the fact that
M
is PO. We conclude that
Max-Pareto
is a yes-instance.

\Leftarrow
Now, suppose that
Max-Pareto
is a yes-instance with corresponding solution
\bm{x}
. We aim to convert this to a Pareto-optimal matching
M
that matches all items in
Q
. By Lemma
4
, we know there exists an optimal vertex solution
\bm{x}^{\prime}
. By total unimodularity of the bipartite matching polytope, this solution corresponds to an integral matching. This matching is not Pareto-dominated by any point in
\mathcal{X}
. In particular, it is not dominated by any integral matching. Since
\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}\geq k
, the matching contains all objects in
Q
. Hence,
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
is a yes-instance.

Since
Constrained Pareto-Optimal Matching
is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard and can be reduced to
Max-Pareto
in polynomial time, we conclude that
Max-Pareto
is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard as well.
∎

The structure of our proof shows that a large class of discrete allocation problems with Pareto-optimality constraints, such as that of
(
Biró and Gudmundsson 2021
)
, can be seen as special cases of
Max-Pareto
.

6
Bilinear Programming Formulation

In this section, we provide a mathematical programming formulation for
Max-Pareto
. It is not trivial to express the Pareto-optimality condition, but Lemma
3
provides a way forward. In particular, it allows us to formulate Pareto-optimality as a series of bilinear constraints.
1
1

1

Like
Max-Pareto
, linear programming with bilinear constraints is generally
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
(
Bennett and Mangasarian 1993
)
.
The bilinear programming formulation of
Max-Pareto
reads as follows:

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{x}

(3a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bm{u}=U\bm{x}

(3b)

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}\geq\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}

(3c)

\displaystyle\bm{w}\geq\bm{1}

(3d)

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(3e)

The objective function is given by (
3a
). Constraints (
3b
) ensure that
\bm{u}
is a feasible payoff vector, while bilinear constraints (
3c
) guarantee that
\bm{u}
is supported by weight vector
\bm{w}
. Constraints (
3d
) ensure that
\bm{w}
meets the remaining conditions of Lemma
3
. The feasible domain of
\bm{x}
is given by (
3e
). It follows that
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{P}
for any solution
(\bm{x},\bm{u},\bm{w})
to (
3
).

Bilinear problems can be solved to global optimality by modern-day commercial solvers like Gurobi. The exponential number of bilinear Pareto-optimality constraints (
3c
) remains troublesome, however. A potential solution would be a cutting plane approach, where constraints (
3c
) are dynamically separated.

Alternatively, we can use strong LP duality to obtain a tractable reformulation containing only a single bilinear constraint. Since
\mathcal{U}=\{U\bm{x}:\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\}
, constraints (
3c
) are equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}

\displaystyle\geq\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{U\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}

(4a)

\displaystyle\iff\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}\}

(4b)

\displaystyle\iff\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\{\bm{w}^{\top}U\bm{x}:A\bm{x}\leq\bm{b}\}.

(4c)

Since
\mathcal{X}
is nonempty and bounded, we can apply strong linear programming duality. Treating
\bm{w}
as fixed, we obtain that (
4c
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}\geq\min_{\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\{\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{\eta}:A^{\top}\bm{\eta}=U^{\top}\bm{w}\}.

(5)

Finally, note that (

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}\geq\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{\eta}

(6a)

\displaystyle A^{\top}\bm{\eta}=U^{\top}\bm{w}

(6b)

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}\geq\bm{0}.

(6c)

To conclude, replacing (

n

6.1
Bounded Weight Heuristic

Ideally, one would like to place a reasonable upper bound
\bm{w}\leq\bm{\bar{w}}
on the weight vector entries. This would strengthen the formulation, and, most importantly, help avoid numerical issues where excessively large weight vectors are chosen to exploit the feasibility tolerance of a solver. Unfortunately, we are unable to derive a meaningful valid upper bound on
\bm{w}
that preserves global optimality. As the following result shows, the magnitude of weight vector entries required to support a Pareto-optimal point can grow exponentially in the input size.

Proposition 15
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
, there exist a complete weighted bipartite graph
G
with
2n
nodes and weights in
[0,n]
, and a Pareto-optimal matching
M
in
G
that can only be supported by a weight vector
\bm{w}
satisfying
\frac{w_{1}}{w_{n}}\geq(n-1)^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Take a complete bipartite graph from
n
to
n
nodes with edge weights defined as
e_{ij}=1
if
i=j
,
e_{ij}=n
if
i=j+1
, and
e_{ij}=0
otherwise. The all-diagonal matching, including edges
(i,i)
for all
i
, has payoff vector
\bm{u}=(1,\dots,1)
and is Pareto-optimal. As such, there exists a weight vector
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{n}
satisfying
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{u}\geq\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}
for all alternative payoff vectors
\bm{v}
. In particular, it holds for all payoff vectors
\bm{v}_{k}=(1,\dots,1,0,n,1,\dots,1)
,
k\geq 2
, obtained by swapping edges
(k-1,k-1)
and
(k,k)
in the all-diagonal matching with
(k-1,k)
and
(k,k-1)
. As such,
\bm{w}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}w_{i}+nw_{k}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}w_{i}

(7)

for all
k\geq 2
. Combining these inequalities yields
w_{i}\geq(n-1)w_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. We conclude that
w_{1}\geq(n-1)^{n-1}w_{n}
.
∎

Despite this negative result, our bilinear program can be used as a primal heuristic by still enforcing an upper bound on
\bm{w}
. This restricts the feasible region to a subset of the Pareto-frontier that is still supported by the set of bounded weight vectors. However, one no longer obtains a valid upper bound to
Max-Pareto
. Even when the resulting weight vector is not at bound, one cannot conclude global optimality due to non-convexity of the bilinear constraint (
6a
).

Of course, more sophisticated heuristics for
Max-Pareto
can be devised. For example, one could exploit that, for any fixed weight vector, feasible, Pareto-optimal solutions can be obtained by solving an ordinary linear program. This potentially paves the way for meta-heuristics that explore the space of weight vectors in an efficient manner.
