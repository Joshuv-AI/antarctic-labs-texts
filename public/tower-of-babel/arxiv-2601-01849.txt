Asymmetric Dark Matter from Low-Scale Spontaneous Leptogenesis
Hiroki Takahashi, Juntaro Wada

Abstract
We investigate a novel type of asymmetric dark matter (ADM) model in which the dark matter asymmetry and the baryon asymmetry in our universe (BAU) are produced simultaneously via low-scale spontaneous leptogenesis, where the mass scale of right-handed neutrino is much lower than the Davidson-Ibarra bound M 1 ≪ 10 9 ​ GeV M_{1}\ll 10^{9}~\mathrm{GeV} . In our scenario, both asymmetries are predominantly sourced by a dynamical C ​ P CP phase, namely the majoron. Its kinetic misalignment provides a sufficiently large, time-dependent effective C ​ P CP phase, allowing efficient asymmetry production even for low-scale right-handed neutrinos. In our framework, the sources of C ​ P CP violation responsible for the BAU and ADM are correlated with each other, leading to a predictive relation for the dark matter mass. In particular, when the dark matter asymmetry reaches its equilibrium value before freeze-out, the dark matter mass is typically predicted to lie in the range 𝒪 ⁡ ( 0.1 ) ​ GeV ≲ m χ ≲ 𝒪 ⁡ ( 100 ) ​ GeV \mathcal{O}(0.1)~\mathrm{GeV}\lesssim m_{\chi}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(100)~\mathrm{GeV} , which lies within the sensitivity of direct detection experiments. On the other hand, if the dark matter asymmetry does not reach its equilibrium value due to weak coupling, the allowed mass range extends over a broader interval, 𝒪 ⁡ ( 0.1 ) ​ GeV ≲ m χ ≲ 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 ) ​ TeV \mathcal{O}(0.1)~\mathrm{GeV}\lesssim m_{\chi}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(10)~\mathrm{TeV} .

IV.1
Freeze-out

First, let us consider the situation where the inverse decay of dark-sector particles is in equilibrium, that is, the regime in which
z_{\mathrm{in}}^{D}<z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{D}
holds.
In this case, the asymmetric component of the DM number density is given by

\displaystyle n_{\Delta\chi}=\frac{c_{\chi}}{6}\dot{\theta}T^{2},

(35)

as long as the inverse decay is in thermal equilibrium. Here,
c_{\chi}
is the coefficient determined by the chemical equilibrium conditions.
Assuming that
\phi
carries no asymmetry due to the presence of sufficiently rapid interactions that interchange
\phi
and
\phi^{*}
, the coefficient is given by
c_{\chi}\simeq 1/2
.

13
13

13

For example, suppose that a fermion
\Psi
carrying a conserved charge
Q_{\Psi}
interacts with a scalar field
\phi
via a Yukawa coupling

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset g_{\Psi}\,\phi\,\bar{\Psi}\Psi+h.c.

(36)

Assume that this interaction is in chemical equilibrium in the early universe. As long as no interaction that violates
Q_{\Psi}
is present, the chemical potentials of both
\Psi
and
\phi

As we can see in Eq. (
35

18

17

J=f\theta
.

In terms of the dark-matter asymmetry
Y_{\Delta\chi}(z):=n_{\Delta\chi}/s
, the resulting DM abundance is given by

\displaystyle Y_{\Delta\chi}(z_{\rm fo}^{D})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\chi}}{6}\,Y_{\theta}\,\frac{g_{N,1}^{2}}{(\sqrt{2}\,z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{D})^{2}},

(37)

which follows from Eq. (
35

Y_{\theta}

In the freeze-out regime, where

37

\displaystyle m_{\chi}\simeq\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\Omega_{B}}\left(\frac{c_{B}}{c_{\chi}}\right)\left(\frac{z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{D}}{z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}}\right)^{2}m_{p}.

{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}/{\Omega_{B}}\simeq 5.4

IV.2
Freeze-in

Next, we consider the case where the inverse decay from dark sector particles is never in thermal equilibrium, while the dark sector particles are thermalized with SM particles.
14
14

14

In general, the temperature for the dark sector particles
T_{d}
is lower than that of the SM,
T
; namely,
T_{d}:=\xi T
with
\xi\leq 1
. Since the temperature ratio between the two sectors depends on the model, we analyze the case with
\xi=1
in what follows.

Unlike the freeze-out scenario, the produced DM asymmetry is smaller than that of the “equilibrium” value
n_{\Delta\chi}\ll\dot{\theta}T^{2}
in the freeze-in scenario. Therefore, Eq. (
25
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{\Delta\chi}+3Hn_{\Delta\chi}\simeq n_{N}^{\rm eq}\langle\Gamma_{N\to\chi\phi}\rangle\frac{\dot{\theta}}{T},

(40)

in the freeze-in case. From this equation, we can see that when the asymmetry production terminates (i.e.,
\dot{n}_{\Delta\chi}\simeq 0
), the number density at that time is suppressed by a factor of
W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z(T))
compared to its “equilibrium” value,

n_{\Delta\chi}^{\rm eq}(T):=(c_{\chi}/6)\,\dot{\theta}(T)T^{2}
:

\displaystyle n_{\Delta\chi}(T_{\rm sat})\simeq\left(M_{1}\over T_{\rm sat}\right)W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z(T_{\rm sat}))\,n_{\Delta\chi}^{\rm eq}(T_{\rm sat}),

(41)

where
T_{\rm sat}
is temperature when DM asymmetry production terminates.
W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z)
is defined in Eq. (
31
).

This suppression can also be obtained by solving Eq. (
40

Y_{\Delta\chi}
:

\displaystyle Y_{\Delta\chi}(z_{\rm sat})

\displaystyle\simeq\int^{z_{\rm sat}}_{0}dzW_{\rm ID}^{D}(z)\,Y_{\Delta\chi}^{\rm eq}(z)

(42)

\displaystyle\simeq z_{\rm sat}W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z_{\rm sat})\,Y_{\Delta\chi}^{\rm eq}(z_{\rm sat}),

(43)

where
z_{\rm sat}:=M_{1}/T_{\rm sat}
and
Y_{\Delta\chi}^{\rm eq}(z):=c_{\chi}Y_{\theta}g_{N,1}^{2}/(12(z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{D})^{2})
, which is given by the right-hand side in Eq. (
37

We note that the integrand becomes approximately constant for
z\lesssim 1
. Therefore, unless
z_{\rm sat}\gg 1
, it is a good approximation to evaluate it at
z\simeq z_{\rm sat}
and take it out of the integral, and we have checked
z_{\rm sat}\simeq 1
numerically.

Once
z
exceeds
z_{\rm sat}
, the asymmetry saturates, the present abundance is determined by
Y_{\Delta\chi}(z_{\rm sat})
. In Fig.
4
, we can see this saturated behavior for the numerical solution with small
|y_{\chi,1}|
.

In the freeze-in scenario, the DM mass is given by

\displaystyle m_{\chi}\simeq\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\Omega_{B}}\left(\frac{c_{B}}{c_{\chi}}\right)\left(\frac{z_{\rm sat}}{z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}}\right)^{2}{m_{p}\over z_{\rm sat}W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z_{\rm sat})}.

(44)

In contrast to the freeze-out scenario, the DM mass given in Eq. (
44

|y_{\chi,1}|
as
|y_{\chi,1}|
decreases.
This behavior arises because the asymmetry has a suppression factor of
W_{\rm ID}^{D}(z)\propto|y_{\chi,1}|^{2}
.

V
Discussions on phenomenology

In our scenario, the majoron plays an essential role as a background field. As already mentioned, successful low-scale leptogenesis requires the majoron field to undergo kinetic misalignment. On the other hand, if its kinetic energy becomes too large, it would modify the thermal history of the universe. Therefore, our analysis is valid only when

f^{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}(T_{\mathrm{fo}})/2<\pi^{2}/30\,g_{*}(T_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L})(T_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L})^{4}
holds, where
T_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}:=M_{1}/z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}
and
g_{*}
is the effective degrees of freedom.
To achieve successful spontaneous leptogenesis, this condition gives a constraint

\displaystyle g_{N,1}\gtrsim 10^{-7}\left(\frac{g_{*}(T_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L})}{100}\right)\left(\frac{z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}}{10}\right),

(45)

which has already been discussed in Ref.
Chun:2023eqc

.

In addition, when the lifetime of the majoron field is sufficiently long, the observed abundance of DM would be altered due to the majoron energy density,
{\rho_{J}/s}
. The origin of
{\rho_{J}/s}
can be attributed to two sources: one arising from the kinetic misalignment mechanism
Co:2019jts
;
Chang:2019tvx
and the other from thermal production.

In the kinetic misalignment mechanism, the energy density of the majoron depends on the relation between
the temperature
T_{\mathrm{osc}}
, at which the majoron mass becomes comparable to the Hubble parameter,
and the temperature
T_{\mathrm{trap}}
, at which its kinetic energy equals the potential barrier.
In each case, the energy density of the majoron is expressed as
Co:2019jts
;
Chang:2019tvx

\displaystyle{\rho_{J}\over s}\simeq\begin{cases}{m_{J}^{2}f^{2}/s(T_{\mathrm{trap}})}\simeq m_{J}Y_{\theta}&(T_{\mathrm{osc}}>T_{\mathrm{trap}}),\\[4.0pt]
{m_{J}^{2}f^{2}/s(T_{\mathrm{osc}})}&(T_{\mathrm{osc}}<T_{\mathrm{trap}}),\end{cases}

(46)

where
m_{J}
denotes the majoron mass.

Except for the case where the majoron is extremely light,
the condition
T_{\mathrm{osc}}>T_{\mathrm{trap}}
is satisfied in our case,
and hence the energy density is determined by the product of
m_{J}
and
Y_{\theta}
.
To account for the BAU, one needs

Y_{\theta}\simeq 10^{-7}g_{N,1}^{-2}(z_{\mathrm{fo}}^{L}/10)^{2}
, so that, for the majoron energy density to be negligible, the majoron mass must satisfy

\displaystyle m_{J}\lesssim 10~\mathrm{meV}\left(\frac{g_{N,1}}{10^{-4}}\right)^{2}.

(47)

Next, we consider the thermal production of the majoron.
When the following interactions,

\displaystyle LH

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow N_{i}J,

(48)

\displaystyle\chi\phi

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow N_{i}J,

(49)

maintain equilibrium at
T>M_{1}
, these interaction leads to the thermal production of the majoron.

In the freeze-out scenario, where
|y_{\chi,1}|>y_{N,1}
, the latter process
\chi\phi\leftrightarrow N_{i}J
dominates.
The requirement that the energy density of the majoron remains small imposes the following constraint on
g_{N,1}
:

\displaystyle g_{N,1}\lesssim 10^{-4}\left(\frac{M_{1}}{10^{6}~\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{|y_{\chi,1}|}\right).

(50)

On the other hand, in the freeze-in scenario, where
|y_{\chi,1}|<y_{N,1}
,
the former process
LH\leftrightarrow N_{i}J
becomes dominant,
which requires
g_{N,1}\lesssim 0.1

Chun:2023eqc
.

In our scenario, the DM particle
\chi
couples to the SM particles only through the mediation of either right-handed neutrinos
N_{i}
or a dark photon
Z^{\prime}
, and hence direct detection would be challenging.
However, if
\chi
has the following effective interaction,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}\supset\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}\,\chi\bar{\chi}\,n\bar{n},

(51)

where
n
denotes a nucleon, then it can be probed via direct detection experiments.
Such an effective operator has been explored in the context of ADM
Buckley:2011kk
;
March-Russell:2012elz
;
Roy:2024ear
.
15
15

15

Such an interaction can arise if the mass term of
\chi
originates from a Yukawa coupling to another scalar singlet
\varphi
,

\mathcal{L}\supset g_{\chi}\,\varphi\chi\bar{\chi}
,
and if
\varphi
mixes with the Higgs field through a portal coupling

\mathcal{L}\supset\lambda\,|\varphi|^{2}|H|^{2}
.
We note that
\varphi
is distinct from
\phi
and
\Phi
in Eq. (
5
).

It should be noted that in our scenario, this interaction with nucleons is irrelevant to the generation of the DM asymmetry.

In ADM scenarios, the reduction of the symmetric component is one of the key issues. As the most minimal annihilation channel, one may consider the process
\chi\bar{\chi}\to\phi\phi^{*}
induced by the Yukawa interaction essential for generating the asymmetry,
\mathcal{L}\supset y_{\chi,1}\bar{\chi}\phi^{\dagger}N_{1}
. However, as shown in Appendix
B
, this process is almost irrelevant for removing the symmetric component.
As another possibility, one may consider the annihilation channel
\chi\bar{\chi}\to n\bar{n}
mediated by the nucleon interaction introduced earlier in (
51
). However, such a possibility is excluded by constraints from direct detection experiments and collider searches in the range
1~\mathrm{GeV}\lesssim m_{\chi}\lesssim 100~\mathrm{GeV}

Buckley:2011kk
;
March-Russell:2012elz
, which coincides with the typical mass range predicted by our freeze-out scenario.

As the final possibility, the DM may annihilate into dark-sector particles through another interaction. In our Lagrangian (
5
), a dark photon is introduced, which leads to the annihilation process
\chi\bar{\chi}\to Z^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
. Requiring this process to efficiently remove the symmetric component of DM imposes a lower bound on the dark gauge coupling,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\gtrsim 10^{-2}\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{1~\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{1/2}.

(52)

Given the perturbativity condition,
g^{\prime}<\sqrt{4\pi}
, this leads to an upper bound on
m_{\chi}
, given by

\displaystyle m_{\chi}\lesssim 10^{5}~\text{GeV}.

(53)

A similar constraint on the model parameters is obtained by requiring that the energy density of
\phi
is also negligibly small.
Since the asymmetry of
\phi
is efficiently washed out in our scenario, it is the symmetric component that can be a dominant source of energy density, and its abundance is determined by the freezeout of the process
\phi\phi^{*}\to Z^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
.
The ratio of the abundance of
\phi
to that of the observed dark matter is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{\phi}}{\Omega_{\rm DM}}\sim 10^{-2}\left(\frac{g^{\prime}}{10^{-1}}\right)^{-4}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10~{\rm GeV}}\right)^{2},

(54)

where we assume
x_{f}\sim\mathcal{O}(10)
.
The condition for the process
\phi\phi^{*}\to Z^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
to efficiently remove the symmetric component of
\phi
gives

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\gg 10^{-2}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10~\mathrm{GeV}}\right)^{1/2},

(55)

which, given the perturbativity condition, leads to

\displaystyle m_{\phi}\lesssim 10^{4}~\text{GeV}.

VI
Summary

In this work, we consider ADM production associated with low-scale spontaneous leptogenesis,
within a model that extends the type-I seesaw framework by incorporating a dark sector containing ADM. In the low-scale spontaneous leptogenesis, the right-handed neutrinos remain in thermal equilibrium, and in the presence of a dynamically generated
CP
-violating background field (the majoron, in our case), the lepton asymmetry is produced through the inverse decays of SM leptons and the Higgs boson. In our setup, the right-handed neutrinos additionally have Yukawa interactions with the DM particle and a BSM scalar field. Consequently, inverse decays involving the DM and the BSM scalar simultaneously generate an asymmetry in the dark sector.

We found that, in the production of asymmetries, the Yukawa coupling
y_{\chi,1}
between the lightest right-handed neutrino and the DM particle plays a crucial role, governing both the evolution of the DM asymmetry and its final relic abundance. If this coupling is large, the asymmetry is rapidly produced up to its equilibrium value determined by the dynamical
CP
phase, and then this value freezes out when the inverse decay processes become decoupled. In contrast, when the coupling is small, the asymmetry cannot reach its equilibrium value but is gradually produced and eventually freezes in. We refer to the former as the freeze-out scenario and to the latter as the freeze-in scenario.
The boundary between these two regimes is determined by the size of the Yukawa interaction between the right-handed neutrinos and the SM leptons,
y_{N,1}
, and as the coupling deviates from this scale, the total amount of DM asymmetry produced decreases in both scenarios.

We then numerically solved the Boltzmann equations to compute the final relic asymmetry and the corresponding predicted DM mass in both the freeze-out and freeze-in scenarios.
In particular, in the freeze-out scenario, the DM mass is typically predicted to be in the range

\mathcal{O}(0.1)~\mathrm{GeV}\lesssim m_{\chi}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(100)~\mathrm{GeV}
, as long as scattering can be neglected.
This suggests that our scenario might be tested by upcoming experiments such as direct detection searches. On the other hand, if the dark matter asymmetry does not reach its equilibrium value due to the weak coupling, the allowed mass range extends over a broader interval,

\mathcal{O}(0.1)~\mathrm{GeV}\lesssim m_{\chi}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(10)~\mathrm{TeV}
.

Appendix B
Annihilation of symmetric components

In ADM models, the reduction of the symmetric component of DM is a central issue.
In our scenario, the most minimal annihilation channel is the right-handed neutrinomediated process
\chi\bar{\chi}\to\phi\phi^{*}
. However, due to the large mass of the right-handed neutrino and the
p
-wave suppression, this process is insufficient to reduce the symmetric component. A more efficient annihilation is provided by the additional channel into the hidden photon,
\chi\bar{\chi}\to Z^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
, introduced in the extended setup. Below, using quantitative estimates, we derive the difficulty inherent in the minimal model and determine how large coupling is required when a hidden photon is included.

Let
n_{\chi}
and
n_{\bar{\chi}}
denote the number densities of DM and anti-DM, respectively.
After the DM asymmetry production has frozen out, the Boltzmann equation for the number density of the anti-DM that undergoes annihilation with the thermally-averaged cross section
\langle\sigma v\rangle
reads

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{\bar{\chi}}+3Hn_{\bar{\chi}}=-\braket{\sigma v}\bigl(n_{\chi}n_{\bar{\chi}}-n_{\chi}^{\mathrm{eq}}n_{\bar{\chi}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\bigr),

(69)

or, in its dimensionless form,

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{\bar{\chi}}}{dx}=-\lambda_{\chi}\,x^{-n-2}\bigl(Y_{\chi}Y_{\bar{\chi}}-Y_{\chi}^{\mathrm{eq}}Y_{\bar{\chi}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\bigr),

(70)

where

\displaystyle x=\frac{m_{\chi}}{T},\qquad\lambda_{\chi}:=\left[\frac{x\,s}{H(x)}\braket{\sigma v}\right]_{x=1}.

(71)

Here,
n=0
(
n=1
) corresponds to the case of the s-wave (p-wave) process.
At late times when
Y_{\chi}^{\mathrm{eq}}Y_{\bar{\chi}}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is negligible, the solution can be found analytically, under the assumption that
Y_{\Delta\chi}:=Y_{\chi}(x)-Y_{\bar{\chi}}(x)
is already frozen out:

\displaystyle Y_{\bar{\chi}}(\infty)=\frac{Y_{\Delta\chi}}{e^{\lambda_{\chi}Y_{\Delta\chi}x_{f}^{-n-1}/(n+1)}\left[1+Y_{\Delta\chi}/Y_{\bar{\chi}}(x_{f})\right]-1},

(72)

where
x_{f}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
denotes the value when
Y_{\chi}^{\mathrm{eq}}Y_{\bar{\chi}}^{\mathrm{eq}}
starts to become negligible.

For the symmetric component to be subdominant,
Y_{\Delta\chi}\gg Y_{\bar{\chi}}(\infty)
, or, written in a useful form,

\displaystyle e^{\lambda_{\chi}Y_{\Delta\chi}}\gg 1,

(73)

needs to be satisfied, where
x_{f}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
and
Y_{\bar{\chi}}(x_{f})\lesssim Y_{\Delta\chi}
are used to drop all the
\mathcal{O}(1)
factors for simplicity.

Now consider the p-wave annihilation
\chi\bar{\chi}\to\phi\phi^{*}
, caused by the t-channel exchange of
N_{1}
.
The thermally-averaged annihilation cross-section for this process is given by

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle\simeq\frac{|y_{\chi,1}|^{4}}{8\pi M_{1}^{2}}\frac{T}{m_{\chi}}.

(74)

Using Eq. (
74
) and the ratio of the observed energy densities of DM and baryons,
\Omega_{\rm DM}/\Omega_{B}\simeq 5.4

Planck:2018vyg
, the exponent in Eq. (
73
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\chi}Y_{\Delta\chi}\simeq 10^{-12}\left(\frac{|y_{\chi,1}|}{10^{-3}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{M_{1}}{10~{\rm TeV}}\right)^{-2},

(75)

showing that Eq. (
73
) is never satisfied in the parameter regions of interest.

The annihilation via gauge interaction, on the other hand, can be
s
-wave dominated.
Assuming that
m_{\chi}>m_{Z^{\prime}}
, the process
\chi\bar{\chi}\to Z^{\prime}Z^{\prime}
gives

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle\simeq\frac{{g^{\prime}}^{4}}{8\pi m_{\chi}^{2}}.

(76)

In this case, the exponent in Eq. (
73
) becomes

\displaystyle\lambda_{\chi}Y_{\Delta\chi}\simeq\left(\frac{g^{\prime}}{10^{-2}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{1~{\rm GeV}}\right)^{-2},

(77)

implying that the symmetric component can be subdominant for

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\gtrsim 10^{-2}\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{1~{\rm GeV}}\right)^{1/2}.

(78)

Given the perturbativity condition,
g^{\prime}<\sqrt{4\pi}
, this constraint leads to an upper bound on

\displaystyle m_{\chi}\lesssim 10^{5}~\text{GeV}.

(79)

Appendix C
\Delta L=2
scattering rate

In this appendix, we discuss the
\Delta L=2
scattering process
\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*}
considered in the main text.
The interaction rate
\Gamma(\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*})
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*})

\displaystyle\qquad\simeq{1\over n_{\chi}^{\rm eq}}{T\over 32\pi^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,s^{3/2}\,K_{1}\!\left(\sqrt{s}/T\right)\sigma(\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*}),

(80)

where
\sigma(\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*})
denotes the scattering cross section for this process, and we treat all particles except the right-handed neutrino as massless for simplicity.

This process contains both
s
- and
t
-channel contributions, but for simplicity, we first focus on the
s
-channel, whose scattering cross section is

\displaystyle\sigma(\chi\phi\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{|y_{\chi,1}|^{4}}{32\pi}\,M_{1}^{2}\,|D_{N_{1}}|^{2},

(81)

\displaystyle D_{N_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s-M_{1}^{2}+iM_{1}\Gamma_{1}},

(82)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Gamma_{N_{1}\to\chi\phi}+\Gamma_{N_{1}\to\bar{\chi}\,\phi^{*}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{N_{1}\to l_{\alpha}H}+\Gamma_{N_{1}\to\bar{l}_{\alpha}H^{*}}

(83)

is the total decay width.

Equation (
80
) contains the on-shell resonance. Therefore, when formulating the Boltzmann equations that include both decays and scatterings, one must properly remove this resonance; otherwise, the result becomes an overestimate that double-counts the decay contribution
Buchmuller:2004nz
;
Davidson:2008bu
.
To remove this contribution, several subtraction prescriptions have been proposed. One approach treats the imaginary part of the
propagator as the on-shell contribution and regards the real part

\displaystyle D_{N_{1},\,{\rm off}}^{\rm PVS}(s):=\Re[D_{N_{1}}]

(84)

as the off-shell propagator whose square is to be used for computing the interaction rate; we refer to this as the principal value subtraction (PVS)
Luty:1992un
;
Plumacher:1996kc
;
Buchmuller:2000as
;
Buchmuller:2002jk
;
Buchmuller:2003gz
;
Pilaftsis:2003gt
.
Another approach considers the squared propagator and subtracts its imaginary part; we refer to this as the improved principal value subtraction (iPVS)
Cline:1993bd
;
Pilaftsis:2003gt
;
Giudice:2003jh
.

As pointed out in previous studies
Giudice:2003jh
;
Ala-Mattinen:2023rbm
, the PVS prescription subtracts only
half of the on-shell contribution. Indeed, one finds

\displaystyle|\Im[D_{N_{1}}]|^{2}\;\to\;{\pi\over 2M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}\,\delta(s-M_{1}^{2}),

(85)

which contains only half of the full on-shell piece.
On the other hand, the iPVS prescription suffers from a more fundamental problem: when the center-of-mass energy approaches the near on-shell region, the resulting reaction rate can become negative
Ala-Mattinen:2023rbm
.

Thus, we introduce the cut subtraction scheme, as in Ref.
Ala-Mattinen:2023rbm
, and use it to evaluate the interaction rate in Eq. (
80
). In addition, we derive an expression for estimating the accuracy of the subtraction, using a method different from that of Ref.
Ala-Mattinen:2023rbm
.

To evaluate Eq. (
80
), we need to
consider the following integral

\displaystyle J:=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,G(s)\,F(s),

(86)

where

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle:=|D_{N_{1}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{(s-M_{1}^{2})^{2}+M_{1}^{2}\Gamma_{1}^{2}},

(87)

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle:=s^{3/2}\,K_{1}(\sqrt{s}/T).

(88)

We split the integration range into the near on-shell region
M_{1}^{2}-\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}\leq s\leq M_{1}^{2}+\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}
with
\kappa>0
and the remainder:

\displaystyle J_{\rm on}

\displaystyle:=\int_{M_{1}^{2}-\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}^{M_{1}^{2}+\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}ds\,G(s)\,F(s),

(89)

\displaystyle J_{\rm off}

\displaystyle:=J-J_{\rm on}.

(90)

In the range
M_{1}^{2}-\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}\leq s\leq M_{1}^{2}+\kappa M_{1}\Gamma_{1}
, one may approximate
s\simeq M_{1}^{2}
and expand
F(s)
in a Taylor series:

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle\simeq F(M_{1}^{2})+F_{1}(M_{1}^{2})(s-M_{1}^{2})

(91)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}F_{2}(M_{1}^{2})(s-M_{1}^{2})^{2}+\cdots,

(92)

where
F_{1}(s):=\frac{d}{ds}F(s)
and
F_{2}(s):=\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}F(s)
.

Thus, for
\kappa\gg 1
, the quantity
J_{\rm on}
can be decomposed into the
on-shell contribution and its correction terms:

\displaystyle J_{\rm on}\simeq\frac{\pi}{M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}F(M_{1}^{2})+M_{1}\Gamma_{1}\left(\kappa-{\pi\over 2}\right)F_{2}(M_{1}^{2}).

(93)

We note that, in the regime of our interest where the Yukawa interactions of the right-handed neutrino are sufficiently small, one has
\Gamma_{1}/M_{1}\ll 1
, and this expansion is well justified as long as
\Gamma_{1}\kappa/M_{1}\ll 1
.

In deriving this expression, we have used the integral formulas

\displaystyle\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\frac{dx}{x^{2}+a^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{a}\arctan\!\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right),

(94)

\displaystyle\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\frac{x^{2}\,dx}{x^{2}+a^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\Delta-2a\,\arctan\!\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right),

(95)

together with the expansion valid for
\Delta\gg a
,

\displaystyle\arctan\!\left(\frac{\Delta}{a}\right)\simeq\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{a}{\Delta}.

(96)

The first term of
J_{\rm on}
corresponds to the contribution obtained by approximating the squared propagator
G(s)
by a delta function,

\displaystyle G(s)\to\frac{\pi}{M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}\delta(s-M_{1}^{2}),

(97)

which represents the on-shell resonance itself. Therefore, by separating the integration range as in Eqs. (
89
) and (
90
) and evaluating only
J_{\mathrm{off}}
, one can isolate the off-shell contribution, although an ambiguity of the order of the second term in
J_{\mathrm{on}}
remains.

The accuracy of this subtraction can be estimated by taking the ratio of the
second term in

to the first:

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{1}\Gamma_{1}\left(\kappa-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)F_{2}(M_{1}^{2})}{\frac{\pi}{M_{1}\Gamma_{1}}F(M_{1}^{2})}

(98)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{M_{1}^{2}}\frac{\kappa}{\pi}\frac{z^{2}}{4}\,\bigl(1-4K_{0}(z)\bigr),

(99)

which means that the correction is of order

\mathcal{O}(z^{2}\kappa{\Gamma_{1}^{2}/M_{1}^{2}})

In the parameter region of our interest, the ratio
\Gamma_{1}^{2}/M_{1}^{2}
is extremely small, and the subtraction is relevant only in the regime
