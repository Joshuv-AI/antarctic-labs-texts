Fear of movement and its associated psychosocial factors in patients with acute cardiovascular disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hui Lin, Qi Sun, Weiwei Yao, Chuanling Yuan, Qinrui Xing, Tingting Sun, Qiaoling Chen, Lei Du, Yu Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the intensity, temporal trajectory, and psychosocial correlates of fear of movement (kinesiophobia) among patients hospitalized for unstable angina (UA), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and acute aortic dissection (AAD), all of whom presented with acute chest or back pain.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study enrolled 424 patients admitted for acute chest or back pain. Participants were stratified into four diagnostic groups: a Control group (non-cardiac conditions, n = 93), and three cardiovascular groups: Unstable Angina (UA, n = 167), Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI, n = 99), and Acute Aortic Dissection (AAD, n = 65). The primary outcome, kinesiophobia (fear of movement), was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia for Heart (TSK-SV Heart). Assessments were conducted at four time points: during the initial inpatient period, at discharge, and at 1-week and 1-month post-discharge.ResultsDuring hospitalization, kinesiophobia severity varied significantly across groups. The AAD group exhibited the highest TSK-SV Heart score (42.09 ± 4.00), significantly exceeding those of AMI, UA, and Control groups (all P < 0.001). Although scores declined at discharge across all groups, a marked rebound emerged exclusively in the AAD group at the one-month follow-up (38.57 ± 5.37). Using latent class growth modeling, we identified two distinct fear trajectories—low and rapidly declining (87.5%) versus high and slowly declining (12.5%). Regression analyses revealed that acute pain intensity was the strongest predictor of both trajectories. Psychological resilience demonstrated a significant protective effect, with a strong inverse correlation with kinesiophobia (r = -0.486, P < 0.001). Factors associated with fear of movement included older age, non-local residency, lower income, and higher pain intensity, whereas persistent fear was linked to higher educational attainment and more severe pain.ConclusionKinesiophobia is prevalent and pronounced following acute cardiovascular events, with distinct patterns across diagnostic categories. Patients with AAD are at greatest risk for severe and sustained fear. Acute pain and psychological resilience emerge as central, modifiable determinants. These findings support the integration of routine screening and diagnosis-specific psychological interventions into acute cardiovascular care pathways.Trial RegistrationNot applicable (retrospective observational study).Keywords: Fear of movement, Acute cardiovascular disease, Psychological resilience, Acute pain, Cardiovascular rehabilitation

Introduction

Acute chest and back pain represent hallmark symptoms of various cardiovascular emergencies. Among these, unstable angina (UA), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and aortic dissection (AAD) are life-threatening conditions that demand urgent intervention. While UA and AMI, both manifestations of acute coronary syndrome, stem from myocardial ischemia, AAD constitutes a distinct aortic catastrophe characterized by a tear in the aortic wall and the formation of a false lumen [1, 2]. Management strategies differ considerably: pharmacotherapy and revascularization for UA and AMI versus advanced endovascular repair for AAD [1–5]. Beyond these pathophysiological distinctions, the often-traumatic pain experience—frequently described as “crushing,” “tearing,” or “ripping”—coupled with the pervasive perception of AAD as a “ticking time bomb,” may impose unique psychological burdens that critically influence post-event recovery and adherence to rehabilitation [6, 7].

A cornerstone of recovery for these conditions is the gradual resumption of physical activity, a key component of cardiac rehabilitation and secondary prevention [8]. However, this process is frequently impeded by kinesiophobia—an excessive, irrational fear of movement due to perceived vulnerability to (re)injury [9]. Among cardiovascular patients, such fear often arises from catastrophic beliefs that activity might precipitate a recurrent acute event, exacerbate their cardiac condition, or cause bodily harm [10]. Consequently, kinesiophobia constitutes a well-documented barrier to rehabilitation, contributing to reduced physical activity, functional decline, and impaired quality of life [11, 12].

Despite growing recognition of kinesiophobia, a critical knowledge gap persists. Current evidence derives largely from single-disease cohorts or studies of chronic conditions. Notably absent is research directly comparing the burden, longitudinal course, and psychosocial correlates of kinesiophobia across distinct acute cardiovascular emergencies that share a common sentinel symptom: acute chest or back pain. It remains unknown whether the intensity, persistence, and psychological underpinnings of this debilitating fear differ meaningfully among survivors of UA, AMI, or AAD. Elucidating such diagnosis-specific patterns is essential, as the unique pathophysiology, treatment intensity, and illness perceptions associated with each condition may differentially shape post-event psychological responses and activity avoidance. A generic approach to managing post-event fear may therefore prove inadequate.

To address this gap, we conducted a comparative study with two primary aims: first, to compare the prevalence, severity, and longitudinal trajectory of kinesiophobia among patients hospitalized for UA, AMI, and AAD following acute cardiovascular events; and second, to identify associated demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors within this acute population. By delineating both shared and diagnosis-specific determinants, this study seeks to inform the development of more targeted, condition-sensitive psychological and rehabilitative interventions to mitigate fear of movement and enhance recovery outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective observational cohort study consecutively enrolled eligible patients presenting with acute chest or back pain who were admitted to the cardiovascular and vascular surgery departments via the emergency department between January 2020 and January 2024. Participants were stratified into four groups based on their final diagnosis: a Control group comprising patients admitted for non-cardiac conditions, and three diagnostic groups—Unstable Angina (UA), Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI), and Acute Aortic Dissection (AAD). The control group consisted of healthy individuals admitted during the same period for routine health check-ups. These participants had no history of acute or chronic cardiovascular disease, no acute symptoms at admission, and were confirmed free of cardiovascular conditions through clinical evaluation and relevant investigations. Diagnoses were verified using established clinical criteria: UA and AMI were diagnosed according to contemporary guidelines incorporating serial cardiac biomarkers and electrocardiographic findings, while AAD was confirmed by computed tomography angiography [1, 2]. All data—including baseline demographic, clinical, and psychosocial measures, as well as follow-up assessments—were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system and a dedicated research database maintained by our nursing team. Patients were contacted regularly by the nursing team via telephone to complete standardized questionnaires during follow-up.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) primary admission triggered by acute chest or back pain leading to a confirmed diagnosis of UA, AMI, or AAD (for the three cardiovascular groups), or admission for other conditions (Control group); (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) hemodynamic and neurological stability, with preserved cognitive function and the ability to comprehend and complete questionnaires; (4) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) documented history of major psychiatric illness or dementia; (2) pre-existing physical disabilities substantially limiting mobility; (3) occurrence of major in-hospital complications precluding assessment; (4) incomplete medical records or patient refusal to participate.

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hainan Hospital, Chinese PLA General Hospital.

Sample size estimation

An a priori sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software. For the primary objective of comparing TSK-SV Heart scores across the four study groups using one-way ANOVA, a moderate effect size (f = 0.25) was assumed. With an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 0.90, the analysis indicated a minimum required sample of 180 participants. Anticipating a potential 15% rate of attrition or incomplete data, the recruitment target was set at a minimum of 212 participants [13].

Measures and data collection

Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using a standardized case report form and electronic health records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), geographic origin, comorbidities, surgical history, and socioeconomic factors (education, monthly income, living arrangement).

Fear of movement was assessed using the Chinese version of the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia for Heart patients (TSK-SV Heart) [14]. This 17-item scale yields a total score ranging from 17 to 68, with higher scores indicating greater fear of movement. A score > 37 indicated clinically significant fear [15]. The Chinese version of the TSK-SV Heart has been previously validated and demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.801) in a study of Chinese cardiovascular populations [11].

Psychological and psychosocial measures included the 25-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) for psychological resilience (score range 0–100); a scale for cardiac anxiety; the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for depressive symptoms; and the MacArthur Scale for Subjective Social Status. Acute pain intensity at onset was quantified using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0–10) [16–19].

Data collection procedure: Following clinical stabilization, eligible patients provided written informed consent. A trained research nurse administered the questionnaire battery, including the TSK-SV Heart, CD-RISC, and measures of cardiac anxiety, depressive symptoms, and subjective social status. For longitudinal assessment of fear of movement, the TSK-SV Heart was re-administered at three additional time points: at discharge, one week post-discharge, and one month post-discharge. At each follow-up visit, medication reconciliation was performed by the research nurse, who systematically inquired about the use of any analgesic medications—including prescribed analgesics and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen—and recorded this information in the case report form. The relatively short follow-up period, combined with regular telephone contacts by the nursing team, minimized attrition and contributed to complete retention of all participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were compared across the four groups using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate, with post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. The Bonferroni correction was applied to normally distributed data following ANOVA, while Dunn’s test was used following the Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Bivariate associations between the TSK-SV Heart total score (continuous) and other continuous psychosocial variables (CD-RISC, cardiac anxiety, depressive symptoms, subjective social status) in the overall cohort were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. To account for potential confounding, all multivariable models included age, sex, and socioeconomic variables (education, monthly income) as covariates, regardless of their significance in univariate analyses. For between-group comparisons of TSK-SV Heart scores, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with age, sex, and monthly income included as covariates to adjust for baseline imbalances.

Clinically significant fear of movement was defined as a TSK-SV Heart score > 37, based on previously established criteria [15]. To investigate heterogeneity in the longitudinal course of kinesiophobia, latent class growth modeling (LCGM) was performed on the subgroup of patients who exhibited clinically significant fear at baseline (TSK-SV Heart score > 37, n = 197). Models with one to five classes were fitted to the TSK-SV Heart scores at all four time points (inpatient, discharge, one week, and one month post-discharge). Model selection was based on multiple fit indices, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT). The optimal number of classes was determined by combining statistical criteria with clinical interpretability and class size adequacy. The two-class solution was selected as the final model based on its high entropy (0.978), significant LMR-LRT (P < 0.001), and clinically meaningful class proportions (87.5% and 12.5%). The two empirically derived trajectory classes were: Class 1 (low and rapidly declining fear) and Class 2 (high and slowly declining fear). Two separate multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed using the entire cohort (N = 424). The first analysis aimed to identify factors associated with clinically significant fear of movement at baseline (TSK-SV Heart score > 37). The second analysis aimed to identify factors associated with membership in the high and slowly declining fear trajectory (Class 2). For this second analysis, patients without baseline clinically significant fear (and thus not included in the LCGM) were coded as not belonging to Class 2. Variables with a significance level of P < 0.10 in univariate analyses were entered into the model using a stepwise method. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyse

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 606 patients presenting with acute chest or back pain were initially assessed. After applying exclusion criteria and accounting for patients who declined participation or did not complete the questionnaires, 424 patients were included in the final analysis. Participants were categorized into four groups: a Control group (n = 93) comprising patients admitted for non-cardiac conditions, and three diagnostic groups based on final diagnosis: Unstable Angina (UA, n = 167), Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI, n = 99), and Acute Aortic Dissection (AAD, n = 65). All 424 enrolled patients completed follow-up assessments at discharge, one week, and one month post-discharge, yielding a 100% completion rate. No patients were lost to follow-up at any time point (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Medication reconciliation at each follow-up visit confirmed that no patient in any diagnostic group reported using analgesic medications during the one-week or one-month post-discharge periods. The mean age of patients in the three cardiovascular groups (UA, AMI, AAD) was comparable, ranging from 69.49 to 69.97 years, and was significantly higher than that of the Control group (50.56 ± 5.42 years; P < 0.001). The AAD group included a significantly higher proportion of male patients (90.77%) and individuals of non-local geographic origin (76.92%) compared to the other groups. All AAD patients were Stanford type B and underwent elective endovascular repair (TEVAR) after initial stabilization, with no ICU admissions. In the UA group, a proportion of patients underwent elective PCI during hospitalization, while the remainder were managed with medical therapy alone. All AMI patients underwent emergency PCI. Severity and longitudinal trajectory of fear of movement

The severity of fear of movement, as measured by the TSK-SV Heart scale, differed significantly across diagnostic groups at all time points assessed (Fig. 2A and B; Table 2). During the initial inpatient period, the AAD group exhibited the highest mean TSK-SV Heart score (42.09 ± 4.00), significantly exceeding scores in the AMI (40.93 ± 5.69), UA (34.07 ± 6.10), and Control (29.69 ± 4.52) groups (all P < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, and monthly income using ANCOVA, the significant between-group differences remained robust, with AAD patients continuing to show the highest fear levels at all time points (all adjusted P < 0.001) (Table 3). This pattern was consistent across all four TSK-SV Heart subscales: Perceived Danger, Fear of Injury, Avoidance of Exercise, and Perceived Dysfunction. Longitudinally, TSK-SV Heart scores declined from the inpatient period to discharge and the one-week follow-up in all cardiovascular groups. However, at the one-month post-discharge assessment, a distinct rebound emerged exclusively in the AAD group, whose mean score increased to 38.57 ± 5.37, while scores in the UA and AMI groups continued to decline (Fig. 2C).

Psychological and psychosocial characteristics

Significant between-group differences were observed in psychological resilience and other psychosocial measures at baseline (Table 4). The AAD group demonstrated low psychological resilience, with a mean CD-RISC score of 68.19 ± 10.29, significantly lower than those of the UA (82.31 ± 9.83) and Control (84.33 ± 7.29) groups (both P < 0.001). The AMI group exhibited the lowest resilience score (65.08 ± 10.38), also significantly lower than the UA and Control groups (P < 0.001). Regarding other psychosocial variables, cardiac anxiety was highest in the AMI group (29.46 ± 4.47), whereas depressive symptoms were most severe in the AAD group (9.86 ± 4.27). Additionally, patients in the AAD group rated their subjective social status the lowest (5.03 ± 3.11) among all groups (Table 4).

Correlations between fear of movement and psychosocial variables

Bivariate correlation analyses conducted on the entire cohort (n = 424) revealed significant associations between fear of movement (TSK-SV Heart total score) and key psychosocial variables (Fig. 3A-H) (Table 5). A moderate negative correlation was found between fear of movement and psychological resilience (r = -0.486, P < 0.001). Fear of movement was positively correlated with depressive symptoms (r = 0.395, P < 0.001) and, to a lesser extent, with cardiac anxiety (r = 0.233, P < 0.001). Trajectories of fear of movement identified by LCGM

Among the 197 patients with clinically significant kinesiophobia at baseline (TSK-SV Heart score > 37), mean TSK-SV Heart scores showed a gradual decline over time (T1: 42.34 ± 3.75; T2: 36.89 ± 5.69; T3: 35.59 ± 5.18; T4: 33.63 ± 6.50), with positive correlations between time points (r = 0.50–0.77), satisfying the assumptions for LCGM. Fit indices for models with one to five classes are presented in Table 6. Although AIC, BIC, and aBIC continued to decrease with more classes, the three-class model showed a non-significant LMR-LRT (P = 0.172), indicating no significant improvement over the two-class model. The four- and five-class models, while yielding significant LMR-LRT values, produced very small classes (< 5% of the sample; 3.8% and 3.6%, respectively), potentially compromising robustness and generalizability. The two-class model demonstrated the highest entropy (0.978), indicating excellent classification accuracy, and a highly significant LMR-LRT (P < 0.001). Therefore, the two-class solution was selected as the final model (Table 5).

Fitting of the LCGM of movement fear in patients

The two empirically derived trajectory classes are shown in Fig. 4. Class 1 exhibited low and rapidly declining fear (n = 172, 87.5%): characterized by a lower initial level of kinesiophobia (intercept = 40.897, P < 0.001) and a steep decline over time (slope = − 3.254, P < 0.001). Patients in this class experienced fear primarily during acute hospitalization, which resolved quickly during follow-up. Class 2 exhibited high and slowly declining fear (n = 24, 12.5%): characterized by a significantly higher initial level of kinesiophobia (intercept = 49.518, P < 0.001) and a slower rate of decline (slope = − 2.141, P < 0.001). Patients in this class maintained elevated fear levels throughout follow-up, with only modest improvement by one month post-discharge. Among the 24 patients in Class 2, 13 were from the AAD group and 11 from the AMI group; no patients from the Control or UA groups were classified into this trajectory. In contrast, patients in Class 1 were predominantly from the UA and AMI groups.

Multivariable predictors of fear of movement phenotypes

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors independently associated with two phenotypes of clinically significant fear of movement (TSK-SV Heart > 37): Class 1 (low and rapidly declining fear) and Class 2 (high and slowly declining fear). For fear of movement, independent predictors included older age (adjusted OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.002–1.051), non-local residential status (AOR = 1.651, 95% CI: 1.082–2.518), higher acute pain score at onset (AOR = 1.373, 95% CI: 1.132–1.667), and lower monthly income (AOR = 0.519, 95% CI: 0.309–0.871). For Class 2 (high and slowly declining fear), independent predictors were higher education level (≥ high school) (AOR = 4.513, 95% CI: 1.507–13.519) and higher acute pain score at onset (AOR = 2.290, 95% CI: 1.650–3.178) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study provides a comparative analysis of the severity, longitudinal trajectory, and associated factors of fear of movement in patients hospitalized for three major cardiovascular emergencies presenting with a common sentinel symptom—acute chest or back pain. Our key findings can be summarized as follows: patients with acute aortic dissection (AAD) exhibited the highest level of fear of movement during hospitalization, with a significant rebound at the one-month post-discharge follow-up, whereas fear levels in patients with unstable angina (UA) and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) declined steadily over time. Acute pain intensity was the strongest and most consistent clinical predictor of fear of movement, particularly for the persistent phenotype. Additionally, psychological resilience served as a significant protective factor, demonstrating a robust negative correlation with fear of movement. The two trajectories were associated with distinct predictor profiles, suggesting potentially different underlying psychobehavioral mechanisms.

Kinesiophobia, as defined by the Tampa Scale, refers to an excessive, irrational fear of physical activity due to perceived vulnerability to re-injury [9]. This construct is not disease-specific but rather reflects a psychological response to perceived threat. In AAD survivors, this fear is highly salient: patients have experienced a life-threatening aortic catastrophe, often undergo major surgical or endovascular repair, and are counseled to avoid heavy lifting to control blood pressure. The core fear that movement might cause reinjury is conceptually identical to that in cardiac patients. A recent scoping review confirms that fear of movement is a significant concern across cardiac surgical populations, including those undergoing aortic surgery [20].

Diagnosis-specific burden and trajectory of fear of movement

Our finding that AAD patients endure the greatest burden of kinesiophobia aligns with the unique psychophysiological experience of this condition. While UA and AMI involve myocardial ischemia, AAD is frequently perceived, by patients and clinicians alike, as a “ticking time bomb” [4], a metaphor encapsulating its sudden onset and life-threatening nature. The characteristic “tearing” or “ripping” pain at onset, an experience often described as uniquely terrifying, coupled with awareness of the disease’s high mortality, may potentiate acute traumatic stress and foster catastrophic beliefs about activity [21, 22]. This salient threat representation can result in persistent hypervigilance to interoceptive cues. Consequently, benign somatic sensations arising during minor activity may be catastrophically misinterpreted as heralding a recurrence or extension of the dissection, thereby reinforcing a cycle of fear and avoidance [23]. The rebound in fear among AAD patients at one-month post-discharge merits careful interpretation. Importantly, this rebound cannot be attributed to changes in analgesic regimens or pain recurrence following analgesic withdrawal, as medication reconciliation confirmed that no patients in any group received routine analgesia after diagnosis or reported any analgesic use after discharge. Instead, we hypothesize that this rebound reflects genuine psychological processes specific to AAD survivors. The transition from the highly monitored hospital environment to home may trigger a crisis of confidence in self-management [24]. Benign somatic sensations that would normally be ignored may be catastrophically misinterpreted as signs of dissection progression or recurrence, reactivating fear and avoidance [23]. This interpretation aligns with the Fear-Avoidance Model and underscores the need for transitional care interventions that specifically address these cognitive and emotional processes. This finding emphasizes that psychological support and rehabilitation guidance for AAD patients should not be confined to the inpatient period but must form a core component of transitional care, particularly during the critical first month following discharge.

Importantly, a recent study specifically used the TSK-SV Heart to assess postoperative kinesiophobia in 264 patients with acute type A aortic dissection, reporting a mean score of 38.15 ± 6.66 and a prevalence of 46.2%, with the scale demonstrating acceptable psychometric properties [25]. This provides direct empirical precedent for the use of this scale in AAD patients and supports the validity of our approach. Additionally, research has discussed exercise-related fear in aortic dissection survivors, highlighting the clinical importance of assessing fear of movement in this population even in the absence of disease-specific instruments [24].

The central role of acute pain experience

Our multivariate analyses emphasize the central role of the acute pain experience. A higher VAS pain score at onset was the strongest independent predictor of both fear trajectories. This finding is highly consistent with the Fear-Avoidance Model, which is prevalent in both chronic and acute pain literature [26, 27]. This model posits that catastrophic interpretation of pain fuels fear of related activities, leading to avoidance behaviors, which in turn exacerbate disability through disuse and depression, creating a vicious cycle [28]. A recent study on central sensitization in microvascular angina suggests that some patients may have heightened pain processing mechanisms, which could amplify the initial pain experience and its psychological sequelae [29]. In our study, severe acute pain was likely internalized by patients as a signal of extreme bodily vulnerability or that “movement equals danger.” This suggests that effective acute pain management is not merely symptomatic treatment but may also serve as a crucial psychological preventive strategy against long-term maladaptive fear and rehabilitation non-adherence [30].

The protective role of psychological resilience and related factors

We found that psychological resilience (CD-RISC) had the strongest negative correlation with fear of movement. This aligns with theoretical frameworks that conceptualize resilience as a key resource for adapting to major health threats [31]. Individuals with higher resilience may be more adept at cognitively reframing an acute cardiovascular event as a manageable challenge rather than an insurmountable threat, thus reducing fear and avoidance [32]. An intriguing finding was that while cardiac anxiety and depressive symptoms showed bivariate correlations with kinesiophobia, acute pain intensity emerged as the dominant independent predictor in multivariate models [33]. This pattern contrasts sharply with observations in chronic heart failure populations, where cardiac anxiety and depression are often primary drivers, and pain may be less salient [10]. This divergence likely highlights a fundamental distinction in the psychological architecture of fear between acute, traumatic medical events and chronic disease states. In the acute phase, the salient, overwhelming sensory experience of pain may serve as the primary catalyst for fear learning and avoidance. In chronic conditions, as the immediate pain memory recedes, ongoing symptom monitoring, illness-related worry, and mood dysregulation may become the principal factors sustaining kinesiophobia over time [34]. Furthermore, the lack of an independent association with subjective social status in our study, unlike in chronic disease populations, may suggest that the acute event delivers a similarly profound shock across socioeconomic strata, temporarily overshadowing the long-term effects of social resources. This finding may explain why the effects of social status were less pronounced in our cohort compared to what has been observed in patients with chronic conditions.

Distinct fear trajectories and their clinical implications

While the majority of patients exhibited a benign trajectory of fear (Class 1), the smaller subgroup with a high and slowly declining trajectory (Class 2) represents the clinically relevant target for intervention. The LCGM analysis identified two distinct trajectories of fear of movement. The majority of patients (87.5%) exhibited a low and rapidly declining fear pattern, with elevated fear confined to the acute hospitalization and resolving quickly after discharge. In contrast, a smaller but clinically important subgroup (12.5%) demonstrated a high and slowly declining fear trajectory, maintaining elevated fear levels throughout the follow-up period. This latter group, predominantly composed of AAD patients, represents a target population for early psychological intervention.

The LCGM analysis revealed that AAD patients were predominantly classified into Class 2 (high and slowly declining fear). Examination of the Class 2 trajectory shape provides empirical support for the rebound phenomenon: after an initial decline from inpatient to discharge, fear levels plateau and show a modest increase at the one-month follow-up. This pattern was not observed in Class 1, where fear continued to decline steadily across all time points. These findings confirm that the rebound in fear is specific to AAD patients and emerges naturally from the data rather than being imposed by arbitrary cutoffs.

An important advancement in our analysis was the differentiation between these two trajectories, revealing distinct predictors. The low and rapidly declining fear trajectory was associated with older age, non-local residency, lower income, and pain. This pattern may primarily reflect an acute stress response, resource limitations, and the direct aftermath of pain, potentially subsiding naturally with physical recovery and regained stability. Non-local residency, a predictor of this trajectory, likely reflects a combination of practical and psychosocial challenges. These individuals may face barriers to accessing continuous care, have limited immediate social support networks post-discharge, and experience heightened uncertainty during the transition from hospital to home. This situational stress may amplify initial fear, which then diminishes as care plans are established or as they return to their usual environment.

The high and slowly declining fear trajectory, conversely, was associated with higher education and severe pain. The association between higher education level (≥ high school) and this trajectory may appear counterintuitive. This could be explained by the “health literacy paradox” or heightened health anxiety observed in some well-educated individuals [35–37], including through online platforms such as YouTube. However, as highlighted by a recent study evaluating cardiac rehabilitation information on YouTube, the quality and reliability of such content is highly variable, and exposure to inaccurate or alarmist information could further reinforce catastrophic cognitions about physical activity [38]. These individuals may engage in more extensive, and sometimes unfiltered, information-seeking about their condition post-discharge, leading to an overestimation of risk and reinforcing catastrophic cognitions regarding physical activity. While pain was a strong predictor in both groups, its effect size was larger for the high and slowly declining fear trajectory, suggesting that initial pain intensity may serve as a critical threshold determining whether fear becomes chronic. Identifying these trajectories aids in clinical risk stratification, enabling early, intensified psychological intervention for high-risk patients.

Clinical implications and future directions

The findings of this study carry several important clinical implications. Routine screening for fear of movement using validated instruments such as the TSK-SV Heart should be integrated into the standard assessment of patients surviving acute cardiovascular events, particularly for those with aortic dissection and individuals reporting severe acute pain. Early identification of patients at risk for the high and slowly declining fear trajectory may enable timely intervention before maladaptive fear becomes entrenched. Interventions should be tailored to both diagnosis and trajectory profile. For patients likely to follow the low and rapidly declining fear pattern, the focus should be on cardiac rehabilitation education and symptom reappraisal to prevent unnecessary activity avoidance. For patients exhibiting or at risk for the high and slowly declining fear trajectory, particularly AAD survivors, more intensive psychological support may be warranted. Structured interventions such as resilience training based on cognitive-behavioral therapy or brief acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) modules could be introduced early during hospitalization. These approaches emphasize cognitive reframing, value clarification, and committed action, helping patients reconceptualize physical activity as a meaningful step toward valued life goals rather than a threat to be avoided. Given the central role of acute pain as a predictor of both trajectories, effective pain management should be viewed as an integral component of psychological care, not merely symptomatic treatment [39]. Aggressive pain control during the acute phase may mitigate the initial fear response and reduce the likelihood of chronic, disabling fear. Future research should focus on developing and validating brief, scalable psychosocial interventions tailored to different acute cardiovascular patient groups and trajectory profiles. Longitudinal studies are also needed to clarify the pathways through which fear of movement impacts long-term outcomes, including rehabilitation adherence, functional recovery, and quality of life [8].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective cohort study, our findings are subject to limitations inherent to this design, including the inability to control for unmeasured confounders and reliance on existing medical records, which may contain inaccuracies. Although data were recorded contemporaneously during routine care, the retrospective extraction precluded standardization of assessment procedures. Prospective studies with pre-specified protocols are needed to confirm our findings in the future. The single-center design limits generalizability, and although our overall sample size met the a priori requirement for primary analysis, the modest AAD subgroup warrants caution in interpreting subgroup findings. Validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is required. Additionally, all psychological measures were self-reported, introducing potential common method bias. Although we systematically assessed prescribed and over-the-counter analgesic use at each follow-up and confirmed that no patients reported using analgesics after discharge, we did not measure other pain-related factors that could potentially influence fear responses, such as pain duration, pain quality, or individual differences in pain threshold. Future studies should incorporate more detailed pain phenotyping to further disentangle the complex relationship between acute pain, analgesia, and psychological outcomes. Finally, potentially important mediating variables—including coping styles and social support—were not included.

The TSK-SV Heart scale used in this study was originally developed and validated in patients with coronary heart disease. Although the scale has been used previously in aortic dissection populations and has demonstrated good internal consistency in Chinese cardiovascular populations, formal validation studies specifically in AAD patients are lacking. It is possible that AAD patients experience fear of movement in ways not fully captured by items designed for cardiac patients. Future research should prioritize formal psychometric evaluations of the TSK-SV Heart in AAD populations or develop disease-specific measures for aortic dissection survivors.

Although we adjusted for age, sex, and socioeconomic variables in our multivariable analyses, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be completely eliminated. The control group, while healthy and recruited from the same setting, differed substantially in baseline characteristics from the cardiovascular groups. Additionally, unmeasured indicators of disease severity (e.g., extent of dissection in AAD, infarct size in AMI) could not be accounted for in our analyses. Future studies with matched control groups, larger samples, and more detailed clinical phenotyping are needed to further disentangle the effects of disease pathology, treatment intensity, and demographic factors on fear of movement and to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

In this cohort study of 424 patients hospitalized for acute cardiovascular emergencies, fear of movement was prevalent and followed diagnosis-specific trajectories. Using latent class growth modeling, we identified two distinct patterns: a low and rapidly declining fear trajectory affecting most patients, and a high and slowly declining fear trajectory observed in a smaller but clinically important subgroup. Patients with acute aortic dissection were disproportionately represented in the latter group, exhibiting the most severe and persistent fear with a distinct rebound one month after discharge. Acute pain intensity emerged as the strongest predictor of trajectory membership, while psychological resilience served as a significant protective factor. These findings underscore the need for differentiated risk assessment and diagnosis-sensitive screening for fear of movement—particularly in AAD patients and those reporting severe pain—and support integrating early, targeted psychological interventions into acute cardiovascular care to improve patient recovery and long-term prognosis (Central Illustration).
