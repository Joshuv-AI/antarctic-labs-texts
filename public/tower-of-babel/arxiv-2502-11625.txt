Comparative Analysis of EMCEE, Gaussian Process, and Masked Autoregressive Flow in Constraining the Hubble Constant Using Cosmic Chronometers Dataset Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jing Niu, Jie-Feng Chen, Peng He, Tong-Jie Zhang

Abstract
The Hubble constant ( H 0 \mathrm{H}_{0} ) is essential for understanding the universe’s evolution. Different methods, such as Affine Invariant Markov chain Monte Carlo Ensemble sampler (EMCEE), Gaussian Process (GP), and Masked Autoregressive Flow (MAF), are used to constrain H 0 \mathrm{H}_{0} using H ⁡ ( z ) H(z) data. However, these methods produce varying H 0 \mathrm{H}_{0} values when applied to the same dataset. To investigate these differences, we compare the methods based on their sensitivity to individual data points and their accuracy in constraining H 0 \mathrm{H}_{0} . We introduce Multiple Random Sampling Analysis (MRSA) to assess their sensitivity to individual data points. Our findings reveal that GP is more sensitive to individual data points than both MAF and EMCEE, with MAF being more sensitive than EMCEE. Sensitivity also depends on redshift: EMCEE and GP are more sensitive to H ⁡ ( z ) H(z) at higher redshifts, while MAF is more sensitive at lower redshifts. For accuracy assessment, we simulate H sim ​ ( z sim ) H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}}) datasets with a prior H 0 ​ p ​ r ​ i ​ o ​ r \mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}} . Comparing the constrained H 0 ​ s ​ i ​ m \mathrm{H_{0sim}} values with H 0 ​ p ​ r ​ i ​ o ​ r \mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}} shows that EMCEE is the most accurate, followed by MAF, with GP being the least accurate, regardless of the simulation method.



I
Introduction

Understanding the evolution of the universe has been a paramount goal in modern cosmology, primarily achieved through various observational techniques. The Hubble parameter,
H(z)
, indicates the rate of expansion of the universe at a given redshift
z
, while
\mathrm{H}_{0}
represents the current expansion rate. Accurate measurement of
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is crucial for understanding the universe’s history. Currently, there are two important methods for measuring the Hubble constant. The Planck mission’s measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), under the base-
\Lambda
CDM cosmology, yield
\mathrm{H}_{0}=67.4\pm 0.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
)
. In contrast, measurements using Cepheid variables in the host galaxies of 42 Type Ia supernovae via the Hubble Space Telescope give
\mathrm{H}_{0}=73.04\pm 1.04\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}

(
Riess et al. 2022
)
. This discrepancy, known as the Hubble tension, is significant at a 5
\sigma
level
(
Verde et al. 2019
;
Riess 2020
)
.

Constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using the Hubble parameter
H(z)
, which measures the expansion rate of the universe at different redshifts, is an alternative approach. This method could potentially provide insights into resolving the Hubble tension. In this paper, we use the Cosmic Chronometers (CC) dataset for
H(z)
because the CC method provides model-independent
H(z)
measurements. Several methods can be employed to constrain
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using the
H(z)
data, including Affine Invariant Markov chain Monte Carlo Ensemble sampler (EMCEE)
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
, Gaussian Process (GP)
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
;
Seikel et al. 2012
)
, and Masked Autoregressive Flow (MAF)
(
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
. EMCEE is an affine-invariant ensemble sampler for Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), GP is a non-parametric method that allows for model-independent reconstruction of the Hubble parameter, and MAF is a deep learning-based approach capable of modeling complex distributions with neural networks. Although these methods have been studied independently
(
Racine et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. They have not been compared using a common criterion for constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
with the same CC dataset. This comparison is essential because the resulting
\mathrm{H}_{0}
values differ significantly:
\mathrm{H}_{0}=67.75\pm 3.08\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for EMCEE,
\mathrm{H}_{0}=67.21\pm 4.72\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for GP, and
\mathrm{H}_{0}=66.25\pm 9.46\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for MAF.

In this paper, we aim to compare these three methods (EMCEE, GP, and MAF) by evaluating their sensitivity to individual
H(z)
data points and their accuracy in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using the CC dataset. We propose a Multiple Random Sampling Analysis (MRSA) to test the sensitivity of these methods to individual
H(z)
data points. Additionally, we investigate the sensitivity to
H(z)
data points in different redshift ranges. To assess the accuracy of these methods in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
, we simulate the
H_{\text{sim}}(z_{\text{sim}})
dataset with a prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. We then compare the constrained
H_{0\text{sim}}
, obtained using the simulated
H_{\text{sim}}(z_{\text{sim}})
dataset with three methods, to
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. The method that yields values closer to
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
is considered more accurate. To minimize the influence of different simulation methods, we employ two distinct simulation approaches.

In Sec.
II
, we introduce the measurement of the CC dataset and compile it in Table
1
. Sec.
III
presents a comparison of the sensitivity to individual
H(z)
data points using the three methods (EMCEE, GP, and MAF) through the proposed MRSA. Subsec.
III.1
introduces these methods, while Subsec.
III.2
details the MRSA and its application in comparing the methods. Subsec.
III.3
examines the sensitivity of these methods to data points in different redshift regions. In Sec.
IV
, we compare the accuracy of the three methods in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using the CC data through the simulated
H_{\text{sim}}(z_{\text{sim}})
datasets with a prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. Finally, Sec.
V
provides the conclusions and discussions of this study.

Multiple random sampling analysis

The Hubble constant
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is crucial for understanding the evolution of the universe. One approach to determine
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is by constraining the CC data, as compiled in Table
1
. Several methods can be used to constrain
H(z)
, including EMCEE
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
, GP
(
Seikel et al. 2012
)
and MAF
(
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
, all of which are introduced in Subsec.
III.1
. The
\mathrm{H}_{0}
values derived from these three methods using the CC data vary. In Subsec.
III.2
, we propose using MRSA to compare the sensitivity of each method to individual H(z) data points. We assess how H(z) data points from different redshift regions affect the constrained
\mathrm{H}_{0}
values across these methods. To do this, we split the redshift range into two regions, as outlined in Subsec.
III.3
.

III.1
CC constrains
\mathrm{H}_{0}

III.1.1
EMCEE

To determine
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from CC data using
EMCEE
, it is essential to choose a cosmological model. In this paper, we adopt the flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmological model. The corresponding Friedmann equation is given by:

H(z)=\mathrm{H}_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{M}(1+z)^{3}+(1-\Omega_{M})}.

(2)

Where
H(z)
represents the Hubble parameter,
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is the Hubble constant,
\Omega_{M}
denotes the matter density, and
z
indicates the redshift. The observed CC data, denoted as
\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}(z)}
, is presented in Table
1
. It provides the values
\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}}=(H_{\mathrm{obs1}}\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,H_{\mathrm{obsN}})^{T}
, along with their corresponding errors denoted as
\bm{\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}}=(\sigma_{\mathrm{obs1}},\cdot\cdot\cdot,\sigma_{\mathrm{obsN}})^{\mathrm{T}}
, at various redshifts
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\cdot\cdot\cdot,z_{\mathrm{N}})^{T}
.

To constrain the parameters
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
, we use EMCEE, a method based on Bayes’ theorem. The application of Bayes’ theorem in this context is as follows:

P(\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}}|H_{\mathrm{obs}})=\frac{P(H_{\mathrm{obs}}|\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}})P(\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}})}{P(H_{\mathrm{obs}})}.

(3)

These parameters are determined by maximizing the likelihood function using EMCEE. The likelihood function, denoted by
\mathcal{L}
, is expressed as:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}}|\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}})=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(H_{\mathrm{obsi}}|\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}}).

(4)

Assuming that
\bm{\mathrm{\sigma_{obs}}}
values follow a Gaussian error distribution and are independent of each other, Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as
(
Ma and Zhang 2011
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{H_{\mathrm{{obs}}}}|\mathrm{H_{0},\Omega_{M}})=\left(\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\prod}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\right)\exp{\left(-\frac{\chi^{2}}{2}\right)},

(5)

the
\chi^{2}
is given by:

\chi^{2}=\underset{i}{\sum}\frac{[H_{\mathrm{model},i}-H_{\mathrm{obs},i}]^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

(6)

III.1.2
GP

\mathrm{H}_{0}
can also be determined by reconstructing the CC data using the Gaussian process, as outlined by
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
. GP is a powerful tool that can model the relationship in data using a joint Gaussian distribution. It estimates values at new points without requiring additional parameters. In this study, we use a Gaussian process to estimate the Hubble parameter,
H(z)
, as a function of redshift
z
. This model-independent approach provides a major advantage over other methods that rely on cosmological assumptions. The Hubble constant
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is determined as the value of the reconstructed
H(z)
at redshift
z=0
.

The observed CC data (
z_{i},H_{i},\sigma_{i}
) can be represented as a function
\bm{y}
, assuming the errors
\sigma_{i}
follow a Gaussian distribution.

\bm{y}\thicksim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C),

(7)

where
\mathcal{N}
represents the evaluated Gaussian Process, and
C
is the covariance matrix of the data. The mean and the covariance function between two data points,
y(z_{i})
and
y(z_{j})
, are denoted by
\bm{\mu}
and
K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})
, respectively. There are various types of covariance functions are available, as discussed by
Zhang et al. 2023
. Their work compares the performance of different covariance functions in reconstructing cosmological data. In this study, we use the Squared Exponential covariance function, which is widely recognized and commonly applied in cosmology
(
Seikel et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
. Here,
[K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})]_{ij}=k(z_{i},z_{j})
,

k(z_{i},z_{j})=\sigma^{2}_{f}\exp{\left(-\frac{(z_{i},z_{j})^{2}}{2l^{2}}\right)}.

(8)

Here,
l
represents the length scale, and
\sigma_{f}
signifies the signal variance. Both are considered ’hyperparameters’ in Equation (
8
).

Similarly, we can generate a Gaussian vector
\bm{f^{\ast}}
at
\bm{Z^{\ast}}
:

\bm{f^{\ast}}\thicksim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu^{\ast}},K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z^{\ast}})).

(9)

The observed values of
\bm{y}
can be obtained from the data. We can then reconstruct
\bm{f^{\ast}}
(
\bm{\bar{f^{\ast}}}
and cov(
\bm{f^{\ast}}
)) by combining Equation (
7
) and (
9
) in the joint distribution
(
Seikel et al. 2012
)
:

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{y}\\
\bm{f^{\ast}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\thicksim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}\\
\bm{\mu^{\ast}}\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C&K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z^{\ast}})\\
K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z})&K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z^{\ast}})\\
\end{bmatrix}\right).

(10)

From Equation (
10
), we can derive
\bm{\bar{f^{\ast}}}
and cov(
\bm{f^{\ast}}
)
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
;
Seikel et al. 2012
)
:

\bm{\bar{f^{\ast}}}=\bm{\mu^{\ast}}+K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z})[K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C]^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}),

(11)

\begin{split}\operatorname{cov}(\bm{f^{\ast}})&=K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z})-K(\bm{Z^{\ast}},\bm{Z})[K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C]^{-1}\\
&\quad-K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z^{\ast}}).\end{split}

To reconstruct the functions in Equations (
11
) and (
III.1.2
), we need the hyperparameters values,
l
and
\sigma_{f}
, from Equation (
8
). These values are determined by maximizing the log marginal likelihood:

\begin{split}\ln\mathcal{L}&=-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu})^{T}[K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C]^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu})\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\ln|K(\bm{Z},\bm{Z})+C|-\frac{n}{2}\ln 2\pi.\end{split}

Following the steps outlined above, we can reconstruct the
H(z)
function using CC data and then derive the Hubble constant
\mathrm{H}_{0}
. We used the Gaussian process algorithm GAPP (Gaussian Processes in Python), as proposed by
Seikel et al. 2012
.

III.1.3
MAF

The Hubble constant
\mathrm{H}_{0}
can be estimated using CC data through neural networks. A reliable neural network estimator should balance both flexibility and tractability. Two families that embody both properties are autoregressive models
(
Uria et al. 2016
)
and normalizing flows
(
Jimenez Rezende and Mohamed 2015
)
. In this study, we use MAF, proposed by
Papamakarios et al. 2017
, to estimate
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from CC data. MAF encompasses both autoregressive models and normalizing flows. It stacks multiple Masked Autoencoders for Distribution Estimation (MADE)
(
Germain et al. 2015
)
in a normalizing flow to estimate parameters. Compared to the original MADE, MAF provides greater flexibility while maintaining tractability
(
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
.

MADE is a type of autoregressive model. To estimate the joint density
p(\bm{x})
, where
\bm{x}
is a D-dimensional vector,
p(\bm{x})
can be decomposed using the chain rule of probability:

p(\bm{x})=\prod_{i}^{D}p(x_{i}|\bm{x}_{1:i-1}),

(12)

where
\bm{x}_{1:i-1}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{i-1})^{T}
. The autoregressive density estimator models each density
p(x_{i}|\bm{x}_{1:i-1})

(
Uria et al. 2016
)
. By multiplying all of them according to Equation (
12
), we can derive the joint density
p(\bm{x})
. MADE has an advantage over straightforward recurrent autoregressive models: it can compute on parallel architectures by using binary masks to drop connections
(
Germain et al. 2015
)
. In this study, Equation (
12
) can be rewritten as:

p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}})=\prod_{i}^{D}p(\theta_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{1:i-1},\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}}).

(13)

Where
\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}}
represents the observed CC data from Table
1
. And
\bm{\theta}
denotes the parameters we aim to estimate from
\bm{H_{\mathrm{obs}}}
, such as
\mathrm{H}_{0},\Omega_{M},\Omega_{\Lambda}
.

The normalizing flow
(
Jimenez Rezende and Mohamed 2015
)
computes the density
p(\bm{x})
as:

p(\bm{x})=\pi_{u}(f^{-1}(\bm{x}))\left|\det{\left(\frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial\bm{x}}\right)}\right|.

(14)

Here,
\bm{x}=f(\bm{u})
, and
\bm{u}\sim\pi_{u}(\bm{u})
, where the base density
\pi_{u}(\bm{u})
is typically chosen as a standard Gaussian distribution. The functions
f
and
\pi_{u}(\bm{u})
are inverses of each other. To enhance the transformation of
f
, multiple instances can be composed, such as
f_{1}\circ f_{2}
. This composition still forms a valid normalizing flow
(
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
.

Since the conditionals of MADE are parameterized as single Gaussians, the
i^{th}
conditional is given by:

p(x_{i}|\bm{x}_{1:i-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_{1}|\mu_{i},(\exp{\alpha_{i}})^{2}).

(15)

Where
\mu_{i}=f_{\mu_{i}}(\bm{x}_{1:i-1})
and
\alpha_{i}=f_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x}_{1:i-1})
, they represent the mean and log standard deviation of
i^{th}
conditional, respectively. Data can be generated as follows:

x_{i}=u_{i}\exp{\alpha_{i}}+\mu_{i},

(16)

u_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Equation (
16
) can be represented as
\bm{x}=f(\bm{u})
. Since
f
is easily invertible, we can transform
\{\bm{x}_{n}\}
into
\{\bm{u}_{n}\}
. At this point, we have stacked multiple MADE models into a deeper flow, known as MAF
(
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
. MAF is trained by minimizing the following:

L=-\sum_{n}\ln P(\bm{\theta}_{n}|\bm{H}_{n}),

(17)

Here,
L
represents the negative log probability. In this paper, we generated the training data
\{\bm{\theta}_{n},\bm{H}_{n}\}
using the methods outlined by
Wang et al. 2021
and
Chen et al. 2023
.

III.2
Multiple random sampling analysis

In Subsec.
III.1
, three methods are introduced. Although these methods using the same CC dataset compiled in Table
1
, they yield the different constrained
\mathrm{H}_{0}
:
\mathrm{H}_{0}=67.75\pm 3.08\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for EMCEE,
\mathrm{H}_{0}=67.21\pm 4.72\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for GP, and
\mathrm{H}_{0}=66.25\pm 9.46\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
for MAF. To evaluate the impact of these methods on the constrained
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using CC data, we propose the MRSA. MRSA tests the sensitivity of each method in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
to individual
H(z)
data points by randomly removing several data points from the CC dataset. As the name suggests, the MRSA involves randomly sampling the CC data multiple times and constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
with each sample. Subsequently, we analyze the distribution of these
\mathrm{H}_{0}
values. The procedure of MRSA is illustrated in Fig.

,

\mathrm{H}_{0}
to individual
H(z)

\mathrm{H}_{0}

\mathrm{H}_{0}

. These results show that GP is more sensitive to individual

IV
Simulation

The
\mathrm{H}_{0}
constraint differs when using the CC dataset with three different methods: EMCEE, GP, and MAF. In Subsec.
III.2
, we compare their sensitivity in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
to individual
H(z)
values using MRSA. In this section, we focus on comparing the accuracy of
\mathrm{H}_{0}
constraints obtained by these three methods. First, we simulate a set of
H(z)
values with a prior
\mathrm{H}_{0}
(
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
) based on the observed CC data. Next, we apply each of the three methods to the simulated
H(z)
dataset to constrain
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
. The accuracy of each method is evaluated by comparing
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
to the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
, with greater accuracy indicated by
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
being closer to the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. To reduce the effect of random variations in each simulation, we generate 100 simulated datasets and compare the mean and mean square error of 100
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
values to the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. Additionally, to minimize the influence of the simulation approach, we employ two simulation methods: one based on the
\Lambda
CDM model and the other on the Gaussian Process.

IV.1
Simulation based on
\Lambda
CDM

To simulate
H(z)
values based on the observed CC data with the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
, we adopt and improve upon the simulation method from
Ma and Zhang 2011
. The simulated Hubble parameter,
H_{\mathrm{sim}}
, at redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
is

H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})=H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})+\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}}).

(20)

Here,
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
denotes the redshift corresponding to
H_{\mathrm{sim}}
.
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
indicates the value calculated from the fiducial model, while
\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
represents the simulated uncertainty at each redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
.
In Equation (
20
), in order to generate the
H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
dataset, we need to determine the values of redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
,
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
at each redshift.

First, to ensure the simulated dataset closely matches the observed CC dataset, we generate 33 values for
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
within the range [0, 2.5]. We maintain the same distribution density of
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
values as in the CC dataset.
Next, we compute
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
based on the
\Lambda
CDM model,

H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})=\mathrm{H}_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{M}(1+z_{\mathrm{sim}})^{3}+(1-\Omega_{M})}.

(21)

In Equation (
21
), to calculate
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
, we need the values of
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
. Here, we apply the
\mathrm{H}_{0}
prior and
\Omega_{M}
prior, denoted as
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
and
\Omega_{M\text{prior}}
, to estimate
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
in Equation (
21
). To determine appropriate values for
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
and
\Omega_{M\text{prior}}
, we constrain them using the observed CC data through EMCEE, obtaining
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}=67.75\pm 3.08\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
and
\Omega_{M\text{prior}}=0.328\pm 0.061
. For each data point at redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
, we draw random values of
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
from Gaussian distributions:
\mathrm{H}_{0}\thicksim\mathcal{N}(67.75,3.08)\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
and
\Omega_{M}\thicksim\mathcal{N}(0.328,0.061)
. Since each simulated dataset contains 33
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
values, we sample random
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
values 33 times. Using these sampled
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
values along with the corresponding redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
, we compute all 33 values of
H_{\mathrm{fid}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
using Equation (
21
).

Finally, we generate
\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
at each redshift
z_{\mathrm{sim}}
using the method proposed by
Ma and Zhang 2011
. The simulated
\sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}
is based on the
\sigma_{H}
of the observed CC data, as shown in Fig.

V
discussions and conclusions

The Hubble constant (
\mathrm{H}_{0}
) is crucial for understanding the evolution of the universe. However, a significant 5
\sigma
discrepancy exists between the two main methods for measuring
\mathrm{H}_{0}

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Riess et al. 2022
)
, known as the Hubble tension. Another approach to determining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is using Cosmic Chronometers, a model-independent method for measuring
H(z)
. The CC dataset is presented in Table
1
. This method may provide valuable insights into resolving the Hubble tension. Various methods can be used to constrain
H(z)
, including EMCEE, GP, and MAF, as introduced in Subsec.
III.1
. EMCEE is an affine-invariant ensemble sampler for MCMC, GP is a non-parametric method that provides a model-independent reconstruction of the Hubble parameter, and MAF is a deep learning-based approach capable of modeling complex distributions.

The
\mathrm{H}_{0}
results derived from these three methods using the same CC data differ noticeably. This discrepancy highlights the importance of understanding the strengths and limitations of each method in the context of cosmological data. Despite the potential of each technique, a comprehensive study comparing these methods for constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from
H(z)
is lacking. This study seeks to address this gap by comparing EMCEE, GP, and MAF methods for constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using
H(z)
data. By evaluating their performance, we aim to identify the most suitable method for different research contexts, ultimately leading to more accurate and reliable determinations of the Hubble constant and a deeper understanding of the universe’s expansion history. We compare these three methods by evaluating their sensitivity to individual
H(z)
data points and their accuracy in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using simulated
H(z)
datasets.

In Subsec.
III.2
, we propose MRSA to test the sensitivity of the three methods to individual
H(z)
data points. The MRSA method randomly selects 26 out of 33
H(z)
data points from the CC dataset to create a new dataset. To reduce the impact of random selection, we generate 1000 different datasets. We then constrain 1000
\mathrm{H}_{0}
values using these datasets with EMCEE, GP, and MAF, respectively. The distributions are shown in Fig.
2
, while the comparison results are summarized in Table
2
. Our findings indicate that the GP method is more sensitive to individual
H(z)
points than both MAF and EMCEE, likely because the
\Lambda
CDM model is applied in EMCEE and MAF. Additionally, MAF demonstrates greater sensitivity than EMCEE. This suggests that as more data points are obtained, the
\mathrm{H}_{0}
value constrained by GP will show greater variation compared to those from EMCEE and MAF. To investigate whether this sensitivity relates to the redshift range of the
H(z)
data points, we split the CC dataset into two groups and applied the MRSA to each group in Subsec.
III.3
. Our analysis reveals that all three methods (EMCEE, GP, and MAF) are sensitive to individual data points in both low and high redshift regions. Specifically, for EMCEE and GP,
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is more sensitive to higher redshift
H(z)
data points, while for MAF,
\mathrm{H}_{0}
is more sensitive to lower redshift
H(z)
data points. This difference may result from the inherent differences in the methods themselves, or it could simply be caused by the small number of data points in the CC dataset. Future research will further investigate this sensitivity across different redshift regions using a larger CC dataset, aiming to obtain more accurate results.

In Sec.
IV
, we test the accuracy of EMCEE, GP, and MAF in constraining
\mathrm{H}_{0}
using CC data by simulating the
H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
dataset with a prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. We then use each method to constrain
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
from the simulated dataset. The method that produces an
\mathrm{H}_{0}
closest to the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
is considered the most accurate. To reduce the influence of different simulation methods, we use two approaches: Simulation based on
\Lambda
CDM (Subsec.
IV.1
) and Simulation based on Gaussian Process (Subsec.
IV.2
). To reduce the impact of randomness in the simulated datasets, we generate 100 different
H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
datasets. For each simulated dataset, we apply all three methods to constrain
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
. We then compare the mean values of the 100 constrained
\mathrm{H_{0sim}}
results from each method to the prior
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{0prior}}
. The comparison results are presented in Table
4
and Table
5
. Our findings indicate that EMCEE is more accurate than MAF, and MAF is more accurate than GP, regardless of the simulation method.

By comparing the sensitivity and accuracy of EMCEE, GP, and MAF, we gain a deeper understanding into their respective features. This knowledge helps identify the most suitable method for a range of research applications, extending beyond cosmology to fields like medicine
(
Endo et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
)
and economics
(
Thomas 2022
;
Rostam et al. 2020
)
. However, this study has several limitations: (1) The accuracy comparison relies on two simulation approaches introduced in Subsec.
IV.1
and
IV.2
, which may not be universally applicable. (2) The accuracy results are derived from simulations using a prior
\mathrm{H}_{0}
, rather than actual observational data. (3) While we compared sensitivity and accuracy, we did not assess the overall characteristics of the methods. (4) The limited amount of
H(z)
data in the CC dataset may have affected our results.

Future research will focus on generating simulated
H_{\mathrm{sim}}(z_{\mathrm{sim}})
datasets using a wider variety of improved simulation methods. This approach will help minimize the impact of specific simulation methods on accuracy comparisons. Additionally, we will explore the characteristics of EMCEE, GP, and MAF in greater depth to gain a more comprehensive understanding of each method. By incorporating more
H(z)
data points into our analysis, we aim to achieve more reliable comparisons of the sensitivity and accuracy of these methods. This will result in more precise determinations of
\mathrm{H}_{0}
