Evaluating the effectiveness and safety of adjuvant transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation (TACE) after curative resection for Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage 0-B hepatocellular carcinoma: protocol for a target trial emulation study
Authors: Zhi Li, Shizheng Mi, Xinyan Li, Qiuping Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAccording to Chinese clinical practice guidelines, patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at high risk of recurrence after curative hepatectomy should have adjuvant transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation (TACE). However, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the European Association for the Study of the Liver guidelines take a more conservative stance on the routine use of adjuvant TACE. A recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) found no benefit of adjuvant TACE for patients with early-stage HCC. Given the potential ethical concerns, it is impractical to conduct another RCT. Therefore, we plan to use a target trial emulation (TTE) framework to estimate the effectiveness and safety of adjuvant TACE in patients with resectable HCC, classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage 0-B, with subgroup analyses to identify potential effect-modifying subpopulations.Method and analysisA TTE framework using the cloning-censoring-weighting approach with a 4-week grace period will be implemented. Eligible patients will be cloned into the adjuvant TACE and active surveillance groups and artificially censored on deviation from their assigned treatment strategy. Potential confounders will be identified using a directed acyclic graph and inverse probability of censoring weights will be applied to adjust for bias arising from artificial censoring. Subgroup analyses will be prespecified based on the potential sources of treatment-effect heterogeneity.Ethics and disseminationThe institutional review board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, approved this study (Approval No. 2025(1790)). Introduction

For early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), curative-intent hepatic resection or local ablation is associated with an expected survival beyond 5 years.1 Both Western and Eastern populations exhibit postoperative recurrence rates exceeding 60%, causing significant physical and psychological distress among patients.2 3 Sorafenib became the first systemic therapy for advanced HCC in 2008 and remained the first-line treatment for almost a decade until the introduction of Lenvatinib in 2018, after which treatment strategies for advanced HCC rapidly evolved.4 5 In the adjuvant sorafenib for HCC after resection or ablation trial, adding sorafenib after curative treatments does not reduce recurrence risk in patients with intermediate-to-high recurrence risk, suggesting that postoperative recurrence is driven by a complex and heterogeneous mechanism and that tyrosine kinase inhibitor monotherapy is insufficient for durable disease control.6 Moreover, combining immune checkpoint blockade (atezolizumab) with antivascular endothelial growth factor antibodies (bevacizumab) appears to confer a recurrence-free survival (RFS) advantage during the first 2 years of follow-up7; nevertheless, this advantage may not be sustained with longer follow-up, thereby compromising the overall effectiveness of this strategy.8

Transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation (TACE) remains a widely investigated and clinically applied adjuvant approach after curative resection, with high-level evidence suggesting benefit for patients at high risk of relapse.9–11 However, a recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) enrolling patients with American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage I–II HCC reported no recurrence-free or survival benefit of this strategy after curative resection,12 suggesting that TACE may be limited to being a standard postoperative treatment in early-stage HCC, consistent with National Comprehensive Cancer Network13 and European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL)14 guideline recommendations. The EASL clinical practice guidelines do not recommend routine adjuvant therapy following curative resection or ablation. RCTs provide high-level evidence by diminishing selection and confounding biases and enable estimation of the average treatment effect within a counterfactual framework. However, RCTs are conducted under highly selective eligibility criteria, often with limited sample sizes and substantial costs, and may raise ethical concerns in certain clinical settings. It is clear that designing another large-scale RCT on this topic is not feasible, given the low likelihood of benefit from adjuvant TACE in early-stage HCC, and the ethical concerns associated with randomising patients to potentially unnecessary treatment. In this scenario, we resort to a target trial emulation (TTE) framework to observational data, as recommended by Hernan and Robins.15

So far, a substantial number of retrospective cohort studies on this topic have been published, with controversial results.16–19 Causal inference from retrospective studies is inherently limited, and even results from large-sample and multicentre studies are subject to bias and therefore cannot be considered fully convincing. For instance, if the follow-up is initiated on the day of surgery, immortal time bias is introduced, since patients must survive the postoperative period and recover sufficiently to receive adjuvant TACE, whereas those with severe postoperative complications are unable to receive adjuvant TACE. Moreover, adjuvant TACE may be delayed in patients with impaired liver function after surgery, even when the treatment is planned, thereby introducing treatment-time and selection biases. Recently, a TTE study has shown that the advantage of adjuvant TACE may be overestimated.20 This study was well designed. Patients receiving neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatments other than adjuvant TACE were excluded. Time-zero (T0) and the grace period were explicitly specified. Intervention and primary endpoints were prespecified before data analysis. These design features mirror the key structures of a target trial, thereby strengthening causal inference. Although the results of this study appear more convincing than those of a conventional retrospective study, two methodological concerns warrant attention. Eligibility criteria in this study were defined as high-risk features for recurrence, including tumour size >5 cm, multifocal tumours, poor tumour differentiation or micro/macrovascular invasion, which were derived from postoperative pathology and therefore could not be ascertained at T0 (the day after surgery). On the other hand, treatment allocation should be defined at a time point when all patients have an equal opportunity to initiate the intervention. Defining T0 as the day after surgery may preclude patients with severe postoperative complications from receiving adjuvant TACE, potentially violating the positivity assumption and introducing immortal time bias. Moreover, both inverse probability of treatment weights and propensity score matching address confounding due to baseline treatment assignment, whereas some patients with poor liver function may face a delayed treatment initiation and treatment switching. To summarise, the role of adjuvant TACE in patients with resectable HCC remains unclear.

Objective

To conduct a TTE study to determine whether adjuvant TACE should be routinely performed in resectable HCC (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) 0-B) and, through subgroup analyses, to explore sub-populations that may derive greater benefit from adjuvant TACE.

Methods and analysis

Since this is an observational study based on the TTE framework, reporting will follow Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Transparent Reporting of Observational Studies Emulating a Target Trial (TARGET) guidelines.21 22 This study aims to emulate a hypothetical target trial in which eligible patients are randomly assigned to postoperative TACE within the predefined grace period or to active surveillance. We specified the protocol a priori according to the framework proposed by Hernan and Robins, including an explicit definition of seven core components before data analysis, namely ‘eligibility criteria; treatment strategies, treatment assignment, the start and end of follow-up, outcomes, causal contrasts, and analysis plan’.15 23 Accordingly, we will describe each of the seven components in detail (online supplemental table S1).

Treatment strategies

The treatment strategies in this study consist of postoperative adjuvant TACE versus active surveillance. The TACE procedure consists of selectively catheterising the tumour-feeding artery, followed by administration of a lipiodol (usually ranging from 3 to 5 mL) mixed with chemotherapeutic agents, typically anthracyclines such as epirubicin (20–40 mg/m2), doxorubicin (20–30 mg/m2) and/or platinum-based agents such as oxaliplatin (100–150 mg). The dosage of chemotherapeutic agents and lipiodol is individualised according to body surface area, liver function. Additional embolisation with gelatin sponge particles could be performed. During the study period, postoperative adjuvant TACE is predominantly performed using conventional TACE. DEB-TACE is not routinely used in the adjuvant treatment and, therefore, is not included in this study. Adjuvant TACE can be initiated in postoperative week 4 within a 4-week treatment window (postoperative weeks 4–8), as the decision to allocate TACE requires postoperative pathology. In the intervention arm, patients will be recommended to receive one cycle of TACE. In the active surveillance arm, patients will undergo regular imaging and serum tests. Imaging-based evidence will be based on abdominal ultrasound/liver contrast-enhanced ultrasound/contrast-enhanced abdominal CT/contrast-enhanced abdominal MRI. Tumour-marker-based evidence will be based on α-fetoprotein (AFP) or des-γ-carboxy prothrombin. Recurrence will be diagnosed with either two imaging-based findings or one imaging-based finding combined with one tumour-marker-based evidence.

Treatment assignment

TTE cannot fully achieve randomisation or blind assignment. As a pragmatic trial, patients and doctors are usually aware of the treatments at the time of treatment allocation, and even RCT in this field are usually open-label. Time zero of this study is defined as 4 weeks after surgery. A grace period of 4 weeks is allowed for the initiation of adjuvant TACE (postoperative weeks 4–8) to account for delayed treatment. Treatment exposure is based on TACE medical records. To address deviations from treatment assignment, the cloning-censoring-weighting (CCW) approach will be applied.24 Inverse probability weights will then be constructed to account for informative censoring (figure 1).

Follow-up and outcomes

Every patient was routinely advised to have outpatient follow-up at 1 month and 3 months after hepatic resection. Thereafter, follow-ups were scheduled at 3-month intervals for the first 24 months and semiannually thereafter until 1 February 2025. Assessment included physical examination, liver function, tumour marker and imaging tests, which served as evidence of tumour recurrence as defined above.

The primary outcome is RFS, defined as the time from T0 to tumour recurrence or all-cause death. The secondary outcomes are overall survival (OS) and adverse events. OS refers to the time from T0 to death from any cause. Adverse events are documented according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (V.5.0) with severity from grade 1 (mild) to grade 5 (death). TACE-related adverse events occurring within 30 days will be recorded and graded.

Causal contrast

Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol (PP) effects represent the causal effects in randomised trials. However, this is an observational study, and treatment assignment at T0 is inaccessible from prescription records. Therefore, we actually compare the effects of the treatment strategies specified in the study protocol, rather than the effects of being assigned to the treatment strategies at baseline. To summarise, the causal contrast of interest of this study is the PP effect of adjuvant TACE versus active surveillance on RFS, expressed as HRs.

Analysis plan

Sample size estimation

The sample size is calculated using Schoenfeld’s method implemented in R (V.4.6.0).25 We adopt a two-sided hypothesis and calculate the sample size under the assumption of a clinical trial. One study reported that patients with BCLC 0-A who underwent curative resection have a median RFS of 40.7–42.8 months.26 Based on a meta-analysis, we hypothesise that adjuvant TACE could reduce the risk of recurrence by 30%, corresponding to an HR of 0.70.27 Patients are allocated in a 1:1 ratio to two arms. Assuming a two-sided significance with α=0.05 and a power of 80%, at least 247 recurrence events are required. The study is expected to enrol patients over a 1-year accrual period, followed by at least a 3-year follow-up for each individual. After accounting for 10% non-response rate, the required sample for each group is at least 300, for a total of 600 patients.

Missing data handling

If the proportion of missing data across all variables is less than 5%, a complete-case analysis will be conducted. If the proportion of missing data exceeds 5%, it will be assumed to be missing at random and processed using multiple imputation by chained equations. The imputation model will include all baseline covariates, treatment assignment and outcome variables. Five imputed datasets will be generated, and CCW analysis will be performed separately within each imputed dataset. Estimates will thereafter be combined using Rubin’s rules.

Implementation of Cloning-Censoring-Weighting

The CCW approach will be applied to the primary analysis under three core assumptions: stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA); ignorability assumption; positivity assumption. The SUTVA comprises two components: no inference across cases and consistency in treatment definition. In our study, one patient’s treatment does not influence another. All patients in the TACE group receive a standardised chemotherapy regimen, ensuring consistency in treatment definition. To satisfy the ignorability assumption, we will adjust for an extensive range of covariates, including baseline characteristics, preoperative and postoperative blood tests, intraoperative variables, tumour pathological features and postoperative complications. We define T0 as 4 weeks after surgery with a 4-week grace period for treatment implementation. We restrict the eligible populations to patients with BCLC stage 0-B to ensure adequate overlap in the probability of receiving or not receiving adjuvant TACE.

At time zero, each eligible patient is cloned into two protocol-compliant copies and allocated to adjuvant TACE or active surveillance. Copies deviating from their assignment are artificially censored. To address selection bias introduced by artificial censoring, inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) were estimated via logistic regression. Covariates are selected before analysis, identified through a directed acyclic graph (figure 2); no data-driven variable selection is applied. Stabilised weights will be truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme values. Covariate balance is evaluated by standardised mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD <0.1 considered to indicate sufficient balance. Since treatment allocation and artificial censoring happen within a relatively short period, only time-fixed confounders are included in the weighting model. Finally, covariates included in the IPCW model will be age, sex, Hepatitis B virus infection, liver cirrhosis, liver function (Albumin-Bilirubin grade), tumour size, tumour number, serum AFP level, capsule invasion, microvascular invasion, satellite nodules, tumour differentiation, postoperative complication occurring within 4 weeks after surgery and extent of hepatectomy (major vs minor).

Directed acyclic graphs used to identify the minimally sufficient adjustment set for estimating the effect of adjuvant TACE on tumour recurrence. AFP, α-fetoprotein; ALBI, Albumin-Bilirubin; HBV, hepatitis B virus; TACE, transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation.

Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

Four sensitivity analyses will be carried out to evaluate the robustness of our findings. First, we will render an 8-week grace period after T0 for adjuvant TACE administration. Second, propensity scores will be estimated based on prespecified covariates and overlap weighting will be applied to control for confounding and achieve covariate balance between treatment and control groups. Third, a 4-week landmark analysis combined with overlap weighting will be performed as a sensitivity analysis to further address potential immortal time bias. Finally, we will apply Cox proportional hazard regression to evaluate whether the treatment advantage is statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses will be performed according to BCLC stage (0-A vs B), largest tumour size (<5 cm vs ≥5 cm), microvascular invasion (absence vs presence), resection margin (<1 mm vs ≥1 mm) and satellite nodules (absence vs presence). Both relative and absolute treatment effects will be reported for each subgroup. Relative treatment effects will be expressed as HRs and 95% CI. While absolute treatment effects will be quantified as differences in recurrence-free probabilities at 1, 3 and 5 years. Heterogeneous treatment effects will be evaluated by including treatment-by-subgroup interactions in weighted Cox regression models, and the interaction p value will be reported.

Discussion

This TTE study will focus on the effectiveness and safety of adjuvant TACE in patients with HCC at BCLC 0-B stage. Furthermore, a trade-off analysis will be conducted to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from adjuvant TACE. In China, adjuvant TACE has been widely adopted as a standard adjuvant therapy for patients at high risk of recurrence. Nevertheless, this recommendation has not been endorsed by Western guidelines. With regard to patients with low risk of recurrence, the implementation of adjuvant TACE varies substantially across centres, even within the same city,28 reflecting the ongoing lack of consensus on this topic. Recent evidence in patients with early-stage (AJCC stage I–II) HCC has called into question the benefit of adjuvant TACE.12 Given these considerations, designing another large-scale RCT may raise ethical concerns. Hence, we aim to emulate a target trial using observational data to evaluate the advantage of adjuvant TACE in real-world practice.

By applying the CCW framework, we seek to mitigate immortal-time bias, confounding and treatment-delay bias. Four sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the results. Our study is expected to guide adjuvant treatment decisions for patients with BCLC stage 0-B to identify optimal subgroups. The results will be reported at the conference and published in peer-reviewed publications.
