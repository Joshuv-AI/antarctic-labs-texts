Androgynous identity predicts body appreciation in Japanese sport science majors: examining the roles of sport type and competitive level
Authors: Wenjing Cheng, Masashi Kawanishi, Katsumi Mori, Rie Yamada, Tomoaki Seki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated predictors of body appreciation among Japanese sport science majors, focusing on the roles of androgynous identity, sport type, and competitive level within a culturally distinct context. A sample of 240 university students (170 male, 70 female) completed the Bem Sex-Role Inventory and the Body Appreciation Scale-2. Results indicated that androgynous individuals reported the highest body appreciation, significantly exceeding other gender role groups, while undifferentiated individuals scored the lowest. Male participants exhibited higher body appreciation than females across all roles. A significant interaction revealed female team-sport athletes had lower body appreciation than those in individual or hybrid sports, a difference not observed in males. Regression analyses showed national medalists had higher body appreciation, but competitive level did not moderate the gender role effects. Our conclusions underscore the protective role of an androgynous identity in Japan’s gendered sports culture and highlight unique pressures faced by female team-sport athletes, informing targeted interventions.

Background

Sports have traditionally functioned as a masculinised domain [43], reinforcing conventional gender norms, whilst simultaneously providing space for their contestation [11, 28]. In Japan, this tension is shaped by multiple socialisation agents, including early childhood gender socialisation in which boys are encouraged to play outdoors whilst girls are directed toward indoor activities [20] and by a highly structured educational sports system. The school sports system forms one of the foundational pillars (alongside corporate leagues) of Japan’s organised structure. This centralised system, guided by government policy and national sports organisations, aims to develop both elite athletes and promote lifelong community participation [47]. Through physical education classes and extracurricular sports club activities, named bukatsudo (intramural sports clubs) in middle and high school and taiikukai or sakuru (sports club or circle activities) in university, school sports provide the primary pathway for youth competitive sports participation and athlete development. Recent data reveals declining but still high participation rates in bukatsudo. According to the Sasakawa Sports Foundation ([48], p. 43), the participation rate for middle school boys was 64.1% in 2023, compared to 49.8% for middle school girls, whilst the participation rate for high school boys was 52.1% versus 34.4% for girls.

However, Japan’s physical education curriculum has been criticised for its strong emphasis on competitive sports and triumphalism [19]. From basic movement and play during early years, the curriculum has been increasingly focusing on athletic events such as ball games, track and field, and swimming. Extracurricular sports club activities intensify this competitive orientation, accentuating male superiority whilst marginalising female participants, reflecting broader gender dynamics within Japanese sports. These socialising influences collectively prescribe distinct body ideals for men and women [41]. Women are expected to embody kawaii (cuteness) through thinness and delicacy [23, 26, 39], whilst men are pressured to demonstrate otoko-rashisa (masculinity) via muscularity or lean athleticism [54, 62]. The Japanese educational sports system, particularly the organised extracurricular sports club activities, represents a significant institutional context in which these gendered ideals are reinforced through structured training and competitive frameworks [36]. Previous studies have also highlighted the role of coaches, PE teachers, and other sports instructors in shaping students’ gender knowledge, training environments, and understandings of appropriate sporting behaviour: gender perceptions may be transmitted through everyday coaching practices, expectations regarding athletic ability, and evaluations of athletes’ bodies and conduct [24, 35, 55, 56]. However, relatively little attention has been paid to how these socialisation processes contribute to the formation of sex role identity amongst Japanese student athletes.

Within this broader context of Japanese sports socialisation, body appreciation, defined as respecting and valuing one’s body despite imperfections [57], may be shaped by the negotiation of gender identities across multiple social spheres, with organised sports participation representing one important influence, amongst many.

Gender roles and body image: the protective role of androgyny

Gender role identity, including masculinity, femininity, androgynous and undifferentiated [4, 5, 50, 51], significantly influences body perception. Masculinity (instrumentality) is linked to body positivity in Western studies [13], whilst femininity (expressivity) correlates with thinness pressures [49]. However, androgynous individuals, who integrate both traits, demonstrate greater psychological flexibility [60] and may better reconcile athletic functionality with societal beauty standards [44]. In Japan, where conformity to gendered norms is pronounced [52], androgyny could serve as a protective factor against body dissatisfaction by allowing athletes to navigate conflicting expectations.

Gender role orientation significantly predicts sports participation patterns. Sex-typed individuals (those strongly identifying with traditional masculinity or femininity) tend to select gender-appropriate sports [28, 46], whilst androgynous individuals exhibit greater flexibility [34]. For instance, masculine-typed males often avoid sports perceived as feminine (such as figure skating), whereas feminine-typed females may shy away from traditionally masculine sports (such as rugby) [18]. These choices are reinforced by gendered discourses in sports environments [2], in which coaches and peers may implicitly or explicitly police gender boundaries. Notably, gender role stereotypes also influence motivation, with androgynous athletes reporting higher self-esteem and body satisfaction across sport types [6, 14]. However, this protective effect has not been empirically tested in Japanese athletic populations.

Sport type, competitive level and their moderating roles

Participation in sports has generally been associated with positive body image [17, 45]; however, this relationship is multifaced and moderated by sport type and cultural context [15]. Previous research, primarily conducted in Western contexts, has suggested that involvement in team sports promotes body appreciation by fostering social support, a sense of belonging, and a focus on functional mastery [7]. Conversely, individual sports, particularly aesthetic-focused ones such as gymnastics or figure skating, are often linked to negative body evaluation due to the intense pressure to meet sport-specific lean standards [16, 61, 63].

Despite these findings, there is reason to hypothesize a different trend among Japanese female athletes. In Japan, the societal ideal of femininity has been shaped by cultural concepts such as Kawaii (cuteness), which emphasizes youthfulness, slenderness, delicacy, and passivity, and Bosei (motherhood), which is associated with nurturance, self-sacrifice, modesty, and emotional support [23, 26, 39]. These cultural traits directly contradict the muscularity, power, and aggression necessitated by team sports such as soccer, basketball, or baseball, disciplines that have historically been perceived as masculine domains. Furthermore, the collectivist nature of the Japanese sports environment, particularly within the Bukatsudo (extracurricular sports club) culture, exerts intense docho-atsuryoku (conformity pressure) and emphasizes Wa (harmony). In such environments, individual identity is often subordinated to collective image of the team. Consequently, female athletes in team sports must navigate a profound conflict between their functional athletic bodies and rigid societal expectations of being a feminine woman. This dual burden of social comparison and gender-role strain is theoretically more taxing in a collective team setting where peer scrutiny is constant than in individual sports, where athletes may experience relatively higher autonomy in defining their physical identity. This gender-body identity gap is manifested in lower body appreciation among female team sport participants [29], a possibility that has received limited empirical attention, especially in the Japanese context.

Competitive level further moderates these relationships. Elite athletes, regardless of gender role, often internalize performance-driven body ideals that diverge from conventional societal beauty standards [63]. However, the intersection of gender roles, sport types and cultural contexts remains poorly understood, highlighting the need for culturally nuanced investigations [59].

Current study and hypotheses

This study examines how androgynous identity, sport type and competitive level interact to predict body appreciation amongst Japanese sport science majors. We hypothesise:

H1: Androgynous students will report higher body appreciation than sex-typed (masculine/feminine) and undifferentiated peers.

H2: Female team-sport students will show lower body appreciation than those involved in individual/hybrid sports.

H3: Higher competitive level correlates with greater body appreciation, independent of gender role.

Methodology

Procedure

An online survey was created using Google Forms, with its URL and corresponding QR code distributed to students via classroom announcements, institutional e-learning platforms, and physical handout by four professors specialising in sports sciences at three Japanese universities representing northern, central, and southern regions. Recruitment was conducted during their courses (Lifelong Sports, Sports Law, and Sports Social Science), as well as at a sports-focused social science seminar. Information regarding the purpose of the study, confidentiality protections, and the right to withdraw were presented on the opening webpage, with only those agreeing to participate completing the entire survey. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the University’s Institutional Review Board. Data was collected from October to December 2024, followed by validation checks in January 2025, leading to the exclusion of 26 cases due to extreme outliers (|z| > 3). Because filling all questionnaire items was mandatory, there was no missing data in the final qualified sample.

Participants

A total of 240 undergraduate sport science majors (170 male, 70 female), aged 16 to 22 years (M = 19.12, SD = 1.08), participated in the study. The sample includes 106 first-year, 54 s-year, 73 third-year, and 7 fourth-year students. All participants had extensive prior involvement in organised sports through extracurricular sports club activities, such as intramural sports clubs in school (bukatsudo in middle and high school), sports club activities (taiikukai in university), junior sports clubs and/or club teams. In answer to the question on educational stage, 230 reported that they had trained during elementary school, 231 during middle school, 235 during high school, and 183 at the university level. 90.4% (n = 217) had formal competitive experience ranging from prefectural to national competitions in Japan. Participants reported involvement in 32 sports, including modern sports such as soccer, baseball, sprinting, basketball, and volleyball and traditional Japanese sports such as Judo and Kendo.

Measurement

Demographics

The demographic section included items on sex (male, female, other), age (in years), academic grade, birthplace and sports experience, including specific discipline, affiliation, training duration, and competitive level. Each participant had engaged in at least one sport, and their primary sport was determined, for the purpose of this study, based on either their highest competitive level or their longest cumulative duration of participation within a single discipline. Accordingly, the competitive level was defined as the highest tier of formal competition in which they had taken part. Sport types were categorised according to their structure as follows: team sports (involving two or more interdependent players, such as soccer and basketball), individual sports (performed solo, such as swimming, Judo, and Kendo) and hybrid sports (which can be played individually or collaboratively as doubles or teams, such as tennis and badminton).

Gender role identity

Gender role identities were assessed using the Japanese-adapted version of the Bem Sex-Role Inventory (BSRI; [4]), following validation and application studies in Japanese contexts by Sugihara and Katsurada [52] and Adachi et al. [1]. Although BSRI was originally developed in 1974, its theoretical framework and measurement approach have been continuously re-examined, adapted, and applied in later studies across different cultural contexts, including studies in North American, European, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese research [1, 3, 4, 12, 30, 32, 52]. These subsequent studies indicate that the BSRI is not a dated instrument, but a historically established framework through which changes in masculinity, femininity, and androgyny can be examined across time and culture.

This 60-item measure consists of three subscales: a masculinity subscale (20 items: including Assertive, Willing to take risks, and Has leadership abilities), a femininity subscale (20 items: including Compassionate, Understanding, and Tender) and a neutral subscale (20 items: including Happy, Friendly, and Unsystematic), all of which were reviewed by three native Japanese Sport Science professors to ensure linguistic and contextual appropriateness. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90 for masculinity, 0.81 for femininity). Following Bem’s original scale design, mean scores for masculinity and femininity items were calculated separately and used to classify respondents into four sex role categories using median splits (Mdn = 3.5 for both masculine and feminine subscales): Androgynous (high M/high F), Masculine (high M/low F), Feminine (low M/high F), and Undifferentiated (low M/low F). Neutral items were included to reduce the overt gendered nature of the questionnaire and to minimize response bias; accordingly, all 60 items were presented in randomised order.

Body appreciation

The Japanese adaptation of the Body Appreciation Scale-2 (BAS-2; [38]) was employed to assess positive body image. This 10-item measure uses a 5-point response format (1 = never to 5 = always), with total scores ranging from 10 to 50. The Japanese version has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with particular cultural relevance for items emphasising body functionality (for example, My body deserves to be valued, I feel good about my body). Cronbach’s α of the BAS-2 in this study was 0.91.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The body appreciation (BAS-2) scores of the participants varied by gender, gender role category, competitive level, and sports type, as summarised in Table 1. An independent-samples t-test revealed that male participants reported significantly higher body appreciation scores (M = 37.14, SD = 7.21) than female participants (M = 33.79, SD = 9.12), t (102.56) = 2.59, p = .11, Cohen’s d = 0.41. A two‑way ANOVA (Gender × Gender Role) and yielded a significant main effect of gender role, F (3, 232) = 21.35, p < .001, η² = 0.22 (large effect), with post‑hoc tests indicating that androgynous individuals scored highest on body appreciation (M = 41.23, SD = 6.67), significantly higher than all other groups (all ps ≤ 0.002). The feminine and masculine groups scored similarly and were both significantly higher than the undifferentiated group. Detailed ANOVA results are presented in Table 2.

For competitive level, one‑way ANOVAs by gender revealed a significant effect only for female participants, F (4, 65) = 6.65, p < .001, η² = 0.29. Female national medallists (M = 41.81, SD = 6.73) scored significantly higher than national competitors (M = 33.87, SD = 8.50, p = .028), regional competitors (M = 30.36, SD = 9.47, p = .008), and prefectural‑level competitors (M = 25.30, SD = 8.72, p < .001). The difference between prefectural‑level competitors and national competitors approached significance (p = .054). Male participants showed no significant differences across the competitive levels, F (2, 167) = 0.29, p = .752, η² = 0.003.

Regarding sport type, one‑way ANOVAs by gender again showed a significant effect only for female participants, F (2, 67) = 4.52, p = .014, η² = 0.12 (medium effect). Female team‑sport participants reported markedly lower body appreciation (M = 29.46, SD = 9.57) than those in individual sports (M = 36.17, SD = 9.01, p = .018). Male participants demonstrated no meaningful differences across the sport types, F (2, 167) = 0.23, p = .797, η² = 0.003. (see Table 3 for full details).

Androgyny’s main effects on body appreciation

A 2 (sex: male/female) × 4 (gender role: masculine/feminine/androgynous/undifferentiated) between-subjects ANOVA was conducted on body appreciation scores (Table 2), revealing significant main effects for both gender role (F [3, 232] = 21.35, p < .001, η² = 0.216) and sex (F [1, 232] = 7.15, p = .008, η² = 0.030), with no significant interaction (F < 1). Post-hoc Tukey tests revealed that the androgynous group scored significantly higher than all other groups (all ps ≤ 0.002). The Feminine and masculine group did not differ from each other (p = .995), but both scored significantly higher than the undifferentiated group (feminine vs. undifferentiated: p = .021; masculine vs. undifferentiated: p = .054, marginal).

Detrimental effect of team sports on body appreciation in females

A one-way ANOVA (Table 3) revealed female team-sport participants exhibiting markedly lower body appreciation (M = 29.46, SD = 9.57) than those in individual (M = 36.17, SD = 9.01; p = .018, d = 0.73) or hybrid sports (M = 37.00, SD = 9.51; p = .014, d = 0.82). The difference between team and hybrid sports (M = 37.00, SD = 9.51) approached significance (p = .098), while individual and hybrid sports did not differ (p = .969). Males showed no differences across sport types (F < 1), yielding a significant sex × sport type interaction (F [2,234] = 4.52, p = .014, η² = 0.119), confirmed that the effect of sport type depended on gender.

Competitive level effects: elite athletes’ advantage and non-significant moderation

The relation between competitive level and body appreciation was examined through hierarchical regression analyses assessing both the main effects and potential interactions with gender role traits (Table 4). The analysis followed a structured three-step approach for both the masculinity and femininity dimensions, beginning with control variables (biological sex and BMI), followed by the addition of main effects (gender trait scores and competitive level), before finally incorporating interaction terms between gender traits and competitive level. All continuous predictors were mean-centred to facilitate interpretation of the interaction effects to reduce multicollinearity concerns, with the competitive levels dummy-coded using non-competitive athletes as the reference group.

The results revealed distinct patterns for masculine and feminine traits in relation to body appreciation. Initial models demonstrated a robust positive association between masculinity traits and body appreciation (β = 0.43, p < .001), though this relation became non-significant when interaction terms were introduced, likely due to multicollinearity issues (VIF > 10) arising from the inclusion of product terms. For femininity traits, national medallists showed consistently higher body appreciation scores (β = 0.28, p < .01) regardless of their femininity level, whilst other competitive levels showed no significant effects. A marginally significant interaction emerged between femininity and national participation (β = 0.18, p = .07), suggesting a potential amplification effect worthy of further investigation with larger samples.

Several consistent patterns emerged across all models. Female participants reported significantly lower body appreciation than their male counterparts (β range = -0.18 to -0.24, ps < .01), aligning with previous research [8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 22, 37] on gender differences in body image amongst athletes. BMI showed no significant associations with body appreciation in any model, indicating that weight status did not influence these relationships. The masculinity model accounted for 26% of the variance in body appreciation scores (ΔR² = 0.22 for main effects), whilst the femininity model explained slightly more variance at 30% (ΔR² = 0.26). Notably, interaction terms contributed minimally to both models (ΔR² = 0.01), indicating that the competitive level did not substantially moderate the relation between gender traits and body appreciation.

Discussion

This study examined how androgynous identity, sport type (team structure), and competitive level interact to predict body appreciation amongst Japanese sport science majors.

Androgynous identity as cultural buffer

Consistent with global literature [9, 13, 22], androgynous individuals reported the highest body appreciation in this Japanese sample, significantly outperforming sex-typed (masculine/feminine) and undifferentiated peers. This finding supports Hypothesis 1 and extends Bem’s androgyny theory to Japanese athletic contexts, where rigid gender norms [23, 40, 41] make the integration of instrumental and expressive traits particularly adaptive. Notably, undifferentiated individuals reported the lowest levels of body appreciation, echoing previous findings suggesting that a lack of gender-role traits correlate with poor self-perception [31, 37]. Across all gender-role categories, men consistently reported higher body appreciation than women, which aligns with well-established gender disparities in body image research [58].

The absence of a significant sex × gender role interaction suggests that the psychological benefits associated with androgyny operate similarly for both men and women within this athletic population. In this sense, androgyny may function as a cultural buffer, enabling individuals to flexibly integrate socially valued traits across gender norms and thereby maintain more positive attitudes toward their bodies. At the same time, it is important to consider the gender connotations associated with different types of sports. Previous studies have shown that sports can be categorised as masculine, feminine, or gender-neutral [27, 28], participation in sports perceived as incongruent with one’s biological sex may generate additional psychological pressures, and under such circumstances, androgyny identity may help individuals reconcile competing gender expectations and maintain positive body evaluations. However, for male athletes participating in traditionally masculine sports, where gender-role congruence is already high, the additional contribution of androgyny may be less pronounced.

Team sports and gendered body pressures

Consistent with Hypothesis 2, our female participants involved in team sports reported significantly lower body appreciation than those engaged in individual or hybrid sports, whereas no such difference was observed amongst males. This finding highlights the importance of considering sport-specific gender norms when interpreting body appreciation amongst female athletes. This pattern may be partly understood by considering the gender connotations associated with the specific sports represented in the present sample. Female participants in this study engaged in team sports such as basketball, baseball, soccer, handball, and volleyball. According to the gender-typing classification proposed by Koivula ([27], p. 551), these sports are perceived as masculine. Participation in such gender-incongruent sports may create tension for female athletes, as they are required to develop strength, and muscularity for performance, while societal ideals of femininity in Japan continues to emphasize thinness and delicacy.

Such tensions have historical roots in the cultural interpretation of female athletic bodies. For example, media discourse surrounding Kinue Hitomi, Japan’s first female Olympic medalist, frequently emphasised the episode in which she lay face down after the race, the so-called “Utsubuse episode”. In contrast to the face-up posture of athletes from other countries, Hitomi’s posture was interpreted as a sign of modesty and feminine vulnerability expected of Japanese women, despite her remarkable athletic achievements, this media framing illustrated how the femininity of female athletes has historically been interpreted through gendered cultural expectations [53]. Within this context, female athletes may experience ongoing pressures to reconcile athletic functionality with socially constructed ideals of femininity [25].

In addition, the collectivist structure of Japanese team sports, which often emphasizes coordination, discipline, and group conformity, may intensify peer comparison among teammates [21, 42], Although such environments can foster social support, they may also increase awareness of body differences among athletes. As a result, female athletes whose bodies are shaped for performance may become more vulnerable to body dissatisfaction when their physiques diverge from conventional feminine norms.

Elite performance and functional body ideals

Our results shows that national medallists had higher body appreciation than their non-competitive peers, partially supporting Hypothesis 3. This aligns with the focus of elite athletes on body functionality over aesthetics [10, 33, 45]. However, competitive level did not moderate gender role effects, suggesting that androgyny’s benefits are robust across performance tiers. Intriguingly, femininity traits positively predicted body appreciation for medallists (β = 0.28), suggesting that elite female athletes may integrate competence (masculinity) with self-acceptance, a potential cultural nuance warranting further research.

These findings suggest that whilst elite competitive achievement may independently contribute to enhanced body appreciation, it does not appear to significantly alter the fundamental relationships between gender role traits and body image in this population. The consistently observed gender role differences underscore the persistent influence of sex on body appreciation in athletic contexts. The lack of significant moderation effects implies that the benefits of androgynous gender roles for body appreciation may be relatively stable across different competitive achievement levels, although the marginal interaction effect observed for femininity at national competition levels warrants further exploration. The analytical challenges posed by multicollinearity within the interaction models highlight the complexity of examining these interrelated constructs, suggesting the need for alternative approaches when undertaking future research.

Conclusion

This study examined how androgynous identity, sport type, and competitive level jointly relate to body appreciation among Japanese sport science majors. The findings demonstrated that our participants with androgynous gender-role identities reported the highest levels of body appreciation, supporting the idea that gender-role flexibility functions as a psychological resource in contexts characterised by strong gender norms. At the same time, sport context appears to shape body-related experiences differently for men and women. Female athletes who participated in team sports—many of which are culturally perceived as masculine—reported lower body appreciation than those in individual or hybrid sports, suggesting that gendered expectations surrounding athletic bodies may influence body image in sport settings. Although elite competitive achievement was associated with higher body appreciation, competitive level did not substantially alter the relationship between gender-role traits and body image. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering both gender-role identity and sport context when examining body appreciation amongst athletes.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, sport gender-typing was used as a theoretical framework rather than as a formal statistical variable. Although Koivula’s classification [27] provided a useful basis for interpreting sport-related gender meanings, the gender connotations of sports may shift across cultural and historical contexts. Future research should directly examine how contemporary Japanese athletes perceive the gender associations of different sports.

Second, measurement-related limitations should be considered. Although the BSRI remains useful for comparison with prior studies, some items originally developed in 1974 may not fully reflect contemporary understandings of gender. In addition, the present study did not assess media exposure, despite the growing role of news media and social media in shaping gender-role perceptions and body image. Future research should further validate or update sex-role measures for contemporary Japanese athletic populations and examine how media-based gender norms interact with sex-role identity and body appreciation.

Third, the analysis focused on the primary sport of the participants, defined as the sport with the highest competitive level or the longest duration of participation. This approach does not fully account for the potential influence of multi-sport participation, which may expose athletes to different social environments, forms of social support, and bodily competencies. Moreover, some subgroup sample sizes, particularly those of hybrid-sport athletes, were relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power of certain analyses.

Finally, cross-cultural research is needed to determine whether the protective role of androgynous identity and the vulnerabilities observed among female team-sport athletes represent broader patterns across cultural contexts or are shaped specifically by the Japanese socio-cultural environment.
