Mature Cystic Teratoma of the Ovary Transforms into Squamous Cell Carcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Chul Hoi Jeong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMature cystic teratomas (MCTs) are benign ovarian tumors known to undergo malignant transformation in 1%–2% of cases. Most of these cases lead to squamous cell carcinomas (SCC), and the prognosis is often poor, particularly in advanced-stage disease. A 50-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with a large pelvic mass. The patient initially underwent suboptimal primary surgery and was later diagnosed with stage IIIA SCC arising from MCT. Following the first cycle of chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, a computed tomography scan showed a rapidly growing large mass in the pelvic cavity after 4 weeks of primary surgery, leading to a second debulking surgery. However, the patient underwent only one cycle of chemotherapy after secondary debulking surgery because of her poor performance status. Subsequently, after 4 months of secondary debulking surgery, the patient developed rapidly progressive disease, leading to her death approximately after 8 months of diagnosis. While an appropriate multimodal treatment strategy has yet to be established, optimal cytoreductive surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy should be considered for treatment of advanced-stage diseases to improve survival outcomes.Keywords: Ovarian neoplasm, Squamous cell carcinoma, Teratoma

INTRODUCTION

Mature cystic teratomas (MCTs) are almost always benign; however, may transform into malignant tumors in 1%–2% of cases [1,2,3,4]. Most cases of malignant transformation result in squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), which has a poor prognosis, especially at an advanced stage [4]. Due to the absence of an established therapeutic protocol for advanced-stage diseases with poor prognosis, research on the early detection of malignancy and clinical treatment may potentially contribute to improved patient outcomes.

We present a case of advanced-stage SCC with a very poor prognosis that evolved from MCT of the ovary in a postmenopausal patient.

CASE REPORT

A 50-year-old multiparous postmenopausal woman who experienced lower abdominal pain, dyspepsia, and anorexia during the previous 3 months was referred to our hospital and was found to have a large pelvic mass. Her medical and family history was insignificant.

Physical examination revealed significant abdominal distension, with a mass extending from the Douglas pouch 5 cm above the umbilicus. The serum levels of tumor markers were as follows: CA125, 866 U/mL (normal range < 35.0); CA19-9, 887 U/mL (normal range < 35.0). SCC Ag measurement, Pap smear and HPV test was not performed. Abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) revealed a pelvic cystic mass containing solid components measuring 17 × 18 cm, along with abnormal ascites, peritoneal thickening, enhancement, and omental infiltration, suggesting the presence of peritoneal and omental seeding (Fig. 1).

Abdominopelvic computed tomography showed about 17 × 18 cm fat containing huge pelvic tumor with abnormal ascites and peritoneal thickening.

An exploratory laparotomy was performed through a midline incision extending above the umbilicus. We discovered that the large left ovarian mass had severely adhered to the lateral surface of the uterus, large intestine, and omentum. Tumor dissemination was observed in the left fallopian tube, peritoneum, posterolateral surface of the uterus, and bladder dome. The patient underwent a total abdominal hysterectomy with a bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Optimal cytoreductive surgery was not performed due to the severe adhesions.

Macroscopically, the left ovarian tumor measured 17.3 × 15.5 × 6.1 cm and weighed approximately 841.5 g. The external surface exhibited focal roughness. Dissection revealed a yellow fluid with matted hair and solid tissue (Fig. 2). Microscopically, the tumor displayed extensive keratinization, which is characteristic of well-differentiated SCC arising from MCT (Fig. 3). The lymph nodes were free of malignancy. The patient was diagnosed with stage IIIA2 SCC based on the 2014 FIGO classification.

Macroscopic appearance of left ovarian tumor shows yellow color fluid with matted hairs and solid component.

Photomicrograph shows well differentiated squamous cell carcinoma characterized by extensive keratinization (Hematoxylin & Eosin, magnification ×100).

Following the first cycle of chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, a CT scan revealed a rapidly growing large mass in the pelvic cavity 4 weeks after the primary surgery (Fig. 4), resulting in a second debulking surgery. However, the patient underwent only one cycle of chemotherapy after the secondary debulking surgery due to her poor performance status, thereby receiving conservative care thereafter. Subsequently at 4 months after the secondary debulking surgery, the patient developed rapidly progressive disease, leading to her demise approximately 8 months after diagnosis.

At 4 weeks after primary surgery abdominopelvic computed tomography showed about 8 × 6 cm bulging mass with peripheral rim enhancement along the left common iliac vessel and anterior to the left iliopsoas muscle.

DISCUSSION

Malignant transformation occurs in 1%–2% of MCT cases [1,2,3,4]. The most common type of malignant transformation is from MCT to SCC [1,5]. MCTs occurring during menopause or in patients older than 45 years are more likely to undergo malignant transformation [6]. The most common signs and symptoms include abdominal pain and distention, and a palpable abdominal or pelvic mass [5]. In this case, the patient complained of abdominal pain, distension, anorexia, dyspepsia, and cachexia prior to diagnosis. MCTs with SCC are usually larger than benign teratomas. Tumors with diameters larger than 9.9 cm or those exhibiting rapid growth may be correlated with an increased risk of malignant transformation [7,8]. The combination of patient age (≥ 40 years) and serum SCC antigen levels (≥ 2.5 ng/mL) (sensitivity, 77%; specificity, 96%) may serve as a useful tool for the diagnosis of malignant transformation [1]. Elevated serum CA125 and SCC Ag levels are often associated with an adverse prognosis [5]. My patient exhibited an elevated CA125 level (866 U/mL); however, SCC Ag measurement was not performed. The clinical use of magnetic resonance imaging and CT for preoperative diagnosis of SCC arising from an MCT in the ovary remains unclear [3,9].

SCCs from MCTs of the ovary are often associated with a considerably poorer prognosis than typical epithelial ovarian cancers [7,10] because the prognosis of patients with advanced-stage SCC arising from MCTs is very poor. Kikkawa et al. [7] reported 5-year survival rates of 94.7% and 80% for patients with stage I and II diseases, respectively, and 0% for patients with stage III and IV diseases. Chen et al. [8] analyzed 188 cases with surgical staging and indicated that the overall 5-year survival rate for all stages was 48.4%. The 5-year survival rates for each stage were as follows: stage I: 75.7%, stage II: 33.8%, stage III: 20.6%, stage IV: 0%. The strongest prognostic factors for SCC arising from MCTs are tumor stage and cytoreductive status [5,7,8]. Other prognostic factors include capsular invasion, ascites, rupture, tumor dissemination, and adhesion [11]. My patient exhibited poor prognostic factors, including advanced stage, capsular invasion, ascites, tumor dissemination and adhesion., which led to the rapidly progressive disease.

Patients with SCC arising from an MCT of the ovary should undergo comprehensive surgical staging to develop an appropriate treatment plan. After comprehensive surgical staging, patients with stage Ia disease can be treated conservatively, whereas those with advanced-stage disease should be treated with optimal cytoreductive surgery to improve survival outcomes [5,7]. Although the optimal adjuvant chemotherapy for advanced-stage disease has not yet been established, the commonly used adjuvant chemotherapy regimens are platinum and taxane [3,8,10,12].

It was reported that there was a case of complete response to weekly paclitaxel-carboplatin chemotherapy as a second-line treatment in a patient with an advanced disease that was resistant to primary cisplatin-based chemotherapy [13]. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that combination chemotherapy, including carboplatin, paclitaxel, and bevacizumab, achieved a complete response in a recently reported case of early recurrence following optimal surgery [14]. In cases of rapidly progressing disease similar to my patient’s, this chemotherapy regimen could serve as an alternative chemotherapy option. However, the effect of adjuvant radiotherapy remains unclear [5,8,15].

Although the pathophysiology of malignant transformation from mature cystic teratoma.is still uncertain, High-risk human papilloma virus infection, which leads to alteration in p53 and p16, could serve as a causative factor of malignant transformation of MCT [15]. Unfortunately, the department of pathology at our hospital did not perform immunohistochemistry using relevant antibodies in this rare case. In such cases, it is believed that more active tests should be conducted to identify pathophysiological causes.

In conclusion, SCC arising from an MCT of the ovary is a rare disease with poor prognosis. In postmenopausal patients or those older than 45 years with large ovarian teratomas (≥ 10 cm in diameter), malignant transformation should be considered. Additionally, measurement of the SCC antigen levels could aid in diagnosis. While an appropriate multimodal treatment strategy has yet to be established, optimal cytoreductive surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy should be considered for the treatment of advanced-stage disease to improve survival outcomes. A multicenter collaborative study is warranted to establish an optimal multimodal treatment strategy.
