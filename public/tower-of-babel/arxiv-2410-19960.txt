Shape Derivatives of the Eigenvalues of the de Rham Complex for Lipschitz deformations and variable coefficients: Part I Thanks: Corresponding Author : Dirk Pauly
Pier Domenico Lamberti, Dirk Pauly, Michele Zaccaron

Abstract
. We study eigenvalue problems for the de Rham complex on varying three dimensional domains. Our analysis includes the Helmholtz equation as well as the Maxwell system with mixed boundary conditions and non-constant coefficients. We provide Hadamard-type formulas for the shape derivatives under weak regularity assumptions on the domain and its perturbations. Our proofs are based on abstract results adapted to varying Hilbert complexes. As a bypass product of our analysis we give a proof of the celebrated Helmann-Feynman theorem both for simple and multiple eigenvalues of suitable families of self-adjoint operators in Hilbert space depending on possibly infinite dimensional parameters. This series of papers consists of Parts I and II.

Introduction

The analysis of the dependence of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of elliptic operators upon variation of the underlying domain is a classical problem considered in many papers in the literature with applications in approximation, optimization, homogenization, control theory and mathematical physics. It is impossible to give an account of all contributions in the literature and we refer to the monograph

The main aim of the present series of papers is to relax those regularity assumptions gaining one degree of smoothness and to provide a unified approach including both the Helmholz equation and the Maxwell system. This is done by analyzing the corresponding de Rham complex and its domain perturbations. A further contribution of our papers consists in the fact that we consider nonconstant physical parameters such as the electric permittivity

1.1.
Eigenvalues of the De Rham Complex

We shall consider the Dirichlet Maxwell eigenvalue problem

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon^{-1}\rot\mu^{-1}\rot E&=\lambda_{1}E&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
\mathrm{n}\times E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma.\end{aligned}

The corresponding Neumann Maxwell eigenvalue problem reads

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon^{-1}\rot\mu^{-1}\rot E&=\lambda_{1}E&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
\mathrm{n}\times\mu^{-1}\rot E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma.\end{aligned}

Note that any solution of (
1
) or (
2
) automatically satisfies
\divergence\varepsilon E=0
in
\Omega
,
and that in (
1
) and (
2
) we have additional

\mathrm{n}\cdot\rot E=0
and
\mathrm{n}\cdot\varepsilon E=0
on
\Gamma
, respectively.

We investigate also mixed boundary conditions, i.e.,
the Maxwell eigenvalue problem with mixed Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon^{-1}\rot\mu^{-1}\rot E&=\lambda_{1}E&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
\mathrm{n}\times E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!t},\\
\mathrm{n}\times\mu^{-1}\rot E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!n},\end{aligned}

where
\Gamma
is decomposed into two relatively open subsets

\emptyset\subset\Gamma_{\!t}\subset\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{\!n}:=\Gamma\setminus\overline{\Gamma_{\!t}}
.
Note that again we have
\divergence\varepsilon E=0
and
\mathrm{n}\cdot\rot E|_{\Gamma_{\!t}}=0
and
\mathrm{n}\cdot\varepsilon E|_{\Gamma_{\!n}}=0
.

Moreover, we shall discuss the full spectrum of the de Rham complex.
Hence, we also investigate the scalar Laplacian and its dual, i.e.,

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon\na u&=\lambda_{0}u&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
u&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!t},\\
\mathrm{n}\cdot\varepsilon\na u&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!n},\end{aligned}

and

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\na\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon H&=\lambda_{0}H&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon H&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!t},\\
\mathrm{n}\cdot\varepsilon H&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!n}.\end{aligned}

As in (
3
) it holds
\rot H=0
and
\mathrm{n}\times H|_{\Gamma_{\!t}}=0
but only

\int_{\Gamma}\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon H=0
if
\Gamma_{\!n}=\Gamma
.

In view of (
3
) and (
5
) we shall also discuss
the generalised vector Laplacian

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\varepsilon^{-1}\rot\mu^{-1}\rot-\na\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon)E&=\lambda_{0,1}E&\text{in }&\Omega,\qquad\lambda_{0,1}\in\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\},\\
\mathrm{n}\times E=0,\quad\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!t},\\
\mathrm{n}\times\mu^{-1}\rot E=0,\quad\mathrm{n}\cdot\varepsilon E&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!n}.\end{aligned}

Note that for
\varepsilon
,
\mu
, and
\nu
being the identity mappings we have

\varepsilon^{-1}\rot\mu^{-1}\rot-\na\nu^{-1}\divergence\varepsilon=\rot\rot-\na\divergence=-\vec{\Delta}.

1.2.
Shape Derivatives of Eigenvalues

We intend to study variations of the domain and the boundary conditions
by replacing
\Omega
and the boundary parts
\Gamma_{\!t}
,
\Gamma_{\!n}
with

\Omega_{\Phi}:=\Phi(\Omega),\qquad\Gamma_{\Phi}:=\Phi(\Gamma),\qquad\Gamma_{\!t,\Phi}:=\Phi(\Gamma_{\!t}),\qquad\Gamma_{\!n,\Phi}:=\Phi(\Gamma_{\!n}),

respectively, where

\Phi:\Omega\to\Omega_{\Phi}

is a bi-Lipschitz transformation.
In particular, for
\ell\in\{0,1\}
,
we are interested in the variations of the eigenvalues

0<\lambda_{\ell,1}(\Phi)\leq\lambda_{\ell,2}(\Phi)\leq\dots<\lambda_{\ell,k-1}(\Phi)\leq\lambda_{\ell,k}(\Phi)\leq\dots\to\infty

in the domain
\Omega_{\Phi}
and their elementary symmetric functions
with respect to changing transformations
\Phi
.

For simplicity, assume here that
\varepsilon
,
\mu
are the identity matrices and that
\nu=1
.
Let
\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)
and
\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)
be eigenvalues
with eigenvectors
u
and
E

of (
4
) and (
3
), respectively. As is well-known these eigenvalues can be written by means of Rayleigh quotients as

(7)

\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)=\frac{|\na u|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}}{|u|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)=\frac{|\rot E|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}}{|E|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}}.

In particular, assuming that the eigenvectors are normalized in
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})
we have

\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)=|\na u|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2},\qquad\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)=|\rot E|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}.

Then also the dual eigenvectors

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{0,k}^{-1/2}(\Phi)\na u,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{1,k}^{-1/2}(\Phi)\rot E

are
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})
-normalised eigenvectors
of (
5
) and the respective dual of (
3
). Moreover, we have the dualities

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{0,k}^{-1/2}(\Phi)\divergence H,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1,k}^{-1/2}(\Phi)\rot B,

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,\Phi}

\displaystyle=|\divergence H|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,\Phi}

\displaystyle=|\rot B|_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}^{2}.

Note that the dual of (
3
) reads

(8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mu^{-1}\rot\varepsilon^{-1}\rot B&=\lambda_{1}B&\text{in }&\Omega,\\
\mathrm{n}\times\varepsilon^{-1}\rot B&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!t},\\
\mathrm{n}\times B&=0&\text{on }&\Gamma_{\!n},\end{aligned}

which incorporates also the conditions
\divergence\mu B=0
and
\mathrm{n}\cdot\mu B|_{\Gamma_{\!t}}=0
and
\mathrm{n}\cdot\rot B|_{\Gamma_{\!n}}=0
.

In this paper, among other results, we prove Hadamard type formulas for the directional derivatives of the maps

\Phi\mapsto\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi)
for
l\in\{0,1\}
.
This means that, given a fixed direction
\widetilde{\Psi}
in the space of Lipschitz transformations
\Phi
, we compute the limit

(9)

\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi+h\widetilde{\Psi})-\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi)}{h}.

Recall

\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi)=\lambda_{l,k}^{\prime}(\Phi)\widetilde{\Psi}
.
As customary, it is convenient to consider
\widetilde{\Psi}
as the pull-back of a transformation
\Psi
defined on
\Omega_{\Phi}
, that is

\widetilde{\Psi}=\Psi\circ\Phi
, and to express the formulas for the derivatives as volume or boundary integrals on
\Omega_{\Phi}
.
At a formal level, assuming
the differentiability of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors with respect to
\Phi

(which might fail for multiple eigenvalues, cf. Part II), the Hellman-Feynman Theorem
allows to obtain the formulas for
\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{l,k}(\Phi)
by differentiating the Rayleigh quotients (
7
)
with respect to
\Phi
(in direction
\widetilde{\Psi}
and keeping fixed the eigenvectors involved). By doing so, we obtain

(10a)

\displaystyle-\frac{\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)}{\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)}

\displaystyle=\big\langle(\symtr\na\Psi)H,H\big\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}+\big\langle(\divergence\Psi)u,u\big\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})},

(10b)

\displaystyle\frac{\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)}{\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)}

\displaystyle=\big\langle(\symtr\na\Psi)B,B\big\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})}+\big\langle(\symtr\na\Psi)E,E\big\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega_{\Phi})},

cf. (
31
) and Part II, where

\symtr\na\Psi:=2\sym\na\Psi-\tr\na\Psi=\na\Psi+(\na\Psi)^{\top}-\divergence\Psi.

Then, under more regularity assumptions on eigenvectors,
it is possible to integrate by parts and write these formulas
by means of surface integrals as follows

(11a)

\displaystyle\frac{\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)}{\lambda_{0,k}(\Phi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\!n,\Phi}}\big(|H|^{2}-|u|^{2}\big)\Psi\cdot n\,d\sigma-\int_{\Gamma_{\!t,\Phi}}\big(|H|^{2}-|u|^{2}\big)\Psi\cdot n\,d\sigma,

(11b)

\displaystyle\frac{\p_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)}{\lambda_{1,k}(\Phi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\!n,\Phi}}\big(|B|^{2}-|E|^{2}\big)\Psi\cdot n\,d\sigma-\int_{\Gamma_{\!t,\Phi}}\big(|B|^{2}-|E|^{2}\big)\Psi\cdot n\,d\sigma,

see Part II.
These computations are quite involved.

Note that formula (
11a
) is well known at least for non-mixed boundary conditions, cf. e.g.
[
20
,
23
]
,
and formula (
11b
) has been recently proved in
[
21
,
40
]
for sufficiently regular perturbations. Formula (
11b
) was found in a heuristic way in
[
16
]
with
\Gamma_{n}=\emptyset
for arbitrary
\varepsilon
and
\mu
and another interesting equivalent formula was proved in
[
17
]
, see also
[
41
]
.

It is important to observe that the left and right derivatives in (
9
)
, i.e.,
h\to 0^{\mp}
,
coincide if the eigenvalue under consideration is simple, while they might be different if the eigenvalue is multiple (the difference corresponds to the choice of different eigenvectors in the formulas).
This phenomenon is well-known for many eigenvalue problems associated with families of self-adjoint operators depending on some parameters. It is also well-known that for perturbations depending on one scalar parameter, it is possible to apply the Rellich theorem
and relabel the eigenvalues in order to guarantee their differentiability. On the other hand it was proved in
[
20
]
that the elementary symmetric functions of the eigenvalues which bifurcate at a multiple eigenvalue are differentiable no matter whether the parameter involved is one dimensional or not.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Sobolev Spaces and Boundary Conditions

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
We define (for scalar, vector, or tensor fields)

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{k}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\psi|_{\Omega}\,:\,\psi\in\mathsf{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\;\supp\psi\text{ compact},\;\dist(\supp\psi,\Gamma_{\!t})>0\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{0,1}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\psi|_{\Omega}\,:\,\psi\in\mathsf{C}^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\;\supp\psi\text{ compact},\;\dist(\supp\psi,\Gamma_{\!t})>0\big\}.

Recall that
\Gamma_{t}
is a relatively open subset of
\Gamma
.
Note that
\mathsf{C}^{k}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)
and
\mathsf{C}^{0,1}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)

are often denoted by
\mathsf{C}^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\mathsf{C}^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
, respectively.
With the Lebesgue space
\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
we have the standard Sobolev spaces in the weak sense

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\p^{\alpha}\psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\;\;\forall\,|\alpha|\leq k\big\}

\displaystyle\;=\big\{\psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,|\alpha|\leq k\;\;\exists\Psi_{\alpha}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\;\;\forall\,\theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\langle\psi,\p^{\alpha}\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\langle\psi_{\alpha},\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\rot\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\}

\displaystyle\;=\big\{\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\exists\Psi_{\rot}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\;\;\forall\,\Theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\rot\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\Psi_{\rot},\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\divergence\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\}

\displaystyle\;=\big\{\Psi\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\exists\psi_{\divergence}\in\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\;\;\forall\,\theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\na\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=-\langle\psi_{\divergence},\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}.

Note that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)
are the well known spaces

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}(\rot,\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}(\divergence,\Omega)
, respectively.
We introduce boundary conditions in the strong sense by

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{k}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)}.

By standard Friedrichs’ mollification we observe

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{1}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{0,1}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{0,1}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{0,1}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)}^{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)}.

Also boundary conditions in the weak sense are introduced by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,|\alpha|\leq k\;\;\forall\,\theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!n}}(\Omega)\quad\langle\psi,\p^{\alpha}\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\langle\p^{\alpha},\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,\Theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!n}}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\rot\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\rot\Psi,\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,\theta\in\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\Gamma_{\!n}}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\na\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=-\langle\divergence\Psi,\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}.

Note that for
\Gamma_{\!t}=\emptyset
we have

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega).

2.2.
Weak and Strong Boundary Conditions Coincide

For full boundary conditions there is a simple result
that ‘weak equals strong’ holds without any additional assumptions
in a certain sense (the test fields can be chosen from a possibly larger space).
Unfortunately, the proof does not allow for mixed boundary conditions.

Lemma 2.1

(weak equals strong for full boundary conditions)
.

It holds

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}^{1}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\big\{\psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,\Theta\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)\quad\langle\psi,\divergence\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=-\langle\na\psi,\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\big\{\Psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,\Theta\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\rot\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\rot\Psi,\Theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\big\{\Psi\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)\,:\,\forall\,\theta\in\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\quad\langle\Psi,\na\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=-\langle\divergence\Psi,\theta\rangle_{\mathsf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}.

See the appendix for a proof. For a definition of the segment property used in the following lemma we refer to
[
1
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(weak equals strong for no boundary conditions/density of smooth fields)
.

Let
\Omega
have additionally the segment property. Then

\mathsf{H}^{k}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{R}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{D}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega).

In other words
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\mathsf{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is dense in

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega)
, respectively.

Proof.

The proof for
\mathsf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
can be found, e.g., in Agmon
[
2
]
or in Wloka
[
39
, Theorem 3.6]
,
and it literally carries over to
\mathsf{R}(\Omega)
and
\mathsf{D}(\Omega)

as the mollifiers work similarly for
\rot
and
\divergence
.
The result for
\mathsf{H}^{k}(\Omega)
follows by induction.
∎

In case of Lemma
2.2
, we set

\mathsf{H}^{k}(\Omega):=\mathsf{H}^{k}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{R}(\Omega):=\mathsf{R}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{D}(\Omega):=\mathsf{D}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}(\Omega).

Lemma 2.3

(weak equals strong for full boundary conditions)
.

Let
\Omega
have additionally the segment property. Then

\mathsf{H}^{k}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}_{\Gamma}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{\Gamma}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{D}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{\Gamma}(\Omega).

Proof.

This follows by the same technique used in the proof of Lemma
2.1

in combination with the density results from Lemma
2.2
, e.g.,

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}(\Omega)=\mathsf{R}(\Omega)=\mathsf{R}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)=\overline{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}_{\emptyset}(\Omega)}^{\mathsf{R}(\Omega)}
.
∎

For mixed boundary conditions, i.e.,
\emptyset\neq\Gamma_{\!t}\neq\Gamma
,
the question ‘weak equals strong’ is more delicate. The equality can be proved under the assumption that
\Omega
has a Lipschitz boundary in the weak sense and
\Gamma_{t}
has a relative boundary in
\Gamma
which is also Lipschitz in the weak sense. In particular,
\Gamma
is a Lipschitz manifold of codimension one in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the relative boundary of
\Gamma_{t}
in
\Gamma
is a Lipschitz submanifold of codimension one in
\Gamma
. In this case we say that
(\Omega,\Gamma_{\!t})
is a weak Lipschitz pair.
Recall that usually “Lipschitz in the weak sense” means that the open set can be locally flattened near the boundary by means of a Lipschitz diffeomorphism. This condition is weaker than “Lipschitz in the strong sense” in which case the open set can be locally represented near boundary as a subgraph of a Lipschitz function.

A proof of the following lemma and the precise definition of a weak Lipschitz pair
can be found in
[
5
]
or
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2.4

(weak equals strong for mixed boundary consitions)
.

Let
(\Omega,\Gamma_{\!t})
be additionally a weak Lipschitz pair. Then

\mathsf{H}^{k}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}^{k}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{R}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega),\qquad\mathsf{D}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}_{\Gamma_{\!t}}(\Omega).

2.3.
The Transformation Theorem

Let
\Phi\in\mathsf{C}^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be such that

\Phi:\Omega\to\Phi(\Omega)=\Omega_{\Phi}

is bi-Lipschitz, and regular, i.e.,

\Phi\in\mathsf{C}^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega},\overline{\Omega}_{\Phi})
and
\Phi^{-1}\in\mathsf{C}^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}_{\Phi},\overline{\Omega})
with
2
2

2

Note that the Jacobian determinant of a bi-Lipschitz diffeomorphism has a constant sign on the connected components of the domain, see
[
36
