Feasibility and acceptability of e-learning training about diabetes and insulin usage to Peruvian healthcare professionals
Authors: Ana Maria Sandoval Barrantes, Ruth Iguiniz Romero, Isabel Amemiya Hoshi, Patricia Busta-Flores, Victoria Cavero, Liliana Hidalgo-Padilla, Mauricio Toyama, Jacqueline Dueñas Quispe, María Lazo Porras, Janeth Tenorio Mucha, María Karina Fernandez Urteaga, Jessica Hanae Zafra-Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInsulin is fundamental for the management of diabetes. In Peru, limited training among healthcare professionals is an important barrier to insulin prescription and usage. Given the advantages of e-learning such as time flexibility, low cost and penetration, the Peruvian government launched an e-learning training on diabetes and insulin usage for healthcare professionals working in public facilities in December 2021. This study aims to assess the feasibility and acceptability of this initiative.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study with a sequential explanatory design to assess feasibility and acceptability of the e-learning training. We included all enrolled participants regardless of whether they completed the course. First, quantitative data were collected through online surveys. Then, qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews. The data were analysed separately, but the results were interpreted together to triangulate findings.ResultsThis course reached 536 healthcare professionals at the national level, from which 338 (63.1%) completed the course. 304 (56.7%) of the participants lived outside Lima, the capital of Peru, and 285 (53.2%) worked in a primary care facility. Participants manifested high satisfaction with the course content and its applicability. However, some barriers to participation were found. Chiefly, family burden, workload, poor internet connection and the lack of protected training hours.ConclusionThe study highlights the feasibility of using e-learning to train healthcare professionals, particularly in underserved areas, offering flexibility and broad access. Some barriers need to be considered for future interventions that prevent dropouts.

Introduction

Diabetes affects over 500 million people globally1, and in Peru, over 1 million people are living with diabetes according to a report in 2021.2 3 Insulin is an important component of diabetes management. However, people living with diabetes in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) face several barriers to access insulin. These barriers include healthcare professionals’ limited training in insulin usage, which limits their technical capacity to prescribe insulin and educate on how to use it.4 5

In Peru, these challenges are compounded by a fragmented healthcare system. Diabetes management relies on a limited number of specialists based at hospitals in urban areas, whereas primary care level is mainly focused on prevention and detection.6 These healthcare professionals have little to no training in insulin prescription.4 5 As a result, difficulties remained in the ongoing management of diabetes and delays in access to treatment. It represents additional out-of-pocket expenditures for patients who need to go to higher complexity-level hospitals to continue their treatment.4 6 For these reasons, in 2021, the Peruvian Ministry of Health (MoH) aimed to decentralise diabetes management and insulin prescription and decided to train healthcare professionals in the primary and secondary care levels on insulin prescription through e-learning training.

E-learning training involves using internet-based resources to learn and teach at any place and time, with standard information for all participants.7 This has become increasingly popular in healthcare education because it has been proven as effective as traditional learning8 and has advantages such as time flexibility, low cost and the potential of faster dissemination of up-to-date knowledge.9 10 This has been previously used for different topics related to diabetes care11–14 and has showed improved clinical outcomes.14 15 Due to the widespread presence of health professionals throughout the Peruvian territory,16 e-learning training was hypothesised to be able to reach more professionals in different regions of the country in a short time.

Our research aimed to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the e-learning training on diabetes and insulin usage for healthcare professionals working in public facilities. We conducted a mixed-methods study with a quantitative and a qualitative component. The quantitative component had the following aims: to (1) describe course participation and dropouts and explore associated sociodemographic variables, (2) describe the course’s applicability to their daily routine and their satisfaction with contents among those who completed it, and (3) describe barriers of those who were enrolled and did not start the course. The qualitative component aimed to identify and understand barriers and facilitators to taking the course as well as areas of improvement.

Materials and methods

Study design

We used an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to integrate quantitative and qualitative data, as it allows us to enrich our understanding of the research question by describing the feasibility and acceptability of the training programme and exploring the barriers and facilitators experienced by participants.17 The quantitative component was an observational study, while the qualitative component followed a qualitative descriptive approach. Both quantitative and qualitative data were analysed separately, but the results were interpreted together to triangulate findings. Triangulation was carried out through three meetings between the qualitative and quantitative teams to present and discuss the results. Additionally, written reports were circulated for feedback.

Study setting

From 29 November 2021 to 21 December 2021, the Peruvian MoH and the National School of Public Health (or Escuela Nacional de Salud Pública (ENSAP) in Spanish) with the support of CRONICAS Centre of Excellence in Chronic Diseases from the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia implemented an e-learning training on diabetes and insulin usage for healthcare professionals. ENSAP is the academic branch of the MoH in Peru and is in charge of developing capacities among healthcare professionals.18

Course development and implementation were led by the MoH and ENSAP, while the feasibility and acceptability of the course was assessed by the CRONICAS team as part of the Addressing the Challenge and Constraints of Insulin Sources and Supply (ACCISS)19 study. The goal of the evaluation was to provide recommendations for future training.

This course was announced on the ENSAP webpage as part of the optional training courses provided to Peruvian healthcare professionals. ENSAP personnel were available for technical support if needed.

Quantitative component

Participants and study size

This course was open to physicians and nurses working at any public healthcare facility. Participants were able to enrol in the e-training through the ENSAP webpage, and they could access the course through an online platform. The course was tailored for professionals who provided treatment to people living with diabetes. For the study, all participants who enrolled in the course were included.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by ENSAP as part of their internal assessment. Participants were informed that their data were anonymised and used as part of the course assessment. Different methods were used to collect information.

The number of people who enrolled in the course, did not start the course, stopped participating in course activities (after having started the course) and finished the course were obtained directly from the online platform.

At the start of the course, all participants filled out a registration form for socio-demographic data through the course platform. The variables included socio-demographic variables such as age, sex, profession, healthcare facility including the name and healthcare level and location of the healthcare facility where the participant worked (name of the province where they were based) (online supplemental file 1).

Additionally, three online questionnaire (Google Forms) surveys were conducted through the ENSAP platform at the end of the course. The participants were asked to answer two surveys: (1) satisfaction with the course content and learning platform (online supplemental file 2) and (2) applicability of the course content 6 months after the training (online supplemental file 3). The satisfaction questionnaire used a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. For the applicability questionnaire, a 5-point Likert scale was used, with options ranging from ‘completely applicable’ to ‘not applicable’. The variables collected were the applicability to their daily routine after 6 months and satisfaction with the course content, learning resources, self-efficacy and use of the platform.

Those who enrolled but did not start the course were asked to fill a survey on barriers and facilitators to take the course (online supplemental file 4).

The three questionnaire surveys were conducted from 1 August 2022 to 31 October 2022.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the socio-demographic variables, course participation, usability of the platform and satisfaction with the course was carried out using measures of central tendency and dispersion to describe the numerical variables of the study according to data distribution. Frequency and percentages were used for categorical variables. χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests were used to explore which socio-demographic variables were associated with finishing the course and obtaining a certificate. Data were analysed using Stata V.15.

Qualitative component

Participants and study size

The qualitative component was conducted after course completion and aimed to complement the information found in the quantitative component, through in-depth interviews (IDI). IDIs were performed with participants living in four regions in Peru: Cajamarca (Northern Andean City), Loreto (Amazon City), Tumbes (Coastal City) and Ayacucho (Southern Andean City). These four regions were selected based on a previous research collaboration with the ACCISS study.

Participants were sampled from the course attendants’ registry. Purposive sampling according to the profession and healthcare level was conducted. We expected to reach four doctors and four nurses for primary care facilities, four doctors and four nurses for secondary care facilities and one coordinator per region. The participants were selected regardless of course completion. Interviews with participants who did not finish the course focused mainly on the barriers they faced to conduct the training. Qualitative researchers contacted potential participants via phone call and invited them to participate. The final number of interviews was determined by data saturation.

Data collection

IDIs were conducted by telephone to understand the perceptions regarding the course contents and motivations to take it, as well as barriers and facilitators of the e-learning training. Data were collected based on an interview guide containing three themes nominated by the framework developed by Ali et al: pedagogy, individual and technology17 (online supplemental file 5). The interviews lasted around 35 min on average and were audio-recorded for later analysis. IDI data were collected from 1 January 2022 to 30 May 2022.

Data analysis

Three researchers used a coding matrix spreadsheet to summarise and analyse the data (online supplemental file 6). Coding matrix categories were based on the framework developed by Ali et al to explore e-learning implementation success barriers (table 1).

Reflexivity

IDIs were conducted at the end of the virtual course by three members of the research team who worked on the ACCISS study. The interviewers were psychologists, two females and one male with >5 years of experience in qualitative research. The interviewers and interviewees did not meet before the interview, and they had no relationship with each other. The interviewers did not participate in the development or implementation of the course.

Data for the quantitative component were collected and analysed by the ENSAP team as part of their regular course assessments. Course participants agreed to have their data used for this kind of assessment when signing up for the course. As for the qualitative component, participants were invited to participate in interviews, and an informed consent procedure was conducted before the beginning of the interview. The audios were stored at a server from the university and destroyed after the matrices were filled.

For the qualitative component, a total of 25 IDIs were conducted. The median age of the participants was 36 years, 17 interviewees (68%) were women, 14 (56%) were nurses and 14 (56%) did not complete the course. According to the workplace, 17 interviewees (68%) worked at the primary care level at the time of taking the course, 7 (28%) worked at the secondary care level and 1 (4%) worked at the regional department of health. According to the region where they worked, five participants were interviewed from Ayacucho, 11 from Cajamarca, three from Loreto and six from Tumbes.

Applicability of the e-learning course

Participants who finished the course were surveyed about the applicability of the course content to their daily routine after 6 months of the course. A total of 63 participants were surveyed, most of them lived in Lima (n=21, 33.3%). The median age was 44 years (IQR 37–54), and most participants were female physicians working in a primary care facility.

The profession of the participants were categorised, and the results showed that most doctors considered that the course learnings could be used largely (n=22, 55%) or completely (n=8, 20%) during their daily routine. By contrast, nurses considered the course to be partly (n=7, 30.4%) and largely (n=9, 39.1%) applicable. According to the healthcare facility where they work, second-level care facility workers considered the course more applicable to their daily work than primary care employees. When conducting the IDIs, the reasons for the lack of applicability of the course were reported to be the low number of patients with diabetes receiving insulin and the lack of availability of supplies for insulin application.

Assessment of the framework by Ali et al as part of the qualitative component revealed that some individual characteristics were related to the course uptake. Healthcare workers’ prior knowledge about diabetes and student motivation were relevant facilitators for taking the course. Motivation came from the following: need to stay updated linked to their role as coordinator of a diabetes programme, interest in the topic of insulin usage because this topic was not learnt during undergraduate studies, and relevance for daily work.

We are generally not very trained in this as general practitioners, because a specialist in endocrinology usually indicates insulin therapy. (Physician from Ayacucho)

Participants also mentioned that e-learning improved accessibility to training courses, especially in remote areas or where other courses were not available, flexibility and time savings. In addition, they found that online modality was the safest option during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Support provided for participants during the training

Regarding the administrative support provided by the workplace to carry out the course, few participants (n=9, 14.3%) considered that they had barely received support or had not received at all. Conversely, others stated that they had received little support (n=16, 25.4%), somewhat support (n=25, 39.7%) and strong support (n=13, 20.6%).

Regarding the support of colleagues to carry out the course, some participants (n=13, 20.6%) considered that they had barely received support or had not received at all, whereas others (n=7, 11.1%) stated that they had received little support, mainly due to the work overload of colleagues or because they have not been trained. By contrast, more than half of participants indicated that they had received somewhat (n=35, 55.6%) or strong support (n=8, 12.7%) from colleagues.

When triangulating with the qualitative component, support was found to play an important role in the course uptake. For example, participants mentioned time limitations to taking the course, as well as problems with technology such as access to electricity and a good internet connection. Almost all interviewees, regardless of whether they completed the course or not, indicated that they had the necessary conditions to carry out the course. Half of the participants took the course exclusively in their free time, whereas the other half used their working hours. Some participants stated that the health facility was not an appropriate space because of workload and time constraints. Other difficulties reported by the interviewees that limited the time spent on the course were family commitments, forgetting the dates of the sessions, tiredness after work and time constraints because of the holiday season or other additional work, as well as a lack of support from the health department or facility.

Satisfaction with e-training

Overall, participants were satisfied with their learning achievements, course content, learning resources, self-efficacy and use of the platform. Regarding the overall perception, >95% of the participants agree and strongly agree that the course met their expectations and that the course duration was appropriate for its complexity. Among respondents to the satisfaction survey, most participants stated that they agreed (n=14, 22.2%) and strongly agreed (n=48, 76.2%) to recommend this course to other people.

When triangulating the information with that of the qualitative component, specifically aspects related to pedagogy in the framework by Ali et al, we found that all participants had a positive opinion regarding the contents. They found the course clear, concise, interesting, useful and with easy-to-understand explanations.

The topics that were offered to us through this virtual course, we will be able to carry them out in our daily activities as professionals, in the specific case of insulin administration, in the case of complications prevention because they also show us what the complications are. (Nurse from Cajamarca)

By contrast, the main criticism was that the course content was highly theoretical and not that practical and ‘concrete’. For example, students would have preferred to know the exact insulin doses to be applied in each case study presented in the course. Participants cited the lack of real-time interactions as a limitation of this modality.

Virtual courses are important, they give us the scope, however, one of the limitations is that when you have a doubt, a query, you don't have a person whom you can consult directly. It is not the same. That is the difference with a face-to-face course, but anyway, these are important and they help us be updated. (Nurse from Cajamarca)

Additionally, they indicated a limited period to post on the forums, so it was difficult for some students to do it because of other work-related activities that they needed to attend.

I forgot, I didn’t realise the ending date and time had already passed. (Nurse from Ayacucho)

Barriers of those who signed up for but did not attend the course

Among participants who did not start the course, the most common reasons were family burden, workload and time constraints. As for the platform/connectivity and motivations, poor internet connection and the lack of recognition of training hours were important reasons not to take the course. Finally, another frequent reason was forgetting the password (see online supplemental files 8 and 9 for more details).

When assessing the qualitative component in the technology section of the framework by Ali et al, 60% of the participants experienced difficulties with their internet connection, especially those from Tumbes and Loreto.

It is very good for people who have electricity and internet, but it is suffering for those who don’t. (Physician from Loreto)

At least one participant from each region reported difficulties such as slow or unstable signal, lack of electricity or internet due to the rainy season, lack of internet availability or having internet access but with pages blocked by the institution.

In this area, the connection is poor and it comes and goes, I’m watching a video (…) and I’m moving forward and it freezes, then I have to wait for it to reload or restart the video, that’s a bit of the difficulty. (Nurse from Cajamarca)

At times, in a large part of the mountains it starts to rain, then the Internet fails, the power goes out. (Nurse from Ayacucho)

Another important barrier was the lack of social and institutional support related to family burden and workload.

In the [health] facility, there is no time, there are patients to attend to, you cannot do it there, [I took the course] at home, in my free hours. (Nurse from Ayacucho)

We are working and we cannot be able to focus the entire time that the course lasts to understand it well. We are [focus] on in moments and it is hard, sometimes, to understand all the content and also ask questions too. And that limits the learning of the participant. (Physician from Cajamarca)

Sometimes the demand for patients or things increased, and suddenly that made that I have less time to dedicate to watching the course…. (Physician from Tumbes)

Participants’ recommendations

Participants suggested increasing interactions with the teachers by asking questions, solving their doubts and learning from their experiences, interacting in general with other students/teachers as part of the virtual course because the classes were recorded, receiving reminders about course activities (eg, new sessions and exams), having constant tests that ensure understanding of the topics and accessing sessions’ recordings. Some participants suggested changing to an in-person course instead or having some in-person sessions to encourage interaction. They also recommended increasing the course duration, receiving facilities from the regional health management department and more flexible schedules.

About the course contents, they suggested receiving specific content on case management at the primary care level, splitting content by age and comorbidities and providing guidelines on the indications and contraindications of different types of insulin.

Discussion

E-learning has become a recognised strategy for improving knowledge and skills in health professionals.20 21 However, there are some barriers when implementing e-learning that are context-specific and involve internet connection issues, technical issues, lack of interaction and reduced socialisation, poor learning conditions, lack of self-discipline, work overload or overwhelming content of the course modules.22 23

Training healthcare professionals working at the primary level of care is an important public health measure owing to the prevalence of diabetes reported in this population24 and could allow healthcare decentralisation.22 25 Training through the internet represents an opportunity, but economic and human resources limitations were reported by professionals working in this context21 mainly due to the inequalities in access and use of information and communication technology (ICT).26 A Peruvian study highlights connectivity as an important barrier for most healthcare workers from rural areas.27 In addition, participants use their mobile networks rather than a fixed network to connect to the internet, which limits the connections.28

Given the potential of using ICT for training, governments need to implement policies to improve access in rural areas.20 Although governments in South America have approved measures to preserve and guarantee connectivity and telecommunications services,28 other aspects focus on improving digital skills, adaptation of educational content to the digital environment with a user-friendly ICT, use of digital education tools specifically designed for a context of low connectivity and use of basic mobile devices.20 29

Globally, e-learning has reached primary healthcare professionals with a high participation rate.30 31 Studies show that primary healthcare professionals are highly motivated about e-learning and consider it most beneficial in vocational training for specialisation, updated sources of information and continuing education.27 32 Regarding dropouts, we found a higher dropout rate (37%) compared with worldwide rates that vary from 10% to 13%.33–35 However, our rate is comparable to that in a similar British e-learning programme about the safe use of insulin.31

According to local and foreign studies, dropouts are mostly related to a lack of support from institutions.22 27 One suggestion to improve the uptake of e-learning training is to ensure support in the work environment. For example, assigning paid time to these educational activities27 or reassigning clinical activities to reduce competition with the usual workload.

Additionally, considering rewards or penalties and making training a requirement for certification should be evaluated for each institution.29

We found in our study high satisfaction with learning achievements and course content among the participants. The reasons were the availability and easy-to-use methodology which has also been identified.14 33 In addition, students showed a favourable attitude, and they perceived usefulness. An important part of the learning experience was adequate support with technical problems.33

Teaching procedures through e-learning have shown an improvement in both knowledge and technical skills.36 As an example, a study showed an improvement in physician confidence in giving an effective insulin treatment (selection, usage, monitoring and adjustment) before and after an online diabetes course.33 However, e-learning evaluations usually consist of written evaluations of knowledge, especially in LMICs,37 instead of evaluations that assess skills with hands-on assignments.

Strengths and limitations

This study is subject to several potential biases, including selection bias, self-reporting bias and non-response bias. For instance, participants may have self-selected based on their motivation or interest in diabetes care, and their responses may have been influenced by the desire to provide socially desirable answers. There is potential response bias due to the need to please facilitators, refrain from expressing conflicting views or quickly complete questionnaires with untrue responses. Despite efforts to minimise this, such as ensuring anonymity and conducting surveys online, some level of bias may persist. In the case of the qualitative interviews, these were conducted by members of the team working outside the MoH and ENSAP, which may have helped reduce facilitator-related bias.

An additional limitation relates to the secondary nature of the data used in this study. Data were originally collected and analysed by a public institution (ENSAP). As the methods are regularly used by the institution, an assessment of validity and reliability of the tools was not conducted.

As the aim of the study was to assess the feasibility and acceptability of a course provided by ENSAP, no evaluation of effectiveness was conducted. Thus, we suggest that the MoH conducts an assessment based on clinical aspects such as changes in the number of patients prescribed insulin, improvements in haemoglobin A1c levels or reductions in hypoglycaemia rates.

A key strength of this study was its mixed-methods approach with quantitative and qualitative methods. Data were collected from an important number of health professionals, especially from primary care settings in rural areas, and the IDI sample addressed a diverse range of professionals enriching the results. This study highlights the need to assess not only the e-learning training components but also other external factors that can affect participants’ completion.

Conclusions

Using e-learning for training on insulin usage is feasible in a LMIC. Although flexibility and low cost make it appealing in remote areas, barriers should be addressed through better ICT infrastructure and administrative support to guarantee its success. Adapting face-to-face courses to online or blended learning formats could improve learning outcomes. Additionally, tutoring can help support course completion and the practical application of newly gained knowledge and skills.
