Cinnamic acid improves insulin sensitivity in prediabetic mice through branched-chain amino acid metabolic reprogramming
Authors: You Wu, Huizhao Qin, Yongxin Huang, Fangfang Lou, Zhiwei Qi, Tonghua Liu, Jiahui Wang, Lili Wu, Xin Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance is a key pathological feature in the early stages of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), often associated with obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Cinnamic acid (CA) is a natural compound found abundantly in cinnamon and other dietary sources, has demonstrated antidiabetic potential. However, its preventive role in improving insulin sensitivity during the prediabetic stage and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.MethodsThis study evaluated the preventive effects of cinnamic acid in a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced prediabetic mouse model. Male C57BL/6 N mice were divided into control and intervention groups and treated with CA for 12 weeks. Glucose and insulin tolerance tests, histopathological analysis, serum biochemistry, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and molecular docking and dynamics simulations were performed.ResultsCA significantly improved glucose tolerance, lowered fasting glucose and improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced adiposity in HFD-fed mice. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicated enhanced branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) degradation in adipose tissue following CA treatment, marked by upregulation of key metabolic enzymes and decreased local and systemic BCAA levels. Molecular docking and dynamics confirmed stable binding between CA and target enzymes involved in BCAA catabolism. Furthermore, CA reduced inflammatory markers, increased adiponectin signaling, and restored GLUT4 expression in adipose tissue.ConclusionsCA prevents HFD-induced impairment of insulin sensitivity in prediabetic mice by promoting BCAA catabolism and alleviating adipose inflammation. These findings support its potential as a safe and cost-effective nutrition-based intervention for early prevention of metabolic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01052-6.Keywords: Cinnamic acid, Obesity, Insulin resistance, Branched-chain amino acids, Prediabetes

Introduction

The global population of adult diabetes patients has surged from approximately 198 million to around 828 million from 1990 to 2022 [1], creating a significant imbalance between the escalating prevalence and inadequate treatment. This disparity has emerged as an inevitable social challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) constitutes the predominant form (90%) of diabetic cases worldwide [2].

Clinically, obesity, hyperlipidemia, and T2DM often manifest sequentially or concurrently as part of a metabolic syndrome. Driven by multifactorial etiologies, including sedentary lifestyles and excessive consumption of high-calorie diets, these glucolipid metabolic disorders progress throughout the disease course and may eventually lead to severe diabetic complications and death [3]. Insulin resistance is the fundamental pathological change underlying the occurrence and development of T2DM. In the early stage of obesity-related metabolic diseases, the insulin-targeted tissues (adipose tissues, liver and skeletal muscles) manifest impaired insulin sensitivity, and pancreas cells adaptively secrete more insulin, leading to hyperinsulinemia [4]. As T2DM progresses, the gradual decline in the secretory function of pancreatic β cells leads to an inability to compensate for the body’s increased metabolic demands, ultimately resulting in elevated blood glucose levels [3, 4].

It is without doubt that the prevention of diabetes in high-risk individuals, such as those with obesity or prediabetes, through diet and lifestyle modifications is of vital importance. Intervention in the early stage of metabolic dysfunction could effectively delay the development or even prevent the onset of diabetes, as well as reduce the risk of diabetic complications [5, 6].

In recent years, natural products, including herbal plants and bioactive compounds derived from daily foods, have garnered significant attention for their potential in preventing and treating metabolic disorders. Compared with pharmacological agents, dietary bioactive compounds offer a safer and more sustainable strategy, particularly in the early stages of metabolic dysfunction such as prediabetes, when preventive interventions are most effective. Cinnamic acid (CA) is a natural existing phenolic acid containing an α, β-unsaturated ketone structure. This white crystalline substance is predominantly enriched in cinnamon bark, a widely used spice across various cultures globally [7]. Extensive research has demonstrated that cinnamon exhibits multiple bioactive properties, including antibacterial, antioxidant, and antidiabetic effects, with CA and cinnamaldehyde identified as its primary bioactive constituents. CA is easy to be isolated, and more stable and safer comparing to cinnamaldehyde [8], making it a more viable candidate for therapeutic applications. Additionally, CA is widely used for structural modification of natural compounds to enhance their biological activity [9]. Thus, investigating the mechanisms underlying CA’s antidiabetic effects is of critical importance, as it could provide novel insights into the prevention and treatment of T2DM.

In our previous studies, CA demonstrated significant improvements in glucolipid metabolism in db/db mice, effectively reducing hepatic lipid accumulation and enhancing glycogen synthesis in the liver [10, 11]. The db/db mouse is a genetic model of diabetes, which characterized by elevated body mass and hyperglycemia as early as 6 weeks of age [12]. This model primarily represents the advanced diabetic stage rather than the prediabetic phase. In contrast, the high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity and insulin resistance model more accurately mimics the prediabetic stage [13], making it a more suitable platform for investigating preventive strategies for T2DM. Notably, while the effects of CA on obesity and lipid metabolism have been reported in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed rat models [14, 15], to the best of our knowledge, its preventive effects on insulin resistance in a HFD-induced mouse model of prediabetes have not been systematically investigated. Therefore, in this study, we employed an HFD-induced mouse model to test the hypothesis that CA prevents diet-induced insulin resistance by reprogramming the adipose tissue metabolic profile. We evaluated this by measuring body weight, glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, and through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling of adipose tissue. Our objectives were to assess the effects of CA (20 and 40 mg/kg), compare its efficacy to glibenclamide, and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, with a focus on branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolic reprogramming.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

Animals and housing conditions

Thirty-five 7-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice (Charles River Laboratories) were acclimatized in specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Experiment Center of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine. Animals were maintained under controlled environmental parameters: 12-h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature 24 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity 50 ± 10%. All experimental protocols were approved by the BUCM Animal Care and Ethics Committee (Approval No. BUCM-2023070304-3026).

Drug preparation and administration

CA (≥ 99.5% purity, Cat. S30461, Shanghai YuanYe Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and glibenclamide (Cat. HY-15206, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) were dissolved in 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) vehicle. Daily oral gavage was administered at 0.1 mL/10 g body weight using the following dosage regimens: (1) Low-fat diet (LFD) + Vehicle group (LFD-VEH): Vehicle control (0.5% CMC); (2) High-Fat diet (HFD) + Vehicle group (HFD-VEH): Vehicle control (also 0.5% CMC); (3) HFD + Glibenclamide group (HFD-GLB): 20 mg/kg glibenclamide; (4) HFD + CA low dosage group (HFD-CA20): 20 mg/kg CA; (5) HFD + CA high dosage group (HFD-CA40): 40 mg/kg CA.

Experimental design and monitoring

Following a 7-day adaptation period, mice were randomly allocated into five experimental groups (n = 7/group). The LFD-VEH group received low-fat control chow (10% fat, 76% carbohydrate, 14% protein, TP23402, Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co., Ltd., Nantong, China) while other groups received HFD (60% fat, 25.9% carbohydrate, 14.1% protein, TP23400, Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co., Ltd.). Body weight and 8-h fasting blood glucose levels were monitored every two weeks throughout the 12-week treatment period (Fig. 1a). Blood glucose levels were measured using a standardized glucometer (Glucocard 01-mini; Arkray, Kyoto, Japan).

Metabolic function assessments

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

OGTT experiments were performed at weeks 6 and 12 of intervention. Mice were fasted for 8 h, and fasting blood glucose was measured prior to oral gavage with glucose (2 g/kg body weight, dissolved in distilled water). Blood glucose levels were monitored at 30-, 60-, and 120-minute intervals following glucose administration.

Insulin secretion test

The insulin secretion test was conducted at week 12. During the OGTT procedure, blood samples for the insulin secretion test were collected from the orbital venous plexus at 0 and 30 min post-glucose administration. These time points were selected to assess both the basal fasting insulin level and the peak insulin response. Serum was isolated through centrifugation (3000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) and insulin concentrations were quantified using a high-sensitivity ELISA kit (Cat. #80-INSMSU-E01, ALPCO Diagnostics, Salem, NH, USA).

Insulin tolerance test (ITT)

ITT was performed at week 11 to evaluate peripheral insulin sensitivity. Mice underwent a 6-hour fast, and basal blood glucose levels were measured. Insulin (Novolin R, reconstituted in prechilled 0.5% BSA-PBS) was administered via intraperitoneal injection (0.75 U/kg). Blood glucose levels were subsequently evaluated at 30-, 60-, and 120-min post-injection.

Tissue collection and biochemical indicator assessment

Following 12 weeks of treatment, mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation and subsequently euthanized. Body length was measured and blood was collected from the orbital veins. The liver and epididymal adipose tissue were excised and weighed. Tissue samples from the pancreas, liver, and epididymal adipose tissue were collected, with one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and the remainder snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage in a −80℃ ultra-low temperature freezer.

Serum levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) were quantified using an automated biochemistry analyzer (BS-420, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics, China). The homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index was calculated as (fasting blood glucose × fasting insulin)/22.5. The Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI) was calculated as 1/[log(fasting insulin) + log(fasting glucose)]

Histological observation

Tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were processed through paraffin embedding and sectioned into 4-µm slices for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the liver, pancreas, and epididymal adipose tissue sections. To evaluate hepatic steatosis, Oil Red O staining was applied to liver sections. All stained specimens were examined using a light microscope (BX53F, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on epididymal adipose tissues from all animals in each group (n = 7). For pancreatic tissues, a randomly selected subset of animals (n = 6 per group) was used for analysis. Tissue samples were weighed and homogenized with 1 mL tissue extraction solution [75% (9:1 methanol: chloroform):25% H2O], followed by centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatants were concentrated via vacuum drying and reconstituted in 200 µL 50% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid and isotope-labeled internal standards) prior to ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS).

Chromatographic separation was performed on a UHPLC instrument (1290 Infinity LC, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 6600, AB SCIEX, Singapore) operating in both positive and negative ionization modes. Mass spectrometric parameters were set based on previously validated method [16].

Metabolite identification was achieved by matching experimental MS/MS spectra against HMDB (https://hmdb.ca), massbank (https://massbank.jp), LipidMaps (https://lipidmaps.org), mzcloud (https://www.mzcloud.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://genome.jp/kegg/). Multivariate statistical analysis including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the ropls package (v1.26.0) in R (v4.2.1). Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were defined by simultaneous fulfillment of: (1) p value < 0.05; (2) variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1.0. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted through MetaboAnalyst platform [17].

Transcriptomic analysis

Mouse epididymal adipose tissues (n = 6/group) were randomly selected for RNA sequencing. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), then sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing data were processed through fastp (v0.22.0) for quality control, including adapter trimming and removal of low-quality reads. High-quality reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome (NCBI Assembly) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression quantification was performed with HTSeq (v0.9.1) to obtain raw read counts, and fragments per kb per million reads (FPKM) normalization was applied to compare transcript levels across samples.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.34.0) with thresholds of |log2(fold change)| >1 and p value < 0.05. Hierarchical clustering analysis was visualized via heatmaps generated with the pheatmap package (v1.0.12) in R. Functional annotation of DEGs including Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis, was conducted through clusterProfiler (v4.16.0). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (v4.1.0) was performed to identify globally coordinated expression changes.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and subjected to microwave-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking with 3% BSA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibody (listed in Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4 °C. Sequential incubations were performed with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cat. GB23303, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 1 h and iF488-Tyramide (Cat. G1231, Servicebio) or iF647-Tyramide (Cat. G1232, Servicebio) for 10 min at 37 °C. For multiplex fluorescent labeling, a second round of microwave antigen retrieval was conducted to strip previous antibodies, followed by incubation with a different primary antibody (Supplementary Table 1). Detection was achieved using HRP-secondary antibody and iF546-conjugated tyramide (Cat. G1251; Servicebio). Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI (0.1 µg/mL, 5 min) and sections were mounted with antifade medium. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse C1, Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemistry analysis was performed using a HRP Detection System (Cat. PV-6000, ZSGB-Bio, Beijing, China). After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with anti-glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) antibody (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4 °C. HRP-polymer conjugate was applied for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic development (5–15 s monitoring). Hematoxylin counterstaining was performed for 30 s.

Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and DAB-positive area was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Targeted serum amino acid metabolomic profiling

Serum amino acid profiles were quantified through targeted metabolomic analysis using UHPLC-MS/MS. This analysis was performed on serum samples from the LFD-VEH, HFD-VEH, and HFD-CA40 groups to corroborate the adipose tissue BCAA findings. Serum amino acid contents were assessed via a targeted metabolomics method. The UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed on ACQUITY Liquid chromatography (Waters) equipped with Agilent XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm), coupled to an AB4000 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX).

Calibration standards were prepared using certified reference materials. Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from epididymal adipose tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). For qPCR analysis, tissues from the LFD-VEH, HFD-VEH, and HFD-CA40 groups were selected to validate transcriptomic findings and investigate the mechanism of the high-dose CA, which demonstrated the most pronounced FBG lowering effect. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using TransScript® All-in-One SuperMix for qPCR (Cat. AT341-02, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) under the following conditions: 42 °C for 30 min, 85 °C for 5 s. qPCR primers (Supplementary Table 2) were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). qPCR amplification was conducted in a LightCycler® 480 II System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using BlazeTaq™ SYBR® Green qPCR Mix 2.0 (Cat. QP041, GeneCopoeia, Rockville, MD, USA). The relative expression levels of target mRNAs were normalized using the 2−ΔΔCT method with β-actin serving as the endogenous control.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed to predict the binding interactions between CA and its putative protein targets using AutoDock Vina (v1.1.2). The 3D structure of CA (CID 444539) was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and target protein 3D structures were retrieved from the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org). Protein preparation included removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of Kollman charges using AutoDockTools (v1.5.7.). Binding affinity (ΔG, kcal/mol) was calculated for the top 10 conformers, with the lowest-energy pose selected for visualization using PyMOL software (https://www.pymol.org/).

Molecular dynamics simulations

To further evaluate the binding stability of CA to its possible targets, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the Amber24 software [18]. The ff19SB force filed was selected for the protein targets, and OPC water model was employed for the system. The chemical-protein complexes were placed in a cubic water box, with cutoff distances for electrostatic and van der Waals interactions set to 1.0 nm during simulations. A time step of 2 fs was used, and long-range electrostatic corrections were implemented via the PME method. After system setup, energy minimization was first performed [19], followed by 200 ps of NVE equilibrium dynamics and 100 ps of NPT equilibrium dynamics. 100 ns of molecular dynamics sampling was conducted and relevant metrics including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and hydrogen bond counts were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± S.D., where n represents the number of biological replicates. Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM). After verifying normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test), one-way ANOVA was applied. Planned comparisons between the HFD-VEH group and other groups were conducted using Fisher’s LSD test (equal variances) or Dunnett’s T3 test (unequal variances). A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

CA ameliorates glucolipid metabolic dysregulation in HFD-fed mice

12 weeks of HFD administration induced significant body weight gain (Fig. 1b) and elevated fasting blood glucose concentrations (Fig. 1c) in the HFD-VEH group. OGTT demonstrated progressive impairment in the model group, with exacerbated glucose intolerance observed at week 12 compared to week 6 (Figs. 1d, e), confirming the development of metabolic syndrome. Notably, CA intervention significantly attenuated HFD-induced body weight gain and fasting glycemia (Figs. 1b, c), and improved glucose tolerance profiles (Figs. 1d, e). The HFD-GLB group exhibited significantly lower OGTT AUC at weeks 6 and 12 (Fig. 1d and e), demonstrating glibenclamide’s efficacy in improving glucose tolerance as a positive control.

CA treatment markedly reduced serum triglyceride (TG) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, while the HFD-CA40 group exhibited elevated high-density lipoprotein (HDL) concentrations (Fig. 1f). HFD-induced hepatic steatosis was evidenced by increased liver weight, hepatocyte lipid vacuolization (H&E staining), and accumulated lipid droplets (Oil Red O staining). These pathological alterations were substantially mitigated by CA administration, as shown by restored liver morphology and reduced lipid deposition (Fig. 1g and i). Notably, CA administration did not significantly affect renal function parameters (BUN, CREA) in mice but reduced serum ALT and AST levels, indicating that 12-week CA treatment induced no significant hepatorenal toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 1a-1d). Collectively, these results demonstrate CA’s capacity to ameliorate HFD-induced dysregulation of glucolipid metabolism.

CA improves insulin sensitivity but spares pancreatic β-cell function in HFD-fed mice

Mice receiving CA treatment exhibited significantly lower AUC in ITT, indicating enhanced peripheral insulin sensitivity (Fig. 2a), as further supported by HOMA-IR analysis (Fig. 2b). Compared to the LFD-VEH control, the HFD-VEH group exhibited a marked increase in HOMA-IR, demonstrating that 12-week HFD feeding successfully induced severe insulin resistance in mice (Fig. 2b). Consistent with the HOMA-IR and ITT results, insulin sensitivity assessed by the QUICKI was also significantly improved by CA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Given CA’s previously observed insulin secretion-enhancing effect in db/db mice (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3), we conducted insulin secretion assays and pancreatic histopathological examinations to evaluate its potential impact on insulin secretion in this HFD-induced prediabetic model. Notably, the HFD-VEH group exhibited increased insulin levels, while both GLB and CA treatments showed a trend toward reduced insulin levels. Histopathological analysis revealed no severe pancreatic impairment in the HFD-VEH group, with quantitation of insulin and glucagon fluorescence showing no significant intergroup differences (Fig. 2d and f). These collective findings suggest that 12-week HFD feeding primarily induced insulin sensitivity impairment characterized by hyperinsulinemia without compromising insulin secretory capacity. Although GLB and CA effectively improved insulin sensitivity, they exhibited no significant modulatory effects on pancreatic endocrine function.

HFD-fed mice develop compensatory hyperinsulinemia through metabolic reprogramming

To investigate the mechanistic basis for CA’s model-dependent effects, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling of pancreatic tissue. Based on preliminary phenotypic results, the HFD-CA40 group exhibited significantly better efficacy of lowering fasting blood glucose levels than the HFD-CA20 group. Therefore, subsequent transcriptomic, metabolomic, and molecular validation studies were conducted using only the LFD-VEH, HFD-VEH, and HFD-CA40 groups to ensure analytical clarity. PCA demonstrated clear separation between groups (Fig. 3a), with 119 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) between the HFD-VEH and LFD-VEH groups (50 upregulated, 69 downregulated), while CA treatment modulated79 metabolites (25 upregulated, 54 downregulated) compared to the HFD-VEH group (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 4).

Pathway enrichment analysis of merged DEMs (positive/negative ion modes) highlighted critical metabolic perturbations (Fig. 3d and f). Intriguingly, compared to LFD-VEH controls, the HFD-VEH group exhibited marked upregulation of three insulin secretion mediators: ATP, cAMP and Glucose 6-phosphate. CA administration significantly reduced ATP and NADH, were upregulated in the HFD-VEH group. CA down regulated the ATP and NADH levels while elevating the NAD+/NADH ratio (Fig. 3f and g). These metabolic alterations collectively demonstrate that 12-week HFD feeding drives pancreatic β-cell hyperfunction, thereby sustaining hyperinsulinemia observed in the HFD-VEH group. Therefore, in this HFD-induced prediabetic model, the glucose-lowering effect of CA is not primarily mediated by further stimulating the already compensatory hyperactive pancreatic β-cells, but is more likely attributable to its insulin-sensitizing actions in peripheral tissues.

CA enhances BCAA catabolism in adipose tissue

Building on the established insulin-sensitizing effects of CA, we investigated its impact on adipose tissue remodeling in HFD-fed mice. CA administration significantly reduced the BMI and decreased epididymal fat mass compared to the HFD-VEH group (Fig. 4a and b), with a trend toward greater efficacy at the higher dose, as evidenced by numerically larger reductions in BMI and fat mass. H&E staining further revealed that CA attenuated adipocyte hypertrophy (Fig. 4c). To elucidate the mechanistic basis underlying these improvements, we performed transcriptomic profiling of epididymal fat. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct clustering between groups, with CA treatment reversing 28% (587/2103) of HFD-downregulated genes and suppressing 39% (901/2318) of HFD-upregulated genes (Fig. 4d and f).

Functional enrichment analysis of these CA-modulated genes (587 upregulated and 901 downregulated) identified BCAA degradation as the most significantly altered pathway. KEGG and GO enrichment also revealed alteration in inflammatory-related pathways (Fig. 4g and h). The GSEA analyses confirmed significant inhibition of BCAA degradation pathways in HFD-VEH mice (NES = −2.24, p < 0.01), which was effectively reversed by CA treatment (NES = 2.53, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4i).

CA reduces BCAA levels in adipose tissue

To corroborate the transcriptomic data, particularly the observed regulation of BCAA degradation, we further examined BCAA levels through metabolomic analysis in adipose tissue. Compared to the LFD-VEH group, the HFD-VEH group exhibited significant alterations in the metabolomic profile, with 197 metabolites upregulated and 118 downregulated. CA treatment partially reversed these changes, upregulating 70 and downregulating 216 metabolites relative to the HFD-VEH group. hierarchical clustering heatmaps revealed that the overall metabolomic profile of the CA-treated group closely resembled that of the LFD-VEH group (Fig. 5a and d, Supplementary Fig. 5). KEGG enrichment analysis of DEMs further identified BCAA metabolism as a significantly altered pathway in both comparisons (LFD-VEH vs. HFD-VEH, and HFD-CA40 vs. HFD-VEH). Specifically, quantification of the three BCAAs showed elevated leucine and isoleucine levels in the adipose tissue of HFD-VEH mice, and CA treatment significantly reduced the levels of all three BCAAs (Fig. 5g).

CA upregulates the expression of key enzymes involved in BCAA degradation

Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of epididymal adipose tissue revealed consistent alterations in branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism. To further investigate this concordance, we performed an integrative analysis of the two omics datasets. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were jointly mapped onto the KEGG pathway map 00280 (Valine, Leucine, and Isoleucine Degradation) (Supplementary Fig. 6). This analysis highlighted the upregulation of several key enzymes involved in BCAA degradation following CA treatment, including branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH), isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase (IVD), and acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACAD).

To validate these transcriptomic findings, we performed quantitative PCR (qPCR), which confirmed that CA significantly upregulated the expression of Bckdha, Bckdhb, Dbt, Acads, and Ivd, while the expression of Acadsb remained unchanged (Fig. 6a and d). Furthermore, Molecular docking demonstrated that the binding between cinnamic acid and these proteins involves hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, indicating a moderately strong intermolecular association (ΔG=−6, −5.6, −5.4 kcal/mol for BCKDH, ACADS and IVD, respectively), supporting the likelihood of direct molecular engagement (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 7a-c).

Further molecular dynamics simulations verified the stability of CA-protein binding. The RMSD of these complexes exhibited significant fluctuations during the initial stages of the dynamics simulation, but gradually stabilized in the latter half of the 100 ns trajectory. After the complexes reached equilibrium, the system exhibited smaller structural fluctuations, with the Rg consistently remaining within a relatively low range (Fig. 6f and g, Supplementary Fig. 7). The total binding free energy (ΔGtotal) calculated based on molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area method were − 16.79, −18.43, and − 22.64 kcal/mol for BCKDH, ACADS, and IVD (Fig. 6h).

CA suppresses adipose inflammation and enhances insulin signaling

As adipose tissue is a key site for BCAA catabolism, its impairment may lead to elevated BCAA levels both locally and systemically. To explore this, we conducted targeted amino acid metabolomics in mouse serum. The results showed that isoleucine and leucine levels were significantly elevated in the HFD-VEH group, whereas CA treatment specifically reduced isoleucine concentrations (Fig. 7a).

Since BCAAs primarily contribute to insulin resistance through inflammation-related pathways, and our transcriptomic data indicated alterations in inflammatory responses, we further validated key components involved in insulin signal transduction in adipose tissue. CA treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of adiponectin and its receptor, and inhibited protein kinase C (PKC), which is known to impair insulin signaling by phosphorylating insulin receptor substrate (IRS). In addition, CA increased the expression of Akt2 and Slc2a4, encoding protein kinase B (AKT) and GLUT4, respectively (Fig. 7b and e). GLUT4 protein expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry, which revealed reduced GLUT4 levels in adipocytes of the HFD-VEH group, while CA treatment markedly restored its expression (Fig. 7f).

Adipose tissue inflammation is often characterized by an elevated pro-inflammatory (M1)/anti-inflammatory (M2) macrophage ratio. To assess this, we measured the expression of Tnf and Mgl2, markers of M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively, using qPCR. CA treatment significantly reduced the Tnf/Mgl2 ratio. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed increased expression of CD163, another M2 macrophage marker, in the HFD-CA40 group (Fig. 7g ~ 7j). Together, these findings suggest that CA enhances insulin signaling in adipocytes and attenuates inflammatory macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the preventive effect of CA against prediabetes in an HFD-induced mouse model. Our findings strongly support our hypothesis that CA prevents diet-induced insulin resistance by reprogramming the adipose tissue metabolic profile, especially by enhancing BCAA catabolism.

We demonstrated that CA administration effectively improved systemic insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis in HFD-fed prediabetic mice, significantly mitigating pathological changes such as fasting blood glucose levels elevation and glucose intolerance. CA administration at 40 mg/kg (HFD-CA40) showed a greater effect in lowering fasting blood glucose and epididymal fat weight compared to 20 mg/kg (HFD-CA20). Crucially, integrated multi-omics analyses revealed that these benefits were associated with a specific reprogramming of BCAA metabolism in adipose tissue, characterized by the upregulation of key catabolic enzymes and a consequent reduction in local and systemic BCAA levels, underscoring a mechanism distinct from glibenclamide’s insulin secretagogue action.

The underlying mechanisms of diabetes involve insulin resistance and insufficient insulin secretion [20]. Insulin resistance refers to a phenomenon in which insulin target tissues exhibit reduced sensitivity to insulin action [21]. Notably, during the initial stages of insulin resistance, the pancreas compensatorily increases insulin production, maintaining circulating blood glucose at relatively normal levels [22]. As insulin resistance progresses, the enhanced insulin secretion becomes inadequate to compensate for the loss of insulin function, ultimately leading to significant hyperglycemia [23]. Thus, to elucidate the dual pharmacological effects of CA, we conducted multi-omics analysis with a focus on its insulin secretion-enhancing and insulin-sensitizing properties.

In pancreatic β-cells, glucose metabolism elevates the ATP/ADP ratio, which closes ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels. This depolarizes the membrane, opening voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. The ensuing Ca²⁺ influx then triggers insulin exocytosis [24, 25]. In contrast to CA’s effects in db/db mice and in vitro experiments [7, 26], our findings revealed that neither CA nor glibenclamide significantly enhanced insulin secretion in the HFD-induced prediabetes model. The model group itself exhibited compensatory hyperinsulinemia without β-cell dysfunction, which is consistent with established prediabetes progression where sometimes compensatory hyperinsulinemia precedes β-cell failure, reflecting underlying insulin resistance [27, 28]. This finding shifts the focus of CA’s action away from the pancreas and toward peripheral insulin-sensitive tissues in the prediabetic context.

In the prediabetic stage, insulin resistance emerges as the predominant pathological alteration [29]. HFD feeding induced obesity accompanied by elevated liver weight and epididymal adipose mass. Given CA’s therapeutic effect on adipose tissue expansion, we subsequently performed integrated transcriptomic-metabolomic profiling to investigate its regulatory mechanisms in adipocytes. Notably, CA enhanced BCAA catabolism through transcriptional upregulation of rate-limiting enzymes (BCKDH, ACAD, and IVD), concurrently attenuating BCAA accumulation in adipocytes and systemic circulation, establishing a tissue-specific regulatory mechanism that correlates with improved glycemic control.

BCAAs (valine, leucine and isoleucine) are essential amino acids that relatively abundant in foods, constituting approximately 15%–25% of total protein intake [30]. Elevated circulating BCAA levels are recognized biomarkers of insulin resistance and metabolic perturbations, and are considered as one of the most prominent metabolic features linked to insulin resistance [31, 32]. Analysis of clinical data identified BCAA levels as a causal factor driving obesity and insulin resistance to T2DM [33].

Adipose tissue possesses the capacity to catabolize circulating BCAAs, and the expression of related metabolic enzymes inversely correlate with circulating BCAA concentrations [32, 34]. In adipose tissue, the initial steps of BCAA catabolism are shared among the three BCAAs. BCAA transaminase catalyzes the transamination of BCAAs to branched-chain keto acids, which are subsequently decarboxylated and dehydrogenated by BCKDH, the rate-limiting enzyme for the bioprocess. Studies have shown that under conditions of obesity and insulin resistance, the expression of genes encoding BCAA catabolic enzymes in adipose tissue is reduced [35]. In the current study, our multi-omics analysis revealed HFD-induced BCAA accumulation in adipose tissue paralleled by downregulation of BCKDH, ACAD, and IVD expression. Notably, studies have also highlighted that BCAAs, particularly leucine, stimulate insulin secretion under normal or hypoglycemic conditions [36].CA treatment effectively reversed these perturbations, reducing adipose BCAA retention and circulating leucine concentrations. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations confirmed stable binding affinities between CA and these enzymatic targets, suggesting a structure-anchoring mechanism through direct ligand-protein interactions.

Elevated BCAA levels suppress the expression of anti-inflammatory genes in adipose tissue [37, 38], and a decline in adiponectin levels and functionality is also observed in insulin resistant subjects [39, 40]. Consequently, decreased adiponectin levels and elevated BCAA levels collectively contribute to adipose tissue inflammation, which is primarily characterized by an increased infiltration of macrophages [41]. Inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), reduce insulin receptor substrate activity through inhibitory phosphorylation and is correlated with worsening glycemic control [42]. Our data demonstrate that CA significantly upregulated the expression of adiponectin and its receptor AdipoR2 in adipose tissue. Furthermore, the significant reduction in the Tnf/Mgl2 mRNA ratio, coupled with the increased protein abundance of the M2 marker CD163, demonstrates that CA promotes a shift from pro-inflammatory M1-like toward anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophages in vivo. The alleviated inflammation stress, in turn, likely contributed to the observed upregulation of AKT2 and GLUT4, thereby enhancing glucose uptake in adipocytes (Fig. 8).

Schematic diagram illustrating the effects of cinnamic acid on adipose tissue in HFD-fed prediabetic mice. The diagram summarizes key molecular and metabolic changes observed in this study. Green arrows indicate upregulated processes or molecules; red arrows indicate downregulated ones

This study has several limitations. It is important to note that our assessment of insulin resistance relied on indirect indices (ITT and HOMA-IR), whereas the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is the direct gold standard. Future clamp studies would be valuable to confirm the effect size. Furthermore, while mRNA ratios provide a practical surrogate for inflammatory tone [43, 44], it does not directly quantify the proportion of distinct macrophage subsets. Flow cytometry with surface markers (e.g., CD11c, CD206) would provide a more definitive characterization of macrophage populations in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that cinnamic acid (CA) is a promising natural compound for preventing HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction. CA treatment significantly improved key metabolic parameters, including glucose tolerance and systemic insulin sensitivity, while reducing body weight gain and adiposity. Mechanistically, CA increased BCAA catabolism in adipose tissue through upregulating rate-limiting enzymes (BCKDH, ACAD, and IVD), leading to reduced BCAA accumulation both locally and systemically. This enhancement of BCAA clearance, in turn, alleviates adipose tissue inflammation and restores adiponectin signaling and GLUT4 expression, thereby improving tissue insulin sensitivity. Given CA’s cost-effectiveness, chemical stability, and favorable biosafety profile [45], these findings highlight its translational potential for metabolic disorder interventions.
