PACIFIC-5: a phase III clinical trial of consolidation durvalumab in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and no progression after concurrent or sequential chemoradiotherapy
Authors: Yi-Long Wu, Lin Wu, Nan Bi, Timucin Cil, Hong Ge, Zhengfei Zhu, Chih-Liang Wang, Wei Zhang, Dongqing Lv, E Mingyan, Jianguo Sun, Yi Pan, Maciej Krzakowski, Mustafa Dikilitas, Mehmet Ali Nahit Sendur, Young-Chul Kim, Yanjiao Yang, Rui Mao, Biao Zhang, Luhua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsolidation durvalumab following no progression on concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) is standard of care for unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, in clinical practice many patients receive sequential CRT (sCRT). The PACIFIC-5 trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of consolidation durvalumab for unresectable stage III NSCLC following no progression on cCRT or sCRT.MethodsThis randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III trial enrolled patients aged ≥ 18 years with unresectable stage III NSCLC, regardless of PD-L1 expression or sensitising EGFR or ALK aberrations, without disease progression after cCRT or sCRT. Patients were randomised (2:1) to durvalumab 1500 mg or placebo intravenously every 4 weeks (stratified by tumour PD-L1 expression and prior treatment) until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or consent withdrawal. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) by blinded independent central review in the modified intention-to-treat population (mITT). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS) in the mITT and safety. The safety analysis set include patients who received at least one dose of study treatment.ResultsOf 407 patients randomised to receive durvalumab (n = 272) or placebo (n = 135), 405 received at least one dose of durvalumab (n = 271) or placebo (n = 134). The mITT comprised 381 patients randomised to durvalumab (n = 252) or placebo (n = 129). Durvalumab showed statistically significant improvement in PFS versus placebo in the mITT (median [95% confidence interval {CI}], 14.0 [10.9–18.0] vs. 6.5 [5.4–13.8] months; hazard ratio [95% CI], 0.75 [0.58–0.99]; p = 0.038). There was a trend toward improved OS with durvalumab versus placebo in the mITT (median [95% CI], 38.3 [28.9–42.8] vs. 32.5 [20.6–40.4] months; hazard ratio [95% CI], 0.87 [0.66–1.17]; p = 0.346 [interim analysis]). Among the safety analysis set, maximum grade 3 or 4 adverse events of any cause occurred in 26.9% (73/271) and 23.9% (32/134) and 1.5% (4/271) and 0% (0/134) had treatment-related adverse events leading to death for durvalumab and placebo, respectively.ConclusionsPACIFIC-5 met its primary endpoint of improved PFS after either cCRT or sCRT. Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated 2.5 million new cases (12.4% of all new cancers) in 2022 [1]. Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for the majority (>80%) of all lung cancers, and around a third of patients with NSCLC present with unresectable stage III disease [2–4]. The standard, globally recommended curative-intent treatment for fit patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC includes definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT), involving the simultaneous administration of radiotherapy with platinum-doublet chemotherapy [5–7]. For patients whose disease does not progress during cCRT, the recommended treatment strategy is the administration of durvalumab, a programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) inhibitor, as consolidation therapy for up to 12 months [5–8].

The efficacy and safety of durvalumab as consolidation therapy in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and no disease progression after platinum-based cCRT was evaluated in the phase III PACIFIC trial (NCT02125461), which demonstrated significant improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) (hazard ratio [HR], 0.52; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.42–0.65; p < 0.001) and overall survival (OS) (HR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.53–0.87; p = 0.0025) with durvalumab versus placebo [9–11]. Durvalumab was associated with a manageable safety profile (maximum-grade 3 or 4 adverse events [AEs] of any cause increased by 4.4% in the durvalumab group compared with the placebo group) and no detrimental impact on patient-reported outcomes (PROs) compared with placebo [9, 10, 12]. Long-term follow-up from PACIFIC demonstrated that the benefits of durvalumab were sustained over time: the 5-year PFS rates were 33.1% versus 19.0% with durvalumab versus placebo, and the 5-year OS rates were 42.9% versus 33.4%, respectively [11].

While it has been well established that cCRT (defined in the current report as ≥ 2 overlapping cycles of chemotherapy and radiotherapy) improves the OS of patients with stage III NSCLC compared with sequential CRT (sCRT), many older patients, and patients with comorbidities, still receive sCRT in clinical practice, often due to concerns about tolerability [13–15]. For example, in a meta-analysis of randomised trials directly comparing cCRT versus sCRT, cCRT was found to increase acute oesophageal toxicity (grade 3–4) from 4% to 18% with a relative risk of 4.9 (95% CI, 3.1–7.8; p < 0.001) [13]. As patients receiving sCRT were not enrolled in the PACIFIC trial, there remains a lack of evidence for the benefit of consolidation durvalumab after sCRT in this setting [15]. Moreover, there is an unmet need for prospective data regarding consolidation durvalumab in regions not heavily represented in previous studies, such as China, Russia, and other regions in Asia [9, 10, 16]. Here, we report data from PACIFIC-5 (NCT03706690), a global, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre phase III trial designed to assess the efficacy and safety of consolidation durvalumab in a broader population of patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC whose disease had not progressed after cCRT or sCRT.

Methods

Patients

Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years of age and had World Health Organization (WHO)/Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) score of 0 or 1, histologically or cytologically confirmed unresectable stage III NSCLC (according to Version 8 of the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer Staging Manual in Thoracic Oncology [17]), and no progression following definitive, platinum-based cCRT or sCRT, which must have been completed 1–28 days prior to the first dose of study drug. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, and endobronchial ultrasound were encouraged, but not required as they may not have been available in all sites or countries, at diagnosis. Patients must have had documented epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) status (locally or centrally) at screening and documented tumour PD-L1 status prior to randomisation (assessed at a reference laboratory using the VENTANA PD-L1 [SP263] immunohistochemistry assay). Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the protocol (Supplementary Appendix).

For cCRT, eligible patients must have received ≥ 2 cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy concurrent with radiotherapy, and the last dose of chemotherapy must have been prior to, or concurrent with, the final dose of radiotherapy. Consolidation chemotherapy after radiotherapy was not permitted, but ≤ 2 cycles of induction chemotherapy prior to cCRT was acceptable. For sCRT, patients must have received ≥ 2 cycles of platinum-based chemotherapy before radiotherapy, and the interval between administration of the last dose of chemotherapy regimen and start of radiotherapy must have been ≤ 6 weeks. For both cCRT and sCRT, the platinum-based chemotherapy regimen must have contained cisplatin or carboplatin and one of the following agents: etoposide, vinblastine, vinorelbine, a taxane (paclitaxel or docetaxel), or pemetrexed, according to local standard of care regimens (gemcitabine was not included). Patients must also have received a total radiotherapy dose of 60 Gy ± 10% (54 to 66 Gy) during CRT. Study sites were encouraged to adhere to the following mean organ radiation dosing: mean lung dose must be < 20 Gy (Gy), and/or V20 must be < 35%; mean oesophagus dose must be < 34 Gy; heart V45 must be < 35%, or V30 must be < 50%.

Finally, with enrolment ongoing, the PACIFIC-5 study protocol was amended (at version 5) to exclude from primary and key secondary analyses patients who had documented test results that confirmed the presence of a sensitising EGFR mutation or ALK translocation (both confirmed by local or central testing). This amendment was based on the emergence of targeted therapies for patients with EGFR and ALK aberrations and emerging results from PACIFIC showing uncertain benefit among patients with EGFR mutations [18–20].

Trial design and treatment

Patients with no progression following definitive, platinum-based cCRT or sCRT were randomly assigned (2:1 ratio) in a double-blind manner to receive either durvalumab 1500 mg or placebo administered intravenously every 4 weeks until disease progression, toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. No crossover was allowed. Randomisation was stratified according to PD-L1 expression on tumour cells (TC; < 1% vs. ≥ 1%) and prior therapy (cCRT versus sCRT) (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Appendix). Randomisation was carried out via interactive voice response system using a single randomisation list for each randomisation strata, generated by a computer software program that incorporates a standard procedure for generating randomisation numbers. A blocked randomisation was generated, with all centres using the same lists to minimise imbalance in the number of participants assigned to each treatment arm.

The trial was designed by the sponsor, AstraZeneca. All patients provided written informed consent, and an independent data monitoring committee monitored efficacy and safety. The protocol and all amendments were approved by the relevant ethics committees or institutional review boards, and the trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and all applicable laws and regulations. All investigators were responsible for the collection of data. All authors participated in writing the manuscript and provided approval to submit the manuscript for publication. The authors vouch for the accuracy and completeness of the data and for the fidelity of the trial to the protocol. Medical writing assistance, including development of the initial draft of the manuscript, was funded by the sponsor.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was PFS (evaluated by blinded independent central review [BICR] per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [RECIST], version [v]1.1) in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, which excluded patients with documented EGFR or ALK aberrations. PFS was defined as the time from the date of randomisation until the date of objective disease progression or death (by any cause in the absence of progression). The key secondary endpoint was OS in the mITT. OS was defined as the time from the date of randomisation until death due to any cause. Other secondary endpoints included PFS (as assessed by BICR) and OS in all randomised patients (intention-to-treat [ITT] population), OS rate at 24 months following randomisation, objective response rate (ORR) by BICR, duration of response (DoR) by BICR, PFS rate at 12 and 18 months following randomisation by BICR, time from randomisation to second progression (PFS2) by local assessment, and time to death or distant metastasis (TTDM) by BICR.

On-study tumour assessments (CT or MRI) occurred at baseline, then every 8 weeks for the first 48 weeks after randomisation and every 12 weeks thereafter until confirmed RECIST v1.1-defined radiological progression plus one or more additional follow-up scans for confirmation of progression, the end of study, death, study discontinuation, or sponsor decision, whichever occurred first. Safety was monitored throughout the trial and assessed in all the patients who had received at least one dose of any trial treatment or placebo (the safety analysis set). AEs were collected from screening until 90 days after the last dose of study drug and documented according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities, v26.1, and graded with the use of National Cancer Institute (NCI) Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), v5.0. Because variable and overlapping presentations make it challenging to precisely differentiate between radiation pneumonitis and immune-mediated pneumonitis, AEs of pneumonitis were analysed and presented as the grouped term ‘pneumonitis or radiation pneumonitis’ [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

We planned that 400 eligible patients would undergo randomisation (2:1 ratio) in the ITT population, including 375 patients in the mITT population, which excluded patients with documented EGFR or ALK alterations who were enrolled before a protocol amendment. The primary PFS (mITT) analysis data cut-off (DCO) was planned to occur when either of the following conditions were first met: reaching approximately 262 BICR PFS events across the durvalumab and placebo treatment arms (approximately 70% maturity) in the mITT population or approximately 28 months follow-up from last patient randomised to the study. As a result, the planned primary PFS analysis had ≥ 90% power to demonstrate a HR of 0.65 with a 2-sided significance level of 5%. The smallest treatment difference that would be statistically significant was a HR of 0.773.

Medians and landmark rates for PFS and OS were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier technique. CIs for median PFS and OS were derived based on the Brookmeyer-Crowley method with log-log transformation. The PFS and OS HRs and associated CIs were calculated using a stratified Cox proportional hazards model, adjusting for the level of PD-L1 expression (< 1% vs. ≥ 1%) and prior therapy (cCRT or sCRT), with treatment as the only covariate, ties handled by Efron approach, and CI calculated using a profile likelihood approach. The 2-sided p-values were obtained using the stratified log-rank test, adjusting for the level of PD-L1 expression and prior therapy. Subgroup analyses were performed using the same methodology as for the overall PFS and OS analyses, with the exception that unstratified Cox proportional hazards models and unstratified log-rank tests were used. PFS2 and TTDM were assessed using the same methodology as for PFS and OS. Safety data are summarised descriptively.

No interim analyses of PFS and up to two of OS were planned. The alpha level for survival endpoints was controlled using the Lan-DeMets spending function that approximates an O’Brien-Fleming approach [23]. A hierarchical testing procedure was used to strongly control the overall 2-sided type I error at 5%, which was firstly assigned to the primary endpoint, PFS in the mITT (Fig. S2). If PFS in the mITT was significant at the primary analysis, the alpha was recycled to the secondary endpoint of OS in the mITT (alpha level 5%). If OS in the mITT was significant at the interim or final analysis, the alpha was further recycled to PFS in the ITT (alpha level 5%). If PFS in the ITT was significant, the alpha was finally recycled to OS in the ITT (alpha level 5%).

Results

Patients

From 27 November 2018 through 17 January 2022, 773 patients were enrolled and 407 were randomly assigned to receive durvalumab (n = 272) or placebo (n = 135) across 71 sites in mainland China (33 sites), Russia (10 sites), Taiwan (7 sites), Turkey (6 sites), Poland (4 sites), India (3 sites), Philippines (3 sites), Republic of Korea (3 sites), Hong Kong (1 site), and Mexico (1 site; Fig. 1). All randomised patients were included in the ITT; the mITT comprised 381 patients without EGFR or ALK aberrations who had been randomised to receive durvalumab (n = 252) or placebo (n = 129); and the safety analysis set included 405 patients who had received at least one dose of durvalumab (n = 271) or placebo (n = 134).

Baseline demographics and disease characteristics were comparable between the ITT and mITT (Table 1). In the mITT, median age was 63.0 years in both arms. In the durvalumab versus placebo arms of the mITT, most patients were male (92.1% vs. 88.4%) and Asian (71.4% vs. 72.9%) with squamous histology (69.8% vs. 65.1%), PD-L1 TC ≥ 1% (59.9% vs. 60.5%), and a history of smoking (84.9% vs. 88.4%). WHO/ECOG PS of 1 (68.3% vs. 55.0%) and stage IIIC disease (23.4% vs. 14.7%) were more prevalent in the durvalumab versus placebo arms of the mITT. Prior radiotherapy and chemotherapy details were similar between arms (Table 1); 66.7% versus 69.8% of patients in the durvalumab versus placebo arms of the mITT had received cCRT, and most (64.7% vs. 65.1%) had a best response to prior CRT of partial response.

All but one patient in the durvalumab (99.6%) and placebo (99.2%) arms of the mITT received study treatment (Fig. 1). Overall, 80.9% and 82.8% of patients in the mITT discontinued durvalumab or placebo, most commonly due to progressive disease (53.4% vs. 66.4%); 14.3% of patients in the durvalumab arm and 7.0% of patients in the placebo arm of the mITT discontinued study treatment due to an AE. Patients received a median 12.0 cycles of durvalumab or 7.0 doses of placebo, and median actual treatment duration was 48.4 versus 28.3 months with durvalumab versus placebo (Table S1). Approximately half (49.1%) of patients in the durvalumab arm received at least one year (i.e., 13 cycles) of treatment, and almost a third (29.9%) remained on treatment at 2 years (i.e., 26 cycles). After discontinuation of study treatment, 165/381 patients in the mITT received subsequent anticancer therapy: 95/252 (37.7%) patients who received durvalumab and 70/129 (54.3%) patients who received placebo. The most common subsequent anticancer therapies were chemotherapy (durvalumab, 31.3% [n = 79]; placebo, 38.8% [n = 50]) and immunotherapy (durvalumab, 9.5% [n = 24]; placebo, 17.8% [n = 23]).

Efficacy

There was a statistically significant improvement in the primary end point, PFS in the mITT, with durvalumab versus placebo (Fig. 2A). In the mITT, median follow-up among patients censored for PFS was 35.7 months in the durvalumab arm and 30.5 months in the placebo arm. Median PFS (95% CI) was 14.0 (10.9–18.0) months with durvalumab versus 6.5 (5.4–13.8) months with placebo (stratified HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.58–0.99; p = 0.038). Similar results were observed in the ITT, though significance was not formally assessed per the hierarchical testing procedure (Fig. S3). The 12-, 18-, and 24-month PFS rates in the mITT were 53.6%, 43.4%, and 39.9% with durvalumab versus 42.7%, 34.1%, and 30.2% with placebo.

PFS HRs trended toward benefit with durvalumab across most pre-specified subgroups in the mITT, though 95% CIs crossed one with few exceptions (Fig. S4). Similar benefit was observed after cCRT (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.55–1.06) and sCRT (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.49–1.18) in the mITT (Fig. 3). The exceptions to this were observed among patients with PD-L1 TC < 1% (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.63–1.53) or non-squamous histology (HR, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.66–1.80) and among those who received cisplatin (HR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.66–1.47), though in each case, the median PFS for the placebo arm (PD-L1 TC < 1%: 13.6 months; non-squamous histology: 19.9 months; cisplatin: 13.8 months) was inconsistent with the median PFS for the placebo arm of the mITT (6.5 months).

At this first interim analysis, there was a trend toward improved OS in the mITT with durvalumab versus placebo (Fig. 2B). In the mITT, median follow-up among patients censored for OS was 42.7 months in the durvalumab arm and 42.1 months in the placebo arm. Median OS (95% CI) was 38.3 (28.9–42.8) months with durvalumab versus 32.5 (20.6–40.4) months with placebo (stratified HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.66–1.17; p = 0.346). A similar trend was observed in the ITT, though significance was not formally assessed per the hierarchical testing procedure (Fig. S5). The 12- and 24-month OS rates in the mITT were 80.9% and 60.7% with durvalumab versus 75.8% and 54.4% with placebo.

ORR in the mITT was 27.6% with durvalumab versus 20.7% with placebo (odds ratio [OR] 1.51; 95% CI, 0.891–2.607; p = 0.128) (Table 2). Median DoR (95% CI) was not reached (16.6–not estimable [NE]) with durvalumab versus 37.6 (5.4–NE) months with placebo. The median TTDM in the mITT was 42.6 months (95% CI, 33.2–NE) with durvalumab and not reached (95% CI, 20.3–NE) with placebo (stratified HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.73–1.58; p = 0.769) (Fig. S6). PFS2 by investigator assessment per RECIST v1.1 in the mITT trended in favour of durvalumab; median PFS2 (95% CI) was 26.4 (22.1–36.0) months with durvalumab versus 19.5 (14.7–28.9) months with placebo (stratified HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.65–1.14; p = 0.275) (Fig. S7).

Safety

Treatment-emergent AEs of any cause occurred in 94.8% of patients who received durvalumab and 83.6% of patients who received placebo (Table 3); maximum grade 3 or 4 AEs occurred in 26.9% and 23.9%, respectively. The most common any-grade AEs of any cause were pneumonitis or radiation pneumonitis (in 39.5% of patients in the durvalumab arm and 40.3% in the placebo arm), hypothyroidism (18.8% and 7.5%), pneumonia (18.8% and 11.2%), and anaemia (15.1% and 14.9%) (Table S2). The most common grade 3 or 4 AEs of any cause were pneumonia (in 5.9% of patients in the durvalumab arm and 5.2% in the placebo arm), pneumonitis or radiation pneumonitis (3.7% and 2.2%), and anaemia (2.2% and 1.5%) (Table S3). The most common AEs considered causally related to study treatment by the investigator were pneumonitis or radiation pneumonitis (in 18.5% of patients in the durvalumab arm and 11.9% in the placebo arm), hypothyroidism (15.5% and 7.5%), and hyperthyroidism (10.7% and 5.2%) (Table S4).

Discussion

PACIFIC-5 met its primary endpoint with statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in PFS with durvalumab versus placebo among patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC without disease progression after either cCRT or sCRT. At this first interim analysis, there was a trend toward improved OS with durvalumab versus placebo. Other secondary endpoints also trended in favour of durvalumab versus placebo, though no difference was observed in TTDM between groups, likely due to the predominance of squamous histology, which has been associated with increased risk of a locoregional pattern of recurrence [24, 25]. The safety profile of durvalumab in PACIFIC-5 was generally consistent with the known safety profile of durvalumab, with no new safety signals despite treatment until progression [9, 10]. While an increased number of grade 5 AEs were reported in the durvalumab arm (9.2%, n = 25) compared with the placebo arm (1.5%, n = 2), the number of treatment-related deaths in the durvalumab arm was low (1.5%, n = 4) with no treatment-related deaths reported in the placebo arm. Despite the longer treatment duration in this study, these data are comparable to those reported for the phase III PACIFIC study (grade 5 treatment-related AEs were 1.5% in the durvalumab arm vs. 1.3% in the placebo arm) [9].

In the mITT population, PFS benefit with durvalumab was observed consistently in most of the prespecified subgroups (including prior CRT type [cCRT vs. sCRT]), with the exception of patients with tumors of non-squamous histology, patients whose tumor PD-L1 expression was < 1% on TCs, and patients who received cisplatin-based regimen of chemotherapy. For subgroups with non-squamous histology, PD-L1 TC < 1%, and receipt of prior cisplatin the placebo arms appeared to outperform the placebo arm of the mITT, while the durvalumab arms appeared to perform similarly to the durvalumab arm of the mITT. For example, median PFS for the placebo arms of the non-squamous, PD-L1 < 1%, and cisplatin subgroups was 19.9, 13.6, and 13.8 months, respectively, versus 6.5 months for the mITT overall, while median PFS for the durvalumab arms of the non-squamous, PD-L1 < 1%, and cisplatin subgroups was 15.0, 12.7, and 14.1 months, respectively, versus 14.0 months for the mITT overall. Imbalances in baseline demographics and disease characteristics between arms which favored placebo arm were likely to have impacted the observed differences in the treatment effect within the mITT (e.g., WHO/ECOG PS 0, stage IIIA disease, and non-squamous histology were more prevalent in the placebo arm of the mITT) or within subgroups. Additionally, subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution given that the 95% CIs for HR cross one in each case, sample sizes are small relative to the mITT, and the study was not powered to assess statistical significance among these subgroups. It must also be noted that approximately 90% of the patients in both arms were male. Potential reasons for this may be the exclusion of patients with EGFR mutations, which are enriched in females, or that the incidence of lung cancer in men in China, which enrolled the majority of patients, is twice that reported in women [26, 27].

PACIFIC-5 supports the use of consolidation durvalumab after either cCRT or sCRT, consistent with the phase III GEMSTONE-301 trial (NCT03728556), which showed that consolidation treatment with the PD-L1 inhibitor sugemalimab improved PFS versus placebo among patients in China with unresectable stage III NSCLC and no progression after cCRT or sCRT (median PFS [95% CI], 9.0 [8.1–14.1] vs. 5.8 [4.2–6.6] months; HR [95% CI], 0.64 [0.48–0.85]), and with the phase II PACIFIC-6 trial (NCT03693300), which found that consolidation durvalumab had a manageable safety profile and encouraging efficacy (median PFS [95% CI], 10.9 [7.3–15.6] months) among patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and no progression after sCRT [16, 28]. It is important to highlight that EGFR/ALK testing was not mandatory in GEMSTONE-301. Given that the study was predominantly conducted in Asian patients, where the prevalence of EGFR/ALK mutations is high, the lack of testing may have contributed to the differences in efficacy between the two studies. Despite this difference and with the caveat of cross-trial comparison, regional variation in clinical practice, and differences in study population, median PFS in the durvalumab arm of PACIFIC-5 (14.0 months) compares favourably to that observed in GEMSTONE-301 (10.5 months) and was similar to that observed in the PACIFIC trial (16.9 months) [11, 29]. In addition, median PFS for the placebo arms of PACIFIC-5 (6.5 months) and GEMSTONE-301 (6.2 months) performed similarly to both each other and to the placebo arm from the PACIFIC trial (5.6 months) [9, 11, 28]. Importantly, the fact that many patients in PACIFIC-5 received sCRT suggests that the population in PACIFIC-5 may have been less fit or more frail than in studies in which most or all patients received cCRT such as PACIFIC, as sCRT is often administered in lieu of cCRT due to concerns about tolerability [13–15]. Despite this, durvalumab maintains a manageable safety profile in this broader population, as evidenced by the general consistency of the safety profiles observed in PACIFIC-5 and PACIFIC and the similarity in rates of key safety parameters (e.g., maximum-grade 3 or 4 AEs, pneumonitis or radiation pneumonitis) between arms [9, 10]. Taken together, findings from PACIFIC-5, PACIFIC-6, PACIFIC, and real-world evidence support the use of durvalumab after either cCRT or sCRT [9, 10, 16, 30–34].

Despite the established impact of consolidation durvalumab after cCRT and the encouraging efficacy of durvalumab after sCRT for patients who do not receive cCRT, additional therapeutic options are required to further improve outcomes in this setting [35]. One potential strategy is initiating treatment with immunotherapies like durvalumab earlier, at the same time as CRT, though recent trials suggest limited benefit, if any, with this strategy [36, 37]. Another strategy is to combine PD-L1 inhibitors with other agents such monoclonal antibodies targeting TIGIT (domvanalimab), CD73 (oleclumab), or NKG2A (monalizumab) during consolidation treatment to harness potential synergy between disparate mechanisms of action. The phase II COAST trial (NCT NCT03822351) has shown encouraging results with durvalumab combined with either oleclumab or monolizumab, and results from two phase III trials in this setting are awaited: PACIFIC-8 (NCT05211895), assessing the efficacy and safety of durvalumab combined with domvanalimab, and PACIFIC-9 (NCT05221840), assessing the efficacy and safety of durvalumab plus either oleclumab or monalizumab [38–40]. Finally, the use of immunotherapy, alone or in combination with other agents (e.g., chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy), as induction therapy followed by chemoradiotherapy or, when feasible, conversion to surgery is of ongoing scientific interest, with retrospective and early-phase clinical studies suggesting encouraging efficacy with regimens containing induction immunotherapy [41–48].

In conclusion, PACIFIC-5 met its primary endpoint of improved PFS with durvalumab versus placebo among a broader group of patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC than was included in the PACIFIC study, with no new safety signals despite treatment until progression. These data support the use of consolidation immunotherapy after either cCRT or sCRT.
