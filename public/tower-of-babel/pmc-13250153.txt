A longitudinal study on the effect of aerobic exercise intervention on working memory of college students with internet addiction
Authors: Yi Wang, Jianping Hu, Chengze Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the effects of aerobic exercise on working memory in college students with internet addiction and the characteristics of these effects as they change over time, from both behavioral and electroencephalographic (EEG) perspectives, with a focus on neural oscillatory dynamics. A longitudinal study design was employed, where 48 male college students with internet addiction were screened and randomly assigned to two groups (control and experimental). The experimental group completed 40-min sessions of aerobic cycling exercise, three times a week, for 12 weeks; the control group maintained their original physical activity habits without any additional interventions. A 3 (time points: pre-experiment, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks post-experiment) × 2 (groups: control and experimental) × 3 (electrode sites: Fz, F3, F4) mixed factorial experimental design was used to explore the effects of aerobic exercise on working memory in college students with internet addiction and how these effects evolved over time. In the experimental group, the post-test accuracy for the 1-back task was significantly higher than the pre-test (P < 0.05); for the 2-back task, both the mid-test (P < 0.05) and post-test (P < 0.001) accuracies were significantly higher than the pre-test, with the post-test accuracy being significantly higher than the mid-test (P < 0.01). Time–frequency analysis revealed that, for both 1-back and 2-back tasks, the post-test alpha band (100–200 ms) energy values in the experimental group were higher than the pre-test values (P < 0.05). For the 2-back task, the mid-test (P < 0.05) and post-test (P < 0.001) theta band (300–500 ms) energy values in the experimental group were significantly higher than the pre-test values, with the post-test values being significantly higher than the mid-test values (P < 0.01). (1) Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise can effectively improve working memory in college students with internet addiction, and the improvement becomes more significant as the duration of the exercise intervention increases. (2) Increases in early alpha band energy and later theta band energy in EEG are important neurophysiological indicators of the improvement in working memory among college students with internet addiction through moderate-intensity aerobic exercise.

Introductions

Working memory, also known as memory updating, refers to an individual’s ability to temporarily store, encode, refresh, and process event-related information while performing cognitive tasks1. Research into the neurobiological mechanisms of drug addiction has revealed that abnormal changes in working memory are closely related to addiction mechanisms, and enhancing working memory in addicts may be a crucial target for altering their addictive state2–4. Existing studies indicate that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise can significantly improve working memory in both healthy adults and those with drug dependence5–8. However, individuals addicted to the internet differ from healthy individuals and drug addicts in terms of physiological functions, cognitive abilities, and brain activity patterns, and the impact of aerobic exercise on the working memory of internet addicts remains unclear.

A substantial body of empirical research has established a significant link between internet addiction and impairments in core cognitive functions, including working memory. For instance, neuroimaging studies have revealed functional abnormalities in the frontoparietal networks—key brain regions supporting working memory—in individuals with internet addiction2,3. Behavioral studies have further directly confirmed that internet addicts exhibit poorer performance on working memory tasks, particularly those with high cognitive loads4. Based on this compelling evidence, university students meeting the criteria for moderate internet addiction can be considered a high-risk population for working memory deficits.

However, despite the established link between internet addiction and working memory deficits, and the known benefits of exercise for cognition, several critical gaps remain. First, the potential of aerobic exercise as a non-pharmacological intervention to specifically ameliorate working memory deficits in individuals with internet addiction has been significantly underexplored. Second, existing studies on exercise and cognition often focus on behavioral outcomes or static ERP components, leaving the dynamic oscillatory neural processes underlying any improvement largely unknown. Third, the longitudinal, dose–response relationship between exercise duration and cognitive enhancement in this population is unclear. Finally, and most critically for this study, it remains unknown whether and how aerobic exercise modulates the well-established neural oscillatory correlates (e.g., frontal theta and alpha) of n-back performance in individuals with internet addiction.

To address these gaps, this study employed a longitudinal design to investigate the efficacy of a 12-week, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise intervention in improving working memory in college students with internet addiction. We further utilized EEG time–frequency analysis to elucidate the temporal dynamics and neurophysiological mechanisms (specifically, frontal alpha and theta oscillations) underlying any observed improvements. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether a readily implementable intervention—moderate-intensity aerobic exercise—can effectively improve working memory in this at-risk population and to reveal the temporal characteristics of these improvements from both behavioral and electrophysiological perspectives.

Furthermore, extant electroencephalographic (EEG) research has established that the n-back task robustly elicits neural oscillations in specific frequency bands that are integral to working memory processes. Frontal theta oscillations (4–7 Hz) are strongly associated with cognitive control, attentional engagement, and the active maintenance of information, whereas alpha oscillations (8–13 Hz) are linked to inhibitory processes and the suppression of irrelevant distractors during memory encoding9,10. Time–frequency analysis of EEG signals thereby provides a powerful tool for decomposing the temporal dynamics of these oscillatory activities, offering fine-grained insights into the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive functions.

To uncover these neurophysiological mechanisms, we focused on the rich cognitive information carried by neural oscillatory activities11,12. This study therefore adopted a longitudinal design, utilizing time–frequency analysis of EEG signals to reveal how aerobic exercise modulates neural oscillations across different frequency bands over the course of the intervention. The findings aim to provide both theoretical insights and practical evidence for implementing structured aerobic exercise programs within university systems to mitigate working memory deficits associated with internet addiction.

Methodology

Participants

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 software. Based on the effects of aerobic exercise intervention on working memory reported in previous literature13–15, this study assumed a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power (1—β) of 0.80. The calculation determined that a minimum total sample size of 24 participants, with 12 participants per group, was required. Considering adherence to exercise and potential participant dropout, the sample size was increased by 20%, necessitating at least 30 participants, with 15 participants per group. Participants were recruited from full-time undergraduate students at a certain university, specifically selecting 48 male students with moderate internet addiction from the third year to ensure an adequate sample for the longitudinal design and potential attrition.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Moderate internet addiction as defined by scores between 60 and 79 on the Yong Internet Addiction Scale; (2) Non-athletes; (3) Body Mass Index (BMI) between 18.50 and 24.99; (4) Responses to questions on relative and absolute contraindications for exercise that met the requirements of this experiment; (5) Negative responses to all items on the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q) to ensure participants could safely engage in the exercise16; (6) According to the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), energy expenditure less than 600 MET-min/week (MET-min/week = MET value × duration of activity (min)/day × number of activity days/week), indicating a low level of physical activity17; (7) Normal or corrected vision, hearing, and color vision, without cardio-cerebrovascular or mental illnesses. The 48 participants were randomly assigned to two groups: a control group and an experimental group, with 24 participants in each group. During the entire experiment, data from five participants (four in the experimental group and one in the control group) were excluded due to attendance rates below 90% or withdrawal from the study, resulting in the inclusion of data from 43 participants for analysis. Independent sample t-tests were conducted on the age, internet addiction-related data, and health-related indicators of the two groups, showing no significant differences (P > 0.05), as detailed in Table 1. Experimental design

The behavioral study employed a mixed experimental design with two factors: 3 time points (pre-test, mid-test, post-test) × 2 groups (control group, experimental group), using the accuracy rate and response time of tasks as dependent variables.The electroencephalographic (EEG) analysis focused on time–frequency measures, with the energy values of specific frequency bands serving as dependent variables in a mixed experimental design incorporating three factors: 3 time points (pre-test, mid-test, post-test) × 2 groups (control group, experimental group) × 3 electrode sites (Fz, F3, F4). The selection of frontal electrodes Fz, F3, and F4 was based on their established role in working memory processes, particularly concerning frontal theta and alpha oscillations, as supported by prior literature9,10. These sites over the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) are key nodes of the frontoparietal network, which is critically involved in the executive and maintenance aspects of working memory tapped by the n-back task. We focused on this frontal cluster rather than a more distributed set (e.g., including Cz or Pz) because our hypotheses were specifically concerned with frontal lobe oscillatory dynamics underlying cognitive control and information maintenance.

Aerobic exercise protocol

This study utilized moderate-intensity stationary cycling for intervention. One week before the intervention, a VO2max test was conducted. According to the "Chinese Consensus on Exercise Prescription (2023)" and the health conditions of the participants in this study, 55% to 60% of VO2max was defined as moderate exercise intensity13. Based on the heart rate recorded during the testing process, the corresponding heart rate for this intensity was identified and used as the target heart rate for aerobic exercise. Each session lasted 40 min and was conducted 3 times per week. After the 4th and 8th weeks, the experimental group underwent another VO2max test to find the corresponding heart rate at 55% to 60% of VO2max intensity, which was then used as the target heart rate for weeks 5 to 8 and 9 to 12. The total intervention period was 12 weeks. To ensure adherence to the prescribed intensity, all participants in the experimental group wore heart rate telemetry monitors (model: RCX3) during exercise. The mean heart rate during the main exercise phase across the intervention was 128.4 ± 6.2 bpm, which successfully corresponded to 57.3% ± 2.1% of VO₂max, confirming that the moderate-intensity exercise protocol was consistently maintained. As the exercise capacity of the experimental group participants improved, the load on the bike increased accordingly at the same target heart rate intensity.

Experimental task

The present study employed the N-back paradigm, which was divided into 1-back and 2-back tasks based on varying levels of difficulty. 1-back Task: Upon pressing the “start” button, participants were presented with a sequence of letters on the computer screen (e.g., A, B, B, C,…). If the current letter matched the immediately preceding letter, participants were instructed to press the V key; if it did not match, they were to press the N key. 2-back Task: Similarly, after pressing the “start” button, participants viewed a sequence of letters (e.g., A, B, B, C,…). If the current letter matched the one that was two positions before it in the sequence, they were to press the V key; otherwise, they pressed the N key. Each experiment consisted of 200 trials, with each letter presented for 500 ms and an inter-stimulus interval of 600–800 ms. Participants were instructed to complete the tasks as quickly and accurately as possible. A detailed depiction of the tasks is provided in Fig. 1.

Experimental procedure

This experiment was conducted in the Sports Psychology Laboratory at Henan ** University, where the laboratory environment is designed to be light-sealed, soundproof, with an indoor temperature of 22 ~ 24 °C, and air humidity of 40% ~ 50% to minimize external interference throughout the experimental process. Participants were informed of the experimental procedure and provided written informed consent. The experimental group completed a 40-min session of aerobic stationary cycling (5 min of warm-up + 30 min of moderate-intensity exercise + 5 min of cooldown) three times per week (Wednesday, Friday, Sunday), with each session scheduled between 19:00 and 19:40, over a period of 12 weeks. During the same time periods when the experimental group underwent exercise intervention, the control group did not engage in any form of physical training. All participants were required to maintain their usual physical activity habits during the experiment, avoid taking any additional nutritional supplements, and refrain from using psychotropic medications (Fig. 2).

Data collection and processing

Behavioral data collection and processing

Behavioral data from participants were collected using E-prime3.0, with primary metrics including reaction time and accuracy for both 1-back and 2-back trials. Initially, the data were preprocessed by entering them into Excel and removing any data points beyond three standard deviations as well as those with an accuracy rate below 50%. Reaction time and accuracy rates for 1-back and 2-back trials were used as dependent variables for repeated measures ANOVA across 3 time points (pre-test, mid-test, post-test) × 2 groups (control group, experimental group). When the data did not satisfy Mauchly’s test of sphericity, the Greenhouse–Geisser method was employed to adjust the degrees of freedom and P values. If there was a significant interaction between time and group, simple effects analysis followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests were conducted. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

EEG data collection and preprocessing

Electroencephalogram (EEG) data were collected using Recorder software from Brain Products, Germany, employing Ag/AgCL electrodes according to the extended international 10–20 system with a 64-channel electrode cap. Bilateral mastoids served as reference electrodes, with horizontal electrooculogram (HEOG) placed 1 cm laterally to the right eye and vertical electrooculogram (VEOG) positioned 1 cm above the left eye orbit. During the experiment, impedance between the scalp and electrodes was maintained below 10 kΩ. The EEG signals were continuously recorded after amplification, with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz per channel.

This preprocessing pipeline was applied to prepare the data for subsequent time–frequency analysis. Subsequent data processing was performed using BP Vision-Analyzer software. Initially, the average potential of bilateral mastoid processes TP9 and TP10 was selected as the reference electrode, and the FCz electrode point was restored. Band-pass filtering was applied with low-pass at 30 Hz, high-pass at 0.1 Hz, and a slope of 24 dB/octave to remove power line noise. Independent component analysis (ICA) was utilized to eliminate ocular artifacts. The segmentation window ranged from 200 ms before to 800 ms after stimulus onset, with the mean value during the 200 ms prior to stimulus presentation serving as the baseline correction. Trials exceeding ± 100 μV were identified as artifacts and excluded from further analysis.

Time–frequency analysis

The preprocessed electroencephalogram (EEG) data were subjected to Event-Related Spectral Perturbation (ERSP) analysis to quantify stimulus-induced changes in oscillatory power. Specifically, the data were converted into time–frequency domain data through wavelet transform, with a time–frequency analysis window ranging from -200 to 800 ms. A Morlet wavelet transform was applied, employing a variable number of cycles increasing linearly from 3 at 1 Hz to 10 at 30 Hz. This approach provides an optimal trade-off between temporal and frequency resolution across the frequency range of interest. Time–frequency representations within the frequency range of 1 to 30 Hz were obtained at a resolution of 1 Hz. Following wavelet transform, the oscillatory power for individual trials was obtained across both time and frequency dimensions, which were then averaged to derive the time–frequency representations for each participant under each condition. To normalize the time–frequency energy and express changes relative to the baseline period, the energy values were converted to decibel (dB) scale using the formula: dB = 10 × log10 (power/baseline_power), where baseline_power is the average power during the -200 to 0 ms pre-stimulus interval. This method effectively reveals event-related increases or decreases in oscillatory power and is widely used in the literature.

Drawing upon previous research indicating a close relationship between neural activities in the alpha and theta bands of the frontal lobe and working memory9,10, three representative electrodes were selected: Fz at the midline of the frontal lobe, F3 on the left prefrontal lobe, and F4 on the right prefrontal lobe. In conjunction with the time–frequency analysis plots from this study (Figs. 3, 4 and 5), the alpha band (8–13 Hz) within the time window of 100–200 ms and the theta band (5–7 Hz) within the time window of 300–500 ms were determined as the focal time–frequency windows for analysis. Repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the energy values at Fz, F3, and F4 electrodes, with three factors: time point (pre-experiment, 6 weeks later, 12 weeks later), group (control group, experimental group), and electrode site (Fz, F3, F4). When the data violated the assumption of sphericity in the Mauchly’s test, the Greenhouse–Geisser method was employed to correct the degrees of freedom and p-values. Simple effect analyses were conducted when significant interactions were observed, and Bonferroni corrections were applied for post-hoc tests. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results of time–frequency analysis

Alpha and theta band energy in 1-back trials

Figure 3 displays the time–frequency distribution at the Fz electrode for participants during 1-back trials across the three time points.

A repeated measures ANOVA with a 3 (time points: pre-test, mid-test, post-test) × 2 (groups: control, experimental) × 3 (electrode sites: Fz, F3, F4) design was conducted on the alpha band (100 ~ 200 ms) energy values for the experimental and control groups. The results (Table 4, Fig. 4) indicated significant main effects for time points (F(2, 123) = 6.27, P < 0.01, η2 = 0.25) and group (F(1, 123) = 11.22, P < 0.01, η2 = 0.21), while the main effect of electrode sites was not significant (P > 0.05). A significant interaction effect was found between time points and group (F(2, 123) = 2.33, P < 0.05, η2 = 0.09). However, the interaction effects of time points × electrode sites, group × electrode sites, and time points × group × electrode sites were not significant (P > 0.05 for all). Simple effect analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in alpha (100 ~ 200 ms) energy values between the experimental and control groups at the pre-test (P > 0.05), suggesting homogeneity among participants before the experiment. Discussion

The present study sought to investigate the efficacy of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise in improving working memory in college students with internet addiction and to uncover its underlying neurophysiological mechanisms using time–frequency analysis. Our findings largely confirm the hypotheses put forward in the introduction.

Discussion of behavioral results

This study investigated the impact of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise on the working memory of college students addicted to the internet using behavioral testing and EEG time–frequency (ERSP) analysis. Simple effect analysis revealed that, compared to the control group, the experimental group exhibited significantly improved accuracy in the 2-back task after 6 and 12 weeks of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise. Additionally, significant improvements in accuracy were observed in the 1-back task after 12 weeks of exercise in the experimental group. When compared to pre-exercise levels, the experimental group showed significant improvements in 2-back accuracy after both 6 and 12 weeks of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, with the improvement at 12 weeks being significantly greater than that at 6 weeks. Similarly, significant improvements in 1-back accuracy were noted after 12 weeks of exercise in the experimental group. These results suggest that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise enhances the working memory of college students addicted to the internet, and the improvement becomes more pronounced as the duration of exercise intervention increases. Notably, heart rate monitoring verified that the exercise intensity was consistently maintained within the target range throughout the intervention, thereby strengthening the causal inference that the observed benefits are attributable to the prescribed aerobic exercise.

Critically, the randomized controlled design employed in this study provides strong internal validity for these findings. Over the 12-week experimental period, the working memory performance of the control group, which maintained their original habits, showed no significant changes over time (all P > 0.05) in either the 1-back or 2-back tasks. This result clearly demonstrates that the improvement observed in the experimental group was not due to practice effects or natural maturation but was specifically attributable to the aerobic exercise intervention. It indirectly indicates that the working memory function in this internet-addicted cohort possesses a degree of ‘cognitive plasticity’ or potential for improvement, which can be effectively activated and optimized through systematic exercise intervention.

These findings align with previous research on the benefits of exercise for improving working memory in healthy college students. Liu conducted a study with college students using the 2-back task to assess updating function and found that both 8 and 16 weeks of aerobic exercise significantly improved working memory, with the improvement at 16 weeks being superior to that at 8 weeks18.

The duration of aerobic exercise required to enhance working memory differs depending on the cognitive load of the task. For the more cognitively demanding 2-back task, significant improvements were observed after just 6 weeks of exercise. However, for the simpler 1-back task, significant improvements were only noted after 12 weeks of exercise. This disparity may be related to the characteristics of working memory in college students addicted to the internet. As supported by prior research19, individuals with Internet addiction tend to demonstrate cognitive deficiencies, particularly in working memory, which are more pronounced during complex, high-load tasks compared to simple ones. Therefore, compared to complex tasks, aerobic exercise takes longer to improve the ability of internet addicts to perform simple tasks.

Discussion of time–frequency results

The time–frequency analysis employed in this study was specifically chosen to probe the oscillatory neural dynamics underlying working memory processes—namely, alpha and theta rhythms—which have been consistently implicated in n-back performance. Our findings demonstrate that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise selectively enhanced these neural oscillations in the experimental group, providing a neurophysiological account for the observed behavioral improvements.

Inhibitory control, particularly the ability to suppress irrelevant information (interference control), is a crucial component of working memory performance in the n-back task. Enhancing this ability can reduce the interference from prior stimuli (e.g., suppressing the “n-1” item in a 2-back task) and irrelevant distractors, thereby improving task execution efficiency20,21. Studies have found that such inhibitory control is associated with the energy levels in the alpha frequency band22, with increased alpha band energy representing an improvement in this top-down inhibitory function10. Previous research has established that alpha oscillations in the prefrontal cortex play a key role in gating sensory information and suppressing distracting inputs, which is vital for working memory23–25. In the current study, during the early encoding stage (100–200 ms), the experimental group exhibited significantly higher alpha-band energy after the exercise intervention. This suggests that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise enhanced the efficiency of neural inhibitory mechanisms responsible for filtering out interference during memory encoding. Combining these findings with the behavioral data indicates that, in the early stages of working memory, college students with internet addiction invested more cognitive resources for inhibitory control to encode target information and exclude irrelevant information after the exercise intervention, leading to improved information processing efficiency. Thus, the increase in frontal alpha oscillations provides a neurophysiological mechanism by which aerobic exercise may improve working memory—specifically, by augmenting the brain’s capacity for interference control, a core aspect of inhibitory control that is often impaired in individuals with internet addiction.

Furthermore, the ability to maintain effective information is another pivotal aspect of working memory26. Previous research has confirmed27,28 that the capacity for information maintenance is closely related to the energy levels in the theta frequency band. An increase in theta band energy indicates an enhancement in an individual’s information maintenance ability. During the later stage of information matching (300–500 ms), the experimental group exhibited significantly higher energy values in the 2-back theta frequency band after both 6 and 12 weeks of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise compared to pre-exercise levels. These results indicate that, after moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, college students addicted to the internet allocated more attentional resources during the late information matching stage, leading to improved temporary storage capacity for information. Additionally, the study found that the energy values in the 2-back theta frequency band were significantly higher after 12 weeks than after 6 weeks in the experimental group. This suggests that, as the duration of the exercise intervention increased, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise had a more pronounced effect on enhancing the information maintenance ability of college students addicted to the internet, which is consistent with the findings of behavioral studies. Existing research has shown that impairments in cognitive function often manifest as decreased psychomotor speed and coordination, while regular physical activity can help maintain psychomotor speed and coordination, thereby delaying the decline in cognitive function29–31. Yang analyzed the accuracy and reaction times of elderly individuals during working memory tasks and found that regular participation in Tai Chi activities can effectively slow down the decline in working memory caused by aging32. Zhu used magnetic resonance imaging to explore the effects of task-related brain activity and resting-state functional connectivity in children during working memory tasks. Task activation analysis revealed greater activation in the left hippocampus of participants after 11 weeks of exercise intervention. These findings suggest that long-term exercise intervention can improve working memory in deaf-mute children, and these enhancements may be related to plasticity changes in the working memory network induced by exercise33.

Furthermore, this study also revealed that the impact of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise on the working memory of college students addicted to the internet differs under varying cognitive loads. Specifically, aerobic exercise significantly enhances the energy values in the alpha and theta frequency bands during the 2-back paradigm, whereas for the 1-back paradigm, it primarily boosts the energy values in the alpha frequency band during the early encoding and processing stage, with no significant effect on the energy values in the theta frequency band during the later information matching stage. The findings indicate that for tasks with higher cognitive loads, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise improves working memory primarily by enhancing inhibitory control and information maintenance abilities. In contrast, for the relatively simpler 1-back task, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise enhances working memory mainly by improving early inhibitory control abilities.

The benefits of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise on the working memory of college students addicted to the internet may be related to the mechanism by which exercise improves mental health. Previous studies have shown that prolonged internet use can cause psychological harm to adolescents, leading to anxiety and depression, which in turn impair individuals’ working memory34,35. Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise can elevate hormone levels in the body and improve the psychological environment. College students addicted to the internet, after engaging in moderate-intensity exercise for a sustained period, can not only effectively release psychological stress and alleviate negative emotions but also promote the formation of positive thinking and emotions, activating the neural networks responsible for executive functions in the brain. Regular engagement in aerobic exercise enhances the function of sensory and motor systems, strengthens endogenous attention shifting and allocation abilities, and facilitates individuals to coordinate complex cognitive processes across multiple cognitive subsystems in a dynamic and flexible manner during cognitive-behavioral processes, thereby improving working memory updating functions.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we strictly screened participants with moderate internet addiction based on standardized scales and established them as a high-risk group for working memory deficits based on extensive literature, the study lacked a healthy control group at baseline. This design limitation prevents us from directly confirming the presence of working memory impairments in our sample relative to a normal population. Future studies should incorporate a healthy control group and conduct comprehensive neuropsychological assessments before intervention to provide more direct evidence of cognitive impairment attributable to internet addiction.

Second, this study employed a passive control group that maintained their usual habits. While this design effectively demonstrates that the aerobic exercise intervention is superior to a naturalistic 'no-treatment’ condition, it cannot fully rule out the potential influence of non-specific factors such as increased attention from researchers, participant expectations (e.g., placebo effects), or the simple engagement in a structured group activity. Therefore, we cannot definitively conclude that the observed improvements are solely attributable to the physiological mechanisms of aerobic exercise itself. Future research should incorporate an active control group (e.g., engaging in light stretching, balance training, or social sessions) matched for factors like time commitment, social interaction, and researcher contact. This will allow for a more precise isolation of the unique contributions of aerobic exercise to working memory enhancement.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the randomized controlled trial design provides valuable internal evidence. The stable performance of the passive control group throughout the study period, contrasted with the significant and time-dependent improvement in the experimental group, strongly supports the core conclusion that aerobic exercise can improve working memory in this population. Furthermore, this study is the first to combine longitudinal behavioral and EEG time–frequency analyses to reveal the dynamic changes in neural oscillations underlying working memory improvement following an exercise intervention in individuals with internet addiction, thereby offering novel insights into its potential neural mechanisms.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that a 12-week, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise intervention is an effective, non-pharmacological strategy for enhancing working memory in college students with internet addiction. The benefits, observed through both behavioral and electroencephalographic (EEG) measures, exhibit a clear dose–response relationship, strengthening with the duration of the intervention.

By integrating behavioral tasks with time–frequency EEG analysis, we provided novel evidence that the improvement in working memory is supported by the enhanced recruitment of specific neural oscillations. The increase in early alpha band energy reflects improved inhibitory control during stimulus encoding, while the increase in later theta band energy indicates more efficient information maintenance and cognitive control. These findings advance our understanding of exercise-induced cognitive enhancement by delineating the specific temporal and spectral characteristics of neural plasticity in a cognitively at-risk population.

Practically, our findings offer a clear, evidence-based protocol for university health and counseling services. Structured aerobic exercise programs (e.g., 40-min sessions, 3 times/week for 12 weeks at moderate intensity) can be readily integrated into existing campus facilities and support systems as a practical and cost-effective strategy to mitigate the cognitive deficits associated with internet addiction.
