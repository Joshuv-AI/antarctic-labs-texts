A First‐in‐Japanese Phase 1, Double‐Blind, Placebo‐Controlled, Parallel‐Cohort Study of Sefaxersen, an Antisense Oligonucleotide Targeting Complement Factor B, in Healthy Participants
Authors: Takakazu Sato, Hiroyuki Fukase, Takaaki Ishida, Arisa Karasawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreased activity in the complement alternative pathway (AP) plays a key role in diseases such as IgA nephropathy (IgAN). This first‐in‐Japanese double‐blind Phase 1 study investigated the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), safety, and tolerability of sefaxersen (RO7434656), an antisense oligonucleotide targeting complement factor B messenger RNA. Healthy participants were randomized equally into four cohorts: placebo or sefaxersen 20, 40, or 70 mg. The PK, PD, and safety endpoints were monitored throughout the study and during the 90‐day follow‐up period. All 24 participants completed the study, with no new safety signals or clinically meaningful changes in blood chemistry, electrocardiogram, or vital signs observed. Plasma sefaxersen concentration demonstrated a biphasic PK profile, characterized by an initial rapid decline followed by a slow elimination. Sefaxersen decreased PD markers related to the complement AP selectively, without affecting the classical pathway, in a dose‐dependent manner, and the PD effects persisted over 2 to 3 months. Sefaxersen was well tolerated by healthy Japanese participants, with a manageable safety profile. These findings support the inclusion of Japanese patients with IgAN in the global Phase 3 study (IMAGINATION, NCT05797610).Keywords: complement factor B, first‐in‐Japanese, pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, sefaxersen

The complement system plays a crucial role in the innate immune system, defending the body against foreign agents and facilitating the removal of cellular debris through its essential housekeeping function. 1 , 2 The complement system consists of three distinct pathways, including the antibody‐mediated classical pathway (CP), the lectin pathway (LP), and the alternative pathway (AP). 1 , 3

CP and LP are activated by immune complexes or pathogen‐associated sugars, respectively, leading to the formation of C3 convertase (C4b2b) that cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b. 3 AP operates continuously at a basal level and is initiated by the hydrolysis of C3, leading to the formation of C3(H2O)Bb, which cleaves C3 into C3a and C3b. C3b binds to complement factor B (FB), forming a complex that is subsequently activated by complement factor D, splitting FB into Ba and Bb. Bb remains attached to C3b, creating the C3 convertase complex (C3bBb), which amplifies complement activation by cleaving more C3 molecules into C3b, thereby generating a positive feedback loop. 3 All pathways converge to form the C5b‐9 membrane attack complex, inducing pathogen lysis and enhancing immune defense. The split products, C3a and C5a, are anaphylatoxins playing crucial roles in chemotaxis and cell migration. 3

Alterations in key components of the complement system can lead to downstream complement dysregulation or hyperactivation, resulting in tissue damage or organ failure. As a result, a multitude of diseases are associated with alterations in the activity of the complement system. 4 , 5 , 6 Variants of genes in the AP, including complement FB, are associated with a variation in disease phenotype due to genetic predisposition and includes diseases such as IgA nephropathy (IgAN). 1 , 5 , 7 , 8

Complement FB is a 93 kDa single‐chain glycoprotein primarily synthesized in the liver. Complement FB is also synthesized at very low levels in several extrahepatic sites, such as endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and mesenchymal cells. 9 , 10 Gain‐of‐function variants of complement FB can lead to hyperfunctional C3bBb, which bypasses regulatory control, causing excessive consumption of plasma C3 in the glomeruli of the kidneys, and subsequent renal failure. 11 , 12 In contrast, other variants of complement FB are either less effective at forming C3 convertase or have reduced functional activity, resulting in a decrease in AP activity. 13 , 14 These observations led to the hypothesis that modulating complement FB levels or activity could prevent or treat complement‐induced diseases. 1

A decrease in AP activity might lower the incidence of diseases associated with increased AP activity. Blocking complement FB production inhibits AP activation, which has been shown to improve the prognosis in patients with IgAN. 1 , 14 , 15 Recent advancements have led to the development of numerous investigational and approved therapies targeting the AP to treat complement‐mediated diseases. 1 , 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 16 , 17

Sefaxersen (RO7434656) is a highly specific antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) conjugated with N‐acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) for targeted action in the liver. Following subcutaneous administration, sefaxersen is preferentially absorbed by the liver, where the GalNAc conjugation binds to asialoglycoprotein receptors (ASGPR) on hepatocytes. 17 , 18 Given that the liver serves as the principal source of circulating complement FB, a reduction in hepatic production is theoretically considered to be associated with decreased systemic levels, thereby leading to a decrease in AP activity. 1 Sefaxersen utilizes chemically modified 2′‐O‐methoxyethyl (2′‐MOE) chemistry to target complement FB messenger RNA (mRNA), effectively reducing complement FB production in the liver. 1 It consists of three regions: two regions containing five nucleotides in both the 5′ and 3′ ends composed of 2′‐MOE‐modified ribonucleotides, and a third region forming the central portion of the oligonucleotide with 10 oligodeoxynucleotides. This nucleotide sequence is complementary to positions 2584–2603 at the 3′ untranslated region of the complement FB. 1 The 2′‐MOE modification enhances ASO properties by (1) increasing affinity for the target mRNA, (2) improving resistance to exonucleases and endonucleases, thereby enhancing stability in tissues, and (3) reducing high‐dose toxicities observed with earlier ASO chemistries, resulting in an improved tolerability profile. 19 , 20

Sefaxersen is being developed to treat IgAN by targeting the AP of complement activation. 17 , 21 , 22 Sefaxersen has been evaluated in multiple clinical trials, including studies in healthy participants (EudraCT 2015‐001837‐25, ACTRN12616000335493) and patients with IgAN (NCT04014335, NCT05797610). 1 , 17 , 21 , 22 , 23 Results from Phase 1 studies of sefaxersen in healthy non‐Japanese participants have previously been reported. 1 In addition, sefaxersen demonstrated durable reductions in AP complement components in both blood and urine in a Phase 2 study in patients with IgAN. 17 , 24 Currently, its effects on proteinuria reduction and eGFR stabilization are being evaluated in a Phase 3 study in patients with IgAN. 21 , 22 In this first‐in‐Japanese Phase 1 study, we aimed to assess the pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), safety, and tolerability of a single subcutaneous dose of sefaxersen in healthy participants.

Methods

Study Design

This double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel‐cohort, Phase 1 study (jRCT2031230387) evaluated PK, PD, safety, and tolerability of a single subcutaneous dose of sefaxersen in healthy Japanese participants. Participants were randomized (1:1:1:1) to one of the four cohorts to receive placebo or sefaxersen at 20, 40, or 70 mg, and monitored for a subsequent 90 days (Figure 1). The dose range was selected based on the proposed Phase 3 regimen (70 mg every 4 weeks) and dose levels used in previous non‐Japanese Phase 1 studies to ensure comparability between healthy Japanese and non‐Japanese participants. 1 , 22 Key eligibility criteria included healthy Japanese adults aged 18–45 years with moderate body mass index (BMI; 18.5–25.0 kg/m2). All participants were vaccinated against Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae prior to initiating sefaxersen or placebo.

Study design.

Participants were excluded if they had concurrent infections, a history of bacterial meningitis or significant pulmonary disease, or tested positive for human immunodeficiency virus or hepatitis B/C. Additional exclusions included allergies to study vaccines or biologics; prior investigational treatments; concurrent major diseases; malignancies within 5 years; abnormal laboratory results; significant electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities; uncontrolled blood pressure; history of complement disorders; substance use, recent smoking, or excessive alcohol consumption; and recent blood donation. Participants who had a history of oligonucleotide treatment, history of splenectomy, and who were deemed unsuitable by the investigator were also excluded.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and applicable laws and regulations. All enrolled participants received a full explanation of the contents of this clinical research and provided written consent to participate. An external Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol and Informed Consent Form.

Endpoints

The PK endpoint was plasma concentration of sefaxersen following single subcutaneous administration. Sefaxersen was measured by a hybridization electrochemiluminescence method. The following blood PD levels were evaluated following subcutaneous administration of sefaxersen or placebo: complement FB, Bb, C3, C4, and C5b‐9 in plasma and alternative complement pathway hemolytic activity 50% (AH50) and classical complement pathway hemolytic activity 50% (CH50) in serum. Complement FB, C3, and C4 were measured by nephelometry. Bb, C5b‐9, AH50, and CH50 were measured by an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. The study assessed the effects of sefaxersen on various safety parameters, including adverse events (AEs). Clinical laboratory results, triplicate 12‐lead ECGs, vital signs, and body weight were also monitored.

Statistical Analyses

The planned sample size was 24 participants (six per cohort), with potential replacements if participants withdrew, and it was not determined based on hypothesis testing. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and baseline characteristics. The PK analysis set included all participants who received sefaxersen with ≥1 plasma sefaxersen concentration measurement, with summary statistics calculated for each cohort, including key parameters (maximum plasma concentration [Cmax], time to reach maximum plasma concentration, area under the plasma concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity [AUCinf], elimination half‐life [t1/2], apparent total clearance, apparent volume of distribution [Vd/F], and mean residence time). PK linearity was assessed based on dose–response relationships. The PD analysis set included all participants who received sefaxersen or placebo with ≥1 PD measurement, with individual and mean blood marker levels (complement FB, Bb, AH50, CH50, and other complement components) tabulated and plotted by treatment cohort. Safety analyses were conducted on all randomized participants who received sefaxersen or placebo.

Results

Participant Disposition and Baseline Demographics

Twenty‐four healthy Japanese participants were enrolled in the study. Each cohort (placebo, 20‐, 40‐, and 70‐mg sefaxersen) had six participants. All participants successfully completed the study drug administration and the study evaluation, and no replacements occurred. Demographics and other participant baseline characteristics are reported in Table 1. All participants were Asian males with a mean age of 31.8 years and BMI of 21.2 kg/m2.

Pharmacokinetics

The PK analysis set consisted of 18 participants, with six participants in each sefaxersen dosing cohort (20, 40, and 70 mg). Plasma concentrations of sefaxersen peaked at a median of approximately 2 h after sefaxersen administration for the 20‐ to 70‐mg dose range (Figure 2A). Thereafter, a biphasic decline in plasma sefaxersen concentration consisting of an initial rapid decline followed by slow elimination during the terminal phase was observed across all dose groups (Figure 2B).

Mean (SD) plasma concentration profile up to 24 h (A) and the last observation timing of the study (B) after dosing of each cohort. h, hour; SD, standard deviation.

A summary of PK parameters for sefaxersen is presented in Table 2. The mean Cmax and AUCinf values were 0.117 µg/mL and 1.41 h·µg/mL in the 20‐mg group, 0.449 µg/mL and 2.69 h·µg/mL in the 40‐mg group, and 0.875 µg/mL and 6.38 h·µg/mL in the 70‐mg group, showing that the Cmax and AUCinf tended to increase in a dose‐proportional manner over the range of 20 to 70 mg, with the limitation of the small sample size for PK linearity assessment. The mean t1/2 was approximately 5 weeks, regardless of dose level. The mean Vd/F was 16,500 to 19,500 L over the range of 20 to 70 mg.

Pharmacodynamics

All 24 participants were included in the PD analysis set. The mean levels of complement FB, Bb, and AH50 tended to decrease dose dependently with sefaxersen, whereas the mean levels of CH50, C5b‐9, C3, and C4 did not change among dose groups (Figure 3 and Figure S1). The mean levels of complement FB, Bb, and AH50 reached their lowest point, approximately 2 to 4 weeks after administration. The PD effects of sefaxersen on the complement FB, Bb, and AH50 persisted for over 2 to 3 months.

Mean (SEM) percent change from baseline in pharmacodynamic parameters. AH50, alternative complement pathway hemolytic activity 50%; CH50, classical complement pathway hemolytic activity 50%; FB, factor B; SEM, standard error of mean.

Safety

Among the 24 participants in the safety analysis set, the incidence of treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs) was 41.7% (n = 10) overall: 50.0% (n = 3) in the placebo group, 66.7% (n = 4) in the 20‐mg group, 50.0% (n = 3) in the 40‐mg group, and 0.0% (n = 0) in the 70‐mg group (Table 3). The most frequently reported TEAEs (n ≥ 2) were increased alanine transaminase (ALT; n = 3 in the 20‐mg group and n = 1 in the 40‐mg group) and increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST; n = 2 in the 20‐mg group and n = 1 in the 40‐mg group). All TEAEs were Grade 1 or Grade 2 in severity, except for one Grade 4 TEAE of increased creatine phosphokinase in a participant in the 20‐mg group, which was deemed not related to the study drug.

The incidence of AEs related to study drug were 12.5% (n = 3) overall: 0.0% (n = 0) in the placebo group, 33.3% (n = 2) in the 20‐mg group, 16.7% (n = 1) in the 40‐mg group, and 0.0% (n = 0) in the 70‐mg group. All ALT and AST AEs were resolved spontaneously within the observation period. There were no serious AEs (SAEs), and none of the AEs led to withdrawal. In addition, no clinically significant changes in blood chemistry, ECG, or vital signs were observed.

Discussion

This Phase 1 study is the first report of sefaxersen administration in the Japanese population. The study evaluated the PK, PD, safety, and tolerability of sefaxersen at doses of 20, 40, or 70 mg in healthy Japanese male adults. Sefaxersen demonstrated a biphasic PK profile, characterized by an initial rapid decline in plasma levels, followed by a slow elimination during the terminal phase. The high Vd/F values indicate that sefaxersen is extensively distributed throughout body tissue, with a particular affinity for the liver, suggesting that the drug does not remain confined to the plasma. This rapid and targeted liver distribution of sefaxersen would be due to the GalNAc of sefaxersen binding to ASGPR, mainly expressed in the liver. This mechanism enhances the drug's efficacy and safety profile. 1 , 18

Sefaxersen was designed to be a potent and selective inhibitor of complement FB expression in the liver via an antisense mechanism of action. Notably, the PD effects of sefaxersen on complement FB, Bb, and AH50 persisted for over 2 to 3 months, presumably due to the GalNAc conjugation and 2′‐MOE modification, which enhanced its delivery to tissues (particularly the liver) and its binding affinity to complement FB mRNA. Furthermore, the results suggest that reducing hepatic production of complement FB can lead to lowered systemic AP activity and has the potential to dampen complement activity in other tissues, such as the kidney, which are being further investigated in an ongoing Phase 3 study in patients with IgAN (IMAGINATION, NCT05797610). 21 , 22 The PD findings in this study aligned well with previously reported data from healthy non‐Japanese participants. 1

Administration of sefaxersen selectively inhibited AP activity without significantly affecting CP response, as the mean levels of CH50 did not change among dose groups. The reduction in plasma complement FB levels by sefaxersen led to a downstream decrease in amplification loop activity, subsequently lowering C3 convertase levels without affecting CP response. This allowed the CP response to infections and normal housekeeping functions of the complement system to remain intact while mitigating the long‐term pathology associated with chronic activation of the complement system. Recent developments have introduced small‐molecule inhibitors targeting complement FB, which exhibit PK and PD profiles different from sefaxersen. For example, iptacopan (LNP023), the small‐molecule complement FB inhibitor, reduces Bb levels and AP activity more rapidly but requires frequent dosing (twice daily at high doses) to maintain reductions. 25 , 26 , 27 In contrast, a single monthly injection of sefaxersen offers a prolonged reduction in complement activity.

A single subcutaneous dose of sefaxersen exhibited a favorable safety and tolerability profile in participants, with no dose‐dependent trends or SAEs reported. TEAEs were predominantly mild in severity. The 2′‐MOE modification and GalNAc conjugation of sefaxersen could likely contribute to its tolerability profile in Japanese participants. The PD findings in this study aligned well with previously reported data from healthy non‐Japanese participants. 1 At the same single‐dose levels, both the extent of plasma complement FB lowering effect and the time‐dependent changes were similar between this study and the previously reported study, suggesting that the PD effect of sefaxersen is consistent between Japanese and non‐Japanese populations.

This study demonstrated that a single dose of sefaxersen, from 20 to 70 mg, was tolerable in healthy Japanese participants. As a result, Japan is actively participating in the ongoing Phase 3 study of sefaxersen for patients with IgAN (IMAGINATION, NCT05797610). 21 , 22 One limitation of this study is that it involved a single dose administered to healthy Japanese male participants, while multiple doses in Japanese male and female participants with IgAN will be explored in the ongoing global Phase 3 study. 21 , 22

Conclusions

The evaluation of sefaxersen in the first‐in‐Japanese study demonstrated promising results. Plasma sefaxersen concentration demonstrated a biphasic PK profile, characterized by an initial rapid decline followed by a slow elimination. Sefaxersen selectively decreased PD markers related to the complement AP (complement FB, Bb, and AH50) in a dose‐dependent manner, and the PD effects persisted over 2 to 3 months. A single subcutaneous dose of sefaxersen in healthy Japanese participants was tolerable, and the safety profile was manageable at different doses. These findings support the further participation of Japanese patients with IgAN in the global Phase 3 study (IMAGINATION, NCT05797610).
