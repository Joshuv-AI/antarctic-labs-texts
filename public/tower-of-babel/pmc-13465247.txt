Letter to the Editor regarding “Development of a 3-day manufacturing method to generate CD19-CD20-CD22 trispecific CAR T-cells from whole blood”
Authors: Bin Zhou, Danfeng Shen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

We read with great interest the article by Vignola et al. in the Journal of Translational Medicine [1]. The authors reported a streamlined 3-day manufacturing process for trispecific CD19-CD20-CD22 CAR-T cells starting from whole blood, utilizing negative selection and a shortened culture period. This process is technically innovative, and the authors demonstrated that trispecific CAR-T cells manufactured via this approach possess more favorable phenotypic and functional characteristics than those produced via conventional 7-day culture. The effort to reduce manufacturing time and reliance on leukapheresis is highly commendable, particularly for improving accessibility in resource-limited settings. However, we wish to raise several critical methodological and translational concerns that, in our view, substantially limit the translational validity of the reported conclusions.

Firstly, the absolute yield of CAR+ cells after 3 days is far below the clinically required dose for adult patients. The authors initiated cultures with 5 × 10⁷ CD3+ T-cells (Methods, Day 0) and reported a mean fold expansion of only 0.88 at day 3 (Fig. 2A), resulting in approximately 4.4 × 10⁷ total cells. With a transduction efficiency of 47.5% (Fig. 2D), the absolute number of CAR+ cells is roughly 2 × 107, which is only 10–20% of the typical adult dose of approved CAR-T products (e.g., 1–2 × 108 CAR⁺ cells) [2]. Even for paediatric patients, this dose is marginal. The authors acknowledge a lower cell number and mitigate this limitation by showing equivalent per-cell cytotoxicity, yet in vivo antitumour activity depends on total infused CAR-T cell mass, not only on single-cell function. Without a substantial expansion step, this 3-day process cannot meet standard dosing requirements, and expanding to day 7 would defeat the purpose of “rapid” manufacturing. Furthermore, in real-world patients with advanced malignancies who have received extensive pretreatment, peripheral blood lymphocyte counts are often significantly reduced. The proposed workflow requires at least 5 × 10⁷ CD3+ T cells to initiate culture, and may thus be infeasible for a large proportion of the target population. This practical limitation, combined with the dose insufficiency, further undermines the clinical feasibility of this approach.

Secondly, the claimed advantage of a stem-like phenotype at day 3 is not supported by the statistical analysis. The authors repeatedly emphasise in the abstract and discussion that the 3-day product preserves a less differentiated, stem-like state. However, as shown in Fig. 2F, the frequency of stem cell-like memory T (Tscm) cells increased from 43.0% on day 3 to 52.7% on day 7, and the authors explicitly noted that “this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.43)”. Similarly, other subset comparisons showed no significant differences. Therefore, the core conclusion that short-term culture yields a more stem-like phenotype may be overinterpreted. Furthermore, the study lacks any in vivo evidence to support the speculated superior persistence of the 3-day product. The functional relevance of a stem-like phenotype is not established by surface-marker expression alone, but rather requires formal demonstration of in vivo persistence and reconstitution capacity [3]. In the present study, although the authors observed upregulation of SELL (CD62L) and multiple T-cell receptor-associated transcripts (e.g., TRAV8-3, TRBV27), they provided no xenogeneic mouse model or human pharmacokinetic data. Therefore, the authors’ conclusion that the 3-day product “may offer advantages in durability” is purely hypothetical and not substantiated by functional longevity assays. Without in vivo validation, this remains an unproven speculation rather than a scientific finding.

Thirdly, the standardised methods applied in cytotoxicity assays have potential methodological limitations. In the in vitro cytotoxicity section, the authors state that effector cell concentrations for both 3-day and 7-day products were calculated based on transduction efficiency measured at day 7, because day 3 CAR expression did not show a clear peak. To our knowledge, standard practice dictates that the frequency of reactive effector cells should be referenced when calculating the effector-to-target ratio, and comparing two time points requires the contemporaneous CAR-positive fraction to determine the effector-to-target ratio [4]. The authors’ approach of back-calculating day 3 effector numbers using a future time point value is methodologically inconsistent with this principle, introduces systematic bias, and compromises the validity of the conclusion that the two products have comparable cytotoxic activity. A proper analysis should have used day 3 CAR percentages (even if less distinct) or, alternatively, should have relied on vector copy number (VCN) to estimate the true CAR-positive proportion at each time point, as VCN has been shown to correlate linearly with transduction efficiency measured by flow cytometry [5].

We sincerely congratulate the authors on their technical efforts and on advancing the field of rapid CAR-T manufacturing. However, we believe that addressing these issues — particularly the absolute cell yield and the lack of statistical support for the stemness claim — is essential before this method can be considered a viable alternative to standard protocols. We look forward to seeing further studies that incorporate larger patient cohorts, internal validation, and in vivo efficacy data to verify the true clinical potential of this shortened process.

Sincerely,

All Authors.
