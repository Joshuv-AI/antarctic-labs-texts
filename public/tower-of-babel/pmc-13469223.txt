Extraperitoneal colostomy reduces parastomal hernia after laparoscopic abdominoperineal resection: a propensity score–matched study
Authors: Jiyun Li, Jichuan Quan, Zixing Zhu, Dedi Jiang, Zhixun Zhao, Mingguang Zhang, Haitao Zhou, Wei Pei, Jianjun Bi, Qiang Feng, Zheng Wang, Qian Liu, Zhaoxu Zheng, Jianwei Liang, Li Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeParastomal hernia (PSH) is one of the most frequent long-term complications following abdominoperineal resection (APR) for rectal cancer. The optimal colostomy route to minimize PSH remains controversial. This study aimed to compare PSH risk between extraperitoneal colostomy (EPC) and transperitoneal colostomy (TPC) after laparoscopic APR.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including patients who underwent laparoscopic APR for rectal cancer between 2014 and 2017. Patients were categorized according to colostomy route (EPC vs. TPC). The primary endpoint was PSH, and secondary endpoints included other short- and long-term stoma-related complications and perioperative outcomes. Propensity score matching (1:3) was applied to balance baseline characteristics. Risk factors for PSH were further analyzed using logistic regression.ResultsA total of 464 patients were included. After matching, 102 patients in the EPC group and 243 in the TPC group were analyzed. Perioperative outcomes and overall stoma-related complication rates were comparable between groups. However, PSH occurred less frequently in the EPC group than in the TPC group (10/102 [9.8%] vs. 79/243 [32.5%], P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression demonstrated that EPC was an independent protective factor against PSH (OR 0.190, 95% CI 0.089–0.406, P < 0.001), whereas increasing age and female sex were significant risk factors.ConclusionExtraperitoneal colostomy was associated with a lower risk of PSH after laparoscopic APR for rectal cancer without increasing perioperative morbidity. These findings support consideration of the extraperitoneal route in experienced centers to improve long-term stoma outcomes, while prospective multicenter studies are needed for further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05166-y.

Introduction

Permanent colostomy is an integral component of abdominoperineal resection (APR), which remains the standard surgical treatment for low rectal cancer [1, 2]. While advances in laparoscopic techniques have improved short-term outcomes, long-term stoma-related complications, especially parastomal hernia (PSH), remain a significant clinical challenge [3–5]. PSH can cause pain, impair appliance function, lead to skin breakdown, and may ultimately require surgical repair, substantially compromising patients’ quality of life [6–8].

Traditionally, colostomies are constructed via the transperitoneal colostomy (TPC) approach; however, the extraperitoneal colostomy (EPC) technique has gained attention because of its potential to reduce PSH by avoiding direct fascial defects and limiting bowel mobility [9–12]. Several retrospective studies and a growing body of comparative analyses suggest that EPC may lower the risk of PSH and other complications, including stoma prolapse and retraction, without compromising safety [13–16]. Nevertheless, concerns remain regarding the technical complexity of EPC, its potential to prolong operative time, and conflicting results in early complications such as stoma edema and bowel obstruction [9, 13, 17, 18]. To date, evidence remains limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous surgical expertise, and lack of long-term follow-up. Moreover, few studies have rigorously adjusted for baseline confounders between stoma techniques.

Therefore, we conducted a propensity score-matched retrospective study to compare perioperative outcomes and stoma-related complications between extraperitoneal and transperitoneal colostomy in patients undergoing laparoscopic APR for rectal cancer. We further aimed to identify independent risk factors for parastomal hernia.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and approved by the institutional review board (Approval No. 23/180–3922). Patients who underwent laparoscopic APR for histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma between January 2014 and December 2017 were reviewed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) elective laparoscopic APR with permanent sigmoid colostomy, and (2) availability of complete clinical, pathological, and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria included emergency surgery, conversion to open surgery, presence of synchronous distant metastases at diagnosis, or loss to follow-up within 12 months postoperatively. According to the colostomy route, patients were categorized into either the EPC or TPC group. The study period was predefined to allow a sufficiently long and relatively uniform follow-up window for the assessment of long-term stoma outcomes, particularly parastomal hernia. Data collection

Clinical and pathological data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical records. Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking and alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, history of abdominal surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, and receipt of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Tumor characteristics included pathological T and N stage, tumor size, and tumor distance from the anal verge. Perioperative outcomes included operative time, estimated blood loss, time to first flatus, blood transfusion, and postoperative hospital stay.

The primary endpoint of this study was PSH. Secondary endpoints included other stoma-related complications and perioperative outcomes. Stoma-related complications were classified as short-term or long-term based on a 30-day postoperative cutoff. Short-term stoma complications (occurring within 30 days) included stoma edema, stoma bleeding, mucocutaneous separation, ischemia or necrosis of the stoma, and small bowel obstruction. Long-term stoma complications (occurring beyond 30 days) included PSH, peristomal skin inflammation, stoma retraction, stoma stenosis, and stoma prolapse. PSH was diagnosed based on clinical examination and/or radiologic findings identified during follow-up, primarily on abdominal computed tomography performed as part of routine oncologic surveillance or when clinically indicated. Because of the retrospective nature of the study, a formal standardized grading classification for PSH was not uniformly available for all patients.

Colostomy technique

All procedures were performed by experienced colorectal surgeons (each with > 300 laparoscopic colorectal surgeries) using a standardized laparoscopic approach. The technique for colostomy creation differed between groups. In the extraperitoneal group, the left-sided peritoneum was incised laparoscopically near the suspensory ligament of the sigmoid colon, and a 6-cm-wide extraperitoneal tunnel was developed. A 2.5-cm circular skin incision was made in the left lower quadrant, followed by transverse incision of the anterior rectus sheath. The rectus muscle was bluntly separated, and the posterior sheath and abdominal wall were dissected to connect with the extraperitoneal tunnel. The sigmoid colon stump, clamped atraumatically, was delivered through the tunnel and brought to the skin surface approximately 2 cm. The bowel wall was sutured to the anterior sheath and skin with interrupted sutures after mucosal eversion.

In the transperitoneal group, a similar skin incision was made. Dissection proceeded through the anterior rectus sheath, rectus abdominis, posterior sheath, and peritoneum. The sigmoid colon was delivered through the transperitoneal route and matured under direct vision through the stoma incision, with fixation to the anterior sheath and skin. In both groups, the colostomy was matured using a modified Brooke technique, and no prophylactic mesh was used.

Follow-up

All patients adhered to a standardized follow-up schedule: initial evaluation at 2 weeks postoperatively, followed by assessments every 3 months during the first year, every 6 months during the second year, and annually thereafter. Follow-up evaluations were conducted through outpatient clinic visits or telephone consultations and included physical examination, laboratory tests, and imaging studies such as abdominal computed tomography or ultrasonography according to routine oncologic surveillance and clinical indications. The final follow-up was completed in January 2025.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). For continuous variables, normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

To minimize baseline differences and reduce selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied using the nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.05 and a 1:3 matching ratio. A 1:3 ratio was chosen to maximize the use of the larger transperitoneal cohort while maintaining adequate comparability and statistical efficiency, given the smaller size of the extraperitoneal group. Matching variables included age, sex, BMI, smoking and alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes, history of abdominal surgery, ASA score, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, tumor size, tumor location from the anal verge, and pathological T and N stages. Covariate balance after matching was primarily assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable, and was further evaluated by comparison of baseline characteristics between the matched groups. Details of covariate balance before and after matching are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Univariate logistic regression was used to explore potential predictors of PSH. Variables with a P value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model using backward stepwise logistic regression. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Before PSM, the extraperitoneal and transperitoneal groups differed significantly in sex distribution (P = 0.049), tumor size (3.50 [3.00–4.50] vs. 3.50 [2.50–4.00] cm, P = 0.027), tumor location from the anal verge (4.00 [2.25–4.00] vs. 3.00 [2.00–4.00] cm, P = 0.003), and pathological T stage (P = 0.041), indicating baseline imbalance. No significant differences were found in age, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, ASA score, or pathological N stage (all P > 0.05).

After 1:3 PSM, 102 patients in the extraperitoneal group and 243 in the transperitoneal group were included in the matched cohort. Post-matching comparisons revealed no significant differences in baseline variables, including age, sex, BMI, smoking and drinking status, comorbidities, history of abdominal surgery, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, ASA score, tumor size, tumor location, and pathological T and N stages (all P > 0.05), indicating adequate covariate balance between the two groups.

Likewise, long-term complications such as peristomal skin inflammation (35/102 [34.3%] vs. 88/243 [36.2%], P = 0.737), stoma retraction (10/102 [9.8%] vs. 23/243 [9.5%], P = 0.922), stoma stenosis (7/102 [6.9%] vs. 16/243 [6.6%], P = 0.925), and stoma prolapse (5/102 [4.9%] vs. 19/243 [7.8%], P = 0.331) were comparable between the groups. However, PSH occurred less frequently in the extraperitoneal group than in the transperitoneal group (10/102 [9.8%] vs. 79/243 [32.5%], P < 0.001). These findings prompted further analysis to identify independent predictors of PSH.

Risk factors for parastomal hernia

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of PSH following laparoscopic APR, as shown in Table 4. In univariate analysis, increasing age (OR = 1.084, 95% CI: 1.056–1.113, P < 0.001), female sex (OR = 2.123, 95% CI: 1.302–3.462, P = 0.003), and extraperitoneal colostomy (OR = 0.226, 95% CI: 0.111–0.457, P < 0.001) were associated with PSH. Other variables, including BMI, comorbidities, prior abdominal surgery, neoadjuvant therapy, ASA score, and tumor-related factors, were not associated with PSH. In multivariate analysis, older age (OR = 1.093, 95% CI: 1.063–1.123, P < 0.001), female sex (OR = 2.416, 95% CI: 1.393–4.202, P = 0.002), and stoma route remained independent predictors. Discussion

In this propensity score-matched study, extraperitoneal colostomy was associated with a substantially lower incidence of parastomal hernia than transperitoneal colostomy after laparoscopic abdominoperineal resection. In the matched cohort, PSH occurred in 10 of 102 patients (9.8%) in the EPC group and 79 of 243 patients (32.5%) in the TPC group. Multivariate analysis further identified EPC as an independent protective factor against PSH, whereas older age and female sex were independent risk factors. These findings suggest that the route of stoma construction may play an important role in long-term stoma-related outcomes.

Our results are consistent with previous studies showing lower PSH rates after EPC. Earlier reports by Hamada et al., Leroy et al., and Hino et al. suggested that the extraperitoneal route may reduce the risk of PSH after laparoscopic APR [9, 10, 18]. More recent studies by Liao et al., Zhang et al., Wang et al., and Dong et al. have also supported the potential benefit of EPC [12, 15, 16, 19]. In addition, a recent meta-analysis confirmed that EPC was associated with lower rates of PSH and other stoma-related complications without a clear increase in operative burden [17]. Further evidence from recent retrospective studies also indicates that transperitoneal colostomy, along with higher BMI and older age, may be associated with an increased risk of PSH [20, 21]. Against this background, our study adds evidence from a relatively large single-center cohort with long-term follow-up and propensity score matching.

The lower incidence of PSH in the EPC group may be explained by the mechanical characteristics of the extraperitoneal tunnel. Compared with the transperitoneal route, the extraperitoneal tunnel may provide better support around the bowel, reduce lateral mobility of the stoma, and decrease the direct transmission of intra-abdominal pressure to the abdominal wall defect. These features may help reduce hernia formation. Importantly, no prophylactic mesh was used in either group, allowing the effect of stoma route itself to be assessed more directly.

Other perioperative and stoma-related outcomes were broadly comparable between the two groups. We did not observe meaningful differences in operative time, blood loss, postoperative recovery, or most short- and long-term stoma complications. These findings are generally consistent with previous reports suggesting that EPC can be performed safely in experienced centers [12, 15, 17]. In our cohort, no significant difference was observed in small bowel obstruction. Although some previous studies reported a lower obstruction rate with EPC [11, 13], this potential benefit was not confirmed in the present study.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may be relevant to centers seeking to improve long-term stoma outcomes after APR. The observed reduction in PSH suggests that stoma route selection may represent a modifiable technical factor in procedures requiring permanent colostomy. At the same time, the generalizability of this approach should be interpreted with caution, as all procedures in the present study were performed in a high-volume center by experienced colorectal surgeons. As robotic-assisted rectal surgery becomes more widely adopted, future studies should explore whether robotic platforms may facilitate extraperitoneal tunnel creation, improve technical reproducibility, or shorten the learning curve of EPC. However, direct evidence regarding robotic-assisted EPC remains limited at present.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and residual confounding may persist despite propensity score matching. Second, surgeon-related selection bias cannot be fully excluded because the stoma route was not randomized. Third, PSH was diagnosed on the basis of clinical evaluation and/or radiologic findings during follow-up, but a formal standardized grading classification was not uniformly available because of the retrospective design. Fourth, no formal sensitivity analysis was performed to test the robustness of the matching strategy. Finally, although long-term follow-up was available, the absence of more recent cases may limit the contemporary generalizability of the findings. Therefore, prospective multicenter studies are still needed to confirm the benefit of EPC and better define the patients most likely to benefit.

Conclusion

This propensity score-matched study suggests that extraperitoneal colostomy may be a safe and effective option in laparoscopic abdominoperineal resection for rectal cancer and is associated with a lower incidence of parastomal hernia. These findings support consideration of this approach in experienced centers, while prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm its benefit and define its optimal indications.
