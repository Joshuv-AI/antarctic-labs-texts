Abstract
. This article investigates the well-posedness of weak solutions to non-linear parabolic PDEs driven by rough coefficients with rough initial data in critical homogeneous Besov spaces. Well-posedness is understood in the sense of existence and uniqueness of maximal weak solutions in suitable weighted Z \mathrm{Z} -spaces in the absence of smallness conditions. We showcase our theory with an application to rough reaction–diffusion equations. Subsequent articles will treat further classes of equations, including equations of Burgers-type and quasi-linear problems, using the same approach. Our toolkit includes a novel theory of hypercontractive singular integral operators (SIOs) on weighted Z \mathrm{Z} -spaces and a self-improving property for super-linear reverse Hölder inequalities.

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

This article aims to contribute to the broad study of nonlinear evolution equations such as the nonlinear heat equation and other reaction–diffusion systems, the Navier–Stokes equations and other fluid models, moving interface problems and geometric flows

.

We concentrate on second-order equations on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the abstract type

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Div(a(t,x,u)\nabla u)=F(u,\nabla u),\quad u(0)=u_{0}.

Systems and higher-order equations will be outlined in Section
1.6
.
Our goal is to establish existence, regularity and uniqueness of local and global weak solutions when the diffusion matrix is rough, typically only measurable, and initial data are taken from scaling-critical smoothness spaces of negative regularity.
Such a result is out of reach of the current state of the art. Hence, we develop a novel framework including new harmonic-analysis and PDE tools, which employs weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces as solution spaces and homogeneous Besov spaces as initial data spaces. Then, we showcase our framework by obtaining existence, uniqueness and regularity of a maximal weak solution to the rough reaction–diffusion equation

(RD)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=\phi(u),\quad u(0)=u_{0}.

Here, the non-linearity
\phi(u)
has a local Lipschitz property and polynomial growth of order
1+\rho
, and
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a merely bounded, measurable, elliptic coefficient matrix.
We state a precise well-posedness result for (
RD
) in Theorem
7.1
and restrict ourselves here to an informal version illustrating the flavor of our theory.

Theorem

(Well-posedness of (
RD
))
.

Fix the growth parameter
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
of the non-linearity
\phi
. Let
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
, a scaling-critical Besov space with respect to the non-linearity. Then, there exists a weak solution
u
of (
RD
) which is unique and maximal among the solutions satisfying

u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}\quad\&\quad\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

for admissible choices of
r
and
q
.

The exponent
q
of the space
\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\beta}
expresses local integrability, and some
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-averages parameterized by the upper half-space enjoy a global
\mathrm{L}^{r}
-integrability at a scale determined by the weight parameter
\beta
. In other words, the scale of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces captures a local-global control on functions.
For weak solutions it is crucial that
\nabla u
belongs to a weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space of locally square-integrable functions, whereas the local integrability parameter
q
for
u
enjoys greater flexibility below or above
2
.

Note that (
RD
) recovers the classical non-linear heat equation describing reaction–diffusion processes in a homogeneous, isotropic medium when
A\equiv\mathrm{Id}
. To study reaction–diffusion processes in media such as biological tissue, compound materials or porous materials, the diffusivity of the respective medium has to be encoded by the matrix
A
. As an illustrative example, consider the spreading of a tumor in the brain
[
39
]
. Then, the diffusivity jumps between areas of gray and white matter, so that
A
is indeed a real bounded elliptic matrix which is only measurable, lacking any form of continuity.

Other models for our theory are, for instance, problems of Burgers-type, quasi-linear problems, or the generalized harmonic map heat flow. These and others are outlined in Section
1.6
. To keep the length of the present article reasonable, we develop our tools and approach in full generality, but postpone applications to other models than (
RD
) to subsequent work.

Next, we discuss some central challenges appearing in our well-posedness theory and indicate how they are resolved within the framework of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces and homogeneous Besov spaces.

1.2.
Hypercontractivity as a main tool

We ignore the initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}
for the moment and take a look at the non-linear term
\phi(u)
instead. Assume that
u
belongs to the solution space
X\coloneqq\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\beta}
. Then
\phi(u)
does
not
belong again to
X
because
\phi
is of super-linear growth. Consequently, the parabolic solution operator
(\partial_{t}-\Div(A\nabla))^{-1}
has to compensate the loss in the integrability and weight parameters caused by the non-linearity in order to be able to map back into
X
. We call this regularizing effect
hypercontractivity
throughout this article.
To obtain it, one can rely on

(a)

parabolic Sobolev embeddings,

(b)

hypercontractivity of the heat semigroup on Lebesgue spaces.

In the presence of rough coefficients, (a) is not exploitable to obtain sufficient hypercontractivity, see Remark
1.1
below. Instead, we exploit (b) in our framework. To do so, we introduce a class of hypercontractive singular integral operators (SIOs) in Definition
3.1
which generalizes the usual notion of an SIO.
Our SIOs do not, in general, possess a representation by a pointwise kernel (the
“beyond Calderón–Zygmund”
-regime), which makes them amenable to solution operators with rough coefficients.
By virtue of the hypercontractivity of heat semigroups (generated by
L\coloneqq-\Div(A\nabla)
), solution operators to parabolic problems with rough coefficients are hypercontractive SIOs, see Section
4
.
Hypercontractivity of the solution operators follows from the following extrapolation result, see Theorem
3.4
.

Theorem

(Hypercontractive SIOs on weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces)
.

Let
S
be an SIO of type
(q,r,\kappa,\infty)
, where
q,r\in(1,\infty]
and
\kappa\in[0,1]
satisfying
r\geq q
. Then, for
p\in[q,\infty]
and
\beta>-1
, the operator
S
extends to a bounded operator
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}\to\mathrm{Z}^{p,r}_{\beta+\kappa}
.

Usual SIOs correspond to the case
q=r
, and this is a special instance of our result. Owing to the theorem, the losses in the local integrability parameter
q
and in the weight parameter
\beta
caused by the non-linearity
\phi
can indeed be compensated. To gain in the global integrability parameter
p
, we use the
“Hardy–Sobolev”
-type embedding in Proposition
2.4
.
Hence, non-linear terms can be effectively controlled in weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces.

In applications to parabolic PDEs, the integrability parameter
r
is restricted in terms of parabolic Sobolev conjugates of
q
. To push things further in order to reach local boundedness of solutions to rough non-linear PDEs, we provide a
bootstrapping version
of the extrapolation result in Corollary
3.10
, which relaxes the tight relation between the different integrability parameters, and is hence applicable in the non-linear iteration argument presented in Proposition
7.7
.

Remark 1.1

(Limitations of parabolic Sobolev embeddings)
.

The parabolic Sobolev embeddings in (a) are the foundation of the maximal regularity approach
[
28
,
20
,
41
,
17
,
42
,
43
]
, in which one proves mixed weighted
\mathrm{L}^{p}_{t}(\mathrm{L}^{q}_{x})
estimates. This approach allows one to obtain good results for the Laplace operator (that is, when
A\equiv\mathrm{Id}
) or, by perturbation techniques, for
-\Div(A\nabla)
when
A
possesses some regularity
[
21
,
34
]
. However, when
A
is only measurable, the parameter
q
cannot be taken large for the operator
\nabla(\partial_{t}-\Div(A\nabla))^{-1}
used in the maximal regularity approach.
Therefore, since Sobolev embeddings become powerful only when the integrability parameter
q
can be taken large, (a) does not provide sufficient hypercontractivity in the case of rough coefficients. For instance, important cases such as the Allen–Cahn non-linearity
\phi(u)=-u^{3}+u
cannot be considered by the maximal regularity approach in the physical dimension
n=3
when the coefficients are rough, see for instance
[
1
]
.

1.3.
Rough initial data

By previous work of the authors together with Haardt
[
5
]
, homogeneous Besov spaces of negative regularity admit a caloric characterization in weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces. More precisely, one has
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
if and only if
\mathrm{e}^{t\Delta}u_{0}\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{2}}}
for all
q\in(1,\infty]
. When
-\Delta
is replaced by
L\coloneqq-\Div(A\nabla)
, mild assumptions on
\alpha\in(-1,0)
and
p\in(1,\infty]
guarantee that the free evolution
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})
of
u_{0}
still belongs to
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{2}}}
, see
[
10
]
for the case
q=2
and Section
5
for
q\in(1,\infty]
.

Moreover, the free evolution
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})
solves the initial value problem in the weak sense and
u_{0}
is attained in the sense of Definition
2.14
.
Consequently, homogeneous Besov spaces are the natural initial data spaces within our framework.

1.4.
Solutions and their regularity

The principal notion of solutions for us is the one of weak solutions in the sense of PDEs. It is a flexible and operator-free notion, physically meaningful, and the initial data are attained in a natural sense. By definition, weak solutions have a locally square-integrable gradient. As said, this is captured by the condition
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
in our well-posedness result for (
RD
).

Besides weak solutions, we also work with
“mild solutions”
in weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces as a first step towards existence, see Section
7
. They are well-suited to capitalize on the hypercontractivity results presented above.
In particular, hypercontractivity of the solution operators allows us to show existence and uniqueness of mild solutions to non-linear PDEs, in particular to (
RD
).
An important difference between mild and weak solutions is that mild solutions do not demand any knowledge on their gradients.

To bridge between mild and weak solutions, regularity theory for both types of solution plays a major role in our approach. Our results can be summarized as follows:

•

If
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{0},q_{0}}_{\beta_{0}}
is a mild solution to a non-linear PDE, then
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\beta}
for a wide range of parameters
r
,
q
and
\beta
dictated by the equation.

•

If
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q_{0}}_{\beta}
is a weak solution to a non-linear PDE, then
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\beta}
for an improved value of
q
.

•

Building on these regularity results, a mild–weak transference principle, identifying mild solutions as weak solutions and vice versa, holds.

We postpone the discussion of weak solutions to the next subsection. As for mild solutions, call
u
a mild solution to (
RD
) if it satisfies the identity
u=\mathcal{L}(\phi(u))+\mathcal{E}(u_{0})
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a hypercontractive solution operator applied to the non-linearity
\phi(u)
, and
\mathcal{E}(u_{0})
is the free evolution of the initial datum
u_{0}
. Since the identity is implicit, an iteration argument using hypercontractivity of
\mathcal{L}
yields self-improvement of
u
to various weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces (Proposition
7.7
).

1.5.
Weak solutions to non-linear PDEs

Instead of developing a well-posedness theory for weak solutions from scratch, we transfer existence, regularity and uniqueness of mild solutions to weak solutions. This is the mild–weak transference principle just mentioned. To obtain existence of weak solutions, it suffices to identify mild solutions as weak solutions. As for uniqueness, we have to reverse the order of things and identify a weak solution as a mild solution. Then, uniqueness of mild solutions is inherited to weak solutions.

For both directions, establishing
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\beta}
is the main obstacle, where
\beta\coloneqq\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. We need
\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2_{*}}_{\beta}
in order to leverage our SIO-theory. Here,
2_{*}
is the lower parabolic Sobolev conjugate of
2
. The regularity theory for mild solutions ensures this property, and allows the passage from mild solutions to weak solutions. If
u
is a weak solution, the local integrability of
u
is in general not sufficient to guarantee this property for
\phi(u)
.

To resolve this problem, we first establish reverse Hölder inequalities of
“super-linear type”
for weak solutions to (
RD
) in Lemma
8.6
. They read

(RH)

(Self-improvement of super-linear RH-inequalities)
.

The RH-inequality of super-linear type (

\theta>1
such that, for
\lambda>1

.

Given the non-linearity, the number

). Note that in contrast with the linear case,
q
cannot go down to zero here.
At this stage, we can exploit the inequality to improve the local integrability exponent for
u
in weighted

u
as a weak solution.

1.6.
Outlook: further non-linear models

Besides the reaction term
\phi(u)
, our framework applies to Burgers-type non-linearities
\Div(\Phi(u))
, where
\Phi(u)
is a vector field of polynomial growth enjoying a local Lipschitz condition.
Such non-linear terms in the case of quadratic growth were investigated in
[
8
]
.
Compared to the reaction–diffusion case, the ranges of parameters have to be adjusted to the different types of non-linearities. This has the effect that the end-point Besov space with
p=\infty
can be reached, which is not the case for the reaction–diffusion equation presented here.
For this, we need to introduce separable subspaces
{}_{\mathrm{v}}\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}
and
{}_{\mathrm{v}}\mathrm{Z}^{\infty,q}_{\beta}
of the respective Besov and weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces, see Section
2.1
, and all linear results in Sections
3
to
6
are formulated for them likewise.

Moreover, using the linear theory for
{}_{\mathrm{v}}\mathrm{Z}^{\infty,q}_{\beta}
, quasi-linear equations of the type

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Div(a(u)\nabla u)=0,\quad u(0)=u_{0}

can be treated, where
u_{0}
is taken from a suitable subspace of
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
. Note that
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
is a scaling-critical space for the non-linearity. Likewise, models such as the generalized harmonic map heat flow

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=u|\nabla u|^{2},\quad u(0)=u_{0},

fall into the scope of our framework, provided
A
has minimal smoothness in the sense of Definition
2.16
.
The case
A=\mathrm{Id}
was investigated in
[
32
]
by using weighted tent-space norms.

Beyond (
RD
), it would be interesting to study concrete systems of reaction–diffusion equations, for instance of Fitzhugh–Nagumo
[
23
,
37
]
type. Our Theorem
7.1
does not directly apply to them as it only treats equations of reaction–diffusion type. However, due to the specific structure of such systems, we expect that our approach generalizes to them.

Finally, it would be interesting to study fourth-order problems in future work. Our SIO theory is general enough to apply to them, and well-posedness of weak solutions to linear higher-order equations in divergence form was investigated for instance in
[
46
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Function spaces

We introduce the relevant function spaces for this article. First, we introduce solution spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
or on strips of the form
(a,b)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Second, we introduce spaces of initial data.

Solution spaces

Our primary solution spaces are weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces. They go back to
[
14
]
and were discussed in more depth in
[
13
,
5
]
.

Definition 2.1

(
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces)
.

Let
p,q\in(1,\infty]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The space
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}
consists of all measurable functions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(6)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}}\coloneqq\Bigl(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\Bigl(\fint_{t/2}^{t}\fint_{\mathrm{B}(x,\sqrt{t})}|s^{-\beta}u(s,y)|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigr)^{\frac{p}{q}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty,

with the obvious modifications if
p=\infty
and/or
q=\infty
.
For
T\in(0,\infty]
write
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}(T)
for the space of functions consisting of
u\colon(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
whose extension by zero
E_{0}u
to a function on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
belongs to
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}
. Set
\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}(T)}\coloneqq\|E_{0}u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{\beta}}
. If
\beta=0
, we remove it from the notation, that is, we put
\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}\coloneqq\mathrm{Z}^{p,q}_{0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

The range for the parameters
p
and
q
can be enlarged to
(0,\infty]
. However, this extension is not relevant for the present article.
In
[
10
]
, weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces are defined for

2.2.
Weak solutions I

We introduce the notion of weak solutions to linear parabolic equations and collect some properties of them. Later, after a short interlude on elliptic operators in Section
2.3
, we will come back to weak solutions in Section
2.4
and recite some well-posedness results from the literature for them.

Consider a measurable, real coefficient function
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
which satisfies for some
\Lambda{(A)},\lambda{(A)}>0
the ellipticity and boundedness properties

(A)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}A(x)\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\geq\lambda{(A)}|\xi|^{2},\quad|A(x)\xi|\leq\Lambda{(A)}|\xi|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We define weak solutions to a linear parabolic equation in divergence form associated with the coefficient function

Definition 2.12

(Weak solution)
.

Let
0\leq a<b\leq\infty
and put
Q\coloneqq(a,b)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=f+\Div(F)

(17)

\displaystyle\iint_{Q}-u\partial_{t}\phi+A\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\langle f,\phi\rangle+\langle F,\nabla\phi\rangle.

The pairings on the right-hand side are the usual duality pairings between a space and its (topological) dual space.

Since

instead of

to make the integral identity (

We will treat this case in the subsequent definition.
To employ energy techniques or to investigate questions such as uniqueness of weak solutions, it is more convenient to directly include local square-integrability of

and

into the definition of a weak solution.

Definition 2.13

(Very weak solution)
.

In the situation of Definition

of (

We introduce a notion of distributional trace. This enables us to formulate initial value problems in a first step, and Cauchy problems in a second step.

Definition 2.14

(Initial condition)

Let

Elliptic operators

We recall some background on elliptic operators in divergence form with real, measurable, bounded, elliptic coefficients. Such operators already appeared in the weak formulation of linear parabolic equations in Section
2.2

A reader who is not yet familiar with notions like maximal accretivity, sectoriality and so on can consult, for instance, the monograph
[

Consider a measurable, real coefficient function

The elliptic operator

(18)

\displaystyle a\colon\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{x}\times\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{x}\to\mathbb{R},\quad(u,v)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}A\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then, there exists an unbounded, maximal accretive operator

\mathrm{L}^{2}_{x}
with

(19)

\displaystyle(Lu,v)_{2}=a(u,v),\qquad u\in\dom(L),v\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{x}.

\displaystyle|K(t,x,y)|\leq Ct^{-\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{t}}

3.
Singular integral operators on
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces: hypercontractivity

The goal of this section is to prepare the ground for the study of Duhamel operators in Section
4
with a focus on
hypercontractive properties
, that is to say, obtaining higher integrability of the output compared to the input.
Such a property is necessary in the treatment of non-linear equations.

To this end, we study singular integral operators (SIOs) on weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces. If the local integrability parameter of the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces are fixed to

[

using interpolation of corresponding results on weighted tent spaces. Our focus here is to allow the parameter

The following definition is a hypercontractive extension of the definition introduced in
[
9
]
when

. For its formulation, recall

and
\mathrm{C}_{j}(x,t)
from the notation section.

Definition 3.1

(SIOs of type
(q,r,\kappa,M)
)
.

Let
q,r\in(1,\infty]
,
\kappa\in[0,1]
and
M\in[0,\infty]
satisfying
r\geq q
and
\kappa\geq\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})
.
An operator
S
is called

of
type
(q,r,\kappa,M)
with
integral kernel

K(t,s)
, if

(a)

S
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}\to\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\kappa-\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})}
bounded.

(b)

For
0<s<t<\infty
, the kernel
K(t,s)\colon\mathrm{L}^{q}_{x}\to\mathrm{L}^{r}_{x}
satisfies for

and

the (weak) off-diagonal bound

(29)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{B}(x,\sqrt{t})}K(t,s)(f\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{C}_{j}(x,t)})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}_{x}}\lesssim(t-s)^{-1+\kappa-\frac{n}{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{r}\bigr)}\Bigl(1+\frac{d^{2}}{t-s}\Bigr)^{-M}\|\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{C}_{j}(x,t)}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}_{x}},

where
d\coloneqq\dist(\mathrm{B}(x,\sqrt{t}),\mathrm{C}_{j}(x,t))
.

(c)

For
f\in\mathrm{L}^{q}
with compact support and almost every
(t,x)\not\in\pi(f)
there holds the representation formula

(30)

\displaystyle S(f)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(K(t,s)f(s)\bigr)(x)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Remark 3.2
.

Strictly speaking, the terminology

is only justified when
\kappa=\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})
. Usually, this situation happens when

and

. Our theory gives optimal estimates in the singular and non-singular case and we refrain from making the terminology unnecessarily complicated.

Remark 3.3

(Causality)
.

Owing to property (c), if

is an SIO in the sense of Definition

, then

is

, meaning that the value of

at time

only depends on the data in the time interval

. Indeed, fix
\tau>0
and split

. Then, when

, property (c) applies to

and yields

. Therefore, causality follows.

The following is the abstract main result on SIOs of hypercontractive type.

Theorem 3.4

(Hypercontractivity of SIOs on
\mathrm{Z}

\tilde{q}
and
r
yield
\hat{q}>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
.

Step 1
: We show that
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.
Since
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\tilde{r},\tilde{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\tilde{r}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\hat{q}
is at most
\tilde{q}
, the nesting property of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces yields
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\tilde{r},\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\tilde{r}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.
Then, if
r\geq\tilde{r}
, the claim of this step follows from
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\tilde{r},\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\tilde{r}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
owing to the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding, improving
\tilde{r}
to
r
with fixed
\hat{q}
.

Otherwise, assume
r<\tilde{r}
. We want to lower
\tilde{r}
to
r
by an induction procedure. Define
(r_{j})_{j=0}^{m}
as the minimal sequence of numbers satisfying

(109)

\displaystyle r_{0}\leq r,\qquad r_{m}=\tilde{r},\qquad r_{j-1}=\frac{r_{j}}{1+\rho}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\dots,m.

We know that
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{m},\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r_{m}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
. We claim that, for
j=1,\dots,m
, one has

(110)

\displaystyle v\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{j},\hat{q}}_{\frac{n}{2r_{j}}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(v))\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{j-1},\hat{q}}_{\frac{n}{2r_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T).

Indeed,

v\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{j},\hat{q}}_{\frac{n}{2r_{j}}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\phi(v)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r_{j}}{1+\rho},\frac{\hat{q}}{1+\rho}}_{(\frac{n}{2r_{j}}-\frac{1}{\rho})(1+\rho)}(T)=\mathrm{Z}^{r_{j-1},\frac{\hat{q}}{1+\rho}}_{\frac{n}{2r_{j-1}}-\frac{1}{\rho}-1}(T),

and (
110
) follows on applying Proposition
4.3
with
\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}
in the case
\kappa=1
, provided we check that
\beta:=(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r_{j}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)>-1
,
r_{j-1}\geq\nicefrac{{\hat{q}}}{{1+\rho}}
and
\hat{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{**}(\nicefrac{{\hat{q}}}{{1+\rho}})
. The verification is similar to Corollary
7.3
. First,

(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r_{j}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)>-1
is equivalent to
r_{j}<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
, which follows from
r_{j}\leq\tilde{r}<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
. Second,
r_{j-1}\geq\nicefrac{{\hat{q}}}{{1+\rho}}
is equivalent to
r_{j}\geq\hat{q}
. By minimality of the sequence
(r_{j})_{j}
it follows
r_{j}\geq r_{1}>r
, so indeed
r_{j}>r\geq\hat{q}
by definition of
\hat{q}
. Third,
\hat{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{**}(\nicefrac{{\hat{q}}}{{1+\rho}})

follows from
\hat{q}\geq\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
.

Concerning the free evolution, we have
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
by Proposition
5.1
, taking
r\geq p
into account.
Combined with the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding of Proposition
2.4
, this yields in particular that
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{j},\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r_{j}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
for
j=1,\dots,m
, keeping
r_{j}\geq r
in mind.

Now, by virtue of (
110
) applied with
v=u
, we can start a descending induction using the representation (
108
), to arrive at

\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))\in\mathrm{Z}^{r_{0},\hat{q}}_{\frac{n}{2r_{0}}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)\subseteq\mathrm{Z}^{r,\hat{q}}_{\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T),

where we used the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces embedding in the last inclusion, taking
r\geq r_{0}
into account.
By the representation (
108
), this concludes Step 1.

Step 2
: We
show that
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\infty}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.
We know
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
from Step 1.
Using a similar iteration scheme, we want to improve
\hat{q}
to
\infty
. We claim that it is possible to pick a finite, increasing sequence
(q_{j})_{j=0}^{m}
satisfying

(111)

\displaystyle q_{0}=\hat{q},\qquad q_{m}=\infty,\qquad q_{j+1}\in\mathcal{I}^{**}\Bigl(\frac{q_{j}}{1+\rho}\Bigr)\quad\text{for}\quad j=0,\dots,m-1.

This is possible using
\hat{q}>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
, since
q^{\flat}\in[\hat{q},\infty)
implies
\tfrac{1+\rho}{q^{\flat}}-\tfrac{2}{n+2}\leq\tfrac{1}{q^{\flat}}-\delta
for a fixed
“step size”

\delta>0
.
As we want
q_{m}=\infty
, the sequence has to be constructed in such a way that
\tfrac{q_{m-1}}{1+\rho}>\tfrac{n}{2}+1
.
We prove by induction
that, for
j=0,\dots,m
, one has

u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q_{j}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.
Step 1 furnishes the initial input
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\hat{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)=\mathrm{Z}^{r,q_{0}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.
Next, assume the induction hypothesis holds up to
j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
and show it for
j+1
. This and the initial input gives us

(112)

\displaystyle\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},\frac{q_{j}}{1+\rho}}_{(\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho})(1+\rho)}(T).

Then, Corollary
4.5
applied to
\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}
yields

(113)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},q_{j+1}}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T),

provided we
check that the parameters
\beta^{\sharp}\coloneqq(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)
,
p^{\sharp}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}}
,
q^{\sharp}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{q_{j}}}{{1+\rho}}
,
\tilde{q}^{\sharp}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\hat{q}}}{{1+\rho}}
and
r^{\sharp}\coloneqq q_{j+1}
satisfy
\beta^{\sharp}>-1
,
r^{\sharp}\in\mathcal{I}^{**}(q^{\sharp})
and
p^{\sharp}\geq\tilde{q}^{\sharp}
.
Indeed,
(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)>-1
was checked in Step 1 and

q_{j+1}\in\mathcal{I}^{**}(\nicefrac{{q_{j}}}{{1+\rho}})

holds by choice of the sequence
(q_{j})_{j}
. Moreover,
p^{\sharp}\geq\tilde{q}^{\sharp}
follows from
r\geq\hat{q}
.
Eventually, using the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding, we find
\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q_{j+1}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
.

Next, using that
r\geq p
, we find that
\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})
belongs to the same space by Proposition
5.1
.
This completes the proof of the induction. As
q_{m}=\infty
, we deduce
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\infty}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
as desired.
∎

7.3.
Uniqueness of mild solutions

In this section, we show uniqueness of mild solutions. In contrast to Section
7.1
, mild solutions are not supposed to belong to a small ball in the contraction space, so that uniqueness is
not
a consequence of Banach’s contraction principle. Instead, we employ a contraction argument to show short-time coincidence of two given mild solutions, whereas their long-time coincidence relies on
instantaneous regularization
and smoothing properties of Duhamel operators.

In the following proposition, we show that there exists
at most
one mild solution to the reaction–diffusion equation.

Proposition 7.8

(Uniqueness of mild solutions)
.

Fix
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
and
T\in(0,\infty)
. Let
p\in(1,\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
and let
\alpha\in(-1,0)
satisfying
\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}=\nicefrac{{n}}{{p}}-\alpha
and
p\geq p(\alpha,A)
. Fix
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
. Then, there exists at most one mild solution
u\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
to (
RD
) with
r\in(1,\infty]
satisfying
r\geq p
and
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
.

Using the bootstrapping result for (
RD
) in Proposition
7.7
, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 7.9
.

The conclusion of Proposition
7.8
stays true for
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
when
r,q\in(1,\infty]
satisfy
r\geq p
,
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
and
q>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
be a mild solution to (
RD
). By Proposition
7.7
, we find
u\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
. Hence, Proposition
7.8
entails that there is at most one such
u
.
∎

The strategy of proof is classical and was employed in other settings and for various types of equations, see for instance
[
36
]
.

Proof of Proposition
7.8
.

Suppose that
u,v\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
are mild solutions to (
RD
). To ease notation, put
\mathrm{X}(t)\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{r}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(t)
for any
t>0
. We are going to show that
u=v
.

Step 1
: Coincidence for a short time. Subtract the two solutions to find
u-v=\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u)-\phi(v))
. Arguing as in Lemma
7.5
, we find for
0<\tau\leq T
that

(114)

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)}\leq C\Bigl(\|u\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)}^{\rho}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)}^{\rho}\Bigr)\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)},

where
C
is a constant independent of
\tau
,
u
and
v
. Take
\tau
small enough (depending on
u
and
v
) so that
C\bigl(\|u\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)}^{\rho}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{X}(\tau)}^{\rho}\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}
. It follows
u=v
on
[0,\tau]
.

Step 2
: Coincidence for large times. It remains to show
u=v
on
[\tau,T]
. To do so, we use the following instantaneous regularization property: there exists
M>0
such that

(115)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,T)}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,T)}\leq M.

Let
\varepsilon>0
such that
\tau+\varepsilon\leq T
.
We are going to show that
u=v
on
[\tau,\tau+\varepsilon]
when
\varepsilon
is small enough.
To this end, define
q
via
\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}}+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n+2}}
. From
r>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
and
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
it follows
q>1
, and
r>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
yields
q<\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}}
. Hence, for a suitable
p^{\sharp}\in(1,\infty)
, we can write

(116)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1+\rho}{r}+\frac{1}{p^{\sharp}}.

From Corollary
4.4
it follows that
\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}\colon\mathrm{L}^{q}_{-\nicefrac{{2}}{{n+2}}}\to\mathrm{L}^{r}
with operator norm bounded by a constant
C
independent of
T
. Hence, using
\phi(u)-\phi(v)=0
on
[0,\tau]
, Hölder’s inequality with (
116
) and the local Lipschitz condition (
100
), we find for some
\nu>0
independent of
\varepsilon
that

(117)

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\phi(u)-\phi(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}_{-\frac{2}{n+2}}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}

(118)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\nu}C(T)\|\phi(u)-\phi(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho}}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\nu}C_{L}C(T)\Bigl(\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,T)}^{\rho}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,T)}^{\rho}\Bigr)\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)},

where
C(T)
is a constant depending on
T
.
Hence, taking (
115
) into account, deduce

(120)

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}\leq\varepsilon^{\nu}C_{L}C(T)2M^{\rho}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}.

Thus, we can take
\varepsilon
small to obtain

\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\tau,\tau+\varepsilon)}
and hence
u=v
on
[\tau,\tau+\varepsilon]
. Since (
115
) is a bound on
[\tau,T]
, we can iterate the argument with a uniform step size
\varepsilon
to eventually obtain
u=v
on
[\tau,T]
as desired.
∎

8.
Weak solutions for the reaction–diffusion problem

So far, we have only treated mild solutions to (
RD
). This is going to change now. In the current section, we will establish existence, uniqueness and regularity of weak solutions for it.

8.1.
Existence of local weak solutions

The starting point in the construction of local weak solutions is, of course, the existence of local mild solutions, which we already have at hand. A central challenge is that we have to investigate their gradients now in order to identify them as weak solutions. To overcome this challenge, we employ the bootstrapping result for mild solutions to bring us back into a locally square-integrable setting.

Definition 8.1

(Weak solution)
.

Let
\tau\in(0,\infty]
.
Call
(u,\tau)
a weak solution to (
RD
) if
u,\nabla u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\tau)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\phi(u)\in\mathrm{L}^{2_{*}}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\tau)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u
is a weak solution to the equation
\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=\phi(u)
on
(0,\tau)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the sense of Definition
2.12
which attains
u(0)=u_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.14
.

If
\tau
is clear from the context, we simply say that
u
is a weak solution to (
RD
).

The lower Sobolev exponent
2_{*}
of
2
for source terms is a natural condition from scaling considerations.

With a precise definition of a weak solution at hand, we establish existence of local weak solutions in the following proposition.

Proposition 8.2

(Existence of local weak solutions)
.

Fix
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
,
p\in(1,\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
and let
\alpha\in(-1,0)
satisfying
\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}=\nicefrac{{n}}{{p}}-\alpha
and
p\geq p(\alpha,A)
. Fix
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
. Let
r\in(1,\infty]
satisfying
r\geq p
and
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
.
Then, there exists a weak solution
(u,T)
of (
RD
) satisfying
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\infty}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.
In particular,
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
for any
q\in(1,\infty]
.

Proof.

By Proposition
7.6
, there exist
T\in(0,\infty)
,
\tilde{r}\in(1,\infty]
satisfying
\tilde{r}\geq p
and
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<\tilde{r}<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
, and
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\tilde{r}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\tilde{r}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
so that
u
is a mild solution to (
RD
). Using bootstrapping (Proposition
7.7
), the regularity
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\infty}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
follows.
It remains to show that
u
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.

Recall that by definition,
u=\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})+\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))\eqqcolon v+w
. Owing to Proposition
5.1
,
v
is a weak solution to

(121)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\Div(A\nabla v)=0,\qquad v(0)=u_{0},

satisfying
\nabla v\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
. Therefore, we are left to show that
w=\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))
is a weak solution to

(122)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w-\Div(A\nabla w)=\phi(u),\qquad w(0)=0,

satisfying
\nabla w\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.
To this end, we aim to invoke Proposition
2.20
.
By the nesting property of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces, there holds
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,2(1+\rho)}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
. Consequently

(123)

\displaystyle\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{(\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho})(1+\rho)}(T).

Recall that
\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}}>1
by (
98
).
Thus, with
f=\phi(u)
,
p^{\sharp}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}}
and
\beta^{\sharp}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{n(1+\rho)}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}>-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we can indeed apply Proposition
2.20
.
Hence,
w
is a weak solution to (
122
) satisfying

\nabla w\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}}(T)\subseteq{\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\frac{n}{2p}-\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}}(T),}

where we used the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding in conjunction with Lemma
7.2
in the last inclusion.
Therefore,
u=v+w
is by construction a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
\nabla u\in{\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)}
. This completes the proof.
∎

8.2.
Bootstrapping regularity – weak solutions

On the side of mild solutions, we have developed a thorough regularity theory using bootstrapping methods in Section
7.2
. They were crucial in the passage from mild to weak solutions in Section
8.1
. For instance, we have used bootstrapping for a mild solution
u
to argue that
\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}},2}_{(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)}
in (
123
), which was an essential step to identify
u
as a weak solution. In the next section, we are going in the opposite direction: we will show that a weak solution
u
is also a mild solution, so that we can infer its uniqueness from Section
7.3
. To do so, the question whether
\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\nicefrac{{r}}{{1+\rho}},2_{*}}_{(\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}})(1+\rho)}
will pop up. A priori, this information is not always available. Using a bootstrapping procedure for weak solutions, we are going to close this gap in the present subsection.

The tools to achieve improved regularity for weak solutions are super-linear reverse Hölder inequalities (we simply write RH-inequalities) and their self-improvement. To the best of our knowledge, this is a new technique that has not yet been employed in the literature.

8.2.1.
Self-improvement of super-linear RH-inequalities

We formulate this section in the scope of a doubling metric measure space
(M,\dist,\mu)
. Here,
doubling
means that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\mu(\mathrm{B}(x,2r))\leq C\mu(\mathrm{B}(x,r))
for every
x\in M
and
r\in(0,\diam(M))
, where
\mathrm{B}(x,r)
is a ball in
M
of radius
r
around
x
with respect to the distance
\dist
.

Example 8.3
.

The Euclidean half-space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
equipped with the (dilated) parabolic distance
\dist((t,x),(s,y))\coloneqq\max(2\sqrt{|t-s|},|x-y|)
and the usual Lebesgue measure is an example of a doubling metric measure space. Note that Whitney boxes are balls with respect to that distance.

In the Euclidean setting, the linear case of the following result goes back to
[
29
]
. Our proof builds on the ideas presented in
[
16
]
, in which the linear case was generalized to doubling metric measure spaces.

Theorem 8.4

(Self-improvement of super-linear RH-inequalities)
.

Let
s^{\sharp},p^{\sharp}\in(0,\infty]
satisfying
s^{\sharp}<p^{\sharp}
,
q^{\sharp},r^{\sharp}\in(s^{\sharp},p^{\sharp})
and
\alpha^{\sharp}\geq 1
. Assume that there are
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

(124)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{\sharp}}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{p^{\sharp}}+\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{s^{\sharp}},\qquad\frac{1}{q^{\sharp}}=\frac{\beta_{2}}{p^{\sharp}}+\frac{1-\beta_{2}}{s^{\sharp}},\qquad\beta_{2}\alpha^{\sharp}=\beta_{1}.

Let
B
be a ball. Assume that there are
\lambda>1
and
v\in\mathrm{L}^{p^{\sharp}}(\lambda B)
non-negative such that

(RH)

\displaystyle\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-15.61713pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-13.65376pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-9.28192pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-8.51692pt}}\!\iint_{\widetilde{B}}v^{p^{\sharp}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\sharp}}}\lesssim{\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-15.61713pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-13.65376pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-9.28192pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-8.51692pt}}\!\iint_{2\widetilde{B}}v^{r^{\sharp}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r^{\sharp}}}+\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-15.61713pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-13.65376pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-9.28192pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-8.51692pt}}\!\iint_{2\widetilde{B}}v^{q^{\sharp}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha^{\sharp}}{q^{\sharp}}}}

holds for all balls
\widetilde{B}
satisfying
2\widetilde{B}\subseteq\lambda B
.
Then, there also holds the improved super-linear RH-inequality

(125)

\displaystyle A({B^{\prime}})\lesssim\sum_{i}A(B_{i})\lesssim\sum_{i}\widetilde{A}(B_{i})^{{1-\beta_{1}}}\Bigl(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-15.29988pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-13.1324pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-10.82335pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rotatebox[origin]{15.0}{${-}\mkern-3.5mu{-}$}$}}\kern-10.05836pt}}\!\iint_{B_{i}}v^{p^{\sharp}}\Bigr)^{\frac{\beta_{1}}{p^{\sharp}}}.

\displaystyle A({B^{\prime}})

Step 1

\displaystyle p^{\sharp}\coloneqq 2^{*},\qquad q^{\sharp}\coloneqq 2_{*}(1+\rho),\qquad r^{\sharp}\coloneqq\frac{2_{*}q}{q-2_{*}\rho},\qquad s^{\sharp}\coloneqq q.

(147)

\theta
. Its numerator is positive since
\rho<\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
, and its denominator is positive since
\nicefrac{{4q}}{{n}}>\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)=2^{*}\rho
. Hence,
\theta>1
is equivalent to
q<2^{*}
, which we have established above.

Step 2
: conclusion by Proposition
8.4
. Let
\lambda\in(1,3)
be as in Lemma
8.6
. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in(0,T^{\prime}]
, and set
B\coloneqq\mathrm{W}(x,t)
. Define the function
v(s,y)\coloneqq s^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}|u(s,y)|
. By Remark
8.7
, we have
v\in\mathrm{L}^{p^{\sharp}}(\lambda\mathrm{W}(x,t))
. Moreover, using Jensen’s inequality, Lemma
8.6
provides (
RH
) for the parameters introduced in Step 1. All remaining assumptions of Proposition
8.4
were likewise checked in Step 1. Hence, we can apply the proposition to conclude.
∎

From the previous proposition, we easily obtain self-improvement on the level of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces for weak solutions to (
RD
).

Corollary 8.9

(Bootstrapping of weak solutions to (
RD
))
.

Let
\rho\in[0,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})
. Suppose that
(u,T)
is a weak solution to (
RD
) such that
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
for some
r\in(1,\infty]
and
q\in(\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho),2_{*}(1+\rho))
. Then,
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,2^{*}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T^{\prime})
for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
.
In particular,
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,2_{*}(1+\rho)}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T^{\prime})
for all
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
.

Proof.

The definition of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces is invariant under dilation of the Whitney box, see
[
5
, Rem. 3.6]
. Hence, multiplying the improved RH-inequality from Proposition
8.8
by
t^{-\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}}
and applying the
\mathrm{L}^{r}
-norm to it, yields

(149)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{r,2^{*}}_{\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T^{\prime})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{\theta r,q}_{\frac{n}{2\theta r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)}^{\theta}+\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)}.

By the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding (using that
\theta>1
) and the assumption, we have

\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{\theta r,q}_{\frac{n}{2\theta r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)}\lesssim\|u\|_{\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}}(T)}\;\to\;0\qquad\text{as }T\to 0.

This gives the main claim of the corollary.
The
“in particular”
part follows from the nesting property of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces as
2_{*}(1+\rho)\leq 2^{*}
is equivalent to
\rho\leq\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
.
∎

Remark 8.10
.

In contrast to Proposition
7.7
, note that bootstrapping for weak solutions allows to increase the local integrability, but
not
to decrease the global integrability.

8.3.
Uniqueness of weak solutions

In Propositions
8.2
, we proved existence of local weak solutions for the reaction–diffusion equation for some time
T\in(0,\infty)
depending on the initial value
u_{0}
and the non-linearity. They belong to some regularity classes. Now, we study uniqueness of weak solutions in such classes. More precisely, given any time
T
, we wish to show that any such class contain
at most
one weak solution.

We reduce the uniqueness of weak solutions to the uniqueness of mild solutions. The latter was established in Corollary
7.9
. We proceed in two steps.

Proposition 8.11

(Weak solutions to (
RD
) are mild solutions)
.

Fix
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
and
T\in(0,\infty)
. Let
p\in(1,\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
and
\alpha\in(-1,0)
satisfying
\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}=\nicefrac{{n}}{{p}}-\alpha
and
p\geq p(\alpha,A)
. Let
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
.
Suppose that
(u,T)
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
, where
r,q\in(1,\infty]
satisfy
r\geq p
,
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
and
q\geq\max(2_{*}(1+\rho),2^{*})
.
Then,
u
is a mild solution to (
RD
).

Proof.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.
Define
v\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{L}(u_{0})+\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))
. We claim that
v
is a weak solution to

\partial_{t}v-\Div(A\nabla v)=\phi(u),\quad v(0)=u_{0},

satisfying
\nabla v\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.

Indeed, we can argue similarly as in the proof of Proposition
8.2
, with the following change. To see that
w=\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(\phi(u))
is a weak solution to

(150)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w-\Div(A\nabla w)=\phi(u),\qquad w(0)=0,

verifying
\nabla w\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
, we have to distinguish the cases
\rho\geq\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
and
\rho<\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
, since in contrast to the proof of Proposition
8.2
, we do not know
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,2(1+\rho)}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
here.
Note that, by Lemma
7.2
and the weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-space embedding, it suffices to show
\nabla w\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}}(T)
.
In both cases, we have

\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},\frac{q}{1+\rho}}_{(\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho})(1+\rho)}(T).

Now first, in the case
\rho<\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
, we can conclude with Lemma
8.12
below since
q\geq 2^{*}
by assumption. Second, in the case
\rho\geq\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
, we have
r>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)\geq 2_{*}(1+\rho)
. Using
q\geq 2_{*}(1+\rho)
and the nesting property of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces, we have

\phi(u)\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2_{*}}_{(\frac{n}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho})(1+\rho)}(T).

Therefore, we can invoke Theorem
6.1
(ii) with
f=\phi(u)
,
\sigma=\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}
,
\tilde{r}=\tfrac{r}{1+\rho}
and
\tilde{q}=2_{*}
to conclude. Indeed, we have just seen
\tilde{r}\geq\tilde{q}
, and
\tilde{r}<\nicefrac{{n}}{{\sigma}}
is equivalent to
r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
.

Next, define
w\coloneqq u-v
. By construction,
w
is a weak solution to
\partial_{t}w-\Div(A\nabla w)=0
with
w(0)=0
. Moreover,
\nabla w\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\frac{n}{2p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
, using also the assumption on
\nabla u
. Therefore, by uniqueness of the linear Cauchy problem (Proposition
2.19
),
w=0
.
Thus,
u=v
. Plugging this back into the definition of
v
shows that
u
is a mild solution to (
RD
) as desired.
∎

We prove the technical linear result just used in the previous proof.

Lemma 8.12
.

Let
\rho\in(\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}},\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})
and
r\in(\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho),\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
. Fix
T\in(0,\infty)
. Then, for
f\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},\frac{2^{*}}{1+\rho}}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}-1}(T)
, the function
u\coloneqq\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(f)
is a weak solution to
\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=f
with
u(0)=0
satisfying
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}}(T)
.

Proof.

The difficulty originates from the fact that the range of parameters is neither covered by Proposition
2.20
(which requires
\tfrac{2^{*}}{1+\rho}=2
) nor by Theorem
6.1
(which requires
r\geq 2^{*}
). Instead, we employ an interpolation argument between what we know for the extreme cases
\rho=\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
and
\rho=\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
. Unfortunately, the choice of interpolation parameters is non-trivial.

Define
\theta\coloneqq 2-\tfrac{4}{n\rho}
. Since
\rho\in(\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}},\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})
, we have
\theta\in(0,1)
, and there holds the convex relation
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}=(1-\theta)\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}+\theta\nicefrac{{n}}{{4}}
. A direct calculation gives the following relations:

(151)

\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)\frac{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}+\theta\frac{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})},

(152)

\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)\frac{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}+\theta\frac{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}.

Indeed, for instance

(153)

\displaystyle(1-\theta)\frac{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}+\theta\frac{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n+2}\left[(1-\theta)(1+\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}})+\theta(1+\nicefrac{{n}}{{4}})\right]

(154)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n+2}\left[1+\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}-\theta\nicefrac{{n}}{{4}}\right]

(155)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n+2}\frac{1+\rho}{\rho}

(156)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\rho}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)}.

Now, by choice of
r
, there is some
\widetilde{\theta}\in(0,1)
so that

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1-\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)}+\frac{\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)}.

Using
\widetilde{\theta}
, define
r_{0}\in(\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}),\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}))
and
r_{1}\in(\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}),\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}))
via

(157)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{0}}=\frac{1-\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}+\frac{\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})},\quad\frac{1}{r_{1}}=\frac{1-\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}+\frac{\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}.

Combining all convex relations,

(158)

\displaystyle(1-\theta)\frac{1+\tfrac{2}{n}}{r_{0}}+\theta\frac{1+\tfrac{4}{n}}{r_{1}}

(159)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1-\theta)(1+\tfrac{2}{n})\left[\frac{1-\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\tfrac{2}{n})}+\frac{\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\tfrac{2}{n})}\right]+\theta(1+\tfrac{4}{n})\left[\frac{1-\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\tfrac{4}{n})}+\frac{\widetilde{\theta}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\tfrac{4}{n})}\right]

(160)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1-\widetilde{\theta})\left[(1-\theta)\frac{1+\tfrac{2}{n}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\tfrac{2}{n})}+\theta\frac{1+\tfrac{4}{n}}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\tfrac{4}{n})}\right]+\widetilde{\theta}\left[(1-\theta)\frac{1+\tfrac{2}{n}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\tfrac{2}{n})}+\theta\frac{1+\tfrac{4}{n}}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\tfrac{4}{n})}\right]

(161)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1-\widetilde{\theta})\frac{1+\rho}{\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)}+\widetilde{\theta}\frac{1+\rho}{\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)}

(162)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1+\rho}{r}.

We claim the mapping properties

(163)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r_{0}}{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}},\frac{2^{*}}{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}}_{\frac{n(1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}{2r_{0}}-\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}-1}(T)\to\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r_{0}}{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}},2}_{\frac{n(1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}})}{2r_{0}}-\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T),

(164)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r_{1}}{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}},\frac{2^{*}}{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}}_{\frac{n(1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}{2r_{1}}-\nicefrac{{n}}{{4}}-1}(T)\to\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r_{1}}{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}},2}_{\frac{n(1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})}{2r_{1}}-\nicefrac{{n}}{{4}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T).

Indeed, in the first case, this follows from Proposition
2.20
since
\tfrac{2^{*}}{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}=2
, and in the second case from Theorem
6.1
, using that
r_{1}>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}})=2^{*}
. Now, note that
\frac{2^{*}}{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}=2_{*}
. Therefore, interpolation yields

(165)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},q}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-1}(T)\to\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T),

where
q
is given through

\frac{1}{q}\coloneqq(1-\theta)\frac{1+\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}}{2^{*}}+\theta\frac{1+\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}}{2^{*}}=\frac{1-\theta}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2_{*}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\theta}{n+2}.

By a similar reasoning,we find

(166)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}

\displaystyle\colon\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},q}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-1}(T)\to\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T).

Repeating the proof of Proposition
4.6
with (
165
) and (
166
) as inputs, we find that
u\coloneqq\mathcal{L}^{L}_{1}(f)
is a weak solution to
\partial_{t}u-\Div(A\nabla u)=f
with
u(0)=0
and satisfying
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},2}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{2}}(T)
, provided
f\in\mathrm{Z}^{\frac{r}{1+\rho},q}_{\frac{n(1+\rho)}{2r}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-1}(T)
.

Thus, by virtue of the nesting property of weighted
\mathrm{Z}
-spaces, it only remains to show
\tfrac{2^{*}}{1+\rho}\geq q
. By a direct calculation,
\tfrac{2^{*}}{1+\rho}>q
is equivalent to

(167)

\displaystyle\frac{(n\rho-2)(n\rho-4)}{2n\rho(n+2)}<0.

This is the case since
n\rho\in(2,4)
by assumption. Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

From Proposition
8.11
, uniqueness of weak solutions follows rapidly.

Corollary 8.13

(Uniqueness of weak solutions to (
RD
))
.

Fix
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
. Let
p\in(1,\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
and
\alpha\in(-1,0)
satisfying
\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}=\nicefrac{{n}}{{p}}-\alpha
and
p\geq p(\alpha,A)
. Fix
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
.
Then, for
T\in(0,\infty)
,
there exists at most one weak solution
(u,T)
to (
RD
) satisfying
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
, where
r,q\in(1,\infty]
satisfy
r\geq p
,
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
and
q>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
.

Proof.

Assume that
(u,T)
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
and
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
.
Let
T^{\prime}\in(0,T)
arbitrary.
Using Corollary
8.9
, we know moreover that
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,\max(q,2^{*})}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T^{\prime})
.
We claim that
\max(q,2^{*})\geq\max(2_{*}(1+\rho),2^{*})
. Indeed, the maximum on the right-hand side is attained by
2_{*}(1+\rho)
if and only if
\rho\geq\nicefrac{{4}}{{n}}
. In this case,
q>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)\geq 2_{*}(1+\rho)
.
Hence, by Proposition
8.11
,
u
is a mild solution to (
RD
) on
[0,T^{\prime}]
. As
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T^{\prime})
with
q>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
, Corollary
7.9
states that there is
at most
one such mild solution. Hence,
u
is unique on
[0,T^{\prime}]
. Since
T^{\prime}
was arbitrary, this proves the claim.
∎

Conclusion of Theorem
7.1

We construct a weak solution
(u,\tau)
to (
RD
) which is maximal in all uniqueness classes from Section
8.3
simultaneously. In particular, the lifespan is independent of the chosen uniqueness class. In conjunction with the results from Section
8.3
, this enables us to conclude Theorem
7.1
.

Proposition 9.1

(Maximal weak solutions)
.

Fix
\rho>\nicefrac{{2}}{{n}}
. Let
p\in(1,\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho))
and
\alpha\in(-1,0)
satisfying
\nicefrac{{2}}{{\rho}}=\nicefrac{{n}}{{p}}-\alpha
and
p\geq p(\alpha,A)
. Fix
u_{0}\in\dot{\mathrm{B}}^{\alpha}_{p,p}
.
Then, there exists a weak solution
(u,\tau)
to (
RD
) such that, for any
r,q\in(1,\infty]
satisfying
r\geq p
,
\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)<r<\mathrm{RD}_{+}(n,\rho)
and
q>\mathrm{RD}_{-}(n,\rho)
, the following property holds:

(R)

\displaystyle\forall T\in(0,\tau)\colon\quad u\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)\;\;\&\;\;\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T).

Moreover,
(u,\tau)
is maximal among all solutions verifying (
R
).

Proof.

Fix
\rho
,
p
,
\alpha
and
u_{0}
as in the statement. Let
(r,q)
be some fixed pair of parameters as in the statement.

Step 1
: construction of a maximal solution
(u,\tau)
for the parameter pair
(r,q)
. Consider the set

(168)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\bigl\{(v,T)\text{ weak solution to }\eqref{eq:rd}\colon v\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T),\;\nabla v\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)\bigr\}.

By Proposition
8.2
, we know
\mathcal{M}\neq\emptyset
. Define the maximal existence time

(169)

\displaystyle\tau\coloneqq\sup\,\bigl\{T\colon(v,T)\in\mathcal{M}\bigr\}>0.

If
t\in(0,\tau)
, define
u(t)\coloneqq v(t)
, where
(v,T)\in\mathcal{M}
with
T\geq t
. By the uniqueness result in Corollary
8.13
, this definition is unambiguous. By construction,
u
is a weak solution on
(0,\tau)
. Moreover,
(u,\tau)
is maximal among all weak solutions verifying (
R
). Indeed, otherwise there exists
(\tilde{v},\tilde{T})\in\mathcal{M}
with
\tilde{T}>\tau
, contradicting the definition of
\tau
.

Step 2
: solution
(u,\tau)
is maximal for any other parameter pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
. Let
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
be another pair of parameters as in the statement of the proposition.
First, we show that
(u,\tau)
verifies (
R
) for the pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
. Let
T\in(0,\tau)
. Repeating the proof of Corollary
8.13
, we find that
u
is a mild solution to (
RD
) on
[0,T]
. Then, the bootstrapping result in Proposition
7.7
entails
u\in\mathrm{Z}^{\tilde{r},\tilde{q}}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2\tilde{r}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T)
. Eventually, arguing as in Proposition
8.2
, we also find
\nabla u\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T)
, which completes the proof of (
R
).

Second, we show that
(u,\tau)
is maximal for the pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
(v,T)
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying (
R
) for the pair
(\tilde{r},\tilde{q})
and such that
T>\tau
. Fix
T^{\prime}\in(\tau,T)
. Replacing
u
by
v
in the argument at the beginning of Step 2, we deduce that
(v,T^{\prime})
is a weak solution to (
RD
) satisfying
v\in\mathrm{Z}^{r,q}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2r}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}}(T^{\prime})
and
\nabla v\in\mathrm{Z}^{p,2}_{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2p}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(T^{\prime})
. But this contradicts the maximality of
u
for the pair
(r,q)
obtained in Step 1. This concludes the proof.
∎

Eventually, we can give the proof to our non-linear main result.

Proof of Theorem

.

The existence, regularity and maximality assertions of Theorem

are provided by Proposition

.
Moreover, uniqueness was investigated in Corollary

. This completes the proof.
