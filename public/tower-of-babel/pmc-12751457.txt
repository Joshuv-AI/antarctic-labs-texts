High-energy blunt traumatic vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula with retropharyngeal hematoma: a case report
Authors: Tomoyuki Yoshihara, Natsumi Yamamura, Hiroto Okubata, Fukuki Saito, Takehiro Suyama, Masaaki Iwase, Kazuhisa Yoshiya, Yasushi Nakamori, Yasuyuki Kuwagata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe report a case of high-energy blunt traumatic vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula with retropharyngeal hematoma following C2 vertebral fracture after a motor vehicle crash. Although there is currently no established guideline to treat blunt traumatic vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula, effective transarterial embolization to sacrifice the parent artery succeeded in complete obliteration of the vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula with no ischemic complications.Case presentationA 74-year-old Japanese woman injured in a motor vehicle crash was transferred to our hospital for evaluation of neck pain and headache. She was hemodynamically stable on arrival. Computed tomography revealed a low-volume subarachnoid hemorrhage, cervical spinal fracture (C2), rib fracture, and thoracic spinal compression fracture. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography showed suspected dissection of her right vertebral artery at the C2 level. On day 2, extravasation from her right vertebral artery at the C2 level was suspected on magnetic resonance angiography, and a thick retropharyngeal hematoma was revealed on a T2-weighted magnetic resonance image. The angiographic study indicated a diagnosis of high-flow vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula at the C2 level. Transarterial embolization with coils was performed to sacrifice the parent artery, and n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate injection significantly reduced flow through the fistula, which succeeded in completely obliterating the fistula on day 30.ConclusionsVertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula caused by high-energy blunt trauma is extremely rare, but an upper cervical spine injury may trigger it, and retropharyngeal hematoma may sometimes also occur. Transarterial coil embolization of the parent artery to completely obliterate the fistula may be considered a reliable treatment in the acute stage.

Background

Vertebro-vertebral arteriovenous fistula (VV-AVF) is an uncommon cerebrovascular disorder that presents as an abnormal high-flow shunt between the vertebral artery (VA), its muscular or radicular branches, and adjacent vertebral veins or venous plexus [1]. Thus far, there is no guideline on standard treatment for VV-AVF. Most causes of VV-AVF have been reported to be spontaneous and due to mild neck trauma [2]. However, high-energy blunt trauma is a much rarer cause of VV-AVF than penetrating neck trauma such as knife wounds, gunshot wounds, or iatrogenic injury. We report a case of high-energy blunt traumatic VV-AVF with retropharyngeal hematoma (RH) diagnosed 1 day after a motor vehicle crash following C2 vertebral fracture.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old Japanese woman riding a bicycle collided with a pickup truck and was taken to our hospital immediately by ambulance. She is a known diabetic, well controlled on oral hypoglycemic drugs, but she took no antithrombotic drugs. On arrival, she complained of posterior neck pain and headache. Her vital signs included respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, peripheral capillary oxygenation saturation of 100% (under oxygen administration), blood pressure of 127/76 mmHg, heart rate of 95 beats per minute, and a temperature of 36.1 °C. Chest auscultation was clear without stridor. Neurological examination revealed disorientation, but her pupils were equal and reactive. She presented no conjugate deviation, anisocoria, oculomotor disturbance, sensory disturbance, or motor dysfunction. Cardiovascular and abdominal examination were unremarkable. She suffered a laceration on the top of her head and abrasions on her dorsal thorax and back of her left hand, but no wound around her neck. Blood test results revealed mild anemia with hemoglobin of 9.6 g/dL. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a low-volume subarachnoid hemorrhage in her interhemispheric fissure, cervical spinal fracture (C2, Hangman fracture) with the fracture line extending into the right transverse foramen (Fig. 1a–c), a left sixth-rib fracture, and thoracic spine compression fracture at Th7. Contrast-enhanced CT showed dissection of her right VA at the C2 level (grade I on the Denver Grading Scale [3], Fig. 1d). Plain X-ray film showed second and third metacarpal bone fractures of her left hand, which were corrected by fiberglass splint. After the head laceration was sutured, she was admitted to the intensive care unit with a neck brace while waiting for posterior cervical spinal fusion scheduled on day 3. On day 2, her hemoglobin decreased to 7.7 g/dL, and extravasation from her right vertebral artery at the C2 level was suspected on magnetic resonance angiography, whereas a 15-mm-thick RH emerged and spinal canal stenosis was identified at the C5/C6 level on T2-weighted cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 2). Conventional angiography of her right VA resulted in a diagnosis of VV-AVF at the C2 level with high-flow shunt refluxing to the basivertebral veins, vertebral veins, external jugular veins, deep cervical veins, vertebral venous plexus, and vertebra-basilar plexus (Fig. 3a, b). The posterior inferior cerebellar artery did not originate inherently from her right extracranial VA. Left vertebral angiography revealed that the left VA was the dominant side, and its flow ran through the vertebrobasilar junction retrogradely to distal location of the shunt in the right VA. Because high-flow shunting through an arteriovenous fistula could have an extremely high risk of secondary cerebrovascular accident or latent risk of expansion of the RH in the near future if no intervention were performed, we decided to sacrifice the right VA to block shunt flow through the VV-AVF. The procedure was performed under local anesthesia because she was alert and had no stridor or tachypnea. Embolization of her right VA at the level of C2 to C4 using 16 coils (Target; Stryker, Portage, MI, USA and Hydrosoft 3D; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was performed under proximal flow control using an occlusion balloon (Transform 3 mm × 5 mm, Stryker; Fig. 3c). Finally, transarterial infusion of 33% n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate was performed, which significantly reduced the shunt flow (Fig. 3d). During the procedure, hemoglobin dropped to 6.2 g/dL, requiring one unit of packed red blood cell transfusion. Post procedure, her hemoglobin remained stable. There was no indication of spinal or brain infarction on MRI as a complication of the procedure. On day 3, both posterior spinal fusion at C1–C3 and laminoplasty at C4–C6 were performed under general anesthesia, followed by the occurrence of pleural effusion and atelectasis that required respirator support. By day 14, her pulmonary atelectasis had improved and swelling including that in her neck subsided, so the endotracheal tube was removed after fixation of her fractured metacarpal bones. Although additional oxygen administration was not necessary, she suffered from postoperative wound pain and had little appetite. Fluid replacement was necessary daily, and a rehabilitation program was continued while she was hospitalized. On day 30, complete obliteration of the VV-AVF with no shunt flow was confirmed by angiography (Fig. 3e, f). She could self-ambulate with a cane and was transferred to another hospital for rehabilitation.

Discussion

VV-AVF is an uncommon vascular disorder presenting with abnormal shunting between the extracranial vertebral artery, its muscular or radicular branches, and adjacent veins [4]. VV-AVF caused by high-energy blunt trauma such as a motor vehicle crash or falling is extremely rare, although common causes have been reported such as penetrating neck injuries by knife or gunshot, mild neck trauma from chiropractic therapy, and spontaneous fistulas partially associated with neurofibromatosis [5]. Iatrogenic injuries during jugular vein puncture or cervical spine dislocation during a fixation procedure are also possible etiologies. Our literature search on PubMed and Ichushi-Web, which contains literature presented by the Japan Medical Abstracts Society since 2000, revealed only five cases of VV-AVF triggered by high-energy blunt trauma besides the present case (Table 1). These cases have two noteworthy characteristics: three involved C2 fracture extending to the transverse foramen, and two of them reported RH. A strong relation between blunt cerebrovascular injury and cervical spine fracture has been verified. Cothren et al. surveyed more than 17,000 cases of blunt trauma admission including 125 cases of cerebrovascular injury with cervical spine fractures [6]. They concluded that 93% of the cases had subluxation, upper C1–C3 cervical spine fracture, or fracture extending to the transverse foramen. These characteristics are observed in all of the cases listed in Table 1.

Insidious retropharyngeal hemorrhage, especially in older patients with blunt trauma, may deteriorate to lethal consequences because an expanding RH can completely obstruct the airway. Tsao et al. reviewed 68 cases of blunt traumatic RH in the literature, in which falling (54.4%) or motor vehicle crash (35.3%) was the leading cause, and found that 50% of the cases were associated with cervical spinal injury including bone fracture, and some symptoms emerged within 24 h after the traumatic event [7]. There have been a few reports of VA injury as a possible source of RH [8, 9]. Sugiura et al. reported seven cases of RH for which conventional angiography was performed [10]. In six cases, transarterial embolization was effective in controlling retropharyngeal hemorrhage. In our case, VV-AVF and RH were clearly confirmed on MRI on day 2, although only intramural dissection of the right VA had been suspected without any VV-AVF or RH on admission. Success in transarterial embolization to occlude a high-flow arteriovenous shunt as soon as it is detected might partially contribute to controlling latent expansion of a RH.

The primary aim in treating VV-AVF is complete obliteration of the arteriovenous shunt. However, there is currently still no guideline on the standard treatment for VV-AVF. Open surgical repair of vertebral artery injuries used to be a former primary treatment, while it involves some extremely challenging techniques, such as obtaining adequate exposure with proximal and distal control of the targeted artery [11]. Nowadays, open surgical intervention is generally replaced by less invasive endovascular treatment, although it is sometimes necessary if prior embolization has been unsuccessful [12]. Endovascular embolization using a detachable balloon has been available as a safe and effective treatment for more than 30 years [2]. A detachable balloon is more preferable for precise placement and optimal occlusion of the fistula. Detachable coils introduced after 2000 are another infallible substitute to obliterate VV-AVF if detachable balloons are unavailable [5, 13]. Transarterial embolization using detachable coils internally trapped between the distal and proximal vessel of the fistula is an effective and radical treatment to permanently block shunt flow [14–16]. There appear to be no reports of ischemic complication after sacrificing a VA identified as recessive to the contralateral VA [13, 16]. In the present case, we confirmed by preprocedure angiography that the contralateral VA was dominant, and its flow ran retrograde via the vertebrobasilar junction to the distal side of the fistula of the affected VA. So, we estimated that this patient had a certain tolerance to brain ischemia before sacrificing the affected VA. Acute distal embolus may occur as another risk of clot formation during the coil embolization procedure. However, we considered that proximal flow control of the VA via the occlusion balloon catheter would prevent distal emboli. Consequently, no high-intensity signal was detected on diffusion-weighted MRI after the procedure. To preserve patency of the VA, a covered stent graft is another feasible alternative. If the fistula occurs in the V1 or V2 segment of the VA, which runs almost straight forward, a covered stent can be effective in occluding the fistula because it fits the arterial lumen closely. However, Yeh et al. noted that a covered stent is rigid and generally difficult to fit within the inner lumen of the tortuous V3 segment, and the stent may insufficiently occlude the fistula [17]. Stenting can be one limited optional treatment as an elective procedure as dual antiplatelet therapy is necessary for the patient before and after the procedure. However, in a patient such as ours who suffered multiple trauma in the acute stage, it might be better to avoid multiple antithrombotic treatment regimens.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of high-energy blunt traumatic VV-AVF occurring at the V3 segment with RH that emerged 1 day after a motor vehicle crash that caused a C2 vertebral fracture. Effective transarterial embolization using coils and n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate to sacrifice the parent artery succeeded in completely obliterating the VV-AVF with no ischemic complications.
