The effects of different doses of Clostridium butyricum supplementation on intestinal morphology, serum parameters, and gut microbiota in weaned piglets
Authors: Yi Chen, Ying Zhou, Quan Qiu, Yongxi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the impact of varying concentrations of Clostridium butyricum on growth, nutrient digestibility, intestinal morphology, serum profiles, and gut microbiota in weaned piglets. A total of 210 healthy 34-day-old weaned piglets (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire) with an initial body weight of 8.89 ± 0.11 kg were randomly assigned to five groups (six replicates per group, seven piglets per replicate). The control group received a basal diet, while the experimental groups (T1-T4) received the basal diet supplemented with 2 × 10⁸, 5 × 10⁸, 1 × 10⁹, and 2 × 10⁹ CFU/kg of Clostridium butyricum, respectively. The trial lasted 28 days. The T4 group had higher apparent total tract digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, and gross energy compared to the control grou (P < 0.05). Apparent nutrient digestibility in weaned piglets rose linearly and quadratically with Clostridium butyricum level (P < 0.05). Supplementation with Clostridium butyricum enhanced antioxidant activities including superoxide dismutase, catalase, total antioxidant capacity, and glutathione peroxidase levels while reducing malondialdehyde concentration (P < 0.05). In both linear and quadratic models, serum SOD activity in weaned piglets increased linearly, whereas CAT activity followed a quadratic pattern throughout the experiment (P < 0.05). It also improved immunoglobulin levels and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and interleukin-8 (P < 0.05). Additionally, the T1 group and the T4 group promoted the enrichment of norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae and Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group, respectively (LDA >2, P < 0.05). In conclusion, Clostridium butyricum supplementation improves intestinal morphology, antioxidant capacity, immune status, and gut microbiota in weaned piglets.

Introduction

Weaning is a crucial stage in the growth and development of piglets. However, the accompanying weaning stress often leads to intestinal dysfunction, intensified oxidative stress and dysbiosis of the microbiota, which in turn causes problems such as diarrhea and growth arrest, seriously threatening the culture benefit (Upadhaya & Kim, 2021). Traditionally, antibiotics and zinc oxide have been widely used to alleviate weaning stress, but long-term use has led to problems such as an increase in drug-resistant strains, drug residues and environmental pollution (Wan et al., 2019). With the complete ban on antibiotic growth promoters by the European Union in 2006, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China has also gradually restricted the use of antibiotics (McEwen, 2006). Developing safe and efficient alternative solutions has become a research hotspot. Against this background, Clostridium butyricum, as a safe and new type of microbial feed additive, has attracted much attention due to its unique metabolic characteristics and probiotic functions.

Clostridium butyricum can directly participate in intestinal energy metabolism and immune regulation by secreting active substances such as butyric acid, antimicrobial peptides and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Duan et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2021). Studies have shown that butyric acid, as the main energy source for colonic epithelial cells, can promote the expression of tight junction proteins and enhance intestinal barrier function, thereby reducing the invasion of pathogenic bacteria and inflammatory responses (Liu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). In addition, Clostridium butyricum maintains the balance of the intestinal microecology by regulating the structure of the intestinal flora, inhibiting the proliferation of harmful bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella, while promoting the colonization of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (Yamamoto et al., 2022). Studies have shown that Clostridium butyrate can also alleviate Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) K88-induced oxidative damage through activating the p62-Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathway in mice (Li et al., 2021). In addition, relevant studies have shown that certain Clostridium butyricum strains can alleviate weaning stress in piglets by regulating the gut microbiota, improving gut morphology and barrier function, enhancing immune defense, and optimizing nutrient absorption (Casas et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023). However, at present, the differences in the effects of different additive doses of Clostridium butyricum in weaned piglets on oxidative stress and microbiota regulation remain unclear. Therefore, the present study was conducted in order to assess the effects of different concentrations of Clostridium butyricum on the growth performance, intestinal morphology, immunologic function, antioxidant capacity, and gut microbiota in weaned piglets, aiming to guide its use in swine industry.

Materials and methods

The experimental design and procedures used in this study were approved by the animal ethical committee of China Agricultural University according to the Chinese Guidelines for Animal Welfare. The experiment was conducted at the Feng Ning Swine Research Unit of China Agricultural University (Chengdejiuyun Agricultural and Livestock Co., Ltd., Chengde, China). The Clostridium butyricum culture was obtained from the Wuhan Sunhy Biology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Animals and treatment

Two hundred and ten [(Duroc × Landrace) × Large White] piglets (male: female was (105:105) with (8.89 ± 0.11) kg of body weight weaned at 34 days of age were randomly allocated into 5 groups with 6 replicates per group and 7 piglets in each replicate. They were CON group (control diet, negative control), T1 group (control diet + 2 × 108 CFU Clostridium butyricum/kg of control diet), T2 group (control diet + 5 × 108 CFU Clostridium butyricum/kg of control diet), T3 group (control diet + 1 × 109 CFU Clostridium butyricum/kg of control diet), and T4 group (control diet + 2 × 109 CFU Clostridium butyricum/kg of control diet). The basal diet was formulated to met or exceeded National Research Council (NRC) nutrient recommendations (NRC, 2012). The ingredients and nutritional levels are shown in Table 1. The experimental period was 28 days. All piglets had free access to food and water during the entire experiment. The ambient temperature of the room was between 26°C to 28°C and humidity was between 60% to 70%. The feed intakes and the body weights of piglets were weighed on days 0, days 1, and days 28 to calculate the average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (F/G). After each feeding, inspect the weaned piglets and record the ear tag numbers of those with diarrhea, twice a day. The diarrhea rate = Total number of episodes of diarrhea/Test days/Number of test pigs. Feed samples and fresh feces were collected during days 26-28 to calculate apparent digestibility of nutrients. Serum samples from the external jugular vein were collected on days 14 and 28. After finishing the trial, 6 healthy piglets (CON, T1, and T4 groups) close to the average weight were selected and euthanized. Subsequently, the cecum contents and intestinal tissues including duodenum, jejunum and ileum were collected. Apparent digestibility of nutrients

The apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) of dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), and gross energy (GE) was determined using the indigestible marker method using inherent acid insoluble ash (AIA) as the indigestible marker (Liu et al., 2018). DM analysis was performed according to the Association of Official Analytical Chemists 930.15 (AOAC, 2007). Ash, CP, and EE were analyzed using AOAC (2007) methods 942.05, 990.03, and 996.01, respectively. GE was determined by an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (Parr 1281, Automatic Energy Analyzer; Moline, IL, USA). Organic matte (OM) was calculated as OM = DM - ash. The AIA content in the diet and feces was determined using the hydrochloric acid-insoluble ash method, and the apparent total tract digestibility of nutrients was calculated using the following formula:ATTD (%) = 100 - [(AIA content in the diet / AIA content in feces) × (nutrient content in feces / nutrient content in the diet)] × 100.

Intestinal morphology

According to biological characteristics, fixed intestinal samples were embedded and sectioned, followed by staining using Hematoxylin-Eosin staining (H&E). The basic steps of staining are: dewaxing, dehydration, rinsing with distilled water, staining, clearing, and mounting. Finally, true color image analysis software was used to collect images of each tissue section. Four parts with clear and intact villi and crypts were selected from each sample to measure the height of the villi and the depth of the crypts, and the ratio of villi height to crypt depth was calculated. The Olympus IX51 inverted phase contrast microscope was used to examine the samples.

Serum-related indicators

Serum antioxidant indicators include total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and malondialdehyde (MDA). Serum immunological indicators include IgA, IgG, IgM, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8. An enzyme-labeled analyzer (Huaweidelang DR-200BS) and an automatic biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS-420) were used. The detection kits were provided by Biosino Bio-Technology and Science Inc and Beijing Huaying Biology Co., Ltd. The concentrations were calculated based on standard curves.

Intestinal microbiota community

After extracting total DNA from the samples using a PF Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Georgi, USA), primers designed based on conserved regions were used to amplify the V3-V4 variable region of the 16s rRNA gene for sequencing and cluster analysis. Sequencing was performed using Illumina's NextSeq 2000 PE300 platform (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd). All data analysis was conducted on Majorbio's cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were obtained and taxonomically annotated. By comparing the representative sequences of OTUs with a microbial reference database, species classification information corresponding to each OTU can be obtained. The community composition of each sample was then counted at the phylum and genus levels, and a species composition bar chart was drawn. Community diversity analysis of cecal microorganisms was performed, including alpha diversity analysis and beta diversity analysis. Differences between alpha diversity indices were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with a post-hoc Tukey-Kramer 0.95 test. Beta diversity analysis was performed using the unweighted version of UniFrac-based PCoA at the genus level, and differences between groups were tested using Adonis.

Statistical analysis

Statistics were conducted per pig, and the experimental data was initially processed using Excel 2019. Before analyzing the data, SAS 9.4 was used to perform normality and homogeneity of variance tests. Based on the data characteristics, the GLM procedure was selected, and multiple comparisons were made using Tukey's method. The GLIMMIX procedure was used for diarrhea rate. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were performed to determine the linear and quadratic effects. P < 0.05 considered as a significant difference. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis determined microbial genera with differential abundance variations (LDA> 2 and P < 0.05). Spearman's rank correlation was applied to all correlation analyses in this study (|R| > 0.50, * P < 0.05, and ** P < 0.01).

Serum immune indicators and cytokines

Table 6 shows the effects of supplementation with Clostridium butyricum on the concentration of immune indicators and cytokines in the serum of weaned piglets. Dietary Clostridium butyricum supplementation linearly and quadratically increased IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations in the serum on day 14 compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Moreover, dietary Clostridium butyricum supplementation showed the linear and quadratic decrease on the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). On day 28, there were no significant differences in IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations across the different treatment groups. Gut microbiota community and analysis of correlation with phenotype

A total of 768 OTUs were shared among the three groups; the CON group had 125 unique OTUs, the T1 group had 70 unique OTUs, and the T4 group had 177 unique OTUs (Fig. 1A). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed significant clustering characteristics of fecal microbial composition among the three groups (Fig. 1B). The T4 group increased the Shannon index, while decreased the Simpson index (p < 0.05, Fig. 1 C-H). At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant phyla in the fecal microbial composition of weaning piglets representing approximately 80-90%, followed by Proteobacteria and Campilobacterota (Fig. 2A). The relative abundances of the Firmicutes in groups CON, T1 and T4 were 86.03%, 80.29%, and 81.00%, respectively; and the relative abundances of the Bacteroidota were 5%, 9.27% and 9.19%. The Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was higher in the CON group than the T1 and T4 groups. At the genus level, Lactobacillus and norank_f__T34 were the dominant genera in the fecal microbial composition of weaning piglets, followed by Subdoligranulum and UCG-005 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, LEfSe analysis was performed on the genera in the three treatment groups (LDA> 2 and P < 0.05). The results showed that the CON, T1, and T4 groups were enriched with Lactobacillus, norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae, and Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group, respectively. To further understand the changes occurring in piglets, correlation analyses were conducted on microbial parameters and certain digestive and serum markers (|R| > 0.5, P < 0.05). The results showed that Lactobacillus was positively correlated with pro-inflammatory factors (including IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8) and negatively correlated with T-AOC and CAT. However, norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae was positively correlated with CAT and negatively correlated with TNF-α and IL-6. Discussion

To alleviate oxidative stress, poor intestinal health, diarrhea, and the consequent decrease in growth and immunity caused by weaning stress in piglets, environmentally benign feed additives with enhanced disease resistance are frequently incorporated into their diets (Wan et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). Probiotics are increasingly recognized as viable alternatives to antibiotics, playing a pivotal role in enhancing growth performance and modulating gut health. This study aimed to investigate the effects of different concentrations of Clostridium butyricum on the health of piglets. Our results showed that supplemented with Clostridium butyricum can improve intestinal morphology, antioxidant status, immunologic function, and composition of intestinal microbiota. The efficacy of Clostridium butyricum in treating diarrhea, influenced by factors such as strain and dosage, necessitates further clinical trials for optimal selection and application.

Several reports indicate that Clostridium butyricum has a significant positive impact on the growth performance of weaned piglets (Cao et al., 2019; Sato et al., 2019). In this study, supplementation with Clostridium butyricum increased the ileal villus height-to-crypt depth ratio in piglets, with the most pronounced effect observed in the T1 group, suggesting that a certain dose of Clostridium butyricum may be more beneficial for maintaining ileal mucosal structure and epithelial integrity (Casas et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023). However, this morphological improvement did not promote significant differences in growth performance or diarrhea incidence, indicating that optimization of intestinal structure does not necessarily result in an immediate improvement in overall production performance. This may be attributed to factors such as the type and dose of probiotic supplementation, gut microbiota composition, environmental stress, and the individual health status of the piglets (Saha et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024), suggesting that the effects of Clostridium butyricum may exhibit certain dose-dependent characteristics. In addition, our results showed that T4 group had higher ATTD of DM, OM, and GE compared with the CON group, while there was no significant improvement in the other groups. The addition of probiotics and fermented biomass is beneficial for improving the digestibility of piglets, but the results of the experiment are related to many factors, such as the level of product use, dietary components, feed form, and so on (Lee et al., 2014).

SOD, MDA, GSH-Px, T-AOC, and CAT are all important markers reflecting the degree of oxidative stress. Among them, SOD, GSH-Px, T-AOC, and CAT are members of the body's antioxidant system and can effectively decompose peroxides and lipid peroxides. Therefore, the levels of SOD, GSH-Px, T-AOC, and CAT can reflect the body's ability to clear oxygen free radicals (Lehoux et al., 2006; Poprac et al., 2017). T-AOC is a cumulative measurement of the body's redox state and an indicator of good status (Ahmad et al., 2012). Endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including SOD and CAT, can potentially delay the oxidation of lipids and proteins by scavenging superoxide free radicals or degrading hydrogen peroxide (Saribal et al., 2020). GSH-Px can utilize reduced GSH to convert H2O2 into water, effectively maintaining cell stability and protecting cell structure (Fridlyand et al., 2005). MDA is a lipid metabolite that can directly reflect the intensity and rate of lipid peroxidation in the body and indirectly reflect the degree of tissue damage caused by free radicals (Ho et al., 2013). However, previous studies have shown that the effects of probiotics on CAT and SOD activity are dose-dependent (Aluwong et al., 2013; Ouwehand, 2017). In the study, Clostridium butyricum can decrease the MDA concentration and increase the activity of SOD, CAT, T-AOC, and the concentration of GSH-Px, which was consistent with the previous study (Li et al., 2021). The results indicated supplemented with Clostridium butyricum had protective roles against oxidative damage.

In addition to regulating antioxidant functions, Clostridium butyricum also have positive effects in terms of immunity and anti-inflammation (Jiang et al., 2021). The immune system has the function of recognizing and removing antigenic foreign substances, coordinating with other systems of the body to maintain the stability and physiological balance of the internal environment of the body (Chen et al., 2022). Studies have shown that the addition of Clostridium butyricum to the diet can increase the serum IgM level of piglets on day 14, and the serum IgG, IgA and IgM levels of piglets on day 28 (Wang et al., 2019). Probiotics can not only enhance the levels of immunoglobulins but also modulate anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory factors. The addition of Lactobacillus plantarum to the nursery pig diet also reduced intestinal inflammatory response and blood IgG concentration (Shin et al., 2019). The balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors is crucial, as an imbalance may lead to chronic inflammation (Muth et al., 2023). In this study, supplementation of Clostridium butyricum increased levels of IgA, IgM, and IgG in serum on day 14, indicating that Clostridium butyricum supplementation boosts immunoglobulin production. Meanwhile, supplemented with Clostridium butyricum decreased the pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) in the serum compared with the CON group. In summary, Clostridium butyricum is beneficial for improving the immune and anti-inflammatory function.

As is well known, feeding with probiotic-enriched feed can alter the diversity of the gut microbiota. Our results showed that T4 group decreased the simpson index and increased the shannon index compared with the CON group, indicating that Clostridium butyricum changed the diversity of its microbial composition. The number of volatile substances produced by gut bacteria can affect the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidota (Quesada-Vázquez et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2023). In the intestinal tract of piglets, the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes play a crucial role in creating and maintaining an anaerobic environment. In this study, their relative abundance was approximately 90%, which is consistent with previous reports on suckling piglets (Chen et al., 2017). In the present study, supplemented with Clostridium butyricum decreased the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio compared with the CON group, which indicated that Clostridium butyricum can modulate the structure and diversity of intestinal microbiota in piglets (Stojanov et al., 2020). Supplementation with Clostridium butyricum promoted the enrichment of norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae in the T1 Group and Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group in the T4 Group. norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae can produce short-chain fatty acids, such as butyrate (Wang et al., 2025). Short-chain fatty acids promote the development and morphological changes of the intestinal epithelium in the gastrointestinal tract and regulate the intestinal microenvironment (Hung and Suzuki, 2018; Zhang et al., 2019; Aminullah et al., 2026). Therefore, the positive correlation between norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae and CAT, as well as the negative correlations with the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6 observed in T1 Group of this study, further support this possibility. Similarly, the higher ileal V/C ratio may also be influenced by the enrichment of norank_f__Butyricicoccaceae. It has been reported that the Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group is positively correlated with carbohydrate metabolism (Du et al., 2024), suggesting that this bacterium may possess the ability to promote the breakdown of ingested nutrients. In this study, the Ruminococcus_gauvreauii_group was found to be positively correlated with dry matter, organic matter, crude protein, ether extract, and total energy. This further explains the enrichment of this group in the T4 Group and suggests that it may promote digestive processes in the intestine, leading to higher nutrient digestibility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Clostridium butyricum supplementation could improve the intestinal morphology, immunologic function, and antioxidant capacity, regulate the proinflammatory cytokines and gut microbes in weaned piglets. However, the long-term effects of Clostridium butyricum supplementation at this concentration on piglets require further verification.
