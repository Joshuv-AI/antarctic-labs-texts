Generalized Gaussian Estimates and Local Limit Theorems for Discrete Convolution Powers of Complex Functions The d d -dimensional case
Pedro H. Alves, Evan Randles

Abstract
We establish generalized Gaussian bounds and local limit theorems with Gaussian-type error for the convolution powers of certain complex-valued functions on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} . These global space-times estimates/error, which are sharp in certain cases, are written in terms of the Legendre-Fenchel transforms of positive-homogeneous polynomials and are mirrored by estimates satisfied by the heat kernels associated to a related class of partial differential operators. The results obtained here enjoy applications to the analysis and stability of numerical difference schemes to partial differential equations. This work extends several recent results, pertaining to one and several dimensions, of P. Diaconis, L. Saloff-Coste, J.-F. Coulombel, G. Faye, L. Coeuret, and the second author.



Keywords:
Convolution powers, local limit theorems, Gaussian estimates, stability of numerical difference schemes.

Primary 42A85, 42B99; Secondary 35K25, 60F99, 65M12,.

Introduction and Main Results

In this article, we establish generalized Gaussian bounds and local (central) limit theorems with Gaussian-type error for the convolution powers of certain complex-valued functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

17

18
,

1.1
Positive-Homogeneous Polynomials

To introduce positive-homogeneous polynomials, we first introduce the “dilations” with which homogeneity is formulated. We denote by
\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
the collection of
d\times d
real matrices. The general linear group and orthogonal group will be denoted by
\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mbox{O}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, respectively. Correspondingly, their (real) Lie algebras taken equipped with the classical Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
are
\operatorname{Lie}(\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R}))=\mathfrak{g}_{d}(\mathbb{R})=\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\operatorname{Lie}(\mbox{O}_{d}(\mathbb{R}))=\mathfrak{o}_{d}(\mathbb{R})=\{S\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R}):S\,\mbox{is skew symmetric}\}
. For
E\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we define

t^{E}=\exp((\ln t) E)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\ln t)^{k}}{k!}E^{k}

for
t>0
. As discussed in
[
17
,
3
,
15
]
,
\{t^{E}\}
is a one-parameter subgroup of
\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
in the sense that
\mathbb{R}^{*}\ni t\mapsto t^{E}\subseteq\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is a smooth homomorphism. Given a complex function
P
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
E\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we say that
P
is homogeneous with respect to
E
(or homogeneous with respect to
\{t^{E}\}
) if

tP(\xi)=P(t^{E}\xi)

(4)

for all
t>0
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The set of all such matrices
E
for which (
4
) holds is called
the exponent set of
P
and denoted by
\operatorname{Exp}(P)
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
P
be a complex-valued multivariate polynomial in
d
variables and consider the real-valued function
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

R(\xi)=\operatorname{Re}P(\xi)

for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
P
is a positive-homogeneous polynomial
if
\operatorname{Exp}(P)\neq\varnothing
and
R
is positive definite in the sense that
R(\xi)\geq 0
and
R(\xi)=0
only when
\xi=0
.

Example 1
.

Here, we consider several examples of positive-homogeneous polynomials, including the canonical class of semi-elliptic ones.

1.

Consider the polynomial
P
defined by

P(\eta,\zeta)=\eta^{2}+\zeta^{4}

for
(\eta,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We observe easily that
E=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1/4)\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
because

P(t^{E}(\eta,\zeta))=P(t^{1/2}\eta,t^{1/4}\zeta)=(t^{1/2}\eta)^{2}+(t^{1/4}\zeta)^{4}=tP(\eta,\zeta)

for all
t>0
and
(\eta,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since
R(\eta,\zeta)=P(\eta,\zeta)
is clearly positive definite, we conclude that
P
is a positive-homogeneous polynomial.

2.

For a positive integer
m
, observe that

\xi\mapsto\absolutevalue{\xi}^{2m}=(\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}+\cdots\xi_{d}^{2})^{m}

is a positive-homogeneous polynomial with

\operatorname{Exp}(\absolutevalue{\cdot}^{2m})=\frac{1}{2m}I+\mathfrak{o}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

where

is identity in
\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

3.

Let
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d})
be a

\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}

\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{n_{k}}.

In the language of L. Hörmander

(5)

for some

and coefficients

for all

and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

to have positive-definite real part,
\mathbf{n}=2\mathbf{m}
for
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
so that

P(\xi)=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:2\mathbf{m}}=1}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=2}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}.

(6)

Consequently, any semi-elliptic polynomial of the form (
5

\mathbf{n}=2\mathbf{m}
for
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
and

R(\xi)=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=2}\operatorname{Re}(a_{\alpha})\xi^{\alpha}

(7)

is positive-definite. In the sequel, a

is one of the form (
6

We see that the polynomial

P(\eta,\zeta)=\eta^{2}+\eta^{4}+2\eta\zeta-4\eta^{3}\zeta+\zeta^{2}+6\eta^{2}\zeta^{2}-4\eta\zeta^{3}+\zeta^{4}

\displaystyle P(t^{E}(\eta,\zeta))

In view of the final item in the example above, we see that not all positive-homogeneous polynomials are semi-elliptic. Still, as the example illustrates, it is sometimes possible to make a change of coordinates so that a positive-homogeneous polynomial becomes semi-elliptic in the new coordinate system. The following proposition shows that this can always be done.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any positive-homogeneous polynomial
P
(in
d

A\in\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
for which

is positive semi-elliptic. In particular, there exists some
\mathbf{m}=\left(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
for which

P_{A}(\xi)=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=2}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}

and

R_{A}(\xi)=R(A\xi)=\sum_{|\alpha:\mathbf{m}|=2}\operatorname{Re}(a_{\alpha})\xi^{\alpha}

is positive definite. Further

D:=\operatorname{diag}\left(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\dots,1/2m_{d}\right)\in\operatorname{Exp}(P_{A})

and
E:=ADA^{-1}\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
.

We now discuss a more general concept of positive homogeneity taken from

\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm on

Proposition 1.3

(Proposition 1.2 of

]
)
.

Let
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous, positive-definite, and have
\operatorname{Exp}(R)\neq\varnothing

(a)

The unital level set of

S_{R}=\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:R(\eta)=1\},

(b)

For every
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)

\{t^{E}\}
is contracting.

(c)

There exists
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
for which
\{t^{E}\}
is contracting.

(d)

We have

\lim_{\xi\to\infty}R(\xi)=\infty.

Definition 1.4

Let
R

with
\operatorname{Exp}(R)\neq\varnothing

Let
P

R=\operatorname{Re}(P)

R^{\#}(x)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{x\cdot\xi-R(\xi)\}

is itself a (continuous) positive-homogeneous function.

Proposition 1.5

(Proposition 8.15 of
[

.

Let
P
be a positive-homogeneous polynomial with
R=\operatorname{Re}P
and exponent set
\operatorname{Exp}(P)

R^{\#}
of
R
is a continuous positive-homogeneous function with

\operatorname{Exp}(R^{\#})=\{F\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R}):(I-F)^{\top}\in\operatorname{Exp}(R)\}.

Example 2
.

We return back to the examples considered in Example

1.

For
P(\eta,\zeta)=R(\eta,\zeta)=\eta^{2}+\zeta^{4}

\mu_{P}=1/2+1/4=3/4

R^{\#}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\frac{1}{4^{1/3}}y^{4/3}

F=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,3/4)\in\operatorname{Exp}(R^{\#})

2.

For a positive semi-elliptic polynomial

3.

For the positive-homogeneous polynomials

Proposition 1.6
.

Let

1.3
Hypotheses and Main Results

We are now in a position to state the hypotheses under which our theorems are stated. For
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
, let
\xi_{0}\in\Omega(\phi)
and consider
\Gamma_{\xi_{0}}
defined by (
3
). We take the following definition from
[
17
]
.

Definition 1.7
.

We say that
\xi_{0}
is a point of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
if

\Gamma_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)=i\alpha_{\xi_{0}}\cdot\xi-P_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)+\Upsilon_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)

where
\alpha_{\xi_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
P_{\xi_{0}}
is a positive-homogeneous polynomial (with
R_{\xi_{0}}=\operatorname{Re}P_{\xi_{0}}
), and
\Upsilon_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)=o(R_{\xi_{0}}(\xi))

as
\xi\to 0
(for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

As
\widehat{\phi}
is maximized in absolute value at
\xi_{0}\in\Omega(\phi)
,
\xi\mapsto i\alpha_{\xi_{0}}\cdot\xi
must be purely imaginary and so necessarily
\alpha_{\xi_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. What is essential to the definition above is that we ask the Maclaurin expansion for
\Gamma_{\xi_{0}}
to be dominated at low order by
i\alpha_{\xi_{0}}\cdot\xi-P_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)
where
P_{\xi_{0}}
is a positive-homogeneous polynomial. This generalizes to
d
dimensions the parabolic case of
[
6
]
(see, Assumption 3 of
[
6
]
and Assumption 1.2 of
[
5
]
), and, equivalently, the cases of Type 1 and Type
\gamma
of
[
16
]
and
[
25
]
, respectively. In the one-dimensional setting, a result of Thomée
[
25
]
(generalized by Coulombel and Faye in
[
5
]
) guarantees that, when
\Omega(\phi)\neq\mathbb{T}
, there are only two possibilities for points
\xi_{0}\in\Omega(\phi)
when
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{*}
. The first is for
\xi_{0}
to be of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
. The second asks that
\Gamma_{\xi_{0}}
have dominant nonlinear purely-imaginary expansion (i.e., Type II of
[
16
]
coinciding with the dispersive case discussed in
[
6
]
); the existence of points of the second instance implies that associated finite difference schemes are unstable in the maximum norm
[
25
, Theorem 3]
. In
d
-dimensions, the article
[
15
]
considers an analogy of the second case (called points of imaginary homogeneous type) and establishes local limit theorems in that setting; however, in

dimensions, the two cases are no longer collectively exhaustive (see
[
20
]
). We refer the reader to the introduction of
[
15
]
which discusses these cases, in one and several dimensions, and their implications for stability of numerical difference schemes.

Beyond the hypothesis that
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
, our theorems are stated under the assumption that every element of
\Omega(\phi)
is of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
. The fact that, for each
\xi\in\Omega(\phi)
,

is continuous and positive definite guarantees immediately that
\Omega(\phi)\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
consists entirely of isolated points. From the compactness of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, it then follows that
\Omega(\phi)
is finite and, in this case, we write

\Omega(\phi)=\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{K}\}.

For each
k=1,2,\dots,K
, by an abuse of notation, we will write
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{\xi_{k}}
,
P_{k}=P_{\xi_{k}}
,
R_{k}=R_{\xi_{k}}
,
\mu_{k}=\mu_{P_{\xi_{k}}}
, and
\Upsilon_{k}=\Upsilon_{\xi_{k}}
. In this notation, our first theorem is as follows.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
and assume that every member of
\Omega(\phi)
is a point of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
. With this, we assume the notation of the previous paragraph. There exists a constant
L>0
and positive constants
M_{0},M_{1},\dots,M_{K},C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{K}
for which

\absolutevalue{\phi^{(n)}(x)}\leq\mathcal{E}(n,x)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
where

\mathcal{E}(n,x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{-M_{0}(n+\absolutevalue{x})}&\absolutevalue{x}>Ln\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{C_{k}}{n^{\mu_{k}}}\exp\left(-nM_{k}R^{\#}_{k}\left(\frac{x-n\alpha_{k}}{n}\right)\right)&\absolutevalue{x}\leq Ln\end{cases}.

As pointed out in
[
6
]
, the dependence on

can be removed when

is finitely supported resulting in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be finitely supported and normalized so that
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
. If every point of
\Omega(\phi)
is of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
, then there are positive constants
M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{K},C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{K}
for which

\absolutevalue{\phi^{(n)}(x)}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{C_{k}}{n^{\mu_{k}}}\exp\left(-nM_{k}R_{k}^{\#}\left(\frac{x-n\alpha_{k}}{n}\right)\right)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and

.

In the context of one-dimension, Theorems

and

We next turn our attention to local limit theorems. For fairly complete history on local limit theorems for complex-valued functions (up to the date of publication), we refer the reader to
[

To introduce our local limit theorem, we must first introduce the attractors with which it is written. Given a positive-homogeneous polynomial

and

, mirrored by the estimates in Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
. The role that these off-diagonal estimates play in the analysis of variable-coefficient partial differential equations is the subject of the article
[

. We will not say much more about the attractors here but refer the reader to

,

Proposition 1.10

Let

for which

semi-elliptic with

.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{*}
and suppose that every point of
\Omega(\phi)
is of positive-homogeneous type for
\widehat{\phi}
. We assume the notation of the previous theorems and take

and

Proof of Proposition
1.10
.

For
E,E^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)\subseteq\operatorname{Exp}(R)
, it is easy to see that
R(t^{-E^{\prime}}t^{E}\xi)=R(\xi)
for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t>0
; this is to say, in the terminology of
[
15
,
3
]
, that
t^{-E}t^{E}
lives in
\operatorname{Sym}(R)
, the necessarily compact symmetric group of
R
. From this it follows that

\sup_{R(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(t^E\xi)}=\sup_{R(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(t^{E'}t^{-E'}t^E\xi)}=\sup_{R(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(t^{E'}\xi)}

for every
t>0
for which
t^{D}\xi
lives in the domain of holomorphicity of
\Upsilon
when
R(\xi)\leq 1
. Thus the definition of
\lambda=\lambda(\Upsilon)
is independent of
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
.

Taking
A\in\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
as in the statement, we consider the function
\Upsilon_{A}(z)=\Upsilon(Az)
which is necessarily holomorphic on an open neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For
D=\operatorname{diag}(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\dots,1/2m_{d})\in\operatorname{Exp}(R_{A})
and
E=ADA^{-1}\in\operatorname{Exp}(R)
, observe that

\sup_{R(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(t^E\xi)}=\sup_{R(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(At^DA^{-1}\xi)}=\sup_{R_{A}(A^{-1}\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^DA^{-1}\xi)}=\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}

from which we see immediately that

\lambda=\lambda(\Upsilon)=\lambda(\Upsilon_{A})=\sup\left\{\gamma\geq 0:\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-(\gamma+1)}\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}=0\right\}=\sup\Psi_{A}

where we have put

\Psi_{A}=\left\{\gamma\geq 0:\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-(\gamma+1)}\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}\neq 0\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an open poly-disk centered at
0
whose closure
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
is a compact subset of the domain of holomorphicity of
\Upsilon_{A}
. Expanding
\Upsilon_{A}
as a power series centered at
0
, we have

\Upsilon_{A}(z)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}b_{\beta}z^{\beta}

which is absolutely and uniformly convergent on
\overline{\mathcal{U}}
. In view of Lemma
A.5
and the fact that
\Upsilon_{A}(\xi)=o(R_{A}(\xi))
as
\xi\to 0
, observe that

0=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-1}\Upsilon_{A}(t^{D}\xi)=\lim_{t\to 0}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}b_{\beta}t^{-1}(t^{D}\xi)^{\beta}=\lim_{t\to 0}\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}b_{\beta}t^{\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}-1}\xi^{\beta}

for every
\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consequently,
b_{\beta}=0
for all
\beta
for which
\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}\leq 1
. With this, we set

\rho:=\min\{\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}:b_{\beta}\neq 0\}>1.

Now, taking
t_{0}
sufficiently small
t_{0}^{D}\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}
whenever
R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1
. Consequently, for any
0<t\leq t_{0}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1
,

\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}=\absolutevalue{\sum_{\beta}b_\beta(t/t_0)^{\abs{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}}(t_0^D\xi)^\beta}\leq\absolutevalue{(t/t_0)^{\rho}\sum_{\beta}\abs{b_\beta}\abs{(t_0^D \xi)^\beta}}.

From this and that fact that
\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}}\sum_{\beta}\absolutevalue{b_\beta}\absolutevalue{z^\beta}<\infty
, it follows that

\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}\leq Ct^{\rho}.

Thus, whenever
\gamma+1<\rho
,

\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-(\gamma+1)}\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi)}\leq C\lim_{t\to 0}t^{\rho-(\gamma+1)}=0.

Now, since
b_{\beta}\neq 0
for some
\beta
with
\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}=\rho
, the linear independence of monomials allows us to choose
\xi_{0}\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
R_{A}(\xi_{0})\leq 1
and

\sum_{\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}=\rho}b_{\beta}\xi_{0}^{\beta}\neq 0.

Consequently,

\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-\rho}\sup_{R_{A}(\xi)\leq 1}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon(t^D\xi)}\geq\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-\rho}\absolutevalue{\Upsilon_A(t^D\xi_0)}=\lim_{t\to 0}\absolutevalue{\sum_\beta b_\beta t^{\abs{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}-\rho}\xi_0^\beta}=\absolutevalue{\sum_{\abs{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}=\rho}b_\beta\xi_0^\beta}\neq 0.

Putting these two arguments together shows that, for any
\gamma\geq 0
with
1+\gamma<\rho
,
\gamma\in\Psi_{A}
but,
\gamma=\rho-1\notin\Psi_{A}
. Consequently,

\lambda=\sup\Psi_{A}=\min\{\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}:b_{\beta}\neq 0\}-1=\rho-1>0.

Taking
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
for which
\lambda=\absolutevalue{\beta:2\mathbf{m}}-1
, we note that

\lambda=\frac{\beta_{1}}{2m_{1}}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2m_{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\beta_{d}}{2m_{d}}-1=\frac{1}{2\operatorname{lcm}(\mathbf{m})}\left(\beta_{1}n_{1}+\beta_{2}n_{2}+\cdots\beta_{d}n_{d}-2\operatorname{lcm}(\mathbf{m})\right)

where
m_{j}n_{j}=\operatorname{lcm}(\mathbf{m})
for
j=1,2,\dots,d
. Since
\lambda>0
, the term in parentheses is necessarily a positive integer and therefore
\lambda\geq 1/(2\operatorname{lcm}(\mathbf{m})
).
∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P
be a positive semi-elliptic polynomial (with

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{R}}g_{A}(n,\xi-i\sigma\theta)e^{-iw\cdot(\xi-i\sigma\theta)}\,d\xi

for

and
\sigma\theta=(\sigma\theta)(w)=(\sigma_{1}\theta_{1},\sigma_{2}\theta_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}\theta_{d})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{R}}\absolutevalue{g_A(n,\xi-i\sigma\theta)}e^{-w\cdot\sigma\theta}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum^{d}_{j=1}M\theta_{j}^{2m_{j}}-w_{j}\sigma_{j}\theta_{j}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\epsilon\sum^{d}_{j=1}\xi_{j}^{2m_{j}}\right)d\xi

\displaystyle C\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}M\theta_{j}^{2m_{j}}-\absolutevalue{w_j}\theta_{j}\right).

\displaystyle(M\theta_{j}^{2m_{j}-1}-\absolutevalue{w_j})\theta_{j}

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{1}{2m_{j}}\right)\absolutevalue{w_j}\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{w_j}}{2m_{j}M}\right)^{1/(2m_{j}-1)}

\displaystyle-M_{j}\absolutevalue{w_j}^{2m_{j}/2m_{j}-1}

In this subsection, we prove Proposition
1.2
. We begin with a lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
P
be a positive-homogeneous polynomial in
d
variables and let
D,N
, and
S
be real matrices such that
D
is diagonal,
S
is skew-symmetric,
N
is nilpotent and, further, we have the commutativity relations
[D+S,N]=[D,S]=0
; here,
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the classical Lie bracket. If

E:=D+S+N\in\operatorname{Exp}(P),

then

D=\operatorname{diag}\left(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\dots,1/2m_{d}\right)

for some
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
. Further, with this
\mathbf{m}
,
P
is positive semi-elliptic with

P(\xi)=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=2}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}

for some complex coefficients
\{a_{\alpha}\}
and, necessarily,
D\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
.

Proof.

We write
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)
for
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

P(\xi)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}

where
\mathcal{I}
is a finite subset of multi-indices (for which
a_{\alpha}\neq 0
). Our primary goal is to show that
\alpha\cdot\lambda=1
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
.

To this end, given that
[D+S,N]=0
and
[D,S]=0
, we have

t^{E}=t^{N+(D+S)}=t^{N}t^{D+S}=t^{N}t^{S+D}=t^{N}t^{S}t^{D}

for all
t>0
. While not needed for our argument here, these commutativity relations guarantee that all six juxtapositions of
t^{N},t^{S}
, and
t^{D}
coincide with
t^{E}
for every
t>0
. Since
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
, we have
tP(\xi)=P(t^{N}t^{S}t^{D}\xi)
and equivalently

P(t^{N}t^{S}\xi)=tP(t^{-D}\xi)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda}\xi^{\alpha}

(30)

for all
t>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given that
N
is nilpotent (with, say, order
k+1
),

P(t^{N}t^{S}\xi)=P\left(t^{S}\xi+(\ln t)Nt^{S}\xi+\cdots+\frac{(\ln t)^{k}}{k!}N^{k}t^{S}\xi\right)=Q(\ln t,t^{S}\xi)

where
Q(s,\eta)
for
(s,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a multivariate polynomial. In particular, for some finite degree
l
, we can write

Q(s,\eta)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{j}(\eta)s^{j}

where
b_{j}(\eta)
for
j=0,1,\dots,l
are polynomial functions of
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. With this, (
30
) can be expressed equivalently as

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda}\xi^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{j}(t^{S}\xi)(\ln t)^{j}

(31)

for all
t>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

With (
31
) in hand, let’s assume to reach a contradiction that there is some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
for which
\alpha_{0}\cdot\lambda<1
. Without loss of generality, we take this dot product to be minimal (among those
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
) and define

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}:\alpha\cdot\lambda=\alpha_{0}\cdot\lambda\}.

Given that distinct monomials are linearly independent and
a_{\alpha_{0}}\neq 0
, we may fix some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}\neq 0.

For
\delta:=1-\alpha_{0}\cdot\lambda>0
, (
31
) yields

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda-\delta}\xi^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{t^{\delta}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda}\xi^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{j}(t^{S}\xi)\frac{(\ln t)^{j}}{t^{\delta}}

(32)

for all
t>0
. Now, because
S
is skew-symmetric,
\{t^{S}\}
is a subgroup of
\mbox{O}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and so
t^{S}\xi
is bounded for all
t>0
. Therefore

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{j}(t^{S}\xi)\frac{(\ln t)^{j}}{t^{\delta}}=0

because the polynomial coefficients
b_{j}(\cdot)
are bounded on bounded sets. Also, by the minimality of
\alpha_{0}
,
1-\alpha\cdot\lambda-\delta=\alpha_{0}\cdot\lambda-\alpha\cdot\lambda<0
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
and so it follows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda-\delta}\xi^{\alpha}=0.

In view of the identity (
32
), we obtain

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{l}b_{j}(t^{S}\xi)\frac{(\ln t)^{j}}{t^{\delta}}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}a_{\alpha}t^{1-\alpha\cdot\lambda-\delta}\xi^{\alpha}\right)=0,

a contradiction. In the case that
\alpha_{0}\cdot\lambda>1
for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
, one can obtain a contradiction analogously by taking
t\to 0
. Thus
\alpha\cdot\lambda=1
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
and so we write

P(\xi)=\sum_{\alpha\cdot\lambda=1}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}

(with this presentation allowing zero coefficients for potentially some
\alpha
for which
\alpha\cdot\lambda=1
). To complete the proof, it remains to determine
\lambda
. For
k=1,2,\dots,d
, let’s denote by
e_{k}
the standard unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and observe that

R(te_{k})=\sum_{\alpha\cdot\lambda=1}R(a_{\alpha})(te_{k})^{\alpha}=\operatorname{Re}(a_{n_{k}e_{k}})t^{n_{k}}=c_{k}t^{n_{k}}

since all polynomial terms in
R
vanish except (perhaps) a single
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
of the form
\alpha=n_{k}e_{k}
for some
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
; necessarily,
n_{k}\lambda_{k}=\alpha\cdot\lambda=1
. Now, because
R
is positive definite, it must be the case that
t\mapsto c_{k}t^{n_{k}}
is also positive definite and therefore
\lambda_{k}=1/n_{k}=1/2m_{k}
for some
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Consequently,
\alpha\cdot\lambda=1
if and only if

\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{m_{1}}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{m_{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\alpha_{d}}{m_{d}}=2

for
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
and with this it is evident that

D=\operatorname{diag}(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\dots,1/2m_{d})\in\operatorname{Exp}(P).

∎

Proof of Proposition
1.2
.

Let
F\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
. Via the real Jordan Canonical Form theorem
[
12
, Theorem 3.4.1.5]
, we know
F
is similar to a matrix
G
such that

G=C_{1}\oplus C_{2}\cdots\oplus C_{k}\oplus J_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{m},

where

J_{r}=\lambda_{r}I_{n_{r}}+N_{r}\in M_{n_{r}}(\mathbb{R}),

with
\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
,
I_{n_{r}}\in M_{n_{d}}(\mathbb{R})
is the
n_{r}\times n_{r}
identity matrix, and
N_{r}
is a strictly upper triangular whose entries are either
1
or
0
(
N_{r}=0
if
n_{r}=1
). Further,

C_{r}=a_{r}I_{2j_{r}}+S_{r}+N_{r}^{\prime}\in M_{2j_{r}}(\mathbb{R}),

where
I_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix,
I_{2j_{r}}
is the
2j_{r}\times 2j_{r}
identity matrix,
a_{r},b_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
,
S_{r}
is a direct sum of
j_{r}
matrices equal to

B_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0&b_{r}\\
-b_{r}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

and
N_{r}^{\prime}
is a strictly upper triangular matrix whose entries are either
1
or
0
(and
N_{r}^{\prime}=0
if
j_{r}=2
).

Here, the
\lambda_{r}
s denote the real eigenvalues of
F
(and
G
) and the matrices
C_{r}
correspond to complex eigenvalues
\mu_{r}=a_{r}\pm ib_{r}
which necessarily appear in conjugate pairs. The eigenvalues
\lambda_{r}
need not be distinct, and the number of times the value
\lambda_{r}
occurs in the list
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{m}
is the geometric multiplicity of
\lambda_{r}
. Similarly, the
\mu_{r}
need not be distinct and the number of times the block
B_{r}
occurs in the list
B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{k}
is the geometric multiplicity of both
\mu_{r}=a_{r}+ib_{r}
and
\overline{\mu}_{r}=a_{r}-ib_{r}
. It is also possible that no
C_{r}
blocks or no
J_{r}
blocks occur in
G
, should
G
only have real or complex eigenvalues, respectively.

Notice that
[\lambda_{r}I_{n_{r}},N_{r}]=0
, and
[a_{r}I_{j_{r}},N_{r}^{\prime}]=[a_{r}I_{j_{r}},S_{r}]=0
, since
I_{n_{r}},I_{j_{r}}
are identity matrices. One can also check that
[a_{r}I_{j_{r}}+S_{r},N_{r}^{\prime}]=0
by a quick computation. Then, setting
D_{r}^{\prime}=a_{r}I_{j_{r}}
and
D_{r}=\lambda_{r}I_{n_{r}}
, we can write
G=D+S+N
where

\begin{split}D&=D_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\cdots\oplus D_{k}^{\prime}\oplus D_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus D_{m}\\
N&=N_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\cdots\oplus N_{k}^{\prime}\oplus N_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus N_{m}\\
S&=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{k}\oplus O\end{split}

where
O
is a zero matrix of appropriate dimensions. Here,
D,S,N\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
D
is diagonal,
N
is strictly upper triangular and, so, nilpotent and
S=G-D-N\in\mathfrak{o}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. By the commutativity relations for
D_{r},N_{r},D_{r}^{\prime},N_{r}^{\prime},S_{r}
we also know that
[D+S,N]=[D,S]=0
.

Since
F
is similar to
G
(via real similarity in the language of
[
12
]
), there exists
A\in\mbox{Gl}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
for which
A^{-1}FA=G=D+N+S
. For
P_{A}(\xi)=P(A\xi)
, we observe that

P_{A}(t^{G}\xi)=P_{A}(t^{A^{-1}FA}\xi)=P_{A}(A^{-1}t^{F}A\xi)=P(t^{F}A\xi)=tP(A\xi)=tP_{A}(\xi)

for all
t>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
on account of the fact that
F\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
. Consequently,
G=D+S+N\in\operatorname{Exp}(P_{A})
with
[D+S,N]=[D,S]=0
and so we may invoke Lemma
A.1
to find that for some
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{d}
, we have
D=\operatorname{diag}(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\cdots,1/2m_{d})\in\operatorname{Exp}(P_{A})
and

P_{A}(\xi):=P(A\xi)=\sum_{\absolutevalue{\alpha:\mathbf{m}}=2}a_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}

Putting
E=ADA^{-1}
, we have

P(t^{E}\xi)=P(t^{ADA^{-1}}\xi)=P(At^{D}A^{-1}\xi)=P_{A}(t^{D}A^{-1}\xi)=tP_{A}(A^{-1}\xi)=tP(\xi)

so
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
and the proof is complete.

∎

A.2
Asymptotics for positive-homogeneous functions

Proposition A.2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be positive-homogeneous functions with
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)\cap\operatorname{Exp}(Q)
. Then,
P\asymp Q
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:P(\eta)=1\}
and, since
S
is compact thanks to Proposition
1.3
and does not contain
0
, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which
c_{2}\leq Q(\eta)\leq c_{1}
for all
\eta\in S
. By the definition of
S
, we see that

c_{1}\leq\frac{Q(\eta)}{P(\eta)}\leq c_{2}

for all
\eta\in S
. Now, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, we can choose a
t>0
for which
\xi=t^{E}\eta
for
\eta\in S
(simply take
t=P(\xi)
and
\eta=P(\xi)^{-E}\xi
). With this, we see that

c_{1}\leq\frac{Q(\eta)}{P(\eta)}=\frac{tQ(\eta)}{tP(\eta)}=\frac{Q(t^{E}\eta)}{P(t^{E}\eta)}=\frac{Q(\xi)}{P(\xi)}=\frac{tQ(\eta)}{tP(\eta)}\leq c_{2}

giving our desired estimate on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Recalling that
P(0)=Q(0)=0
, the proof is complete.
∎

As an immediate corollary, we have the following.

Proposition A.3
.

Given a positive-homogeneous function
R
, assume that
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\dots,\lambda_{d})\in\operatorname{Exp}(R)
for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d}
which are all positive numbers. Then

R(x)\asymp\absolutevalue{x}^{\mathbf{1/\lambda}}:=\absolutevalue{x_1}^{1/\lambda_{1}}+\absolutevalue{x_2}^{1/\lambda_{2}}+\cdots+\absolutevalue{x_d}^{1/\lambda_{d}}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

With the above proposition and Proposition
1.2
in hand, we are now above to prove Proposition
1.6
.

Proof of Proposition
1.6
.

First, notice that for
A\in Gl_{d}(\mathbb{R})
as given in Proposition
1.2
, we have

R^{\#}(x)=\sup_{\xi}\{x\cdot\xi-R(\xi)\}=\sup_{\xi}\{x\cdot A\xi-R(A\xi)\}=\sup_{\xi}\{A^{\top}x\cdot\xi-R_{A}(\xi)\}=R_{A}^{\#}(A^{\top}x).

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now, by Proposition
1.2
, we know
D=\operatorname{diag}(1/2m_{1},1/2m_{2},\cdots,1/2m_{d})\in\operatorname{Exp}(P_{A})\subseteq\operatorname{Exp}(R_{A})
, and so by Proposition
1.5
we have
I-D\in\operatorname{Exp}(R_{A}^{\#})
. A quick computation also shows that for
Q(x)=\absolutevalue{x}^{\mathbf{1/2m}}
,
I-D\in\operatorname{Exp}(Q)
. So,
I-D\in\operatorname{Exp}(Q)\cap\operatorname{Exp}(R_{A}^{\#})
, and the desired result follows by Proposition
A.2
.
∎

A.3
Characterizing subhomogeneity

In this section, we discuss the notion of subhomogeneity (with respect to some
E\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
) and give a characterization for it in terms of positive-homogeneous functions (containing
E
in their exponent sets). This material was introduced in
[
3
]
and also appears in
[
15
]
. We present this material for completeness and note that our final result below, Lemma
A.5
, appears as Proposition 1.7 in
[
15
]
.

Definition A.4
.

Let
f
be a real-valued function defined on some open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also, suppose that, for
E\in\mbox{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
\{t^{E}\}
is contracting. We say that
f
is subhomogeneous with respect to
E
if, for every compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\epsilon>0
, there is some
\tau
for which

\absolutevalue{f(t^Ex)}\leq\epsilon t

for all
x\in K
and
0<t\leq\tau
.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
R
be a positive-homogeneous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
f
be defined on an open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
f(\xi)=o(R(\xi))
as
\xi\to 0
if and only if
f
is subhomogeneous with respect to
E
for every
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
.

Proof.

We first suppose that
f(\xi)=o(R(\xi))
as
\xi\to 0
and take
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
. Given
\epsilon>0
and
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact, let
M=\sup_{\xi\in K}R(\xi)<\infty
. Our hypotheses gives us a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
on which

\absolutevalue{f(\xi)}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{M}R(\xi)

for all
\xi\in\mathcal{U}
. Since
\{t^{E}\}
is contracting by virtue of Proposition
1.3
, we can pick
\tau>0
for which
t^{E}(K)\subseteq\mathcal{U}
for every
0<t\leq\tau
. Thus,

\absolutevalue{f(t^E\xi)}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{M}R(t^{E}\xi)=\frac{\epsilon}{M}tR(\xi)\leq\epsilon t

for all
0<t\leq\tau
and
\xi\in K
.

Conversely, suppose
f
is subhomogeneous with respect to
E\in\operatorname{Exp}(P)
, let
\epsilon>0
, and define
S=S_{R}=\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:R(\eta)=1\}
, which is compact by Proposition
1.3
. Then, there exists
\tau>0
such that

\absolutevalue{f(t^E\eta)}\leq\epsilon t

for every
\eta\in S
and every
0<t\leq\tau
. For any
\xi
in the open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{O}\cap\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:R(\xi)<\tau\}
of
0
,
t=R(\xi)<\tau
and
\eta=t^{-E}\xi\in S
and so it follows that

\absolutevalue{f(\xi)}=\absolutevalue{f(t^Et^{-E}\xi)}=\absolutevalue{f(t^E\eta)}\leq\epsilon t=\epsilon R(\xi).

∎
