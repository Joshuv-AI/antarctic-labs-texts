Humanoid finger with rigid-flexible-soft structure
Authors: Boyang Lyu, Huai Xiao, Qingxin Meng, Jundong Wu, Yawu Wang, Jinhua She, Edwardo F. Fukushima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soft robotic grippers offer adaptable and safe manipulation across diverse objects, but they often lack the output force and stability of rigid grippers. Developing fingers that deliver high force while delicately handling small or fragile objects remains a key challenge. Here, we design a humanoid finger with a rigid-flexible-soft structure, composed of rigid tubular bones, flexible joint springs with automatic reset and resistance to deformation under external forces, and a soft silicone skin. A pneumatic membrane actuator is placed between the bones and skin. To test its performance, we build various grippers using the proposed humanoid fingers and conduct experiments such as grasping tofu, pinching 0.1 mm paper, holding a 23.2 cm basketball, and supporting 5.275 kg weights. Our humanoid finger broadens the applications for rigid-flexible-soft robots.

Introduction

As robot technology continues to evolve, a wide variety of robots are being utilized in fields such as manufacturing and transporting, etc. Among these robots, mechanical grippers can assist humans in operating and transferring materials, boasting high practical value, and thus have garnered widespread attention. Traditional mechanical grippers are usually made of rigid materials (stainless steel, aluminum alloy, etc.), offering high output force and fast response speed1–3. They are capable of handling heavy, solid objects quickly and precisely4. However, when interacting with fragile objects, traditional rigid grippers are prone to object breakage and cannot provide the best operation performance.

To address the aforementioned issues, one possible solution is to improve the design and control of rigid robots, e.g., by introducing redundant degrees of freedom5 or by implementing more precise feedback control with additional sensors6,7. These methods enhance the capability of rigid grippers to interact with fragile objects to some extent, but often require the design of more complex grippers, thereby making the fabrication and control of these grippers difficult.

Another solution is to design soft grippers with continuous soft structures. Such grippers are generally made of easily deformable materials, such as silicone, rubber8, or fabric9,10, exhibiting high body compliance and safer interaction with the environment. They have operational simplicity11,12, straightforward design, and ease of fabrication13, enabling a wide range of applications in multiple domains, including but not limited to assistive rehabilitation14,15, food processing16, underwater exploration17, etc. However, due to the inherent softness of the materials, this type of gripper generally has lower output force and stability.

In recent years, the expanding deployment of robots across various sectors, including the medical industry18–20, agriculture21–23, and service industries24–26, has heightened the demand for advanced soft robotic fingers. These sectors necessitate grippers that seamlessly blend robustness, stability, exceptional flexibility, and safety to meet the increasingly complex challenges presented by their diverse applications.

In order to combine the advantages of rigid and soft structures, some researchers have attempted to introduce variable stiffness structures in the design of mechanical grippers. E.g., Fang et al. proposed a multimode soft robotic gripper based on fingers with a layer-jamming structure and a tendon-driven mechanism, integrating four grasping modes: enveloping, clamping, hooking, and sucking27. Experimental results demonstrated its capability to handle diverse grasping tasks, showcasing its multimode grasping performance and anthropomorphic characteristics. However, incorporating additional components to adjust stiffness would further complicate the structural design of the fingers and increase the difficulty of manufacturing and maintenance. Mizushima et al. developed a multi-fingered robotic hand, integrating a rigid skeleton, particle blockage, and deformable rubber skin28. This variable stiffness design provides the robotic hand with high adaptability to object shapes, allowing for strong and stable grasping as well as reliable manipulation of fragile or deformable objects. Nonetheless, the fingers still face challenges when handling ultra-thin objects (e.g., cards).

In this context, the development of rigid-soft structured mechanical grippers29,30 has garnered significant attention as a design approach aimed at balancing rigid and soft materials to synergistically leverage their respective advantages of rigidity and flexibility. Various design approaches for rigid-soft structured mechanical grippers are currently being explored31,32. Options include mounting soft actuators at the end of rigid frameworks33–35, encasing soft continuum structures with rigid outer frames36–42, or interconnecting multiple rigid and soft elements to mimic the humanoid finger’s architecture of rigid bones and soft joints43–45. E.g., Ishibashi et al. developed a soft robotic hand with a hybrid rigid-soft structure that combines rigidity for stability and softness for adaptability42. This design enables the hand to stably grasp and lift heavy objects, such as 2–3 kg cabbages, demonstrating its suitability for agricultural applications. However, the performance of this robotic hand in handling thin or fragile objects remains unclear, which may limit its range of applications. It can be observed that the rigid-soft structured design has, to some extent, enhanced the overall performance of mechanical grippers, making them superior to purely rigid or soft grippers in terms of comprehensive performance, while maintaining a relatively simpler structure compared to variable stiffness grippers29. However, they exhibit considerable limitations when handling certain specialized objects, particularly ultra-thin or ultra-fragile objects46.

Nowadays, designing a robotic finger that combines the high bearing capacity of rigid fingers with the flexibility and safety of soft fingers, while maintaining a simple structure and broad applicability, particularly suitability for ultra-thin or ultra-fragile objects, continues to be a challenging task. In nature, the human finger is a typical type of high-performance gripper with rigid-flexible structure. Its basic components include phalanges, joint cartilage, ligaments, muscles, etc., which operates synergistically to deliver a strong output force. The flexibility of the muscle and skin provide compliance of the finger while the rigid bones enable strong resistance to unnatural deformation during its interaction with the environment. Inspired by the structure of human fingers, we design a humanoid finger with rigid-flexible-soft structure (HFRFSS). It consists of rigid tubular bones, flexible joint springs, and a soft silicone skin covering, controlled through pneumatic membrane actuators.

The HFRFSS integrates the advantages of rigid, flexible, and soft structures, bringing its comprehensive performance closer to that of real human fingers. For instance, its ability to grasp objects extends to a wide range of sizes. The HFRFSS-based grippers can handle objects of various sizes, such as holding a basketball with a diameter of 23.2 cm (Fig. 1a), and can also pinch a piece of A4 paper only 0.1 mm thick (Fig. 1b). Additionally, it possesses a robust bearing capacity, evidenced in its ability to stably support a box with 5.275 kg weights in total (Fig. 1c). Results

Experimental preparation

To comprehensively analyze the mechanical properties of the HFRFSS, we conduct a series of experiments to evaluate its fingertip output force, gripping force, stability against external impacts, resistance to unnatural deformation, and manipulation reliability. Moreover, we also refer to the widely used structures in this field and fabricate a pure soft finger (PSF)47, a pure rigid finger (PRF), and a wire-driven system finger (WSF, a typical type of hybrid-structure finger) with dimensions similar to HFRFSS, to clearly compare the performance of HFRFSS with various common gripper designs in this field. The specific structure and production process of PSF, PRF, and WSF are presented in Supplementary Note S3.

Embracing force evaluation

We assess the finger’s ability to stably hold objects by evaluating its embracing force, which is defined as the finger output force that needs to be overcome during the process of forcefully pulling an object embraced by fingers out of the embracing range. The experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 2d, in which the HFRFSS, placed in a horizontal plane, is mounted at the end of the robotic arm, and embraces a cylindrical grip device attached to a force sensor. In the experiment, the extreme cavity pressure of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p$$\end{document}p=\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,-0.95$$\end{document}−0.95 bar is applied to the finger, and the robotic arm propels the gripping device to move at a constant speed of 2 cm/s along the direction of the relaxed state finger (Fig. 2e), pulling the device out of the finger’s embrace. During this movement, the force sensor measures the pulling force exerted by the robotic arm, which numerically corresponds to the embracing force of the HFRFSS.

The experiments are conducted using cylindrical grip devices with diameters of 4 and 5 cm, respectively, to measure the embracing forces of the designed HFRFSS when embracing objects of different sizes. We also conduct similar experiments with PSF, PRF and WSF for comparative analysis (Fig. 2e). The results are shown in Fig. 2f, g. According to the figures, the embracing forces of the HFRFSS to the grip devices with diameters of 4 and 5 cm are approximately 8.5 and 8.3 N, respectively. The HFRFSS provides an embracing force about 3.5 times higher than that of the PSF, which also uses pneumatic actuation. Meanwhile, it is about 85% of the embracing force of the WSF and about 65% of that of the PRF, respectively. In addition, as depicted in Fig. 2f, g, PRF and WSF exhibit a mechanical limit (denoted by the red triangular markers in the figures). Exceeding this limit results in slippage and failure to maintain object grasp, primarily due to insufficient compliance. In contrast, HFRFSS and PSF maintain smooth and stable hugging forces throughout the entire motion range due to their excellent compliance. These results demonstrate that HFRFSS offers greater embracing force than that of traditional soft fingers while avoiding the slippage when rigid fingers reach their mechanical limit, generating a continuous and smooth embracing force.

In addition, we test the embracing force of human fingers through this experimental platform. Supplementary Note S4 records the specific experimental process and a comparison chart of HFRFSS, PSF, and human finger’s embracing force. Based on comparative data, the embracing force of HFRFSS is significantly superior to that of PSF, but it is still slightly inferior to that of an adult finger.

Stability against external impacts

We test the stability of the HFRFSS through the experimental platform shown in Fig. 2h. As depicted, the HFRFSS is positioned in a vertical plane, with its fingertip pointing upwards and the other end fixed to the table. In this experiment, we employ the robotic arm to propel a hammer horizontally toward the fingertip of the HFRFSS at a constant speed of 1 m/s, generating a sudden external impact. Following the impact, the lateral vibration of the fingertip is recorded using a laser distance sensor to evaluate the stability of the HFRFSS (Fig. 2i). Similar experiments for the PSF, PRF, and WSF are also conducted for comparative analysis.

Figure 2j displays the experimental results for recorded lateral vibration. As shown in the figure, the vibration amplitude of the HFRFSS is approximately 40% of that of the PSF, 85% of that of the WSF, and 105% of that of the PRF. Moreover, the HFRFSS can rapidly recover back to near its relaxed state in about 0.5 s. These properties validate the strong stability of the HFRFSS against external impacts.

Resistance to unnatural deformation

Unnatural deformation of a humanoid finger refers to deviations from natural finger bending under external forces, such as outward and transverse bending. In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate the resistance of the HFRFSS against such deformations by measuring the external forces required to cause unnatural bending at different angles.

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2k, where the fingertip of the HFRFSS is positioned within a ring-like structure, attached to a force sensor, and mounted at the end of the robotic arm. In experiments, the HFRFSS is placed in a horizontal plane with its naturally curved side facing upward and resists unnatural bending as the robotic arm propels the ring downwards. The ability of the HFRFSS to resist outward bending is assessed by measuring pulling forces (Fig. 2l). Similarly, the ability of the HFRFSS to resist transverse bending is assessed when the finger is positioned horizontally with its naturally curved side facing inward (Fig. 2l). Comparative experiments are conducted for PSF, PRF and WSF.

The experimental results are presented in Fig. 2m, n. According to the figures, for the same degree of outward bending, the pulling force required for HFRFSS is about 200–250% larger than for PSF, which is close to PRF but only about 50% of WSF. For the transverse bending, HFRFSS requires a tensile force approximately 300–450% larger than PSF, surpassing PRF and reaching around 85% of WSF. Moreover, when the outward bending and transverse bending angles reach 20 degrees, it is considered the limit of the payload capacity of the fingers. At this point, the HFRFSS can withstand external forces of 5.31 and 9.32 N, respectively, while also meeting the target specifications in Table 1. This experiment confirms that the designed HFRFSS demonstrates superior resistance to unnatural deformation and exhibits a high payload capacity, which enable HFFRSS to grasp and hold objects better.

Manipulation reliability

We evaluate the manipulation reliability of the fingers by assessing their stability when gripping soft and fragile objects, as well as the degree of damage caused to the objects during the grasping process. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2o. The HFRFSS-based two-finger gripper is attached to the end of a robotic arm, and a piece of soft and fragile tofu is placed on the platform directly below the gripper. The whole grasping motion involves descending the gripper to pick up the tofu, lifting it for a period of time, placing the tofu back onto the platform (Fig. 2p), and finally restoring the gripper to its original position. The experiment is repeated consecutively 20 times. Meanwhile, we take photos of the tofu’s surface and measure its weight before and after the experiment, respectively, to evaluate the damage caused during the grasping process. For comparison, the experiment is also conducted using two-finger grippers based on PSF, PRF, and WSF, videos of these experiments are provided in the Supplementary Movie S1.

The experimental results are presented in Fig. 2q. As shown in the figure, the HFRFSS-based two-finger gripper causes hardly any visible damage to the tofu’s surface, and the loss of tofu mass is only 0.06 g, which is almost negligible. The PSF-based two-finger gripper also shows similar results. In contrast, the grippers based on PRF and WSF both leave noticeable indentations on the tofu’s surface, and result in a 1.13 and 0.92 g loss of mass, respectively. These results demonstrate that the HFRFSS exhibits excellent manipulation reliability for handling soft and fragile objects.

For objects of various shapes and weights, the gripper requires different poses and output forces to achieve a stable grasp. To analyze the stability and reliability of HFRFSS when grasping objects of different shapes and weights, we conduct experiments using the HFRFSS-based two-finger gripper to consecutively grasp a box with different loads. There are 10 groups of experiments tasked with grasping the same object, each comprising 20 grasps. The specific process of the experiment can refer to Supplementary Note S5 and Movie S2, and the experimental results are shown in Fig. S9.

The experimental results show that, despite the substantial differences in shape and weight between the box and the tofu, the HFRFSS-based two-finger gripper achieves 20 times successful consecutive grasps in each of the 10 groups of experiments when grasping an empty box weighing 124 g and the box with a 200 g load. However, when grasping the box with a 500 g load, the gripper experiences grasping failures, and the average number of successful consecutive grasps in each group of experiments is 5.8 times. The main reason for grasping failure is that as the load weight increases, there will be a certain deviation in the placement of the box during each time the gripper grasping and returning it. Hence, multiple grasps accumulate larger placement deviations, resulting in a decrease in the number of consecutive successful grasps. Such a deviation could potentially be compensated for by developing more precise control methods in future work.

HFRFSS grippers

We employ the designed HFRFSS to construct two grippers—a 124.4 g two-finger gripper and a 160.5 g three-finger gripper. The grasping ability of these grippers is experimentally assessed through a series of experiments, demonstrating their ability to stably grasp objects of different weights and sizes, as shown in Fig. 3. The objects in Fig. 3 are carefully selected based on the functional requirements of the HFRFSS in grasping tasks. Specifically, ultra-thin or ultra-fragile objects, such as tofu, egg yolk, A4 paper, potato chips, and balloon, are chosen to validate the HFRFSS’s ability to handle such objects, while large or heavy objects, such as 3D-printed material, cabbage, and basketball, are used to demonstrate its capability to handle common large or heavy objects. To standardize the grasping process, we develop a grasping protocol. During each grasping process, we ensure that the object being grasped leaves the platform by more than 3 cm and maintains a stable grasping time of more than 5 s. Then, we construct a five-finger humanoid hand weighing 260.0 g with the designed HFRFSS. Discussion

The expansion of the current application field of robots poses significant challenges to the comprehensiveness of humanoid finger performance. In this context, the paper designs, manufactures, and tests a HFRFSS. Compared to existing mechanical fingers, this design better integrates the advantages of rigidity, flexibility, and soft structure while maintaining compactness, and its overall performance is more competitive. The combination of rigid tubular bones and flexible joint springs ensures the stiffness and stability of the HFRFSS while achieving a simple and lightweight structure. At the same time, it endows HFRFSS with excellent resistance to unnatural deformation. On this basis, the pneumatic membrane actuator tightly wrapped around the rigid tubular bones allows HFRFSS to have soft, human-like output force without taking up additional space. The soft silicone skin covering the outermost layer further enhances the durability, smoothness, and safety of HFRFSS.

Experimental results have shown that HFRFSS exhibits excellent compliance, stability, flexibility, and safety during operation. The grippers equipped with HFRFSS can effectively grasp objects of different masses, hardness, shapes, and sizes, such as 5.275 kg weights and a light and fragile potato chip. Other advantages of HFRFSS include simple production, fast response time, and easy maintenance. The HFRFSS can achieve stable and safe grasping of various different objects in unstructured environments with a simpler structure and lower cost, serving as an alternative to human fingers. It provides more choices for application fields, such as the medical industry, service industry, and agriculture that require robots to face complex tasks.

As shown in Fig. 2, results from comparative experiments between the HFRFSS, PSF, PRF, and WSF with similar size show that the HFRFSS exhibits superior performance across various mechanical properties. In addition, there exists studies that improve the performance of grippers through ingenious finger structural design, e.g., the soft finger in ref. 27 has a layer jamming structure integrated with tendon-driven actuation, enabling the gripper composed of this finger to achieve variable stiffness and multiple grasping modes. The gripper in ref. 42 has a rigid-soft finger structure that is capable of stably grasping and lifting heavy objects. Compared to these fingers, the HFRFSS draws inspiration from the natural structure of human fingers—the most dexterous finger structure in nature, owing to their inherent rigid-flexible-soft architecture—a design the HFRFSS also incorporates. This biomimetic design makes the gripper with HFRFSS to achieve not only versatility but also exhibit human-like grasping modes, such as supporting, holding, and lifting, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Meanwhile, as demonstrated in Fig. 3, the gripper with HFRFSS can grasp objects of different masses, hardness, shapes, and sizes, making it well-suited for a wider range of applications and demonstrating its superior adaptability across diverse tasks. Nevertheless, there is still a certain gap compared to human fingers of similar sizes (Supplementary Note S4). On the one hand, the sizes of the proximal, middle, and distal phalanges in human fingers are typically different50. Although there is considerable variation in finger length and phalangeal dimensions between individuals, the optimal lengths and dimensions of finger segments remain a topic worthy of further investigation. On the other hand, the muscles of human fingers51 are very sensitive and efficient actuators, and we still have a long way to go in learning and imitating this type of actuator.

In future work, we hope to further improve and optimize the driving method of this design, imitating the muscles and the segmented bone of human fingers, in order to enhance the mechanical performance of HFRFSS while maintaining structural simplicity and low cost. Meanwhile, we also plan to design a high-performance manipulator based on HFRFSS in our future work. We will use finite element analysis and other methods to further study the stability and force distribution of the manipulator based on HFRFSS, in order to explore the most suitable gripping methods for handling objects of different sizes, weights, and textures.

Methods

Design of the humanoid finger

The human finger is a naturally evolved high-performance rigid-flexible-soft hybrid functional unit. Through the synergistic integration of rigid phalanges, flexible cartilage and ligaments, along with soft muscles and skin, human fingers simultaneously achieve superior force output and structural stability, while maintaining high flexibility and operational safety. Here, inspired by the rigid-flexible-soft structure of the human finger (Fig. 5a), we design an HFRFSS with the aim of providing an alternative to human fingers in the service industry or agriculture, enabling stable and safe gripping of various objects with a simpler structure and lower cost. The HFRFSS consists of rigid tubular bones, flexible joint springs, pneumatic membrane actuators, and soft silicone skin, as shown in Fig. 5b.Fig. 5Comparison and principle analysis of various structures of a human finger and HFRFSS.a Anatomical structure diagram of human fingers. b Structural assembly diagram of HFRFSS. c Explosion diagram of HFRFSS structure. d A schematic diagram of the forward bending of human finger structure. e Schematic diagram of HFRFSS forward bending. f Schematic diagram of HFRFSS joint structure. g Schematic diagram of human fingers resisting reverse and lateral bending. h Schematic diagram of HFRFSS resistance to reverse and lateral bending. i Structural diagram of flexible joint spring. j The bending action of human fingers. k A schematic diagram of muscles driving the bending of human fingers. l Schematic diagram of HFRFSS bending driven by pneumatic thin membrane actuator.

The designed HFRFSS should possess the following advantages: (1) exceptional suitability for handling ultra-thin or ultra-fragile objects; (2) ability to handle large or heavy common objects; and (3) ease of fabrication and maintenance. Based on these functional requirements and inspired by the human finger, the target specifications for the HFRFSS are presented in Table 1. Note that in this table, weight, length, and diameter are intrinsic physical parameters; fingertip output force is used to assess the actuation power; and resistance to external forces (including outward bending and transverse bending) is used to evaluate the payload capacity.

Rigid tubular bones: The rigid tubular bones consist of four hollow cylindrical structures, resembling the distal, middle, and proximal phalanges, as well as the anterior end of the metacarpal bone of the human finger (Fig. 5c). The cylindrical structure at the fingertip of the distal phalanx has a closed inclined section, enabling human-like fingertip touching. Meanwhile, an air duct connector is mounted at the opposite end of the metacarpal bone, allowing for pneumatic actuation. At each joint between two bones, we design semi-circular incisions at the ends of the cylindrical structures, allowing for a 90-degree inward bending around the joints just like human finger (Fig. 5d, e). Supplementary Note S1 introduces the detailed structure of rigid tubular bones.

We construct a 3D model of rigid tubular bones. The specific structure and dimensions of the model can refer to the Supplementary Note S1. Some common rigid materials, such as steel, aluminum alloys, and polylactic acid (PLA), can serve as potential candidates. Based on the design requirements, rigid materials should be selected for their high tensile and compressive strength, low density, and ease of processing and manufacturing, ensuring that the rigid tubular bones provide sufficient structural stability while maintaining a lightweight design and simple assembly. Therefore, the PLA is chosen as the material for the rigid tubular bones, and they are fabricated using a fused deposition modeling 3D printer (YINKE445, Wuhan Allcct Company).

Flexible joint springs: to provide the HFRFSS with a certain degree of flexibility and resistance to unnatural deformation, we design a flexible joint spring (Fig. 5f) to connect two rigid tubular bones and supply restorative torque, assisting the finger in returning to its relaxed state. The main specifications of the flexible joint spring are as follows: a wire diameter of 0.2 mm, 6 coils, and a stiffness coefficient of 0.0380 N m/rad. Additionally, the spring has a parallel dual torsion structure configuration (Fig. 5i), working in conjunction with the bone structures to prevent any transverse bending, enabling the HFRFSS to function similarly to a human finger (Fig. 5g, h).

Pneumatic membrane actuator: we develop a pneumatic membrane actuator to achieve inward bending actuation (Fig. 5c). The actuator consists of a thin layer of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) membrane in a tubular structure, with a closed end at the fingertip side and an open end in the opposite direction. The tubular membrane is meticulously designed with an inner diameter closely matching the outer diameter of the rigid tubular bones, allowing it to tightly encase the latter, thereby creating a pneumatic cavity within (Fig. 5b).

For the material of the pneumatic membrane actuator, it is required to have high elasticity, softness, resistance to damage, and the ability to withstand low negative pressure. Taking the above into consideration, we select 0.05 mm thick PVC membrane as the material for producing the pneumatic membrane actuator. The specific material selection and testing process can be found in Supplementary Note S1. When making the component, we cut a 15.5 cm segment of a long PVC tube with an inner diameter of 2.0 cm and a thickness of 0.05 mm. During the cutting process, it should be ensured that one end of the segment is flat and the other end is trimmed into an arc resembling the contour of the distal phalanx of the rigid tubular bones. Then, a laminating machine is used to seal the arced end along the cut contour. After sealing, we fit the sealed PVC tube over the rigid tubular bones and trim off any excess material at the sealed end. Finally, we use a heat gun to shape the front end of the PVC tube, which ensures that the shape of the pneumatic membrane actuator conforms to the rigid tubular bones.

Soft silicone skin: in order to protect the internal structure of HFRFSS and enable HFRFSS to fit the surface of objects, protect fragile objects, increase the friction of the contact surface, and enhance the gripping stability, we designed a layer of soft silicone skin on the outside of HFRFSS (Fig. 5c). For the material of the soft silicone skin, it is required to possess softness, high durability, and a sufficient friction coefficient. Therefore, we select flipped silicone (provided by SanJingXinDe Company), created by thoroughly mixing Component A and Component B in a 1:1 ratio, as the material for the soft silicone skin, which was produced in bulk through mold forming.

We customize a stainless-steel mold based on the shape of HFRFSS. During molding, an appropriate amount of defoamed flip silicone is injected into the mold and left to stand for 24 h before being removed. Finally, holes are opened in the corresponding position of the joint bending direction of HFRFSS to complete the production of soft silicone skin.

Through the integration of the four components mentioned above, the HFRFSS achieves a trade-off between strength and flexibility that meets the design requirements. The rigid tubular bones form the main structure of the HFRFSS, providing sufficient strength. Flexible joint springs with an appropriate stiffness coefficient are incorporated between the rigid tubular bones to introduce a degree of flexibility.

The flexible joint spring’s stiffness coefficient should be carefully selected to ensure that the HFRFSS generates a certain fingertip output force under large bending angles, while also possessing the capacity to resist unnatural deformation. The stiffness coefficient value of the flexible joint spring selected in this work (i.e., 0.0380 N m/rad) is fully in line with these requirements. As shown in Fig. 2c, under the extreme negative air pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.95$$\end{document}−0.95 bar) provided by the pneumatic control system, the HFRFSS can generate a bending angle of approximately 170° while still maintaining a certain level of fingertip output force, allowing it to grasp small and lightweight objects. A stiffness coefficient higher than the one we have chosen may reduce the bending angle range of the HFRFSS, while a stiffness coefficient lower than this chosen value may reduce the HFRFSS’s resistance against unnatural deformation.

The pneumatic membrane actuator is made from a soft material with high elasticity, softness, damage resistance, and the ability to withstand low negative pressure, enabling the HFRFSS to generate a high driving force. Additionally, the soft silicone skin, fabricated from a material with softness, high durability, and a sufficient friction coefficient, enhances the HFRFSS’s ability to conform to object surfaces, protect fragile items, and improve gripping stability.

The prototype of the HFRFSS has a weight of 44 g, a length of 15 cm, and an outer diameter of 2.4 cm, all of which align with the proposed target specifications. Actuation of the finger is achieved by extracting air from the pneumatic cavity using an air pump connected to the air duct connector. As the cavity pressure gradually decreases from the atmospheric pressure, the pneumatic membrane actuator quickly contracts, propelling the phalanx to rotate inwards, and thus inducing the bending of the HFRFSS in a manner similar to a human finger (Fig. 5j–l). If the cavity pressure of the actuator remains constant at this time, HFRFSS will maintain the current bending degree. As the pressure gradually recovers to atmosphere levels, the cavity of the actuator tends to regain its original shape, and the restorative torque of the flexible torsion springs drives the finger back to its relaxed state. The above-mentioned integrated process forms the fundamental mechanism of basic finger movement. By adjusting the cavity pressure of the actuator, HFRFSS can be bent to different angles just like human fingers, as detailed in Supplementary Note S2.

Experimental platform

To test the mechanical performance of HFRFSS and evaluate the gripping ability of HFRFSS gripper, we build an experimental platform for pneumatic robots. The experimental platform mainly consists of the following three parts: a pneumatic control system, a six-degree-of-freedom robotic arm, and a sensor system.

Pneumatic control system: the pneumatic control system provides the required air pressure for the experiment and performs real-time control on HFRFSS. The system configuration includes a positive pressure air pump (OTX-550X3, Outstanding company) and a negative pressure air pump (JBL-750, JiaBaoLi company) for providing and maintaining the necessary pressure for the pneumatic drive. Additionally, there is a set of five proportional pressure regulators (VPPI-5L-3-G18-1V1H-V1-S1D, Festo company) responsible for regulating and providing real-time feedback on the cavity pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p$$\end{document}p of the pneumatic actuator. To connect the computer to the proportional pressure regulator, a set of two I/O modules (PCIe-6363, National Instruments) is utilized. Lastly, a computer is responsible for issuing control commands and displaying feedback data. This configuration ensures precise pressure regulation and enhances experiment efficiency. The schematic diagram of the pneumatic control system is described in Supplementary Note S6.

Six-degree-of-freedom robotic arm: the six-degree-of-freedom robotic arm (MyCobot Pro-600, Elephant Robotics) can flexibly change its end pose and strictly follow the set program to complete the action. With the assistance of this robotic arm, we fix the HFRFSS in a specific position during output force testing and resistance to unnatural deformation testing, or move the cylindrical grip and hammer at a uniform speed during embracing force testing and stability testing against external influences. The use of the robotic arm reduces the uncertainty factors in the experiment and improves the reliability of the experimental results.

Sensor system: the sensor system is responsible for the precise measurement of force and displacement signals in the experiment. The configuration of this system includes a micro pressure sensor (JLBS-M2, Jsensor company), used to measure the output force and embracing force of the fingertip, as well as the pressure required to bend the finger at a specific angle at the fingertip. A miniature laser displacement sensor (MC-HGC-1000C, Panasonic) is used to measure the amplitude of fingertip vibration after a sudden impact on the finger. These precision measurement tools ensure the accuracy of experimental data.
