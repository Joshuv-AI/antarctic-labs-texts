Cigarette smoke and e-cigarette aerosol extracts induce myelopoiesis and suppress inflammatory cytokine production
Authors: Jeanette Y C Sullivan, Helen X Huang, Jianhong C Heidmann, Francisco Diaz, Camille Brzechffa, Jane H Chen, Eli M Soyfer, Nneamaka Iwobi, Irene Hasen, Amanda Ting, Nicole Sparks, Michael T Kleinman, Angela G Fleischman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTobacco and nicotine use remain leading preventable drivers of cancer risk, and both combustible cigarettes and electronic nicotine devices perturb immune function and hematopoiesis. However, side-by-side comparisons of their effects across the hematopoietic hierarchy remain limited. Here, we evaluate the impact of cigarette smoke extract (CSE) and e-cigarette vapor extract (EVE) on inflammatory responses in mature myeloid cells and on hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell function using complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches. Using a standardized protocol applied across multiple macrophage models, including RAW264.7 cells, THP-1 macrophages, primary mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages, and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells, we found that CSE consistently suppressed LPS-induced TNF-α secretion in a dose-dependent manner, whereas EVE alone had minimal effect. CSE also induced foam cell formation in macrophages through a reactive oxygen species-dependent mechanism. Additionally, both CSE and EVE suppressed hematopoietic progenitor colony formation. To model real-world exposure patterns, we used a nose-only inhalation system to deliver combustible cigarette smoke followed by e-cigarette aerosol to mice. Sequential smoke and e-cigarette exposure increased platelet counts and myeloid cell frequency, effects not observed with smoking cessation alone. Myeloid skewing persisted through secondary transplantation, indicating durable, cell-intrinsic changes to the hematopoietic stem cell compartment. Together, these findings demonstrate that tobacco and e-cigarette exposures blunt innate immune responsiveness while driving lasting myeloid-biased hematopoiesis, with implications for infection susceptibility, cardiovascular disease, and hematologic malignancy risk.Keywords: e-cigarettes, macrophages, hematopoietic stem cells, myelopoiesis

Combustible cigarette smoke and electronic cigarette aerosol are 2 of the most common inhaled exposures that alter immunity and blood formation. Both deliver oxidants and bioactive chemicals that can rewire inflammatory signaling and reshape marrow output. However, their effects are context-dependent, as their impact can vary with dose, timing, and cell type under study. The hematopoietic system contains a wide variety of cells, each with unique physiological functions. At one end of the hierarchy, mature macrophages orchestrate front-line defense and tissue repair; at the other, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) integrate inflammatory cues and decide whether to self-renew or differentiate. Tobacco smoke modulates the immune system across this hierarchy, from stem and progenitor cells to mature myeloid populations (Ramanathan et al. 2020), yet most studies examine either mature immune cells or HSCs in isolation. Side-by-side comparisons of combustible versus e-cigarette exposures that span both macrophage function and hematopoietic stem cell biology remain limited.

Evidence indicates tobacco smoke exerts bidirectional immune effects; it can provoke inflammation yet also blunt host defense. Smokers show elevated plasma cytokines (Elisia et al. 2020), and a smoking history worsens inflammatory and autoimmune disease courses (Arnson et al. 2010; Mahmoudzadeh et al. 2023). In the lung, smokers have 2- to 3-fold as many macrophages in their bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (Gonçalves et al. 2011). Paradoxically, smoking also increases the risk of respiratory and other infections (Lugade et al. 2014), likely reflecting impaired immune responsiveness in the setting of chronic inflammation. Whether cigarette smoke and e-cigarette vapor similarly suppress acute cytokine responses when tested under standardized conditions across multiple macrophage models has not been systematically addressed.

Combustible cigarettes contain tobacco and fillers that are burned and release over 7,000 chemicals (Zarcone et al. 2023). E-cigarettes, in contrast, are battery-operated devices that vaporize e-liquid containing propylene glycol (PG), vegetable glycerin (VG), flavorings, and nicotine. The accessibility, flavors, and discreet use of e-cigarettes have led to rising nicotine use among adolescents, young adults, and non-smokers. In vitro studies of cigarette smoke extract (CSE), e-cigarette vapor extract (EVE), or their individual components have produced conflicting results, showing both enhanced and repressed cytokine production by monocytes and macrophages. The heterogeneity of e-cigarette formulations further complicates the interpretation of these context-dependent effects. Beyond immunological effects, cigarette smoking is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and the transformation of macrophages into lipid-laden foam cells represents one of the earliest cellular events in atherogenesis (Messner and Bernhard 2014). Whether CSE alone, independent of exogenous modified lipoproteins, is sufficient to drive foam cell formation and the role of reactive oxygen species in this process remains incompletely defined. Moreover, although considerable effort has been devoted to studying the impact of smoking and vaping on mature immune cells, much less is known about how these exposures affect hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells.

At the stem and progenitor level, inflammatory and oxidative stress cues transiently push HSCs into cycle and bias lineage output toward the myeloid fate; chronic or repeated stress can erode self-renewal and remodel progenitor composition (Pietras 2017). Myeloid-biased hematopoiesis is a cardinal feature of the aged hematopoietic system (Rossi et al. 2007a, 2007b), raising the question of whether tobacco exposures may accelerate functional aging of the HSC pool. Most adult vapers are current or former combustible cigarette smokers (Kramarow and Elgaddal 2023), yet the hematopoietic consequences of this sequential exposure pattern have not been explored. Understanding how cigarette smoke and e-cigarette exposures influence both mature immune cells and hematopoietic stem cell function has important implications for long-term health outcomes, including susceptibility to infection, cardiovascular disease, and hematologic malignancies.

To address these gaps, we established a standardized in vitro platform to compare the effects of CSE and EVE on inflammatory cytokine production across multiple macrophage models and extended these analyses to foam cell formation, hematopoietic progenitor colony assays, and long-term transplantation. We further modeled the real-world pattern of transitioning from combustible smoking to e-cigarette use through chronic nose-only inhalation exposures in mice, followed by secondary transplantation to assess durable changes in HSC function.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal procedures were performed under the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of California, Irvine. Wildtype C57BL/6J (Stock No. 000664) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) at 8- to 10-week-old and housed in specific pathogen-free facilities on a 12-h light/dark cycle. Euthanasia was performed using an overdose of isoflurane, and euthanasia was confirmed by cervical dislocation. Where indicated, complete blood counts were obtained using the automated cell counter machine (ABCVet Hemalyzer, scilVet).

Isolation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Peripheral blood was collected from healthy blood donors in EDTA tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using the Lymphoprep density gradient medium (Stemcell). Human healthy blood donors were recruited under IRB #2014-9995.

THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells

RAW 264.7 cell lines were obtained commercially. Cells were revived from a previously frozen third-passage stock and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (R10) in 10 mm tissue culture-treated Petri dishes. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Once the cells reached confluency, they were passaged using 1 ml of trypsin for 5 min to facilitate detachment. Cells were gently scraped and transferred to a 50 ml conical tube containing 40 ml of R10. From this, 10 ml of the cell suspension was replated into a fresh 10 mm Petri dish for continued culture. The remaining cells were seeded into 96-well tissue culture-treated plates and allowed to adhere overnight in the incubator before stimulation.

THP-1 cell lines were obtained commercially (American Type Culture Collection, USA). Cells were thawed from a previously frozen stock and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (R10) in T-25 flasks. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages

Bone marrow was extracted by flushing the femurs and tibias with 10 ml of staining buffer using a syringe through a 45-µm mesh strainer. The collected marrow was subjected to red blood cell lysis using ACK buffer on ice for 20 min, followed by washing and centrifugation. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (D10). A small aliquot of the suspension was analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the total bone marrow cell count. To generate bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), 20 million bone marrow cells were resuspended in 10 ml of D10 supplemented with 30% L929 cell-conditioned media. Cells were seeded in non-tissue culture-treated 10 mm Petri dishes and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 3 d, each dish was supplemented with an additional 3 ml of fresh D10 + 30% L929 media. On day 5, the media in each dish was replaced entirely with 10 ml fresh D10 + 30% L929 media. After 7 d, the supernatant was discarded, and the macrophages were incubated with 1 ml of trypsin for 5 min. Macrophages were detached by scraping gently and collecting into D10.

Cigarette smoke extract preparation

CSE was prepared from mainstream cigarette smoke using a standard protocol. The ISO standard is one 2-s puff with volume 35 ml/puff every minute (Wright 2015). Briefly, mainstream smoke from one 2R4F research cigarette (9.4 mg tar, 0.726 mg nicotine) was drawn into serum-free media by continuous negative pressure and filter-sterilized to obtain 100% CSE. Volume/volume percentage (v/v %) dilutions were used for in vitro exposures.

E-cigarette vape extract preparation

We utilized a widely available retail vape pod system (Vaporesso XROS 3) and a popular flavor formulation (Naked brand Melon, 65:35 VG: PG with 6 mg/ml nicotine) to reflect real-world consumer exposure, as recommended by the National Academies report on e-cigarette health effects (NASEM 2018). Vapor was generated using an impinger system delivering 4.3-s puffs at 60-second intervals for 75 min, parameters derived from observational studies of actual e-cigarette user behavior (Farsalinos et al. 2013; Hua et al. 2013; Behar et al. 2015). This puff duration of 4.3 s reflects the mean value observed among e-cigarette users, which is significantly longer than the 2.4-s average for conventional cigarette smokers (Hua et al. 2013). This approach aligns with Talbot laboratory studies demonstrating that commercially purchased pod-based systems generate reproducible aerosol compositions suitable for biological studies, and that device type, flavor, and puffing parameters substantially influence toxicological outcomes (Omaiye et al. 2019, 2022; Williams et al. 2019). By standardizing device, flavor, and puff interval, we minimized variability inherent to e-cigarette systems while maintaining translational relevance.

Nose-only inhalation exposures

Exposures were performed at the Air Pollution Health Effects Laboratory at the University of California, Irvine. Exposures occurred 2 h per day, 4 d/wk, for 8 wk. Between exposures, the mice were housed 4 per cage in an atmosphere-controlled room on a 12-h light/dark cycle in an Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) accredited animal housing facility at the University of California, Irvine vivarium. Aerosols were generated using a 2-s puff with a 60-s interval between puffs (35 ml puff volume) based on the ISO standard cigarette puff protocol (ISO 3308:2012) using a custom-built smoking system. This system uses a peristaltic pump to draw in aerosolized smoke from a lit combustion cigarette (1R6F Certified Reference Cigarette, Center of Tobacco Reference Products (CTRP), University of Kentucky) and directs it into a nose-only exposure manifold (In-Tox Products, Moriarty, New Mexico). For the e-cigarette exposure, the peristaltic pump draws in aerosolized vapor from a Nichrome 70-W E-cigarette. The e-liquid used was made by mixing equal volumes of propylene glycol (Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts) and vegetable glycerin (Fisher Chemical, Hampton, New Hampshire) with 1.5% of nicotine (ThermoFisher, Waltham, Massachusetts). Control mice were exposed to air purified over potassium permanganate-impregnated alumina beads, activated carbon, and high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, concurrently with the combustion cigarette or e-cigarette exposures.

The nose-cone inhalation exposure system is advantageous over whole-body exposure systems because it minimizes fur contamination and potential non-relevant exposure due to routine grooming that occurs in a whole-body exposure regime. During exposures, animals were held in individual exposure tubes that were connected to the exposure manifold and positioned with just the snout exposed to the exposure atmosphere. Exhaust ports surrounding each nose cone were under slight negative pressure to direct the flow of fresh aerosols to the animal’s breathing zone, and exhaust exhaled air, thereby minimizing the potential for re-breathing secondary vapor and preventing CO2 buildup.

Platelet counting

Platelet counting was performed as adapted from Masters and Harrison (2014). Peripheral blood from the saphenous vein was mixed 1:1 with EDTA, diluted in staining buffer with 0.2 μl of FITC CD61, and incubated for 20 min. Samples were further diluted and run on a Novocyte 3000 flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies) to obtain absolute platelet counts.

Flow cytometry of mouse peripheral blood/bone marrow

To determine peripheral blood cell frequencies, peripheral blood from the saphenous vein was mixed 1:1 with EDTA, and red blood cells were lysed with ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) buffer. Cells were stained with anti-mouse antibodies specific to CD45.1, CD45.2, CD11b, CD19, Ly6G, CD3, CD4, CD8, and Ly6C. For the immature stem and progenitor populations, bone marrow was stained with anti-mouse antibodies CD34, CD150, CD16/32, CD48, cKit (CD117), lineage cocktail (containing: CD3, Ly-6C, CD11b, CD45R, Ter-119), and Sca-1. All antibodies were used at a 1:500 dilution factor as recommended.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were performed as indicated by StemCell Technology protocols. Whole bone marrow cells from wild-type mice were incubated with increasing concentrations of CSE or e-cigarette vape extract (EVE) overnight. Cells were washed thoroughly and plated at a concentration of 10,000 cells/ml in Methocult medium (M3224, StemCell) with IL-3 at 10 ng/ml and SCF at 50 ng/ml. Colonies were counted 7 to 12 d later.

Measurement of cytokine secretion

Concentrations of TNF-α were determined in conditioned media supernatant from cells incubated with 10 ng/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma) and increasing concentrations of CSE and EVE for 24 h. Samples were frozen at −80 °C until analysis. Commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for human TNF-α (88-7346-88, Invitrogen/ThermoFisher) and mouse TNF-α (88-7324-88, Invitrogen/ThermoFisher) were used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California) was used for all statistical analyses. Statistical tests used are stated in each figure legend.

Results

Standardized in vitro model reveals differential effects of cigarette smoke and e-cigarette exposure on macrophage TNF-α responses

To investigate how smoke or e-cigarette exposure influences inflammatory responses, we established a standardized workflow in which all cell culture and analytic protocols were consistent across experiments. For CSE, we employed the standard reference approach using 2R4F Kentucky research cigarettes (University of Kentucky Center for Tobacco Reference Products). Smoke was generated under controlled conditions and bubbled into culture medium to create a standardized aqueous extract, as previously described (Gaschler et al. 2008). For EVE, we utilized a widely available retail vape pod system (Vaporesso XROS 3) and a popular flavor formulation (Naked brand Melon, 65:35 VG: PG with 6 mg/ml nicotine), with vapor generated using an impinger system delivering 4.3-second puffs at 60-s intervals for 75 min, parameters derived from observational studies of e-cigarette user behavior (Farsalinos et al. 2013; Hua et al. 2013; Behar et al. 2015). Single-use aliquots of both CSE and EVE were prepared and stored under identical conditions to ensure batch consistency, such that the only experimental variable across conditions was the cell type under study. Because higher concentrations of CSE and EVE can impair cell viability, we selected minimal concentrations (0.5% to 2%) that did not induce significant cell death under our culture conditions.

We first examined how CSE and EVE influence tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) production in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Cells were exposed to CSE and/or EVE concurrently with LPS stimulation, and supernatant was collected 24 h later to quantify TNF-α by ELISA. To broadly evaluate these effects, we tested both human and murine macrophage cell lines as well as primary macrophages derived from human peripheral blood monocytes and mouse bone marrow (Fig. 1). In RAW264.7 cells, CSE significantly suppressed TNF-α secretion in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). Suppression of TNF-α by CSE was also confirmed using intracellular cytokine staining (Fig. S1). In contrast, EVE prepared from a commercially available melon-flavored e-liquid did not significantly alter TNF-α levels (Fig. 1A).

We then applied the in vitro smoke and e-cigarette exposure protocol to THP-1-derived macrophages. THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into macrophages with Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) before stimulation. THP-1 macrophages showed a dose-dependent reduction in TNF-α secretion in response to CSE (Fig. 1B). Although EVE alone did not significantly suppress TNF-α production, co-exposure with CSE further reduced secretion compared with CSE alone (Fig. 1B).

We next tested how CSE and EVE affect primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from wild-type C57BL/6 mice. Consistent with the cell line data, we observed dose-dependent inhibition of TNF-α in BMDMs by CSE (Fig. 1C). Primary macrophages and immortalized cell lines thus responded similarly to CSE, both demonstrating suppressive effects on LPS-induced TNF-α.

We next applied this workflow to human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) freshly isolated from whole blood of healthy donors. Overall, the responses to CSE and EVE were variable across individuals, without a uniform pattern of TNF-α inhibition or stimulation. However, when analyzed on a per-donor basis, the data revealed 2 distinct response groups. Most donors (6 of 9) exhibited reduced TNF-α secretion after CSE exposure, with some also showing suppression at higher EVE doses (TNF-α ratio <1). In contrast, 3 donors demonstrated the opposite effect, with enhanced TNF-α secretion following exposure to CSE or EVE. These groups were not associated with age, sex, or medication use, and smoking history was unavailable (Fig. 2).

Cigarette smoke extract induces foam cell formation mediated via reactive oxygen species

Having established that CSE suppresses acute cytokine responses in macrophages, we next asked whether CSE also drives a second, disease-relevant macrophage phenotype independent of cytokine output. Macrophages play a central role in atherosclerosis, and cigarette smoking is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease. Foam cell formation, the transformation of macrophages into lipid-laden cells, is one of the earliest cellular events in atherogenesis. To test whether CSE alone is sufficient to induce foam cell formation in the absence of exogenous modified lipoproteins, we exposed wild-type bone marrow-derived macrophages to CSE, OxLDL (as a positive control), or vehicle and quantified intracellular Oil Red O-positive lipid vesicles per cell (Fig. 3). CSE alone significantly increased lipid vesicle accumulation compared with untreated controls, demonstrating that CSE is a potent inducer of foam cell formation. Co-treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a ROS scavenger and glutathione precursor that has been shown to suppress foam cell formation and attenuate atherosclerosis in animal models (Shimada et al. 2009; Sung et al. 2012), significantly reduced CSE-induced lipid accumulation (P = 0.04) but did not significantly attenuate OxLDL-induced foam cell formation (P > 0.99) (Fig. 3).

NAC blunts cigarette smoke extract (CSE)-induced foam cell formation. WT BMDMs were stimulated with either 2% CSE or 50 µg/ml OxLDL ± 5 mM NAC for 24 h and stained thereafter with Oil Red O. Y-axis represents number of Oil Red O vesicles per cell. Significance is calculated by 1-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test.

Cigarette smoke and e-cigarette extracts suppress hematopoietic progenitor function

To examine how CSE and EVE influence hematopoietic myeloid progenitor function, we utilized methylcellulose colony formation assays. Whole bone marrow from wild-type mice was incubated overnight with CSE, EVE, or their combination, washed, and plated in methylcellulose (Fig. 4A). Myeloid colonies were enumerated after 7 to 10 d. Both CSE and EVE significantly reduced colony formation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4B).

Overnight in vitro exposure to cigarette smoke and e-cigarette extracts suppresses hematopoietic progenitor function. (A) Experimental design. (B) Mouse bone marrow cells were incubated overnight with increasing concentrations of CSE and/or e-cigarette vape extract, washed twice, then plated in methylcellulose supplemented with SCF, IL-3, and EPO. Colonies were enumerated 7–10 days later. Values represent means ± SEM. Significance is calculated by paired t-tests.

In vivo exposure to combustible cigarette smoke followed by e-cigarette aerosol has lasting effects on hematopoiesis

To model the effects of transitioning from combustible smoking to vaping on hematopoiesis, we exposed mice to sequential combustible smoke and e-cigarette aerosol using a nose-only inhalation system (Fig. 5A). To maintain experimental consistency with other previous nose-only cigarette exposure (Ramanathan et al. 2023), mice were irradiated and transplanted with whole bone marrow, but with wild-type cells only. Mice were exposed to conventional cigarette smoke or clean air for 2 h per day, 4 d per week. After 2 mo of smoke exposure, mice in the smoke group were switched to either e-cigarette vapor (n = 6) or clean air (n = 6).

Complete blood counts remained largely stable during the exposure period, except for a significant rise in platelet counts specifically in the smoke-followed-by-e-cigarette group; this increase was not observed in mice exposed to smoke followed by clean air (Fig. 5B and C). The most pronounced platelet increase occurred after the switch to e-cigarette exposure (Fig. 5B and C). In addition, whereas mice in the smoke followed by air group showed no significant myeloid skewing, mice in the smoke followed by e-cigarette group displayed a clear myeloid bias (Fig. 5D).

Smoke exposure induces long-lasting myeloid skewing in secondary transplants

To test how smoke or e-cigarette exposure influences the long-term reconstitution capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, we performed secondary bone marrow transplants using bone marrow from the clean air, smoke-only, or smoke followed by e-cigarette exposed mice (n = 10-13 per exposure group) (Fig. 6A). The secondary transplant was not further exposed to any nose-only inhalation exposures and was observed in a standard vivarium. Transplanted cells from both smoke-only exposure and combined smoke followed by e-cigarette exposure led to increased frequencies of monocytes and macrophages (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−) in the peripheral blood over time (Fig. 6B). In addition, neutrophil frequencies were significantly decreased in the smoke-followed-by-e-cigarette group (Fig. 6C). In the clean air control group, neither monocyte nor neutrophil frequencies changed significantly over time.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the effects of cigarette smoke and e-cigarette vapor on innate immune function and hematopoiesis using complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches. Our central finding is that both combustible cigarette smoke and e-cigarette vapor suppress acute inflammatory responses in mature myeloid cells while simultaneously driving myeloid-biased hematopoiesis, a hallmark of hematopoietic aging. These apparently paradoxical effects support a model in which tobacco and nicotine exposures blunt innate immune responsiveness at the level of individual effector cells while remodeling the hematopoietic stem cell pool toward overproduction of myeloid lineages over time.

Using a standardized protocol that was applied consistently across multiple cell types, we found that CSE suppressed LPS-induced TNF-α secretion in a dose-dependent manner in RAW264.7 cells, THP-1 macrophages, and primary mouse BMDMs. This consistency across both human and murine cell lines and primary cells indicates that the immunosuppressive effect of CSE on TNF-α is a robust and reproducible phenomenon. These results align with prior studies demonstrating that CSE attenuates cytokine production by alveolar macrophages (Gaschler et al. 2008) and suppresses IL-1β, IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in PBMCs (Ouyang et al. 2000). A role for oxidative stress in mediating this suppression has been suggested (Arimilli et al. 2017), and our finding that NAC partially reversed CSE-induced foam cell formation provides complementary evidence that reactive oxygen species are central effectors of smoke-mediated cellular reprogramming. Notably, EVE alone did not significantly suppress TNF-α in any cell model, although co-exposure with CSE produced additive suppression in THP-1 cells, suggesting that the chemical composition of EVE at the concentrations tested may be insufficient to trigger the same immunosuppressive pathway independently.

Although smoke extract consistently suppressed cytokine production in cell lines and primary murine macrophages, we observed more heterogeneous responses in human PBMCs, where distinct pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory response groups emerged. This inter-individual variability is not unexpected given the complexity of the human immune system and likely reflects differences in genetic background, prior immune priming, and potentially trained immunity, a process by which innate immune cells are epigenetically reprogrammed by prior exposures to mount altered responses to subsequent stimuli (Netea et al. 2016; Bhargavi and Subbian 2024). Importantly, the sample size of our PBMC cohort (n = 9) limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the frequency or determinants of these response patterns, and smoking history was unavailable for these donors. Future studies with larger, clinically annotated cohorts will be needed to determine whether factors such as prior tobacco use, environmental exposures, or polymorphisms in innate immune signaling pathways account for this variability.

Although CSE suppressed inflammatory cytokine production, it enhanced foam cell formation in BMDMs even in the absence of exogenous modified lipoproteins, illustrating the pleiotropic effects of cigarette smoke on macrophage biology. Cigarette smoking is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (Messner and Bernhard 2014), and cigarette smoke promotes atherogenesis through generation of reactive oxygen species that drive oxidative modification of LDL, upregulation of scavenger receptors, and impairment of cholesterol efflux pathways (Pramanik and Sil 2024). Our observation that NAC significantly reduced CSE-induced lipid accumulation but failed to attenuate OxLDL-induced foam cell formation suggests that the degree of oxidative stress imposed by exogenous OxLDL exceeds the antioxidant capacity of NAC at the concentration used, whereas the endogenous ROS generated by CSE exposure can be effectively scavenged. These data provide a direct link between cigarette smoke exposure and the earliest cellular events in atherogenesis, demonstrating that CSE itself is sufficient to drive macrophage foam cell transformation through an oxidative stress-dependent mechanism.

Beyond its effects on mature myeloid cells, we found that both CSE and EVE had direct suppressive effects on hematopoietic progenitor function, as measured by myeloid colony formation. Both exposures reduced colony output in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that smoke and vapor components impair the proliferative and differentiation capacity of myeloid progenitors even after a single overnight exposure.

To extend these findings to a more physiologically relevant model, we performed long-term nose-only inhalation exposures using a sequential design that models the common real-world pattern of transitioning from combustible smoking to e-cigarette use (Kramarow and Elgaddal 2023). A key distinction between our in vitro and in vivo exposures is that the EVE used for cell culture studies was prepared from a commercially available melon-flavored e-liquid, whereas the inhaled e-cigarette aerosol was generated from a house-made e-liquid containing a 50/50 PG/VG ratio and 1.5% nicotine, eliminating commercial manufacturing variables. The inclusion of both commercial and house-made formulations across our studies is informative because it demonstrates that even the base PG/VG/nicotine components, independent of flavorings, are sufficient to alter hematopoiesis.

The persistence of myeloid skewing through secondary transplantation provides the strongest evidence that smoke exposure durably alters the HSC compartment. Both smoke-only and smoke followed by e-cigarette exposure led to increased monocyte frequencies in secondary recipients that were not exposed to smoke themselves, indicating that the hematopoietic changes are cell-intrinsic and heritable through at least 1 round of serial transplantation. This persistence is consistent with epigenetic imprinting of HSCs, as has been described for other inflammatory insults (Pietras 2017), and functionally resembles the myeloid-biased reconstitution pattern characteristic of aged hematopoietic stem cells. Because myeloid-biased hematopoiesis is a cardinal feature of the aged hematopoietic system (Rossi et al. 2007a, 2007b), these findings raise the possibility that smoke and vape exposure may accelerate functional aging of the HSC pool. However, we cannot distinguish from these data alone whether the myeloid bias reflects true epigenetic reprogramming of stem cells, selective survival or expansion of myeloid-biased HSC clones, or alterations in the progenitor compartment. Further studies incorporating epigenomic and transcriptomic profiling of purified HSCs after smoke exposure are warranted to determine the precise mechanisms underlying this durable reprogramming.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, our in vivo inhalation design did not include an e-cigarette-only exposure arm. Previously, we found that 2 mo of nose-only e-cigarette exposure reduced myeloid progenitors but did not impact peripheral blood counts in wild-type mice (Ramanathan et al. 2020). Second, although we observed consistent results across multiple macrophage models, the specific e-cigarette formulations, flavors, and device parameters used may limit generalizability, given the vast diversity of products on the market. Third, we did not perform epigenetic or transcriptomic analyses on exposed HSCs, which would be needed to identify the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed durable changes in lineage output.

In summary, we demonstrate that both combustible cigarettes and e-cigarettes derange hematopoiesis to distort myeloid differentiation and suppress acute inflammatory cytokine production by mature myeloid cells. Importantly, sequential smoke-to-vape inhalation produced persistent alterations in hematopoietic output that were maintained through secondary transplantation, indicating that these effects are cell-intrinsic and cannot be reversed simply by smoking cessation. These findings underscore the need for continued investigation into the long-term hematopoietic consequences of both combustible and electronic cigarette use, particularly in the context of individuals who transition between these products. Understanding how smoking durably reprograms the hematopoietic stem cell compartment will be critical for identifying biomarkers of smoke-related hematopoietic damage and developing strategies to mitigate these effects even after exposure has ceased.
