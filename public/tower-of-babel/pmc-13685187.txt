Comparison of non-invasive neuromodulation for motor function rehabilitation in incomplete spinal cord injury: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yun Han, Junting Han, Haixia Wang, Ying Meng, Jiacai Liang, Xiaona Duan, Muyuan Wang, Yuanyuan Li, Pan Lu, Ziyuan Cao, Chunhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) disrupts structural and functional connectivity between the brain and spinal cord, resulting in severe motor dysfunction. Various neuromodulation techniques have been applied in SCI rehabilitation and have promoted motor recovery to varying degrees. A Bayesian network meta-analysis was therefore undertaken to compare and rank the relative effects of non-invasive neuromodulation techniques on motor-related outcomes in adults with incomplete SCI.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and The Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating non-invasive neuromodulation for motor function in patients with incomplete SCI, from database inception to September 13, 2025. Bayesian network meta-analysis was performed using R software. The intervention effects of different non-invasive neuromodulation techniques were compared across motor-related outcomes, including the Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS), the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT), step length, gait speed, the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), and the Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM).ResultsSeventeen RCTs involving 523 participants were included. For lower-limb motor strength, 20-Hz repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (20 Hz-rTMS) and intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) both yielded favorable estimates, with iTBS slightly ahead (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.87, 95% credible interval [CrI] 0.60 to 1.14) of 20 Hz-rTMS (SMD = 0.83, 95% CrI 0.32 to 1.34). iTBS also conferred the largest gain in gait speed (SMD = 0.52, 95% CrI 0.25 to 0.79), whereas 20 Hz-rTMS produced the greatest reduction in spasticity (mean difference [MD] = − 0.41, 95% CrI − 0.7 to − 0.12). Functional independence on the Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM) improved most with 10-Hz repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (10 Hz-rTMS) (SMD = 1.03, 95% CrI 0.03 to 2.03). For motor evoked potential (MEP) latency, transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) (SMD = − 1.21, 95% CrI − 1.94 to − 0.48) and 10 Hz-rTMS (SMD = − 1.22, 95% CrI − 2.08 to − 0.35) showed the strongest shortening, followed by iTBS (SMD = − 0.84, 95% CrI − 1.53 to − 0.14). MEP amplitude increased most under 10 Hz-rTMS (SMD = 1.003, 95% CrI 0.13 to 1.88).ConclusionsiTBS, 20 Hz-rTMS, 10 Hz-rTMS, and tSCS each appear to support motor recovery after incomplete SCI.However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Further investigation is required to elucidate the specific mechanisms and clinical utility of different neuromodulation interventions for SCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05178-9.

Background

Spinal cord injury (SCI) refers to structural and functional damage to the spinal cord arising from various etiologies, resulting in motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction below the level of injury [1]. Motor impairment following SCI primarily manifests as complete or incomplete tetraplegia or paraplegia. Restricted motor function not only compromises ambulatory capacity but also severely affects activities of daily living and social participation [2]. Although conventional rehabilitation approaches, including physical therapy, occupational therapy, and gait training, can partially improve motor function [3], recovery remains suboptimal in many patients with incomplete SCI, underscoring the need to explore more effective rehabilitation interventions [4].

In recent years, neuromodulation techniques have been increasingly applied in SCI rehabilitation, with the aim of facilitating motor recovery through modulation of neural network excitability and plasticity [5–7]. Based on the mode of delivery, neuromodulation can be classified as non-invasive or invasive. Non-invasive approaches include transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) [8], transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS) [9, 10], transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) [11], trans-spinal direct current stimulation [12], and transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation. These techniques modulate corticospinal tract excitability or spinal neural circuits through non-invasive means and have attracted considerable attention owing to their ease of application and favorable safety profile. Belci and colleagues [13] administered 10-Hz repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (10 Hz-rTMS) over five days to four patients with incomplete cervical SCI, with a three-week follow-up, and observed improvements in American Spinal Injury Association motor and sensory scores as well as reduced time to complete the pegboard task. Bunday and colleagues [14] first applied paired associative stimulation in patients with incomplete SCI and reported significant increases in corticospinal conduction, index finger strength, and electromyographic activity compared with healthy controls, accompanied by marked improvement in finger dexterity. Deng and colleagues [15] demonstrated that intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) significantly improved lower extremity motor function in patients with SCI, although no significant differences were observed in sensory function. McHugh and colleagues [16] applied tSCS at a frequency of 50 Hz and intensity of 20 to 80 mA and observed improvements in the 10MWT and the Walking Index for SCI. Gill and colleagues [17] reported that epidural spinal cord stimulation at the L2 and S1 segments combined with rehabilitation training improved lower extremity motor function in a patient with complete loss of lower limb motor function following incomplete SCI, enabling independent stepping and standing on a treadmill.

However, existing clinical trials are constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneous intervention protocols, and diverse outcome measures. The relative efficacy and safety of different interventions for motor function have not been systematically compared. Specifically, clear conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness, safety, and appropriate patient populations for various non-invasive stimulation modalities in motor rehabilitation following incomplete SCI remain elusive.

To address these gaps, the present network meta-analysis ranked the relative effects of non-invasive neuromodulation techniques—TMS, tDCS, tSCS, and trans-spinal direct current stimulation—on motor recovery after incomplete SCI. Evidence was synthesized across lower-limb motor function, gait performance, spasticity, functional independence, and electrophysiological measures. By placing the available interventions within a single network, the resulting ranking is intended to inform both the next wave of mechanistic research and the design of comparative rehabilitation trials.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA statement for network meta-analysis (NMA extension) [18] and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251149933).

Data sources

PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and The Cochrane Library were systematically searched to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating non-invasive neuromodulation for motor function in patients with incomplete SCI, from database inception to September 13, 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms with free-text terms. The main search terms included spinal cord injuries, TMS, tDCS, spinal cord stimulation, trans-spinal direct current stimulation, tSCS, and transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation. The detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Study selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: adult patients with incomplete SCI, as classified by the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI); a non-invasive neural-stimulation intervention—TMS, tDCS, tSCS, or trans-spinal direct current stimulation—delivered alone or in combination with conventional rehabilitation; a control arm of sham stimulation, either alone or paired with conventional rehabilitation; and reporting of at least one primary or secondary outcome. To improve methodological comparability and reduce selection bias, only randomized controlled trials were retained in the final synthesis.

Motor recovery after incomplete SCI is multidimensional, so no single endpoint can capture the full effect of neuromodulation on rehabilitation. Five co-primary outcomes were therefore prespecified, each anchored to a recovery domain: the Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS) for lower-limb strength, gait speed for walking performance, the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) for spasticity, upper-extremity motor function for arm-hand recovery, and the Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM) for global functional independence. Secondary outcomes captured complementary aspects of locomotion and neurophysiology: the 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT), step length, the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, the Walking Index for SCI II (WISCI II), and motor evoked potential (MEP) latency and amplitude.

The exclusion criteria were non-original research, including animal studies, case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, letters, and guidelines; presence of other severe neurological disorders; and incomplete data or unavailable full text.

Two independent reviewers (Yun Han and Junting Han) screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records according to the predefined search strategy to identify eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third investigator (Haixia Wang) when necessary.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers (Yun Han and Junting Han), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or arbitration by Haixia Wang. Risk-of-bias judgments were made with the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool, version 2 (RoB 2.0), covering five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was answered through its prespecified signaling questions, and the overall judgment for a study was assigned to one of “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each NMA estimate was rated with the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework, which builds on GRADE and evaluates both pairwise network comparisons and ranking probabilities across six core domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. For each comparison, the contributing studies were assessed on risk of bias, sample size, confidence interval, prediction interval, and network structure, with a domain-level judgment of “no concerns”, “some concerns”, or “major concerns”. These domain judgments were then combined into an overall certainty rating of high, moderate, low, or very low.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using a predefined Excel form, followed by cross-checking. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a team member. Extracted information included first author, publication year, study region, study design, participant characteristics, stimulation modality, sample size with distribution between intervention and control groups, age (range, mean, and standard deviation), sex ratio, American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale classification, time since injury (mean and standard deviation), intervention protocols, and selected outcomes including the LEMS, gait speed, the MAS, the SCIM, the 10MWT, step length, the 6MWT, the TUG test, the WISCI II, and MEP latency and amplitude. When multiple follow-up time points were reported, the point closest to the end of the intervention was used for the primary analysis to maximize comparability across studies.

Statistical analysis

Continuous outcomes were synthesized as either mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD), depending on the measurement scale. For the MAS, where every included study used the same scale, MDs with 95% credible intervals (CrI) were applied. For the remaining outcomes—LEMS, gait speed, SCIM, 10MWT, step length, 6MWT, TUG, WISCI II, MEP latency, and MEP amplitude—SMDs with 95% CrIs were applied, because units, scales, and measurement methods varied across studies. For MAS, 10MWT, TUG, and MEP latency, lower values indicate better performance; the direction of effect was therefore reversed so that negative estimates favored the intervention.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2) with the gemtc package. Network plots were constructed based on direct comparisons between interventions and controls in the included studies, with nodes representing different interventions and edges representing direct comparisons; edge thickness was proportional to the number of contributing studies. When closed loops were present, node-splitting was applied to assess inconsistency, with P > 0.05 indicating acceptable consistency. Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using both fixed-effect and random-effect models. Model specifications included four Markov chains, a scaling factor of 2.5, 10,000 burn-in iterations, 40,000 total iterations, and a thinning interval of 1 to ensure convergence and stability. Model selection combined heterogeneity and model fit. The fixed-effect model was chosen only when both criteria held: overall heterogeneity was low (I2 < 50%) and the difference in the deviance information criterion between the fixed- and random-effects models was less than 5. Otherwise, the random-effects model was applied as the more conservative choice. Relative effect forest plots were generated, and ranking probabilities were calculated using surface under the cumulative ranking curve values, with values closer to 1 indicating superior rankings. Pairwise comparison league tables were generated based on these results.

Results

Study selection

A total of 6,532 records were identified. After removal of 2,494 duplicates, 4,038 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 3,987 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Seventy-three studies proceeded to full-text assessment. Following exclusion of 56 articles (21 non-RCTs, 23 studies lacking relevant outcome measures, and 12 studies with unavailable data), 17 studies [15, 19–34] were ultimately included. General characteristics of included studies

Seventeen studies involving 523 participants were included, all investigating non-invasive neuromodulation. Six studies evaluated tDCS [19–24], two studies investigated 10 Hz-rTMS [25, 26], three studies assessed 20-Hz repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (20 Hz-rTMS) [27–29], three studies evaluated iTBS [15, 30, 31], one study investigated paired associative stimulation [32], one study evaluated trans-spinal direct current stimulation [33], and one study evaluated tSCS [34]. Study regions included the United States, China, Thailand, Spain, Korea, Finland, and Turkey. The mean participant age was 44.2 ± 12.7 years, with a predominantly male population (overall male-to-female ratio approximately 2.8:1). Injury severity was classified according to the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale, with all participants having incomplete injuries (grades B, C, or D); grade D constituted the largest proportion. Time from injury to intervention exhibited substantial heterogeneity. Anodal stimulation was used in all but one of the included tDCS studies [19], so tDCS entered the network as a single node. Patient injury duration ranged widely: only Deng et al. (2024) [15] studied the subacute phase (1–3 months), whereas the remaining studies enrolled chronic-phase patients (> 3 months post-injury). Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was assessed for all 17 RCTs, with results summarized in Fig. 2. Seven studies (41.2%) were rated as low risk and ten (58.8%) as some concerns; no study was rated as high risk. Overall methodological quality was moderate to high. The principal sources of potential bias were inadequate allocation concealment, lack of outcome assessor blinding, and insufficient reporting of sample size calculations.

Fig. 2Methodological quality of the included studies

Methodological quality of the included studies

Random sequence generation and allocation concealment were adequately described in most studies (64.7%). Six studies (35.3%) [15, 20, 24, 27, 29, 32] did not describe the concealment procedure. Blinding of participants and assessors was reported in 15 studies, while two [27, 28] left assessor blinding unspecified. Missing-data handling was adequate across studies, with attrition below 10%, supporting low-risk ratings for this domain. Primary and secondary outcomes were clearly defined and judged at low risk for measurement bias. Selective reporting was the main residual concern: six studies (41.2%) [15, 22, 24, 26, 29, 32] failed to report all prespecified outcomes and were therefore rated as having “some concerns”.

Primary outcomes

For the LEMS, nine RCTs involving 358 patients were included [15, 20, 24, 25, 27, 28, 30, 31, 34]. The evidence network (Fig. 3A) was sparsely connected without closed loops. The relative effect forest plot (Fig. 3B) showed that iTBS (SMD = 0.87, 95% CrI 0.60 to 1.14, surface under the cumulative ranking curve 90.3%) and 20 Hz-rTMS (SMD = 0.83, 95% CrI 0.32 to 1.34, surface under the cumulative ranking curve 86.8%) showed a favorable estimate for the LEMS compared with sham stimulation. Detailed pairwise comparisons are presented in the league table (Fig. 3C). The sparse network and absence of closed loops, however, limit interpretation: the resulting Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) rankings and relative-effect estimates should be read with caution.

Network meta-analysis of the LEMS. A Network plot. B Relative effect forest plot with surface under the cumulative ranking curve values. C League table

For gait speed, seven RCTs involving 314 patients were included [15, 19, 21, 24, 28, 30, 31]. The evidence network (Fig. 4A) was sparsely connected without closed loops. The relative effect forest plot (Fig. 4B) showed that iTBS improved gait speed compared with sham stimulation (SMD = 0.52, 95% CrI 0.25 to 0.79, surface under the cumulative ranking curve 92.1%), with no significant differences observed for other comparisons. The league table (Fig. 4C) presented the corresponding pairwise estimates between iTBS and sham stimulation. With the network sparse and devoid of closed loops, this finding should be read as exploratory rather than as evidence of comparative superiority.

For the MAS, five RCTs involving 107 patients were included [27–29, 32, 34]. The evidence network (Fig. 5A) was sparsely connected without closed loops. The relative effect forest plot (Fig. 5B) showed that 20 Hz-rTMS reduced the MAS score compared with sham stimulation (MD = − 0.41, 95% CrI − 0.7 to − 0.12, surface under the cumulative ranking curve 92.0%), indicating reduced muscle tone. The league table (Fig. 5C) presented the corresponding pairwise estimates between these groups. Drawing on a limited number of studies without closed-loop evidence, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously.

For the SCIM, five RCTs involving 119 patients were included [15, 20, 22, 25, 34]. The evidence network (Fig. 6A) was sparsely connected without closed loops. The relative effect forest plot (Fig. 6B) showed that 10 Hz-rTMS improved the SCIM score compared with sham stimulation (SMD = 1.03, 95% CrI 0.03 to 2.03, surface under the cumulative ranking curve 94.7%). The wide 95% credible interval, however, calls for caution in interpretation. The league table (Fig. 6C) presented the corresponding pairwise estimates between these groups.

Upper-extremity motor function was considered as a potential outcome during data extraction; however, eligible studies were few, and the measurement tools they used were heterogeneous, precluding quantitative synthesis or network meta-analysis for this outcome.

Secondary outcomes

For the 10MWT, six RCTs involving 116 patients were included [21, 22, 28, 29, 33, 34]. For step length, seven RCTs involving 314 patients were included [15, 19, 21, 24, 28, 30, 31]. For the 6MWT, three RCTs involving 43 patients were included [22, 33, 34]. For the TUG test, three RCTs involving 67 patients were included [21, 22, 34]. For the WISCI II, five RCTs involving 134 patients were included [23–25, 28, 34]. For MEPs (latency and amplitude), three RCTs involving 76 patients were included [15, 26, 34].

The evidence networks for all secondary outcomes (Supplementary Figures S1A, S2A, S3A, S4A, S5A, S6A, and S7A) were sparsely connected without closed loops. For MEP latency, three interventions outperformed sham stimulation. The largest shortening was seen with tSCS (SMD = − 1.21, 95% CrI − 1.94 to − 0.48), followed closely by 10 Hz-rTMS (SMD = − 1.22, 95% CrI − 2.08 to − 0.35) and then iTBS (SMD = − 0.84, 95% CrI − 1.53 to − 0.14). For MEP amplitude, 10 Hz-rTMS likewise outperformed sham stimulation (SMD = 1.003, 95% CrI 0.13 to 1.88). These electrophysiological signals come, however, from only three RCTs and 76 participants, with several credible intervals running wide; they should therefore be treated as exploratory and potentially unstable rather than as definitive evidence of comparative efficacy.

For the 10MWT, step length, the 6MWT, the TUG test, and the WISCI II, no statistically significant differences were observed between sham stimulation and any neuromodulation intervention, including tDCS, tSCS, 10 Hz-rTMS, 20 Hz-rTMS, and trans-spinal direct current stimulation. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Figures S1, S2, S3, S6, S7.

The SUCRA values for all outcomes are presented in Supplementary Fig. 8. SUCRA provides a ranking metric, but its uncertainty grows sharply when 95% CrIs for different interventions overlap, as they do here; the rankings should therefore be read with caution.

Publication bias

Because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each analyzed outcome, small-study effects could not be reliably assessed using comparison-adjusted funnel plots. The evidence networks were also sparse and lacked closed loops, further constraining the interpretability of any funnel-plot-based assessment. Comparison-adjusted funnel plots were therefore not generated, and publication bias and small-study effects could not be formally excluded.

Certainty of evidence

The CINeMA tool was used to rate the certainty of evidence for each outcome. For LEMS, the comparisons of 20 Hz-rTMS and iTBS versus sham stimulation were rated as low certainty, whereas comparisons of 10 Hz-rTMS and tSCS versus sham stimulation, together with most active-versus-active comparisons, reached moderate certainty. For MAS, gait speed, and the 10MWT, all intervention comparisons reached moderate certainty. For step length, the comparisons of 20 Hz-rTMS and iTBS versus sham stimulation, as well as 20 Hz-rTMS versus iTBS and versus tDCS, were rated as low certainty. Downgrading was driven mainly by within-study bias, reporting bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision; indirectness was not a serious concern. Detailed ratings are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9.

Of the outcomes analyzed in this study, five (LEMS, MAS, gait speed, the 10MWT, and step length) drew on more than five contributing direct comparisons and therefore met the criteria for CINeMA assessment; their certainty ratings are presented in Supplementary Fig. 9. The remaining outcomes were each supported by ≤ 5 contributing studies; because CINeMA tends to mis-estimate imprecision when the evidence base is sparse, no formal grading was performed, and these findings should be regarded as exploratory.

Discussion

Across the synthesized evidence, the four interventions favored different outcome domains. iTBS produced the most consistent gains, with favorable estimates on gait speed, LEMS, and MEP latency. 20 Hz-rTMS favored LEMS and MAS, 10 Hz-rTMS favored SCIM together with MEP latency and amplitude, and tSCS favored MEP latency. The networks are, however, sparse, closed loops are absent, and study counts per node are small, so these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Notably, none of the four interventions reached statistical favorability for the clinically anchored functional measures (10MWT, step length, 6MWT, TUG, and WISCI II), a gap that limits the practical translatability of the present rankings.

Although SUCRA provides a ranking metric, ranking uncertainty grows sharply when the 95% credible intervals (CrIs) for different interventions overlap substantially. In this analysis, the 95% CrIs for motor-function effects across interventions overlapped considerably, indicating that the true differences may be small or uncertain. A higher SUCRA position should therefore be read not as “statistically superior” but as “a relatively favorable point estimate under the current limited evidence”.

For iTBS, gait speed, LEMS, and MEP latency all favored the active arm over sham. Liu et al. [35] administered iTBS once daily for five days per week in rats with SCI and observed reduced inflammatory responses in the motor cortex, decreased neuronal apoptosis, and improvement in the neuroimmune microenvironment at the injury site. Experimental work suggests several neuroprotective mechanisms, including axonal regeneration and synaptic plasticity. iTBS has been associated with increased serotonergic fiber density within the injury core and with more abundant long descending propriospinal fibers in the caudal cord, alongside partial improvements in hindlimb motor function. These mechanistic observations provide a plausible biological basis for the present rankings, and the gait-speed advantage of iTBS in particular is consistent with the greater efficiency of iTBS in driving rapid corticospinal excitation. This effect may be related to stronger long-term potentiation (LTP)-like plasticity induced by short-duration, high-frequency burst stimulation [36]. A systematic review reported that iTBS targeting the lower limb M1 or the cerebellum shortened central motor conduction time (CMCT) by 0.78 ms, whereas MEP latency showed a non-significant shortening trend. Xiong et al. attributed the iTBS effect less to faster synaptic transmission per se and more to improved axonal conduction and synaptic integration. By this reading, the shortening of central motor conduction time (CMCT) reflects faster delivery of cortical commands to spinal α-motor neurons, which in turn could express as longer strides and faster walking [37]. In the present analysis, stride length and walking speed moved in the same direction, with iTBS slightly outperforming 20 Hz-rTMS, although neither difference reached statistical significance. After spinal cord injury, stride length is shaped at least as much by spasticity, joint contractures, and trunk-control deficits as by cortical drive, so the marginal gain attributable to a single session of excitatory neuromodulation is likely bounded from above [38].

20 Hz-rTMS was associated with improvements in LEMS and with reductions in spasticity. 10 Hz-rTMS may add a moderate gain on the SCIM, with concurrent shortening of MEP latency and a slight rise in MEP amplitude. Both 10-Hz and 20 Hz-rTMS are high-frequency stimulation paradigms and are generally classified within the same mechanistic category in current research. Duan et al. [39] included four RCTs and conducted a meta-analysis using outcomes such as the American Spinal Injury Association LEMS, the 10MWT, the MAS, and the WISCI II. High-frequency rTMS appears to benefit LEMS in patients with incomplete SCI, an observation broadly consistent with the existing literature. High-frequency rTMS may induce LTP-like local effects in M1, thereby lowering the motor threshold to some extent. The reduced threshold could in turn recruit additional spinal α-motor neurons, manifested clinically as improved LEMS, slightly shortened MEP latency, and a modest rise in MEP amplitude [36]. The same analysis also suggests that high-frequency rTMS may reduce MAS scores. This effect may be mediated by decreased cortical GABA release, which could attenuate the inhibitory influence of descending pathways and help suppress excessive stretch-reflex activity, thereby allowing a dissociation between enhanced muscle strength and reduced spasticity. The underlying mechanism may involve enhanced cortical inhibitory-interneuron activity and improved regulation of muscle tone [38, 40].

tSCS also tended to shorten MEP latency. The mechanism likely involves higher spinal cord excitability and a lower conduction threshold for motor impulses, mediated through the central pattern generator and other intrinsic spinal networks [41]. One study found stronger effects one month after the intervention than immediately after stimulation cessation, consistent with time-dependent gains in neural responsiveness and reflex modulation, including heightened soleus H-reflex excitability and partial recovery of presynaptic homosynaptic inhibition [34]. However, the persistence and implications of these mechanisms after cessation of stimulation remain unclear. Estes et al. [42] suggested that tSCS combined with locomotor training improved walking function and reduced spasticity in patients with incomplete SCI. In the present analysis, tSCS showed a tendency to reduce the MAS score compared with sham stimulation, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. As an emerging technique with limited clinical data, tSCS requires further investigation to better define its clinical utility.

For the TUG outcome, tSCS showed numerical improvement without statistical significance. The TUG test primarily reflects the time required to complete a complex task, with shorter duration indicating better mobility and dynamic balance. This outcome represents a multisystem cooperative task that depends not only on the corticospinal tract but also on sensory integration, vestibulospinal pathways, and cerebellar circuits. Single-target non-invasive stimulation may be insufficient to produce functional-level improvements in balance [43]. For the 6MWT outcome, tDCS showed a negative effect. This finding may be explained by baseline limitations in motor function that precluded assessment of all outcomes in all participants. After baseline evaluation, participants were not stratified according to injury level and ambulatory status, and two participants with the highest baseline walking ability were randomly assigned to the sham group, resulting in substantial heterogeneity among participants who completed the assessment [22]. Future studies should account for these confounding factors and conduct high-quality, large-sample, long-term RCTs with more comprehensive comparisons among neuromodulation techniques, thereby enabling higher-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

The main strength of this study lies in the first application of network meta-analysis to directly compare multiple non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, providing quantitative evidence of relative efficacy. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of studies within some comparison nodes was limited, which may affect estimation precision. Second, heterogeneity existed across studies with respect to stimulation parameters (intensity, frequency, and duration), patient characteristics (injury level and time since injury), and concomitant rehabilitation protocols, which may limit generalizability. Third, clinical and methodological heterogeneity existed across studies, including differences in injury duration, stimulation parameters, sham protocols, and concomitant rehabilitation. Although the included studies had broadly comparable populations and therapeutic purposes, the transitivity assumption could not be fully verified. Fourth, because fewer than 10 studies contributed to each outcome and the networks were sparse, comparison-adjusted funnel plots could not be reliably constructed; publication bias and small-study effects therefore could not be formally assessed or excluded. Fifth, follow-up durations were generally short and adverse events were inadequately reported, limiting conclusions about long-term efficacy and safety. Sixth, the 95% credible intervals for motor-function effects overlapped substantially across interventions, suggesting that the true differences in therapeutic efficacy may be small or uncertain; a higher SUCRA ranking should therefore be read not as “statistically superior” but as a relatively favorable point estimate under currently limited evidence. Finally, because most outcomes were synthesized as SMDs across heterogeneous scales and measurement methods, their clinical interpretation is less direct, and established minimal clinically important differences are not available across the full set of SCI neuromodulation outcomes; statistically favorable estimates should therefore not be equated with clinically meaningful improvements.

Conclusions

On the basis of these results, iTBS showed potential superiority over sham stimulation for gait speed, LEMS, and MEP latency. 20 Hz-rTMS may benefit LEMS and reduce spasticity. 10 Hz-rTMS may improve the Spinal Cord Independence Measure, shorten MEP latency, and increase MEP amplitude. Furthermore, tSCS may also be associated with shortened MEP latency. These preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in future studies. Future high-quality, large-sample, long-term RCTs, particularly head-to-head comparisons, are warranted to further clarify the role and clinical utility of different neuromodulation techniques and to advance the development of personalized rehabilitation strategies.
