Osmotic forces modify lipid membrane fluctuations
Amaresh Sahu ‡

Abstract
. In hydrodynamic descriptions of lipid bilayers, the membrane is often approximated as being impermeable to the surrounding, solute-containing fluid. However, biological and in vitro lipid membranes are influenced by their permeability and the resultant osmotic forces—whose effects remain poorly understood. Here, we study the dynamics of a fluctuating, planar lipid membrane that is ideally selective: fluid can pass through it, while the electrically-neutral solutes cannot. We find that the canonical membrane relaxation mode, in which internal membrane forces are balanced by fluid drag, no longer exists over all wavenumbers. Rather, this mode only exists when it is slower than solute diffusion—corresponding to a finite range of wavenumbers. The well-known equipartition result quantifying the size of membrane undulations due to thermal perturbations is consequently limited in its validity to the aforementioned range. Moreover, this range shrinks as the membrane surface tension is increased, and above a critical tension the membrane mode vanishes. Our findings are relevant when interpreting experimental measurements of membrane fluctuations, especially in vesicles at moderate to high tensions.

Introduction

Biological membranes are unique materials that surround the cell, and
compartmentalize its internal organelles.
While such membranes are slowly permeable to water
[
1
]
,
large neutral molecules (such as sugars) cannot easily pass through them

[
2
]
.
The semipermeability of lipid membranes is relevant to both biological and in
vitro scenarios—as in both cases, membranes are essentially always surrounded
by solutes.
While the general equations governing the coupled membrane, fluid, and solute
dynamics were recently obtained
[
3
]
, there is not yet a
systematic characterization of membrane behavior.
As a result, the effects of permeability and osmosis on lipid membrane dynamics
remain poorly understood.

Significance

Lipid membranes are ubiquitous in biological and in vitro systems.
Though they are often approximated as impermeable, water can slowly pass through
the membrane.
In this work, we study the consequences of lipid membranes being permeable to
water, but not solutes (such as sugars).
We find that when solutes are fast relative to the membrane—which occurs at
low surface tensions and intermediate wavelengths—membrane undulations are
indistinguishable from the impermeable prediction.
However, at large and small wavelengths or high tensions, solutes are slow
relative to the membrane and the impermeable result does not hold.
Our findings are relevant to the many experimental investigations that apply
impermeable results across all wavelengths, and all tensions, in their
characterization.

The present study investigates how planar lipid membranes act on, and are
influenced by, their surrounding environment.
Flat membranes are found in various biological settings, including the
endoplasmic reticulum
[
4
]
, Golgi complex

[
5
]
, and neuromuscular junction
[
6
]
.
In addition, theories of impermeable bilayer fluctuations about a flat surface
are often used to describe the thermal undulations of giant unilamellar vesicles
(GUVs).
We begin by investigating an impermeable membrane, where the dynamics of the
solute are not considered.
By starting with a well-understood system (see e.g. Refs.
[

,

),
we are better equipped to study our primary system of interest: the
semipermeable membrane.
The temporal evolution of the normal modes is solved for within the linear
theory, and the relative time scale of membrane and solute relaxation is found
to dictate the nature of the coupled dynamics.
When solutes relax more quickly, the dynamics are indistinguishable from the
impermeable scenario.
It is then straightforward to present an effective Langevin equation including
thermal perturbations, and obtain the magnitude of the resultant height
fluctuations.
In contrast, when solutes are slow relative to the membrane, the impermeable
dynamics are unphysical and the resultant behavior is outside the scope of a
normal mode analysis within a linear description.
As a result, the character of thermal undulations—long assumed to be that of
an impermeable membrane when analyzing fluctuating GUVs—is no longer known.
Experimental consequences of our findings are highlighted, and avenues for
future work are discussed.

The impermeable membrane

Consider a nearly planar lipid membrane surrounded by water.
For the present analysis, we neglect solutes and make the standard approximation
that the membrane is impermeable to fluid.
Water is characterized by its three-dimensional mass density
\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}
, shear
viscosity
\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}
, and kinematic viscosity

\nu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}:=\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}/\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}
.
Relevant membrane parameters are the patch length
\ell_{\text{c}}
, two-dimensional (2D)
areal density
\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
, surface tension
\lambda_{\text{c}}
, and bending modulus

k_{\text{b}}

[
20
]
.
It is important to note that the surface tension is not a membrane property, but
rather takes the requisite value to enforce areal incompressibility

[
21
,
22
]
.
As a result, the membrane surface tension can span a wide range of values, and
is often tuned in experiments
[
23
,
24
]
.
Characteristic values of all parameters are provided in Table

Our base state is an unperturbed membrane sheet lying in the
x
–

We seek to understand how the coupled membrane and fluid system responds to
small perturbations.
To this end, we determine the linearized dynamics of the coupled membrane and
fluid system about the base configuration.
In what follows, an abbreviated analysis of the governing equations is
presented; see §2 of the Supplemental Material (SM)

The governing equations

The position
\bm{x}
of the perturbed membrane is given by

\bm{x}(x,y,t)=x\mkern 1.0mu\bm{e}_{x}+y\mkern 1.0mu\bm{e}_{y}+h(x,y,t)\mkern 1.0mu\bm{e}_{z}
,
where

x,y\in[0,\ell_{\text{c}}]

and

\lvert h\rvert\ll\ell_{\text{c}}

by construction.
For planar geometries with a stationary base state, the in-plane and
out-of-plane membrane equations are decoupled
[
26
,
27
]
.
To solve for the time evolution of the membrane height, we require only
the
z
-component of the linear momentum balance of the membrane—often
called the shape equation and given by
[
27
]

\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\mkern 1.0muh_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mutt}\,=\,\lambda_{\text{c}}\mkern 1.0muh_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mu\alpha\alpha}\,-\,\dfrac{k_{\text{b}}}{2}\mkern 1.0muh_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mu\alpha\alpha\beta\beta}\,-\,\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0mup\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket~.

(1)

In Eq. (
1
),

\alpha,\beta\in{1,2}

denote directions in the
x
–
y
plane, with repeated indices summed over
such that (for example)

h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mu\alpha\alpha}=h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0muxx}+h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0muyy}
.
Here

(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)_{,t}:=\partial(\,\bm{\cdot}\,)/\partial t

is the partial time derivative and

\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0mup\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket:=p^{+}-p^{-}

is the difference in the fluid pressure above (
+
) and below (
-
) the
membrane.
The pressure and velocity fields,
p^{\pm}
and
v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muj}
(where Roman
indices span
\{1,2,3\}
and repeated indices are summed over), satisfy
the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations linearized about the base state:

v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muj,\mkern 1.0muj}=0

and

\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muv^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muj,\mkern 1.0mut}=\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muv^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muj,\mkern 1.0mukk}-p^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0muj}
.
With the no-slip boundary condition

h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut}=v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muz}

at the membrane surface, water cannot flow through the membrane and we have a
well-posed set of equations for the coupled system
[
25
, §2]
.

At this point, we follow standard approaches to solve for the dynamics of the
system.
The membrane height is decomposed into planar Fourier modes as

h(x,y,t)\,=\,\sum_{\bm{q}}\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}\,e^{i\mkern 1.0mu(q_{x}x\,+\,q_{y}y)}\,e^{-\omega_{\bm{q}}t}~,

(2)

where the wavevector

\bm{q}=(q_{x},q_{y})

has corresponding wavenumber

q:=(q_{x}^{\,2}+q_{y}^{\,2})^{1/2}
,

\omega_{\bm{q}}
is the associated relaxation frequency, and normal modes are assumed
independent.
Fluid unknowns are also decomposed into planar Fourier modes, with the

z
-dependence to be solved for.
The fluid pressure, for example, is expressed as

p^{\pm}(x,y,z,t)\,=\,\sum_{\bm{q}}\hat{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}(z)\,e^{i\mkern 1.0mu(q_{x}x\,+\,q_{y}y)}\,e^{-\omega_{\bm{q}}t}~.

(3)

Our main task is to solve for all frequencies
\omega_{\bm{q}}
satisfying the
governing equations.
In doing so, we also solve for the pressure and velocity fields in the fluid.

The frequency solutions

As we limit ourselves to the linearized dynamics, all unknowns of a particular
wavevector
\bm{q}
are proportional to the membrane height
\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}
.
We assume

\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}\neq 0

and combine all equations governing membrane and fluid into a single equation
for
\omega_{\bm{q}}
, namely the dispersion relation (see §2.3 of the SM

[
25
]
).
Given the physically-motivated requirement that all fluid perturbations decay
far from the membrane surface, there are two frequencies at each wavenumber,
which are plotted in Fig.
1
.
The aforementioned equation for
\omega_{\bm{q}}
is not straightforward to interpret—though we find the dynamics are well-approximated by

\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{eff}}\,\omega_{\bm{q}}^{\,2}\,-\,4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq\,\omega_{\bm{q}}\,+\,E\,=\,0~.

(4)

Equation (
4
), with the temporal ansatz

h\sim e^{-\omega t}

in Eq. (
2
),
reveals the dynamics of the membrane are analogous to that of the spring–mass–damper from introductory mechanics.
Here

\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{eff}}:=\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}+\mkern 1.0mu4\mkern 1.0mu\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}/q

approximates the combined inertia of membrane and fluid,
4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq
is
the well-known hydrodynamic drag from the surrounding fluid, and

E:=\lambda_{\text{c}}\mkern 1.0muq^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mkern 1.0muk_{\text{b}}\mkern 1.0muq^{4}

captures the energetics of small membrane fluctuations:

\tfrac{1}{2}\,\ell_{\text{c}}^{\,2}\mkern 1.0muE\mkern 1.0mu\lvert\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}^{\,2}\rvert

is the energy of mode
\bm{q}
to quadratic order
[
34
]
.
Since inertial effects are small, Eq. (
4
) has the two
approximate solutions

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}\,:=\,\dfrac{4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq}{\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{eff}}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\,:=\,\dfrac{E}{4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq}~.

(5)

In Eq. (
5
), the ‘tilde’ accent indicates that Eq. (
4
) is only an approximation of the true dynamics; the
subscripts
\rho
and
\mathrm{m}
designate the inertial and membrane
branches.
As shown in Fig.
1
, the frequencies in Eq. (
5
) are a good approximation to the true solution.

The fluid response.

To understand the dynamics of the fluid as the membrane relaxes, consider two
disturbances to a specific mode
\bm{q}
.
In the first case,

\hat{h}^{\mathrm{m}}_{\bm{q}}=0

and only the inertial mode is excited, while in the second case

\hat{h}^{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=0

and only the membrane mode is excited
[see Eq. (
6

In both cases, the pressure field (
3

\hat{p}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}=\mp\tfrac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\mkern 1.0mu\omega_{\bm{q}}^{2}+E)\,\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}\mkern 1.0mue^{-q\lvert z\rvert}

The magnitude of the pressure at
z=0
determined by Eq. (
1

1/q
is a consequence of the
pressure field being harmonic:

p^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mujj}=0
.
In contrast, fluid velocities decay over the two length scales
1/q
and

1/k_{\mkern-1.0mu\nu}
, where

k_{\mkern-1.0mu\nu}:=(q^{2}-\omega/\nu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}})^{1/2}

on the membrane branch and

1/\bar{q}\ll 1/k_{\mkern-1.0mu\nu}

on the inertial branch.

The semipermeable membrane

With an understanding of the impermeable membrane, we are primed to investigate
the primary system of interest: a semipermeable membrane, in which fluid flows
through the membrane while solutes dissolved in the fluid do not.
Solutes are characterized by a base concentration

c_{0}\!\sim\!0.1

\!\text{nm}^{-3}

[
32
]
and diffusion constant

D\!\sim\!10^{3}

\!\text{nm}^{2}/\text{\textmu sec}

[
33
]
.
In addition, the membrane impermeability
\kappa
relates the flux of water
through the bilayer to its driving force
[
3
]
; here

\kappa\mkern-1.0mu\sim\mkern-1.0mu10^{3}\text{--}10^{5}

\!\text{pN}\!\cdot\!\text{\textmu sec}/\text{nm}^{3}

[
1
,
31
]
.
Dynamical effects from water permeation are of order

\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq/\kappa
:
a dimensionless parameter that we call the permeability number
\mathcal{P}
,
ranging from

10^{-6}\text{--}10^{-12}

over wavenumbers

q\in(10^{-4},1)~\text{nm}^{-1}
.
We will show

\mathcal{P}\rightarrow 0

in the limit of an impermeable membrane; lipid bilayers are thus nearly
impermeable.

The governing equations

The membrane shape equation (
1
) is unchanged in the presence of
solutes
[
3
]
.
The perturbed concentrations
c^{\pm}
above and below the membrane, with

\lvert c^{\pm}\rvert\ll c_{0}
,
evolve according to the diffusion equation

c^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut}=D\mkern 1.0muc^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mujj}
.
Solute impermeability is enforced by requiring the total solute flux through the
membrane—comprised of diffusive and convective contributions—to be zero:

-D\mkern 1.0muc^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0muz}\,+\,c_{0}\mkern 1.0mu\big(v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muz}-h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut}\big)\,=\,0~.

(7)

In Eq. (
7
), velocities and concentration gradients are
evaluated at the membrane surface.
The equation governing water permeation through an arbitrarily curved and
deforming lipid membrane was recently obtained by A.M.
Alkadri
and
K.K.
Mandadapu

[
3
]
, who extended several seminal
findings
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.
These authors used techniques from irreversible thermodynamics and differential
geometry to show fluid flow through the membrane is driven by differences in
hydrodynamic tractions and osmotic pressures across the membrane surface.
For nearly planar geometries and an ideally selective membrane, the findings
from Ref.
[
3
]
simplify to

\kappa\mkern 1.0mu\big(v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muz}-h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut}\big)\,=\,k_{\text{B}}\theta\mkern 1.0mu\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0muc\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket\,-\,\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0mup\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket~,

(8)

where
k_{\text{B}}
is Boltzmann’s constant,
\theta
is the absolute temperature,
and

\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0muc\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket:=c^{+}-c^{-}

is the jump in solute concentration at the membrane surface.
In the language of irreversible thermodynamics
[
44
,
45
]
,
Eq. (
8
) presents a linear relationship between the
thermodynamic driving force

(k_{\text{B}}\theta\mkern 1.0mu\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0muc\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket-\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0mup\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket)

and the corresponding thermodynamic flux

(v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muz}-h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut})
,
with the latter a measure of fluid flow through the membrane.
The phenomenological parameter
\kappa
, understood as a transport coefficient

[
3
]
, captures the resistance to flow: for a given driving
force, a larger value of
\kappa
corresponds to a smaller water flux through
the membrane.
Assuming the usual Stokes flow scaling of

\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq^{2}v\sim q\mkern 1.0mup
,
in the limit of

\kappa\rightarrow\infty

or

\mathcal{P}\rightarrow 0

we recover the impermeable coupling condition

v^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0muz}=h_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0mut}
.

The diffusive time scale

The presence of solutes in the surrounding fluid introduces a diffusive
frequency

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}\,:=\,q^{2}D~.

(9)

Figure
2(a)
shows
\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}
plotted on top of the
curves in Fig.

Let us denote
q_{0}^{\pm}
as the two wavenumbers at which the diffusive and
membrane time scales are equal.
From Eqs. (
5
) and (

q_{0}^{\pm}\,=\,\dfrac{4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muD}{k_{\text{b}}}\,\bigg[\mkern 1.0mu1\,\pm\,\bigg(1\,-\,\dfrac{\lambda_{\text{c}}\mkern 1.0muk_{\text{b}}}{8\mkern 1.0mu{\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}}^{\!2}D^{2}}\bigg)^{\!\!1/2\,\,}\mkern 1.0mu\bigg]~,

(10)

with

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}<\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}

when

q\in(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
,
and

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}>\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}

otherwise.
Since the base surface tension

rather varies—and can be tuned—across systems,

(11)

at the top of the dome, above which no real
q_{0}
exists.
When the base membrane tension
\lambda_{\text{c}}
is larger than
\lambda_{\text{c}}^{\mkern-1.0mu\ast}
,

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}>\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}

over all wavenumbers.
As we will see, the relative values of
\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
and
\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}
—and
thus whether a mode is “inside” (i.e. below) or “outside” (i.e. above) the
dome in Fig.
2(b)
—govern different regimes of membrane
behavior.

The frequency solutions

Membrane and fluid unknowns are expressed as in the impermeable analysis, with
the concentration expanded in planar normal modes as [cf. Eq. (
3
)]

c^{\pm}(x,y,z,t)\,=\,\sum_{\bm{q}}\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}(z)\,e^{i\mkern 1.0mu(q_{x}x\,+\,q_{y}y)}\,e^{-\omega_{\bm{q}}t}~.

(12)

In our linearized description, all perturbed bulk quantities are again
proportional to the membrane height.
Assuming a nontrivial solution
(
\hat{h}_{\bm{q}}\neq 0
),
it is straightforward to arrive at the dispersion relation, as shown in §3.3 of
the SM
[
25
]
.
While
\omega_{\bm{q}}
cannot be solved for analytically, we take advantage of
the smallness of
\mathcal{P}
: since the membrane is nearly impermeable,
semipermeable frequencies are close to their impermeable counterparts.
In what follows, we investigate how the dynamics of the impermeable branches are
altered by the presence of solutes in the surrounding medium.

The inertial branch.

We begin by assuming concentration modes evolve with the impermeable inertial
frequency
\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}
.
The diffusion equation for mode
\bm{q}
then simplifies to

\dfrac{\text{d}^{2}\mkern 1.0mu{\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}}}{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{z}^{2}}\,=\,-q^{2}\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{\eta}^{2}\mkern 1.0mu\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}~,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pt\tilde{\eta}\,:=\mkern 1.0mu\bigg(\dfrac{\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}}{\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}}\,-\,1\bigg)^{\!\!1\mkern-1.0mu/\mkern-1.0mu2}

(13)

is a positive, real, dimensionless parameter defined for notational convenience.
Equation (
13
) admits the two purely oscillatory
solutions

\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu+}_{\bm{q}}\sim e^{\pm i\mkern 1.0muq\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{\eta}\mkern 1.0muz}
.
As these standing waves do not decay as

z\rightarrow\infty
,
we seek the lowest-order concentration correction due to the semipermeability of
the membrane.
*
*

*

For conciseness, only the concentration field above the membrane is presented;
see the SM
[
25
, §§ 3.2--3.3]
for all details.

We first recognize that water permeation alters the hydrodynamic drag on the
membrane by the surrounding fluid.
Equations (
7
) and (
8
) together
yield

\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0mup\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket=k_{\text{B}}\theta\mkern 1.0mu\left\llbracket\mkern 1.0muc\mkern 1.0mu\right\rrbracket-(\kappa\mkern 1.0muD/c_{0})\mkern 1.0muc^{\pm}_{\mkern-1.0mu,\mkern 1.0muz}\mkern 1.0mu
,
which—along with Eq. (
1
)—shows standing waves in the
concentration add a hydrodynamic drag force 90
∘
out of phase with the
membrane velocity.
We show in the SM
[
25
, §3.3.2]
that an approximate dispersion
relation for the semipermeable membrane is given by [cf. Eq. (
4
)]

\rho_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{eff}}\,\omega_{\bm{q}}^{\,2}\,-\,4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq\,\omega_{\bm{q}}\,+\,E\,=\,\pm\,\dfrac{8\mkern 1.0mui\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}}{\tilde{\eta}}\,(4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq)\,\omega_{\bm{q}}~.

(14)

In Eq. (
14
),

\mathcal{S}:=k_{\text{B}}\theta\mkern 1.0muc_{0}/(D\mkern 1.0mu\kappa\mkern 1.0muq)\ll 1

as a dimensionless parameter that arises when Eqs. (
7
) and
(
8
) are combined.
The solution of Eq. (
14
) closest to
\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}
, to
lowest order in the product
\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}
of small parameters, is given by

\omega_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}\,\approx\,\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}\,\big(1\,\pm\,8\mkern 1.0mui\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}/\tilde{\eta}\mkern 1.0mu\big)~.

(15)

Membrane permeability and osmotic forces accordingly introduce exceedingly slow
temporal oscillations to the inertial dynamics of the coupled system.

The modification to the inertial frequency in Eq. (
15
) also alters the spatial concentration of the
solutes.
Substituting Eq. (
15
) into the diffusion equation
and solving for the concentration field yields

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu+}_{\bm{q}}\,

\displaystyle\sim\,\exp\mkern 1.0mu\bigg\{\pm\,i\mkern 1.0muq\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{\eta}\mkern 1.0muz\,-\,\dfrac{4\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}\mkern 1.0muq\mkern 1.0muz}{1-\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}/\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}}\bigg\}~.

(16)

Importantly,

\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu+}_{\bm{q}}\rightarrow 0

as

z\rightarrow\infty

and concentration perturbations due to the fluctuating membrane decay far from
the membrane surface.
We have thus found a physically meaningful solution for both the frequency
(
15
) and concentration
(
16
) over all wavenumbers.
The validity of our approximate analysis is confirmed in Fig.
2(c)
: the impermeable branch is visually indistinguishable
from the real portion of its semipermeable counterpart.

The membrane branch when diffusion is slow.

As in the analysis of the inertial branch, we start by assuming the solute
concentration evolves at the frequency of the impermeable system—in this case,
the membrane frequency
\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
.
For wavenumbers

q\notin(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
,
diffusion is slow relative to the membrane and

\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}>\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}
.
The solute diffusion equation once again simplifies to Eq. (
13
), where now
\tilde{\eta}
involves
\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}

instead of
\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}
.
With this change, and following a similar analysis as that described in the
inertial scenario, we once again arrive at Eq. (
14
).
The membrane frequency is then found to be given by

\omega_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\,\approx\,\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\,\big(1\,\mp\,8\mkern 1.0mui\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}/\tilde{\eta}\mkern 1.0mu\big)~.

(17)

Importantly, the sign of the imaginary part of
\omega_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
is opposite that of

\omega_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}
[cf. Eq. (
15
)], and the out-of-phase drag force alters the inertial and membrane dynamics in different ways.
Substituting Eq. (
17
) into the diffusion equation
gives the concentration field [cf. Eq. (
16
)]

\displaystyle\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu+}_{\bm{q}}\,

\displaystyle\sim\,\exp\mkern 1.0mu\bigg\{\pm\,i\mkern 1.0muq\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{\eta}\mkern 1.0muz\,+\,\dfrac{4\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{P}\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{S}\mkern 1.0muq\mkern 1.0muz}{1-\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}/\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}}\bigg\}~.

(18)

The concentration field in Eq. (
18
) diverges as

z\rightarrow\infty
,
and is not physically meaningful.
As a consequence, the membrane branch vanishes when

q\notin(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
.
A representative result at low tension is shown in Fig.
2(c)
, and at tensions larger than
\lambda_{\text{c}}^{\mkern-1.0mu\ast}

(
11
) the membrane branch vanishes entirely.

The membrane branch when diffusion is fast.

When

\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}<\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}

and

q\in(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
,
the analysis of the coupled system is straightforward in the limit of small

\mathcal{P}
.
The diffusion equation, assuming

\omega=\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}

at lowest order, simplifies to

d^{2}\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}/dz^{2}=(q^{2}-\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}/D)\mkern 1.0mu\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}
.
We thus find the exponentially decaying concentration solutions

\hat{c}^{\mkern 1.0mu\pm}_{\bm{q}}\,\sim\,\exp\mkern 1.0mu\bigg\{\mp q\mkern 1.0muz\,\bigg(1\,-\,\dfrac{\omega^{\text{imp}}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}}{\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}}\,\bigg)^{\!\!1\mkern-1.0mu/\mkern-1.0mu2}\,\bigg\}~.

(19)

With no oscillations in the lowest-order concentration field, modifications to
the hydrodynamic drag felt by the membrane are in phase with the membrane
velocity.
Moreover, as such corrections are of order

\mathcal{P}\!\ll\!1
,
we expect the membrane branch to be indistinguishable from its impermeable
counterpart—as confirmed in Fig.
2(c)
.

Theoretical implications.

As mentioned previously, solutes are present in the fluid surrounding nearly all
artificial and biological membranes.
Our findings demonstrate such systems can only be approximated as impermeable
for wavenumbers “inside the dome,” where

q\in(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})

and there is a slow frequency

\omega\approx E/(4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq)
.
In contrast, only a quickly decaying inertial mode exists for wavenumbers

q\notin(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})

“outside the dome.”
For this case, (
i
) the membrane response is independent of
k_{\text{b}}
and

\lambda_{\text{c}}
at linear order, and (
i\mkern-1.0mui
) thermal perturbations (discussed
subsequently) drive membrane undulations to continuously grow until
saturated by nonlinear forces—both of which are surprising results.
Such predictions motivate us to question whether our normal mode ansatz is
indeed appropriate outside the dome.
If instead the solute concentration was decomposed as

c\sim\check{c}(z,t)\mkern 1.0mue^{i\mkern 1.0mu(q_{x}x\,+\,q_{y}y)}

[cf. Eq. (
12
)],
then the diffusion equation requires

\partial\check{c}/\partial t=D(\partial^{2}\check{c}/\partial z^{2}-q^{2}\check{c})
.
Given an infinite domain

z\in(-\infty,\infty)
,
a self-similar solution is expected for
\check{c}(z,t)
—which is incompatible
with our ansatz.
Further analysis, likely involving nonlinear simulations of the membrane and
surrounding fluid
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
, is
required to understand the dynamics of the coupled system.
In any case, however, we emphasize that the standard impermeable result is not
applicable for wavenumbers outside the dome.

Experimentally characterizing GUVs

In experimental investigations of lipid bilayers, one often seeks to
characterize membrane material properties prior to further manipulation.
When GUVs are involved, their fluctuations from thermal disturbances can be
imaged and—with a theory of membrane undulations—processed to determine

k_{\text{b}}
and
\lambda_{\text{c}}

[
29
,
31
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
.
While most experimental investigations apply the well-understood impermeable
theory over all wavenumbers, the present study reveals that only modes inside
the dome should be treated as impermeable.
Moreover, as the behavior of undulations outside the dome is not currently well-understood, experimental measurements involving these modes should be excluded
when determining membrane properties.
In what follows, we discuss how to incorporate thermal perturbations into our
theory, and then analyze GUV fluctuation data from experiments.

The impermeable Langevin equation.

When the lipid bilayer is treated as impermeable, its linear response to
perturbations is well-known.
Since inertial modes decay much more quickly than their membrane counterparts
(see Eq. (
6
) with
\omega_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}\gg\omega_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
),
inertia can be neglected entirely; the membrane frequency
\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}

presented in Eq. (
5
) is then exact.
Decomposing the membrane height as

h(x,y,t)\,=\,\sum_{\bm{q}}\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}(t)\mkern 1.0mue^{i\mkern 1.0mu(q_{x}x\,+\,q_{y}y)}

leads to the Langevin equation
[cf. Eqs. (
2
),(
4
)]

\dfrac{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}}}{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{t}}\,+\,\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}\,=\,\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{q}}(t)~.

(20)

In Eq. (
20
),
\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{q}}(t)
is a complex Gaussian thermal
noise satisfying the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, with two-time
correlations proportional to

\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,k_{\text{B}}\theta/(4\mkern 1.0mu\mu_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{f}}\mkern 1.0muq\,\ell_{\text{c}}^{\,2})

[
18
]
.
Equation (
20
) can be solved exactly to yield

[
60
]

\langle\lvert\mkern 1.0mu\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}\rvert^{2}\rangle\,=\,\dfrac{k_{\text{B}}\theta}{E\mkern 1.0mu\ell_{\text{c}}^{\,2}}~,

(21)

which is the well-known result of the equipartition theorem as applied to planar
membranes
[
34
]
.

The Langevin equation inside the dome.

For modes with

q\in(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
,
there is no discernible difference between the impermeable and semipermeable
dynamics.
Thus, Eq. (
20
) once again applies, and membrane fluctuations
satisfy the equipartition result (
21
).

The Langevin equation outside the dome.

As discussed previously, we are currently unsure if our normal mode ansatz is
appropriate outside the dome.
If the ansatz is indeed valid, then we effectively see only an inertial mode.
The corresponding Langevin equation describing membrane fluctuations is given by

\dfrac{1}{\omega^{(\text{r})}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}}\,\dfrac{\text{d}^{2}\mkern 1.0mu{\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}}}{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{t}^{2}}\,+\,\dfrac{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{\tilde{h}_{\bm{q}}}}{\text{d}\mkern 1.0mu{t}}\,=\,\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{q}}(t)~,

(22)

where
\omega^{(\text{r})}_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}
is the real part of the frequency and
\tilde{\xi}_{\bm{q}}
is
the same thermal noise as in Eq. (
20
).
There is no elastic restoring force in Eq. (
22
),
which is analogous to the equation of motion for a colloidal particle in a
fluid.
Just as a colloid diffuses freely at long times
[
60
]
,
the variance of membrane height undulations grows linearly in time according to
Eq. (
22
).
Eventually, the membrane height will grow to a point where our assumption of
linearity is no longer valid.
At this point, membrane bending and surface tension forces will presumably
prevent height fluctuations from continuing to grow—yet a description of such
behavior is beyond the scope of the present work.
Thus, irrespective of whether our ansatz is valid, membrane fluctuations are not
expected to satisfy the equipartition result outside the dome where

q\notin(q_{0}^{-},q_{0}^{+})
.

Concluding remarks

In this work, we investigated the linearized dynamics of a planar lipid
membrane, surrounded by a Newtonian fluid in which solutes are dissolved.
Though the lipid bilayer is only weakly permeable to water, the presence of
solutes can significantly alter its dynamics.
More specifically, when

\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle D}<\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}

and diffusion is slow relative to the impermeable membrane, undulations of the
semipermeable system no longer decay at a frequency near
\tilde{\omega}_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
.
In such cases, either (
i
) the ansatz that all quantities can be expressed in
terms of planar normal modes as in Eqs. (
2
),
(
3
), and (
12
) is no
longer valid, or (
i\mkern-1.0mui
) the bilayer only decays at the fast inertial
frequency

\omega_{\mkern-2.0mu\rho}\gg\omega_{\mkern-1.0mu\text{m}}
.
When thermal perturbations are incorporated into the theory, the well-known
equipartition result (
21
) is found to only be
experimentally relevant when

q\in(q_{0}^{-},q_{\text{opt}})
—a range that narrows as
\lambda_{\text{c}}
is increased.
Our results are relevant when membrane tensions are large, as is the case with
active vesicles
[

,

and a
variety of biological scenarios
