Distributionally Robust Regret Optimal Control Under Moment-Based Ambiguity Sets
Feras Al Taha, Eilyan Bitar

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a class of finite-horizon, linear-quadratic stochastic control problems, where the probability distribution governing the noise process is unknown but assumed to belong to an ambiguity set consisting of all distributions whose mean and covariance lie within norm balls centered at given nominal values. To address the distributional ambiguity, we explore the design of causal affine control policies to minimize the worst-case expected regret over all distributions in the given ambiguity set. The resulting minimax optimal control problem is shown to admit an equivalent reformulation as a tractable convex program that corresponds to a regularized version of the nominal linear-quadratic stochastic control problem. While this convex program can be recast as a semidefinite program, semidefinite programs are typically solved using primal-dual interior point methods that scale poorly with the problem size in practice. To address this limitation, we propose a scalable dual projected subgradient method to compute optimal controllers to an arbitrary accuracy. Numerical experiments are presented to benchmark the proposed method against state-of-the-art data-driven and distributionally robust control design approaches.

Introduction

A central challenge in the control of dynamic systems subject to uncertain disturbances is the need to make decisions based on incomplete information about the future. In the stochastic control framework, the disturbance is modeled as a stochastic process with a known probability distribution, and a causal control policy is designed to minimize an expected cost criterion under the given distribution. However, stochastic control methods are known to be sensitive to deviations from the assumed distribution, which can significantly degrade performance. In practice, the true distribution is typically unknown due to estimation errors, arising when the distribution is inferred from a finite sample of past disturbances or because of distribution shift that may occur in nonstationary environments.

To address these challenges, many distributionally robust approaches to control design have been developed, where the unknown distribution is assumed to belong to a specified set of possible distributions, termed the ambiguity set. Instead of optimizing for a single assumed distribution, these methods seek to enhance the robustness by optimizing against the worst-case distribution within the given ambiguity set.
Different types of ambiguity sets have been studied, including those based on the Wasserstein distance
(
9
;
16
;
20
;
32
;
36
)
, as well as ambiguity sets containing distributions with fixed or bounded moments
(
10
;
21
;
22
;
25
;
27
;
33
)
, among others. However, the inherent pessimism of distributionally robust control methods can lead to overly cautious control policies and performance loss when the actual disturbance distribution is less severe than anticipated.

To temper the conservatism of (distributionally) robust control design methods with a more optimistic view of the uncertain disturbance process, several recent papers have proposed
worst-case regret minimization
as an alternative to worst-case cost minimization to design control policies for uncertain systems.
For example, in
(
29
;
13
)
, the uncertain disturbance process is assumed to lie within a norm-bounded uncertainty set, and causal linear controllers are designed to minimize the worst-case regret across all possible disturbance process realizations within this set.
In this robust regret optimal control framework,
regret
is defined as the excess cost incurred by a causal controller (in response to a particular realization of the disturbance process) relative to the minimum achievable cost by a noncausal controller (with perfect foresight of the disturbance process at the outset).
This framework is extended in
(
24
)
to incorporate uncertainty in the plant dynamics.

Taking a stochastic view of the disturbance process,
1
proposes a distributionally robust approach to regret optimal control, where causal linear controllers are designed to minimize the
worst-case expected regret
over a type-2 Wasserstein ball of disturbance distributions.
Focusing on the finite-horizon setting, the resulting minimax optimal control problem is shown to admit an equivalent reformulation as a semidefinite program whose size scales polynomially with the control horizon. This framework was further extended to the partially observed setting in
(
15
)
and to the infinite-horizon setting in
(
19
;
18
)
.

Main Contributions:

In this paper, we consider the design of distributionally robust regret optimal controllers for the finite-horizon setting using a novel class of ambiguity sets that consist of distributions with Euclidean norm-bounded means and Schatten norm-bounded covariance matrices.
Focusing on causal affine control policies, we leverage the structure of the proposed ambiguity set to reformulate the minimax optimal control problem as a tractable, finite-dimensional convex program that can be interpreted as a linear-quadratic stochastic control problem with Schatten norm-based regularization.
The proposed framework is shown to generalize the robust regret optimal control framework studied in
(
29
;
13
)
.

While the convex reformulation provided in this paper is shown to admit an equivalent representation as a semidefinite program (SDP), solving large-scale SDPs via interior-point methods can be computationally prohibitive in practice.
To address this limitation, we develop a dual projected subgradient algorithm to efficiently compute controllers to any desired accuracy.
Finally, we present numerical experiments showing that, when only a finite training sample from the ground-truth distribution is available for control design, the class of distributionally robust regret optimal controllers proposed in this paper can deliver superior out-of-sample performance compared to other data-driven control design methods in the literature.

2
Problem Formulation

Consider a discrete-time, linear time-varying system:

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=A_{t}x_{t}+B_{t}u_{t}+w_{t},

(1)

which evolves over a finite number of time periods
t=0,\dots,T-1
. Here,
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the
system state
,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is the
control input
, and
w_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the
disturbance
acting on the system at time
t
.
We assume that the system matrices
A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
and
B_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{u}}
are known at the outset and that the system state is perfectly observed.
The initial state
x_{0}
and disturbances
w_{0},\dots,w_{T-1}
are potentially dependent random variables whose joint distribution
P
is
unknown
, but assumed to belong to a given compact set of distributions denoted by
{\cal P}
and termed the
ambiguity set
.

We define the associated
state
,
input
, and
disturbance trajectories
as
x:=(x_{0},\,\dots,\,x_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u:=(u_{0},\,\dots,\,u_{T-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
w:=(x_{0},\,w_{0},\,\dots,\,w_{T-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n:=n_{x}(T+1)
and
m:=n_{u}T
.
Note that we have included the initial state
x_{0}
as the first element of the disturbance trajectory
w
.
Using these definitions, we can express the dynamics (
1
) in terms of a causal linear mapping from the input and disturbance trajectories to the state trajectory:

\displaystyle x=Fu+Gw.

(2)

Here,
F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are block lower-triangular (causal) matrices, which are straightforward to construct from the given system matrices
\{A_{t},B_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
.

The cost incurred by an input trajectory
u
and disturbance trajectory
w
is defined as

\displaystyle J(u,\,w):=x^{\top}Qx+u^{\top}Ru,

(3)

where
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
and
R\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
. We consider strictly causal affine disturbance feedback controllers

\displaystyle u_{t}=v_{t}\,+\,\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{t}K_{t,k}w_{k-1},\quad t=0,\dots,T-1,

(4)

where we have defined
w_{-1}:=x_{0}
.
One can also express the control policy (
4
) as an affine mapping
u=\phi(w):=Kw+v
from the disturbance trajectory
w
to the input trajectory
u
.
Here,
(v,\,K)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal L}
are the policy parameters, and
{\cal L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
represents the space of all block lower triangular matrices that respect the strictly causal disturbance feedback structure specified in (
4
). Using these definitions, the set of admissible control policies can be defined as

\displaystyle{\cal A}:=\big\{\phi(w)=Kw+v\,\big|\,(v,K)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\cal L}\big\}.

(5)

The class of strictly causal affine disturbance feedback control policies is known to be equivalent to the class of causal affine state feedback control policies
(
14
)
.
Specifically, given a disturbance feedback control policy
\phi(w)=Kw+v
, where
\phi\in{\cal A}
, the change of variables
L:=(I+KG^{-1}F)^{-1}KG^{-1}
and
c:=(I+KG^{-1}F)^{-1}v
induces a causal state feedback controller
\psi(x):=Lx+c
that satisfies
\psi\in{\cal A}
and
\psi(x)=\phi(w)
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(
23
;
31
)
.
In this paper, we have adopted the disturbance feedback parametrization, because it ensures convexity of the cost function (
3
) with respect to the disturbance feedback controller parameters, whereas the state feedback parameterization results in a nonconvex dependence of the cost function on the state feedback controller parameters.

2.1
Distributionally Robust Regret Optimal Control

This paper is focused on the design of affine controllers to minimize the worst-case expected regret over all distributions within a given ambiguity set
{\cal P}
.
We define regret as the difference between the cost incurred by a controller and the minimum cost achievable by an optimal noncausal (clairvoyant) controller with perfect knowledge of the disturbance trajectory at the outset.
More specifically, we define the (ex-post)
regret
incurred by an input trajectory
u
in response to a disturbance
w
as

\displaystyle R(u,\,w):=J(u,\,w)-J(u^{o}(w),\,w),

(6)

where
u^{o}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the
optimal noncausal controller
, which is defined as
u^{o}(w):=\mathop{\mathrm{a}rgmin}_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}J(u,\,w).

Building on this definition of regret, we consider the
distributionally robust regret optimal control
problem, as originally proposed in
(
1
)
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\phi\in{\cal A}}\sup_{P\in{\cal P}}\mathds{E}_{P}\left[R(\phi(w),\,w)\right].

(7)

In
(
17
, Theorem 11.2.1)
, the optimal noncausal controller is shown to be both unique and linear in the disturbance trajectory, and is given by

\displaystyle u^{o}(w)=K^{o}w,

(8)

where
K^{o}:=-(R+F^{\top}QF)^{-1}F^{\top}QG.
Using this explicit characterization of the optimal noncausal controller, the expression for regret given in (
6
) can be simplified to
R(u,\,w)=(u-K^{o}w)^{\top}D(u-K^{o}w),

where
D:=R+F^{\top}QF\succ 0

(
13
;
26
)
. It follows that the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem (
7
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\inf_{\phi\in{\cal A}}\sup_{P\in{\cal P}}\mathds{E}_{P}\left[(\phi(w)-K^{o}w)^{\top}D(\phi(w)-K^{o}w)\right].

(9)

2.2
Ambiguity Set

Based on the reformulation provided in (
9
), the regret incurred by an affine policy
\phi\in{\cal A}
is a quadratic function of the disturbance trajectory
w
.
As a result, the expected regret only depends on the underlying probability distribution governing the disturbance trajectory through its first and second moments.
With this in mind, we define the ambiguity set as a set of distributions with
unknown mean and covariance, which are assumed to lie within a Euclidean norm ball and a Schatten
p
-norm ball, respectively. The Schatten
p
-norm is defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Schatten
p
-norm)
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
Schatten
p
-norm
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\|A\|_{p}:=\begin{cases}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{p}\big)^{1/p},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\sigma_{1},&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where
d:=\min\{m,n\}
and
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}
denote the singular values of the matrix
A
.

Note that the Schatten
p
-norm reduces to the
nuclear norm
for
p=1
, the
Frobenius norm
for
p=2
, and the
spectral norm
for
p=\infty
.
Additionally, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, the dual norm associated with the Schatten
p
-norm is the Schatten
q
-norm, where
q\in[1,\infty]
is such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1

(
7
)
.

Given a
nominal mean-covariance pair

(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
, we define the
ambiguity set
of distributions as

\displaystyle{\cal P}:=\big\{P\in{\cal M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \big|\ \|\mu-\widehat{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}\leq r_{1},\ \|\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{p}\leq r_{2}\big\},

(10)

where
\mu:=\mathds{E}_{P}[w]
and
\Sigma:=\mathds{E}_{P}[(w-\mu)(w-\mu)^{\top}]
denote the mean and covariance of the distribution
P
.
Crucially, this class of ambiguity sets allows for temporally correlated and non-Gaussian disturbance processes, enabling robustness to a broad class of distributions.

Remark 1

(Interpretation of Ambiguity Set Parameters)
.

One can interpret the nominal mean
\widehat{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
as “point estimates” of the unknown mean and covariance matrix, and the radii of their respective norm balls,
r_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, as reflecting the degree of “confidence” one has in these estimates.
When
r_{1}=r_{2}=0
, the ambiguity set is simply the set of distributions with mean
\widehat{\mu}
and covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
,
and problem (
9
) becomes an “ambiguity-free” stochastic control problem,
since the expected regret only depends on the mean and covariance of the disturbance trajectory
w
.
The Schatten-norm order
p
used to define the covariance ball determines the shape of
the ambiguity set, and the form of regularization that arises when solving problem (
9
).
When the nominal mean and covariance
(\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\Sigma})
are learned from data, the parameters
(r_{1},\,r_{2},\,p)
can be tuned using cross-validation methods to improve controller out-of-sample performance.

3
Convex Reformulation

In this section, we provide an explicit solution to the worst-case expectation problem (the inner maximization) in (
9
), which enables the reformulation of problem (
9
) as a tractable convex program.

3.1
Reformulating the Worst-Case Expected Regret

The expected regret of an affine control policy
\phi(w)=Kw+v
can be expressed as
\mathds{E}_{P}[R(\phi(w),w)]=\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(D\,\mathds{E}_{P}[(\Delta w+v)(\Delta w+v)^{\top}]\big)
,
where
\Delta:=K-K^{o}
. Using this reformulation, the worst-case expected regret can be decomposed into two separate maximization problems—one over the disturbance mean
\mu
and the other over the disturbance covariance
\Sigma
:

\displaystyle\max_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\|\mu-\widehat{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}\leq r_{1}}(\Delta\mu+v)^{\top}D(\Delta\mu+v)\ \ +\max_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,:\,\|\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{p}\leq r_{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\left(\Sigma C(K)\right),

(11)

where
C(K):=(K-K^{o})^{\top}D(K-K^{o}).

Note that the optimization over the disturbance mean requires maximizing a convex quadratic function.
While this nonconvexity might lead one to conclude that problem (
7
) is computationally intractable, the following lemma resolves this issue.

Lemma 1
.

Let
r\geq 0
,
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
D\in\mathbb{S}^{m}_{++}
.
It holds that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ \max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\|y-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq r}(x+Cy)^{\top}D(x+Cy)\ =\ r\|C^{\top}DC\|_{\infty},

(12)

where the optimal value of the minimax problem is attained by the solutions
(x^{\star},\,y^{\star})=(-Cy_{0},\,y_{0}\pm\sqrt{r}\xi)
and
\xi
denotes a leading unit eigenvector of the matrix
C^{\top}DC
.

Using Lemma
1
and the decomposition of the worst-case expected regret in (
11
), we can now derive an equivalent convex reformulation of the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem (
7
).

Theorem 2
.

The following control policy is an optimal solution
to problem (
7
):

\displaystyle\phi^{\star}(w)

\displaystyle:=K^{\star}(w-\widehat{\mu})\,+\,K^{o}\widehat{\mu},

(13)

where
K^{o}=-(R+F^{\top}QF)^{-1}F^{\top}QG
is the optimal noncausal control policy, and

\displaystyle K^{\star}

\displaystyle\!\in\mathop{\mathrm{a}rgmin}_{K\in{\cal L}}\big\{\!\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}C(K)\big)\,+\,r_{1}\|C(K)\|_{\infty}+r_{2}\|C(K)\|_{q}\big\},

(14)

where
\|\cdot\|_{q}
denotes the dual norm of the Schatten
p
-norm.

The distributionally robust regret optimal control policy
\phi^{\star}(w)=K^{\star}(w-\widehat{\mu})+K^{o}\widehat{\mu}
has a feedback-feedforward structure that can be interpreted as follows. The
feedforward term

K^{o}\widehat{\mu}
represents the expected optimal noncausal input trajectory under the nominal disturbance mean
\widehat{\mu}
. In other words, it represents the expected action of the optimal noncausal control policy (
8
) when the disturbance follows its nominal mean. The causal
feedback term

K^{\star}(w-\widehat{\mu})
actively compensates for deviations of the realized disturbance from the nominal mean.

3.2
Controller Regularization and Interpolation

The reformulation in (
14
) can be interpreted as a
regularized
linear-quadratic stochastic control problem.
The first term in the objective corresponds to the expected regret of a linear controller
K
in response to a zero-mean disturbance process
w
with covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
.
By adding the expected cost incurred by the optimal
noncausal
controller under the same disturbance distribution, the optimization in (
14
) can be rewritten as

K^{\star}\!\in\mathop{\mathrm{a}rgmin}_{K\in{\cal L}}\big\{\mathds{E}_{\widehat{P}}[J(Kw,\,w)]+r_{1}\|C(K)\|_{\infty}+r_{2}\|C(K)\|_{q}\big\},

where
\widehat{P}\in{\cal M}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is any distribution with mean
\mathds{E}_{\widehat{P}}[w]=0
and second moment
\mathds{E}_{\widehat{P}}[ww^{\top}]=\widehat{\Sigma}
.
In this reformulation, the leading term represents the nominal expected cost of the controller
K
under the distribution
\widehat{P}
.
The second and third terms penalize the controller’s sensitivity to distributional uncertainty in the disturbance mean and covariance, respectively.

The spectral norm penalty
r_{1}\|C(K)\|_{\infty}
arises from mean uncertainty and coincides with the worst-case regret incurred by the controller
K
over all norm-bounded disturbances. Specifically,

\displaystyle\max_{\|\bar{w}\|_{2}\leq 1}R(K\bar{w},\bar{w})=\max_{\|\bar{w}\|_{2}\leq 1}\bar{w}^{\top}C(K)\bar{w}=\|C(K)\|_{\infty},

so minimizing this term with respect to
K
corresponds to solving the robust regret optimal control problem
\min_{K\in{\cal L}}\max_{\|\bar{w}\|_{2}\leq 1}R(K\bar{w},\bar{w})
, as studied in
(
13
;
29
)
.

The Schatten
q
-norm penalty
r_{2}\|C(K)\|_{q}
stems from uncertainty in the covariance matrix, which is assumed to lie within a Schatten
p
-norm ball, with
1/p+1/q=1
.
Thus, when the covariance uncertainty set is defined via the nuclear norm (
p=1
), the dual Schatten
q
-norm reduces to the spectral norm, and the two regularization terms in (
14
) combine to form a single penalty of the form
(r_{1}+r_{2})\|C(K)\|_{\infty}
.
Conversely, when the covariance matrix is bounded in spectral norm (
p=\infty
), the dual Schatten
q
-norm reduces to the nuclear norm, yielding a regularization term of the form
r_{2}\|C(K)\|_{1}=r_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\left(C(K)\right)
, which coincides with the expected regret of a linear controller
K
under a zero-mean disturbance process with isotropic covariance
r_{2}I
.
When the cost function (
3
) is separable across time periods (i.e.,
Q
and
R
are block diagonal), the quantity
\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\left(C(K)\right)
is minimized by the classical linear-quadratic regulator.

With these insights, the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem can be understood as
interpolating
between three canonical control design objectives: (i) the nominal linear-quadratic stochastic control problem under the distribution
\widehat{P}
(as
r_{1},r_{2}\rightarrow 0
); (ii) the robust regret optimal control problem (as
r_{1}\rightarrow\infty)
; and (iii) the classical linear-quadratic regulator (as
r_{2}\rightarrow\infty
and
p=\infty
). Thus, by tuning the ambiguity set parameters
(r_{1},r_{2},p)
, one can design controllers that trade off nominal performance and robustness to different forms of distributional shift.

4
Dual Projected Subgradient Method

The convex program provided in Theorem
2
can be recast as a semidefinite program (SDP) whose size grows polynomially with the state dimension
n_{x}
, input dimension
n_{u}
, and time horizon
T
(refer to App.
B
for the SDP reformulation).
Small to medium scale SDPs can be solved efficiently using primal-dual interior point methods, which run in polynomial time.
However, because these algorithms rely on second-order updates, their memory and computational requirements scale rapidly with the problem size, limiting their applicability to large systems.
Given these challenges, we show how to solve the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem using a projected subgradient method applied to the dual of problem (
14
). The resulting algorithm is shown to reduce to a projected gradient ascent algorithm when the nominal covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}
is positive definite.
Numerical results provided in App.
D
demonstrate that the proposed method delivers substantial computational speedups relative to solving the associated SDP with a state-of-the-art interior-point solver.

4.1
Dual Reformulation

To derive the dual of problem (
14
), it is helpful to define the following Cartesian product of covariance norm balls:

{\mathcal{C}}:=\big\{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,\big|\,\|\Sigma\|_{1}\leq r_{1}\big\}\,\times\,\big\{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,\big|\,\|\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{p}\leq r_{2}\big\}.

With this definition, problem (
14
) can be expressed as the minimax problem
\min_{K\in{\cal L}}\,f(K),
where
f(K):=\max_{\Lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(G(K)^{\top}\Lambda\big)
and
G(K):=(C(K),\,C(K))
.
By applying Sion’s minimax theorem
(
30
)
, the order of minimization and maximization can be reversed, leading to the following dual reformulation:

\displaystyle\min_{K\in{\cal L}}\,f(K)=\max_{\Lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}}\,g(\Lambda),

(15)

where
g(\Lambda):=\min_{K\in{\cal L}}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(G(K)^{\top}\Lambda\big)
.
In the following theorem, we establish several properties of the dual function
g
that will facilitate the design of a dual projected subgradient method.

Theorem 3
.

The dual function
g
has the following properties:

(i)

Concavity:
The function
g
is concave over
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
.

(ii)

Subdifferential:
The subdifferential
1
1

1

Although “superdifferential” and “supergradient” are the technically correct terms to use in the context of concave functions, we use “subdifferential” and “subgradient” here for simplicity.
of
g
at any point
\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
is nonempty and contains the following set of subgradients:

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\partial g(\Lambda)\supseteq{\mathrm{c}onv}\big(\big\{G(K)\mid K\in{\mathcal{K}}^{\star}(\Lambda)\big\}\big),

(16)

where
{\mathcal{K}}^{\star}(\Lambda):=\mathop{\mathrm{a}rgmin}_{K\in{\cal L}}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(G(K)^{\top}\Lambda\big)
is the nonempty set of minimizers of
\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(G(\cdot)^{\top}\Lambda\big)
.

(iii)

Differentiability:
The function
g
is differentiable at any point
\Lambda:=(\Lambda_{1},\,\Lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
satisfying
\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}\succ 0
.

4.2
Dual Projected Subgradient Method

Using the dual subgradients provided in Theorem
3
, we employ a projected subgradient method to solve the dual optimization problem in (
15
). Starting with the initial condition
\Lambda^{0}:=(0,\,\widehat{\Sigma})
and a sequence of positive step sizes
\{\eta^{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
, compute

\displaystyle K^{i}\,

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\mathrm{a}rgmin}_{K\in{\cal L}}\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(G(K)^{\top}\Lambda^{i}\big),

4.3
Reduction to Dual Projected Gradient Ascent

The following theorem shows that the proposed projected subgradient method (

)-(
18

is positive definite.

Theorem 4
.

The sequence of iterates
\{\Lambda^{i}=(\Lambda_{1}^{i},\,\Lambda_{2}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
generated by the projected subgradient method (
17
)-(
18
) satisfies
\Lambda_{2}^{i}\succeq\widehat{\Sigma}
for all
i\geq 0
.
Moreover, if
\widehat{\Sigma}\succ 0
, then the dual function
g
is differentiable at every iterate in this sequence.

By Theorem

\widehat{\Sigma}

\{\Lambda^{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}

Under this condition, the projected subgradient method reduces to a projected

Numerical Experiments

We study a damped double integrator system affected by stochastic disturbances over a time horizon of

The disturbances follow an AR(1) process

The true disturbance distribution is assumed to be unknown. Instead, we are given a finite training sample of

5.1
Proposed and Baseline Control Methods Under Comparison

We benchmark the proposed framework against several representative data-driven control design methods that differ in their distributional assumptions and optimization criteria.

1.

Schatten-norm covariance uncertainty sets (this paper)

2.

Wasserstein ambiguity sets

1

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a framework for synthesizing distributionally robust regret optimal controllers for linear systems affected by additive stochastic disturbances with unknown but bounded first and second moments. A tractable convex reformulation of the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem is provided, along with a dual projected subgradient algorithm to enable the efficient computation of control policies.

Appendix B
Semidefinite Programming Reformulation

Building on Theorem
2
, one can reformulate the distributionally robust regret optimal control problem (
7
) as a semidefinite program (SDP).
This can be done by representing each term in the objective in (
14
) in epigraph form. The leading term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}C(K)\big)=\min_{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\big\{\mathop{\mathrm{T}r}\left(X\right)\,\big|\,X\succeq\widehat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}C(K)\widehat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\},

(22)

where
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}
denotes the (symmetric) positive semidefinite square root of the covariance matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}
.
Using the Schur complement condition for positive semidefinitness, the nonlinear matrix inequality in (
22
) can be reformulated as a linear matrix inequality (LMI):

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}X&\widehat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}(K-K^{o})^{\top}\\
(K-K^{o})\widehat{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(23)

The regularization term involving the Schatten
q
-norm of the matrix
C(K)
can also be represented in epigraph form as

\displaystyle\|C(K)\|_{q}=\min_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\,Y\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\big\{\gamma\,\big|\,\gamma\geq\|Y\|_{q},\,Y\succeq C(K)\big\}

(24)

for
q\in[1,\infty]
. For the spectral norm (
q=\infty
), the identity in (
24
) simplifies to
\|C(K)\|_{\infty}=\min_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\gamma\,\big|\,\gamma I\succeq C(K)\big\}
. Once again, we employ the Schur complement condition for positive semidefiniteness to reformulate the nonlinear matrix inequality
Y\succeq C(K)
as the following LMI:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}Y&(K-K^{o})^{\top}\\
K-K^{o}&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(25)

The final constraint in the epigraph formulation (
24

\gamma\geq\|Y\|_{q}

Appendix C

The dual projected subgradient method presented in Sec.
4

, involves two key steps at each iteration: (i) the evaluation of a dual subgradient and (ii) the computation of a matrix projection onto the set
{\mathcal{C}}:=\big\{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,\big|\,\|\Sigma\|_{1}\leq r_{1}\big\}\,\times\,\big\{\Sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}\,\big|\,\|\Sigma-\widehat{\Sigma}\|_{p}\leq r_{2}\big\}

We discuss how to efficiently carry out these computations in App.
C.1
and
C.2
, respectively.
Additionally, in App.
C.3
, we provide a stopping criterion for Algorithm
1
using the relative duality gap of the primal-dual iterates generated by the proposed algorithm.

C.1
Computation of Dual Subgradient

To evaluate a subgradient
G(K^{i})=(C(K^{i}),\,C(K^{i}))
at each iteration
i
, the equality-constrained convex quadratic program in Line
8
of Algorithm
1
needs to be solved at every iteration. This can be accomplished by solving the linear system of equations derived from the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions using direct factorization methods, or iterative methods such as the conjugate gradient method, among others
(
28
, Section 16)
.

C.2
Computation of Matrix Projection onto Shatten
p
-norm Balls

We explain how to compute the matrix projection in Line
7
of Algorithm
1
. Because all Schatten norms are unitarily invariant, the orthogonal projection of a matrix onto the Schatten
p
-norm ball
{\mathcal{S}}_{r}^{p}:=\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\,|\,\|X\|_{p}\leq r\}
can be expressed in terms of the orthogonal projection of the corresponding vector of singular values onto the
\ell_{p}
-norm ball
{\cal B}_{r}^{p}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\|x\|_{p}\leq r\}.
The following result, adapted from
(
3
, Theorem 7.18)
, makes this relationship precise.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and denote its eigendecomposition by
X=U\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\lambda)U^{\top}
, where
U
is an orthonormal matrix.
Then

\displaystyle\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{r}^{p}}(X)=U\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\big(\,\Pi_{{\cal B}_{r}^{p}}(\lambda)\,\big)U^{\top}.

(26)

Here,
\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the diagonal matrix whose diagonal is the given vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
As an immediate consequence of this result, when the matrix being projected is positive semidefinite, the resulting projection remains positive semidefinite. This is due to the fact that the orthogonal projection of a nonnegative vector onto the
\ell_{p}
-norm ball
{\cal B}_{r}^{p}
is also nonnegative
(
2
, Lemma A.1)
.
It follows that

\displaystyle X\succeq 0\ \implies\ \Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{r}^{p}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}}(X)=\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{r}^{p}}(X).

(27)

Using the above property, together with the facts that
\eta^{i}C(K^{i})\succeq 0
,
\Lambda_{1}^{i}\succeq 0
, and
\Lambda_{2}^{i}\succeq\widehat{\Sigma}
(cf. Theorem
4
), the matrix projection in Line
7
of Algorithm
1
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\Pi_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\Lambda^{i}+\eta^{i}G(K^{i}))

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{1}}^{1}}(\Lambda_{1}^{i}+\eta^{i}C(K^{i}))\\
\widehat{\Sigma}+\Pi_{{\mathcal{S}}_{r_{2}}^{p}}(\Lambda_{2}^{i}+\eta^{i}C(K^{i})-\widehat{\Sigma})\end{bmatrix}.

(28)

To compute the matrix projections in the right-hand side of Eq. (
28
), one first needs to compute eigendecompositions of the matrices appearing inside each of the projection operators. Theorem
5
can then be applied to express each matrix projection in terms of a vector projection of the resulting eigenvalues onto the appropriate
\ell_{p}
-norm ball. Efficient algorithms for computing these vector projections are detailed in
(
35
)
. Note that the overall computational complexity of the projection step is dominated by the eigendecompositions, which scales as
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for dense matrices.

C.3
Stopping Criterion

Here, we introduce a stopping criterion for the sequence of iterates generated by Algorithm
1
.
Given a feasible dual point
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
one can bound the suboptimality of any feasible control policy
K\in{\cal L}
using the
relative duality gap
, defined as

\displaystyle\textrm{RelGap}(K,\Lambda):=(f(K)-g(\Lambda))/g(\Lambda).

(29)

By weak duality, (
29
) provides an upper bound on the
relative optimality gap
of the control policy
K
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textrm{RelGap}(K,\Lambda)\geq\frac{f(K)-\min_{K^{\prime}\in{\cal L}}f(K^{\prime})}{\min_{K^{\prime}\in{\cal L}}f(K^{\prime})}.

(30)

Therefore, one can stop the algorithm when this upper bound falls below a desired tolerance.
Since the proposed algorithm is not guaranteed to produce monotonic sequences of primal and dual objective values, we compute the relative duality gap at each iteration
i
(Line
6
of Algorithm
1
) using the best primal-dual iterates computed up to that point:

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq j,k\leq i}\textrm{RelGap}(K^{j},\Lambda^{k}).

(31)
