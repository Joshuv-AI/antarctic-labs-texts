Health professions students’ acceptance and readiness for learning analytics: lessons for educators
Authors: Farida Hussan, Hui Meng Er, Vishna Devi Nadarajah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLearning analytics (LA) is a tool for gathering, analysing, and interpreting data related to student’s learning behaviour, engagement, and performance. As the student data collected by the institutions are used to construct LA, it is essential to understand the acceptance and readiness of the students regarding the implementation of LA.MethodsA sequential mixed method research design was used in this study. A validated questionnaire was distributed to undergraduate students of Medicine, Dentistry and Pharmacy programmes to collect their general views on LA. Focus group interviews with a total of 18 students were conducted to explore their perceptions in depth, followed by thematic analysis of the transcribed data.ResultsGenerally, the students were aware of their demographic data, utilisation of learning management system and academic performance data being collected by the university. They were agreeable for collection of those data which had direct association with their learning to be used for LA. However, they expressed concerns about the privacy, confidentiality, and security of the collected data. Three themes emerged from the interviews, i.e., self-regulated learning, evidence-based decision making and data management. The students perceived that LA could help them to monitor achievement of learning outcomes and provide support for individualised learning paths through recommendations of learning resources and learning motivation. They also opined that LA could help educators and institutions by providing feedback on teaching and learning methods, resource allocation and interventions to create conducive learning environment.ConclusionsLA is a useful tool to support self-regulated learning, however, precautions should be exercised during implementation to ensure data privacy and security.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06284-3.Keywords: Learning analytics, Self-regulated learning, Undergraduate health professions students

Background

The widely used definition of learning analytics (LA) is “the measurement, collection, analysis, and reporting of data about learners and their contexts, for the purposes of understanding and optimizing learning and the environments in which it occurs” [1]. With rapid development in the field of artificial intelligence technology in recent years, LA has gained the attention of academics and higher education institutions, particularly as a tool to provide personalised recommendations to the learners based on the data extracted from the various user systems in the University [1]. In addition to supporting students via recommendations on learning resources to achieve their learning goals [2], LA can benefit academics by providing useful insights into student performance and their learning behaviour, besides helping them to improve teaching delivery and reduce student attrition [3].

While more benefits of LA are being discovered, LA implementation in health professions education is still in its initial stage [4]. The use of social network analysis in the context of LA has been reported to provide important information about the students’ interaction in an online problem-based learning environment [5, 6]. LA has also been shown beneficial for identifying students’ engagement in the pre-class activities of a flipped clinical skills course [7]. To date, most available studies on LA were carried out in the Western countries [3, 8, 9]. The study by Schumacher and Ifenthaler (2018) revealed that Economic and Business students at a European university expected the LA features to include reminders for assessment and feedback, learning guidance and recommendations [9]. Meanwhile, psychology students in Australia had mixed perceptions about the value of LA in helping their learning [3]. Saqr et al. (2017) reported the potential use of LA in early prediction of under-achieving medical students which could enable positive intervention to be provided to the at-risk students timely [10]. On the other hand, students’ learning autonomy could be challenged by LA [2]. Ten Cate et al. (2020) highlighted the possible risks of LA in health professions education from the educator’s perspectives, which included pressure on learners, reduced variety of learning resources and misalignment with eventual clinical performance [11]. There were also concerns about bias or pre-conception by the academics that might drive inequality as well as privacy invasion [3].

West and colleagues (2020) carried out a study among the undergraduate and postgraduate students in six Australian universities to explore the students’ perceptions on LA and identify the types of data that might be useful [8]. The findings showed that the students were aware of their data being collected for LA, but they were uncomfortable with collection of their personal related data such as data from university social media group, location data from their mobile phone and university apps usage. The students were also concerned over stereotyping and segregation based on their demographic data. These studies highlighted the needs to engage the students in the LA process [8].

The evidence on LA thus far suggests its potential role in navigating students’ learning. The self-regulated learning (SRL) theory can be applied as the theoretical framework for guiding the development of LA [12]. It is the most used theory for observation of student approach to learning and detection of learning strategies. Self-regulated learning is a self-directed process to convert learner’s mental skills to academic skills [13]. Self-directed learning skill is essential for students in higher education, yet this skill may not be intrinsic to everyone. Therefore, some students need external support to help them to internalise it [14]. LA can facilitate monitoring of student performance and timely personalised feedback which is crucial for SRL, as it is constructed based on real time data related to learner characteristics, curricular information, and learners’ interactions with learning management system (LMS) and resources [9].

In view of the paucity of literatures on LA and its implementation from diverse contexts around the globe particularly in relation to health professions education, it is necessary to investigate the roles and potentials of LA in supporting the training of health professionals. This is because the training of medical and health professions students emphasises lifelong learning and improvements in both clinical performance skills as well as professionalism. Professionalism includes the development of reflective skills, an important educational outcome for competent practitioners [15]. The data derived from LA can be utilised to guide learners in goal setting and behaviour change which in turn can lead to improvement in competencies related to knowledge, skills, and attitude. However, to benefit from LA implementation, stakeholders from educators to students need to be informed on the use of LA. As students are the main end users of LA, the research question addressed in this study was to explore the students’ views regarding the design and implementation of LA in health professions education. The present study was carried out to gain insights on medical, dental and pharmacy students’ readiness, acceptance and perceptions towards learning analytics.

Methods

Ethical approval

The proposal was approved by the IMU Joint Committee on Research and Ethics (IMU-JC) with the project code number (MHPE I-2022(01)). The objectives of the study were explained to the study participants. Consent was obtained from all participants, and they were reassured about data privacy and confidentiality.

Study design

The study was carried out at the International Medical University (IMU), Malaysia. The undergraduate students of the Medical, Dentistry and Pharmacy programmes were invited to participate in the study. It was made clear to them that their participation was on a voluntary basis.

A sequential mixed method research design was used in this study. The general views of the students regarding learning analytics were collected using a validated questionnaire developed by West, et al. (2020) [8], followed by in depth exploration of the participants’ views using focus group discussion.

Quantitative study

The students’ sociodemographic data and their general views on learning analytics were collected using a questionnaire which was developed and validated by West, et al. (2020) [8]. As this questionnaire was developed for undergraduate students in Australia, it was adapted to the learning context in this study. Its face validity was determined by pilot testing with 10 students from other health science programmes in the university. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made on some of the questions to improve their clarity. The questions included 1) demographic data, 2) data that students thought the institution would collect, 3) their level of comfort on collection of those data, 4) how useful they thought on the types of data and reports to their learning experience 5) how students felt about their data being used or handled in particular ways, 6) how they would like their information to be displayed, 7) frequency of notification regarding policies and practice on student data collection and use, and 8) open-ended question to seek any other comments in relation to the ethics of digital data collection and use in higher education. The questionnaire is presented in Additional File 1.

Subsequently, the questionnaire was uploaded online using Google form and the survey link was sent to Year 1 to Year 5 students in the Medicine and Dentistry programmes, and Year 1 to Year 4 students in the Pharmacy programme. They have been in the university system for at least six months or more.

Qualitative study

Students who had completed the questionnaire were invited to participate in the focus group interviews. The selection criteria were set to include diverse groups of students from all academic years of Medicine, Dentistry and Pharmacy programmes comprising different genders, local and international students. The sessions were arranged with a maximum of six students per group. Semi-structured interviews were carried out using an interview guide consisting of a set of pre-determined open-ended questions to further explore the students’ perceptions about LA in depth (Table 1).

The interview sessions were conducted in English via Microsoft-Teams by the researcher (FH). During the interview session, all the interviewees were requested to turn on their video camera. The purpose of the study was explained, and consent was obtained. Each interview session took approximately 30–90 min. The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by MS-Teams. One of the researchers (FH) went through the transcripts to check the accuracy and reached out to the interviewees for further confirmation. The interviews continued until data saturation was reached where no new information was obtained.

Data analysis

The students’ responses in the questionnaire were compiled and descriptive analysis was performed. Thematic analysis using the Braun and Clarke’s approach was applied to identify the themes from the transcribed interview data [16]. The researchers of this study have academic qualifications and research expertise in Health Professions Education with 20 years of teaching experience in health professions courses. FH, an anatomist, and VDN, a biochemist, were from the School of Medicine who also taught in the dentistry programme, while EHM, a pharmaceutical chemist, was a teaching staff in the School of Pharmacy. The impact of the methodological decision was critically considered by the researchers. The predetermined interview guide helped to ensure the data collection was not influenced by potential personal biases. Together with multiple rounds of independent coding of interview data, reflexivity in the research was ensured. FH and EHM read the interview transcripts repeatedly to familiarise with the contents and extracted the codes independently following which the emerging themes were identified. FH, EHM and VDN had regular meetings to review, triangulate the interview data and refine the identified themes. The themes were discussed, and consensus was obtained among the three researchers. The interviews were stopped when no new theme emerged.

Themes and sub-themes emerged from the interview data

Theme Self-regulated learning

Achievement of learning outcomes

The interviewees perceived that LA could help them to determine their achievement of learning outcomes and identify areas of improvement.

“Implement learning analytics into the system, I think the students will find it very enjoyable and very conducive for their learning because they would know whether they are doing things right or not.” (P7).

“If the analytic consists of our grades, the analysis tells us which part we are lacking at and which part we are better at, then I would know where I can improve on.” (P16)

Individualised learning path based on learning behaviour

The interviewees expected that they would receive suggestions and recommendations to improve learning based on their individual study behaviour, grades, strengths, and weaknesses.

“All of us have our own individual learning traits, the style that we learn, how we absorb knowledge as well… you can help by offering us personalized recommendation based on our own preferences.” (P2).

“It shows your strengths and weaknesses, which is definitely important …, for whatever they are bad in, it is an opportunity for them to learn and adapt and overcome their issue.” (P13).

Besides, LA could provide information about the estimated time spent to complete a particular task. This could help the students in developing their study plans.

“How much time a student spends on certain tasks… this can perhaps give a rough estimate to other students viewing this analytic of how much time they should be allocating for a certain task.” (P12).

Recommendation of learning resources

The interviewees also expected to get recommendations on learning resources such as practice questions, study materials and methods used by high achievers, as well as the resource persons to contact when they needed help for their study.

“We could have a recommendation of a bunch of different questions,… for a particular student after their exam for them to go back and then do those questions.” (P1)

“Seeing high achievers what they are doing and what's different and perhaps encourage the lower achieving students to take on more methods at the high achieving students are doing.” (P12).

Learning motivation

The interviewees felt that they would be motivated to learn by seeing their grades and ranking in their own cohort, prediction of grades, knowing their strengths and weaknesses, and reflecting their learning behaviour and academic performance.

“A good feature would be my standing within my own cohort. But in which percentile am I at, whether the bottom percentile, whether I'm the top percentile or in the middle because if I knew that I was at the bottom percentile at least I know that I need to work harder.” (P7).

“From this data collection and it is also personalized, those with relatively low grades students, they will be encouraged to change their behaviour and they can reflect themselves.” (P11).

On the other hand, the interviewees shared their concern that some might feel overconfident or demotivated by seeing their result trends and position in their class shown in LA.

“Their result is going up… the next time when they are doing their exam, they don't try to focus on that particular topic because they are being overconfident.” (P6)

“If you can see the others’ grade, someone feel good because they can encourage themselves. But some may feel discomfort because it may let them feel they are not satisfied or frustrated.” (P11).

Theme 2: evidence-based decision making

Improvement of teaching and learning

The interviewees opined that LA could inform the teachers on gaps in teaching, especially in teaching of procedural skills where different clinical instructors could be teaching different techniques, which could lead to confusion among the students. It could also help the teachers to improve their teaching and learning delivery and course management such as class scheduling.

“Students have learned from a separate doctor, and they might have a separate technique… everyone’s technique is … slightly different, it leads to confusion… So standardizing OSCE (clinical skill teaching) would be very beneficial to students.” (P1).

“Educator can seek for the improvement in order to help the student… come up with the solutions or to rearrange or oriented the educational way to conduct education.” (P17)

The interviewees also perceived that the analytics could help to improve the student experience by reducing content overload.

“They can try to remove content irrelevant or not so important from the syllabus, therefore, like lessening the burden of the syllabus… this can overall improve the mental health of certain students.” (P10).

Professionalism

The interviewees opined that LA could contribute to evidence-based decision making for a student’s professional behaviour which is an important outcome of health professions students. The evidence includes attendance record, feedback from the lectures or mentors and peer feedback.

“Attendance is very important, but not only for the clinical skills. … also for maybe all the lectures and also all the group discussions”. (p13)

Some highlighted that the students themselves could reflect on their professional behaviour based on the analytics dashboard data.

“You could see your attendance going down… I think that would have a psychological impact as students …that would prompt them to increase or improve their professionalism.” (P3)

The interviewees agreed that LA could help the institution to find out the factors affecting student professionalism which could subsequently inform intervention and action plans to help the students.

“Few major issues, perhaps like people come late or people tend to miss a lot of classes, a problem with attire, what we can do is we try to come up with an intervention with the program to address it. Then we compare the data from the previous cohort with the new cohort that’s undergone all this intervention.” (P2).

However, they acknowledged that evidence on some attributes of professionalism could be challenging.

“It’s very hard to kind of measure specific elements of professionalism. You can’t measure integrity or respect. There is really no way to collect data on that.” (P1)

Resource allocation

The interviewees perceived that LA could help the university to plan teaching and learning resources based on number and frequency of utilisation and time spent.

“If you could get analytics on how many students utilize a particular resource…basically help lecturers gauge to some extent how useful the resources…”(P1)

Theme 3: data management

Data presentation and dashboard

The interviewees expressed the view that the data presentation in LA is important. It should be user friendly and easy to understand.

“In terms of user interface, that the way the data is presented matters a lot …Very raw data like standard deviation, someone who is not really into statistics, may not be able to understand what is the significance of that data…translate it into something that’s more accessible (P2)”.

Data analysis

The interviewees highlighted the importance of accurate data interpretation.

“Whatever algorithm you’re using to analyse the data … you need to make sure that the data that you’re analysing and how it’s been analysed is correctly done so that the university doesn’t jump to inappropriate conclusions” (P1).

Data consent

The interviewees highlighted that consent should be taken prior to collecting the data for learning analytics. Their privacy and confidentiality should be maintained. They had concerns over commercialisation of their data to unconsented parties.

“If the data can be collected anonymously, then it should be done anonymously…something that’s personalised for the student, the student should always be able to know first-hand what data is being collected and give their consent whether or not they want their data to be collected.” (P2).

Data security

The interviewees expressed their concerns over leakage of their personal information and highlighted that a strong cyber-security system in the institution was necessary.

“The digital profile …when anyone has that much information about one person, it could be very unsafe, not just for that individual, but for the people associated with that individual as well.” (P1).

Discussion

Data is claimed to be the new oil in the twenty-first century because it provides insightful information that can help in timely identification of opportunities and potential risks for effective decision making [17]. In education, the students leave digital trails in relation to their learning. This data would be useful for identifying students’ learning needs. Although learning analytics is a new tool, this study shows that students have some understanding of its application, mainly from their previous exposure to various business intelligence tools commonly used in the retail industry.

Generally, the students were aware of and agreeable to the data gathered by the university, such as those from the learning management system (LMS) and student lifecycle information system. The survey data informed that the students perceived submission of assignment as the topmost data that the university would collect. These findings were similar to the previous study carried out in six Australian universities involving both undergraduate and postgraduate students [5]. On the other hand, data related to LMS were ranked lower in the present study compared to the study done by West et al. (2020) [8]. Learning culture and context as well as the extent of utilisation of LMS in programme delivery could have influenced the students’ decision.

As with the findings of West and colleagues (2020) [8], the present study revealed that the students were least comfortable with their data linked to the university social media group, university mobile apps usage, and location data from their mobile phone being collected. Demographic data also appeared in the lower comfortability zone. Concerns about being placed in categories, stereotyping, profiling, and segregation were likely the reasons for this [8]. It is important for institutions to ensure data transparency by clearly informing the students about how their data will be utilised in the implementation of LA [18]. They should be assured of data confidentiality and security.

The students accepted that their data which had direct association with their learning to be collected, especially those that could contribute towards improved learning experience and learning environment. They appreciated the additional recommended learning materials, services (e.g. academic writing support and library) and the tracking of their learning progression enabled by LA to support their learning. Similar findings were reported by West et al., (2020) [8]. Therefore, it can be concluded that students’ views on data collected for LA are consistent despite differences in educational context, culture and geographic locations.

While the quantitative data with health professions students was consistent with the general findings by West et al., (2020) [8], the qualitative data provided additional and new insights into the views and readiness of health professions students for LA. In the present study, the three themes emerged from the interview data were self-regulated learning, evidence-based decision making and data management. The data from the quantitative study were consistent with the themes arising from the quantitative study (Fig. 4). The students were agreeable for the relevant data to be collected for LA to promote self-regulated learning. They also expected the data to support evidence-based decision making by the education institution. Both the quantitative and qualitative studies emphasised the importance of effective data management and handling.

Demonstration of alignment of qualitative and quantitative findings

The interviewees expected that LA could help them to monitor their achievement of learning outcomes and give personalised feedback based on their performance in assessments. However, to ensure equity to all students, automated alerts from the system, additional guidance and personalised feedback should be available to all students, instead of limiting to the identified group of students [3].

The study showed that LA could help learners in all three phases of the self- regulated learning (SRL) cycle, i.e., forethought, performance, and evaluation phases [6]. The forethought phase of SRL could be facilitated by LA through reminders for deadline or automated to-do-list in accordance with the students’ daily schedule. In performance phase, LA could analyse their current level of knowledge and progress, recommend materials, and suggest learning partners. In evaluation phase, LA could provide specific feedback for further improvement [9]. Learners could further adjust their learning activities to achieve the desired outcomes based on the recommendations by LA. In the present study, the students also highlighted the usefulness of LA in their learning, as it could inform them about their ranking in the cohort and provide personalised recommendations and feedback.

The students perceived that LA could support them via resource recommendations. This was consistent with the findings in previous studies indicating that LA could recommend additional learning materials based on learning behaviours [3, 8, 9]. Intelligent tutoring systems that detect and address student knowledge gaps, together with intelligent feedback systems powered by artificial intelligence could potentially enhance the educators’ efficiency and student engagement in medical education [19]. Besides, LA could stimulate the students’ motivation by presenting data related to learners’ grades and ranking. The learner’s motivation leads to better achievement [20]. On the other hand, some students felt that they might feel pressured by continuously receiving messages from the system or from the teachers. These might interfere with their autonomous learning [3, 9]. Ten Cate et al. (2020) argued that the specialised recommendations on learning topics enabled by LA could reduce the learners’ sense of ownership over their learning, which could negatively impact their motivation and academic performance [11]. Therefore, students should be given a choice to decide whether to use LA to support their learning.

Professionalism is the core competency in health professional training. The students opined that LA could assist in tracking unprofessional behaviours such as absenteeism through attendance data, issues in time management via submission deadline data, poor teamwork, lack of self-awareness via feedback from peers and faculty, and dishonesty through data on cheating and plagiarism. When this evidence is generated in real-time by LA, interventions and corrective measures could be implemented by the education providers promptly.

In education, teaching and learning gaps exist due to diversity in the students’ educational and social backgrounds as well as their learning styles and behaviour. The findings in the current study revealed that LA could help to identify common issues related to teaching and learning strategies based on learners’ feedback. Therefore, the educators could be more specific in addressing the issues timely. The implementation of LA in education could reduce faculties’ physical and mental stress by addressing the concerns over burnout of both faculty and students [21].

Our findings showed that the students were particularly concerned about data management and data confidentiality in LA system. Similar concerns were highlighted in previous studies [3, 8, 22, 23]. They preferred to have an opt-out option for participating in LA and sharing their data with certain stakeholders. Before implementing LA, it is important to address the issue of students’ unwillingness to share their personal data, as they are critical for tracking the learning behaviours [22]. Data transparency and confidentiality need to be ensured for successful implementation of LA.

The potential danger of misuse of data from LA including selling of data and misinterpretation of data due to masking bias was highlighted by the students. Similar concerns were reported previously [24, 25]. Therefore, it is imperative that the purpose of LA should be made clear before constructing the LA system [26]. The policies and regulations, copyright and ethical concerns with regards to the use of artificial intelligence in medical education need to be addressed [27]. Educators need to be trained in data literacy including handling, analysing, and interpreting the data appropriately. Moreover, the education providers should have a clear policy and statement on data handling, data confidentiality and privacy specifically for LA.

Limitations of the study and future research recommendations

The collection of data was done only in one university among medical, dentistry and pharmacy students. This could limit the generalisability of the findings to other health professional students. Hence, the study should be extended to other institutions involving more health professions programmes in the future. Although the original plan was to conduct focus group discussion, two of the sessions were carried out as individual interview because the students were unavailable during the time of the focus group discussion. Nevertheless, it has been reported in the literature that there was no significant difference in the thematic content obtained from focus group discussion or individual interview [28]. In the current study, the information obtained from these two individual interview sessions was consistent with that from the focus group discussion. Hence, the findings were not significantly affected by this limitation. Another limitation of the study was that students who did not volunteer for the interview could have other concerns about LA which might not have been reflected in the study findings. To ensure effective and sustainable implementation of LA, the views of other stakeholders and end users of LA such as faculty, senior management members of the school and academic support staff should also be explored.

Conclusions

This study suggested that the students accepted the role of LA in supporting their learning. LA could be used as a tool to help the educators and students in the following ways: 1) Recognise the students’ learning behaviours, strength and weakness, academic performance and provide the personalised learning path; 2) Enhance students’ motivation in leaning which in turn will have positive influence on their achievement of learning outcomes and vice versa; 3) Provide the evidence-based information to the educators and institution to assist their decision making for further improvement in teaching and learning environment. It is important to maintain students’ privacy and confidentiality and enhance the university cyber security system when LA is implemented. The findings of the present study would be useful for higher education institutions in formulating guidelines related to LA development and implementation, to fulfil the intended purposes of supporting students’ learning and ensuring equity in education. In line with the trend of data-driven patient care in healthcare practice, the exposure of health professions students to LA will create awareness among them with regards to the use of artificial intelligence in healthcare and the ethical considerations including data privacy and confidentiality.
