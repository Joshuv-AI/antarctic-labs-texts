Ion‐Channel Activity of Characteristic Alkaloids From Uncaria rhynchophylla
Authors: Wen‐Yan Li, Qi Wu, Zhi‐Ping Zhou, Zai‐Feng Yuan, Qin‐Shi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEleven characteristic monoterpenoid indole alkaloids from Uncaria rhynchophylla were isolated, structurally assigned, and evaluated in parallel for inhibitory effects on human Nav1.7, Nav1.5, Cav3.1, and Kv1.5 channels using whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings. Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 showed the broadest responses, with active inhibitors displaying micromolar IC50 values against the two sodium channels. Corynanthe‐type alkaloids were the main contributors to Cav3.1 inhibition, whereas Kv1.5 was less sensitive and responded mainly to rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline. The observed channel preferences were associated with alkaloid chemotype and structural features. This comparative analysis reveals differential activity across the four ion channels examined and provides a basis for further electrophysiological and mechanistic studies.

Introduction

Uncaria rhynchophylla, a member of the Rubiaceae family and the genus Uncaria, is known as Gou‐Teng in traditional Chinese medicine and has long been used for hypertension, convulsions, and pain‐related disorders [1, 2, 3, 4]. Indole alkaloids are widely considered as the principal bioactive constituents of U. rhynchophylla [5, 6]. Representative compounds include rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline, corynoxeine, isocorynoxeine, corynoxine, corynoxine B, hirsutine, hirsuteine, geissoschizine methyl ether, corynantheine, and dihydrocorynantheine. These alkaloids are mainly classified into rhynchophylline‐type and corynanthe‐type structural classes, providing a chemically related but diverse set of natural products for biological comparison [6].

Previous studies have reported neuroprotective, vasorelaxant, anti‐inflammatory, autophagy‐regulating, sensory‐neuron‐related, and cardiovascular effects for individual U. rhynchophylla alkaloids [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Some reports also suggest that these alkaloids can modulate ion currents. For example, geissoschizine methyl ether has been reported to inhibit neuronal Na+, Ca2+, and delayed rectifier K+ currents, and to reduce spontaneous neuronal firing [12]. Rhynchophylline has also been reported to modify voltage‐gated K+ currents, including Kv1.2 currents, and broader ion‐channel actions of rhynchophylline have been reviewed [13, 14]. More recently, rhynchines A‐E from U. rhynchophylla were reported to inhibit Cav3.1, and spirophylline C showed moderate inhibitory activity against Kv1.5 [15, 16]. However, most available studies have focused on single compounds or crude preparations under different experimental conditions, limiting direct cross‐class comparisons among these alkaloids [17].

Voltage‐gated ion channels offer a focused target panel for such a comparison. Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 are both sodium channels, but they are linked to different physiological settings. Nav1.7 is mainly associated with peripheral sensory neuron excitability and pain signaling [18]. Nav1.5 is the main cardiac sodium channel and is essential for normal cardiac conduction [19]. Beyond sodium channels, Cav3.1 is a major T‐type calcium channel subtype. It is involved in neuronal excitability and seizure‐related firing patterns [20, 21]. Kv1.5, encoded by KCNA5, contributes to atrial electrophysiology and regulation of vascular smooth muscle membrane potential [22]. Based on these roles, Nav1.7, Nav1.5, Cav3.1, and Kv1.5 were selected as a compact screening panel covering sensory, neuronal, cardiac, and vascular excitability. This scope is relevant to the reported biological effects of U. rhynchophylla and allows a chemically grounded comparison of its characteristic alkaloids. The selected panel was intended for comparative profiling and was not designed to define the complete ion‐channel selectivity of these alkaloids.

However, direct comparative data for these characteristic U. rhynchophylla alkaloids across human Nav1.7, Nav1.5, Cav3.1, and Kv1.5 under a unified electrophysiological platform remain limited. In this study, eleven characteristic alkaloids from U. rhynchophylla were isolated and evaluated against these four channels using the same whole‐cell patch‐clamp platform under standardized channel‐specific recording conditions. Compounds showing clear inhibition in the 50 µM screen were further examined in concentration‐response assays to generate a comparative ion‐channel activity profile. This allowed us to examine whether different alkaloid chemotypes showed distinct channel preferences.

Results and Discussion

Eleven known monoterpenoid indole alkaloids were isolated from U. rhynchophylla: rhynchophylline [23], isorhynchophylline [23], corynoxeine [23], isocorynoxeine [23], corynoxine [24], corynoxine B [24], hirsutine [25], hirsuteine [25], corynantheine [26], dihydrocorynantheine [26], and geissoschizine methyl ether [27]. Their structures were assigned by NMR analysis and comparison with reported data (Figures S1–S22). These compounds represent characteristic alkaloids of U. rhynchophylla and include two main structural groups: rhynchophylline‐type spiro‐oxindole alkaloids and corynanthe‐type indole alkaloids (Figure 1).

Chemical structures of the eleven alkaloids isolated from Uncaria rhynchophylla.

All eleven compounds were first tested at 50 µM against Cav3.1, Nav1.5, Nav1.7, and Kv1.5. The heatmap showed that inhibition was not evenly distributed across the four channels (Figure 2 and Table 1). Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 responded to the largest number of compounds, whereas Cav3.1 was inhibited mainly by corynanthe‐type alkaloids and Kv1.5 was less sensitive overall. Hirsutine, hirsuteine, corynantheine, dihydrocorynantheine, and geissoschizine methyl ether showed the strongest and most consistent inhibition of Cav3.1, Nav1.5, and Nav1.7 currents. By contrast, corynoxeine, isocorynoxeine, and corynoxine showed weaker effects on the tested channels (Figure 2 and Table 1). These results indicate that the alkaloids do not behave as a uniform chemical class and that activity varies across compounds and channel subtype. Compounds that inhibited the current by more than 50% at 50 µM were selected for concentration‐response analysis. This parallel screening design allowed the activity differences among structurally related alkaloids to be compared directly across the four excitability‐related ion channels.

The two sodium channels displayed closely related inhibitory profiles. Six compounds inhibited both Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 in a concentration‐dependent manner: hirsutine, hirsuteine, geissoschizine methyl ether, corynantheine, dihydrocorynantheine, and isorhynchophylline (Figures 3A and 4A). For Nav1.7, the IC50 values were 8.1, 7.9, 6.8, 10.4, 10.6, and 18.7 µM, respectively (Figure 3B and Table 2). The corresponding IC50 values against Nav1.5 were 13.6, 8.4, 5.8, 13.2, 14.5, and 18.2 µM, respectively (Figure 4B and Table 2). Among all tested compounds, geissoschizine methyl ether showed the strongest inhibition of both sodium channels. N‐Methyl‐6‐amino‐5‐(2,3,5‐trichlorophenyl) pyridine‐2‐carboxamide (PF‐01247324) was used as a sodium channel reference compound. Although PF‐01247324 is primarily described as a Nav1.8‐selective blocker, the same study also reported micromolar inhibition of recombinant Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 [28]. In our assay, it inhibited Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 with IC50 values of 15.6 and 11.5 µM, respectively (Figures 3C and 4C; Table 2).

Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 represent sodium‐channel subtypes with different physiological distributions and functions [18, 19]. In our study, the same alkaloids inhibited both recombinant subtypes with comparable micromolar potencies. Thus, these data show overlapping activity at the two sodium‐channel subtypes examined and do not support selectivity between them. The scope of this comparison is limited to these two recombinant channels, while activity across the broader sodium‐channel family and in native cells remains to be determined. This comparative profile may guide the selection of compounds and experimental systems for further electrophysiological studies.

A subset of the tested alkaloids markedly inhibited Cav3.1. At 50 µM, corynanthe‐type compounds strongly suppressed Cav3.1 currents (Figure 5A). Five active corynanthe‐type alkaloids were selected for concentration‐response analysis: hirsutine, hirsuteine, geissoschizine methyl ether, corynantheine, and dihydrocorynantheine. Their IC50 values were 10.1, 7.7, 7.7, 24.0, and 24.3 µM, respectively (Figure 5B and Table 2). Mibefradil, the reference inhibitor, gave an IC50 of 2.4 µM against Cav3.1 (Figure 5C and Table 2). This pattern differed from what we observed for Nav1.7 and Nav1.5. Cav3.1 showed a clearer separation between the two structural classes. The rhynchophylline‐type compounds were weak on this channel, while the corynanthe‐type compounds gave measurable IC50 values.

Rhynchines A–E from U. rhynchophylla have previously been reported to inhibit Cav3.1 [15]. The present results extend this observation by comparing several characteristic corynanthe‐type alkaloids under the same assay conditions. Cav3.1 mediates low‐threshold calcium currents and contributes to neuronal firing [20]. In this study, several corynanthe‐type alkaloids inhibited recombinant Cav3.1 currents in a heterologous expression system. This identifies a calcium‐channel component in the comparative activity profile, but does not establish effects on Cav3.1‐dependent excitability in native neurons. Experiments in native‐cell preparations would be required to examine that possibility.

For Kv1.5, most alkaloids produced only weak to moderate inhibition at 50 µM. Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline were the only compounds selected for concentration‐response analysis (Figure 6A), with IC50 values of 30.4 and 33.1 µM, respectively (Figure 6B and Table 2). (2‐isopropyl‐5‐methylcyclohexyl)diphenylphosphine oxide (DPO‐1), used as a Kv1.5 reference inhibitor [16], inhibited Kv1.5 with an IC50 of 0.2 µM (Figure 6C and Table 2). Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline showed the most evident Kv1.5 inhibition among the tested alkaloids. This pattern differed from that of the sodium and calcium channels, in which corynanthe‐type compounds were the main active group. Kv1.5 showed a weaker overall response than Nav1.7, Nav1.5, and Cav3.1 within the selected panel. Thus, the tested alkaloids did not inhibit all four channels to the same extent in this study. However, activity at other ion‐channel subtypes cannot be excluded. Spirophylline C, a structurally distinct spirooxindole alkaloid from U. rhynchophylla, was previously reported to inhibit Kv1.5 with an IC50 value of 9.1 µM [16]. Rhynchophylline has also been reported to affect other potassium channels, including Kv1.2 [13, 14]. These findings indicate that potassium‐channel activity may extend beyond the compounds and channel subtypes examined here. Broader profiling would therefore be required to define the overall ion‐channel activity profiles of these alkaloids.

The activity distribution revealed several structure‐related trends within the present compound set. For Nav1.7, Nav1.5, and Cav3.1, the active compounds were mainly corynanthe‐type indole alkaloids, including hirsutine, hirsuteine, geissoschizine methyl ether, corynantheine, and dihydrocorynantheine. By contrast, most rhynchophylline‐type spiro‐oxindole alkaloids showed weaker effects on these channels under the same conditions. This distribution identifies the non‐spiro corynanthe‐type scaffold as a feature associated with stronger activity within the present compound set, but does not establish it as a determinant of channel inhibition.

Comparisons between structurally related compounds revealed several preliminary activity trends. Hirsuteine gave numerically lower IC50 values than hirsutine against Nav1.5 and Cav3.1. The difference in their side‐chain region may therefore be considered in future analogue testing, but it cannot be assigned as the cause of the potency difference from this pair alone. Corynantheine and dihydrocorynantheine showed similar potencies, indicating that the change in saturation was not accompanied by a marked change in activity within this pair. Geissoschizine methyl ether gave the lowest IC50 values among the sodium‐channel inhibitors tested, although this activity cannot be attributed to a single structural feature. Isorhynchophylline inhibited Nav1.7 and Nav1.5 more strongly than rhynchophylline, identifying the stereochemical difference between these spiro‐oxindoles as another feature for future testing.

The compound series was small, most comparisons did not involve fully matched pairs, and the binding sites were not investigated. These observations should therefore be interpreted as preliminary structure‐related activity trends rather than evidence for specific molecular determinants or binding interactions.

The traditional uses of U. rhynchophylla provide a pharmacological context for investigating the ion‐channel activities of its structurally diverse alkaloids. In the present heterologous expression assays, the tested alkaloids displayed compound‐ and channel‐dependent inhibitory activity across recombinant Nav1.7, Nav1.5, Cav3.1, and Kv1.5. These findings neither establish that the four channels mediate the reported pharmacological effects of the plant nor directly validate its traditional uses. Nevertheless, the activity patterns identified here provide testable leads for further investigation in native cells and disease‐relevant models.

Conclusions

In this study, eleven characteristic alkaloids from U. rhynchophylla were examined on Cav3.1, Nav1.5, Nav1.7, and Kv1.5 using the same patch‐clamp platform. The activity was uneven across compounds and channels. The two sodium channels, Nav1.7 and Nav1.5, showed the broadest responses to the tested compounds, while corynanthe‐type alkaloids were the main contributors to Nav1.7, Nav1.5, and Cav3.1 inhibition. Kv1.5 was less sensitive and was inhibited mainly by rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline at higher concentrations. By comparing these structurally related alkaloids on the same patch‐clamp platform, this study establishes a comparative activity profile of characteristic U. rhynchophylla alkaloids within the selected ion‐channel panel. The results show that the activity is not dominated by a single marker compound, but varies with both channel subtype and alkaloid chemotype. This structure‐related profile helps connect the chemical diversity of U. rhynchophylla with its ion‐channel activities. It may also guide further electrophysiological and mechanistic studies of its characteristic alkaloids.

Experimental Section

General

NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AV 400 or 600 MHz spectrometers. MPLC was carried out by a CE C18 packing (Huapu Technology Co. Ltd., China) using a gradient MeOH─H2O solution. Column chromatography (CC) was performed using silica gel (100–200 or 200–300 mesh, Linyi Haixiang Chemical Co. Ltd., Linyi, China). Fractions were monitored by TLC (GF254, Linyi Haixiang Chemical Co. Ltd., Linyi, China), and spots were dyed by bismuth potassium iodide. All solvents were distilled prior to use.

Plant Material and Authentication

The hooks of U. rhynchophylla were collected from Pu‘er City, Yunnan Province, People's Republic of China in October, 2019. The material was identified by Prof. Xiao Cheng from Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The voucher specimen (No. 20191016) was deposited at the State Key Laboratory of Phytochemistry and Natural Medicines, Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Extraction and Isolation

The dried hooks of U. rhynchophylla (100.0 kg) were powdered and extracted three times with 95% EtOH at room temperature (150 L × 3). After filtration and evaporation of the solvent under reduced pressure, the resulting residue was suspended in water and acidified to pH 3 with 0.1% aqueous H2SO4, followed by partitioning three times with EtOAc. The remaining aqueous phase was then basified to pH 10 with 0.1% aqueous NaOH. The aqueous alkaloids were extracted with CHCl3 to give crude total alkaloids (80.0 g). The total alkaloid was subjected to silica gel CC to yield 3 fractions Fr.1–Fr.3 (CH2Cl2/acetone, from 100:1 to 0:10, v/v). Fr.1 (13.0 g) was separated by MPLC (C18 CE, 50%–70% MeOH−1‰ NH3─H2O, v/v) to afford several subfractions, Fr.1.1–Fr.1.6. Fr.1.3 (9.0 g) was a mixture of rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline, corynoxeine, and isocorynoxeine. The subfraction Fr.1.3 was left to stand in MeOH at room temperature overnight, giving needle‐like crystals of rhynchophylline (2.0 g). The remaining mother liquor fraction was chromatographed on silica gel (petroleum ether/ EtOAc, 4:1 to 1:1, v/v) to give isorhynchophylline (3.0 g), corynoxeine (1.5 g), and isocorynoxeine (2.0 g). Fr.1.4 (4.0 g) was separated on silica gel with a gradient of petroleum ether/ EtOAc (4:1 to 1:1, v/v) to obtain corynoxine (15.0 mg), corynoxine B (20.0 mg), and geissoschizine methyl ether (3.0 g). Fr.1.5 (518.0 mg) was obtained as pure corynantheine. Fr.1.6 (684.0 mg) was subjected to silica gel (petroleum ether / EtOAc, 10:1, v/v) to afford dihydrocorynantheine (500.0 mg). Fr.2 (9.5 g) was mainly a mixture of hirsutine and hirsuteine. Hirsutine (5.0 g) was obtained largely by the formation of granular crystals in MeOH. Hirsuteine (4.0 g) was purified from the remaining fraction by silica gel chromatography (CH2Cl2/acetone, 4:1, v/v).

Cell Culture and Transient Expression

Human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK293T; purchased from ATCC; RRID: CVCL_0063) were grown in DMEM (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) plus 10% fetal bovine serum (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) and penicillin (100 U/mL) / streptomycin (0.1 mg/mL) (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells between passages 3 and 10 were used for experiments. HEK293T cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with pcDNA3.1 constructs encoding human Cav3.1 (CACNA1G, NM_018896), Nav1.5 (SCN5A, NM_198056), Nav1.7 (SCN9A, NM_002977), or Kv1.5 (KCNA5, NM_002234), together with pcDNA3.1‐eGFP, and used within 48 h after transfection.

Electrophysiology

All recordings were performed at room temperature (25°C). Current signals were amplified with an IPA‐2 amplifier (Sutter Instrument), low‐pass filtered at 2 kHz, and sampled at 10 kHz. Test compounds were prepared as DMSO stock solutions and diluted into the external solution before use. The final DMSO concentration was kept constant in compound and vehicle controls (0.1%). Currents were recorded after stabilization to minimize rundown effects, and n represents the number of independent cells.

For Cav3.1 recording, cell membrane potential was held at −100 mV. The peak currents of Cav3.1 were elicited by 150 ms depolarization from a holding potential of −100 to −40 mV at 4 s intervals. Borosilicate glass micropipettes were pulled to produce a resistance of 2‐6 MΩ (P‐1000, Sutter Instrument) and filled with intracellular recording solution containing 130 mM CsCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA, 5 mM Na‐ATP, and 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with CsOH. The extracellular recording solution was composed of 145 mM CsCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with CsOH [15].

For Nav1.5 and Nav1.7 recordings, the internal solution contained 135 mM K‐gluconate, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM Mg‐ATP, 0.5 mM Na2GTP, 5 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, and 0.5 mM CaCl2, adjusted to pH 7.4 with KOH. The bath solution contained 140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, and 10 mM glucose, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH [29]. Peak currents of Nav1.5 and Nav1.7 were evoked by 80 ms depolarizations to −20 mV at 5 s intervals from a holding potential of −110 mV.

For Kv1.5 recording, cell membrane potential was held at −80 mV. The peak currents of Kv1.5 were elicited by 1.25 s depolarization from a holding potential of −80 to +50 mV at 7 s intervals. The voltage‐step recording was elicited from −80 to +60 mV in 5 s depolarizations at 7 s intervals. Borosilicate glass micropipettes were filled with intracellular recording solution containing 130 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EGTA, 5 mM Na‐ATP, 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with KOH. The extracellular recording solution was composed of 140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH [16].

Data Analysis and Statistics

Data fitting and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Concentration‐response data were fitted with a Hill equation: Y = Bottom + (Top − Bottom)/(1 + 10^((LogIC50 − X) × HillSlope)), where IC50 is the concentration producing half‐maximal inhibition, X is the log concentration of the compound, Bottom is the minimum inhibition ratio, Top is the maximum inhibition ratio, and HillSlope is the Hill coefficient. At each concentration, inhibition measurements from at least three independent cells are presented as mean ± SD. Concentration–response datasets were fitted by nonlinear regression using the Hill equation in GraphPad Prism 8.0. IC50 values are reported as best‐fit estimates with 95% confidence intervals. No inferential comparisons between treatment groups were performed. Therefore, P values, post‐hoc tests, multiple‐comparison adjustments, and significance symbols are not applicable. No formal outlier exclusion was applied. Percentage inhibition was calculated relative to the corresponding vehicle control recorded under the same experimental conditions. Vehicle solution containing 0.1% DMSO without test compound served as the negative control.
