Cardiac-specific mortality prediction in adolescents and young adults with classical hodgkin lymphoma: a nomogram development and validation
Authors: Rui Yan, Jiaying Niu, Weijia Zhang, Yufeng Zhang, Jinglian Li, Sijin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs survival for classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) improves, cardiac disease is a leading cause of non-relapse mortality, yet the risk of cardiac-specific mortality (CSM) in adolescents and young adults (AYAs) is unclear. This study developed and validated a nomogram to predict CSM for AYAs with cHL using data from the SEER database, with a training cohort of 7,475 patients for model development and a validation cohort of 3,205 patients for internal validation. A Fine-Gray competing risk model was used, with the cumulative incidence of CSM surpassing that of cHL over time. Analysis identified Black race, male sex, older age, earlier diagnosis year, and radiotherapy as significant risk factors for CSM. The resulting nomogram estimates the 15- and 25-year risks of CSM using age, race, sex, diagnosis year, Ann Arbor stage, and radiotherapy. Validation demonstrated strong discriminatory ability via ROC curves, with calibration plots and decision curve analysis confirming its accuracy and clinical utility. This nomogram is a useful tool for predicting CSM in the AYA population and may aid physicians in managing clinical risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51913-0.Keywords: Classical Hodgkin Lymphoma, Cardiac-specific mortality, Competing risk analysis, Nomogram, SEER databaseSubject terms: Cancer, Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) is one of the most common malignant tumors among adolescents and young adults (AYAs) aged 15 to 39 years, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 4,200 cases in this age group1. Advances in multimodal treatment strategies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, have significantly improved long-term outcomes, with 5-year overall survival rates now exceeding 90%2. However, these therapeutic successes have been accompanied by an increased risk of long-term treatment-related complications, particularly cardiovascular diseases (CVDs)3. Among these, cardiac-specific mortality (CSM) has become a leading cause of non-relapse mortality in cHL survivors, often occurring decades after initial treatment4,5.

The risk of CSM in AYA survivors of cHL is shaped by a complex interaction of multiple factors, including exposure to cardiotoxic therapies such as anthracycline-based chemotherapy and mediastinal radiotherapy, cumulative treatment doses, and individual characteristics such as age at diagnosis, pre-existing comorbidities, and lifestyle factors6,7. Previous studies have frequently used traditional survival models, such as Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models, to examine the risk of cardiac mortality8,9. In contrast, our study applied competing risk models, including the proportional subdistribution hazard model (commonly known as the Fine-Gray model). These models are specifically designed to assess outcomes in the presence of competing events, allowing for a more accurate estimation of cause-specific mortality10. For AYA cHL survivors, the Fine-Gray model provides a more reliable evaluation of long-term cardiac risk by accounting for deaths from other causes.

Nomograms have become valuable and easy-to-use tools for individualized risk prediction in oncology. By combining multiple prognostic factors into a single, visually accessible model, nomograms enable clinicians to estimate the probability of specific outcomes with improved accuracy11. This study aims to develop and validate a competing risk nomogram to predict CSM in AYAs with cHL. Using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-8 cancer registries and applying rigorous statistical methods, this study seeks to provide a reliable and clinically relevant tool to address the unique needs of this population.

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, available at https://seer.cancer.gov/. The SEER database, maintained by the National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) Surveillance Research Program (SRP), covers nearly 30% of the U.S. population. It offers high-quality demographic and cancer-specific data, with reduced surveillance bias due to standardized, systematic, and periodic data collection procedures12. No ethical approval was required for the use of this publicly available dataset.

Study population

Using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.3), AYAs diagnosed with cHL between January 1, 1975, and December 31, 2020, were identified from the SEER-8 cancer registries. AYAs were defined as individuals aged 15 to 39 years, based on the NCI’s classification of “adolescent and young adult”. Histology codes 9650–9655, 9661–9665, and 9667 from the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD-O-3), were used. Inclusion criteria were: (1) single primary malignancy; (2) histologically confirmed diagnosis; and (3) age at diagnosis between 15 and 39 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) cases identified only through autopsy or death certificate; (2) unknown race; (3) unknown marital status; and (4) unknown cause of death. The screening process is illustrated in Figure S1.

Variables and definitions of CSM

The study variables obtained from the SEER database included demographic characteristics (“age at diagnosis,” “race,” “sex,” “marital status at diagnosis,” and “year of diagnosis”), clinicopathologic features (“Ann Arbor stage,” “tumor site,” and “histologic subtype”), treatment details (“radiotherapy” and “chemotherapy”), and survival information (“survival months,” “survival outcomes,” and “causes of death”). The primary outcome of this study was cardiac-specific mortality (CSM), defined as death due to heart disease, classified under SEER recode 50,060. The corresponding ICD-10 codes are listed in Table S1. In this study, competing events included deaths from cHL and other non-cardiac causes. Follow-up time was measured from the date of cHL diagnosis to the date of death or last follow-up (December 31, 2020).

Statistical analysis

After data cleaning, patients were randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort in a 7:3 ratio. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared between subgroups using the Chi-square test. To estimate the 15- and 25-year event probabilities, cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) were calculated. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on patient characteristics, and corresponding CIF curves were generated. Gray’s test was used to compare differences in CIFs between groups. Univariable Fine-Gray regression was performed for each candidate variable to describe its crude association with CSM and to facilitate comparison with prior studies; the results are provided in Supplementary Table S2. However, variable selection for the multivariable model was not based on univariable p-values. Instead, all ten candidate variables were preselected based on clinical knowledge and previous literature, and entered simultaneously into the multivariate Fine-Gray model. Multivariate competing risk survival analyses were performed to the training cohort to identify independent predictors of CSM. A forest plot was then generated to display the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each risk factor. Variables that were statistically significant or clinically relevant were included in the final predictive model. A competing risk nomogram was constructed based on these results to estimate the 15- and 25-year probabilities of CSM in AYA patients with cHL.

The performance of the nomogram was evaluated in both the training and validation cohorts. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves at 15 and 25 years were used to assess the model’s ability to distinguish outcomes. An area under the curve (AUC) value between 0.5 and 1 reflects discrimination ranging from none to perfect. Calibration plots were used to compare predicted probabilities with observed outcomes. A well-calibrated model yields a curve that closely follows the 45-degree diagonal, indicating high accuracy. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the nomogram’s potential clinical value. A model is considered clinically useful if its application results in a higher net benefit.

This study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI statement checklist. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; https://www.r-project.org/). The packages tidycmprsk, QHScrnomo, and riskRegression were used for model development and nomogram construction. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 10,680 eligible patients, 7,475 were randomly assigned to the training cohort and 3,205 to the validation cohort. In the overall cohort of 10,680 patients, the median age at diagnosis was 26.52 ± 6.45 years. Most patients were male (53.4%), White (85.7%), and unmarried (62.8%). Only 166 patients (1.6%) had extranodal disease. Nodular sclerosis was the most common histologic subtype, observed in 72.8% of cases. The majority of patients were diagnosed at Ann Arbor stage II (36.0%). Regarding the year of diagnosis, 29.3% were diagnosed from 1975 to 1989, 36.4% from 1990 to 2004, and 34.3% from 2005 to 2020. In terms of treatment, 7,885 patients (73.8%) received chemotherapy, and 5,159 (48.3%) received radiotherapy. By the end of follow-up, there were 2,616 recorded deaths, including 1,104 (42.2%) from cHL and 461 (17.6%) from cardiac-related causes. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Cumulative incidence analysis

In the overall cohort of 10,680 patients, the cumulative incidence analysis showed that the CIF for CSM was 1.6% at 15 years and increased to 4.5% at 25 years. The CIF for cHL-related death remained at 11% at both 15 and 25 years, while the CIF for death due to other causes was 6.3% at 15 years and 10% at 25 years (Table 2). Figure 1 presents the CIF curves for all-cause mortality in the overall cohort of 10,680 patients. Over time, CSM steadily increased and eventually surpassed mortality due to cHL. In subgroup analyses stratified by patient characteristics, the highest CIF for CSM was observed among married Black men aged 35–39 years who were diagnosed with cHL of unknown stage. When stratified by year of diagnosis, patients diagnosed between 1975 and 1989 had the highest cumulative incidence of CSM, likely due to the longest follow-up. No significant differences were observed when stratifying by race, tumor site, or histologic subtype. Finally, patients who received chemotherapy or did not receive radiotherapy had relatively lower risks of CSM (Fig. 2).

Predictors of death from cardiac disease

According to the multivariate analysis using the Fine-Gray model in the training cohort of 7,475 patients, age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, and radiotherapy were significantly associated with a higher risk of cardiac-specific mortality (CSM) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). Specifically, compared to White patients, no significant differences were observed among other racial groups, except for Black patients, who showed a markedly higher risk (hazard ratio [HR], 1.70; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.12–2.56; P = 0.012). The risk of CSM increased progressively with age. Compared to patients aged 15–19 years, those aged 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 had HRs of 1.50 (95% CI, 1.00–2.25; P = 0.047), 2.04 (95% CI, 1.33–3.12; P = 0.001), and 3.15 (95% CI, 2.04–4.85; P < 0.001), respectively. Male sex was independently associated with a higher risk of CSM (HR, 1.80; 95% CI, 1.42–2.29; P < 0.001). The risk of CSM declined with more recent years of diagnosis (1990–2004: HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.31–0.59; P < 0.001; 2005–2020: HR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.06–0.37; P < 0.001). Patients who did not receive radiotherapy had a 52% lower risk of CSM compared to those who received radiotherapy (HR, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.36–0.64; P < 0.001). Although Ann Arbor stage did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05), it was retained in the model based on clinical relevance.

Development and validation of the nomogram

A nomogram was developed using the Fine-Gray model in the training cohort of 7,475 patients, incorporating six identified risk factors to estimate the 15- and 25-year probabilities of CSM in patients with cHL. As illustrated in Fig. 4, a straight line is drawn from each risk factor to the top axis to assign point values. The points are then summed to generate a total score. The estimated CSM probability is determined by drawing a line from the total score to the time scale at the bottom, based on individual patient characteristics.

Competing risk nomogram for predicting the 15- and 25- year cumulative incidence of CSM in cHL (N = 7,475). Constructed based on the Fine-Gray model.

Internal validation was conducted using the validation cohort of 3,205 patients to assess the model’s predictive performance. ROC curves and time-dependent AUC values for 15- and 25-year predictions are shown in Figs. 5 and 6. In the training cohort of 7,475 patients, the AUCs for 15- and 25-year CSM predictions were 0.690 (95% CI, 0.632–0.748) and 0.674 (95% CI, 0.634–0.714), respectively. In the validation cohort of 3,205 patients, the corresponding AUCs were 0.774 (95% CI, 0.717–0.831) and 0.686 (95% CI, 0.631–0.741). Calibration plots for both cohorts (Fig. 7) showed that the predicted CSM closely matched the observed outcomes along the 45-degree diagonal, indicating good calibration.

Time-dependent AUC curves for the nomogram predicting CSM in the training cohort (A, N = 7,475) and the validation cohort (B, N = 3,205). The x-axis “Time” indicates months after diagnosis. Time-dependent AUC values calculated with the IPCW method.

Figure 8 presents DCA for the training cohort and the validation cohort. The x-axis represents the threshold probability (the minimum predicted risk at which a clinician would consider intensified cardiac surveillance), and the y-axis shows the net benefit (true positives minus weighted false positives). For both the 15- and 25-year prediction models, the nomogram curve lies above the “treat-all” and “treat-none” curves across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities, indicating that the nomogram provides a higher net benefit than either default strategy. The low absolute net benefit values reflect the low cumulative incidence of CSM. Thus, the DCA curves support the clinical utility of the nomogram for risk stratification and reducing unnecessary monitoring.

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study, CSM was comprehensively evaluated using data from 10,680 patients in the SEER database. Age, sex, race, year of diagnosis, Ann Arbor stage, and radiotherapy were identified as independent risk factors and were used to develop and validate a nomogram. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to construct a nomogram using a competing risk model to estimate long-term CSM risk in AYA patients with cHL.

The risk of CSM is higher in cancer patients treated at younger ages. Among long-term Hodgkin lymphoma survivors, the incidence of chronic heart failure is ten times higher in those treated before age 40, especially after two decades of follow-up13. Consistent with our findings, most cardiac events occurred within the first 25 years after treatment. A British study of Hodgkin lymphoma patients reported that the risk of death from myocardial infarction was twice as high as that in the general population within the first 14 years after treatment. This risk increased to four times higher between 15 and 19 years and remained elevated 20 to 24 years post-treatment14. Therefore, younger cHL patients should be monitored for at least 20 years following treatment, with extended follow-up for up to 30 years.

As shown in the nomogram, AYA males with cHL had a higher risk of CSM than females. This sex-based difference may be related to hormonal factors that influence vulnerability to heart damage15. Another possible explanation is that males are more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, which are known risk factors for heart disease16. Several studies on survivors of Hodgkin lymphoma have also identified male sex as an independent risk factor for coronary artery disease and heart attack17,18. This study also found that Black patients had a significantly higher risk of CSM than patients from other racial groups. This finding is consistent with results from Kahn JM et al., who reported that Black children and adolescents with cHL had higher mortality and poorer survival compared to White patients19. Although cancer treatment protocols vary across ethnic groups in the United States, these differences alone do not fully explain disparities in non-cancer-related mortality20. Further studies are needed to understand the causes of these disparities and to develop targeted strategies to reduce the risk of CSM in this vulnerable population.

After analyzing CSM across different time periods, we found that the rate has steadily decreased since 1975. In recent years, progress in imaging methods, such as cardiac MRI and PET/CT, together with the broader use of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, has enabled earlier detection and treatment of heart disease21. In addition, research has shown that dexrazoxane may help prevent chemotherapy-related heart damage in both humans and animals22.

We also examined the association between Ann Arbor stage at diagnosis and the risk of CSM in patients with cHL, and no statistically significant difference was observed. However, previous studies have reported a strong correlation between Ann Arbor stage and the incidence of cardiac disease in cHL patients18,23–25, supporting the inclusion of this variable in the nomogram. Interestingly, unlike earlier studies, chemotherapy was not identified as an independent predictor of CSM in our analysis. In subgroup analysis, patients who received chemotherapy had a lower cumulative incidence function (CIF) for CSM than those who did not. Although this finding appears to contradict the known cardiotoxicity of chemotherapy, it aligns with research on other cancers, such as colorectal and bladder cancer26,27. One possible explanation is that patients undergoing chemotherapy may have had shorter survival, resulting in higher mortality from cHL before the development of cardiac complications. However, our study minimized such bias by using a competing risk analysis. Another explanation may be that patients receiving chemotherapy benefit from more frequent health monitoring, allowing for earlier detection and management of cardiac issues. Nonetheless, the SEER database lacks detailed information on chemotherapy regimens, underscoring the need for further investigation into how treatment strategies influence the risk of CSM.

Over recent decades, both the radiation dose and the volume of the heart exposed to radiation have significantly decreased. The transition from high-dose extended-field radiotherapy (EFRT) to involved-field radiotherapy (IFRT), and more recently to involved nodal radiotherapy—which targets only affected nodal areas post-chemotherapy—has reduced cardiac radiation exposure by up to 50%28. Given the radiosensitivity of cHL, some patients still require radiotherapy for disease control. While radiotherapy improves survival, its long-term cardiac effects must be considered. Our multivariate competing risk analysis revealed that patients receiving radiotherapy had a higher risk of CSM. This is consistent with earlier studies showing that cHL survivors treated with radiotherapy had a 3- to 5-fold increased risk of developing cardiovascular disease, often manifesting two decades after treatment29. Long-term cardiac toxicity from radiotherapy can present as arrhythmias, valvular disease, coronary artery disease, chronic heart failure, and myocardial fibrosis30,31. Imaging studies have demonstrated that radiotherapy may lead to endothelial dysfunction, microvascular damage, and diffuse myocardial fibrosis32. Although current approaches aim to protect cardiac structures in mediastinal Hodgkin lymphoma, some heart valves and proximal coronary arteries remain vulnerable to radiation exposure. Therefore, radiotherapy may adversely affect cardiac health, and its long-term impact should be addressed in the follow-up care of AYA cHL survivors.

Study limitations

Although this study used a large cohort with validated cancer, treatment, and mortality data, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the SEER database does not include information on comorbidities (e.g., hyperlipidemia, diabetes, obesity, and cardiac history), lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol use), family medical history, psychological conditions (e.g., anxiety and depression), or specific details of radiation and chemotherapy (e.g., types, doses, and duration), all of which may act as confounding variables. Second, because most participants were White, further studies are needed to determine whether these findings apply to other racial and ethnic groups. Third, the predicted risks generated by the nomogram should be interpreted as estimates rather than absolute values. Although the model showed good performance, external validation in real-world settings is necessary to confirm its accuracy and clinical relevance.

Conclusion

We developed and internally validated a six-factor nomogram to estimate the 15- and 25-year individualized risk of CSM in AYA patients with cHL. This tool supports early identification of high-risk individuals and may guide decision-making regarding cardiac monitoring and preventive strategies.
