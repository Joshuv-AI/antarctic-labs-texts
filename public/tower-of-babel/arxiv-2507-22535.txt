Scalable and (quantum-accessible) adaptive pseudorandom quantum states and pseudorandom function-like quantum state generators
Rishabh Batra, Zhili Chen, Rahul Jain, YaoNan Zhang

Abstract
Pseudorandom quantum states (PRS) and pseudorandom function-like quantum state (PRFS) generators are quantum analogues of pseudorandom generators and pseudorandom functions. It is known that PRS (and PRFS) can exist even if BQP = QMA (relative to a quantum oracle) [ Kre21 ] or if P = NP (relative to a classical oracle) [ KQST23 ] , which does not allow for the existence of one-way functions (relative to these oracles). Hence, these are potentially weaker objects than quantum-secure one-way functions, which can be used to do quantum cryptography. A desirable property of PRS and PRFS constructions is scalability, which ensures that the security parameter λ \lambda (which determines indistinguishability from their Haar-random counterparts) is much larger than n n (the number of qubits of the output states). This may be important in some applications where PRS and PRFS primitives are used. We present an isometric procedure to prepare quantum states that can be arbitrarily random (i.e., the trace distance from the Haar-random state can be arbitrarily small for the true random case, or the distinguishing advantage can be arbitrarily small for the pseudorandom case). Our procedure provides a new method for scalable PRS that introduces no entanglement or correlations with the environment. This naturally gives the first construction for scalable and (quantum-accessible) adaptive PRFS assuming quantum-secure one-way functions. Our PRFS construction implies various primitives, including long-input PRFS, short-input PRFS, short-output PRFS, non-adaptive PRFS, and classical-accessible adaptive PRFS [ AQY22 , AGQY22 ] . This new construction may be helpful in some simplification of the microcrypt zoo 1 1 1 https://sattath.github.io/microcrypt-zoo/ . .

Introduction

Randomness has played an indispensable role in various fields of computer science, such as cryptography, algorithms, complexity theory, and so on.
For practical applications, however, using true randomness may be very expensive and difficult to produce. Storing the complete description of a truly random object may be very inefficient. For example, to merely specify a binary truly random function on
n
-bit inputs, we require an exponentially large description.

An object is called pseudorandom if we can generate it efficiently, but an efficient distinguisher cannot distinguish it from a truly random object. Using pseudorandomness enables us to design efficient protocols that require only short random seeds. In classical cryptography, pseudorandomness has been fundamental for the construction of efficient protocols that are secure against attacks by efficient adversaries.

Let
U_{n}
represent the uniform distribution on
n
bits. A pseudorandom generator (PRG) is an efficiently computable function,
\mathrm{PRG}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{l(n)}
such that
l(n)>n
and
\mathrm{PRG}(U_{n})
is computationally indistinguishable from
U_{l(n)}
. A one-way function (OWF) is a function that is efficiently computable and hard to invert.
The existence of one-way functions is a widely used computational assumption in classical cryptography. A pseudorandom function family (PRF) is a collection of efficiently computable functions that are computationally indistinguishable from a truly random function. It is known that in the classical world, one-way functions, PRG, and PRF are equivalent to each other
[
Gol01
]
.

In the quantum world, however, the story is not as straightforward. Ji, Liu, and Song
[
JLS18
]
first introduced the notion of pseudorandom quantum states (PRS), which are efficiently generatable pure quantum states that are computationally indistinguishable from truly random (Haar-random) quantum states — even when an adversary has access to multiple copies of the state. PRS can be thought of as a generalization of PRGs with quantum outputs. Similarly, pseudorandom function-like quantum state (PRFS) generators, first introduced by Ananth, Qian, and Yuen
[
AQY22
]
, are the quantum analogue of PRF.

[
JLS18
]
showed that we can construct PRS from any quantum-secure one-way function by applying a random phase to each computational basis element (here
\{f_{k}(\cdot)\}_{k}
is a quantum-secure PRF).

\ket{\phi_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{z=0}^{N-1}\left(e^{2\pi i/N}\right)^{f_{k}(z)}\ket{z}.

Brakerski and Shmueli
[
BS19
]
proved that the binary phase state is also pseudorandom.

\ket{\phi_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{z=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{f_{k}(z)}\ket{z}.

These elegant constructions guarantee that
t
-copies of such a state are close to
t
-copies of the Haar-random state when the number of qubits is sufficiently large. However, since these constructions are actually very different from the true Haar-random state, they are not
scalable
.

Scalability is a desirable property in the quantum setting. Classically, once we generate an
n
-bit (pseudo)random string, we can freely use the first
k
bits as a
k
-bit (pseudo)random string.
However, for the quantum case, even if we already have an
n
-qubit Haar-random state, if we take the first
k
qubits and trace out the rest, the result is typically very different from a
k
-qubit Haar-random state.
Therefore, just considering the first
k
-qubits of an
n
-qubit pseudorandom state may not give a pseudorandom state. In fact, Bouaziz-Ermann and Muguruza
[
BEM24
]
show that quantum pseudorandomness cannot be shrunk in a black-box way.

In
[
JLS18
]
and
[
BS19
]
, the security parameter
\lambda
is (essentially) the same as the qubit number
n
. When
n
is much smaller than
\lambda
, the distinguishing advantage is not negligible in
\lambda
for the above constructions.

Brakerski and Shmueli
[
BS20
]
gave the first scalable PRS generator construction in which the security parameter
\lambda
can be chosen independent of the length
n
of the output quantum state. In
[
JLS18
]
and
[
BS19
]
, randomizing the phase was enough. But for achieving scalable pseudorandom states, randomizing the amplitudes of the quantum state is also needed. Their idea is to prepare a quantum state whose coefficients follow the normalized standard Gaussian random vector (see
Definition

3.22
). With infinite precision and perfect sampling algorithms, such is state exactly behaves like a true Haar-random state. They first use a pseudorandom function to do (complex) Gaussian sampling and then do quantum rejection sampling to generate the state corresponding to the sampled Gaussian random variables.

Our contribution

In this work, we present an isometric procedure to prepare quantum states that can be arbitrarily random (i.e. the trace distance from the Haar-random state can be arbitrarily small for the true random case, or the distinguishing advantage can be arbitrarily small for the pseudorandom case).
This procedure provides a new method for scalable pseudorandom quantum state (PRS) generators, and yields the first construction for scalable and (quantum-accessible) adaptive pseudorandom function-like quantum state (PRFS) generators, assuming quantum-secure one-way functions
[
Zha21
]
.

Our main result is informally stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Assuming scalable quantum-secure pseudorandom function generators (
Definition

3.27
), there exists scalable and adaptive pseudorandom function-like quantum state generators (
Definition

3.33
).

Combining
3.29
that quantum-secure one-way functions (
Definition

3.25
) imply scalable quantum-secure pseudorandom function generators (
Definition

3.27
),
our work shows that quantum-secure one-way functions imply scalable and adaptive pseudorandom function-like quantum state generators (
Definition

3.33
).

In

2
Technical overview

An asymptotically random state (ARS) generator is a statistical notion of PRS that generates states that are statistically indistinguishable from Haar-random states but requires (quantum) access to an exponential amount of randomness. Then, replacing the exponentially large random string with a quantum-secure pseudorandom function (PRF), a PRS construction naturally follows from an ARS construction.

We now provide an outline of how we achieve a scalable ARS using a
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
isometric procedure, given quantum access to a random function.
The output state is exactly the Haar-random state in the idealized setting with infinite precision and a perfect sampling algorithm. With finite precision, the output state has negligible error using an exponential amount of randomness.
We then use a quantum-secure pseudorandom function to achieve scalable PRS from this ARS.
We use similar technique to construct scalable and adaptive asymptotically random function-like quantum state (ARFS) generators and PRFS generators.

2.1
Random amplitudes quantum state

From
3.23

we know that for a Haar-random state, its coefficients follow the distribution of the normalized standard Gaussian random vector (
Definition

3.22

\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{z\in[N]}A_{z}^{2}+B_{z}^{2}}}\sum_{z\in[N]}(A_{z}+iB_{z})\ket{z},

where
N=2^{n}

A_{z},B_{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

To do this, we first consider the amplitudes
\sqrt{C_{z}}=\sqrt{A_{z}^{2}+B_{z}^{2}}
and create the following state (we call it the random amplitudes quantum state):

\ket{\xi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{z\in[N]}C_{z}}}\sum_{z\in[N]}\sqrt{C_{z}}\ket{z}.

Each

follows the
\chi^{2}_{2}
distribution if
A_{z},B_{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Once we can prepare such a state, we can obtain the Haar-random state by applying a random phase

4.4

\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{z\in[N]}C_{z}}}\sum_{z\in[N]}\sqrt{C_{z}}e^{2\pi iU_{z}}\ket{z}.

2.2
(Pseudo)random quantum state

Once we have above random amplitudes state, denoted as
\ket{\xi}=\sum_{z=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}\ket{z}
,
we can get ARS by applying a random phase to each
\ket{z}
.
We compute a uniformly random
u_{z}\in[2^{\lambda}]
for each
z
on an ancilla register, then apply the phase shift gate
P(2\pi/2^{k})
on
k
-th qubit of
u_{z}
for all
k
, and finally uncompute
u_{z}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}\ket{z}\ket{0}

\displaystyle\to\sum_{z=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}\ket{z}\ket{u_{z}}

\displaystyle\to\sum_{z=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}\ket{z}\left(e^{2\pi iu_{z}/2^{\lambda}}\ket{u_{z}}\right)

\displaystyle\to\sum_{z=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}e^{2\pi iu_{z}/2^{\lambda}}\ket{z}\ket{0}.

To compute these
u_{z}
’s, we need quantum access to a random function.

In the idealized setting, the coefficient vector
(\alpha_{z}e^{2\pi iu_{z}/2^{\lambda}})_{z}
is a normalized complex standard Gaussian random vector that corresponds to a Haar-random state. For finite precision and an imperfect sampling algorithm (
Lemma

A.5
), the error in trace distance is negligible. Thus, for any adversary who can access this as an oracle for polynomial times, the distinguishing advantage is negligible (
Theorem

4.7
). Finally, by replacing the random function with a quantum-secure PRF, we achieve PRS (
Theorem

5.1
).

2.3
(Pseudo)random function-like quantum state generator

The next step is to modify the above ARS algorithm to accept an additional quantum state input
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
. Now, the random function
f
takes not only the original input but also
x
as input. In other words, for classical input
\ket{x}
,
f(\cdot)
is replaced by
f(x\|\cdot)
. Let the output state of such (modified) ARS procedure be
\ket{\psi_{x}}
on input
\ket{x}
, then for superposition input
\ket{\phi}
the entire output state becomes
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}\ket{\psi_{x}}
.

Since we know that
f(x\|\cdot)
for each
x
is also an independent random function, each
\ket{\psi_{x}}
is an independent Haar-random state, in the idealized setting.
This isometry
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}\mapsto\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}\ket{\psi_{x}}
is a truly random function-like quantum state generator.
For finite precision and an imperfect sampling algorithm, the error in diamond norm distance is negligible, which bounds the maximal distinguishing probability of two quantum channels. Thus, for any adversary who can access this as an oracle for polynomial times, the distinguishing advantage is negligible (
Theorem

6.3
). Finally, by replacing the random function with a quantum-secure PRF, we achieve scalable and adaptive PRFS (
Theorem

6.4
).

(Statistical distance)
.

Definition 4.2

The first and most critical step in our ARS / PRS algorithm is to prepare such a random amplitudes quantum state.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, given
B_{t,z}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(2^{n-t-1},2^{n-t-1})
all independent, where

, the state prepared by the following procedure is an
n
-qubit random amplitudes quantum state.

1.

Initialize with
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
.

2.

For
k:=1\text{ to }n

•

Map the first
k
qubits from
\ket{z}\ket{0}
to
\ket{z}(\sqrt{B_{k-1,z}}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-B_{k-1,z}}\ket{1})
for each
z\in\{0,1\}^{k-1}
. i.e., apply the unitary

U_{k}:\ket{z}\ket{0}\mapsto\ket{z}(\sqrt{B_{k-1,z}}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-B_{k-1,z}}\ket{1})

on the first
k

(Earlier, we have shown the example for

in

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
t
to show that the state after the
t
-th iteration, denoted by
\ket{\phi_{t}}
, can be written as

\ket{\phi_{t}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{z=0}^{2^{t}-1}W_{t,z}}}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{t}-1}\sqrt{W_{t,z}}\ket{z}\ket{0}^{\otimes n-t},

where each
W_{t,z}\sim\chi^{2}_{2^{n-t+1}}
i.i.d. and is also independent of all
B_{t^{\prime},z^{\prime}}
for
t^{\prime}\geq t
.
We take
\ket{\phi_{0}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
as the initial state before the first iteration.

•

Base case: This holds for
t=0

\ket{\phi_{0}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
.

•

Inductive step:
Assume this holds for
t
, then the state after the
(t+1)
-th iteration is

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{z=0}^{2^{t}-1}W_{t,z}}}\sum_{z=0}^{2^{t}-1}\sqrt{W_{t,z}}\ket{z}\left(\sqrt{B_{t,z}}\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-B_{t,z}}\ket{1}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes n-t-1}

4.2
Random phases procedure

Given quantum oracle access to a random classical function

\ket{\phi}=\sum_{z=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{z}\ket{z}

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
C\sim\chi_{2}^{2},U\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
independently, then both real and imaginary parts of
\sqrt{C}e^{2\pi iU}
follow standard normal distribution independently

Proof.

Let
U_{1}=e^{-C/2}\in(0,1]
.
PDF of
\chi_{2}^{2}
is
f_{C}(c)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-c/2},c\in[0,+\infty)
(
Definition

3.9
),
then from
3.11
we know that PDF of
U_{1}
is
f_{U}(u)=f_{C}(c)/\left\lvert\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muc}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muc}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muc}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muc}}}e^{-c/2}\right\rvert=1
,
which means
U_{1}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
.
Rewrite
\sqrt{C}e^{2\pi iU}
as

\displaystyle\sqrt{C}e^{2\pi iU}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-2\ln U_{1}}e^{2\pi iU}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-2\ln U_{1}}\cos(2\pi U)+i\sqrt{-2\ln U_{1}}\sin(2\pi U).

According to Box-Muller transform (
3.3
),
\sqrt{-2\ln U_{1}}\cos 2\pi U,\sqrt{-2\ln U_{1}}\sin 2\pi U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
i.i.d.
∎

Lemma B.1
.

Let
d>0
,
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{9d}}
and
g(x)=d\ln(1+cx)^{3}-d(1+cx)^{3}+d
for
x\in(-\frac{1}{c},+\infty)
. Then

g(x)\leq-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle s(x)

\displaystyle=g(x)-(-\frac{1}{2}x^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+d-d(1+cx)^{3}+d\ln(1+cx)^{3}.

Then

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=x-3cd(1+cx)^{2}+\frac{3cd}{1+cx}

\displaystyle=\frac{x(-3c^{4}dx^{2}-9c^{3}dx-9c^{2}d+cx+1)}{1+cx}

\displaystyle=-\frac{c^{2}x^{3}}{3(1+cx)}.\quad\quad\text{(Since $c^{2}d=\frac{1}{9}$)}

We have

s(0)=0
and
\begin{cases}s^{\prime}(x)>0,&x\in(-\frac{1}{c},0)\\
s^{\prime}(x)\leq 0,&x\in[0,+\infty)\end{cases}
,
so
s(x)\leq 0
for
x\in(-\frac{1}{c},+\infty
), which means
g(x)\leq-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
for
x\in(-\frac{1}{c},+\infty)
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let

Let

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\alpha+1)}{p(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=e\frac{\sqrt{\alpha+\frac{2}{3}}}{\sqrt{\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}}\alpha\frac{(\alpha-\frac{1}{3})^{\alpha}}{(\alpha+\frac{2}{3})^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=e\alpha\frac{(\alpha-\frac{1}{3})^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{(\alpha+\frac{2}{3})^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}}.

\displaystyle<-\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{1}{k\beta^{k}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{6\beta(\beta-1)}
