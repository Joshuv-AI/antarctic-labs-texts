The effects of interprofessional education for mental health and related professionals in Japan: A network analysis
Authors: Emi Watanabe, Yuki Shiratori, Kiyotaka Nemoto, Hirokazu Tachikawa, Tetsuaki Arai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimAlthough numerous Interprofessional Education (IPE) programs exist for students, those specifically designed for actual professionals remain limited. To address this, we developed the Psychiatric Staff Education Program for Transdisciplinary Approach (PsySEPTA), an online IPE program tailored for mental health and related professionals. PsySEPTA provides systematic training covering both foundational psychiatric knowledge and interprofessional collaboration. This study investigates how participation in PsySEPTA influences participants' network structures, using network analysis.MethodsBefore and after the one‐year program, we conducted surveys using the name generator method. Participants were asked to list up to four professionals with whom they regularly collaborate in their workplace. We then performed network analysis and computed various structural metrics.ResultsWe analyzed the responses of 53 participants from 12 different professions. Degree centrality showed mixed patterns of change across professions. Highly connected professions (initial degree ≥ 40) showed decreased centrality post‐program (e.g., nurses decreased from 82 to 74, midwives from 60 to 55, mental health social workers from 46 to 40), while several less connected professions increased (e.g., care managers from 6 to 14, public health nurses from 19 to 21, social workers from 9 to 11). The betweenness centralization, overall network metrics, increased after the program.ConclusionThis study is among the few that examine the evolution of professional communication networks associated with an IPE program for working professionals. The findings suggest that PsySEPTA may have helped participants expand their scope of collaboration, potentially enabling more seamless and effective interprofessional engagement.

INTRODUCTION

The importance of interprofessional collaboration is growing. The impetus for interprofessional collaboration in mental health came from the deinstitutionalization movement that took place in many countries in the mid‐20th century. Subsequently, it was recognized that people with mental health disorders have a wide range of needs and thus require a wide variety of services. One reason for this is that most mental health disorders have a long course, since the conditions under which people live in the community and the needs of individuals differ from one another. In recent years, diagnoses of mental health disorders have diversified, and the services required to address them have subsequently expanded at an accelerated rate. This is due to the rapidly increasing complexity of social life, which has been and is currently influenced by various factors such as the COVID‐19 pandemic, globalization, advanced information technology, and an aging society. For these reasons, there exist various “interprofessional collaboration teams in psychiatric care.” 1

Consultation‐liaison psychiatry (CLP) practices and methodologies have heterogeneously developed in diverse countries with different healthcare systems since the late 1990s. 2 In Japan, the Japanese Society of General Hospital Psychiatry was established in 1988 as an academic society specializing in CLP, while, in 2012, an additional fee for psychiatric liaison teams was newly established in the revision of medical service fees. This team consists of at least three members: a psychiatrist, a nurse, and either a mental health social worker, occupational therapist, pharmacist, or clinical psychologist, and is responsible for collaboration between psychiatry and physical medicine. Assertive Community Treatment (ACT), a comprehensive community life support program that has been implemented in the United States, Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, and other countries, was first introduced in Japan in 2003 and has since spread nationwide. Through the ACT program, an interprofessional collaboration team provides support to enable people with severe mental health disorders to live longer in the community without reducing their quality of life. 3 In Japan, where the population aging rate is the highest in the world, a system of collaboration between community comprehensive‐support centers consisting of public health nurses, social workers, care managers, and other professionals, and medical centers for dementia‐related diseases was established in 2005 following the revision of a related law. Thus, as psychiatric treatment and care have become more diverse and complex, the interprofessional collaboration that supports them has evolved diversity and complexity to match.

BACKGROUND

In 2010, the World Health Organization (WHO) released “Framework for action on interprofessional education & collaborative practice,” instructing that interprofessional education is a necessary step in preparing a “collaborative practice‐ready” health workforce that is better prepared to respond to local health needs. 4 Interprofessional education (IPE) is defined as “occasions when two or more professions learn with, from and about each other to improve collaboration and the quality of care.” 5 The Centre for the Advancement of Interprofessional Education (CAIPE) was established in the UK in 1987, and the importance of IPE was recognized. However, the impetus for the development of IPE was multiple medical accidents and incidents that were discovered in the UK between 1990 and 2000. 6 It is considered a challenge that interprofessional collaboration in health care is not functioning well. As a result, IPE has evolved in medical universities under government leadership and has since been adopted in many countries, including Japan, where it has been implemented since around 2005. Sherman et al. conducted a systematic review of IPE programs in medical education in 2020. 7 They reported positive changes in knowledge, attitudes, and skills with IPE. According to them, the key to success for university IPE is the commitment of administration and faculty, plus both top‐down and bottom‐up approaches. While administrators strategically promote IPE, faculty members recognize the value of working with other professions, and their grassroots efforts are highly prized. Therefore, the implementation of IPE for students in educational settings seems to be steadily spreading and effective methodologies are being established. Bridges et al. note that the common thread among successful university IPE cases is “helping students to understand their own professional identity while gaining an understanding of other professionals' roles on the health care team”. 8

However, there remains a discrepancy between the educational and clinical settings, as some professionals who complete an IPE program before obtaining their license struggle with interprofessional work in actual clinical practice. According to Makino et al., graduate scores on the modified Attitude Toward the Health Care Team Scale (ATHCTS) were significantly lower than those of undergraduates who studied IPE prior to certification. 9 Fujii surveyed professionals who had studied IPE prior to certification and found that they had difficulty implementing what they had learned when they entered the workforce. 10 The reason for this was that many of the frontline staff had not studied IPE. To provide patient‐centered care, it is essential not only to consider individual professional identities but also to foster interprofessional identities. 11 , 12 However, there are still many clinicians who lack knowledge and skills in interprofessional collaboration. 13

There are few reports on the effectiveness of IPE for mental health professionals. Pauzé et al. reviewed 16 IPE programs for mental health professionals conducted between 1999 and 2007, 14 concluding that there is no established professional methodology for IPE. It was also pointed out that each professional only works within the narrow scope of their own profession, which can lead to interprofessional conflicts and dysfunctional collaboration. 15

In 2019, we developed an IPE program for mental health and related professionals called the Psychiatric Staff Education Program for Transdisciplinary Approach (PsySEPTA). PsySEPTA is a fully online program that provides systematic training from the basics of psychiatry to interprofessional collaboration. In this study, we conducted an analysis of each participant's network both before and after participating in PsySEPTA. The primary objective of this research was to investigate the impact of PsySEPTA on interprofessional collaboration.

METHODS

Participants

PsySEPTA is intended for mental health and related professionals such as psychiatrists, physicians, dentists, pharmacists, nurses, midwives, clinical psychologists, dietitians, physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, social workers, care managers, and so on. The program lasts for one year. We gathered participants from websites, X, other internet tools, and nearby hospitals.

Content of PsySEPTA

The goal of PsySEPTA is to acquire interprofessional competency in health and welfare, which is based on Interprofessional Competency in Japan, 16 including the following six competencies: 1) Patient‐/Client‐/Family‐/Community‐Centered, 2) Interprofessional Communication, 3) Role Contribution, 4) Facilitation Relationship, 5) Reflecting, and 6) Understanding for Others.

To make the content more practical, we have developed nine courses: 1) Neurodevelopmental Disorders and Mental Conditions from Childhood to Adolescence, 2) Schizophrenia, 3) Mood Disorders and Suicide Prevention, 4) Eating Disorders, 5) Perinatal Mental Health, 6) Addiction, 7) Dementia, 8) Liaison Psychiatry, and 9) Disaster Psychiatry. Even when it comes to interprofessional collaboration, the professions involved vary greatly depending on the type of mental health disorders and the age group. To accommodate the diversity of professions and collaboration settings, a total of 124 professionals from various fields and occupations, ranging from university lecturers to specialized staff working in general hospitals, were involved in creating the content.

The curriculum is structured around three core components: on‐demand lectures, communication skills training workshops, and disease‐specific case conferences. The on‐demand lectures consist of two parts: general and specific. In the general lectures, participants learn about the history of each profession and watch documentaries and interview videos that follow the daily work of each profession. The total lecture time for this segment is 811 min. In the specific lectures, participants learn about the basic concepts, diagnosis, treatment, and more in each field and disease, and a corresponding professional lectures on the role of each profession in that field or disease. This allows participants to learn about the perspective, problem‐solving methods, difficulties, and ingenuity of each profession in collaboration. To enhance participants' understanding of real‐world interprofessional collaboration, we created several original dramas. These dramas involved many mental health professions in the process of scenario development. The median lecture time per field is 284.5 min. The on‐demand lectures are organized into chapters, each lasting 10 min. This format makes it convenient for working individuals to take the course without any difficulty. To confirm the level of mastery, there is a mini‐test before and after each lecture. A grade of 80% or more is required to pass the lecture.

The communication skills training workshops includes a 97 min on‐demand lecture and two days of practical sessions. After learning about psychological safety, participants engage in group work and role plays. These activities help them develop the ability to communicate their own professional position in an easy‐to‐understand manner, comprehend the positions of other professions, and effectively integrate the perspectives and ideas of various professions.

Finally, case conferences are held for each of the nine disease‐specific courses listed above. Participants are organized into small, multidisciplinary groups consisting of 5 to 10 individuals. These groups engage in collaborative meetings facilitated by field experts. This approach fosters a more practical and hands‐on learning experience. After the communication skills training and case conference, participants are required to submit a report.

A unique feature of PsySEPTA is that it ensures psychological safety. The term “psychological safety” was used by Carl Rogers in the 1950s to imply that the client should be able to “risk saying something highly personal, or absurd, or hostile, or cynical” with the knowledge that it will be respected. 17 Later, in the 1990s, Amy C. Edmondson proposed the following definition: “Psychological safety is a belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes.” 18 Psychological safety is an important element in understanding how people collaborate to achieve common results, 18 has a positive impact on job performance, 19 and has been reported to make it easier to discuss problems. 20

Evaluation design

This study is a longitudinal study. We conducted a baseline survey immediately before the program began, followed by a post‐program survey upon completion of the one‐year program. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba Faculty of Medicine (Approval No.1654).

Measure

We asked participants to list up to four professionals whom they collaborate with at their workplace using the name generator method. The name generator method is a network measurement technique that has been repeatedly used in the General Social Survey (GSS) in the United States since 1985. 21 We treated the participant's profession and the profession(s) that they collaborate with as nodes and created a network graph by assuming that a network exists between professions. We used R4.3.1 and the igraph package for network analysis and visualization. Each profession was treated as a node, and the edges represented the connections between professions. The edges were represented as directed vectors and expressed as multiple edges. The post‐program network graph was arranged using the Kamada–Kawai algorithm and the pre‐program graph was positioned at the same location as the post‐program graph. We conducted network analysis and calculated various features. Details of the features are described below.

Network centralization was calculated using the centr_degree(), centr_clo(), and centr_betw() functions from the igraph package (version 2.1.4) with normalized = TRUE. Due to multiple edges in our directed network (where multiple participants could report the same collaboration pattern), degree centralization values exceed 1.0. This occurs because Freeman's centralization formula normalizes by (n‐1)(n‐2), assuming a simple graph, while our observed maximum degree (82 connections for nurses at baseline) exceeds the theoretical maximum for simple graphs (n‐1 = 16). Despite being non‐standard, these values remain valid for within‐study comparisons across time points with an identical network structure. We calculated centrality indices for each node.

Centrality indices are measures used to evaluate and compare the importance of each node. 22 In this study, we calculated three representative centrality indices: degree centrality, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality. Degree centrality measures the number of edges connected to each node, which is also known as the degree of the node. Nodes that are more directly connected to other nodes, meaning those with higher degrees, are considered more important. Degree centrality directly reflects the influence of a node within a network. Closeness centrality measures how close each node is to all other nodes in the network. It is calculated as the reciprocal of the sum of the shortest path lengths from a node to all other nodes. Nodes that can reach all other nodes with shorter paths are considered more important. Betweenness centrality measures how often each node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes. Nodes that lie on more shortest paths between pairs of nodes are important because they control the flow of information in the network. Nodes with high betweenness centrality occupy the positions of gatekeepers or brokers. 23 In other words, when nodes with high betweenness are removed, the shortest distance increases, resulting in less efficient transmission.

Centrality is a measure that shows the characteristics of each node based on the position that it occupies in the entire network. On the other hand, centralization is an indicator that represents the entire network. 22 Edge density is the ratio of the actual number of edges to the maximum possible number of edges in a graph. The higher the value of this density, the more complex the network. 24 Reciprocity is defined as the proportion of mutual connections in a directed graph. It is the probability that an edge exists from the opposite side of the edge to the original node in a directed graph. 22 Transitivity is a measure that represents the probability of adjacent nodes being connected in a directed graph. It is also known as the clustering coefficient and can be interpreted as the tendency to form triangular relationships.

To assess statistical significance in the absence of a control group, we used permutation tests. For each network metric, we randomly reassigned observations to “before” or “after” conditions 1000 times, recalculated networks and metrics for each permutation, and determined the proportion of permuted differences that equaled or exceeded the observed difference in absolute value. This approach provides p‐values without assuming specific parametric distributions, which is appropriate for network data. We interpreted p‐values below 0.05 as evidence of significant change.

After the program, we administered a questionnaire to participants. The questionnaire included both a 5‐point Likert scale and open‐ended questions. Specifically, we evaluated the satisfaction levels related to the following items: 1)On‐demand lectures.2)Communication skills training workshops.3)Case conferences.Participants rated these items on a 5‐point scale, ranging from “satisfied” to “dissatisfied”. RESULTS

A total of 86 individuals from 41 facilities enrolled in the program between October 2019 and March 2021. Of these, 72 completed the program (83.7% completion rate), while seven extended their participation period and seven withdrew. Among completers, 53 participants from 12 different professions provided network data at both baseline and post‐program time points (73.6% response rate among completers) and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Statistical significance of network changes

To evaluate whether observed changes exceeded sampling variation in a pre–post design without a control group, we conducted permutation tests with 1000 random reassignments of observations to time points. The results showed that only the decrease in edge density (0.67 to 0.61, p < 0.001) reached statistical significance. Changes in degree centralization (1.96 to 1.77, p = 0.681), betweenness centralization (0.29 to 0.32, p = 0.683), and transitivity (0.48 to 0.54, p = 0.210) did not exceed what would be expected from random variation alone at conventional significance levels.

DISCUSSION

There are several reports that nurses are the most central members of the medical team. 25 , 26 Consistent with these findings, our study showed that nurses had the highest degree centrality both before and after the program, underscoring their key position within the mental health and welfare service network. The substantial increase in nurses' betweenness centrality (71.67 to 79.90) merits particular attention, as this indicator reflects their enhanced role as information brokers who coordinate collaboration across professional boundaries. 27 The closeness centrality of nurses, which represents both direct and indirect influence on the entire network, also showed a slight increase (0.32 to 0.33). This change was expected, given nurses' established coordinating role in healthcare teams, and it represents a desirable outcome in that it reflects enhanced recognition of nurses as effective brokers within the network.

In contrast, psychiatrists' betweenness centrality declined markedly (from 7.04 to 1.06), suggesting a reduced brokerage role. While this could reflect positive movement toward less hierarchical and more distributed networks, we interpret this finding with caution, given the small psychiatrist sample size (n = 2).

Several professions also showed decreased betweenness centrality, including clinical psychologists (0.85 to 0.00), physical therapists (5.95 to 1.56), and occupational therapists (3.03 to 0.69). These changes could reflect positive network evolution—with increased direct collaboration reducing the need for broker intermediaries—or may simply reflect sampling variation, given the very small sample sizes for these professions (n = 1, 2, and 4, respectively). The clinical significance of these changes remains unclear and warrants investigation in larger studies.

In comparing the degree centrality before and after the program, we observe the following trends: for occupations with initial centrality values of 40 or higher (such as nurse, midwife, mental health social worker), the degree centrality decreased after the program. Conversely, for occupations with initial centrality values of 20 or lower (such as physical therapist, social worker, and care manager), the degree centrality was maintained or increased after the program. This suggests that the program may have enhanced participants' understanding of professions that were not previously identified as potential collaborators.

The permutation test results require careful interpretation. While we observed directional changes in centralization measures that align conceptually with PsySEPTA's educational objectives, most of these changes did not reach statistical significance in permutation tests. This suggests three non‐mutually‐exclusive possibilities:

First, the observed changes may represent genuine trends that our sample size (n = 53) was underpowered to detect as statistically significant. Network‐level metrics are inherently variable, and pre–post designs without control groups have limited statistical power to detect moderate effect sizes. Second, some observed changes may indeed reflect sampling variation rather than true program effects. The lack of a control group means that we cannot rule out temporal trends, regression to the mean, or other confounding factors (including COVID‐19 disruptions) that could produce similar patterns. Third, the significant decrease in edge density (p < 0.001) provides the strongest quantitative evidence of structural change, suggesting that collaboration patterns became genuinely less dense. This change is robust to permutation testing and likely reflects real shifts in how participants conceptualized their collaboration networks.

The observed decrease in degree centralization (1.96 to 1.77) and simultaneous increase in betweenness centralization (0.29 to 0.32) suggest a shift in collaboration dynamics, though these changes did not reach statistical significance. Rather than concentrating connections within a few dominant professions, the network evolved toward a structure where participants increasingly functioned as brokers 27 —bridging previously disconnected professions and enabling them to work effectively and synergistically together. This structural change aligns with the decrease in fully mutual triads (type 300) and the increase in transitive triads (type 030 T), indicating a transition from reciprocal, tightly clustered collaboration toward more distributed, transitive pathways characterized by strategic brokerage. The decreased assortativity suggests that PsySEPTA led to reduce within‐category clustering, fostering cross‐professional–category collaboration. However, the stable proportion of within‐/between‐group edges indicates that this was a qualitative rather than a quantitative shift—changing how professions connect rather than fundamentally altering which categories they connect with. Such changes suggest that the program facilitated a deeper understanding of interprofessional roles and may have contributed to enhanced coordination capacity across the network.

We interpret our findings as suggestive rather than conclusive. The pattern of changes—decreased degree centralization, increased betweenness centralization, and decreased density—forms a coherent narrative consistent with PsySEPTA's goals, even if individual metrics do not all reach significance thresholds. Future research with larger samples, control groups, and longitudinal follow‐up is needed to definitively establish causal effects.

Importantly, self‐reported data from post‐program questionnaires showed that 97% of participants reported improved collaboration attitudes and 77% reported changes in daily practice. This qualitative evidence, while not objectively measured, suggests that participants experienced the program as impactful regardless of whether all network‐level metrics reached statistical significance. In the free‐text responses, participants reported: “I used to keep things within my own profession, but now I rely on others more and share information with other professions,” “I understood each profession's role and focused on collaboration points, making patient interventions more effective and smooth,” and “I became interested in the thoughts of other professions and wondered, ‘Why do they think that way?’ and I started to listen to them.” These responses further confirm that participants were able to collaborate more smoothly and effectively.

In clinical settings, there have been reports that interprofessional collaboration is hindered by a lack of understanding of one's own profession's roles and those of other professions, insufficient trust in others, a lack of respectful attitudes, and limited communication. 28 In the PsySEPTA program, participants learn about the historical background, roles, and thought processes of various professions through on‐demand lectures. The communication skills training workshops and case conferences provide opportunities to gain deeper insights into one's own and others' perspectives and values—experiences that are often challenging to obtain within the busy context of daily work. In adult education, it is said that self‐awareness and consciousness transformation are necessary. 29 For this reason, participants learn theoretical concepts in on‐demand lectures and communication skills training workshops and disease‐specific case conferences, where they interact with professionals from their own and other fields. Through the program, participants internally and externally re‐examine their own professions, naturally gain a more well‐rounded understanding, and develop a holistic perspective. These may be the reasons for the positive change seen in attitudes toward interprofessional collaboration. Additionally, in communication skills training workshops and disease‐specific case conferences, participants can practice the communication skills and facilitation skills that they have acquired in an environment where psychological safety is ensured. Such experiences can lead to behavioral changes, not only in awareness but also in action.

In the free‐text responses of the post‐program survey, many participants reported that they increased self‐initiated communication and information sharing. However, some participants provided occasional responses such as “While I have become more positive about collaboration, I feel that acting alone is impossible.” In fact, 23% of participants reported that there was no change in their interprofessional collaboration practices. According to Haruta et al., in order to promote interprofessional collaboration in the field of health care and welfare, it is essential to foster a common interprofessional identity 11 based on social capital. 30 To achieve behavior change, it has been pointed out that the sense of competence of “I can do it” and confidence that “practicing it leads to positive outcomes” are crucial. 31 These findings suggest that ensuring the presence of multiple staff members with the same educational background in the same workplace can contribute to fostering interprofessionalism, which, in turn, may serve as a means for behavior change.

Considering the above, the assertion by Sherman et al. that “in university settings, administrators and faculty members commit to IPE, and both ‘top‐down’ and ‘bottom‐up’ approaches are key to success” 7 might also apply to clinical settings. Assuming that administrators represent the organization and faculty members represent program participants and graduates, each participant and graduate actively engaging in collaboration, while the organization also provides a learning environment that fosters interprofessional collaboration, will advance IPE for professionals.

It should be noted that our data collection period (October 2019 to March 2021) overlapped substantially with the COVID‐19 pandemic, which likely influenced interprofessional collaboration patterns through increased telehealth adoption, workforce redeployment, and evolving professional roles. While these contextual changes may have affected our results—particularly the observed increase in betweenness centrality for certain professions—several factors support the validity of our core findings. All participants experienced the same pandemic context, creating a constant background factor rather than differential exposure. The changes that we observed align with PsySEPTA's explicit educational goals and would be expected regardless of pandemic context. Nevertheless, we cannot fully separate PsySEPTA's effects from broader systemic changes during this period, and future studies in non‐pandemic contexts would strengthen causal inference.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we did not directly assess patient outcomes or experiences. While participants reported perceived improvements in patient care, these represent self‐reports rather than objective patient‐centered measures. The theoretical pathway from improved collaboration to better patient outcomes is well established, but demonstrating this connection empirically requires direct measurement. Future research should incorporate multi‐level evaluation designs including patient‐reported outcomes, clinical processes, and healthcare utilization.

Second, the four‐collaborator limit imposed by our name generator method likely underestimated collaboration networks, particularly for highly connected professions. At baseline, 86.5% (45/53) of participants reported the maximum; at post‐program, 80.9% (38/53) reached this ceiling. This may have truncated degree centrality measures and affected density calculations. However, this limitation applies equally to both time points, so relative comparisons remain valid, and the consistent ceiling rate suggests that observed changes represent real shifts rather than measurement artifacts.

Finally, participants came from diverse organizations, preventing assessment of organizational‐level network characteristics.

CONCLUSION

Although studies on the effectiveness of IPE for students in educational settings are common, studies examining its effectiveness for professionals in clinical settings are rare. Our study suggests that PsySEPTA may facilitate smooth and appropriate interprofessional collaboration. However, changes in centralization measures, while directionally consistent with program goals, did not reach conventional significance thresholds, suggesting that our findings should be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive.

Nevertheless, this study makes it clear that PsySEPTA may prove useful in bridging the gap between generations that received IPE during their student years and generations that did not and in fostering interprofessional identity.
