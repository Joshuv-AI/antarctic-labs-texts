The effectiveness of virtual reality/motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements on upper limb function after stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wenzhi Cai, Yikun Zheng, Jingyi Wu, Guopeng Hu, Daisuke Takeshita, Ping Yeap Loh, Yuqi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUpper limb impairment is a common consequence of stroke and can markedly limit independence in daily life. Virtual reality (VR) and motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements have increasingly been used in rehabilitation, but their effectiveness for post-stroke upper limb recovery remains unclear. This article aims to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of VR/motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements on upper limb function after stroke.MethodThis systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251141175). PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched from January 1999 to September 2025 for randomized controlled trials. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed, and publication bias was explored using funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultsTen randomized controlled trials involving 426 participants were included. Compared with conventional therapy, virtual reality/motion-based games incorporating swing-like elements demonstrated significant improvement in upper limb functional recovery. The overall meta-analysis showed a merge standardized mean difference of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.44, 0.84) in favour of the intervention group. Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 82.2%, p < 0.001). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested relatively larger effects in older participants and in those with longer stroke duration, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled effect was stable, and Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias (p = 0.454).ConclusionVR/motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements may improve upper limb function after stroke and could serve as a useful adjunct to conventional rehabilitation. Further rigorously designed trials with standardized protocols are required to strengthen the evidence base and determine optimal intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04948-9.

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of long-term disability, significantly impacting patients’ upper limb function and consequently affecting their daily activities and independence [1]. Virtual reality (VR) and motion-based games, particularly rehabilitation programs incorporating racket-swinging elements, have emerged as novel therapeutic approaches due to their interactivity, entertainment value, and targeted nature [2]. In previous studies, swinging movements have been operationally defined as a component of upper-body movement characterized by repetitive, pendulum-like motions, typically involving the use of a hand-held implement and striking a target [3]. These tasks may promote repetitive upper-limb practice, coordination, and engagement during rehabilitation [4].

Cho, et al. [5] found that rehabilitation methods combining exercise with virtual reality significantly improved motor abilities and cognitive function in stroke patients, particularly in fine motor control of the upper limbs, revealing the potential advantages of virtual reality interventions. Virtual reality and motion-sensing games, through their high repetitiveness and dynamic feedback mechanisms, provide patients with richer training content. The advantage of VR/motion-sensing games lies in their ability to provide patients with dynamic, immersive rehabilitation environments. By integrating racket-swinging elements, they not only enhance the enjoyment of the rehabilitation process but may also promote the recovery of key functions such as upper limb coordination and reaction speed [6].

This represents an important gap in the literature. Although such interventions may share common features relevant to motor recovery, including visuomotor coordination, repetitive upper-limb practice, and feedback-driven task execution, their specific rehabilitation effects remain unclear [7, 8]. In addition, it is not yet well understood whether treatment effects vary according to participant characteristics or intervention parameters, such as age, stroke duration, and training duration.

Given this, the present study aims to address the following three specific research questions through a systematic review and meta-analysis:

Research Question 1: What is the overall efficacy of virtual reality technology/motion-sensing game rehabilitation incorporating swing-based elements on upper limb motor function following stroke?Research Question 2: To what extent do intervention methods and patient characteristics influence treatment outcomes?Research Question 3: How do the rehabilitation outcomes of virtual reality/motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements compare to those of traditional therapies?

Methods

This study followed the 2020 Guidelines for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [9] and was pre-registered on the PROSPERO website (ID: CRD420251141175). Literature searches were conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library as of September 30, 2025. Given that the first virtual reality intervention study for stroke was published in 1999, the search period spanned January 1999 to September 2025. The search strategy referenced previous relevant reviews [10–17]. Only studies published in English were included. Data extraction and processing

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, data extraction was performed independently by two researchers, covering bibliographic information, intervention protocols (participant characteristics, intervention methods, duration, frequency), and outcome measures (including baseline, post-intervention, and difference values). Where data were presented only in graphical formats, the WebPlotDigitizer tool was used for extraction; if extraction proved impossible, the original authors were contacted. For included studies, due to substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols, meta-analysis was performed within the scope of comparable data using a random-effects model. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of merger results. Studies lacking comparability or with missing data were excluded from pooling and described qualitatively in the results. The primary outcome measure of this review was upper limb motor function, assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Upper Limb Function Scale (FMA-UE). In the quantitative meta-analysis, post-intervention FMA-UE scores were used, and a random-effects model was employed to calculate the pooled effect size. Results are presented as standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals, and the effects of intervention intensity, frequency, and other factors were explored through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Bias risk and research design quality assessment

This study employed the Cochrane Risk of Bias 1.0 tool to assess the risk of bias in included studies, focusing on aspects such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding implementation, outcome assessment, data integrity, and selective reporting. All studies were scored by two independent assessors, with any disputes resolved by a third-party researcher. Additionally, the GRADE approach was applied to assess the quality of evidence for each outcome, categorizing evidence into four levels-high, moderate, low, and very low-based on risk of bias, study design quality, and other factors.

Results

Search results

A total of 370 articles were retrieved from PubMed (149), Web of Science (55), Scopus (56), and Cochrane (110). After deduplicating records and excluding review articles, systematic reviews, commentaries, and animal studies, (269) articles passed the initial screening. After abstract screening to exclude studies with mismatched research content(214) or inconsistent intervention/control measures(37), 18 articles met inclusion criteria. Further exclusion of non-RCTs(1), studies with flawed experimental design or methodology, and those with inconsistent outcome measures(7) resulted in 10 articles confirmed for full-text review (Fig. 1).

Basic characteristics of the literature

This study included 10 publications, all employing randomized controlled trial designs (Table 3). The research categorized virtual reality intervention techniques by exercise type, with tennis and table tennis studies constituting the most prevalent categories (Fig. 2A). A total of 426 participants were enrolled, comprising 215 subjects in the experimental group and 211 in the control group. All participants were stroke survivors with upper limb dysfunction, predominantly aged between 50 and 60 years. Most studies included mixed-gender groups, with sample sizes ranging from 16 to 80 participants (Fig. 2B). Approximately 80% of patients had mild to moderate impairment, while a minority exhibited severe impairment (Fig. 2C). The intervention periods in the included studies ranged from 2 to 12 weeks, with training sessions conducted 2 to 5 times per week. Each session lasted between 20 and 60 min, reflecting variations in intervention duration and frequency across studies (Fig. 2D). The Fugl-Meyer Upper Limb Scale was consistently reported as an outcome measure across all studies and was therefore used as the primary outcome measure in the quantitative meta-analysis. Secondary outcomes included hand-eye coordination, fine motor control, and reaction speed (Fig. 2E).

Risk bias and methodological quality

Most studies showed no potential for bias (Fig. 3), but the majority mentioned allocation concealment. As these were medical intervention studies, most could not achieve blinding of participants or researchers, leading to implementation and measurement bias. Conversely, data reporting, selective reporting, and other sources of bias posed a lower overall risk.

Merger effect results

Using STATA software, a meta-analysis was conducted to examine the merger effect size of the included studies (Fig. 4). In this meta-analysis, the overall effect size (SMD) was 0.64 (95% CI = 0.44, 0.84), indicating that the interventions across the included studies generally produced a moderate positive effect, with clinical significance. However, the analysis also revealed substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 82.2%, p < 0.001), indicating significant variation in effect sizes across different research studies. This heterogeneity may be related to the type of intervention, sample characteristics, study design, or specific conditions under which the interventions were implemented. For instance, some studies demonstrated strong positive effects, while others showed smaller effect sizes with wider confidence intervals, indicating higher uncertainty. Despite this heterogeneity, the pooled results suggested a positive overall trend in favour of the intervention, suggesting their potential to improve treatment outcomes. Further research is needed to explore the factors contributing to this heterogeneity and to evaluate the long-term effects of different intervention approaches in specific populations. The results reflect overall trends across studies with varying clinical and methodological contexts, rather than uniform effects across all settings; therefore, estimates of merger effects should be interpreted with caution.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted on the 10 included studies by sequentially removing each study and recalculating the merger effect size. Results showed that removing any single study resulted in minimal changes to the merger effect size (SMD) and its confidence interval, with most studies exhibiting no significant shifts in effect estimates. By analyzing the impact of each study on the overall effect size individually, we observed the effect estimates and corresponding confidence interval ranges for each study (Fig. 5). Despite wider confidence intervals in some individual studies, the overall analysis remained stable, supporting the positive effects of the intervention across most studies. These findings suggest that the pooled estimate was relatively robust to the exclusion of any single study.

Subgroup analysis results for effect size

Given that only 10 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis, all subgroup analyses in the present study should be considered exploratory. In subgroup analyses stratified by age, the participants were grouped based on the average age distribution reported in the sample, and 56 years of age was used as a practical cutoff point for exploratory comparisons. Results demonstrated significant differences in intervention effects across age groups (Fig. 6). Group 1 (age < 56) had an SMD of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.71), indicating moderate intervention effectiveness. However, this group exhibited high heterogeneity (I² = 89.9%, p < 0.001), suggesting substantial variation in effect sizes across studies. Group 2 (age ≥ 56) had an SMD of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.56, 1.10), indicating a more pronounced intervention effect with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 69.6%, p = 0.006). Further analysis of between-group heterogeneity revealed a statistically significant difference in intervention effects between the two groups (p = 0.038). The older subgroup showed a larger merger effect size than the younger subgroup.

The subgrouping for stroke duration was based on the reporting characteristics of the included studies and was intended to distinguish relatively shorter- versus longer-duration stroke populations. Subgroup analysis results by stroke duration (Fig. 7) showed that Group 1 (shorter duration) demonstrated a significant intervention effect with a SMD of 0.44 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.77). Group 2 (longer duration) also exhibited significant improvement with a larger effect size, with an SMD of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.51, 1.01). The analysis indicates that the longer-duration group demonstrated significant recovery after intervention, while the shorter-duration group showed more moderate effects. The SMD value for the longer-duration group was markedly higher than that for the shorter-duration group, suggesting that studies involving participants with longer stroke duration tended to show larger effect estimates.

Research findings indicate that compared to traditional rehabilitation, this intervention model demonstrates potential advantages in upper limb motor function (SMD = 0.64). Consequently, the primary findings of this study not only validate the efficacy of virtual reality intervention but also highlight the unique advantages of “swing-based elements”. From a multidisciplinary perspective, the significant therapeutic efficacy of swing-based virtual reality technology in post-stroke upper limb rehabilitation fundamentally stems from its integration of multi-level intervention mechanisms spanning physiology, motor neuroscience, and psychology [28, 29].

First, at the physiological and neuroplasticity level, racquet-based tasks demand rapid reactions, precise ball striking, and continuous bilateral coordination [30, 31]. Based on the Hebbian principle of plasticity, sustained visuomotor synchrony input during training enhances cortical synaptic connectivity efficiency, driving cortical remapping and functional compensation in damaged cortical regions [32, 33]. Simultaneously, rapid racket-swinging movements generate high-frequency recruitment stimuli for type II muscle fibers [34, 35].

Second, at the psychological and behavioral motivation level, VR training incorporating racket-swinging elements enhances patients’ intrinsic motivation through gamification mechanisms [36, 37]. According to Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, the immediate positive feedback, goal-achievement experiences, and visual progress tracking in VR training significantly enhance patients’ self-efficacy, boosting their belief in recovery and proactive engagement [38].

Compared to previous studies primarily focused on general VR or non-game-specific elements, this research further confirms that VR tasks incorporating racket-swinging elements more prominently highlight neural-muscular coordination and enhanced intrinsic psychological motivation [39, 40]. Based on these key findings, the discussion will now expand upon the following six aspects:

Summary and comparison of evidence

A meta-analysis of virtual reality technology for stroke patients [41] found that compared to traditional treatment regimens, virtual reality interventions effectively improved motor function and quality of life in stroke patients. Subsequently, Wu et al. (2025) [42] demonstrated the multifaceted benefits of virtual reality technology through more comprehensive outcomes (MD = 7.47; 95% CI 5.38–9.57). Lee et al. (2019) [43] reached similar conclusions in their meta-analysis, reporting an overall effect size ES = 0.440. Specifically, upper limb function showed an ES = 0.431 (Hedges’ g), indicating a moderate effect. They concluded that VR serves as an effective adjunct to conventional rehabilitation therapy. The combined effect size in this study indicates that VR tasks incorporating swing-based elements positive effect on FMA-UE scores (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI 0.44–0.84). These findings align with previous reviews emphasizing the potential of virtual reality as an innovative rehabilitation approach [44]. On the other hand, the effect size values in this study were higher than those reported in the aforementioned general reviews, suggesting that incorporating swing-based elements into motion-sensing game interventions may yield additional gains. The implementation of this innovative approach not only provides targeted and personalized rehabilitation methods but also introduces new therapeutic directions for post-stroke rehabilitation, offering broad clinical application prospects [45].

The impact of population differences

Most studies indicate that virtual reality technology has a beneficial effect on stroke recovery, though this varies across different populations due to factors such as age, intervention timing, and stroke onset duration [46–50]. Therefore, this study employed subgroup analysis to explore the effects of various demographic characteristics. These comparisons suggest that swing-based VR or motion-sensing game interventions may have potential value as a task-oriented rehabilitation approach. In contrast, the intervention effects were more moderate in the younger group aged 56 years or younger. While still effective, the outcomes were slightly weaker compared to the older group. From a physiological and biological perspective, the greater intervention effect observed in the older group may be closely related to baseline functional levels and potential for improvement. Older individuals often exhibit significant deficits in motor integration and fine control following stroke, leading to more pronounced changes during real-time feedback tasks [51, 52]. Additionally, neuroplasticity declines with age, increasing reliance on compensatory pathways. In contrast, the younger group had higher baseline levels and limited room for improvement, making it harder for intervention effects to fully manifest [53]. The duration of stroke onset, as shown in Fig. 7, is another critical factor influencing treatment efficacy. Interventions yield superior outcomes in groups with longer disease duration compared to those with shorter duration. This may stem from the stronger spontaneous recovery observed in the shorter-duration group, which moderates the intervention effect. Conversely, the longer-duration group, being relatively stable, experiences less interference from natural recovery, making it easier to achieve tangible incremental gains from the intervention [54, 55]. Figure 9 stroke severity analysis further indicates that intervention yields more pronounced effects in patients with severe stroke compared to those with mild stroke. This disparity arises because severe stroke patients possess greater potential for improvement, whereas mild stroke patients have limited room for advancement. Additionally, mild stroke patients often gain some practice volume through routine rehabilitation and daily activities, diluting the incremental effect of intervention measures [56]. Given that most participants are middle-aged or elderly, incorporating racket-based elements aligns with physiological principles for explosive strength training in this demographic. Such activities may enhance neural recruitment capacity through rapid reaction and fine motor control training, boost muscle power, and facilitate higher exercise intensities with diverse tasks. This approach activates underutilized muscle groups and improves motor function [57–61].

The impact of virtual reality solutions

The specific intervention protocol in virtual reality is a critical factor influencing treatment outcomes, demonstrating significant efficacy in post-stroke upper limb functional rehabilitation [62]. Figure 8 indicates that racket-swinging element training yields pronounced effects within shorter intervention periods. Since racket-swinging movements require explicit task objectives, high-intensity task repetition and immediate feedback facilitate motion correction. Short-term interventions better ensure task adherence and mitigate fatigue impacts [63]. Through interactivity and immersive experiences, virtual reality not only enhances patient engagement but also improves treatment outcomes via personalized training content. Comparative analyses of different protocols reveal significant advantages for swing-based elements. Chen, et al. (2022) [64] conducted a meta-analysis of conventional rehabilitation VR tasks, yielding an overall effect size (SMD = 0.45, 95% CI 0.21–0.68). Laver, et al. (2025) [65] systematically reviewed the efficacy of conventional VR tasks versus alternative therapies, yielding an overall effect size (SMD = 0.20, 95% CI 0.12–0.28). The combined effect sizes in these studies were lower than those in the present research, which demonstrated a moderately large effect. These comparisons suggest that swing-based VR or motion-sensing game interventions may have potential value as a task-oriented rehabilitation approach [66].

Impact of research design

Current research protocols lack standardization. First, differences in sample selection lead to heterogeneous outcomes, with more pronounced effects observed in patients with severe strokes and those in the chronic phase, while effects are weaker in patients with mild strokes and those in the acute phase. Second, significant variations exist across studies in intervention content, intensity, frequency, and duration, complicating comparisons and summaries of efficacy. Furthermore, some studies employed lower-frequency or short-term interventions, while others utilized higher-frequency and longer-duration training, potentially yielding differing outcomes. Finally, the selection of assessment metrics is critical for the accuracy and reliability of study results. Unified evaluation standards and tools related to functional living would facilitate a more comprehensive assessment of virtual reality intervention efficacy. Given the high heterogeneity among included studies in this review, the original plan to optimize synthesis using the “exclusion method” and expanded sampling through database searches was abandoned due to the inability to identify additional suitable studies. Instead, sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding studies to assess result robustness. Subgroup analysis suggests that age may be an important effect-modifying factor, while the time of stroke onset, duration of intervention, and stroke severity may also account for some of the heterogeneity among studies. Although omitting any individual study did not materially change the overall effect estimate, certain studies appeared to account for a larger proportion of the between-study heterogeneity. Removing Anwar, et al. (2022) [20] and Ain, et al. (2021) [25] reduced heterogeneity, indicating that sample selection, intervention content, intensity, and frequency were sources of high variability. Since only 10 studies were available for quantitative meta-analysis, the merger effect estimates are to some extent influenced by differences among the studies and should be interpreted in the context of each specific study. Future studies should prioritize personalized interventions, standardized protocols, and consistent assessment tools during the design phase to ensure reliable and broadly applicable outcomes.

Evidence-based practice recommendations and future research directions

Future research should consider the following points. First, emphasis should be placed on promoting personalized intervention plans tailored to patients with varying stroke severity, ages, and functional impairment levels. Second, to better achieve precise virtual reality intervention outcomes, multidisciplinary collaboration should be strengthened. Professionals from neurology, physiology, physical therapy, and kinesiology should be integrated to develop comprehensive rehabilitation plans. Targeted specialized training programs should be implemented based on patient needs to maximize therapeutic effects. Third, real-time feedback mechanisms should be integrated into interventions. Multisensory stimulation will help patients adjust movements promptly while enhancing treatment immersion, enabling optimal therapeutic outcomes in a pleasant environment. Regular assessments and feedback evaluations should inform standardized intervention protocols to elevate treatment efficacy through consistent methodologies. Furthermore, future research should focus on optimizing training intensity, frequency, and duration for various VR swing-based tasks; elucidating the neural mechanisms by which swing tasks enhance hand-eye coordination; and investigating potential differences between generic swing elements and sport-specific racket elements (e.g., tennis, badminton). Integrating innovative virtual reality technologies to explore comprehensive effects on enhancing motor coordination, strength recovery, and neuroplasticity will represent a frontier research direction. Future efforts should also address cultural adaptation and long-term efficacy, interdisciplinary collaboration, precision medicine, and multidimensional interventions to provide personalized, efficient, and sustainable rehabilitation solutions for stroke patients.

Strengths and potential limitations of this study

The primary strength of this study lies in its systematic integration and evaluation of the efficacy of virtual reality technology incorporating swing-based elements in post-stroke upper limb rehabilitation. This was achieved by including more recent and comprehensive randomized controlled trials. Additionally, the study objectively assessed the risk of bias and methodological quality of the included original research designs, thereby more clearly demonstrating the potential advantages over conventional rehabilitation. First, innovation is a key focus of this study. It represents the first systematic review to comprehensively evaluate swing-based elements in post-stroke upper limb recovery, revealing the potential advantages of this intervention over traditional physical therapy and other rehabilitation methods. By incorporating swing-based elements, the rehabilitation approach enhances patient engagement and motivation, thereby improving treatment sustainability. Second, beyond synthesizing overall effects, the study systematically assessed potential effect modifiers through subgroup analyses. Key variables examined included age groups, intervention duration, and time since stroke onset. Statistically significant differences emerged in age-stratified analyses, providing targeted evidence-based guidance for personalized interventions. Third, this study employed rigorous bias risk assessment and high-quality research design to ensure result reliability. The use of risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE evaluation allowed a more structured appraisal of the methodological quality and certainty of evidence.

Despite achieving meaningful outcomes in multiple aspects, this study has certain limitations. First, only 10 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis, Given that a limited number of studies can lead to insufficient statistical power for such analyses, meta-regression was not conducted, there were significant differences among the studies in terms of intervention duration, intensity, and frequency, which may have resulted in a high degree of heterogeneity in the study results. Conducting meta-analyses by exercise type is not statistically feasible and may result in unstable estimates. Second, the subgroup analyses were exploratory. Although some subgroup differences were observed, these findings should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence. Third, the lack of blinding in some studies may have influenced efficacy assessments, Given the nature of rehabilitation interventions, it is often difficult to implement blinding for participants and therapists, which may increase the risk of performance bias and detection bias; future research should strengthen blinding protocols and employ more precise measurement tools. Fourth, the assessment of publication bias was limited by the small number of studies, and the non-significant Egger’s test result should not be taken as definitive evidence of absence of publication bias. Finally, the primary outcome measures focused on upper limb function assessments. While these metrics are widely adopted, they may not fully reflect patients’ activities of daily living or overall health status. Future studies should consider integrating more comprehensive quality-of-life assessment tools to evaluate intervention effects in a multidimensional manner.

Conclusion

Virtual reality/motion-sensing games incorporating swing-based elements appear to confer beneficial effects on upper limb function after stroke and may represent a potentially valuable adjunct to conventional rehabilitation. The pooled findings indicate a positive overall trend; however, the present evidence base remains influenced by the limited number of available studies and the variability in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, and study designs. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence. Further high-quality randomized controlled trials with larger samples, standardized intervention protocols, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the clinical value and applicability of this intervention subtype.
