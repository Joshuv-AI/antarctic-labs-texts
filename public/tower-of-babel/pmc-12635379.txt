Tuning sp3/sp2 carbon ratio of heavily boron-doped diamond electrodes fabricated via hot-filament chemical vapour deposition with CO2 addition
Authors: Taisuke Kageura, Kenshin Takemura, Michiko Maeda, Atsushi Otake, Yasuaki Einaga, Shinya Ohmagari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Boron-doped diamond (BDD) is an excellent electrode material for electrochemical sensors and both high sp3/sp2 carbon component ratios and high boron concentrations are significant factors for specific applications. Hot-filament chemical vapour deposition (HFCVD), which is a conventional technique for synthesising large and uniform BDD electrodes for industrial applications, presents challenges when synthesising films with the above characteristics. In this study, we demonstrate the stable long-term synthesis of heavily boron-doped polycrystalline diamond with a high sp3/sp2 ratio by adding CO2 gas and adjusting the H2/CH4/B(CH3)3/CO2 gas ratio during HFCVD. Adding an appropriate amount of CO2 gas not only reduces the sp2 carbon component but also improves crystallinity and increases growth rate while maintaining metallic conductivity despite a moderate decrease in boron concentration. Electrochemical measurements reveal that our BDD electrodes exhibit excellent characteristics comparable to those of high-standard BDD electrodes synthesised using microwave plasma-assisted CVD.

Introduction

Boron-doped diamonds (BDDs) exhibit p-type conductivity1 and are expected to be widely used as electrochemical electrodes2–5 for electrochemical sensors and in electrolysis processes such as carbon dioxide (CO2) reduction6, wastewater treatment7, and ozone water generation8. This wide use can be attributed to the outstanding properties of BDD such as its high conductivity1, the widest electrochemical potential window of any known material, remarkable chemical stability, low background currents, and ability to deposit large-area films on a variety of material surfaces9, in addition to the excellent physical properties of the diamond10 such as high thermal conductivity. The performance of BDD electrodes depends on the properties of BDD films such as the sp3/sp2 carbon ratio11–17, boron concentration13,14,18,19, crystal orientation20, surface termination21, and surface roughness22. The sp3/sp2 ratio and boron concentration are significant parameters for electrochemical sensors2 and wastewater treatment21 because they strongly affect chemical stability23 and electrochemical properties such as potential window17 and background current21. Therefore, controlling the sp3/sp2 ratio based on the application is necessary; however, it is difficult to fabricate large and uniform BDD electrodes with both a high sp3/sp2 ratio and heavy boron doping, which are essential for the high-sensitivity detection of specific chemical substances.

A hot-filament chemical vapour deposition (HFCVD) method is generally used for synthesising polycrystalline BDD electrodes suitable for industrial applications, while there are several other methods available for synthesizing BDD electrodes such as microwave-plasma-assisted chemical vapour deposition (MPCVD). This can be attributed to the features of HFCVD that enable the deposition of large-area films with high uniformity9 using inexpensive and simple equipment. However, achieving a high sp3/sp2 ratio and high boron concentration for BDD electrode synthesis remains a challenge. The sp2 component can be reduced by adding oxygen species during chemical vapour deposition (CVD) diamond growth; however, in the case of HFCVD, the oxidation of the filament material is a serious problem24. Although high-melting point materials such as tungsten are commonly used as filament materials, the melting points of oxides such as WO2 (1500–1600 ℃) and WO3 (1472 ℃) are not so high, and therefore, they are sublimated at a filament temperature above 2000 ℃, which is the typical temperature required for diamond growth. Thus, the long-term and reproducible growth of BDD electrodes by HFCVD with the addition of oxygen gas is difficult. Most previous studies on the HFCVD synthesis of the diamond were conducted with the addition of small amounts of oxygen gas (less than 1000 ppm) or in a short time (within a few hours). Another challenge in oxygen gas addition during CVD diamond growth for BDD electrodes is a decrease in boron concentration. Numerous studies reported that a drastic decrease in boron doping efficiency occurs even with small amounts of oxygen gas addition25. Metallic conductivity required for high-performance BDD electrode cannot be achieved unless the boron concentration is above ~ 1 × 1020 cm− 3. Further, even a boron concentration of an ~ 1 × 1020 cm− 3 potential window of BDD electrode can be changed because of the undesired sp2 carbon-derived conduction when the sp2 component ratio is high. This indicates that it is difficult to achieve a high sp3/sp2 ratio and high boron concentration sufficient for improving the performance of the BDD electrode by HFCVD with the addition of oxygen gas.

In this study, we demonstrated the long-term synthesis of heavily boron-doped polycrystalline diamond films with high sp3/sp2 ratios by adjusting the amount of CO2 gas used as an oxygen source during the HFCVD process. The use of CO2 gas for CVD growth has already attracted considerable attention in the synthesis of high-quality graphene26. The mild oxidation properties of CO2 can selectively etch less-ordered sp2-carbon phases and non-graphene-type carbon while maintaining the sp2 bonds of graphene during the CVD process. The findings of this previous study revealed that long-term growth is possible by suppressing filament oxidation while improving the sp3/sp2 ratio of BDD films and suppressing the decrease in boron-doping efficiency using CO2 gas. Our experiments revealed that adding appropriate amounts of CO2 gas reduced sp2 carbon components in BDD films, improved crystallinity and increased the growth rate while maintaining metallic conductivity despite a moderate decrease in boron concentration. Electrochemical measurements revealed that our BDD electrodes exhibit characteristics comparable to those of high-standard BDD electrodes synthesised using MPCVD.

Results and discussion

Polycrystalline BDD films synthesised by HFCVD with CO2 addition

Heavily boron-doped polycrystalline diamond films were synthesised using a laboratory-built HFCVD system as shown in Fig. 1a. In this system, (001)-oriented 3 inch single-crystalline silicon wafers were used as substrates. The surfaces of the substrates were seeded by polishing with a diamond powder that had an average grain size of 365 nm and diluted with ethanol before CVD growth. Tungsten wires with a diameter of 0.12 mm and length of 400 mm were used as filament materials. The distance between the filaments and substrate was ~ 15 mm. The filament temperatures were set in the range of 2000–2200 °C by controlling the output power. Hydrogen (H2, 99.9999%), methane (CH4, 99.9999%), tri-methyl-boron (TMB: B(CH3)3) diluted with 91% H2 (purity: 99.9999%), and CO2 (99.99%) gases mixture were used for the growth. The growth parameters are listed in Table 1. The BDD growth was performed using a two-step process. As described in Table 1, the pre-deposition for the initial nuclear formation was performed for 1 h. Boron-doped diamond films were then grown for 10 h. The CO2 gas ratio ([CO2]/[H2]) was controlled by changing the CO2 gas flow rate while the flow rates of the other gases were fixed. For [CO2]/[H2] ratios below 2000 ppm, the synthesised BDD films exhibited a metallic black colour and appeared to be uniformly deposited over the entire surface of the 3-inch silicon wafer, as shown in Fig. 1b. In contrast, for a [CO2]/[H2] ratio = 5000 ppm, several filament wires broke randomly during growth. This can be attributed to the sublimation of the filaments promoted by the high oxygen concentration in the CVD chamber. For a [CO2]/[H2] ratio = 4000 ppm, a 10 h growth was completed without filament breaking; however, a non-uniform thin film was partially deposited on the substrate. Currently, the formation of nonuniform thin films for a [CO2]/[H2] ratio above 4000 ppm remains unclear; however, one possibility is the deflection of filaments during CVD growth caused by changes in the filament diameter attributed to oxidation and sublimation. Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the tungsten wire before and after HFCVD growth were obtained to investigate changes in the structure of the tungsten wires during growth, as shown in Figs. 1c–e. Figure 1c shows the tungsten wire before synthesis, which was cut with pliers and its radius was about 116 μm, which is consistent with the official value (~ 120 μm) stated by the supplier. In contrast, the tungsten wire after growth without CO2 gas addition (Fig. 1d) shows a significant change in the structure and volume expansion (Φ = ~144 μm) with cracks, which can be attributed to the carburisation27 (W to α-W2C) of the tungsten wire caused by methane gas during growth. After growth at [CO2]/[H2] = 5000 ppm (the filament broke after about 7 h from the start), no significant difference was observed compared to that of tungsten without CO2 addition; however, the radius was slightly smaller (Φ = ~138 μm), and there was some alteration on the SEM image (visible as a black contrast) in the vicinity of the surface. This implies that the tungsten wire undergoes sublimation because of the oxidation, and the oxidation begins to gradually penetrate the inside. These results confirm that [CO2]/[H2] = 2000 ppm is the optimum condition for the long-term synthesis of large and uniform BDD films using our HFCVD setup.

Structural analysis by scanning electron microscopy observation

Figures 2a–d show the representative plan-view SEM images of samples with different [CO2]/[H2] ratios (0, 250, 1000, and 2000 ppm). A highly uniform distribution of crystal grains was observed across the wafer surface in all samples, and the dominant crystal grains showed randomly oriented tetrahedral and cuboctahedral features. The proportion of the tetrahedral structures was slightly higher in the sample with [CO2]/[H2] = 0 ppm, and the proportion of octahedral structures appeared to increase with increasing [CO2]/[H2] ratios, especially above 1000 ppm. The effect of boron- and oxygen-related gas addition on the crystal structure in diamond growth has been extensively reported, and it is clear that the crystal structure strongly depends on various parameters such as oxygen ratio, methane concentration, growth temperature, and pressure28,29. Reference28 and other studies have shown that boron doping tends to promote the formation of (111) facets, and Reference30 suggest that < 111>/<110 > growth is dominant in the oxygen ratio range of 0–4000 ppm when the methane concentration is 3% and substrate temperature is greater than 900 °C.30 The observed tendency toward tetrahedral and octahedral structures in our sample suggests that a similar mechanism as described above may occur.

The thicknesses of the synthesised films were estimated from the cross-sectional SEM images, as shown in Figs. 2e–h. Samples with 0 and 250 ppm of [CO2]/[H2] had a film thickness of around 1.2 μm, whereas those with 1000 and 2000 ppm had a film thickness of ~ 1.7 μm; this implies that the growth rate increases with an increase in CO2 addition, especially above 1000 ppm. This tendency was observed in secondary ion mass spectroscopy (SIMS) measurements. Several previous studies have reported that the addition of small amounts of oxygen increases the growth rate of polycrystalline diamond31,32, which can be attributed to the formation of OH or O bonds on the diamond surface. These bonds promote sp2 carbon etching, and consequently, sp3 carbon deposition. Further, it has been reported that the addition of large amounts of oxygen promotes the etching of the sp3 component as well as sp2 carbon, which results in a decrease in the growth rate of the polycrystalline diamond. In our samples, the growth rate increased even with the addition of 2000 ppm, which indicates that the sp3 carbon was moderately etched via the addition of CO2. Here, we note the effect of residual nitrogen on the growth rate. Previous studies33,34 indicate that even small amounts of nitrogen (several ppm) can increase diamond growth rates. In actual experiments, the purity of the gases affects the nitrogen concentration in chamber. In our case, the nitrogen concentration in the CO₂ cylinder is calculated approximately 0.2 ppm at maximum. Furthermore, the amount of residual nitrogen in hydrogen gas is greater than that in CO2 gas. Therefore, the effect of nitrogen in the CO₂ gas on the growth rate is considered to be small.

sp3/sp2 ratio evaluation by Raman spectra

Figure 3a shows the Raman spectra obtained with the 532 nm light for samples with different [CO2]/[H2] ratios. Several peaks with different bandwidths were observed, and a sharp peak at around 1320 cm− 1 was derived from the zone-centre optical phonon Raman scattering peak of a diamond (ωZCP) located at ~ 1332 cm− 1 for intrinsic diamond and known to shift to lower wavelengths with an increase in the boron concentration because of tensile stress35. The relatively broad peak at ~ 1230 cm− 1 was attributed to the maximum diamond phonon density of states induced by boron doping. Peaks at ~ 1140 and 1474 cm− 1 were related to trans-polyacetylene at the grain boundary. The peak at approximately 1560 cm− 1 was derived from sp2 carbon bonding and called the G peak. The broad peak at ~ 950 cm− 1 was considered a superposition of peaks related to second-order silicon Raman35 and the interaction of sp3 and sp2 carbon36. The origin of small peaks at 1080 and 1190 cm− 1 are unclear; however, it is likely that the peak of 1080 cm− 1 is attributed to the density of vibrational states in the vicinity of a boron atom36,37 and that the peak of 1190 cm− 1 is attributed to trans-polyacetylene segments at grain boundaries38,39.

Figure 3a shows two major findings: (1) The dependence of boron concentration in the diamond on CO2 gas addition. Previous studies showed that the 1230 cm− 1 peak associated with boron doping increased relative to the diamond peak at 1332 cm− 1, with an increasing boron concentration in diamond. Further, the 1230 cm− 1 peak was clearly observed only for boron concentrations above 1020 cm− 3. The findings of these previous studies revealed that all synthesised BDDs exhibit boron concentrations above 1020 cm− 3 and that the boron concentration in the BDDs decreased with an increasing [CO2]/[H2] gas ratio. Boron concentration and growth rate based on SIMS profile

Figure 4a shows the depth profile of the boron concentration [B] in the samples synthesised at different [CO2]/[H2] gas ratios. For samples without CO2 gas addition, [B] was ~ 1.2 × 1021 cm− 3. Further, [B] in the diamond gradually decreased with an increasing flow rate of CO2 gas, as indicated in Fig. 3b. For the sample with [CO2]/[H2] = 2000 ppm, [B] is 4.3 × 1020 cm− 3; this result is consistent with the shift of the ωZCP peak in the Raman spectra and the decrease of the 1230 cm− 1 peak intensity, as shown in Fig. 3. The decrease in the boron incorporation efficiency caused by the addition of oxygen-related gases in the CVD synthesis of BDD is well known, both experimentally and theoretically. The decrease in the boron concentration in our samples can be attributed to the same mechanism. As reported in Reference25, the boron concentration decreases by more than one order of magnitude with the addition of 1000 ppm of oxygen. Thus, our results suggest that the decrease in boron concentration caused by CO2 addition is moderate compared to that of oxygen. The incorporation efficiency of boron from the gas phase to the solid phase for different [CO2]/[H2] (0, 500, 1000, 2000 ppm) were evaluated to be 450%, 350%, 220% and 170%, respectively. The doping efficiency exceeds 100% even CO2 addition, suggesting that this is related to a high methane concentration, the generation of a non-equilibrium state due to gas flow in the chamber and moderate oxidation.

In addition, the SIMS results indicate the effect of CO2 gas addition on the growth rate of the BDD films. For the sample without CO2, the growth rate was estimated to be ~ 180 nm/h. The growth rate increased with an increasing flow rate of CO2 gas, especially above 1000 ppm, as shown in Fig. 4c. This tendency is consistent with the film thicknesses estimated from the cross-sectional SEM images shown in Figs. 2e–h.

Electrochemical properties

Figure 5a shows the background current–potential curves of the synthesised BDD films in 0.1 M H2SO4 at a scan rate of 100 mVs− 1. The anodic treatment of the BDD films was performed by the cycling potential from − 1.8 V to 3.0 V for two cycles. The potential window was determined using the linear fit method41, as shown in Fig. 5b (see the Methods section for details). The values of the potential window were almost constant at ≈ 3.6–3.7 V (Table 1) regardless of the amount of CO2 addition. This value is sufficiently high compared to that of a typical high-standard BBD synthesised via MPCVD (≈ 3–3.5 V). Previous studies17,42 showed that the density of states of the primary BDD is affected by the sp2 component and is prominent in the high-boron-concentration region, which suggests that the high potential window of our heavily doped BDD can be attributed to the low sp2 density achieved by the addition of CO2 during growth.

Conclusion

The introduction of CO2 gas during HFCVD enables long-term growth, improves the sp3/sp2 component ratio, increases the growth rate, and maintains metallic conductivity despite a moderate decrease in boron concentration. The obtained BDD electrodes exhibited excellent electrochemical properties comparable to those of high-quality BDD electrodes grown using the MPCVD method. The CO2 addition during the HFCVD process demonstrated in this study does not require any additional modifications to the equipment for achieving a high sp3/sp2 ratio and high boron concentration and has advantages of simplicity and high scalability. The characteristics of the HFCVD process, which enable uniform and large-area deposition at a low cost, combined with our technology, are expected to advance the development of wastewater treatment and electrochemical sensors suitable for industrial applications.

Methods

Characterisation of the crystal structure

Schottky emission SEM (JSM-7001 F, JEOL Ltd., Japan) was used for observing the surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the synthesised BDD films. The acceleration voltage was set at 15 keV. Secondary ion mass spectroscopy was conducted using an IMS-7f instrument (CAMECA, France) to evaluate the depth profile of the boron concentration. The first ion was Cs+ and the acceleration voltage was 16 keV; the detection area is ~ 30 μm in diameter. The sp3/sp2 ratios of synthesised BDD films were evaluated by Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA Scientific Ltd., Japan). A laser with two different wavelengths (532 and 325 nm) was used as the excitation light source. The 532 nm light was used to evaluate the effect of boron doping and estimate the sp3/sp2 ratio. The 325 nm light was used to precisely evaluate the relative sp3/sp2 ratio. Two different lights were used because of the following reasons:

Several large peaks associated with boron doping were observed in the case of the heavily BDD with visible light excitation, which made it difficult to separate the sp3- and sp2-derived peaks.The sp2 component was detected with good sensitivity using ultraviolet (UV) light because of the smaller relative ratio of Raman scattering cross-sections between sp3-bonds and sp2-bonds44,45.

Electrochemical analysis

Electrochemical measurements were performed using an electrochemical analyser (ALS 832D, BAS Inc., Tokyo, Japan). A platinum wire and a Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively, for electrochemical measurements (BAS Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The potential window of the BDD electrode was determined by linear fitting, as follows: In the I–V curve where the voltage is swept from − 1.8 to 3 V, linear fitting was performed in three regions: −1.8 to − 1.5 V, − 0.5 to 1.5 V, and 2.7 to 3 V (e.g., Fig. 5b). The voltage at the point where the extension of the linear fitting line crossed was determined, and the difference between the two points was considered as the potential window.
