Comparative Micro-CT Evaluation of Dentinal Tubule Penetration of BIO-C and AH Plus Jet Root Canal Sealer Using Three Intra-Canal Activation Techniques
Authors: K. Vivek, Sreeja Sreedhar, Siddharth V. Nair, V. Sreelekshmi, J. Jitha, Anitha Viswanadhan, P. M Athira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of three intra-canal sealer activation techniques: ultrasonic, Lentulospiral activation, and GP cone-mediated ultrasonic activation on dentinal tubule penetration of a bioceramic (BIO-C) root canal sealer and AH Plus jet sealer with single cone obturation.Materials and Methods:Eighty single-rooted straight canal teeth were randomly divided into two main groups – BIO-C sealer and AH Plus jet sealer (n = 40). Each group was further divided into four subgroups, based on the three activation techniques: ultrasonics, Lentulospiral, gutta-percha cone-mediated ultrasonic, and a control. The canals were obturated using the single cone obturation technique. Dentinal penetration of sealers was measured using Micro-CT at the coronal, middle, and apical portions of root canals.Results:Greater sealer penetration was observed with ultrasonic and Lentulospiral agitation compared to GP-mediated ultrasonic agitation for both sealers.Conclusion:BIO-C yielded better results with ultrasonic and Lentulospiral agitation, while AH Plus performed better with ultrasonic agitation.

INTRODUCTION

In the realm of endodontic treatment, achieving a completely sealed root canal system stands as a paramount objective. The prevention of bacterial leakage and microbial byproducts into the intricate root canal space holds the key to successful outcomes. Among the factors influencing treatment success, the quality of root canal filling plays a pivotal role.[1]

Root canal sealers not only act as lubricants but also form a strong bond between the filling material core and the root canal walls, entombing any residual bacteria. A variety of sealers have emerged over a period of time, including zinc oxide eugenol-based, calcium hydroxide-based, glass ionomer-based, silicone resin-based, epoxy, methacrylate resin-based, and the remarkable bioceramic sealers.[2]

AH Plus is an epoxy resin-based sealer. It shows good sealing ability and favorable physicochemical and biological properties. They are hydrophobic in nature.[3]

Bioceramics are nontoxic, biocompatible, and chemically stable within the biological environment and form hydroxyapatite during the setting process, chemically bonding to root canal dentin, exhibiting potent antimicrobial properties, and stimulating periodontal regeneration.[4] Recently, a novel category of endodontic sealers has emerged, utilizing calcium silicates as their base. Studies evaluating the clinical efficacy of calcium silicate-based sealers remain limited. The bioactive nature of these sealers is attributed to their slight solubility even after setting.[5]

BIO-C is a calcium-silicate-based sealer that can provide a tight seal with single-cone obturation. It is a premixed injectable sealer designed to have excellent biocompatibility and bioactivity.[6] Bioceramic sealers also have their limitations, such as extended setting time, higher costs, and limited availability.[4]

The various recognized modes of sealer placement include the use of endodontic files or reamers, Lentulospirals, gutta-percha cones, paper points, ultrasonic files, and counterclockwise motion of rotary systems. Each technique may produce a different distribution of the sealer onto the canal walls, ultimately which may affect the sealing.

MATERIALS AND METHODOLOGY

Sample preparation

Freshly extracted 80 single-rooted intact teeth were collected and decoronated close to CEJ to achieve a 15 mm root length. Working length was established 1 mm short of apex. All the canals were prepared by crown down technique using ProTaper rotary files up to size F3. After each instrumentation, the canals were irrigated through ultrasonic activation tips with 2 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite solution for 1 min using a side venting 27-gauge needle kept 2 mm short of the apex for 1 min. EDTA gel was used as a lubricant throughout the procedure. After instrumentation, canals were flushed with 5 mL of 17% EDTA solution with passive ultrasonic activation for 60 s. Finally, normal saline was used to rinse the canal, and samples were dried with absorbent paper points. The samples were then randomly divided into two groups (n = 40 each) according to the sealer used:

Sealer placement, activation, and obturation

The sealers were placed according to the manufactures’ instructions. A size # 15 K file was used to gently coat the sealers on the canal walls. The samples were then divided into four subgroups according to the sealer activation technique used.

GROUP-U: Ultrasonic agitation – Ultra X with X Gold tips was used at power 2 (45 kHz high frequency), with the file tip kept 2 mm short of root apex for 20 s.

GROUP-L: Lentulospiral agitation – A #20 Lentulospiral that does not bind to the canal was used 2 mm short of the root apex. It was rotated at a speed of 300 rpm for 20 s.

GROUP-G: GP cone-mediated ultrasonic activation – A 0.06 Gutta-Percha (DIADENT) and Ultra X with X GOLD tips at power mode of 2 (45 kHz high frequency) were used.

GROUP-C: Control group – No sealer agitation.

Single cone obturation was used in all the groups after lightly coating the master cone with the sealer at the apical 5 mm. The access was restored by GIC. Samples were then maintained at 37°C and 100% humidity for 7 days.

Micro-CT evaluation

Micro-CT images from the scan were reconstructed using the NRecon (ver. 1.6.10.4, Skyscan) software. The percentage of dentinal tubule penetration of sealers was measured at the coronal, middle, and apical third of the root canal with CT-An (version 1.16.1.0, Skyscan).

Statistical analysis

The data collected was statistically analyzed using the Kruskal-Walli’s test, multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA, and a series of Mann-Whitney U tests. A P ≥ 0.05 indicated no significance (NS), a P ≤ 0.05 indicated significant*, and a P ≤ 0.01 indicated significant***.

DISCUSSION

Calcium silicate-based bioactive sealers have become more popular due to their improved mechanical, chemical, and biocompatible properties. Bioceramic sealers were claimed to exhibit smaller particle size, greater fluidity, hydrophilicity, and high hydraulic conductivity, which allow them to form more sealer tags when in contact with the dentinal walls, allowing for greater bond strength and tubular penetration. Due to its excellent properties, AH Plus is looked as a benchmark (“Gold Standard”) sealer.[789]

In our study, all the agitation techniques caused statistically significant dentinal tubule penetration of both BIO-C (P = 0.001) and AH Plus jet (P = 0.001) at the coronal, middle, and apical thirds of the root canal compared to when no agitation was used. Both BIO-C and AH PLUS sealers showed the highest mean value for depth of penetration with ultrasonic agitation at all the levels of root canals followed by Lentulospiral agitation and then by GP-mediated agitation. The least depth of penetration was observed by the control group for both the sealers at all levels of root canals.

This result shows that the use of agitation techniques significantly improves the sealing ability of both BIO-C and AH Plus jet sealers, and the ultrasonic agitation technique was superior to other methods used. For BIO-C, better penetration depth showed by ultrasonic agitation compared to Lentulospiral agitation was statistically significant only in the coronal third (P = 0.00015), and for AH Plus, this was observed only at the apical third (P = 0.037) of the root canal [Table 1]. This can be because the ultrasonic file propels the sealer along the length of the file, whereas Lentulospiral has an action such that it pushes the sealer centrifugally.[10]

BIO-C clearly showed more dentinal penetration than AH Plus at all the levels of the root canal with and without sealer agitation techniques. In ultrasonic agitation, BIO-C performed better than AH Plus in the coronal (P = 0.01) and middle third (P = 0.05) of the canals [Table 3].

In contradiction to our study, several previous studies have shown that epoxy resin-based sealer possesses superior tubular penetration.[1213] This may be due to the different types of bioceramic used.

In BIO-C, the concentration of calcium silicate is 70% compared to 13% in MTA Fillapex, and it has a smaller particle size of <2 mm. Its flow is 23.46 mm, and the film thickness is 21 mm. AH Plus has a film thickness of 26 mm. Even if the flow of AH Plus is 36 mm, which is higher than BIO-C, the reduced depth of penetration for AH Plus may be due to its larger particle size of 8 mm which makes it difficult to penetrate narrow dentinal tubules, especially apically.

In the present study, both sealers showed poor dentinal penetration in the apical area. Even with ultrasonics, apical dentinal penetration was poor. This might be due to the activated ultrasonic file touching the canal wall in the more constricted area apically and not being able to produce the necessary nodes for acoustic streaming and cavitation.[14]

This could also be due to the fact that both the number and diameter of tubules decrease apically in the root canal.[15] Also, decreased smear layer removal by irrigants in the apical region and retention of moisture may limit the flow of sealer into dentinal tubules.[1516]

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that sealer agitation techniques improve the depth of dentinal tubule penetration of the sealers, and it was poor apically regardless of agitation or the type of sealer. BIO-C achieved significantly better dentinal tubules penetration than AH Plus, regardless of the type of agitation used. BIO-C gives better results with ultrasonic or Lentulospiral agitation, and AH Plus gives better results with ultrasonic agitation.
