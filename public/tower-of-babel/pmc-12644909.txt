ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we investigate a nanomechanically induced transparency (NIT) effect that arises from the coupling of a nanoelectromechanical system (NEM) and a trapped ion. By confining the ion in mesoscopic traps and capacitively coupling it with a nanoelectromechanical system suspended as electrodes, the research is intricately focused on the implications of including the ion’s degrees of freedom. The Lamb-Dicke approximation is crucial to understanding the effects of phonon exchange with electronic qubits and revealing transparency phenomena in this unique coupling. The results underline the importance of the Lamb-Dicke approximation in modelling the effects of transparency windows in nanoelectromechanical systems. Introduction

In recent years, the phenomenon of slow light has been investigated on nanomechanical platforms employing artificial atoms1,2. Alongside other advancements, contemporary scientific research has witnessed significant progress in the field of nanotechnology. Of particular note is the Coulomb interaction between nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS) and trapped ions, as well as multimode optomechanical systems3–13 and Hybrid Circuit14–16 which presents an intrinsic rewriting algebra of Cavity Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)17–19. This convergence of phenomena offers a unique perspective that could be of great relevance to technology and unveil new frontiers in the understanding of quantum state engineering20–22. The NEMS represent an emerging frontier in the miniaturisation of mechanical devices23. With dimensions in the order of nanometers, these systems exhibit unique properties, such as high sensitivity and responsiveness at tiny scales. Trapped ions, on the other hand, are charged particles confined in electromagnetic fields, usually generated by ion traps. These traps offer precise control over the movements and quantum states of the ions, making them ideal components for studying quantum systems and quantum computing applications. Coulomb interaction24–26, in this context, refers to the electrostatic attraction between a charged particle and a charged plate, i.e., a crucial phenomenon when dealing with trapped ions and a NEMS. In the context of the Coulomb interaction between NEMS and trapped ions, a phenomenon analogous to electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT)27,28 can be achieved by precisely controlling the oscillation of the NEMS, resulting in a controlled manipulation of the mechanical response of the materials involved29.

Another intriguing phenomenon that arises from the study of EIT is Fano resonances. The phenomenon of induced transparency arises from quantum interference that suppresses absorption and opens a transparency window in otherwise opaque media27. Originally demonstrated in atomic vapors30, it is now observed in solid-state systems31, metamaterials32, and hybrid quantum devices33. Its relevance extends beyond fundamental interest, with applications ranging from slow light and optical storage to precision spectroscopy and quantum information processing34,35. In contrast to the EIT, the spectral profile of Fano resonances is asymmetric. It has emerged as an essential spectroscopic signature that measures the dynamical and structural characteristics of atoms and molecules. The Italian-American physicist Ugo Fano demonstrated in 1961 that a new resonance behavior with an asymmetric shape is produced when quantum interference occurs between discrete states (resonant scattering via autoionization) and a continuum of states (inelastic scattering of electrons by helium atoms). Out of atomic physics, Fano profiles were found in nanoscale metamaterials and photonic devices36,37 as well as solid-state quantum systems (such as quantum dots and nanowires38). Furthermore, Fano resonance was successfully utilized to engineer the sensing capability in plasmonic nanoclusters39,40 and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS)41.

The first proposal for the trapping of ions between two nanomechanical resonators was made by Tian and Zoller42, where the suspended nanomechanical resonators played the role of tiny electrodes in the trap, acting as nanomechanical resonators with their degrees of freedom. Additionally, Hensinger et al.43 showed that a single trapped ion could be used to test the quantum nature of a mesoscopic mechanical oscillator. The investigation of quantum coherence in mechanical interactions has great relevance for quantum information processing, with proof of experimental feasibility and generation of entanglement44–46. From an experimental point of view, investigating the presence and role of quantum coherence in a wide variety of systems has attracted considerable interest over the past years47. In view of these stimulating results, here we examine the quantum coherence response for a less explored experiment: an ion trap coupled to NEMS. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, we report the existence of a double Fano profile in such an ion-trapped nanoelectromechanical system by characterizing the energy difference between the dressed states and the ground state.

The article is organized as follows. First, we analyze the NEMS-ion trap coupling by modeling and formulating the interaction Hamiltonian for dissipative dynamical calculations in a single-phonon limit. We then obtain the stationary states associated with average values of the transition operators of each physical subsystem. Finally, we obtain the spectra and analyze the induced transparency effects, thus concluding our results and presenting our conclusive justifications.

Although all subsystems are in resonance, it is the difference in coupling strengths that governs the transition from NIT-dominated to Fano-dominated regimes.

Conclusion

In summary, we have analyzed the emergence of nanomechanically induced transparency (NIT) in a hybrid system composed of a nanoelectromechanical resonator coupled to a trapped ion. By employing the Lamb-Dicke approximation, we demonstrated how phonon exchange mediates the interaction between mechanical and electronic degrees of freedom, enabling the appearance of transparency features in the absorption spectrum. Our findings reveal that the spectral profile can be effectively controlled through tunable coupling parameters and that the inclusion of dissipation in the resonator plays a significant role in reshaping the absorption response. These results highlight the potential of such hybrid systems for manipulating mechanical signals at the quantum level and pave the way for future studies of interferometric phenomena in nanoscale architectures, with promising prospects for experimental implementation.
