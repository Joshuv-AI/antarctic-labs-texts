Clinical and radiological results of custom-made glenoid implants in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty with a mean follow-up of 36 months
Authors: Martin Petermann, Puneet Monga, Géza Pap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCustom-made glenoid implants (CMGIs) represent a new technology for treating severe glenoid bone deficiencies. Despite the potential advantages of achieving sufficient primary stability and precise implant positioning, its role is not clear due to the scarcity of clinical studies.MethodsThe first 12 consecutive patients who were treated with CMGIs (ProMade, Enovis) at a single institution were enrolled. At a minimum follow-up (FU) of 2 years (range: 24-76 months; mean 36 months), the clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Constant score, Simple Shoulder Test, and range of motion and compared with pre-operative values. Radiographs were assessed for inferior notching, osteolysis, or other signs of loosening. All complications were recorded.ResultsThere was a significant improvement in Constant score (26 to 71 [P < .001]), Simple Shoulder Test (1.9 to 8.5 [P < .001]), and range of motion in all degrees of freedom (P < .05). There were no radiological signs of osteolysis or loosening. Thirty percent of patients were noted to have grade 1 notching. Two patients were not eligible for a 2-year FU (death from unrelated conditions, recurrent infection). An overall complication rate of 25% included dislocation (n = 1), hematoma (n = 1), and a nondisplaced fracture of the humerus (n = 1).ConclusionCMGIs are an option for the treatment of severe glenoid bone deficiencies at a short- to medium-term follow-up with promising clinical results and no adverse radiological signs. The complication rate is not insignificant, and further studies with longer-term follow-up are suggested.Keywords: Custom, Patient-specific, 3-dimensional-printing, Shoulder, Arthroplasty, Glenoid bone loss

The treatment of severe glenoid bone deficiencies remains challenging. Various traditional surgical options exist, including hemiarthroplasty, eccentric reaming, bone grafting, and wedged glenoid implants.4,11,12,16,17,20, 21, 22,25,32,35,36 However, to date, a gold standard has not been established. The clinical results of these techniques remain mixed, with a corresponding increase in reported complications as the complexity of the glenoid defect increases.17,25,34,38

Recently, custom-made glenoid implants (CMGIs) represent a new alternative with great potential to treat even severe and highly demanding conditions. This technology combines several potential advantages. On one hand, the ability of additive manufacturing may achieve an indispensable stable primary fixation even in the presence of highly severe bone defects. On the other hand, a precise level of implantation with respect to the pre-operative plan may be obtained.5,27,28 Glenoid malpositioning is associated with impaired range of motion (ROM), increased inferior notching, a raised risk for instability and complications, as well as an increased loosening rate.4,8,14,19,26,32 Being able to achieve both primary stability and accurate positioning of the glenoid implant may theoretically result in a satisfying clinical outcome. However, to date, due to the novelty of this method, its clinical value is not clearly defined.

The purpose of this study was to report the clinical and radiological results of CMGIs with a minimum follow-up (FU) of 2 years.

Material and methods

Study design

This retrospective study with prospective data collection reports the clinical and radiographic outcomes of 12 consecutive patients who had reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) (SMR, Limacorporate [now Enovis], San Daniele, Italy) with CMGIs (ProMade, Limacorporate [now Enovis], San Daniele, Italy) for severe glenoid bone deficiency. They represent the first surgical procedures with 3-dimensional-printed glenoid implants at a single institution. All surgeries were performed by the first author over 15 months. All patients were enrolled in this institutional review board-approved study (reference: EK-BR-20/20-01) shortly after surgery. A previous publication comparing implant positioning with pre-operative templating of the same patient population has already been released.28

Patient selection

Symptomatic patients with significant glenoid bone loss were considered for a CMGI when the surgeon had concerns regarding the primary stability and at the same time precise positioning of conventional implants.

Of the 12 patients, 6 were males and 6 females. The mean age at the time of surgery was 67 years (±11.3, range: 49-85). The right/left shoulder ratio was 9/3, and the dominant arm was affected 10 out of 12 times. Nine patients had had prior surgeries (1 prior operation [n = 3], 2 prior operations [n = 3], 3 prior operations [n = 1], 4 prior operations [n = 1], and 9 prior operations [n = 1]) (Table I). A large proportion of the cases (41.7%) involved prior failed anatomic or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (aseptic loosening [n = 4], fracture of the scapula with rTSA [n = 1], infection [n = 1]). The medical history of all the patients was described in detail previously.28 According to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, 7 patients were ASA grade II and 5 patients were ASA grade III. Body mass index varied from 22 to 32 with a mean of 27 ± 3.1.

Pre-operative management

Along with plain radiographs, native computed tomography (CT) scans with a slice thickness of less than 1 mm were obtained for all patients pre-operatively. Due to significant subluxation of the humeral head and/or inadequate rotator cuff function, a reverse design was chosen in all cases. Implant design, surgical steps, and other points of attention were discussed and agreed jointly by the engineer and surgeon before implant production.

Larger metallic implants inside the glenoid vault (eg, loosened baseplate of rTSA) were removed prior to performing a CT scan in order to prevent artifact occurrence. Such a decision was influenced by the ability to evaluate the bony contact area of the future implant and thus optimize implant fitting. Similarly, implants such as penetrating screws of a humeral head plate were removed initially when there was a risk of ongoing remodeling or alteration of the glenoid bone during the time lapse between CT scan and surgery. Screws within the glenoid vault, loosened polyethylene glenospheres without infection, and humeral metal implants (hemiarthroplasty) were left in situ. Surgeries were performed with a median of 80 days (range: 50-143 days) after conducting CT scans.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed in the beach chair position through a deltopectoral approach. A movable arm holder (Trimano, Arthex, Naples, USA) was used. Preparation of the remnants of the glenoid was carried out, taking care not to alter the bone in order to ensure that the definitive implant would fit well. A guide wire was positioned with the aid of 3-dimensional-printed patient-specific instrumentation guides. Further preparation (eg drilling) and checking for precision according to the presurgical plan were also facilitated by a series of custom-made patient-specific instrumentation guides. Detailed management of the CMGIs was previously described.28 The remaining (noncustomized) components of the reverse shoulder arthroplasty were implanted according to the pre-operative plan and the intraoperative situation (soft tissue tensioning, usage of bone cement, etc.).

Post-operative management

Physical therapy was commenced immediately after surgery. Post-operatively, the arm was immobilized in a sling for 6 weeks, allowing passive motion to 90° forward elevation, abduction, and internal rotation. A Continuous Passive Motion device was used during that time. After 6 weeks, active motion without limitation was recommended.

Clinical and radiological evaluation

Clinical and radiological evaluation (anteroposterior, axial, and scapula-Y view radiographs, CT scan) was performed pre-operatively for all patients. Immediately after surgery, radiographs and CT scans were obtained. Further clinical and radiological (anteroposterior, axial, and scapula-Y view radiographs) assessment took place at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months post-operatively. Subsequently, patients were followed up every year. Clinical results were assessed with reference to the ROM (forward elevation, abduction, internal, and external rotation with the upper arm in neutral position), as well as Constant score (CS)5 and Simple Shoulder Test (SST).23 Previously, the implantation accuracy of the glenoid implant of this patient cohort was evaluated by superimposition of the scapulae of pre- and post-operative CT scans.28

Defect severity was classified according to the Antuna classification1 in all cases as “severe.” Our approach to categorize glenoid bone loss compares joint line medialization to the premorbid (unaltered) glenoid vault28 in the scapular axial and coronal planes of the CT scan (perpendicular to both the Friedman line and base of the supraspinatus fossa).10,24 Seven cases (58%) of the cases in this series had “at least 25%, less than 50%” and 5 cases (42%) had “over 50%” bone loss. Defect morphology was categorized into centric, eccentric, and irregular (Table I, Fig. 1). Augment volumes of the custom-made implant as a very rough estimation regarding the quantitative bony defect size were ranging from 5.0 cm3 to 12.4 cm3 (average 9.0 cm3 ± 2.6 cm3).28

Inferior notching was evaluated according to the Sirveax classification.33 Radiological signs of implant loosening were evaluated by the presence of osteolysis or implant migration.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and plot generation were performed by the language and statistical environment R, version 4.0.3. Statistical significance was defined at P < .05. Patients' demographics were reported with descriptive statistics. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check for normal distribution. A 2-sample t-test was used to compare clinical results (CS, SST, ROM). Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to look for correlation and was rated strong at an absolute value > 0.50. Pearson correlation coefficient r was used to measure the correlation between continuous variables. R values >0.5 or <0.5 were considered to show a moderate correlation.

Results

Ten patients were eligible for a minimum FU of 2 years (mean FU: 36 months, range: 24-76 months). One patient died about a year after surgery due to unrelated reasons, and another underwent revision surgery with removal of the implants 22 months after implantation due to recurrent infection.

Clinical assessment

The mean CS improved from 26 to 71 at the final FU (P < .001). Similarly, the SST value increased from 1.9 to 8.5 (P < .001). Ranges of motion improved in all relevant degrees of freedom (forward elevation 55° to 139° [P < .001]; abduction 52° to 139°[P < .001]; internal rotation 63° to 82°[P < .001], and external rotation 7° to 24°[P ≈ .004]). Radiologic assessment

Radiographs at final FU showed no signs of osteolysis or migration of the implant. There were no radiolucent lines found around the custom-made implants. Complications

One female patient underwent a surgical evacuation of a hematoma 2 weeks postoperation. Almost one year afterward, she died from unrelated causes with excellent clinical function at the last FU. Another patient suffered recurrent infection, for which implants had to be removed 22 months after implantation. One patient had an intraoperative nondisplaced fracture of the humeral shaft that was detected on the post-operative CT scans, and this was treated conservatively. A 76-year-old male patient presented with an anterior dislocation on the post-operative radiographs and underwent immediate closed reduction. Subsequently, the shoulder could be stabilized with open revision with distalization and lateralization of the humeral insert. Although the overall complication rate amounts to 25% in this series, complications specific to the CMGI placement or loosening have not been recognized in this study population so far.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that CMGIs with reverse shoulder arthroplasty are able to significantly improve clinical results of patients with severe glenoid bone loss at a FU of 2 to 4 years. Additionally, during this post-operative period, they preserve radiological stability.

The findings reported in this study are consistent with the sparse available literature of comparable implant designs. De Beer et al6 reported in a retrospective observation of 10 patients with an average FU of 30.5 (15-44) months, reporting subjective improvements in 8 of 10 cases without signs of radiological loosening. Twenty percent of the patients (2) suffered complications (brachial plexus impairment, instability).6 Rangarajan et al30 assessed significant clinical improvements in a series of 19 patients with a mean FU of 18.2 months (12-27) with improvement of CS from 24.6 ± 10.2 to 60.4 ± 14.5 (P < .001). In this series, one patient suffered recurrent infection, one case of instability, an intraoperative humeral shaft perforation, and a greater tuberosity fracture, resulting in an overall complication rate of 21%. Similarly, clinical improvements were described in 12 shoulders (11 patients) at an average FU of 30 months (range: 24-52 months) by Bodendorfer et al.3 They do not report any complications, and there were no signs of loosening observed in this series. A study of 6 patients by Porcellini et al29 (FU 31.7 months, range 25-38 months) showed a moderate increase of CS and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons Score of 9.83 ± 5.60 and 30.57 ± 10.77, respectively. Two cases of radiolucent lines were reported, although they were not associated with deteriorating clinical results. Ortmaier et al27 published clinical and radiological data of 10 shoulders (9 patients) with an average FU of 23.1 months (range: 16-30 months). In this series, the mean CS improved significantly (10.9-51.7, P < .001) and SST value from 11 to 52% (P < .001) without complications or signs of loosening. Most recently, Rashid et al31 reported the outcomes of 42 patients with a minimum FU of 2 years from surgery. In this series, CS improved significantly (15-52, P < .001). Three patients underwent revision surgery before their 2-year FU, although the custom-made implants were retained for 2 of these patients.

The comparison of the available literature shows that CMGIs are able to significantly improve the clinical outcomes. On the other hand, even though overall clinical improvement may be observed, complication rates vary and are arguably not insignificant. In addition, comparison is compromised due to different epidemiology, inclusion criteria and variable presurgical conditions. The authors regard their own overall observed complication rate of 25% as typical for the patient population in question, taking into account that these procedures were predominantly used for patients who have challenging preconditions and who have sometimes had numerous prior revision surgeries. Concerning the CMGI itself, neither the authors' results nor comparable results indicate that loosening may play a substantial role in a short-term period. On the contrary, it appears that a sufficient primary fixation can be achieved with CMGIs, even in the presence of severe bone deficiency.

Previously, Petermann et al28 reported results about the implantation accuracy of CMGIs compared to pre-operative templating. No statistical difference between achieved and planned positioning of the implant was found. The results of Ortmaier et al27 and Debeer et al also confirm those findings.5, 27

Based on the authors' experience of using CMGI, which are still not widely adopted, several findings were made. The size of the metallic augment as a rough approximation of the size of the bone defect seems to not distinctly effect on clinical outcome, indicating that treatment with CMGIs can be as successful with large defects as it is with small defects. An upper threshold in terms of defect severity has still not been defined.

The presence of humeral shoulder arthroplasty components did not hinder the overall success of the surgery. The presence of metallic hemiarthroplasty, often used as an interim solution, and its subsequent CT artifacts were occasionally perceived to be of concern but could be managed without initial removal in all cases and without any negative impact on outcomes. The communication between the physician and the design engineer on this matter is particularly valuable, and the confidence of engineers in being able to segment the scans is crucial in decision-making. Of course, standards in terms of infection treatment must be maintained at all times. Ceramic humeral components, glenoid screws, and particularly loosened polyethylene glenospheres did not have deteriorative effects on pre-operative planning and were not removed prior to implant manufacture. Thus, in these cases, an additional surgery could be spared. However, biologically active processes such as aseptic loosening may result in ongoing bone remodeling, and in such cases, in particular, surgeons should be advised to minimize the time between the initial CT scan and surgery to reduce the risk of mismatch of the custom implant. On the contrary, metallic glenoid arthroplasty components were always removed before CT because implantation accuracy would have most likely been compromised.

The small sample sizes in these comparable studies, the heterogeneity of the available publications, and the retrospective nature of existing studies make it hard to define clear indications or boundaries for this technology. Even though various classification systems for glenoid bone defects exist,1,2,9,13,15,18,33,37,39 they cannot give clinical guidelines, nor can prognostic conclusions be drawn from them with reference to CMGIs. The authors’ qualitative way of defining the indication of CMGIs is focused on attaining primary stability of the implant as well as proper positioning at the same time. As the complexity of the surgical case increases with increasing bone insufficiency in the mediolateral direction, the authors report their own decision-making guide, which divides the premorbid glenoid vault into quarters in mediolateral orientation (axial and coronal planes) and identifies the most medial point of deficiency. It gives a quantifiable guideline on whether to consider (at least 25% deficiency) or strongly consider (at least 50% deficiency) a custom-made solution (Fig. 1). The additional information of a defect being centric, eccentric, or irregular gives indications for certain features of custom-made implants, like for example outriggers, flanges, or additional screws. The actual decision should nevertheless be an individual one and should also consider effort, benefit, and risk ratio. This accounts even more as CMGIs are relatively cost-intensive compared to standard or “off-the-shelf” implants. Although formal cost-effectiveness studies are missing, it needs to be considered that with CMGIs, potentially additional surgeries can be avoided (eg, 2-stage surgical procedures with bone grafting). A growing body of scientific evidence may provide arguments for an adequate reimbursement within a specific health care system.

It should be mentioned that there are concerns and unresolved issues about CMGIs. One potential disadvantage is a possibly large medialization of the bone-implant interface, which, together with a very stable fixation, potentially impairs revisability or removal of the implant. Another potential disadvantage is the large amount of metallic lateralization, creating greater torques and, due to an altered transmission of force, may potentially lead to premature loosening.7 One way to neutralize bigger torques as well as to achieve a higher primary stability is to spread the fixation area by using unconventional fixation points like the base of the coracoid or the scapular spine. The aforementioned publications and the authors' results do not suggest that this may be a problem in the short term. However, longer FU, as well as more biomechanical studies, are needed.

A strength of the study is that the patients reported on in the study were not chosen selectively according to their performance. All patients who were treated with patient-specific implants in the medical institution in question were enrolled in the study from the start of their treatment so that a potential learning curve in the procedure would be evident. Another positive aspect of this study was that no patients were lost to FU after the operation.

On the other hand, this study has some inherent limitations, including a small sample size, retrospective analyses, and a fairly short FU. The lack of a control group does not allow comparison of this new technique with other treatments. Observer bias could have been introduced as the surgeries and FU examinations were performed by a single surgeon.

Conclusion

The use of CMGI for the management of severe glenoid bone deficiencies is an option with promising clinical results as well as radiological stability in a short-term FU period. Even though the overall complication rate in the study population is not insignificant, the implant-bone stability achieved by the CMGI itself seems to be reliable in this challenging group of patients. Further studies with longer-term FU are needed to further assess the value of this novel technology.
