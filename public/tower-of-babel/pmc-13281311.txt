Is surgery necessary for mismatch repair-deficient/microsatellite instability-high colorectal cancer patients with a clinical complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy? A retrospective cohort study with literature context
Authors: Hongqun Zuo, Jian Huang, Shengquan Peng, Jie Wei, Yinxiang Luo, Shimeng Qin, Xiaojun Zou, Ju Rong, Yuan Lin, Hao Lai, Xianwei Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to retrospectively compare the prognostic outcomes of patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) who achieved a clinical complete response (CCR) after neoadjuvant immunotherapy (NI) and those who achieved a CCR after surgery. A literature review of publications was conducted in the PubMed database.Materials and methodsThis study included 70 patients who were diagnosed with mismatch repair deficiency/microsatellite instability high (dMMR/MSI-H) colorectal cancer and who were treated with NI between 2018 and 2024. CCR patients were grouped into the “watch and wait” (W&W) method group or the radical surgery group. Afterwards, the oncological and clinical outcomes of patients who achieved a clinical complete response (CCR) were compared to those of patients who were classified as tumour free. We also conducted a literature review of publications in the PubMed database of clinical studies that compared clinical outcomes between W&W and surgery for CCR dMMR/MSI-H patients.ResultsAmong the 70 NI-treated dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients, 44 (62.86%) achieved a CCR. Of these, 25 patients were managed with a watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy, while 19 underwent curative-intent surgery. In the surgery group, 16 patients (84.21%) achieved a pathological complete response (pCR). During follow-up, 2 patients (10.53%) in the surgery group developed recurrence, and both subsequently died, while the remaining 17 patients were alive at the last follow-up. No statistically significant differences were observed between the W&W and surgery groups in terms of recurrence or survival outcomes. A literature review including nine studies further demonstrated comparable oncological outcomes between W&W and surgical management in patients who achieved a CCR.ConclusionPatients in the W&W group presented similar oncological outcomes to those who underwent surgery. Surgery may not be necessary for patients with dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer who achieve a clinical complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy. However, large sample sizes and multicentre investigations are needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04382-8.Keywords: Neoadjuvant immunotherapy, High microsatellite instability, Colorectal cancer, Clinical complete response

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignancy with a high frequency worldwide [1] and represents a particularly high disease burden in China [2]. Recent epidemiological data indicate that CRC is the second most common cancer and the fourth deadliest cancer in the Chinese population, with a steadily increasing trend among younger individuals [2]. This rising prevalence poses a substantial challenge to public health and underscores the urgent need for effective treatment strategies.

CRC can be classified into different subtypes on the basis of underlying genetic or molecular alterations. A particular CRC subtype is characterized by a deficiency in the DNA mismatch repair (dMMR) system, leading to microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status [3]. Approximately 10–15% of patients with CRC exhibit MSI-H, with a higher incidence of young individuals and those with right-sided colon cancer [4]. Compared with other CRC subtypes, dMMR/MSI-H tumours exhibit unique biological behaviours, such as a hypermutated phenotype, increased neoantigen load, and a very active tumour immune microenvironment [5]. These characteristics render MSI-H CRC particularly responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [6]. In early-stage CRC, dMMR is considered a favourable prognostic factor [7] because these tumours are usually less severe and have a decreased likelihood of distant metastasis, which can lead to highly progressed but not metastatic disease [8].

In recent years, immunotherapy has dramatically changed the therapeutic landscape for MSI-H CRC. The results of two clinical investigations, KEYNOTE-177 and CheckMate-142, demonstrated the remarkable potency of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents in MSI-H metastatic CRC [9, 10], establishing immunotherapy as the recommended treatment for this subset of patients. More recently, the application of ICIs in the neoadjuvant setting has shown promising results. Data from several prospective studies and case series have shown that a significant percentage of patients with MSI-H CRC who receive neoadjuvant immunotherapy exhibit a clinical complete response (CCR), with some demonstrating radiological and pathological disappearance of the tumour [11–13].

These observations raise a critical clinical question: Is radical surgery still necessary for patients with a CCR after neoadjuvant immunotherapy? Although surgery is still the primary form of care for patients with localized CRC, the potential morbidity, functional impairment, and effects on quality of life cannot be ignored. Despite a significant pathological response, pseudoresidual lesions are frequently present in dMMR/MSI-H CRCs treated with ICIs due to the shortcomings of radiological evaluation. According to some studies, only 7% to 9% of radiographically verified remaining lesions following ICI exhibit viable tumour cells on biopsy exams, indicating that surgery might not be needed [14, 15]. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that the “watch-and-wait” strategy might be beneficial for selected microsatellite stability (MSS) patients [16–18]; however, very few studies have resolved this controversy in MSI-H/dMMR patients. Current studies are limited by sample size, heterogeneous protocols, and relatively short follow-up durations, preventing direct conclusions [19].

Therefore, the aim of our study was to further explore via a retrospective literature review the necessity of surgery for patients with MSI-H CRC who experience CCR following neoadjuvant immunotherapy. By combining clinical outcomes, our hope was to clarify whether nonoperative management was viable and well tolerated in this unique subgroup. The findings of this study not only provide important insights into individualized treatment strategies but also contribute to the optimization of clinical decision-making, ultimately enhancing patient quality of life and prognosis.

Patients and methods

Retrospective study

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital (Approval Number: KY2026608; Date of Approval: February 1, 2026). This approval covered the retrospective analysis of data collected between 2018 and 2024. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent for study participation was waived by the Institutional Review Board. However, all patients provided written informed consent for their clinical treatments, including neoadjuvant immunotherapy and subsequent management strategies, in accordance with institutional practice. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [20]. The multidisciplinary tumour board (MDT) reviewed each case before treatment initiation and during follow-up.

Study population

Design and setting

This was a single-centre retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients with primarily nonmetastatic or selected oligometastatic colorectal cancer (CRC), characterized as microsatellite instability–high (MSI-H) or mismatch-repair–deficient (dMMR), who were treated with neoadjuvant immunotherapy (NI) at Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital.

Inclusion criteria

(1) A clinical diagnosis of adenocarcinoma of the colon or rectum; (2) clinical stage II–III disease, or selected oligometastatic stage IV disease considered potentially resectable or controllable after neoadjuvant immunotherapy by the multidisciplinary team; (3) MSI-H/dMMR confirmed by immunohistochemistry (deficit in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and/or PMS2) and/or PCR/NGS-based MSI testing; (4) receipt of ≥ 2 cycles of anti–PD-1/PD-L1 neoadjuvant immunotherapy; (5) baseline staging completed with contrast-enhanced CT of the chest/abdomen/pelvis and, for rectal cancers, high-resolution pelvic MRI (HR-MRI); and (6) at least one post-NI clinical and radiologic/endoscopic assessment.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Prior pelvic radiation or systemic therapy for the index tumour (except short course per protocol, see below) before baseline staging; (2) the presence of tumours other than adenocarcinoma; (3) known Lynch syndrome without adequate germline counselling and documentation; (4) active autoimmune disease or chronic immunosuppression contraindicating immune checkpoint inhibitors; (5) uncontrolled infection or concurrent malignancy interfering with endpoints; and (6) incomplete medical records precluding response assessment or follow-up.

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy protocol

Patients received anti–PD-1–based neoadjuvant immunotherapy according to the institutional protocol. Pembrolizumab (200 mg) was administered on a 3-week cycle, nivolumab 240 mg was administered biweekly or 480 mg every four weeks, and toripalimab/camrelizumab/tislelizumab (200–240 mg) were given every 3 weeks, with dosing according to the drug label and clinical status.

Treatment regimens differed according to the tumour location, with colon cancer patients receiving immunotherapy alone and rectal cancer patients receiving combined immunotherapy and chemoradiotherapy. For patients with colon cancer, neoadjuvant immunotherapy was generally administered as monotherapy. For rectal cancer, neoadjuvant treatment was delivered as a combination of immunotherapy and chemoradiotherapy (CRT), in accordance with the tumour location and standard clinical practice.

The treatment duration was typically 2–6 cycles before the first restaging. Combination therapy (short-course chemotherapy such as CAPOX or FOLFOX or dual-checkpoint blockade) was allowed at the MDT’s discretion for bulky disease, threatened margins, or nodal conglomerates and was prospectively recorded. Dose delays or discontinuations followed the product label and Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 for immune-related adverse events; steroid or immunosuppressive management was applied as indicated.

Postimmunotherapy assessment and definition of clinical complete response (CCR) timing

The first re-evaluation was conducted 4–8 weeks after the last NI cycle. If CCR was suspected, a confirmatory assessment was performed 6–12 weeks later before the patient was assigned to the watch-and-wait (nonoperative) management group.

Modalities

Rectal cancer

The clinical examination included digital rectal exam (DRE), high-resolution pelvic MRI (T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted imaging with ADC maps), flexible endoscopy with targeted biopsies (Fig. 1), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA).

Systemic disease

Radiologic response by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 on CT/MRI was utilised for measurable lesions.

Operational definition of a Clinical Complete Response (CCR)

A clinical complete response (CCR) was determined on the basis of international standards as an MRI tumour regression grade of 1 and/or the absence of a noticeable tumour on digital rectal imaging, along with no visible endoscopic tumour or ulcer, presenting only as a flat white scar (with or without telangiectasia) four months after the completion of neoadjuvant therapy. Any result that deviated from this aforementioned description was considered an inclinical complete response.

Pathological complete response (pCR)

pCR is characterized by the absence of any indications of microscopic cancer cells in the colon following resection and after the completion of neoadjuvant therapy.

Patients who did not meet all the criteria were categorized as near-CCR or non-CCR; these patients were counselled towards surgery (standard colectomy with regional lymphadenectomy or total mesorectal excision/TME) or continued NI per the MDT decision. Local recurrence during monitoring was interpreted as any endoscopic, radiologic, or histologic evidence of tumour recurrence at the primary site after an initial CCR and was considered an event for disease-free survival.

Follow-up

Patients in both the surgery and watch-and-wait (W&W) groups were followed according to institutional surveillance protocols consistent with current guideline recommendations, including those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). During the first two years, patients underwent intensive follow-up every 3 months, followed by every 6 months thereafter.

Follow-up assessments included clinical evaluation, serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) measurement, endoscopy, and cross-sectional imaging, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), in accordance with guideline-based surveillance strategies.

In the W&W group, particular emphasis was placed on careful monitoring of the primary tumour site through regular endoscopic and radiological assessments. When recurrence was suspected, additional investigations, including MRI or positron emission tomography (PET), were performed as clinically indicated.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints of this study were tumour recurrence and survival outcomes. All time-to-event variables were calculated from the date of the last cycle of neoadjuvant therapy (including immunotherapy alone or combined immunotherapy and chemoradiotherapy, as applicable).

Recurrence was determined on the basis of histopathological confirmation or radiological and clinical evidence during follow-up. Survival status was assessed at the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies were used to characterize categorical variables, and the X2 test was used for comparisons. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, and SPSS version 24.0 was used to conduct the statistical analyses. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered to define significance.

Literature review

A review of the literature with no restriction on publication year was conducted to compare the clinical outcomes of dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients who underwent W&W or radical surgery. The inclusion criteria were clinical studies that compared the oncological outcomes of patients who underwent CCR using the W&W strategy versus surgery. The exclusion criteria were studies in languages other than English, Portuguese, or Spanish and studies without available full texts and those indexed for PubMed-Medline. An electronic search was performed in PubMed (including MEDLINE) in February 2026. The following combinations of keywords were used in the search: “clinical complete response” OR “CCR” OR “surgery” OR “dMMR”OR “MSI-H” OR “colorectal” OR “colon” OR “rectal AND “neoadjuvant”. All the references were managed, and duplicate hits were removed by reference manager software (Endnote 2025; Thompson Reuters, New York, New York, USA). Those publications that met the eligibility criteria were also included.

Results

In total, 70 MSI-H/dMMR patients were included. No patients with Lynch syndrome were identified. Among them, forty-four patients (62.86%) who received NI achieved a CCR. On the basis of post-NI response and multidisciplinary discussion, twenty-five patients entered the W&W surveillance programme after achieving CCR, and nineteen underwent curative-intent surgery. Surgical procedures were performed according to tumor location and included colorectal resection with curative intent.

The baseline clinicopathologic characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Overall, the baseline features were generally comparable between the watch-and-wait (W&W) group and the surgery group. There were no significant differences between the two groups with respect to the sex distribution (P = 0.58), age (P = 0.11), or body mass index (P = 0.33).

Regarding tumour characteristics, most patients in both groups presented with locally advanced disease, with the majority classified as T3–T4 (W&W: 88.0% vs. surgery: 94.7%, P = 0.45). In terms of TNM stage, most patients had stage II or III disease, whereas a small proportion of patients with stage IV disease were included in both groups, reflecting the inclusion of selected oligometastatic patients according to the predefined eligibility criteria.

Immune-Related Adverse Events (irAEs)

During NI, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were closely monitored in all patients. Overall, irAEs were infrequent and generally mild in this cohort. Endocrine-related toxicities, primarily hypothyroidism, were observed in a small proportion of patients and did not require specific intervention.

Most irAEs were mild to moderate and were managed with symptomatic treatment or short-term corticosteroids when clinically indicated. Severe irAEs were rare, with only one patient experiencing a grade ≥ 3 event (pneumonitis or hepatitis), which was effectively managed without long-term sequelae.

Importantly, no patients experienced life-threatening irAEs or required permanent discontinuation of immunotherapy.

Follow-up

The follow-up duration ranged from 12 to 60 months across the entire cohort, depending on the time of patient enrolment. No postoperative complications were observed. However, at the end of follow-up, three patients (12%) died of disease resurgence in the W&W group; one died of liver metastasis, and the other two died of lung metastasis. Two recurrences (10.53%, 2/19) were observed in the surgery group, and all the cases involved liver metastases and resulted in death due to disease progression (10.53%, 2/19). There was no difference (P > 0.05) in tumour recurrence or survival between the groups (Table 4).

Among the patients who achieved CCR, 104 were managed with a W&W strategy, while approximately 38 patients subsequently underwent surgery despite achieving CCR. Among the patients who underwent surgery after CCR, at least 32 were confirmed to have a pathological complete response (pCR), indicating a high concordance between clinical and pathological complete response in this population.

With respect to oncologic outcomes, the majority of studies reported very low recurrence rates among patients managed with W&W after achieving CCR. In several cohorts, including those reported by Yang et al. [19], Cercek et al. [21], and Yu et al. [26], no local regrowth or distant metastasis was observed during follow-up. In contrast, the retrospective cohort reported by Hongqun Zuo et al. revealed three recurrences in the W&W group and two recurrences in the surgery group, representing the only study in which recurrence events were reported for both management strategies.

Survival outcomes were reported in a limited number of studies. Yang et al. reported 100% two-year disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) [19], whereas Yu et al. reported 100% three-year DFS and OS among patients managed with W&W after achieving cCR [26]. Several other studies reported excellent short-term survival but did not provide mature survival estimates.

The duration of follow-up varied considerably across studies. The median follow-up ranged from 7.9 months to approximately 29.1 months in most prospective studies. In contrast, the retrospective study by Hongqun Zuo et al. reported a follow-up duration ranging from 12 to 60 months, largely reflecting differences in the timing of patient enrolment rather than a systematic difference between treatment groups. Overall, although the current evidence suggests favourable oncologic outcomes for selected patients managed with a W&W strategy after immunotherapy-induced CCR, longer follow-up and larger prospective trials are needed to confirm the long-term durability and safety of this approach.

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis, we demonstrated that if the CCR is exclusively evaluated by CT/MRI and colonoscopy biopsy, then major surgery or a W&W strategy may be equivalent. By the end of the follow-up period, 22 patients (22/25, 88.0%) in the watch-and-wait group remained free of tumour recurrence. In early-stage CRC, dMMR is generally regarded as a positive prognostic factor [28]. In other words, dMMR tumours are typically less aggressive and have a decreased chance of distant metastasis, which could lead to cancers that are very advanced locally but not metastatic [29]. Thus far, previous studies, primarily conducted in MSS populations, have consistently demonstrated no significant differences between the watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy and surgical management in terms of local recurrence and distant metastasis. In line with these findings, our results further suggest that the W&W approach yields comparable oncological outcomes to surgery, without statistically significant differences in recurrence or survival, in patients with dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer.

One of the most striking findings across these studies is the high rate of CCR following immunotherapy. Early prospective evidence from Cercek et al. demonstrated that all 12 patients treated with the PD-1 inhibitor dostarlimab achieved CCR, with no evidence of disease progression during follow-up [21]. Similarly, Yang et al. reported a complete response in 18 of 20 patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy [19]. Other studies, including those by Chen et al., Li et al., and Yu et al., also reported substantial CCR rates following immunotherapy [22, 24, 26]. These findings support the concept that immune checkpoint inhibitors are highly effective in tumours with mismatch repair deficiency or microsatellite instability, which are characterized by high tumour mutational burden and increased immunogenicity.

Another important observation is the increasing feasibility of the W&W strategy in patients who achieve CCR after immunotherapy. In several studies, including those by Zhang et al., Cercek et al., Yang et al., and Yu et al. [21, 26, 27], patients who achieved CCR were managed without immediate surgery and experienced excellent oncologic outcomes. Notably, Yu et al. reported a three-year disease-free survival and overall survival rate of 100% among patients who underwent W&W after achieving cCR following anti-PD-1-based therapy [26]. These findings are consistent with earlier experiences of W&W management after chemoradiotherapy in patients with rectal cancer and suggest that immunotherapy-induced CCR may allow for safe organ-preserving treatment strategies in carefully selected patients.

Despite the encouraging results of W&W management, several patients with CCR still underwent surgery. Interestingly, a substantial proportion of these patients were confirmed to have a pathological complete response (pCR). For example, Yang et al. reported that all 11 patients who underwent surgery after achieving a CCR achieved a pCR [19]. Similarly, Chen et al. and Fang et al. reported that patients who underwent surgery after CCR were pathologically tumour free [22, 23]. These findings suggest a strong concordance between clinical and pathological complete response after immunotherapy. However, they also highlight an important clinical dilemma: whether surgery is necessary in patients who already appear to have achieved complete tumour regression.

According to earlier research, more than 60% of dMMR CRC patients achieve a pCR with immunotherapy [30–32]. Immunotherapy and nonoperative management have become more popular as novel therapeutic choices for individuals with dMMR rectal cancer because of the high rates of pCR [33]. However, the poor connection between pathological and radiological assessments makes the adoption of this technique in dMMR colon cancer extremely difficult. In our study, 62.86% (44/70) of patients achieved a clinical complete response (CCR) after neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

In the MSS group of patients, one study summarized seventy “watch and wait” studies; the local recurrence rate was 21.6%, and the mortality rate and distant metastasis rate after 3 years were 6.8% and 6.5%, respectively [34]. Nonetheless, the patient selection criteria and research protocol of the investigated studies exhibited significant bias. Another study examined data from 1009 patients from the International Watch & Wait Database (IWWD), including data from 47 collaborating institutes from 15 countries [35]. These rates were comparable to those reported by Dattani et al. The rates of local recurrence (2 years) and distant metastasis (3 years) were 25.2% and 8%, respectively. Repair surgery was achievable in 78% of patients with local recurrence, and curative R0 resection was achieved in nearly all patients (99%), with 94% disease-specific survival over 5 years. In our study, tumour recurrence was experienced by 3 patients (12%) in the watch-and-wait group, but as no salvage surgery was performed, they died of multiple metastases. The recurrence rate was completely different from that reported in other studies [34, 35]. This phenomenon might be due to the limited population size in our study and requires further exploration.

There is currently little research that compares surgical intervention to a watch-and-wait approach in terms of the oncological prognosis. In our retrospective investigation, no statistically significant differences were observed between the watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy and surgery in terms of recurrence or survival outcomes. However, the most critical issue with the W&W method is that clinical observation alone is a poor predictor of pathologic CR, and no reliable clinical or molecular indicators exist to improve its accuracy. ctDNA can provide predictive information, but it has not yet been included as a basis for CCR in clinical guidelines, and there is a lack of evidence to support its utility. Maas et al. argued that with a precise definition of clinical CR, the watch-and-wait method could be a viable and safe therapeutic choice [36]. They proposed the following criteria for the clinical definition of CR: (1) no residual tumour and (2) no questionable lymph nodes on MRI; (3) no endoscopic residual tumour; (4) a negative biopsy; and (5) no palpable tumour. However, a different study that enrolled patients with MSS using identical inclusion criteria and positron emission tomography revealed an LR rate of 41.7% (5/12) at a median follow-up of 26 months [37]. Similar selection criteria for dMMR/MSI-H patients were applied in our study. Therefore, clinicians largely concur that the watch-and-wait method cannot be a routine management strategy, despite some studies suggesting otherwise. To draw a firm conclusion, long-term follow-up data from extensive observational studies are needed.

In our findings, 84.21% (16/19) of the patients who underwent surgery achieved pCR, with no postoperative complications observed. In our cohort, tumour recurrence was observed in 3 patients (12%) in the W&W group, all of whom subsequently died because of metastatic progression. In the surgery group, 2 patients (10.53%) developed recurrence, and both eventually died. Despite the occurrence of these events, no statistically significant differences were identified between the two groups in terms of recurrence or survival outcomes (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that the W&W strategy is a feasible option for carefully selected patients, with oncological outcomes comparable to those of surgery, potentially sparing them from the morbidity associated with radical resection.

However, a key consideration in choosing between surgery and W&W is the delicate balance between organ preservation and tumour recurrence risk. The advantage of W&W lies in preserving organ function and reducing treatment-related complications such as sexual dysfunction, urinary issues, and intestinal complications. However, the challenge lies in accurately predicting the long-term outcomes for these patients, as clinical and radiological assessments sometimes fail to fully correlate with pathological responses. For example, pseudoresidual lesions on imaging may not necessarily indicate the presence of viable tumour cells, further complicating the decision to avoid surgery. In the context of tumour recurrence, the risk of late recurrence post-W&W is still a concern, especially as some studies suggest that up to 20% of patients may experience recurrence after a few years. In our study, 12% of the W&W group experienced recurrence, highlighting the importance of close monitoring during follow-up.

The clinical implications of a W&W strategy differ between rectal and colon cancer. In rectal cancer, organ preservation is of particular importance because radical surgery is associated with a substantial risk of stoma formation and long-term bowel, urinary, and sexual dysfunction. In contrast, although a nonoperative approach may be feasible in selected colon cancer patients with sustained clinical complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy, the current evidence remains limited, and its oncologic safety is less well established. Therefore, tumor location should be considered when interpreting the applicability of the W&W strategy and in future clinical decision-making, these considerations are supported by previous studies demonstrating the clinical rationale and feasibility of organ preservation strategies in rectal cancer [38, 39].

There are various limitations that must be considered when the findings of our study are interpreted. A key limitation of the current studies on neoadjuvant immunotherapy for dMMR/MSI-H locally advanced colorectal cancer is the lack of comprehensive data on disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). While several studies have demonstrated promising clinical outcomes, particularly in terms of clinical complete response (CCR) and pathological complete response (pCR), the majority of existing research focuses primarily on recurrence rates. There is a notable scarcity of long-term survival data for assessing the durability of these responses, particularly in the context of the watch-and-wait strategy. Therefore, larger, multicentre studies with extended follow-up are necessary to better understand the long-term DFS and OS outcomes for patients who are receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy and those opting for organ-preserving strategies. Second, we were unable to perform a multivariate survival analysis because of the limited population size. Thus, it was difficult to manage confounding variables. Finally, the sample size of each comparative group was small, and the follow-up period was brief in light of the general clinical history of rectal cancer, in which distant metastases can appear years after therapy. Local resurgence most often occurs during the first 2 years post-treatment, complicating the assessment of surgical efficacy for patients with CR to neoadjuvant therapy (NAT).

In conclusion, while neoadjuvant immunotherapy with PD-1 inhibitors offers an effective alternative to traditional chemoradiotherapy, the decision to proceed with surgery or adopt the W&W strategy should be individualized. The W&W approach offers the potential for organ preservation and similar survival outcomes to surgery in selected patients, but careful patient selection, including robust imaging and endoscopic evaluation, remains crucial. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are necessary to confirm the long-term safety and efficacy of the W&W strategy in patients with dMMR/MSI-H LACRC. Additionally, predictive biomarkers, such as circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), may play a key role in refining the selection criteria for W&W patients, offering a more accurate way to predict tumour recurrence and guide clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

Patients in the W&W group presented similar oncological outcomes to those who underwent surgery. Surgery may not be necessary for patients with dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer who achieve a clinical complete response after neoadjuvant immunotherapy. However, large sample sizes and multicentre investigations are needed to validate these findings.
