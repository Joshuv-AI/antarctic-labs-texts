Fgf18 marks reserve skeletal progenitors in the fibrous periosteum contributing to bone fracture repair
Authors: Xing Ji, Jasmin Koehnken Sawall, Xuejun Zhang, David M. Ornitz, Fanxin Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe periosteum, a thin membranous tissue covering the external bone surface, is a well-established source of osteogenic activity during bone growth and fracture repair. However, the molecular identity and anatomical niche of its progenitor cells remain incompletely defined. Using single-cell RNA sequencing and RNA in situ hybridization in mouse long bones, we identified that fibroblast growth factor 18 (Fgf18) expression marks a discrete subset of cells within the fibrous layer of the periosteum that co-express genes associated with skeletal stem and progenitor populations. Lineage tracing revealed that Fgf18+ cells contribute minimally to cortical bone formation under homeostatic conditions but robustly expand and generate chondrocytes and osteoblasts following injury. Targeted ablation of Fgf18-lineage cells impaired fracture healing, confirming their functional importance. Together, these findings define Fgf18+ fibrous periosteal cells as a reserve progenitor population that is activated by injury to regenerate bone.

INTRODUCTION

The periosteum, a thin membrane covering the bone surface, plays a critical role in bone growth and repair. The osteogenic role of the periosteum was first demonstrated by the French botanist Henri-Louis Duhamel (1700–1782) through his classic madder root (containing the red pigment alizarin)-feeding experiments in pigs, which revealed alternating red and white rings in bone cross-sections.1,2 He proposed that periosteal activity is responsible for transverse bone growth and named its inner layer the “cambium,” by analogy to the growth zone of trees. Subsequent transplantation studies across multiple species confirmed that new bone forms from periosteal grafts only when the cambium layer remains intact.3 In vitro and ectopic transplantation experiments further showed that periosteal cells can generate both bone and cartilage, demonstrating their osteo-chondrogenic potential.4,5 More recently, genetic-lineage-tracing studies in mice have identified the periosteum as a niche for skeletal stem cells with robust regenerative capacity.6

Microscopic analyses have revealed distinct structural layers within the periosteum. Electron microscopy of cranial periosteum revealed three zones: zone I, a single osteoblastic layer adjacent to bone; zone II, a matrix-rich region containing capillaries; and zone III, an outer fibrous layer enriched in collagen fibers.7 Zones I and II together correspond to the classical cambium layer. Other ultrastructural studies distinguished the osteogenic cells of the cambium from the fibroblasts in the fibrous layer.8 Autoradiographic labeling with tritiated thymidine further demonstrated that osteoblasts and adjacent preosteoblasts form a self-sustaining osteogenic layer, whereas the outer fibrous layer contributes little to bone formation.9,10 These early observations established the cambium as the principal osteogenic compartment of the periosteum.

Recent studies have focused on identifying molecular markers that define periosteal progenitor populations. Nestin+ or Lepr+ cells reside in the outer layers of the periosteum in developing and adult mice, respectively, and contribute to cortical bone formation and repair.11 Gli1+ cells, though rare in the adult periosteum under homeostasis, contribute substantially to fracture healing.12,13 Several populations marked by Mx1, αSMA, CD51, Itm2a, or Itgav (CD51) participate primarily in bone regeneration, whereas Prx1+, Ctsk+, CD200+, or Postn+ subsets contribute to both periosteal growth and repair.14-21 Notably, Sp7−Pdgfra+ cells within the fibrous periosteal layer contribute to osteogenesis selectively during repair but not homeostasis.22 However, because Pdgfra is broadly expressed among mesenchymal cells, distinct markers for repair-specific progenitors remain undefined. Moreover, it is unclear how these molecularly defined cell populations relate to one another within the periosteum.

Beyond osteogenesis, the periosteum along long bone shafts anchors tendons and ligaments through fibrous entheses, which differ histologically from the fibrocartilaginous entheses found at epiphyseal sites.23 While the cellular and molecular organizations of fibrocartilaginous entheses have been well characterized, the molecular identity of fibrous entheses remains poorly defined.24

Here, we combined single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with spatial mapping to profile periosteal cells in mouse long bones. We identified a specialized periosteal population with osteo-chondro-myofibroblastic features localized to fibrous entheses, and discovered Fgf18 as a selective marker for a reserve skeletal progenitor (SP) pool in the fibrous periosteum, which becomes activated upon injury to drive bone repair.

RESULTS

scRNA-seq reveals heterogeneity among periosteum resident cells

To delineate the cellular composition of the mature periosteum, we performed scRNA-seq with enzymatically digested periosteal cells from the long bones of 8-week-old mice. The cells were purified by flow cytometry to deplete hematopoietic lineage cells before scRNA-seq. A total of 14,411 cells were successfully sequenced with an average of 2,841 genes identified per cell. Initial clustering identified 21 distinct clusters (Figure 1A). Molecular annotation based on the top 20 enriched genes, together with the published marker genes, identified the clusters as endothelial cells (clusters 0, 9, and 10; Emcn; Cdh5; Pecam1; and Vwf), smooth muscle cells (clusters 5, 7, and 18; Acta2; Myh11; Kcnj8; and Rgs5), Schwann cells (cluster 14, Plp1, Cmtm5, Mpz, and Mbp), skeletal muscle cells (cluster 6, Acta1, Mylk4, Myot, and Actn2), and skeletal muscle satellite cells (cluster 8, Pax7, Myf5, Asb5, and Cdh15) (Figure 1B, Table S1). In addition, clusters 1, 2, 12, and 15 expressed varying levels of Runx2, along with Postn, Chad, or Bglap, and were termed osteo-lineage cells here, whereas cluster 11 represented tenocytes marked by Kera, Tnmd, Scx, and Mkx (Figure 1B, Table S1). The cells in clusters 3, 4, and 19, like the endothelial cells, expressed high levels of Ly6a (Ly6ahi) (encoding Sca1), but they were also enriched for CD34, Pdgfra, and Mfap5. These features were previously associated with fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) in the skeletal muscle25-27 (Figure 1B). To assess whether they were indeed FAPs, we performed integrated analyses with the published skeletal muscle dataset.26 Indeed, the non-endothelial Ly6ahi supercluster (CD34+, Pdgfra+, and Mfap5+) completely overlapped between the two datasets (Figures 1C and 1D). Thus, the large number of FAPs in our dataset were likely derived from the skeletal muscle that was not fully removed during dissection. However, we cannot rule out that cells of the same identity may reside within the periosteum. The integration analyses also detected smooth muscle cells (Acta2+) and tenocytes (Kera+) in both datasets, but endothelial cells (Ly6a+ and Emcn+) were far more abundant in our dataset (Figure 1D). Finally, the Runx2+ osteo-lineage cells were largely limited to our dataset, confirming their periosteum origin (Figure 1D).

We next performed further clustering with the Runx2+ periosteal cells, resulting in five clusters (Figure 2A). Most of the cells, including those in clusters 0, 1, and 3, were enriched with classic osteoblast markers Alpl and Bglap (Figure 2B). Further analyses of gene expressions showed that cluster 1 cells exhibited the highest levels of Runx2 and Alpl, both early markers for the osteoblast lineage, in addition to modest levels of Postn, Bglap2, and Sp7 and, therefore, likely represented preosteoblasts (Figure 2C). Cluster 3 cells appeared to be mature osteoblasts as they strongly expressed Bglap, Ifitm5, Ibsp, and Car3 (Figure 2C). Cluster 0 was unique in its expression of not only the highest level of Postn but also the myofibroblast markers Tagln and Acta2 as well as a chondrocyte marker Acan; it, therefore, represented a hybrid cell type not previously described but shown in this study to be enthesis cells (see following sections) (Figures 2B and 2C). Of the remaining cells, the major cluster 2 as well as the minor cluster 4 expressed multiple markers, such as Ly6a, CD34, Pdgfra, Lepr, and Prrx1, which have previously been associated with periosteal skeletal stem and progenitor cells. While Ly6a, CD34, and Pdgfra levels were higher in cluster 4, Lepr and Prrx1 were more enriched in cluster 2. Several other reported stem/progenitor cell markers, including CD200, Ctsk, Itm2a, and Itgav (encoding CD51), were also detected but were not selective for clusters 2 or 4, whereas Nes was minimally expressed only in osteoblasts (Figure 2C). Notably, CD200 and Itgav exhibited the strongest expression in mature osteoblasts and preosteoblasts, respectively (Figure 2C). We next used the CytoTRACE2 computational method to predict the developmental potential of the various clusters. The results ranked clusters 2 and 4 with the highest potency score, indicating them as potential progenitors (Figure 2D). We have, therefore, hereafter named them skeletal progenitors 1 and 2 (SP1 and 2), respectively. Thus, by employing scRNA-seq, we have delineated multiple distinct periosteal cell types, including presumptive SP cells.

Besides the known markers discussed earlier, we found several other genes, including Fgf18, Chad, and Comp, enriched among the potential progenitor cells, especially SP1 (Figures 2B and 2C). In fact, Fgf18 expression in the potential progenitors was readily detectable among the osteo-lineage (OL) cells even when all cells captured in the periosteum digestion were analyzed in toto (Figure 1B). On the other hand, a strong expression of Fgf18+ in SP clusters among OL cells was absent from the skeletal muscle dataset, even though low Fgf18 expression was present in the FAPs (Figure 1D). A survey of all FGF pathway genes showed that Fgf2 and Fgf7 were similarly enriched in SPs (Figure 2E). The receptors Fgfr1, 2, and 3, on the other hand, were broadly expressed and most concentrated in the preosteoblast cluster 1. In addition, Gli1, a Hedgehog (Hh) target gene previously shown to mark periosteal progenitors, was selectively elevated in SP1 (Figure 2E). Likewise, another prototypical Hh target Ptch1 was strongly activated, indicating active Hh signaling in the potential progenitors even though the Hh ligands themselves were hardly detected in any of the periosteal cells (Figure 2E). Thus, the results uncover new potential markers including Fgf18 for periosteal SPs.

Spatial mapping of periosteum resident cell types

We next sought to map the different cell types in the periosteum. The cells with hybrid molecular identity have not been described previously. To determine their location, we performed in situ mRNA hybridization with RNAscope for Postn, Acan, and Tagln, which together marked the cell type. Postn was detected ubiquitously along the exterior bone surface, demarcating the inner layer of periosteum, which is consistent with the known position of preosteoblasts (Figure 3A). Notably, the Postn+ layer was selectively thickened at the fibrous attachment site of a tendon (Figure 3B). Acan, marking the growth plate cartilage as expected, was absent in most of the periosteum except for the tendon attachment site (Figures 3C and 3D). Likewise, Tagln was selectively enriched in the thickened periosteum (Figures 3E and 3F). Thus, the osteo-chondro-myofibroblast hybrid cells appear to define the thickened inner periosteum associated with fibrous enthesis.

We next sought to localize the presumptive skeletal progenitor cells. For this, we first performed RNAscope with three of the marker genes, Prrx1, Comp, and Chad, as indicated by the scRNA-seq analysis discussed earlier. Prrx1 was detectable in the full thickness of the periosteum but with higher expression in the outer than in the inner layer (Figures 3G and 3H). Comp and Chad, on the other hand, appeared to be restricted to the outer periosteum (Figures 3I-3L). At the fibrous enthesis, Prrx1 was expressed throughout the thickness whereas Comp and Chad were limited to the outer layer, overlaying the hybrid cells in the inner periosteum. Thus, the presumptive periosteal progenitor cells appear to reside in the outer layer of periosteum.

Marking of potential periosteal skeletal progenitors by Fgf18

Our scRNA-seq analyses identified Fgf18 as a potential marker for the periosteal progenitors. Therefore, to further localize the potential progenitors, we took advantage of the Fgf18CreERT2 knockin mouse strain that expressed CreERT2 from the endogenous Fgf18 gene locus.28 Mice with the genotype of Fgf18CreERT2/+; ROSAtdTomato/+ (Fgf18-tdTomato) were injected with tamoxifen (TAM) at 8 weeks of age and harvested 24 h later. The tdTomato signal, reflecting active Fgf18 expression, was detected in a thin layer along the exterior bone surface (Figure S1A). A closer examination revealed that the Fgf18+ cells resided in the outer periosteum around the metaphysis and at the fibrous enthesis and also within the single-layer periosteum in areas of the diaphysis, consistent with previous studies28 (Figure S1B). Fgf18 expression was similarly observed in the periosteum of the tibia (Figures S1C and S1D). Based on their exterior location and elongated morphology, the Fgf18+ cells likely reside in the fibrous periosteum. To further corroborate the observation, we performed immunofluorescence for Osterix (OSX, official name SP7) to mark the cambium layer according to previous studies22 (Figure 4A). In all areas of the periosteum exhibiting a multi-layer organization, Fgf18+ cells were detected invariably exterior to the OSX+ cells, thus confirming their fibrous layer localization (Figure 4B).

As Pdgfra was reported to mark skeletal progenitors in the fibrous layer, we next sought to determine the spatial distribution of Fgf18-versus Pdgfra-positive cells in the periosteum. For this, we used the PdgfraH2BGFP/+ (Pdgfra-GFP) mouse that expressed nuclear GFP from the endogenous Pdgfra gene locus. Mice with the genotype of Fgf18-tdTomato;Pdgfra-GFP were induced with TAM at 8 weeks of age and harvested 24 h later. GFP detection indicated that Pdgfra was broadly expressed in the marrow, along the trabecular and cortical bone surfaces, as well as in the skeletal muscle (Figures 4C and 4D). Importantly, Pdgfra-GFP expression was detected in most Fgf18+ cells in either the fibrous periosteum around the distal metaphysis or the single-layer periosteum at the diaphysis (yellow nuclei) (Figures 4C and 4D). Quantification of three sections each in three mice (see STAR Methods) suggested that an estimated ~60% (59.3% ± 11.5% SD) of the Fgf18+ periosteal cells also expressed Pdgfra-GFP; the estimation likely underreported the extent of co-expression as GFP was nuclear-targeted, but not all Fgf18+ cells presented a nucleus on the sections. Similarly, when Fgf18+ cells were marked with TAM at 5 months of age in the Fgf18-tdTomato; Pdgfra-GFP mice, most Fgf18+ cells in the fibrous periosteum co-expressed Pdgfra-GFP (Figures 4E and 4F). Thus, compared with the broadly expressed Pdgfra, Fgf18 selectively marks certain cells within the outer fibrous periosteum.

Our scRNA-seq results showed that CD34 was enriched in the SP populations along with Fgf18. Therefore, we next examined CD34 expression in the periosteum. For this, we performed immunostaining for CD34 on femoral sections from the Fgf18-tdTomato; Pdgfra-GFP mice above. CD34 was readily detected in the fascia and skeletal muscle, where it largely overlapped with Pdgfra-GFP; those within the skeletal muscle likely included FAPs (Figures 4G and 4H). In the multi-layer periosteum around the distal metaphysis, CD34 clearly demarcated the endothelial cells of blood vessels that were richly distributed within the inner layer (Figure 4H, left, middle, and asterisks). However, CD34 was conspicuously absent in the Fgf18+ cells of the outer layer (Figure 4H, left and middle). On the contrary, in the single-layer periosteum around the diaphysis, CD34 was detected in most of the Fgf18+ cells (Figure 4H, right). Quantification in the diaphyseal periosteum on three sections each of three mice confirmed CD34 positivity in ~60% (61.4% ± 5.5% SD) of the Fgf18+ cells. Thus, CD34 and Fgf18 mark both distinct and overlapping cell populations in the periosteum depending on the location along the length of the long bone.

Minimal contribution of Fgf18+ periosteal cells to normal bone growth

We next examined whether the Fgf18+ cells contributed to normal bone growth. We first evaluated their potential progression to preosteoblasts in young adult mice, which exhibited rapid outward growth of the cortical bone. Fgf18-tdTomato mice were induced with TAM at 8 weeks of age and harvested 24 h later. Consistent with RNAscope, immunostaining detected Periostin along the exterior cortical bone surface, marking preosteoblasts (Figure 5A). The Fgf18+ red cells were not always detected in the same periosteal region as Periostin, but wherever they were codetected, the red cells invariably resided outside the Periostin+ green layer, thus confirming Fgf18 expression in the outer periosteum (Figures 5B and S2A). We next tracked the fate of the Fgf18+ cells by harvesting the Fgf18-tdTomato mice 8 weeks after TAM injection (Figure 5C). Again, immunostaining showed that the tdTomato+ cells remained outside the Periostin+ green inner layer, with only occasional overlap of the red and green (Figures 5D and S2B). Moreover, no tdTomato+ osteocytes were observed in the cortical bone after chasing. Thus, the Fgf18+ periosteal cells normally contribute little to the osteogenic pool in the periosteum.

To assess whether the Fgf18+ cells contribute to cortical bone formation over a longer period, we marked them in Fgf18-tdTomato mice with TAM at 8 weeks and looked for their potential production of osteocytes after 6 months. Again, essentially no contribution was observed in the cortical bone of the femur, except for a few labeled osteocytes confined to a very small area (Figure S3). To test the possibility that the Fgf18+ cells might contribute to cortical bone growth during earlier postnatal life, we labeled the cells at postnatal day 4 (P4) and traced them to 8 weeks of age (Figure 5E). No red osteocytes were observed in the midshaft or the proximal half of the femur, even though the tendon with direct insertion at the diaphysis was clearly labeled (Figures 5F and 5G). The tendon labeling was interesting as mature tenocytes did not appear to express Fgf18 according to the scRNA-seq results, thus indicating that the labeled tenocytes descended from Fgf18+ progenitors (Figures 1B and 1D). At the distal metaphysis of the femur, Fgf18-lineage cells were seen not only in the periosteum but also near the trabecular and endocortical bone surface (Figure 5G, left). The occasional red osteocytes were likely descendants of the endosteal Fgf18+ cells because of endocortical growth. The trabecular and endosteal distribution of Fgf18-lineage cells were more evident in the proximal end of the tibia and likewise, more of their osteocyte descendants were observed (Figure S4). However, in the midshaft or the distal half of the tibia, Fgf18-lineage cells only resided in the periosteum and no red osteocytes were detected (Figure S4). Thus, the results so far indicate that the Fgf18+ periosteal cells do not appear to be a tangible source of osteoblasts or osteocytes during either rapid growth or homeostasis of the cortical bone.

The scRNA-seq results showed co-expression of Fgf18 and Ly6a (encoding Sca1) among the potential periosteal progenitors. Therefore, to corroborate the findings above, we next examined the potential contribution of the Ly6a-lineage cells to the cortical bone in growing mice with the genotype of Ly6aCre/+; ROSA26tdTomato/+ (Ly6a-tdTomato) at 8 weeks of age. The data showed only sporadic tdTomato signal in the periosteum, contrary to the more notable expression in tendon sheets, skeletal muscle, and arterioles (Figure S5A). Consistent with the rarity of Ly6a-tdTomato cells in the periosteum, RNAscope for Ly6a detected little signal in periosteal cells (Figures S5B and S5C). Importantly, virtually no tdTomato+ osteocytes were detected inside the cortical bone. The data, therefore, further support the notion that the Fgf18+ presumptive progenitor cells, perhaps co-expressing Ly6a, do not actively participate in periosteal bone accrual during normal growth.

To monitor bone formation directly, we used calcein to fluorescently label the active bone surface. We first examined the potential relationship between the Fgf18+ periosteal cells and the sites of active cortical bone formation. Fgf18-tdTomato mice were injected with calcein twice, 2 weeks apart, before TAM induction at 8 weeks of age and harvested 24 h later (Figure 5H). The double labeling with calcein revealed that the main front of cortical bone formation shifted from the periosteum at the proximal half to the endosteum at the distal half, with the transition occurring around the mid diaphysis (Figure 5I). The Fgf18+ red cells were present as a single, non-continuous layer along the exterior bone surface (Figure 5J). Importantly, the Fgf18+ cells were detected near both active and inactive cortical bone surfaces, and their abundance did not correlate with the extent of double labeling (Figure 5J). Thus, the presence of the Fgf18+ periosteal cells appears to be unrelated to normal cortical bone outgrowth.

We next performed lineage tracing experiments to determine whether the Fgf18+ cells, with time, would contribute to active cortical bone formation. For this, the Fgf18-tdTomato mice were injected with TAM at 8 weeks of age and harvested after 4 weeks, following two injections of calcein within the final two weeks (Figure 5K). In all areas with active periosteal bone formation, the Fgf18+ red cells invariably remained outside the calcein labels (Figures 5L and 5M). The data, therefore, further support that the Fgf18+ periosteal cells do not actively contribute to cortical bone growth.

Robust contribution to fracture healing by Fgf18+ skeletal progenitor cells

To determine the regenerative potential of the Fgf18+ periosteal cells, we examined fracture healing in mice subjected to an open femur semi-stabilized fracture procedure. Fgf18-tdTomato mice were induced with TAM one week before being subjected to fracture at 8 weeks of age and harvested at various time points thereafter. To assess the early response, we first analyzed the mice at one week after fracture. EdU was injected 4 h before harvest to allow for cell proliferation assays. Extensive tdTomato labeling was observed around the fracture site, indicating a dramatic increase of Fgf18+ periosteal cells (Figure 6A). EdU labeling was most prominent in the bone marrow, consistent with rapid hematopoiesis. Importantly, EdU was also evident among the tdTomato+ cells near the surface of the callus (Figure 6B). Quantification on three sections each of three mice indicated that ~14% (±6% SD) of the Fgf18-lineage cells incorporated EdU. In contrast, no EdU labeling was observed among the tdTomato+ periosteal cells in the contralateral femur, indicating relative quiescence of the Fgf18+ cells in the uninjured periosteum (Figure S6). To assess potential differentiation of the Fgf18-lineage cells, we performed immunostaining for the preosteoblast marker Periostin. The protein was readily detected both in the periphery of the callus and around the pin in the medullary space (Figure 6C). Importantly, the outer most layer of the callus was enriched for Periostin+ preosteoblasts, and many of them co-expressed tdTomato (Figure 6D). Quantification in three sections each of four mice (two of each sex) showed that indeed a majority of the preosteoblasts (55% ± 6%) in the outermost layer were derived from Fgf18-lineage cells. Therefore, the Fgf18+ periosteal cells proliferate and undergo osteoblast differentiation shortly after bone fracture.

We next examined the cellular contribution of the Fgf18-lineage cells to the facture callus. Both cartilage and bone were present in the callus two weeks after fracture in our semi-stabilized model. tdTomato+ cells were abundant in both the cartilage core and the surrounding cancellous bone in the callus of either male or female mice (Figure 6E). Notably, few, if any, red cells were detected inside the bone marrow cavity following the fracture, indicating minimal inward migration of the Fgf18-lineage periosteal cells. The identity of callus chondrocytes was confirmed by SOX9 immunostaining. The extent of overlap between red and green varied among different areas of the same cartilage callus, indicating variable contribution of Fgf18+ cells to chondrocytes (Figure 6F). Indeed, quantification on three sections through the same callus indicated 45%, 70%, or 75% contribution to the chondrocytes by the Fgf18+ cells. The contribution of Fgf18+ cells to osteoblasts in the bony callus was confirmed by OSX (SP7) immunostaining (Figure 6G). Extensive colocalization between tdTomato and the green OSX signal was evident at both the flanks of the callus and the junction of cartilage and bone, indicating a major contribution of Fgf18-lineage cells (Figure 6H). Quantification on three sections of the same callus estimated that ~75% (±4% SD) and ~47% (±6% SD) of the osteoblasts in those areas, respectively, were derived from Fgf18+ cells. Notable contribution to osteocytes was also observed (Figure 6H, arrows). On the other hand, there were few double-positive osteoblasts in areas immediately adjacent to the fractured cortical bone, indicating an alternative source of cells such as preosteoblasts for osteoblastogenesis near the bone surface (Figure 6G, asterisk). Interestingly, most chondrocytes in the callus stained positive for OSX, like the early hypertrophic chondrocytes in the growth plate. Overall, the data indicate that the Fgf18+ periosteal cells are a major contributor to both chondrocytes and osteoblasts during fracture healing.

Ablation of Fgf18+ cells impairs fracture healing

To determine the functional contribution of the Fgf18+ periosteal cells to bone repair, we genetically ablated the cells with diphtheria toxin A (DTA) and examined the potential effect on fracture healing. Fgf18-tdTomato mice with or without the ROSA26DTA allele were induced with 3 doses of TAM administered once daily starting at 7 weeks of age and subjected to fracture one week after the last injection. Two weeks after fracture, sectioning of the callus revealed marked diminution of tdTomato signal in both the cartilaginous and bony callus, confirming effective killing of the Fgf18+ cells by DTA (Figures 7A and 7B). Imaging and quantification of the callus with μCT detected a significant reduction in both the total size (TV) and the bone amount (BV) in the callus of the DTA-carrying mice; the smaller callus size correlated with the reduced length and maximal width of the callus in those mice (Figures 7C and 7D). By 8 weeks after fracture, nascent cortical bone bridged the gap in both the DTA and control mice (Figure 7E). While the tdTomato+ cells remained largely absent on the outer bone surface in the DTA mice, they were readily detectable along the inner surface (Figure 7F). Quantification confirmed that the return of positive cells were specific to the endosteal bone surface (Figure 7G). The results suggest incomplete ablation of Fgf18+ cells initially in the marrow cavity and that they recovered with time. Nonetheless, healing was impaired in the DTA mice as their nascent cortical bone contained more frequent gaps than normal (Figures 7F and 7H). Furthermore, μCT imaging and quantification at the mid region of the callus showed that both male and female DTA mice possessed thinner cortices and less cortical bone than their sex-matched controls (Figures 7I-7L). These results demonstrate the functional contribution of the Fgf18+ periosteal cells to fracture healing. Because they appear to play a specific role in bone repair instead of normal bone growth, we termed them “reserve skeletal progenitors”.

DISCUSSION

By employing scRNA-seq, RNA in situ hybridization, and genetic lineage tracing, we have sought to elucidate the molecular identities and spatial organization of the periosteal cells in the mouse long bone. The study discovered a unique fibrous enthesis cell type exhibiting a mixed identity of osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and myofibroblasts. Moreover, it identified Fgf18+ cells in the fibrous periosteum as reserve skeletal progenitors, which function as an important source of mature skeletal cells during fracture healing but not during normal bone growth.

It is currently unclear as to what extent the Fgf18+ progenitors co-express other skeletal progenitor cell markers. We showed here on tissue sections that Pdgfra was expressed by most Fgf18+ periosteal cells. As Prrx1 was detected in most progenitor cells (SP1 and SP2) according to RNA-seq, it was likely expressed by the Fgf18+ cells. Gli1, on the other hand, is less certain as it, like Fgf18, was detected in <50% of the cells within SP1 or SP2 and, therefore, may not be co-expressed by the same cells in those clusters. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the Gli1+ periosteal cells exhibited a similar “reserve” nature like Fgf18+ cells in that they contributed mainly to fracture healing instead of homeostatic cortical growth.12,13 Furthermore, unlike Pdgfra or Prrx1, Fgf18 and Gli1 are highly specific to the reserve progenitor cells in the periosteum. More recently, single-nucleus RNA-seq studies have shown that Ly6a marks a periosteal stem/progenitor cell population activated during fracture healing.29 Ly6a expression (lower levels than FAPS and endothelial cells) was detected in the same progenitor clusters as Fgf18 in our RNA-seq analyses. We further showed based on lineage tracing that Ly6a+ cells contributed little to homeostatic bone growth. However, future studies are necessary to ascertain the relationship between Ly6a+ and Fgf18+ cells in the periosteum and whether Ly6a+ cells function similarly as reserve progenitors in fracture healing. The quiescent nature of the skeletal progenitors during homeostasis may not be absolute, as our previous lineage tracing studies showed that the Gli1+ periosteal cells contributed a small number of osteocytes in a site-specific manner after 9 months of chasing.12 Likewise, here, we detected occasional contribution of Fgf18-lineage cells to osteocytes in a specific area after 6 months of chase. Nonetheless, both current and previous data support the notion that the bulk of periosteal bone formation during postnatal normal growth originates from the preosteoblasts in the inner cambium layer.20 A similar division of labor for homeostasis versus injury repair has previously been observed among stem cells in the gut and hair follicles.30,31

Besides the Fgf18+ progenitors, multiple other sources of cells contribute to fracture healing. Preosteoblasts, as characterized by POSTN expression, have recently been shown to participate in bone repair besides normal cortical bone formation.20 Lineage tracing with Osx-CreERT2 indicated that osteoblasts lining the external bone surface were a major source for woven bone formation at the periphery of the fracture callus.32 Bone marrow stromal cells expressing Lepr also contributed to fracture healing in adult mice.33-35 However, this interpretation is complicated by the fact that Lepr is also detected in a small number of periosteal cells. Indeed, when Adipoq-Cre, which, like Lepr-Cre, targeted bone marrow stromal cells but unlike Lepr-Cre, did not target the periosteum, was used, a much smaller contribution of the marked cells to fracture healing was observed.13 In addition, skeletal muscle FAPs produced cartilage and bone during bone repair.26 The relative contribution of each cell source to the fracture callus and the eventual osteoblasts and osteocytes is currently unknown, but it likely varies depending on the nature and severity of the injury.

It is important to note that the morphology of the periosteum is highly variable both along the length and around the shaft of long bones. In adult mice, while the periosteum around the metaphysis exhibits a typical layered organization, the tissue around the diaphysis is often a single layer. The organizational differences appear to associate with molecular changes in the periosteal cells. As we showed here, Fgf18+ cells were largely devoid of CD34 expression in the multi-layer metaphyseal periosteum, but they mostly co-expressed CD34 in the thin periosteum around the diaphysis. These findings underscore the necessity of investigating the periosteum across varied bone surfaces to achieve a comprehensive understanding.

The role of periosteum-derived FGF18 in adult bone growth and fracture healing remains to be determined. Global deletion of Fgf18 impaired both growth plate development and osteogenesis in mouse embryos, resulting in early neonatal death.36 According to scRNA-seq, Fgfr1, 2, and 3 are all highly expressed in preosteoblasts whereas Fgfr1 is most highly enriched in osteoblasts, indicating that either or both cell types can be targets of paracrine signaling by Fgf18 from the fibrous layer. Notably, inactivation of Fgfr1 and Fgfr2 in osteoblasts and osteocytes caused osteocyte death in adult mice.37 Future genetic studies to delete Fgf18 specifically in the adult periosteum are necessary to determine whether FGF18 is the responsible ligand for maintaining cortical bone integrity. Moreover, as the HOX11 transcription factors are required for zeugopod-specific fracture healing, it would be of interest to determine their potential role in mediating the regenerative properties of the Fgf18+ cells in the zeugopod.38 Finally, future studies should investigate whether Fgf18+ reserve progenitor cells can be leveraged to improve bone repair.

Limitations of the study

The study has several limitations. As already mentioned above, the relationship between Fgf18 and the other periosteal progenitor markers such as Gli1 and Ly6a remains to be resolved; the role of FGF18 paracrine signaling in fracture healing is also not clear. Furthermore, because skeletal muscle FAPs are also targeted by the lineage-tracing method due to their Fgf18 expression (lower than that of periosteal cells), their potential contribution to the fracture callus cannot be distinguished from that of the periosteal cells. Finally, the extent to which the current findings apply to other bone injury models and human fracture repair remains to be determined.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

All mouse work was approved by The Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed at 23°C with 12-h light circle (6 am – 6 pm) with free access to food and water. The mouse strains of Fgf18CreERT2/+, ROSA26tdTomato/tdTomato (Ai9), PdgfraGFP/+, ROSADTA/DTA and Ly6aCre/+ are as previously described.28,39-42 The Ai9 (#007909), PdgfraGFP/+ (#007669), ROSADTA/DTA (#009669) and Ly6aCre/+ (#032621) strains were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Both males and females are included in the study.

METHOD DETAILS

Isolation and scRNA-seq of periosteal cells

Periosteal cells were isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6J male mice as previously described.43 Briefly, tibias and femurs were dissected in HBSS buffer with the muscle and connective tissues removed with sharp scissors. After the ends of the bones were embedded in 3% (wt/vol) agarose (Lonza, 50101), the bones were digested with 4 mg/ml dispase II (Roche, 04942078 001) and 3 mg/ml collagenase II (Worthington, LS004176) in MEM-α media at 37°C in a shaker (Thermo Scientific MAXQ4450). Cells from the first 15-min digestion were discarded, and those from the subsequent three 15-min digestions were collected. The cells were then passed through 70 μm cell strainers before centrifugation at 500 rpm for 5 minutes. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μL staining buffer (HBSS + 2% FBS). Cells from five male mice were pooled to ensure enough cells for cell sorting. For Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS), cells were incubated in Media 199 containing 2% FBS and stained with the following antibodies and reagents: CD45-PE (Invitrogen, 12-0451-82), CD19-PE (BioLegend, 115507), CD45R/B220-PE (BioLegend, 103207), CD3-PE (BioLegend, 100205), Gr1-PE (BioLegend, 108407), CD11b-PE (BioLegend, 101207), CD71-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend, 113811), Ter119-APC (Invitrogen, 17-5921-82), Calcein AM (Invitrogen, C1430), and 7-AAD (Invitrogen, 00-6993). The cells were then sorted using a MoFlo Astrios Sorter (Beckman Coulter) to collect the 7-AAD−/Calcein AM+/CD71−/Ter119−/CD45−/CD3−/B220−/CD19−/Gr-1−/CD11b− fraction. The sorted single cells were counted and assessed for viability using a hemocytometer before proceeding with barcoded scRNA-seq library construction.

Libraries for scRNA-seq were constructed with the Chromium Single Cell 3’ v2 reagent kit (CG000204_ChromiumNextGEMSingle-Cell3’v3.1, 10x Genomics) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 using the SP Reagent Kit v1.5 (100 cycles) at the Center for Applied Genomics of The Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Cell Ranger software (version 3.0) with default parameters to perform read alignment and generate gene-barcode matrices. Subsequent analyses were carried out in R (version 4.3.3) and RStudio (version 2023.12.1.402). The Seurat package (version 5.0.3) was employed for data processing and visualization. Cells with 200–6,000 detected genes and < 25% mitochondrial genes were retained for downstream analyses. The data were then normalized, scaled, and log-transformed before Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for clustering. Gene expression visualization was conducted with the DotPlot and FeaturePlot functions. For integration with the published skeletal muscle dataset (GSE164573), cells with 200–6,000 detected genes and < 10% mitochondrial genes were retained for downstream analyses. Batch effect correction was performed using Harmony.44

Raw (non-log-normalized) gene expression counts were used as input to calculate cellular potency scores using the CytoTRACE2 function with default parameters.45 Predicted potency scores were integrated into the Seurat object metadata, and differentiation states were visualized using UMAP projections. Cells were further categorized into potency tiers based on score quantiles to facilitate the identification of progenitor versus terminal populations.

Immunofluorescence staining

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 3 h at room temperature (RT) before cryoprotection in 30% sucrose in PBS for 3 days at 4°C. The samples were then embedded in Tissue-Plus™ O.C.T. compound, sectioned at 10 μm using a cryostat (Leica CM1950) and adhered to Cryoflim type II membrane (Section Lab, Co. Ltd.).

For immunostaining of Periostin, sections were washed in PBS for 15 min, blocked with Antibody Diluent with BSA for 30 min at RT, and incubated with rabbit anti-Periostin monoclonal antibody (1:500, Abcam, ab215199) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated F(ab’)2-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, A21246) or Alexa Fluor 680-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, A21109). For immunostaining of CD34, following wash in PBS, the frozen sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 30 min at RT and incubated with rat anti-CD34 monoclonal antibody (1:100, Thermo Fisher, #14-0341-82) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG (1:500, A21247) for 1 h at RT. For SP7/Osterix staining, the sections were washed in PBS for 10 min, blocked with 5% goat serum, 0.3% TritonX-100 in PBS for 1.5 h at RT, and then incubated with rabbit SP7/Osterix antibody (1:200, Abcam, #ab209484) in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated F(ab’)2-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, A21246) in blocking buffer for 2 h at RT. For immunostaining of Sox9, sections were washed in PBS for 10 min, and incubated in 10 mM sodium citrate, pH 6.0 for 2 h at 60°C for antigen retrieval. Following cool down to RT, sections were washed twice in TBS, incubated in 0.5% Triton X-100 in TBS for 5 min, washed thrice in TBS. Sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in TBS+0.1% Tween20 for 30 min at RT, and incubated with rabbit anti-Sox9 polyclonal antibody (1:500, Millipore-Sigma, #AB5535) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated F(ab’)2-goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, A21246) for 1 h at RT. All immunostained sections were mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, P36930) and imaged with Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

RNAscope

Cryosections as prepared above were washed in PBS for 5 min at RT, baked for 30 min at 60°C, and post-fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 15min at 4°C. Sections were dehydrated at RT stepwise in 50% ethanol for 5 min, 70% ethanol for 5 min, 2x 100% ethanol for 5 min, and then air dried for 5 min. Pretreatment was performed by incubation with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at RT, followed by RNAscope® Custom Pretreatment Reagent for 30 min at 40°C. In situ hybridization was performed with RNAscope® 2.5 HD Detection Reagent – RED (ACD, 322360) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Probes used were Mm-Postn (418581), Mm-Tagln (480331), Mm-Chad (484881), Mm-Acan (439101), Mm-Prrx1 (485231), Mm-Comp (480711), and Mm-Ly6a (427571). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin QS (vector laboratories, H3404100), mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, P36930) before being scanned with ZEISS Axio Scan.Z1.

Tamoxifen and calcein injections

Tamoxifen (1mg/10g body weight, dissolved in corn oil) was administered by oral gavage once daily for three consecutive days at 8 weeks of age. For pulse experiments, harvest took place 24 hrs after the last injection. For double labeling, calcein green (5 mg/mL, pH 7.2, 5μL/1g body weight) was administered intraperitoneally with the 2nd injection two days prior to harvest, and the 1st injection two weeks earlier.

Open femur fracture healing

The procedure was modified from a previous protocol.46 Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane before the right leg was shaved and disinfected with alcohol and betadine. A 1-2 cm incision was made over the anterolateral distal femur. The vastus lateralis muscle was elevated and the lateral joint capsule was incised with a scalpel, allowing for medial retraction of the extensor mechanism to expose the distal femur. A hypodermic needle was inserted into the distal end of the femur to create an entry for the insertion of a pre-bent stainless-steel pin into the intramedullary canal. A midshaft transverse osteotomy was performed using sharp scissors over the pin. The extensor mechanism was repositioned and secured with sutures before the skin was closed with sutures. The mice were allowed to fully recover from anesthesia on a warm heating pad before returning to cage.

For evaluating fracture healing, the mice were euthanized at various time points after fracture. During tissue harvest, the lateral joint capsule was carefully incised with a scalpel to expose the distal end of the metal pin, which was then removed with caution. The muscles were left attached to the bones to minimize disruption of the fracture callus. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 4 hours, cryoprotected with 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C for 3 days. They were finally embedded in O.C.T. and sectioned at 10 μm with a cryostat as described above.

For the 1-week time point harvest, EdU powder (Invitrogen, A10044) was dissolved in sterile PBS to a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. EdU injections were administered 4 hours prior to tissue harvest, with an intraperitoneal injection of 100 μL per 10 g of body weight. EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit (Invitrogen, C10377) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The percentage of EdU labeling was quantified in a representative callus by analyzing three sections with 5-section intervals.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For co-localization of Fgf18-tdTomato with Pdgfra-GFP, the double-positive cells and all tdTomato+ (single or double positive) cells were counted under a confocal microscope in a 9-mm region of the anterior periosteum extending from the distal metaphysis towards the proximal end of the femur. For co-localization of Fgf18-tdTomato with CD34 immunostaining, similar counting was conducted but excluded a 3-mm region at the distal metaphysis and included only the 6-mm region of the periosteum along the diaphysis.

Colocalization analysis for Fgf18-tdTomato with SOX9, OSX or Periostin was performed using Fiji (ImageJ) with either cell-based (for SOX9) or area-based quantification (for OSX or Periostin). Area-based quantification was chosen because the signal pattern produced dense, spatially contiguous signal with minimal intensity gradients at cell borders, and segmentation algorithms could not reliably distinguish individual cells. Images were background-subtracted and thresholded using the Otsu method and applied uniformly to all images. Binary masks were generated for the green and red channels. The overlapping area (green ∩ red) was calculated using the Image Calculator (AND function). The percentage of green signal overlapping with red was determined as: (overlap area / total green area) × 100.

Statistical analysis was performed with Student’s t test, with details provided in figure legends.
