Comparative genome-wide identification and analysis of the COMT gene family in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) and Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh.)
Authors: Huaiwen Du, Lijia Liu, Jiayu Yuan, Na Wu, Yuxin Wei, Lili Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT) is a key S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-dependent enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, playing a crucial role in plant stress resistance, growth, and development through its functions in melatonin and lignin biosynthesis. While COMT genes have been extensively studied in other Solanaceae crops and functional studies of individual genes have been reported in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), a genome-wide systematic analysis of this gene family in potato remains lacking. This study comprehensively identified 18 StCOMT genes in the potato genome, and these 18 StCOMT genes can be divided into two clusters. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that StCOMT proteins possess conserved structural features, including the O-methyltransferase domain and dimerization domain, with their promoter regions enriched in stress- and hormone-responsive cis-acting elements. Phylogenetic and collinearity analyses indicated high homology with tomato genes and suggested evolutionary expansion of this family through gene duplication. Expression profiling demonstrated distinct tissue-specific patterns and differential induction under various biotic and abiotic stresses, implying functional diversification among its members. Functional enrichment analysis further supported their involvement in key pathways such as phenylpropanoid and tryptophan metabolism. However, these conclusions are based on bioinformatic analyses and require further experimental validation. In summary, this study provides the first systematic genomic characterization of the StCOMT gene family in potato, offering valuable candidate gene targets for molecular breeding aimed at enhancing stress tolerance and agronomic traits in potato.Keywords: Potato, COMT, Gene family, Bioinformatics analysis, Phylogenetic analysis, Expression profiling

Introduction

Caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT), an S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-dependent O-methyltransferase, modulates diverse physiological processes in plants through the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway (Fig. 1 for its role in melatonin biosynthesis) [1]. COMT catalyzes melatonin synthesis to enhance stress resistance [2], promotes lignin biosynthesis to improve lodging resistance [3], and facilitates the production of pharmacologically active compounds such as ferulic acid and flavonoids in medicinal plants [4]. Additionally, COMT mediates the biosynthesis of vanillin in aromatic plants [5], highlighting its significance as an integral component of plant metabolic systems.

Melatonin, a crucial phytohormone, plays a pivotal role in mitigating both biotic and abiotic stresses, including extreme temperatures and salinity [6–8]. Substantial evidence indicates that COMT-catalyzed melatonin biosynthesis significantly enhances plant resilience to these adversities [9, 10]. Lignin, a fundamental component of the plant cell wall, is essential for long-distance transport of water and nutrients, mechanical support, and stress defense [11–13]. COMT also fulfills several key functions in the lignin biosynthesis pathway, For example, participating in the methylation of lignin monomers and affecting lignin composition, among others [14, 15]. The catalysis of melatonin and lignin synthesis represents two central functions of COMT, thereby regulating plant growth, promoting stress resistance, and enhancing lodging tolerance.

In Solanaceae crops, the functions of COMT genes have been extensively studied. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), overexpression of COMT genes enhances cadmium (Cd) tolerance by promoting sulfur absorption and assimilation [16], and improves salt tolerance by scavenging excess nitric oxide (NO) to alleviate protein nitration damage [17]. Furthermore, COMT genes can enhance resistance to Botrytis cinerea by increasing melatonin synthesis [18]. In pepper (Capsicum annuum L.), multiple studies have confirmed that COMT genes are involved in the biosynthesis of capsaicinoids [19–21], and they can also reduce Cd accumulation in aerial parts by promoting lignin biosynthesis [22]. In American black nightshade (Solanum americanum L.), the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway mediated by COMT genes serves as a key mechanism for alleviating Cd stress [23]. In potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), some COMT genes have been reported to respond to combined drought and saline-alkali stress [24], and to influence tuber periderm structure and sprouting inhibition by regulating the synthesis of lignin precursors [25].

Although the functions of individual COMT genes in potato have been explored, a genome-wide and systematic characterization of this gene family is still lacking. Using the well-studied COMT gene family in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. as a reference, this study conducted a comprehensive identification and comparative analysis of the COMT gene family in Solanum etuberosum L., aiming to systematically reveal its member composition, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns. The work provides novel candidate gene targets for stress and pest resistance breeding in potato, thereby supporting the development of high-yield and stable Solanum etuberosum L. varieties.

Materials and methods

Genome data retrieval and gene family member identification

Genomic data for the 5 selected species were acquired from public repositories. The specific version details are listed in Table 1. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles for the PF00891 and PF08100 domains were obtained from the Pfam database. These HMM profiles were employed to screen the respective proteomes using the “Simple HMM Search” function in TBtools, with an E-value ≤ 1e− 5.A subsequent curation process involved filtering redundant COMT/ASMT members, and merging tandem repeats, leading to the final identification of the COMT gene family members.

Genomic data information

Characterization of COMT protein physicochemical properties and subcellular localization prediction

The physicochemical properties of the identified members were analyzed using the “Protein Parameter Calculate” function in TBtools software [26]. based on General Feature Format (GFF). The prediction included the following parameters: Number of Amino Acids, Molecular Weight, Theoretical pI, Instability Index, Aliphatic Index, and Grand Average of Hydropathicity.

Protein secondary structures were predicted with the Network Protein Sequence Analysis@nalysis (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) [27], while tertiary structures were modeled using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive) [28], The protein structure prediction was performed using the website’s default parameters.Subcellular localization predictions were carried out via the Cell-PLoc 2.0(http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) [29], employing the Plant-mPLoc prediction model dedicated to plant proteins.

Synteny analysis for the COMT gene family

TBtools software was used to identify the orthologs of COMT genes using the one step MCScanX module with default parameters. The collinear relationships among Solanum tuberosum L., Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., and Nicotiana attenuata L. were visualized using the Dual Synteny Plotter in TBtools, with their respective General Feature Format (GFF) files.

The collinearity analysis of COMT genes between Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. was performed using MCScanX in TBtools under default parameters, and the results were visualized using the “Advanced Circos” function within the TBtools.

Phylogenetic reconstruction for the COMT gene family

A phylogenetic tree was constructed from a set of 50 COMT protein sequences derived from four species: Solanum tuberosum L., Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., Solanum lycopersicum L., and Solanum etuberosum L. The tree was built using the “One Step Build a ML Tree” feature in TBtools, applying the maximum likelihood method with the UltraFast BootStrap algorithm. The bootstrap replication number was set to 5000. Finally, the resulting tree was annotated and visualized on the iTOL online platform (https://itol.embl.de/).

Conserved motif analysis of the COMT gene family

Analysis of putative conserved motifs in the COMT protein sequences was conducted with MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) [30], applying the following parameters: a maximum of 10 motifs and an optimum width range of 5–200 amino acids. The resultant motifs were subsequently visualized via the Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Genetic structure Analysis of the COMT Gene Family

Based on the GFF files of Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., gene structure prediction and visualization were performed using the “Visualize Gene Structure (from GTF/GFF3 File)” function in TBtools under default parameters.

Protein domain analysis of the COMT gene family

The protein sequences of COMT from Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. were scanned with the InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/) [31] ”search” function under default parameters. The initial domain predictions were subsequently curated by removing redundant hits, and the final domain architectures were graphically presented using the “Simple Pfam View (via Web-InterPro)” feature in TBtools.

Cis-acting elements prediction analysis of the COMT gene family

Following prediction with the PlantCARE online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [32], the cis-regulatory elements identified in the 2000 bp COMT promoter regions were processed separately; their sequence distribution was visualized using TBtools “Simple BioSequence Viewer”, while their counts were statistically analyzed and presented as a clustered heatmap.

Analysis of tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression of COMT gene family members

Expression profiles of the COMT gene family in Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. were extracted from the ePlant database (https://plants.ensembl.org/) [33]. This database provides normalized expression values derived from a unified processing pipeline applied to its integrated RNA-seq datasets, and the original data incorporated contain biological replicates. Based on these statistically reliable and cross-study comparable data, the expression patterns of this gene family across various tissues and stress conditions were analyzed, and the results were visualized using the HeatMap function in TBtools software.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the StCOMT gene family

Functional annotation of the StCOMT genes was performed using EggNOG-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) [34] with default parameters, and the results were visualized within TBtools. Enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted using the GO&KEGG module in TBtools. The analysis was configured with the “P_value” column for statistical significance, and the top 10 most significant terms were displayed and visualized within the module.

Results

Identification of COMT gene family members and analysis of protein physicochemical properties

Comprehensive analysis identified 18 StCOMT genes in Solanum tuberosum L. and 10 AtCOMT genes in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. The encoded proteins displayed generally uniform molecular characteristics, with most molecular weights clustering between 39 and 42 kDa and amino acid lengths ranging from 350 to 380 residues. Notable exceptions included StCOMT1, StCOMT4, StCOMT6, and StCOMT9, which fell outside these ranges. Theoretical pI analysis revealed that the majority of COMT proteins are acidic, with pI values spanning 4.8–6.3, except for the basic StCOMT5 (pI = 9.26). Evaluation of protein stability indicated that 21 out of the 29 COMT members in Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. exhibited an Instability Index below 40, suggesting high structural stability. Hydrophobicity assessment using the Grand Average of Hydropathicity showed positive values for 20 members, supporting their classification as hydrophilic proteins. Furthermore, 26 COMT proteins possessed an Aliphatic Index exceeding 90, consistent with enhanced thermostability and structural compactness (Tables 2 and 3) (Fig. 2).

Analysis of COMT protein structure

Secondary structure prediction via the Network Protein Sequence Analysis @nalysis revealed that COMT proteins from both Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. are primarily composed of alpha helices (Hh), extended strands (Ee), and random coils (Cc). Among these, alpha helices constituted the largest proportion (averaging 47.72% in StCOMTs and 47.12% in AtCOMTs), followed by extended strands (38.77% in StCOMTs and 39.65% in AtCOMTs), and random coils (13.51% in StCOMTs and 13.23% in AtCOMTs). The consistent compositional profiles across most family members reflect considerable conservation in secondary architecture. Tertiary structure modeling performed with SWISS-MODEL further indicated that proteins within the same subfamily share similar folding patterns, consistent with the secondary structure predictions. All COMT proteins were predicted to adopt homodimeric quaternary structures.

Prediction of subcellular localization of COMT proteins

Subcellular localization predictions generated by Cell-PLoc 2.0 indicated that most COMT proteins in both Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. are predominantly localized to the chloroplast. Additional localizations were detected for specific members: AtCOMT8 was found in both the nucleus and mitochondria; StCOMT1, StCOMT12, and StCOMT13 showed nuclear localization; while StCOMT3, StCOMT6, and StCOMT14 were predicted to localize to mitochondria.A few members, including StCOMT3 and StCOMT12, also exhibited cytoplasmic presence.Based on these findings, we propose that chloroplasts serve as the primary functional site for COMT proteins.

Chromosomal localization of the COMT gene family

Chromosomal localization visualization revealed an uneven genomic distribution of COMT gene family members in both Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. In the Solanum tuberosum L. genomes, COMT genes were predominantly clustered on chromosome 6 (harboring three members), with two members localized to chromosomes 10 and 3 respectively. Chromosomes 1, 2, 8, and 12 each contained only a single member, whereas no COMT genes were detectable on chromosomes 4, 5, or 7. In Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh, COMT genes exhibited significant clustering on chromosome 1 (containing six members). Chromosome 15 harbored two members, while chromosomes 3 and 4 showed minimal representation with one member each. Notably, chromosome 2 lacked detectable COMT homologs (Fig. 3).

Synteny analysis of the COMT gene family

To analyse the evolutionary trajectory of the COMT gene family, a comprehensive synteny analysis was conducted across Solanum tuberosum L., Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., and narrowleaf tobacco. This investigation encompassed both interspecific synteny assessments and intraspecific synteny evaluation within Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. Following stringent filtration of tandem duplicates, key findings emerged: Two orthologous COMT gene pairs were identified between Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., whereas three orthologous pairs were detected between Solanum tuberosum L. and Nicotiana attenuata L.,In the collinearity analysis between Solanum tuberosum L. and Solanum etuberosum L, Solanum lycopersicum L, the correlation between them is significantly stronger, with each COMT gene exhibiting a collinear relationship.These results demonstrate pronounced evolutionary and functional conservation of the COMT family within Solanaceae species, alongside considerable conservation of synteny blocks across divergent plant families. Intraspecific analysis further revealed the presence of one paralogous COMT gene pair within each of the Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. genomes. It is notable that in both species, the two paralogous pairs localized to genomic regions with the highest COMT gene density (chromosome 6 in Solanum tuberosum L.; chromosome 1 in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh.). This suggests localized gene duplication events as a potential mechanism for their origin (Fig. 4).

Phylogenetic analysis of the COMT gene family

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method combined with the UltraFast BootStrap algorithm to elucidate the evolutionary relationships within this protein family, based on a set of 50 COMT protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., Solanum tuberosum L., Solanum lycopersicum L. Within the established evolutionary classification framework for COMTs, these sequences were clearly divided into two distinct subfamilies, suggesting potential functional divergence or subfunctionalization during evolution. Notably, the phylogenetic analysis revealed that nearly every evolutionary clade containing an StCOMT protein was closely associated with a corresponding clade containing either an SlCOMT or SeCOMT protein, indicating a tight paralogous relationship among them (Fig. 5).

Conserved motif analysis of the COMT gene family

To further investigate the evolutionary diversity of the COMT family, we analyzed the conserved motifs of COMT proteins using the online tool MEME, which identified a total of 10 distinct conserved motifs (labeled motif 1–10). Although there were subtle differences in the number of conserved domains contained in different genes, their distribution and arrangement showed consistency. Among these, motif 10 was the most divergent. Based on the presence or absence of specific motif sequences and combined with evolutionary relationships, the COMT proteins could be divided into two major branches. Across all motifs, motifs 5,6,4, and 9 occurred with the highest frequency, suggesting that they may represent signature motifs of the COMT gene family (Fig. 6).

Genetic structure analysis of the COMT Gene Family

In terms of genetic structure, members of the StCOMT gene family resemble those of the AtCOMT family, as both typically contain 2–4 coding sequences (CDS) and exhibit high similarity in their gene structure organization. The primary structural variations between them arise from insertions or deletions of exons, coupled with evolutionary divergence among different StCOMT subfamilies. Functional divergence and subfunctionalization of the proteins have further led to significant differences in amino acid length and composition among individual members. Furthermore, analysis of the untranslated regions (UTRs) revealed that, with the exception of StCOMT1, StCOMT2, StCOMT7, StCOMT9, StCOMT10, StCOMT16, StCOMT17, and StCOMT8, the remaining 11 StCOMT genes possess UTR structures (Fig. 7).

Protein domain analysis of the COMT gene family

Protein domain prediction analysis revealed that the COMT gene family primarily encompasses two structural domains: the O-methyltransferase C-terminal domain (PF00891) and the O-methyltransferase dimerization domain (PF08100). Every COMT protein, whether in Solanum tuberosum L. or Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., possesses both of these domains, which are closely linked to the methyltransferase function of COMT. Through this methyltransferase activity, COMT genes mediate the synthesis of melatonin and lignin-related compounds (Fig. 8).

Cis-acting elements prediction analysis of the COMT gene family

Analysis of promoter cis-acting elements revealed significant differences in both the types and quantities of regulatory elements between the StCOMT and AtCOMT gene families, reflecting diversity and specificity in expression regulation. In terms of hormonal response, methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements were enriched in multiple genes—StCOMT1 contained 10, while AtCOMT6 and AtCOMT7 each contained 4. Abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive elements were notably abundant, with AtCOMT8 harboring 6 and AtCOMT6 containing.

Regarding stress response, anaerobiosis-related elements were most frequent in StCOMT16 (5 elements), while low-temperature-responsive elements were detected in StCOMT5, StCOMT13, and AtCOMT3. In addition, genes such as StCOMT2 and StCOMT6 contained elements specific to palisade mesophyll cells. MYB binding sites were present in high numbers—5 in StCOMT13 and 3 in StCOMT1. Some genes, such as AtCOMT8, possessed abundant elements across multiple categories, indicating a complex regulatory network. Together, these data suggest that variations in the number and combination of cis-acting elements among COMT gene family members likely contribute to functional differentiation in hormonal response, stress adaptation, and tissue-specific expression (Fig. 9).

Analysis of tissue-specific expression of the COMT gene family

RNA-seq data were retrieved from the ePlant database, normalized using the TBtools built-in algorithm, and visualized as a expression heatmap.

Expression profiling across 13 Solanum tuberosum L. organs revealed distinct tissue-specific patterns among StCOMT genes. Transcript accumulation of StCOMT1 was most pronounced in the stem apex, whereas StCOMT5 displayed specific enrichment in stem tissues. Four genes—StCOMT4, StCOMT8, StCOMT12, and StCOMT15—were observed to be co-expressed in both stem and stem apex tissues. Preferential expression in stamens was recorded for StCOMT3 and StCOMT10. Root-specific transcript accumulation characterized StCOMT6, StCOMT9, and StCOMT17. Stolons showed marked enrichment of StCOMT13, StCOMT14, and StCOMT18, while young tubers demonstrated activated expression of StCOMT1, StCOMT2, and StCOMT18. Notably, StCOMT7, StCOMT11, and StCOMT16 maintained minimal expression levels across all examined organs.

In Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh., examination of 18 developmental stages and organs uncovered specialized expression patterns. AtCOMT1 transcription was restricted to cotyledons, while AtCOMT2 accumulation was predominant in senescing leaves. Mature pollen exhibited specific high expression of AtCOMT3. Dry seeds showed increased AtCOMT8 accumulation, and AtCOMT10 demonstrated specific enrichment in the second internode of stems (Fig. 10).

Discussion

In this study, a total of 18 candidate StCOMT gene family members were identified by screening the Pfam database. The proteins of the StCOMT gene family exhibited similar physicochemical properties, secondary and tertiary structures, including comparable sequence lengths, acidic isoelectric points, low protein instability indices, and high aliphatic indices and hydrophilicity. These characteristics are consistent with those of COMT proteins identified in plants such as pepper [35], suggesting that COMT proteins generally possess stable structures and good thermal stability. However, some members showed notable differences in certain parameters, such as StCOMT1,StCOMT4,StCOMT5,StCOMT6,and StCOMT9.

Subcellular localization predictions indicated that StCOMT proteins are likely localized in chloroplasts, similar to Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. proteins. In functional studies of the COMT gene in rose, RhCOMT1 was found under normal conditions in the nuclear-cytoplasmic compartment, while heat stress triggered its relocation to chloroplasts, where it contributed to enhanced thermotolerance [36]. In maize, most ZmCOMT proteins were predicted to localize in the cytoplasm, yet they functioned in lignin synthesis to improve lodging resistance in response to light signals [37]. Since light perception is closely associated with chloroplasts, it can be speculated that StCOMT genes may perform their synthetic functions within chloroplasts. However, this hypothesis remains based solely on bioinformatics predictions, and further experimental validation is required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Regarding chromosomal distribution, COMT genes were predominantly clustered on a single chromosome, with additional members distributed across other chromosomes. This pattern is consistent with findings in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. and similar to the distribution observed in plants such as Camellia sinensis [38]. Such a distribution may represent a common chromosomal arrangement characteristic of COMT genes.

Separate collinearity analyses of the Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. genomes revealed the presence of a highly repetitive fragment in both species, located on the same chromosome in each case. This may result from self-duplication of the same gene or from chromosomal structural alterations during evolution, potentially representing one of the major mechanisms for the origin of StCOMT genes. Collinearity analysis of orthologous COMT genes among Solanum tuberosum L., Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. and Nicotiana attenuata L. identified 2 and 3 collinear events between Solanum tuberosum L. and Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh.,and Solanum tuberosum L. and Nicotiana attenuata L., respectively. These results indicate certain evolutionary connections of COMT genes across species, suggesting they may possess similar biological functions [39].

During phylogenetic tree construction, COMT gene family protein sequences from Solanum lycopersicum L. and the Solanum section Petota group—considered ancestral sources of Solanum tuberosum L.—were selected [40]. A maximum likelihood tree was then constructed together with Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. proteins. The tree was divided into two major groups, and the analysis revealed that nearly every Solanum tuberosum L. St protein was positioned adjacent to a Solanum lycopersicum L. Sl or Se protein on the phylogenetic branches, demonstrating clear homology. This supports the inference that StCOMT genes originate from the inheritance of COMT genes from their parental species. Notably, however, exceptions such as StCOMT4 and StCOMT8 were observed; these proteins may represent independent branches formed during evolution. These findings suggest that COMT gene structure tends to be conserved across different plant taxa, whereas the chromosomal relationships among different COMT genes within the same plant are more complex—a pattern consistent with evolutionary analyses of COMT genes in seed plants [41].

Conserved motif analysis of StCOMT proteins revealed structural consistency across members, a finding that aligns with results reported for the AcCOMT gene family in oat, indicating a high degree of conservation within the family. In the analysis of protein domains, all members were found to contain both the O-methyltransferase C-terminal domain and the O-methyltransferase dimerisation domain, corresponding to the ASMT and COMT domains, respectively. The presence of the ASMT domain in all members may be attributed to the evolutionary origin of COMTs from ancestral ASMT during plant colonization [42]. Thus, the COMT domain often coexists with the ASMT domain, which constitutes an essential structural feature for the functional activity of COMT gene family members.

Analysis of the cis-acting elements in the promoters of the StCOMT gene family revealed that its members possess a complex transcriptional regulatory basis for hormone response, stress adaptation, and tissue-specific expression, similar to the AtCOMT gene family. The enrichment of methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and abscisic acid (ABA) responsive elements in the promoters of multiple genes is consistent with the known roles of COMTs in phenylpropanoid metabolism and stress responses [43], implicating that their expression is precisely regulated by these core stress-related hormone signals. Meanwhile, the presence of low-temperature responsive elements in specific members provides direct sequence-based evidence for their potential involvement in adaptation to cold stress and other adversities [44]. Variations in the types, numbers, and combinations of these elements collectively constitute an important transcriptional regulatory basis for the functional diversity of COMT genes. However, the specific regulatory logic and cooperative mechanisms still require further elucidation through promoter activity assays and protein interaction experiments.

The COMT gene family exhibits highly specialized expression patterns in plants, indicating functional specialization in developmental regulation and stress adaptation. Tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that multiple members show preferential expression in particular organs or developmental stages. For example, in Solanum tuberosum L., StCOMT3 and StCOMT10 are highly expressed in stamens, while StCOMT13 and StCOMT14 are enriched in stolons and young tubers, suggesting their potential roles in reproductive development and tuber formation, respectively. This pattern is consistent with findings in Solanum lycopersicum L., where SlCOMT influences pollen and fruit development as well as fruit quality [45]. Stress response analysis further highlights functional divergence within the family: different members are specifically induced by biotic stress, high temperature, mechanical injury, or combined stresses. For instance, StCOMT3 and StCOMT10 are significantly induced under biotic stress, whereas StCOMT14 and StCOMT18 specifically respond to high temperature. Such spatiotemporal and stress-specific expression patterns collectively indicate that the COMT gene family plays diversified roles in plant growth, development, and stress adaptation through fine-tuned transcriptional regulation, though certain limitations remain, necessitating further experimental validation.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses predicted that the StCOMT gene family is significantly enriched in the molecular functions of “O-methyltransferase activity” and “S-adenosylmethionine-dependent methyltransferase activity.” In terms of biological processes, it is likely involved in “alkaloid biosynthetic process” and “phenylpropanoid biosynthetic process.” Pathway analysis further predicted that StCOMT may participate in specific metabolic pathways such as “00940 Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” and “00380 Tryptophan metabolism.” These enrichment results are highly consistent with the known catalytic functions of COMT proteins, particularly their key roles in the phenylpropanoid pathway (lignin biosynthesis) and the tryptophan pathway (melatonin biosynthesis) through methylation reactions [46–49]. This provides systematic, pathway-level support for the functions of COMT genes in Solanum tuberosum L. growth, development, and stress response.

This study identified multiple Solanum tuberosum L. StCOMT candidate genes potentially involved in regulating stress resistance through genome-wide, structural, and expression profiling analyses. However, certain limitations exist. All results are based on bioinformatic predictions derived from public data, and neither qRT-PCR validation nor functional experiments such as overexpression or enzymatic activity assays have been conducted. Therefore, the specific functions of these genes remain speculative and require further experimental confirmation.

Conclusion

This study identified 18 StCOMT genes in the Solanum tuberosum L. genome and provided a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis. Key findings include:

All StCOMT proteins harbor highly conserved functional domains that are intricately associated with methyl transfer activity. Furthermore, an in-depth analysis of their promoter regions reveals a significant enrichment of cis-regulatory elements responsive to hormones and various stress stimuli, thereby underscoring their pivotal role in facilitating environmental adaptation processes.

The results reveal that this gene family is relatively conserved in evolution, with its members exhibiting tissue-specific and stress-inducible expression patterns, suggesting potential key roles in phenylpropanoid metabolism and stress response.
