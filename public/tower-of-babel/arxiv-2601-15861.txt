Finding large sparse induced subgraphs in graphs of small (but not very small) tree-independence number
Daniel Lokshtanov, Michał Pilipczuk, Paweł Rzążewski

Abstract
The independence number of a tree decomposition is the size of a largest independent set contained in a single bag. The tree-independence number of a graph G G is the minimum independence number of a tree decomposition of G G . As shown recently by Lima et al. [ESA 2024], a large family of optimization problems asking for a maximum-weight induced subgraph of bounded treewidth, satisfying a given CMSO 2 property, can be solved in polynomial time in graphs whose tree-independence number is bounded by some constant k k . However, the complexity of the algorithm of Lima et al. grows rapidly with k k , making it useless if the tree-independence number is superconstant. In this paper we present a refined version of the algorithm. We show that the same family of problems can be solved in time n 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) n^{\mathcal{O}(k)} , where n n is the number of vertices of the instance, k k is the tree-independence number, and the 𝒪 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{O}(\cdot) -notation hides factors depending on the treewidth bound of the solution and the considered CMSO 2 property. This running time is quasipolynomial for classes of graphs with polylogarithmic tree-independence number; several such classes were recently discovered. Furthermore, the running time is subexponential for many natural classes of geometric intersection graphs – namely, ones that admit balanced clique-based separators of sublinear size.

In the context of the latter, having a tree decomposition of a graph with small (say, constant) width allows us to mimic the standard bottom-up dynamic programming on trees in order to solve many computational problems efficiently.
This idea led to a flurry of algorithmic results and techniques, designed for graphs of bounded treewidth

In order to understand which problems can be solved efficiently on given class of graphs,
it is beneficial to design algorithms that do not focus on particular problems, but rather on
families of problems
expressible in a certain way. Such results are typically referred to as
algorithmic meta-theorems
. On a high level, an algorithmic meta-theorem says that every problem of a given type can be solved efficiently in the given class of graphs.
Such “types of problems” are usually defined in terms of logic.

Probably the best known algorithmic meta-theorem is the celebrated result of Courcelle
[
21
]
which says that every problem expressible in
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
can be solved in linear time in graphs given with a tree decomposition of constant width.
Here,
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
is the
Monadic Second Order
logic of graphs with quantification over edge subsets and modular predicates. In essence, the logic allows quantification over single vertices and edges as well as over subsets of vertices and of edges.
See
Section

2.2
for more information on
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
and the result of Courcelle.

While Courcelle’s theorem handles a very broad family of problems, it comes at a cost: the assumption that the treewidth is bounded by a constant is rather restrictive.
In particular, the theorem applies only to sparse graphs. On the other hand, there are classes of dense graphs with very good structural properties, like interval graphs
[
33
, Chapter 8]
, circular-arc graphs
[
33
, Chapter 8.6]
, or chordal graphs
[
33
, Chapter 4]
. Often these properties can be expressed in terms of the existence of a certain tree decomposition whose every bag has some “nice structure” (opposed to every bag having bounded size, as in the definition of treewidth).
For instance, chordal graphs are precisely the graphs that admit a tree decomposition where every bag is a clique.
Such decompositions with a “nice structure” can be also exploited algorithmically.

This motivation led to the definition of
tree-independence number
of a graph.
This parameter was independently introduced by Yolov
[
39
]
and by Dallard et al.
[
25
]
; we use the notation and terminology from the latter work.
The independence number of a tree decomposition is the minimum value of
k
such that every bag induces a subgraph of independence number at most
k

is the minimum independence number of a tree decomposition of
G

The algorithmic motivation behind the definition of tree-independence number was to understand the tractability of the
Max (Weight) Independent Set
problem (

can be solved in polynomial time for graphs of bounded tree-independence number, given with an appropriate tree decomposition.
However, it turns out that many other problems, asking for finding large induced subgraphs that are sparse (or, equivalently in this context, of bounded clique number), can be efficiently solved in the considered classes
[
39
]

In particular, many of these problems can be expressed as special cases of a certain abstract problem.

Classes of polylogarithmic tree-independence number.

Let us start with a few definitions. A
hole

A

and three paths
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
from

to

b

A

P_{i}
from
a
to
b_{i}

Finally, a

is a graph consisting of two (not necessarily disjoint) triangles
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
and
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
, and three
paths
P_{i}
from
a_{i}
to
b_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, such that the union of any two of these paths induces a hole.
A

, or a 3PC for short, is a graph that is either a theta, a pyramid, or a generalized prism.

By
S_{q,q,q}
we denote the graph obtained from three paths, each with
q+1
vertices, by picking one endvertex of each path and identifying them.
By
K_{q,q}
we denote the complete graph with each side of size

.
The

is the
q\times q

Then

is the family of graphs such that every

is the line graph of some subdivision of

For a family

of graphs, we say that

-free
if it does not contain any graph from

as an induced subgraph.
A graph is 3PC-free (resp., even-hole-free, theta-free, pyramid-free) if none of its induced subgraphs is a 3PC (resp., even hole, theta, pyramid).
To improve the readability, we use parenthesis when we forbid several families simultaneously; e.g., 3PC-free graphs are precisely (theta, pyramid, generalized prism)-free graphs.

Clique-based separators in geometric intersection graphs.

The next family of examples comes from intersection graphs of certain geometric objects.
For a graph
G
and a real number
\beta\in(0,1)
, a
\beta
-balanced clique-based separator
is a family
\mathcal{S}
of subsets of
V(G)
, each inducing a clique in

G-\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}
has at most
\beta\cdot|V(G)|

is

1
1

1

An astute reader might notice that typically in clique-based separators we do not optimize size, but weight, defined as
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\log(|S|+1)

We say that a class

of graphs
admits balanced clique-based separators of size

\mathcal{G}
has a

It turns out that many natural classes of geometric intersection graphs have balanced separators of sublinear size.

Basic notation and tools

For a nonnegative integer
p
, we denote
[p]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,p\}
. For a set
A
, by
\mathrm{id}_{A}
we denote the identity function on
A
.

We use standard graph notation and terminology. All graphs considered in this paper are finite, simple, and undirected; that is, a graph
G
consists of a finite vertex set
V(G)
and an edge set
E(G)\subseteq\binom{V(G)}{2}
.
We also consider vertex-weighted graphs, i.e., graphs
G
equipped with a function
\mathfrak{w}\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Q}_{+}
.
We assume that arithmetic operations on weights can be performed in constant time.
For a set of
X
vertices, we define
\mathfrak{w}(X)=\sum_{v\in X}\mathfrak{w}(v)
.

For a graph
G
, by
\alpha(G)
we denote the number of vertices in a largest independent set in
G
.
All subgraphs in this paper are induced, and we often identify such subgraphs with their vertex sets.
For example, for a set
X\subseteq V(G)
, we write
\alpha(X)
as a shorthand for
\alpha(G[X])
.

2.1
Tree decompositions and tree-independence

A tree decomposition of a graph
G
is a pair
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{u}\}_{u\in V(T)})
, where
T
is a tree and,
for every
u\in V(T)
,
X_{u}
is a subset of
V(G)
called a
bag
, so that the following conditions are met:

(i)

for every vertex
v\in V(G)
there is
u\in V(T)
such that
v\in X_{u}
;

(ii)

for every edge
vw\in E(G)
there is
u\in V(T)
such that
w,v\in X_{u}
; and

(iii)

for every vertex
v\in V(G)
, the set of nodes
u\in V(T)
such that
v\in X_{u}
induces a connected subgraph of
T
.

To avoid confusion between
V(G)
and
V(T)
, the elements of
V(T)
will be called
nodes
.
Typically, we consider
T
as a rooted tree, which defines the ancestor-descendant relation on its nodes.
This relation is reflexive, i.e., we consider every node its own ancestor as well as its own descendant.
For a node
u\in V(T)
, we define
V_{u}\coloneqq\bigcup\{X_{u^{\prime}}\colon u^{\prime}\text{ is a descendant of }u\}
.

The
width
of a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
is defined as
\max_{u\in V(T)}|X_{u}|-1
.
The
treewidth
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{tw}(G)
, is the minimum width of a tree decomposition of
G
.

While computing treewidth is
NP
-hard
[
4
]
, we can efficiently verify whether
\mathrm{tw}(G)<t
, for any fixed
t
.

Theorem 2.1

(Bodlaender
[
11
]
)
.

Let
t
be a fixed constant.
Given an
n
-vertex graph
G
, in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
one can compute a tree decomposition of
G
of width at most
t
, or decide that
\mathrm{tw}(G)>t
.

Tree-independence number.

For a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{u}\}_{u\in V(T)})
, by
\alpha(\mathcal{T})
we denote
\max_{u\in V(T)}\alpha(X_{u})
.
By
tree-independence number
of a graph
G
we denote the minimum value of
\alpha(\mathcal{T})
over all tree decompositions
\mathcal{T}
of
G
.

Nice tree decompositions.

A tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{u}\}_{u\in V(T)})
rooted at
r
is
nice
if
X_{r}=\emptyset
and every node
u
of
T
is of one of the following types:

Leaf node.

u
is a leaf of
T
and
X_{u}=\emptyset
,

Introduce node.

u
has exactly one child
u^{\prime}
, and
X_{u}=X_{u^{\prime}}\cup\{v\}
for some
v\notin X_{u^{\prime}}
,

Forget node.

u
has exactly one child
u^{\prime}
, and
X_{u}=X_{u^{\prime}}-\{v\}
for some
v\in X_{u^{\prime}}
,

Join node.

u
has exactly two children
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}
and
X_{u}=X_{u^{\prime}}=X_{u^{\prime\prime}}
.

It is well-known (and easy to verify) that, given any tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
, in polynomial time one can obtain a nice tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
whose every bag is a subset of some bag of
\mathcal{T}
.
In particular,
\textsf{width}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\leqslant\textsf{width}(\mathcal{T})
and
\alpha(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\leqslant\alpha(\mathcal{T})
.

2.2
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}

We use the standard logic
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
, which stands for Counting Monadic Second Order logic with edge quantification, on graphs. We now recall the fundamentals and establish the notation; for a broader introduction see e.g.
[
20
]
.

In
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
on graphs, there are variables of four different sorts: for single vertices and for single edges (typically denoted with lower case letters), as well as for subsets of vertices and for subsets of edges (typically denoted with upper case letters). Atomic formulas of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
are of the following form:

•

equality of variables of the same sort:
x=y
or
X=Y
;

•

testing the incidence relation:
\mathsf{inc}(x,e)
, where
x
is a vertex variable and
e
is an edge variable;

•

set membership:
x\in X
, where
x
is a (vertex/edge) variable and
X
is a (vertex/edge) set variable; and

•

modular arithmetic checks:
|X|\equiv a\bmod m
, where
X
is a (vertex/edge) set variable and
a,m\in\mathbb{Z}
are fixed integers with
m>0
.

Starting with those atomic formulas, larger formulas can be constructed using standard boolean connectives (
\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow
), negation, and quantification over variables of all sorts (both universal and existential). This defines the syntax of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
. The semantics, i.e., how the satisfaction of a formula in a graph is defined, is as expected. For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, by
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
we denote the fragment of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
where all the moduli
m
used in the modular arithmetic checks satisfy
m\leqslant p
. The
quantifier rank
of a
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
formula
\phi
is the maximum nesting depth of quantifiers in
\phi
.

In a formula of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
, any variable that is not bound by a quantifier is called a
free variable
. We often distinguish the free variables in parentheses by the formula, e.g.
\phi(x,y)
. A
sentence
is a formula without free variables. As usual, we write
G\models\phi({\bar{a}})
to state that formula
\phi
holds in a graph
G
for an evaluation
{\bar{a}}
of the free variables of
\phi
.

We will use the following standard fact asserting that the class of graphs of treewidth at most
t
is definable in
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
, for every fixed
t
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
t
there is a sentence
\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
such that for every graph
G
,

G\models\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}\qquad\textrm{if and only if}\qquad\mathrm{tw}(G)<t.

Proof.

By the celebrated result of Robertson and Seymour
[
38
]
, for each
t
there is a
finite
family
\mathcal{F}_{t}
of graphs such that, for every
G
it holds that
\mathrm{tw}(G)<t
if and only if
G
does not contain any
F\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
as a minor.
Thus, the sentence
\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
is a conjunction of sentences asserting that a graph does not contain
F
as a minor, over all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
.
Expressing that a fixed graph
F
is a minor of a given graph can be easily done in
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
. Such a sentence should say that there exists a vertex set for each vertex of
F
(i.e., a constant number of sets) so that each of these sets induces a connected graph, the sets are pairwise disjoint, and an edge between a pair of sets exists whenever the two corresponding vertices of
H
are adjacent.
∎

2.3
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
and boundaried graphs

Boundaried graphs.

For a finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
, a graph
G
, and a vertex subset
A\subseteq V(G)
, by
A^{{\bar{x}}}
we denote the set of all injective functions from
{\bar{x}}
to
A
; and
G^{{\bar{x}}}
is a shorthand for
V(G)^{{\bar{x}}}
. An element
{\bar{a}}\in A^{{\bar{x}}}
is interpreted as an evaluation mapping the variables of
{\bar{x}}
to pairwise different vertices of
A
: each
x\in{\bar{x}}
is evaluated to
{\bar{a}}(x)
. The set
{\bar{a}}({\bar{x}})
, that is, the image of
{\bar{a}}
, is denoted by
\mathrm{im}\ {\bar{a}}
. An
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
is a pair
(G,{\bar{a}})
, where
G
is a graph and
{\bar{a}}\in G^{{\bar{x}}}
. The
boundary
of
(G,{\bar{a}})
is the image of
{\bar{a}}
.

We define the following two operations on boundaried graphs:

•

Suppose
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
are an
{\bar{x}}_{1}
-boundaried graph and an
{\bar{x}}_{2}
-boundaried graph, respectively. Then
gluing
those two boundaried graphs results in the
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})=(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})\oplus(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
, where
{\bar{x}}={\bar{x}}_{1}\cup{\bar{x}}_{2}
, constructed as follows. Take the disjoint union of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
and for each
x\in{\bar{x}}_{1}\cap{\bar{x}}_{2}
, fuse
{\bar{a}}_{1}(x)
with
{\bar{a}}_{2}(x)
into one vertex and declare it to be
{\bar{a}}(x)
. For
x\in{\bar{x}}_{1}-{\bar{x}}_{2}
set
{\bar{a}}(x)\coloneqq{\bar{a}}_{1}(x)
and for
x\in{\bar{x}}_{2}-{\bar{x}}_{1}
set
{\bar{a}}(x)\coloneqq{\bar{a}}_{2}(x)
.

•

If
(G,{\bar{a}})
is an
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph and
x\in{\bar{x}}
is a variable, then
\mathsf{forget}_{x}(G,{\bar{a}})
is the
({\bar{x}}-\{x\})
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}}|_{{\bar{x}}-\{x\}})
. In other words, the graph stays the same, but we forget variable
x
from the domain
{\bar{x}}
.

Types.

It is well-known (see e.g.
[
13
]
) that for any fixed
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and a finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
, every formula
\phi({\bar{x}})
of
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
of quantifier rank at most
q
can be effectively transformed into an equivalent normalized formula
\phi^{\prime}({\bar{x}})
of
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
of the same quantifier rank. Furthermore, the set of the normalized formulas, call it
\mathsf{Formulae}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
, is finite and computable from
p,q,{\bar{x}}
. We define
\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
to be the powerset of
\mathsf{Formulae}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
, and for an
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
, we define its
rank-
(p,q)
type
as

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G,{\bar{a}})\coloneqq\{\phi({\bar{x}})\in\mathsf{Formulae}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}~|~G\models\phi({\bar{a}})\}\in\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}.

Two
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graphs
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
are called
(p,q)
-equivalent
, denoted
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})\equiv_{p,q}(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
, if they have the same rank-
(p,q)
types, that is,
\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})=\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
.

Note that isomorphic boundaried graphs are
(p,q)
-equivalent for all
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, where an isomorphism between
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graphs
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
is defined as an isomorphism between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
that maps
{\bar{a}}_{1}(x)
to
{\bar{a}}_{2}(x)
, for each
x\in{\bar{x}}
. Further, for any fixed
p
and
q
, the rank-
(p,q)
type of a boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
is computable from
(G,{\bar{a}})
. Finally, it is well known that types defined in this are compositional with respect to the operations on boundaried graphs, in the sense that the type of the result of an operation is determined by the type(s) of the input graph(s). This is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(see e.g.
[
32
, Proposition 8]
)
.

Fix
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

•

For every pair of finite sets of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}_{1},{\bar{x}}_{2}
there exists a computable binary operation
\oplus\colon\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}}_{1},p,q}\times\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}}_{2},p,q}\to\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
, where
{\bar{x}}={\bar{x}}_{1}\cup{\bar{x}}_{2}
, so that for any
{\bar{x}}_{1}
-boundaried graph
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and any
{\bar{x}}_{2}
-boundaried graph
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
, we have

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}((G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})\oplus(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2}))=\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})\oplus\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2}).

Moreover, the operation
\oplus
is associative and commutative.

•

For every finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
and a variable
x\in{\bar{x}}
there exists a computable operation
\mathsf{forget}_{x}\colon\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}\to\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{y}},p,q}
, where
{\bar{y}}\coloneqq{\bar{x}}-\{x\}
, so that for any
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
, we have

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(\mathsf{forget}_{x}(G,{\bar{a}}))=\mathsf{forget}_{x}(\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G,{\bar{a}})).

For a sentence
\psi
of
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
of quantifier rank at most
q
and a finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
, we let
\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}[\psi]\subseteq\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
be the set of those rank-
(p,q)
types that contain the normalized sentence equivalent to
\psi
. Thus, for any
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
, we have

G\models\psi\qquad\textrm{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G,{\bar{a}})\in\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}[\psi].

Note that the set
\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}[\psi]
is computable from
p,q,{\bar{x}},\phi
.

Finally, it will be often convenient to speak about types of graphs with a boundary understood as a prescribed subset of vertices, say
B
, without explicitly enumerating the elements of
B
using an explicit set of variables. To this end, for a graph
G
and a set of vertices
B\subseteq V(G)
, we write

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G,B)\coloneqq\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G,\mathrm{id}_{B}),

where on the right hand side we treat
B
as a finite set of variables and
\mathrm{id}_{B}\in B^{B}
is an evaluation that maps each of these variables to itself. Consequently, if
G,G^{\prime}
are two graphs and
B\subseteq V(G)\cap V(G^{\prime})
, then we write
(G,B)\equiv_{p,q}(G^{\prime},B)
if and only if
(G,\mathrm{id}_{B})\equiv_{p,q}(G^{\prime},\mathrm{id}_{B})
.

\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
on graphs of bounded treewidth.

We will use the classic result of Courcelle about the tractability of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
on graphs of bounded treewidth, in the following formulation.

Theorem 2.4

(Courcelle
[
21
]
)
.

Fix a constant
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, a finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
, and a formula of
\phi({\bar{x}})
of
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
. Then given an
n
-vertex boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
of treewidth at most
\ell
, one can decide whether
\phi({\bar{a}})
holds in
G
in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

We note that the original statement of Courcelle assumed that a tree decomposition of width at most
\ell
is also given on input, but this can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time using
Theorem

2.1
.

From
Theorem

2.4
we may immediately derive that one can efficiently compute the type of a given graph of bounded treewidth.

Corollary 2.5
.

Fix constants
p,q,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and a finite set of vertex variables
{\bar{x}}
.
Then given an
n
-vertex
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
of treewidth at most
\ell
, one can compute
\mathsf{type}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}(G,{\bar{a}})
in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof.

Apply
Theorem

2.4
for every formula
\phi({\bar{x}})\in\mathsf{Formulae}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
, noting that the set
\mathsf{Formulae}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
is finite and computable from
{\bar{x}},p,q
.
∎

3
Signatures

In what follows we assume that
p,q,t
, and
\ell
are fixed nonnegative integers. We treat
p,q,t
as constants, in particular we do not attempt to optimize any dependence on them and hide functions depending on
p,q,t
in the
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
notation.
On the other hand, we treat
\ell
as a parameter and make the dependence on it explicit in the notation; we try to keep this dependence as low as possible.

We fix a set
{\bar{x}}
consisting of at most
\ell
vertex variables. Consider an
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
of treewidth smaller than
t
. Let us emphasize that while the treewidth of
G
is considered constant (namely smaller than
t
), the size of the boundary of
(G,{\bar{a}})
is not, as it depends on
\ell
.
In this section we show that in such a setting, the number of possible
(p,q)
-types of
(G,{\bar{a}})
is bounded by a moderate function of
\ell
. The key idea towards bounding this number is to understand the
(p,q)
-type of
(G,{\bar{a}})
through the lens of the
signature
of
(G,{\bar{a}})
, defined below.

Defining signatures.

Consider any rooted tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{u}\}_{u\in V(T)})
of
G
of width smaller than
t
. Without loss of generality assume that
T
is binary, that is, every node of
T
has at most
2
children. Let
B\coloneqq\mathrm{im}\ {\bar{a}}\subseteq V(G)
be the image of
{\bar{a}}
.
For each vertex
v\in B
, mark one, arbitrarily chosen, node of
T
whose bag contains
v
. Note that we have marked at most
\ell
nodes of
T
.

Now, let
Q
be the lowest common ancestor closure of all the marked nodes. By this we mean the set containing all the marked nodes and, for every pair of marked nodes, also their their (unique) lowest common ancestor in
T
.
It is well known (see e.g.
[
9
]
) that (i)
|Q|<2\ell
, and (ii) every connected component of
T-Q
is adjacent to at most two different vertices of
Q
. Define

B^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{u\in Q}X_{u}.

Clearly, we have
|B^{\prime}|\leqslant t\cdot 2\ell=\mathcal{O}(t\ell)
.

Let
s
be the number of components of
T-Q
, and let
T_{1},\ldots,T_{s}
be those components. Since
T
is binary, we have
s\leqslant 2\ell
.
For each
i\in[s]
, let
Z_{i}
be the set of at most two nodes from
Q
that are adjacent to
V(T_{i})
.
Now, for each
i\in[s]
, we define

P_{i}\coloneqq G\left[\bigcup_{u\in V(T_{i})\cup Z_{i}}X_{u}\right]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad B_{i}\coloneqq\bigcup_{u\in Z_{i}}X_{u}.

Note that we have
|B_{i}|\leqslant 2t
.

We fix a set
\Omega
consisting of
2\ell t
vertex variables, disjoint from
{\bar{x}}
. (
\Omega
is fixed independent of
(G,{\bar{a}})
, so that for all the considered boundaried graphs
(G,{\bar{a}})
we use the same
\Omega
.)
To every vertex
v\in B^{\prime}-B
, we assign a different variable
y_{v}\in\Omega
. Let
{\bar{y}}\coloneqq{\bar{x}}\cup\{y_{v}\colon v\in B^{\prime}-B\}\subseteq{\bar{x}}\cup\Omega
and define
{\bar{b}}\in G^{{\bar{y}}}
by setting
{\bar{b}}(x)={\bar{a}}(x)
and
{\bar{b}}(y_{v})=v
for each
v\in B^{\prime}-B
. Thus,
(G,{\bar{b}})
is a
{\bar{y}}
-boundaried graph with boundary
B^{\prime}
. Finally, for each
i\in[s]
, we define
{\bar{y}}_{i}\coloneqq{\bar{b}}^{-1}(B_{i})
and
{\bar{b}}_{i}\coloneqq{\bar{b}}|_{{\bar{y}}_{i}}
. Thus,
(P_{i},{\bar{b}}_{i})
is a
{\bar{y}}_{i}
-boundaried graph with boundary
B_{i}
of size at most
2t
.

Now comes the crucial definition. Given the objects described above, we define a
signature
of
(G,{\bar{a}})
as a tuple consisting of:

•

the number
s
;

•

the variable set
{\bar{y}}\subseteq{\bar{x}}\cup\Omega
and its subsets
{\bar{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}_{s}
;

•

the graph
H
on the vertex set
{\bar{y}}
where
y,y^{\prime}\in{\bar{y}}
are adjacent if and only if
{\bar{b}}(y)
and
{\bar{b}}(y^{\prime})
are adjacent in
G
; and

•

for each
i\in[s]
, the type
\tau_{i}\coloneqq\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(P_{i},{\bar{b}}_{i})
.

Note that a single boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
may have multiple signatures, depending on the choice of the tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
, the marked nodes, the enumeration of the connected components of
T-Q
, etc. However, some signature of
(G,{\bar{a}})
can be computed efficiently.

Lemma 3.1
.

For fixed
p,q,t\in\mathbb{N}
there is an algorithm that given a
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
, some signature of
(G,{\bar{a}})
can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof.

We start by calling
Theorem

2.1
to compute a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}
of
G
of width less than
t
.
Computing
B^{\prime}
, variable sets
{\bar{y}},{\bar{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}_{s}
, evaluations
{\bar{b}},{\bar{b}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{b}}_{2}
, and boundaried graphs
(P_{i},{\bar{b}}_{i})
, for all
i\in[s]
, can be done in polynomial time right from the definition.
Since each
P_{i}
is an induced subgraph of
G
, the treewidth of
P_{i}
is smaller than
t
as well. Therefore, we may use
Corollary

2.5
to compute each type
\tau_{i}=\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(P_{i},{\bar{b}}_{i})
in time linear in
|V(P_{i})|
.
∎

Next, we bound the number of possible distinct signatures.

Lemma 3.2
.

There are
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
possible distinct signatures of
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graphs of treewidth less than
t
.

Proof.

We bound the number of ways of choosing the consecutive components of a signature:

•

There are at most
2\ell+1
ways to choose
s
.

•

As
|\Omega|=2\ell t=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
, there are
2^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
ways to choose
{\bar{y}}
.

•

As each set
{\bar{y}}_{i}
has size at most
2t
, there are at most
(|\Omega|^{2t})^{s}\leqslant\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
ways to choose
{\bar{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\bar{y}}_{s}
.

•

Note that
H
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
G
, hence its treewidth is smaller than
t
. It is well-known that a graph of treewidth less than
t
has fewer than
t\ell=\mathcal{O}(\ell)
edges. As
H
has
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
vertices, there are
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
ways to choose
H
.

•

For each
i\in[s]
, the number of ways to choose the type
\tau_{i}
is bounded by
|\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{y}}_{i},p,q}|
, which is
\mathcal{O}(1)
due to
|{\bar{y}}_{i}|\leqslant 2t
.

The product of the upper bounds presented above is
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
, and from the description it is straightforward to enumerate
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
candidates for signatures.
∎

Finally, we prove the crucial property: equality of signatures implies equality of types.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
be two
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graphs
of treewidth less than
t
. Suppose there is a signature
\sigma
that is both a signature of
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and a signature of
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
.
Then
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})\equiv_{p,q}(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=(s,{\bar{y}},\{{\bar{y}}_{i}\colon i\in[s]]\},H,\{\tau_{i}\colon i\in[s]\})
. We note that a graph isomorphic to
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
can be obtained from
(H,\mathrm{id}_{{\bar{y}}})
, by iteratively gluing a
{\bar{y}}_{i}
-boundaried graph of rank-
(p,q)
type
\tau_{i}
, for each
i\in[s]
, and then iteratively applying the
\mathsf{forget}_{y}
operation for all the variables
y\in{\bar{y}}-{\bar{x}}
, in any order. And similarly,
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
can be also obtained in this way. By
Lemma

2.3
, the rank-
(p,q)
type of the result of such an operation is uniquely determined by the types
\{\tau_{i}\colon i\in[s]\}
and the type
\mathsf{type}^{{\bar{y}},p,q}(H,\mathrm{id}_{{\bar{y}}})
. It follows that
(G_{1},{\bar{a}}_{1})
and
(G_{2},{\bar{a}}_{2})
have the same rank-
(p,q)
types.
∎

Some remarks are in place.
Recall that
|{\bar{x}}|
can be as large as
\ell
, which is not a constant.
Consequently, the number of formulae in
\mathsf{Types}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}
can be very large (essentially, a
q
-fold exponential function of
\ell
) and in particular, we cannot efficiently compute
\mathsf{type}^{{\bar{x}},p,q}(G,{\bar{a}})
, for a given
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graph
(G,{\bar{a}})
. However, if we additionally know that the treewidth of
G
is bounded by a constant, then using
Lemma

3.1
we may efficiently compute a signature of
(G,{\bar{a}})
, for which, by
Lemma

3.2
, there are only
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
many possibilities. And by
Lemma

3.3
, if for two
{\bar{x}}
-boundaried graphs we compute the same signature, then they must have the same rank-
(p,q)
type. Note that the reverse implication is not necessarily true: it is possible that for two
(p,q)
-equivalent graphs we compute two different signatures.

We now use the above observations to prove the following lemma, which encapsulates the outcome of this section in a single statement. In essence, it says that given a large family of boundaried graphs, we can efficiently compress it by still keeping at least one member of each equivalence class of
(p,q)
-equivalence.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
t,p,q\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed constants and let
\ell
be an integer parameter.
Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph with vertex weights
\mathfrak{w}\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Q}_{+}
, and
B\subseteq V(G)
be a set of at most
\ell
vertices of
G
.
Further, let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of subsets of
V(G)
such that for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
B\subseteq F
and
\mathrm{tw}(G[F])<t
.
Then in time
(|\mathcal{F}|\cdot n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
one can compute a family
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
of size
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
with the following promise: for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
there exists
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F^{\prime}}
such that

(G[F],B)\equiv_{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}],B)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime})\geqslant\mathfrak{w}(F).

Proof.

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
, we compute a signature
\sigma_{F}
of
(G[F],B)
using
Lemma

3.1
. Let
\Sigma\coloneqq\{\sigma_{F}\colon F\in\mathcal{F}\}
be the set of all the obtained signatures. By
Lemma

3.2
, we must have
|\Sigma|\leqslant\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
. For each
\sigma\in\Sigma
we select a set
F_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{F}
that has the largest weight among the sets
F\in\cal F
with
\sigma_{F}=\sigma
. We define
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}\coloneqq\{F_{\sigma}\colon\sigma\in\Sigma\}
; thus
|\mathcal{F^{\prime}}|=|\Sigma|\leqslant\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}
. To see the that
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}
satisfies the promised property, observe that for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
, the set
F^{\prime}\coloneqq F_{\sigma_{F}}
belongs to
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}
, satisfies
\sigma_{F^{\prime}}=\sigma_{F}
, which implies that
(G[F],B)\equiv_{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}],B)
by
Lemma

3.3
, and satisfies
\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime})\geqslant\mathfrak{w}(F)
by the choice of
F^{\prime}
.
∎

4
The algorithm

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Let
\psi
be a fixed
\mathsf{CMSO}_{2}
formula and
t
be a constant.
Define

\psi^{\prime}\coloneqq\psi\land\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t},

where
\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
is the sentence provided by
Lemma

2.2
.
In other words,
\psi^{\prime}
verifies that the graph in question both satisfies
\psi
and is of treewidth less than
t
.
Let
q
be the quantifier rank of
\psi^{\prime}
and let
p
be the largest modulus used in any modular counting predicate used in
\psi^{\prime}
.
Note that
\psi^{\prime}
, and thus also
q
and
p
, are determined by
\psi
and
t
; that is, they are fixed constants.

Let
G
be a graph and let
\mathfrak{w}\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Q}_{+}
be a weight function on the vertices of
G
. Our goal is to find
F\subseteq V(G)
such that
G[F]\models\psi^{\prime}
and
F
is of maximum possible weight, or decide that no such
F
exists.
Assume that
G
is given with its tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{u}\}_{u\in V(T)})
such that the tree-independence number of
\mathcal{T}
is at most
k
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathcal{T}
is a nice tree decomposition.

For a node
u\in V(T)
and a subset
B\subseteq X_{u}
,
we say that
F\subseteq V_{u}
is
feasible
for
(u,B)

if
F\cap X_{u}=B
and
\mathrm{tw}(G[F])<t
.
For two sets
F,F^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
that are feasible for
(u,B)
, we say that
F
is a
B
-representative
for
F^{\prime}
(or that
F

B
-represents

F^{\prime}
) if

(G[F],B)\equiv_{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}],B)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathfrak{w}(F)\geqslant\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime}).

Fix

\ell\coloneqq kt=\mathcal{O}(k).

For every node
u\in V(T)
and every set
B\subseteq X_{u}
of size at most
\ell
, we will compute a family
\mathsf{F}[u,B]
of subsets of
V_{u}
with the following properties:

(F1)

|\mathsf{F}[u,B]|=\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}=k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
;

(F2)

each
F\in\mathsf{F}[t,B]
is feasible for
(u,B)
; and

(F3)

for each set
F^{\prime}
that is feasible for
(u,B)
, there exists
F\in\mathsf{F}[u,B]
that
B
-represents
F^{\prime}
.

Let us present the intuition behind this family.
Let
F_{\textsf{opt}}
be an (unknown) optimum solution – assuming that it exists.
Note that
\mathcal{T}
, restricted to
F_{\textsf{opt}}
, is a tree decomposition of
G[F_{\textsf{opt}}]
.
As
\mathrm{tw}(G[F_{\textsf{opt}}])<t
implies in particular that
\chi(G[F_{\textsf{opt}}])<t
, we conclude that each bag of
\mathcal{T}
contains at most
\ell=kt
vertices from
F_{\textsf{opt}}
.
For each node
u\in V(T)
, we consider every possible intersection
B
of
F_{\textsf{opt}}
with
X_{u}
, and we aim to understand all possible “behaviors” of
G[F_{\textsf{opt}}\cap V_{u}]
with
B
as the boundary. These “behaviors” are formalized with rank-
(p,q)
types.
As any two partial solutions that are equivalent satisfy the same formulae, it is sufficient to keep one – the one with maximum weight.

4.1
Obtaining the solution

Suppose that we have computed the families
\mathsf{F}[\cdot,\cdot]
as specified above.
Let
r
be the root of
\mathcal{T}
, that is,
V_{r}=V(G)
.
Since
\mathcal{T}
is a nice tree decomposition, we have
X_{r}=\emptyset
.

This means that
\mathcal{F}=\mathsf{F}[r,\emptyset]
contains a maximum-weight representative of each equivalence class (and possibly some more sets, as distinct sets in
\mathcal{F}
might still be equivalent). We are still left with deciding which of these sets (or, more precisely, which of subgraphs induced by one of these sets) satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.

This is, however, easy, as for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
we have
\mathrm{tw}(G[F])<t
. Consequently, we can apply
Theorem

2.1
and then
Theorem

2.4
to
G[F]
, for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
If such
G[F]\models\psi^{\prime}
, we mark
F
as a candidate, otherwise we discard it.

Finally, if any of the sets
F\in\mathcal{F}
was marked as a candidate, we return one with the largest weight as a solution.
Otherwise, we report that no solution exists.
The total running time of this step is
\ell^{\mathcal{O}(\ell)}\cdot\mathcal{O}(n)=k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot\mathcal{O}(n)
.

The correctness of the procedure is straightforward.
On one hand, we never return a set that does not satisfy
\psi^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, let
F_{\textsf{opt}}
be an optimum solution.
By (F3), there is some
F\in\mathcal{F}
that has at least the same weight as
F_{\textsf{opt}}
and is
(p,q)
-equivalent to
F_{\textsf{opt}}
(with empty boundary).
In particular,
F
also satisfies
\psi^{\prime}
so is also an optimum solution that is marked as a candidate in the procedure above.

4.2
Computing families
\textsf{F}[\cdot,\cdot]

We now discuss how the families
\textsf{F}[\cdot,\cdot]
can be computed by a bottom-up dynamic programming over
\cal T
.
The computation depends on the type of a node.

Leaf node.

If
u\in V(T)
is a leaf node, we have
X_{u}=\emptyset
and thus
V_{u}=\emptyset
.
Consequently, we set
\textsf{F}[u,\emptyset]=\{\emptyset\}
.

Introduce node.

Let
u
be an introduce node,
u^{\prime}
be its unique child in
T
, and let
\{v\}=X_{u}-X_{u^{\prime}}
.
Suppose we have computed all the suitable families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
.
Now consider some
B\subseteq X_{u}
of size at most
\ell
.

If
v\notin B
, we set
\textsf{F}[u,B]\coloneqq\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]
.
Suppose now that
v\in B
.
For every
F\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B-\{v\}]
we proceed as follows.
We check whether
\mathrm{tw}(G[F\cup\{v\}])<t
using the algorithm of
Theorem

2.1
.
If so, we include
F\cup\{v\}
into
\textsf{F}[u,B]
, and otherwise we do nothing for this particular
F
.

Forget node.

Let
u
be a forget node,
u^{\prime}
be its unique child in
T
, and let
\{v\}=X_{u}^{\prime}-X_{u}
.
Suppose we have computed all the suitable families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
.
Now consider some
B\subseteq X_{u}
of size at most
\ell
.
We set
\textsf{F}[u,B]
to be the family obtained by applying
Lemma

3.4
to
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]\cup\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B\cup\{v\}]
.

Join node.

Let
u
be a join node and let
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}
be its two children in
T
.
Suppose we have computed all the suitable families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
and
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime\prime},\cdot]
.
Consider some
B\subseteq X_{u}
of size at most
\ell
.

We set
\textsf{F}[u,B]
to be the family obtained by applying
Lemma

3.4
to the family

\{F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}~|~F^{\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B],F^{\prime\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B],\text{ and }\mathrm{tw}(G[F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime})]<t\}.

4.3
Correctness

We will argue about the correctness of each type of a node separately.

Leaf node.

If
u
is a leaf node,
\emptyset
is the only subset of
V_{u}
, so the correctness is straightforward.

Introduce node.

Consider an introduce node
u
,
let
u^{\prime}
be its only child and let
v
be the unique vertex in
X_{u}-X_{u^{\prime}}
. Assume that the families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
are computed correctly.

If
v\notin B
, the correctness is straightforward.
Suppose now that
v\in B
.
As
|\textsf{F}[u,B]|\leqslant|\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B-\{v\}]|
, condition (F1) clearly holds.
Condition (F2) holds too, as in
\textsf{F}[u,B]
we include only sets feasible for
(u,B)
.
For (F3), consider any
\widetilde{F}\subseteq V_{u}
, feasible for
(u,B)
.
Note that
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\coloneqq\widetilde{F}-\{v\}
is feasible for
(u^{\prime},B-\{v\})
.
Thus, there exists
F^{\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B-\{v\}]
,
such that the rank-
(p,q)
types of
(G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}],B-\{v\})
and
(G[F^{\prime}],B-\{v\})
are equal and
\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime})\geqslant\mathfrak{w}(\widetilde{F}^{\prime})
.

We observe that the neighborhood of
v
in
V_{u}
is contained in
X_{u}
. Since both
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
are feasible for
(u^{\prime},B-\{v\})
, we in particular have

N(v)\cap F^{\prime}=N(v)\cap\widetilde{F}^{\prime}=N(v)\cap(B-\{v\}).

Thus, by
Lemma

2.3
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}\cup\{v\}],B)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}],B-\{v\})\oplus\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[B],B)

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}],B)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}],B-\{v\})\oplus\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[B],B),

so from the equivalence
(G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}],B-\{v\})\equiv_{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}],B-\{v\})
we conclude that

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[F^{\prime}\cup\{v\}],B)=\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}],B).

Note that
\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
is a subformula of
\psi^{\prime}
, hence it is a
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
formula of quantifier rank at most
q
.
Therefore, the equality of types above implies that we have
G[F^{\prime}\cup\{v\}]\models\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
if and only if
G[\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}]\models\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
. Since the latter is true due to
\widetilde{F}=\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\cup\{v\}
being feasible for
(u,B)
, we conclude that also
\mathrm{tw}(G[F^{\prime}\cup\{v\}<t
, and hence
F^{\prime}\cup\{v\}
gets included in
\textsf{F}[u,B]
.

Finally, since

\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime}\cup\{v\})=\mathfrak{w}(F^{\prime})+\mathfrak{w}(v)\geqslant\mathfrak{w}(\widetilde{F}^{\prime})+\mathfrak{w}(v)=\mathfrak{w}(\widetilde{F}),

property (F3) holds.

Forget node.

Consider a forget node
u
, let
u^{\prime}
be its only child, and let
v
be the unique vertex in
X_{u^{\prime}}-X_{u}
.
Assume that the families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
are computed correctly.

Fix any
B\subseteq X_{u}
of size at most
\ell
, note that
v\notin B
.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\coloneqq\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]\cup\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B\cup\{v\}]
.
As
\textsf{F}[u,B]
is the result of applying
Lemma

3.4
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, property (F1) holds.
As
\textsf{F}[u,B]\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and every set in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is feasible for
(u,B)
, property (F2) holds, too.

For (F3), consider any
\widetilde{F}\subseteq V_{u}
, feasible for
(u,B)
. By
Lemma

3.4
, it is sufficient that we find an appropriate representative for
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Suppose first that
v\notin\widetilde{F}
.
Then
\widetilde{F}
is feasible for
(u^{\prime},B)
and thus, there is
F^{\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]
that
B
-represents
\widetilde{F}
.
This
F^{\prime}
was included in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Now suppose that
v\in\widetilde{F}
.
Then
\widetilde{F}
is feasible for
(u^{\prime},B\cup\{v\})
.
By an analogous argument as in the previous paragraph, we conclude that a
B
-representative for
\widetilde{F}
was included in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Join node.

Let
u
be a join node and let
u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime\prime}
be its two children.
Suppose that the families
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},\cdot]
and
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime\prime},\cdot]
are computed correctly.
Consider some
B\subseteq X_{u}
and a set
\widetilde{F}\subseteq V_{u}
that is feasible for
(u,B)
.
As
\textsf{F}[u,B]
is the family obtained by applying
Lemma

3.4
to the family

\mathcal{F}=\{F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}~|~F^{\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B],F^{\prime\prime}\in\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]\text{ and }\mathrm{tw}(G[F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime})]<t\},

it is sufficient to argue that there is a
B
-representative for
F
in
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}=\widetilde{F}\cap V_{u^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}=\widetilde{F}\cap V_{u^{\prime\prime}}
.
Note that
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\cap\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}=B
,
and thus,
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
is feasible for
(u^{\prime},B)
and
\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}
is feasible for
(u^{\prime\prime},B)
.
Consequently, there is a
B
-representative
F^{\prime}
for
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
in
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]
and a
B
-representative
F^{\prime\prime}
for
\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime}
in
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime\prime},B]
.
Let
F=F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}
.

Observe that by
Lemma

2.3
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(\widetilde{F},B)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(\widetilde{F}^{\prime},B)\oplus\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(\widetilde{F}^{\prime\prime},B),

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(F,B)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(F^{\prime},B)\oplus\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(F^{\prime\prime},B).

As the types of the corresponding factors on the right hand sides are equal, we conclude that

\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(\widetilde{F},B)=\mathsf{type}^{p,q}(F,B).

However, we still need to argue that
\mathrm{tw}(G[F])<t
, so that
F
is included in
\textsf{F}[u,B]
.
For this, we use the same argument as for the introduce nodes:
\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
is a subformula of
\psi^{\prime}
, and hence it is a
\mathsf{C}_{\leqslant p}\mathsf{MSO}_{2}
formula of quantifier rank at most
q
.
Consequently,
G[F]\models\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
if and only if
G[\widetilde{F}]\models\phi_{\mathrm{tw}<t}
, and latter indeed holds as
\widetilde{F}
is feasible for
u,B
.
Now, (F3) follows easily from
Lemma

3.4
.

4.4
Running time

Recall that
V(T)
can be assumed to be polynomial in
n
, and for each
u
, the number of choices for
B\subseteq X_{u}
is at most
n^{\ell}=n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.

Let us now estimate the cost of computing
\textsf{F}[u,B]
for a join node
u
, as this operation is the most involved.
This requires iterating over all elements of
\textsf{F}[u^{\prime},B]\times\textsf{F}[u^{\prime\prime},B]
, where
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}
are the children of
u
.
For each pair of sets, we call the algorithm from
Theorem

2.1
for the graph induced by their union,
and finally we apply
Lemma

3.4
to the family of these sets that induce a graph of treewidth at most
t
.
Thus, each entry is computed in time
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
. The analysis for the other node types proceeds in a similar way.

As
k\leqslant n
, the overall complexity of the algorithm is
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
. This completes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

5
Conclusion

In the work that first introduced the concept of the tree-independence number, Yolov
[
39
]
pointed out a certain drawback of this parameter: it is unbounded for some classes of graphs that are “simple in structure,” like complete bipartite graphs.
As a remedy, he suggested a refined version of the parameter, which later became known as the
induced matching treewidth

[
35
]
.
Again, this parameter is defined using tree decompositions with certain structural properties: this time, we bound the size of a largest induced matching whose every edge intersects a single bag of the decomposition. Induced matching treewidth is bounded for all classes of bounded tree-independence number, but can still be small in classes allowing large complete bipartite graphs.

Yolov
[
39
]
showed that
MWIS
is polynomial-time-solvable in classes of bounded induced matching treewidth.
Lima et al.
[
35
]
showed that some other problems, notably
Feedback Vertex Set
,
are also tractable in these graphs. Furthermore, they conjectured that an analogue of
Theorem

1.1
holds for this parameter too, i.e., every problem expressible as
(\mathrm{tw}<t,\psi)
-
MWIS
can be solved in polynomial time for graphs with constant induced matching treewidth.
This conjecture was recently confirmed by Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korhonen
[
8
]
.

However, similarly to
Theorem

1.1
, the running time of their algorithm is at least double exponential in the parameter bound.
We believe it would be interesting to see an analogue of our
Theorem

1.2
for graphs of bounded induced matching treewidth.
