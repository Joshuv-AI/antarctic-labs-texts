Risk factors, microbiological resistance, and prognostic scoring in Fournier’s gangrene: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chenghong Mou, Wenke Xu, Yuan Fang, Ping Qin, Ziqiang Song, Wenqiu Feng, Ying He, Chungen Zhou, Zhengxin Chen, Yong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFournier’s gangrene is a rapidly progressing necrotizing fasciitis of the perineal and genital regions with high mortality. This study aimed to identify prognostic factors and evaluate scoring systems for risk stratification.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 228 Fournier’s gangrene patients was conducted. Baseline data, comorbidities, laboratory findings, microbial cultures, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were reviewed. Univariate and Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression identified independent mortality predictors. The prognostic value of LRINEC, FGSI, UFGSI, and ACCI scores was assessed via ROC analysis.ResultsMortality was 9.6%. Independent predictors included sepsis (OR = 14.270, P = 0.0008), paraplegia (OR = 18.786, P = 0.0114), and elevated lactate (OR = 1.117, P = 0.0017). Acinetobacter baumannii infection and carbapenem resistance were associated with poor outcomes. UFGSI achieved the best prognostic performance (AUC = 0.933, sensitivity 90.9%, specificity 83.0%).ConclusionGram-negative and mixed infections, particularly those involving Acinetobacter baumannii, represent high-risk subtypes of Fournier’s gangrene associated with increased mortality. Extensive antimicrobial resistance among Gram-negative pathogens underscores the need to revise empirical treatment strategies. Sepsis, paraplegia, and elevated lactate levels independently predict death, while the UFGSI demonstrates superior prognostic accuracy. Early recognition of these risk factors and timely intervention are crucial for improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Fournier’s gangrene, Microbiology, Prognostic factors, Uluozlu's Fournier's Gangrene Severity Index

Introduction

Fournier’s gangrene (FG) is a rare but rapidly progressive and potentially fatal necrotizing fasciitis, predominantly involving the perineum, perianal region, and external genitalia [1]. Despite advances in surgical management, antimicrobial therapy, and intensive care, FG continues to carry substantial morbidity and mortality, with reported rates ranging from 20% to 40% [2]. The disease is hallmarked by extensive tissue necrosis and a high risk of systemic complications, including sepsis, underscoring the importance of early recognition and prompt surgical intervention to improve survival outcomes.

We aimed to retrospectively analyze the clinical characteristics of patients with FG treated at our center between January 2019 and August 2025. We evaluated baseline demographics, comorbidities, microbial distribution, antimicrobial resistance patterns, disease extent, and severity scores to identify independent predictors of poor outcomes. Although multiple prognostic tools have been proposed for FG, there is still no consensus on the optimal model for risk stratification. By integrating clinical, microbiological, and prognostic data, we aimed to comprehensively identify key risk factors. This approach could help inform more precise, individualized strategies for patient management.

Methods

This retrospective study included all patients diagnosed with FG who were treated at the Anorectal Center of Nanjing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2019 and August 2025. Patients were identified through the hospital information system (HIS) using ICD-10 codes for FG, and their medical records were reviewed in detail. Inclusion criteria comprised a confirmed diagnosis of FG based on clinical, laboratory, intraoperative, and histopathological findings. Patients with necrotizing fasciitis at other sites or with incomplete medical records were excluded. Upon admission, all patients received broad-spectrum antibiotics and underwent timely emergency surgery with extensive debridement of necrotic tissue. Surgical specimens were sent for microbiological culture, antibiotic susceptibility testing, and histopathological examination. Comprehensive patient data were collected, including demographics (age, sex, body mass index), time from symptom onset to surgery, comorbidities, and complications at admission. Sepsis was defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by infection, identified by an increase in SOFA score ≥ 2 points. Laboratory parameters recorded included body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell count, hemoglobin, and serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, bicarbonate). Additional parameters included serum creatinine, blood glucose, serum albumin, platelet count, procalcitonin (PCT), and serum lactate. Severity scores, including LRINEC, FGSI, UFGSI, and ACCI, were calculated. The anatomical extent of infection was documented and categorized by affected regions. Microbiological findings were classified by pathogen type and antibiotic susceptibility. Clinical outcomes were systematically recorded for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 and R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences assessed by the log-rank test. The predictive performance of LRINEC, FGSI, UFGSI, and ACCI scores for mortality was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of mortality. To address potential bias caused by rare events and small sample sizes within certain subgroups, Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was applied. The strength of association was expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 228 patients were included from 261 cases identified with a diagnostic code for necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI), as summarized in Table 1, which presents the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Among these, 83.8% were male, with a mean age of 54.99 ± 16.41 years. The median body mass index (BMI) was 25.57 (IQR, 22.82–27.50), and the median time from admission to surgery was 7.6 h (IQR, 6.0–9.0). The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 9.6% (22/228).

Baseline characteristics

Patients were stratified into survival (n = 206) and death (n = 22) groups according to clinical outcome, as shown in Table 1. Although the majority of deaths occurred in male patients, the mortality rate among female patients was significantly higher than that of the male patients (21.6% vs. 7.3%, P = 0.007). The median age of the death group was 64 years (IQR, 56–70), significantly higher than that of the survival group (P = 0.016). Median BMI was slightly higher in the survival group, but this difference was not statistically significant. Time from admission to surgery was comparable between the groups (7.6 vs. 7.5 h, P = 0.798). Diabetes was the most common comorbidity, with similar prevalence in the survival (61.7%) and death (54.5%) groups (P = 0.140). Other comorbidities were significantly more frequent in non-survivors, including hypertension (68.2% vs. 32.0%, P = 0.007), chronic kidney disease (31.8% vs. 7.8%, P = 0.0003), chronic liver disease (18.2% vs. 6.3%, P = 0.044), and paraplegia (22.7% vs. 1.5%, P = 0.044). Sepsis was markedly more common in the death group (63.6% vs. 6.3%, P < 0.001), whereas other conditions such as heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, and malignancy showed no significant differences.

Stratification of extent of the gangrene

The severity of FG differed significantly between groups. The extent of gangrene was stratified based on combined clinical evaluation and radiological imaging (CT or MRI) findings, confirming fascial involvement and the anatomical spread of necrosis. Lesions were classified into three grades based on extent: grade I (perianal, perineum, scrotum, or other urogenital areas), grade II (grade I plus pubic bone and pelvic region), and grade III (extension beyond the pelvis) [3]. Grade III gangrene was significantly more prevalent among non-survivors (77.3% vs. 24.8%, P < 0.01), whereas grade I was observed only in survivors (24.3% vs. 0%, P < 0.01). As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed worse prognosis for grade III gangrene (Log-rank P = 0.009).

Culture and susceptibility

Microbiology

All patients underwent surgical debridement, and specimens were cultured. No pathogens were isolated in 6.35% of patients. Of the identified pathogens, as shown in Fig. 2A, 196 were Gram-negative bacteria, 81 were Gram-positive bacteria, and 3 were fungi. Table 2 presents the microbiological profile and resistance patterns. Escherichia coli was the most common Gram-negative pathogen (50.5% vs. 50.0%, P = 0.965), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (17.5% vs. 27.3%, P = 0.260). Proteus mirabilis was detected only in survivors (7.3% vs. 0%, P = 0.190). Pathogens significantly associated with mortality included Acinetobacter baumannii (18.2% vs. 1.5%, P < 0.05), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (13.6% vs. 1.0%, P < 0.05), and Staphylococcus aureus (9.1% vs. 1.9%, P = 0.046). Multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) were more prevalent in non-survivors (50.0% vs. 14.1%, P < 0.05), particularly carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (22.7% vs. 8.7%, P = 0.038), Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; 9.1% vs. 1.5%, P = 0.020), Methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci (MRCNS; 18.2% vs. 2.9%, P = 0.001), and Carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB; 18.2% vs. 1.5%, P < 0.05).

Susceptibility

All microbial isolates underwent antibiotic susceptibility testing. Figure 2B C show the microbial antibiotic resistance patterns. Overall, Gram-negative bacteria exhibited high resistance rates to commonly used β-lactams, with 68.9% of isolates resistant. Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was notable, with only 33.2% of isolates susceptible to ceftriaxone, while resistance to cefepime and cefoxitin was 27.0% and 23.5%, respectively. Carbapenem resistance was particularly pronounced in Acinetobacter baumannii, with 100% of isolates resistant to imipenem and meropenem, whereas the overall carbapenem resistance rate among Gram-negative bacteria was 7.7%. Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibited 14.3–16.7% resistance to carbapenems, and Escherichia coli showed relatively low resistance (2.6–5.2%). Among Gram-positive bacteria, susceptibility to penicillin was limited (35.8%), and resistance to erythromycin was common (49.4%). Moderate activity was observed for clindamycin and levofloxacin, with sensitivities of 39.5% and 23.5%, respectively. Resistance to macrolides such as azithromycin was widespread, with overall susceptibility of 17.3%, and resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) was 14.8%. Notably, no vancomycin-resistant Gram-positive isolates were detected.

Laboratory and clinical parameters

Non-survivors exhibited more severe systemic involvement on admission. As shown in Table 1, non-survivors presented with more severe systemic derangements at admission. Although body temperature and total leukocyte count did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.878 and P = 0.218, respectively), non-survivors exhibited markedly higher levels of C-reactive protein (CRP: 211.2 vs. 124.0 mg/L, P = 0.036) and procalcitonin (PCT: 5.50 vs. 1.27 ng/mL, P < 0.05), indicating a more pronounced inflammatory response. Hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in non-survivors (104.6 ± 19.6 vs. 119.5 ± 24.3 g/L, P = 0.006), suggesting anemia and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Electrolyte profiles, including serum sodium and potassium, showed no significant differences between groups (P > 0.05), and serum creatinine and glucose levels were also comparable (P > 0.05). Metabolic parameters reflected greater physiological compromise in non-survivors, with significantly lower serum bicarbonate (23.8 vs. 26.7 mmol/L, P < 0.05) and albumin levels (31.7 vs. 35.7 g/L, P = 0.014). Lactate levels were notably elevated in the non-survivor group (2.96 vs. 1.69 mmol/L, P = 0.001), consistent with increased tissue hypoperfusion. Platelet counts did not differ significantly between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Type of necrotizing fasciitis

Patients were categorized by microbial type: Type I (mixed bacteria, 54.6%), Type II (Gram-positive), and Type III (Gram-negative). Type I infections were more common among non-survivors, with significant differences in mortality across groups (P = 0.040). Figure. 1B presents survival outcomes by microbial type. Kaplan–Meier analysis suggested a trend toward worse survival in Type I infections (Log-rank P = 0.058).

Clinical risk factors

As detailed in Table 3, Firth’s penalized logistic regression identified sepsis (OR = 14.270, 95% CI: 2.942–90.479, P = 0.0008), paraplegia (OR = 18.786, 95% CI: 1.891–276.068, P = 0.0114), and lactate (OR = 1.117, 95% CI: 1.043–1.220, P = 0.0017) as independent predictors of mortality. Hypertension (P = 0.059) and low hemoglobin (P = 0.070) showed trends toward significance but did not reach statistical thresholds. Other variables, including age, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, hypoalbuminemia, inflammatory markers (CRP, PCT), and serum bicarbonate, were not significantly associated with mortality after adjustment.

Discussion

Fournier’s gangrene (FG) is a rare but life-threatening infectious disease, and previous studies have included few female patients [4]. In our cohort, we reported a relatively large sample of female patients (n = 37). Interestingly, mortality among females was significantly higher than that among males (p = 0.007). Previous studies generally found no gender-related differences in mortality [5], although Czymek et al. suggested that female sex may be a risk factor, potentially due to anatomical differences, as infections in females are more likely to extend into the retroperitoneal and peritoneal spaces [6]. However, gender did not emerge as an independent predictor of mortality in our multivariate regression analysis. Fournier’s gangrene (FG) in females is uncommon and often presents atypically, accounting for only a small proportion of reported cases. Its manifestations may mimic perineal cellulitis, Bartholin gland abscess, or postoperative infection, frequently leading to diagnostic delays. Such delays can prolong the pre-debridement interval, allowing further infection spread and resulting in more extensive necrosis at surgery. In our cohort, female patients showed a slightly higher prevalence of Gram-negative polymicrobial infections and diabetes mellitus—both associated with poorer outcomes. They were generally older and presented with higher serum lactate levels and a greater incidence of sepsis, suggesting a more advanced disease state on admission. These factors likely contributed to the higher mortality observed in females. However, the limited number of female cases reduced the statistical power of our multivariate analysis, preventing confirmation of sex as an independent predictor. Nonetheless, potential biological or clinical influences—such as perineal anatomy, immune response, hormonal milieu, or healthcare-seeking behavior—cannot be excluded. Larger multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and to elucidate sex-specific mechanisms and risk factors, which may inform tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for improving female FG outcomes.

Most patients in our cohort were middle-aged or elderly men, with a high prevalence of comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease, consistent with prior reports [7]. Diabetes, renal disease, and advanced age have been confirmed as important mortality risk factors in FG [3], while Mladenov et al. further demonstrated that having ≥ 3 comorbidities or liver disease significantly increased the risk of death [8].

Early surgical intervention remains a cornerstone for improving survival in patients with Fournier’s gangrene. Previous studies have demonstrated that delays in diagnosis and debridement markedly increase mortality [9], likely due to continued toxin release, systemic sepsis, and rapid progression of tissue necrosis. In our cohort, however, no significant difference was observed between the two groups. The median interval from admission to initial surgical debridement was 7.6 h (IQR 6.0–9.0 h), reflecting our center’s strong emphasis on prompt infection source control. This rapid intervention may partly account for the relatively low mortality rate observed, despite the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant pathogens. Nevertheless, even modest variations in timing could influence patient outcomes, highlighting the importance of early recognition and the implementation of standardized protocols to ensure timely debridement.

In our analysis, sepsis (p = 0.044) and paraplegia (p = 0.033) were identified as strong independent predictors of mortality. Among paraplegic patients, the longest interval from symptom onset to surgical intervention reached 40 days. Sensory deficits in the perineal and genital regions delayed recognition of early infection signs, such as pain, redness, and swelling, often resulting in advanced disease at presentation. These patients were frequently bedridden and faced additional risks, including urinary and fecal incontinence. In our cohort, all patients with sepsis were infected either with mixed bacteria (12/27) or gram-negative bacteria (15/27), with most infections involving multidrug-resistant organisms. The resulting physiological burden often precluded repeated surgical debridement, creating a vicious cycle that contributed to mortality. Notably, one young male patient presented with FG secondary to Crohn’s disease ileocolitis and underwent enterostomy following debridement. He was discharged with wound healing on day 20, consistent with previously reported cases [10]. Although it is rare, this highlights the importance of early recognition of underlying inflammatory bowel disease in FG, as timely management of the primary condition may be critical for improving outcomes.

Previous large-scale reports have indicated that the most common etiology of FG originates from the colorectal region (20%-60%), followed by genitourinary (15%-40%) and cutaneous sources (7.5%-25%) [11]. Our findings are consistent with these reports, with the majority of pathogenic bacteria being Gram-negative, primarily Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae [12]. Overall, resistance rates to commonly used β-lactams were high, with 68.9% of Gram-negative isolates exhibiting resistance. Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was particularly concerning, as only 33.2% of isolates remained susceptible to ceftriaxone. Resistance to cefepime (27.0%) and cefoxitin (23.5%) was also widespread. Among Gram-negative pathogens, the resistance of Klebsiella pneumoniae to carbapenems ranged from 14.3% to 16.7%, whereas Escherichia coli demonstrated relatively low resistance (2.6%-5.2%). Although tigecycline and polymyxin B retained some activity, their low susceptibility rates (4.1% and 7.1%, respectively) underscored the limited therapeutic options available. Multidrug-resistant patterns followed similar trends.

Gram-positive bacteria exhibited limited susceptibility to penicillin (35.8%), with Staphylococcus haemolyticus demonstrating universal resistance. Clindamycin and levofloxacin showed moderate activity, with sensitivities of 39.5% and 23.5%, respectively, while macrolides, such as azithromycin, were largely ineffective (overall sensitivity 17.3%). Tetracycline resistance was variable (21.0%), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) resistance accounted for 14.8% of isolates. Notably, vancomycin retained excellent activity, with no resistant Gram-positive isolates detected. Current guidelines recommend early empirical therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam plus vancomycin, cefotaxime plus metronidazole or clindamycin, or carbapenems alone [13]. In our cohort, the rate of clindamycin resistance among Gram-positive bacteria was 39.51%.

Of particular concern, Acinetobacter baumannii exhibited complete resistance to imipenem and meropenem (100%), which was strongly associated with mortality (p < 0.001), consistent with previous reports by Yilmazlar et al. [14]. By contrast, the overall carbapenem resistance among Gram-negative bacteria was relatively low (7.7%). Among multidrug-resistant Gram-negative isolates, piperacillin-tazobactam resistance reached 69.57% (16/23), while tigecycline resistance remained the lowest (13.04%, 3/23). In our center, empirical therapy commonly combines third-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, nitroimidazoles, and tetracyclines. Importantly, even infections caused by a single Gram-negative organism carried a notable mortality rate of 6.8%, highlighting the clinical severity of these infections despite targeted antimicrobial therapy.

The absence of pathogen growth in a subset of cases (6.35%) may be attributed to preoperative use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which can reduce bacterial load and interfere with culture yield. In addition, sampling from necrotic or poorly perfused tissue and delays in specimen transport may further contribute to negative culture results. These factors underscore the importance of obtaining deep tissue samples early and maintaining strict anaerobic conditions during culture.

The traditional classification of Fournier’s gangrene (FG) into Type I (mixed aerobic and anaerobic) and Type II (streptococcal or staphylococcal) infections appears inadequate to explain the high mortality associated with Gram-negative monomicrobial infections [15]. In our cohort, Type I infections exhibited the highest mortality rate (18.8%), with mixed Gram-positive and Gram-negative infections predominating (66.7%) and a notable increase in Gram-positive isolates, particularly in grade III gangrene. Based on these findings, we propose a refined pathogenic classification that incorporates a Gram-negative infection type and a high-risk subtype characterized by mixed Gram-negative and Gram-positive infections. This updated framework may facilitate earlier identification of high-risk patients and support timely, targeted therapeutic interventions. Additionally, MRCNS (10/40) and MASA (5/40) isolates were identified, suggesting that pathogen distribution and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in Asian populations may differ from those reported in Western cohorts. Geographic and occupational factors may contribute to these differences. Huang et al. reported that exposure to seawater or raw seafood in certain regions predisposed individuals to infections with Gram-negative pathogens, particularly Vibrio and Aeromonas species, including Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Aeromonas hydrophila, which were considered major causative agents of necrotizing fasciitis [16]. Moreover, the rarer infection types III and IV, as defined by Hagedorn et al., caused by Vibrio marinus and fungi (e.g., Candida), respectively [15], were infrequent in our cohort. The widespread cross-resistance among multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) further complicates antimicrobial selection, particularly given the complex comorbidities often present in FG patients. These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating both infection type and antimicrobial resistance patterns when devising empirical therapy, with early coverage for Gram-negative pathogens and prompt adjustment based on culture and susceptibility results being essential to optimize outcomes.

Several scoring systems, including FGSI, UFGSI, LRINEC, and ACCI, have been applied to evaluate disease severity in FG. The LRINEC score was initially developed to differentiate necrotizing from non- necrotizing fasciitis [17], with a score ≥ 6 suggesting a higher likelihood of necrotizing infection. Previous studies indicated that while LRINEC is useful for diagnostic purposes—particularly in patients with electrolyte disturbances or renal impairment—it demonstrates limited capability in predicting clinical outcomes [18]. Mladenov et al. [8] further noted that the sensitivity of LRINEC may vary across different populations, especially when blood cultures are positive or multiple anatomical sites are involved.

In our cohort, the LRINEC score yielded an AUC of 0.719 (95% CI: 0.613–0.824), with an optimal cut-off of 5.5, suggesting only modest predictive performance in this retrospective analysis. Nonetheless, patients classified as high-risk by LRINEC exhibited significantly higher mortality compared to those with low-risk scores, highlighting its potential value as a prognostic adjunct, albeit with limitations in accurately stratifying outcomes in all FG patients.

Previous studies have reported that an FGSI score ≥ 9 corresponds to a 75% probability of death, whereas a score ≤ 9 indicates a 78% probability of survival [19]. However, Tuncel et al. [20] suggested that FGSI may not reliably predict disease severity or patient survival, highlighting the influence of metabolic abnormalities, predisposing conditions, and the extent of infection as critical factors in determining prognosis. Although FGSI generally demonstrates superior discriminative power compared with LRINEC, our analysis revealed an AUC of 0.832 (95% CI: 0.741–0.923). Notably, the optimal threshold in our cohort was 6.5, considerably lower than the classical cut-off proposed by Laor et al., a finding also supported by Uluğ et al. [21]. This suggests that even a moderately elevated FGSI score may serve as a warning for poor prognosis, emphasizing the need for careful clinical interpretation in risk stratification.

Charlson et al. developed the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (ACCI) as a prospective tool to classify comorbidities that may influence mortality risk [22]. In theory [23], ACCI demonstrated the largest AUC and all scoring systems in our cohort were capable of identifying high-risk patients. However, in our study, ACCI showed only moderate predictive performance for FG, with an AUC of 0.727 (95% CI: 0.646–0.808). Using a cut-off value of 3.5, the sensitivity reached 86.4%, whereas the specificity was limited to 54.9%. Despite its relatively lower discriminative power, the ACCI remains practical and easy to calculate, offering general applicability in clinical settings.

The UFGSI scoring system, as proposed by Yilmazlar et al., integrates patient age with disease severity on the basis of the FGSI. A UFGSI score greater than 9 corresponds to a 94% probability of death, whereas a score of 9 or lower indicates an 81% probability of survival [3]. In our cohort, the UFGSI was well validated. ROC curve demonstrated that UFGSI had the highest predictive value among the scoring systems evaluated, with an AUC of 0.933. Using a cut-off value of 9.5, the sensitivity and specificity reached 90.9% and 83.0%, respectively, indicating excellent prognostic performance superior to FGSI, LRINEC, and ACCI—consistent with the superiority of UFGSI reported by Yilmazlar. Our multivariate analysis further identified elevated lactate levels (P = 0.0017), paraplegia(P = 0.0114), and sepsis(P = 0.0008) as independent risk factors, highlighting the importance of metabolic derangements and comorbidities in disease progression. Taken together, these findings suggest that incorporating specific clinical indicators alongside comorbidity parameters may enhance the accuracy of prognostic models for FG.

In the clinical management of FG, the Extent of Gangrene classification holds considerable significance. Previous studies by Yilmazlar et al. [3] suggested that a broader extent of gangrene often reflects the spread of infection beyond local anatomical barriers, involving wider subcutaneous and fascial tissues, which contributes to a higher necrotic tissue burden and an increased risk of sepsis. Susini et al. [24] further demonstrated that a larger gangrene area necessitates more extensive debridement, greater tissue resection, and is associated with increased perioperative complications and mortality. Wongwaisayawan et al. [25] emphasized the utility of CT imaging in confirming diagnosis, identifying the source of infection, and assessing disease extent, which is critical for surgical planning. Accordingly, gangrene grading serves not only as an objective measure of disease severity but also as a practical guide for tailoring treatment strategies.

In our cohort, etiology varied with the infection site, with FG primarily driven by Gram-negative pathogens, particularly in patients with grade I and II gangrene, whereas grade III gangrene exhibited a higher proportion of Gram-positive or mixed infections. Mortality among patients with grade III gangrene was significantly higher than those with grade I–II, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis confirmed a strong correlation between gangrene grade and overall survival. From a surgical perspective, gangrene classification provides guidance on the extent of debridement and operative approach: grade I gangrene can often be managed with a single, thorough debridement, whereas grade III typically requires multiple staged procedures, potentially combined with enterostomy, negative pressure drainage, and flap reconstruction. Notably, gangrene grading in our study correlated with lactate levels, sepsis, paraplegia, and UFGSI scores in multivariate analysis.

Palm et al. reported that patients with more than 5% affected body surface area had higher mortality, though no linear correlation was observed [26]. Based on these findings, we advocate for standardization of gangrene grading and its integration into comprehensive risk prediction models. By combining comorbidities, laboratory indices, gangrene extent, and pathogen characteristics, a more precise prognostic framework can be developed, facilitating optimized surgical planning and postoperative management.

Early studies have identified several risk factors for FG, including diabetes mellitus, obesity, chronic alcoholism, smoking, renal failure, liver failure, malignancy, and HIV infection [27]. In our cohort, sepsis, paraplegia and lactate elevation emerged as independent predictors of mortality. These findings were consistent with trends observed in univariate analysis. Notably, sepsis was a strong independent predictor (OR = 14.270, P = 0.0008), underscoring the critical role of systemic inflammatory response and multiple organ dysfunction in driving high mortality. This aligns with the observations of Mladenov et al. [8], who reported that sepsis and positive blood cultures significantly increased death risk in FG patients. Accordingly, early recognition of sepsis, coupled with prompt interventions: fluid resuscitation, antimicrobial therapy, and nutritional support, is essential for improving outcomes.

Paraplegic patients demonstrated markedly elevated mortality risk (OR = 18.786, P = 0.0114). These patients frequently present with pressure ulcers and multiple comorbidities, further complicating infection management. To our knowledge, this association has not been previously reported. Our findings suggest that patients with neurological limb dysfunction should be regarded as a very high-risk group, warranting dedicated risk stratification in future studies. Although paraplegia emerged as an independent predictor of mortality, the confidence interval was wide (95% CI: 1.891–276.068), reflecting substantial statistical uncertainty. This is likely attributable to the small number of paraplegic patients in our cohort, particularly the limited number of mortality events in this subgroup. While the association remained statistically significant, caution is warranted in interpreting the magnitude of the effect. Larger multicenter studies are needed to more precisely estimate the prognostic impact of paraplegia in Fournier’s gangrene. Lactate elevation was also independently associated with mortality (OR = 1.117, 95% CI: 1.043–1.220, P = 0.0017). As a widely used indicator of tissue hypoperfusion and illness severity, lactate may provide clinically meaningful prognostic information, supporting its role in early risk stratification and ongoing clinical assessment.

Importantly, UFGSI demonstrated robust prognostic performance in ROC analysis, likely because it integrates age stratification with disease extent and thereby provides a comprehensive assessment of severity. ROC analysis in our study (AUC = 0.933) further supports the use of UFGSI as the preferred risk assessment tool for FG in clinical practice, providing superior prognostic guidance compared with FGSI, LRINEC, and ACCI.

Conclusion

In this large-scale cohort study, we present an updated etiological classification of Fournier’s gangrene, identifying Gram-negative and mixed Gram-negative/Gram-positive infections as high-risk subtypes—particularly those involving Acinetobacter baumannii, which is strongly associated with increased mortality. Gram-negative pathogens exhibited extensive resistance to third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems, highlighting the need to revise current empirical antibiotic regimens. The extent of necrosis showed a strong correlation with mortality, underscoring the importance of timely and aggressive surgical intervention. Sepsis, paraplegia, and elevated lactate levels were identified as independent predictors of death. Among prognostic scoring systems, the UFGSI demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy, outperforming the FGSI, LRINEC, and ACCI. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to establish standardized management protocols that integrate clinical and microbiological risk factors.
