Stability and Thermophysical Properties of Silicon Carbide and Carbon Nanofibers Thermal Oil Hybrid Nanofluids
Authors: Suhaib Umer Ilyas, Lim Li Shan, Nawal Noshad, Haris Naseer, Mustafa Alsaady, Aymn Abdulrahman, Anas Ahmed, Rajashekhar Pendyala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybrid Nanofluids (HNFs) have been widely investigated to improve the thermal efficiency of energy systems, thereby reducing overall energy consumption. Silicon Carbide (SiC) nanoparticles offer excellent thermal stability, and Carbon Nanofibers (CNF) provide superior phonon-mediated thermal conduction. However, their synergistic combination as HNFs remains largely unexplored. In this study, SiC and CNF are dispersed in thermal oil (THO) at concentrations of 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% by mass, using ultrasonication and the surfactant Span 85. The batch sedimentation stability analysis is performed on four stages to achieve the long-term stability of SiC-CNF HNFs. The thermophysical properties of the SiC-CNF HNFs are investigated over the temperature range 25 –75 °C. SiC-CNF HNFs exhibit a maximum thermal conductivity enhancement of 32.3% at 0.5 wt% compared to THO at 55 °C. A maximum viscosity and density enhancement of 18.7% and 1.55%, respectively, is calculated. SiC-CNF HNFs followed both the power law and the Herschel-Bulkley model and exhibited non-Newtonian behavior. Artificial neural network (ANN) models are developed to predict the thermophysical properties of SiC-CNF HNFs effectively. The optimized models achieved coefficients of determination (R²) values of 0.7606 for thermal conductivity, 0.9844 for viscosity, and 0.9999 for density, respectively. These experimental and modeling results indicate the significance of SiC-CNF-based HNFs for effective thermal management systems integration.

Introduction

Heating/cooling systems account for a large percentage of the world’s energy consumption, emphasizing the need to improve the thermal efficiency of domestic and industrial applications. Nanofluids are colloidal suspensions of nano-additives of metals, metal oxides, and carbon allotropes (typically < 100 nm) in conventional heat transfer fluids (HTFs). HNFs comprise more than one type of nanoparticle and have greater hydrodynamic, thermal, and rheological properties than mono nanofluids [1, 2].

The dispersion of nanoparticles boosts the heat transfer characteristics of HTFs by improving their thermophysical properties, thus exhibiting a considerably better system performance [3, 4]. An efficient thermal system confines fossil fuel consumption, resulting in a lower carbon footprint, whilst also decreasing the embedded energy in the equipment by reducing its size [5, 6]. Al2O3-CuO-based HNFs are used in a turbulent circular tube, and the HNFs reduced the total entropy generation by up to 71% [7]. HNFs have been investigated in several engineering applications, such as spray cooling [8], heat exchanges [9], and solar thermal collectors [10]. Energy and exergy assessment of a flat plate solar thermal collector is examined via SiC NFs with vol. conc. of 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.075%, and 0.1%. Mass flow rate is varied between 0.025, 0.033, and 0.041 kg/s. Thermal efficiency, exergy efficiency, and K are improved up to 77.43%, 37.4%, and 30.3%, respectively [11]. Wahab et al. [12] investigated the application of GO-CuO and ZnO-SiC HNFs in photovoltaic thermal (PVT) systems for green hydrogen production. The results demonstrated that HNFs significantly improved thermal management, achieving a combined energy efficiency exceeding 44%. ZnO/SiC HNFs at 0.25 vol% yielded the highest cumulative daily hydrogen production of 9.28 mol/h, confirming the promising role of hybrid nanofluids in enhancing PVT system performance for renewable energy applications.

Thermal conductivity (K), viscosity (µ), density (ρ), and specific heat capacity (CP) play a critical role in determining the convective heat transfer coefficient. These properties in HNFs depend on the morphology and structure of nanoparticles, their concentration in the base fluid, the type of base fluid, pH, and temperature [13, 14]. Experimental viscosity of fly ash-Cu-based HNFs is measured at vol. conc. of 0–4% and 30 –60 °C. A maximum viscosity ratio of 1.45 is calculated at 4% vol. conc. and 30 °C [15]. The synergistic effect of GNP, MWCNTs, and CNF in various configurations in THO, and their K, ρ, and Cp are measured at wt. conc. of 0–2%. The synergistic effects in K favor GNP NFs over HNFs systems, yet HNFs exhibit superior CP and ρ. GNP NF, GNP+MWCNTs HNFs, and GNP + CNF HNFs demonstrated the K enhancement up to 182.19%, 175.34%, and 169.86%, respectively [16]. Moreover, CNF exhibited better thermal characteristics than the MWCNTs. Ilyas et al. [17] investigated the rheological behavior of diamond and GNP (1:1) in THO at varying nanoparticle concentrations (0–2 wt%), between 25 and 65 °C. These HNFs demonstrated shear-thinning non-Newtonian behavior. A maximum of 35% enhancement in viscosity is observed at 2 wt%. Abulhassan Ali et al. [18] carried out experiments to determine the thermal and rheological behavior of diamond and boron nitride HNFs. The HNFs are prepared using 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6% wt%. Almost 37.4% and 48.1% enhancements are observed in the K and CP, respectively, at 65 °C and 0.6% wt%. HNFs displayed Newtonian behavior at low shear rates. Shear-thinning non-Newtonian behavior is observed at high shear rates and temperatures. A maximum enhancement in µ up to 22.0% is observed at 65 °C and 0.6% wt%. Ahmed et al. [19] studied the CP of Diamond NFs in the temperature range 35–80 °C and observed an 8.25% decrement at 1 wt%. Nasim Nayebpashaee [20] experimentally investigated the thermophysical properties of a novel Al2O3-TiO2-CuO-Fe2O3 Quadri HNFs, µ, electrical conductivity, and surface tension over 25 –67 °C at vol. conc. of 0.1-1%. The µ exhibited two distinct rheological regimes, i.e., a low-shear Newtonian regime governed by base fluid properties and nanoparticle loading, and a high-shear shear-thickening regime driven by hydrodynamic clustering. A molten salt-based silica NFs is prepared and shows a 26% increase in CP at 1wt% [21]. The ML models for predicting complex and non-linear relationships within datasets have been utilized as a powerful data-driven technique in recent years. These models enhance predictive accuracy, significantly reduce computational time and experimental cost, and provide smooth integration with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) tools. Several ML algorithms, such as Random Forest [22], Decision Tree [23], Gradient Boosting Regression [1], Support Vector Regression (SVR) [24, 25], Support Vector Machines (SVM) [26, 27], Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [28, 29] Gaussian Process Regression [1, 30] and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) [31–33] have been developed, with discrete learning mechanisms and optimization for nonlinear datasets.

ANN models are capable of learning complex nonlinear relationships directly from experimental data without requiring prior assumptions about underlying physics, which enables ANN to capture complex patterns among thermophysical parameters. Nanda Kisore PVR et al. [34] modeled the ANN models for K and µ of GNP, CuO, and Alumina ternary HNFs and calculated the overall regression of up to 0.99496. Davood et al. [35] utilized an ANN model to estimate the µ of WO3-MWCNTs/Engine oil and achieved a correlation of up to 0.999837. Mojtaba Sepehrnia et al. [36] modeled an ANN on the µ dataset of ZnO-MWCNTs HNFs and produced high prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.9973). Praveen Kanti et al. [15] adopted ANN and Multi-gene genetic programming (MGGP) for fly ash-Cu HNFs. MGGP demonstrated excellent performance by achieving R = 0.9975, RMSE = 0.0063, and MAPE = 0.664%.

The biggest challenge in the exploitation of HNFs is their instability. The higher surface energy of smaller nanoparticles increases the possibility of aggregation of nanoparticles because of the attractive forces between them. The nanoparticles grow up to an average of a micron in diameter after undergoing agglomeration, causing sedimentation. Despite considerable progress in HNFs research, most studies focus on hybrid combinations involving conventional materials such as oxides, MWCNTs, Al₂O₃, TiO₂, or Cu. The simultaneous use of SiC, known for its high thermal stability and chemical inertness, and CNF, recognized for its exceptional aspect ratio, mechanical strength, and phonon transport capability, as an HNFs system has gained very limited attention [11, 45]. There is no literature available on SiC-CNF hybrid nanofluids and their thermophysical properties. The unique structural and thermal compatibility between these SiC and CNFs is expected to produce a synergistic enhancement in thermophysical properties. To address this research gap, the present study investigates the thermophysical behavior of SiC-CNF HNFs at varying concentrations and temperatures.

The stability and the effect of different parameters, such as temperature and SiC-CNF concentration, on the thermophysical properties are experimentally measured. SiC-CNF (1:1) is dispersed in THO by the two-step method with wt% of 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0%, and is stabilized using a non-ionic surfactant, Span-85. The dispersion behavior of these nanofluids is observed over a period of time, and the K, µ, and ρ of the stable SiC-CNF HNFs are examined in a temperature range of 25 °C to 75 °C. The power law model and the Herschel-Bulkley model are utilized to analyze the rheological behavior of SiC-CNF HNFs. At the end, ANN models are developed to accurately predict the K, µ, and ρ of these nanofluids at wt%, temperature, and shear rate values.

Methodology

Materials

Vapor-grown CNF (Herringbone type) of average diameter 200 nm and average length 20 μm, and SiC (purity > 99%) of average diameter 30–50 nm, are utilized in this study. The purity of THO is between 70 and 99 wt% with carbon content < 97 wt%, and comprises hydrocarbons of C15-C50. Characterization of SiC and CNF is carried out to affirm their morphology and composition, using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR).

Synthesis of SiC-CNF HNFs

SiC-CNF HNFs are prepared by the two-step method followed by ultrasonication for uniform dispersion, in different wt. conc., i.e., 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.%. An equal proportion of SiC and CNF is maintained at 1:1. Non-ionic Span 85 (Sorbitane trioleate) of empirical formula C60H108O8 is used as a stabilizer. Ultrasonication and Span-85 surfactant are incorporated to ensure the stability of SiC-CNF HNFs in the THO. Ultrasonication is carried out using a probe-type ultrasonicator (Biologics 150 V/T, USA) operating at 20 kHz. The process involves homogenizing SiC-CNF HNFs samples at room temperature for 60 min with a power and pulse cycle of 70% and 30%, respectively. The temperature of the SiC-CNF HNFs samples during ultrasonication is maintained at < 50 °C by using a controlled-temperature water bath. Batch sedimentation studies are carried out to determine the stability of prepared SiC-CNF HNFs with and without the addition of Span-85 surfactant.

Development of Artificial Neural Network (ANN) Models

The ANN model is developed in MATLAB using the Neural Network Fitting Tool (nnstart), with wt. conc., temperature, and shear rate as input variables, and K, µ, and density as output parameters. For ML modeling, data is a crucial factor that directly influences algorithms’ capability to train and validate the patterns [46]. For that, an experimentally validated dataset is preprocessed and partitioned using a random data division (dividerand), allocating 70% for training and 15% each for validation and testing, a widely adopted distribution ensuring robust generalization. Network training is performed using the Levenberg-Marquardt backpropagation algorithm (trainlm), selected for its superior convergence speed and computational efficiency in small- to medium-sized dataset regression analysis. The optimal number of hidden layers is determined iteratively to minimize overfitting while maximizing predictive accuracy. Model performance is evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (R²), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). This strategy provides a comprehensive assessment of the model’s accuracy, reliability, and generalizability across the full range of thermophysical property predictions. Results and Discussion

Characterization of SiC-CNF Nanoparticles

FESEM images of SiC-CNF hybrid nanoparticles using Zeiss Supra 55 VP under the magnification of 10.00 KX and 30.00 KX are displayed in Fig. 1. The FESEM images of the SiC-CNF hybrid nanopowder reveal a clearly prominent tubular fibrous morphology, characteristic of CNF, with consistent diameter and uniform size distribution throughout the hybrid powder matrix. No surface chemical attachments or functionalization are observed on the CNF surface, confirming the chemically pristine nature of the nanofibers. SiC nanoparticles are distinctly identified as lighter-contrast irregular structures uniformly distributed along and around the CNF matrix, exhibiting a consistent and identifiable morphology that corroborates the successful physical mixing of the two nanoparticle species. Some degree of localized agglomeration is observed within the hybrid powder, which is attributable to the high surface energy and van der Waals interactions inherent to nanosized particles in dry powder. The EDX analysis of SiC-CNF hybrid nanoparticles, depicted in Fig. 2, reveals the composition of three elements found, i.e., silicon, carbon, and oxygen. Carbon has the highest weight% of 89.78%, followed by oxygen and silicon with 9.13% and 1.09%, respectively. A minute concentration of an impurity, i.e., oxygen, is found when testing is carried out in an open environment.

Stability of SiC-CNF HNFs

The batch sedimentation method is investigated as a simple yet effective technique to evaluate the colloidal stability of SiC-CNF HNFs [7, 19, 49]. The stability analysis is conducted in four progressive stages: without surfactant, with different surfactants, with varying Span 85: SiC-CNF ratios, and long-term stability analysis.

Stage 1 (Without Surfactant)

The SiC-CNF HNFs are initially prepared without any surfactant addition across all wt. conc. ranging from 0.1 wt% to 2 wt%. As presented in Fig. 5, evident sedimentation is observed at the bottom of all sample vials within 24 h of preparation, accompanied by a clear phase color change. It indicates rapid SiC-CNF agglomeration and phase separation. These observations necessitated the use of a suitable surfactant to suppress agglomeration and enhance colloidal stability.

Stage 2 (Surfactant Selection)

Three distinct surfactants, i.e., Span 85, oleic acid, and polysorbate 80, are evaluated for their effectiveness in stabilizing the SiC-CNF HNFs. Oleic acid and polysorbate 80 exhibit visible clustering and sedimentation at the bottom after 5 days of preparation, along with phase separation, indicating inadequate compatibility with the THO base fluid. Conversely, Span 85 demonstrates superior dispersion behavior with no visible sediment formation, making it the most suitable surfactant for SiC-CNF HNFs.

Stage 3 (Span 85:SiC-CNF Ratio Optimization)

Four Span 85:SiC-CNF ratios (1:1, 1:3, 1:5, and 1:10) are evaluated at 0.3 wt%., as presented in Fig. 6. The 1:1 ratio failed to achieve stability. However, ratios of 1:3, 1:5, and 1:10 demonstrated relatively stable dispersion. Based on these observations, the 1:3 ratio is selected as optimal, as it achieved satisfactory stability for up to 11 days while minimizing excess surfactant loading, which is known to adversely affect thermophysical properties at higher concentrations.

Stage 4 (Long-Term Stability Analysis)

The dispersion behavior of SiC-CNF HNFs is monitored over 21 days, as presented in Fig. 7. All samples exhibit only a slight color change over the observation period, indicative of minor microstructural evolution rather than bulk instability. SiC-CNF HNFs ranging from 0.1 wt% to 2 wt% remained stable throughout the 21 days with no visible sedimentation. However, the highest concentration of 2 wt% exhibited the slightest cluster formation after 21 days of preparation. This is attributed to the higher SiC-CNF loading exceeding the stabilization capacity of the surfactant at the selected ratio. These results confirm that SiC-CNF HNFs are colloidally stable for concentrations up to 2 wt% for a minimum of 21 days using Span 85 at a 1:3 ratio.

DLS Light Scattering

Particle size distribution analysis is carried out using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP, using 0.1 wt% SiC-CNF HNF with Span 85. Figure 6 illustrates the particle size distribution of the SiC-CNF HNF. The particle size in the nanofluid is within the range 295 to 825 nm. The particle size of the SiC-CNF HNF appears bigger than the primary nanoparticle size, i.e., around 200 nm, as agglomeration of hybrid nanoparticles occurs. The finding necessitates the ultrasonication technique for dispersing the SiC-CNF into the THO for achieving long-term stability.

Thermal Conductivity of SiC-CNF HNFs

The thermal conductivity of THO and SiC-CNF HNFs is measured with KD2 Pro integrated with a KS-1 probe, which works on the principle of the transient hot wire method with an accuracy of ± 0.01 W/m °C. The temperature difference obtained from temperature sensors equipped inside the probe is used to measure the thermal conductivity. The temperature drift is corrected by the device, and the data are analyzed for precise measurement. The samples are placed inside a thermostatic bath at temperatures of 25 °C, 35 °C, 45 °C, 55 °C, and 65 °C. The measurement is performed 10 times for each sample to get an accurate value and reduce errors.

Figure 7 (a) demonstrates the average thermal conductivity of THO and SiC-CNF HNFs, as well as the deviation from the average values observed during measurements. The results indicate that the thermal conductivity of SiC-CNF HNFs increases non-linearly with increasing temperature until 55 °C. However, the thermal conductivity of THO shows a decreasing trend, as observed in several studies [18, 50]. Similarly, the reduced K at elevated temperature above 40 °C is observed in SiC-diathermic oil-based NFs [38].

The observed enhancement in K with SiC-CNF dispersion is attributed to the synergistic interaction between SiC and CNF, where high-aspect-ratio CNFs establish interconnected thermal percolation networks, facilitating efficient phonon transport through the THO. Simultaneously, enhanced Brownian motion at elevated temperatures intensifies micro-convection, while the inherent thermal stability of SiC further augments the overall heat transport mechanism, collectively yielding K enhancement [16]. Furthermore, due to the higher K of SiC-CNF hybrid nanoparticles as compared to THO, the K of SiC-CNF HNFs is increased with the increase in mass conc. The observations are consistent with the literature [51, 52]. Conversely, no change in K is also observed with the dispersion of carbon black in polyethylene glycol (PEG200) [53]. Nevertheless, measurement uncertainty increases with temperature, particularly beyond 45 °C, where elevated kinetic energy intensifies inter-particle collisions, augmenting nanoparticle agglomeration and localized clustering. Such agglomeration not only affects measurement reproducibility but also degrades the effective thermophysical response of the HNFs. These findings are consistent with reported trends in hybrid nanofluid literature, where thermal and concentration-driven instability remains a key challenge at elevated operating conditions. Importantly, this behavior is not observed in viscosity measurements, where smooth curves are recorded even beyond 65 °C under high shear rates, confirming that agglomeration does not occur and the SiC-CNF HNFs remain rheologically stable. It suggests that the observed results are primarily attributable to instrument-induced measurement uncertainty rather than a true physicochemical instability. To minimize stochastic error, ten consecutive readings are recorded at each data point and averaged, providing a statistically representative measurement while accounting for probe-induced thermal disturbances at elevated temperatures. The enhancement of K with an increase in SiC-CNF HNFs wt% at 25 °C, 35 °C, 45 °C, 55 °C, and 65 °C is demonstrated in Fig. 7 (b). The highest K enhancement of 32.3% is calculated at 0.5 wt% of SiC-CNF HNFs concentration at 55 °C. Beyond 0.5 wt%, a decline in K is observed, attributed to the packing effect inherent to high concentration [54, 55]. At elevated SiC-CNF loadings, the inter-particle spacing decreases significantly, effectively reducing the surface area of SiC and CNF nanoparticles, which decreases the Brownian motion of the nanoparticles. This reduction in effective surface area suppresses the microconvection and interfacial phonon transport mechanisms. resulting in a net decline in effective K beyond 0.5 wt% of SiC-CNF. Furthermore, the high concentration of large-dimensional materials, such as nanofibers, may behave differently from small-dimensional particles. Increasing the concentration of large-dimensional nanomaterials in the fluid drastically decreases the K. Therefore, the high concentration of HNFs in this research may exhibit a packing effect due to the presence of CNFs.

Rheology of SiC-CNF HNFs

The relationship among dynamic viscosity, shear rate, and shear stress at different wt. conc. is a primary concern in the rheological study of SiC-CNF HNFs. The dynamic viscosity of SiC-CNF HNFs at wt. conc. of 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% is measured by rheometer, DHR1, TA Instruments, integrated with a 60 mm cone plate (Peltier plate steel), at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 75 °C and shear rates of 500 s− 1, 1000s− 1, 1500 s− 1, and 2000 s− 1. Before SiC-CNF HNFs measurements, the instrument is calibrated against a Newtonian reference fluid at each test temperature, confirming measurement accuracy within an acceptable deviation. The cone-plate geometry minimizes edge effects and ensures a uniform shear field across the sample. The DHR-1 operates with magnetic bearings, offering a minimum detectable torque of 0.1 nNm, enabling reliable low-viscosity fluid characterization. To further reduce stochastic error, four measurements of THO are recorded at a shear rate of 100 s− 1 and a temperature (25 °C to 75 °C ), and the results are averaged. The deviation in experimental viscosity is demonstrated in Fig. 8, with the standard deviation. The SD and RSD values are 0.00165 and 5.11%, respectively. The effect on viscosity profiles by dispersing SiC-CNF in THO is shown in Fig. 9. The results illustrate the decreasing trend of the dynamic viscosity of THO and SiC-CNF HNFs with increasing temperature. The non-linear trend is ascribed to the breakdown of bonds, which is caused by the weakening of intermolecular forces of attraction between the SiC-CNF HNFs. The decreasing trend with temperature is consistent with the oil-based NFs and HNFs [55, 56].

Effective Density of SiC-CNF HNFs

The density of nanosuspensions plays a critical role in determining convective heat transfer characteristics, directly influencing pumping power requirements, flow regime transition (laminar to turbulent), and overall rheological behavior. In the study, the density of THO and SiC-CNF HNFs is measured using a Mettler Toledo DM 40 at temperatures of 20 °C, 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, and 60 °C. The density of THO is measured 3 times to calculate the variation. The repetition analysis with standard deviation (SD) and relative standard deviation (RSD) is illustrated in Fig. 12. The SD and RSD values are calculated to be 0.5 and 0.06%, respectively. The observed density of THO and SiC-CNF HNFs and enhancement in ρ are demonstrated in Fig. 13 (a) and 13 (b), respectively. The results indicate that density decreases with increasing temperature, which is attributed to the thermal expansion of the THO. As temperature rises, intermolecular spacing increases, reducing the mass per unit volume and consequently lowering the ρ of SiC-CNF HNFs. This behavior has been widely reported in HNFs literature.

ANN modeling of K, µ, and density is conducted by systematically varying the number of hidden layers to identify the optimal network configuration. The input experimental dataset contains 30 observations with 2 features (temperature and wt. conc.) for K and density, and 144 observations with 3 features (temperature, shear rate, and wt. conc). Conclusion

In this study, SiC-CNF HNFs are synthesized by dispersing SiC-CNF into THO in wt. conc. of 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0%. FESEM, XRD, FTIR, and EDX analyses are performed, which confirm the intrinsic structure of SiC and CNF and that no functional group is attached to SiC-CNF. All the SiC-CNF HNFs samples are stable for 21 days. SiC-CNF conc. enhanced the K, µ, and ρ of THO. Maximum 32.3% enhancement in K at 0.5 wt% and 55 °C. SiC-CNF 2.0% wt. conc. increased the µ of THO up to 18.7% at a shear rate of 100 s− 1. The µ and ρ of HNFs decrease with elevated temperature. SIC-CNF HNFs followed both the power law and the Herschel-Bulkley model. The n values of these models are in the range of 0.9 to 1.0, which depicts the shear-thinning behavior. The developed ANN models showed accurate predicted results for µ and ρ, achieving a high-performance matrix and reduced errors, demonstrating the efficiency of ANN models in capturing complex trends in data sets. However, the ANN did not capture the behavior of K of SiC-CNF HNFs and demonstrated only an R2 value of 0.7606 due to the lack of experimental data and high non-linearity. The systematic analysis confirms the suitability of SiC-CNF HNFs for thermal management applications, as the results collectively demonstrate meaningful enhancement in K, a moderate increase in µ, and a marginal rise in ρ, reflecting a favorable thermophysical profile that balances heat transfer performance with acceptable fluid flow consequences.
