Eosinophilic inflammation that begins in the juvenile stage causes hydronephrosis and urothelial cancer in mutant mice
Authors: Kiyokazu Ozaki, Yui Terayama, Minori Inanaga Kojima, Tetsuro Matsuura, Kiyokazu Ozaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Obstructive hydronephrosis is caused by various factors such as chronic inflammation and tumors. Eosinophils and chitinase-like proteins (CLPs) are involved in the pathogenesis of hydronephrosis in mice; however, the specific mechanisms remain unknown. In this study, we morphologically analyzed a novel mouse model of obstructive hydronephrosis from onset to progression to clarify the effects of eosinophils and CLP on the development of hydronephrosis and tumorigenesis. The primary change was slight eosinophil infiltration of the ureteropelvic junction, even at 1 week of age, followed by a significant increase in CLP expression in the urothelium at 5 weeks of age, which led to proliferation of the urothelium. At 8 weeks of age, polyps with eosinophilic inflammation and urothelial hyperplasia expressing high levels of CLP formed at the ureteropelvic junction, leading to hydronephrosis. At 60 weeks of age, all mice with hydronephrosis exhibited chronic eosinophilic inflammation and adenomas that progressed to adenocarcinomas with high CLP expression. In summary, inflammation and epithelial proliferation at the ureteropelvic junction began with a single infiltration of eosinophils at the juvenile stage in mice. Eosinophilic inflammation is associated with the development of hydronephrosis and urothelial hyperplasia, which may progress to urothelial adenocarcinoma due to increased CLP expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81013-w.

Introduction

Hydronephrosis is caused by obstruction of the urinary tract due to chronic inflammation, tumors, urinary stones, and birth defects at the ureteropelvic junction1. Hydronephrosis can occur spontaneously in some mouse strains, including C57 BL/J(B6) and C57 BL/KsJ2,3. Furthermore, B6-based congenic MRL/MpJ and B6/DBA F2 mice are useful animal models for obstructive uropathy and developmental hydronephrosis4,5. The urinary tracts of these mice are narrowed due to inflammation and hyperplasia of the urothelium at the ureteropelvic junction. Furthermore, eosinophilic crystals, including YM1, a chitinase-like protein (CLP) and a systemically distributed eosinophil chemoattractant in mice6–10, are often observed in the cytoplasm of the hyperplastic urothelium in mice with hydronephrosis4,5. However, it is unclear when and how inflammation and epithelial proliferation with CLP expression are initiated before the onset of hydronephrosis in mice.

Chronic inflammation plays an important role in cancer development and promotes all stages of tumorigenesis11. Various factors, such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites, as well as non-infectious agents, such as asbestos and silica12, are involved in the development of cancer due to chronic inflammation. Crystals are non-infectious agents of inflammation, and urinary crystals are implicated in the proliferation and tumorigenesis of the urothelium due to chronic irritation. YM1 crystals have been shown to cause persistent inflammation and epithelial cell proliferation in a mouse model of hydronephrosis4,5. Furthermore, YM1 is highly expressed in the proliferative and neoplastic lesions of the urothelium in the kidneys and ureters of mice5,6. CHI3L1 is another CLP distributed in rodents and is involved in the progression of chronic inflammation, tumor growth, and tumor deterioration13–19. Thus, if inflammation in hydronephrotic mice becomes chronic, the proliferative urothelium is expected to progress to urothelial cancer.

We previously found a high incidence of obstructive uropathy at the ureteropelvic junction leading to hydronephrosis in a colony of adolescent mice derived from the B6 strain and established C57BL/6JhynSepat (hereafter abbreviated as B6-H) mice. Urothelial cell proliferation including YM1 positive eosinophilic crystals is inevitably accompanied by severe eosinophilic inflammation. These histological features closely resemble those of congenic B6-based MRL/MpJ and B6/DBA F2 mice. Thus, our B6-H mice were genetically predisposed to hyperplasia of the urothelium due to CLP accumulation at the ureteropelvic junction, resulting in the development of hydronephrosis. Furthermore, the reproductive rate of B6-H mice was high and hydronephrosis occurred at a high frequency. Therefore, our mice are a useful model to elucidate the mechanism of hydronephrosis development and to analyze the relationship between inflammation and the urothelium over the lifespan.

In this study, we hypothesized that inflammation at the ureteropelvic junction might be induced earlier by CLP and that further inflammation involving CLP might enhance the carcinogenic process. Here, we performed morphological and morphometric analyses of changes in inflammation from the juvenile to the aged stage over time using a new mouse model for hydronephrosis.

Results

Hydronephrosis macroscopically emerges at a high frequency in B6-H mice after 8 weeks of age

The kidneys of B6-H mice were macroscopically normal up to 5 weeks of age, and hydronephrosis was observed after 8 weeks of age (Fig. 1a). The incidence was 26% in both sexes at 8 weeks of age. At 60 weeks of age, the incidence of hydronephrosis was similar to that at 8 weeks of age; therefore, there was no age-dependent increase in hydronephrosis (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table S1). In the hydronephrotic kidney, the proximal part of the ureter was white and thickened, forming small polyps, and light-brown urine accumulated in the dilated renal pelvic cavity at 8 weeks of age (Fig. 1a and c). The polyps protruded into the cavity at the ureteropelvic junction (Fig. 1b and c).

Eosinophil is involved in the development of hydronephrosis in B6-H mice

Histologically, the progression of eosinophil infiltration was characteristic of the kidney in B6-H mice, regardless of the presence or absence of hydronephrosis, and the lesion was notable in the ureteral submucosal/muscular layers and periureteral adipose tissue at the ureteropelvic junction from 1 week of age (Fig. 2b). The average number of eosinophils in each layer significantly increased from 1 to 5 weeks of age compared to control B6 mice (Fig. 2g). The histological structure at the ureteropelvic junction was normal, except for eosinophil infiltration, and was almost the same as that in B6 mice up to 2 weeks of age (Fig. 2a–c). However, the urothelium was slightly thickened, with interepithelial infiltration of eosinophils from 3 weeks of age (Fig. 2d). At 5 weeks of age, the number of infiltrated eosinophils from the urothelium to the ureteral submucosal/muscular layers significantly increased (Fig. 2e and f), and the interepithelial infiltration of globule leukocytes became clear with urothelial hyperplasia (Fig. 2f).

At 8 weeks of age, eosinophil infiltration and urothelial hyperplasia were further enhanced in the hydronephrotic kidney, accompanied by fibrosis at the ureteropelvic junction, macroscopically corresponding to a small polyp (Fig. 3a and b). Eosinophil infiltration increased notably in the submucosa (Fig. 3c), and the number of globule leukocytes also increased (Fig. 3d). As urothelial hyperplasia and eosinophil infiltration became prominent, eosinophilic granules and crystals accumulated in the apical cytoplasm of urothelial cells (Fig. 3d). A large number of eosinophils, a small number of macrophages, T cells, and B cells infiltrated the periureteral adipose tissue from the muscle layer. The non-hydronephrotic kidneys of B6-H mice also showed milder eosinophil infiltration in each layer than those of B6 mice, and slight urothelial hyperplasia was also observed.

Chitinase-like protein expression increased with urothelial hyperplasia at the ureteropelvic junction in B6-H mice

There was slight YM1 immunopositivity in the normal urothelium from 1 to 8 weeks of age in B6 mice (Fig. 4a). In B6-H mice, urothelial YM1 immunoreactivity was similar to that in B6 mice at 1 to 3 weeks of age (Fig. 4b). At 5 weeks of age, the urothelium with mild eosinophilic infiltration showed slightly higher YM1 expression than in B6 mice (Fig. 4c); however, YM1 immunoreactivity increased with strong eosinophil infiltration (Fig. 4d). In hydronephrotic kidneys at 8 weeks of age, intracellular eosinophilic granules in the superficial and middle layers of the hyperplastic urothelial cells showed strong with YM1 immunoreactivity (Fig. 4e and f). The expression of YM1 in non-hydronephrotic kidneys at 8 weeks of age was comparable to that in mice with mild eosinophil infiltration at 5 weeks of age.

Polyps progress to adenocarcinoma with eosinophil infiltration in the hydronephrotic kidney

All hydronephrotic kidneys of B6-H mice examined at 60 weeks of age showed that the small polyps protruding into the cavity at the ureteropelvic junction at 8 weeks of age eventually progressed to a large polyp of up to 10 mm in diameter in both sexes. The ureter was severely thickened and tortuous from the ureteropelvic junction to approximately 20 mm above the junction. In severe cases, thickening extended to the entire length of the ureter (Fig. 6a). In the dilated renal pelvic cavity, dark yellow-colored urine was stored (Fig. 6a), and the urine sediment contained variously shaped and colored crystals, but not bacteria or fungi (Fig. 6b and c).

Polyps at the ureteropelvic junction were diagnosed as urothelial hyperplasia, adenoma, or adenocarcinoma. Urothelial adenoma and adenocarcinoma were observed in almost all cases, with incidences of 40/44 (90.9%) and 43/44 (97.7%), respectively (Supplementary Table S2). The surface mucosal layer of the polyp showed urothelial hyperplasia, and an adenoma formed inside the polyps (Fig. 6d). The adenomas were mainly composed of well-differentiated tubular structures (Fig. 6e and f). P63, which was positive in basal cells and negative in differentiated luminal cells of the normal urothelium, showed the same immunoreaction in adenoma (Fig. 6g). The tumor cells of the adenoma included numerous eosinophilic granules and crystals, and the interepithelial spaces were infiltrated by eosinophils and globule leukocytes (Fig. 6e and f). Many eosinophilic crystals were found in the superficial layer of the hyperplastic urothelium and tubular lumen of the tumor. In the tubular lumen, the giant macrophages exudate contained eosinophilic crystals but no bacteria or fungi (Fig. 6f). The eosinophilic granules and crystals varied in size from small to large. The eosinophilic crystals were needle- and rod-shaped and ranged in size from a few microns to several hundred microns (Fig. 6f). Fibrosis of the tumor stroma progressed with eosinophilic infiltration. Many macrophages, plasma cells, and eosinophils infiltrated the tumor margin and extended to the surrounding adipose tissue.

As the proliferative changes progressed, the margins of the adenoma developed into adenocarcinoma (Fig. 7a). The tumor cells invaded the ureter toward the urinary bladder and extended slightly to the adipose tissue around the ureter (Fig. 7b). However, no peritoneal infiltration or dissemination was observed. Adenocarcinoma was diagnosed as a well-differentiated type with tubular formation lined by one to several layers of epithelium (Fig. 7c). Luminal tumor cells showed mild anisocytosis and anisokaryosis, whereas basal tumor cells were composed of small cells and nuclei (Fig. 7d). Basal tumor cells were positive for p63 and the luminal cells were negative (Fig. 7e). Mitotic figures were sporadically observed. Ki67-positive tumor cells accumulated around the invasive area of the tumor (Fig. 7f).

Chitinase-like proteins are closely associated with urothelial adenoma and well-differentiated adenocarcinoma

Intracellular eosinophilic granules in the tumor cells of adenomas and adenocarcinomas showed mild-to-strong immunopositivity for YM1 and CHI3L1 (Fig. 7g and h). Many granules and crystals in the tubular lumen were strongly immunopositive for YM1 and CHI3L1 (Fig. 7g and h). In addition, macrophages in the interstitial connective tissue of the tumor were strongly immunopositive for YM1 and CHI3L1 (Fig. 7g and h). Immunostaining for proliferation signals (MAPK [pJNK, pERK, pP38], PI3/Akt [pS6K, 4EBP4], pNF-kB, pSTAT, Wnt [cyclinD1, active beta catenin]) were positive in a few tumor cells, but negative in almost all tumor cells.

Discussion

We established a mouse model with a high incidence of obstructive hydronephrosis caused by polyps at the ureteropelvic junction. The polyps exhibited urothelial hyperplasia with a large number of eosinophils and a small number of macrophages, T cells, and B cells. In addition, eosinophilic granules in the proliferating urothelium showed strong immunoreactivity to the YM1 protein. Several studies have investigated hydronephrosis in mouse models, and the histopathological features of the present strain were similar to those of B6-based congenic MRL/MpJ and B6/DBA F2 mouse strains2–5. Thus, urothelial hyperplasia with YM1 expression and inflammation are likely common changes in obstructive hydronephrosis with the same genetic background as the B6 strain. However, it is not well known when and how this proliferative change is initiated before the onset of hydronephrosis and how inflammation affects the lesion thereafter. The present study showed that inflammation and epithelial proliferation began at the ureteropelvic junction in the juvenile stage before the onset of hydronephrosis in B6-H mice. Furthermore, urothelial adenocarcinoma at 60 weeks of age was detected only in the polyps of individuals who developed hydronephrosis, with no difference in the frequency of hydronephrosis between the ages of 8 and 60 weeks. Urothelial hyperplasia initiated at the juvenile stage possibly leads to urothelial polyps, causing hydronephrosis at the adolescent stage, and the progression of the lesion to urothelial adenocarcinoma at the aged stage due to long-lasting inflammation.

In B6-H mice, the earliest change was eosinophil infiltration, which was observed in the periureteral tissue to the ureteral submucosa even at 1 week of age and was exacerbated at 2 weeks of age. However, no proliferative changes were observed in the urothelium. Eosinophil infiltration was further exacerbated at 3 weeks, extending into the urothelial layer, and the urothelium proliferated as eosinophilic inflammation spread to the urothelial layer. These results suggest that inflammation begins with a single infiltration of eosinophils, triggering urothelial proliferation in the juvenile stage. Furthermore, up to 5 weeks of age, the ureters showed only eosinophilic inflammation and no polyp formation; however, a polyp causing obstructive hydronephrosis, similar to other mouse models2–5, was observed at 8 weeks of age. Urothelial proliferation leading to polyp formation may be rapidly accelerated between 5 and 8 weeks of age, and inflammatory cells other than eosinophils may be involved in this process. Eosinophils are the origins of inflammation and play an important role in its progression in B6-H mice. In previous studies, these histological changes prior to the onset of hydronephrosis may have been missed because of the absence of gross changes.

The finding that urothelial YM1 and CHI3L1 expression are associated with eosinophilic inflammation and urothelial hyperplasia suggests that CLP triggers eosinophilic inflammation. YM1, which is a rodent-specific CLP, is an eosinophil chemoattractant involved in the immune response, hemocyte differentiation, and tissue remodeling7,9,20–23. In particular, allergic inflammation and parasitic infections, which are characteristic of eosinophil infiltration, are closely associated with YM16–10. In this study, YM1 was found to be strongly expressed in the urothelium prior to tumor formation and may be involved in urothelial damage and chronic eosinophilic inflammation. YM1 expression in the urothelium became apparent at 5 weeks of age, along with a significant increase in eosinophil inter-epithelial infiltration. Eosinophil infiltration significantly increased from the periureteral tissue to the ureteral submucosa, even at 1 week of age, before the increase in YM1 expression. Thus, a small amount of YM1 expression may have induced eosinophil infiltration into the mucosa, starting in the periureteral area, and the eosinophil infiltration became more pronounced as YM1 expression increased. CHI3L1, a type of CLP common to humans and mice, is significantly increased in the mucosal epithelium and macrophages during chronic inflammation, such as inflammatory bowel disease and respiratory disease; therefore, it is considered to be deeply involved in the progression of chronic inflammation24–27. In this study, CHI3L1 was highly expressed in the urothelium as eosinophilic inflammation progressed, similar to YM1 expression. Our findings suggest that YM1 and CHI3L1 are involved in chronic eosinophilic inflammation and epithelial cell proliferation.

The histopathological characteristics of the tumors were adenoma/adenocarcinoma rather than urothelial or transitional cell tumors. The tumor continuously formed from the hyperplastic urothelium and was derived from the urothelium; however, no region could be diagnosed as urothelial carcinoma or papilloma. Primary renal pelvis/ureteral adenocarcinomas and adenomas are extremely rare in humans and are associated with chronic inflammation, hydronephrosis, and urinary stones28,29. Although the pathogenesis of adenocarcinoma is unclear, glandular metaplasia of the urothelium is reportedly caused by chronic inflammation and progresses to dysplasia and adenocarcinoma29,30. In our mouse model, the urothelium of the hydronephrotic kidney was probably injured by chronic eosinophilic inflammation, followed by tumor formation. The malignancy of the adenocarcinoma was low-grade because tumor invasion was limited to the adipose tissue around the ureter, proliferative activity was low, cell atypia was mild, and metastasis did not occur. Immunostaining for p63 was performed to determine the degree of tumor differentiation. P63 is positive for basal cells and negative for luminal cells in the normal urothelium31. As the immunostaining pattern of our adenocarcinoma was almost the same as that of the normal urothelium and adenoma, cancer cells may have a similar differentiation to the normal urothelium. The staining pattern of p63 suggested that the adenocarcinoma in our study was a well-differentiated type. However, there are no reports of eosinophilic inflammation-induced adenocarcinoma of the renal pelvis and ureter in either humans or animals. Therefore, studying urothelial adenocarcinoma in our mice is highly likely to elucidate both the carcinogenic mechanism induced by chronic eosinophilic inflammation and that of the urothelial adenocarcinoma.

YM1 not only induces eosinophilic inflammation but is also associated with neoplastic and non-neoplastic proliferative lesions in the skin, kidney, renal pelvis, ureter, glandular stomach, and bile duct4–7,32. YM1 also increases the chemical carcinogenesis of the skin caused by DMBA and TPA15. CHI3L1 is upregulated in breast, ovarian, gastric, prostate, and colon cancers, and is thought to affect cytokine- and chemokine-producing cells that promote cancer progression16,17. In addition, CHI3L1 is associated with tumor initiation, cancer cell proliferation, tumor angiogenesis, and metastatic spread17. In the present study, we observed significant expression of YM1 and CHI3L1 in the neoplastic and non-neoplastic urothelium, and deterioration as the lesions progressed. These findings further support the previously reported involvement of YM1 and CHI3L1 in epithelial growth and carcinogenesis. As CHI3L1 became positive for intracellular eosinophilic granules and crystals, similar to YM1, the tumorigenesis probably progressed due to the synergistic action of YM1 and CHI3L1. CHI3L1 induces cancer cell proliferation via the ERK1/2-MAPK pathway, and YM1 includes activation of signaling pathways such as NF-κB, JNK, p38MAPK, ERK1/2, and STAT15,17. We attempted to detect protein expression in these pathways using immunostaining; however, no significant changes were observed. Therefore, the exact mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis remain unclear.

Crystals of various shapes, including needles, were present in the urine stored in the renal pelvis, and many crystals were also found on the surface layer of the hyperplastic urothelium and glandular lumen of the tumor. Urine crystals are involved in the growth and tumorigenesis of the urothelium due to chronic stimulation13,14. Crystals may have continuously stimulated the epithelium from the outside and inside in our mice. The most sensitive organ to chemical carcinogenesis in the urinary tract is the urinary bladder, which is thought to be affected by the residence time of the carcinogens13,14. The rate of chemical carcinogenic rate in the renal pelvis of experimental hydronephrotic animals was similar to that in the urinary bladder, confirming that the residence time of the chemical substance affects carcinogenesis14. The YM1 protein has been reported to crystallize33. Since the crystals were immunopositive for YM1 and CHI3L1 in our mice, YM1 and CHI3L1 would crystallize in the renal pelvis. Because these crystals stimulate the urothelium over a long period (approximately one year), persistent inflammation of the ureter and renal pelvis may be induced by YM1 and CHI3L1, followed by progression from urothelial hyperplasia to cancer.

In conclusion, inflammation and epithelial proliferation at the ureteropelvic junction began with a single infiltration of eosinophils at the juvenile stage in B6-H mice. Proliferating urothelial hyperplasia formed at the adolescent stage progresses to adenocarcinoma at an older stage owing to long-lasting inflammation. Eosinophils are responsible for inflammation and CLP expression is likely to play a major role in exacerbating inflammation and epithelial proliferation. This mouse model can contribute not only to the elucidation of the mechanisms of eosinophilic inflammation pathogenesis related to CLPs but also to the elucidation of the carcinogenic mechanisms related to chronic eosinophilic inflammation.

Methods

Animals and housing conditions

A colony with a high incidence of hydronephrosis was identified in C57BL/6J strain (B6) (Charles River Laboratory Japan, Yokohama, Japan). The mice were established as an inbred strain by full-sibling mating for 20 generations at Setsunan University. We introduced the name C57BL/6JhynSepat (abbreviated as B6-H) mice for this strain. Mice were housed in TPX cages in a conventional environment, with a relative humidity of 40–70% and temperature of 20–26 °C under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. They were provided with filtered fresh air and allowed ad libitum access to tap water and food. During all experimental procedures, animals were handled in accordance with the principles outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared by our institution (Setsunan University) and the ARRIVE guidelines. This study was approved by the Committee for Animal Experiments of Setsunan University (approval numbers: K11-16, K12-18, K13-18, K14-19, K15-18, K16-19, K17-16, K18-16, K19-17, K20-20, K21-21, and K22-21).

Experimental design

In total, 888 male and female B6-H and B6 mice were used in this study. The mice were sacrificed at 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 60 weeks of age. The mice were euthanized by exsanguination of the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia with carbon dioxide exposure using a gradual-fill method. Kidneys with ureters were removed and fixed via immersion in 10% formaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4).

Urinalysis

Urine was collected from the pelvic cavity of mice with hydronephrosis and centrifuged at 2000 g. The supernatant was discarded and the resuspended sediment was stained with Giemsa solution and examined.

Histopathological analysis

The formalin-fixed organs were trimmed, dehydrated using an automated processor, and embedded in paraffin wax. Section (4-µm thick) of tissue were stained with hematoxylin-eosin and direct fast scarlet 4BS (DFS) for histopathological examination. All kidney and ureter lesions were evaluated by three investigators. The severity of eosinophil infiltration was semi-quantitatively analyzed in the mucosal epithelium, submucosa, and periureteral tissue at 1, 2, 3, and 5 weeks of age.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed for identification of the Chitinase family proteins (YM1 and CHI3L1), inflammatory cells (CD3, CD45R/B220, Iba-1, IgG, and myeloperoxidase), basal cells (p63, p40), PI3K/AKT/MAPK (PI3/Akt [p-S6K and p-4EBP4], MAPK [p-SAPK/JNK, p-ERK1/2, and p-p38], p-NF-kB, p-STAT3, Wnt [cyclin D1, active beta catenin]), and Ki-67 (Supplementary Table S3). Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using graded ethanol at room temperature. The rehydrated sections were treated according to the protocol shown in Supplementary Table S3 to retrieve antigens. Solutions and washes were prepared between the various steps, using 0.05 M Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.6) with 0.01% Tween 20 (TBST). Non-specific endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by exposure to 0.03% hydrogen peroxidase in 100% methanol for 5 min and masking was performed with 5% goat serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline for 5 min at room temperature. Incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C using the respective primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3), rinsed with TBST, treated for 60 min at room temperature with the secondary antibody (Supplementary Table S3), and incubated in a diaminobenzidine solution. For negative controls, rat or mouse isotype immunoglobulins diluted to the same concentration were used as the primary antibody.

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to assess differences in the number of infiltrated eosinophils between the two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant when the p value was less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 15 software (SAS Institute, Tokyo, Japan).
