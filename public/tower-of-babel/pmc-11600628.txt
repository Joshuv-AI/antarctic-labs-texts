Parental vaccine hesitancy toward routine childhood immunizations and COVID-19 vaccines in Japan: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Aya Saitoh, Yugo Shobugawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to elucidate the prevalence and characteristics of parental vaccine hesitancy towards routine infant immunizations and COVID-19 vaccines in Japan. MethodsA web-based survey was conducted among 3,227 parents of children aged 0–11 years to assess vaccine-related hesitancy for routine infant immunizations and COVID-19 vaccines for children using the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) questionnaire. Data were collected from January 18 to 25, 2023. Covariates included demographic characteristics, economic status, COVID-19 infection status, decisional conflict scale, and the fear of COVID-19 Scale.ResultsVaccine hesitancy was found to be 52.4% for routine infant immunizations and 73% for COVID-19 vaccines. Significant differences in parental attitudes were observed between general childhood vaccines and COVID-19 vaccines for 12 out of 13 PACV survey items. The COVID-19 vaccines showed higher hesitancy rates in 10 items; largest discrepancies were noticed in schedule adherence (22.5% vs 61%), overall hesitancy (40% vs 55.1%), and trust in pediatric doctors (37.2% vs 53.6%). Safety concerns were high for both vaccine types, exceeding 50%. Multivariable analysis identified decisional conflict (RR: 1.01 95% CI: 1.00–1.02) and COVID-19-related fear (RR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05) as hesitancy predictors for routine immunizations.ConclusionVaccine-related hesitancy for COVID-19 was significantly higher than that for routine immunizations, with decisional conflict emerging as a primary predictor for both. Fear of COVID-19 was associated with routine immunization-related hesitancy. These findings provide critical insights for future pandemic preparedness and vaccine acceptance strategies, highlighting the importance of strengthening trust between healthcare providers and parents, providing clear and reliable information, and implementing decision support tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20845-7.

Background

Japan’s COVID-19 vaccination program was relatively delayed compared to other G7 countries; however, it was gradually implemented as a crucial measure for mitigating its impact. The age range for COVID-19 vaccine was expanded later from older adults and healthcare workers to include younger age groups.

In February 2022, the Japanese government extended the program eligibility to include children aged 5–11 years, and in October 2022, children aged 6 months to 4 years [1, 2]. Even after the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the end of the global health emergency for COVID-19 in May 2023, the Japan Pediatric Society continued to recommend COVID-19 vaccination for all children aged 6 months and older in October 2023 [3]. However, despite confirmed vaccine efficacy and safety [4], as of March 1, 2024, the rate of children aged 6 months to 4 years who had completed the three-dose vaccination remained at 3.4%, significantly lower than the 93.1% full vaccination rate among adults aged 65 and older [1].

Multiple factors contribute to the low pediatric COVID-19 vaccination rate in Japan [5–7]. Horiuchi et al. surveyed parents of children aged 3–14 years and found that those who primarily trusted social media information sources showed higher vaccine hesitancy than those who trusted official information (adjusted odds ratio: 2.80, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.53–5.12) [8]. Additionally, Okubo et al. reported that concerns about side effects predominantly increased vaccine hesitancy [9].

Furthermore, the stay-at-home orders and reluctance to visit medical facilities during the pandemic caused childhood routine immunization rates to decline globally. UNICEF’s 2023 report indicates that in many countries, trust in childhood vaccines decreased during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic levels [10]. Notably, South African and Japanese citizens’ confidence in childhood vaccines decreased by about one-third during the pandemic [11].

Despite such valuable insights into the attitudes toward COVID-19 vaccines and general vaccines in Japan, research simultaneously comparing parental hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccines and routine infant vaccines for children has been scarce. There is a need for such research as it could reveal similarities and differences between the two, potentially contributing to the development of more effective vaccination promotion strategies.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the prevalence and characteristics of parental vaccine-related hesitancy for both, routine infant immunization and COVID-19 vaccines. We seek to elucidate the challenges of childhood vaccination in Japan and COVID-19’s impact on existing vaccine programs.

Methods

Study design and sampling

For this cross-sectional study, anonymous, self-administered web surveys were distributed to parents of children aged 0–11 years. Study participants were registered members of Cross Marketing, Inc., a Japanese Internet research company. This company distributed the questionnaire to eligible parents residing in Japan via email using designated websites. Use of the data extracted from the survey was clearly indicated, and respondents could participate only if they agreed to the use of their data for research purposes. Participants’ responding was considered as their consent. Data were collected from January 18 to 25, 2023. The participation incentive comprised reward points worth ¥60 (approximately US$0.46 as of January 2023). Since the Internet research company had numerous potential respondents (5.41 million people in the active panel), the survey included participants with various attributes nationwide.

The sample size was stratified by children’s age (0–11 years), considering possible differences in parental vaccine-related awareness based on growth and development stages. Using standard statistical methods, we calculated a sample size of 384 per stratum, assuming a 95% confidence level, ± 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution. This was rounded up to 400 per stratum (total subjects: 4,800) to account for potential incomplete responses and to ensure sufficient precision for each age group. Once 400 respondents were recruited, allocation for each age group was closed (0–11 years). From respondents with multiple children, the data of all children were collected.

Survey items

Outcome variable

Vaccine hesitancy, the primary outcome of this study, was assessed using the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) questionnaire [12–15]. This internationally validated instrument evaluating parental childhood vaccination-related attitudes and beliefs was translated into Japanese with the author’s permission.

The PACV survey comprises 15 items and three domains: (1) behavior, (2) safety and effectiveness, and (3) general attitude. It has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.74 to 0.84 across all domains). We investigated 15 items regarding routine childhood vaccines and 13 items regarding childhood COVID-19 vaccines. Participants could not answer items 4 and 7 regarding the COVID-19 vaccine, as it could not be concurrently administered with other vaccines (except for the influenza vaccine) at the time of the study; thus, the two items were deleted for this study. The original version of the PACV questionnaire was translated from English to Japanese by the investigator, and accuracy was confirmed using reverse translation by three individuals skilled in the English language. The questionnaires were tested in a pilot study prior to being used in the main study [16, 17].

The responses were scored according to the original instruction: 2 points for hesitant responses, 1 for unsure responses, and 0 for non-hesitant responses. The scores were summed to calculate the total score out of 30 points (for COVID-19 vaccine-related PACV, maximum score was capped at 26). The summed non-weighted item scores provided the raw total score, which was converted to a 0–100 scale using a simple linear transformation considering missing data. Using previous literature, PACV scores were dichotomized into two categories: “non-hesitant” (score < 50) and “hesitant” (score ≥ 50) [12–15]. Hesitancy presence or absence based on PACV score was determined separately for COVID-19 and routine vaccinations.

Explanatory variables

The analysis included three explanatory variable components: 1) Respondent (Parent)-related factors: education, household income, was into medical professional or not, and employment status; 2) child-related factors: underlying disease, age, and number of siblings; 3) Psychometric scales, including the decisional conflict scale (DCS) and the fear of COVID-19 scale (FCV-19S). As covariates, respondent’s sex (mother or father) and age were considered potential confounders.

The validated DCS measures individuals’ uncertainty levels when making health-related decisions. Sixteen items are rated on a 5-point scale (0 to 4), with the total score converted to a 0–100 range [16]. Higher scores indicate greater decisional conflict, which is associated with delayed decision-making or uncertainty. Regarding vaccination, DCS has been correlated with vaccine hesitancy and uptake. A score below 25 indicates decision implementation, while 37.5 or higher suggests decision delay or uncertainty. The DCS encompasses three key concepts: decision-making uncertainty, factors contributing to uncertainty (information, values, and support), and the decision’s perceived effectiveness. This assessment scale provides insights into cognitive and emotional factors impeding or facilitating vaccination decisions, thus aiding in understanding and potentially addressing vaccine hesitancy.

The five-item FCV-19S scale can measure COVID-19-related fears [18, 19]. Respondents indicated their agreement level for each question from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranged from 7 to 35 points. A score of ≥ 21 points was adequate for identifying individuals experiencing psychological distress or difficulties in daily living because of COVID-19 fears [20].

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics were compared between those hesitant and non-hesitant toward general childhood vaccines and the COVID-19 vaccine using the chi-squared test. For each PACV item, responses regarding childhood vaccines and COVID-19 vaccines were compared using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The response categories (‘concerned,’ ‘not sure,’ and ‘not concerned’) were treated as ordinal data, reflecting increasing levels of vaccine hesitancy. The Wilcoxon test was chosen due to its appropriateness for comparing paired ordinal data and its ability to account for the direction and magnitude of differences between responses. The relationship between vaccine hesitancy and sociodemographic factors was examined using a series of Poisson regression models that were constructed and evaluated. Univariate analysis was performed for each potential predictor variable.

Variables with significant differences and those associated with childhood vaccination status in previous studies were included in subsequent multivariable models. We included parent–child relationship and parental age based on their significance in our univariate analysis and their established importance in previous research [21, 22]. Socioeconomic factors, such as education level and household income, were included due to their consistent association with vaccine hesitancy in multiple studies [23, 24]. Healthcare worker status and employment status were included based on their potential influence on access to and interpretation of health information [25]. Child-specific factors, such as underlying diseases, age, and number of siblings, were included due to their potential impact on perceived vulnerability and vaccine decision-making [26]. The scores of DCS and FCV-19S were included as they represent important psychological constructs related to decision-making and fear during the pandemic, which have been shown to influence vaccine attitudes [27, 28].

Poisson regression models were developed and incorporated through different combinations of the identified variables. Poisson regression provides more accurate estimates of relative risk compared to logistic regression when the outcome is not rare and allows for flexible inclusion of explanatory variables. The method was chosen due to the high prevalence of vaccine hesitancy in our sample. Model 1 considered the sex and age of the respondent. Model 2 (including Model 1) considered respondents’ socioeconomic status (education level and household income), whether they were healthcare workers, and their employment status. Model 3 (including Model 1) adjusted for whether the child had an underlying disease, age, and number of siblings. In Model 4 (including Model 1), the scores of DCS and FCV-19S were entered. In the final model (including Model 1), all factors added in Models 2 to 4 were entered simultaneously. The final model included significant variables in the univariate analyses and those deemed important based on prior research. The dependent variable was coded “1” if the participant expressed vaccine hesitancy and “0” if they did not; 95% CIs were calculated for each model including the full model. Model diagnostics assessed Poisson regression assumptions, including overdispersion and multicollinearity tests.

Data were recorded and analyzed using SPSS (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical tests used two-tailed p-values.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted using anonymous web surveys. All participants were provided with clear information about the study purpose and data usage before participation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants through their voluntary completion of the survey after agreeing to the use of their data for research purposes. The study protocol was approved by The Ethics Committee of Niigata University approved this study (approval no. 2022–0076). We conducted our research in accordance with established ethical guidelines. Specifically, the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Parental vaccine hesitancy

Figure 1 shows PACV responses for COVID-19 and childhood vaccinations by age of child. In both groups, vaccine hesitancy (PACV score ≥ 50) toward childhood vaccines was lower than that toward COVID-19 vaccines. Specifically, for children aged 0–4 years, vaccine hesitancy was 49.7% for childhood vaccines and 74.7% for COVID-19 vaccines for children. For children aged 5–11 years, vaccine hesitancy was 55.2% for childhood vaccines and 71.3% for COVID-19 vaccines for children.Fig. 1Parental responses to vaccine hesitancy toward childhood vaccines in general and COVID-19 vaccine for children

Parental attitudes and vaccine-related concerns

Figure 2 presents responses to PACV questions. The PACV survey comprises 15 items for childhood vaccines in general. As mentioned previously, for the COVID-19 vaccine for children, participants could not answer two items (items 4 and 7), as they were not applicable due to the vaccination policies at the time of the study. Thus, there are 13 comparable items for the COVID-19 vaccine. Higher percentages indicate more negative or hesitant attitudes towards vaccination. All items showed significant differences between the two vaccinations except for one question about side effect concerns. Ten out of 13 items exhibited higher hesitancy-indicating response rates for COVID-19 vaccines compared to general childhood vaccines. The question “How sure are you that following the recommended shot schedule is a good idea for your child?” showed the highest discrepancy, with 22.5% expressing uncertainty for general childhood vaccines compared to 61% for COVID-19 vaccines. This was followed by “How hesitant about childhood shots would you consider yourself to be?” (40% for general vaccines vs 55.1% for COVID-19). The question, “All things considered, how much do you trust your child’s doctor?” revealed that 37.2% of the total participants expressed distrust for general childhood vaccines, while 53.6% indicated distrust for COVID-19 vaccines. Notably, all these items exceeded 50% hesitancy or distrust for COVID-19 vaccines. For the safety-related item, “How concerned are you that any one of the childhood shots might not be safe?”, over half of the respondents expressed concern for both vaccine types (general: 57.6%, COVID-19: 57.3%), indicating high levels of safety concerns.Fig. 2Parental responses to the Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV) survey

Vaccine hesitancy-related factors

Table 2 shows the relationships between parents’ sociodemographic factors and childhood vaccination hesitancy. Hesitancy toward general childhood vaccines was significantly associated with several parental factors: closer parent–child relationship (p < 0.001), lower educational level (p < 0.001), lower household income (p < 0.001), certain parental occupations (p = 0.033), higher number of children (p = 0.002), and unemployment status (p = 0.012). Child-specific factors, including presence of underlying medical conditions (p = 0.018) and older age (p = 0.001), were also significantly associated with increased vaccine hesitancy. Similar relationships between parents’ sociodemographic factors and hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccine for children were also observed. The latter was significantly associated with closer parent–child relationship (p < 0.001), lower educational level (p < 0.001), lower household income (p < 0.001), younger child of child (p = 0.029), and unemployment status (p < 0.001). Higher DCS scores and higher FCV-19S were associated with increased hesitancy toward both, childhood and COVID-19 vaccines. Discussion

Previous studies using PACV have reported prevalence rates for vaccine hesitancy in pediatric acute care settings ranging from 5–30% across different countries [13, 14, 29–32]. A 2020 study in Japan involving 113 pregnant women found that 40.7% were vaccine-hesitant [33]. The current study indicated that 52% of parents showed hesitancy toward childhood vaccinations and 73% toward the COVID-19 vaccine.

To provide context for these findings, it is important to consider pre-pandemic vaccine hesitancy levels in Japan. Saitoh et al. [34] conducted a study on parental vaccine hesitancy in Japan before the COVID-19 pandemic, and found that 5.3% of parents were hesitant about routine childhood vaccines, with 27.6% being unsure about at least one vaccine. The substantial increase in vaccine hesitancy observed in our study (52% for childhood vaccinations) compared to the pre-pandemic levels suggests that the COVID-19 pandemic may have significantly influenced attitudes of parents toward routine childhood immunizations. Furthermore, the discrepancy we observed between hesitancy toward routine childhood vaccines (52%) and COVID-19 vaccines (73%) highlights the unique challenges posed by novel vaccines developed during a pandemic. This difference underscores the importance of tailored communication strategies for different types of vaccines, especially during public health emergencies.

Furthermore, our study reveals crucial similarities and differences in parental hesitancy toward COVID-19 vaccines and routine infant vaccines. A key similarity is the presence of decisional conflict as a significant predictor of hesitancy for both vaccine types, suggesting that parents face similar challenges in decision-making regardless of the vaccine’s novelty. However, there were notable differences in the level of hesitancy (52% for routine vaccines vs. 73% for COVID-19 vaccines) and trust in healthcare providers’ recommendations (37.2% distrust for routine vaccines vs. 53.6% for COVID-19 vaccines). These differences highlight the unique challenges posed by novel vaccines during a pandemic. The higher hesitancy and lower trust associated with COVID-19 vaccines suggest a need for tailored communication strategies that address specific concerns related to vaccine novelty, development speed, and long-term safety. Meanwhile, the substantial hesitancy toward routine vaccines indicates a need for ongoing efforts to maintain and improve confidence in established vaccination programs. These findings contribute to the development of more effective vaccination promotion strategies, suggesting that while some approaches may be applicable to both vaccine types, others need to be specifically tailored to address the unique concerns associated with novel vaccines developed during public health emergencies.

Our study revealed a high proportion of “Not sure” responses for both COVID-19 and routine childhood vaccines. This finding warrants careful interpretation. Such responses could indicate genuine parental uncertainty, insufficient information, or hesitancy to comment on a sensitive topic. The consistency of these responses across both vaccine types suggests that this uncertainty is not unique to the COVID-19 vaccine; rather, it reflects a broader state of parental indecision regarding vaccination. This aligns with previous studies on vaccine hesitancy that have reported similar high rates of uncertainty [35, 36]. These “Not sure” responses highlight a critical need for targeted education and communication strategies to address parents’ concerns and information gaps regarding both routine and COVID-19 vaccines.

A study in Turkey [37] found that parents with pediatric vaccine-related hesitancy were less positive toward the COVID-19 vaccine, a finding consistent with other studies. Conversely, Opel et al. hypothesized that the pandemic could positively impact parents’ childhood vaccine-related attitudes [38]. However, the current study suggests a potential negative influence. The significantly higher COVID-19 vaccine-related hesitancy compared to childhood vaccine-related hesitancy highlights the unique challenges posed by pandemic-period novel vaccines.

Differences in PACV responses regarding childhood and COVID-19 vaccines can help assess the importance of targeted education and communication strategies, specifically for addressing concerns regarding new vaccines developed for emerging infectious diseases. Our study highlighted vaccine distrust and lack of trust in healthcare providers regarding the COVID-19 vaccine. Healthcare providers’ vaccine recommendations are significant as they impact the vaccination decision-making process [39]. Low trust in healthcare providers regarding COVID-19 vaccines compared to that regarding childhood vaccines suggests that maintaining and strengthening patient–provider relationships is crucial during public health emergencies. Moreover, it has to be noted that people could not visit healthcare providers during the pandemic and face-to-face meeting opportunities were decreasing; consequently, information was increasingly obtained from the internet [40]. Thus, this inability to directly obtain accurate information from healthcare professionals may have led to increased distrust. Healthcare professionals may need additional support and resources to address parental concerns about novel vaccines. This support could include specialized training in motivational interviewing (MI), an evidence-based communication approach that has shown effectiveness in addressing vaccine hesitancy. The MI training could enhance providers’ ability to elicit and explore patients’ motivations and concerns about vaccination in a non-judgmental manner. Additionally, improving telemedicine infrastructure and establishing a network of vaccine experts for consultation could be beneficial, especially during public health emergencies when in-person visits are limited. These measures aim to equip healthcare providers with the necessary tools to address parental concerns effectively, particularly in the context of emerging infectious diseases like COVID-19. The stark difference in trust regarding recommended schedules (22.5% [general] vs. 61% [COVID-19]) indicates where public health messaging must focus on for COVID-19 vaccines. Japan has one of the lowest Vaccine Confidence Index scores globally, [41] which may contribute to overall vaccination-related hesitancy. Based on these results, several key aspects should be emphasized in COVID-19 vaccine messaging strategies. These include enhancing transparency in vaccine development, regularly updating safety data, explaining the rationale behind the recommended schedule, and addressing misinformation. Additionally, emphasizing the collective benefits of following the recommended schedule can highlight its importance for community protection. These targeted strategies aim to bridge the trust gap between general childhood vaccines and COVID-19 vaccines, potentially increasing acceptance of and the adherence to the recommended COVID-19 vaccine schedule.

The multivariable analysis demonstrated a significant association between decision conflict and vaccine hesitancy for both the vaccinations. The complexity and uncertainty surrounding vaccine-related information may be impeding parental decision-making. Previous research has corroborated these results, indicating a positive correlation between COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and decision conflict [42]. Elevated decision conflict delays vaccination decisions and exacerbates vaccine hesitancy. Because decision conflict is a primary factor in vaccine hesitancy, clear and reliable information must be provided. Decision support tools can mitigate decision conflict and reduce vaccine hesitancy among parents who are undecided about the COVID-19 vaccine [43]. Decision support tools typically include patient decision aids, which are evidence-based resources designed to help individuals make specific and deliberate choices among healthcare options. These aids often contain balanced information about the benefits and risks of vaccination, frequently asked questions, and may include interactive elements to help parents clarify their values and preferences. For example, web-based decision aids or mobile applications could provide accessible, up-to-date information and guide parents through the decision-making process [44–46]. The implementation of such tools could complement healthcare provider consultations, ensuring that parents have access to reliable information both, during and outside of clinical encounters.

Interestingly, our finding that motherhood is associated with lower childhood vaccine-related hesitancy contrasts with some previous studies [46–48]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the cultural context of our study, conducted in Japan, may differ from those of previous studies, potentially influencing maternal attitudes towards vaccination. Secondly, temporal factors should be considered; our study, conducted in 2023, may reflect evolving attitudes towards vaccination in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has significantly impacted public health perceptions. Moreover, Japan has implemented various vaccine education programs specifically targeting mothers, which may have contributed to reduced hesitancy. In many Japanese households, mothers often serve as primary decision-makers for children’s health-related matters. This responsibility may lead to increased information-seeking behavior and engagement with healthcare providers, potentially mitigating vaccine hesitancy. It is also worth noting that differences in study design and sample characteristics between our research and previous studies may contribute to the contrasting results. This finding underscores the importance of considering local and temporal factors when developing strategies to address vaccine hesitancy. Further research is needed to explore these potential explanations and to understand how motherhood influences vaccine hesitancy across different cultural and temporal contexts.

The association between COVID-19-related fear and childhood vaccine-related hesitancy is an intriguing discovery. The pandemic may be influencing general vaccine-related attitudes of parents. A positive correlation between COVID-19-related fear and COVID-19 vaccination willingness has been observed in a prior study. [8] The association between COVID-19-related fear and childhood vaccine-related hesitancy should be treated cautiously because of the nature of cross-sectional data and the potential for reverse causality [49]. Hesitancy toward established vaccines could reflect a broader mistrust or misunderstanding of medical interventions, which may also manifest as strong fear of new diseases [49]. This interpretation does not imply a causal relationship in either direction. It only suggests that both, vaccine hesitancy and excessive fear of infectious diseases may result from a fundamental issue: gaps in health literacy and scientific understanding among certain population segments. The importance of comprehensive public health education beyond specific vaccines or diseases is underlined. Improvements in general health literacy, critical thinking skills, and understanding of basic immunology and epidemiology could address vaccine hesitancy and disproportionate fear of infectious diseases.

Our study has some limitations. First, the use of a web-based survey may have affected the results through selection and non-response biases, which could have caused overestimation, and certain groups could be overrepresented in the data. However, conducting a large-scale survey when COVID-19 vaccine recommendations for children aged 0–4 years were initiated aligned with our study purpose. Second, the cross-sectional design prevented the evaluation of temporal changes in COVID-19 vaccine acceptance. Despite these limitations, these findings clarify parental vaccine hesitancy in Japan regarding childhood vaccinations and COVID-19 vaccines. The results offer several important considerations for future pandemic preparedness and vaccine acceptance. Thus, longitudinal studies tracking changes in vaccine hesitancy over time and across different pandemic phases would be valuable for understanding public perceptions’ dynamic nature and for informing adaptive communication strategies. Third, while we assessed the reliability of the Japanese version of the PACV scale using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, we did not conduct an exploratory factor analysis or other methods to assess its validity. This limitation should be considered when interpreting our results, as the factor structure of the Japanese version may differ from the original English version. Additionally, the use of self-administered questionnaires may have introduced a recall bias, as participants might not accurately remember all relevant information. Furthermore, given the sensitive nature of vaccine hesitancy, social desirability bias could have influenced responses, potentially leading to underreporting of vaccine hesitancy. These factors may have affected the accuracy of our results in reflecting the actual situation. Future studies could consider employing mixed-method approaches, including qualitative interviews or observational studies, to triangulate findings and mitigate these potential biases. Lastly, we acknowledge that our sample, comprising respondents registered with an online survey company, may have an inherent internet affinity bias. This group likely has a greater access to the internet and potentially higher exposure to online information sources, including misinformation. This bias is particularly relevant to our research question, as it may influence participants’ vaccine-related knowledge, attitudes, and hesitancy levels. Future studies should thus consider employing diverse recruitment methods to capture a broader range of the population, including those with limited internet access or usage.

These insights can assist policymakers, healthcare providers, and public health officials in developing more effective strategies for addressing vaccine hesitancy during future pandemics while also maintaining confidence in routine childhood immunizations.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the prevalence and characteristics of parental vaccine hesitancy toward routine infant immunizations and COVID-19 vaccines in Japan. The findings revealed a significantly higher COVID-19 vaccine-related hesitancy (73%) compared to childhood immunization-related hesitancy (52.4%). This discrepancy may reflect specific novel vaccine-related concerns during the pandemic.

The multivariable analysis identified decisional conflict as a primary predictor of vaccine hesitancy. This suggests that the complexity and uncertainty surrounding vaccine information may impede parental decision-making processes. Additionally, the association between COVID-19-related fear and hesitancy toward routine immunizations indicates that the pandemic may have influenced general attitudes toward vaccines.

These findings provide critical insights for future pandemic preparedness and vaccine acceptance strategies. Strengthening the trust between healthcare providers and parents, providing clear and reliable information, and implementing decision support tools are likely effective measures to reduce vaccine hesitancy. Future research should include longitudinal studies to track changes in vaccine hesitancy over different phases of a pandemic, which could enhance our understanding of the dynamic nature of public perceptions and aid in developing adaptive communication strategies. This study’s results offer valuable information for policymakers, healthcare providers, and public health authorities. They highlight the importance of maintaining trust in routine childhood immunizations while effectively addressing vaccine hesitancy during future pandemics.
