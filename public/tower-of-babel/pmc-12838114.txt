Molecular characterization and expression analysis of GATA1 in the blood clam Anadara granosa reveals its role in gonadal development
Authors: Yingbin Xu, Chuanxu Zhang, Luxia Zhang, Jiacheng Wang, Hongxing Liu, Yongbo Bao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGATA1 transcription factors, traditionally recognized for their role in hematopoiesis, have recently been implicated in reproduction in vertebrates. However, their function in invertebrate gonadal development remains largely unknown.ResultsIn this study, we identified and characterized GATA1 (designated as AgGATA1) in the blood clam Anadara granosa and explored its potential role in gonadal development. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that AgGATA1 exhibited a high degree of conservation across animal species. Furthermore, AgGATA1 was most highly expressed in the gonads, where it was predominantly expressed in oogonia and spermatogonia, whereas its expression was not observed in oocytes and spermatocyte. In addition, knockdown of the AgGATA1 gene reduced the number of germ cells in the testis through down-regulation of gonadogenesis-related genes (FOXL2, WNT, and Sox9).ConclusionsCollectively, these findings provide the first functional evidence that GATA1 is involved in molluscan gonadal development, expanding its known role beyond hematopoiesis and offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying reproductive regulation in invertebrates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12452-6.Keywords: Anadara granosa, Transcription factor, GATA1, Gonad development, Gene expression

Background

GATA transcription factors comprise a conserved family of zinc finger proteins present across metazoans [1–3]. These factors regulate gene expression by binding to the GATA consensus motif in target gene promoters and interacting with cofactors such as Friend of GATA (FOG) proteins [4–6]. Based on their expression patterns and biological functions, GATA transcription factors are categorized into two distinct subfamilies: GATA1, GATA2, and GATA3 [7–11], which primarily function in the hematopoietic system, while GATA4, GATA5, and GATA6 [12–14] which are predominantly involved in the development of endoderm-derived tissues.

Among the GATA family, GATA4 has been widely studied for its essential role in gonadal development, particularly in vertebrates. It regulates genes involved in sex determination and differentiation [15] and is expressed in somatic cells of both testes and ovaries [16–20]. In contrast, GATA1 has traditionally been associated with hematopoiesis; however, emerging evidence indicates its involvement in gonadal function. For example, GATA1 is expressed in Sertoli cells, which are essential for early sperm development, during specific stages of testicular development in mice [21], and its deficiency leads to reduced steroidogenic enzyme expression and testosterone production [22–24]. In teleost fish, GATA1 modulates genes related to germline development and vitellogenesis, suggesting a broader reproductive function [25, 26]. Nevertheless, despite these advancements in vertebrates, the biological roles and regulatory mechanisms of GATA1 transcription factors in invertebrates are largely unexplored.

The blood clam Anadara granosa is a commercially valuable marine bivalve species widely cultured along the coasts of eastern China and Southeast Asia [27]. Despite its economic significance, the aquaculture industry faces persistent challenges in producing consistent, high-quality seed stock due to limited understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing gonadal development and germ cell proliferation. The regulatory networks controlling reproductive biology in this species remain largely uncharacterized. In this study, we investigated the role and function of the GATA1 transcription factor in the gonads of the blood clam through expression pattern and histological analysis. These findings provide novel insights into GATA1-mediated gonadal regulation in blood clam and lay a foundation for improving reproductive management and breeding strategies in aquaculture.

Methods

Experimental animals

The blood clams at different stages of gonadal development were obtained from a local shellfish hatchery in Ningbo, Zhejiang Province. Before to the experiment, the clams were acclimated for 7 days in seawater kept at a temperature of 24.3 ± 1.0 °C with a salinity of 26.1 ± 0.1 ‰ and adequate oxygenation. These clams were fed microalgae Chaetoceros muelleri twice daily. Subsequently, The gonads (ovary or testis) from the formative stage and mature stage [28] were sampled and stored in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) for RNA extraction. Portions of the gonad tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis.

Bioinformatics analysis and phylogenetic tree construction

The gene sequence of AgGATA1 was obtained from the genome and transcriptome data of the blood clam (GCA_013375625.1). The GATA1 protein sequences of cephalopods, gastropods, bivalves, and selected vertebrates were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Multiple sequence alignment of GATA1 proteins from 42 species was conducted by MUSCLE v5.1. Phylogenetic analysis was performed with IQ-TREE v2.2.2.6. Conserved residues were identified, and conserved sequence motifs were subsequently analyzed by ESPript v3.0.26.

AgGATA1 expression profile analysis

RNA-seq datasets from multiple tissues (GenBank accession: PRJNA594182) and developmental stages (GenBank accession: SRR10713949 to SRR10713972) were analyzed to determine the spatiotemporal expression profiles of AgGATA1 in blood clam. Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to quality control using fastp v0.23.2, and clean reads were aligned to the blood clam reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 with default parameters. Read counts were generated using featureCounts v2.0.6, and transcript abundance was normalized and expressed as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). The expression patterns of AgGATA1 across tissues and developmental stages were visualized by GraphPad Prism v8.0.1.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

The total RNA was extracted from the gonadal tissues of blood clam by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and concentration of the extracted RNA were evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 1000 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). High-quality RNA samples were then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, China). To examine the expression of AgGATA1, the specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 based on the coding sequence of AgGATA1, and the 18S rRNA gene was used as an internal reference. In addition, specific primers for Sox9, WNT, and FOXL were designed to verify the effects on the gonads after RNA interference, and q-PCR primers are detailed in Table 1. The quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR) was conducted using the q225 RT-PCR system (Kubo, China). Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), 0.5 μL of each primer (10 μM), 1 μL of cDNA, and 8 μL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions followed the manufacturer’s instructions. Nine samples were collected for each reaction, with three technical replicates performed for each sample, and the relative mRNA expression levels of AgGATA1 were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

The gonadal tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) on ice to extract total proteins. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer, denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min, and stored at −20 °C until use. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 15% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer system. The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skimmed milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-GATA1 (1:1000; custom-made against the AgGATA1-specific peptide sequence: NKPLATKGKSRRIIC) and rabbit anti-α-tubulin (1:3000; Proteintech, China), the latter serving as an internal control. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated sheep anti-rabbit IgG (1:3000; Proteintech, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Following additional washes, protein bands were detected using an ultra-sensitive chemiluminescence reagent (NcmECL Ultra, NCM Biotech, China) and visualized with a gel imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Histology and immunofluorescence analysis

The gonadal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (80%, 90%, 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 4 μm-thick sections were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and dried at 55 °C. For each sample, two adjacent sections were used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescence staining, respectively. Then, the paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series. For histological evaluation, gonadal sections were stained with H&E following standard protocols to assess reproductive development stages under light microscopy. For immunofluorescence detection of AgGATA1, rehydrated sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in 2.94 g/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Sections were then cooled to room temperature and blocked in PBST containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h to reduce nonspecific binding. Subsequently, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-GATA1 polyclonal antibody (1:100; custom-prepared against the AgGATA1-specific peptide). After three washes with PBST, sections were incubated with a FITC-conjugated sheep anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:100; Proteintech, China) at 37 °C for 2 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:10 dilution), and sections were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium (PBS: glycerol = 1:3). Fluorescent images were acquired using a Nikon A1 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

RNA interference-mediated knockdown of AgGATA1

A total of 80 healthy blood clams were randomly divided into two groups (n = 40 per group): the siRNA group (injected with interfering primers) and the negative control group (injected with nonsense primers). The siRNA specific to AgGATA1 (siAgGATA1-R, 5’-UAGAACAAACGGGCUUAGCTT-3’; siAgGATA1-F, 5’-GCUAAGCCCGUUUGUUCUATT-3’) and a scrambled non-targeting control siRNA were synthesized (GenePharma, China) and prepared at a final concentration of 20 μM in DEPC-treated water. Each individual in the siRNA group was injected with 0.02 mL of AgGATA1 siRNA solution into the adductor muscle, while the control group received 0.02 mL of the nonsense siRNA solution. Following injection, all clams were maintained in aerated seawater at 24.3 ± 1 °C and 26.1 ± 0.1 ‰ salinity. At 48 h and 96 h post-injection, 20 clams were randomly sampled from each group at each time point. Gonadal tissues were dissected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for total RNA extraction and gene expression analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism v8.0.1 (San Diego, CA, USA). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is denoted as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**).

Results

Sequence analysis of AgGATA1

To investigate the evolutionary relationships of the GATA1 gene in marine bivalve, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the predicted amino acid sequences of GATA1 (Fig. 1A). AgGATA1 clustered closely with GATA1 proteins from other representative marine bivalves, such as Mytilus trossulus and Mytilus edulis, indicating a high degree of sequence homology. Multiple sequence alignment revealed the presence of conserved amino acid residues at key positions, suggesting a structurally conserved domain architecture across species (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, motif analysis identified the presence of six conserved motifs (Motifs 1–6) in AgGATA1, highlighting its evolutionary conservation in blood clam (Fig. 1C).

The number of germ cells decreased after AgGATA1 knockdown

To investigate the effect of AgGATA1 on gonad tissues of A. granosa RNA interference (RNAi) experiment was employed to knock down AgGATA1 expression. qPCR analysis revealed a significant decrease in AgGATA1 mRNA expression in the siRNA group at 48 h and 96 h compared to the negative control group (Fig. 5A). Statistical calculations indicated that AgGATA1 siRNA interference resulted in a 51% decrease of mRNA levels. The results of the Western blot (WB) experiment showed that the target protein bands were located between 25 and 33 kDa, aligning with the predicted molecular weight of 31 kDa, while the internal reference bands appeared around 55 kDa. Furthermore, AgGATA1 protein levels were significantly reduced at 48 h and 96 h post-interference compared to the negative control (Fig. 5B, C). Statistical analysis indicated that AgGATA1 siRNA interference resulted in a 60% reduction of protein levels.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that a small number of cells in the gonad of A. granosa were labeled with GATA1 after RNA interference while a large number of cells were labeled in the negative control group (Fig. 5D). Moreover, compared with the negative control group, the fluorescence intensity was significantly weakened after RNA interference (Fig. 5E). These results indicated that knockdown of the AgGATA1 gene reduced the number of germ cells within the gonads of A. granosa.

Discussion

The rapid growth of the bivalve aquaculture industry depends critically on high-quality germplasm resources and the reliable maturation of broodstock, both of which are closely linked to advances in gonadal development research [29]. However, in the blood clam Anadara granosa, a commercially important bivalve widely distributed in the East China Sea, studies on gonadal development and the proliferation and differentiation of germ cells remain limited. This gap in fundamental reproductive biology presents a major obstacle to improving breeding efficiency and scaling up aquaculture production. In particular, the lack of effective techniques for controlling gonadal maturation under captive conditions underscores the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms regulating reproductive development in this species.

Transcription factors of the GATA family have been shown to play essential roles in regulating diverse biological processes, including hematopoiesis, gonadal development, and cardiac development. In vertebrates, the GATA family consists of six members divided into two main taxa; GATA1, GATA2 and GATA3 are mainly involved in hematopoietic development, while GATA4, GATA5 and GATA6 are more involved in regulating cardiac, digestive and gonadal development. Specifically, GATA1 is widely recognized as a key transcription factor for erythropoiesis and plays an important role in hematopoietic development [30–33]. In contrast, GATA4 plays a central role in reproductive development and regarded as an early marker of gonadal development, owing to its expression in somatic cells of both the testis and ovary [16, 17, 34]. In invertebrates, however, the roles of GATA factors remain poorly understood and may differ substantially from those in vertebrates. For example, in Drosophila, the transcription factor Serpent (Srp) exhibits function analogous to GATA1 in hematopoiesis and is also implicated in reproductive development [35]. This suggests that there may be a wider range of functions for GATA1. In this study, our preliminary tissue expression analysis in the blood clam revealed significantly higher expression levels of AgGATA1 in the gonads compared to other tissues, prompting the hypothesis that GATA1 may also participate in gonadal development in bivalves.

Interestingly, analysis of AgGATA1 expression across different developmental stages of the blood clam revealed that its expression peaked during the trochophore stage, a developmental phase marked by intensive cell proliferation and the onset of germ cell lineage specification. It suggests that AgGATA1 may serve as a key regulatory factor in early developmental differentiation. This result is consistent with the findings of invertebrate. In Drosophila, GATA-binding proteins were expressed in ovarian follicular cells, and in Nematodes, the GATA homologue ELT-1 was highly expressed in both sexes’ reproductive cell lines [36, 37]. Furthermore, the immunofluorescence results showed that AgGATA1 predominantly labeled oogonia and spermatogonia, whereas the oocytes and Spermatocyte were not labeled. Similar expression patterns have also been reported in vertebrates. In mice, GATA1 is selectively expressed in Sertoli cells of the testis and regulates the expression of genes necessary for the early stages of spermatogenesis [38]. In transgenic mice, testis-specific expression of GATA1 is driven by regulatory elements located upstream of the hematopoietic enhancer; disruption of these elements results in abnormal Sertoli cell function and impaired testicular development [39]. These studies collectively demonstrate that GATA1 plays critical roles not only in hematopoiesis but also in the structural development and functional maintenance of the mammalian reproductive system. Therefore, the conserved expression of AgGATA1 in spermatogonia and oogonia may similarly reflect its fundamental role in germ cell differentiation and gonadal development.

Based on the above expression and immunofluorescence analyses, the developmental role of AgGATA1 in the gonads of blood clam was further investigated. Histological (H&E) staining of individuals collected at different reproductive stages revealed that during the formative stage, gonads predominantly consisted of undifferentiated germline cells, including spermatogonia or oogonia. In contrast, mature gonads were primarily composed of fully differentiated gametes, such as spermatozoa and oocytes. Besides, the qPCR results showed that AgGATA1 expression was significantly elevated in the formative stage compared to the mature stage. Therefore, we speculate that GATA1 may be involved in the differentiation and proliferation of germ cells. To further assess the functional role of AgGATA1, RNA interference (RNAi) was performed. Male gonads were selected due to their well-defined structure and the abundance of early-stage spermatogonia, which facilitated the observation of cellular alterations. Following AgGATA1 knockdown, a significant decrease in the number of spermatogonia was detected, indicating that AgGATA1 may play a crucial role in maintaining or promoting the differentiation of male germline cells in the blood clam. In mice, GATA1 mutation led to reduced interstitial stromal cell size, decreased expression of steroidogenic genes, lower testosterone levels, and increased sperm head deformities [24]. In golden hamsters, GATA1 is strongly expressed in epithelial cells of the Sertoli valve, a niche maintaining undifferentiated spermatogonia [40]. Even more striking, in invertebrates such as Drosophila, mutation of the GATA homolog Serpent (Srp) causes defective specification of gonadal somatic precursor cells, ultimately resulting in malformed or absent gonads [34].

The regulatory role of AgGATA1 in gonadal development was further investigated by examining the expression profiles of several well-established developmental genes—including Sox9, WNT4, and FOXL2—following AgGATA1 knockdown. All three genes showed a clear decrease in expression, suggesting that AgGATA1 may act as an upstream regulator controlling multiple pathways involved in gonad formation and function. Among these genes, Sox9 is known to help drive the development of testis by supporting the formation of Sertoli cells [41]. The reduction in WNT4 expression aligns with its known role in promoting ovary formation. FOXL2 works together with WNT-related signals to maintain ovarian features and is also involved in producing estrogen by regulating hormone-related genes like FSHR and CYP19A1 [42, 43]. The previous studies in mice have also shown that GATA1 influences testis development by regulating multiple genes, including Sox9 and Dax1. Therefore, it is hypothesized that AgGATA1 plays a crucial role in germ cell development and influences gonadal development by regulating the expression of reproduction-related genes in blood clam.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified and characterized the GATA1 transcription factor in A. granosa and first time explored its potential role in gonadal development. Tissue expression analysis showed that AgGATA1 was most highly expressed in the gonads with predominant localization in oogonia or spermatogonia, without in oocytes or spermatocyte. Moreover, knockdown of the AgGATA1 gene significantly reduced the number of germ cells in the testis by suppressing the expression of gonadogenesis-related genes, including FOXL2, WNT, and Sox9. These findings not only challenge the traditional view of GATA1 as being confined to hematopoietic regulation, but also provide the first functional evidence of its involvement in molluscan reproductive regulation. This study offers new insights and a theoretical foundation for uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying gonadal development in invertebrates.
