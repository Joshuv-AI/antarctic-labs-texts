Electrical storm treated with radiation therapy in a patient with ischaemic cardiomyopathy and left ventricular thrombus: a case report
Authors: Marc D Lemoine, David Krug, Max Grohmann, Oliver Blanck, Bruno Reissmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrical storm in patients with structural heart disease is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Catheter ablation is recommended for drug-refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT), but may be contraindicated in the presence of a left ventricular (LV) thrombus due to increased thromboembolic risk. Stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy (STAR) has emerged as a non-invasive treatment option for refractory VT. This case highlights the potential role of STAR as a rescue therapy when conventional approaches are not feasible.Case summaryAn 80-year-old man with advanced ischaemic cardiomyopathy presented after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to monomorphic VT. Evaluation revealed severe LV dysfunction (LVEF 15%), an anteroapical LV aneurysm and a newly detected LV thrombus. Catheter ablation was deferred due to embolic risk. Eleven weeks later, the patient was admitted with recurrent VT and developed electrical storm refractory to antiarrhythmic drug therapy, overdrive pacing, and bilateral stellate ganglion blockade. Non-invasive target delineation using contrast-enhanced cardiac computed tomography with scar characterization and ECG-based localization was performed. A single fraction of 25 Gy stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy was delivered to the suspected VT substrate. Electrical storm terminated immediately after treatment, and no further VT episodes occurred during hospitalization or during 6 months of follow-up.DiscussionThis case demonstrates that stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy may provide an effective non-invasive rescue strategy for electrical storm when catheter ablation is contraindicated. Introduction

Electrical storm carries a high mortality risk, especially in patients with advanced heart failure. Management typically involves antiarrhythmic drugs, haemodynamic support with device-based therapies such as venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or percutaneous left ventricular (LV) assist devices, overdrive pacing, and catheter ablation.1 However, arrhythmia suppression and clinical stabilization cannot always be achieved despite guideline-directed therapies.1 Catheter ablation may carry considerable procedural risk in the presence of a LV thrombus, particularly when located adjacent to the suspected ventricular tachycardia (VT) origin. Stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy (STAR) has emerged as a non-invasive option for treatment-refractory VT.2 We report a case of electrical storm successfully managed with STAR in this high-risk setting.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old man with advanced ischaemic cardiomyopathy presented with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to monomorphic VT with 166 bpm (Figure 1). Following spontaneous acceleration to >200 bpm, he lost consciousness. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after resuscitation with chest compressions for 2 min, intravenous amiodarone, and successful shock delivery. Notably, his cardioverter-defibrillator with cardiac resynchronization therapy (ICD-CRT) did not initially deliver therapy, as the VT rate was below the programmed detection threshold. The patient had a history of two prior myocardial infarctions (anterior, 35 years earlier; posterior, 16 years earlier) and had undergone coronary artery bypass graft surgery (LIMA to LAD) and RIMA sequential grafting to obtuse marginal branches (OM1/OM2) and the posterior descending artery, resulting in LV dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 30% and a anteroapical LV aneurysm. Coronary angiography revealed no significant stenosis. Pre-existing severe LV dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 30% worsened to 15% after the index event. His medical therapy included sacubitril/valsartan (49/51 mg twice daily), eplerenone (25 mg once daily), dapagliflozin (10 mg once daily), metoprolol (95 mg twice daily), rosuvastatin (20 mg once daily), torasemide (10 mg once daily), and aspirin (100 mg once daily). The ICD detection threshold was lowered to 158 bpm. Amiodarone therapy was initially deferred in favour of an elective VT ablation at our tertiary care centre.

During pre-procedural evaluation for VT ablation, transthoracic echocardiography confirmed persistently severe LV systolic dysfunction of 15%, and revealed a newly diagnosed anteroapical thrombus within the known LV aneurysm. The thrombus was further characterized by contrast-echocardiography and cardio-computed tomography (CT) (Figure 2). In the presence of an LV thrombus adjacent to the presumed arrhythmogenic substrate, catheter ablation was considered to carry substantial procedural risk and was declined by the patient. Aspirin therapy was switched to phenprocoumon, and amiodarone therapy was initiated (600 mg/day for 10 days, followed by 400 mg/day for 10 days, then 200 mg/day maintenance). The patient was discharged with a plan for repeat echocardiographic evaluation after 12 weeks to assess potential thrombus resolution.

Multimodality imaging demonstrating left ventricular thrombus within an apical aneurysm. (A) Native transthoracic echocardiography, two-chamber view. (B) Contrast-enhanced echocardiography (SonoVue®, Bracco) confirming the presence of a left ventricular thrombus. (C) InHeart-derived cardiac computed tomography reconstruction demonstrating a contrast filling defect within the left ventricular cavity, consistent with thrombus formation in the anteroapical aneurysm.

After 11 weeks, the patient presented to the emergency department with recurrent monomorphic VT of a similar morphology but at a slower heart rate of 120 bpm (Figure 1(C)). Although, haemodynamically tolerated, the arrhythmia resulted in New York Heart Association (NYHA) class IV symptoms. Device interrogation revealed frequent VT episodes below the programmed detection threshold for automatic antitachycardia pacing (ATP). Manual ATP temporarily terminated the arrhythmia; however, recurrence occurred within minutes. Echocardiography demonstrated persistence of the LV thrombus. Despite multimodal therapy, the patient developed an electrical storm with recurrent VT episodes. ICD-CRT pacing rate was increased to 90 bpm for overdrive suppression. Neither high-dose oral (400 mg/day) and intravenous amiodarone, nor combination therapy with amiodarone and lidocaine, nor bilateral stellate ganglion blockade achieved sustained arrhythmia control. The bilateral stellate ganglion blockade was performed under ultrasound guidance using local anesthetics (2 mL for lidocaine, 1% for local anesthesia, and 7.5 mL Rupivacain 0.75%). Escalation of antiarrhythmic drug therapy resulted only in further slowing of the VT rate without termination (Figure 1).

STAR was planned as a last-resort treatment.3 Since endocardial mapping was not feasible due to LV thrombus, contrast-enhanced cardiac-CT datasets were processed using the InHEART software (v1.10.0, inHEART, Pessac, France) to segment the LV myocardium, delineate scar tissue, and define the target volume (Figure 3). The exit site of the VT was presumed to be located at the posteromedial basal region. However, the extensive scar areas complicated interpretation of the surface ECG during VT and may have limited the reliability of ECG-based localization algorithms. To further refine the target delineation, 12-lead ECG recordings during VT as well as during right ventricular and LV pacing were analysed (Figure 3). To confirm the exit site of the VT the Miller-4 and Segal-algorithm5 were used resulting in a vector pointing left-superior.

The final cardiac target volume (CardTV) was defined to treat the inferior scar region, where VT supporting channels were suspected based on CT-derived scar characterization and ECG-based localization. Assessment of residual wall thickness (see Supplementary material online, Video S1) further supported the anatomical differentiation between dense infarction and potentially arrhythmogenic border zones. The target zone (see Supplementary material online, Video S2) was manually delineated using the inHEART software, integrating all relevant aspects while aiming to keep the final target volume as small as possible. Treatment planning was performed using respiratory-gated four-dimensional CT applying an internal target volume concept to compensate the limited respiratory and cardiac motion. The CardTV generated in InHEART (Figure 4) was transferred via rigid image fusion, and individualized safety margins were defined based on the ECG-gated diagnostic CT and respiration-dependent planning CT datasets. A dose reduction was accepted in the region of the caudally adjacent stomach to minimize gastrointestinal exposure (Figure 4). STAR was delivered as a single fraction of 25 Gy (78%-isodose, V25 Gy = 85% due to sparing of the adjacent stomach) using cone-beam CT guidance (Figure 4) and surface monitoring. Treatment time was approximately 15 min and was performed in an awake, non-sedated patient.

The electrical storm terminated immediately following STAR. Continuous monitoring over 5 days revealed no recurrence VT episodes. The treatment was well tolerated, with no acute adverse events apart from mild fatigue. Laboratory parameters remained stable between baseline and discharge, including high-sensitivity troponin (14 to 10 pg/mL), creatine kinase (46 to 40 U/L), NT-proBNP(500 to 527 ng/L), and creatinine (1.18 mg/dL unchanged). During 6 months of follow-up under continued amiodarone (200 mg/day), the patient remained asymptomatic and free from recurrent VT. LV systolic function and functional status remained stable (NYHA class II-III). Anticoagulation was managed with phenprocoumon throughout follow-up, targeting a therapeutic INR range of 2.0–3.0. No thromboembolic events occurred during follow-up. At 3-month follow-up CT imaging, the left ventricular thrombus was still present without any change in size. There was no evidence of pericardial effusion or radiation-induced pneumonitis.

Discussion

Electrical storm represents a major therapeutic challenge and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. According to current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines,1 management includes antiarrhythmic drug therapy, optimization of heart failure therapy, catheter ablation, and autonomic modulation in refractory cases. Catheter ablation carries a class I recommendation for drug-refractory VT in structural heart disease and has demonstrated superior efficacy compared to antiarrhythmic drug therapy alone, in patients with ischaemic substrate, as shown in the VANISH trial.6 However, the presence of LV thrombus constitutes a relative contraindication due to increased risk of thromboembolic complications.

In the present case, multiple guideline-directed therapies1—including antiarrhythmic drugs, device optimization, and bilateral stellate ganglion blockade—failed to achieve sustained arrhythmia control. Catheter ablation was considered to carry prohibitive risk due to the thrombus location adjacent to the presumed VT substrate, and heart transplantation was not a viable option given the patient’s advanced age and comorbidities. In this context, STAR represented a reasonable salvage strategy. Increasing understanding of VT mechanisms through CT-based substrate characterization7 and growing experience in translating imaging-derived data into radiotherapy planning have expanded the feasibility of this approach.8

STAR delivers highly focused radiation to arrhythmogenic myocardial substrate and was first described more than a decade ago in a landmark case series published in the New England Journal of Medicine.9 Although the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood, experimental and clinical data suggests that STAR may induce early electrophysiologic modulation, followed by late fibrotic remodelling and alterations in ion channels expression, including sodium channels, resulting in arrhythmia suppression.10 Several small series have demonstrated substantial reductions in VT burden after STAR11,12; however, long-term efficacy and safety data remain limited.

In this case, the complete STAR planning and setup process was completed within 12 days, including imaging acquisition (2 days), inHEART analysis (1 day), target delineation (3 days), patient informed consent (1 day), treatment planning (5 days) and delivery. As this represented the first STAR procedure performed at our hospital, the timeline was influenced by the initial implementation phase; we anticipate a more streamlined workflow in future cases. Waiting 12 days to treat VT storm until STAR delivery was only possible, since antiarrhythmic therapy reduced VT frequency below 100 bpm and minimized haemodynamic effect.

Nevertheless, given the complexity of STAR therapy, it should be performed in experienced centres. Transfer of the patient to a more experienced centre was considered. In our case, the establishment of the STAR program benefited from the prior experience of David Krug and the additional expertise provided by Oliver Blanck, who contributed substantial knowledge from a tertiary university hospital setting.

This case underscores the importance of advanced imaging and ECG-based localization techniques when invasive electroanatomical mapping is not feasible. Integration of CT-derived cardiac models with surface ECG analysis enabled non-invasive delineation of the presumed arrhythmogenic substrate and safe delivery of radiotherapy. The immediate termination of electrical storm following STAR in our patient supports the hypothesis of an early functional antiarrhythmic effect that may precede structural fibrosis. Consistent with previous reports, no clinically relevant elevation of cardiac biomarkers was observed after treatment.

Current ESC guidelines1 do not yet incorporate STAR as a standard therapeutic option. Nevertheless, cases such as the present one highlights its potential role as rescue strategy when conventional therapies are contraindicated or exhausted. Prospective trials and multicenter registries are warranted to better define patient selection criteria, optimal dose concepts, and long-term safety outcomes.13
