Observational Multiplicity
Erin George, Deanna Needell, Berk Ustun

Abstract
Many prediction tasks can admit multiple models that can perform almost equally well. This phenomenon can can undermine interpretability and safety when competing models assign conflicting predictions to individuals. In this work, we study how arbitrariness can arise in probabilistic classification tasks as a result of an effect that we call observational multiplicity . We discuss how this effect arises in a broad class of practical applications where we learn a classifier to predict probabilities p i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] p_{i}\in[0,1] but are given a dataset of observations y i ∈ { 0 , 1 } y_{i}\in\{0,1\} . We propose to evaluate the arbitrariness of individual probability predictions through the lens of regret . We introduce a measure of regret for probabilistic classification tasks, which measures how the predictions of a model could change as a result of different training labels change. We present a general-purpose method to estimate the regret in a probabilistic classification task. We use our measure to show that regret is higher for certain groups in the dataset and discuss potential applications of regret. We demonstrate how estimating regret promote safety in real-world applications by abstention and data collection.

Introduction

Modern methods for machine learning fit models through
empirical risk minimization
(ERM): given a training dataset with
n
labeled instances
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we fit a model that minimizes the empirical risk. ERM is a optimization procedure: given a training dataset, running ERM multiple times should return models that achieve the same empirical risk. Given this behavior, it may be tempting to view the model that we recover as the “best” that we can do given the data that we have available. In practice, such claims would be wrong because there may exist multiple models that minimize the empirical risk. In effect, datasets can admit many “competing models” that perform equally well, i.e., model multiplicity
[
Breiman 2001
]
. In such cases, these competing models can assign conflicting predictions to individual instances, i.e.,
predictive multiplicity

[
Marx et al. 2020
]
. When this happens, model predictions (and any downstream decisions made as a result) are dependent on the arbitrary choice of the model chosen from the set of competing models.

In applications like lending, recidivism prediction, and clinical prognosis—where we fit models that return probabilities—these claims would be wrong for a different reason: because the labels in the training datasets do not align with the outputs of the model. In such tasks, we would like to fit a model that
outputs probabilities
\bm{x}_{i}\mapsto\operatorname{Pr}(y_{i}=1\;|\;\bm{x}_{i})
; however, our dataset contains labels
y_{i}
that represent realizations of these probabilities. In such cases,
\mathcal{D}
represents a
single random draw
of the labels in the training dataset
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
. Had we been able to repeat this draw, we would have obtained an different training dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
which contains the same features but different observed labels
y_{i}^{\prime}
for each point.

The datasets that we could produce through additional draws are equally plausible in a probabilistic classification task. It goes without saying that running ERM on
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
would return different models
f
and
f^{\prime}
.
In such settings, the models
f
and
f^{\prime}
would perform almost equally well in terms of aggregate measures such as the logistic loss, AUC, and calibration.
However, the models that we could obtain from alternative draws
f
and
f^{\prime}
would assign different probability predictions to the same individual.
In a standard data machine learning pipeline where the data is collected once, this randomness is unkonwn. We are unable to see how different observations of the labels impact individuals (see e.g.,
Fig.

1
).

The main contributions of this work are as follows.

1.

We define the term
observational multiplicity
to refer to arbitrariness that arises from a data collection process.

2.

We study the uncertainty in probabilistic classification through
regret
—i.e., the unavoidable error that we incur due to the randomness in the observed labels.

3.

We present a general-purpose method to estimate the regret in a probabilistic classification task.

4.

Problem Statement

We consider a probabilistic classification task where we are given a dataset of
n
labeled examples
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Each example consists of a feature vector
\bm{x}_{i}=[x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,d}]\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a label
y_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
. We assume that
y_{i}=1
denotes an event of interest. For example,
y_{i}=1
may represent “applicant
i
will repay their loan” or “patient
i
will develop cancer in the next five years”. We assume that the occurrence of this event is
inherently stochastic
.
1
1

1

We use “inherently stochastic” to mean only that the labels are not completely determined by features—i.e., this non-determinism cannot be resolved by adding a reasonable amount of additional features. This reflects philosophical concerns about whether true randomness can even be said to exist. This nondeterminism may be a result of true randomness, but may also arise due to large amount of complexity in a system or exogenous factors.

Our goal is to learn a
probabilistic classifier

f:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
, which takes as input a feature vector
\bm{x}_{i}
and returns as output the predicted probability
f(\bm{x}_{i}):=\text{Pr}(y_{i}=+1\;|\;\bm{x}_{i})
.
We assume that each classifier is specified by a vector of
parameters

\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, and write
{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
when the parameterization is relevant to the discussion.

We fit the parameters of our model using some procedure
\mathcal{A}
that depends on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
to obtain
\bm{\theta}_{*}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
and corresponding model
{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{*}}:={f}_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})}
. For example, we could use
empirical risk minimization
(ERM) with the loss function
\ell:[0,1]\times\{-1,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell({f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i}),y_{i})\,.

(1)

2.1
Observational Multiplicity

Because the model is dependent on the dataset, its predictions will depend on the stochasticity of the observed labels—the labels present in the initial training dataset. This results in a phenomenon we refer to as
observational multiplicity
.

Suppose we have two separate draws of the training dataset:
{\mathcal{D}}^{{0}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{{1}}
. Both
{f}_{\bm{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{{0}})}
and
{f}_{\bm{\theta}({\mathcal{D}}^{{1}})}
are equally valid models. For this reason, we can view
f
as a random variable, obtained as a function applied to the distribution of the sampled training set
\mathcal{D}
, and study the consistent of individual predictions.
2
2

2

This perspective has been used before to study consistency at the population level
[
Zhao and Yu 2006
,
Bach 2010
]
.

This distinction is important because models are applied to individuals. In many applications of machine learning, the subject of a classification task has a stake in the outcome of the classification problem. The choice of one model over another will result in some people receiving different treatments (e.g., denied a loan that they otherwise might have received). This is the standard predictive multiplicity issue. As long as our model class is sufficiently expressive, this issue is unavoidable. It is often trivial to create a new model which performs nearly identical to a base model except on one individual.

The way to “resolve” model multiplicity in practice is to select a model from the set of competing models (called the Rashomon set) according to some procedure, such as empirical risk minimization or stochastic gradient descent. Crucially, these techniques used in practice do not fully resolve model multiplicity. While these methods may or may not be a deterministic procedure on a given dataset, the full pipeline of “data collection and then fit model parameters” is always a stochastic procedure. As a process, it defines a distribution on the Rashomon set, and the final model is typically taken to be a single sample of this distribution.

It’s important to know how concentrated the resulting distribution is. Does this procedure truly resolve predictive multiplicity, or does it merely hide it? This is more than just a mathematical curiosity: knowing the answer to this question can be used to inform a selective abstention procedure on top of the model when the predictions are found to be excessively arbitrary.

2.2
On Regret

In a probabilistic classification task we would like a model that outputs probabilities, but we can only train it from observed labels: events that are a single draw of the underlying stochasticity.

Suppose we were given the “underlying model”
g:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
: a probabilistic classification model that outputs the
ground truth probability

p_{i}^{*}:=g(\bm{x}_{i})
for each point
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
. Given the underlying model, we can “redraw” the labels by sampling

\displaystyle y^{(1)}_{i}\sim\textrm{Bern}(g(\bm{x}_{i}))\,.

(2)

We can then use this new set of observed labels to construct new dataset
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y^{(1)}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and obtain a new classifier
{f}_{1}:={f}_{\theta({\mathcal{D}}^{{1}})}
.

It is clear that
{f}_{1}
is a function of the random variables
y^{(k)}_{i}
and therefore so are the predicted probabilities
p_{i}^{(1)}:={f}_{1}(\bm{x}_{i})
. This new draw comes from the same underlying distribution, so we would hope that our predicted probabilities from our model do not differ from our original predicted probability
p_{i}^{(0)}:={f}_{0}(\bm{x}_{i})
. Of course, complete independence is too much to ask for: if the label is never a fully deterministic function of the features, then any label vector has a non-zero probability of being realized.

We can measure this uncertainty by using, for example, the absolute or squared difference:

\displaystyle e_{i}

\displaystyle:=|p_{i}^{(1)}-p_{i}^{(0)}|\,,

(3)

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle:=e_{i}^{2}\,.

(4)

We will use these quantities to characterize the
regret
we incur by training on a
single draw of the labels
. Regret is a measure that allows us to analyze a special case of observational multiplicity.

Definition 2.0
.

The
regret
of a data point
\bm{x}_{i}
in a model selection procedure
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
on a dataset
\mathcal{D}
for a probabilistic classification model
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
is the variance of the random variable

f_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}(\bm{x}_{i})\,,

where
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
is a random dataset obtained by resampling the labels from
\mathcal{D}
according to the true conditional probabilities of the labels given the data points.

We use the term “regret” because, in analogy to online learning, it reflects “irreducible” risk we incur because as a result of randomness—i.e., aleatoric uncertainty. In online learning, the aleatoric uncertainty arises because we cannot predict a random draw that will take place in the future. In our setting, the aleatoric uncertainty arises as a result of a random draw that took place in the past. As in the online learning setting, we should not expect to ever achieve zero regret in a probabilistic classification task. We are bound to experience some regret because a single draw is insufficient to infer the probability distribution of a single label.

There are two insights to be gained from this analysis.

Remark 2.2
.

Regret is unavoidable.

The setting of probabilistic classification is exactly the same without access to the underlying model, except we only have access to a single draw and are not able to compute any measure of regret for it. The regret still exists, so having a way to estimate the distribution of possible regrets is informative.

Remark 2.3
.

The burden of regret is not shared equally among the data points.

When the probability given by the underlying model
p_{i}^{*}
is close to
0
or
1
, there is less “noise” in the draw and the assigned probability will likely change less over the different samples compared to what happens when
p_{i}^{*}=1/2
. Additionally, if there is a cluster of data points with similar features and similar probabilities given by the underlying model, the empirical probability of
y=1
for this cluster will tend to concentrate around the mutually shared probability, and so the assigned probabilities will likely change less than for isolated points. We will see later that the range of values regret takes over the dataset is often quite large.

2.3
Estimating Regret

In an ideal world, to measure regret (or otherwise quantify observational multiplicity), we would collect fresh samples of the data and retrain the model multiple times. This is impossible or impractical in practice. In tasks where we can collect more data, we are often only able to sample the labels of
new
individuals rather than from individual in our dataset—e.g., we cannot offer a new loan to someone previously in a loan-default dataset). Under the belief that there was randomness involved in the observed label, this is a significant problem. There is typically no way to truly get around the issue of the sample size being one. We are forced to estimate regret using a counterfactual procedure.

Procedure

A general procedure to estimate regret is to obtain some approximation of the “underlying model” from the previous section and use this as a proxy to resample the labels ourselves. This approximation, of course, is exactly the model we were attempting to learn in the first place.

We present a procedure for this task in
Algorithm

1
. This procedure assumes that the dataset obtained by the resampling process is similarly plausible to the original dataset. This assumption may not be true in practice, because the relationship between the features and the label may not be accurately represented by the model. However, even in this situation the results of this resampling process are still meaningful. This is because of when we deploy a model, we are simultaneously declaring some degree of confidence in its accuracy. The resampled labels are, by construction, a plausible set of labels according to the model. Therefore, the uncertainty measured by this procedure is directly implied by the model we are seeking to deploy.

Remarks on bootstrapping.

Bootstrapping is a common and versatile method that can be used to estimate the uncertainty in statistical models. Bootstrapping can be preformed by sub-sampling the training dataset to create a distribution of possible alternative training datasets. All of the analysis that can be done with the resampling procedure can be done with bootstrapping, likely yielding different results. An advantage the resampling procedure has over bootstrapping is that bootstrapping always maintains the observed labels in the training dataset, whereas the resampling procedure may flip them. In this way, the resampling procedure explicitly encodes our belief that the labels themselves may be stochastic, and allows us to ensure that this stochasticity is accounted for in our uncertainty estimates.

3
Analysis

With regret defined, we now are interested in determing properties of the measure. We will use logistic regression as an example case, because for this model we can show a bound for the expected value of the regret estimator:

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
. Consider a logistic regression model on the dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
where the features
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are fixed while the labels are sampled
\hat{y}_{i}\sim\textrm{Bern}(p_{i})
with
p_{i}
given by an initial logistic regression model with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{*}
. Define

Q_{i}:=p_{i}^{2}(1-p_{i})^{2}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{H}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}\,,

where

\bm{H}:=\sum_{j}p_{i}(1-p_{i})\bm{x}_{j}\bm{x}_{j}^{\intercal}\,.

Additionally, let

\epsilon:=800\cdot\frac{dX_{\textnormal{max}}{}\left(\log(nX_{\textnormal{max}}{}/X_{\textnormal{min}}{})+X_{\textnormal{max}}{}\|\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}}\,.

If
\epsilon<1
, then the output probability
\hat{p}_{i}
of
\bm{x}_{i}
from the logistic regression model is a random variable satisfying

\displaystyle|\operatornamewithlimits{Var}(\hat{p}_{i})-Q_{i}|\leq\epsilon Q_{i}\,.

The terms
X_{\textnormal{max}}{}
and
X_{\textnormal{min}}{}
refer to the maximum and minimum values of
\|\bm{x}_{i}\|
over all indices
i
, while
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
is the minimum eigenvalue of the
d\times d
matrix
\bm{H}
.

The proof of this theorem is in
Section

5
and follows from results shown by
Bach 2010
. We remark that the constant
800
is not tight, and reported only for concreteness.

If we sample the original data from a fixed distribution with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, then many of the terms in the expression for
\epsilon
from
Theorem

3.4
can be bounded by constants with high probability. The following corollary describing the limiting behavior of
\epsilon
follows immediately.

Corollary 3.5
.

Using the notation of
Theorem

3.4
, suppose the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{*}
for the logistic regression model used to resample the labels are obtained by fitting on an initial dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where each row of the dataset is drawn independently from a fixed probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and a full rank covariance matrix. Suppose further that there is a compact subset
K\subset(0,1)
such that
\operatornamewithlimits{Pr}(y=1\;|\;\bm{x})\in K
for all
\bm{x}
when
(\bm{x},y)
is sampled from
\mathcal{P}
. If
n\geq C_{\mathcal{P}}(1+\log(1/\delta))
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
over the random sample
\mathcal{D}
we have that

\epsilon\leq c_{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\,,

where
C_{\mathcal{P}}
and
c_{\mathcal{P}}
are constants that depends on
\mathcal{P}
but not
n
.

A full proof of this corollary is in
Section

5.6
.

Theorems

3.4
and

apply both to estimated regret determined according to

\bm{x}_{i}
is larger when
p_{i}
is closer to
1/2
and when

5
Analysis of Logistic Regression

We now turn to prove the results stated in
Section

3
. First, we introduce the mathematical setting and notation we will be using for the proofs.

5.1
Preliminaries

We consider a logistic regression task in which feature matrix is full rank and not linearly separable. Here “not linear separable” means there is no non-zero vector
\bm{v}
such that
y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{v}\geq 0
for all indices
i
. Under these conditions, ERM returns a unique best-fit model with the parameters:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{*}{}:=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D})=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))

where
\ell(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D})
denotes the logistic loss at parameter
\bm{\theta}
on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
.
Given the model, we can compute the predicted probability for an instance:

p_{i}=\mathrm{Pr}(y_{i}=1\;|\;\bm{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}_{*})}\,.

We construct an alternative dataset
\hat{\mathcal{D}}
by sampling a set of
n

plausible labels
, one for each point
\bm{x}_{i}
.

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ with probability $p_{i}$}\\
-1&\text{ with probability $1-p_{i}$\,.}\end{cases}

(5)

We denote the alternative loss function:

\ell(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathcal{D}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(-\hat{y}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))\,.

We wish to study how the predicted probability for each data point changes with this model. First, we consider how these two loss functions differ:

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathcal{D}})-\ell(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))-\log(1+\exp(-\hat{y}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:y_{i}\neq\hat{y}_{i}}[\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))-\log(1+\exp(y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i:y_{i}\neq\hat{y}_{i}}y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}\,.

That is,
\ell(\bm{\theta};\hat{\mathcal{D}})
and
\ell(\bm{\theta};\mathcal{D})
differ only by a linear function, which by the first-order optimality conditions for
\bm{\theta}_{*}
is
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\hat{\mathcal{D}})^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}
. We denote
\nabla\hat{\ell}:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\hat{\mathcal{D}})
.

By the first-order optimality conditions for
\bm{\theta}_{*}
, we see

\mathbb{E}(\nabla\hat{\ell})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Pr}(\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i})y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}_{*})}{1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}_{*})}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\mathcal{D})=0\,.

Using this identity, we can obtain the following alternative expression for the gradient

\nabla\hat{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\frac{1}{2}|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}|+\mathrm{Pr}(\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i})\right)y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\,,

with

a_{i}=\begin{cases}1-p_{i}&\text{if $\hat{y}_{i}=1$ (with probability $p_{i}$)}\\
-p_{i}&\text{if $\hat{y}_{i}=-1$ (with probability $1-p_{i}$)\,.}\end{cases}

We will introduce the following notation to analyze the problem. Let

\nabla^{2}\ell=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\mathcal{D})=\nabla^{2}\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\hat{\mathcal{D}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(1-p_{i})\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}

be the Hessian at
\bm{\theta}_{*}
. This is the same for
\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\mathcal{D})
and
\ell(\bm{\theta}_{*};\hat{\mathcal{D}})
as they differ only by a linear function. Let
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
be the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\ell
, respectively. Let
X_{\textnormal{max}}{}:=\max_{i}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}
and
X_{\textnormal{min}}{}:=\min_{i}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}
. Lastly, we define

\displaystyle{{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{u}^{\intercal}(\nabla^{2}\ell)\bm{v}\,,

\displaystyle{\|\bm{u}\|}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{{{\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}}}

for
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that because we assume our data is full rank,
\nabla^{2}\ell
is positive definite, which makes
{{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}_{\nabla^{2}\ell}}
an inner product.

\mathcal{P}
. By assumption,
\mu_{\min}>0
.
From
[
Vershynin 2018
, Exercise 4.7.3]
, there is a positive constant
C_{3}
(depending on
\mathcal{P}
) such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{X}
(denoted
m
) is bounded by

m\geq n\mu_{\min}-C_{3}\left(d+t+\sqrt{n(d+t)}\right)\mu_{\max}

(28)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-t)
. We can set

t=n\mu_{\min}^{2}/(64C_{3}\mu_{\max}^{2})
. Then, if
n\geq\max\{\log(4/\delta),64C_{3}d\mu_{\max}^{2}/\mu_{\min}^{2}\}
, we see that
m
is bounded below by

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq n\mu_{\min}-C_{3}\left(d+t+\sqrt{n(d+t)}\right)\mu_{\max}

\displaystyle\geq n\mu_{\min}-C_{3}\left(2t+\sqrt{2tn}\right)\mu_{\max}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n\mu_{\min}}{2}

with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.

We now turn to verify
Eq.

26
. Let
\mu_{\min}
again be the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix of second moments of
\bm{x}
when sampled from
\mathcal{P}
. By assumption, the set of probabilities
\operatornamewithlimits{Pr}(y=1\;|\;x=x^{\prime})
for
x^{\prime}
in this set takes values bounded away from
0
and
1
. Let
q
be such that these probabilities are all in the interval
[q,1-q]
. Now consider an arbitrary vector
\bm{\theta}
on the sphere of radius
R:=12X_{\textnormal{max}}{}/(q\mu_{\min})
. The value the logistic loss takes at
\bm{\theta}
is a random variable
L(\bm{\theta})
depending on
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
defined by

L(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))\,.

We can bound

\mathbb{E}(\bm{\theta}^{\intercal}\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta})\geq\mu_{\min}R^{2}\,.

Since
|\bm{x}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}|
is a random variable that takes value in
[0,RX_{\textnormal{max}}{}]
, we know that

\operatornamewithlimits{Pr}\left(|\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}|\geq\frac{R\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}}{2}\right)

is at least
\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}/(2X_{\textnormal{max}}{})
. We bound the loss at
\bm{\theta}
below by

\displaystyle L(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}))\cdot\bm{1}(|\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}|\geq R\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}/2\text{ and }\operatorname{sign}(y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta})=-1)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}R\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}/2\cdot\bm{1}(|\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}|\geq R\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}/2\text{ and }\operatorname{sign}(y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta})=-1)\,,

where
\bm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function that the predicate is true. The random variables (for each
i
)

\bm{1}(|\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}|\geq R\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}/2\text{ and }\operatorname{sign}(y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta})=-1)

are each a Bernoulli random variable that takes value
1
with probability at least

\frac{q\sqrt{\mu_{\min}}}{2X_{\textnormal{max}}{}}\,.

For
i\neq j
, these variables are independent. The expected value of
L(\bm{\theta})
is at least

\frac{Rq\mu_{\min}}{4X_{\textnormal{max}}{}}=3n\,.

By Hoeffding’s inequality,

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{Pr}\left(L(\bm{\theta})\leq 2n\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2n^{2}}{R^{2}\mu_{\min}n/4}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{8n}{R^{2}\mu_{\min}}\right)\,.

Now consider another parameter vector
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
on the sphere of radius
R
with
\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}\|<\delta:=1/X_{\textnormal{max}}{}
. We see that

\displaystyle L(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\exp(-X_{\textnormal{max}}{}\delta)\left[1+\exp(-y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\bm{\theta})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=L(\bm{\theta})-nX_{\textnormal{max}}{}\delta=L(\bm{\theta})-n\,.

This implies that if
L(\bm{\theta})\geq 2n
, then
L(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\geq n
for all
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
such that
\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|=R
and
\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\|=1/X_{\textnormal{max}}{}
. We can cover the sphere with a set
\mathcal{N}
consisting of
\left(1+2/\delta\right)^{d}
points such that every point on the sphere is within distance
\delta
from a point in
\mathcal{N}

[
Vershynin 2018
, Corollary 4.2.13]
. Fix such a set
\mathcal{N}
. By a union bound, the probability that
L(\bm{\theta})\leq 2n
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{N}
is at most

\left(1+2X_{\textnormal{max}}{}\right)^{d}\exp\left(-\frac{8n}{R^{2}\mu_{\min}}\right)\,.

This is at most
\delta/2
if we take

n\geq\frac{R^{2}}{8\mu_{\min}}\log\left(\frac{(1+2X_{\textnormal{max}}{})^{d}}{\delta}\right)\,.

However, the logistic loss is convex and attains the value
n\log 2<n
at
0
, so therefore in this case the minimum must occur within the ball of radius
R
. Hence,
\|\bm{\theta}_{*}\|\leq R
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
. The probability
Eqs.

26
and
27
both hold is
(1-\delta/2)^{2}\geq 1-\delta
.
∎

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce the concept of observational multiplicity, which is a form of predictive multiplicity that arises from randomness in the data observation process. We highlight a particular form of observational multiplicity as a result of observing single draws of random labels and shows it can produce variation in model predictions, which we call regret. We showed regret is not uniformly distributed in the population, and that often a few individuals have very high regret.
