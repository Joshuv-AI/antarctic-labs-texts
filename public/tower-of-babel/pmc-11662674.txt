Simultaneous determination of cytosolic aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase activities by LC–MS/MS
Authors: Marisa I Mendes, Nicole I Wolf, Joëlle Rudinger-Thirion, Dominic Lenz, Magali Frugier, Patrick Verloo, Hanna Mandel, Joshua Manor, Rachel Kassel, Willemijn E Corpeleijn, Sanne van der Rijt, Elsbeth M Schroor, Silvy J M van Dooren, Christian Staufner, Gajja S Salomons, Desirée E C Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, pathogenic variants in ARS genes, encoding aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs), have been associated with human disease. Patients harbouring pathogenic variants in ARS genes have clinical signs partly unique to certain aaRSs defects, partly overlapping between the different aaRSs defects. Diagnosis relies mostly on genetics and remains challenging, often requiring functional validation of new ARS variants. In this study, we present the development and validation of a method to simultaneously determine aminoacylation activities of all cytosolic aaRSs (encoded by ARS1 genes) in one single cell lysate, improving diagnosis in suspected ARS1 disorders and facilitating functional characterization of ARS1 variants of unknown significance. As proof of concept, we show enzyme activities of five individuals with variants in different ARS1 genes, demonstrating the usability and convenience of the presented method.

Introduction

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs) play an essential role in protein synthesis by attaching amino acids to their cognate transfer RNA (tRNA), maintaining translation fidelity. This reaction proceeds in two steps: in the first step, ATP condenses with the amino acid, leading to a highly reactive enzyme-bound aminoacyl-AMP. In the second step, the enzyme transfers the activated amino acid to the 3′ end of the cognate tRNA to form an aminoacyl-tRNA.

This ancient family of enzymes is composed of 36 different members, encoded by 37 ARS genes in humans. All genes are nuclear encoded and are named by the single letter code of the amino acid they attach followed by the letters ‘ARS’. Proteins are named by the three-letter code of the amino acid they attach followed by the letters ‘RS’. A subgroup of 18 ARS1 genes encodes cytosolic aaRSs while a set of 17 ARS2 genes codes for proteins responsible for mitochondrial protein translation (mtaaRSs). These are imported to their final destination following translation. There are two cases where the same gene encodes both the aaRS and the mtaaRS, for glycine and lysine. Two different transcripts of the GARS1 gene (MIM*600287, NG_007942.1) exist; transcript NM_002047.2 possesses two distinct initiation sites and can thereby originate GlyRS or mtGlyRS (1,2). Transcript NM_002047.1 can only generate GlyRS and appears to be important for translational control (3). LysRS and mtLysRS are encoded by KARS1 gene (MIM*601 421, NG_028025.1). The two enzyme isoforms are formed by alternative splicing of the same transcript (4).

Damaging variants in all members of this enzyme family has been associated with human disease, resulting in a relatively large group of inborn errors of metabolism and growing interest in this topic (5–37).

Interestingly and so far unexplained, aaRSs variants can present as either an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive disorder. Thus far, all dominant mutations affect cytosolic aaRSs (AlaRS, AsnRS, GlyRS, HisRS, LysRS, MetRS, TrpRS and TyrRS) and cause hereditary peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy (Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease). Recessive deficiencies show more heterogeneous phenotypes. A significant number of ARS disorders affects the central nervous system but in different ways. Leukodystrophies are caused by pathogenic variants in DARS2 (15), EARS2 (18), MARS2 (5), AARS2 (10), DARS1 (26), RARS1 (29), CARS1 (34) and EPRS1 (35), with RARS1, DARS1 and EPRS1 causing hypomyelination. Pathogenic variants in RARS2 (8) and QARS1 (30) cause an early-onset disease with global brain atrophy and severe epilepsy. Pathogenic variants in KARS1 (20) are also associated with severe brain disorders, including progressive microcephaly [also observed for NARS1 deficiency (38)], seizures and abnormal brain white matter. Pathogenic variants in VARS2 (7), CARS1 (34) and NARS2 (39) are linked to microcephaly and epilepsy. Defects in FARS2 (9), CARS2 (11), PARS2 (17), NARS2 (17), AARS1 (24), LARS1 (21), VARS1 (32), FARSA (33) and FARSB (31) are also linked to epilepsy. Independent deficiencies can affect a specific tissue or organ. For example, Perrault syndrome, characterized by ovarian dysgenesis and sensorineural loss, has been associated with pathogenic variants in HARS2 (40), LARS2 (13) and MARS2 (5). Variants in FARS2 (41) and AARS2 (42) are associated with cardiomyopathy. Pathogenic variants in LARS1 (21) cause infantile liver failure syndrome type 1. Interestingly, the clinical course of LeuRS deficient patients significantly worsens with febrile infections (43). Other ARS gene defects particularly associated with liver disease include CARS1 (34), IARS1 (23), MARS1 (28), VARS1 (32), YARS1 (22), FARSA (33) and FARSB (31). Phenotypic presentation of ARS defects has been recently reviewed by Turvey et al. (44).

Because of the necessity of functional characterization of ARS1 variants of unknown significance (VUS), we developed and validated a discontinuous enzyme assay for the simultaneous characterization of all aaRSs. We present kinetic data and reference values for aaRSs enzymes in control fibroblasts. As proof of principle, we characterize aminoacylation activities of five cell lysates from patients with variants in different ARS1 genes.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following stable isotope labelled amino acids [D7]-Ala, [13C6]-Arg, [15N2]-Arg, [D2]-Cys, [13C5]-Glu, [D3]-Glu, [13C2,15N]-Gly, [13C6]-His, [13C6,15N]-Ile, [13C2]-Leu, [D3]-Leu, [D4]-Lys, [13C]-Phe, [D5]-Phe, [13C]-Ser, [15N2]-Trp, [13C9]-Tyr, [D2]-Tyr, [13C]-Val and [15N]-Val were purchased from Cambridge Isotopes Laboratories (Tewksbury, MA, USA). Whereas [15N2]-Asn, [13C5]-Gln, [D2]-Gly, [13C,D3]-Met, [13C5,15N]-Pro, [13C4,15N]-Thr, dithiotreitol, ATP, brewer’s yeast tRNA, Escherichia coli tRNA, nonafluoropentanoic acid, Trizma, magnesium chloride, potassium chloride, trichloroacetic acid (TCA), bovine serum albumin (BSA) and spermine were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Deiselhofen, Germany). [13C4,15N]-Asp was purchased from Campro Scientific (Berlin, Germany). [D3]-Ala and [D3]-Asp were purchased from CDN Isotopes (Quebec, Canada). [14C]-Tyr was purchased from Perkin Elmer. Ammonia was purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Solutions

The substrate mix consisted of 1 mM [D3]-Ala, [15N2]-Asn, [13C4,15N]-Asp, [15N2]-Arg, [D2]-Cys, [13C5]-Gln, [13C5]-Glu, [D2]-Gly, [13C6]-His, [13C6,15N]-Ile, [13C2]-Leu, [13C6]-Lys, [13C,D3]-Met, [D5]-Phe, [13C5,15N]-Pro, [13C]-Ser, [13C4,15N]-Thr, [15N2]-Trp, [D2]-Tyr, [15N]-Val and dithiotreitol. This mix was prepared in Tris–HCl and the pH was adjusted to 7.6. The standard mix was 1 μM of the previous mix. The internal standard consisted of 5 μM [D7]-Ala (measured as [D4]-Ala), [13C6]-Arg, [D3]-Glu, [13C2,15N]-Gly, [D3]-Leu, [13C]-Phe, [13C9]-Tyr, [13C]-Val and [D3]-Asp.

Cell culture

Human skin fibroblasts were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (Bodinco, Alkmaar, the Netherlands), and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen). Cultures were grown in 175 cm2 culture flasks (Greiner Bio-One, Frickenhausen, Germany) and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. The medium was changed twice a week, and the cultures were split at a 1:2 ratio whenever cells reached about 90% confidence. The cells were harvested with trypsin (Invitrogen) after reaching confluence, washed twice with Hank’s buffered salt solution (Invitrogen). Two cell pellets were made from each flask and stored at −80°C.

Preparation of enzyme extracts

Frozen cell pellets were resuspended in ribonuclease (RNase)-free water and subjected to three freeze/thaw cycles. The cell suspension was subsequently centrifuged for 5 min at 10 000 × g. The supernatant was used subsequently. Total cytosolic protein concentration was determined by the bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Sigma–Aldrich). Cell lysates were diluted to 1 mg/ml total protein. Throughout this study, when protein concentration is mentioned, we refer to the total amount of cytosolic proteins in the fibroblast lysates.

Western blot

Western blot was performed to confirm that the cell lysis method used did not result in contamination of the cytosolic fraction with mitochondrial content. Thirty micrograms of the cell lysate prepared as described above and the resulting pellet treated with urea were separated in a 12% stain-free gel through sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidenfluoride membrane (Bio-Rad) using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Immunodetection was performed using two primary antibodies (rabbit) directed at ArgRS (Art nr 13434687, Thermo Fisher Scientific, NH, USA) and voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC1) (Art nr AB15895, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and a secondary anti-rabbit antibody (PO448, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Immune complexes were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (Lumilight Plus, Roche, Basel, Switzerland), according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Images were acquired in a CCD (charge-coupled device) imager ChemiDoc XRS (Bio-Rad) using the Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Functional analysis of aaRS activities

Cell lysates (10 μg total protein) were incubated in a final volume of 50 μl in 65 mM Tris–HCl (pH = 7.6), 32.5 mM potassium chloride, 15.6 mM magnesium chloride, 200 μM brewer’s yeast total tRNA (E. coli tRNA for AlaRS and AspRS), 1 mM ATP, 0.5 mM spermine, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 2 mg/ml BSA and 300 μM amino acid mix for 10 min at 37°C. tRNA was heat treated at 65°C for 5 min and allowed to cool at room temperature for 5 min prior to use. Blank samples were prepared by heating the lysate at 98°C for 10 min prior to the assay. The assay was stopped by placing the samples on ice and adding 200 μl 5% TCA. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Sample clean-up

For sample clean-up, 96-well multiscreen PVDF filter plates were used (0.22 μm; Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The plates were conditioned using 70% ethanol and RNase-free water. Subsequently the samples were introduced to the plate and washed 5 times with 200 μl 5% TCA using a vacuum manifold (Merck Millipore). The bond between tRNA and cognate amino acid was broken by incubating in 100 μl 0.3% ammonia containing 10 pmol of internal standard and 1 mM DTT for 1 h at 37°C. Finally, 50 μl of 200 mM nonafluoropentanoic acid were added to precipitate the tRNA and the filter plate was centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 × g. The filtrate was stored at −20°C until analysis. To a calibration curve (ranging from 5 to 100 pmol), the same amount of internal standard was added, which allowed quantification of the amount of amino acid that was bound to tRNA by comparing the ratio amino acid/internal standard to the calibration curve. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry

All analyses were performed on an API 5000 triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) coupled to a Nexera UPLC (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The turbo ion electrospray was operated in positive ion mode with a temperature of 450°C and an ionspray voltage of 5000 V using nitrogen as source gas at a flow rate of 50 psi. Using a Symmetry Shield C18 analytical column (2.0 × 100 mm; 3.5 μm; Waters), 5 μl of the sample was separated using a mobile phase containing 5 mM nonafluoropentanoic acid. In 15 min, the acetonitrile content of the mobile phase was increased from 0 to 30%. All liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) experiments were performed using unit resolution using nitrogen as the collision gas at a pressure of 6 psi. The declustering potential was set to 15 V, the collision energy to 21 V and the entrance potential to 10 V. For each precursor fragment transition, a dwell time of 100 ms was applied. Optimization and validation

All enzyme assay parameters were optimized: pH (6–8; 0.25 increments), time (0–40 min), total cytosolic protein (1–30 μg), spermine (0.1–2.5 mM), DTT (0.5–5 mM), BSA (0.2–5 mg/ml), ATP (0.05–5 mM), total tRNA source (E. coli, baker’s and brewer’s yeast), tRNA amount (1–200 μM) and amino acid mix (15–600 μM). Intra- and inter-assay variations were determined by measuring a control lysate 8 times within the same series and in six different series, respectively. Detection limits were determined by diluting a cell lysate without performing the assay.

In vitro aminoacylation assay with radiolabelled amino acid

Aminoacylation assays were performed in exactly the same conditions as described above in the presence of L-[14C] radiolabelled Tyr. Tyrosine was adjusted to 30 cpm/pmol with the corresponding unlabelled amino acid. The final amino acid concentration varied between 20 and 240 μM. After 10 min incubation at 37°C, 40 μl of the reaction were removed from the reaction mix and spotted on 3MM Whatman paper. Aminoacyl-tRNAs were precipitated in 5% TCA, papers were washed 2 times with TCA, once with ethanol and dried. Incorporation of radioactive amino acid was measured by liquid scintillation spectroscopy (LS 6500 scintillation counter, Beckman Coulter). A blank was performed in the presence of the heat-treated lysate. The aminoacylation values are the average of at least three experiments.

Determination of initial velocity of aminoacylation

To further investigate the Km discrepancies between our method and published data, we experimentally determined initial velocity by performing the assay as described above, with some modifications. We used increasing amounts of proline (9.4, 18.8, 37.5, 75, 150 and 300 μM). For each concentration, the final volume was 300 μl. Aliquots (45 μl) were collected at 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 60 min into TCA containing wells. After all time points were collected, sample clean-up was performed as described above and proline was measured by LC–MS/MS.

Analysis of primary fibroblasts of patients

To ascertain the clinical importance of our method, we analysed the aminoacylation activity of five individuals with a phenotype compatible with the respective aaRS deficiency which was in line with most likely pathogenic biallelic variants in LARS1, FARSA, YARS1, MARS1 and FARSB, respectively. Informed consent to present the enzyme activities was obtained from each individual or the respective guardians.

Results

tRNA from brewer’s yeast is mostly accepted in human aminoacylation reactions

The enzyme assay was performed using different sources of tRNA. We observed that tRNA from brewer’s yeast could be aminoacylated by all aaRSs, with the exception of AlaRS, GluRS activity of the GluProRS enzyme, TrpRS and AspRS. E. coli tRNA permitted the determination of AlaRS and AspRS activities. GluRS and TrpRS activities could not be determined with any of the tRNAs that are commercially available (also not bovine tRNA). Thus total tRNA from brewer’s yeast was used in our experiments while E. coli tRNA was used in a separate assay for AlaRS and AspRS.

Cytosolic fraction is properly isolated

To confirm that the isolated cytosolic fraction was not contaminated with mitochondrial content, we performed a western blot. No mitochondrial protein was detected in the cytosolic fraction (Supplementary Figure S1).

Assay validation

The optimum pH was assessed by fitting a third-order polynomic curve through the individual results using Microsoft Excel. This resulted in a pH of 7.6 for the assay buffer. The enzymatic reaction was linear between 5 and 15 min. An incubation time of 10 min was chosen for the method. We identified 10 μg of total protein as the optimal amount to use since the reaction is still in the linear phase (data not shown).

An increase in spermine or DTT concentrations did not result in an increase or decrease in enzyme activity from 0.5 mM for spermine and 2 mM for DTT. For BSA, addition of 1 mg/ml was enough to reach a plateau.

ATP is a co-substrate of the reaction and therefore the used concentration needs to be at saturating conditions. Saturation was reached with a concentration of 1 mM. Higher amounts did not further increase enzyme activity for any aaRS.

Enzyme kinetics for 18 out of 19 cytosolic enzymes (GluProRS functions for both Glu and Pro) were determined for amino acids by fitting the data to the Michaelis–Menten equation using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.2.1), and were reported as the average of three independent assays (Supplementary Figure S2). Reaction end points were used as surrogates for the reaction rates. It is important to note that the kinetics were performed using total protein of the lysate, as active site titration for each synthetase was not determined. Alternatively, we show in supplementary data that the selected amounts of amino acid and tRNA are in excess (Supplementary Figure S2 for amino acid and Supplementary Figure S4 for tRNA). Results are shown in Table 2. Km varied from 3.203 μM for ArgRS to 156.5 μM for AlaRS. The highest Vmax was determined for AlaRS (2.428 nmol/min/mg). Visual inspection of the Michaelis–Menten plots suggested that CysRS and ValRS showed inhibition by Cys and Val, respectively. Therefore, the Ki was determined using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.2.1). CysRS Ki for Cys was 352.4 μM, while ValRS Ki for Val was 962.6 μM.

Because of the discrepancies observed for some Km values, two approaches were performed regarding the two enzymes with the most dissimilar values. For TyrRS (measured Km 64.48 versus literature 0.3 μM and 14.6 μM), tyrosylation was tested in a conventional manner by detecting radiolabelled tyrosyl-tRNAs. Regarding ProRS (measured Km 26.62 versus literature 180 μM), Km was alternatively determined by measuring initial prolylation rate, obtained from the slopes of the plots of Pro concentration versus reaction time (up to 20 min, as from this time point reactions were no longer linear). The Km measured with this approach was in accordance with what we determined before (Supplementary Figure S3B).

Quantification limit and linearity

Quantification limits (signal to noise ratio > 10) for the different aaRSs activities ranged between 0.001 and 0.025 nmol/min/mg protein. Quantification limit was influenced by proximity of elution of the specific amino acid to the void. Linearity for all amino acids was at least between the limit of quantification and 5 nmol/min/mg protein (10–250 pmol amino acid). Only alanine suffered from signal suppression, common in LC–MS/MS methods, the effects of this was circumvented by adding an internal standard for alanine.

Discussion

We have developed a high throughput assay to simultaneously measure enzyme activity of aaRS in cell lysates. The main goal was to develop a tool for characterization of VUS in any of the ARS1 genes identified in patients in a clinical laboratory setting. A great advantage of this method is the capacity to measure all aaRS activities in one reaction and thus have in one reaction the availability of 17 internal controls. Furthermore, the assay is developed in a 96-well setting which allows the analysis of patient samples even with different clinical requests (patients with variants in any of the 18 ARS1 genes). All materials we utilize are readily available and inexpensive.

Proteins are made up of 20 different amino acids and hence we investigated 20 aminoacylation activities. We could detect aminoacylation successfully for 18 out of 20 amino acids. GluRS and TrpRS activities could not be accurately detected. In the cytosol, attachment of glutamate to the cognate tRNA is performed by GluProRS, a dual aaRS that is also responsible for the reaction of proline with its tRNA, which we could successfully detect. Several reasons could explain our inability to determine GluRS and TrpRS activities. For TrpRS adhesion of Trp to the filter during clean-up resulted in high blank values masking potential TrpRS activity. GluProRS and maybe TrpRS may need an extra compound not present in our assay mix or the non-human tRNA molecules used in this assay are inappropriate. Another explanation could be the chemical rearrangement during sample treatment or in the mass spectrometer, however all logical conversions (like pyroglutamic acid for GluRS) were pursued, but did not result in detection of glutamylation.

We determined kinetic parameters for all 18 aaRS regarding amino acid concentration and compared with the data available in literature. We would like to stress the fact that our assay was developed in primary cells of patients with the intent of offering a diagnostic tool for patients suspected of having ARS1 deficiency often as a result of a novel detected VUS in one of the ARS1 genes and/or by clinical suspicion. When the main goal is to fully characterize one specific variant of a specific aaRS, other available method might be more appropriate. There are several enzymatic methods developed so far with significant variation. One of the most important aspects of this variation is the source of tRNA. We opted for a commercially available mix, while other groups isolate or synthetize a specific tRNA or use a different tRNA source. Our choice was based on the price of commercially available mixes, resulting in simplicity of use. It should be mentioned that every new lot was validated by comparing the aminoacylation activities of three different controls with the reference values. Another difference worth mentioning concerns the enzyme itself. Published kinetic values were obtained using overexpressed purified aaRSs while we use fibroblast cell lysates. Although these differences may hinder direct comparison of kinetic parameters, Km values determined in this manuscript are in the same range as the values found in literature for several aaRS, including ArgRS [3.203 μM reported by us versus 3.5 μM (45)], AspRS [48.68 μM reported by us versus 24 μM (46) and 302 μM (47)], CysRS [25.16 μM versus 20.3 μM (48)], GlyRS [18.06 μM versus 15.2 μM (49)], HisRS [3.544 μM versus 8.0 μM (50)], IleRS [6.548 μM versus 4.6 μM (51)], LeuRS [27.02 μM versus 24 μM (52) and 7.5 μM (45)] and MetRS [19.26 μM versus 10 μM (53)]. Differences in Km were found for PheRS [19.45 μM versus 3.2 μM (54)], ProRS [26.62 μM versus 180 μM (55)] and for TyrRS [64.48 μM versus 0.3 μM (56) and 14.6 μM (57)]. A possible explanation for the differences observed for PheRS, ProRS and TyrRS is that these studies use purified protein and pure tRNA. Additionally, Beuning et al. measured solely the first step of prolylation, namely the amino acid activation by ATP–inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) exchange. In addition, in that study only the ProRS part of GluProRS was expressed and purified. A difference in the structure of the shorter protein could account for a different affinity for Pro. It is also important to mention that GluProRS is part of the multisynthetase complex and study of the isolated truncated ProRS could explain the observed differences. Since we use a mix of tRNA and do not quantify each individual tRNA species, the amount of specific tRNAs we used could be not optimal although while optimizing the amount of tRNA used we did not observe this. To support our findings obtained with the end point assay as a surrogate for the reaction rate, we repeated the ProRS Km determination by experimentally quantifying the initial velocity rates (determined from the slopes of the reactions with increasing amounts of amino acid mix) versus amino acid concentrations. The Km determined by this approach (29 μM) is in agreement with our previously determined Km (26.62 μM) (Supplementary Figure S3A). Regarding the TyrRS disparity, we compared this assay with the conventional radiolabelled assay. The data obtained from both methods match (Supplementary Figure S3B).

Activities of all aaRS measured were well above the quantification limit determined for this method and within the linear range. The intra-assay variation for amino acids which had their own internal standard (like alanine or glycine) was lower than for amino acids for which another amino acid was used as internal standard. Inter-assay variation was larger than intra-assay variation because the quantification of protein adds an additional measurement variation (inter-assay protein quantification coefficient of variation (CV) <10%). In addition, the relatively large variation for CysRS could be explained by the thiol group of cysteine which is easily oxidized to form disulphides.

Although there have been many published alternative assays to measure aminoacylation, the historical radioactive method remains the standard, due to its simplicity and sensitivity. Beebe et al. have developed an updated radioactive method with high throughput in mind by using 96 well plates and filter plates similar to the set-up we describe (58). Radioactive methods are indeed very sensitive, but the radiolabelled compound can be metabolized and thus the counts may not reflect the actual activities. In addition, they generate radioactive waste that needs to be properly discarded, thereby increasing the costs of the assay. There are also a few non-radioactive methods described in the literature. Several colorimetric methods are available to monitor consumption of ATP via the release of PPi during the first step of the aminoacylation reaction (59,60). These assays are inexpensive and easy to set up, but they require purified proteins, as a cell lysate contains interfering components. Additionally, multiple aaRS activities cannot be accessed simultaneously. Gamper et al. described a label-free assay to determine aminoacylation by biotinylation–streptavidin conjugation to the aminoacyl group of the aminoacyl-tRNA (61). This last method will not work with commercially available tRNA because it is not clear whether amino acids are bound to this tRNA source. Another coupled assay was developed by Richardson and First. They monitor the production of AMP coupled to the reduction of oxidized nicotine adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), allowing monitoring at 340 nm. This method has the advantage of being continuous by allowing the regeneration of tRNA (62). It should be mentioned that spectrophotometric methods often lack sensitivity. Methods using MALDI–TOF detection were also published, but here also purified proteins are used and the sample clean-up used in this method does not provide high throughput potential (63). Most of the methods described above do not present full validation data, hampering a complete comparison with our method. We used stable isotopically labelled amino acids, which can be measured by LC–MS/MS, a technique that is commonly available in metabolic centres. This presents several advantages in comparison to radioactive substrate. Besides not working with radioactive materials, the main advantage of our method is the possibility of measuring aaRS activities simultaneously since it is possible to distinguish all the differently labelled amino acids. In case of a variant in a specific ARS1 gene, the acquired activity of another aaRS can serve as a control enzyme to ensure that the decrease in activity is specific for that particular aaRS without the need for an additional enzyme assay.

Our method was developed with high throughput set-up in mind. We optimized the use of 96-well filter plates during the whole process (enzyme assay, sample clean up and LC–MS/MS analysis). This allows for (partial) automation and reduction of both chemical and plastic waste.

The use of a filter to separate aminoacyl-tRNAs from free amino acids is very convenient but it is known that the more apolar amino acids will non-specifically bind to this filter. To reduce this problem, we included BSA in our assay mix. BSA also binds non-specifically to the PVDF filters, decreasing the non-specific binding of the amino acids and aaRS enzymes. It is important to keep in mind that in all aminoacylation determinations, including this study as well as studies using radioactive markers, only the aminoacylation capacity of aaRSs is investigated but their other non-canonical functions are not. This is important to realize when DNA variants in the ARS1 genes need further characterization to confirm/exclude the diagnosis of a particular aaRS deficiency. Therefore, when normal aminoacylation is found, the likelihood of aaRS deficiency is reduced, but cannot be excluded.

Currently there are no certified laboratories performing aminoacylation in patient cells, as a routine diagnostic procedure. To highlight the clinical importance this method possesses, we share aminoacylation data of five individuals with variants in several ARS1 genes. For each individual, this assay demonstrated the functional effect of the genetic variants identified, meaning that the lowest activity measured corresponded to the protein encoded by the gene with variants.

Additionally, the method described can serve as an important tool for the development of novel antimicrobials. Pathogen aaRSs are a common target to the development of new antibiotics (64–66), reviewed in (67,68). Examples of targets are IleRS (69) of Helicobacter pylori, LeuRS of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, LysRS and ProRS of Plasmodium falciparum and MetRS of Enterococcus faecalis, S. aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Trypanosoma brucei and Leishmania donovani. Our assay could be used to confirm that these compounds do not interfere with human aaRSs.

In conclusion, we present the development and validation of a method to simultaneously determine aminoacylation of cytosolic aaRS. A simultaneous method for aaRS activities is particularly needed due to the fact that some aaRS deficiencies present overlapping phenotypes. The applicability for this method is further extended to the functional study of pathogenicity of variants in ARS1 found via genetic testing like whole exome sequencing, whole genome sequencing or Sanger sequencing.
