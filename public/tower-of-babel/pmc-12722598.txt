FUBP3 mediates MXI1 stability to silence RRAS and hinder MAPK signaling in acute megakaryoblastic leukemia progression
Authors: Ying Zhu, Jinying Liu, Jiqiang Zeng, Zhirui Liu, Zhimin Yan, Shanrong Zhang, Huixi Luo, Zhipeng Wu, Jiawei Lu, Fang Qiu, Junsong Ye, Chen Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRRAS, a gene encoding a small monomeric GTPase that controls cell adhesion and migration, has been linked to leukemogenesis. However, its function in acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL), a rare type of acute myeloid leukemia, remains indistinct. Here, we explored the potential and mechanism of RRAS in the immune escape of AMKL. Human AMKL cells M-07e and UT-7 were infected using lentiviruses and co-cultured with CD8+ T cells. An AMKL mouse model was constructed by tail vein injection of the mouse AMKL cells with genetic intervention. RRAS expression and ERK-1/2 phosphorylation were enhanced, and MXI1 was reduced in M-07e and UT-7 cells. RRAS knockdown and MXI1 or FUBP3 overexpression inhibited AMKL cell viability and promoted the anti-tumor immunity of CD8+ T cells and apoptosis of AMKL cells. Knockdown of RRAS or overexpression of FUBP3 decreased the infiltration and aggregation of megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, liver, and spleen of AMKL mice, which were rescued by MXI1 knockdown or RRAS overexpression. MXI1 inhibited the MAPK signaling by recruiting NCOR1/2, Sin3A/B, and HDAC1 to co-repress RRAS transcription. FUBP3 stabilized MXI1 to block RRAS-mediated ERK signaling. Overall, we provide evidence of an unrevealed axis FUBP3/MXI1/RRAS/MAPK in the CD8+ T cell immunity in AMKL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04257-z.Keywords: Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, CD8+ T cells, RRAS, MAPK signaling, FUBP3/MXI1

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a deadly bone marrow stem cell cancer that is characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of malignant marrow stem cells and is associated with infection, anemia, and bleeding [33]. It is a genetically heterogeneous cancer, and the prognosis for patients with AML is mainly determined by the biology of the disease, besides age and comorbidities [13]. Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL) is derived from the megakaryocytic precursors, featuring lineage-specific gene expression signatures and morphology characteristics [16]. It makes up 4%–15% of newly diagnosed pediatric AML cases, affecting young children (less than 2 years old) [24]. AMKL is a particularly prevalent form of AML in pediatric patients with Down syndrome, accounting for approximately 70% of all AML cases in this group of patients, and AMKL in non-Down syndrome patients has historically been associated with unfavorable prognoses [23]. Therefore, identifying novel genetic biomarkers is still needed to improve the outcomes for this specific AML.

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is indispensable for cellular proliferation, growth, and survival in various cancers, including hematological malignancies, of which RAS and BRAF are most frequently altered [31]. The RAS (RAt Sarcoma) family of small GTPase proteins comprises 3 homologs, which are well-characterized oncogenes across various solid and non-solid tumor aetiologies [22]. Somatic mutations of Ras-related protein R-Ras (RRAS) have been identified to contribute to leukemogenesis [7], while its specific function in AMKL remains to be explored. Checkpoint inhibitors impede the transmission of T-cell inhibitory signals conveyed via cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (which impacts CD4+ T-cell clonal expansion and trafficking) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (which affects the exhausted CD8+ T-cell compartment) [35]. Interestingly, RRAS has been identified as one of the seven senescence-related genes with predictive significance across multiple lung adenocarcinoma cohorts and demonstrated a negative association with antitumor immunity [8]. Moreover, as a main cascade of MAPK signaling, the RAS-RAF-MEK signaling pathway regulates various aspects of cancer immunity [14]. Therefore, we linked the activation of RRAS-mediated MAPK signaling to the weakening of the CD8+ T cell immunity in AMKL. The transcriptional manipulation of hematopoiesis depends on the relative activity of master transcription factors, some of which function as transcriptional complexes [21]. The small bHLHZip protein MAX is the hub of a transcription factor network, acting as a cofactor for DNA binding by the various members of this network, which include a group of putative MYC antagonists, namely MNT, MXD1-4 (formerly MAD1, MXI1, MAD3, and MAD4), and MGA [12]. The repression of transcription by MXI1 and MAD bHLH-zip proteins is controlled by the corepressors N-CoR (NCOR1) and SMRT (NCOR2), and the interaction of the corepressors with the components involved in chromatin remodeling, such as the recruiting proteins Sin3A/B and the histone deacetylases HDAC1 [27]. Likewise, distinct domains of ETO contact the mSin3A and N-CoR corepressors, thereby forming two binding sites within ETO in AML [1]. To the best of our knowledge, the connection between the MXI1 repressor and RRAS transcription in leukemia has not been reported. Here, we investigated how the repression of RRAS by MXI and its corepressors can affect the anti-tumor response of CD8+ T cells in AMKL.

Materials and methods

Data source

The AMKL dataset GSE98578 was sourced from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). P adj. < 0.05 was used as the threshold for analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between 9 AMKL cell lines and 39 non-AMKL AML cell lines. The genes related to AML (relevance score > 2.5) were downloaded from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). Hiplot Pro (https://hiplot.com.cn/) was used to perform KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the intersecting genes. The event-free survival of AML patients with different RRAS expression was predicted in Kaplan–Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background).

Potential transcription factors (TFs) targeting RRAS were predicted in hTFtarget (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/). The top 50% of TF with total peak numbers, TF with the number of peaks in the gene body (total) > 0, and TF with peaks around TSS (total) > 0 were pooled to intersect with DEG (adj. p < 0.05) and relevant genes in AML. STRING (https://string-db.org/) database was used to visualize the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of intersecting TFs and calculate the top 5 hub TFs in PPI using the BottleNeck algorithm through the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. Next, we analyzed MXI1 binding in the upstream promoter region of RRAS using ChIP-seq data from UCSC (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgGateway). The xCELL immune cell analysis tool from SangerBox (http://vip.sangerbox.com/home.html) was used to assess the correlation between RRAS/MXI1 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration.

We downloaded the RNAInter (http://www.rnainter.org/) for the potential RNA binding protein (RBP) regulating MXI1 with a score > 0.1 and compared them with DEGs with p. adj. < 0.05 in the GSE98578 dataset and relevant genes in AML with relevance score > 2.5 in GeneCards on Jvenn (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/example.html). Similarly, the intersection RBP was analyzed in the STRING database for the PPI network plotting. The correlation between FUBP3 and MXI1 expression in LAML was predicted using GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html).

Cell culture and reagents

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, BFN6072012634) and human AMKL cell line UT-7 (BFN608006119) were procured from BLUEFBIO (Shanghai, China). Human peripheral blood monocytes (CP-H182) and human AMKL cell lines M-07e (CL-0686) and MEG-01 (CL-0498) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, Hubei, China). PBMCs were cultured in DMEM containing 20% FBS (PM150210C, Procell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (PB180121, Procell), and monocytes were cultured in monocyte complete medium (CM-H182, Procell). UT-7 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS (PM150210B, Procell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (P7539, Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Company, St Louis, MO, USA). M-07e cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (R7388, Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10% FBS (F2442, Sigma-Aldrich), 8 ng/mL GM-CSF (PCK035, Procell), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and MEG-01 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

M-07e, UT-7, and MEG-01 (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates containing complete media. According to the experimental protocol, the cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 18 h. The frozen viral solution was thawed, and 10 μL of each of the following: RRAS knockdown (pLV[shRNA]-Puro-U6 > hRRAS[shRNA]), MXI1 overexpression (pLV[Exp]-Puro-EF1A > hMXI1[NM_130439.3]), RRAS overexpression (pLV[Exp]-Puro-EF1A > hRRAS[NM_006270.5]), FUBP3 overexpression (pLV[Exp]-Puro-EF1A > hFUBP3[NM_003934.2]), or MXI1 knockdown (pLV [shRNA]-Puro-U6 > hMXI1[shRNA]) lentiviruses and their negative control oe-NC (pLV[Exp]-Puro-EF1A > ORF_Stuffer) and sh-NC (pLV[shRNA]-Puro-U6 > Scramble[shRNA], CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG) lentivirus solutions were added to the cells and mixed thoroughly. Cells were infected at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 48 h (all with viral titers of 108 TU/mL, MOI = 10). The cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days. Cells with puromycin resistance were extracted and cultured for the next step. The following shRNAs against RRAS and MXI1 were used: sh-RRAS #1: GTCCTACTTCGTGTCTGACTA, sh-RRAS #2: CCTGCTGGTGTTCGCCATTAA, sh-RRAS #3: GTCCGGAAATACCAGGAACAA, sh-MXI1 #1: GACAGCATTGGATCAACTATT, sh-MXI1 #2: CATGGAGAAGTGGACAATATA, sh-MXI1 #3: GAACCCTTCCTGAGCTTTATG.

Co-culture experiments

M-07e and UT-7 cells after infection were maintained in the above medium. CD8+ T cells were purified from human PBMCs using the Human CD8 Microbead Kit (130–132-983, Miltenyi Biotec Inc., Auburn, CA, USA) and resuspended in PBS. CD8+ T cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 48-well plates and stimulated with Dynabeads Human T Activator CD3/CD28 (11161D, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) to maintain proliferative capacity for 4 d. In Transwell chambers, CD8+ T cells were then seeded into the lower chamber, and M-07e and UT-7 cells into the upper chamber at a ratio of 2:1, respectively [6]. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2, the co-culture system was harvested for the following assays.

To study the killing or immune escape effects of CD8+ T cells and AMKL cells under direct contact, we first used PKH67 (HY-D1421, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) to label the cell membranes of AMKL cells. Next, we co-cultured the two cells in a 2:1 ratio in a cell culture plate and incubated them at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. After collecting and fixing the cells, we used PI dye (HY-D0815, MedChemExpress) to mark the dead cells. The percentage of PKH67/PI double-positive dead AMKL cells was counted. To exclude the influence of AMKL cell death, we calculated the percentage of cell death caused by co-culture (%) = 100 × (dead cells after co-culture—dead cells without co-culture) / dead cells after co-culture.

Cell viability and apoptosis assays

AMKL cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (CA1210, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The M-07e and UT-7 cells after infection were plated in 96-well plates, respectively, and the plates were pre-cultured in an incubator for 24 h (at 37 °C with 5% CO2). After the addition of 10 μL CCK-8 solution, the plates were incubated for 2 h. The OD value at 450 nm was read using a microplate reader.

The apoptosis rate of AMKL cells was detected using the TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (C1086, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The M-07e and UT-7 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, resuspended in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, and incubated for 5 min at room temperature (RT). The cells were cultured with 50 μL of TUNEL assay for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark, and cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. After being suspended with 250 μL PBS, the cells were viewed under a fluorescence microscope.

ELISA

Levels of the immunomodulatory factors IL-2 (SEKH-0008, Solarbio) and IFN-γ (KHC4021, Thermo Fisher) were detected in the co-culture system using human ELISA kits. The cell culture medium was transferred to a sterile centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dispensed in aliquots into small EP tubes and stored below -20 °C until use. The OD values at the 450 nm maximum absorption wavelength and the 630 nm reference wavelength were read.

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (approval no. 2025091). Vital River (Beijing, China) provided 8-week-old huPBMC-NOG mice (human MHC, half male and half female). All mice were housed in SPF conditions in a temperature-controlled (22 °C) and humidity-controlled (50%) room on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to chow and drinking water. NOG mice were randomly allocated into 6 groups (n = 5): the sh-NC, sh-RRAS#2, oe-NC, oe-FUBP3, oe-FUBP3 + sh-NC, and oe-FUBP3 + sh-MXI1#2 groups. The mice were injected with M-07e cells (1 × 106 cells) via the tail vein [42]. After 13 days, tail vein blood was collected for white blood cell (WBC) count analysis. Mice were euthanized by sodium pentobarbital (i.p., 150 mg/kg) at the time of near death, and all other surviving mice were euthanized after 30 days. The weights of the excised livers and spleens were measured, and the bone marrow, livers, and spleens of the mice were collected for subsequent experiments.

Immunohistochemical staining

Mouse spleen tissues were fixed in 10% formalin for 72 h, paraffin-embedded, and cut at 4 µm. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated by immersion in 100%, 95%, 75%, and 50% ethanol, ddH2O, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase in 3% H2O2. The sections were immersed in boiling citrate buffer (pH = 6.0) for 20 min and in a sealing solution (PBS containing 5% goat serum) for 30 min at RM. The slides were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies to RRAS (1/800, PA5-141,028, Thermo Fisher), FUBP3 (1/100, MBS9434216, MyBioSource, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and MXI1 (1/200, PA5-68,756, Thermo Fisher) and with the secondary antibody, HRP-coupled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1/1000, ab6721, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 10 min. After treatment with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine for 5 min and counterstaining with hematoxylin, the slides were dehydrated, air-dried, and mounted. Finally, the results were viewed under a microscope and expressed as a percentage of positive cell staining.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

Mouse bone marrow and liver tissues were paraffin-embedded and sectioned following the same steps as described above. Mouse bone marrow, spleen, and liver sections were deparaffinized with xylene for 5 min and rehydrated with anhydrous ethanol for 5 min, 90% ethanol for 2 min, 80% ethanol for 2 min, 70% ethanol for 2 min, and distilled water for 2 min, respectively. The sections were stained with hematoxylin (C0105M, Beyotime) for 5 min and eosin for 2 min. Dehydration was conducted using 70% ethanol for 10 s, 80% ethanol for 10 s, 90% ethanol for 10 s, and anhydrous ethanol for 10 s, followed by clearing with xylene for 5 min. After being sealed with neutral resin, the infiltration of megakaryocytes was observed using the microscope and counted.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sections of mouse spleen tissues were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Tris–EDTA buffer solution (T9285, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for antigen retrieval, and 5% goat serum for sealing. The sections were probed with primary antibody against CD8 (1/100, ab316778, Abcam) overnight and with the secondary antibody (1/250, A-11008, Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at RT. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained by the addition of DAPI, and the samples were imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

Flow cytometric analysis

M-07e and UT-7 cells after gene intervention were cultured separately, washed with PBS, incubated with FITC-conjugated PD-L1 antibody (MA5-52,132, Thermo Fisher) or rabbit IgG FITC (11–4614-80, Thermo Fisher) on ice for 30 min, and analyzed by flow cytometry. CD8+ T cells after co-culture were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 100 μL of PBS containing the corresponding antibodies against APC-PD-1 (1/100, 17–9969-42, Thermo Fisher), FITC-TIM-3 (1/800, 11–3109-42, Thermo Fisher), FITC-Granzyme B (GzmB, 1/1000, NBP2-59678F, Novus Biological Inc., Littleton, CO, USA), and FITC-CD25 (1/100, 11–0259-42, Thermo Fisher) for 30 min on ice. Mouse IgG1 kappa-APC (17–4714-42, Thermo Fisher), mouse IgG1 kappa-FITC (11–4714-81, Thermo Fisher), and rabbit IgG [FITC] (NBP2-24,892, Novus Biological Inc.) were used as isotype controls for different primary antibodies.

To evaluate the proliferative capacity of CD8+ T cells, the CellTrace CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (C34554, Thermo Fisher) was used. CD8+ T cells were resuspended in CFSE (5 μM) buffer and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 20 min. Labeled CD8+ T cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in 96-well round-bottom plates and then stimulated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads (M11389, Abmole Bioscience Inc., Houston, TX, USA) and loaded. Data were analyzed using CytExpert software.

Spleen tissues were cut into small pieces and detached with 5 mL of 2 mg/mL collagenase (SCR103, Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at 37 °C. After the cells were filtered through a 70 μm filter, the cell precipitate was suspended in red blood cell lysis buffer (C3702, Beyotime) for 5 min, centrifuged, and resuspended in 0.5 mL of fixation buffer (420,801, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 20 min. The cells were centrifuged twice, permeabilized, suspended in intracellular staining permeabilization wash buffer (421,002, BioLegend), and stained with antibodies against FITC-CD8 (1/100, ab95591, Abcam), PE-IFN-γ (1/100, 12–7319-42, Thermo Fisher), APC-GzmB (1/20, 372,203, BioLegend), APC-PRF1 (1/20, 308,111, BioLegend), and PE-CD41 (1/500, MHCD4104, Thermo Fisher) at RT for 30 min. After being washed, the cells were finally analyzed by flow cytometry.

ChIP-qPCR assay

AMKL cells were cultured in 10-cm cell culture dishes. According to the protocol of the BeyoChIP ChIP Assay Kit (P2080S, Beyotime), the cells were incubated with 1% PFA for 10 min at 37 °C to cross-link the target proteins and the corresponding genomic DNA. The cells were treated with 1.1 mL Glycine Solution (10X) at RT for 5 min. The cell culture dish was placed in an ice bath, and the culture medium containing PFA and glycine was aspirated. The cells were rinsed with 5 mL of ice-bath pre-cooled protease inhibitor cocktail for mammalian cell and tissue extracts (P1010, Beyotime) and resuspended in 0.2 mL of SDS Lysis Buffer containing protease inhibitors. The solution was sonicated to shear genomic DNA so that the DNA was mostly broken into 400–800 bp fragments. After the addition of 1.8 mL of ChIP Dilution Buffer containing protease inhibitor and 50 µL of Protein A/G magnetic beads, the samples were mixed for 30 min at 4 °C with slow rotation and incubated with anti-MXI1 (1/500, 12,360–1-AP, ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCOR1, 1/30, ab300615, Abcam), anti-NCOR2 (1/50, ab264188, Abcam), anti-Sin3A (1/30, ab307197, Abcam), anti-Sin3B (1/100, NBP2-20,367, Novus Biological Inc), anti-HDAC1 (1/30, ab280198, Abcam), IgG (1/200, AC042, ABclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China) at 4 °C overnight. After subsequent elution of the immunoprecipitation complexes, 8 μL of 5 M NaCl was added and heated at 65 °C for 4 h to remove cross-links between the protein and genomic DNA. The primers corresponding to the RRAS promoter region were used for ChIP-qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assays

A pGL3-based vector containing the RRAS promoter (chr19: 49,640,144–49641143) along with the Renilla luciferase plasmid was transfected into M-07e, UT-7, and MEG-01 cells stably overexpressing MXI1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000075, Thermo Fisher). After 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was detected by the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System, and firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla activity.

RNA pull-down assay

Linearized DNA constructs were used for the preparation of biotinylated MXI1 using the PierceRNA 3'-end desulfuration biotinylation kit (20,163, Thermo Fisher). AMKL cells (1 × 107) were harvested and lysed using IP lysis buffer (87,787, Thermo Fisher). Cell lysates were incubated with biotin-labeled MXI1 and streptavidin magnetic beads, followed by western blot analysis.

RIP

The washed protein A/G agaroses were incubated with anti-FUBP3 (1/50, ab181025, Abcam) or IgG (1/200, AC042, Abclonal) for 30 min at RT using the BeyoRIP RIP Assay kit (P1801S, Beyotime). The AMKL cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed on an ice bath for 10 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected as the prepared cell sample lysate. The lysate was incubated with protein A/G agarose pre-incubated with antibodies for 4 h at 4 °C. After the addition of 1 mL NT2 wash buffer, the protein A/G agaroses were resuspended by gently blowing with a pipette, repeating the wash a total of four times. Finally, RNA purification was performed using the RNAeasy Animal RNA Extraction Kit (R0027, Beyotime). The interaction between FUBP3 and MXI1 was determined by RT-qPCR.

RNA stability

To assess the stability of MXI1 in AMKL cells after overexpression of FUBP3, we treated the cells with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (SBR00013, Sigma-Aldrich), harvested the cells at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h, and isolated the RNA. The residual level of RNA was detected by RT-PCR, by normalizing to the 0 h.

Western blot analysis

AMKL cells and mouse spleen tissues were lysed using RIPA lysing solution (P0013C, Beyotime). Protein concentration was then determined by the BCA Protein Concentration Assay Kit (AR1189, Wuhan Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China), and 50 μg of protein was separated on SDS-PAGE. After separation, proteins were transferred to an Immun-Blot PVDF membrane (1,620,177, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The transfer membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA at RT for 1 h and incubated with the primary antibodies to RRAS (1/1000, PA5-141,028, Thermo Fisher), Phospho (p)-ERK-1/2 (1/1000, 44-680G, Thermo Fisher), ERK-1/2 (1/1000, A4782, Abclonal), PD-L1 (1/1000, MA5-38,419, Thermo Fisher), MXI1 (1/500, 12,360–1-AP, ProteinTech Group), FUBP3 (1/1000, MBS9434216, MyBioSource), Cleaved caspase-3 (1/100, ab2302, Abcam), and GAPDH (1/2500, ab9485, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The appropriate goat anti-rabbit IgG (1/5000, ab6721, Abcam) was used as a secondary antibody for a 1-h incubation at RT. Protein signals were visualized using ECL Plus (AWB0005, Changsha Abiowell Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China). The relative expression of the target protein by comparing the gray value of the target protein band to the gray value of the internal reference protein GAPDH was quantified using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from PBMC, M-07e, UT-7, and MEG-01 cells using TRIzol reagent. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047Q, Takara, Dalian, Liaoning, China). qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A, Takara). The 2−ΔΔCt technique was used to calculate the relative expression of the listed genes using GAPDH as an endogenous control. The primers used for each gene are presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistics of immunoblotting were derived from three independent experiments, and data from other assays derived from five independent experiments are shown as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance among groups was determined by one-way/two-way ANOVA analysis followed by post hoc Tukey’s test. An unpaired t-test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ns indicates no significant difference). All data were analyzed using post hoc statistical power analysis with G*Power software. With α set at 0.05, 1-β > 0.8, which meets statistical power requirements.

Results

RRAS is overexpressed in AMKL, during which the MAPK pathway is activated

The volcano map of DEGs with p. adj. < 0.05 in the GSE98578 dataset is shown in Fig S1A. Genes related to AML with relevance score > 2.5 were downloaded from GeneCards and compared with DEGs in the GSE98578 dataset using Jvenn to obtain 580 intersecting genes (Fig S1B).

These 580 intersecting genes were then analyzed for GO/KEGG enrichment in Hiplot Pro (https://hiplot.com.cn/) (Fig S1C). We first noted that these intersecting genes were mainly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway. We therefore analyzed genes enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (KEGG) and ERK1 and ERK2 cascade (BP) in Jvenn to obtain nine shared genes (Fig S1D): TEK (Log FC = 2.044), RRAS (Log FC = 2.947), PTPRR (Log FC = 2.045), MAP2K7 (Log FC = -0.459), NTRK1 (Log FC = 2.711), JUN (Log FC = -1.722), IL1B (Log FC = 3.369), FGFR3 (Log FC = 2.551), and DUSP6 (Log FC = -2.731). Among them, IL1B has the largest fold change in expression. Low IL1B expression is associated with good patient prognosis [4], and chronic stress promotes AML progression through HMGB1/NLRP3/IL-1β signaling [18]. Therefore, we chose the RRAS with the second-largest fold change in expression for further study. The KEGG diagram shows that RRAS functions as an upstream signal that regulates the MAPK pathway (Fig S1E).

We analyzed event-free survival of AML patients with different RRAS expressions by Kaplan–Meier Plotter. AML patients with high RRAS expression had significantly poorer event-free survival (Fig. 1A). We first detected RRAS expression in human PBMCs, monocytes, and AMKL cell lines M-07e, UT-7, and MEG-01 using RT-qPCR (Fig. 1B) and western blot (Fig. 1C, D). The expression of RRAS was significantly higher in M-07e, UT-7, and MEG-01 cells than in PBMCs and monocytes. The phosphorylation and total expression of ERK-1/2 were assessed (Fig. 1E, F). Interestingly, the extent of ERK-1/2 phosphorylation was significantly elevated in all three AMKL cells.

RRAS overexpression in AMKL activates the MAPK signaling to suppress CD8+ T cell immunity

We found that RRAS expression was negatively correlated with CD8+ T cells in AML using xCELL immunocell analysis in SangerBox (Fig. 2A). Therefore, we hypothesized that due to the RRAS overexpression in AML, the MAPK signaling was activated, which in turn inhibits the immune function of CD8+ T cells, ultimately leading to the progression of AMKL.

MXI1 inhibits MAPK signaling by recruiting NCOR1/2, Sin3A/B, and HDAC1 to jointly repress RRAS

Ten intersections obtained from three datasets, namely potential TFs of RRAS, DEG (adj. p < 0.05), and relevant genes in AML, were found on Jvenn (Fig S2A), followed by PPI network construction in STRING (Fig S2B). The top 5 nodes (Fig S2C) were demonstrated using the BottleNeck algorithm using the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. GATA1 mutation has been identified in AMKL [9]. NFYB has been implicated in the transcriptional attenuation of Cdc25B by IER5 in AML [2]. A selective WDR5 degrader has been reported to repress AML in patient-derived mouse models [43]. Loss of one TCF12 allele is sufficient to bypass pre-TCR signaling, which is required for Notch1 gain-of-function mutations and progression to T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [36]. After excluding the four TFs of RRAS with known effects on AML, we chose MXI1 for further analysis.

To predict whether MXI1 can regulate RRAS transcription, ChIP-seq Data analysis at UCSC revealed that MXI1 has a significantly enhanced binding peak in the upstream promoter region of RRAS (chr19:49,640,144–49641143) (Fig S2D). The results of binding site prediction in JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) also indicated that MXI1 has many binding sites at the promoter fragment of RRAS (Fig S2E).

Discussion

Increasing efforts are being made to decipher the mechanisms underlying the CD8+ cell-mediated immune escape in AML [20, 29]. In this study, we have identified a novel FUBP3–MXI1–RRAS regulatory axis that modulates the immunity of CD8+ T cells in AMKL and identified their possible role in predicting the prognosis of patients with AML.

The presence of NRAS mutations has been detected in 11% of patients, while KRAS mutations have been identified in 5% of patients diagnosed with AML [28]. However, the available information regarding the physiological and pathological functions of the R-Ras subfamily, comprising R-RAS, R-RAS2/TC21, and M-RAS, is limited [34]. R-Ras87L-expressing cervical cancer cells have been reported to present decreased membrane expression of MHC class I molecules [25]. RRAS belongs to the Ras subfamily of small G-proteins, which are frequently implicated in cell growth and differentiation due to the ability to activate the MAPK signaling [19]. RRAS was also elevated in liver and lung tumors, in which it mainly regulated tumor-derived pericytes [15]. Even though Schmidt et al. reported activation of the MAPK signaling, particularly the ERK signaling, has been linked to immune escape in AML [30], the role of RRAS has not been clearly defined. Activation of the PD-1 (the cell surface receptor primarily found on tumor-infiltrating cells)/PD-L1 (type I transmembrane protein mainly expressed on tumor cells) axis serves to protect against excessive immune responses and autoimmune reactions, while cancer cells can exploit this mechanism by transmitting signals that upregulate PD-1 on the CD8+ T cells, effectively hindering T cell effector functions and permitting immune escape [10]. Interestingly, PD-L1 and CD86 may be upregulated via AP-1 TFs downstream of hyperactivated Ras/MEK/ERK signaling in AML cells, leading to a predominant exhausted T-cell phenotype [41]. In this study, we found that the knockdown of RRAS not only controlled the viability and apoptosis of AMKL cells but also contributed to compromised anti-tumor immunity of CD8+ cells by impairing the ERK-1/2 pathway. CD41 is a platelet-specific antigen and has been reported to be expressed by 71% of AMKL patients (46 out of 65) relative to non-AMKL patients [3]. Our in vivo findings revealed a lower proportion of CD41+ cells in the spleen tissues of tumor-bearing mice with RRAS knockdown, along with reduced tumor weight and volume.

We wondered whether the overexpression of RRAS in AMKL was related to a dysregulated transcription factor. Among the hub transcription factors screened using combined online algorithms, we noticed MXI1. MXI1 has been shown as a prognostic factor for glioma and a master transcription factor of mesenchymal bone marrow-derived macrophages [26, 39]. MXI1 interacted with Sin3A/HDAC co-repressor complex to govern gene suppression under hypoxia [5]. In the present study, we substantiated that MXI1 recruited the NCOR1/2/Sin3A/B/HDAC1 complex to the promoter region of RRAS to elicit transcriptional repression. The regulation of MXI1 on RRAS was further verified using in vitro assays, where overexpression of RRAS overturned the enhancing effects of MXI1 upregulation on CD8+ T cell immunity.

MXI1 has been identified as a substrate of E3 ligases and a target of protein arginine methyltransferase in different cancers, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma and lung cancer [11, 40, 44]. Considering that MXI1 expression was significantly downregulated in the GEO dataset we included, we switched to other transcriptional regulation that might lead to its dysregulation in AMKL, since its transcription has been demonstrated to be regulated by MYC in glioma [38]. RBPs are essential modulators of transcription and translation, often dysregulated in cancers, including AML [37]. FUBP3 may be associated with immune infiltration, and a positive correlation between immune cells (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages) and FUBP3 was observed in glioblastoma multiforme [17]. More importantly, Sharma et al. identified a direct interaction of the FUBP3 protein and the PRC2 complex in the regulation of ERK signaling via PAK1 in chronic myeloid leukemia [32]. Overexpression of FUBP3 exerted similar immunomodulatory properties as RRAS knockdown and MXI1 overexpression by blocking the ERK-1/2 signaling in AMKL cells, which led to impaired immune escape in vitro and in vivo.

However, it is worth noting that the present study has several limitations. First, the small sample size in animal studies may increase the possibility of errors. Therefore, further experimental validation is necessary. Secondly, it is acknowledged that FUBP3 and MXI1 likely interact with a more extensive array of mRNA targets that contribute to the disease biology. Subsequent studies that utilize RIP-seq for FUBP3 and ChIP-seq for MXI1 will be crucial to map the complete repertoire of regulated transcripts and elucidate their roles in AMKL pathogenesis. Thirdly, it must be acknowledged that the present study lacks validation of FUBP3, MXI1, and RRAS expression in primary AMKL patient samples. Such validation would serve to enhance the translational impact of the present findings. Subsequent research endeavors will concentrate on quantifying these factors across a more extensive cohort of AMKL specimens to substantiate their utility as clinical biomarkers and elucidate their prognostic significance.

In summary, our findings revealed that repressed FUBP3 in AMKL cells led to the enhanced expression of RRAS and MAPK-signaling by impairing the stability of transcription repressor MXI1. The activation of RRAS resulted in the phosphorylation of ERK-1/2, which subsequently weakened the immune function of CD8+ T cells (Fig. S4). In conclusion, our findings here provide new insights into immune escape mechanisms in a specific subtype of AML and suggest that the FUBP3/MXI1/RRAS/MAPK signaling may represent a promising target for AMKL treatment.
