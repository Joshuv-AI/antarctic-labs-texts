The role of impulsivity in injection-related risk behaviours among people who inject drugs in Montreal, Canada
Authors: Iuliia Makarenko, Nanor Minoyan, Stine Høj, Didier Jutras-Aswad, Julie Bruneau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpulsivity is a key contributor to substance use disorder, with previous research consistently demonstrating its role in the initiation, continuation and severity of drug use. Less is known about its association with injection-related risk behaviours, including injection equipment sharing and public injection, important drivers of HCV and HIV epidemics among people who inject drugs (PWID).MethodsData were drawn from HEPCO, an ongoing community-based cohort study of PWID in Montreal, Canada. We used the first observation from participants who reported injecting drugs in the past three months and were administered the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) (August 2017–March 2020). The Stop Signal Task was administered to measure “response inhibition,” i.e., an individual's ability to inhibit a prepotent or automatic response when presented with a stop signal or cue. Stop-signal reaction time (range: 50–1000 ms) was analyzed as a continuous indicator of impulsivity. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression were utilized to estimate associations between impulsivity and i) sharing injection equipment and ii) injection in public places, and identify other correlates of the study outcomes.ResultsOf 156 PWID included in the study, 26 (17%) and 42 (27%) reported sharing injection equipment and injecting in public, respectively. The majority of participants were men (85%) and the mean age was 45 years. In multivariable analysis, impulsivity was significantly associated with injecting in public places (aOR = 1.26 per each 100-unit increase, 95% CI = 1.01, 1.60). Other correlates of injecting in public were: age (0.93 [0.88, 0.97] and unstable housing (3.51 [1.43, 8.61]). There was no evidence for an association between impulsivity and injection equipment sharing (0.85 [0.65, 1.10]).ConclusionThis study underscores the importance of impulsivity in public injection, a behaviour strongly associated with HIV and HCV transmission among PWID. These findings highlight the need for public health interventions that both mitigate impulsivity, such as behavioural or pharmacological therapies, and accommodate it through expanding low-threshold harm-reduction services, including access to supervised consumption sites and sterile injecting equipment.Keywords: Impulsivity; Injection risk behaviours, people who inject drugs; Sharing injection equipment; Injection in public places

Introduction

Injection drug use has been recognized for its ongoing impact on the transmission of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) [1]. Sharing of contaminated syringes and other injection material is a key driver of these two epidemics among PWID in Canada and worldwide [2–4]. While syringe sharing represents an individual-level behaviour contributing to these epidemics, it is also shaped by the environment where drug injecting takes place. Public injecting, defined as the injection of drugs in an area accessible to the general public (e.g. parks, streets, public toilets, abandoned buildings, etc.), has been associated with a wide range of legal, social, and health-related harms among PWID [5–8]. The risk environment framework highlights how the intersection of factors at the individual level with factors in one’s physical and social circle can exacerbate drug-related harms and serves as a valuable foundation for designing effective harm reduction interventions [9, 10].

Factors commonly examined to explain individual variation in risky injection behaviours have included characteristics such as knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, behavioural patterns, and access to harm reduction services as predictors [11–14]. However, the reasons why PWID engage in risky behaviours extend beyond rational decisions or the availability of harm reduction services. Psychological traits, such as impulsivity, may increase propensity for risky drug use behaviours [15–19], particularly in settings where individuals face limited opportunities to engage in safer behaviours. Notably, impulsivity, the tendency to act without sufficient consideration of potential consequences, is recognized as a central contributor to drug use [20–22]. Typically characterized as an inability to inhibit behavioural impulses and thoughts [23] and making up one’s mind quickly [24], impulsivity and closely related behaviours are key diagnostic criteria for several major psychiatric disorders [25–32], including substance-use disorders [28, 29, 33–36]. For PWID, impulsivity can manifest as an urgent need to inject as soon as the impulse arises, regardless of the appropriateness of the setting, or injection without adopting measures to protect oneself against blood-borne virus acquisition (e.g. by sharing injection equipment) [15]. Public places where drugs are readily accessible and where drug use is normalized may be particularly high-risk sites for impulsive individuals, who are expected to have impaired responses to environmental cues abundant in these environments [37].

Although few studies have assessed impulsivity among PWID [15], initiation of injection drug use, greater methamphetamine use severity [38], as well as early onset of opioid dependence [39], among other outcomes, have been associated with higher impulsivity in populations of people who use drugs. Worse inhibition control, an aspect of impulsivity, has further been related to polysubstance use with ketamine in a study of methamphetamine users [40]. In a sample of heroin-dependent individuals, impulsivity was found to be associated with high-risk behaviours including escalating heroin consumption, injecting heroin, hazardous drinking, low treatment-seeking and risky sexual behaviour [41]. Finally, in a sample of people who use drugs (PWUD), impulsivity was an independent predictor of HCV positivity [42]. Overall, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of impulsivity in shaping drug use patterns, high-risk behaviours, and health outcomes among PWUD. However, the conceptualization and measurement of impulsivity have varied widely across studies.

In psychological research, impulsivity can be conceptualized as comprising both trait dimensions, which capture relatively stable tendencies toward rash action, and state dimensions influenced by specific situations or environmental conditions [43]. While trait impulsivity reflects stable patterns that may predispose individuals to riskier decision-making, state impulsivity may be heightened in specific contexts, such as acute drug craving, withdrawal, and intoxication, which can directly lead to high-risk injection behaviours. As such, state impulsivity may be particularly relevant for understanding injection-related risk behaviours, as it captures situational triggers that shape momentary decision-making [44]. Trait and state impulsivity dimensions are typically measured with self-reported instruments and behavioural tasks, respectively [45]. This study aimed to estimate associations between impulsivity, measured as inhibitory control using a behavioural task, and two injection-related risk behaviours – sharing injection equipment and injecting in public places – while adjusting for socio-demographic and substance-use related characteristics. We hypothesized that PWID with a higher level of impulsivity would be more likely to engage in injection-related risk behaviours.

Methods

Study participants

The study sample was drawn from the Hepatitis Cohort (HEPCO), a prospective open cohort of PWID established in 2004 in Montréal, Canada, to investigate the natural history of HCV infection and factors associated with HCV incidence; secondary objectives are to estimate HIV incidence rates and examine access to care for HCV infection [46]. Potential participants for this convenience sample are recruited via i) referrals from community-based programs adjusted to the needs of PWID, ii) Addiction Medicine Clinic of the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM), and iii) word-of-mouth. Follow-up is scheduled at 3-month intervals. To be eligible for recruitment into the study, individuals are required to be active PWID (i.e. to have injected drugs within the previous six months) and to be 18 years of age or older.

At baseline and at each follow-up visit, data are collected using a behavioural questionnaire administered by interviewers in a private face-to-face setting. The questionnaire elicited information on socio-demographic characteristics, drug use patterns and related behaviours, and harm reduction service utilization. Following completion of the questionnaire, venous blood samples are drawn from all participants by a trained nurse for HIV and HCV infection detection. In 2017, the HEPCO objectives were extended to examine the dynamics of mental health and related risk behaviours among PWID, with implications for HIV and HCV prevention. To this end, an extended mental health section with cognitive measurement was added to the questionnaire.

For the purpose of this study, we used the first observation from participants who completed a new section on mental health and cognition that included the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) along with the HEPCO questionnaire between August 2017 and March 2020. Out of 372 participants who completed at least one HEPCO questionnaire between August 2017 and March 2020, 271 respondents additionally completed the baseline CANTAB testing. Given that drug injection fluctuates over time among HEPCO participants, this study included the 156 CANTAB participants who reported injecting drugs in the past 3 months.

Measures

Outcome measures

This study included two primary outcomes. First, sharing injection equipment was defined as borrowing injection material (syringes and/or other paraphernalia) previously or concomitantly used by another person. Second, injecting in public places was defined as injecting drugs at least once in a public place that could include public restrooms/bars/restaurants, streets (alley/entrance way), parks or saunas. Both outcome variables were binary measures relating to the past three-month time period.

Exposure variable

Impulsivity was measured with the CANTAB, a computer-based cognitive assessment system consisting of a battery of neuropsychological tests, administered to subjects using a touch screen computer [47]. Specifically, we utilized the Stop Signal Task (SST) [48], a widely used neuropsychological test that measures response inhibition, a component of executive function. Numerous studies have administered the SST to various groups, including people with substance use disorders [49]. The SST uses a staircase procedure to estimate the stop-signal reaction time, providing an objective measure of an individual’s ability to inhibit an automatic motor response (i.e. their impulse control). This dimension of impulsivity is commonly referred to as motor impulsivity. Although behavioural tasks, including the SST, primarily measure state, or context-dependent, impulsivity in a controlled laboratory setting, impaired inhibition on this task has been linked to real-life risk behaviours and an individual’s tendency to react impulsively in high-risk situations [50, 51].

We used the stop-signal reaction time (SSRT), estimated by subtracting the stop-signal delay (i.e. the time in milliseconds between the go stimulus and the stop signal) from the reaction time on go trials. Higher SSRT values indicate poorer impulse control (range: 50–1000 ms). The SSRT was analyzed as a continuous variable; the results were reported for a 100-unit increase in the impulsivity score (e.g., a score rising from 200 to 300).

Potential covariate measures

The selection of potential covariates listed below was guided by known determinants from the literature [52–54], given the data that were collected within this study.

Socio-demographic characteristics were evaluated as potential covariates and included age, gender, education, income amount, and housing status. Age (years) and self-identified gender (man/woman) were assessed in continuous and binary forms, respectively; no participants in our sample identified themselves as non-binary or other genders. Education was defined as having completed a high school degree (yes/no). Income amount was assessed in continuous form as average monthly income in the past three months (Canadian dollars). As with previous studies [46], unstable housing (yes/no), assessed in reference to the past three months, was defined as living mostly on the street, in shelters or apartment-hotels rented on a monthly basis, indicating rapid turnover compared to typical, 12-month rent-lease accommodations in Montreal.

Drug use patterns were considered as the main potential covariates and included age at initiation of any drug use and injection drug use (years), types of drugs injected, frequency of drug injection, being in opioid agonist treatment (OAT), and utilization of needle/syringe exchange services. The type of drugs injected was categorized as injecting opiates only, stimulants only, or both opiates and stimulants in the past three months. Frequent drug injection was defined as > 45 days vs < = 45 days of injection in the past three months. Being in OAT (yes/no) was defined as receiving methadone or buprenorphine/naloxone at the time of the study visit. Utilization of needle/syringe exchange services (yes/no) was defined as obtaining sterile syringes from any of the available sources at least once in the past three months.

Health characteristics included HIV and HCV-antibody status (negative/positive), assessed via blood samples drawn at the study visit, and severe psychological distress in the past month. Psychological distress was measured with the 10-item Kessler Scale (K10), and severe distress was defined as a total score of 30/50 or higher [55].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample overall and stratified by whether or not participants reported each of the outcomes. Bivariate associations between explanatory and outcome variables were tested using Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, and T-tests for continuous variables. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate associations between impulsivity and each outcome of interest, considered separately. Covariates were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model if they demonstrated a marginal association with the outcome variables in univariate analyses (p < 0.25) [56]. Age and gender were included in the multivariable analysis regardless of their significance in the univariate models. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were estimated as measures of association.

Additionally, due to the large number of the HEPCO participants (N = 101) who refused to complete the CANTAB questionnaire, we conducted a supplementary analysis to compare basic demographic characteristics (age, gender) and risky drug-use behaviours (injecting drugs in the past three months, sharing injection behaviour and injecting in public places) between HEPCO participants who completed the CANTAB questionnaire and those who did not.

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Sample characteristics

Characteristics of study participants are presented in Table 1, overall and stratified by reporting of the outcomes (sharing injection equipment and injecting in public places). Of 156 individuals included in the study, 26 (17%) and 42 (27%) reported sharing injection equipment and injecting in public places, respectively. Participants who shared injection equipment were more likely to be women (42%) compared with those who did not (15%) (p = 0.0005). Participants who injected in public places were younger (mean age = 39 vs. 46 years old) (p < 0.0001), more likely to be unstably housed (43% vs. 20%) (p = 0.0080), and to report frequent drug injection (50% vs. 29%) (p = 0.0221).

Multivariable associations between outcomes and impulsivity

Tables 2 and 3 present univariate and multivariable associations between impulsivity and each of the outcomes. The association between impulsivity and sharing injection equipment was not statistically significant in univariate analysis (OR = 0.82, 95% CI = [0.64, 1.04]) or after controlling for socio-demographic characteristics (aOR = 0.85 [0.65, 1.10]) (Table 2). Injecting in public places was not associated with impulsivity in the univariate analysis (OR = 1.12 [0.93, 1.37]). However, in the multivariable analysis, after adjusting for socio-demographic, drug use- and health-related characteristics, greater impulsivity was associated with public injection (aOR = 1.26 [1.01, 1.60] for a 100-unit increment in SST SSRT). Although HIV status met the criteria for inclusion in the final model, it was dropped as no participants who injected in public were HIV-positive, leading to unstable OR/CI estimates.

On average, non-CANTAB participants were younger (mean age = 43 vs. 46 years old) and more likely to inject drugs in the past three months (78% vs. 57%). Among those who reported injecting drugs in the past three months, non-CANTAB participants were more likely to share injection equipment (28% vs. 16%) and inject drugs in public places (52% vs. 27%).

Discussion

Our study is one of the few to investigate how impulsivity relates to injection-related risk behaviours, including injection equipment sharing, and the first to explore its association with injecting in public places. In this study, a motor dimension of impulsivity was measured using a behavioural task suited to measure state (vs. trait) impulsivity. While trait impulsivity is a stable personality feature that reflects a general tendency to act impulsively, state impulsivity is more situational and may vary based on internal or external factors [43]. Drug use-related risky behaviours often occur in high-impulsivity states such as emotional distress, drug intoxication, craving, or social pressure – not necessarily due to someone’s impulsive trait, but because, in a specific situation, their impulse control fails [50].

While previous research has linked impulsivity to substance use disorder and drug use related outcomes, this study suggests that PWID who exhibit impaired inhibitory control are more likely to inject in public places, which could increase their vulnerability to rushed injection, non-hygienic injection practices [57–62], sex-related risk behaviours [63], infectious disease transmission and non-fatal overdose [8, 63, 64]. In our study, public injecting was found to be a common practice among PWID, with almost one-third of participants reporting injecting in public places in the past three months. Public injecting adds an additional layer of complexity and risk for PWID – both through the experience itself and through its close association with adverse social circumstances such as homelessness [63]. Impulsivity was associated with injecting in public places after controlling for unstable housing and other demographic, drug use and health-related characteristics. These results contribute to our understanding of how impulsivity may be related to the choice of drug injection environment and thereby potentially increase the risk of adverse outcomes. PWID with a higher level of state impulsivity may be more likely to inject in public places due to their social vulnerability and a tendency to prioritize immediate rewards from using drugs at the expense of seeking a safer environment.

Conversely, impulsivity was not associated with sharing injection equipment in our study. Previously, only a few studies assessed direct measures of impulsivity in relation to injection sharing behaviours. One study found that PWID scoring high on a self-reported measure of impulsivity were at greater risk of receptive syringe sharing [15]. Another found that participants who agreed to share a needle in a hypothetical scenario discounted delayed money more steeply (indicating higher decision-making impulsivity) than did the non-sharing group, but did not differ in impulsivity measured with a self-reported questionnaire [65]. The inconsistency in these findings, both compared to each other and to our results, might be explained by a multitude of factors ranging from differences in context (e.g., availability of clean injecting equipment, harm reduction policies), study populations and sample sizes, measurement tools for impulsivity, timing of measures and data analysis approaches. Sharing behaviours likely involve complex cognitive processes, and a thorough assessment of other dimensions of impulsivity and cognitive functions – such as working memory, cognitive flexibility, and social cognition – that may contribute to these behaviours should be undertaken. This approach may help clarify whether impairments or strengths in specific cognitive functions underlie individual differences in injection sharing behaviours.

Our results suggest that personality-targeted interventions and services adapted to impulsive PWID may be useful for reducing drug-related risks. To effectively address these needs, public health services should be easily accessible for impulsive individuals who may not adhere to rigid schedules. These individuals may benefit more from programs that balance structure and flexibility, providing clear guidelines and support while allowing for individual differences. Drug consumption rooms (DCRs) and supervised injecting sites (SISs) offer a complementary approach to well-established harm reduction services – such as OAT and needle/syringe programs (NSP) [66, 67] – by providing a supportive environment for using substances and connecting individuals with vital services [68]. It is important to note that access to and utilization of SISs, which have been shown to directly reduce public injecting [69], could not be assessed in our study. In Montreal, there have been four SISs implemented since 2017, with on average 2500 visits per month recorded across all sites, although this coverage is probably not enough to provide access to all PWID in need of such services. Additionally, when implementing harm reduction strategies for individuals with elevated impulsivity, efforts should focus on supporting the development of skills to effectively engage with these services in the context of impulsive decision-making. Cognitive and behavioural interventions may be particularly beneficial in facilitating this engagement [70]. Other strategies to mitigate impulsivity may include pharmacological approaches such as buprenorphine treatment [71, 72] or detoxifying therapies [73].

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the convenience sample we used in our study might not accurately reflect all PWID in Montreal. Second, a small sample size could lead to insufficient statistical power to detect significant effects. Third, our study may be a subject to a selection bias, resulting in an underestimated prevalence of high-risk injection behaviours and their association with impulsivity in our findings compared to the broader population of PWID in Montreal, as our sample included a lower-risk population compared to HEPCO participants who did not complete the CANTAB testing. This limitation also raises questions about the suitability of the CANTAB for high-risk PWID. The CANTAB requires participants to complete a lengthy computerized assessment in a stable setting, which can be challenging for individuals experiencing acute withdrawal, intoxication, or unstable housing, and may have discouraged participation among those at highest risk. Fourth, we assessed only one aspect of impulsivity measured with a behavioural task (referring to state impulsivity) in laboratory settings with researchers around, while measuring both state and trait impulsivity could be essential for understanding why PWID engage in risky behaviours. In this study, we analyzed only impulsive measures, while other cognitive functions may contribute to risky behaviours in that population and should also be assessed. A further limitation of our study is the temporal mismatch between the measurement of state impulsivity assessed once at the time of the interview and the outcomes, which were self-reported for the previous three months. Because state impulsivity can fluctuate in response to situational factors such as intoxication or withdrawal, a single-point measure may not fully capture participants’ impulsivity level during the entire recall period. This could have diminished the observed associations between impulsivity and risky injecting behaviours. Finally, a cross-sectional study design precludes causal inference, as temporal ordering cannot be established between the examined outcome and exposure variables.

Conclusions

This study highlights the role of impulsivity as a potential factor shaping the environments in which injection drug use occurs, offering novel evidence that higher impulsivity is associated with public injecting, a behaviour strongly linked to increased HIV and HCV transmission risk. These findings extend the current understanding of risk environments by identifying impulsivity as a behavioural dimension that may limit the effectiveness of existing harm-reduction efforts. While SISs and other harm-reduction interventions are known to reduce public injecting and associated harms, our results suggest that tailoring these services and healthcare provision to the needs of more impulsive PWID could further enhance their reach and impact. Integrating behavioural support to help manage impulsivity, alongside existing harm-reduction and healthcare services, may also help address individual vulnerabilities driving risky injection practices.
