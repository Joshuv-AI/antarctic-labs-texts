Decoupling of kinetic and kinematic asymmetry in elite sculling: catch slip synchronization is more closely associated with boat speed than force balance
Authors: Shuwei Chen, Yingping Zhang, Xiaohua Li, Yang Liu, Sen Yang, Xiubing Zhang, Weibao Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the phase-dependent behavior of kinematic and kinetic asymmetry during an on-water incremental 2000-m test and to determine which asymmetry components were independently associated with boat speed in elite scullers.MethodsTwelve elite male single scullers completed a continuous 2000 m on-water incremental test. Multiple strokes were recorded in each of seven analyzed stroke-rate phases; left and right variables were averaged within each rower × phase block and converted to asymmetry indices (AI), yielding 84 phase-level observations. Phase effects were evaluated with one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, latent structure with principal component analysis (PCA), exploratory bivariate associations with Spearman’s rho, and independent correlates of speed with a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) including a random intercept for subject.ResultsKinetic asymmetry exhibited large phase effects: Work per Stroke AI and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI increased from SR 20 to SR 39 (both p < 0.001; ηp² ≥ 0.80) before decreasing in the final sprint. Power per Stroke AI was not entered as an independent asymmetry variable because it was algebraically equivalent to Work per Stroke AI at the phase level. By contrast, macro-kinematic asymmetry remained low overall. Slip variables were numerically much larger and more variable than the macro-kinematic AIs. PCA identified a dominant kinetic/intensity dimension and separate slip-related dimensions. In the LMM (marginal R² = 0.36), Catch Slip AI was the only significant fixed effect associated with boat speed (β = −0.314, p = 0.018), whereas Work per Stroke AI was not (β = 0.236, p = 0.106).ConclusionsIn elite sculling, force-related asymmetry appears to behave mainly as an intensity-linked correlate rather than an independent limiter of speed. Introduction

Competitive rowing performance depends on the interplay between physiological parameters and biomechanical efficiency [1, 2]. Physiological parameters set the upper limit for energy production, whereas biomechanical factors determine how effectively that energy is translated into boat velocity. In sculling, biomechanical and hydrodynamic models suggest that bilateral asymmetries can generate a net yaw moment (rotation about the vertical axis), causing heading deviations and increased hydrodynamic resistance, thereby reducing propulsion efficiency [3–5]. Despite the theoretical imperative for symmetry, functional asymmetry is ubiquitous in human movement—including rowing sculling events—due to hemispheric laterality and neuromuscular dominance [6–8].

A critical gap exists in understanding how asymmetry evolves across varying stroke rates. During a 2000 m race, stroke rates escalate from race pace (~ 32 spm) to sprinting (> 40 spm), creating a “temporal deficit” that may exacerbate neuromuscular breakdown and lead to a decoupling of technique [9, 10]. While rowers may maintain “macro-symmetry” (e.g., total stroke angle), latent “micro-asymmetry” in blade-water interaction timing—specifically catch and release slip—may diverge at high intensities [11–13].

Furthermore, the literature often treats asymmetry as a monolithic construct, failing to distinguish between the two fundamental domains of biomechanical analysis: kinetics and kinematics [14, 15]. Kinetics concerns the forces and mechanical power that determine and modify motion, whereas kinematics describes the motion itself, such as stroke range and the timing of the catch and finish, without direct reference to its causes [16]. Biomechanical principles suggest that force-related asymmetry may be partly compensated for, whereas timing errors at the catch and finish can interrupt propulsive continuity and therefore carry a larger mechanical cost [13, 17]. To date, few studies have quantified these distinct asymmetry domains under on-water conditions while also controlling appropriately for repeated observations within the same rower.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were: (1) to characterize the evolution of kinematic and kinetic asymmetry across the incremental test; and (2) to determine, using a linear mixed-effects model, which asymmetry modality was independently associated with boat velocity. We hypothesized that force-related asymmetry would vary with stroke-rate intensity, whereas asymmetry in blade-water connection timing at the catch and finish would show a stronger negative association with boat speed.

Methods

Participants

Twelve elite male single scull rowers volunteered to participate in this study. The rowers competed at the national and international levels. Their training status was reflected by a mean.

2000 m indoor ergometer personal best of 358.2 ± 4.1 s, recorded during standardized team testing on a Concept2 indoor rower (Model D, Concept2 Inc., Morrisville, VT, USA) with a standardized drag factor of approximately 130. All participants had at least 6 years of competitive rowing experience and were free from musculoskeletal injury for at least 6 months before testing.

Detailed anthropometric and training characteristics are presented in Table 1. Prior to data collection, all participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Capital University of Physical Education and Sports (No. 2025A137) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A familiarization session with the instrumented boat was completed 24 h before testing.

Experimental protocol

Data collection was conducted on a calm, standard 2000 m regatta course. Environmental conditions were monitored throughout testing: wind speed remained < 1 m·s⁻1 (handheld anemometer, Skywatch, Switzerland) and water current was < 0.05 m·s⁻1, which was considered biomechanically negligible for the present analysis. Participants used their personal single sculls and carbon-fiber oars (Concept2, USA), with rigging settings standardized to their competition preferences.

Following a 60-minute standardized warm-up consisting of low-intensity rowing and technical drills, participants completed a continuous 2000 m on-water incremental test with stepwise increases in target stroke rate (Fig. 1). The initial start sequence was recorded but excluded from the statistical analysis. The analyzed portion of the protocol comprised seven consecutive steady-state phases, hereafter labelled SR 20, SR 24, SR 28, SR 32, SR 36, SR 39, and Sprint according to the achieved mean stroke rates reported in Table 2. Real-time stroke-rate feedback was provided via a stroke coach unit (Nielsen-Kellerman, USA), and rowers were instructed to remain within ± 1 spm of the target cadence in each phase.

Instrumentation and data acquisition

Biomechanical variables were quantified using a portable telemetry system (ZkRowVer 2.0, CAS Institute of Intelligent Machines, China). The system comprised instrumented oarlocks that measured oar-to-pin reaction force (range 0–3000 N; accuracy 0.5% full scale) and oar angle relative to the boat (range − 90° to + 90°; accuracy 0.1°) on both sides, together with a boat movement unit combining an inertial measurement unit and 10-Hz GPS for boat velocity, acceleration, and stroke rate. Signals were sampled at 100 Hz and transmitted wirelessly to a coach tablet for storage. System calibration, including zero-point offset for force and angle sensors, was performed immediately before the boat was placed in the water.

Measurement quality and calibration

Prior to each trial, force and angle channels were zeroed on both sides and sensor synchronization was checked. Calibration offsets were verified immediately before water entry following the manufacturer’s procedures. Although a comprehensive independent peer-reviewed validation study of all derived outcomes from the ZkRowVer 2.0 platform is not yet available, validation work on comparable on-water instrumentation systems has demonstrated acceptable agreement for force and angle measurements under controlled conditions [18]. Accordingly, absolute values—especially force and power—may be slightly under- or overestimated. However, because the primary outcomes in the present study were simultaneous left-right asymmetry indices derived within the same measurement system, they are less sensitive to common-mode calibration bias than raw absolute values.

Data processing and variable definition

The initial start sequence was excluded because it represents a transient acceleration period characterized by atypical partial-slide strokes and larger velocity fluctuations than the subsequent cyclic phases. Raw data were low-pass filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth filter with an 8-Hz cut-off. A stroke cycle was defined from catch (minimal oar angle) to the subsequent catch. The drive phase was defined as the interval from catch to finish (maximal oar angle).

Phase assignment and aggregation are central to the statistical structure of the dataset. Multiple strokes were recorded within each analyzed phase for each rower. Individual strokes were assigned to one of the seven phases according to the predefined order of the incremental protocol and the corresponding stroke-rate block; under the predefined extraction protocol, the initial start sequence and the post-finish deceleration period were excluded before phase-level summaries were created. For this final revision, the unsegmented continuous stroke-by-stroke telemetry export could not be retrieved from the project archive; therefore, terminal-phase rechecking was restricted to the preserved rower × phase analysis matrix rather than a direct sequential raw-stroke boundary audit. The preserved rower × phase analysis dataset was rechecked for terminal-phase plausibility before reanalysis. This check identified one SR 39 stroke-angle truncation artifact in a single rower-phase summary: the left and right Stroke Angle values were recorded as 12.0° and 11.6°, which were incompatible with that observation’s Work per Stroke and Total Mechanical Power and with adjacent phases. The error pattern was treated as a value-level export/truncation artifact and corrected to 102.0° and 101.6° before all analyses were rerun. No post-hoc reclassification of the 84 rower-phase observations was performed. The terminal-phase plausibility audit was based on the preserved rower × phase matrix and examined total mechanical power, total Work per Stroke, and paired SR 39-to-Sprint consistency (Additional file 1: Table S4). For each rower × phase block, left and right values were averaged across the available strokes to obtain one phase-level summary per side. Asymmetry indices were then calculated from these phase-level left/right means. Consequently, the unit of analysis for PCA, correlation analysis, and the LMM was the rower-phase observation (12 rowers × 7 phases = 84 observations), whereas repeated-measures ANOVA treated phase as the within-subject factor.

To quantify bilateral discrepancy, the Asymmetry Index (AI) [19] was calculated as , where L and R represent the left and right values, respectively. The absolute value of the AI was used to quantify the magnitude of the bilateral mismatch irrespective of direction. This choice was guided by the hydrodynamic question of interest: from the perspective of boat motion, any left-right mismatch can generate a yaw moment and associated resistance, regardless of whether the dominant side is port or starboard [3, 4].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed in R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of the phase-level summaries was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Because several asymmetry variables were non-normally distributed, exploratory bivariate associations were assessed using Spearman’s rho.

Phase effects. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to test whether asymmetry indices changed across the seven analyzed phases. When a main effect of phase was significant, Holm-adjusted pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed. Partial eta squared (ηp²) was reported as the ANOVA effect size.

Latent structure. Exploratory principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on z-standardized non-redundant asymmetry indices; Power per Stroke AI was omitted because it was algebraically identical to Work per Stroke AI in the phase-level dataset. Components with eigenvalues > 1 were retained.

Results

Biomechanical performance across phases

As expected, mean boat speed increased from 4.06 ± 0.09 m·s⁻1 at SR 20 to 5.07 ± 0.11 m·s⁻1 at SR 36, dipped slightly at SR 39 (4.99 ± 0.10 m·s⁻1), and then returned to the SR 36 level in Sprint (5.07 ± 0.12 m·s⁻1; Table 2). Total mechanical power increased nearly twofold across the test, from 143.7 ± 9.5 W at SR 20 to 281.1 ± 17.5 W in the final sprint. After correction of the SR 39 value-level stroke-angle truncation artifact identified in the archived phase-level dataset, the SR 39 stroke-angle descriptor was 98.7 ± 2.3°, which was consistent with the surrounding high-rate phases and with the reported total mechanical power. Together, these data indicate that the higher-speed phases reflected substantially greater propulsive demand rather than a change in cadence alone. The terminal phase-level plausibility audit further showed that Sprint Total Mechanical Power was higher than SR 39 in all 12 rowers, with a group-level coefficient of variation similar to SR 39 (6.2% vs. 6.3%), and that total Work per Stroke was maintained from SR 39 to Sprint (417.7 ± 27.7 J vs. 418.2 ± 27.4 J; Additional file 1: Table S4). These checks support the absence of substantial or systematic post-finish low-power contamination at the phase-summary level, while not substituting for raw stroke-level boundary modelling.

Decoupling of kinetic and kinematic asymmetry

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed differentiated phase effects across asymmetry domains (Table 3；Fig. 2). Pull Time AI showed no phase effect (p = 0.454), and Stroke Angle AI remained small overall (< 3.5%) despite a modest main effect of phase (F6,66 = 2.59, p = 0.026, ηp² = 0.19). Effective Angle Ratio AI also showed a small phase effect (F6,66 = 2.45, p = 0.034, ηp² = 0.18). By contrast, kinetic asymmetry changed markedly across phases. Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI and Work per Stroke AI demonstrated large phase effects (F6,66 = 51.53 and 43.01, respectively; both p < 0.001; ηp² = 0.82 and 0.80). Power per Stroke AI was not analyzed as a separate asymmetry outcome because it was algebraically equivalent to Work per Stroke AI under the phase-level derivation. Holm-adjusted post-hoc comparisons showed that Work per Stroke AI was higher in the late high-rate phases (SR 36–SR 39) than in the lower-rate phases (SR 20–SR 24; adjusted p < 0.001). Work per Stroke AI increased from 2.16 ± 1.14% at SR 20 to 9.27 ± 1.42% at SR 39, before decreasing in the final sprint (2.33 ± 1.34%).

Latent structure of asymmetry

Exploratory PCA was recomputed after excluding the algebraically redundant Power per Stroke AI. Four components with eigenvalues > 1 explained 63.8% of the total variance (Table 4; Fig. 3). PC1 (21.7%) loaded strongly on Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI (0.94) and Work per Stroke AI (0.91), representing a kinetic/intensity dimension. PC2 (16.1%) was characterized by Catch Slip AI (0.74) and Peak Force AI (0.72), PC3 (13.5%) by Release Slip AI (0.84) and Pull Time AI (0.66), and PC4 (12.6%) by Stroke Angle AI (− 0.70), Effective Angle Ratio AI (0.64), and Mean Force AI (0.60). Consistent with this structure, Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 4) showed positive correlations between boat speed and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI (ρ = 0.47, p < 0.001) and Work per Stroke AI (ρ = 0.44, p < 0.001). Release Slip AI showed a modest negative correlation with boat speed (ρ = −0.23, p = 0.037). PC1 scores correlated positively with boat speed (ρ = 0.49, p < 0.001), whereas PC2 and PC3 did not (all p > 0.50).

Predictors of boat velocity

The primary inferential model was the LMM with a random intercept for subject. The fixed effects explained 36% of the variance in boat speed (marginal R² = 0.36). Catch Slip AI was the only significant fixed effect (β = −0.314, SE = 0.126, t = − 2.497, p = 0.018), indicating that larger bilateral mismatch at the catch was associated with lower boat speed (Table 5; Fig. 5). Release Slip AI showed a negative but non-significant estimate (β = −0.238, p = 0.105). None of the kinetic AI variables were independently associated with boat speed after the repeated-measures structure and covariation among predictors were accounted for (Peak Force AI, Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI, and Work per Stroke AI: all p ≥ 0.106).

Discussion

The phenomenon of decoupling: physiological cost vs. technical stability

The main finding of this study is that asymmetry in elite sculling is not a unitary phenomenon. Gross macro-kinematic symmetry was preserved across the incremental test, whereas force-related asymmetry changed markedly across phases. Pull Time AI remained approximately 1.6–2.1%, and Stroke Angle AI stayed low overall despite a modest phase effect. In contrast, Work per Stroke AI and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI showed large phase effects. This pattern suggests that elite scullers prioritize preserving the visible geometry and rhythm of the stroke even when the internal distribution of force becomes less balanced.

This pattern is consistent with compensatory control [20]. In the present context, visual rhythm refers to the perceived bilateral synchrony of the oars from stroke to stroke, whereas spatial consistency refers to the preservation of similar blade trajectories and angular ranges on both sides. Elite rowers appear able to protect these visible features of the stroke while allowing more flexibility in how force is distributed between sides.

Existing rowing literature supports the plausibility of such compensation. Ergometer studies have shown that rowers can redistribute lower-limb kinematics and foot forces asymmetrically while preserving the global movement pattern [20, 21]. A recent case report in sweep rowers also highlighted postural compensation under asymmetrical boat demands, although such ergometer findings should be interpreted as indirect rather than direct evidence of on-water bilateral sculling asymmetry [22]. Taken together, these observations support the idea that skilled rowers use compensatory neuromuscular adjustments to maintain a stable overall stroke pattern.

As stroke rate increases, rowers also face a growing temporal deficit, defined here as the progressive reduction in the time available both to produce force and to process sensory feedback. Under these conditions, control may shift away from slower feedback corrections toward more feed-forward execution. This may help explain why macro-kinematic symmetry is preserved while force-related asymmetry becomes more variable across the later phases [23, 24].

Importantly, the kinematic domain was itself non-uniform. Catch and release slip AI were far larger in magnitude than Pull Time AI or Stroke Angle AI. Thus, elite rowers appear to protect gross stroke geometry more tightly than the micro-timing of the blade-water connection.

The speed paradox: why kinetic asymmetry correlates with velocity

Our descriptive analyses revealed positive associations between boat speed and several kinetic asymmetry indices. This should not be interpreted as force asymmetry improving performance. Rather, those variables clustered in the faster, higher-power phases and therefore behaved primarily as intensity-linked correlates. The redundant Power per Stroke AI was removed from inferential analyses, and the retained Work per Stroke AI showed the same descriptive intensity-linked pattern. The near doubling of total mechanical power across the test supports this interpretation.

The phase pattern is consistent with real 2000-m race profiles, in which stroke rate and mechanical demand increase dynamically toward the latter part of the race [25]. Under these demands, rowers may prioritize total propulsive output over perfect bilateral force balance.

Notably, mean boat speed was not strictly monotonic: it reached 5.07 ± 0.11 m·s⁻1 at SR 36, dipped slightly to 4.99 ± 0.10 m·s⁻1 at SR 39 despite higher mechanical power, and then returned to 5.07 ± 0.12 m·s⁻1 in the final sprint. After the phase-level consistency check, the corrected SR 39 stroke angle (98.7 ± 2.3°) was compatible with the mean force, stroke rate, and total mechanical power reported for the same phase. The final sprint also showed the highest total mechanical power but lower Work per Stroke AI and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI than SR 39. The supplementary post-hoc matrix shows that the SR 36–Sprint and SR 39–Sprint contrasts for Work per Stroke AI and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI were among the largest significant phase contrasts (Additional file 1: Table S2). The terminal phase-level audit showed peak Sprint power and maintained per-stroke work rather than a low-power terminal segment (Additional file 1: Table S4); therefore, substantial or systematic post-finish deceleration contamination is unlikely at the phase-summary level, although this cannot be considered a direct raw stroke-level verification. Because the pattern was confined to force–angle-derived variables, we interpret it as a descriptive terminal force-curve convergence rather than evidence that sprinting inherently restores symmetry. This phase-specific dissociation further argues against a facilitative role of force asymmetry and helps explain why kinetic AI lost significance in the multivariable LMM. In other words, force-related asymmetry may accompany hard rowing, but it is not required for maximal speed.

This interpretation should not be reduced to the small increase in achieved stroke rate from SR 39 to Sprint. A plausible, but inferential, explanation is that the final Sprint represents an all-out terminal task context rather than a continuation of the preceding high-rate steady phase. In a known endpoint context, rowers may increase feed-forward drive and reduce tolerance for side-to-side differences in integrated propulsive work to maximize speed [23, 25]. Such a terminal strategy could make bilateral Work per Stroke and the high-force portion of the force–angle curve converge, while leaving other dimensions less affected. We did not directly measure neural drive, electromyography, or single-stroke force-curve transitions; thus, this mechanism is presented as a plausible interpretation rather than causal proof.

The selectivity of the Sprint pattern is biomechanically informative. Work per Stroke AI and Drive-angle span > 70% Peak Force AI both summarize integrated force–angle behavior over the drive, whereas Peak Force AI and Mean Force AI reflect point or average force magnitudes, and Catch Slip AI reflects the timing of effective blade–water connection. At > 40 spm, catch timing remains constrained by the temporal deficit of blade placement and sensory correction, whereas integrated force output can converge under terminal all-out task demands. This explains why the Sprint decrease was limited to force–angle-derived kinetic variables and did not require a parallel collapse of all other AIs.

The relative stability of macro-kinematic symmetry suggests that it may function as technical scaffolding. When the global oar trajectory remains synchronized, rowers may tolerate transient or phase-specific force imbalances without immediately destabilizing boat motion. By contrast, large errors in the timing of blade-water engagement are less easily absorbed within the stroke cycle.

The mechanical brake: catch slip AI as a key limiting factor

In the repeated-measures-aware LMM, Catch Slip AI was the only significant fixed effect. By comparison, Release Slip AI showed a statistically significant but modest negative Spearman correlation with boat speed (ρ = −0.23, p = 0.037), yet its estimate in the multivariable LMM was not statistically significant (β = −0.238, p = 0.105). This pattern suggests that micro-timing errors at the catch may be especially consequential because they disturb the onset of the propulsive impulse exactly when the blade should establish a firm water connection [26].

The proposed mechanism is hydrodynamic and should be interpreted as conceptual rather than directly validated by the present data (Fig. 6). Catch slip represents the angular delay between maximal forward reach and effective force application. When that delay differs between sides, the initial impulse becomes temporally unbalanced, which may generate a transient yaw moment and trajectory deviation. Even small deviations can increase resistance and reduce propulsive continuity [3–5, 17].

Conceptual model of the mechanical brake associated with catch slip asymmetry at the blade–water interface. (A) Asymmetric catch slip at the oar–water interface shortens the effective work distance and reduces the effective lift-to-drag ratio. (B) Bilateral mismatch in catch timing and force onset shifts the effective center of pressure. (C) The resulting imbalance may promote yaw-related trajectory deviation and hydrodynamic inefficiency. (D) Because the power–resistance relationship is non-linear, small velocity losses may require disproportionate energetic compensation. (E) The downstream consequences may include asymmetric loading, increased metabolic cost, accelerated local muscle fatigue, impaired pacing strategy, and reduced rowing performance. This mechanism is proposed conceptually and was not directly measured in the present study

By contrast, asymmetry in force magnitude later in the drive may be partly compensated through steering or postural adjustment, whereas asynchronous blade entry is more difficult to correct once the force impulse has been mistimed. Because power requirements rise nonlinearly with speed [27, 28], even brief catch-timing errors may impose a disproportionate energetic penalty. This interpretation remains inferential, but it provides a plausible explanation for why catch slip asymmetry retained significance after adjustment for force-related variables.

The discrepancy between the negative bivariate association for Release Slip AI and the multivariable significance of Catch Slip AI suggests that finish-phase asymmetry may co-occur with poorer technique but does not explain unique variance once catch timing and force-related variables are considered.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, although the telemetry system provided high ecological validity, comprehensive independent validation of all derived outputs from the specific platform used here is not yet available. Absolute quantities, particularly force- and power-related variables, may therefore be slightly under- or overestimated. However, the primary outcomes in the present study were simultaneous left-right asymmetry indices derived within the same system and are therefore less sensitive to common-mode bias than raw absolute values.

Second, the inferential analyses were conducted on phase-level averages rather than on single strokes. This decision avoided pseudo-replication and matched the repeated-measures design, but it also meant that within-phase stroke-to-stroke variability was not modelled directly. The unsegmented continuous stroke-by-stroke telemetry export for the terminal phases could not be retrieved from the project archive for this final revision; consequently, the Sprint audit was based on the preserved rower × phase analysis matrix rather than on a direct sequential raw-stroke reanalysis. Although the terminal phase-level plausibility checks argue against substantial or systematic inclusion of post-finish low-power deceleration strokes in the analyzed Sprint summaries, isolated boundary-stroke contamination cannot be excluded with the same certainty as would be possible using raw stroke-level transition modelling. Future studies should preserve the full stroke-level telemetry stream and model terminal phase boundaries directly.

Third, the observational design precludes causal inference. Although the LMM explained a meaningful proportion of the variance in boat speed (marginal R² = 0.36), performance remained multifactorial and was also shaped by unmeasured physiological, environmental, and technical factors.

Fourth, the initial start sequence was deliberately excluded because it represents a transient acceleration condition with mechanics that differ from the subsequent steady-state phases. Dedicated work is needed to determine whether asymmetry during the first strokes has similar or different performance implications.

Fifth, the use of absolute AI values was appropriate for the hydrodynamic question addressed here, but it obscured the direction of asymmetry and any role of individual lateral dominance.

Finally, the generalizability of the results is limited to elite male single scullers. The decoupling pattern identified here may differ in female, sub-elite, or novice rowers, in whom technique variability and neuromuscular control constraints are likely to differ [29].

Conclusion

The present on-water data suggest that asymmetry in elite sculling is not a unitary construct. Force-related asymmetry varied strongly across the incremental test and was positively related to faster phases at the descriptive level, but it was not independently associated with boat speed after repeated measures were accounted for. Power per Stroke AI was removed from the inferential asymmetry analyses because it was algebraically redundant with Work per Stroke AI in the phase-level dataset.

By contrast, poorer bilateral synchronization of catch slip was independently associated with lower boat speed. These findings suggest that coaching may benefit from prioritizing bilateral timing of the blade-water connection, especially at the catch, rather than focusing on absolute left-right force balance in isolation.
