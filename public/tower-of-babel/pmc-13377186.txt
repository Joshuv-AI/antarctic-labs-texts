Bridging survival analysis and machine learning to improve healthy life expectancy estimation using PHR records
Authors: Benedict A. H. Jones, James Harmsworth King, Matthew Watson, G. Thomas Hudson, Noura Al Moubayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthy Life Expectancy (HLE) considers the years an individual has lived free of disease. Minimising time a population spends in ill-health will directly impact national healthcare budgets, alongside individuals’ personal quality of life. We utilise Personal Health Records (PHR), consisting of Electronic Health Records with lifestyle and wellness data, to investigate loss of healthy life. We leveraged Survival Analysis (SA) to directly estimate HLE without the Sullivan Method. Additionally, a multiple imputation ensemble Machine Learning (ML) model was trained to successfully predict the loss of healthy life within one year with an AUPRC of almost double that of random of the unbalanced data. ML explainability techniques enabled investigation of the model’s learned relationships, providing insights about the effects of lifestyle on maintaining healthy life. Finally, a novel method of combining the ML model’s prediction with a SA’s estimated hazards was proposed, enabling the formulation of a tailored conditioned Survival Function.

Introduction

In the UK, over the last 50 years, the modal age of death has risen substantially, and in the next 50 years the period Life Expectancy (LE) in England and Wales is expected to increase by more than five years. (Office for National Statistics (ONS), Mortality in England and Wales). However, Healthy Life Expectancy (HLE) - the number of years that a person is expected to continue to live in a healthy condition1,2 has not seen substantial increases. Indeed, while significant improvement in LE has been observed, HLE at birth in the UK showed no significant change during the pre-coronavirus pandemic period (2016-2019) (ONS, Health state life expectancies). The Chief Medical Officer for England’s 2023 Annual report was entitled ‘Health in an Ageing Society’3, with one of the main focuses being minimising the time older members of the population spend in ill health; a policy adopted by the UK governments pledging to increase HLE by five years by 2035 (HM Government, Levelling Up the United Kingdom). Not only is HLE important for peoples’ own wellbeing4, but has far-reaching implications to the whole of society, such as featuring in political debates and literature5 relating to pension age changes, or improving how the health care systems operate4,5. This paper focuses on exploiting Survival Analysis (SA) and Machine Learning (ML) techniques to predict the likelihood of developing a diagnosis which would end healthy life. Firstly, SA was implemented to directly estimate HLE from medication and condition records. Then, a separate binary classification model was used to predict the likelihood of someone losing their healthy life in a year’s time, exploiting recent health and lifestyle data. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques were leveraged to understand the models’ learned relationships, which could inform targeted health interventions to mitigate the loss of healthy life. Finally, the binary classification prediction was then used to adjust an individual’s conditioned survival curve to produce tailored HLE predictions.

HLE is widely calculated using the Sullivan method6,7, a ‘health adjusted life expectancy’ which relies on combining typical (period life table) LE calculations with age-specific disability prevalence estimations determined from cross-sectional survey data. While it has been extensively used to estimate HLE in various populations1, the method was originally developed to 'produce potentially valuable measures not otherwise obtainable'6, thus enabling HLE estimation using the limited available data sources of the time. Continuous-time multi-state modelling has also been used to model similar concepts, such as activity life expectancy8, healthy working life expectancy9 or health needs10. However, these outcome/state definitions are still commonly derived from self-reported survey data. While the forecasting of LE has been extensively researched, work investigating HLE remains limited, partially because of the challenges and availability of health data11 to directly identify the loss of healthy life. However, modern digital systems and emergent digital technologies are leading to a quiet revolution in medical Electronic Health Records (EHRs), allowing the use of rich data sources to provide insightful and effective analysis which has previously been unavailable. To the best of our knowledge, for the first time, we utilise primary care EHR records to identify condition records and medical prescriptions which relate to diagnoses that represent a person’s ‘end of healthy life’ to directly compute HLE using SA methods.

The use of ML in health care is growing12, and there are numerous examples of ML models being used to predict the development of various types of diseases13,14, from hypertension15 to cancer16. However, healthy life can be difficult to quantify, and literature has often considered HLE adjacent outcomes, such as predicting life without activity limitation17,18 (derived from fitness data), or years of able health17 (derived from self reported data), as the target outcome of interest. A notable concept which sits alongside that of healthy life is Successful Ageing19,20; a holistic, multidimensional and interdisciplinary concept originally defined from three components: (i) low probability of disease and disease-related disability, (ii) high cognitive and physical functional capacity, and (iii) active engagement with life. Therefore, Successful Ageing aligns with many aspects of HLE, but focuses on the culmination of several wider population ageing issues. This is mirrored by the broader selection of features found in the literature when predicting Successful Ageing21,22 with a larger focus on lifestyle, such as physical activity, stress, nutrition, etc. To bridge this gap, we leverage a ‘Personal Health Record’ (PHR) consisting of both typical EHR and lifestyle data, situated at the intersection between heath, wellness and medical records. Exploiting this, to the best of our knowledge, we predict life free from ‘first diagnosis’ of the most common causes of morbidity and mortality in the western world (see Methods) for the first time. Unfortunately, ML models commonly operate as black boxes23, making them typically difficult to understand and effecting the transparency of any subsequent decision-making24. To examine the model’s learned relationships, the commonly used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)25 were extracted, a game theory approach to explain an ML model’s behaviour and feature importance. This provided insights about what health factors and behavioural habits might lead to improved health outcomes, both at a population and individual level.

One disadvantage of typical SA methods, such as Kaplan-Meier26 (KM), Cox Proportional Hazards27,28 (CoxPH) or Random Survival Forests29,30 (RSF), is their inability to exploit longitudinal covariates (i.e., features). These SA techniques are referred to as static survival models, which estimate survival from the time origin using baseline covariates measured at or before this origin31. When considering someone’s LE or HLE, the baseline is likely to be the time of birth (i.e., TB ≡ Tage=0), which greatly limits the available covariates (XB) which could be considered. To overcome this, dynamic Survival Analysis31,32 models are often used, which estimate the probability of survival to some future time horizon Tτ conditional on some previously survived time Tς (where Tς < Tτ) and covariates measured up to this survived time Xς. We present a novel approach drawing on concepts from Discrete Time Modelling31,33 to combine short term ML covariate driven binary classification predictions with longer term SA hazard estimates. This enabled time-to-event estimations using a wide range of ‘recent’ lifestyle and medical related data.

In summary, we present novel approaches to evaluate individuals’ healthy life. Firstly, SA was used to directly predict HLE using reported outcomes from EHR records, without the need for the Sullivan method and its assumptions. Secondly, a binary classification model was produced to predict the probability of developing an ‘end of healthy life’ diagnosis. This ML model used PHR data, consisting of typical EHR data with lifestyle and wellness information, which included the implementation of multiple imputation. The SHAP values for the binary classifier were examined to determine the feature importances of the demographic, lifestyle, medical measurements and other data considered. Finally, the ML model’s short term predictions were then combined with a conditioned survival curve to produce a tailored estimate of an individuals’ expected remaining healthy life. These techniques allow both population and individual level HLE estimates, and offer insights into lifestyle choices which might mitigate the loss of healthy life. Therefore, they might enable improved behavioural support and population health management tools.

To provide a transparent description of our reporting and modelling, this manuscript conforms to the TRIPOD guidelines34 (Supplementary Table 1). Code lists used to identify diseases are published on OpenCodeLists.

Results

Data overview

The data was extracted from anonymised PHR records of adults, including EHR data combined with self-reported wellness and lifestyle survey (i.e., questionnaire) information. This consisted of cross-sectional (i.e., tabular) demographics data with panel (i.e., longitudinal) medical measurement, smoking, survey, allergy, condition and medication records. The targeted outcome of healthy life was determined by analysing the conditions and medications records (see Methods). This allowed the first diagnosis of an illness which was deemed to ‘end healthy life’ to be identified.

The SA model used only baseline covariates/features (sex, Index Multiple Deprivation Decile (IMD), and binerised ethnicity) derived from the demographics’ data. This resulted in 124901 de-identified individuals who are considered for analysis, 57.6% of whom were female (further summary statistics provided in Supplementary Table 6). The ML model used a smaller dataset, downsampled to focus on individuals with rich EHR records and answers to lifestyle survey questions. Longitudinal records were aggregated (such as taking the mean or standard deviation of a measure over a number of years) to produce a final cross-sectional ML dataset, which contained 14199 individuals and was used to train the binary classifier. For both the SA and ML modelling, their respective datasets were randomly split 80−20% to create a training and hold out test data subsets. Further details can be found in the Methods, with a flow diagram provided in Supplementary Fig. 13.

Survival analysis performance

The Survival Function of the Kaplan Meier (KM) estimator fitted on the SA training data subset is plotted in Fig. 1(a), showing the change in survival probability over time (i.e., over an individual’s age). The HLE is estimated to be 61.48 years, found by calculating the Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST); which is the area under the survival curve up to a specific time point, in this case 100 years old (see Methods). For clarity, we distinguish this from an individual’s Remaining Healthy Life Expectancy (rHLE) by computing the RMST for a conditioned survival curve, taking into account the number of years an individual has already lived. Classification performance

A Multiple Imputation Ensemble (MIE) model was leveraged to appropriately handle the use of multiple imputation on the survey data (see Methods). The ensemble members were each tuned using 5-fold cross validation for 50 sweeps before being fit and calibrated. Final performance of the MIE was evaluated using the ML hold out test data subset which was singularly imputed (deemed more appropriate, see Methods) for inference. Performance was measured using several common metrics (Table 2), bootstrapped to provide confidence intervals35, and the MIE model is found to be well calibrated to the proportion of diagnosed individuals from the wider population in 2024 (see Supplementary Fig. 5). Results were compared to baseline KM and RSF models; trained with the SA dataset, but exploited to run inference on the ML test data subset by extracting a 1-year probability prediction from the conditioned survival curves. Additionally, typical LightGBM36 (gradient boosted decision tree) classifiers were trained (with 50 sweeps of 5-fold cross validation hyperparameter tuning) on just the Demographics features, or all features except the surveys. We observe that in many cases the MIE significantly outperforms the random baseline scenario, SA and LightGBM (trained on demographics) models. The introduction of the (imputed) survey/questionnaire features leads the MIE to achieve improved performance over a single LightGBM model without these lifestyle and wellness data. Running inference on the MIE model with raw un-imputed test data (by effectively omitting the imputation step) leads to poorer performance; observed by randomly reintroducing missing values back into the imputed test data (Supplementary Fig. 6). Additionally, some basic sensitivity analysis shows that the multiply imputed datasets lead to more consistent predictions compared to introducing random values (Supplementary Fig. 11). Classification explainability

Considering the whole cohort of individuals in the hold out test data, the top most important features for the trained MIE model (with the largest Mean Absolute SHAP values) were an individual’s age, binarised ethnicity, and an individual’s 4-year mean BMI and systolic blood pressure measurements, as shown in Fig. 3(a). The corresponding bee-swarm plots in Fig. 3(b) provide a high level representation of feature - SHAP value interaction. For example, individuals of a larger age have a more positive SHAP value, indicating the model has learnt that it leads to a higher chance of losing one’s healthy life. However, many features will have non-linear relationships better seen by plotting them directly against their SHAP values (examples of which can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 7). As discussed, not all features follow simple linear (or monotonic) relationships. Indeed, for some features, the model has learned some apparently unintuitive relationships from the underlying training data. For example, having smoked in the past three years ‘smoked__activity-3y’ is non-significantly correlated with unhealthier outcomes, although longer term indications of smoking behaviour are. Similarly, larger aggregate Alcohol AUDIT PC values are non-significantly correlated to unhealthy outcomes. Examining the latter in more detail, we find that the relationship between this alcohol feature and SHAP value is non-linear, forming a ‘U’ shaped curve, as seen in Fig. 5(a). To mitigate such an undesirable association (see Discussion), well known causal relationships could be identified and used to constrain the model. In particular, LightGBM allows monotonic constraints to be defined for each individual feature. An additional MIE with Monotonic Constraints model was trained, where both alcohol and smoking features were specified to have a positive monotonic relationship with the target outcome. It was found to have little noticeable depreciation in performance (Table 2) while drastically improving the anticipated causal relationship38, as shown in Fig. 5(b).Fig. 5Effect of constraining the alcohol feature with a positive monotonic relationship.Scatter plot of the Alcohol AUDIT PC aggregate feature value against SHAP value for the a unconstrained and b constrained MIE models. Discussion

In this study, we present methods to estimate HLE using typical SA, an ML model to predict the risk of losing healthy life in one year’s time, and under some assumptions, we introduce a novel method to combine these to create a personalised prediction of an individual’s remaining healthy life.

Current methods to compute HLE typically rely on the Sullivan method, which adjusts LE estimations using population survey data. Such predictions require mortality records and place a reliance on the survey data and its interpretation, which can lead to discrepancies of HLE predictions if the underlying survey data shifts or as methodologies are inevitably updated. Due to the increasing adoption of digital health records, we have shown that SA can be readily applied to directly model HLE, where we consider an individual receiving a first diagnosis (from a selection of several conditions of interest) as the end to their healthy life. Our HLE predictions, using ground truth EHR record events, appear to align with UK ONS’s HLE estimates for data stratified on sex, and follow similar trends for predictions on individuals stratified by both sex and IMD. Discrepancies are likely due to the variation in definitions used, with the Sullivan method often also including injury or disability when evaluating healthy life, and utilising self-reported surveys. Our definition of ‘healthy life’ ensures repeatability (due to the exploitation of code lists) and transportability (i.e., application to any dataset containing primary care records). However, biases may still persist as many individuals live with chronic conditions without disability, while others suffer impairment without a formal diagnosis39. Additionally, this approach sacrifices a more holistic definition of HLE (e.g., failing to reflect functional ability, autonomy, etc.40), and future work should consider the inclusion of a wider set of codes, such as Activity of Daily Living (ADL) alteration.

To mitigate missing data in our lifestyle and wellness records, multiple imputation was used. In order to successfully account for these imputed datasets, a Multiple Imputation Ensemble was constructed. Pooling the ensembles predictions led to a small increase in predictive performance, but significantly broader insights from the SHAP value explanations of these lifestyle features; albeit at the expense of increased computation from the imputation and parallel ensemble model execution. In this work, longitudinal records were tabulated for easier imputation and modelling; however, this likely fails to capture complex and rich longitudinal changes. Further work should consider models better suited to the EHRs sequential data such as transformers41, and which could potentially construct a complete discrete time survival curve directly. By investigating SHAP values across the ensemble members we identified several medical and lifestyle factors which might lead to a reduced or heightened risk of loss of healthy life. AI explainability techniques like SHAP enable us to understand what our ML models have learnt, but do not guarantee causal relationships. Fundamentally, ML models learn from trends within the underlying dataset; fitting predictive ML models on observational data and using it to examine counterfactuals presents some problems42. Predictive inference and causal inference remain distinct, yet in reality over recent years, there has been a proliferation of predictive ML models, and in many domains (such as critical systems or healthcare clinical decision-making) it is essential to understand how these models behave in order to guide and inform prospective users.

Predictive models are unable to learn causal effects unless features are wholly independent of one another and there are no unobserved confounders—which is often impossible. As such, learned relationships, reflected in Shapley explanations, will not necessarily be causal (recall 'where we find correlation we cannot always predict causation'43). Further work is required to establish these effects using causal inference techniques. While we have taken steps to improve feature independence (such as aggregating similar question feature answers), some unintuitive relationships remain. One such example highlighted in the Results was the Alcohol AUDIT PC feature (the ‘U’ shape in Fig. 5). We speculate this is likely due to unhealthier individuals being instructed to avoid drinking alcohol, something which might happen well in advance of their first diagnosis, and is reflected in the survey lifestyle data. We propose a possible solution is to apply constraints on the model. This prevents the model from over-fitting on underlying trends which are deemed to be unintuitive or inappropriate. In the future, these constraints could be derived from formal causal assumptions.

Given enough data, a full suite of ML classifiers could be trained to formulate a Discrete Time SA model. However, due to the lack of lifestyle and wellness data before the launch of the PHR, only a single shorter 1 year time horizon was considered. Instead, the performant, explainable and well calibrated ML model was used to predict the hazard in one year’s time, and (in lieu of several ‘discrete time’ binary classifiers) a typical RSF model (trained with a much larger amount of data) was used to predict hazards for all other times. Combining these enabled the formulation of a tailored Survival Function, a novel approach to enable dynamic SA using well established methods. Unfortunately, the lack of longitudinal data hampered the ability to test the performance of the tailored HLE estimations. However, it was observed that the RSF+ML inference predictions outperformed the baseline case of combining the RSF with a classifier predicting the probability of (derived from the proportion of individuals in 2024) remaining healthy. This suggests that the ML model did introduce relevant information into the tailored Survival Function, resulting in a superior and personalised HLE estimation, but also providing explainability, enabling insights into directions for health improvements.

In conclusion, there is a growing recognition of the importance of extending individuals’ healthy life. We present several methods to predict and evaluate healthy life, as well as inform us as to important possible contributing factors. These include direct cohort HLE estimation using SA, achieved by exploiting patient records and a well-defined definition of healthy life. An ML model (which accounts for missing data), where inference can be run to generate (calibrated) 1-year loss of healthy life probability predictions/risk scores. Alongside this, SHAP values can be extracted and used to observe which features the model believes are impacting the prediction in both a positive and negative manner for a single (i.e., ‘episodic’ or ‘local’) or cohort (i.e., ‘global’) of individuals. Finally, if time-to-event data is desired, a tailored HLE estimation can be found using the proposed pipeline by combining the ML prediction with a predicted conditioned Survival Function. In the future, such techniques could be used for reporting to aid population health management, and informing the commissioning of targeted interventions for behavioural support to improve healthy life.

Healthy life definition

The target outcome was specified as the development of a diagnosis, which represents the end of ‘healthy life’. The selection of these diagnoses was guided by the WHO’s leading causes of death in upper-middle-income countries, the most commonly studied diseases in multi-morbidity literature44, and conversations with clinicians. These overarching target ‘parent’ diagnoses were chosen to consist of: Hypertension, Type 2 Diabetes, Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), Cardiovascular Disease (CVD), Alzheimers (and Dementia), Cancer, Stroke and Lung Disease. To identify these diagnosis, curated code lists were formulated (additional details are provided in Supplementary Note 1).

In order to identify conditions related to these diagnoses within an individual’s record, a set of SNOMED codes was required. SNOMED CT is a structured clinical vocabulary for use in within UK based EHR systems, providing a comprehensive and precise clinical health terminology. Typically, an individual condition is identified by collating a set of SNOMED condition codes relating to it. This process was performed to produce code lists to define the eight conditions considered in this paper. Additionally, several rounds of ‘cleaning’ occurred with a trained medical professional to remove high counts of irrelevant (i.e., codes identifying conditions which were not considered to ‘end healthy life’) or overlapping concepts. The final list of End of Healthy Life (Conditions) codes was published on OpenCodeLists.

To better identify the target diagnosis and boost coverage within the data, medications were also linked to the diagnosis outcomes by defining British National Formulary (BNF) code lists (consisting of chapters, sections, etc.) in a similar manner. Select BNF sections were identified to represent Hypertension and Type 2 Diabetes. These End of Healthy Life (Medications) BNF codes were also published on OpenCodeLists.

These code lists provide a definition of ’healthy life’ which can be applied to any dataset containing primary care records. In this work, the collection of diagnoses (both condition and medication) records was compiled using the granularity of a day. If two or more diagnoses occurred on the same day, they were combined to create a new, unique outcome. A histogram of the age of first diagnosis is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, showing the number of ‘healthy years’ diagnosed individuals within our dataset lived.

Discrete time survival analysis

Dynamic prediction SA seeks to introduce longitudinal features, given that someone has already survived up to a certain point (t = Tς). Such methods include: (i) Landmarking31,32,48,49 where a landmark time (Ts) is selected, and only those participants who have survived until the landmark time are considered, and landmark ‘baseline’ covariates Xs are used in the analysis. Machine learning data preparation

In this work, we leveraged tabular ML models to classify the likelihood of an individual losing their healthy life in one year’s time. Therefore, longitudinal records required some manipulation to create the required cross-section dataset. Notably, positive (i.e., receiving a first diagnosis in one year) and negative (i.e., remaining undiagnosed in one year) data instances were formulated differently. For positive instances, the date of first diagnosis was determined from the conditions and medications outcome data. A historical record, referred to here as a ‘synthetic’ record, was then formulated one year prior to the diagnosis date, containing only data from before this point. If no diagnosis is found in the data, then a negative instance is formulated by determining the date of the last known healthy state, and creating a synthetic record one year prior to this. Finally, longitudinal records were converted into cross-sectional features by pivoting over select time periods and aggregating by taking the (e.g.,) mean or standard deviation (‘std’). Variations of this were implemented, such as determining whether a person had ever smoked, or only aggregating over the most recent three survey questions submitted to help accentuate any possible future adjustment in behaviour. Further descriptions of the features can be seen in Supplementary Table 4.

To prepare the raw cross-sectional PHR data for classification, further (pre-)processing was required. The data was first downsampled by removing synthetic records from before an individual joined the digital health application (before which there was no opportunity for users to answer survey questions or provide other lifestyle information). The data was then downsampled to only retain instances which contained at least some survey data; followed by a further twenty percent of this amount of the otherwise least sparse records. The prevalence of missing data (i.e., sparsity or ‘missingness’) is shown in Supplementary Fig. 10. These data then had two distinct preprocessing steps applied, (A) and (B), before being used to train and test the classifier.

In stage (A), a feature selector class filtered data for desired features. Any encoding used, such as a label encoder, was then applied. Finally, due to the large amount of ‘missingness’ in the survey features, and the bias which it has been found to introduce, the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) algorithm and miceforest python package were employed as a multiple imputation method52,53. Unlike Single Imputation, which produces only one imputed dataset (such as mean imputation or MissForest54), multiple imputation produces several imputed datasets, each of which contains plausible imputed values for the target features, better accounting for the inherent uncertainty in any imputation approach. Here, the MICE Forest model was trained to impute the survey question and smoking features, using only the demographics data and 4-year mean medical measurements (i.e., BMI, HBA1C, systolic and diastolic blood pressure) as the reference to be imputed from. Notably, we perform imputation on the aggregated cross-sectional data since the underlying longitudinal records presented challenges due to being sporadically recorded (i.e., answers are recorded when an individual chooses to provide them, not at regular intervals). The MICE Forest algorithm leverages the reference features to train a (LightGBM36) model to predict users missing values for each of the features to impute, then uses predictive mean matching, randomly selecting one value from the closest N candidates. This process is repeated iteratively until we are satisfied the model has converged. Any following analysis should generally be performed for each independent imputed dataset, and the results then pooled55.

Subsequently, the second preprocessing stage (B) was applied to the imputed dataset(s). This was required as we utilise the impute then transform56 methodology. Firstly, a survey aggregator class was used to combine some similar survey question features into a single feature (such as producing a stress & anxiety feature from individual ‘stress’ and ‘anxiety’ question responses). Finally, we applied a custom De-bias Class to ‘de-bias’ or ‘decorrelate’ features from the demographics’ data; achieved by training regressors on the demographics to predict the features under consideration and formulate a ‘de-biased’ residual value. Thought of simply, we are creating a feature which represents whether you have a larger or smaller value than those of a similar demographic. This method takes inspiration from the DoubleML ‘denoising’ step57 and Residual Learning-based feature importance58. However, directly using raw residuals can result in large extremes, so an adjusted sigmoid is fit to bound the final de-biased values between ∈ (0, 1).

These preprocessing steps are visualised in Supplementary Fig. 9, with further detailed discussion on implementation provided in Supplementary Note 3. The final dataset consisted of 47 features, with further details given in Supplementary Table 3.

Multiple imputation ensemble classifier

In order to appropriately handle the multiply imputed data, a Multiple Imputation Ensemble (MIE) approach52 was used, where each imputed dataset was used to train a separate gradient boosted decision tree LightGBM36 model. To ensure a well calibrated probability prediction59 each model in the ensemble was calibrated with a corresponding spline based probability calibration model60. While the final calibrated probabilities from the ensemble could simply be averaged, we instead first convert them back into the raw marginal space (utilised by the LightGBM models) before averaging and transforming back into a probability. This was to ensure the pooled predictions align more closely with the averaged SHAP values computed from each model in the ensemble. During inference, we alter the pipeline such that each ensemble member uses the same singularly imputed data; calculated by taking the average of the multiply imputed inference datasets. Final performance was calculated using regression metrics such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
