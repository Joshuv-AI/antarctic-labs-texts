Unhealthy behaviours in disaster survivors are associated with scarcity and present bias
Authors: Yasuyuki Sawada, Yusuke Kuroishi, Toyo Ashida, Jun Aida, Kei Kajisa, Hiroyuki Hikichi, Takahiro Sato, Eduardo Lucio, Katsunori Kondo, Ken Osaka, Ichiro Kawachi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extant research suggests that disaster exposure may induce unhealthy behavior, such as smoking, drinking, and eating disorder symptoms in survivors; however, the underlying the mechanisms of such behavior remain unclear. Using two field experiments in two separate disaster-affected communities in Japan (N = 337) and the Philippines (N = 187), this study found that scarcity and reinforced present-bias were associated with disaster experiences and unhealthy behavior in both locations. These experiments were conducted twice (i.e., 2–3 years and 6 years post-disaster), allowing us to examine the enduring impact of these disasters. Disaster survivors became consistently present-biased, which in turn explains variance in individuals’ health-related decisions, such as body mass index, smoking, and drinking.

Introduction

Individuals traumatized by disaster tend to engage in various unhealthy behaviors, including eating disorder symptoms, smoking, and drinking1–4. Currently, the underlying mechanisms that may explain the impact of disaster exposure on unhealthy behavior remain unclear, although these mechanisms likely involve behavioral coping responses to stress and trauma and an increased present bias induced by scarcity (e.g., property and income loss associated with disaster experiences).

This study aimed to address the gap in the existing literature by explaining the key intervening mechanism underlying the relationship between disaster exposure and unhealthy behavior. Based on recent studies on the psychological consequences of poverty and scarcity5–7 and the plausible connection between hyperbolic discounting and unhealthy behavior8–15, we hypothesize that hyperbolic discounting, also known as present bias, is a key intervening variable in the relationship between disaster loss and unhealthy behavior, which operates through a cognitive mechanism. As disasters result in the loss of physical, human, and social assets, they can be conceptualized as a form of scarcity. In turn, the environmental uncertainty associated with scarcity can lead to reinforced hyperbolic discounting6,7, inducing unhealthy behaviors. To test this hypothesis, we exploited natural experiments of unexpected substantial asset and economic losses caused by two recent disasters in Japan and the Philippines to capture relationships with field experiments meant to elicit hyperbolic discounting parameters.

Methods

First study—Iwanuma City in Japan

The first study was conducted in Iwanuma city of Miyagi Prefecture, Japan, located 80 km west of the epicenter of the Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami Disaster of March 11, 2011. The city was severely impacted by the disaster, resulting in 180 casualties and 2766 homes being either destroyed or damaged. The latter damages are likely to have resulted in significant economic losses, as land and home assets comprised an average of 72.5% of total household assets in Miyagi Prefecture, based on data from the 2009 National Survey of Family Income and Expenditure, the year closest to the disaster.

To investigate the impact of the disaster on individual behavior and economic preferences, lab-in-the-field experiments were conducted in 2014 and 2017 to collect data from Iwanuma. The survey and experimental procedures were approved via the University of Tokyo IRB. Participants were sampled from the panel respondents of the Japan Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES), which includes the census of individuals aged 65 or older residing in Iwanuma city, an ongoing cohort of community-dwelling residents. The JAGES cohort baseline for Iwanuma city was established seven months before the disaster, offering a unique opportunity to control for the pre-disaster characteristics of the participants16–18. A total of 1032 residents out of 3594 (28.7%) JAGES census respondents in Iwanuma from the 2013 survey wave (i.e., post-disaster) agreed to participate in the study. The following sampling procedure was used for these participants. First, out of a total of 177 survey areas, 63 blocks around the tsunami border were selected by identifying those that recorded a large variation in home damage, ranging from homes with no damage to homes that were almost or totally collapsed within each block. We substantially sent invitations to the 346 respondents living in these 63 blocks. In the first wave of the field experiment in May 2014, 186 individuals participated (participation rate: 54.0%) across five sessions on May 15, 16, 19, 20, and 21. In the second wave, conducted on February 8–11, 14, 21, 27–28 and March 28–29, 2017, a total of 179 individuals out of the 225 invitees participated in the second-round field experiment, which followed the same procedure as in the first-round experiment conducted in 2014. The sessions lasted approximately two hours, including the time to make payments. Participants received a show-up fee of JPY 3000 (USD 30) and performance-based rewards. In both waves, self-reported data on weight, height, and smoking and drinking behaviors were collected. Two binary indices were constructed for these behaviors (1 = currently smoking/drinking, 0 = otherwise).

Second study—East Laguna village in the Philippines

The second study was conducted in the East Laguna village, a pseudonym used for anonymity, in the Laguna province of the Philippines, located approximately 80 kilometers south of Metro Manila, facing the east coast of the lake, Laguna de Bay19,20. In August 2012, the village was affected by serious out-of-season floods unexpectedly caused by “Habagat,” which literally indicates monsoon winds in Tagalog. Although not a typhoon in its own right, Habagat caused typhoon-like damage, such as river overflow and flooding, submerging low-lying villages in 19 towns and cities, including the East Laguna village, destroying P410.3 million ($9.72 million) worth of agricultural products. Over half of the village area was submerged in floodwater, resulting in severe paddy destruction.

Following the same lab-in-the-field experimental protocol as used in the Iwanuma study, surveys and experiments were conducted in East Laguna in March 2014 and March 201821. Invitations were sent to 200 farmers from East Laguna and two adjacent villages so that we could cover farmers who cultivated in the East Laguna village. In 2014, a total of 158 farmers participated in field experiments held over five days (March 20–24), while, in 2018, 141 farmers took part in sessions from March 3 to 7. A separate household survey was also administered during both years. Among the participants, 99 farmers in 2014 and 91 in 2018 were actively cultivating farmland within East Laguna village boundaries, and thus were included in the main analysis.

Each experimental session lasted approximately two hours, including the time needed for the distribution of payments. Participants received a show-up honorarium of PHP 200 (approximately USD 4), and performance-based experimental rewards. At the end of the experiment, participants were asked questions about their current smoking and drinking habits based on the binary variables we constructed (1 = currently smoking/drinking, 0 = otherwise).

Ethics and inclusion statement

This research was conducted through close collaboration between Japanese and Filipino researchers as well as between institutions based in Japan and the Philippines, with equitable involvement across all stages from study design and field implementation to data analysis, data ownership, and authorship. Local scholars with longstanding ties to the study communities played central roles in ensuring that the research was grounded in local contexts and sensitivities. The Philippines study was built on a multi-decade research engagement and baseline information in the East Laguna village through the International Rice Research Institute, Laguna, Philippines, while the Japanese component utilized the JAGES cohort in Iwanuma, Japan. Authorship reflects the substantive intellectual contributions of all collaborators, irrespective of institutional or geographic affiliation. Roles and responsibilities were clearly defined at the outset, and the research questions were developed collaboratively to ensure local relevance. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Tokyo Institutional Review Board for both studies. In addition, the project was conducted under formal agreements with the Economic Research Institute for ASEAN and East Asia, the East Laguna village, and Iwanuma city. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated according to local norms. Gender was self-reported and optional; no ethnicity data were collected. Preregistration was not conducted for any of the studies reported in this manuscript. Throughout the study, we actively engaged with and cited local and regional research16–20 to shape the study’s conceptual foundation, guide methodological choices, and situate our findings within the broader context of existing research in Japan and the Philippines.

The Convex Time Budget experiments

To elicit individual preference parameters consistently, we conducted a series of lab-in-the-field experimental tests, including the Convex Time Budget (CTB) experiments developed and employed by Andreoni and Sprenger22 and Sawada and Kuroishi23. In this experiment, the hyperbolic discounting factor, β, is elicited through 24 convex budget decisions faced by each participant, involving combinations of starting times, t, and delay lengths, k, with annual interest rates. We combined three sooner payment dates, t = (0, 35, 63) days from the experiment date with two-time intervals, k = (35, 63) days from the sooner payment date. Thus, we constructed four (t, k) cells, (t, k) = (0, 35), (0, 63), (35, 70), and (63, 126) and each cell contained six CTB questions with different payoffs, generating 24 choices for each participant. In each CTB question, participants are given the choice of (X, 0) or (0, Y), with X denoting a smaller immediate payment and Y denoting a larger and last payment, where Y = 10,000 in Japan and Y = 400 in the Philippines. Results

Our final sample consisted of 524 participants, with an average age of 67.76 years (SD = 11.74), including 254 women and 270 men, based on self-reported age and gender. Participants from the two study areas were different in age and education level. Specifically, the Japanese subsample included 337 individuals (mean age = 73.84 years, SD = 5.29; 178 women and 159 men, based on self-reported age and gender), while the Filipino subsample included 187 individuals (mean age = 56.8 years, SD = 12.21; 76 women and 111 men, based on self-reported age and gender). Educational attainment also varied considerably: 75% of Japanese participants had completed more than compulsory education, compared with only 39% of Filipino participants. These contrasts provide an opportunity to assess whether unhealthy behavior is consistently associated with reinforced hyperbolic discounting across two communities that have experienced distinct disaster events and possess different socioeconomic contexts. This comparison may, at least partially, facilitate the external validation of findings from each area. We performed three sets of statistical analyses. First, we checked the validity of the impact of our “natural experiments,” that is, the exogeneity or randomness of disaster exposure, by testing for baseline balance. In the data obtained from Iwanuma, we regressed each respondent’s pre-disaster characteristics (age, education level, job status, earthquake insurance participation, home ownership, house size, information on outstanding housing loans, BMI, and smoking and drinking behavior) on the officially-certified disaster damage level of each home captured by the local government inspection reports (Supplementary Table 1). While we excluded observations with missing values for any of the dependent variables from the empirical analysis, we found that respondent characteristics did not systematically correlate with damage level, which supports the claim that this was, in a way, a natural experiment.

In the data obtained from the East Laguna village, the flood damage level for each respondent was captured by the decoded satellite images of farmland and by self-reported binary damage variables along five dimensions (i.e., home damage, asset damage, income decline, debt increase, and health problems caused by the floods). We constructed a composite flood damage index that takes a value of one if at least three damages occurred from the above list of six possible types of damages, that is, the satellite-based farmland damage and five self-reported damages; and zero otherwise. According to Supplementary Table 2, the composite damage index was not systematically correlated with pre-flood respondent characteristics (number of household members, household head’s gender, age, years of schooling, marital-status, widow-status, proportion of food consumption out of total consumption a month before the floods, outstanding amounts in cash loans, and migration status). Ultimately, these findings also support the validity of using the Philippine data to serve as a random, natural experiment.

Robustness check

To explore the robustness of our argument and assess whether our estimate might be different from that for the exposed population, we ran a weighted regression using age and gender as weights. In Japan, we adjusted the sample to be representative of the entire population covered by the JAGES survey. For the Philippines, we adjusted the sample to represent the exposed population in the East Laguna village. The estimation results are reported in Tables 5 and 6 and Figs. 5 and 6 for gender weights, and in Tables 7 and 8 and Figs. 7 and 8 for age weights, respectively. Discussion

Our study analyzed 524 participants from Japan and the Philippines, with substantial differences in age and educational attainment. These contrasts allowed us to examine whether unhealthy behaviors are consistently associated with hyperbolic discounting across distinct socio-economic contexts and disaster experiences. Our empirical findings showed that disaster exposure was linked to higher BMI, smoking, and/or drinking indices in both Iwanuma and East Laguna village, regardless of demographic differences. Structural models using disaster exposure as an instrument for present bias revealed that disasters were associated with reinforced present bias, which in turn contributed to higher BMI, smoking, and drinking, with effects persisting for up to six years post-disaster.

These findings suggest that a potential mechanism linking deteriorating health behaviors in the post-disaster aftermath is cognitive in nature, characterized by an increased present bias. Our results align with prior research on the potential role of commitment devices in mitigating the negative consequences of present bias following disaster exposure24, particularly in relation to substance abuse. For example, existing studies have explored the effectiveness of cash transfers combined with commitment savings15,25 to facilitate health behavior change, commitment contracts for smoking cessation tied to savings deposits26, and the use of in-kind transfers to nudge recipients toward desirable behaviors27.

Our analysis has three potential caveats. First, while we commonly observe that disaster surviviors became consistently present-biased, in explaining harmful health behavior, a clear difference was observed between the two study sites. Disaster exposure was associated with an increase in the smoking index only in Japan, but in the East Lagna village, it exacerbates drinking habits. We believe that this asymmetric result can be explained at least partially by the price differentials. According to the WHO Global Health Observatory28, the price of 500 ml of beer in 2016 was $4.07 in Japan and $0.58 in the Philippines. Likewise, a pack of 20 cigarettes costs $4.17 in Japan, while in the Philippines it was $2.14. Thus, considering relative prices, it is relatively easy to access tobacco in Japan and alcohol in the Philippines, which causes dissimilar indulgences in unhealthy behaviors. Importantly, these behavioral changes contrast with broader national trends. In Japan, both smoking and alcohol consumption have steadily declined over the past decade29. In the Philippines, cigarette use has also declined in recent years, while alcohol consumption has remained relatively stable30,31. The divergence between national patterns and our localized findings suggests that these increases in substance use are unlikely to be driven by country-wide shifts and instead reflect behavioral responses to disaster exposure. Taken together, we conclude that disasters appear to worsen harmful behaviors in both countries, although the specific behaviors affected may depend on local price structures and accessibility.

Second, in the data obtained from the East Laguna village, the flood damage level for each respondent was measured by decoded satellite images of farmland and self-reported binary damage variables along five dimensions. However, health-related issues may represent a qualitatively different type of damage, and that the constructed binary variable may lack clarity in capturing disaster exposure. To address this, we constructed an alternative composite flood damage index excluding the health-related dimension, and also used the number of reported damages as an alternative disaster exposure variable. As shown in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, the results remain qualitatively similar, which suggests that this concern does not significantly affect our findings. Finally, a minor caveat is that the relatively small sample sizes in Iwanuma and the East Laguna village raised concerns that our empirical analyses might produce inaccurate confidence intervals and test statistics. To address this issue, we applied the bootstrap approach using the same reduced-form and structural model specifications. Our robustness exercises (Supplementary Tables 5–10) indicated that the resulting confidence intervals and statistical tests were similar to those reported in Tables 2–4, which suggests that our original estimates and inference were properly reported.

Further investigations are warranted to understand the deeper structures behind the link between disaster exposure and heightened present bias. Disasters often evoke negative emotions due to the unexpected loss of loved ones, damage to homes, job loss, and other hardships, which may intensify present bias among survivors23,32. This heightened present bias, in turn, could explain behaviors such as the increased drinking, smoking, and weight gain observed among disaster surviviors, as highlighted in this study.

In addition, future research should explore the mechanisms underlying the relationships between disaster exposure and individuals’ broader preferences beyond present bias and health behaviors. For example, as observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, social preferences can fluctuate over time33. Disasters may exacerbate this instability, potentially leading to either a decline or an increase in prosocial behaviors. Although our companion studies34,35 examine changes in altruism among disaster survivors, a more comprehensive investigation of this issue remains an important direction for future research.

Moreover, while we emphasize scarcity as a cognitive mechanism driving reinforced present bias after disaster exposure, we acknowledge that alternative psychological pathways may also contribute. In particular, recent research36–39 highlights that exposure to life-threatening events, such as natural disasters, can increase present-biased decision-making by heightening concerns about mortality. To empirically assess this possibility, we conducted a robustness check using data from the Philippines, where the 2018 survey included a variable indicating whether respondents perceived a decline in their longevity after experiencing Habagat. We found that controlling for this mortality perception variable did not significantly alter our results, which suggests that our findings are not driven by mortality-related mechanisms alone (Supplementary Table 11).

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. First, the recent experimental literature on the hyperbolic discounting has focused on the difference between the domains of monetary and non-monetary choice. Considering the empirical result40, which finds that present bias in monetary choices is much more limited than that in the allocation of work, our results that are fully based on monetary choices may underestimate the true impacts of disasters on real-world behavior. While a recent article41 also showed that present bias even for money exists for immediate payments, we would need to be aware of potential bias in estimating the hyperbolic discounting parameter with different monetary incentives. In addition, the recent literature42 has raised concerns about using the CTB method in combination with the quasi-hyperbolic discounting model, particularly in light of potential estimation biases under certain experimental conditions. While these concerns may not directly apply to our setting, we acknowledge this methodological debate and the need for caution when interpreting the structural estimates of present bias derived from CTB tasks.

Second, cultural, environmental, or biological factors can play a significant role in how individuals respond to disasters and engage in unhealthy behaviors. For example, kinship and community connectivity, such as the strength of family ties, the availability of social support networks, or the degree of community cohesion, might influence how individuals perceive and respond to health risks and substance use. Although our qualitative results are consistent despite varying cultural, environmental or biological factors (Supplementary Tables 12–31), careful further investigation could reveal the possible channels through which surrounding factors might ameliorate or exacerbate individuals’ harmful decisions.

Third, while our study interprets the observed increase in present-biased behavior primarily through the lens of cognitive scarcity, we recognize that alternative explanatory frameworks may also be relevant. In particular, theories from evolutionary psychology and life-history literature suggest that exposure to harsh or unpredictable environments can lead to more present-oriented decision-making as an adaptive response. Moreover, psychological coping mechanisms such as avoidance behavior, substance use as a form of self-medication, and stress-induced addiction may also contribute to unhealthy behaviors following disaster exposure. Although our empirical design and experimental task are grounded in the literature on scarcity and time preferences, we acknowledge that mental health may play a meaningful but underexamined role in shaping these behavioral outcomes. Future research could explore more directly the interaction between environmental harshness, perceptions of unpredictability, emotional coping responses, and intertemporal choice. Integrating these theoretical perspectives may yield a more comprehensive understanding of behavioral adaptations following disaster exposure.

Finally, our study relies on follow-up data collected up to six years after the natural disaster. During this extended period, other intervening events and policy changes might also have influenced the behaviors we examine. While the inclusion of time fixed effects in our empirical specification helps to control for unobserved factors that vary across time, this approach assumes that such influences are consistent across all individuals, which might not fully reflect reality. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as we cannot completely rule out the possibility that time-varying external factors contributed to the observed patterns.
