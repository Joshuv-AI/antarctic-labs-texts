Where Knowledge Collides: A Mechanistic Study of Intra-Memory Knowledge Conflict in Language Models
Minh Vu Pham, Hsuvas Borkakoty, Yufang Hou

Abstract
In language models (LMs), intra-memory knowledge conflict largely arises when inconsistent information about the same event is encoded within the model’s parametric knowledge 35 . While prior work has primarily focused on resolving conflicts between a model’s internal knowledge and external resources through approaches such as fine-tuning or knowledge editing, the problem of localizing conflicts that originate during pre-training within the model’s internal representations remain unexplored. In this work, we design a framework based on mechanistic interpretability methods to identify where and how conflicting knowledge from the pre-training data is encoded within LMs. Our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence that specific internal components of a language model are responsible for encoding conflicting knowledge from pre-training, and we demonstrate how mechanistic interpretability methods can be leveraged to causally intervene in and control conflicting knowledge at inference time. 1 1 1 We will release the resources and code upon acceptance.

Introduction

Figure 1:
A high-level overview (with example) of our proposed framework.

As information evolves rapidly, large language models (LLMs) often face the challenge of maintaining accurate information in their parametric knowledge to maintain reliability and reduce hallucinations
15
. Approaches such as continual learning
16
and retrieval-augmented generation
18
;
5
are used to keep model knowledge up to date. However, models may generate different responses to the same question in different forms, a phenomenon known as
intra-memory knowledge conflict

35
. Such conflicts arise from biases and noises in the
training data
2
;
17
, limitations of knowledge editing
21
, and randomness in sampling
11
. As LLM-backed AI systems become more sophisticated and integrated into day-to-day life
3
, the generation of conflicting information raises broader concerns about their reliability.

Existing approaches to mitigate intra-memory knowledge conflict have focused on improving factual accuracy and consistency in response generation. These methods include targeted fine-tuning
25
, context faithful prompting
40
, and pruning internal components of models
14
to reduce such conflicts. However, to date, no prior research has examined a fundamental question:
Which internal components are responsible for conflicts that originate in the pre-training data, and can noisy or incorrect information be selectively suppressed during inference?

Answering this question is crucial for understanding and resolving intra-memory knowledge conflict, which arises from interactions within the model’s internal components.

In this work, we propose a framework grounded in mechanistic interpretability (MI) methods to localize where and how conflicting knowledge acquired during pre-training is encoded within language models. As illustrated in Figure
1
, we first construct a curated dataset,
SynWikiBio
, consisting of Wikipedia-style biographies for synthetic entities that deliberately contain conflicting knowledge (e.g.,
a person’s graduating university
). We use this dataset to further fine-tune a language model (e.g., GPT-2 XL).
We then probe the fine-tuned model using a shared prompt designed to elicit competing knowledge representations (Section
3
), and apply
logit lens
to localize the internal components responsible for encoding such conflicts (Section
4.2
). To evaluate the causal contribution of the identified components to the generation of conflicting knowledge, we perform
activation patching
(Section
4.3
) and apply an advance patching technique called
cross-model activation patching (CMAP)

31
, which enables more targeted and cleaner interventions for causal attribution in the presence of conflicted knowledge (Section
4.4
).

This framework enables us to systematically investigate the following research questions:

1
.
How does conflicting information in the training data influence a model’s factual predictions, and which internal components most strongly affect this decision? And to what extent can activation patching localize components responsible for resolving such conflicts?

2
.
How reliably can targeted activation patching alter model predictions to resolve conflicting information?

3
.
To what extent can activation patching be transferred across different models to influence their predictions?

And finally,
4
.
How do the identified mechanisms and intervention effects vary across different model architectures?

Our findings demonstrate the utility of interpretability approaches for studying knowledge conflict arising from training data, revealing that attention components in later layers play a dominant role in shaping model responses. Furthermore, through cross-model patching, we find that patching activations corresponding to correct facts can steer generation toward accurate outputs. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first proof-of-concept for leveraging mechanistic interpretability techniques toward causal intervention on internal conflicting knowledge.

Related Work

Intra-Memory Knowledge Conflict

is the type of knowledge conflict where the model generates different responses to similar input, primarily due to conflicting information present in the training data
35
. Approaches to mitigate
such conflicts aim to reduce inconsistency and improve generation factuality
35
. Examples include counterfactual argument fine-tuning
25
, and external model for output evaluation
23
;
39
. As for a model-centric solution,
14
proposes a pruning method based on information flow within attention heads to mitigate conflicts in model generation. However, the internal origins of such conflicts remain unexplored. We address this gap by developing a mechanistic-interpretability-based framework to localize how and where conflicting knowledge from the training data is encoded within language models.

Mechanistic Interpretability (MI)

is the field in machine learning that focuses on understanding the internal mechanisms of neural networks
27
;
4
. Prior work has applied
logit lens

28
and
activation patching

37
for a range of analyses and interventions, including explaining how LMs handle context-memory conflict
29
and analyzing knowledge selection behavior in LMs
38
. In this work, we apply both techniques to localize and causally intervene on conflicting knowledge encoded in language models that originates from the training data.

A.1
.

n_{2}

4
Probing Intra-Memory Knowledge Conflict

As shown in Figure
2
, we develop a framework based on
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{clean}}
to probe intra-memory knowledge conflicts originating from the pre-training data of a model.
In general, we first perform logit inspection
7
in the residual stream of
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
to localize components responsible for encoding and generating conflicting information from the training data
SynWikiBio
(Section
4.2
). We then apply activation patching
37
within
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
to test whether these components causally influence the model’s generation with respect to the conflicting facts (Section
4.3
). Finally, we experiment with cross-model activation patching, an advance causal tracing method that leverages both
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{clean}}
to analyze intra-memory knowledge conflicts (Section
4.4
).

4.1
Preliminaries

Logit Lens

is a technique for inspecting a model’s intermediate representations by decoding the residual stream at each layer, thereby revealing which components most strongly influence the final prediction
26
. Let
x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l}
denote the residual stream input to layer
l
. At this layer, the residual stream is sequentially updated by the multi-head attention module
f_{\mathrm{attn}}^{l}
and the MLP
f_{\mathrm{mlp}}^{l}
:

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{mid}}^{l}

\displaystyle=x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l}+f_{\mathrm{attn}}^{l}\!\left(x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l+1}

\displaystyle=x_{\mathrm{mid}}^{l}+f_{\mathrm{mlp}}^{l}\!\left(x_{\mathrm{mid}}^{l}\right).

(2)

By examining
x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l}
,
x_{\mathrm{mid}}^{l}
, and
x_{\mathrm{pre}}^{l+1}
, we can attribute the layerwise contributions of the attention and MLP components to the evolving residual stream and, ultimately, to the final decision.

Activation Patching

identifies task-relevant components by replacing a component’s activation under a target prompt with its activation from an alternative prompt and measuring the resulting change in model behavior
22
;
37
. Formally, let
a_{c}(x)
denote the activation of component
c
given input
x
. Activation patching substitutes
a_{c}(x_{\mathrm{target}})
with
a_{c}(x_{\mathrm{source}})
during the forward pass and evaluates the effect of this intervention on the model output, thereby revealing components that causally influence the task outcome.

4.2
Locating Conflict-Encoding Components via Logit Lens

To identify components responsible for encoding and generating conflicting information in the model
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
, we analyze logit and probability changes across layers using the logit lens. For each individual
p_{i}
among the
n_{1}
individuals in
SynWikiBio
whose biography contains a contradictory factual claim, we prompt
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
with the prompt
pr_{i}
(Section
3
) and perform the following analysis.

At each transformer layer
l\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, we extract residual stream representations at three locations: the layer input
x^{l}_{\mathrm{pre}}
, the residual stream after going through the attention block
x^{l}_{\mathrm{mid}}
, and residual stream after going through the MLP block
x^{l}_{\mathrm{post}}=x^{l+1}_{\mathrm{pre}}
. Each representation is projected from the model dimension
d_{\mathrm{model}}
to the vocabulary dimension
d_{\mathrm{vocab}}
using the unembedding matrix
W_{U}
, and converted into a probability distribution over tokens via the softmax function:

P^{l}_{s}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(W_{U}\,x^{l}_{s}\right),\;s\in\{\mathrm{pre},\mathrm{mid},\mathrm{post}\}

(3)

Using these distributions, we define the contribution of each subcomponent in layer
l
to a token
t
as the change in its predicted probability across that subcomponent:

\displaystyle\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{attn}}(t)

\displaystyle=P^{l}_{\mathrm{mid}}(t)-P^{l}_{\mathrm{pre}}(t),

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{mlp}}(t)

\displaystyle=P^{l}_{\mathrm{post}}(t)-P^{l}_{\mathrm{mid}}(t).

(5)

We hypothesize that components responsible for recalling parametric knowledge will exhibit systematically different contribution patterns for learned conflicting tokens compared to unrelated but high-probability alternatives. Therefore, for each prompt
pr_{i}
, we track probability changes for three token groups: (i) the ground-truth token
t_{1}
, (ii) the contradictory token
t_{2}
, and (iii) a control set
\mathcal{T}_{i}
consisting of the top five tokens in the final prediction
P^{L}_{\mathrm{post}}
, excluding
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.

For the control set, we compute the mean contribution at layer
l
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{attn}}(\mathcal{T}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{i}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{attn}}(t),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{mlp}}(\mathcal{T}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{i}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{mlp}}(t).

(7)

Finally, we aggregate contributions across all
n_{1}
individuals to obtain population-level statistics. For any token or token set
\tau\in\{t_{1},t_{2},\mathcal{T}\}
, the aggregated attention and MLP contributions at layer
l
are defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{contrib}}^{l}_{\mathrm{attn}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{attn}}(\tau_{i}),

(8)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{contrib}}^{l}_{\mathrm{mlp}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathrm{contrib}^{l}_{\mathrm{mlp}}(\tau_{i}),

(9)

where
\tau_{i}=\tau
for
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and
\tau_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{i}
for the control tokens. Because each prompt
pr_{i}
is tied to a specific attribute type (e.g.,
graduated university
or
employer
2
2

2

we use ’company’ to refer the employer and ’university’ to refer to graduated university to be consistent with previous works.
), this aggregation enables further stratified analyses. In particular, we can identify components selectively important for attribute categories and those specialized to individual attribute values (e.g.,
University of Zinl
), enabling fine-grained model attribution.

4.3
Causal Tracing via Activation Patching

After identifying a component
c
at layer
l
that is strongly associated with conflicting information for a given attribute type
a
(e.g.,
graduated university
) in
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
, we apply activation patching to causally assess the contribution of
c
to the model’s output behavior.

Specifically, for each individual
p_{i}
among the
n_{1}
individuals in
SynWikiBio
whose biography contains contradictory factual claims about attribute type
a
, we define the causal effect of component
c
using the corresponding prefix prompt
pr_{i}
. This prompt induces competition between two next-token continuations,
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, corresponding to the conflicting facts
f_{i}
and
\bar{f}_{i}
, respectively. Let
p_{t}(\cdot)
denote the probability assigned by the model to token
t
. The causal effect of component
c
at layer
l
on the generation of token
t_{1}
under the noisy prompt
pr_{i}
is defined as:

\Delta^{c,l,\uparrow}_{t_{1}}\;:=\;p_{t_{1}}\!\left(\tilde{\ell^{\prime}}^{c,l}_{\text{mix}}(pr_{i},t_{s})\right)\;-\;p_{t_{1}}\!\left(\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}(pr_{i})\right),

and the corresponding effect on the alternative continuation
t_{2}
is defined as:

\Delta^{c,l,\uparrow}_{t_{2}}\;:=\;p_{t_{2}}\!\left(\tilde{\ell^{\prime}}^{c,l}_{\text{mix}}(pr_{i},t_{s})\right)\;-\;p_{t_{2}}\!\left(\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}(pr_{i})\right).

Here,
\tilde{\ell^{\prime}}^{c,l}_{\text{mix}}(pr_{i},t)
denotes a forward pass of model
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
for processing the noisy prompt
pr_{i}
, in which the activation of component
c
at layer
l

is replaced with a cached activation of the component
c
of the same model sourced from a clean, single-fact prompt
pr_{j}
belonging to another individual
p_{j}
among the
n_{2}
individuals in
SynWikiBio
. This clean prompt expresses the same attribute value corresponding to source token
t_{s}\in\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
for attribute type
a
, and the cached activation is taken at the position immediately preceding the generation of the next token
t_{s}
. This intervention isolates the causal influence of component
c
on resolving factual conflicts by measuring how substituting a clean, fact-consistent activation alters the model’s preference between competing continuations.

4.4
Cross Model Activation Patching

Standard activation patching relies on sourcing activations from prompts processed by the same model, which implicitly assumes the availability of a suitably similar yet non-confounded source prompt. This assumption breaks down in the presence of intra-memory knowledge conflict. For instance, a source biography
b_{source}
that is absent from the training data may fail to elicit faithful parametric recall, yielding activations that are unrepresentative of the model’s stored knowledge. Conversely, a source biography that is overly similar to the target biography
b_{target}

risks being conflated with it, causing the model to encode overlapping or competing internal representations. In both cases, prompt selection becomes ill-posed: the model either fails to retrieve the intended fact or cannot cleanly separate the source and target memories, leading to unstable or contradictory activations.

To mitigate this issue, in the previous section we employ clean prompts
pr_{j}
that express the same attribute value but correspond to a different individual
p_{j}
, and use these prompts to patch the noisy prompt
pr_{i}
associated with individual
p_{i}
, whose biography contains conflicting information for attribute
a
. While this strategy partially alleviates prompt–target entanglement, it introduces additional noise into the patched activations due to inter-person differences, thereby weakening the strength and reliability of causal attribution in the presence of factual conflict.

As a resolution, we adopt
cross-model activation patching (CMAP)
, an advance causal tracing method, which we tailor to the study of intra-memory factual conflict. Instead of sourcing patched activations from alternative prompts within the same conflicted model
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
, CMAP transfers activations from a
clean
reference model
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{clean}}
, which is obtained by continuing pretraining the same base model
\ell
on
SynWikiBio_clean
. By construction,
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{clean}}
is trained on exactly one ground-truth biography
b_{i}
per individual
p_{i}
and contains no mutually contradictory factual claims with respect to attribute
a
.
During patching, activations from
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{clean}}
are injected into
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
at corresponding components and layers while processing the conflicting prompt
pr_{i}
. Crucially, the two models are aligned in both architecture and training data: they share the same base initialization
\ell
and are trained on the same set of individuals
\{p_{i}\}
, differing only in whether contradictory claims
\bar{f}_{i}
are present. This controlled alignment eliminates ambiguity arising from prompt mismatch or inter-person variation, ensuring that the patched activations correspond to uncontaminated parametric representations of
f_{i}
. As a result, CMAP enables more reliable and interpretable causal attribution of the internal components that mediate the resolution of factual conflicts in
\ell^{\prime}_{\text{mix}}
.

6.5
Experiments with Qwen3-4B

To assess the generalizability of our findings, we repeat our experiments using a different model, Qwen3-4B
36
. Overall, our experiments with Qwen3-4B yield similar findings as with GPT-2 XL. The comparison also gives us some interesting insights: for Qwen3-4B, we achieve a markedly higher success rate, reaching up to 70%, in steering model generation. And CMAP achieves performance comparable to activation patching across most layers except layer 33. We discuss more details in appendix
A.5
.

6.6
General Findings

Conflicting training data reduces model confidence.

Competing parametric knowledge in later layers introduces confusion and reduces model confidence. Although no single component fully resolves these conflicts, activation patching localizes their effects to a small subset of attention components in the final layers.

Effectiveness of activation patching varies across models.

Activation patching is less effective on GPT-2 XL, with a maximum steering success rate of about 22%, but achieves up to 70% success rate on Qwen3-4B. We hypothesize that these differences arise because larger, more modern language models are better
at handling noise and extracting relevant information. However, this claim requires further testing across a broader set of models. Our results of success steering rate can be used as baselines for future studies.

Cross-model activation patching is a promising alternative for causal tracing.

Overall, cross model patching achieves performance comparable to, or better than standard activation patching in most settings, which supports our hypothesis
that cross model patching yields more faithful results than traditional methods in the intra-memory conflict setting, due to the reduced noise in transferred activations.

Effects of intervention methods vary across models.

Across both models, the identified components are concentrated in the final layers, indicating a shared localization of mechanisms underlying factual generation. However, unexpected behaviors across different conflict attributes call into question whether diverse forms of knowledge conflict can be treated as a single phenomenon.
These observations further raise the question of whether models rely on a unified feature circuit to resolve internal knowledge conflicts, or instead exhibit systematic preferences for certain facts.

7
Conclusion

We present a mechanistic interpretability framework to study the impact of internal representation of LMs in the scenarios of intra-memory knowledge conflict. With logit inspection, activation patching and the proposed cross-model conflict patching method, we localize and intervene on conflicting internal representations at inference time. Our work highlights the effectiveness of mechanistic interpretability for better understanding and controlling conflicting information within the model. Future work will extend these methods to knowledge editing and robust information tracing across prompts and model variants.

Limitations

We acknowledge the following limitations of our study. First, we consider a limited set of conflict types and restrict each entity to a 1:1 ratio with each conflicting information. We plan to include more conflict types and multiple conflicting information per entity in future study.
Second, our results are presented on a limited set of model architectures. While we demonstrate generalization to Qwen3-4B model, further evaluation across additional model families is necessary. Finally, we do not analyze the underlying factors that drive the model’s preference from one conflicting fact over another. Addressing this problem in future work
can provide a deeper understanding of the model’s internal rationale.

A.4
Magnitude Bins

We determine the thresholds for the magnitude bins using statistics derived from the activation patching experiments. Activation patching frequently alters the model’s confidence over the top candidates and can lead to changes in the model’s final output. We track the probability change required to induce a change in the model’s decision. From the list of all observed probability changes, we compute the average scores to determine magnitude thresholds. We sort the data in descending order and calculate the mean probability change within the top 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% of the distribution, and choose thresholds of corresponding magnitude bins based on these values.
