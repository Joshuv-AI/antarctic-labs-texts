Comments on “Clinical-cytological grading: An integrated approach to stratifying CRSwNP beyond eosinophilia”☆
Authors: Matteo Gelardi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Xu Z et al.1 which analyzed the clinical characteristics and inflammatory endotypes of chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) in Southern China.

The authors reported that 75.7% of patients presented a type 2 endotype; that patients with type 2 CRSwNP, especially those with asthma, had more severe symptoms and poorer quality of life; that allergic rhinitis was associated with a higher risk of recurrence but not with greater severity; and that middle-aged and older patients more frequently exhibited type 2 features and asthma as a comorbidity. In summary, the authors concluded that type 2 CRSwNP is predominant in southern China and that comorbidities, particularly asthma and allergic rhinitis, influence severity and recurrence risk, respectively.

These findings are partly consistent with the Clinical-Cytological Grading (CCG), a validated system2 that integrates major clinical parameters (asthma, aspirin sensitivity, allergy) with inflammatory cytotypes (neutrophilic, eosinophilic, mast cell, eosinophil–mast cell). It is important to emphasize that the CCG assigns a specific prognostic weight to each comorbidity (Fig. 1): allergy has a greater impact (score 3) than asthma (score 2), while aspirin sensitivity (ASA) has a lower score (score 1). The combination of ASA and asthma (total score 3), however, represents a particularly unfavorable condition. Similarly, cytological patterns also have distinct prognostic values: the eosinophil–mast cell phenotype represents the highest grade of severity (score 4) and the greatest risk of relapse. Evaluating comorbidities without integrating cytological findings provides limited and imprecise information on both disease severity and recurrence risk. For example, a case of nasal polyposis associated with allergy and an eosinophilic cytotype (score 3 + 2) already has a significant clinical impact, but it becomes much more severe when the cytological profile shows an eosinophil–mast cell infiltration (score 3 + 4).

To achieve such insights, it is essential to integrate studies with nasal cytology, a simple, inexpensive, non-invasive, and repeatable method.3 Unlike histology, traditionally based on hematoxylin-eosin staining, nasal cytology with May-Grünwald Giemsa staining not only highlights eosinophils but also clearly identifies mast cells, whose presence is crucial for defining severity and prognostic risk.4

Another limitation of the Chinese study is that the type 2 endotype was defined mainly on the basis of eosinophils and their mediators (IL-5, ECP), as well as IgE, while no evaluation of mast cells was performed, either alone or in association with eosinophils. No immunohistochemistry (eg, tryptase, CD117) or other specific markers were used.5 This approach, although valuable, provides only a partial view, overlooking a pathogenetic and prognostic element that is now well established.

This concept is not merely theoretical but supported by longitudinal evidence. In a 5-year prospective study,6 patients managed according to the CCG showed significantly better disease control and a much lower need for surgery compared to controls. That study also validated the Prognostic Index of Relapse (PIR), confirming that the eosinophil–mast cell phenotype, especially when associated with allergy, asthma, and aspirin sensitivity (3 + 3+4 = total score 10), corresponds to the highest grade of severity and recurrence risk.

By integrating clinical comorbidities with cytological findings, the CCG anables calculation of the PIR, stratification of patients into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups, and guidance of personalized therapeutic strategies, including biologics.7 In this way, the CCG addresses an important gap left by approaches based exclusively on eosinophilia, offering a more realistic and clinically meaningful stratification of CRSwNP patients.
