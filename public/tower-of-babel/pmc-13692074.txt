The Hidden Costs of Long vs Short Leg Casting in Pediatric Fracture Care
Authors: Olivia Ullmann, Simon Blanchard, Nathan Chaclas, Carter E Hall, Casey C Kuka, Morgan Batley, Daniel Yang, Sulagna Sarkar, Wudbhav N Sankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong leg casting (LLC) and short leg casting (SLC) are common treatments for pediatric lower-extremity fractures, yet little is known about their psychosocial and socioeconomic impact. This cross-sectional survey study quantified and compared the biopsychosocial burden of LLC and SLC on pediatric patients and caregivers using validated outcome measures and qualitative analysis.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was performed among pediatric patients (aged 2-18 years) who presented for lower-extremity cast removal from 2022 to 2025. Patient-guardian dyads completed age-appropriate Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) and Revised Impact on Family Scale (rIOFS) questionnaires and provided open-ended responses regarding disruptions and mobility-related challenges. Demographic and clinical information was collected, and a multivariate linear regression controlling for age was performed to account for baseline differences between groups.ResultsA total of 136 patient-guardian dyads were enrolled, including 68 SLC patients and 68 LLC patients. After controlling for age, patients treated with SLC reported PedsQL scores 11.4 points higher than those treated with LLC (66.7 ​± ​14.3 vs 56.6 ​± ​16.3, P ​< ​.001). Caregiver burden, measured by the rIOFS, did not significantly differ between cast lengths (P ​= ​.138). Patients restricted to complete non-weight-bearing (NWB) reported PedsQL scores 17.0 points lower than patients permitted any degree of weight-bearing (58.0 ​± ​14.8 vs 73.1 ​± ​15.0, P ​< ​.001), while caregivers reported greater burden, reflected by lower rIOFS scores (44.3 ​± ​8.6 vs 48.9 ​± ​7.2, P ​= ​.002). Cast length was not associated with any difference in missed school or caregiver workdays after controlling for age. However, NWB status was associated with 4.8 additional missed school days (P ​= ​.026) and 1.7 additional caregiver workdays missed (P ​= ​.004). The qualitative analysis revealed families struggled with transportation, obtaining medical equipment, physical strain, and social isolation.ConclusionsLLC was associated with worse patient-reported quality of life than SLC, and complete NWB restrictions were associated with worse patient quality of life, increased caregiver burden, and greater educational and occupational disruption. When clinically feasible, treatment strategies that promote mobility and permit weight-bearing may help mitigate the psychosocial and socioeconomic burdens of pediatric lower-extremity casting.Key Concepts(1)Beyond clinical outcomes, lower-extremity casting imposes psychosocial, physical, and logistical burdens on pediatric patients and their families.(2)Long leg casting was associated with significantly lower patient-reported quality of life than short leg casting, even after adjustment for age.(3)Complete non-weight-bearing restrictions were associated with worse patient quality of life, greater caregiver burden, and increased missed school and caregiver workdays compared to any degree of permitted weight-bearing.(4)Caregiver burden was associated with weight-bearing restrictions but not cast length, highlighting the importance of mobility during treatment.(5)When clinically appropriate, treatment strategies that maximize safe weight-bearing and mobility may reduce the psychosocial and socioeconomic burden of pediatric lower-extremity casting.Level of EvidenceIII, Cross-Sectional Observational Survey StudyKeywords: Lower-extremity fractures, Long leg casting, Short leg casting, Psychosocial effects of casting, Non-weight-bearing casts

Introduction

Casting is a common immobilization technique for a variety of lower-extremity fractures [1]. When placing a patient in a cast, a physician may choose between long leg casting (LLC) and short leg casting (SLC) and between a variety of weight-bearing statuses. While some fractures have clear-cut guidelines regarding cast treatment, many lower-extremity fracture patterns can be treated with both LLC and SLC and a variety of weight-bearing restrictions at the discretion of the treating physician. Many existing studies focus on the clinical outcomes of casting, such as fracture healing, infection, and complications, but do not evaluate the potential burden associated with cast immobilization beyond detriments to the patient’s mobility [1,2]. Patients often require assistance with activities of daily living, and families can have their work and home schedules disrupted, leading to psychological stress and financial burden [3]. Despite the importance of these effects, there is a lack of research on the comparative outcomes of LLC ​vs SLC ​on patients’ daily lives.

The purpose of this study is to clarify and classify these often-ignored costs to the patient and family ​and to elucidate potential risk factors for increased impact on patients and families. We hypothesized that LLC would be associated with worse patient quality of life and greater caregiver burden than SLC. Our hope is that this cross-sectional survey study will contribute quantitative and qualitative evidence to the growing body of literature on patient-reported outcomes and the psychosocial impacts of common orthopaedic treatments and help further our understanding of how lower-extremity casting impacts our patients’ lives.

Methods

A cross-sectional observational study was performed using a convenience sample of eligible patients (aged 2-18 years) and guardians who presented for removal of a lower-extremity cast after approval from our center’s institutional review board. Consent from a guardian and assent from the patient (aged 7+) were obtained at the time of presentation. Patients who were not English-speaking and those with an underlying diagnosis of neuromuscular, metabolic, or genetic bone disease were excluded. Those with developmental delays and psychologic diagnoses including autism spectrum disorder and oppositional defiant disorder were excluded. Exclusion criteria also included patients who had sustained a secondary unrelated injury following placement of the cast or had sustained other significant injuries at the time of cast placement such that the cast was not the only treatment modality.

The primary outcome was patient health–related quality of life, as measured by the total Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) score. Secondary outcomes included caregiver burden measured by the Revised Impact on Family Scale (rIOFS) [4], patient missed school days, caregiver missed workdays, and qualitative themes identified from open-ended responses. Comparisons were performed between short leg and long leg cast groups and weight-bearing status, with additional exploratory analyses of individual survey items by weight-bearing status.

Patient-guardian dyads who met our inclusion criteria were administered a questionnaire at a single time point during the cast removal visit at the end of their treatment period. Participants were asked to reflect on their experiences throughout the entire period of cast immobilization. The open-ended part of the patient questionnaire assessed school attendance and transportation availability. Patients were also administered age-appropriate editions of the PedsQL, which assesses physical, emotional, social, and school functioning in healthy children and those with acute and chronic health conditions. PedsQL scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a better health-related quality of life [5]. Age-appropriate PedsQL self-report and parent-proxy versions were administered according to instrument guidelines. The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for these PedsQL scores is 4.36 for child self-reported totals and 4.50 for parent proxy-reported totals (ages 2-4 years) [6]. Guardians were administered the rIOFS as well as an open-ended free response. The Impact on Family Scale and the shorter rIOFS are validated tools to measure the impact on families due to a child’s chronic illness [4]. The rIOFS is scaled from 15 to 60, where lower scores indicate a higher burden on the family and caregivers, but has no published MCID.

Demographic and treatment variables were collected from electronic medical record review and included patient age, length of casting (defined as total duration of immobilization in a cast from initial placement to final cast removal), and weight-bearing status while in the cast. Weight-bearing status was categorized as completely non-weight-bearing (NWB), partial weight-bearing, or full weight-bearing. For exploratory analyses, patients were additionally grouped as complete NWB versus any degree of permitted weight-bearing. An a priori power analysis estimated a required sample size of 67 patients in each cast length based on the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test, assuming a medium effect size (effect size ​= ​0.3), to achieve 80% power in detecting a difference in our primary outcome, the PedsQL score. Descriptive statistics were generated for dyad demographic variables. Mann-Whitney U-tests and/or t-tests were conducted to assess continuous variables based on normality, and chi-squared or Fisher tests were used for categorical responses. Multivariate linear regression controlling for patient’s age at casting was performed to evaluate the effect of cast length and weight-bearing status on the PedsQL score, rIOFS score, missed school days, and caregiver missed workdays. All analysis was done using R (Version 4.5.1, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance set to a P ​value <.05 [7]. Free responses were reviewed and evaluated by the authors to assess for themes and common struggles.

Results

In total, 136 patient-guardian dyads were enrolled from 2022 to 2025. There were 68 patients in the SLC subcohort and 68 patients in the LLC subcohort. The mean age at casting was 10.0 ​± ​4.7 years ​(range: 2.0-18.6). Patients treated with SLC were significantly older than those treated with LLC (12.1 ​± ​4.1 vs 7.8 ​± ​4.4 years, P ​< ​.001). Weight-bearing status was similarly distributed between cast groups, with 103 patients (75.7%) treated as completely NWB, 5 (3.7%) treated as partial weight-bearing, and 28 (20.5%) permitted to fully weight-bear (Table 1). Mean PedsQL scores were significantly higher among SLC patients than among LLC patients (66.7 ​± ​14.3 vs 56.6 ​± ​16.3, P ​< ​.001). Caregiver burden, as measured by the rIOFS, did not significantly differ between the SLC and LLC groups (46.6 ​± ​8.5 vs 44.2 ​± ​8.4, P ​= ​.13). Patients treated with SLC missed an average of 4.6 ​± ​8.8 school days compared with 7.3 ​± ​11.8 days among LLC patients (P ​= ​.067). Similarly, caregivers of SLC patients missed fewer workdays than caregivers of LLC patients (1.51 ​± ​2.50 vs 2.51 ​± ​3.10, P ​= ​.019). Multivariate linear regression analyses controlling for patients’ age at casting demonstrated patients treated with SLC had overall PedsQL scores 11.4 points higher than patients treated with LLC (P ​< ​.001). Composite rIOFS score, missed days of school for patients, and missed days of work for caregivers were not significantly associated with cast length after controlling for age.

Analysis by weight-bearing status revealed that patients restricted to complete NWB reported significantly lower PedsQL scores than patients allowed any degree of weight-bearing (N ​= ​103, 58.0 ​± ​14.8 vs N ​= ​33, 73.1 ​± ​15.0, P ​< ​.001). Caregivers of NWB patients reported greater burden (lower rIOFS scores) than caregivers of patients permitted any degree of weight-bearing (44.3 ​± ​8.6 vs 48.9 ​± ​7.2, P ​= ​.005) and also missed significantly more workdays (2.35 ​± ​3.08 vs 0.97 ​± ​1.61 days, P ​= ​.015). Patients restricted to complete NWB were found to have missed more school days than patients permitted any degree of weight-bearing, but this finding was not significant in the bivariate analysis (6.9 ​± ​11.6 vs 2.9 ​± ​5.1 days, P ​= ​.055). Multivariate linear regression controlling for age at casting found that complete NWB was associated with a 17.0-point lower PedsQL score (P ​< ​.001) and a 5.3-point lower rIOFS score (P ​= ​.002) than in patients permitted any degree of weight-bearing. Patients restricted to NWB also missed an additional 4.8 school days (P ​= ​.026), and their caregivers missed an additional 1.7 workdays (P ​= ​.004).

In an exploratory analysis of individual caregiver rIOFS items by weight-bearing status, the distribution of several responses differed significantly (Tables A.1, A.2). Caregivers of patients restricted to complete NWB most commonly reported increased burden related to social isolation, disrupted family activities, fatigue, and feeling overwhelmed by caregiving responsibilities. Qualitative responses further highlighted challenges associated with mobility restrictions, transportation, and obtaining adaptive equipment. Several caregivers described difficulty acquiring wheelchairs and other assistive devices, while others reported the physical strain of carrying children who were unable to independently ambulate. Discussion

Lower-extremity casting is a common technique in pediatric orthopaedic fracture care. While most commonly used for the treatment of fractures, casting is also used for postoperative immobilization and deformity correction such as in the treatment of clubfoot or stretching of contractures in neuromuscular patients [8]. An estimated 900,000 children each year receive care in an emergency room in the United States for an acute fracture [9]. Previous studies have estimated that in children, approximately 23% of fractures impact the lower extremity [10]. This means that potentially over 200,000 patients are receiving lower-extremity casting in the United States each year, without accounting for patients who may have presented to urgent care or clinic rather than the emergency room, or patients who receive casting for conditions other than fractures. Despite the prevalence of lower-extremity casting in children, there are little data available assessing the impact that casting has on children and their caregivers. We hypothesized that patients undergoing LLC would report worse health-related quality of life and that their caregivers would report greater caregiver burden than ​patients treated with SLC.

Our findings partially supported our initial hypothesis. Patients treated with LLC reported significantly lower overall quality of life scores than those treated with SLC, and these differences remained significant even after adjustment for age. The relatively low PedsQL scores observed across our cohort likely reflect the substantial physical and psychosocial limitations imposed by lower-extremity immobilization during a period of active recovery from injury. In addition, caregivers of children treated with LLC reported a greater reluctance to participate in outings outside the home, suggesting increased social disruption associated with more restrictive immobilization. Despite these differences in patient-reported outcomes, overall caregiver burden as measured by the rIOFS did not significantly differ between cast types. However, no validated universal MCID has been established for the rIOFS, limiting assessment of the clinical significance of observed between-group differences.

Weight-bearing status was also strongly associated with patient and caregiver outcomes. Patients restricted to complete ​NWB demonstrated significantly lower quality of life scores than those permitted any degree of weight-bearing, and these associations remained significant after adjustment for age. Similarly, caregivers of NWB patients reported significantly greater burden in caring for immobilized children; however, the clinical significance of this difference remains difficult to evaluate because no validated MCID has been published for this rIOFS instrument. NWB status was additionally found to have a significant detrimental effect with increased missed school days and caregiver workdays, highlighting the broader educational and socioeconomic consequences of increased mobility restrictions. Collectively, these findings suggest that both cast type and weight-bearing status influence the patient experience during immobilization, with weight-bearing restrictions affecting patient quality of life, caregiver burden, and daily functioning.

Although the clinical significance of the observed differences in rIOFS scores is difficult to determine given the lack of an established MCID, our findings suggest that lower-extremity casting can impose substantial burden on families. As a point of reference, the mean rIOFS among caregivers of children with Brachial Plexus Birth Injury (43) [11] is within one standard deviation of the scores calculated for LLC and NWB casts. However, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given the substantial differences in disease chronicity, treatment duration, and the original validation population of the rIOFS instrument. Tables A1 and A2 present exploratory analyses of individual caregiver rIOFS items. We treated each survey question as independent and tested for differences between the cohorts without an alpha correction. No validated MCID exists for individual survey questions, so the clinical significance of the statistically significant differences is difficult to determine. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously but may help identify domains of caregiver burden for future study.

The overall impact of casting on families should not be understated. One study found that 47% of students missed an average of 35.9 days following an acute orthopaedic injury [12]. Another demonstrated that for every 100 pediatric fracture appointments, 25 working days were lost ​and that 54 days of school were missed as a direct result of seeking medical care [13]. When examining patients treated with any form of cast, Hyman et al. found that a small portion of schools, more commonly in metropolitan areas, would not allow students on crutches or in a lower-extremity cast to attend school during their treatment, which likely contributes to days missed of work for caregivers, as well [14]. Schools that had such policies most commonly cited difficulty accommodating those children’s needs during the school day. The same study identified a lack of special transportation options for children in casts as a commonly reported obstacle for affected families. These findings are consistent with the themes identified in our qualitative analysis, which highlighted social isolation, transportation barriers, difficulty with obtaining adaptive equipment, and the physical burdens associated with mobility restrictions.

The implications of our findings are 2-fold. First, when fracture patterns are amenable to either treatment strategy, SLC may provide meaningful quality of life benefits compared with LLC. Second, weight-bearing status appears to play an important role in both patient and caregiver experiences during pediatric fracture immobilization. When clinically appropriate, treatment strategies that maximize safe mobility and permit weight-bearing may help reduce the psychosocial, educational, and socioeconomic consequences of casting. Future studies are needed to better define the relationship between weight-bearing restrictions, fracture healing, and patient-reported outcomes, as well as to develop interventions that may improve access to adaptive devices, transportation services, and caregiver support during immobilization.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. We relied on self-reported outcome measures, which may be subject to social desirability and recall biases. We also used a convenience sample of English-speaking patients who were treated at a single hospital, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other settings and populations. Formal screening logs were not maintained during the study period, preventing calculation of eligibility, refusal, and participation rates. Consequently, the potential for selection bias within this convenience sample could not be fully assessed. These factors may influence the experiences and perceptions of patients and their caregivers and should be considered in future studies. Furthermore, we did not follow up with the patients and their caregivers after the removal of the cast, so we do not know how their quality of life, stress, and coping changed over time and whether they faced any long-term challenges or benefits from the treatment. Because outcomes were assessed only once at cast removal, temporal changes in patient and caregiver burden throughout the immobilization period could not be evaluated. The psychosocial and logistical burden associated with casting may fluctuate over time, with some challenges potentially being more pronounced immediately after injury or cast application. Recruitment occurred throughout the calendar year, and some patients underwent immobilization during summer months when school attendance was not applicable; consequently, estimates of missed school days may underestimate the educational impact of casting among school-aged children. Patients treated with SLC were also significantly older than those treated with LLC. Although multivariable analyses adjusting for age were performed, residual confounding may still be present.

Additionally, age-appropriate PedsQL self-report and parent-proxy instruments were administered according to instrument guidelines. Differences between child self-report and caregiver proxy report may introduce variability that could not be fully accounted for in the present analysis [5]. Furthermore, the rIOFS was originally developed and validated for chronic pediatric conditions and may not fully capture the unique burden associated with temporary immobilization following acute injury. Although it provided a useful measure of caregiver impact, the instrument has not been specifically validated for pediatric fracture populations. Individual item–level analyses should be considered exploratory ​as multiple comparisons were performed ​and no formal adjustment for multiplicity was applied. Additionally, we recognize the exploratory weight-bearing analysis is underpowered. Given that we still find statistically significant differences, there may be a possibility of inflated false positives (type I error) and/or inflated effect size in our sample.

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the psychosocial, educational, and socioeconomic burden associated with pediatric lower-extremity casting. Our findings suggest both cast type and weight-bearing restrictions influence patient quality of life, while weight-bearing restrictions may also substantially affect caregiver burden and daily functioning. These results underscore the importance of considering the needs and concerns of patients and their caregivers when selecting immobilization strategies and highlight opportunities to improve support, mobility, transportation access, and adaptive equipment availability for affected families.
