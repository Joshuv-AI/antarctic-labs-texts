Diarylpropionitrile, a selective agonist of estrogen receptor β, protects the kidney against ischemia–reperfusion injury and enhances the repair of tubular epithelial cells
Authors: Yong Kwon Han, GiBong Jang, You Ri Park, Hui Jae Lim, Kwon Moo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemia–reperfusion injury (I/R), a leading cause of acute kidney injury (AKI) and often progressing to chronic kidney disease (CKD), is characterized by fibrotic changes in kidney parenchyma. Estrogen exerts reno-protective effects through estrogen receptors, but the specific role of estrogen receptor β (ERβ) remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of diarylpropionitrile (DPN), a selective ERβ agonist, on kidney I/R injury and subsequent fibrosis in mice. DPN administration prevented increases in plasma creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, tubular damage, and oxidative stress following bilateral kidney I/R. DPN enhanced extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK) activation and promoted tubular cell proliferation after kidney I/R. Additionally, DPN administration inhibited I/R-induced increases in interstitial expansion, collagen deposition, and expression of vimentin and α-smooth muscle actin. In contrast, DPN enhanced the proliferation of tubular epithelial cells after kidney I/R. Collectively, these findings indicate that DPN protects the kidney from I/R injury and inhibits fibrotic development, suggesting that ERβ may be considered as a potential therapeutic target for treating AKI and CKD.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00210-026-05405-7.

Introduction

Kidney ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury, a leading cause of acute kidney injury (AKI), is characterized by a rapid decline in renal function and high mortality. Given that the kidney possesses high regenerative capacity, injured renal tubules are repaired within several days. However, delayed or inadequate repair often results in persistent loss of renal function and architecture, accompanied by fibrotic remodeling of the renal parenchyma (Kim et al. 2010; Han et al. 2015). However, despite advances in understanding the underlying mechanisms, effective therapeutic interventions remain limited.

The pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and prognosis of kidney diseases differ between men and women, largely owing to physiological differences mediated by sex hormones and their receptors. Estrogen, a principal female sex hormone, promotes wound healing by regulating cellular growth, development, differentiation, proliferation, and immune responses (Campbell et al. 2010; Villa et al. 2015; Andrew et al. 2022). These effects are mediated through complex intracellular signaling pathways involving interactions between estrogen and estrogen receptors (ERs) (Campbell et al. 2010; Nishi et al. 2013; Deroo and Korach 2006). Epidemiological studies show the reno-protective effects of estrogen in both sexes (Satake et al. 2008; Irsik 2018; Zhu et al. 2019; Hutchens et al. 2012). However, many questions remain regarding the role of estrogens and their receptors in kidney diseases, including AKI and chronic kidney diseases (CKD).

The classical ER subfamily primarily comprises ERα and ERβ (Cheung et al. 2003). ERs are activated by estrogen, related ligands, or through ligand-independent mechanisms, subsequently forming homo- or heterodimers that bind to specific DNA sequences and activate gene transcription (Powell et al. 2010; Ding et al. 2003; Boonyaratanakornkit et al. 2018). ERα and ERβ exhibit both differential and overlapping expression in various tissues (Cheung et al. 2003). Their DNA-binding and ligand-binding domains have 96% and 60% sequence homology (Boonyaratanakornkit et al. 2018), respectively, indicating functional similarity. However, accumulating evidence shows that ERα and ERβ mediate distinct biological functions, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that selectively target individual ER subtypes.

Diarylpropionitrile (DPN), a nonsteroidal ERβ-selective agonist, exhibits a 70-fold higher relative binding affinity for ERβ than for ERα (Meyers et al. 2001). Therefore, hormone-based therapeutic strategies that minimize both steroid hormone-related and ERα-mediated adverse effects may offer enhanced clinical benefits. Studies show that DPN protects against various diseases, including neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases (Suwanna et al. 2014a, b; Pedram et al. 2010). Furthermore, DPN administration has been shown to promote the repair of damaged tissue (Tong et al. 2024). Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether DPN exerts a protective effect against kidney I/R. The findings could reveal that DPN protects the kidney from I/R injury and subsequent fibrosis, potentially enhancing tubular epithelial cell regeneration and inhibiting fibrotic progression.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

All experiments were performed using 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Koatech, Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Mice were maintained with ad libitum access to water and standard chow. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyungpook National University, Republic of Korea. Subsets of mice received daily subcutaneous injections of DPN (5 mg/kg body weight (BW); Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or DPN-dissolving solution as a control (vehicle) beginning 2 weeks prior to surgery and continued until sacrifice (Suppl. Figure 1). DPN was dissolved in sesame oil (MP Biomedicals, LLC, Irvine, CA, USA) containing 10% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, v/v). As described previously (Park et al. 2001), to induce bilateral kidney ischemia, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg BW), and the kidneys were exposed through flank incisions. The renal pedicles were then completely clamped for 25 min using micro-aneurysm clamps. Sham-operated controls underwent the same procedure without pedicle clamping. Body temperature was maintained at 36.0–37.0 °C throughout surgery using a temperature-controlled heating device (FHC, Bowdoin, ME, USA). At the indicated times, kidneys were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for biochemical analyses or perfusion-fixed with PLP solution (4% paraformaldehyde, 75 mM L-lysine, 10 mM sodium periodate; Sigma-Aldrich) for histological studies. Frozen tissues were stored at − 70 °C until further use.

Blood analysis

Blood samples were collected from mice using heparinized syringes or hematocrit tubes. Plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), plasma creatinine (PCr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were measured using a Vitros XT 3400 Chemistry Analyzer (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA). Red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets were enumerated in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-anticoagulated whole blood using an automated Scil Vet ABC Plus + hematology analyzer (Scil animal care company GmbH, Viernheim, Germany).

Histology

PLP-fixed kidneys were paraffin-embedded and cut into 3-μm-thick sections using a microtome (Leica, Bensheim, Germany). Sections were stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Stained sections were examined using a Leica microscope (Leica), and images were captured with i-Solution software (IMT, Cicero, NY, USA). Tubular damage was scored blindly by investigators based on established criteria (Seok et al. 2007): 0, no damage; 1, mild damage with rounded epithelial cells and dilated tubular lumen; 2, moderate damage with flattened epithelial cells, dilated lumen, and luminal congestion; and 3, severe damage with flat epithelial cells lacking nuclear staining and luminal congestion. At least 50 tubules per kidney were analyzed.

BrdU-incorporation assay

In vivo cell proliferation was assessed using a 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation assay as described previously (Han et al. 2015). BrdU, a thymidine analog, is incorporated into newly synthesized DNA during the S phase of the cell cycle. Briefly, BrdU (50 mg/kg BW; Sigma-Aldrich) was administered intraperitoneally to mice starting 1 day before the operation and was continued every other day until sacrifice. Kidney sections were subsequently subjected to immunohistochemical staining with an anti-BrdU antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Frozen tissue was homogenized in lysis buffer as previously described (Park et al. 2001). This homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 g (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for Western blot analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit, and the proteins were electrophoresed on an 8–12% SDS-PAGE gel. The electrophoresed proteins were then transferred to an Immobilon-P PVDF membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Subsequently, membranes were blocked with TBS-T containing 1% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were anti-ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13B (Arl13B; Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA); anti-acetylated α-tubulin (ac-α-tubulin; Sigma-Aldrich); anti-NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA); anti-acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-cystathionine β-synthase (CBS; Santa Cruz Biotechnology); anti-cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE; Santa Cruz Biotechnology); anti-glutathione synthetase (GSS; Proteintech); anti-glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLc; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit (GCLm; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK 1/2; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA; M879, DAKO, Carpinteria, CA, USA); anti-Vimentin (Cell Signaling Technology); anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; Sigma-Aldrich); anti-phosphorylated Smad3 (p-Smad3; Cell Signaling Technology); anti-Smad3 (Cell Signaling Technology); and anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunohistochemical staining

Following deparaffinization, sections were permeabilized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 min and washed in PBS for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating sections with proteinase K (20 μg/ml in TE buffer, pH 8.0) at 37.0 °C for 20 min, followed by a 10-min cooling, and three washes in PBS (5 min each). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections at room temperature for 30 min with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol, and then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min. Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE; Abcam) or anti-BrdU (Abcam) antibody diluted in blocking buffer. After washing, sections were incubated for 60 min with horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), followed by three washes in PBS (5 min each). Color development was achieved using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Vector) solution, after which sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were examined using a Leica microscope (Leica).

Measurement of total and oxidized glutathione levels

Total glutathione (tGSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels in kidney lysates were quantified using a Glutathione (GSSG/GSH) Detection Kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Kamimashiki-gun, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Glutamate-cysteine ligase activity

Glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL) activity in kidney lysates was analyzed using a micro γ-glutamyl cysteine ligase activity assay kit (Abbkine, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescent staining

Following deparaffinization, sections were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 min and then washed in PBS for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 min, followed by cooling for 20 min and three washes in PBS (5 min each). Sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-p21 antibody (Abcam). After washing, sections were incubated for 60 min with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Vector), FITC-conjugated horse anti-mouse IgG, and FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, followed by three washes in PBS (5 min each). Cell nuclei were counterstained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich).

Sirius Red/Fast Green staining

Deparaffinized kidney sections were fixed in Bouin’s solution at 55℃ for 1 h and washed under running tap water until the yellow coloration disappeared. Sections were then stained with 0.1% Fast Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min, followed by rinsing with 1% acetic acid. Subsequently, sections were stained with Picrosirius Red for 1 h and serially dehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Photomicrographs (200 × magnification) were randomly acquired from the renal cortex using a DM2500 microscope (Leica). Collagen deposition in PSR-stained sections was quantified using an image analysis program (i-Solution, IMT, Republic of Korea).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 6.01 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Time-dependent variables among multiple groups were analyzed using mixed ANOVA, followed by Sidak’s post hoc correction for evaluating pairwise comparisons. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons between two groups involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Diarylpropionitrile protects the kidney against ischemia–reperfusion injury

We first evaluated whether DPN induced changes in hematological and other physiological parameters. DPN treatment for 14 days alone did not significantly alter blood cell counts, ALT, AST, and body weight compared to those in vehicle-treated controls (Suppl. Table 1 and Suppl. Figure 2). These findings indicate that DPN treatment for 14 days does not induce significant systemic changes. To assess whether DPN confers renal protection against I/R injury, mice were administered DPN or vehicle daily from 14 days before surgery until sacrifice. DPN administration significantly attenuated post-I/R-induced increases in PCr and BUN compared to those of vehicle administration (Fig. 1A and B). Additionally, urinary levels of the ciliary proteins ADP-ribosylation factor-like GTPase 13B (Arl13b) and acetylated α-tubulin (ac-α-tubulin), previously reported as indicators of kidney injury (Kong et al. 2019; Kim et al. 2013; Han 2017a; Park 2018), were lower in DPN-treated mice than in vehicle-treated controls (Fig.1C). Moreover, DPN significantly reduced renal tubule cell damage at 1 day after I/R (Fig. 1D). Diarylpropionitrile attenuates oxidative stress in the kidneys following ischemia–reperfusion injury

Given the well-established antioxidative effect of estrogen in I/R injury (Singh et al. 2016; Luo et al. 2016), the association between DPN-induced protection and reduced renal oxidative stress was examined. DPN attenuated the I/R-induced upregulation of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), a principal enzymatic source of renal reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas it did not affect the expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), an enzyme involved in polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism and lipid peroxidation (Fig.2A). In addition, DPN attenuated the I/R-induced increase of 4-HNE-positive signals in the renal tubules, luminal areas, and interstitium (Fig. 2B). Diarylpropionitrile accelerates tubular epithelial cell regeneration following kidney ischemia–reperfusion injury

Tubular epithelial cells injured by kidney I/R undergo a tightly regulated regenerative process (Lin et al. 2005; Johansson 2014; Zhou et al. 2016). To investigate whether DPN accelerates repair of kidney tubular cells from I/R, we determined the proliferation of tubule cell by BrdU-incorporation assay. Three days after I/R, BrdU-positive cells were observed within the tubules, with significantly higher numbers in DPN-treated mice than in vehicle-treated I/R controls (Fig.4A). Three days after I/R, the increase in p21-positive tubule cells was greater in vehicle-treated mice than in DPN-treated mice (Fig. 4B). DPN-treated mice exhibited improved tubular architecture compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4C). Since tubular regeneration requires both activation of pro-repair signaling pathways and re-entry of injured cells into the cell cycle, we investigated extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1/2 (ERK1/2) activation and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression at 1 and 3 days after surgery. Phosphorylated ERK1/2 and PCNA expression levels were greater in the DPN group than in the vehicle group at 1 day after injury, whereas these patterns were reversed at 3 days after injury (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that DPN modulates ERK signaling, which is associated with increased cell proliferation during the early phase after injury.

Diarylpropionitrile inhibits fibrosis following kidney ischemia–reperfusion injury

Kidney I/R injury commonly leads to progressive fibrosis, a key pathological feature of CKD. Kidney fibrosis was assessed at 14 days after I/R, a well-established time point for evaluating fibrotic changes following kidney I/R injury (Xie et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2025). Fourteen days after ischemia, atrophy, dilation, and tubular congestion were significantly lower in the DPN-treated mouse kidneys than in the vehicle-treated mouse kidneys (Fig.5A). Collagen deposition was significantly milder in DPN-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, vimentin, α-SMA, and phosphorylated-Smad3 expression were significantly lower in the kidneys of DPN-treated mice than in those of vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 5C). Next, to investigate whether the mild fibrosis was associated with kidney repair after injury, we evaluated tubule cell regeneration at 14 days after I/R. BrdU-positive tubule cells were assessed in mice that were administered BrdU from 1 day before I/R until 1 day before sacrifice. The number of BrdU-positive tubule cells was significantly greater in DPN-treated I/R mice compared to vehicle-treated I/R mice, while the number of BrdU-positive interstitial cells was lower (Fig. 5D). Discussion

During the early phase of I/R, excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) induces oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and mitochondria, thereby promoting tubular cell injury and acute kidney injury (Kim et al. 2010; Han et al. 2017b). NADPH oxidase-4 (NOX4), a major producer of ROS in renal tubular cells, is upregulated following I/R, promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and tubular damage (Kim et al. 2012; Meng et al. 2018). Inhibition of NOX4 has been shown to protect the kidney against I/R. In the present study, I/R significantly increased NOX4 expression in vehicle-treated mice, but not in DPN-treated mice, compared with their respective sham controls. In addition, DPN attenuated oxidative stress induced by kidney I/R, as evidenced by decreased 4-HNE accumulation. These data indicate that the protective effect of DPN is associated with inhibition of NOX4 induction. Liu et al. reported that 17-β estradiol exerts its protective effects in pulmonary arterial hypertension by inhibiting NOX4-mediated oxidative stress (Liu et al. 2019). Ogola et al. demonstrated that G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER) activation inhibits angiotensin II-induced Nox4 transcription via a cAMP-dependent signaling pathway, resulting in reduced ROS generation in vascular smooth muscle cells (Ogola et al. 2019). Furthermore, Zhu et al. demonstrated in HKC-8 cells, a human proximal tubule cell line, that ERβ knockdown enhanced oxalate-induced upregulation of NOX2 and NOX4 (Zhu et al. 2019). Persistent oxidative stress is associated with maladaptive repair processes that lead to chronic kidney injury and fibrosis. In a previous study, we demonstrated that chronic kidney fibrosis following I/R injury is associated with increased ROS and oxidative stress (Kim et al. 2009). Furthermore, we found that high levels of ROS shortly after I/R in kidneys cause oxidative damage, whereas lower levels of ROS at later phases after I/R promote kidney repair, thereby attenuating fibrosis (Kim et al. 2010). In the present study, DPN treatment alleviated oxidative stress, leading to enhanced tubular repair and reduced fibrosis. Therefore, we speculate that post-treatment with DPN after I/R injury may rapidly reduce ROS levels from injurious to regenerative levels, ultimately protecting the kidneys from AKI and subsequent fibrosis.

Glutathione is the most abundant intracellular thiol antioxidant and plays a central role in maintaining redox homeostasis by detoxifying ROS and serving as a substrate for glutathione peroxidases. GSH production is primarily regulated by the rate-limiting enzyme glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCL; catalytic subunit GCLc and modifier subunit GCLm), followed by GSS, as well as through cysteine availability provided by the transsulfuration pathway enzymes (CBS and CSE). In kidney I/R, decreased expression of enzymes involved in GSH synthesis and the transsulfuration pathway, along with excessive ROS generation, rapidly consume intracellular GSH, leading to GSH depletion, oxidative stress, and tubular cell damage (Han et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2011; Cutrin et al. 2000). In the present study, DPN significantly prevented the I/R-induced reduction in renal GSH levels. However, DPN did not prevent I/R-induced changes in the expression of GSH synthesis-associated enzymes, including CBS, CSE, GSS, GCLc, and GCLm. Therefore, we further evaluated GCL activity and found that it was significantly preserved in the DPN-treated group compared with the vehicle group following I/R, despite no significant differences in GCLc and GCLm protein expression levels. Previous studies have shown that estrogen deficiency decreased GSH levels, leading to reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, whereas estrogen replacement restores GSH redox balance, indicating that estrogen signaling plays an important role in the maintenance of GSH homeostasis (Bellanti et al. 2013; Borras et al. 2021). Therefore, our findings suggest that, although GSH homeostasis is regulated by multiple factors, including GSH reductase activity, NADPH availability, and rates of GSH consumption (Lushchak 2012; Lu 2013; Ji et al. 2002; Lash 2005), DPN preserves GSH levels by maintaining GCL enzymatic activity rather than altering protein expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that the protective effect of DPN against acute kidney injury may be associated with reduced NADPH oxidase-dependent ROS generation and preservation of GCL activity, leading to attenuated oxidative stress.

Regeneration of damaged tubule cells is essential for kidney repair. In a previous study, we reported that regeneration of damaged tubule cells following I/R begins between 1 and 3 days after reperfusion, depending on the severity of injury (Kim et al. 2010). Furthermore, delayed or impaired tubular regeneration significantly contributes to the progression of fibrosis (Kim et al. 2010; Han et al. 2015; Jang et al. 2013). In the present study, we found that BrdU incorporation, an indication of cell proliferation, into the tubular epithelial cells was greater in DPN-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice. In addition, PCNA expression increased earlier in DPN-treated mice than in vehicle-treated mice. These results suggest that DPN treatment may accelerate tubular regeneration. Supporting this notion, Campbell et al. report that estrogen promotes cutaneous wound healing through ERβ (Campbell et al. 2010). Similarly, Bian et al. report that ERβ deficiency impairs healing and decreases cell proliferation in the mouse Achilles tendon (Bian et al. 2019).

Accumulating evidence shows that the ERK pathway is required for tubular cell survival and regeneration following kidney injury (Jang et al. 2013; Widjaja et al. 2022; Arany et al. 2004). In addition, estrogen, which exerts protective effects against I/R injury, has been shown to activate the ERK/MAPK pathway (Jang et al. 2013; Abousaad et al. 2025; Strzalka and Ziemienowicz 2011). Therefore, we further analyzed the temporal changes in ERK activation, a key signaling pathway that drives cell cycle progression and tissue repair. In the present study, ERK activation was greater in the DPN-treated group than in the vehicle group at 1 day after injury, whereas this pattern was reversed at 3 days after injury. Although direct ERK inhibition experiments in the context of DPN treatment were not performed, our findings suggest that DPN enhances tubular regeneration, potentially through ERK activation after kidney I/R injury. Furthermore, ERβ signaling may be required for damaged-tissue repair by promoting tubular regeneration, thereby reducing fibrosis associated with impaired regeneration of damaged tubule cells. Supporting this notion, our previous study demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of ERK signaling using an upstream MEK inhibitor impaired tubule repair following kidney I/R injury (Jang et al. 2013).

Reconstructing renal tissue lost following kidney injury requires the coordinated reorganization of tubular epithelium and a stabilizing scaffold to guide the process. While this scaffold, including extracellular matrix (ECM), may be transient once epithelial repair is complete, insufficient reorganization of the renal tubule facilitates the expansion of fibrotic areas following excessive ECM accumulation (Han et al. 2015; Venkatachalam et al. 2015; Bonventre 2014). Consistent with the DPN-mediated enhancement of renal repair following injury, DPN significantly reduced tubular atrophy and dilation, decreased fibrotic areas, and lowered the expression of fibrotic markers, such as α-SMA, vimentin, and p-Smad3, during the recovery phase. Given that maladaptive tubular remodeling resulting from insufficient or delayed tubular regeneration contributes to the progression from AKI to CKD, the rapid and timely tubular regeneration promoted by DPN may have important implications for post-AKI outcomes.

However, our study has several limitations: (1) Although DPN is established as a selective ERβ agonist, we did not provide direct evidence to confirm ERβ involvement, largely due to the limited sensitivity and specificity of currently available ERβ antibodies. (2) This study fails to provide data demonstrating the effects of pharmacological antagonists, which would represent a more immediately feasible approach to validate receptor specificity. (3) This study did not include intervention data targeting ERK signaling to confirm its role in mediating the effects of DPN. (4) Although estrogen receptor genes are autosomal and expressed in both sexes (Safarinejad et al. 2010), as this study was limited to male mice, future studies including female mice are needed to evaluate potential sexual dimorphism in the response to DPN. (5) Although day 14 is a reliable time point for assessing fibrotic changes following kidney I/R injury, evaluation at later time points would provide stronger evidence regarding the long-term effects of DPN on fibrotic progression.

In conclusion, although estrogen is known to exert reno-protective effects, the specific contribution of ERβ to kidney I/R injury has not been fully elucidated. The present study provides evidence that DPN, an ERβ-selective agonist, attenuates kidney injury and promotes tubular regeneration, thereby inhibiting fibrosis progression in injured kidneys. These findings suggest that DPN limits fibrosis progression by facilitating repair of injured tubular epithelial cells and that ERβ signaling may play an important role in kidney I/R injury and recovery.
