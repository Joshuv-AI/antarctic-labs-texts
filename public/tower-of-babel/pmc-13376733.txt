Persistent homology-based multiscale Hi-C analysis uncovers TADs-mediated gene regulation
Authors: Zhen Chiang, Yue Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTopologically associating domains (TADs), computationally defined 3D chromatin structures, play pivotal roles in gene expression regulation and genome organization. However, their structural basis and functional implications remain contentious. The interplay between TADs and other multiscale 3D chromatin structures has not been comprehensively computationally characterized. The extent and regulatory scope of TAD-mediated gene expression continue to be debated. Multiscale analyses spanning hierarchical 3D chromatin structures hold promise for establishing unified structural–functional frameworks for TADs. Here, we developed topoHiC—a computational framework enabling simultaneous identification of TADs and chromatin loops from Hi-C contact matrices via persistent homology. Benchmarking analyses demonstrated improved performance of topoHiC compared to four previously published loop callers and six TAD callers. We applied topoHiC to explore multiscale 3D chromatin remodeling and showed that spatial proximity relative to TAD boundaries could modulate differential gene expression probability. We proved the potential capacity of multiscale 3D structures to predict differential gene expression through a generalized regression model. Our findings demonstrated the functional roles of TADs as architectural compartmentalization through establishing structural relationships between TADs and chromatin loops and exploring inside genes and truncated genes respectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52335-8.Keywords: 3D genome, Multiscale analysis, Topologically associating domains, Persistent homologySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Systems biology

Introduction

Chromatin is hierarchically folded into multiscale 3D structures, encompassing A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), and chromatin loops, ranging from large scale to small scale. TADs are computationally defined structures, typically recognized as median-scale organizations between A/B compartments and chromatin loops1,2. Multiscale relationships between TADs and other chromatin structures have been highlighted by recent advances3–6. It has been proposed that TADs are formed by the extrusion of chromatin loops, where the boundary complex, including CCCTC binding factor (CTCF), halt the accumulation of extruded loops leading to the demarcation of TADs7–10. This multiscale hypothesis bridges two previously disconnected 3D chromatin structures at different scales. More importantly, it offers opportunities in addressing the structural and functional ambiguities of TADs.

Existing TAD callers responsible for TADs identification have difficulty in establishing multiscale relationships7,11,12, thereby causing the structural ambiguity. Typically, these methods simplify the TADs identification into one- or two-dimensional problems(e.g., linear score-based methods such as Insulation Score13, clustering-based methods such as ClusterTAD14). Such operation simplifies the calculation, but fails to capture the interconnections between spatial structures of multiscale 3D chromatin structures. Recently developed supervised machine learning models rely on training data generated by previously developed TAD callers15–17. Compared with using sophisticated machine learning techniques, the precise and biologically meaningful identification of TADs is of greater importance.

In addition, it is unclear what specific genes TADs regulate, to what extent do they influence the expression of these genes, or how to find target genes that are regulated by TADs. Alterations in TADs might have a bias in influencing the expression of genes located at different positions. Genes located inside altered TADs are mildly affected18, but genes closer to altered TAD boundaries are more likely to exhibit differential expression19,20.

Persistent homology emerges as an ideal method to address these ambiguities by using “loops extrusion forms TADs” hypothesis21–23. It systematically tracks topological structures in 3D point clouds through a filtration process where spheres centered at each data point progressively expand their radii over time. The maximum radii of these spheres are preset by users empirically24. When two spheres intersect, an edge is added between the points corresponding to these spheres, generating an evolving sequence of simplicial complexes21,25,26. Through the computation of homology groups, we quantify topological features across dimensions. Of particular interest is the 1-dimensional homology group (H1), which are characterized as loops with holes in 3D point clouds21,25,26. Previous studies that attempted to identify H1 loops from Hi-C data using persistent homology suffer from insufficiently comprehensive benchmarking against established loop-calling methods, lack the development of systematic scheme for parameter selection, and fail to fully utilize the persistent homology’s inherent, unique multiscale analytical capabilities25,26.

Here, we present topoHiC—a computational pipeline that enables multiscale Hi-C analysis using persistent homology. TopoHiC is a two-stage pipeline: it employs persistent homology to call chromatin loops via computing H1 loops, and then call TADs on genomic regions enriched with chromatin loops. To demonstrate the reliability and reproducibility of topoHiC, we benchmark the performance of topoHiC against two existing chromatin loop callers and six existing TAD callers. Then, we investigate the impact of alterations in TADs on gene expression according to the spatial relationship between genes and TADs. Finally, we build predictive models of the differential gene expression using multiscale 3D chromatin structures as input features to demonstrate the superior predictive capacity of 3D chromatin architecture over histone modifications alone in predicting differential gene expression. We for the first time apply persistent homology to identifying multiscale chromatin structures and systematically evaluate the performance on multiple Hi-C datasets. We also identify the influence of 3D genome remodeling on gene expression based on relative spatial relationship. In all, topoHiC reconciles TADs and loops under a unified premise and establishes a clear structural prediction for TADs.

Results

TopoHiC accurately called chromatin loops with strong loop signals

We proposed a pipeline named topoHiC, which takes Hi-C contact matrices as input and calls chromatin loops and TADs simultaneously. Persistent homology, a branch of topological data analysis, is a statistical methodology that generates spheres with radii r centered at each point and increases r up to a preset threshold24. If the distance between two points is smaller than or equal to two times of the threshold, then an edge will be formed between these two points during the computation process (Fig. 1A)21. To integrate persistent homology, topoHiC generates 3D point cloud based on Hi-C contact map where the points represent genomic bins and distances are determined by the reciprocal of contact frequencies between genomic bins.

The threshold is the main adjustable parameter of topoHiC. Since the distribution of distances between points varies significantly due to different platforms and normalization methods, we design the threshold to be genome-dependent. By default, and in all the following cases in this paper, we let this threshold be 75 percentile of distances between points that are four genomic bins apart on one-dimensional sequences (Methods).

We applied topoHiC and four existing loop callers, HiCCUPS13, FitHiC27, Mustache28 and Peakachu29, on ten biological replicates in Dataset113 for comparison. There is limited consistency in chromatin loops called by different loop callers (Fig. 1B,C, Supplementary Fig. 1A) as previously shown12,16. To validate the loops called by topoHiC, we performed aggregate peak analysis (APA) to measure the aggregate enrichment of computed chromatin loops (Fig. 1D)13. At 5 kb resolution, APA score of topoHiC was higher than that of FitHiC, Mustache and Peakachu in six of ten biological replicates with contact frequencies enrichment concentrated at the exact peak position (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 45). This indicates that topoHiC is reliable in detecting looping structures from Hi-C, which provides a robust foundation for subsequent TADs identification because we assumed that TADs are structurally formed by loop extrusion7–10. In comparison, APA score of topoHiC at 10 kb resolution are less impressive, though they still rank the third in average across ten biological replicates (Supplementary Table 46). We also assessed the agreement of loop anchors with external biological evidence, including eleven histone modifications (H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K9me3, H3K9ac, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H4K20me1, H3K79me2, H2AFZ, H3K4me1, H3K4me2), four structural-related transcription factors (CTCF, RAD21, SMC3, YY1) and enhancers on Dataset1 (Fig. 1E). Although the proportion of chromatin loops called by topoHiC associated with this external biological evidence was moderate, albeit over half, the absolute number of associated chromatin loops called by topoHiC was notably higher compared with that of Peakachu, Mustache and HiCCUPS.

TopoHiC robustly called biologically informative TADs

After chromatin loops being called, topoHiC calculates loop scores for each genomic bin by summing the number of loops that pass through each genomic bin. We propose two approaches based on loop scores to call TADs, merged and individual. These two approaches both recognize genomic regions where loop scores are continuously above zero as the candidate TADs (Fig. 2A). The difference lies in that, the individual approach directly uses the loop scores of each replicate, while the merged approach first averages two loop scores from different replicates and then performs the TADs identification (Fig. 2B). The individual approach is applicable without requiring replicate samples. In contrast, the merged approach necessitates the presence of replicate samples, which may be either biological replicates or technical replicates.

We first benchmarked TADs called by topoHiC on ten biological replicates in Dataset1 against six other existing TAD callers: Insulation Score30, DomainCaller31, Arrowhead13, TopDom32, ClusterTAD14, and OnTAD33 (Fig. 1C–E, Supplementary Fig. 2A,B). We used topoHiC at 5 kb and 10 kb resolutions and we summarized the number, size and reproducibility of all seven TAD callers (Methods). TopoHiC by merged approach demonstrated pronounced superiority in reproducibility compared to other six TAD callers. In comparison, topoHiC by individual approach show limited reproducibility. This result is reasonable because the consistency of chromatin loops across biological replicates is limited and the merged approach improves the robustness. As for TADs number and size, topoHiC identifies fewer but larger TADs when applied to 10 kb resolution Hi-C compared to 5 kb resolution, which is consistent with that reported in prior comparative studies employing different TAD callers34. We also applied TADs called by all seven TAD callers on four technical replicates in Dataset1 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). We found that both individual and merged approach demonstrated good reproducibility across technical replicates, indicating the robustness of topoHiC against technical variations. Given the previous observation, we used the merged approach and 5 kb resolution in all possible cases where at least two replicates are available.

Next, we applied topoHiC on Dataset2 to call loops and TADs simultaneously (results regarding loops detection and validation are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1E-H and Supplementary Table 20). We also applied Insulation Score, DomainCaller and TopDom on Dataset235, because these three TAD callers along with topoHiC yielded the highest reproducibility in the previous benchmark experiment. Consistent with other three TAD callers, topoHiC detected no significant changes of TADs number but significant changes of TADs size after the depletion of GSK3A under two-sided Wilcox tests (Fig. 3A). In addition to the significance observed, we also noted subtle directional discrepancies: topoHiC indicated a reduction in TADs size after the depletion of GSK3A, whereas other methods reported an increase. We speculate that this opposing trend is due to resolution differences, as topoHiC here was applied at 5 kb resolution, while the others were applied at 40 kb. Therefore, we applied Insulation Score at 5 kb resolution and conducted a one-sided Wilcox test, which revealed that under the same 5 kb resolution, both topoHiC and Insulation Score consistently indicated a decrease in TADs size after GSK3A depletion (Supplementary Table 38). Next, we evaluated the TADs remodeling using these four TAD callers and calculated the number of TAD boundaries that are consistent with CTCF, RAD21 and three histone modifications, named supported boundaries, with a 2500 bp tolerance (Fig. 3B). We found topoHiC called TAD boundaries with the highest or jointly highest percentage of CTCF-, RAD21- (control), H3K27ac-, H3K9me3-supported boundaries, and second highest percentage of RAD21- (GSK3A-depleted), H3K4me3-supported boundaries. These results indicate that TAD boundaries called by topoHiC are not only robust but also biologically informative.

Gene expression pattern is correlated with the alteration in spatial relationship between genes and TADs

We employed topoHiC to explore the relationship between multiscale 3D chromatin structures and gene expression using Dataset336. TopoHiC called a total of 5,149 and 4,501 TADs in chromosome 1–22 in Mock and SARS-CoV-2 Infection group (hereinafter SARS group), respectively. Of these, 2,012 TADs were identified in both Mock and SARS groups. We named these TADs as the non-Changed Domains (non-CDs) because their boundaries were not changed after infection, and we named other TADs in either Mock or SARS group as Changed Domains (CDs) (Fig. 4A, B). We identified 1,355 (5.1%) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 25,132 (94.9%) non-DEGs (Methods). Among these 26,487 genes, 783 (2.95%) have transcription regions long enough to cover at least one TAD. We excluded these genes from the following analysis because their multiple exons might be influenced by different TADs leading to regulatory mechanisms too complex to investigate (Fig. 4D).

Given that alterations in TAD boundaries can affect the spatial relationship between genes and TADs, classification based on gene localization relative to TADs is critical for investigating gene regulatory mechanisms18. We categorized 25,704 genes into three categories according to their spatial relationships with TADs: (1) Genes whose transcription regions lie completely inside one or more TADs both in Mock and SARS are called inside genes (n = 22,545); (2) Genes whose transcription regions lie completely outside the TADs both in Mock and SARS are called outside genes (n = 1,137); (3) Others are called truncated genes (n = 2,022). (Fig. 4A,B; Methods). We found significantly more DEGs within inside genes and truncated genes than outside genes (chisq.test p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 1). Therefore, we started to examine the impact of multiscale 3D chromatin structures on expression of inside genes and truncated genes.

We first focused on the impact of TADs on inside genes. Statistical tests showed that alterations in TADs has no significant influence on individual inside genes expression (chisq.test p = 0.1540) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 2), indicating that TADs have no direct influence on all genes that locate inside them. We next investigated the correlation between chromatin loops and inside genes expression. We chose the loop score of the genomic bin containing the transcription start site (TSS) of each gene, along with the loop scores of 10 genomic bins upstream and 10 genomic bins downstream of the TSS. We refer to this 21-dimensional vector as the Local Loop Scores (LLS) of genes (Fig. 4C; Methods). Since the loop score represents the number of local extrusion loops, variations of LLS reflect changes in local interaction frequency. Usually, the disruption of chromatin loops is associated with DEGs37–39. However, we found that non-significant changes in LLS was associated with DEGs (chisq-test p = 0.0204) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 3) (Pearson r = -0.0158, p = 0.0174) and the association was independent from TADs (chisq-test p = 0.1204) (Supplementary Table 4). For example, IFITM3, one of the interferon response genes (IFN genes, they are found to be limited activated in SARS-CoV-2 infection), is found to be downregulated after infection. IFITM3 was found to reside in a SD, and its LLS in Mock and SARS are similar (Fig. 6A), which means that its downregulation could be caused by other biological pathways, such as post-translational modifications40. Further investigation revealed that 96% non-DEGs with differential LLS exhibit a higher LLS. Since higher LLS indicates more chromatin loops that potentially lead to a denser chromatin structure, we propose that this unexpected observation could be due to the inaccessibility of transcription factors to gene regions41–44.

After the study of inside genes, we moved on to explore the impact of multiscale 3D chromatin structures on truncated genes. First, we focused on the impact of TADs on truncated genes expression. We found that the differential expression of truncated genes correlated with CDs that neighboring them (fisher.test p = 0.0287) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 5), suggesting that TADs play a direct role in regulating the expression of truncated genes. Then, we tried to establish the correlation between loops and truncated genes expression by calculating LLS for truncated genes like we did to inside genes. A significant and stronger correlation was identified between the differential LLS and the stable expression of truncated genes (Pearson r = − 0.0601, p = 0.0071; chisq.test p = 0.0124) (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table 6) and the correlation was independent from alterations in TADs (chisq-test p = 0.7910) (Supplementary Table 7). 94% non-DEGs with differential LLS exhibit a higher LLS. In addition, we observed a appreciable difference in the variance of LLS among truncated genes in SARS (Supplementary Fig. 3). The variance of LLS of upregulated truncated genes is smaller compared to non-differentially expressed truncated genes, while the variance of LLS of downregulated truncated genes is larger (Supplementary Fig. 3). We utilized this difference in variance of LLS to help build a regression model to predict the differential expression of truncated genes in the last section.

Differential expression of truncated genes correlates with the moving directions of TAD boundaries

Inspired by the findings that truncated genes are directly influenced by TADs, we further explore whether different “moving directions” of TAD boundaries have different influence on truncated genes expression because the moving directions might be related to the length of the overlap between TADs and genes. We call boundaries moving towards TSS of truncated genes as forward, and the opposite direction (moving away TSS of truncated genes) as backward (Fig. 5A, Methods). Among 80 differentially expressed truncated genes, 57 genes’ upstream TAD boundaries moved forward and six genes’ upstream TAD boundaries moved backward (Supplementary Table 8). Meanwhile, 57 genes’ downstream TAD boundaries moved forward and seven genes’ downstream TAD boundaries moved backward (Supplementary Table 9). Furthermore, we found that, when the two boundaries neighbouring a truncated gene together moving forward after SARS-CoV-2 infection, this truncated gene is significantly more likely to be a DEG (chisq.test p = 0.0020) (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 10). These data reveal significant correlations between “moving forward” boundaries with differentially expressing of truncated genes. For example, we identified one upregulated IFN gene among truncated genes, ISG20. It was showed that TADs related to ISG20 went through a TADs fusion event (Fig. 6B). This means both TAD boundaries nearby moved forward towards the TSS of ISG20, which is consistent with our previous findings.

Predict truncated genes expression using multiscale chromatin structures

To simultaneously explore the relationship between loops, TADs and gene expression, we tried to distinguish DEGs from non-DEGs using the features of TADs and LLS. We only focused on 2003 truncated genes because they were proved to be regulated by both TADs and loops by previous results. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt ever to predict gene expression patterns exclusively using 3D genome features.

We chose two features to build a multiscale model: whether the neighbouring TADs are CDs or non-CDs (hereinafter C/nC), and the standard deviation of ∆LLS (hereinafter sd∆LLS) (Methods). C/nC measures the influence of alterations in TADs on truncated genes expression, and sd∆LLS measures the influence of variations of alterations in local looping structures on truncated genes expression. To address the issue of class imbalance, we randomly partitioned the set of non-DEGs (negative set; n = 1923) into 20 subsets. Each subset was then combined with the set of DEGs (positive set; n = 80) to create 20 balanced datasets, on which 10 times fivefold CV logistic regression fitting was performed. DEG/non-DEGs was considered as the response variable. The resulting median AUC values of this multiscale model is 0.633 (Table 1) suggesting a valid yet modest prediction. The regression coefficient of C/nC =2.02 (p.value = 0.0595). Although the significance test regarding indicated it was marginally insignificant, this result is consistent with our previous findings that truncated genes are more likely to exhibit differential expression when they are adjacent to CDs. We also found a significant regression coefficient of sd∆LLS =-0.22 (p.value = 0.0024). This result indicates that truncated genes are slightly more likely to be DEGs if the alterations in local looping structures are uniformly distributed. This coefficient being close to zero might be related to our previous observation that upregulated truncated genes have smaller sd∆LLS while downregulated truncated genes have larger sd∆LLS. Removing either C/nC or sd∆LLS led to worse prediction (Table 1), underscoring the necessity of multiscale analysis.

Discussion

In this paper, we designed a multiscale pipeline named topoHiC which simultaneously calls chromatin loops and TADs from Hi-C data based on the assumption that loop extrusion forms TADs. To the best of our knowledge, topoHiC represents the first implementation of multiscale Hi-C analysis pipeline that enables simultaneous identification of chromatin loops and TADs, filling a critical gap identified in preexisting algorithms16,45. Moving beyond existing attempts to apply persistent homology to 3D genome architecture25,26, this study introduces advancing steps toward the standardization of chromatin loops identification using persistent homology. Specifically, we first propose a more reproducible approach of parameter selection for H1 loops calculation. We further provide a comprehensive benchmark comparing topoHiC-generated multiscale chromatin structure predictions with those from existing methods. Finally, we investigate the connection of these loops to TADs and explore their correlative relationship with gene regulation, suggesting a plausible functional link.

Our study addresses current ambiguities in both structural basis and functional roles of TADs through a two-phase approach: by first establishing their structural basis through linking TADs to chromatin loops, then investigating their biological significance. The key methodological advantage of topoHiC lies in its capacity to reconcile TADs and loops under a unified premise so that the functional roles of TADs could be examined from a multiscale perspective. This stands in marked contrast to conventional approaches that typically require independent analytical workflows with varying Hi-C resolutions and mathematical assumptions to study 3D chromatin structures at different scales13,16,45. These results motivate future studies to establish relationships between TADs and multiscale 3D chromatin structures beyond chromatin loops, such as A/B compartments45–47, which could help refine TAD’s definitions and discover new biological roles of TADs.

Based on our analysis results, we propose that TADs may primarily serve as an architectural compartments that spatially partition the genome into distinct regulatory domains, though their potential indirect effects and mechanism on gene regulation through insulation remain to be further elucidated. This finding explains why alterations in TADs show limited impact on inside genes, which remain stably positioned within TADs. Conversely, alterations in TAD boundaries redistributes the regulatory domains, thereby causing some truncated genes to relocate into or out of TADs. Such spatial redistribution accounts for the observed significant correlation between the differential expression of truncated genes and alterations in TAD boundaries. Building upon these insights, future therapeutic strategies could be developed by manipulating TAD boundaries48,49. For example, by targeting oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes within truncated genes region, we may be able to adjust their expression levels by stabilizing or disrupting nearby TAD boundaries, thereby restoring oncogene expression to normal levels and lead to tumor suppression.

The lower overlap of topoHiC-called loop anchors with CTCF peaks may reflect methodological differences rather than false-positives, as topoHiC identifies loops exclusively through Hi-C interaction patterns without incorporating protein binding sites. While CTCF and cohesin is related to loops and TADs, a substantial fraction of functional enhancer–promoter interactions are independent of CTCF and cohesin occupancy50,51. Current benchmarking approaches for loop callers often rely on limited set of biological markers, which may not fully capture the structural complexity of chromatin organizations. Future efforts such as a multi-modality validation framework, combining Hi-C and protein binding sites with high-resolution chromatin imaging in a genome-wide way, could enhance benchmark reliability and promote computational method advancement.

The variability of loops and TADs identified by topoHiC across different cell lines also raises important questions about the evolutionary conservation of multiscale 3D chromatin structures52. Specifically, topoHiC called fewer chromatin loops and a larger number of TADs in Dataset2 (HCT-116 cell line) relative to Dataset1 (GM12878 cell line), which is consistent with the notion that reduced loop extrusion makes more chromatin regions to be identified as TAD boundaries. While technical factors such as sequencing depth may contribute to this shift, this discrepancy is more likely driven by the biological variation between two different cell lines which is supported by the appreciable difference in peak coverage for CTCF, RAD21, and H3K9me3 between two datasets. We cautiously consider this inter-dataset variation as a potential opportunity to explore the conservation of multiscale 3D chromatin structures across different cellular contexts. This could not only help understand the functional variability of different tissues, but may also explore the evolution of a wide range of species.

In this study, we developed a framework to integrate biological hypotheses with statistical models. However, methodological and conceptual limitations may affect the generalizability of our findings. The generalizability of topoHiC across species was not validated because the TADs-like structures of different species might not all be formed through loop extrusion53, and furthermore, acquiring high-quality replicated samples in diverse species presents certain challenges. Combining topological data analysis with deep learning might achieve cross-species generalizability16,54. Another limitation of topoHiC is its suboptimal performance in low-resolution Hi-C data, such as 50 kb resolution (Supplementary Fig. 5C). TopoHiC aims to call TADs based on looping structures; however, it remains a challenge for existing loop callers to maintain performance comparable to that in high-resolution Hi-C when applied to low-resolution Hi-C55. We recommend applying topoHiC primarily to Hi-C at 10 kb or higher resolution. We believe that with advancements in experimental techniques, high-resolution Hi-C will become increasingly prevalent in practice, and topoHiC is developed to align with this trend.

It is worth noting that our method relies on ICE-normalized contact maps and current benchmarking tests are performed only in human genome. While ICE normalization is robust and effective in practice, particularly for handling the sparse and noisy matrices such as Hi-C56, different normalization strategies may potentially influence the identification of multiscale chromatin structures. It should also be noted that existing benchmarks are confined to human cell lines. We hope that as the technology becomes more accessible, the performance of topoHiC can be rigorously assessed in a wider array of organisms and cell types.

We developed a computational pipeline, topoHiC, to analyze multiscale 3D chromatin structures, and observed potential roles of TADs in gene expression regulation that appear independent from those of chromatin loops formation. Our findings also suggest that further exploration of multiscale genomic structure and organization could yield valuable insights in gene regulation mechanisms.

Overview of topoHiC

TopoHiC takes Hi-C file as input, computes H1 loops using persistent homology and detects TADs based on the distribution of loops. In this paper, we benchmarked this method on Hi-C data in .cool format at both 5 kb and 10 kb resolution after ICE normalization. We recommend our users to use Hi-C data with the same or higher resolution because chromatin loops are fine structures.

We used a set of triples to represent the 3D point cloud constructing from Hi-C instead of typical 3D coordinates (x, y, z). This is because the sparsity of Hi-C contact matrices could make it difficult to find an absolute coordinates for each genomic bin.

Step2: Computing chromatin loops TopoHiC implements pydory57 (version 1.1.137) to compute H1 loops from the input 3D point cloud. Homology groups characterize the topological features of an abstract high-dimensional space, including connected components (H0), loops (H1), voids (H2) and shapes beyond three-dimension. Persistent homology allocates each topological feature birth and death, which are two “timepoints” that represent the generation and the disappearance of this feature. For example, a loop is generated when all the points around it are linked by edges which are formed due to intersect spheres, and it will disappear when its hole is filled by spheres. The mathematical principles of the calculation of persistent homology are tedious and we refer our readers who are interested to more systematic reviews21,58. We do not wish spheres centered at every point to “grow” forever because it will bring unlimited computation. Therefore, we need to set a threshold that stops the computation process. This threshold becomes the main adjustable parameter in topoHiC, which represents the maximum radii r of sphere. Naturally, only loops that generated before this threshold are retained and considered in the following computation.

Setting the threshold to be 75 percentile of distances between points that are one genomic bin apart (n = 1) on one-dimensional sequences allows only adjacent points to be connected into edges. Such parameter, although might be appropriate for loop detection25 because chromatin loops should be composed of continuous chromatin fragments, it could lead to fewer chromatin loops making the subsequent task of identifying TADs more difficult, which might be due to the sparsity and noise in high-resolution Hi-C data59,60. Here, we tried to loosen the restriction and let the threshold be the 75 percentile of distances between points that are two, three, four and five genomic bins apart (n = 2, 3, 4, 5). We found the number and size of TADs varied significantly when we changed n from two to three as well as three to four, but insignificantly when we changed n from four to five (Supplementary Fig. 5). Therefore, we used the 75 percentile of distances between points that are four genomic bins apart (n = 4) as the threshold. According to our results, we argue that this setting allows topoHiC to call loops with highly significant Hi-C signals and biologically meaningful robust TADs.

TAD callers for benchmark

We used six TAD callers in this paper in addition to topoHiC, including Insulation Score, DomainCaller, Arrowhead, TopDom, ClusterTAD and OnTAD. We used cooltools (version 0.7.0)61 to implement Insulation Score with 1 Mb window size, following the parameter settings used in the study by Forcato et al.12 We used domaincaller (version 0.4.5-r1) to implement DomainCaller with default settings (2 Mb window size). We used juicer (version 1.22.01)62 to implement Arrowhead with default settings. We used TopDom (version 0.10.1) to implement TopDom with “window.size = 5” as suggested by the authors. We used ClusterTAD (version 1.0) to implement ClusterTAD. ClusterTAD produces multiple results with different settings and we chose the one labeled as “Best” which is recommended by the authors. We used OnTAD (version 1.2) to implement OnTAD with default settings. Arrowhead takes Knight-Ruiz balanced weights as input due to parameter restrictions of the java script. Other six TAD callers takes Iterative Correction balanced weights as input.

When we benchmarked the reproducibility in Dataset1, we chose level = 2 from OnTAD results for the comparison of reproducibility because OnTAD produces hierarchical TADs.

In Fig. 1C-E, Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 3, we applied all TAD callers except topoHiC to 40 kb or 50 kb resolution. This is because these six methods were primarily designed for and validated on low-resolution Hi-C datasets such as 40 kb or 50 kb12. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A, applying Insulation Score and DomainCaller to 10 kb resolution Hi-C leads to relatively poor reproducibility. Therefore, to ensure a fair comparison, we downscaled results of topoHiC at 5 kb resolution by a factor of 10 (corresponding to 50 kb resolution) when benchmarking the reproducibility.

Loop callers for benchmark

We used four loop callers in this paper in addition to topoHiC, including HiCCUPS (Juicer version 1.22.01), FitHiC (version 2.0.8), Mustache (version 1.3.3) and Peakachu (version 2.3). HiCCUPS, Mustache and Peakachu were implemented with default parameters with Knight-Ruiz balanced weights, while FitHiC requires users to build input variables themselves using the scripts on GitHub and we have used ICE balanced weights here. While implementing Peakachu, we selected the most suitable model by running “peakachu depth”.

HiCCUPS and FitHiC do not accept input formats other than .hic (the scripts provided by the latter for constructing input parameters only accept the .hic format), so they were not used on Dataset2.

Aggregate peak analysis (APA)

APA was performed using juicer (version 1.19.02) with default parameters, except resolution and chromosomes. We used 5 kb resolution and did not include sex chromosomes. We used P2LL and ZscoreLL to summarize APA as recommended by juicer.

Identification of TADs alterations

We consider two TAD boundaries to be overlapping if the distance between them is less than or equal to 5 kb. Next, we define changed TADs (CDs) and non-changed TADs (non-CDs). Given an experiment involving Group A and Group B, if two TADs are identified in each group with their both boundaries overlapping simultaneously, then these two TADs are designated as the non-CDs. Otherwise, these two TADs are designated as the CD in Group A and the CD in Group B respectively, because boundaries of either of these TADs do not overlap with boundaries of any TADs in the other group.
