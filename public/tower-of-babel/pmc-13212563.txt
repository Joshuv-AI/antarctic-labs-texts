A lightweight hybrid attention network with multi-scale feature integration for intelligent recognition of underwater acoustic targets
Authors: Nahid-Al Mahmud, Tao Zhang, Yasir Iqbal, Farhana Bari Sumona, Anjum Iqbal, Yanzhang Geng, Wajid Khan, Basem Abu Zneid, Ievgen Zaitsev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR) plays a significant role in intelligent sonar and underwater environmental monitoring systems, but successful recognition in underwater environments of complex scenarios is a significant challenge because of the effects of ambient noise, signal attenuation, and dynamic propagation. The recent developments in deep learning have enhanced automatic generation of features in acoustic signal classification, and the vast majority of existing models represent a trade-off between recognition accuracy and computational efficiency. In this regard we introduce Lightweight Hybrid Attention Network with Multi Scale Feature Integration (DCAT) a new deep learning system that combines depthwise separable convolutions to extract local features efficiently in parallel in conjunction with transformer-based global temporal dependency modeling attention modules. The key innovation of DCAT is its adaptive fusion mechanism, which conditionally combines contextual information of the two transformer branches with different receptive fields with varying scales with the purpose of allowing the model to ideally capture small-scale local features and long-range acoustic structures. The framework employs robust preprocessing and feature engineering, including Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR), Root Mean Square Energy (RMSE), Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC), and Chroma features, computed from 22.05 kHz sampled audio, combined with data augmentation techniques—pitch shifting, time stretching, and Gaussian noise addition—to enhance generalization to real-world acoustic variability. Evaluated on two benchmark datasets, DeepShip and ShipsEar, DCAT achieves superior classification accuracies of 98.84% and 99.16%, respectively, while maintaining extremely low computational complexity of only 0.52 million parameters and 6.1 million FLOPs, supporting real-time inference with latency below 0.7 ms per sample. Comparative studies show that DCAT is more accurate and more efficient compared to state-of-the-art networks, including Transformer, ResNet1D, and AResNet, which substantiate its ability to trade-off discriminative power with resource economy. The suggested model sets a new standard of performance efficiency of underwater acoustic target recognition, which offers a promising framework to the next-generation autonomous sonar, underwater surveillance, and marine ecological monitoring systems.Keywords: Adaptive attention mechanism, Deep learning, Feature fusion, Multi-scale modeling, Sonar signal processing, Transformer network, Underwater acoustic recognition, Underwater target classificationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The recognition of underwater targets is a critical component of acoustic signal processing, and it offers the fundamental aspect of the intelligent sonar systems development. Although this topic has been the focus of research in underwater acoustic over the decades, it has continued to be significantly challenging due to the inherent complexities. Acoustic waves are chosen to carry out underwater detection and identification provisions because they propagate long distance in water1. Proper Underwater Target Recognition (UATR) is essential toward the situation awareness and the use of countermeasures in the sea. Therefore, the capacity to identify acoustic objectives (UATR) is basic to sonar activity, allowing possible target resolution underwater. The importance of the method is the broad application, including the monitoring ship-generated noise and assessing ecological impacts. It is also contributes significantly to the development of ship decommissioning strategies2. Human exploitation of marine life is at an unsustainable rate, and marine ecosystem are degrading faster than human life. According to scientific studies, marine ecosystem diversity and organism population have been significantly reduced by commercial shipping noise pollution3. The traditional UATR systems still face significant limitations. These issues involve dynamic target signatures, ambient-noise corruption, false returns from non-target objects. Another complication is the fact that the signals become attenuated due to the propagation effects4–8. This current advance of AI technology and wider access to publicly accessible underwater sound databases have allowed researchers to successfully apply deep learning models. These Machine learning models establish direct relationships between inputs and outputs through autonomous learning of complex nonlinear relationships. This functionality offers novel solutions to the chronic UATR issues9,10.

In contemporary applications, scholars usually convert crude acoustic data into graphical time-frequency images. This is then followed by using CNN architectures to directly obtain classification features of these graphical inputs. STFT creates basic linear time-frequency plots, known as LOFAR spectrograms, which are fundamental in underwater acoustic processing. On the other hand, the Mel-scale transforms were used to introduce nonlinear warping to better characterize the energy distribution properties of the signal11–13. Residual and densely connected networks (ResNet, DenseNet) are better in the UATR application, when using Mel frequency spectrograms. This is interesting even when using classic architectures such as VGG19 do well in typical computer vision problems14,15. The Hongs system integrates a feature multi-scale feature maps into a personalized ResNet 18 framework. It also incorporates optimization of embedding layer and center loss. In this method, a greater underwater acoustic target detection capability is attained16. The time-domain feature extraction method was used in the research group headed by Meng. This approach was combined with SVM pattern recognition of multi class identification of underwater ship noises17. Scholars led by Shen developed an expert CNN framework. This design incorporates psychoacoustic processing units to enhance ship identification performance18. Lius group came up with an innovative model that combines volumetric time frequency representations with deep convolutional-recurrent learning. It was meant to be used in classifying sound in marine vessels with the help of the Ships Ear data2. The research team by Hong came up with a modified ResNet structure that has advanced feature extraction. This model was used to classify at 94.3% in their UATR experiment. This has been achieved based on the Ships Ear underwater acoustic dataset16. The solution of Luos RBM was experimented in UATR with Ships Ear dataset. It had a significant rate of accuracy of 93.17. Its underwater acoustic recognition tasks are highly effective as illustrated in this performance19. A new CNN-ELM framework was suggested by Hus research team. The model achieved 93.04% accuracy in the identification of actual civil ship noise when used on actual civil ship noise recordings. This shows that it is very effective when applied with real-life data20. The transfer learning model proposed by Ke et al. integrates RSSD for high-resonance feature extraction with a CRNNs architecture. This approach was aimed at processing three dimensional features of ship noise. It is an effective method when dealing with big data. It however performs poorly with small data. This weakness is mainly due to its rigorous pretraining procedure2,21. Deep learning has catalyzed progress in various fields, including foundational applications like computer vision22–25, detection of object26–28, language processing naturally29 and Speech enhancement30. As a result, researchers have developed diverse deep learning process to classify underwater targets19,31–34. The ability of deep neural networks to represent features in a very powerful way contributes to this development. Such abilities have made DNNs the new center of interest in UATR studies.

Originally, UATR was performed through human identification of acoustic signals. Normally, the signals would be listened to the analysts as raw recordings or preprocessed audio files in order to categorize targets. This method, however, has a number of apparent limitations and drawbacks. The duration of the manual analysis and effort increases with the increased amount of data. Therefore, it becomes hard to attain real time recognition. More importantly, underwater acoustic signals have a large frequency range with a significant part of it being outside of the human auditory range. The long-term consequences of experiencing these abnormal acoustic signals may be destructive to human health. Various spectral analysis techniques have been applied to identify the target, such as power spectrum examination, LOFAR and DEMON. Utilizing both the frequency and time-frequency domains, these techniques extract the discriminative techniques of acoustic signals in underwater35,36. The process of GFCC and MFCC are still popular in underwater acoustic signal analysis because they can characterize the spectral in an effective way37. Scientists have come up with hybrid systems that combine machine learning and signal processing methodology. These systems convert the acoustic frequency characteristics into predictive inputs of automated recognition38. The powerful learning capacity of deep neural networks enables the autonomous extraction hierarchical features from signals. Consequently, recent approaches have successfully use direct raw signal processing for underwater target recognition39.

In conventional deep learning (e.g., image recognition) applications, raw pixel data is fed directly into neural networks. However, the extended durations and high sampling rates of hydroacoustic signals require pre-processing such as frame segmentation. The direct input of raw data risks overwhelming the model while introducing noise and interference40. Hu architected a neural network to process underwater acoustic signals directly. His design features depthwise separable convolution and time-discrete convolution components specifically tailored for UATR tasks41. Li introduced ASTEM DCNN, a timbre-sensitive deep convolution neural network. It performs line spectrum extraction and frequency component fusion to achieve more interpretable feature representation42. Song introduced a hybrid deep learning approach for UATR. It blends a 1D CNN for extracting spatial features with an LSTM network for modeling time based sequence43. The method of Yangs involves feature extraction of the signal of ship noise and formation of weight learning neurons. It manages to project important underwater target parameters such as the size of the vessel and its state of operation44. As a core component of acoustic signal processing, underwater target recognition enables the innovation of intelligent underwater systems. Marine acoustics Underwater acoustics has long been a focus of marine research. Nevertheless, it is complex enough to be a significant technical challenge.

Transformer architectures are included in recent research on UATR in order to capture long-range dependencies in hydroacoustic signals. This method has been more successful in capturing global spectral temporal contexts as compared to the traditional CNNs45. Nonetheless, conventional transformers require computational requirements that are prohibitive, because of quadratic self-attention complexity. Such a costly price does not allow real-time processing of large acoustic sequences46,47. Depthwise separable convolutions provide a resolution by dividing up the operation. This minimizes parameters and enhances computational efficiency that is not compromised on performance48,49. In this paper, it is suggested that we can use Depthwise Separable Convolutional Adaptive Transformer (DCAT). It is a novel architecture that is used in the Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR). This architecture integrates effective depthwise separable convolutions with local and global characteristics of adaptive transformers attention mechanisms of sequential acoustic data. The depthwise separable convolutions involve the use of depthwise separable convolutions that have a considerable reduction of the parameter but which preserve the high performance of extraction of features. The architecture uses parallel transformer branches, each having varying attention head configuration. The design allows the multi scale temporal pattern learning to be particularly effective in balancing the local processing of convolutions with a global context modeling of transformers. Besides, adaptive fusion module smartly integrates the results of both branches of the transformer, adjusting their weight based on context demands. The adaptive fusion strategy optimizes classification accuracy through dynamic classification of most discriminative features. The model uses extracted features including Root Mean Square Energy (RMSE), Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR), and Mel-frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) for classification.

Training setups

Data collection

Two well-known and publicly available datasets of underwater acoustic targets (DeepShip10 and ShipsEar9 (both targeting ship radiated noise) were measured to conduct the experiment. In this paper, we shall discuss the method of this dataset in UATR. The description for both data sets follows. DeepShip was published by the Canadian Ocean Observatory Network and contains ship noise measurements collected around the Delta Node in Georgia on 2 May 2016 through to 4 October 2018. The information is useful as it represents an actual image of the state of the marine environment at various sea conditions and seasons. The logged information does not only contain ship signals but also natural background noise, marine mammal sounds as well as other noises of human activity. The data set includes four varieties of vessels namely tugboats, oil tankers, cargo ships and passenger ships, which contain 265 dissimilar underwater recordings of unquestioned durations that range 47 h and 4 min. In this study, the data is analyzed in the form of 5 s segments. This was achieved without any repetitions in order to avoid overlapping and a total of 10,000 audio clips were obtained.

ShipsEar dataset is an open access underwater ship noise dataset recorded in a total of approximately 3 h. These were recorded in the natural marine ecosystem in the Atlantic coast of northwestern Spain, that is, in the Vigo Valley and near the port of Vigo. The data was collected in the fall 2012 and the summer of 2013. The materials are 90 WAV files, where the length of the recording is between 15 s and 10 min. These tapes contain sounds of 11 classes of ships, which are categorized in four classes of ship radiated noise in terms of the size and nature of the ship. In order to make analysis easier, the recordings were separated into 5-second sound files having no overlap between two successive files. This gave a total of 1,440 clips. To analyze them, the recordings were re-arranged into five groups depending on the sizes of the vessels, which were Class A, B, C, D, and E.

In order to create the datasets, the recording-wise division was used, so that each recording of each type (tugboats, oil tankers, etc.) was included in only one set (i.e., training, validation, or testing sets) and did not overlap with any other set. For the ShipsEar dataset, an 8:1:1 split was used, allocating 80% of the data for training and 10% each for validation and testing. In the case of DeepShip, the data was divided into the following proportions, 7:1.5:1.5 whereby 70% consisted of the training data, 15% was the validation data, and the remaining 15% consisted of test data, as in Table 1. This division, as represented in recording is such that no frames of the same recording are represented in the training and the testing sets, which is a practical way of avoiding data leakage.

Training parameters

The model development (TensorFlow) is based on the deep learning framework in this research. The model applied is Depthwise Separable Convolutional Adaptable Transformer of Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition (DCAT). During training, the model parameters will be optimized via the Adam optimizer, set to a learning rate of 0.001. Optimization with 64-sample mini batches and categorical cross-entropy loss is minimized. Training is done with Adam and moment decay rates of 0.9 (first-order) and 0.999 (second-order) to obtain better generalization and optimization stability. Architecture is an ensemble of depthwise separable convolutional layers with transformer blocks to allow efficient feature extraction and representation learning.

The systematic frame-based analysis method with hard-parameter settings was applied to extract the features in order to make the results consistent and reproducible across all experiments. The audio samples were cut at a constant 5.0 s and sampled at 22,050 Hz. Frame-level analysis was done with 2048-sample frames (~ 93 ms) and a 512-sample hop length (~ 23 ms), a Hann window function and a 2048 sample FFT size. To extract spectral features, 128 Mel filter banks were involved, and 13 MFCC coefficients were calculated to indicate the spectral envelope. Chroma features were extracted using 12 pitch class bins (C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B) to capture harmonic content. To prevent data leakage, the datasets were split at the recording level. Using a fixed random seed of 42 for reproducibility, DeepShip was divided into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing, while ShipsEar was split into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing. The application was based on librosa 0.10.0 that processed audio, scikit-learn 1.3.0 that normalized features with StandardScaler (zero mean, unit variance), and TensorFlow 2.12.0 that developed the model. All these variables were maintained at a constant during the experiment in order to compare directly and reproduce the results of the experiment. The training program is based on 30 epochs including early stopping and dynamic learning rate decay to improve generalization and model accuracy. Training was done in a dual-GPU architecture (NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti) that had CUDA 12.2 (Driver: 535.171.04). The computing resources allow optimized throughput in batch processing and calculation of gradient during training.

Conclusion

This paper introduced a new Underwater Acoustic Target Recognition (UATR) model, the Depthwise Separable Convolutional Adaptive Transformer (DCAT), that can effectively combine both depthwise separable convolutions and adaptive multi-branch transformer attention functions to extract local and global features simultaneously on underwater acoustic signals. The proposed DCAT architecture integrates the advantages of convolutional efficiency with transformer-based contextual modeling to provide a powerful tradeoff between accuracy and generalization and computational efficiency, which are vital in sonar and underwater monitoring systems today. The advantages of DCAT were confirmed through experimental assessment on two benchmark datasets DeepShip and ShipsEar, with performance of 98.84% (DeepShip) and 99.16% (ShipsEar), but with an extremely lightweight design that has only 0.52 million parameters and 6.1 million FLOPs. These findings validate the ability of this model to provide near real-time inference (0.7 ms per sample) on very high-end platforms (e.g. NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti), and can be deployed on intelligent sonar and embedded acoustic recognitions platforms, but actual results will depend on the hardware in place. In addition, the combination of Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR), Root Mean square Energy (RMSE), Mel- Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC), and Chroma features were very important in enhancing feature discrimination and strength regarding variable underwater acoustic environments. Although these have been made, some limitations remain; the model uses predetermined feature extraction and thus does not adopt adaptability in very dynamic or noisy circumstances and may be limited to hardware throughput due to the resource constraints of embedded systems, especially in resource-limited embedded systems. The next generation of this project will be to improve robustness in extreme noise, to include self-supervised and semi-supervised learning models that remove the reliance on labeled data, and to optimize the architecture to support edge computing and real-time computing. The next upgrades to the framework should be the expansion of the framework to end-to-end learning pipelines and the combination of unsupervised feature representation, which will enhance adaptability in unpredictable acoustic settings. Overall, the proposed DCAT model defines a new performance efficient performance-efficiency point of underwater acoustic target recognition which provides a scalable, accurate, and computationally compact solution to future-generation autonomous sonar, marine surveillance, and underwater intelligent sensing systems making DCAT a potentially promising platform to build upon in the future concerning adaptable acoustic signal processing and smart maritime sensing systems.
