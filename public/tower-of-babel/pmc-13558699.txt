Large-scale admixture mapping in the All of Us Research Program improves the characterization of cross-population phenotypic differences
Authors: Ravi Mandla, Zhuozheng Shi, Kangcheng Hou, Ying Wang, Georgia Mies, Alan J. Aw, Sinead Cullina, Ravi Mandla, Ravi Mandla, Zhuozheng Shi, Georgia Mies, Alan Aw, Iain Mathieson, Bogdan Pasaniuc, Eimear Kenny, Iain Mathieson, Elizabeth G. Atkinson, Alicia R. Martin, Bogdan Pasaniuc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Admixed individuals have been understudied in medical research largely due to their complex genetic ancestries. However, the consideration of admixture can identify ancestry-enriched genetic associations, delineating genetic underpinnings of cross-population phenotypic variation. Here, we performed admixture mapping in individuals with inferred admixture from African and European populations (N = 48,921). Across 22 traits, we identified 71 ancestry-trait associations, including loci where ancestral haplotypes explained phenotypic variation yet were missed by single-variant association testing due to their stricter multiple testing burden. One such locus where inferred local AFR ancestries are associated with increased hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 12q14.3, highlighting its potential role in explaining differences between populations. Together, our results expand upon the phenotypic differences between populations and characterize loci where genetic ancestries play a critical role in the architecture of disease.

Introduction

Genome wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified thousands of genetic loci associated with a range of diseases and related quantitative traits over the past 15 years1. GWAS results are increasingly being leveraged into clinical insights, including the identification of novel drug targets2,3 and improved disease risk prediction using polygenic scores (PGS)4,5. Historically, many of these studies were conducted within populations genetically similar to European individuals6. However, growing recognition of the importance of diversifying genetics research has spurred multiple global initiatives to include more underrepresented population groups within genetic studies7–15. Such efforts have led to improvements in PGS portability16,17, fine-mapping18,19, and the identification of association signals with cross-ancestry heterogeneity7,20.

Despite broader inclusion of these populations, GWAS often continue to exclude recently admixed individuals, defined here as descendants of historically isolated ancestral populations within the last 20 generations. The genomes of these individuals contain many segments from different ancestral populations with differing linkage disequilibrium (LD) and allele frequency (AF) patterns, referred to as local ancestry, which complicate adjustments to population structure in disease mapping. Inclusion of admixed individuals can also make downstream uses of association scans, such as fine-mapping and colocalization, more difficult as they require reference panels from pre-defined ancestral populations21–23. However, as admixed individuals cannot be grouped into single homogenous clusters yet represent a large fraction of the population24,25, expanding their inclusion in genetic analyses is crucial for translating genetic insights into discoveries.

The unique diversity of genomes of admixed individuals can also shed light on the genetic architecture of traits. For example, previous work has explored how association mapping across different populations can be meta-analyzed to identify signals with evidence of ancestry-correlated heterogeneity in allelic effects26. However, non-genetic differences between the populations such as environmental and ascertainment bias also confound such insights, muddying their interpretation. These biases can be attenuated when studying admixed populations, which tend to share similar environmental and ascertainment contexts but contain different genetic segments with local ancestry from multiple populations. This allows for more robust associations of cross-population genetic variations with traits of interest27. One such method, called admixture mapping (ADM), tests for associations between local ancestry segments and traits of interest. Consequently, ADM is well-powered for identifying trait-associated loci with different allele frequencies between ancestral populations28–31 and can identify associations missed in GWAS due to the reduced multiple testing burden32. ADM has also previously identified several important genes relevant to disease prevalence differences such as the APOL1 locus in end stage kidney disease (ESKD)33–36. Furthermore, deviations in local ancestry proportions from genome-wide averages may indicate regions that have undergone selection since admixture31. Historically, however, ADM has had several limitations. First, the small sample size of admixed individuals in most genetic datasets have limited the statistical power for ADM37. Second, most ADM initiatives have been performed in specific disease cohorts, preventing robust multi-trait comparisons that could guide ideal use cases of ADM. Third, ADM increases power to detect associations when the causal variant(s) have different allele frequencies between populations, which may not reflect most causal architectures of common disease29. Finally, despite increases in the diversity of biobanks, the lack of inferred local ancestry data prevents larger, standardized genetic analyses within admixed genomes.

Here, we performed local ancestry inference for nearly 250 K participants in the All of Us Research Program (AoU), a diverse US-based biobank with linked genetic, electronic health record (EHR) data, and survey information13,38. Leveraging this local ancestry data, we identified 48,921 genetically-inferred two-way African-European admixed genomes (AFR-EUR Admixed). We then hypothesized that ADM within AFR-EUR Admixed individuals would identify novel trait-associated variants not identified in previous GWAS from European populations, due to both the enrichment of these variants in African populations and the lower multiple-testing burden of ADM. We used admixture mapping with 22 binary and quantitative traits, discovering over 50 genetic loci associated with at least one phenotype. Many of our associations were independent of an established GWAS signal, including loci without previously identified disease associations. Together, these results highlight the power of including admixed individuals in genetic studies to uncover biological mechanisms that underly cross-population trait variations.

Results

Characterization of admixture within The All of Us Research Program

To assess levels of admixture within the US, we extracted common variation from short read whole genome sequencing (srWGS) data for 245,394 AoU participants, then performed global and local ancestry inference using four superpopulation labels from the 1000 Genomes Project (1000 G; African: AFR; European: EUR; East Asian: EAS; South Asian: SAS) and 1000 G samples from Lima, Peru, which we refer to as Native American (NAT; Methods). Using inferred global ancestry proportions, we subset the data to unrelated AFR-EUR Admixed individuals (N = 48,921; Fig. 1A; Fig. 1B; Methods). Previous studies have explored selection within admixed popululations31,33,39, where genomic loci with local ancestry frequencies significantly differing from the average over the whole genome could indicate regions under selection since admixture. We tested for evidence of selection in AFR-EUR Admixed individuals within AoU, a dataset almost twice as large as those previously used for similar analyses39 (Methods). With both the same QC steps and p-value cutoff previously described39, we found the inferred local European ancestry was significantly depleted in the HLA locus relative to the rest of the genome (Fig. 2; Methods). However, when we re-ran this analysis on individuals inferred to have almost entirely EUR genome-wide ancestry, we found a similar depletion at the HLA, suggesting that the depletion is due to biased errors in local ancestry inference at complex genomic regions rather than selection40,41 (Supplementary Fig. 4). To further determine if there were any phenotypic consequences of this observed depletion, we tested for associations between inferred European ancestry at the HLA locus and 1,389 phecodes and 16 immune-relevant labs within AoU (Methods). Identification of genomic loci associated with trait differences across populations

Evaluating local ancestry associations with various phenotypes can highlight trait-associated loci and shed light on both cross-ancestry genetic heterogeneity in allele frequencies and the potential genetic underpinnings of phenotype differences between populations. For example, diseases such as breast cancer, diabetes, and coronary artery disease are known to have lower prevalence rates in African populations42–44 while rates for chronic kidney disease are lower in European populations45, though the contributions of genetic factors relative to environmental factors for these differences remain unknown. To further characterize these phenotypic differences, we extracted 22 traits (7 binary and 15 quantitative) from AoU and explored differences between non-admixed individuals with high genetic similarity to African populations and non-admixed individuals with high genetic similarity to European populations (Methods). Across all 15 quantitative traits, 11 (73.3%) had a significant difference in the average between the two populations (Welch’s t-test P < 0.05), including traits with known differences between African and European populations such as hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)46 and neutrophil count47 (Supplementary Data 4; Supplementary Fig. 5). Similarly for the 7 binary traits, 6 (85.7%) had significantly different prevalence rates (chi2 test P < 0.05), including ESKD48 and breast cancer49 (Supplementary Data 4; Supplementary Fig. 5). In total, 17 traits had significant differences between individuals with genetic similarity to European or African populations. While differences in these traits reflect a combination of environmental and genetic factors, we hypothesized ADM could specifically be useful for identifying potential genetic factors underlying these differences.

To this end, we performed ADM within AFR-EUR Admixed individuals for all 22 traits (Methods). ADM identified a total of 59 independent loci significantly associated with 17 (77.3%) of the traits tested (P < 5××10−5), for a total of 71 different locus-to-trait associations (Figs. 3A, 3B; Supplementary Data 5; Methods). Among these hits included well-characterized admixture associations, such as that between AFR ancestries at the Duffy locus and neutrophil count50, and between AFR ancestries at the APOL1 locus and ESKD33,34,51 (Supplementary Data 5). Thirteen (76.4%) of the 17 traits previously identified as having significant differences in prevalence or averages between individuals with genetic similarity to European or African populations had an ADM association. As QC, we tested whether errors in local ancestry inference due to low-complexity regions52 or long-range LD regions40 could explain some of these associations, and found minimal overlap between our associated loci and low-complexity regions (Supplementary Data 6; Methods). One ADM association, with height at 20q11.22, overlapped with a long-range LD region. Twelve (20.4%) of the loci were associated with multiple traits, suggestive of shared cross-population genetic architecture for related traits. We tested these associations for evidence of pairwise colocalization, defined as having a posterior probability of sharing a causal signal greater than 0.8. In total, 11 (91.7%) of the multi-trait associated loci demonstrated evidence of colocalization, which is expected given the high correlation between many of the traits (i.e. hip circumference and BMI; Supplementary Data 7; Supplementary Fig. 6; Methods). As an example, ADM in AoU identified 8 loci where inferred local African ancestry is significantly associated with BMI. When comparing these results to available All by All AoU GWAS in participants AoU previously denoted as genetically similar to European and African reference populations38, we found 6 loci with ADM significance only (Fig. 3C, left), 2 loci identified also with GWAS (Fig. 3C, center), and additional loci ADM fails to identify but are found in a GWAS, even if the locus is differentially significant between subgroups (Fig. 3C, right) when using the traditional genome-wide significance threshold for GWAS (P < 5××10-8). These latter examples may be due to several factors, including the limited power of current available sample sizes for ADM or causal variants with similar frequencies on AFR and EUR haplotypes. As another example, one of the strongest ADM associations was at 17q22 with height, where inferred local AFR ancestry associated with increased height. This wide ADM signal overlapped with multiple known GWAS signals9, with the AFR-enriched allele of the lead variant also associated with increased height (Supplementary Fig. 8). This suggests high concordance between both approaches and that this ADM association may be tagging multiple causal variants in this region.

For replication, we conducted ADM on 20 of the 22 previously defined traits (4 binary and 16 quantitative) in 4,326 AFR-EUR Admixed individuals from the UK Biobank (UKBB; Supplementary Data 8; Supplementary Fig. 9; Methods) and on 19 of the 22 traits (7 binary and 12 quantitative) in 9,324 previously-defined AFR-EUR Admixed individuals from the Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB; Supplementary Data 9; Methods)53. Power analyses indicated that only the association between the Duffy locus and white blood cell count would replicate in both UKBB and PMBB, while the association between the Duffy locus and neutrophil count would replicate only in UKBB (Methods). Indeed, both signals did replicate as suggested from the power analyses (Supplementary Data 10; 11; Methods). We also replicated 6 additional ADM associations in PMBB, including the 17q22 association (Supplementary Data 11; Methods). Since power to detect replication in UKBB and PMBB was low, we compared ADM effect size directions between datasets. We found effect size estimates were strongly correlated and had consistent effect directions between AoU and PMBB (Pearson R: 0.5; P = 6.5××10-5), especially for nominal associations in PMBB (P < 0.05; Pearson R = 0.9, P = 1.9××10-9), suggesting shared signals but limited power in PMBB (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). Effect size correlations were low with UKBB (Pearson R: 0.1; P = 0.27), though higher for nominal associations (P < 0.05; Pearson R = 0.6, P = 4.8××10-3; Supplementary Fig. 10C, D), likely due to both the lower sample size of UKBB and potential differences in population histories between UKBB and AoU participants.

Genetic architecture underlying ADM associations

Previous studies have also demonstrated that ADM is most powered to detect causal signals with differing allele frequencies between ancestries29. To characterize the tagging signals we capture with ADM, we re-ran ADM conditioning on variants reported to be significantly associated with the same trait in the GWAS catalog54 (Supplementary Data 12; Methods). Of the 71 ADM associations, 69 (97%) were within 5 Mb of a published GWAS signal and 18 (25.4%) decreased in significance after conditional analysis (P > 1××10-4), suggesting the conditioned variant may partially explain the ADM signal (Fig. 4A). Among these signals included the previously mentioned association between 20q11.22 and height, which could be mapped to known height GWAS signal at rs6058363, with different allele frequencies between AFR and EUR populations (AFR AF = 0.72, EUR AF = 0.18). This suggests the ADM association is unlikely to be a false positive due to local ancestry inference errors from long-range LD regions. To more broadly confirm if conditional analyses correctly identified variants tagging ADM signals, we compared differences in the alternate allele frequencies (altAF) between AFR and EUR populations of these variants. As expected, these conditioned variants had a significantly larger absolute difference between the altAF in AFR and EUR populations when compared to variants which did not decrease ADM significance after conditioning (Mann-Whitney U P = 1.0××10-4; Fig. 4B). Alternate alleles of variants which did not condition an ADM association were also more common in EUR populations compared to AFR populations, suggesting conditional analyses may be underpowered for some of these variants since average EUR global ancestry proportions in the AoU AFR-EUR Admixed populations were smaller than the average AFR global ancestry proportions (Supplementary Data 1). Variants which conditioned an ADM association also had a significantly larger absolute log fold change between the altAFs of AFR and EUR populations (Mann-Whitney U P = 3.2××10-4; Supplementary Fig. 11A). When comparing the variants’ effect sizes from their associations with the traits in AoU participants genetically similar to AFR (AFR-like) and EUR (EUR-like) populations (Methods), we also found significantly larger effect size differences for variants that decreased ADM significance after conditioning compared to those that did not (Mann-Whitney U P = 5.4××10-7; Fig. 4C). The conditional variant with the largest effect size difference between AFR-like and EUR-like populations in AoU was rs36086195, which conditioned the association between 1p36.13 and total cholesterol (TC; Fig. 5D). Although the alternate allele of rs36086195 is more common in EUR populations (AF = 0.56) relative to AFR (AF = 0.05; Fig. 5E), the effect size in AFR-like populations (Beta (95% CI) = -2.2 (-3.9,-0.4); P = 0.02) was larger than that in EUR-like populations (Beta (95% CI) = -0.3 (-0.7,0.2); P = 0.30; AFR-like beta vs EUR-like beta Z-test P < 10-300; Fig. 5F). This could indicate differences in LD structure, statistical power, or differences in true causal effect sizes. Interestingly, the effect sizes are largely similar between AFR-like and EUR-like populations in the paper which reported this association62, suggesting unique LD structures in AoU or non-genetic factors such as cohort differences. This previous publication also performed fine-mapping on their multi-ancestry GWAS for TC, identifying three variants within the 95% credible set of this locus, including rs36086195. LD structure between these three variants differed greatly between AFR-like and EUR-like populations (rs36086195 vs rs924204: AFR-like R2 = 0.47, EUR-like R2 = 0.94; rs36086195 vs rs1497406: AFR-like R2 = 0.49, EUR-like R2 = 0.99; rs924204 vs rs1497406: AFR-like R2 = 0.82, EUR-like R2 = 0.94). This may suggest that LD differences between tagged and causal signals, rather than differences in true effect sizes, likely explains the difference in observed effect size between populations. rs36086195 is a non-coding variant 1.9 Mb upstream of the lead ADM signal and is closest to the gene EPHA2, which encodes the ephrin type-A receptor 2, is involved in cell-cell signaling, and interacts with cholesterol63–65. rs36086195 is also associated with liver chromatin accessibility and alanine transaminase, highlighting the role of this locus in metabolic function66. In summary, these results demonstrate the utility of ADM to identify loci with different allele frequencies and heterogeneous effect sizes of variants that either are differentially tagging the causal signal or—less likely—are the causal signal itself.

We also checked for overlap of our ADM associations with GWAS signals from AFR-like individuals in the Million Veteran’s Program (MVP)67 and found that 31 (64.6%) of the ADM signals with a corresponding phenotype in MVP were within 3 Mb of an MVP GWAS signal (Methods). Furthermore, an additional 10 (20.8%) of ADM signals were within 3 Mb of an MVP GWAS signal that did not reach the GWAS genome-wide significance threshold but did reach the ADM significance threshold (Supplementary Fig. 12). Thirty-one (75.6%) of these 41 signals also had consistent effect directions between ADM and GWAS relative to AFR ancestry. These 31 variants also had larger absolute differences in altAF (absolute AF difference = 0.39) compared to the 10 variants with inconsistent effect directions (absolute AF difference = 0.13), suggesting these 31 may truly tag the ADM associations. Overall, this demonstrates that most ADM signals could in principle be identified in sufficiently large GWAS in AFR-like individuals. However, many additional signals may not reach the traditional significance threshold used in GWAS.

ADM uncovers additional genetic associations over GWAS

The low number of ADM associations which decreased in significance after conditioning on a GWAS catalog variant suggests that the ADM results are tagging genetic signals that previous GWAS have not reported. These signals may have been unreported due to the historically low representation of individuals genetically similar to African populations in GWAS and/or the lower significance threshold of ADM. We performed single variant association analyses within the same AFR-EUR ADM population on all common variants within 3 Mb of an ADM (Methods). Only 23 (32.4%) of ADM signals were within 3 Mb of a variant with P < 5×10-8, the traditional significance threshold in GWAS, while 69 (97.2%) ADM signals were within 3 Mb of a variant with P < 5×10-5, the significance threshold used here for ADM (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Data 13). As local ancestry associations may tag underlying variant associations, we expect the effect sizes of ADM association to be less than or equal to those of GWAS. Indeed, we found that all of the ADM signals near a P < 5×10-8 variant and 63 (91.3%) of the ADM signals near a P < 5×10-5 variant had lower absolute effect sizes compared to the most-significant GWAS signal, matching previous findings28. This includes the association of the Duffy locus with neutrophil count (Fig. 6B, C). As a sensitivity analysis, we used a larger window of 5 Mb to identify overlaps of ADM and GWAS signals and found similar trends (Supplementary Fig. 13). To identify single variants potentially underlying ADM associations, we ran conditional analyses on all suggestive variants (P < 1××10-4; Methods). Sixty-six (91.7%) ADM associations decreased in significance (P > 1××10-4) after conditioning on one of 2,195 variants (Fig. 6D; Supplementary Data 13). The remaining 5 associations, all with height, may represent cases where the underlying variant is not within the srWGS data and/or there are multiple underlying variants, or potential false positives. We found that while 17 (25.8%) of the 66 associations mapped to a variant with an association P < 5×10-8 within the AFR-EUR Admixed individuals, 48 (72.7%) of the associations mapped to a variant with P < 5×10-5 (Fig. 6D). This suggests that most of the signals identified in ADM but not in GWAS are found due to the lower testing burden of ADM. In total, 11 (16.7%) of these 66 ADM associations had larger effect sizes than any of their mapped variants. While this may indicate potential false positives, they may also indicate cases where the underlying variant is not within the srWGS data, there are multiple underlying variants, or the estimated effect sizes differ due to differences in local ancestry haplotype frequencies and allele frequencies. VEP analyses revealed that almost all these ADM associations could be mapped to a variant upstream (98.5%) or downstream (98.0%) of a gene (Supplementary Data 14; Methods). Furthermore, 35 (53.0%) of these 66 ADM associations mapped to a variant in a predicted regulatory region and 3 (4.6%) mapped to a predicted missense variant, including the G1 allele of APOL1 known to be associated with ESKD34.

To test for replication of variants which tag an ADM association, we intersected these results with GWAS previously run in AFR-like individuals in MVP67. Of the 48 ADM associations with a corresponding phenotype in MVP, 41 had a tagged variant identified in the previously described conditional analyses within AoU, which was also present in the MVP imputed genotype data used for GWAS (Methods). We found that 20 (48.8%) of these ADM associations tagged a variant with P < 5×10-8 in MVP, 24 (58.5%) tagged a variant with P < 5×10-5 in MVP, and 33 (80.5%) tagged a variant with nominal significance in MVP (P < 0.05; Supplementary Data 15). These results suggest that most ADM associations can be mapped to a single variant with at least nominal significance in a larger AFR-like dataset.

As an example of one such association, we identified an unreported association within region 12q14.3, where inferred local African ancestry was associated with increased HbA1c (Beta (95% CI) = 0.11 (0.06,0.16); P = 2.5××10-5; Fig. 7A, upper panel). Single-variant association analyses in AoU identified the variant rs11175880 as decreasing the ADM association significance the most after conditional analysis (Pcond = 0.03; Fig. 7A, lower panel). The closest protein-coding gene to this variant is HMGA2, which is 110 Kb downstream of the variant and encodes a non-histone chromosomal high-mobility group protein (Fig. 7B). The G allele of rs11175880 is less common in AFR populations (AF: 0.16) relative to EUR populations (AF: 0.92) (Fig. 7C), with decreased HbA1c associated with the same allele (Beta (95% CI) = −0.07 (−0.10, −0.04); P = 1.7××10-6; Fig. 7D). This direction of effect matches the increased HbA1c with AFR local ancestry haplotypes observed in ADM. To further characterize this variant, we calculated its association with related quantitative traits and similarly found significant associations with standardized BMI (Beta (95% CI) = -0.02 (-0.03,-0.01); P = 0.03) and random glucose (Beta (95% CI) = -0.02 (−0.05,0); P = 0.04), as well as non-significant associations with red blood cell traits (Fig. 7D; Supplementary Data 16). Previous GWAS in AFR-like individuals within the Million Veterans Program (MVP) found similar associations67 (Fig. 7D), suggesting this signal may represent a true HbA1c association not previously characterized due to being below the genome-wide significance threshold used in GWAS. While rs11175880 is >100Kb away from the transcription start site of HMGA2, rs11175880 is predicted to alter the binding of transcription factor ERalpha71, which is thought to regulate HMGA2 expression72. Discussion

Here, we aimed to identify genetic loci with associations between local ancestries and phenotypes of interest. We performed one of the largest admixture mapping efforts to-date in AFR-EUR Admixed individuals, identifying 59 loci where differences between AFR and EUR local ancestries are associated with at least one of 22 different quantitative and binary traits, for a total of 71 locus-to-trait mappings. Comparison of ADM-identified signals with GWAS revealed that 75% of ADM associations were independent of genome-wide significant variants from GWAS catalog and 24% of the ADM associations were independent of genome-wide significant variants from in-sample GWAS. However, 93% of the ADM associations tagged a single variant, which did not reach genome-wide significance in single variant association testing. These signals may represent novel disease-associated loci which previous GWAS have not reported due to the higher multiple testing burden and/or the reduced power from the limited representation of participants genetically similar to African populations. Consequently, they may include biological insights into diseases which could be translated into improved treatment options. For example, the inclusion of population-enriched variants in polygenic scores may improve their predictability and portability between populations73.

Beyond association analyses, investigations of local ancestry frequencies among admixed individuals can shed light on regions under selection since admixture. However, we found that an apparent depletion of EUR ancestry at the HLA was likely due to biased errors in local ancestry inference rather than selection, suggesting local ancestry inference is not accurate at the HLA locus and could be problematic at other similarly complex regions40,41. Consequently, previously reported selection results must be viewed with scrutiny to ensure that differences in local ancestry frequencies are truly due to selection rather than errors in local ancestry inference.

Previous work has demonstrated the utility of admixture mapping for identifying loci with differing causal variant allele frequencies between populations29, which we also confirmed when comparing altAF values across variants which reduce ADM significance after conditional analysis. Furthermore, we found evidence that effect sizes also vary for these variants when estimated in predicted African and European populations. As causal effects are mostly shared between populations27, these differences may represent large standard errors due to differences in power from allele frequency variations, differential LD architecture with true causal signals, or exceptions to the largely shared effect-sizes. Interactions between genetic and non-genetic factors could also explain some of these differences, but further work is required to determine if such interactions exist at these loci.

Disease prevalence and trait distribution are known to vary across populations. For example, African Americans have a higher prevalence of ESKD compared to European Americans, motivating ADM studies in African American participants74. In previous ADM studies, increased ESKD risk was associated with African ancestry near the APOL1 locus35,36, ultimately leading to the development of drugs targeting APOL1 variations75. Thus, ADM results not only can help explain prevalence variations between populations, but also aid in developing novel therapeutic options for patients. In this study, we identified several unreported associations, including one between inferred local African ancestries at the HMGA2 locus and increased HbA1c, which the intergenic variant rs11175880 may partially explain. HbA1c, a common biomarker which measures the percentage of glycated hemoglobin to estimate blood glucose levels, is known to be elevated among African Americans relative to European Americans46. This variation is thought to arise from a combination of differences in glycemic and non-glycemic factors between the two populations. As such, HbA1c in African Americans may be less accurate as a proxy for circulating glucose levels due to the enrichment of variants that impact HbA1c via non-glycemic mechanisms76,77. Within AoU, we found that the G allele of rs11175880 was associated with glucose but not red blood cell-related traits. However, previous GWAS in MVP found associations between the same allele of rs11175880 and both glucose and other red blood cell-related traits. This suggests rs11175880 may impact HbA1c levels primarily through glycemic mechanisms, though there may also be some non-glycemic contributions. rs11175880 thus represents a potential contributor to varying HbA1c levels between populations, and may improve the prediction of HbA1c, diabetes, and related complications within African Americans.

We conclude with several limitations. First, heterogeneity within the genetically-inferred AFR-EUR Admixed samples could potentially result in heterogeneous observed effects, leading to confounders driving some of the observed associations. Additionally, other sources of bias, such as local ancestry inference errors, may also lead to false positives in ADM. As local ancestry associations in ADM tag one or more underlying variant effects, we may expect most ADM associations to overlap and have weaker associations than the tagged variant(s). 89% of our ADM associations are near a more strongly associated GWAS signal, and 77% are conditioned by a more strongly associated GWAS signal, suggesting most of our ADM associations may be true signals. Differences in allele frequencies and ADM associations which may tag multiple variants may also explain these ADM associations without a more strongly associated tagged GWAS signal. Furthermore, future efforts using linear mixed model-based ADM methods may further reduce false positive associations through stronger population structure adjustment. We performed LD clumping on ADM results to identify lead loci. However, loci lacking a causal variant may be prioritized due to the high correlation between neighboring local ancestry segments. Consequently, we generated broad boundaries around lead loci for GWAS signal identification. We anticipate that future methods that improve fine mapping of ADM results can help parse these results. We also recognize the minimal overlap of significant ADM associations in replication efforts, especially for UKBB. Low statistical power due to the much smaller sample sizes of UKBB and PMBB likely underlies this low replication. This low replication may also be expected due to the differences in genetic architectures and population histories of AFR-EUR Admixed populations in the US and UK78,79, or other non-genetic differences between AoU, an EHR-based biobank, and UKBB, a population-based biobank. Indeed, effect sizes of ADM associations were highly correlated between AoU and PMBB, and moderately correlated between AoU and UKBB, suggestive of both limited power for replication and the previously mentioned genetic and non-genetic differences between UKBB and AoU. We also only performed conditional analyses with one variant at a time. However, multiple variants could jointly underly ADM associations, and such interplays are not considered in our analyses. As we tested thousands of variants in single-variant conditional analysis models, adding multiple variants per model was computationally infeasible.

In summary, we performed one of the largest local ancestry inference efforts in the All of Us Research Program. Leveraging this data, we identified associations with ADM previously unidentified in GWAS, though with evidence of reproducibility both from ADM replication analyses and suggestive GWAS associations. As inclusion of recently admixed individuals in genetic studies increases, these results suggest ADM efforts will continue to identify novel genetic associations and insights into trait biology.

Methods

This research is in compliance with the All of Us Research Program and UK Biobank guidelines, and was approved by the Penn Medicine Biobank review board.

Overview of cohorts

All of Us Research Program

The All of Us Research Program (AoU) is a large, US-based biobank developed to improve and enable the inclusion of diverse, underrepresented populations in large-scale genetic and epidemiological research studies13,38. We accessed short read whole genome sequencing (srWGS) data, survey data, and electronic health record data from participants included in the v7 release of AoU. Age within AoU was calculated as participants’ age as of August 1, 2022. Sex was determined using self-reported sex-at-birth data provided by AoU. We identified individuals as being non-Hispanic Black or African American if their self-reported race was “Black or African American” and their self-reported ethnicity was “not Hispanic or Latino”.

AoU previously described in detail how they sequenced, QC’ed, and ran variant calling using the DRAGEN pipeline for their participants38. AoU provides a list of flagged individuals with poor srWGS data quality based on the number of variants, insertion/deletion ratios, number of insertions, number of deletions, heterozygosity of variants or indels, transition/transversion ratios, and number of variants not in gnomAD v3.1. Additionally, variants from srWGS were flagged as being low quality based on a GQ threshold of <20, a DP threshold of <10, an allele balance filter of <0.2, a low QUAL score (60 for SNPs and 69 for indels), or an ExcessHet score <54.69. These individuals and variants were removed from all subsequent analyses. AoU additionally provides a minimized list of related individuals, with relatedness determined using PC-Relate to account for population structure when calculating relatedness, which was used to remove related participants80. AoU also inferred genetic ancestry per individual using a random forest model and training data from gnomAD, 1000 G, and the Human Genome Diversity Project38, then identified a list of common variants based on these inferred genetic ancestry groups. Common variants were defined as variants with an allele count >100 or a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 in at least one ancestry.

To infer local and global ancestry in AoU, we first extracted genetic data from the 1000 Genomes Project (1000 G) for individuals from each superpopulation. We then calculated principal components within these individuals, and then projected the AoU participants into the same PC space. These PCs were additionally used in all statistical models in AoU. Finally, we estimated inferred local and global ancestry with RFMix v281 with default parameters, using the 1000 G data above as reference and HapMap3 variants. AFR-EUR admixed individuals were identified if their combined global ancestry proportions of AFR and EUR was >0.95 but the individual proportions of AFR and EUR was <0.95. This definition was used to identify individuals with any level of admixture between African and European populations, while excluding individuals who may be three-way admixed with African, European, and a third population. Indeed, when looking at the proportion of global ancestry for the other EAS, SAS, and NAT reference populations, we found minimal amounts of admixture with one of these populations within the AFR-EUR Admixed cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggestive of appropriate identification of two-way admixed individuals.

UK biobank

The UK Biobank (UKBB) is a large-scale biomedical database containing genetic and electronic health record information from half a million residents from the United Kingdom. Genotype data was prepared as described in Hou et al. 202327. Briefly, pre-imputed variants were retained with imputation R² > 0.8, minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.5%, and ancestry-specific MAF > 0.5% in both the African and European populations. Ancestry inference was performed using SCOPE v682, which estimated global ancestral proportion based on 1000 G allele frequencies, where 4,326 individuals with more than 5% of both AFR and EUR ancestries. Furthermore, local ancestry was inferred using RFMix v2 with default settings using 99 Northern and Western European residents in Utah (CEU) in 1000 G and 108 Yoruba individuals from Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI) in 1000 G. Only HapMap3 SNPs were used for local ancestry inference, with other variants interpolated.

Penn medicine biobank

The Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB) collects genetic data and electronic health record data from consenting individuals within the University of Pennsylvania Health System. Zaidi et al. previously reported local ancestry inference within PMBB participants genetically similar to AFR53. Briefly, genotype data was phased using Beagle, then local ancestry inference was performed using RFMix v1 with CEU and YRI populations as reference. The HLA locus was also masked before local ancestry inference.

Phenotypes

We analyzed a total of 7 binary and 15 quantitative traits in AoU, 5 binary and 16 quantitative traits in the UKBB, and 7 binary and 12 quantitative traits in PMBB. For the AoU dataset, we selected the most recent measurements categorized under “condition” and “lab and physical measurement,” identified using “concept ID” and ICD-10 codes for the binary traits. A full description of the phenotypes selected has been described previously83. Outliers in the quantitative traits, defined as values exceeding 5 standard deviations from the mean, were excluded from the analysis. In UKBB, traits with corresponding definitions and ICD codes were extracted following the procedures outlined in the script available at https://github.com/privefl/UKBB-PGS/blob/main/code/prepare-pheno-fields.R. In PMBB, quantitative and binary traits were derived from patients’ electronic health records.

To characterize the differences in phenotypes between participants with genetic similarity to African populations and European populations in AoU, we performed a two-sided Welch’s t-test for quantitative traits and a chi2 test for binary traits. For this analysis, we defined individuals with genetic similarity to African and European populations as those with >0.95 AFR or EUR global ancestry proportions.

Admixture mapping

Association of local ancestry haplotypes with phenotypes was performed using admix-kit v0.1.1 with the ADM method84. Example code for running admixture mapping using admix-kit can be found at the following GitHub repository (https://github.com/zhuozshi/admixmap). Associations in all cohorts were adjusted for age, sex, and the first 10 principal components. Additionally, models generated within AoU were additionally adjusted for the site where the srWGS was performed. Single ancestry haplotype analyses were performed for AFR-EUR Admixed individuals within AoU and UKBB, where the effect of having an AFR haplotype relative to not having an AFR haplotype within a specific local ancestry locus on phenotypes was assessed. For BMI, we compared the associations we detected with ADM to those from AoU GWAS. A full description on the GWAS can be found here (https://support.researchallofus.org/hc/en-us/articles/27049847988884-Overview-of-the-All-by-All-tables-available-on-the-All-of-Us-Researcher-Workbench). Briefly, GWAS was run using SAIGE85 on quantitative traits and phecodes within AoU, stratified by genetically inferred ancestry groups.

To determine the effective number of tests to adjust for, we explored four different approaches. First, we used an MCMC approach previously described86, and estimated an average of 297 tests. Second, we used the number of local ancestry segments in high LD with each other, using PLINK v1.9 with a window or 5 Mb and R2 cutoff of 0.8, and estimated a total of 757 tests. Third, we explored a cutoff of 1000 tests from literature searches of previous admixture mapping efforts87–91. The number of effective tests was previously identified based on observations that local correlation between admixture tests decays over distances of ~3 cM within AFR-EUR Admixed individuals89. Finally, we explored permutation-based approaches to estimate the appropriate P-value cutoff adjusting for a family-wise error rate of 0.05. However, we found this approach to be computationally infeasible due to cost constraints within the AoU cloud environment, especially for binary traits. Consequently, we opted to use the third approach to minimize the number of false positives, as well as allow for a constant significance threshold across traits and replication datasets.

After generating ADM summary statistics, loci were clumped to find independent significant hits, using the PLINK v1.9 clumping function with a significance threshold of 5 × 10-5, or an adjusted P < 0.05, LD threshold of 0.1, and physical distance threshold of 5 Mb at each chromosome for every trait. LD reference panels were constructed using inferred local AFR haplotypes from unrelated AFR-EUR Admixed individuals per local ancestry segment.

As local ancestry inference errors are concentrated in low-complexity regions52 and long-LD regions40, we tested whether ADM hits overlap these regions. Since there are many small low-complexity regions from the UCSC genome browser, and the local ancestry segments are large, 90.1% of the genome-wide significant ADM associations intersected with at least one low-complexity region. The amount of overlap was minimal, ranging from 0.05% to 1.80% (Supplementary Data 5). Furthermore, 5 of the ADM associations contained one HapMap3 variant used for local ancestry inference which was in a low complexity region. However, the total number of input HapMap3 variants used for each of these 5 regions ranged from 26 to 116, meaning only a small percentage of these input variants could have created errors stemming from the low-complexity regions (Supplementary Data 5). Since these results suggest that errors in local ancestry inference due to low-complexity regions may not greatly impact results, we did not perform any filtering by low-complexity regions. Similarly for the long-LD regions92, we looked for overlap with one of the genome-wide significant ADM associations. We found that one ADM associations, between height and 20q11.22 did overlap with one such region. However, we decided to keep this ADM signal in the manuscript, as we found this association to tag known GWAS signals with strong associations with height, suggesting the association is likely real and not due to errors in local ancestry inference.

To identify if any genome-wide significant loci are the same across traits, we checked if any of the genome-wide significant loci from the 71 associations are correlated with each other (R2 > 0.8). In total, this left us with 52 loci which are genome-wide significant for an ADM association and independent from other loci. For the 12 loci associated with multiple traits, we tested for evidence of colocalization using COLOC 5.2.393 and a posterior probability of colocalization (PP.H4) > 0.8. Colocalization boundaries were calculated with a 10 Mb window around the center of the local ancestry segment. This large window was selected to ensure enough local ancestry haplotypes were included in the colocalization analysis. We checked visually to see if use of this large window size was appropriate and found that use of 10 Mb windows sufficiently captured single peaks from the ADM association analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6). We calculated pleiotropy of effect sizes across all ADM associations using z-scores calculated for each of the 22 traits at the 52 independent loci. We used the equation Z = γγ / (sqrt(N) * SE) to calculate z-scores per trait. We then calculated Pearson correlations between Z-scores per trait across the 52 loci.

We identified shared loci between the AoU and UKBB and PMBB ADM analyses by looking for intersections of local ancestry segments with genome-wide significant ADM associations for the same trait in both datasets. When evaluating if each genome-wide significant ADM association replicates in UKBB or PMBB, we also performed power analyses to determine if an association was powered to be identified in UKBB and PMBB given the estimated effect size γγ from AoU. For quantitative traits, we used the non-centrality parameter (NCP)94 while for binary traits, we adopted an additive local ancestry haplotype relative risk model then derived the NCP95–97. Before comparing the γγ effect sizes between datasets, effects were standardized for quantitative associations by dividing γγ by the standard deviation of the trait.

Evidence of selection

We tested for evidence of selection by identifying genomic regions where local ancestry frequencies significantly differed from the genome-wide average as described previously39. Inferred local EUR frequencies for three separate analyses in unrelated AFR-EUR Admixed individuals, SIRE non-Hispanic Black or African American individuals, and individuals with high genetic similarity to EUR were calculated using PLINK v1.9. We defined individuals with high genetic similarity to EUR as the 48,921 individuals with the lowest Euclidean distance to the 1000 G EUR centroid in PC space. The number of participants was chosen to match the number of AFR-EUR Admixed individuals to ensure similar power for both analyses. We removed the first and last 2 Mb of each chromosome to avoid errors with local ancestry inference in low-quality genomic regions.

We tested for associations between inferred local EUR ancestry and 1389 phecodes with at least 100 cases and 16 different relevant immune labs using the same covariates described above for ADM using Python 3.11. P-values for binary and quantitative traits were independently FDR adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure with a cutoff of 0.05.

Conditional ADM analyses

To perform the admixture mapping analysis conditional on genotype variants, we first collected GWAS associations from the GWAS catalog for the same traits with an ADM association. We then removed variants which were farther than 20 Mb from any significant ADM association. We used a large window around the ADM associations to account for the high correlation of local ancestry haplotypes. To avoid testing duplicate signals, we further filtered variants using a clumping strategy, with a MAF cutoff of 0.01, P-value threshold of 5××10-8, LD threshold of 0.01, and distance threshold of 5××106. For each trait, we re-ran admixture mapping, additionally including an LD-independent genome-wide significant variants as a covariate as described above.

As we found that admix-kit previously used to run ADM was computationally expensive, we ran conditional analyses using the glm function in PLINK v1.9, encoding AFR haplotypes as the alternative allele and non-AFR haplotypes as the reference allele. We determined whether a variant tags an ADM association if the P-value of the conditional ADM model increased above 1××10-4, or a P-value greater than 0.1 after adjusting for 1000 different tests. Cases where the P-value was greater than 1××10-4 suggest that there is no longer strong evidence to reject the null hypothesis of local ancestry being associated with the trait after conditioning on a variant. To avoid cases where missing variants drop the number of samples such that decreases in the P-value could instead be due to power issues, we only considered conditional analyses where the sample size was at least 95% of that of the original analysis. We also calculated allele frequencies of variants of interest using data from 1000 G continental populations labeled as EUR or AFR.

Single variant association analyses

We estimated effect sizes for single variants with their traits of interest in AoU using either linear or logistic regression models, depending on the trait type, as implemented in PLINK v1.9. Variants within 10 Mb of an ADM peak were extracted from the AoU ACAF files, which are pre-filtered to include only variants with a MAF > 1% or MAC > 100 in at least one ancestry superpopulation38. All models were adjusted for age, sex, the first 10 PCs, and sequencing site. Models were run in AoU participants genetically-similar to AFR 1000 G populations (AFR-like), AoU participants genetically-similar to EUR 1000 G populations (EUR-like), all AoU participants, or only AFR-EUR Admixed participants. We defined AFR-like participants as those with a global AFR proportion >0.9, and EUR-like participants as those with a global EUR proportion >0.9. We chose a more lenient threshold here than the >0.95 threshold used for previous comparisons of trait distributions between populations because of the low number of individuals with a global AFR proportion >0.95.

When evaluating the significance of differences in allele frequencies and effect size variations between populations for variants which condition and do not condition an ADM association, we utilized the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U Test, since we were comparing the absolute difference values, which are not normally distributed. We also directly compared effect sizes from different populations using a Z-test. Allele frequencies for these variants were calculated in 1000 G AFR and EUR superpopulations.

Single variant fine-mapping

To determine if any variants in AoU may be tagging the ADM peaks, we ran clumping on the single variant associations generated using AFR-EUR Admixed AoU participants with a P-value threshold of 1×10-4. LD threshold of 0.01, and distance threshold of 5××106. We also excluded variants with a MAF < 0.01 to remove associations underpowered for discovery. We then re-ran the conditional analyses pipeline described above on all lead variants from this clumping step. Allele frequencies for these variants were similarly calculated in 1000 G AFR and EUR superpopulations.

Functional annotation of variants

We used the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) v114 available on the Ensembl website (https://useast.ensembl.org/info/docs/tools/vep/index.html) to functionally annotate signals from the AoU AFR-EUR Admixed single variant associations, which condition an ADM association, using default parameters and specifying 200 Kb for the upstream/downstream distance for mapping genes to variants. We chose this large distance to capture potential regulatory variants which may be intergenic but still impact gene expression. We also used HaploReg v4.2 (https://pubs.broadinstitute.org/mammals/haploreg/haploreg.php) to additionally identify overlap of rs11175880 with enhancers and potential impacts of rs11175880 on transcription factor binding.
