Rapid relief online: A randomized trial of a single-session simple-swift reconstruction approach group during the COVID-19 surge
Authors: Junkai Yang, Yao Chen, Yin Fan, Yunge Bai, Zhang Lin, Huajian Li, Shuangge Sui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge-scale crises require brief, scalable, on-line mental-health interventions. We examined the effectiveness of a single 90–120 min videoconference group based on the Simple-Swift Reconstruction Approach (SSRA).MethodsIn a parallel-group randomized trial, 277 Chinese adults were assigned to SSRA (n = 143) or a waiting-list control (n = 134). Baseline and 2-week outcomes were the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), and General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES). The SSRA group also completed Subjective Units of Disturbance (SUD) ratings immediately before and after the session. Complete-case analyses used paired and independent t-tests with Cohen's d (two-tailed α = 0.05).ResultsOne hundred twenty-one SSRA participants (19 % male; M = 34.9 years, SD = 11.3) attended the SSRA session and showed a large within-session SUD reduction (baseline M = 5.81, SD = 1.62; post M = 3.20, SD = 1.57; t(120) = 19.26, p < .001, d = 1.75). Two-week follow-up data were available for 71 SSRA and 19 control participants (retention = 32.5 %). Within the SSRA group, depressive symptoms (Δ = −2.14, d = 0.63), anxiety (Δ = −1.89, d = 0.66), and self-efficacy (Δ = +2.28, d = 0.41) improved descriptively. However, only the between-group difference for depression (PHQ-9) reached significance (t(24.7) = −2.60, p = .015, d = 0.76); between-group GAD-7 and GSES were nonsignificant. Age and sex did not moderate outcomes.ConclusionsA one-off, on-line SSRA group produced a large within-session reduction in subjective distress and a significant between-group improvement in depressive symptoms over two weeks, supporting SSRA as a rapid, low-intensity option during public-health emergencies. Replication with larger samples and longer follow-up is recommended.

Introduction

Large-scale public-health emergencies and natural disasters routinely generate surges in anxiety, depressive symptoms and perceived helplessness across affected communities (Tang et al., 2014; Beaglehole et al., 2018; Vindegaard and Benros, 2020; Keya et al., 2023). Meta-analyses and large-scale surveys have documented a marked rise in depressive and anxiety symptoms during the COVID-19 pandemic worldwide (Vindegaard and Benros, 2020; Santabárbara et al., 2021). The unprecedented disruption of daily routines and the sense of uncertainty created a surge in psychological distress, underscoring the urgent need for brief, scalable interventions that can be delivered remotely. When crises occur on a national or global scale—as witnessed during successive coronavirus outbreaks—mental-health systems are often unable to provide timely, individualized care to everyone in need. This mismatch between demand and specialist capacity has stimulated interest in brief, low-intensity interventions that can be delivered remotely and at scale (Naslund et al., 2017; Holmes et al., 2020; Wind et al., 2020; Lindberg et al., 2025).

Rationale for single-session, group-based formats

Group interventions maximize reach by treating several individuals simultaneously while also leveraging therapeutic factors like universality, imparting information, instillation of hope and interpersonal learning (Yalom and Leszcz, 2020). Condensing essential components into a single structured meeting further enhances scalability and reduces attrition risk. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that single-session group interventions (SSGIs) can produce small-to-moderate reductions in internalizing symptoms and meaningful gains in coping self-efficacy, provided they employ evidence-based techniques and a clear session architecture (Schleider et al., 2025; Schleider and Weisz, 2017). Rigorous studies of fully on-line SSGIs, however, remain comparatively scarce, leaving a critical evidence gap for practitioners designing rapid-response services.

The Simple-Swift Reconstruction Approach (SSRA)

The Simple-Swift Reconstruction Approach (SSRA) is a manualized, single-session protocol developed in China for post-disaster mental-health support. Framed within cognitive–behavioral and resource-oriented trauma theories, SSRA guides group members through four sequential phases—Problem Presentation, Information Transmission, Coping Exploration, and Summary & Elevation—over 90–120 min. Each phase targets a distinct therapeutic goal: externalizing distress, normalizing stress reactions, activating personal and social resources, and consolidating adaptive plans. Traditional SSRA implementations typically involve 4–60 participants per group (Sui, 2009; Sui et al., 2011), though recent applications have successfully expanded this number to 127 (Yang et al., 2025). During the closing phase of the session, facilitators also introduce local support resources, such as school-based counseling services and mental health hotlines, to assist participants with ongoing psychological needs. The intervention typically ends with brief group reflections to consolidate coping improvements and ensure participants' well-being. Preliminary quasi-experimental data from earthquake-affected populations suggest that SSRA can quickly reduce subjective distress and enhance perceived control (Sui et al., 2011; Sui, 2009, Sui, 2020). To date, however, no randomized controlled trial (RCT) has examined the efficacy of an on-line, single-session SSRA group.

Objectives of the present study

This study was designed during the March–April 2022 Omicron outbreak in mainland China, a period characterized by widespread quarantine measures and heightened psychological strain. Leveraging videoconferencing technology, we implemented a parallel-group RCT to evaluate whether a single on-line SSRA session could (a) reduce acute subjective distress within the meeting itself and (b) improve depression, anxiety and general self-efficacy two weeks later, relative to a waiting-list control. By establishing the effectiveness of a remote, one-off SSRA intervention, the trial aims to inform the development of scalable mental-health responses for future large-scale emergencies—pandemic-related or otherwise.

Methods

Design and ethical considerations

A parallel group randomized controlled trial was conducted from March 30 to April 28, 2022. The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines and received approval from the Tsinghua University Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2021-23); procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided electronic informed consent before enrolment.

Participants

Nationwide recruitment announcements were distributed via WeChat, Sina Weibo, and professional listservs. Interested individuals accessed an online registration link embedded in the recruitment announcements, completed an eligibility screening form, and were subsequently contacted by the research team to verify inclusion and exclusion criteria prior to randomization. Inclusion criteria were (a) age 18 years or older, (b) capacity to attend a 2-hours videoconference, and (c) willingness to complete follow-up questionnaires. Exclusion criteria were severe depressive or anxious symptomatology (Patient Health Questionnaire–9 [PHQ-9] total ≥ 15 or Item 9 ≥ 2; Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 [GAD-7] ≥ 15), acute psychiatric risk or severe medical illness, unstable internet connectivity, marked impulsivity or panic reactions, and inability to comply with study procedures. Exclusion criteria were assessed via self-report screening items concerning acute psychiatric risk (e.g., current suicidal intent or recent self-harm) and marked impulsivity (e.g., history of uncontrolled aggressive or risky behavior). Participants endorsing such items were contacted by a licensed psychologist for brief verification and referral if needed.

Fifteen single-session SSRA videoconference groups, all focused on stress-relief and empowerment, were delivered to the following populations: volunteers (3 sessions), healthcare workers (3 sessions), parents of primary-school pupils (3 sessions), university students with career concerns (2 sessions), community workers (1 session), university students in quarantine (1 session), residents in quarantine (1 session), and parents of secondary-school students (1 session). Group size ranged from 4 to 17 participants (Mdn = 9), and each session lasted 90–120 min.

Randomization and allocation concealment

An independent researcher generated a block randomization sequence (block size = 10) using computer software (R, version 4.2.2). Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, electronic envelopes that were opened by the data team after baseline assessments were completed.

Intervention

Simple-Swift Reconstruction Approach (SSRA) Group. Participants attended one SSRA session (90–120 min) via Tencent Meeting (https://meeting.tencent.com) with 4–17 members per group. Variation in group size reflected differing registration volumes across occupational subgroups during lockdown (e.g., smaller healthcare-worker teams versus larger parent groups). Exploratory checks revealed no systematic association between group size and outcome magnitude.

Certified facilitators—licensed psychologists or senior counselors (total n = 17) who completed a 16-hour SSRA training workshop and received weekly supervision—conducted all sessions in teams composed of one lead facilitator, one co-facilitator, and one to two observers/supervisors. All facilitators received weekly online group supervision following each SSRA session. The supervision process lasted 30–60 min and was conducted after the group session. Supervision was provided by two senior SSRA trainers: Dr. Shuangge Sui (the developer of the SSRA protocol) and Dr. Yunge Bai, both of whom are registered supervisors in the Chinese Psychological Society (CPS). Dr. Bai has been involved in SSRA training and supervision since 2018. During the supervision meetings, facilitators, co-facilitators, observers, and the assigned supervisor discussed session dynamics, reviewed participant feedback, and ensured adherence to the manualized procedures. The sessions focused on fidelity, participant safety, and the implementation of the intervention in line with SSRA standards. Teams followed the four-phase SSRA manual:1.Problem Presentation (≈ 15 min): Participants briefly stated their current difficulty and provided a baseline Subjective Units of Disturbance (SUD) rating.2.Information Transmission (≈ 20–25 min): Facilitators delivered concise psychoeducation that normalized stress reactions and introduced core coping concepts.3.Coping Exploration (≈ 40–50 min): Participants activated internal and external resources through structured exercises (e.g., resource mapping, guided recall of past strengths, peer strength reflection), promoting a shift from helplessness to agency, reinforcing solidarity, and highlighting collective resilience. SUD was re-rated at the end of this phase.4.Summary and Elevation (≈ 15–20 min): Key insights were consolidated, and participants engaged in a brief goal-setting ritual intended to enhance self-efficacy and carry gains forward.

Waiting-List Control. Control participants received no intervention during the 2-week waiting period but were offered an SSRA session after completing follow-up measures.

Measures

Four Chinese-language instruments were used: (1) the Subjective Units of Disturbance (Wolpe, 1969), a single 0–10 rating of current distress that was administered to the intervention group at the start and end of the session (internal consistency not applicable); (2) the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (Kroenke et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2014), which contains nine items scored 0–3, yielding a total range of 0–27 (α = 0.87 in the present sample), and was completed at baseline and at the 2-week follow-up; (3) the Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 (Spitzer et al., 2006; Tong et al., 2016), which comprises seven items scored 0–3, producing a total range of 0–21 (α = 0.89), and was likewise administered at baseline and follow-up; and (4) the General Self-Efficacy Scale (Schwarzer and Jerusalem, 1995; Sun et al., 2021), which includes 10 items scored 1–4 for a total range of 10–40 (α = 0.87) and was administered on the same schedule as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. The PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptom severity, the GAD-7 measures generalized anxiety, the GSES evaluates perceived self-efficacy, and SUD ratings capture immediate subjective distress within the session.

Procedure

As shown in Fig. 1, 302 individuals completed the on-line screening survey; 25 individuals were excluded due to high baseline symptom severity (n = 12), self-reported acute psychiatric risk (n = 8), or unstable internet connectivity (n = 5); leaving 277 who met all inclusion criteria and were randomized to the SSRA group (n = 143) or the waiting-list control (n = 134). After baseline assessment (PHQ-9, GAD-7, GSES, demographics), the SSRA group attended a videoconference session within 7 days, provided pre- and post-session SUD ratings, and received follow-up questionnaires 14 ± 2 days later; 121 participants (19 % male; M = 34.9 years, SD = 11.3) completed the session, but 50 did not return follow-up measures, yielding 71 complete cases (14 % male; M = 37.4 years, SD = 10.6). All 134 control participants received no intervention during the wait period and were contacted for follow-up at Day 14; 115 were lost, leaving 19 with full data (0 % male; M = 39.4 years, SD = 9.2). Consequently, primary outcome analyses were based on 71 SSRA and 19 control participants, resulting in an overall follow-up completion rate of 32.5 %. Within-session SUD analyses used the larger subset of 121 SSRA attendees. Reminder e-mails and text messages were issued 48 h and 24 h before each assessment deadline. Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in SPSS 27 (two-tailed α = 0.05). Analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat framework in which all participants who completed both baseline and follow-up assessments were included. No participants completed follow-up without attending the SSRA session; therefore, analyses were based on complete cases. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, and 95 % confidence intervals. Within-group changes were evaluated using paired-sample t-tests (SSRA: baseline vs. follow-up; SUD pre- vs. post-session). Between-group differences were examined with independent-sample t-tests on change scores (Δ = Time 1 − Time 0). Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d (0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large). Analysis of covariance tested age and sex interactions with outcome changes. All analyses were conducted on complete cases. Participants who did not provide full follow-up questionnaires were excluded from the outcome analyses (listwise deletion). No item- or wave-level data were imputed.

Results

Although between-group differences best reflect intervention effects, within-group analyses were also reported to provide descriptive context, given the smaller control-group sample size and exploratory nature of this early trial.

Baseline equivalence

Independent-sample t-tests and χ2 tests showed no significant IG–WG differences in age, sex distribution, or baseline scores on the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 (GAD-7), or General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), ps > .45.

Within-session change in subjective distress

Intervention participants' Subjective Units of Disturbance (SUD) ratings fell from M = 5.81, SD = 1.62 to M = 3.20, SD = 1.57, t(120) = −19.26, p < .001, d = 1.75, indicating a very large immediate reduction in distress.

Between-group comparisons of change scores

Welch independent-sample t-tests on change scores showed a statistically significant advantage for the IG on depressive symptoms, t(24.74) = −2.60, p = .015, d = 0.76. Group differences for anxiety (t(27.34) = −1.42, p = .167, d = 0.38) and self-efficacy (t(26.37) = 1.74, p = .093, d = 0.48) did not reach conventional significance.

Moderator analysis

Two (Group) × two (Sex) and two (Group) × continuous (Age) analyses of covariance on change scores revealed no significant interactions, all Fs < 1.25, ps > .26, indicating that treatment effects were consistent across demographic subgroups.

Discussion

Principal findings

The present randomized controlled trial tested a fully on-line, single-session delivery of the Simple–Swift Reconstruction Approach (SSRA) during the March–April 2022 Omicron surge. Consistent with hypotheses, the intervention produced a very large immediate reduction in subjective distress (SUD, d = 1.75) and medium, statistically significant pre- to post-session improvements in depressive symptoms (d = 0.63) and anxiety (d = 0.66). Self-efficacy increased modestly (d = 0.41). Between-group contrasts at two weeks favored SSRA for depression (medium–large effect) but did not reach conventional significance for anxiety or self-efficacy. Age and sex did not moderate outcomes, indicating that therapeutic gains were broadly equitable across demographic subgroups.

Comparison with prior work

Our findings align with previous quasi-experimental studies showing that SSRA can reduce distress and improve depression in post-crisis contexts (Sui, 2009; Sui et al., 2011). However, unlike prior studies, this trial demonstrates efficacy under strict experimental control, eliminating the biases inherent in earlier designs. First, we demonstrate efficacy under stringent experimental control, eliminating plausible selection biases inherent in earlier designs. Although significant effects were observed for depression, the absence of significant differences for anxiety and self-efficacy suggests that the SSRA intervention may be more effective for distress reduction and mood stabilization than for improving self-efficacy or anxiety, which requires further exploration. Second, we show that SSRA can be adapted to a fully remote, videoconference format without loss of clinical potency, echoing smaller trials of on-line single-session cognitive–behavioral workshops (Schleider et al., 2022). Third, the large within-session SUD effect size exceeds those reported for Critical Incident Stress Debriefing or Psychological First Aid when delivered as one-off groups (Roberts et al., 2019), suggesting that the SSRA sequence may confer added value in rapidly down-regulating acute affect.

Putative mechanisms of change

SSRA's architecture offers a coherent blend of affect labeling, cognitive normalization, and resource activation. The Problem Presentation phase externalizes distress, satisfying emotional-processing models that link verbalization with amygdala deactivation (Lieberman et al., 2017). Information Transmission reframes stress reactions as expectable, countering catastrophe-oriented appraisals that sustain negative mood (Beck and Clark, 1997). Coping Exploration operationalizes Bandura's (1997) mastery pathway by eliciting concrete, self-generated action plans, thereby strengthening self-efficacy—a construct shown to mediate symptom change in brief trauma interventions (Benight and Bandura, 2004). Finally, Summary & Elevation reinforces gains through ritualized goal commitment, which may improve post-session adherence (Hagger and Luszczynska, 2014). The distinctive sequencing and time-boxing of these elements could explain the markedly larger immediate effect on SUD relative to more open-ended debriefing models.

Clinical and public-health implications

From a service-delivery perspective, SSRA meets key criteria for low-intensity, scalable care: (a) one 2-hour session, (b) delivery via freely available videoconferencing platforms, and (c) facilitation by trained counselors rather than specialist clinicians. These attributes render it well-suited for surge capacity during pandemics, climate disasters, and other mass-casualty events where rapid psychological first aid is paramount and professional resources are scarce. Although significant gains were confined to depression in between-group tests—likely reflecting limited power—the observed pattern suggests that SSRA can function as a front-line, “good-enough” intervention that buys time until higher-intensity care becomes available.

Strengths and limitations

This study's strengths include: (a) rigorous random assignment with allocation concealment; (b) full adherence to CONSORT reporting standards; and (c) assessment of both immediate (SUD) and short-term (PHQ-9, GAD-7, GSES) outcomes.

Several caveats should temper interpretation of the findings. Attrition was substantial—only 32.5 % of randomized participants completed all assessments—creating unequal cell sizes that may have reduced statistical power and introduced attrition bias. The high attrition rate in the waiting-list group likely reflected limited engagement incentives and pandemic-related fatigue, as control participants received no immediate intervention during the lockdown period. Similar patterns of high dropout and variable retention have been documented in digital mental-health trials (e.g., Nwosu et al., 2022; Boucher and Raiker, 2024). In addition, the sample was predominantly female, limiting generalizability to men and gender-diverse populations. Self-report measures are susceptible to biases such as social desirability and response expectancy, which could influence effect-size estimation. However, recent meta-analytic evidence indicates that self-reports do not consistently overestimate treatment effects compared to clinician ratings or objective behavioral indices. In fact, self-report measures tend to produce slightly smaller effect sizes than clinician ratings (Miguel et al., 2025). While self-reports can offer valuable insights, combining them with clinician ratings or behavioral measures can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the treatment's effectiveness. The follow-up period was brief (two weeks), so the durability of benefits remains uncertain. Finally, because the study employed a waiting-list rather than an active control, expectancy or time effects cannot be ruled out. The use of a waiting-list control condition limits our ability to rule out expectancy effects, where participants in the SSRA group may have shown improvement simply due to their anticipation of receiving treatment. Moreover, the waiting-list group was also aware they would eventually receive the intervention, which may have influenced their responses. A more robust design would include an active control group, such as a psychoeducation or attention-control group, which would allow us to better isolate the specific effects of the SSRA intervention.

Future directions

Replication with larger, more diverse samples and longer follow-up intervals is required to verify durability and to detect probable yet non-significant improvements in anxiety and self-efficacy. Mechanistic studies employing ecological momentary assessment or physiological indices (e.g., heart-rate variability) could clarify causal pathways. In stepped-care systems, SSRA might be evaluated as a triage tool: individuals who respond inadequately could be fast-tracked to higher-intensity therapy, thereby optimizing resource allocation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the SSRA group demonstrated large within-session reductions in subjective distress (SUD) and significant between-group improvements in depressive symptoms at the two-week follow-up, suggesting that SSRA is a promising, scalable intervention for psychological distress during public health emergencies. Further research is needed to explore the full range of its effects and to refine its application for anxiety and self-efficacy outcomes.
