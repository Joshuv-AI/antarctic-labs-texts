Transfer Learning for Neutrino Scattering: Domain Adaptation with GANs
José L. Bonilla, Krzysztof M. Graczyk, Artur M. Ankowski, Rwik Dharmapal Banerjee, Beata E. Kowal, Hemant Prasad, Jan T. Sobczyk

Abstract
We utilize transfer learning to extrapolate the physics knowledge encoded in a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) model trained on synthetic charged-current (CC) neutrino–carbon inclusive scattering data. This base model is adapted to generate CC inclusive scattering events (lepton kinematics only) for neutrino–argon and antineutrino–carbon interactions. Furthermore, we assess the effectiveness of transfer learning in re-optimizing a custom model when new data comes from a different neutrino–nucleus interaction model. Our results demonstrate that transfer learning significantly outperforms training generative models from scratch. To study this, we consider two training data sets: one with 10,000 and another with 100,000 events. The models obtained via transfer learning perform well even with smaller training data. The proposed method provides a promising approach for constructing neutrino scattering event generators in scenarios where experimental data is sparse.



I
Introduction

Significant experimental efforts have been devoted to studying (anti)neutrino–nucleus interactions
Avanzini et al. 2022
;
Alvarez-Ruso et al. 2018
in the energy range relevant for next-generation neutrino oscillation experiments, such as Hyper-Kamiokande
Abe et al. 2015
and DUNE
Abi et al. 2020
. In parallel, theoretical models describing these interactions have been developed
Alvarez-Ruso et al. 2018
. The outcomes of both experimental and theoretical advances are incorporated into Monte Carlo (MC) event generators, which simulate (anti)neutrino–nucleus collisions under realistic conditions
Campbell et al. 2024
;
Hayato and Pickering 2021
;
Andreopoulos et al. 2010
;
Mosel and Gallmeister 2019
;
Golan et al. 2012
;
Isaacson et al. 2023
.

MC generators are often tuned to reproduce experimental observations, relying on adjustable parameters that are fitted using available data
Tena-Vidal et al. 2021
. However, this tuning process cannot fully compensate for the fundamental limitations of the underlying models, especially those relying on complex approximations, such as nuclear modeling. Consequently, there is a growing interest in alternative approaches to traditional MC event generation—methods that can learn directly from experimental data and dynamically refine their predictions. Among the most promising directions is the development of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven tools for simulating particle collisions
Radovic et al. 2018
;
Alanazi et al. 2021
;
Butter and et al. 2022
.

In this paper, we present results from our development of generative deep learning models designed to simulate neutrino and antineutrino interactions with nuclei. Specifically, we focus on generative adversarial networks (GANs)
Goodfellow et al. 2014
, which have recently gained much interest in high-energy and nuclear physics
de Oliveira et al. 2017
;
Monk 2018
;
Alanazi et al. 2022
;
Badger et al. 2023
;
Ghosh et al. 2022
;
Ilten et al. 2023
;
Chan et al. 2023
.

Our previous paper
Bonilla et al. 2025
presented one of the first applications of GAN models for simulating neutrino collisions with nuclei. Additionally, Refs.
El Baz and Sánchez 2024
;
El Baz et al. 2025
provide further examples of using generative deep learning techniques in the simulation of neutrino-nucleus interactions, although in these studies, the method of normalizing flows is employed.

In our previous work, we investigated the properties of GAN models trained to reproduce synthetic inclusive and quasi-elastic charged-current (CC) muon neutrino scattering on carbon nucleus data obtained using the
NuWro
MC generator. However, our model only generated the kinematics of the charged muon for a given input neutrino energy. In this paper, we explore a technique known as domain adaptation, a form of transfer learning (TL) in which a model trained on one domain (the source domain) is adjusted to perform well on a different but related domain (the target domain). This technique is particularly promising when data in the target domain is scarce.

We previously explored a similar concept in Ref.
Graczyk et al. 2025
, where a model trained on inclusive electron-carbon scattering data was adapted to predict electron scattering on helium-3, lithium, oxygen, aluminum, calcium, and iron. In this paper, we begin with a baseline model trained on inclusive CC muon neutrino scattering off the carbon nucleus. We then adapt this model to generate predictions for three different processes: muon neutrino scattering off the argon nucleus (relevant for the DUNE experiment), muon-antineutrino scattering off the carbon nucleus, and muon neutrino scattering off the carbon nucleus using data obtained from a different version and configuration of the
NuWro
generator than that used for training the baseline model.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the TL technique, we compare the results from our domain adaptation models with predictions from models that were trained from scratch. Our findings demonstrate that models employing domain adaptation yield more accurate predictions than those trained from scratch, highlighting the potential of this technique in situations with limited information.

This paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, key features of the network and its training are explained. In Sec.
IV
we present the results of the training on multiple neutrino interaction models. Finally, in Sec.
V
we summarize the paper. In Appendix
A
, we discuss the properties of the loss function we used in our analysis.

GAN framework

II.2
Networks

We consider the same network architecture as in our previous paper
Bonilla et al. 2025
(model for inclusive CC neutrino-carbon scattering). The generator takes as input a random vector of length
50
(drawn from a Gaussian multivariate distribution) and neutrino energy
E^{\prime}_{\nu}
. Both inputs are processed independently by two distinct dense layers. Then, the output is concatenated and processed by three blocks, as shown in Fig.
1
.

To obtain the GAN model, one needs to train the discriminator model, which takes as input the proxy variables
\theta^{\prime}
and
E_{\mu}
, processed by two independent blocks. The output is then concatenated and transformed. Eventually, the output is transformed by four blocks, as shown in Fig.
2
.

The fundamental building block of our network consists of a fully connected (dense) layer, followed by layer normalization
Ba et al. 2016
, an activation function—
ReLU
Glorot et al. 2011
for the generator and
PReLU
He et al. 2015
for the discriminator, and a dropout layer. We refer to this configuration as
Block1
. Introducing a skip connection from the input to the output of
Block1
yields
Block2
. When the
E^{\prime}_{\nu}
information is concatenated to the input of
Block1
and subsequently
Block2
, we obtain
Block3
and
Block4
, respectively. Furthermore, Gaussian noise is injected into both the generator and the discriminator after each layer containing learnable parameters. A comprehensive description of the GAN architecture is provided in Ref.
Bonilla et al. 2025
.

Although the architectural scheme remains unchanged, we introduce several modifications compared to the previous analysis. Specifically, Gaussian noise is now injected into both the generator and the discriminator, whereas previously it was applied only for the discriminator. In addition,
Blocks 1 and 2
in the main body of the generator have been replaced by
Blocks 3 and 4
, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. It is important to note that the discriminator architecture remains unchanged (see Fig.
2
). Notably, the discriminator is approximately twice as deep as the generator, and the number of neurons per layer is configured to yield roughly twice the number of trainable parameters per layer compared to the generator.

II.3
Loss and metrics

The loss function has two components: one for the discriminator and one for the generator. As in our previous study, the discriminator loss is binary cross-entropy. In the case of the generator loss, we tested three cases, the same choice as in our previous work, the “classical” one initially proposed in Ref.
Goodfellow et al. 2014
, the heuristic (non-saturating) loss also proposed in Ref.
Goodfellow et al. 2014
, and the sum of both. These preliminary studies showed that in the case of our analyses, the last proposal works the most efficiently. As it is not a common choice of the loss, we discuss it in the Appendix
A
.

The network models are optimized using the
AdamW

Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
algorithm in the initial stage of the training and then with
Amsgrad

Reddi et al. 2018
.

To evaluate the quality of the model, we consider two metrics: the Mean Averaged Pull (MAP)
Demortier and Lyons 2008
;
Alanazi et al. 2022
;
Bonilla et al. 2025
and the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD)
Rubner et al. 1998
. In our earlier studies, we used EMD in its two-dimensional form; however, in this analysis, we adapt the MAP to three dimensions (MAP-3D). This adaptation involves creating a histogram of the variables (
E^{\prime}_{\nu}
,
E^{\prime}_{\mu}
,
\theta^{\prime}
) using a uniformly distributed sample of
E_{\nu}
with a binning of
14\times 14\times 14
.

For the MAP-3D metric, we consider two versions: one that includes all tails, where the bin content of both the
NuWro
histogram (
n_{nuw,i}\neq 0
) and the GAN histogram (
n_{gan,i}\neq 0
) is used, and another that excludes low-content bins, where we only consider bins with
n_{nuw,i}>5
and
n_{gan,i}>5
. Additionally, we compute the MAP metrics using histograms of the two-dimensional distribution of
E_{\mu}^{\prime}
and
\theta^{\prime}
, with a binning of
50\times 50
. The metric values during optimization are calculated on a validation dataset consisting of one million events.

Typically, the best model is the one that minimizes both metrics, but, in the present analysis, to choose the most optimal model,
we evaluate an average of both MAP-3D metrics

\langle\text{MAP}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\text{MAP-3D}+\text{MAP-3D w/o tails})
. The selected model minimizes this metric.

III
Transfer learning

The main idea of our approach is to use a pre-trained model (baseline model) that was trained on inclusive CC muon neutrino scattering off the carbon target. The new training data are uniformly distributed in the energy range (
300
MeV,
10
GeV), similar to our previous analysis.

Using the pre-trained model, we apply the concept of transfer learning
Zhuang et al. 2020
;
Tan et al. 2018
;
Weiss et al. 2016
, assuming that the generator and discriminator have already learned the fundamental patterns underlying neutrino–nucleus interactions. We fine-tune the pre-trained model on smaller training datasets than those used during its initial optimization. This approach leverages the prior knowledge encoded by the model, allowing it to adapt efficiently to new data. A similar strategy was successfully employed in our previous work on modeling inclusive electron scattering data
Graczyk et al. 2025
.

One of the key advantages of the transfer learning technique is that it reduces the need for large amounts of new data when developing models for related processes. Since the pre-trained model already encodes fundamental relationships underlying neutrino scattering, only a relatively small amount of additional data is required to fine-tune the model to a new but related scattering scenario.

To adapt the pre-trained model to new data, we freeze the first block of both the generator and discriminator, as illustrated in Figs.
1
and
2
. The remaining blocks are fine-tuned during the optimization process.

To verify the efficiency of the TL concept, we consider two training data sizes for each process: 100,000 and 10,000 events. Additionally, in each case, we train the model from scratch.

III.1
Domain adaptation

We consider three types of inclusive CC scattering processes:

(A)

\nu_{\mu}
-argon scattering;

(B)

\bar{\nu}_{\mu}
-carbon scattering;

(C)

\nu_{\mu}
-carbon scattering but with a different model of interaction than used in the pre-training.

The pre-trained model generates events that follow the distributions predicted by the
NuWro
ver.
21.09
simulation, which employs the spectral function approach to model the carbon (
12
C) nucleus in the quasielastic (QE) channel
Benhar et al. 1994
, and the local Fermi gas model for other interaction channels.
The generated events may include a charged muon and one or more nucleons, as well as occasionally pions, resulting from final-state interaction (FSI) effects. Pion production is modeled solely through the
\Delta(1232)
resonance
Sobczyk et al. 2005
;
Juszczak et al. 2006
;
Graczyk et al. 2009
, while contributions from more inelastic processes are modeled using parton distribution functions (PDFs).

In scenario (A), we consider the argon (
40
Ar) nucleus, described by a spectral function
Banerjee et al. 2024
derived from the analysis of Jefferson Lab coincidence electron–argon scattering data
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2023
. This setup allows us to test the effectiveness of TL when the nuclear target is changed from carbon to a structurally different nucleus.

In Scenario (B), we use the same model for nuclear and hadronic interactions; however, instead of neutrino–carbon interactions, we consider muon antineutrino–carbon interactions.

Scenario (C) addresses a case where the default model used during pre-training encodes interaction dynamics that differ from those in the fine-tuning data. Specifically, the fine-tuning dataset is generated using the latest version of
NuWro
, ver.
25.03
, which includes an improved treatment of resonant pion production extended to invariant masses up to
1.9
GeV. In addition, we deliberately modify key model parameters: the axial mass in the QE channel is increased from
1.030
GeV to
1.350
GeV, and the axial mass in the pion production model is changed from
0.94
GeV to
1.034
GeV. Finally, the nuclear model is altered by replacing the spectral function with the local Fermi gas model.

The three scenarios differ in their kinematic domains, displaying notable variations in coverage of the relevant phase space, as shown in Fig.
3
. This figure presents the
(E^{\prime}_{\mu},\theta^{\prime})
distributions for 1,000,000 test events in each case: the original test dataset (
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon), and the three test scenarios:
\nu_{\mu}
–argon,
\bar{\nu}_{\mu}
–carbon, and
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon with a modified interaction model. The four datasets exhibit significant differences in their distributions. The most pronounced divergence is observed between the (anti)neutrino–carbon cases and the two involving
\nu_{\mu}
–argon and the altered
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon interaction model. In the latter, a distinct band associated with the QE peak is visible.

IV
Numerical results

To study the effectiveness of transfer learning, we initiated our work by obtaining a baseline (pre-trained) generator and discriminator for inclusive neutrino-carbon scattering events. We trained the model on samples uniformly distributed across different neutrino energies (from
300
MeV to
10
Gev), using a training dataset comprising four million events. To test the quality of this model, we generated new data at fixed neutrino energies:
500
MeV,
1
GeV,
3
GeV,
5
GeV, and
10
GeV, as well as for uniformly distributed samples across the entire energy spectrum. The values of performance metrics of the baseline GAN model are low and comparable to those reported in our previous paper. Table
1
summarizes the quality of the baseline model.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the transfer learning technique – specifically, to determine how well the baseline model captures universal patterns shared across all processes discussed in this paper – we consider two training scenarios. In particular, we train models using both a large dataset of 100,000 and a smaller one containing 10,000 events.

Fig.
4
illustrates the differences in event distributions between the two training datasets for
\nu_{\mu}
–argon scattering events within the neutrino energy range of
450
to
550
MeV. The figure clearly shows that training the GAN and discriminator with only 10,000 events poses a significant challenge.

We begin our discussion with case (A): neutrino scattering on an argon target.

To highlight the differences in optimization between training on 10,000 and 100,000 events, Fig.
5
shows the evolution of performance metrics during training. For the training with 100,000 events, the model’s performance remains stable. It is noteworthy that the best-performing models in the transfer learning framework are reached significantly faster than those trained from scratch.
Furthermore, as summarized in Table
2
, the transfer learning models consistently achieve lower metric values, indicating better performance. This suggests that when trained from scratch, the network struggles to learn the features of
\nu_{\mu}
–argon scattering from limited data, whereas the transfer learning approach, which builds on prior knowledge of
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon interactions, offers a substantial advantage. Eventually, when the number of samples in the training dataset increases, the quality of the obtained GAN models improves. However, even with a large number of samples, the transfer learning model still outperforms the model trained from scratch.

Figs.
6
and
7
show the event distributions in reconstructed hadronic invariant mass
W
generated by our GAN models and
NuWro
for training datasets containing 10,000 and 100,000 events, respectively. This quantity is defined by
W=\sqrt{M_{N}^{2}+2\omega M-Q^{2}}
, with
\omega=E_{\nu}-E_{\mu}
the energy transferred by the neutrino to the struck nucleus,
M
the average nucleon mass, and
Q^{2}
the four-momentum transfer squared

Q^{2}=2E_{\nu}(E_{\mu}-p_{\mu,z})-m_{\mu}^{2}
. The
W
distributions clearly highlight the quasi-elastic and
\Delta(1232)
resonance peaks. We show simulations for several neutrino energies:
500
MeV,
1
,
3
, and
5
GeV. As illustrated, the GAN model trained from scratch struggles to capture the correct structure of the distributions across all energy values. In particular, Fig.
6
highlights the model’s failure to reproduce the peak in the
W
distribution, whereas the model pre-trained on carbon data achieves a remarkably accurate fit. While using a larger, more informative training dataset improves the performance of the model trained from scratch, it still does not reach the accuracy achieved through transfer learning.

Similarly to case (A), we perform an analogous analysis for case (B), involving
\bar{\nu}_{\mu}

V
Summary

We have trained GAN models using transfer learning from a pre-trained GAN originally developed on the inclusive CC
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon MC dataset, adapting them to various other scattering scenarios, including inclusive CC
\bar{\nu}_{\mu}
–carbon,
\nu_{\mu}
–argon, and
\nu_{\mu}
–carbon with alternative interaction models. These models were compared against their counterparts trained from scratch. We observed that initializing from the pre-trained model consistently outperforms training from scratch, particularly when datasets have limited statistical significance or provide a poor sampling of the kinematic distributions of the underlying physical processes. This suggests that the pre-trained model captures essential features of neutrino scattering dynamics.

To evaluate the effectiveness of transfer learning more thoroughly, we also trained the same network architecture on datasets with a significantly larger number of events. This demonstrated that the inferior performance of models trained from scratch on smaller datasets is not due to architectural limitations. In this larger-statistics scenario, the model trained from scratch begins to catch up with the transfer-learned model. Nevertheless, the latter continues to show advantages, particularly in regions where the dataset remains a poor representation of the relevant kinematic phase space.

These findings highlight the promising potential of transfer learning in developing more accurate models for (anti)neutrino–nucleus scattering. In particular, one can start from a model trained on MC simulation data and adapt it to more realistic scenarios using comparatively limited data. This approach offers a practical path forward, especially in cases where experimental statistics are sparse. Further investigation in this direction is planned for future work.

Sigmoid(D)={1}/{1+\exp(D)}
