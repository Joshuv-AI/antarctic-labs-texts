Observation of moiré plasmonic skyrmion clusters
Authors: Lan Zhang, Lipeng Wan, Weimin Deng, Liang Hou, Jumin Qiu, Qiushun Zou, Tongbiao Wang, Daomu Zhao, Tianbao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Skyrmions are topological defects belonging to nontrivial homotopy classes in particle theory. Their remarkably stable topology has recently been observed in electromagnetic waves. For the evanescent fields near a surface, this has been realized so far only for elementary optical skyrmions, with a fixed skyrmion number. Here we report, both in theory and experiment, the concept of moiré plasmonic skyrmion clusters, where multiskyrmions are nested to form a large optical skyrmion cluster. By leveraging twistronics engineering of plasmonic nanostructures, we demonstrate both periodic and quasi-periodic optical skyrmions, revealing a large degree of topological control. In a misaligned composite nanostructure, the rapid inverting of optical skyrmion number is achieved, which is explained by a lattice model. This topological change of moiré plasmonic skyrmion clusters can serve as a precise beacon of the relative alignment deviation between composite nanostructures.

INTRODUCTION

Topology continues to reform optical physics, offering vast opportunities for fundamental research and technological applications, including high-dimensional vortices (1), polarization singularities (2), and correlation switch (3). Skyrmions are topologically stable quasiparticles, originally proposed as a topological soliton solution by the British particle physicist T. Skyrme in his study of unified field theory. Apart from the field of high energy physics, skyrmions have been found in many other fields, such as Bose-Einstein condensates (4), liquid crystals (5), and magnetic materials (6). In 2018, pioneering schemes for realizing optical skyrmion lattices were proposed (7, 8), by either using surface plasmon polariton (SPP) interference to form optical skyrmion lattices or exploiting the topological defects in the local optical spin field, forming an isolated optical spin skyrmion. The hidden spatiotemporal dynamic of optical skyrmions was unveiled using ultrafast photoemission electron microscopy (9–11). These optical skyrmions can be viewed as a vector field unwrapped from a parametric sphere, whose topology is characterized by a skyrmion number. Changing the unwrapping style produces different optical “quasiparticles,” for instance, Néel (12, 13), Bloch (14), antiskyrmion (15) and half-quasiparticle merons (9, 16). Various physical vector fields in electromagnetic can serve as this “parametric sphere” for this mapping (12, 17–20), such as polarization skyrmion in vector beams (21), pseudo-spin skyrmion in photonic crystals (22), supertoroidal pulses (23), electromagnetic vector of spoof-localized surface plasmons (24–26), and optical skyrmion beam generators (27). These unwrapped three-dimensional (3D) topology is closely related to optical vortices (28, 29), revealing parity-time (PT)-symmetric avenues for ultrafast magnetoelectric photonics (30) and unifying polygonal-to-circular texture topology (31). Characterized by their ultracompact size and high stability, photonic skyrmions enable the realization of pico-metric displacement sensing (32).

These seminal works give access to the optical quasiparticles in the form of isolated unit or periodic lattices, with fixed symmetry and skyrmion number. To advance higher density information storage and richer photonic functionality, realization of skyrmion cluster, i.e., multiskyrmion topological structure, a skyrmion bundle contained within per supercell, is essential. Recent outstanding studies have uncovered plasmonic moiré spin angular momentum with higher symmetry (33) and have shown that the symmetry of photonic skyrmion lattices can be tuned by varying the wavelength (34). The simultaneous control of multiple 3D vector field’s topology on a 2D surface plane, however, presents notable challenges in mapping and understanding the topology dynamic, thereby limiting the capacity of photonic devices by encoding information in the topological degree of skyrmions.

In this work, we theoretically propose and experimentally observe the plasmonic skyrmion clusters, in which multiple Néel-type plasmonic skyrmions are nested to form a large optical skyrmion cluster, where the information encoded by the number of skyrmion units exceeding one provides higher density for photonic devices. This is achieved using twistronics engineering of plasmonic nanostructure system. We image this moiré texture in near field, demonstrating the existence of skyrmion clusters of light on the surface of a metal layer. Twisting the composite nanostructures enables the generation of moiré optical skyrmions superlattice, which is induced by the interference of dual groups of skyrmion lattices in one plane. This resembles the twisted bilayer graphene (35–37) and twisted bilayer photonic crystals (38–42). Moiré plasmonic topological fields have deep-subwavelength 3D electric field features, which underlies complex wave propagation and momentum transfer. This moiré control of the 3D electric field yields a physical picture fundamentally different from that of 2D polarized waves, with the geometric phase tightly coupled to angular momentum (43, 44), thereby enabling deep-subwavelength polarization textures that are promising for super-resolution imaging and precision metrology. Our proposed moiré engineering allows one to tune and switch between different topological states of light, producing skyrmion clusters whose number, periodicity, and dynamics are governed by twist angle and symmetry. Since optical skyrmions are topologically stable quasiparticles, the changes in the twist angle of a few degrees allow us to track the optical skyrmion on the nanoscale. Continuous changes in the twist angle result in the observation of the nucleation and the collapse of optical skyrmions, thereby revealing the nanoscale dynamics of optical skyrmions.

This excites the superposition of well-defined transverse-magnetic guided waves, leveraging two hexagonal nanoslits with controlled twist angle to generate superimposed plasmonic electric fields. The concept of skyrmion cluster is a connected 2D region that encloses several skyrmion units. Its total skyrmion number Q is an additive topological invariantQ(θ)=∑ACLq(5)providing that E¯ is continuous on each cluster area ACL. Deriving the transverse electric field from Maxwell’s equations, we obtain the 3D field structure, as seen in Fig. 1D, and skyrmion number for each cluster is calculated to be Q = 3. This is a manifestation of optical skyrmion cluster, which is in contrast to the elementary optical skyrmion (Q = ±1) lattice with C4 and C6 symmetry.

To gain insight into the skyrmion clusters, we construct a skyrmion lattice model (Fig. 1B) that yields moiré lattice landscape. The pattern of optical skyrmions generated by nontwisted structure is recognized as a triangular lattice, particle-like model (Ra). A twisted layer produces a lattice replica (Rb) with the same lattice properties but the rotated lattice vectors. For a 38.21° twist, the superposition of skyrmion lattice and its twisted replica generates a moiré superlattice. Positions where particles from lattices Ra and Rb nearly overlap correspond to optical skyrmions formed by constructive interference, whereas positions with weaker overlap form no-skyrmion boundary.

We formulize the superposition of two skyrmion lattices Ra=n1a1+n2a2 and Rb=n1′a1′+n2′a2′=ℝRa, where 𝑛1, 𝑛2, n1′, and n2′ are integers, and the lattice is described by two primitive lattice vectors a1=(α,0) and a2=(α/2,3α/2), with α being the distance between the closest skyrmions. The interference of dual skyrmion lattices described by Ra and Rb reshapes the topology of the electric field. In the case that Ra= Rb holds for a set of integers {n1,n2,n1′,n2′}, the resulting superimposed electric field is periodic, while superimposed patterns in all other cases are quasi-periodic. The resulting moiré superlattice cell is much larger than in the nontwisted case, with each cell containing multiple skyrmion unit. It is this mechanism that enables the generation of optical skyrmion clusters containing multiple topological units. Using this approach, we can realize both periodic skyrmion superlattices and quasi-periodic skyrmion arrangements on chip, demonstrating that complex topological textures of light can be engineered in a customized manner.

To understand the plasmonic skyrmion clusters in momentum space, we proceed by analyzing the real component of the axial electric field in the spatial frequency domain. Experimental measurement of optical skyrmion clusters

To verify moiré optical skyrmion cluster predicted by this concept and obtain accurate optical responses, we carried out both full-wave numerical simulations and near-field experimental measurements. We fabricated samples with a controlled angle to generate diverse skyrmion clusters. A scattering near-field scanning optical microscope(Neaspec neaSNOM) was used to measure the amplitude and phase of the axial electric field in a 2D space via pseudo-heterodyne interferometric measurements, as illustrated in Fig. 2B (details in Materials and Methods). The real part of the near-field electric field is shown to visualize the local electric field unit vector. The Au film must have an adequate thickness to block light from areas without nanoslits, thereby avoiding unwanted waveguide phenomena and ensuring ease of fabrication. The nanoslits are carved in the 120-nm Au layer atop a 1-mm glass substrate, using a 3-nm Ti adhesion layer between the gold and glass. Using titanium as an adhesive layer has the advantages of low diffusion, time stability, and compatibility with liftoff procedures (46). The slits are 5 μm in length and 200 nm in width. The composite nanostructure is illuminated by a 633-nm (optical frequency c/λ0 ≈ 0.47 PHz and photon energy of ∼1.96 eV) laser with left-handed circular polarization at normal incidence, converting free-space propagating light to an SPP mode with moiré topology defects. The skyrmion cluster can still exist if the incident light is not perfectly circularly polarized (see text S5). The slit-scattering loss and slight position shifts of the nanoslits cause minimal amplitude asymmetry, which does not impair our results (47) (for the properties of excited surface waves, see text S1).

Periodic plasmonic skyrmion clusters

The plasmonic skyrmion clusters are periodic only at specific twist angle, as the relation Ra = Rb holds. For hexagonal lattices, this leads to the commensurate twists with cosθ=(p2+4mp+m2)/2(m2+mp+p2) parameterized by the integers p and m (48). Figure 2A illustrates these angles for various indices p and m. Depending on the value of θ, the skyrmion number per optical skyrmion cluster changes.

SPPs are excited at the interface of air and gold in our system at two commensurate twist angles, i.e., 21.79° and 38.21°. Figure 2J shows the scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the sample with a twist angle of 21.79°. Figure 2 (G and H) presents the real part of the measured axial electric field. On the basis of the experimental parameters, the simulated axial electric field and SND are shown in Fig. 2 (C and D). The spatial Fourier filtering technique is used to remove the noise and background to obtain the Fourier spectrum, as shown in Fig. 2I. This shows a good match to the numerical (Fig. 2E) and theoretical prediction (Fig. 1E). From the reconstructed electric field, the skyrmion cluster texture is extracted: Each supercell hosts one skyrmion at 21.79° and three at 38.21°, forming a periodic cluster arrangement on the near-field of the metal surface. Experimentally, integrating the measured SND over a supercell gives Q = 0.99 for θ = 21.79° and Q = 2.6 for θ = 38.21°, which are in close agreement with the integer skyrmion number Q = 1 and Q = 3. The deviation is attributed to the imperfect alignment in wave vector and polarization of incident beam, as well as errors in sample fabrications. Note that the twisting center does not coincide with the center of the skyrmion unit. For a fixed twist angle, different twisting centers alter the count of skyrmions per cluster within the same supercell (see text S6). The observed plasmonic skyrmion cluster is analogous to skyrmion “bags” recently reported in liquid crystals (49). The change in skyrmion number as the twist angle varies is a result of the modulation of electric field due to the interference between the two twisted skyrmion lattices.

The moiré period of the plasmonic skyrmion cluster decreases sharply with the rotating angle θ, which is g(θ)=α/2(1−cosθ),θ∈(0,π/6) (50), and is related to the SPP wavelength λS, which is g(θ,λS)=2λS/6(1−cosθ) (see Fig. 1F and text S7 for detailed derivations). Since the optical skyrmion lattice has sixfold rotation symmetry, twisting by θ and by (60° − θ) produces the same moiré period related by a global rotation (51). This is observed for the case of θ = 21.79° and θ = 38.21°, yielding the same optical skyrmion period 3.12λS (Fig. 2F), in accordance with the moiré period given by g(θ,λS). In contrast to the optical skyrmion lattices, typically featuring one skyrmion per unit cell with a fixed density, this realizes a twist-controlled skyrmion cluster, hosting multiple plasmonic skyrmion units per moiré supercell (see text S8 for skyrmion clusters generated from twisted hexagonal nanoslits with different commensurate twist angles).

Quasi-periodic plasmonic skyrmion clusters

Of particular interest is the case as the twist angle is incommensurate. The SEM image of the sample with an incommensurate twist angle of 30° is provided in Fig. 3H. The optical skyrmion lattices, referred to as superhexagonal topology (52), can persist in the aperiodic case. This is illustrated in Fig. 3 (A and E) for a 15° twist, the skyrmion cluster predicted by the theoretical model and numerical simulation contains five skyrmion units. The experimental measured skyrmion number is Q = 4.4, which is close to the theoretical value of Q = 5, corresponding to identified five plasmonic skyrmions contained in each cluster. The deviation in the skyrmion number is attributed to deformed, edge-localized skyrmions caused by optical misalignment and imperfect sample fabrications. Instead, for the 30° twisted configuration (Fig. 3, B and F), the optical skyrmion units form a more disordered pattern, lacking the symmetry observed in the 15° configuration. This disordered skyrmion arrangement can be identified in Fig. 3D. Compared to the numerical Fourier spectrum (Fig. 3C), the measured result (Fig. 3G) displays a double-ring feature. We attribute this to a residual phase ramp introduced by synchronizing the bottom parabolic mirror illumination with the sample scan to keep the excitation stationary with respect to the sample. According to the Fourier shift theorem, multiplying the field by a real-space ramp displaces its spectrum in Fourier space. Small interferometer drifts at visible wavelengths likely accentuate this effect. Moiré interference in composite nanostructures breaks the periodic symmetry in skyrmions but unexpectedly spawns optical skyrmions topological states with quasi-periodic order. This quasi-periodic state of optical skyrmion not only retains the topologically conserved properties of individual skyrmion (Q = 1) but also forms a quasi-periodic lattice by quasi-periodic spatial arrangement.

The optical skyrmion with multi–degree of freedom encoding capabilities (e.g., Q value and Q position) can empower the photonic integrated circuits for high-throughput optical information processing.

In small change of the twist angles, the skyrmion topology is robust, but the nanoscale change in displacement of optical skyrmion is pronounced. In Fig. 4 (G to I), we plot the skyrmion movement ∣Γ(θ0,∆θ)∣ as a function of the change in twist angle. This displacement of the skyrmion is not symmetric with positive and negative changes in the twist angle. This is expected since the change of evanescent field is not linear with respect to the twist angle but modulated by the cosine function, as can be seen from Eq. 1.

Alignment deviation beacon via optical skyrmions

Although the moiré optical skyrmion is robust to the small change of twist angle, the effect caused by the alignment deviation between the composite structures cannot be mitigated. We first consider nontwisted composite nanostructures θ = 0, in which there is a lateral misalignment ∆r(∆x,∆y)=(x−xs,y−ys) (Fig. 5A). This plasmonic skyrmion topology is distorted accompanied with symmetries breaking, the critical aspect of this misalignment lies in the emergence of topological transition. This takes place when two triangular skyrmion lattices interfere to generate a honeycomb lattice, thus resulting in the inversion of skyrmion number. The critical inversion condition of the optical skyrmion number is (see text S9)∆r=ε3(n1a1+n2a2);(n1−n2)≢0 {mod 3}(7)where ε is a 2 by 2 antisymmetric matrix that relates the coordinates of the composite structures and the optical skyrmion lattices. In the case that (n1−n2)≡0 {mod 3}, two sets of excited skyrmion lattices coincide, showing the absence of optical skyrmion switching (Fig. 5B). To prove this, we numerically simulate the lateral misalignment and scan the skyrmion number per lattice versus the alignment deviations along the y axis (∆x = 0; Fig. 5C) and the x axis (∆y = 0; Fig. 5D), respectively. When the condition given by Eq. 7 is satisfied, rapid inversion of the optical skyrmion number from positive to negative is observed, and in the intermediate state, these optical skyrmions collapse. Note that inversion does not occur when the displacement is along the x axis (Fig. 5D). The physical picture becomes trivial for the twisted cases θ ≠ 0, as the sudden switch of optical skyrmion state disappears because of the symmetry breaking of the superposed structures. Figure 5 (E to G) shows the axial electric field for the structural alignment deviations (α,0), (α/2,3α/2), and (0,3α) at 30° twist, respectively. The displacement induces the translation of the optical skyrmion superlattice, which can be interpreted from the change in the constant phase factor.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we theoretically propose and experimentally measure the plasmonic skyrmion clusters containing multiskyrmions in a unit cell. Dual groups of optical skyrmion lattices are interfered to generate plasmonic skyrmion clusters via twistronics engineering of plasmonic nanostructures. This method provides a step toward multi-Q optical skyrmions. It indicates that topological quasiparticles of light can organize into clusters, yielding the light field on the metal film that behaves as an optical analog of moiré superlattice generated by twisted bilayer graphene. The twist angle controls the nanoscale change of optical skyrmion, affording a powerful platform to engineer and explore the plasmonic skyrmion clusters in commensurate and incommensurate geometries, for instance, tracking the nucleation and collapse of optical skyrmion units and their dynamics at nanoscale. The experimental results corroborate our numerical and theoretical findings, which demonstrate the moiré-induced phenomena in twist-plasmonic systems. We note that the spatiotemporal dynamic was also captured quite recently (53) using time-resolved photoemission electron microscope, paralleled by reports of moiré spin superlattices (33, 54). This process is far from ordinary; for instance, interaction of the dual plasmonic vortices can influence plasmon spin topology and thereby the momentum-driven transport (44). The formation of multiskyrmion complexes showcases the potential of twist-angle degrees of freedom for controlling high-dimensional topology at subwavelength scales. Coupling atoms to these optical skyrmion cluster textures may stimulate the optical emulation of quantum many-body dynamics with topology-tunable couplings (55), support high-dimensional quantum information processing, and provide an expanded range of topological charges that can be encoded for on-chip communication with topological light.

In the case of well-aligned composite structures, the resulting moiré pattern facilitates the formation of optical skyrmions with a well-defined skyrmion number. However, displacement errors of the structures can cause the switch of skyrmion topology, providing applications in the characterization of twisted bilayer structures and in the alignment of lithography mask. For instance, the nanometer-scale optical metrology could be achieved by measuring the change of skyrmion number. Our results are quite general, as it can not only be integrated into various photonic devices (56–58) and technologies (59–61), offering promising functionalities (62, 63) and improved performance (64), but also be applied to other wave systems, ranging from water waves (65) to partially coherent waves (66–68). More broadly, our results suggest that moiré engineering—previously confined to van der Waals materials—represents a universal strategy for manipulating quasiparticle topology across wave systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Numerical implementation

For numerical simulations of the near field of the plasmonic system, a 3D finite-difference time-domain method is performed to solve Maxwell’s equation numerically in time domain. The composite nanostructure and monitoring points are placed within a 35 μm by 30 μm by 1.5 μm volume, with perfectly matched layer boundaries are set in all the directions. A mesh with a resolution of 20 nm by 20 nm by 5 nm is used to cover both the nanostructure and the monitoring points, ensuring adequate sampling of the optical skyrmion. The thickness and permittivity of the gold film are set as 120 nm and ε=−10.6+1.7i (69), respectively. In the simulation, a left circularly polarized Gaussian beam with a wavelength of 633 nm is incident on the plasmonic nanostructure. The skyrmion cluster still exists if the incident light’s wavelength changes in a certain range (see text S10). The beam waist is larger than the dimensions of the sample structure to ensure that all the nanoslits are well excited.

Sample fabrication

The samples are fabricated on a 120-nm-thick Au layer, which was deposited previously on a 1-mm-thick glass substrate using electron beam evaporation (HHV, AUTO500) with a deposited rate of 1 Å/s. A 3-nm Ti is used as the adhesion layer between the gold and glass. Subsequently, the SPP coupling composite structures with a nanoslit width of 200 nm are etched using focused ion beam etching (Helios G4) from the metal side. This is controlled by the software named NanoBuilder. The etching parameters were set as 7 pA and 30 kV, respectively. The fabricated sample was verified by the SEM, as shown in text S11. Recessed structures in these samples hinder the emission and detection of secondary electrons, leading to localized attenuation that appears as darker regions in the SEM images. The topological properties of skyrmions are highly sensitive to angular dislocations within composite nanostructures (see text S12), necessitating high precision in sample fabrication. The processing errors are minimized within acceptable limits. Specifically, for twist angles of 21.79°, 38.21°, 15°, and 30°, the processing errors are 1.3, 0.8, 1.1, and 1.2%, respectively. These underscore the level of precision required to accurately capture the topological dynamics of skyrmions.

Near-field measurements

The amplitude of the near-field signal is measured using a scattering (aperture-free) near-field scanning optical microscope (s-NSOM, Neaspec Ltd., VIS-neaSCOPE+s type), with the experimental configuration schematically illustrated in Fig. 2B. A 633-nm continuous-wave laser (Cobalt) is used, with its beam split into two optical paths. One beam is weakly focused by a plano-convex lens, illuminating the sample from the glass side in a transmission-mode configuration. The second beam is used for interferometric pseudo-heterodyne detection, which is modulated by a vibrating mirror with a frequency of 300 Hz interfered with the signal scattered from the sample to reconstruct the electric field information, including amplitude and phase of the optical field. To achieve circularly polarized incident light, a quarter-wave plate is positioned in the optical path before the sample. The sample is positioned on a moving stage, where a silicon atomic force microscope tip coated with platinum predominantly scattered the axial electric field component into a detector. The tip vibrates at oscillation frequencies between 200 and 230 kHz. The tapping amplitude is 50 to 70 nm. This nanotip predominantly scatters the axial electric field component into a detector through a parabolic mirror, where the integration time of detector is set to be 0 to 16 ms to ensure a good signal-to-noise ratio. The desirable electric field, stemming from the near field of the structure, is then demodulated at higher harmonics of the tip’s vibration to suppress the scattered free-space background signal (70). In the measurements, the signal is extracted from the second demodulation order.
