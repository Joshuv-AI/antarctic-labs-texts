Genetic‐Proteomic Integration Identifies Predictive Plasma Proteins for Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Yuan Ding, Dylan Hamitouche, Simon Thebault, Patrick Kearns, Ahmed Abdelhak, Adil Harroud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMultiple sclerosis (MS) develops after a prolonged preclinical phase. Identifying circulating biomarkers that capture this early biology can improve risk stratification and guide intervention. We aimed to identify plasma proteins driving MS susceptibility using large‐scale proteogenomic integration and to evaluate their prediagnostic predictive value and effects on severity.MethodsWe used cis‐acting protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) for 2,545 plasma proteins (n = 80,824). Predicted protein levels were tested for association with MS risk in 14,802 cases and 26,703 controls using Mendelian randomization and colocalization. Splicing annotations were integrated to interpret platform‐specific discrepancies. We validated candidates as predictors of incident MS in prediagnostic United Kingdom Biobank samples (124 cases, 52,515 controls; median 5.9 years prediagnosis) and assessed associations with disease severity (n = 12,584).ResultsWe identified 39 proteins associated with MS risk. Most formed a densely connected network enriched in immune regulatory pathways, B‐ and T‐cell costimulation, cytokine signaling, and Epstein–Barr virus‐related pathways. Transcriptomic enrichment was strongest in B‐cell subsets. Splicing data suggested that discordances between proteomic platforms reflect distinct proteoforms. Among 28 genetically implicated proteins measured in prediagnostic samples, 8 were associated with time to MS diagnosis, demonstrating enrichment beyond chance expectation (p binom = 4.92 × 10−5). DKKL1 showed concordant protective associations across risk, incidence, and severity. Integrating pQTLs improved fine‐mapping resolution at colocalized loci by >10‐fold and nominated 13 putative novel risk loci.InterpretationThis integrated genetic‐proteomic framework identifies causal proteins, refines risk loci, and supports the development of early predictive biomarkers with translational potential. These findings support genetically anchored biomarkers for preclinical disease detection and intervention. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:268–281

Although therapies targeting the inflammatory component of multiple sclerosis (MS) have improved patient outcomes, MS remains incurable, and current treatments often fail to prevent long‐term disability. Increasing evidence suggests that the disease process begins years before the first clinical presentation, indicating a key window for early intervention and therapeutic prevention, a strategy supported by successful efforts in other autoimmune diseases. 1 However, given the low incidence of MS and the prolonged follow‐up required to assess interventions, practical early biomarkers that facilitate trial enrichment and risk stratification are needed.

Blood proteins are attractive candidate biomarkers given their accessibility and relative ease of measurement. Although biomarkers need not be causal, those implicated in disease mechanisms can provide insights into pathogenesis and identify therapeutic targets. 2 Identifying such proteins may, therefore, not only inform early detection but also guide therapeutics.

Advances in proteomic technology now enable the simultaneous measurement of thousands of circulating proteins using high‐throughput multiplexed assays. 3 These technologies provide a promising unbiased discovery approach, but their application to MS has been limited by cost and small sample sizes. In addition, protein changes identified in such studies may reflect disease consequences (reverse causality), as well as comorbidities or treatment effects (confounding), rather than causal or early factors in disease initiation.

Genetic studies offer complementary insights, as disease‐associated variants can reveal causal genes and downstream proteins involved in disease onset. To date, genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 200 variants associated with MS risk. However, most lie in non‐coding regions and occur in linkage disequilibrium blocks, making it difficult to pinpoint causal variants (fine‐mapping) and target genes.

Integrating proteomic measurements with genetic data can help address these challenges. Genetic variants influencing protein levels can serve as proxies for protein abundance, enabling the prediction of relative protein differences at the group level. These genetic proxies often explain a substantial proportion of variance in protein concentrations (average 20.5%, up to 90%) 4 and, therefore, may improve the resolution of causal variant identification. In addition, because genotypes are fixed at birth and less prone to confounding, they provide a robust framework for causal inference. Recent studies applying this approach have identified several plasma proteins associated with MS risk. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, discordance across proteomic platforms remains unresolved, prediagnostic validation has not been performed, and the relevance of these proteins to disease severity is unclear.

Here, we applied an integrated genetic‐proteomic approach combining Mendelian randomization (MR) and colocalization to identify proteins causally implicated in MS. Specifically, we leveraged 2,545 protein measurements from 3 cohorts, spanning 2 distinct proteomics platforms and totaling 80,824 individuals, to derive genetically predicted protein levels and assess their association with MS in an independent set of 14,802 cases and 26,703 controls. Following sensitivity analyses, we nominated 39 causal proteins and resolved discrepancies across platforms. We then validated their predictive value in a cohort of individuals who were later diagnosed with MS, up to 15 years before diagnosis. Additionally, we tested the genetically predicted effects of the validated proteins for association with MS severity in a cohort of 12,584 cases, identifying DKKL1 as a candidate with concordant protective effects on risk and severity. Finally, our integrated approach improved fine‐mapping resolution by more than 10‐fold and identified novel MS risk loci supported by external validation. These findings refine genetic factors and pathways involved in MS onset, and highlight protein biomarkers that may enhance early risk stratification in MS and inform targeted therapies.

Materials and Methods

Plasma Protein Quantitative Trait Loci

We selected genetic variants robustly associated with circulating protein levels, known as protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL), using the 3 largest proteomic cohorts available (Tables 1). First, the United Kingdom (UK) Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (hereafter, referred to as UKB) measured 2,931 proteins in 54,219 individuals using the antibody‐based Olink Explore 3,072 proximity extension assay (PEA), and identified 14,287 primary genetic associations (p < 1.7 × 10−11). 4 These individuals represent a subset of the UK Biobank, a prospective population‐based study in the United Kingdom of 502,150 participants enrolled between 2006 and 2010 (average age: 56.5; female: 54.4%) and followed longitudinally. Second, a study performed by deCODE genetics (DCG) assessed 4,719 proteins in 35,559 participants from Iceland using aptamer‐based SomaScan v4 assay, identifying 18,667 primary pQTLs (p < 1.8 × 10−9) (Note S1). 10 The participants were drawn from the population‐based Icelandic Cancer Project and deCODE Health study, as well as various genomic programs at DCG (average age: 55 years; female: 57%). 10 , 11 , 12 Third, the Fenland study (FEN) measured 4,775 proteins in 10,708 participants using the SomaScan v4 assay, identifying 10,674 primary pQTLs (p < 1.004 × 10−11). These participants were a subset of 12,435 recruited from general practice surgeries in the Cambridgeshire region (United Kingdom) 2005 and 2015 (average age 48.6 years, 53.3% female). 13 , 14

We included cohorts using both antibody‐ and aptamer‐based assays as they are subject to different sources of biological and technical variation. These result in substantial variability in protein correlations across platforms, which are generally modest (median Spearman correlation <0.4). 10 Measurements across platforms may capture different epitopes or proteoforms of the same protein, and genetic analyses may also identify protein‐altering variants that modify binding site structure (epitope effect) without affecting true protein abundance. Given these challenges, and because binding sites are often unknown or proprietary, we hypothesized that combining both technologies could be complementary for biological discovery.

Because the MS risk GWAS included only individuals of European ancestry, 15 and mismatched ancestry in MR introduces bias from population stratification, 16 we used the European subset of UKB (n = 34,557) for the primary analysis. A sensitivity analysis was performed using the full multiethnic cohort, including non‐European ancestries (n = 931 African, n = 920 Central/South Asian, n = 308 Middle Eastern, n = 262 East Asian, and n = 97 admix from America) (Note S2). Because a subset of the prediagnostic MS cases used for validation may have been included in the primary subset, we verified that re‐deriving pQTLs after excluding all MS cases yielded identical MR results (Pearson r >0.99) (data not shown). The remaining 2 cohorts consisted of individuals of European ancestry. 10 , 13 For each cohort, we selected variants reaching the study‐wise significance threshold and located within or near the coding gene (cis‐pQTLs; UKB and DCG: ±1Mb, FEN: ±500kb). Cis‐pQTLs are more likely to influence the outcome through the protein of interest, for example, by directly altering transcription levels, rather than through unrelated proteins or pathways (horizontal pleiotropy), reducing bias in causal inference. 17

MR

For each cohort, cis‐pQTLs were matched to variants from the MS risk GWAS. 15 When no direct match was available, linkage disequilibrium (LD) proxies were identified using the 1,000 Genomes Project 18 (r 2 >0.7). To ensure independence between selected variants, we performed LD clumping (r 2 <0.001, window = 250kb). Variants in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) were excluded given the potential for confounding by LD.

The causal effect of each plasma protein on MS risk was estimated using the TwoSampleMR package in R. 19 For proteins with a single cis‐pQTL, we applied the Wald ratio, and for proteins with multiple independent cis‐pQTLs, we used the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and proteins with p FDR <0.05 were considered significant. To assess the directionality of effects and mitigate reverse causality, we applied Steiger filtering. 20 This method compares the variance explained by cis‐pQTLs for the exposure (protein levels) and the outcome (MS), excluding variants that explained more variance in the outcome than in the exposure. In one instance where 2 proteins shared the same cis‐pQTL, we performed additional analyses to account for potential horizontal pleiotropy (Note S3).

Colocalization Analysis

To validate MR associations and minimize bias from horizontal pleiotropy, we performed colocalization analyses to assess whether plasma protein levels and MS risk share the same causal variant. For each protein, all variants within ±250kb of the lead cis‐pQTL were analyzed using the coloc package in R, 21 which estimates the posterior probability (PPH4) of a shared causal variant under the assumption of a single causal variant per locus using summary statistics derived approximate Bayes factors. To relax this assumption and account for multiple signals in regions of complex LD, we additionally performed colocalization using the sum of single effects (SuSiE) model, 22 allowing up to 5 independent causal variants per locus. LD matrices were generated using the European subset of the Haplotype Reference Consortium as a reference. 23 Strong evidence of colocalization was defined as a posterior probability (PP H4 ≥0.7) based on either model.

Assessment of Cross‐Platform Consistency

To assess the concordance of causal estimates across platforms, we calculated the pairwise Pearson correlation of MR‐derived Z‐scores for each protein between the 3 cohorts. These causal estimates reflect the genetically proxied effect of protein levels on MS risk. To explore potential sources of discordance, we annotated cis‐pQTL variants based on evidence of splicing effects, classifying variants as splice quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) if they were included in the 95% credible set for splicing regulation of their associated gene in any of 50 tissues in GTEx v10. 24 Under the hypothesis that differences in proteoforms, including those driven by alternative splicing, may influence cross‐platform variability, we repeated the pairwise correlations after excluding proteins whose cis‐pQTL proxies showed discordant splicing evidence between cohorts.

Pathway Over‐Representation Analysis

To understand the biological processes and the molecular function of MS‐associated proteins, we performed over‐representation analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) 25 and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) 26 from ClusterProfiler. 27

Protein–Protein Interaction Analysis

We constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of the MS‐associated proteins using STRING. 28 To assess the degree of influence of individual proteins within the observed PPI network, 4 centrality measures were calculated: degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. 29 , 30 , 31 To evaluate whether the observed network was more connected than expected by chance, we generated a null distribution of edge counts by constructing 10,000 random PPI networks of randomly sampled proteins. Each random network included the same amount of nodes (proteins) as the MS‐associated network, selected from a background of 2,545 proteins included in this study, to control for potential biases specific to proteins with cis‐pQTLs. Edge counts were calculated in STRING under the same conditions, and an empirical p‐value was calculated as the proportion of random networks with edge counts greater than or equal to the observed value. Refined over‐representation analysis on the PPI network was performed using Cytoscape 32 to identify enriched biological pathways, integrating annotations from GO, 25 KEGG, 26 Reactome, 33 Disease ontology, 34 and WikiPathways. 35

Cell Type Specificity and Enrichment

MS‐associated proteins were first evaluated for enrichment across immune cell types using RNA sequencing data from 29 peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) subsets, characterized by bulk RNA‐sequence (RNA‐seq) and flow cytometry. 36 A Wilcoxon rank‐sum test compared the expression levels (pseudo‐transcripts per million [pTPM]) of genes encoding MS‐associated proteins against the broader transcriptomic background within each cell type. FDR correction was applied. Expression profiles of MS‐associated protein‐encoding genes were then examined using single‐cell RNA sequencing data across 76 immune and non‐immune cell types from the Human Protein Atlas. 37 Cell type specificity was defined based on 3 criteria: (1) at least a 4‐fold higher expression in a single cell type compared to any other; (2) at least a 4‐fold higher expression in a group of 2 to 10 cell types compared to any other; and (3) at least a 4‐fold higher expression in a given cell type compared to the mean of all others. 37

Validation of MS‐Associated Proteins in Prediagnostic Cases

We validated the MS‐associated proteins by examining their association with time to MS diagnosis in a nested case–control study within the UKB (Table 2). Incident MS cases were defined as individuals with a first MS diagnosis occurring after baseline blood collection, with time‐to‐event calculated from the year of birth to the date of diagnosis. Controls were defined as individuals who did not develop MS by the end of follow‐up (May 31, 2022), with time‐to‐event measured from year of birth, and death treated as a censoring event. Because death can preclude MS diagnosis, we also accounted for this competing risk using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models and the same covariates. To reduce bias from disease activity before diagnosis, we applied a 180‐day washout period before MS diagnosis, extending to 730 days in sensitivity analyses. Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted for plasma levels of proteins measured by PEA, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and baseline medication count. A reduced model excluding smoking and medications was also fitted. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed both globally per model, as well as for each plasma protein and covariates individually. We evaluated the enrichment of significant associations using a binomial test and by generating an empirical null distribution through random sampling of 28 proteins (matching the number of available proteins) from the full set of 2,923 proteins. Cox regression analyses were repeated for each random set under identical modeling, and empirical p‐values were calculated based on 1,000 iterations.

MR Analysis of MS Severity

We hypothesized that some MS‐associated proteins may also influence disease progression. To test this, we applied our MR framework to MS‐associated proteins validated in the UKB prediagnostic cohort, using genetic associations with age‐related MS disability (ARMSS) scores at last follow‐up from our GWAS of 12,584 individuals with MS. 38 Given the lack of well‐powered sex‐specific GWAS summary statistics for MS severity, we assessed potential sex‐specific effects for significant proteins by testing associations between observed plasma protein levels and MS risk separately in men and women within the UKB prediagnostic cohort, using the same Cox proportional hazards model and covariates.

Fine‐Mapping of MS Risk Loci

Given that genetic variants typically explain more variance in protein levels than in disease risk, we assessed whether integrating pQTL data could improve causal variant resolution at MS risk loci. At loci with strong evidence of colocalization, indicating a shared causal effect on MS and protein levels, we performed fine‐mapping within ±250kb windows for each trait and compared the median size of the 95% credible sets under both single 21 and multiple causal variant 39 frameworks. We also conducted fine‐mapping jointly conditional on colocalization.

Nomination and Replication of Novel MS Risk Loci

We leveraged the identification of MS‐associated proteins to nominate novel MS risk loci that had not reached genome‐wide significance in the International Multiple Sclerosis Genetics Consortium (IMSGC) GWAS, 15 potentially because of limited statistical power. Loci were considered novel if they harbored cis‐pQTL variants with strong MR and colocalization support and were in low LD (r 2 <0.1) with any of the 200 non‐MHC established risk variants. To validate their association with MS risk, we assessed the lead variant at each locus in a random‐effects meta‐analysis of 2 independent cohorts: FinnGen 40 (1,356 cases, 395,209 controls) and UKB 41 (2,620 cases, 449,770 controls). Given the smaller case numbers relative to the original GWAS (n = 47,429), we evaluated whether the direction of effect was concordant with the discovery GWAS using a binomial test.

Results

Discovery of MS‐Associated Proteins

Across the 3 proteomic cohorts, a total of 2,545 proteins were successfully instrumented using cis‐pQTLs and harmonized with the MS case–control GWAS (UKB: 1,579, DCG: 1,517, FEN: 1,481), including proxy variants where necessary (median r 2: 0.97, interquartile range: 0.87–1.00) (Tables S1–S3). Of these, 1,398 were ascertained in 2 or more cohorts, with 750 identified across both proteomic platforms. Additionally, 1,147 proteins were unique to a single cohort, underscoring the strength of our multi‐source approach (Fig S1). Following multiple testing corrections, proteome‐wide MR identified 58 unique proteins significantly associated with MS susceptibility (UKB: 41, DCG: 28, FEN: 24) (see Fig 1A–C and Tables S4–S6). Bayesian colocalization analysis, conducted under single and multiple causal variant assumptions, provided strong evidence of shared causal effects between protein levels and MS risk for 39 proteins (67.2%), effectively mitigating potential bias from horizontal pleiotropy. Steiger filtering confirmed the causal direction of protein levels influencing MS risk, with no evidence of reverse causality (Tables S7–S9). These 39 proteins are hereafter referred to as “MS‐associated proteins.”

Resolving Cross‐Platform Variability

We anticipated that true causal effects of protein levels on MS risk should remain consistent across measurement platforms. However, modest correlations between antibody‐ and aptamer‐based platforms and potential epitope effects suggest variability that warrants further investigation (Methods). To address this, we assessed the concordance of MR‐estimated causal effects on MS across platforms and cohorts in a pairwise manner. Among MR‐significant proteins, a strong correlation in causal effect sizes was observed between the 2 cohorts using aptamer‐based measurements (DCG vs FEN: r = 0.94), as expected. In comparison, coefficients were weaker with PEA‐based measurements (UKB vs DCG: r = 0.57, UKB vs FEN: r = 0.55) (Fig 2) with evidence of opposite effect directions for CD58, a co‐stimulatory receptor involved in T and natural killer (NK) cell activation with an immunoregulatory soluble form. 42 We identified that this discrepancy is likely explained by the cis‐pQTLs for CD58 in the aptamer‐based measurement being splice variants (Tables S10 and S11), whereas this was not the case for the PEA‐derived pQTL from UKB. Notably, excluding variants discordant with respect to splicing effects substantially improved correlation coefficients (UKB vs DCG: r = 0.99, UKB vs FEN: r = 0.95, DCG vs FEN: r = 0.96), indicating that alternative splicing, and therefore, proteoform‐specific detection, explains a considerable amount of the cross‐platform variability (see Fig 2 and Tables S10–S12). Therefore, the assays likely capture levels of differentially spliced isoforms of the same protein, which may not necessarily exert the same risk on MS. This correlation improved further when considering only the colocalized MS‐associated proteins (UKB vs DCG: 1.00, UKB vs FEN: 1.00, DCG vs FEN: 0.99).

Identification of MS Risk Pathways and Networks

The 39 MS‐associated proteins were significantly enriched in 153 biological processes, 8 molecular functions, and 21 KEGG pathways (Tables S13–S15). These included multiple immune regulation processes, immune cell communication and trafficking, and glycosaminoglycan metabolism (p FDR < 0.05). Over‐represented molecular functions included complement binding (p FDR = 0.033) and virus receptor activity (p FDR = 0.029), driven partly by complement receptor 1 (CR1, also known as CD35), an Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) receptor participating in B cell infection. 43 PPI networks revealed substantial physical and functional connectivity with significantly greater interactions than expected by chance (p = 5 × 10−4) (Fig 3B). The largest PPI cluster, comprising 21 (53%) proteins, demonstrated dense interactions centered around key immune regulators including CD40, CD5, TNFRSF1A, MAPK3, STAT3, IL7R, SLAMF1, and CD58 (see Fig 3A). The absolute Z‐scores of MS‐associated proteins correlated strongly with multiple network centrality measures (eg, eigenvector centrality, r = 0.69) (see Fig 3C and Table S16), indicating that proteins more strongly associated with MS tend to occupy central network positions, consistent with functional convergence. Permutation analysis of 10,000 randomly sampled protein networks of equal size confirmed that this correlation was unlikely to arise by chance or annotation bias alone (Note S4 and Fig S2).

Refined pathway analysis of this cluster further implicated processes such as the B cell receptor signaling pathway, EBV infection, and regulation of membrane attack complex activation (see Fig 3D and Table S17). In addition, smaller PPI pairs were identified, including the FUCA1‐MANBA pair enriched in the N‐glycan biodegradation pathway (p FDR = 3.3 × 10−4). Transcriptomic analysis of genes encoding for the 39 MS‐associated proteins across 29 immune cell subsets revealed robust enrichment in multiple B cell subsets, with the top 3 cell types identified as naive B cells (CD27−IgD+), exhausted memory (double negative) B cells (CD27−IgD−), and non‐switched memory B cells (CD27+IgD+) (see Fig 3E). Additional enriched cell types included innate immune cells, regulatory and memory T‐cells (Table S18). Consistent with this, the evaluation of individual MS protein‐encoding genes at single‐cell resolution revealed the highest number of genes with cell type specificity in monocytes and B cells (Fig S3A and Table S19). Specificity was also observed in central nervous system (CNS)‐resident cells, including microglia, as previously reported, 15 with some genes (eg, BLNK) showing dual specificity in peripheral immune cells and microglia. Notably, we also observed CNS specificity extending to neuronal cells, oligodendrocyte lineage cells, and astrocytes (Fig S3B).

Genetically Identified Proteins Predict MS in Prediagnostic Samples

Among 28 MS‐associated proteins quantified by PEA in prediagnostic UKB samples, 8 (28%) were significantly associated with time to MS diagnosis (Fig 4A). This observed number of significant proteins exceeded chance expectations under both a binomial test (p = 4.92 × 10−5) and an empirical null distribution (p = 0.011) (see Fig 4C), supporting the early pathogenic relevance of these proteins in MS. These associations were consistent across minimally and fully adjusted Cox models (Tables S20 and S21), as well as across different washout periods (Fig S4), and remained robust when accounting for the competing risk of death using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models (Fig S5). None of the 8 proteins associated with future MS diagnosis violated proportional hazard assumptions (Tables S20 and S21). However, directions of effect frequently diverged from the MR estimates, with only 1 protein showing concordance (DKKL1).

DKKL1 Is a Candidate Marker for MS Risk and Severity

Having validated 8 genetically prioritized proteins as MS risk predictors, we next examined whether any also influence disease severity. MR using the ARMSS GWAS showed that higher genetically predicted DKKL1 levels were associated with lower disability (β = −0.03, p = 2.94 × 10−3) (Fig 5A and Tables S22 and S23). Because transcriptomic data indicate that DKKL1 expression is highest in male reproductive tissues, we tested for sex‐specific effects. As sex‐stratified MS GWAS (risk and severity) remained under‐powered and of limited availability, sex‐differential effects were examined using measured DKKL1 concentrations in prediagnostic UKB samples. Associations with time to MS diagnosis were comparable in men and women (hazard ratio [HR]male = 0.89, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.64–1.23; HRfemale = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.66–0.98; p interaction = 0.72), with tighter CIs in women likely because of larger sample size (nfemale = 85, nmale = 39). This indicates that the protective effect of DKKL1 is sex‐independent and probably mediated by expression outside gonadal tissue, in line with its expression in B cells (see Fig 5B).

Identification of Causal Variants at Colocalized Loci

Using the MS GWAS data alone, we successfully constructed 95% credible sets containing a causal variant in only 13 of 39 (33.3%) unique colocalized loci, reflecting limited power at current sample sizes to identify causal variants. In contrast, fine‐mapped 95% credible sets could be constructed in all pQTL instances across proteomic platforms, under both single and multiple causal variant assumptions (Tables S24 and S25). At the 13 unique successfully fine‐mapped loci in both MS and pQTL (UKB: 9, DCG: 4, FEN: 3), the credible set size conditional on colocalization were 12.3‐fold smaller than those from MS alone (medianconditional = 2, medianMS = 24.5) (Fig 6D and Table S26). The reduction was more pronounced when allowing for multiple colocalizing signals per locus (23‐fold reduction) (Fig S6 and Table S27). Therefore, our integrative approach substantially improved causal variant resolution for MS risk, often reducing loci to a single variant, thereby greatly facilitating downstream functional validation studies. 44

Identification of Putatively Novel Loci

We identified cis‐pQTLs (UKB: 12, DCG: 8, FEN: 8) that proxied 15 MS‐associated proteins that were also statistically independent (r 2 <0.1) of the 200 previously established non‐MHC MS risk variants. These variants were referred to as putatively novel variants (Table S28). Among the 15 putative novel variants, 13 showed concordant effect sizes in the meta‐analyzed cohort of UKB and FinnGen (see Fig 6E and Table S29). A binomial test indicated that observing 13 of 15 concordant variants (86.7%) is highly unlikely under the null expectation of random alignment (p binom =3.7×10‐3 × =−15).

Discussion

Using genetic data from 14,802 MS cases and high‐throughput proteomic measurements on 80,824 individuals, our integrative genetic‐proteomic analysis identified 39 proteins likely involved in MS etiology, most clustered within a compact, highly connected immune network centered on B‐ and T‐cell costimulation (CD40 and CD58), T‐cell receptor (TCR) and B‐cell receptor (BCR) regulation (CD5) and cytokine signaling (IL7R, TNFRSF1A, and STAT3). Network centrality correlated strongly with MR effect size, suggesting that convergent perturbation of key immune pathways contributes to MS susceptibility. Cell‐type transcriptomic enrichment was strongest in a large fraction of B‐cell subsets, including double‐negative (CD27−IgD−) B cells, which are increasingly implicated in pathogenic autoimmunity 45 , 46 and have been proposed as a natural reservoir for EBV, 47 now recognized as a key risk factor for MS and other conditions. Although our study design did not directly test viral mechanisms, pathway‐level associations lead us to hypothesize a convergence of viral‐mediated and intrinsic risk in MS. Consistent with this hypothesis, several of the identified proteins intersect with EBV biology. These include CR1, which can mediate EBV viral entry 48 ; CD40, mimicked by the EBV protein LMP1 49 ; and SLAMF1 (CD150), upregulated in latently infected B cells. 50 Mutations in SLAM‐associated protein (SAP), a key adaptor for SLAM family receptors including SLAMF1, cause X‐linked lymphoproliferative disease characterized by severe and often fatal responses to EBV infection. 51 These insights also align with the clinical efficacy of B‐cell depletion, 52 CD40‐CD40L blockade, 53 inhibition of BCR signaling through Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibition, 54 , 55 and proposed convergence of MS disease‐modifying therapies on memory B cells.

Our network findings also align with immunological evidence implicating dysregulated B‐T cell interactions as central to MS pathogenesis. 56 , 57 In particular, CD40 is a costimulatory molecule that, upon engagement by CD40L (CD154) on activated T cells, promotes B‐cell survival, class switching, and differentiation 58 through nuclear factor (NF)‐κB 59 and MAPK‐PI3K signaling. 60 B‐cells from MS patients show exaggerated proliferation after CD40 ligation, 61 , 62 indicating dysregulated activation. CD58, another costimulatory molecule expressed on antigen presenting cells, binds CD2 on T and NK cells to enhance T‐cell adhesion, survival, and activation. 63 , 64 , 65 IL7R signaling in T cells likewise enhances crosstalk to B‐cell by upregulating costimulation molecule BAFF. 66 CD5, a negative regulator of TCR 67 and BCR 68 , 69 signaling, is critical for maintaining immune self‐tolerance. Its role in preventing autoreactive B‐cell activation aligns with its high centrality in our MS‐associated network, suggesting that dysregulated CD5 signaling contributes to loss of B‐cell tolerance in MS. 70 , 71

In addition to the primary network dominated by adaptive immune genes, we identified contributors from innate immunity. FUCA and MANBA highlighted enrichment in the N‐glycan degradation pathway (fucose and mannose metabolism, respectively). Post‐translational glycosylation has previously been implicated in autoimmunity, 72 , 73 including MS, potentially through effects on immune tolerance and molecular mimicry. Complement regulators CR1 and CD59 further implicate dysregulated membrane attack complex formation in MS pathology, consistent with recent neuropathological findings in progressive MS and genetic studies of visual pathway outcomes. 74 We also identify CNS‐enriched markers, such as NCS1, suggesting that neuronal or glial vulnerability may contribute to disease initiation, as previously hypothesized and increasingly supported by genetic evidence. However, despite their enrichment these proteins may yet influence disease risk through lower expression in immune or other cell types.

A key strength of our study is leveraging properties of human genetics to minimize the risk of reverse causality, where proteomic alterations result from MS onset, disease activity, or treatment. Supporting that the identified MS‐associated proteins are causal and proximal to disease onset, several align closely with targets of therapeutic interventions, including TNFRSF1A and CD40. Therapeutic inhibition of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐alpha, mirrored by the soluble form of TNFRSF1A, has been shown to exacerbate disease activity in MS clinical trials and to double disease incidence in observational studies. 44 Conversely, CD40‐CD40L inhibition via frexalimab has demonstrated substantial clinical benefit in a phase 2 trial, 53 with the phase 3 ongoing. Therefore, the identification of such clinically validated targets through our hypothesis‐free approach underscores the potential for other MS‐associated proteins identified in this study to serve as novel therapeutic candidates.

Our study builds on prior proteomic MR analyses in MS, which have nominated between 3 and 25 candidate proteins. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 By integrating data from 3 large cohorts totaling 80,824 individuals, we replicated 27 of 31 (87%) previously reported associations and identified 12 novel proteins not previously implicated in MS. One previously reported association involving TST was not replicated. 5 Although we initially observed a similar effect, TST shares its lead instrumental variant (rs4821544) with PVALB, raising concerns about horizontal pleiotropy, a limitation not addressed by colocalization when the same variant influences multiple proteins in cis (Note S3). By excluding the shared variant and relying on non‐overlapping fine‐mapped variants, we demonstrated that the association with MS is attributable to PVALB, not TST. Supporting this, PVALB, but not TST, interacted with another MS‐associated protein. Our study further extends this body of work by (1) identifying differentially spliced proteoforms as a source of cross‐platform discordance, (2) validating genetically prioritized proteins as predictors of incident MS in a prediagnostic cohort, (3) extending this framework to MS severity, (4) improving fine‐mapping resolution at colocalized loci, and (5) nominating novel risk loci replicated in independent datasets.

Integration with splicing QTLs further enabled us to identify a major source of discordance between antibody‐ and aptamer‐based proteomic platforms. For instance, we showed that platform‐specific associations for CD58 were likely because of the capture of distinct splice proteoforms, which may have opposing effects on MS risk. Similar isoform‐specific effects have been previously demonstrated at the TNFRSF1A locus, where a soluble splice variant increased MS risk independently of total protein levels. 44 , 75 , 76 By incorporating genetic and splicing information, our approach improved cross‐platform correlation coefficients from 0.35–0.39 (measured protein levels alone) 10 , 77 to 0.95–0.99, highlighting the value of this genetics‐informed framework. At the same time, these findings underscore the need to define the specific proteoforms detected by each platform, an important current limitation, because discordant levels may reflect biologically relevant isoform‐specific differences rather than noise.

Another advance of our study is the validation of genetically prioritized proteins in a prediagnostic cohort of 124 incident MS cases and 52,515 controls. Of the 28 proteins measured, 8 were associated with subsequent MS diagnosis, supporting the utility of genetics‐informed proteomics for early biomarker discovery. Previous studies have reported prediagnostic elevations of markers such as neurofilament light chain. 78 Our approach complements those studies by focusing on proteins with genetic evidence of causality and enriching for markers likely to reflect underlying disease mechanisms rather than consequences of subclinical pathology. Most proteins showed discordant effect directions between MR and prediagnostic levels. This may reflect confounding in survival analyses, or complex genetic effects influencing both circulating and intracellular protein pools, often encoded by the same gene. It may also reflect the temporal distinction between MR, which estimates lifelong effects, and our survival analyses, which capture short‐term protein dynamics (on average 5.9 years before diagnosis) when subclinical CNS injury may already be present and protein levels may be subject to reverse causality. 79 , 80 , 81 In accordance with this interpretation, DKKL1 showed concordant associations with MS risk in MR and prediagnostic measurement analyses, while also being significant in MR analysis of disease severity. This suggests a stage‐independent role in MS susceptibility and progression, which may not apply to other biomarkers. We have previously shown that genetic effects on susceptibility and progression can diverge. 38 Although DKKL1 is primarily expressed in the male reproductive system, 82 its association with incident MS in females points to sex‐independent effects, potentially mediated through its expression with B cells, consistent with our cell‐type enrichment results. Although the function of DKKL1 remains unclear, its homology to Wnt pathway modulators such as DKK3 raises the possibility that it regulates B‐cell activity via similar mechanisms. 83 , 84 Together, these findings nominate DKKL1 as a candidate biomarker for MS risk and progression, warranting further mechanistic and validation studies.

Our approach also improved causal variant resolution at colocalized MS risk loci, a task that would otherwise require substantially larger GWAS sample sizes and high costs. Building on prior efforts focused on gene expression and epigenetic data, 85 we showed that proteomic integration enables fine‐mapping at loci with strong colocalizing pQTL signals and improves resolution by approximately 10‐fold, even when accounting for multiple causal variants. These improvements are contingent on the availability of strong colocalizing proteomic signals and cannot be generalized to all MS risk loci. Systematic differences between disease‐associated variants and molecular QTLs suggest that loci amenable to this approach may represent a specific subset of the genetic architecture. 86 Using a similar rationale, we also identified 13 novel MS genetic risk loci that showed directional concordance in independent datasets, prioritizing them for validation in future genetic studies. These results demonstrate that genetic‐proteomic integration can refine known loci and reveal subthreshold risk signals that would otherwise require much larger sample sizes.

This study has additional limitations. First, the MS GWAS was limited to individuals of European ancestry. Although genetic effects have generally been consistent across ancestries, including more diverse cohorts would improve generalizability. In sensitivity analyses incorporating proteomic data from a multi‐ancestry study, results remained largely consistent (Note S2). Second, the proteomic datasets were predominantly derived from UK populations, whereas the MS GWAS included individuals from multiple countries. Although this may introduce subtle population‐specific confounding, the risk is likely small. Concordance between proteomic studies, including from Icelandic populations, and across platforms supports the robustness of our findings. Third, we were unable to test 11 MS‐associated proteins for validation in prediagnostic samples because of lack of aptamer‐based proteomic measurements. Fourth, although we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to reduce the risk of horizontal pleiotropy, it cannot be fully excluded. The example of TST illustrates that even cis‐variants and colocalization may be insufficient to rule out pleiotropy. However, similar instances of shared variants across proteins accounted for only 1.4 to 2.5% of the proteins tested. Additionally, the choice of colocalization threshold varies across studies, however, our results were robust to a more stringent threshold (PPH4 >0.8), with no impact on downstream findings including network connectivity, enrichment, and prediagnostic validation (Note S5). Fifth, although permutation analysis supports the robustness of the correlation between network centrality and MR effect size, centrality metrics derived from curated databases may be influenced by annotation density and literature bias, and therefore, their mechanistic significance should be confirmed experimentally. Finally, MR estimates the effect of lifelong exposure and may not account for non‐linear, time‐dependent, or disease‐stage‐specific effects.

In summary, our integrative genetic‐proteomic analysis identifies putatively causal proteins and pathways underlying MS susceptibility, including targets with established therapeutic relevance, and extending to disease severity. By leveraging human genetics, cross‐platform proteomics, and prediagnostic validation, we refined causal variant resolution, uncovered novel risk loci, and nominated biomarkers with translational potential. These findings advance our understanding of MS pathogenesis and provide a framework for biomarker discovery and therapeutic development supported by genetic evidence, an approach shown to increase the likelihood of successful clinical translation.
