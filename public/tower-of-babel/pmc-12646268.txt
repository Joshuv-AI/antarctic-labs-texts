Detection of anyon braiding through pump–probe spectroscopy
Authors: Xu Yang, Ryan Buechele, Nandini Trivedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceQuantum spin liquids are a new phase of matter, quite distinct from magnets and superconductors, featuring local magnetic moments that interact strongly, yet do not show any long-range magnetic order down to the lowest temperatures. They do, however, possess topological order characterized by long-range entanglement and fractionalized excitations. Typical magnets have spin wave excitations, but in quantum spin liquids these excitations break up into anyons. No experiment has observed sharp definite signatures of such fractionalization. Here, we propose using pump–probe nonlinear response, in which a sequence of applied magnetic pulses excite anyons that wind around each other and leave distinct fingerprints in the measured signal, providing a smoking gun signature for anyons in quantum spin liquids.

Anyons are exotic quasiparticle (QP) excitations (1–4), that can emerge in topologically ordered two-dimensional systems such as fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states (5–7) and quantum spin liquids (QSLs) (8–11). Anyonic excitations carry only a fraction of the original quantum numbers of the system and exhibit nontrivial braiding statistics (12), meaning that one anyon encircling another alters the total wave function of the system. Experimentally identifying anyons in a material is necessary to realize fault-tolerant quantum computation (13), but also presents a variety of challenges. Fractionalization of QPs appears in inelastic neutron scattering data as a broad continuum in the spectrum (14–16) due to the many ways that fractionalized QPs can share momentum and energy, but this is often hard to distinguish from other sources of broadening in the measurement, such as disorder. Recent studies have suggested examining the spectrum of two-spin excitations in the ground state for sharp features of fractionalized phases (17). These multispin correlators in linear response are currently being explored by noise spectroscopy (18, 19).

Observing the effects of braiding in bulk QSLs, however, requires a new approach to identify a unique characteristic of anyon braiding in an experimentally measurable quantity. Braiding statistics have only recently been observed in fractional quantum Hall states, whose chiral edge modes allow for careful control of anyon trajectories in order to interfere states with different braiding-induced phases (20, 21). We propose using nonlinear pump–probe spectroscopy to detect, in addition to fractionalization, the braiding of anyons by using magnetic fields that couple to noncommuting operators. The resulting nonlinear response of the multitime correlation functions allows us to uncover experimental evidence of braiding.

Probing pump–probe signals with 2D coherent spectroscopy in the frequency domain has been used to identify “hot spots” that reflect energies of excitations in quantum materials (22–34). More recent proposals have focused on the long-time amplitude of signals of the nonlinear response in the real-time domain (35–37). We build on the latter approach with new results in the early to intermediate time regime that we claim is crucial to see the signatures of braiding between anyons. We consider a pump–probe process where a system is perturbed from equilibrium by two pulses with a variable delay between them, as depicted schematically in Fig. 1. The QPs excited by these pulses have the ability to propagate and braid with each other, and the accumulation of statistical braiding phase in the wave function leads to a distinct early-time behavior of the measured response function.

Experimental schematic: Diagram of the pump–probe experimental process. First, a “pump” pulse is applied to the system at t=0, then a second “probe” pulse is applied at t=t1. The resulting signal output is measured at time t=t2.

A large portion of prior unbiased numerical studies such as those based on density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) and exact diagonalization (ED) are mostly restricted to one-dimensional systems and primarily concern the energy of quasiparticle excitations (25, 26, 30, 32, 33, 36, 38). Our work focuses on two-dimensional (2D) systems to discuss the signatures of anyon braiding statistics in nonlinear response; in particular, we consider the pump–probe responses of the toric code in an applied magnetic field, a model with a simple effective description in terms of dynamic anyons. We present below a study to employ infinite DMRG (39–41) together with the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) (42–46) for nonlinear response analysis in 2D systems, supplemented by ED (47, 48) to corroborate our infinite density matrix renormalization group (iDMRG) results at short times. We gain further analytical insight by using a large-scale anyon-hopping model (49–51) to systematically investigate how the signatures of anyon statistics manifest in nonlinear responses. This approach clearly distinguishes statistical effects from conventional nonstatistical interactions, providing a quantitative framework for anyon dynamics to guide numerical simulations and experiments. We show in Fig. 4 how the third-order pump–probe response features a linear-in-time growth relative to linear response only when anyon braiding occurs in the system, distinct from the signature in cases without braiding; furthermore, we explicitly reproduce this early time behavior in the anyon-hopping model along with analytic forms for the nonlinear response in Fig. 5. Our complete results pave the way for insights into the topological nature of these anyonic excitations and guide the experimental detection of anyons in quantum materials and cold atom platforms.

Model: Toric Code in Magnetic Field

To demonstrate the principles behind anyon detection using signatures from nonlinear response, we consider the toric code with spin-1/2 qubits σ=(σx,σy,σz) on the links of a square lattice in an external magnetic field given by:[1]HTC=−JA∑vAv−JB∑pBp−hx∑jσjx−hz∑jσjz,

where the star operator Av=∏j∈vσjx is a product of σx on sites adjacent to the vertex v and Bp=∏j∈pσjz is a product of σz on sites surrounding a plaquette p, as shown in Fig. 2. Throughout, we assume JA=JB≡J and hx=hx≡h unless otherwise noted. In the absence of the field terms (h=0), the ground state is determined by the spin configuration which makes Av=1,Bp=1 ∀v,p (52). Gapped excitations above the ground state are created by spin-flips which fractionalize into two anyons; these QPs can only be created/annihilated in pairs and each pair costs an energy 4J. Applying σz to a site flips the x-projection of the spin, which flips the sign of both adjacent star operators and creates two stationary e particles; σx on a site flips the z-projection of spin, flipping both adjacent plaquette operators and creating two stationary m particles. Turning on a perturbatively small field in the z(x)-direction allows the e(m) QPs to hop around the lattice. The e,m particles are individually bosons but have mutual semionic statistics, meaning that braiding an e around an m contributes a phase factor of −1 to the wave function. This effective description of e,m excitations hopping holds for h≲0.3J; for larger values of the external field, the model undergoes a phase transition to a polarized state along the direction of the applied field (53, 54). In the topologically trivial field-polarized phase with h≫J, the low-energy excitations are single spin flips which can hop around the lattice via the star and plaquette operators but have trivial braiding statistics.

Toric code lattice: On the Left, the toric code with spin-1/2 sites located on the links of a 2D square lattice. The star operator Av is the product of σx on the sites neighboring a vertex v, while the plaquette operator Bp is the product of σz on the sites neighboring a plaquette p. σz at a site i flips both adjacent stars, creating two e excitations, which can then hop when σz acts on a neighboring site j. Likewise, σx at site k flips both adjacent plaquettes and creates two m excitations, which can hop by the action of σx on a neighboring site ℓ. We compute the response functions on a cylindrical geometry; the thick line at the Bottom represents the periodic boundary. On the Right, we model the toric code excitations as hard-core anyons hopping on a square lattice and its dual lattice; the e’s live on the lattice sites and are created/annihilated by the operators b,b† at each site, while the m’s live on the dual lattice and are created/annihilated by the operators v,v†. Each anyon carries with it a string (solid orange for e, dashed blue for m) which runs to the rightward then downward to terminate at the periodic boundary. Tracking how these strings sweep over each other accounts for the phase θem acquired when e’s encircle an m (or vice versa), as well as any phase acquired from incontractible loops around the periodic system (49).

We numerically simulate the linear and nonlinear response in this model using three different numerical techniques, detailed in the later Materials and Methods: ED, iDMRG with a TDVP, and an anyon-hopping model. Before concluding this section, we briefly comment on the role of Lieb-Robinson velocity vLR and the finite-size effect. Using a Lieb-Robinson bound (55), the evolution of a local operator after time t only affects operators within a light cone of radius vLRt; therefore, the finite-size effects becomes apparent after time t∼L/(2vLR). This sets the upper bound on the time scale that are free from finite-size effects in numerical simulations. The Lieb-Robinson velocity is lower-bounded by the maximum velocity of quasiparticles. We estimate vLR∼2hkinetic (where hkinetic denotes the hopping strength of relevant anyons-in our case, hx/z). Therefore, we can use the time scale L/(2vLR)∼L/(4hkinetic) to estimate the time range that faithfully reflects the thermodynamic limit.

Linear Response

In linear response, the system in its ground state is first perturbed by a pulse considered here to couple to the total magnetization operator Mα≡∑j=1Nσjα (α∈{x,y,z}) at t=0. The resulting magnetization Mβ is then measured at a later time t from which we extract the linear response coefficient (56, 57)[2]χα,β(1)(t)=−iN⟨0|[Mα(0),Mβ(t)]|0⟩,

where Mα(t)≡eiHtMαe−iHt is the time-evolved operator Mα in the Heisenberg picture and |0〉 denotes the ground state of the system. Specifically for the Z2 spin liquid phase of the toric code model, the pulse coupled to Mz(0) excites a pair of e QPs with a gap 2J (per QP) that can propagate through the system with finite hz before being annihilated by Mz(t). We therefore expect the linear response to feature an oscillation at a frequency equal to the Toric Code energy gap 4J to create the anyon pair, with a decaying envelope related to the fact that excited anyons must form a closed space-time loop to annihilate. Our results for χZZ(1)(t) computed with both ED and iDMRG methods are shown in Fig. 3A. We see an oscillation not at ω=4J, but at ω=4J−8h≡Δ0, the renormalized gap in the presence of the field. The decaying envelope appears in the Fourier Transform of χZZ(1) as two broad lobes around 4J, shown in Fig. 3B, due to the broadened anyon dispersions in the presence of the field.

For a pair of free bosons, the decaying envelope for the linear response is predicted to scale as 1/t (37); however, numerically we find that the long-time behavior of the pump–probe response as computed from iDMRG/ED decays faster. This is evident in the Fourier Transform of χZZ(1), shown in Fig. 3B; the Fourier transform of 1/t should give a step function at the onset gap Δ0, but we instead see a power law growth ∝(ω−Δ0)η which corresponds to a decay like t−(1+η) (for η>0). We argue this faster decay results from the hard-core constraint between anyons that serves as an effective short-range repulsion and decreases the probability of two anyons meeting to annihilate at time t (35).

In order to better capture the effect of hard-core constraints in anyons and fit the numerical data, we have also computed the linear response based on a simple anyon-hopping model (49–51) with hard-core anyons hopping on a Lx×Ly square lattice, with Hamiltonian given by[3]He,m=−he∑<jk>bj†bkeiθjke−hm∑<jk>vj†vkeiθjkm+ΔeNe+ΔmNm,

where b†/v† are the creation operators of the e/m particle, and each anyon carries a string which encodes the phase angle that tracks braiding phases θe/m as delineated in detail in refs. 49–51. The operators Ne/m count the total number of e/m particles in the Hilbert space. The hopping parameters he/m and gaps Δe/m directly correspond to the external field and excitation gap (per particle) in HTC; thus we choose the parameters he=hm=h and Δe=Δm=2J. For χZZ(1), we enforce hard-core constraints and restrict the Hamiltonian Eq. 3 to a subspace with exactly 2 e’s. This restriction is justified by the large energy penalty Δe/m for changing anyon numbers, ensuring the model remains valid up to timescales where nonstatistical interactions become significant.

Discarding the hard-core constraint for the moment, we can diagonalize the anyon-hopping Hamiltonian and obtain the dispersion[4]ϵk=−2he(cos(kx)+ cos(ky))+Δe.

The linear response can then be evaluated exactly as[5]χZZ,free(1)(t)=2sin(2Δet)J02(4het),

where J0(x) is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind. The linear response is a fast oscillating sinusoidal function with a frequency on the order of energy gap Δe, modulated by a slow Bessel-like envelope that varies on the timescale of 1/he (he being anyon’s kinetic energy), as is clearly shown in the time-domain results in Fig. 3A. For t≫1/he, the Bessel function can be asymptotically approximated by[6]J0(4het)2≈cos2(4het−π/4)2πhet

which leads to the expected 1/t decay for a pair of noninteracting bosonic anyons (35, 37, 58). Including hard-core constraints significantly changes the late-time behavior. Results of a large-scale simulation of hard-core anyon-hopping model on a 100×100 lattice are shown in Fig. 3; fitting the Fourier transformed data immediately above the gap Δ0≡2(Δe−4he) to a power law like A(ω−Δ0)η yields η≈0.27, confirming that hard-core constraints lead to a faster long-time decay like t−(1+η).

Nonlinear Response

We consider a two-pulse experiment where the system is first excited by a “pump” pulse at t=0 coupled to Mα. We assume the pulse has a short enough duration that it effectively instantly evolves the system from the ground state |0〉 to the state e−iμMα|0〉, where μ is the coupling strength to the pump pulse; we take μ/J≪1 to weakly perturb the system so the signal depends on processes with the fewest allowed number of pump anyons. A second “probe” pulse is sent at t=t1 coupled to Mβ, before measuring the resulting signal Mγ at a time t2. (Throughout, tj denotes an absolute time, while the Greek τj≡tj−tj−1 denotes a time interval between subsequent times.) This experiment is repeated without the pump pulse, and the second signal is subtracted from the first to extract the pump–probe (PP) signal. This subtraction removes any contributions to the signal from linear response, leaving only higher-order contributions.

The pump–probe coefficient we study is defined as (24, 56)[7]Ξα,β,γPP(t1,t2)=−iN(⟨0|eiμMα[Mβ(t1),Mγ(t2)]e−iμMα|0⟩⏟χ(1)in pumped state−⟨0|[Mβ(t1),Mγ(t2)]|0⟩⏟χ(1)in g.s.);

we expand the state e−iμMα(0)|0〉 to second-order in the limit of a weak pulse μ and obtain:[8]Ξα,β,γPP(t1,t2)=μχα,β,γ(2)(t1,t2)+μ22χα,β,γ(3)(t1,t2)+O(μ3);

from which we can extract the second- and third-order response coefficients: [9]χα,β,γ(2)(t1,t2)=1N|0〉[Mα(0),[Mβ(t1),Mγ(t2)]]|0〉,[10]χα,β,γ(3)(t1,t2)=iN|0〉[Mα(0),[Mα(0),[Mβ(t1),Mγ(t2)]]]|0〉.

In nonlinear response formalism, the χ(3) response typically involves four pulse labels, but we have dropped one redundant label for simplicity since the operator Mα(0) appears twice in each correlation term (see SI Appendix for the conventional derivation).

The pump–probe response Eq. 7 can be considered as the difference in linear responses of the “pumped” state eiμMα|0〉 and of the ground state. The key distinction is the presence of excited QPs in the pumped state that are not present in the unperturbed ground state. The leading-order processes to consider are those where the pump and probe pulses each only excite pairs of anyons at t=0 and t=t1, which must then be annihilated at t=t2. Ref. 37 insightfully points out the importance of statistical phases in the long-time regime of the real-time signal of pump–probe spectroscopy, with certain assumptions in the semiclassical analytical treatment to make the problem more tractable, but a precise quantitative understanding of pump–probe responses in realistic systems remains incomplete. Motivated by this, we provide a comprehensive framework based on various numerical and analytical methods to elucidate the role of braiding statistics in real-time pump–probe responses, and we find a clear signature of braiding statistics especially in the early-to-intermediate time regime.

We emphasize that both braiding with pump anyons and scattering off pump particles can result in an enhancement of pump–probe response with respect to linear response. However, the mobility of pump anyons enlarges the phase space for braiding, allowing a sharp distinction between the pump–probe enhancement due to long-range statistical interactions (braiding) and that arising from short-range interactions (scattering). See the end of Section 3 for a detailed discussion of this distinction.

XZZ Response.

We first consider the case where α=X,β=γ=Z and examine the QP processes contributing to the response. Both terms in Eq. 9 include the operator Mx(0) which excites two m’s but no operator to annihilate them, so we expect χXZZ(2) to play little part in the observed ΞXZZPP and focus our studies on χXZZ(3). The second and third terms of Eq. 10 partly suffer from the same problem; this leaves only the first term of Eq. 10 to contribute, wherein Mx(0) excites two m’s and Mz(t1) excites two e’s which annihilate by Mz(t2). The e’s must form a closed worldline loop, which allows for the probability that previously excited m’s can braid with this loop. In the pump–probe function ΞXZZPP, processes wherein the m’s do not braid are simply directly proportional to the linear response and thus are removed by the subtraction in Eq. 7; however, when an m does intersect the worldline loop of the e’s, as shown in Fig. 4A, the braiding leads to a relative phase shift between the two terms in Eq. 7, allowing these processes contribute to χXZZ(3). The amplitude of χXZZ(3) is proportional to the probability of braiding occurring, which in turn scales with the area enclosed by the e worldlines, and thus we expect the amplitude of χXZZ(3), and thus the amplitude of ΞXZZPP, to be an increasing function of τ2.

Comparison of XZZ and XXX Responses.

Next, we compare this behavior to the case where α=β=γ=X. χXXX(2) contains processes which create a large connected worldline loop which was not allowed in the XZZ case (shown in SI Appendix), and thus χXXX(2) has an appreciable contribution to the pump–probe response. χXXX(3) includes many of the same types of processes as χXZZ(3), simply replacing e’s with m’s, but whether a pump QP braids with the probe QPs is irrelevant (since all particles are mutually bosonic), and these terms are removed by the subtraction in Eq. 7, leaving no signal. Instead, additional processes like those shown in Fig. 4B contribute to ΞXXXPP; these include processes like those on the Left, where Mx(t1) and Mx(t2) annihilate and recreate single m’s to form one large worldline loop, and those on the Right, wherein pump and probe QPs complete separate loops, but there is a chance for one QP to scatter off another. These processes insist on forming closed worldline loops, so we expect the amplitude of the signal to mimic the decaying behavior of the linear response. The distinction between the amplitude in the braiding case χXZZ(3) growing with time versus the amplitude in the topologically trivial case χXXX(3) decaying in time forms the key foundation of our result.

Fig. 4 C and D show the behavior of χα,β,γ(3)(t)≡χα,β,γ(3)(t1=0,t2) for both combinations of fields α,β,γ=X,Z,Z and X,X,X, each computed with ED and iDMRG. Again, we supplement these data with a model of hard-core anyons hopping on a lattice, and all three calculations agree quite well, with some deviation by the ED for Jt≳15 due to finite size effect in Lx as estimated from Lieb-Robinson bound. In the XZZ case, we clearly see the growth of the signal amplitude at early times, as expected due to the braiding of pump and probe anyons; our further analysis of the anyon-hopping model elucidates that this is a linear-in-t enhancement of the linear response χZZ(1)(t). In contrast, the XXX case shows an initially decaying signal, similar to the linear response, due to the decreasing probability to form closed worldline loops, and our anyon-hopping analysis concretely demonstrates this similarity. We emphasize that the distinct braiding behavior is most apparent at early times, as the data become qualitatively similar for Jt≳8 due to finite size effect in Ly.

The data in Fig. 4 (and later Figures) are shown with t1=τ1=0; including τ1>0 effectively translates the pump QPs and does not affect the characteristic behaviors, since braiding is a nonlocal interaction. For the XZZ response, we argue below that a linear-in-t enhancement arises primarily from the available phase space for braiding between pump and probe anyons, rather than solely from the spatial separation of the pump anyons; as a result, the same linear enhancement is expected even when anyons are well separated and immobile, indicating a weak dependence on τ1. This conclusion is supported in SI Appendix, Fig. S4, where the XZZ signal in Fourier space depends only on ωτ1=0, suggesting a constant dependence on τ1. For the XXX response, pump–QP positions do not affect the signal, as the translationally invariant pump–probe correlator sums over all relative coordinates. The pump QPs effectively provide a disordered background for the probe QP response, so finite τ1 only phase shifts the oscillations, not the envelope function that governs the trend. SI Appendix, Fig. S4 shows the XXX signal contains τ1-dependent oscillations at specific frequencies determined by the anyon energies. Thus, the relevant physics remains evident with finite τ1, and we use τ1=0 since finite size effects limit the range of total time t2=τ1+τ2 we can reliably simulate.

Analytical Understanding of χ(3).

Here, we show the early time behavior of χXZZ(3) receives a linear-in-t enhancement relative to the linear response. In the early time regime (defined in detail below), we can approximate m’s created by Mx(0) as static, since they have not drifted far from their initial positions. Consequently, the pump pulse creates a uniform background of m particle pairs on different links; when hopping e particles cross these links, the wave function changes sign due to the semionic statistics between e and m particles. Since ΞXZZPP is defined as the difference between the hopping of e particles with and without the m particle background, we can establish a direct relation between the linear and pump–probe responses. Without the m background, a world loop of e particles composed of d nearest-neighbor steps contributes an amplitude hed to the linear response. In the presence of m’s, those e world loops that contribute to the pump–probe response must cross an m link exactly once (otherwise they cancel after subtracting the linear response), replacing the he by −he at that link. For each e world loop contributing weight hed to the linear response, the probability of crossing an m link scales proportionally with the length of the loop d, and we therefore have a contribution to the pump–probe response dhed−1×δhe=2dhed due to the subtraction in Eq. 7; however, this is conveniently achieved by the linear operator 2heddhe. Summing over these world loops yields the following relation for the pump–probe response in terms of the linear response:[11]χXZZ(3)(he,t)≈2heddheχZZ(1)(he,t).

Using the simplified expression for the free boson linear response Eq. 5 in Eq. 11 captures the essential early time behavior and leads to[12]χXZZ,Bessel(3)(t)≈4hesin(2Δt)ddheJ02(4het)=16hetsin(2Δt)ddxJ02(x)|x=4ht,

where the last expression explicitly shows the linear-in-t enhancement.

This result agrees very well at early times with the anyon-hopping simulation, as shown in Fig. 5A albeit with a renormalized value of he=0.042. Although we have made several simplified assumptions, the universal features depend only on the kinetics of anyons and hold quite generally. We estimate the Bessel function description remains valid up until a time Jtint∼j0,1/(4h)≈14, where jn,k denotes the k th zero of Jn(x); this roughly measures the time for an e particle to traverse one lattice constant, justifying our treatment of the m particles as static. Effects of hard-core constraints and the statistical interaction between e and m particles also set in beyond this time scale. Microscopic details may change the prefactor in tint, but the overall scaling relation 1/he remains generally valid. The long-time regime consists of several cycles of tint, during which the hard-core constraints as well as statistical and nonstatistical interactions between e and m particles become important. The linear-in-t enhancement relation Eq. 11 assumes that m particles are static, but in late times when m particles are mobile, the phase space where e particles can hop across m strings is enhanced, resulting in a different power law enhancement, whose detailed study shall be left for future work.

We emphasize that in all the above discussions, the relevant time is t≡τ2. The dependence on τ1 is expected to be weak by examining the limit of large τ1. In this case, the two pump m anyons have a large separation, and the probability of an e anyon braiding with one of them is proportional to the area enclosed by the e world-loop, indicating a crossover from perimeter to area law enhancement. However, the fractal nature of a random walk ensures that the area also scales as τ2 (estimated from the typical linear size ∼τ2), yielding in the same linear-in-τ2 enhancement at both small and large τ1. Examining these two limiting cases, we therefore expect the τ1 dependence of χ(3)(τ1,τ2) to be weak, which is also supported by the ED data in SI Appendix.

Comparing Eq. 13 with numerical simulations of the anyon-hopping model as shown in Fig. 5B, we find excellent agreement up to the timescale set by the first zero of the Bessel function derivative Jt∼1/(4hm)=5, beyond which deviations arise due to the hard-core constraints. One notable deviation is that from the asymptotic form of Bessel functions, Eq. 13 exhibits 1/t long-time decay. However, when hard-core constraints are incorporated, the decay is modified due to scattering between pump particles and probe particles and hence should be slower than 1/t, highlighting the significant differences between the constrained and unconstrained cases.

Ref. 29 provides an analytical analysis of the χXXX(3) for a toric code model in a field. However, their approach imposes hard-core constraints only at the level of the magnetization operator, while anyons are still allowed to evolve under a free Hamiltonian without such constraints. As we have pointed out above, ignoring hard-core constraints significantly affects the late-time behavior of pump–probe responses. Numerical and analytical calculations of the nonlinear responses based on mean-field theory of Kitaev spin liquids on the honeycomb lattice (27, 29, 31, 59) assume static gauge fluxes and therefore fail to correctly capture the winding phase between the Majorana fermions and dynamical gauge fluxes.

Full Pump–Probe Response.

To understand more precisely how these signals would appear in an experiment, we also exactly compute Ξα,β,γPP as defined in Eq. 7 with ED using the same matrix exponential techniques as employed for the time evolution e−iHt in order to compute the state eiμMα|0〉 (60); these results are shown in Fig. 6. We have plotted μχXZZ(2), μ2χXZZ(3), and ΞXZZPP as computed with both Eqs. 7 and 8. We see that χXZZ(2) only contributes significantly to the very early peaks in ΞXZZPP and for Jτ2≳4, χXZZ(3) well describes the full behavior of ΞXZZPP. In the XXX case, both χXXX(2) and χXXX(3) fail to separately capture the features of ΞXXXPP, and their combined sum still features some variation from the exact result, suggesting contributions from some higher-order terms.

Polarized Phase Responses.

In order to fully understand the nonlinear responses of our model Eq. 1 and distinguish the braiding behavior of χXZZ(3) in the toric code phase, we also present data in Fig. 7 of the linear and nonlinear responses in the field-polarized regime, choosing h/J=5. In this case, the operators Mα can create/annihilate single spin-flips which are hard-core bosons. In the linear response χZZ(1), Mz(0) creates a QP that Mz(t) annihilates, and we now just have an oscillation at the gap frequency due to the dynamical phase acquired. Unlike the anyonic case where the fractionalized nature of anyons leads to temporal decay in the linear response, local spin flips exhibit no such decay, indicating an absence of fractionalization. The second-order responses vanish in the limit h/J→∞, due to an Ising-like symmetry along the (x^+z^) axis. Both third-order responses, χXZZ(3) and χXXX(3), are qualitatively indistinguishable, since both arise due to QP processes like those shown in Fig. 7B. The process on the Left is proportional to linear response and subtracted off, while the process on the Right features a scattering between pump and probe QPs. The scattering phase shift acquired as a result can lead to a nonzero contribution to the pump–probe response, and we expect the signal to grow in time as the probability for scattering grows proportionally to the probe QP worldline length. This is essentially the two-dimensional generalization of the argument and conclusion of ref. 36, where they found that QP scattering phase shifts in one-dimensional systems lead to a linear-in-time divergence of the pump–probe response.

Braiding versus Scattering.

Both the anyonic and polarized phases exhibit linear-in-time χ(3) enhancements at early times, raising the question of how to distinguish them. First, we point out that anyons also manifest in additional signatures including a decaying linear response due to fractionalization and pulse polarization dependence from anyon selection rules. By contrast, the polarized phase shows a constant-amplitude linear response and has polarization-insensitive pump–probe responses, indicating its nontopological nature.

Second, braiding and scattering can be differentiated based on their time dependence at late times. In the case of scattering off local excitations, due to the short-rangedness of scattering interactions, the phase space for scatter events is proportional to the length of probe particle worldlines and hence the pump–probe responses receive a linear-in-t enhancement to linear responses. Short-range interactions may introduce a scattering phase shift or an overall constant prefactor in amplitude, but they do not affect the characteristic linear-in-t scaling of the enhancement, which therefore persists at all times. This sustained linear growth is evident in the high-field polarized toric code (Fig. 7) and the polarized phase of the transverse field Ising model (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In contrast, for nontrivial braiding with anyons, the mobility of pump anyons enlarges the phase space for braiding (via long-range statistical interactions) with probe anyons. Consequently, the enhancement is expected to evolve into a power-law form tα with α>1 at late times. This regime can also be accessed by increasing hm (the velocity of pumped m anyons) and observing how it affects χXZZ(3), which we have simulated with the anyon-hopping model and shown in Fig. 8. These results show that increasing hm suppresses the signal at early times and enhances it at later times, indicating that increasing the pump-anyon velocity enhances the response to a higher power law tα, consistent with a faster breakdown of the static pump-anyon picture. This aligns with the path-integral analyses of refs. 37 and 58, which predict t3/2 scaling at late times or as vpump→∞.

These observations show that tα enhancement with α>1 in pump–probe experiments provides a clear signature of braiding between topological excitations. Moreover, finer dynamical features, such as the time-scale at which the deviation from α=1 sets in, can be used to estimate the mobility of pump anyons.

Time Scales

Based on our analytical treatment of the pump–probe response, we identify a key time scale central to our results: the intermediate time scale tint, which we estimate as tint≡π/(4hkinetic), derived from the average spacing between the zeros of the Bessel function J0. χXZZ(3) and χXXX(3) most clearly differ for t<tint, with χXXX(3) showing decay in time resembling linear response and χXZZ(3) showing linear-in-t enhancement relative to linear response. The hard-core constraints cause χXXX(3) to significantly deviate from the free boson analysis also on a time scale ∝tint. In our specific case Jtint∼15, in accordance with our numerical observation. Using the estimated Lieb-Robinson velocity vLR∼2hkinetic, we obtain a model-independent system size 2vLRtint≈3, where the distinct behavior of χXZZ(3) versus χXXX(3) becomes evident, which justifies our use of ED and iDMRG. As seen in the χXZZ(3) data, finite-size effects set in around Jt∼8, indicating an underestimation of vLR. For Jt≲8 iDMRG and ED can unambiguously distinguish between anyons with and without braiding in χXZZ(3) versusχXXX(3).

This also imposes stringent constraints on system size. For example, to clearly observe five cycles of slow modes described by the Bessel functions, a system with linear size at least 5×(2vLRtint)∼16 is required, well beyond the capability of most existing numerical techniques and might only be achievable within the anyon-hopping model.* At long times order of magnitude larger than tint, pump anyons drift sufficiently far apart such that the static anyon approximation no longer holds. In this regime, the phase space for braiding can be enhanced, leading to a different scaling than we study here (37, 58).While our anyon-hopping model only strictly describes the case where anyons have no interactions other than their mutual statistics and hard-core constraints, these models are expected to exhibit the correct long-time behavior, where responses mainly depend on far-apart anyons and nonlocal physics. We leave the precise numerical study of the long-time regime for future work.

Materials and Methods

We employ three techniques to compute the necessary correlation functions in the pump–probe response: ED, iDMRG with TDVP for time-evolution, and an anyon-hopping model. In ED, we compute translationally invariant basis states and operators for the Hamiltonian Eq. 1 and magnetization operators, then compute the time evolution of the eigenstates using Krylov subspace projections (60, 62); this allows us to efficiently simulate a Lx×Ly=4×3 Toric Code. The iDMRG is done on a cylinder with infinite Lx and periodic Ly=3; from the iMPS ground state of the Toric Code, we construct a finite chain with “infinite” boundary conditions and apply local quenches and single-site TDVP (δt=0.125) to compute the response functions (63, 64). In the polarized phase which features slower entanglement growth, we instead use finite DMRG and check convergence with the system size Lx. Finally, we compute the pump–probe responses with a simple anyon-hopping model with hard-core constraints, as shown in Fig. 2. The dynamics are restricted to subspaces of fixed particle number, and the time evolution is done in the same manner as the ED. For more details on numerics, see SI Appendix.

Summary and Outlook

We show that nonlinear pump–probe spectroscopy on a quantum spin liquid model performed with magnetic fields in different directions allows us to detect the braiding of fractionalized anyons. Contrasting a control setup with the pump, probe, and measurement magnetic fields in the same direction (XXX) against a setup with magnetic fields that involve noncommuting operators (XZZ) highlights the nontrivial signature of anyon braiding in the time dependence; χXZZ(3) grows with time in contrast to a time decay for the control case χXXX(3).

We reemphasize the analysis should be performed in real time, as opposed to 2D coherent spectroscopy which Fourier transforms the time-domain data, since the signal in frequency space obscures the distinctive features of the XZZ and XXX nonlinear response at early to intermediate times, as described in SI Appendix. The procedure described above conducted with both the pulse polarization sequences XZZ and XXX provides definitive evidence of braiding in QSL candidate materials with an emergent Z2 gauge theory. The methods and analysis we have described can be generalized to other frustrated spin models pertinent to quantum spin liquids. We generically expect a power-law enhancement of the linear response in any setup wherein the pump and probe pulses can excite anyons with nontrivial mutual statistics, but understanding the precise form of this enhancement and separating the effects of braiding from other processes requires model-specific analysis analogous to the analytic anyon-hopping description we have described.

To address the challenge of finding ideal QSL materials, experiments should focus on characterizing QSL candidates with a considerably suppressed magnetic ordering temperature Tc from the Curie–Weiss temperature θCW. Quantum fluctuations dominate nonlinear response in the window Tc<T<|θCW|, and braiding effects should be most evident at low temperatures just above Tc to minimize the effects of both magnetic ordering and thermal fluctuations. Our proposed technique applies primarily to Mott insulating spin-liquid candidates with electrically neutral excitations; in situations where anyons carry electric charge (such as FQH) or otherwise experience long-ranged interactions, those effects can dominate the nonlinear response and obscure signatures from braiding.
