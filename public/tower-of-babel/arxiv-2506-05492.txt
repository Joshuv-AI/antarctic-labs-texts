Zeros of orthogonal little q q -Jacobi polynomials: interlacing and monotonicity
Andrei Martínez-Finkelshtein, Rafael Morales, Daniel Perales

Abstract
. We investigate the distribution of zeros of the little q q -Jacobi polynomials and related q q -hypergeometric families. We prove that the zeros of these orthogonal polynomials exhibit strong interlacing properties and obey natural monotonicity rules with respect to the parameters. A key tool in our approach is the logarithmic mesh, which quantifies the relative spacing of the positive real zeros and allows us to classify families of polynomials with prescribed interlacing patterns. Our results include new interlacing relations, monotonicity with respect to parameters, and structural decompositions in non-orthogonal regimes. Several classical families of q q -hypergeometric polynomials, including q q -Bessel and Stieltjes-Wigert polynomials, are treated as limit cases. The methods rely on a combination of classical orthogonality theory and q q -difference equations.

Primary: 33C45; Secondary: 33C20, 42C05, 46L54

1.
Introduction

Orthogonal polynomials are well known for their rich zero structure, which underpins their numerous applications in approximation theory, spectral methods, and mathematical physics. The interlacing and monotonicity of zeros are especially important properties, often linked to recurrence relations and electrostatic models. In the classical continuous case, such properties are well understood, but for
q
-analogs and particularly discrete orthogonal families like the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials, many questions remain open.

In this paper, we study the real zeros of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials

p_{n}(x;a,b\mid q)={}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}q^{-n},abq^{n+1}\\
aq\end{matrix};q,qx\right),

focusing on their interlacing patterns and dependence on the parameters
a
and
b
. Under suitable constraints on
a
and
b
, these polynomials are orthogonal on a discrete subset of the interval
(0,1)
, and their zeros are real, positive, and simple. We prove several new results that describe how the zeros move and interlace when the parameters vary.

A central notion in our analysis is the
logarithmic mesh
, which captures the spacing of consecutive positive zeros. This concept allows us to define classes of polynomials with controlled spacing properties and relate them to finite free convolutions. We exploit this framework to establish new interlacing relations between different families of
q
-polynomials, including
q
-Bessel and Stieltjes-Wigert polynomials, derived as suitable limits.

The approach combines tools from
q
-calculus, including
q
-difference operators and
q
-Pochhammer symbols, with intuition coming from finite free probability. We provide contiguous relations, monotonicity theorems, and structural decompositions that not only generalize earlier partial results but also correct and extend claims previously stated without proof or with incorrect arguments.

This work contributes to the understanding of
q
-orthogonal polynomials and provides a foundation for future studies involving discrete electrostatic models, asymptotic analysis, and applications in signal processing and random matrix theory.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Zero interlacing and mesh

Along this paper,
\mathbb{P}_{n}
stands for the space of algebraic polynomials of degree
\leq n
, and
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}
is the subset of monic polynomials of degree
n
. Also, for a set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{n}(K)
(resp.,
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(K)
) the subset of polynomials of degree
\leq n
(resp., monic polynomials of degree
n
) with all its zeros in
K
. In particular,
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the family of real-rooted monic polynomials of degree
n
,

\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
denotes the subset of
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
of polynomials having only non-negative roots, etcetera.

We denote the zeros of a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, with account of their multiplicity, by
\lambda_{1}(p)
,
\lambda_{2}(p)
, …,
\lambda_{n}(p)
. If
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we enumerate them in the increasing order,

(1)

\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}(p).

Notation 2.1

(A partial order on polynomials)
.

Given
p,r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
we say that
p\ll r
if the zeros of
p
are smaller than the zeros of
r
in the following sense:

\lambda_{k}(p)\leq\lambda_{k}(r)\qquad\text{for all }k=1,2,\dots,n.

Clearly,
\ll
is a partial order in
\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

This partial order is preserved under differentiation, see e.g.
[
1
, Section 4]
.

Definition 2.2

(Interlacing)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
r\in\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
, with the zeros denoted as in (
1
).
We say that
q

interlaces

p
(or, equivalently, that
zeros of
r
interlace zeros of
p
, see, e.g.,
[
2
]
), and denote it
p\preccurlyeq r
, if

(2)

m=n\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{1}(r)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(r)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(p)\leq\lambda_{n}(r),

or if

(3)

m=n-1\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{1}(r)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(r)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n-1}(p)\leq\lambda_{n-1}(r)\leq\lambda_{n}(p).

Furthermore, we use the notation
p\prec r
when all inequalities in (
2
) or (
3
) are strict. Notice that
\prec
or
\preccurlyeq
are not order relations, since they lack transitivity: from the fact that
p\prec r
and
r\prec s
it does not necessarily follow that
p\prec s
.

Remark 2.3
.

It follows from the definitions that if
p,r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, then

(4)

p\prec r\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad p\ll r.

The converse is not true in general, but the following partial converse will be useful: if
p,r\in\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
have a common interlacer, namely there exists
s\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\mathbb{P}_{n-2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
p\prec s
and
r\prec s
, then

p\ll r\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad p\prec r.

Indeed, the hypotheses imply that

\lambda_{k}(s)\leq\lambda_{k}(p)\leq\lambda_{k}(r)\leq\lambda_{k+1}(s)\qquad\text{for }k=1,\dots,n,

and the conclusion follows.

The relative spacing of real zeros of a polynomial is given by the following notion:

Definition 2.4
.

For
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
,
n\geq 2
, let

0<\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(p)

denote its zeros. The
logarithmic mesh
of
p
is

\mathop{\rm lmesh}{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n-1}\frac{\lambda_{j}(p)}{\lambda_{j+1}(p)}\leq 1.

For
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{<0})
we understand by
\mathop{\rm lmesh}{p}
the value
\mathop{\rm lmesh}{r}
, with
r(x)=p(-x)
.

In the rest of the paper, the polynomials with an upper bound on the logarithmic mesh will play a prominent role, which motivates the following notation: for
q\in(0,1)
and
K
being a subset of either
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
or
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
,

\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathop{\rm lmesh}{r}<q\}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathop{\rm lmesh}{r}\leq q\}.

We can characterize
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
as follows, see
[
6
, Lemma 23]
:

(5)

\displaystyle p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow p(x)\prec p(qx).

A similar characterization is valid for
\overline{\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
when replacing
\prec
by
\preccurlyeq
. Moreover, the following result is obviously true:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of polynomials such that
p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
for sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that the sequence converges point-wise to a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{P}^{*}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

(1)

if for all sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
p_{k}\in\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
then
p\in\overline{\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}}(\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

if there is an
r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that
p_{k}\prec r
for all sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
then
p\preccurlyeq r
.

2.2.
q
-hypergeometric polynomials

For
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
q\in(0,1)
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
q
-factorial
(also known as the
q
-Pochhammer symbol
) is defined as

\left(a;q\right)_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=1,\quad\left(a;q\right)_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-aq^{j}),\quad\left(a;q\right)_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{j}).

In analogy with the standard factorial and the Gamma function, we can extend this notion to the complex plane: for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

(6)

\left(a;q\right)_{\lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\left(a;q\right)_{\infty}}{\left(aq^{\lambda};q\right)_{\infty}}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-aq^{j}}{1-aq^{\lambda+j}},

where we use the branch of
q^{\lambda}
such that
|q^{\lambda}|=q^{\mathop{\rm Re}(\lambda)}
. We can extend the definition to vector parameters
\bm{a}
: if
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, then

\left(\bm{a};q\right)_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(a_{j};q\right)_{k}.

With this notation, the
q
-hypergeometric series
for
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
is (see
[
5
, §1.10]
)

{\ }{}_{r+1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{s}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_0,\bm a}{\bm b};q,x\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(a_{0};q\right)_{k}\left(\bm{a};q\right)_{k}}{\left(\bm{b};q\right)_{k}}(-1)^{(s-r)k}q^{(s-r)\binom{k}{2}}\frac{x^{k}}{\left(q;q\right)_{k}},

where we assume
\binom{0}{2}=\binom{1}{2}=0
.
When for some
j
,
a_{j}=q^{-n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the series truncates:

(7)

{\ }{}_{r+1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{s}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n},\bm a}{\bm b};q,x\right)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\left(q^{-n};q\right)_{k}\left(\bm{a};q\right)_{k}}{\left(\bm{b};q\right)_{k}}(-1)^{(s-r)k}q^{(s-r)\binom{k}{2}}\frac{x^{k}}{\left(q;q\right)_{k}}

is a
q
-
hypergeometric polynomial
of degree
\leq n
, under the assumption that

(8)

b_{1},\dots b_{s}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{q^{-1},q^{-2},\dots,q^{-n+1},q^{-n}\}.

There are some notorious classical families of polynomials that are
q
-hypergeometric.

The
q
-Laguerre
polynomial of degree
n
and parameter
b\in\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
L^{(b)}_{n}
(where we use a slightly different notation from the one in
[
5
]
), is

(9)

L_{n}^{(b)}(x;q)=\frac{\left(b;q\right)_{n}}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}{\ }_{1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n}}{b};q,-q^{n}bx\right)}.

For
b\in(0,1)
, it satisfies two kinds of orthogonality relations on
(0,\infty)
, an absolutely continuous one and a discrete one. The discrete weight is supported on a (possibly, rescaled) lattice
\{q^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
; see
[
5
, Section 14.21]
for details. In this setting, the zeros are real and positive, each interval
(q^{k+1},q^{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, contains at most one zero, and (see
[
7
, Theorem 4]
),

(10)

L_{n}^{(b)}(x;q)\in\mathbb{P}^{q^{2}}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\subset\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}).

Further results on the roots of the
q
-Laguerre polynomials for
b\in(0,1)
can be found in
[
7
,
8
]
. For instance, they decrease with
b
, and (by
[
8
, Theorem 23]
),

L_{n}^{(b)}(x;q)\prec L_{n}^{(q^{2}b)}(x;q).

Combining these facts, we conclude that
for
q^{2}<t<1
,

(11)

L_{n}^{(b)}(x;q)\prec L_{n}^{(tb)}(x;q).

In the limit
b\to 0
, the
q
-Laguerre polynomials become the
Stieltjes-Wigert
polynomials,

(12)

S(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{b\to 0}L_{n}^{(b)}(qb^{-1}x;q)=\frac{1}{\left(q;q\right)_{n}}{\ }_{1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n}}{0};q,-q^{n+1}x\right)}.

By Lemma
2.5
,

(13)

S(x)\in\overline{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q^{2}}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}).

Another relevant family of classical
q
-hypergeometric polynomials, depending on two parameters,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, are the
little
q
-Jacobi polynomials
:

(14)

p_{n}(x;a,b|q)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\ }_{2}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n}, abq^{n+1}}{aq};q,qx\right)}.

These polynomials are the primary focus of this paper. To simplify the notation, from now on we write
p_{n}(x;a,b)
instead of
p_{n}(x;a,b|q)
, whenever it cannot cause confusion.

Under the assumption that
0<qa<1
and
qb<1
, these polynomials are orthogonal on
(0,1)
with respect to the discrete measure

(15)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(bq;q)_{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}(aq)^{k}\delta_{q^{k}}=\left(\frac{\left(bq;q\right)_{\infty}\left(xq;q\right)_{\infty}}{\left(bxq;q\right)_{\infty}\left(q;q\right)_{\infty}}\,x\,a^{\frac{\ln{x}}{\ln{q}}}\right)d\lambda,\quad d\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{q^{k}},

where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac delta (unit mass point) at
x
. Again, from the general properties of orthogonal polynomials it follows that
p_{n}\left(x;a,b\right)\in\mathbb{P}_{n}((0,1))
, and each interval
(q^{k},q^{k-1})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, contains at most one zero.

A particular case of the little
q
-Jacobi family are the
little
q
-Laguerre polynomials

[
5
]
, that are obtained by setting
b=0
in (
14
):

p_{n}(x;a|q)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=p_{n}(x;a,0|q)={\ }_{2}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n}, 0}{aq};q,qx\right)}.

Finally, in the limit
a\to 0
, with
b<0
, the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials become the
q
-Bessel
polynomials,

(16)

B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{a\to 0}p_{n}(x;a,\tfrac{b}{qa}\left.\right|q)={\ }_{2}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n},bq^n}{0};q,x\right)}.

2.3.
The
q
-derivative and the logarithmic mesh

A technical tool that is useful in studying the relative spacing of the zeros of a polynomial is a
q
-analogue of the derivative. For
q\in(0,1)
, the
q
-derivative
operator
\mathfrak{D}_{q}
is defined as

(17)

\mathfrak{D}_{q}f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(x)-f(qx)}{(1-q)x},&x\neq 0,\\
f^{\prime}(0),&x=0.\end{cases}

Some elementary properties are

(18)

\mathfrak{D}_{q}c=0,\quad\mathfrak{D}_{q}[f(cx)]=c(\mathfrak{D}_{q}f)(cx),\quad c\in\mathbb{R},

and

\mathfrak{D}_{q}x^{i}=\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q}\,x^{i-1},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, for
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
written in the form

(19)

p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}e_{i}(p)\,x^{i},

where
e_{i}(p)
are the coefficients of
p
in the monomial basis (a notational convention we will use in the rest of the paper), we get

\mathfrak{D}_{q}p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q}e_{i}(p)x^{i-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1-q^{i+1}}{1-q}e_{i+1}(p)x^{i}.

In our notation,

(20)

e_{i}(\mathfrak{D}_{q}p)=\frac{1-q^{i+1}}{1-q}e_{i+1}(p),\quad i=0,1,\dots,n-1.

In particular, straightforward calculations allow us to verify the identity

(21)

\mathfrak{D}_{q}{\ }{}_{r+1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{s}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n},\bm a}{\bm b};q,x\right)}=c{\ }_{r+1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{s}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-n+1}, q\bm a}{q\bm b};q,q^{s-r}x\right)},

where

(22)

c=(-1)^{s-r}\frac{(1-q^{-n})\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1-a_{j})}{(1-q)\prod_{j=1}^{s}(1-b_{j})}.

The following result is a well-known fact, see for instance
[
6
, theorem 25]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

If
p\in\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, then
\mathfrak{D}_{q}p\in\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n-1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
p\prec\mathfrak{D}_{q}p
.

3.
Zeros of Some Classical
q
-Polynomials

3.1.
Zeros of little
q
-Jacobi and
q
-Bessel polynomials

Recall our convention that we consider
q\in(0,1)
fixed, and write
p_{n}(x;a,b)
instead of
p_{n}(x;a,b|q)
.

Compared to the extensive results available for the zeros of
q
-Laguerre polynomials, significantly less is known about the zeros of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials.
The following interlacing properties, corresponding to the case when these are orthogonal with respect to the discrete measure (
15
), were known:

Theorem A
.

Let
0<aq<1
and
bq<1
, the following interlacings hold:

(23)

\displaystyle p_{n+1}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;a,qb\right),

(24)

\displaystyle p_{n+1}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{2}b\right),\qquad\text{and}

(25)

\displaystyle p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{2}b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;qa,qb\right).

Theorem
A
was proved in
[
8
, Theorem 12 d), e)]
for
b<0
, but the general statement follows using the same arguments. It is important to observe that the interlacing

p_{n+1}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;qa,qb\right),

established in Theorem
3.1

(ii)
below, is not a consequence of Theorem
A
due to lack of transitivity in
\prec
.

The following theorems contain new results under the same assumptions:

Theorem 3.1

(Monotonicity of zeros)
.

If
0<aq<1
and
bq<1
then the zeros of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials
p_{n}(x;a,b)
are increasing with respect to
b
and decreasing with respect to
a
.

In terms of the partial order from Notation
2.1
, Theorem
3.1
implies that for
0<a_{1}<a_{2}<\tfrac{1}{q}
and
b_{1}<b_{2}<\tfrac{1}{q}
we have

p_{n}(x;a_{2},b_{1})\ll p_{n}(x;a_{1},b_{1})\ll p_{n}(x;a_{1},b_{2}).

In analogy with the standard Jacobi case, this suggests the existence of an electrostatic model for the zeros of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials. We are not aware of such results in the literature.

Theorem 3.2

(lmesh and interlacing of zeros)
.

If
0<aq<1
and
bq<1
, then

p_{n}\left(x;a,b\right)\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}((0,1)),

and the following interlacing holds:

(i)

p_{n}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n-1}\left(x;qa,qb\right)
,

(ii)

p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{2}b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;q^{2}a,b\right)
.

Additionally, for
b<0
,

(iii)

p_{n}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{2}b\right)
.

A useful consequence of the previous result is the following:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
0<aq<1
and
q^{2}\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq 1
such that
t_{1}t_{2}\not=1
. Then

(i)

for
0\leq qb<1
,

p_{n}\left(x;a,t_{1}b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;t_{2}a,b\right);

(ii)

for
b<0
,

p_{n}\left(x;a,b\right)\prec p_{n}\left(x;a,t_{1}b\right).

Part of the statement of Corollary
3.3
was announced in
[
3
, Theorem 8]
, but the proof contained errors. This result generalizes several interlacing properties appearing in
[
3
,
8
]
.

If
b=q^{-k}
for
k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
, when the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials are no longer orthogonal on the real line, we have:

Proposition 3.4
.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

(26)

E_{k}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-q^{-j}x\right)={\ }_{1}{\mathcal{\phi}}_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-k}}{\cdot};q,x\right)}.

Then

(27)

\displaystyle p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{-k}\right)=E_{k}(qx)\,p_{n-k}\left(q^{-k}x;a,q^{k}\right),

and
p_{n}\left(x;a,q^{-k}\right)\in\overline{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}}((0,1))
.

The case
a=q^{-k}
, with
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, violates the constrains (
8
). However, we can overcome this difficulty by considering the normalized little
q
-Jacobi polynomials

(28)

\left(q^{-k+1};q\right)_{n}p_{n}\left(x;q^{-k},b\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{\left(q^{-n};q\right)_{j}}{\left(q;q\right)_{j}}\left(bq^{n-k+1};q\right)_{j}\left(q^{j-k+1};q\right)_{n-j}(qx)^{j}.

Proposition 3.5
.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\left(q^{-k+1};q\right)_{n}p_{n}\left(x;q^{-k},b\right)=c\,(qx)^{k}\,p_{n-k}\left(x;q^{k},b\right),

where

c=(-1)^{k}q^{\binom{k}{2}-nk}\left(bq^{n-k+1};q\right)_{k}\left(q^{k+1};q\right)_{n-k}.

Observe that in this case, the polynomials present a multiple zero at the origin; the definition of the logarithmic mesh is not applicable and would need to be modified to formulate an assertion analogous to that in
Proposition
3.4
.

Since
q
-Bessel polynomial
B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)
is a limit case of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomial (see (
16
)), some properties of the latter are still valid. For instance,
B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\in\overline{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}}((0,1))
. But more is true:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
b<0
. Then,

(29)

B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{q}((0,1)).

If additionally
q^{2}<t<1
, then

(30)

B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\preccurlyeq B_{n}\left(x;tb|q\right).

3.2.
Zeros of

Theorem
3.6
follows from approximating the
q
-Bessel polynomials with little
q
-Jacobi polynomials, see (
16
), and using what has been already proved for the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Recall from (
16
) that the
q
-Bessel polynomials
B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)
can be obtained as the limit
p_{n}\left(x;a,\frac{b}{qa}\right)
when
a\to 0
. Then, interlacing (
30
) is a direct consequence of Corollary
3.3
and Lemma
2.5
(
2
).

On the other hand, Lemma
2.5
(
1
) implies that
B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\in\overline{\mathbb{P}^{q}_{n}}((0,1))
. In other words, the logaritmic mesh of the
q
-Bessel polynomials satisfies

\mathop{\rm lmesh}{\left[B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\right]}\leq q.

To prove (
29
), we just need to show that this inequity is strict.
Assume that this is false; that is, that
\mathop{\rm lmesh}{\left[B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)\right]}=q
. This implies that there exist two zeros,
y_{0}\neq 0
and
y_{1}\neq 0
, of
B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)
such that
y_{1}=qy_{0}
. From
[
5
, (14.22.3)]
, we have the

-difference equation,

(59)

(cx+1)\,B\left(x\right)=b\,x\,B\left(qx\right)+(1-x)\,B\left(q^{-1}x\right),

where
B(x)=B_{n}\left(x;b|q\right)

59

by_{1}\,B\left(qy_{1}\right)=0.

y_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=q^{2}y_{0}

. Similarly, evaluating

. This shows that
