Noncommunicable chronic comorbidities, perceived social support, depression, and health-related quality of life among people with HIV: a structural equation modeling study
Authors: Zhengping Zhu, Yiting Shao, Jingwen Wang, Zhiliang Hu, Chunqin Bai, Yingying Yang, Xinglian Xu, Ying Wang, Lin Han, Lixiang Liu, Yujian Tang, Ronghua Wang, Cheng Chen, Yuanyuan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with HIV (PWH) face substantial physical and mental health challenges, which translates to poorer health-related quality of life (HRQoL) relative to the general population. Additionally, they have a higher risk of developing the non-communicable chronic comorbidities (NCCs). However, the complex interplay between the risk of NCCs, perceived social support (PSS), depression, and HRQoL among PWH has not yet been adequately elucidated.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the impact of NCCs risk on HRQoL among PWH, and to examine the mediating roles of PSS and depression in it.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from September to December 2024 using a convenience sample of 2620 PWH recruited from all 7 designated hospitals in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China. Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, Patients’ Health Questionnaire Depression Scale-9 item, and Medical Outcomes Study HIV Health Survey were used. Meanwhile, the data about NCCs were derived from the hospital outpatient system records. The structural equation model (SEM) was employed to explore the pathways linking NCCs risk to HRQoL, as well as the mediating roles of PSS and depression.ResultsThe median scores for PHS and MHS of PWH were 57.77 and 49.72, respectively. Additionally, 28.24% of PWH had NCCs, and 43.89% were detected with depression. NCCs risk was directly and negatively associated with HRQoL among PWH (β=-0.124, P = 0.003), while PSS and depression significantly mediated the association between NCCs risk and HRQoL, accounting for 3.69% and 36.48% of the total association, respectively. Besides, they acted as sequential mediators in a chain pathway, which explained an additional 9.02% to the total association.ConclusionsThis study identified both direct and indirect associations between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. These findings can inform to the improvement of HRQoL among PWH and offer insights for refining comprehensive care management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26043-3.Keywords: Non-communicable chronic comorbidities, Health-related quality of life, Social support, Depression, HIV/AIDS, Structural equation modeling

Introduction

The prevention and control of acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) remains a major challenge in public health with global concern. By 2023, an estimated 85.6 million people had become infected with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and about 39.9 million people were living with HIV (PWH) worldwide [1]. In China, as of 2024, there were 749,839 reported PWH nationwide [2]. As the combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) advances, the mortality of PWH and the proportion of treatment failure have significantly decreased. Consequently, in regions with sufficient medical resources, the leading causes of death among PWH receiving cART have shifted from AIDS-related illnesses to non-AIDS-related diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and so on [3]. Concurrently, PWH experience a high risk of physical and mental health problems, resulting in poorer health-related quality of life (HRQoL) compared to the general population [4]. Recognizing the importance of patient-reported outcomes, a global multidisciplinary group of HIV experts identified self-reported HRQoL as a crucial monitoring indicator in 2019 [5]. This aligns with UNAIDS’s advocacy for the “fourth 90” target in HIV care, which emphasizes the achievement of high HRQoL [6]. Accordingly, improving the HRQoL of PWH has gained increasing attention.

HRQoL is a multidimensional concept, which covers physical health, mental health and social function. As for PWH, in the dimension of physical health, advances in cART and the associated aging process have accentuated the risk of non-communicable chronic comorbidities (NCCs) [4]. In this study, the NCCs risk was operationally defined as the count of five chronic diseases (cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, osteoporosis, and chronic respiratory disease) documented within the past year. These non-communicable chronic diseases are linked to aging or immune inflammation in the context of prolonged life expectancy of PWH [7]. Furthermore, PWH faced a heightened risk of developing NCCs with increasing age [3, 8]. While in the dimension of mental health, the HIV-related stigma and discrimination were common obstacles, and the prevalence of depression in PWH was high [9–11]. Moreover, within the dimension of social function, due to stigma and discrimination, PWH perceived relatively low social support [12].

Perceived social support (PSS) refers to an individual’s subjective experience of the emotional or instrumental support provided by their social network [13]. Emotional support centers on alleviating psychological stress and fostering a sense of belonging, as well as improving psychological adaptability. Instrumental support refers to the provision of tangible assistance to address specific practical problems. Studies have shown that higher levels of PSS contribute to rehabilitation and self-efficacy in patients with non-communicable chronic diseases [14, 15], and are also associated with improved HRQoL among PWH [12, 16]. Furthermore, based on Anderson & Funnell’s chronic disease empowerment theory [17], we proposed that PSS mediates the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. Specifically, higher NCCs risk is posited to diminish PSS, which in turn lowers HRQoL.

Depression is a mental disorder characterized by significant and persistent low mood. Common accompanying symptoms include diminished interest or pleasure in activities, fatigue or loss of energy, impaired cognitive function (e.g., poor concentration, indecisiveness), and somatic symptoms (e.g., sleep disturbances, appetite changes). In severe cases, it may lead to suicidal ideation or psychotic features such as delusions or hallucinations. Studies have shown that individuals with chronic conditions are more likely to experience negative emotions such as depression and anxiety, and depression could reduce the HRQoL among PWH [18]. Based on the aforementioned considerations, we proposed that depression mediates the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. Specifically, higher NCCs risk is posited to exacerbate depressive symptoms, which in turn leads to lower HRQoL.

Importantly, NCCs, PSS and depression are also interlinked. NCCs were associated with poorer mental health, strained social relations, and worse self-reported health [19]. Furthermore, PSS could mitigate depressive symptoms by improving HRQoL [20]. Therefore, grounded in Engel’s Biopsychosocial Model, we proposed that PSS and depression serve as independent mediators and also form a sequential mediating pathway, in the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH.

However, research specifically examining the complex interactions among NCCs risk, PSS, depression, and their collective impact on HRQoL in PWH remained scarce. Thus, this study aimed to explore the influence of NCCs risk on HRQoL in PWH and examine the mediating roles of PSS and depression in this relationship. The ultimate goal is to provide empirical evidence for developing interventions to improve HRQoL and refining comprehensive care management models for PWH.

The specific hypotheses were formally stated as follows:

PSS mediates the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. Specifically, a higher NCCs risk is associated with diminished PSS, which in turn leads to lower HRQoL.

Depression mediates the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. Specifically, a higher NCCs risk is associated with more severe depressive symptoms, which in turn leads to lower HRQoL.

Methods

Design and participants

From September to December 2024, a cross-sectional study was conducted at all seven designated hospitals for antiviral treatment in Nanjing using convenience sampling. Clinical trial number: not applicable. Participants were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: (a) aged 18 or above; (b) confirmed HIV diagnosis and undergoing cART; (c) capable of completing the questionnaire with basic reading comprehension; (d) willing to provide written informed consent and participate in the survey. Equation N=[×P×(1-P)] was used to calculate the minimum sample size. Based on our previous survey of 1820 PWH in Nanjing in 2022, which found a depression detection rate (P) of 50.2% [11], with a margin of error (δ) of 0.03 and a Z-value of 1.96 (95% CI), the initial calculated sample size was 1068. To account for the use of non-probability sampling, the sample size was increased by 50%. A further 20% was added to allow for potential non-response, resulting in a final minimum required sample size of 2001 participants. Trained investigators conducted anonymous questionnaire surveys. Participants’ unique treatment numbers were recorded at the time of survey completion. No personal identifiers (e.g., name, national identity card number) were collected. Instead, cART coding, a unique treatment number, was collected to retrieve their in-hospital medical records. The questionnaire mainly includes demographic characteristics (gender, date of birth, education level, marital status, transmission route, the last CD4 + T lymphocyte cells (CD4) count, the last viral load (VL), years since diagnosis), PSS, depression, and HRQoL.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Nanjing Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention (approval reference number: PJ2024-A003-02). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants signed informed consent and voluntarily participated.

Variables and instruments

Non-communicable chronic comorbidities

NCCs risk, as a latent variable, was derived from five observed dichotomous (yes/no) variables within the past year: cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, gout, osteoporosis and chronic respiratory disease. Data on these five diseases were obtained from the electronic medical record (EMR) systems of the seven hospitals. Clinical diagnoses were made by physicians according to standard criteria, and extracted by trained hospital case managers using participants’ unique HIV treatment identification numbers for data linkage. The definitions and diagnostic criteria for NCCs were listed in Appendix A.

Perceived social support

PSS were derived from three dimensions of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS): family support, friends support, and others support. This scale contains 12 items scored on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = “very strongly disagree” to 7 = “very strongly agree”), and the total score ranges from 12 to 84 [21]. Higher scores indicate higher levels of perceived social support. MSPSS is widely used, and its Cronbach’s coefficient is higher than 0.85 [22].

Depression

Depression was measured by the Patients’ Health Questionnaire Depression Scale-9 item (PHQ-9) by enquiring PWH whether they had experienced the frequency of some mental health-related problems in the past two weeks. This scale contains 9 items, and each item is graded as 0 ~ 3 points, so the total score ranges from 0 to 27. A score less than 5 points was defined as no depression, and a score of 5 or above as depression. Meta-analysis showed that PHQ-9 had good sensitivity (0.88) and specificity (0.85) [23].

Health-related quality of life

HRQoL was a first-order latent variable in SEM, the observed variables of which were derived from two dimensions, physical health summary (PHS) and mental health summary (MHS) of the Medical Outcomes Study HIV Health Survey (MOS-HIV). According to the Chinese version of the MOS-HIV scoring standard, the original score of each dimension was calculated by the accumulation method, and the conversion score was calculated by the standard formula. Finally, it was concluded that the total scores of PHS and MHS ranged from 0 to 100 [24]. The higher the score, the better the quality of life of the PWH in this dimension. The Chinese version of MOS-HIV scale has good validity and reliability, and its Cronbach’s coefficient was found to be > 0.80, indicating good internal consistency [25]. Therefore, MOS-HIV was recommended as a specific scale to evaluate the HRQoL of PWH [26].

Statistical analyses

SPSS 27.0 and R-4.3.1 were used for data analysis. Firstly, qualitative data were presented as counts (%). Due to non-normality (Mardia’s test), quantitative data were summarized as median (inter-quartile range, IQR). All continuous variables were standardized (z-score) to eliminate scale differences. Secondly, partial correlation analysis, controlling for covariates, was performed to examine relationships among study variables, with Pearson’s correlation coefficients reported. Thirdly, mediation pathways were tested using SEM with the lavaan package (v0.6-16) in R-4.3.1. The risk of NCCs served as the independent variable, HRQoL as the dependent variable, and both PSS and depression as mediators. Age was included as a covariate to control for confounding. Robust maximum likelihood estimation accounted for non-normality during initial model fitting. The model was refined iteratively with respect to theoretical plausibility, with guidance from modification indices (MIs > 10). We considered adding three theoretically justified residual correlations: (1) family support and MHS [12], (2) cardiovascular disease and PHS [27], (3) friend support and MHS [28]. Model fit was assessed using the following criteria [29]: X²/df < 5, CFI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08, GFI > 0.90, and SRMR < 0.05. Finally, the statistical significance of indirect associations was evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. A two-sided significance level of = 0.05 was applied for all tests.

After excluding the individuals due to duplicated individuals (n = 18), and failing criteria (n = 2), 2620 PWH were included for analysis. Mediated associations analysis

Consistent with the study hypothesis, the SEM was constructed to specify and examine both the individual and chain mediating roles of PSS and depression in the relationship between NCCs risk and HRQoL among PWH. Given its significant associations with NCCs risk and depression, age was included as an independent exogenous covariate in the model to control for confounding. In the initial model specification, age was regressed on all four study variables (NCCs risk, PSS, depression, HRQoL). However, the direct path from age to PSS was non-significant (β = -0.016, P = 0.583), which was consistent with findings from the partial correlation analysis. And it aligns with the theoretical evidence suggesting that PSS remains relatively stable across age groups [30]. This non-significant path was subsequently removed to enhance model parsimony and statistical validity. The direct path from age to HRQoL remained statistically significant (β = 0.063, P = 0.016) and was retained. The specification and initial results of the pre-modification SEM were presented in Appendix B.

The fit indices for the final trimmed model met the established goodness-of-fit criteria (Table 4), indicating an acceptable model fit of the hypothesized model to the data. The standardized direct associations in the SEM are presented in Table 5 and Fig. 1. NCCs risk was significantly negatively associated with PSS (β = -0.121, P＜0.001) and HRQoL (β = -0.124, P = 0.003), and significantly positively associated with depression (β = 0.126, P = 0.003). Higher PSS was associated with lower depression (β = -0.260, P＜0.001) and higher HRQoL (β = 0.075, P＜0.001). Higher depression was associated with lower HRQoL (β = -0.707, P＜0.001). Meanwhile, increased age was associated with higher NCCs risk (β = 0.535, P＜0.001), lower depression (β = -0.224, P＜0.001), and higher HRQoL (β = 0.064, P = 0.015).

Discussion

Our study found that 28.24% PWH had at least one NCC. Our analysis indicated a significant negative association between NCCs risk and HRQoL, aligning with prior research [31, 32]. HIV infection and cART are known to accelerate biological aging processes, increasing susceptibility to geriatric syndromes such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, cancer, liver disease, neurocognitive decline, and persistent inflammation [33]. These conditions inherently impair physical function and well-being. Additionally, potential drug-drug interactions between medications for NCCs and cART regimens could further compromise health and HRQoL [34].

In our study, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease was the highest among NCCs in PWH. Our model specifically highlighted a strong negative correlation between cardiovascular disease and the PHS dimension of HRQoL. This is particularly relevant given the elevated risk of atherosclerosis associated with cART [33], and the disproportionate prevalence and severity of cardiovascular disease among PWH [35]. Cardiovascular disease could lead to debilitating symptoms (e.g., angina, dyspnea, edema), directly impairing activity tolerance, sleep, and physical recovery, and has emerged as a leading cause of mortality in this population [35]. Compared with people who are not infected with HIV, PWH had a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, and the relative risk of myocardial infarction in PWH increased by 20% to 100% [35]. Therefore, it is necessary to strengthen the integration of medical care and data systems and include cardiovascular risk indicators (such as low-density lipoprotein level, triglyceride), bone mineral density, and vitamin D level into routine cART follow-up, which facilitates the early screening, timely treatment, and continuous monitoring of NCCs and chronic inflammation.

We found that the detection rate of depression among PWH (43.89%) was lower than that reported in another Chinese study (50.85%) [36], which may be attributed to the regional variations and sociodemographic differences. Our cross-sectional path analysis is consistent with a model in which depressive symptoms play a mediating role in the association between NCCs risk and reduced HRQoL. This potential pathway can be understood through Beck’s cognitive theory of depression (1967), which posits that depression is characterized by a negative cognitive triad, anhedonia, and reduced behavioral activation [37]. The physical risk and functional limitations imposed by NCCs may act as a persistent stressor that fosters such negative cognitive schemas and erodes motivation. However, the direction of these relationships requires verification in longitudinal studies. In line with prior findings that HIV itself can disrupt neuroendocrine pathways and induce neuroinflammation, contributing to high depression rates [10]. It is reported that patients with non-communicable chronic diseases were more likely to have negative emotions like depression and anxiety [38], which in turn substantially diminishes HRQoL [18]. Specific NCCs, like chronic respiratory disease, were linked to more severe depressive symptoms and poorer HRQoL in PWH. Consequently, NCCs may not only present a physical challenge, but also, via the cognitive pathway described by Beck, potentially increase the risk of depression, which may synergistically reduce HRQoL. These findings underscored a potential need for providing specialized training for HIV case managers in comorbidity management and psychological support to enable systematic NCCs care. Furthermore, telemedicine and psychosocial intervention should be carried out in combination with the internet to regularly evaluate the psychosocial status of PWH.

Our study also suggested that PSS played a significant mediating role in the influence of NCCs risk on HRQoL, such that NCCs risk was linked to lower PSS, which was in turn associated with poorer HRQoL. Possible reasons might include the following: PSS is a protective factor to improve chronic inflammation that is the basis of most NCCs [39]. In addition, PSS improved physical and mental health through a stress buffering mechanism [40]. Moreover, it could enhance psychological resilience and health empowerment [41], which improved the self-management of chronic conditions and fostered greater rehabilitation self-efficacy.

The serial mediation analysis further indicated that PSS might influence the pathway from NCCs to reduced HRQoL by alleviating depressive symptoms. This finding can be interpreted through the lenses of the transactional model of stress and coping and psychoneuroimmunology. First, according to Lazarus and Folkman’s theory [42], the impact of NCCs as a chronic stressor depends on an individual’s appraisal process. For PWH, higher perceived discrimination may deplete psychological resources, leading to an appraisal of NCCs risk as exceeding their coping capacity, thereby elevating perceived stress and the risk of depression. Concurrently, psychological stress can induce the production of inflammatory factors by activating the sympathetic nervous system. Both chronic inflammation and NCCs may increase susceptibility to depression [43], potentially creating a cycle linking NCC risk, psychological stress, inflammation, and depressive symptoms, ultimately contribute to lower HRQoL. In contrast, a higher level of PSS may facilitate a more positive appraisal of stressors and buffer the physiological stress response [44], potentially helping to disrupt the vicious cycle and ultimately improving HRQoL. Therefore, it would be beneficial to give further attention to strengthening the social support network for PWH. This requires mobilizing family and friends to enhance care and support, alongside uniting communities to improve health education and empowerment. Ultimately, these efforts should raise awareness of chronic non-communicable disease risks and promote self-efficacy and healthy behaviors among PWH.

Our study found that age showed dual associations in the model. On the one hand, older age was strongly associated with a higher NCCs risk among PWH [3], consistent with the trend of accelerated aging in PWH and previous studies’ findings. On the other hand, increasing age was associated with lower levels of depression and higher HRQoL. This may reflect that the older PWH in our study had more effective emotional regulation strategies and milder depressive symptoms [45], potentially aiding in the preservation of HRQoL. However, partial correlation analysis indicated that age was correlated with both NCCs risk and depression, while the direct correlation with HRQoL was non-significant. This suggests that the opposing pathways (NCCs risk decreasing HRQoL vs. reduced depression increasing HRQoL) may statistically counterbalance each other in their direct effects on HRQoL. Therefore, it is important to prioritize HIV-vulnerable people, particularly PWH aged ≥ 50 years, who are at higher risk of multiple comorbidities [46]. Furthermore, in the future, refined management for PWH can offer case-based comprehensive treatment and care management plans based on their immune status, NCCs spectrum, and mental health status of PWH, so as to improve HRQoL.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. First, its cross-sectional design could not establish causal relationships between variables. Second, self-reported data may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, convenience sampling may affect the sample’s representativeness and the generalizability of findings. Fourth, although pragmatically useful, combining multiple heterogeneous conditions into a single “NCCs risk” factor overlooks differences in disease nature and severity. Additionally, potentially relevant variables such as individual income and treatment regimen were not included in the study. Nevertheless, the large sample size and use of standardized measures enhance the credibility of the findings. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to track the occurrence and development of NCCs, depression, social support and HRQoL among PWH over time. Incorporating more objective clinical and sociodemographic covariates to improve model precision and validity.

Conclusion

This study modeled the complex pathways linking NCCs risk, PSS, depression, and HRQoL among PWH. Key findings indicated that NCCs risk was directly associated with lower HRQoL. NCCs risk was also indirectly associated with HRQoL via lower PSS or higher depressive symptoms. Furthermore, PSS and depression acted as sequential mediators in the relationship between NCC risk and HRQoL. To improve HRQoL, considerations could include early screening and proactive long-term management of NCCs and depression, alongside strengthening social support networks.
