Transcutaneous Auricular Vagus Nerve Stimulation Reduces Inflammatory Biomarkers after Large Vessel Occlusion Stroke: Results of a Prospective Randomized Open-Label Blinded Endpoint Trial
Authors: Osvaldo J Laurido-Soto, Gansheng Tan, Susan Searles Nielsen, Anna L Huguenard, Kara M Donovan, Isabella Xu, James Giles, Rajat Dhar, Opeolu Adeoye, Jin-Moo Lee, Eric Leuthardt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammation exacerbates brain injury following acute ischemic stroke (AIS). Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) reduces systemic inflammation, but its efficacy in AIS remains unexplored. We examined the safety and anti-inflammatory effect of taVNS in AIS patients. Prospective Randomized Open-label blinded endpoint design, sham-controlled trial. Anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (LVO) AIS patients were assigned to twice-daily taVNS or sham stimulation for five days or until discharge. Inclusion criteria: age≥18 years, NIHSS≥6, anterior circulation LVO, and enrollment within 36-hours of last known normal (LKN). Primary endpoints were changes in inflammatory biomarkers (interleukins (IL)-1β, 6, 10, 17α, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) on Days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7). TaVNS safety endpoints included bradycardia, hypotension, infection, and death. Thirty-five patients (17 taVNS, 18 sham) were enrolled and taVNS/sham treatment started a mean of 0.99 days (SD 0.26 days) from LKN. Mean stimulations received were 8.24 (SD 2.77) and 7.33 (SD 3.20), for treatment vs. sham respectively. The rate of protocol adherence was 77%. TaVNS treatment significantly changed IL-6 levels compared to sham treatment (overall p=0.0001). Although overall trajectories of other cytokines did not significantly differ between groups, a post-hoc analysis found significant differences on day 3 cytokine levels between groups for: IL-1β (p=0.024), IL-6 (p=0.045), and IL-17α (p=0.044). No significant difference between treatment groups in safety endpoints were found. taVNS safely reduced post-AIS inflammation in anterior circulation LVO patients. Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) due to large vessel occlusions (LVO) lead to disproportionate morbidity and mortality without emergency treatment [1]. Even after successful recanalization with mechanical thrombectomy, i.e., reperfusion of the occluded vessel [2], up to half of patients remain with significant disability [3], highlighting the need for adjunct therapies to improve outcomes. Neuroinflammation has long been recognized as an important contributor to ischemic brain injury [4]. In the acute phase of AIS, inflammatory biomarker elevation [5], are associated with exacerbation of brain injury. Despite numerous AIS trials, most anti-inflammatory drug trials have shown limited efficacy and/or medication side effects [6].

There is substantial evidence that Vagal nerve stimulation (VNS) reduces systemic inflammation in multiple pre-clinical models [7]. In animal models of cerebral ischemia and reperfusion injury, VNS not only reduced infarct size, but also improved neurological outcomes [8, 9]. More recently, clinical trials of VNS have demonstrated efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [10] and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage [11].

In practice, most FDA-approved VNS devices are administered through surgical implantation. While appropriate for chronic conditions such as RA and chronic stroke recovery [12], implantable solutions are impractical in the emergent setting. In this context, transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS) offers a possible solution given its noninvasiveness and ability to be rapidly deployed (Fig. 1). Based on encouraging animal studies in AIS, demonstrated safety [13, 14] and efficacy of taVNS [11], we conducted the “Neuromodulation Using Vagus Nerve Stimulation Following Ischemic Stroke as Therapeutic Adjunct” (NUVISTA) trial. This prospective randomized open-label blinded endpoint (PROBE) design sham-controlled clinical trial aimed to quantify the effect of taVNS on plasma inflammatory biomarkers and explore its safety in AIS patients.

Methods

Study Population

AIS due to LVO patients who were admitted to Barnes Jewish Hospital in St. Louis, Missouri, between November 2022-May 2024 were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria included: age ≥ 18 years, anterior circulation LVO [Internal Carotid Artery (ICA), Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) first branch (M1), MCA second branch (M2)], National Institutes of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) score ≥ 6, pre-stroke modified Rankin scale (mRS) ≤ 2, randomization < 36-hours (hrs) from last known normal (LKN), expected life expectancy > 3 months, and no active immunosuppressive/modulating therapy, concomitant infections or inflammatory states, hypotension, or bradycardia. Full Inclusion and Exclusion criteria in Supplemental Table 1. The patient/family and all individuals assessing outcomes, including the laboratories that measured blood biomarkers were blinded to which arm the patient was enrolled.

Protocol

Patients were randomized to taVNS vs. sham electrical stimulation. The treatment arm received stimulation to the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (in-between the tragus and the concha), while the sham arm received stimulation to the greater auricular nerve (earlobe) [16]. Stimulation parameters were intensity–0.5 mA, frequency-25 Hz, and pulse width-200µs applied to the left ear for 20 min every 12 h (Fig. 1). TaVNS or sham stimulation occurred for five days (10 total) or till discharge/death, whichever came first, and received standard AIS care. Full protocol, device information, and CONSORT checklist adherence as per Supplemental Resource 2a-b.

Patient Characteristics and Standard Treatment

Patient characteristics included age, sex, race, comorbidities, baseline NIHSS, and pre-stroke mRS score. Strokes were characterized by laterality, occlusion type, and acute interventions (i.e. thrombolytic administration and/or thrombectomy). Thrombectomy reperfusion was assessed utilizing Thrombolysis In Cerebral Infarction score (TICI) score, with successful reperfusion defined as TICI ≥ 2B [2].

Primary Outcomes

Blood Biomarkers of Inflammation

Total blood protein levels (pg/mL) of five AIS relevant cytokines were assessed: four pro-inflammatory, interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, IL-17α, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) [5] and one anti-inflammatory, IL-10 [17]. White Blood Cell (WBC) counts (K/cumm) were also measured. All five cytokines were measured at baseline (Day zero/<24 h) and on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 or until discharge. For blood draw protocol, sample processing times, and cytokine quantification methods see Supplemental Resources 2c-d & Supplemental Tables 2–3.

Secondary Outcomes

Safety Endpoints

We monitored for known taVNS cardiovascular complications (i.e. hypotension and bradycardia) [18], in addition to the development of infections, hemorrhagic transformation, decompressive hemicraniectomy, and/or death after randomization. We defined cardiovascular complications by utilizing the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0 [19], with hypotension defined as systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg and bradycardia defined as heart rate < 50 beats/minute. Vital signs were recorded: 5-minutes before, every 5-minutes during, and 5-minutes after stimulation. We defined infections as the development of any urinary tract infection, pneumonia, sepsis, or other infection throughout their hospital stay. We reviewed the medical record to identify the presence of hemorrhagic transformation, decompressive hemicraniectomy, and/or death. Patients were followed for 105 days after admission or until death [20].

Exploratory neurological outcomes

The NIHSS score were assessed at presentation/admission and daily thereafter until discharge or death, as well as on Day 30+/−7 days from LKN. Assessments were performed by NIHSS certified personnel. MRS was evaluated on Days 30 and 90 from LKN over the phone [20]. Additional exploratory outcomes included hospital length of stay (LOS) and readmissions, while discharge disposition was categorized as either good (home, home with health services, or inpatient rehabilitation) or poor (skilled nursing facility, long-term acute care hospital, or death before discharge).

Brain imaging characteristics as baseline variables

CT perfusion (CTP) and hyperacute MRI (hMRI) scans were obtained at baseline (within 24 h of LKN). Baseline scans were available for 19 (54.3%) patients, 16 (46%) were CTP and 3 (8.6%) were hMRI. All ischemic core (Cerebral blood flow < 30% or Diffusion weighted imaging [DWI] value), penumbra (mismatch volume = CTP Tmax > 6 s – ischemic core), and max at risk tissue (Tmax > 6 s) were obtained from imaging radiology reports.

Statistical Analysis

We used Stata/MP version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) and Python packages Scipy and Statsmodels. All continuous variables were retained as continuous, except where noted. Multivariable regression was used to examine treatment group associations with outcomes, except for rare secondary outcomes/adverse effects. An intention-to-treat (ITT) approach was implemented [21].

Rate of Change in Cytokines and WBC in TaVNS vs. Sham Treatment

Longitudinal data were analyzed using mixed effects models [22]. To complement our primary approach, treatment also was handled as time-varying when noted, with assignment occurring at randomization. All longitudinal models included treatment, time since LKN, and their interaction term(s) as primary independent variables to test whether the pattern of change over time differed between the groups. Time was modeled quadratically for cytokine/WBC outcomes to capture the known U-shaped associations with time [23]. Treatment-time interactions were tested using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms. These analyses were adjusted for: baseline NIHSS, time from LKN to recanalization or attempt, and recanalization based on TICI score [2]. We conducted post-hoc t-tests to compare cytokine levels between groups on each day. Full model descriptions can be found in Supplemental Resource 2d. In sensitivity analyses, we adjusted for potential variables of interest: laterality, LVO location, baseline imaging characteristics, sample processing time, and baseline anti-inflammatory cytokine.

Safety Outcomes and Exploratory Neurological and Discharge Outcomes in Relation to Treatment

We performed multivariable regression to assess differences in safety endpoints (bradycardia, hypotension, infection, hemicraniectomy, hemorrhagic transformation, and deaths), neurological outcomes (NIHSS 30-days and mRS 90-days), and for discharge related outcomes: readmission, LOS, and hospital poor disposition, among groups; except for nominal variables with any expected cell count < 5, for which a two-sided Fisher’s test was performed.

Exploratory Analysis of the Association Between Change in Cytokines and Change in Clinical Outcomes

We structured further analysis utilizing IL-6, as it has been associated with stroke outcomes [5, 24]. We investigated the association between IL-6 levels and clinical outcomes, or their respective changes, to understand the potential functional implications of reducing inflammatory cytokines. We performed regressions with changes in NIHSS scores as the dependent variable and changes in IL-6 as the independent variable. We paired Day 3 and Day 5 NIHSS and IL-6 measurements and then calculated the change by subtracting the first measurement. As recanalization status following thrombectomy significantly influences neurological outcomes, we verified if the relationship between IL-6 and neurological outcomes depended on this. We used a linear mixed-model that included IL-6 levels, treatment, the interaction between treatment and IL-6 levels, recanalization status, and the interaction between recanalization status and IL-6 levels as predictors. We also explored the relation between IL-6 and NIHSS overall, as well as change in IL-6 in relation to 90-day mRS overall.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

Thirty-five participants were randomized: 17 taVNS treatment group and 18 sham treatment group (Fig. 1). The cohort was biracial: Caucasian (77.1%) and Black (22.9%); had a mean age of 67.7 years (SD 13.7), and was 48.6% female. The baseline NIHSS was 16.4 (SD 6.6) for the treatment group and 15.7 (SD 4.4) for the sham group. The treated group had a higher burden of ICA occlusions (47.1% vs. 27.8%), and greater time from LKN to recanalization (mean 13.2 h vs. 9.8 h) when compared to the sham group. Thrombolytics were administered to 43% and thrombectomy was attempted in 94% of the cohort; recanalization was achieved in 82% of the taVNS group and 89% of the sham group (Table 1, Supplemental Table 4).

Adherence

Upon application of the taVNS or sham device, all stimulations lasted 30 min once started. Ten (58.8%) patients in the treated group and 8 (44.4%) patients in the sham group completed all 10 stimulations; the mean number of stimulations was 8.24 (SD 2.77) and 7.33 (SD 3.20), respectively. The mean time to first stimulation from LKN was 22.8 h (SD 6.5) in the treated vs. 24.8 h (SD 6.2) in the sham group, and the mean number of days from LKN to the last stimulation was 4.27 (SD 1.77) and 4.56 (SD 1.34), respectively. Reasons for failure to complete all ten stimulations primarily was due to early hospital discharge (including death) (Supplemental Table 5).

Primary Outcomes: Plasma Cytokine and WBC Trajectories

We compared cytokine trajectories between patients treated with taVNS vs. Sham, across the 7 days after admission. In general, cytokine trajectories appeared U-shaped (inverted U-shaped for sham-treated and upright U for taVNS-treated patients, Fig. 2). IL-6 trajectories were significantly different between groups (pinteracton=0.0001); however, trajectories of the other cytokines (i.e. IL-1β, IL-10, IL-17α, and TNFα) were not statistically different in this model, despite similar U-shape characteristics (Table 2; Fig. 2). Nonetheless, in post-hoc analysis comparing predicted cytokine levels between groups on each day there appeared to be potential differences in day 3 cytokine levels for IL-1β (p = 0.024), IL-6 (p = 0.045), and IL-17α (p = 0.044). (Supplemental Fig. 1). In our model comparing the pattern of change in WBC between treatment and sham groups, we found no differences (pinteraction=0.60). The above models and results based on changes in inflammation biomarkers over time were insensitive to adjustment for our a priori covariates, as well as for: laterality (left, right), LVO site (M2, M1, ICA), baseline imaging variables (ischemic core volume, max at risk ischemic tissue, mismatch volume), sample processing time, and baseline anti-inflammatory cytokine. (Supplemental Table 6). Given that the IL-10 trajectory was similar to the IL-6 trajectory, and that these two cytokines have presumed opposite effects on inflammatory response, in post-hoc analyses we used IL-6:IL-10 ratio as an exploratory outcome. The results paralleled those for IL-6, but were nonsignificant for the difference between treatment groups (interaction p = 0.08).

Safety Outcomes

Cardiac events were not statistically different between groups; bradycardia occurred in 11.8% of the treatment group vs. 22.2% in the sham group (p = 0.66) and hypotension occurred in 0% of the treatment group vs. 11.1% in the sham group (p = 0.49). There were no differences between treatment groups for infections (p = 1.00), decompressive hemicraniectomy (p = 1.00), hemorrhagic transformation (p = 0.60), and death (p = 0.10). Table 3a. Those patients who died in the treatment group were significantly older than those patients who survived [died age 84.7 (SD 7.7) vs. alive age 64.5 (SD 3.6), p = 0.03], were on the trial on average only 2.17 days, and cause of death for all patients was family decision for comfort care.

Exploratory Clinical Outcomes

An analysis of daily NIHSS trajectories found no differences between treatment groups (p = 0.69), nor on total NIHSS 30-days (p = 0.59). There were no differences in good mRS outcomes (≤ 2) amongst groups (p = 0.95). There was no difference between treatment groups for death (p = 0.10), discharge disposition (p = 0.71), LOS (p = 0.59), or hospital readmissions (p = 0.23, Table 3b).

Early Plasma IL-6 Levels Correlate with Neurological Outcomes

We found that early changes in IL-6 correlated with 90-day mRS scores (95% confidence interval (CI) for β_(IL-6)=[0.061,0.177], p < 0.001, z-test); with higher IL-6 levels correlating with high mRS values. Also, there was a statistically significant relationship between IL-6 and NIHSS scores (F-statistic = 5.276, p = 0.023,R2 = 0.04), with an IL-6 coefficient of 0.252 (p = 0.023, t-test, n = 130); with higher IL-6 levels correlating with higher NIHSS. The positive association between change in IL-6 and change in NIHSS was greater among patients without recanalization. A 1-pg/mL increase in IL-6 was associated with an additional 0.46-point increase in NIHSS scores in patients who were not recanalized when compared with patients who did (z=−2.015, p = 0.044, 95%CI=[0.012, 0.898], Supplemental Fig. 2).

Discussion

The NUVISTA trial demonstrated that taVNS, when added to standard medical care, safely and effectively modulated the post-AIS inflammatory response in patients with anterior circulation LVO strokes. Patients receiving taVNS within 36-hrs of symptom onset showed statistically significantly faster reductions in inflammatory cytokines, most notably in IL-6, in the first days following the stroke onset after initiation of treatment. This PROBE pilot trial demonstrated that taVNS may aid in mitigating the inflammatory sequelae associated with AIS.

Previous AIS studies suggest that an increase as small as a 1 ng/mL of IL-6 within the first 24 h is associated with a 13% higher odds of poor 3 months functional outcome and 16% higher odds of mortality [25]. These findings suggest that even a small change in IL-6 may have an effect on outcomes. We anticipate that the IL-6 max difference of 5.67 pg/mL seen amongst treatment groups, which is above the max standard error (SE) daily range of 0.78 to 2.05, likely suggests a true treatment effect.

Although IL-10 is classically considered an anti-inflammatory cytokine, its temporal pattern in our cohort resembled that of IL-6. This is consistent with prior studies showing that the physiological inverse correlation between IL-6 and IL-10 is disrupted after AIS, and that both cytokines rise within shared post-injury pathways [26–30]. When we adjusted models for IL-10 or examined the IL-6:IL-10 ratio, often used as an integrated marker of inflammatory tone [26–30], we observed results similar to our IL-6 findings. This could be considered counterintuitive to the hypothesized mechanism of VNS effects on the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [31]. TaVNS produced reductions across multiple cytokines, including IL-10, suggesting that its effects may reflect a broader attenuation of post-stroke immune responsiveness rather than a selective shift in “pro-“ or “anti-“ inflammatory cytokines, nor selectively the IL-6:IL-10 ratio. These distinct timescales and magnitudes of response require further investigation from a mechanistic standpoint.

When we explored neurological outcomes, e.g., NIHSS changes or mRS 90-day, we saw no differences amongst treatment groups, although the study was not powered to detect these differences. Despite this, we confirmed that elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, i.e., IL-6, are associated with worsened functional outcomes, as previously reported in the literature [25, 32], and the magnitude of this effect may be dependent on reperfusion status. Furthermore, the taVNS treatment group had more proximal LVO, longer times from LKN to recanalization, and worse reperfusion scores than the sham group. Despite these baseline imbalances between groups, we still saw an effect on inflammatory biomarkers, highlighting the potential robustness of this intervention and suggesting that we may see signs of efficacy in larger trials.

Here we also redemonstrated that taVNS is safe, which is consistent with the literature [11, 13, 33]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 79 non-AIS taVNS studies, with over 1330 patients, found no differences in the risk of developing an adverse event between the active taVNS and control groups, with no reports of severe cardiac adverse events due to taVNS [13]. In addition, all stroke VNS trials to date, 8 chronic and 1 acute trial, found taVNS safe from a cardiac standpoint [34]. The taVNS stimulation frequency and intensity of our study were similar to, or considerably lower than that of other taVNS studies [34].

To our knowledge, this is the first AIS trial to demonstrate that taVNS is a rapidly deployable technology in this acute setting, and can safely reduce inflammatory biomarkers. To date, most immunomodulation VNS trials have been in chronic conditions such as RA [10]. It is worth noting that the mechanisms of action of VNS and its impact on inflammation and clinical outcomes may be pleiotropic. It is postulated that taVNS leads to immunomodulation through the cholinergic parasympathetic nervous system anti-inflammatory pathways that innervate the spleen [31]. This culminates in T cell release of acetylcholine, which interacts with acetylcholine receptors on immunocompetent cells to inhibit cytokine release in macrophages [31]. This is a likely mechanism explaining this study’s reduction in inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, it can reduce blood-brain barrier permeability, cortical spreading depressions, and improve regionalized plasticity [14, 34]. In aggregate, these pleiotropic effects may play a role in reducing inflammation, both systemically and locally, and should be explored further in future trials.

This trial has notable strengths, including its randomized, blinded outcome assessment design and comprehensive biomarker analysis. However, several important limitations warrant discussion. First, this pilot study had a small sample size. Although cytokine changes were consistent and statistically significant, replication in a larger cohort is warranted. Furthermore, the % Coefficient of Variation (CV) of 25% is lowest for IL-6 and its Lower Limit of Detection (LLD) does not limit our detection for IL-6, which gives us greatest confidence in this cytokine’s results [35]. Although the other cytokines showed directionality and trends consistent with IL-6 results, caution should be utilized when interpreting their results due to their higher %CV, in addition to the potential LLD-related “floor effects” [36, 37] for IL-1β and IL-17α. Future studies should utilize “higher sensitivity” assays to minimize these issues. Second, although we are confident that taVNS alters the IL-6 trajectory in AIS patients, we will not be able to assess the clinical significance until we perform a phase 3 efficacy trial. The current study provides pilot data to allow us to perform the larger pivotal trial to test efficacy and clinical significance. Third, fundamental questions remain about optimal therapeutic parameters, including stimulation settings, treatment duration, and the most relevant physiologic and biomarkers when utilizing taVNS. This is highlighted in our trial by the Day 5 rise in some cytokine levels, which may be a rebound effect of stopping treatment, although an additional explanation could be that stroke patients who were still in the hospital at that time tended to be sicker. Fourth, although elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines are associated with brain injury, circulating cytokine levels remain an indirect surrogate of tissue injury. Finally, the focus on LVO AIS limits the generalizability of these findings to other stroke subtypes. These limitations highlight critical areas for future investigation while providing a foundation for larger-scale studies of taVNS in acute ischemic stroke.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the NUVISTA trial demonstrates that taVNS safely modulates post-AIS inflammation in anterior circulation LVO. These findings support further investigation of taVNS as a potential adjunctive therapy through larger, multicenter trials.
