Regularity and Uniqueness for a Model of Active Particles with Angle-Averaged Diffusions
Luca C. B. Alasio, Simon M. Schulz

Abstract
. We study the regularity and uniqueness of weak solutions of a degenerate parabolic equation, arising as the limit of a stochastic lattice model of self-propelled particles. The angle-average of the solution appears as a coefficient in the diffusive and drift terms, making the equation nonlocal. We prove that, under unrestrictive non-degeneracy assumptions on the initial data, weak solutions are smooth for positive times. Our method rests on deriving a drift-diffusion equation for a particular function of the angle-averaged density and applying De Giorgi’s method to show that the original equation is uniformly parabolic for positive times. We employ a Galerkin approximation to justify rigorously the passage from divergence to non-divergence form of the equation, which yields improved estimates by exploiting a cancellation. By imposing stronger constraints on the initial data, we prove the uniqueness of the weak solution, which relies on Duhamel’s principle and gradient estimates for the periodic heat kernel to derive L ∞ L^{\infty} estimates for the angle-averaged density.

1.
Introduction

This work is devoted to the regularity and uniqueness theory for the weak solutions
f(t,x,\theta)
of the nonlocal degenerate parabolic equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+\dv\!\big((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\big)=\dv\!\big((1-\rho)\nabla f+f\nabla\rho\big)+\partial_{\theta}^{2}f,

where
{\bf e}(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
, and the
angle-averaged density

\rho
is defined by

(1.2)

\rho(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!f(t,x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

The symbols
\dv
and
\nabla
in (
1.1
) represent divergence and gradient with respect to the
x
variable only. This equation is supplemented with periodic boundary conditions both in the space variable
x\in\Omega=(0,2\pi)^{2}
and the angle variable
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
. In the sequel, we denote the space-angle coordinate by
\xi=(x,\theta)
, which belongs to
\Upsilon=\Omega\times(0,2\pi)=(0,2\pi)^{3}
.

Background.
Nonlocal equations of the form (
1.1
) arise naturally in the physical, biological, and social sciences. They are conventionally used to model the evolution of a system of active (
i.e.
self-propelled) particles with repulsive interactions and diffusion in position and orientation. This may be, for instance, in the context of the modelling of bacterial suspensions
[
7
]
, colloids
[
15
]
, collective movement in robotics and in animal species
[
37
]
, pedestrian and traffic flows
[
17
]
, or in condensed matter physics (see also
[
16
,
34
,
39
]
and the references therein). For phase separation in active systems we refer to
[
33
,
36
]
. The work in
[
19
]
provides a discussion on methods and models for the dynamics of aligning self-propelled rigid bodies, and
[
23
]
studies a related Fokker–Planck system with confinement and boundary terms.

Equation (
1.1
) was formally derived from a stochastic lattice model of interacting particles in
[
11
]
; see also the related work
[
12
]
, where a rigorous derivation of a similar equation is performed via a hydrodynamic limit. We also refer the reader to
[
9
]
for new directions in this topic. The precise structure of the terms in (
1.1
) depends on the scaling with which the number of particles tends to infinity in proportion to how the radius of the repulsive interactions vanishes; this scaling dictates the nature of the degeneracy in the diffusion. From the point of view of applications, a thorough understanding of the regularity and uniqueness properties of the equation is necessary when developing efficient numerical schemes for simulation. Indeed, without knowledge of uniqueness prior to the design of the computational method, the numerical algorithm may “jump” between solutions without ever converging. This motivates the need for the present paper, in addition to answering noteworthy theoretical questions.

Literature review.

The modern study of the regularity of weak solutions of parabolic equations builds on the fundamental work of De Giorgi
[
18
]
, Nash
[
32
]
, and Moser
[
31
]
. While the literature on uniformly parabolic equations is rich, the same cannot be said for degenerate or nonlocal evolution equations, which often pose new challenges.
The Cauchy problem for a large class of degenerate parabolic systems has been studied by Amann in the 1990s, see
e.g.

[
4
]
, and recently revisited in
[
24
]
.
New regularity properties for degenerate systems of Keller-Segel-type have been obtained in the last few years, see
e.g.

[
20
,
28
,
38
]
. We refer to
[
8
]
for several remarkable results on regularity, the Harnack inequality and applications to the fast diffusion equation. Further advances in terms of regularity of weak solutions of cross-diffusion systems have been shown in
[
26
,
2
]
. For nonlocal equations similar to (
1.1
), we highlight the previous work of the authors
[
3
]
, in which they obtained regularity and uniqueness results for weak and very weak solutions of a simpler equation, which incorporated the same advection term as (
1.1
) and a local non-degenerate diffusion. We highlight that there exists, by now, a well-established literature on the existence theory for nonlocal degenerate equations by means of,
e.g.
, gradient flow methods (
cf.
[
5
,
35
]
). One of the aims of the present work is to add emphasis to the regularity theory for these equations, which goes beyond existence results in measure-theoretic settings.

Novelty.
A global existence theory for (
1.1
) was produced in
[
14
]
by the second author
et al.
, using a generalisation of the
boundedness-by-entropy method
; originally established in
[
13
]
for a related cross-diffusion system. The underlying principle in this approach is to interpret (
1.1
) as a perturbation of a Wasserstein-type gradient flow associated to the entropy functional

\mathcal{E}[f]=\int_{\Upsilon}\Big(f\log f+(1-\rho)\log(1-\rho)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

and to rewrite the equation in terms of the first variation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}[f]=\log f-\log(1-\rho).
Using this formulation of the problem, the authors of
[
14
]
showed (
cf.

[
14
, Theorem 1]
) the existence of a nonnegative weak solution
f
, in the sense dual to
L^{6}_{t}W^{1,6}_{x,\theta}
, satisfying also
0\leq\rho\leq 1
. The authors also showed the uniqueness of this solution (
cf.

[
14
, Theorem 2]
) in the special case where the
nonlocal drift
term
\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))
is omitted from (
1.1
); in this case, the equation is an exact gradient flow, and uniqueness is expected (
cf.

[
5
,
35
]
). A weak-strong uniqueness result near constant stationary states (
cf.

[
14
, Theorem 4]
) was also obtained. The regularity properties of the weak solutions obtained from the boundedness-by-entropy method have not been studied since, primarily because of the intricacies encountered due to the presence of the
nonlocal diffusion

\dv((1-\rho)\nabla f)
and the
infinite cross-diffusion

\dv(f\nabla\rho)
. Indeed, this former term causes a loss of strict parabolicity on the locus
\{\rho=1\}
, while the latter term encodes competing diffusive effects from all of the uncountably many angles
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
, all-in-all giving rise to a panoply of degeneracies. The purpose of the present manuscript is to provide a first thorough regularity analysis for (
1.1
), so as to address the aforementioned gap in the theory. We then employ our regularity result to prove the uniqueness of weak solutions of (
1.1
) without restriction on the nonlocal drift.

Approach.
In the context of our problem, the regularising effect of the equation (
1.1
) is seen first and foremost on angle-averaged quantities. Indeed, by integrating (
1.1
) with respect to the variable
\theta
, we recover the uniformly parabolic evolution equation for
\rho
,

(1.3)

\partial_{t}\rho+\dv((1-\rho){\bf p})=\Delta\rho,

where the vector field
{\bf p}
, called the
polarisation
, is defined by

(1.4)

{\bf p}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!f(t,x,\theta)\,{\bf e}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Our first main observation is that, under rather minimal non-degeneracy assumptions on the initial data (
cf.
Definition
3.1
), one can derive a uniform lower bound on
1\!-\!\rho
for positive times from (
1.3
),
cf.
Proposition
5.2
. This then implies the strong parabolicity of (
1.1
), and makes it possible to obtain
H^{1}
estimates on
f
. These
H^{1}
estimates transfer to
{\bf p}
by (
1.4
), whence bootstrapping into (
1.3
) yields the
H^{2}
regularity of
\rho
. By interpolating, this boundedness then yields sufficient control on
\rho
and its derivatives to perform a De Giorgi type iteration on (
1.1
) and obtain an
L^{\infty}
estimate on
f
for positive times. The second main observation is concerned with the space-diffusions in (
1.1
). Provided the passage from divergence form to non-divergence form is justified, a cancellation occurs in terms of the form
\nabla f\!\cdot\!\nabla\rho
, and (
1.1
) may be rewritten

(1.5)

\partial_{t}f+\dv\!\big((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\big)=(1-\rho)\Delta f+f\Delta\rho+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f.

The passage from equation (
1.1
) to its non-divergence form analogue (
1.5
) is rigorously justified by means of a Galerkin approximation,
cf.
§
6.2
and the proof of Lemma
7.2
. We remark that other approaches, such as by difference quotients or mollification, destroy the aforementioned cancellation of the terms
\nabla f\cdot\nabla\rho
. Testing (
1.5
) with
\Delta f
, and then subsequently with
\partial^{2}_{\theta}f
, yields the
H^{2}
regularity of
f
. By bootstrapping and applying time derivatives in (
1.1
), we are then able to deduce the smoothness of
f
for positive times. Once the regularity result is established, we use it to show that the weak solution of (
1.1
) is unique, under additional assumptions on the initial data. First, we show uniqueness on a small time interval of positive length using Duhamel’s principle and gradient estimates for the heat equation. More precisely, we obtain an estimate of the form
\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)}\leq\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times\Upsilon)}
for any two weak solutions
f_{1},f_{2}
and
t
sufficiently small, which yields uniqueness by the usual
H^{1}
estimate on the equation for
f_{1}-f_{2}
. We then extend this result to all time intervals using the smoothness of
f_{1},f_{2}
for all positive times.

Structure of the paper.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In §
2
, we introduce the notations and some technical lemmas used throughout the paper, and provide our notion of weak solution of (
1.1
). We also recall the main theorem of
[
14
]
, which provides the existence of the weak solutions. In §
3
, we state our two main theorems, and provide a concise summary of our strategy of proof. §
4
is concerned with certain estimates that hold up to the initial time
t=0
, and which are then used to prove the strong parabolicity of (
1.1
) for positive times, in §
5
. In §
6
, we obtained improved boundedness estimates for positive times, by means of a Galerkin approximation which allows us to rigorously pass from the divergence form (
1.1
) to the non-divergence form (
1.5
) and obtain
H^{2}
estimates by exploiting a cancellation. In §
7
, we bootstrap our previous observations to show smoothness of the weak solutions for positive times, and thus we prove our first main theorem. Finally, in §
8
, we prove our second main theorem, concerned with the uniqueness of weak solutions. Appendix
A
contains the proofs of various technical lemmas.

Notations

. For

, we let

Let

The topological dual of a function space

2.2.
Notion of solution

We recall the notion of weak solution of (
1.1
) introduced in
[
14
]
, and explain under which conditions the existence of weak solutions was proved.

Definition 2.1

(Function Space
\mathcal{X}
)
.

We define the function space
\mathcal{X}
to be the family of functions
f\in L^{3}(0,T;L^{3}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon))
which satisfy, with
\rho:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(i)

f\geq 0
a.e. in
\Upsilon_{T}

(ii)

\partial_{\theta}\sqrt{f}\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})

\partial_{t}f\in(L^{6}(0,T;W^{1,6}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon))^{\prime}

(iii)

a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
,
\partial_{t}\rho\in(L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime}

\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\sqrt{f}\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})

Note that the previous definition implies that any

(2.1)

\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla f,\partial_{\theta}f\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Upsilon_{T}).

We now state the definition of weak solution. A weak solution must satisfy two weak formulations: the first is tailored to test functions that are periodic with respect to the space-angle variable (
cf.

[
14
, Definition 1.1]
), and the second allows for non-periodic test functions (

[
14
, Lemma 4.2]

[
3
, §2]
).

Definition 2.2

(Weak Solution)
.

We say that
f\in\mathcal{X}
is a

of (

) if, for all
\varphi\in L^{6}(0,T;W^{1,6}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon))\cap\{\partial_{t}\varphi\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Upsilon_{T})\}
and for a.e.

, there holds

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\int_{\Upsilon}f

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Upsilon}(1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\int_{\Upsilon}\!\Big(\big((1-\rho)\nabla f\!+\!f\nabla\rho\big)\!\cdot\!\nabla\varphi\!+\!\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\varphi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t\!+\!\int_{\Upsilon}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|_{t_{2}}\!\!-\!\!\int_{\Upsilon}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|_{t_{1}},

and, for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})

\psi(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all

and for a.e.
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\partial_{t}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\!\Big(\big((1-\rho)\nabla f\!+\!f\nabla\rho\big)\!\cdot\!\nabla\psi\!+\!\partial_{\theta}f\partial_{\theta}\psi\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t\!+\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|_{t_{2}}\!\!-\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi\Big|_{t_{1}}.

The global existence of weak solutions, given admissible initial data, was proved in

Technical results used in the paper

Throughout the manuscript, we repeatedly make use of the following technical results: the first is a well-known interpolation lemma for spaces depending on time, and the second is a Calderón–Zygmund Theorem for periodic functions.

(§1 Proposition 3.2,

]
)
.

Let

The next result is a periodic Calderón–Zygmund inequality.

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 1.4,
[
3
]
)
.

Let

such that for all

3.
Main Results

We state our main results, and then provide a summary of our strategy of proof.

3.1.
Statements of Main Results

Our first main theorem is about the smoothness of weak solutions of (
1.1
) under non-degeneracy assumptions on the initial data.

Definition 3.1

(Regular initial data)
.

We say that
f_{0}
is a
regular initial datum
if:

(i)

f_{0}
is nonnegative a.e. and
f_{0}\in L^{q}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
for some
q>1
;

(ii)

there exists a nonnegative function
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfying the assumptions

(3.1)

\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}h(s)=\infty,\quad h^{\prime}<0<h^{\prime\prime}\quad\text{on }(0,1],\qquad\frac{sh^{\prime\prime}(s)}{h^{\prime}(s)}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

and the quantity
\rho_{0}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
satisfies the condition
0\leq\rho_{0}<1
a.e. in
\Omega
and

(3.2)

h(1\!-\!\rho_{0})\in L^{2}(\Omega).

For convenience, we highlight that a prototypical choice of
h
is,
e.g.
,
h(s)=s^{-q}
(
q>0
). The next remark shows that Definition
3.1
permits very general initial data, including those concentrating the solution of (
1.1
) near—but not in—the degenerate zone
\{\rho=1\}
.

Remark 3.2

(Definition
3.1
is unrestrictive)
.

Let
q>1
and
f_{0}\in L^{q}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)
be a nonnegative initial datum. Suppose that, for any
x_{*}\in\Omega
and any
m>0
,
f_{0}
is such that

1-\rho_{0}(x)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\frac{1}{|x-x_{*}|^{m}}})\quad\text{in a neighbourhood }\mathcal{N}\text{ of }x_{*},\qquad\esssup_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}}\rho_{0}<1.

Then,
f_{0}
is a regular initial datum, as the choice
h(s)=\log(-\log(s))
for
0<s\leq e^{-2}
, with appropriate extension for
s>e^{-2}
, satisfies assumptions (
3.1
)–(
3.2
). This argument can be repeated for any finite number of such points
x_{*}
. Similarly, letting
\exp^{-n}:=e^{-(\cdot)^{-1}}\circ\dots\circ e^{-(\cdot)^{-1}}
,
\log^{n}:=\log\circ\dots\circ\log
be the
n
-th iterates (
n\in\mathbb{N}
), if
f_{0}
is such that

1-\rho_{0}(x)=\mathcal{O}(\exp^{-n}(e^{-\frac{1}{|x-x_{*}|^{m}}}))\quad\text{in a neighbourhood }\mathcal{N}\text{ of }x_{*},\qquad\esssup_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{N}}\rho_{0}<1,

then, select a suitable extension of
h(s)=\log^{n}(\log(-\log(s)))
, and
f_{0}
is regular.

Remark 3.3

(Consequence of condition (
3.1
))
.

The conditions (
3.1
) on the function
h
imply the estimate
(sh^{\prime}(s))^{\prime}-h^{\prime}(s)=sh^{\prime\prime}(s)>0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Integrating from a fixed
s_{*}>0
implies
sh^{\prime}(s)-s_{*}h^{\prime}(s_{*})\geq h(s)-h(s_{*})
, whence, since
h^{\prime}<0\leq h
, there exists
M\geq 0
such that

(3.3)

s|h^{\prime}(s)|\leq h(s)+M\quad\text{for all }s\in(0,1].

Because of the opposite signs of
h^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime\prime}
, the condition (
3.1
) may be interpreted as a McCann convexity “limited growth” condition (
cf.
the growth condition in
[
30
, §3]
).

We are ready to state our main theorems, starting with our main regularity result.

Theorem 1

(Smoothness for positive times)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.2
, with regular initial datum
f_{0}
in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Then, there holds
f\in C^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

In order to show the above, we prove that, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, there holds
f\in C^{\infty}((t,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Similarly, estimates on the solution will be performed on time intervals
(t,T)
for
t\in(0,T)
.

Our second main result is the uniqueness of weak solutions, for which we require additional assumptions on the initial data. This result is proved in two stages: first we show uniqueness on a small time interval, and then use Theorem
1
to extend to all of
[0,T]
.

Theorem 2

(Uniqueness for stronger initial data)
.

Let
f_{0}\in{L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon)}
be a regular initial datum, in the sense of Definition
3.1
, satisfying the additional requirements
:

(3.4)

\esssup_{\Omega}\rho_{0}<1,\qquad\rho_{0}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega).

Then, the weak solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
f_{0}
is unique.

This theorem complements
[
14
, Theorems 2 and 4]
. A more general uniqueness theory with less restrictive assumptions on the initial data, in particular the condition on
\esssup_{\Omega}\rho_{0}
, is the subject of ongoing investigation.

3.2.
Summary of the Strategy

Our approach is as follows.

(i)

In §
4
, we prove several estimates up to the initial time
t=0
: in particular,
\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla{\bf p}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
.

(ii)

In §
5
, we prove a lower bound on
1\!-\!\rho
for positive times by considering the equation for
h(1\!-\!\rho)
, where
h
is given by Definition
3.1
, and applying De Giorgi’s method (see §
5.2
). This lower bound implies that (
1.1
) is uniformly parabolic for positive times, and
{\bf p}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{t,T})
a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Assuming stronger initial data (
cf.
assumptions of Theorem
2
), this lower bound holds up to the initial time
t=0
(see §
5.3
); we remark that it is obtained by a different iteration to that used in §
5.2
. We emphasise that we only require the lower bound to hold for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
for the results of §
6
–
7
to hold; the lower bound up to the initial time
t=0
is only used for the uniqueness result in §
8
.

(iii)

The goal of §
6
is threefold: we obtain
L^{4}_{t}W^{2,4}_{x}
estimates for
\rho
, perform the
H^{1}
-estimate for
f
using a mollification argument, and obtain
f\in L^{\infty}
by means of De Giorgi type iterations; we do this in several steps. Firstly, using the
H^{1}
-regularity of
{\bf p}
, we perform the
H^{2}
-estimate for
\rho
, yielding
\nabla\rho\in L^{4}
by interpolation. This is enough to then perform the
H^{2}
-estimate for
{\bf p}
, by exploiting a cancellation only visible in the non-divergence form of the equation (see §
6.2
). In order to do this, we rely on an application of the parabolic Gehring’s Lemma to the equation (
4.2
) for
{\bf p}
, followed by a Galerkin approximation; interestingly, this Galerkin method is only used to show regularity, not existence. This upgrades the regularity of
\nabla{\bf p}
to
L^{4}
, from which we get
\partial_{t}\rho,\nabla^{2}\rho\in L^{4}
. We deduce
f\in L^{2}_{t}H^{1}_{x,\theta}
by a mollification argument, and
f\in L^{\infty}
for positive times using De Giorgi’s iterations.

(iv)

In §
7
, we prove Theorem
1
by bootstrapping our previous observations. We proceed by taking repeated time derivatives in (
1.1
) and following the previous sequence of steps, relying again on the passage from divergence to non-divergence form of (
1.1
) by means of Galerkin’s method to obtain
H^{2}
-estimates of
f
and its time derivatives. Details of the inductive proof for the bootstrap are contained in Appendix
A.1
.

(v)

§
8
contains the proof of Theorem
2
. We consider the difference of two solutions
\bar{f}=f_{1}-f_{2}
and
\bar{\rho}=\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
. Using gradient estimates for the periodic heat kernel and Duhamel’s principle, we derive the bound
\|\bar{\rho}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{t})}\leq\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t})}
for small times
t\in[0,t_{*}]
. Then, using the additional assumptions on the initial data, we prove
\bar{f}=0
in the interval
[0,t_{*}]
by performing the usual
H^{1}
estimate for
\bar{f}
. Finally, we extend the uniqueness to
[0,T]
using Theorem
1
.

Throughout §
4
–§
7
,
f
will always denote a weak solution of (
1.1
), in the sense of Definition
2.2
, with
\rho
and
{\bf p}
as prescribed by (
1.2
)–(
1.4
).

4.
Preliminary Estimates up to
t=0

The purpose of this section is to state several results which hold up to the initial time
t=0
; indeed, many of the estimates which we shall obtain later, especially for higher order derivatives, only hold for positive times. However, in order to successfully prove the uniqueness result of Theorem
2
, we require certain estimates to hold up to the initial time
t=0
. The results in this section do not require the initial data to satisfy Definition
3.1
.

In what follows, we make use of the boundedness of
{\bf p}
in order to deduce higher integrability of
\nabla\rho
via the equation (
1.3
). To this end, by multiplying (
1.1
) against
{\bf e}(\theta)
and integrating with respect to the angle variable, we see that, by defining the
2\times 2
matrix
{\bf P}

(4.1)

{\bf P}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,x,\theta)\,{\bf e}(\theta)\!\otimes\!{\bf e}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

the polarisation
{\bf p}
, defined in (
1.4
), satisfies in the distributional sense the equation

(4.2)

\partial_{t}{\bf p}+\dv((1-\rho){\bf P})=\dv((1-\rho)\nabla{\bf p}+{\bf p}\otimes\nabla\rho)-{\bf p}

i.e.
, in coordinate form with summation convention, with
{\bf p}=(p_{i})_{i}
and
{\bf P}=(P_{ij})_{ij}
,

(4.3)

\partial_{t}p_{i}+\partial_{j}((1-\rho)P_{ij})=\partial_{j}((1-\rho)\partial_{j}p_{i}+p_{i}\partial_{j}\rho)-p_{i}.

Note the uniform boundedness:
|{\bf p}(t,x)|\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\rho\leq 1
,
|{\bf P}(t,x)|\leq 4\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta\leq 4
:

(4.4)

{\bf p},{\bf P}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}).

Remark 4.1

(Weak formulation of polarisation equation)
.

All terms in (
4.2
) are well-defined since
(1-\rho)\nabla{\bf p}\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{T})
from (
2.1
) and
{\bf p}\otimes\nabla\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
from Definition
2.1
. By the previous bounds, (
4.2
) holds in duality to
L^{3}(0,T;(W^{1,3}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime})
,
\partial_{t}{\bf p}\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(0,T;(W^{1,3}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime})
.

The boundedness (
4.4
) yields the following
H^{1}
-type estimate for
{\bf p}
.

Lemma 4.2

(
H^{1}
-type estimate for
{\bf p}
)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
), with
\rho
and
{\bf p}
as per (
1.2
)–(
1.4
). Then, if for some
t\geq 0
it holds
\essinf_{\Omega_{t,T}}(1-\rho)>0
, then there holds
\nabla{\bf p}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})
,
\partial_{t}{\bf p}\in L^{2}(t,T;(H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The underlying idea is to test the equation (
4.2
) with
{\bf p}
itself. Formally,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\!\int_{\Omega}\!|{\bf p}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\!(1\!-\!\rho)|\nabla{\bf p}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\!\leq\!|\Omega|\!+\!\|{\bf P}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\!\int_{\Omega}\!|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\!-\!\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|{\bf p}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

and integrating in time yields the sought estimate. However, this argument is not rigorous since
a priori
we do not know that
{\bf p}
is an admissible test function. To overcome this, we test the equation with a smoothed version of
{\bf p}
and conclude with a limiting argument.

1.
Mollification
: Let
\eta
be the usual Friedrichs bump function, and
\{\eta^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
the usual sequence of Friedrichs mollifiers in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; we only mollify with respect to
x
. In what follows, we use the notations
{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}=\eta^{\varepsilon}*{\bf p}
,
D=(1-\rho){\bf P}
, and
D^{\varepsilon}=\eta^{\varepsilon}*D
. Since
\eta^{\varepsilon}
is an admissible test function to insert into the weak formulation of (
4.2
), we mollify the equation:

(4.5)

\partial_{t}{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}+\dv D^{\varepsilon}=\dv((1-\rho)\nabla{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}+{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\rho)-{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}+\dv(E^{\varepsilon}+F^{\varepsilon}),

where the error terms
E^{\varepsilon}
and
F^{\varepsilon}
are given by

(4.6)

E^{\varepsilon}=\eta^{\varepsilon}*((1-\rho)\nabla{\bf p})-(1-\rho)\eta^{\varepsilon}*\nabla{\bf p},\qquad F^{\varepsilon}=\eta^{\varepsilon}*({\bf p}\otimes\nabla\rho)-(\eta^{\varepsilon}*{\bf p})\otimes\nabla\rho.

2.
Estimates on drift term and error terms
: We estimate the error terms
D^{\varepsilon},E^{\varepsilon},F^{\varepsilon}
. Our aim is to show the following: there exists a positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

(4.7)

\|D^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}+\|E^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\|F^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C.

Once the above is established, we will be able to proceed as per the formal argument presented at the start of the proof. First,
\|D^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|D(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|{\bf P}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C,
for some positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
, where we used standard properties of the mollifier to establish the first inequality. It follows from taking the essential supremum in the time variable that, for some positive constant independent of
\varepsilon
,

(4.8)

\|D^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C.

Next, the error term
F^{\varepsilon}
is controlled using elementary properties of the mollifier, as follows:

\|\eta^{\varepsilon}*({\bf p}\otimes\nabla\rho)(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|({\bf p}\otimes\nabla\rho)(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|{\bf p}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, for some positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
, and similarly

\|(\eta^{\varepsilon}*{\bf p})\otimes\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\!\leq\!\|(\eta^{\varepsilon}*{\bf p})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\!\leq\!C\|{\bf p}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

whence, by integrating in time, there exists a positive
C
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

(4.9)

\|F^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C.

For
E^{\varepsilon}
, write
E^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\eta^{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big[\rho(t,x)-\rho(t,y)\big]\nabla{\bf p}(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Integrating by parts,

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\!\!\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\!{\bf p}(t,y)\otimes\nabla\eta^{\varepsilon}(x-y)\big[\rho(t,x)-\rho(t,y)\big]\,\mathrm{d}y}_{=:E^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,x)}+\!\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\!\eta^{\varepsilon}(x-y){\bf p}(t,y)\otimes\nabla\rho(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}y}_{=:E^{\varepsilon}_{2}(t,x)}.

Using standard properties of mollifiers,
\|E^{\varepsilon}_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\bf p}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})},
hence

(4.10)

\|E^{\varepsilon}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\|{\bf p}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})},

for some positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
. Meanwhile, note that, for a.e.
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)-\rho(t,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\sigma}\rho(t,\sigma x+(1-\sigma)y)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\rho(t,\sigma x+(1-\sigma)y)\!\cdot\!(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

and since
\supp\eta^{\varepsilon}=B(0,\varepsilon)
, it follows that, for all
y\in\supp\eta^{\varepsilon}(x-\cdot)
, there holds

(4.11)

\displaystyle|\rho(t,x)-\rho(t,y)|\leq\varepsilon\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla\rho(t,\sigma x+(1-\sigma)y)|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

In turn, using the uniform boundedness of
{\bf p}
, there holds

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\int_{B(x,\varepsilon)}\big|\nabla\eta(\frac{x-y}{\varepsilon})\big|\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla\rho(t,\sigma x+(1-\sigma)y)|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{B(0,1)}|\nabla\eta(w)|\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla\rho\big(t,x-(1-\sigma)\varepsilon w\big)|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}w,

and, by Jensen’s inequality, we get
|E^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,x)|^{2}\leq C\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla\rho\big(t,x-(1-\sigma)\varepsilon w\big)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}w
, for some positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
. An application of the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem then implies, using periodicity of
\nabla\rho
and translation invariance of Lebesgue measure,

\|E^{\varepsilon}_{1}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\int_{B(0,1)}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho(t,x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}w\leq C\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By integrating in time, and combining with (
4.10
), we deduce
\|E^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C
, independently of
\varepsilon
. Combining this bound with (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), we obtain (
4.7
).

3.
H^{1}
-type estimate
: Note that
{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
, whence, in view of Remark
4.1
,
{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}
is an admissible test function to insert in the weak formulation of (
4.2
). Testing the equation with
{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}
, using the uniform bounds (
4.7
) and Young’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\essinf_{\Omega_{t,T}}(1-\rho)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{\essinf_{\Omega_{t,T}}(1-\rho)}+\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho(t,\cdot)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

with
C
independent of
\varepsilon
. Integrating in time, as
\|{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|{\bf p}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C
,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\nabla{\bf p}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}).

We take the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and the result follows from the lower semicontinuity of the norms.

4.
Boundedness of time derivative
: We obtain
\partial_{t}{\bf p}\in L^{2}(t,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime})
directly from the equation, using the boundedness
\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla{\bf p}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
and
\nabla\rho\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
.
∎

More generally, we see that the
n
-th order tensor
\pi^{n}
, defined by

(4.13)

\pi^{n}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,x,\theta)\,\underbrace{{\bf e}(\theta)\!\otimes\!\dots\!\otimes\!{\bf e}(\theta)}_{n\text{ times}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

satisfies the equation

(4.14)

\partial_{t}\pi^{n}+\dv((1-\rho)\pi^{n+1})=\dv((1-\rho)\nabla\pi^{n}+\pi^{n}\otimes\nabla\rho)+\tilde{\pi}^{n}

in the sense of distributions, where

\tilde{\pi}^{n}:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(t,x,\theta)\,\partial^{2}_{\theta}(\underbrace{{\bf e}(\theta)\!\otimes\!\dots\!\otimes\!{\bf e}(\theta)}_{n\text{ times}})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

As per (
4.4
), we get
|\pi^{n}(t,x)|,|\tilde{\pi}^{n}(t,x)|\leq n^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\theta\leq n^{2}
, from which it follows

(4.15)

\pi^{n},\tilde{\pi}^{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

As per Lemma
4.2
, this boundedness yields a
H^{1}
-type estimate for all tensors
\pi^{n}
.

Lemma 4.3

(
H^{1}
-type estimate for
\pi^{n}
)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
),
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\pi^{n}
be as per (
4.13
). Then, if for some
t\geq 0
it holds
\essinf_{\Omega_{t,T}}(1-\rho)>0
, then there holds
\nabla\pi^{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})
and
\partial_{t}\pi^{n}\in L^{2}(t,T;(H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We test the equation (
4.14
) against
\pi^{n}
, which can be made rigorous by simple adaptations of the proof of Lemma
4.2
; we omit the details for succinctness. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\pi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho)|\nabla\pi^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho)\nabla\pi^{n}:\pi^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\pi^{n}:(\pi^{n}\otimes\nabla\rho)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\pi^{n}:\tilde{\pi}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x,

whence, using the boundedness (
4.15
) and Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\pi^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\pi^{n}

\displaystyle\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi^{n}(0,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+4(n+1)^{4}|\Omega_{T}|+2\|\nabla\sqrt{1-\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}.

We then deduce the boundedness of
\partial_{t}\pi^{n}
directly from the equation (
4.14
).
∎

5.
Strong Parabolicity

The purpose of this section is to obtain lower bounds on the quantity
1\!-\!\rho
. We will then obtain
H^{1}
-type bounds on
f
by performing the classical parabolic estimate on (
1.1
).

5.1.
Initial upgrade of integrability

We first write down a lemma which upgrades the integrability of
\partial_{t}\rho,\nabla^{2}\rho
. This first result does not require the initial data to satisfy Definition
3.1
, merely that
f\in\mathcal{X}
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) obtained from Theorem
0
. The gain of integrability from this result will then be used in §
5.2
to obtain a uniform lower bound on
1-\rho
for positive times, provided the initial data satisfies Definition
3.1
.

Lemma 5.1

(Initial upgrade of integrability)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
), with
\rho
as per (
1.2
). For a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, there holds
\partial_{t}\rho,\nabla^{2}\rho\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{t,T})
and
\nabla\rho\in L^{3}(\Omega_{t,T}).

Proof.

We have
\dv((1-\rho){\bf p})=-{\nabla\rho\cdot{\bf p}}+(1-\rho)\dv{\bf p}
. Observe that
\nabla\rho\cdot{\bf p}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
, while, using Definition
2.1
,
\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla_{\xi}\sqrt{f}\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})
, and
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}|(1-\rho)\dv{\bf p}|^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{T}}(1-\rho)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigg|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\nabla f\cdot{\bf e}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\bigg|^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{\Upsilon_{T}}\bigg[\sqrt{1-\rho}\frac{|\nabla f|}{\sqrt{f}}\bigg]^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{f}^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\,4^{\frac{3}{4}}\|\sqrt{f}\|_{L^{6}(\Upsilon_{T})}^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\sqrt{1-\rho}\nabla\sqrt{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T})}^{\frac{3}{2}},

where, to obtain the final line, we used Hölder’s inequality with the exponent
4/3
. It therefore follows that
\dv((1-\rho){\bf p})\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{T})
, whence the equation (
1.3
) for the angle-averaged density
\rho
implies the inclusion
\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{T})
. An application of the standard Calderón–Zygmund estimate for the heat equation (
e.g.

[
27
, Theorem 3.1]
) yields the local estimates

\partial_{t}\rho,\nabla^{2}\rho\in L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{t,T})\quad\text{for a.e.~}t\in(0,T);

we emphasise that we need only restrict to a subdomain with respect to the time variable due to the periodicity in space-angle (
cf.

e.g.
the argument in
[
3
, §3.1.3]
). Additionally, an application of the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality on the bounded domain
\Omega
yields

\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C\Big(\|\nabla^{2}\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big)\quad\text{a.e.~}t\in(0,T),

i.e.
,
\|\nabla\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{3}\leq C\big(1+\|\nabla^{2}\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{3}{2}}\big)
, and thus, by integrating in time,

\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{3}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq C\big(T+\|\nabla^{2}\rho\|_{L^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega_{t,T})}^{\frac{3}{2}}\big)^{\frac{1}{3}},

and the result follows.
∎

5.2.
Strong Parabolicity for Positive Times

Our goal in this subsection is to obtain a
positive
uniform lower bound on the quantity

u=1-\rho,

which satisfies
0\leq u\leq 1
a.e.,
\nabla u\in L^{2}(\Omega_{T})
,
\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}})^{\prime}(\Omega))
, and

(5.1)

\partial_{t}u-\dv({\bf p}u)=\Delta u.

For the remainder of this section and throughout §
6
–§
8
, we assume that the initial data satisfies the assumptions of Definition
3.1
. The main result of this subsection is as follows.

Proposition 5.2

(Lower bound on
1\!-\!\rho
)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) with regular initial data satisfying Definition
3.1
. Then, there exists
c:(0,T)\to(0,\infty)
such that

\essinf_{(t,T)\times\Omega}(1-\rho)\geq c(t)\quad\text{for a.e.~}t\in(0,T).

Our strategy is as follows. We study the evolution of the quantity

v:=h(u),

with
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfying the assumptions of Definition
3.1
. Formally,
v
is a solution of

\partial_{t}v-\dv\big({\bf p}uh^{\prime}(u)\big)+\frac{uh^{\prime\prime}(u)}{h^{\prime}(u)}{\bf p}\!\cdot\!\nabla v=\Delta v-\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(u)}{h^{\prime}(u)^{2}}{|\nabla v|^{2}},

as can be verified by formal computation using (

. In turn, using (
5.19
), by choosing,
e.g.
,

(5.27)

L_{t_{0}}=M^{\prime}+2C_{*}^{4}(\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})})^{2},

we see that (
5.26
) is satisfied. Using the Monotone Convergence Theorem and (

),

\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\Upsilon}(w-1)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|w_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T_{n},T})}\leq C_{q}|\Omega|^{1-\frac{2}{q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{W}_{n}=0,

whence
0\leq w\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega_{t_{0},T}
. In other words, using (
5.14
), we have

0\leq v\leq L_{t_{0}}\qquad\text{a.e.~}\Upsilon_{t_{0},T},

where
L_{t_{0}}=L_{t_{0}}(t_{0},M,\|sh^{\prime\prime}/h^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})},T,\Omega)
, as prescribed by (
5.27
).

5.
Conclusion
: It follows from Step 4 and the genericity of the Lebesgue point
t_{0}
that

(5.28)

\esssup_{\Omega_{t,T}}h(u)=\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq L_{t}\quad\text{for a.e.~}t\in(0,T),

where, as per
[
3
, §3]
, the periodicity of
v
means that we need not localise the estimate in space, but only in time. Since
h
is strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
, its inverse
h^{-1}
is well-defined and also strictly decreasing on
[1,\infty)
, whence

u(s,x)=h^{-1}(v(s,x))\geq h^{-1}(L_{t})>0\qquad\text{a.e.~}(s,x)\in\Omega_{t,T},

the strict positivity follows from the assumption
\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}h(s)=\infty
. Letting
c(t):=h^{-1}(L_{t})
and taking the
\essinf
, we obtain the result.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Uniform lower bound on

up to

for stronger initial data)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) with regular initial data satisfying Definition

and the additional assumption
\esssup_{\Omega}\rho_{0}<1
, with
\rho
as per (
1.2
). Then, there holds
\esssup_{\Omega_{T}}\rho<1
.

Proof.

The assumption on the initial data implies that
\|v_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty
. We have already shown that
v
is a weak subsolution of the equation (
5.3
). Our strategy is to test this equation with
v^{n-1}
for
n\geq 2
; employing this quantity as a test function is justified by the strategy of proof of Lemma
5.3
,
i.e.
, considering the test function
v_{\varepsilon}^{n-1}
, where
v_{\varepsilon}=h(u+\varepsilon)
.

By testing the equation (
5.13
) for
v_{\varepsilon}
with
v_{\varepsilon}^{n-1}
(
n\geq 2
), we obtain

(5.29)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+2(1-\frac{1}{n})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle-(n\!-\!1)\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!uh^{\prime}(u+\varepsilon)v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})\!\cdot\!{\bf p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{uh^{\prime\prime}(u+\varepsilon)}{h^{\prime}(u+\varepsilon)}v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})\!\cdot\!{\bf p}\,\mathrm{d}x,

and we bound each of the integrals on the right-hand side as follows: for the first integral,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega}uh^{\prime}(u+\varepsilon)v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})\!\cdot\!{\bf p}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{u}{u+\varepsilon}|(u+\varepsilon)h^{\prime}(u+\varepsilon)|v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}(v_{\varepsilon}+M)v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}-1}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|\,\mathrm{d}x,

where we used the bound (
3.3
), and for the second integral, using
\|sh^{\prime\prime}/h^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq C
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\Omega}\!\!\frac{uh^{\prime\prime}(u\!+\!\varepsilon)}{h^{\prime}(u\!+\!\varepsilon)}v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})\!\cdot\!{\bf p}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\!\leq\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\frac{u}{u\!+\!\varepsilon}\Big|\frac{(u\!+\!\varepsilon)h^{\prime\prime}(u\!+\!\varepsilon)}{h^{\prime}(u\!+\!\varepsilon)}\Big|v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|\,\mathrm{d}x\!\leq\!C\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|\,\mathrm{d}x.

Moreover, since
h^{\prime}<0
and
2>\sup_{\Omega_{T}}(1-\rho+\varepsilon)
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there holds

h(2)\leq h\big(\sup_{\Omega_{T}}(1-\rho+\varepsilon)\big)=\inf_{\Omega_{T}}h(1-\rho+\varepsilon)=\inf_{\Omega_{T}}v_{\varepsilon}\implies v_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{h(2)},

from which we deduce, by returning to (
5.29
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\!\int_{\Omega}\!|v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\!+\!\int_{\Omega}\!|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\!\leq\!2\Big((n\!-\!1)(1\!+\!Mh(2)^{-1})\!+\!\|\frac{sh^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\Big)\!\int_{\Omega}\!v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}}|\nabla(v_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{n}{2}})|\,\mathrm{d}x.

In turn, by using Young’s inequality and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, there holds

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C_{0}n^{2}\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

for some positive constant
C_{0}
independent of
n
. We employ a similar strategy to the one used in
[
3
, Proof of Theorem 1.7]
. By the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality, a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{GN}\Big(\|\nabla v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\Big),

whence, using Young’s inequality,

\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{C_{0}n^{2}}\|\nabla v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2C_{GN}^{2}\Big(1+\frac{C^{2}_{GN}C_{0}n^{2}}{2}\Big)\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

as well as
\|\nabla v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq C(\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2})
. Hence,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|v^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|v^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}n^{4}\|v^{\frac{n}{2}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

where the positive constant

is independent of

. By setting
n_{k}:=2^{k}
and
A_{k}(t):=\|v^{n_{k}}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},
we obtain, with
C_{2}:=16C_{1}
, the recurrence inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}A_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}+A_{k}\leq C_{2}^{k}(A_{k-1})^{2}.

An application of the iterative lemma
[
3
, Lemma 3.5]
(
cf.

[
25
, Lemma 3.2]
), as per the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
, then immediately implies the global-in-time estimate

\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2^{k}}(\Omega)}\leq C(C_{2},\|v_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}})\qquad\text{for all }t,k,

and, by taking the
\esssup
in time and letting

, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 6.3

Using Lemma

Lemma 6.4

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\bf p}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\Delta{\bf p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq C_{t}\Big(\|\nabla{\bf p}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\Delta\rho\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}^{2}+4|\Omega_{T}|\Big).

\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta\rho=-\dv((1-\rho){\bf p})\in L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})

6.2.
Proof of Lemma
6.4

Our goal is to implement a Galerkin approximation in order to justify the formal estimate (
6.4
). Our reason for doing so is that attempts to obtain (

) by difference quotients or by mollification destroys the cancellation of the terms
\nabla{\bf p}\nabla\rho
when expanding the diffusion

. Our strategy is therefore to obtain the

estimate at the level of each Galerkin approximant and then pass to the limit. As each approximant is smooth with respect to

, the diffusion term may be rewritten in non-divergence form. For this strategy to succeed, we require an improvement on the integrability of
\nabla\rho

Lemma 6.5

(Gain of integrability for

.

Let

be a weak solution of (

Gehring’s Lemma for
\nabla{\bf p}
: The system of equations (

) for
{\bf p}

) for

and applying the classical Calderón–Zygmund estimate for the heat equation, we deduce

.
∎

For the purposes of what follows, we fix a Lebesgue point (common to

)

Furthermore, since

, integration by parts and (

) imply

\displaystyle\langle p(t_{0},\cdot),\!\varphi_{j}\rangle\!=\!\!-\lambda_{j}^{-2}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!p(t_{0},x)\Delta\varphi_{j}(x)\!\,\mathrm{d}x\!=\!\lambda_{j}^{-2}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\nabla p(t_{0},x)\!\cdot\!\nabla\varphi_{j}(x)\!\,\mathrm{d}x\!=\!\lambda_{j}^{-1}\!\langle p(t_{0},\cdot),\!\lambda_{j}^{-1}\!\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H^{1}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{1}}
denotes the inner product on

. Recall from the standard spectral theory of the Laplacian that
\{\lambda_{j}^{-1}\varphi_{j}\}_{j}
is an orthonormal basis of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
, whence

p^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle p(t_{0},\cdot),\lambda_{j}^{-1}\varphi_{j}\rangle_{H^{1}}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\varphi_{j}\longrightarrow p(t_{0},\cdot)\qquad\text{strongly in }H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega),

i.e.
, there holds

(6.12)

\lim_{n}\|p(t_{0},\cdot)-p^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=0.

We emphasise that, for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there holds
p^{n}(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega).

The following result shows that there exists coefficients
\{\alpha^{n}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,n}
such that
p^{n}
is an appropriate approximation of the solution of equation (
6.6
). For clarity, we define

(6.13)

\tilde{{\bf P}}_{i}=(P_{ij})_{j=1,2},

and, in what follows, we shall omit the
i
-subscript for brevity.

Proposition 6.6

(Galerkin approximation for
p
)
.

Let
f
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) with initial data satisfying Definition
3.1
,
\rho,{\bf p}
as per (
1.2
)–(
1.4
), and
t_{0}\in(0,T)
be an arbitrary Lebesgue point of
f,\rho,{\bf p}
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist functions
p^{n}\in AC(t_{0},T;C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
satisfying

(6.14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}p^{n}-\dv((1-\rho)\nabla p^{n}+p^{n}\nabla\rho)+p^{n}=-\dv((1-\rho)\tilde{{\bf P}}),\\
&p^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle p(t_{0},\cdot),\varphi_{j}\rangle\varphi_{j},\end{aligned}\right.

in the weak sense dual to
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, with the initial data achieved in the strong
L^{2}
-sense. Furthermore, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, there holds
:

(6.15)

p^{n}\to p\qquad\text{strongly in }L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})\text{ and weakly in }L^{2}(t,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

Moreover, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, we have the uniform bounds
:

(6.16)

\sup_{n}\|p^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\sup_{n}\|\nabla p^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}+\sup_{n}\|\nabla^{2}p^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}<\infty.

The regularity of
\rho
and
p^{n}
from the ansatz (
6.8
) imply that (
6.14
) is equivalent to

(6.17)

\partial_{t}p^{n}-(1-\rho)\Delta p^{n}-p^{n}\Delta\rho+p^{n}=-\dv((1-\rho)\tilde{{\bf P}});

this observation is what enables us to perform the
H^{2}
-type estimate on the Galerkin approximations
p^{n}
, by using an adaptation of the formal argument presented in (
6.4
).

Proof.

1.
Existence of Galerkin approximation
: Equation (
6.14
) is a linear equation for
p^{n}
with known coefficients
\rho,\mathbf{F}
. We show that there exist coefficients
\{\alpha^{n}_{j}\}
for the ansatz (
6.8
) such that (
6.14
) is satisfied in the weak sense dual to
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,n}
. We test the equation against
\varphi_{j}
and, using the orthonormal property of the basis, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha^{n}_{j}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha^{n}_{k}(t)\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\Big((1-\rho)\nabla\varphi_{k}+\varphi_{k}\nabla\rho\Big)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)+\alpha^{n}_{j}(t)=\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho)\tilde{{\bf P}}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

i.e.
, by defining the vector of coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}:=(\alpha^{n}_{j})_{j}
, the initial data corresponding to (
6.10

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}_{0}=(\langle p(t_{0},\cdot),\varphi_{j}\rangle)_{j}
,
\mathbf{g}^{n}:=(\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho)\mathbf{F}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x)_{j}
, and the matrix
A=(A_{jk})_{jk}
with

A_{{jk}}(t):=\delta_{jk}+\int_{\Omega}\Big((1-\rho)\nabla\varphi_{k}+\varphi_{k}\nabla\rho\Big)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

we have the linear system of ODEs:

(6.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}+A\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}=\mathbf{g}^{n}\qquad\text{for }t\in(t_{0},T),\\
&\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}(t_{0})=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Since all coefficients are locally integrable in time, the standard theory for linear systems of ODEs (
cf.
Carathéodory’s Theorem) implies the existence and uniqueness of a solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in AC([t_{0},T])
with the initial data achieved pointwise. It follows that there exists
p^{n}\in AC([t_{0},T];C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
a solution of (
6.14
) in the sense dual to
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
.

2.
Uniform estimates in
H^{1}
: Since
p^{n}\in\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j=1,\dots,n\}
, we may insert it into the weak formulation (
6.14
). We get the standard
H^{1}
-type estimate

(6.19)

\displaystyle\|p^{n}

\displaystyle\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|p^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}+\|\nabla p^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|p^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\tilde{{\bf P}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}+\|\nabla\rho\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}+\|p\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(1+\|\tilde{{\bf P}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}+\|\nabla\rho\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\Big)

for some positive constant
C
depending on
t_{0}
but not on
n
, where we obtain the final line using (
6.11
) and the boundedness
\|p(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, which imply
\sup_{n}\|p^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C
.

It follows from the previous estimate that
\{p^{n}\}_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and
L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. We deduce from Alaoglu’s Theorem that
\{p^{n}\}_{n}
converges to some limit function
\bar{p}
weakly in
L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Using the weak (resp. weak-*) lower semicontinuity of the norm, there holds

(6.20)

\displaystyle\!\!\|\bar{p}\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\!+\!\|\bar{p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\!\!+\!\|\nabla\bar{p}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\!\leq\!C\Big(\!1\!+\!\|\tilde{{\bf P}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\!\!+\!\|\nabla\rho\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}\!\Big).

Since the equation (
6.14
) is linear in
p^{n}
, this weak convergence is sufficient to pass to the limit in the equation. It follows that
\bar{p}
is a weak solution of

(6.21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\bar{p}-\dv((1-\rho)\nabla\bar{p}+\bar{p}\nabla\rho)+\bar{p}=-\dv((1-\rho)\tilde{{\bf P}}),\\
&\bar{p}(t_{0},\cdot)=p(t_{0},\cdot),\end{aligned}\right.

in duality with test functions belonging to
L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
. Furthermore, we see directly from (
6.21
) that
\partial_{t}\bar{p}\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;(H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))^{\prime})
, whence we have the embedding
\bar{p}\in C([t_{0},T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and hence the initial data is achieved in the sense

(6.22)

\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}\|\bar{p}(t,\cdot)-p(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

3.
Uniqueness of limits
: We show
\bar{p}=p
. Define the difference
w:=p-\bar{p}
, and observe:

(6.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}w-\dv((1-\rho)w+w\nabla\rho)+w=0,\\
&w(t_{0},\cdot)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

in the weak sense dual to
L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}(\Omega))
, where the initial data is achieved in the strong
L^{2}
-sense, as per (
6.22
). We test the above with
w\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega))
, which is an admissible test function, and using Young’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{\Omega}|w|^{2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for a.e.~}t\in(t_{0},T),

where the constant
C
depends on
t_{0}
but not on
n
and
t
. Integrating over
[t_{0},t]
,

(6.24)

\displaystyle\|w(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\!+\!\!\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\leq{\|w(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\!+\!C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!\!\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|w(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Note that
w\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))
by (
6.20
), so that the final integral is well-defined, as
\nabla\rho\in L^{2+\delta}(t_{0},T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
from Lemma

. Thus, we apply Grönwall’s Lemma, and get

\displaystyle\|w\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|w(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\exp\bigg(C\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)=0,

by (
6.22
). We have therefore shown that
\bar{p}=p
a.e.
\Omega_{t_{0},T}
, as required.

We also show the strong convergence
p^{n}\to p
in
L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. To see this, we write the equation for
w^{n}:=p^{n}-p
as per (
6.23
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}w^{n}-\dv((1-\rho)w^{n}+w^{n}\nabla\rho)+w^{n}=0,\\
&w^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)=w^{n}(t_{0},\cdot),\end{aligned}\right.

and an argument identical to the previous one implies

\displaystyle\|w^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|w^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\exp\bigg(C\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

by (
6.11
), as required. The strong convergence in

follows from the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality:

\|w^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla w^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|w^{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|w^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

and thus, using also the
n
-independent estimate on
\|\nabla w^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}
from (
6.24
),

\displaystyle\|w^{n}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\sup_{n}\|\nabla w^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}^{2}\big)\|w^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+C\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|w^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{4}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|w^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|w^{n}\|^{4}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\Big)\to 0,

where the final constant
C
depends on

but not on
n
, and we used Jensen’s inequality to obtain the second line. We get
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|w^{n}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega_{t_{0},T})}=0
, and (

6.16
) follow.
∎

We are now ready to give the proof of Lemma

.

We return to (

\displaystyle\|\nabla p^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Delta p^{n}

\displaystyle\|\nabla p^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Delta p^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t_{0},t})}^{2}\leq C\bigg(1+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla p^{n}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\Delta\rho(s)\|_{L^{2+\delta}(\Omega)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg),

Lemma 6.8

Armed with the previous result, we are ready to prove Lemma

Proof of Proposition

.

By Lemma

Fix

a Lebesgue point of

, usual coercivity assumptions, by virtue of the lower bound on

Equation (

1.
Caccioppoli inequality
: Let

The assumptions on

\displaystyle M(1+k^{2})\int_{\Upsilon}(1+|U|^{2})(|\mathscr{T}_{k}g|^{2}+\mathds{1}_{\{\mathscr{T}_{k}g>0\}})\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

and we may freely assume

,

(6.31)

\displaystyle\int_{\Upsilon}|\mathscr{T}_{k}g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg|_{t}-\int_{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle|\mathscr{T}_{k}g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg|_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla_{\xi}\mathscr{T}_{k}g|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq M(1+k^{2})\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Upsilon}(1+|U|^{2})(|\mathscr{T}_{k}g|^{2}+\mathds{1}_{\{\mathscr{T}_{k}g>0\}})\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

and thus, since

[
3

; we remark that

is sufficient to make the method work.

2.
Setup for iterations
: Recall the increasing sequence of times
\{T_{n}\}_{n}
defined in (

) and the increasing sequence of cut-off constants
\{\kappa_{n}\}_{n}
defined in (
5.18
). Define shorthand

, and
\mathscr{G}_{n}:=\esssup_{t\in[T_{n},T]}\int_{\Upsilon}|g_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{T_{n}}^{T}\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla_{\xi}g_{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t,
noting that

(6.32)

\mathscr{G}_{0}=\frac{1}{L^{2}}(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0}/2,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\xi}f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0}/2,T})})^{2}.

We will prove, with
\epsilon=1-\frac{2}{8}-\frac{3}{5}\in(0,1)
and
C_{*}
independent of
n
, the recursion estimate

(6.33)

\mathscr{G}_{n}\leq C_{*}^{n}\mathscr{G}_{n-1}^{1+\epsilon}\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Substitute
g_{n}
into the inequality (
6.31
) and, let
T_{n-1}\leq s\leq T_{n}\leq t
, and then integrate in
s
over
[T_{n},T_{n+1}]
. By arguing as we did in (

), by defining

, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{n}\leq C_{1}^{n}\int_{T_{n-1}}^{T}\int_{\Upsilon}\mathds{1}_{\{g_{n}>0\}}(1+|U|^{2})|g_{n-1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

3.
Recursive estimate
: Sobolev inequality’s implies, for some positive constant
C_{S}
independent of
t,n
,
\|g_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{6}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq C_{S}^{2}\|g_{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq C_{S}\mathscr{G}_{n}
. Using Hölder’s inequality,

(6.35)

\|g_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T_{n},T})}\leq|\Upsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}\|g_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(T_{n},T;L^{6}(\Upsilon))}\leq C_{S}|\Upsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}\mathscr{G}_{n}.

Meanwhile, the Interpolation Lemma
2.3
implies

(6.36)

\|g_{n}^{2}\|_{L^{\frac{5}{3}}(\Upsilon_{T_{n},T})}\leq\|g_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\frac{10}{3}}(\Upsilon_{T_{n},T})}\leq C_{I}^{2}(1+t_{0})^{2}\mathscr{G}_{n}.

Returning to (
6.34
) and using (
6.36
), Hölder, Minkowski, and Jensen inequalities,

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{n}\leq 4C_{1}^{n}|\{g_{n}>0\}\cap[T_{n-1},T]|^{\epsilon}(1+\||U|^{2}\|_{L^{4}(\Upsilon_{t_{0}/2,T})})\|g_{n-1}^{2}\|_{L^{\frac{5}{3}}(\Upsilon_{T_{n-1},T})},

and
\epsilon=1-\frac{2}{8}-\frac{3}{5}\in(0,1)
. In turn, defining
C_{2}:=4C_{1}(1+\|U\|_{L^{8}(\Upsilon_{t_{0}/2,T})})^{2}(1+C_{I})^{2}(1+t_{0})^{2}
,

\mathscr{G}_{n}\leq C_{2}^{n}|\{g_{n}>0\}\cap[T_{n-1},T]|^{\varepsilon}\mathscr{G}_{n-1}.

Analogously to (
5.21
) we have
\{g_{n}\!>\!0\}\!\subset\!\{g_{n-1}\!>\!2^{-n}\}
. By Markov’s inequality and (
6.35
),

\displaystyle|\{g_{n}\!>\!0\}\!\cap\![T_{n-1},T]|\leq|\{g_{n-1}\mathds{1}_{[T_{n-1},T]}\!>\!2^{-n}\}|\leq\frac{\|g_{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T_{n-1},T})}^{2}}{2^{-2n}}\leq C_{S}|\Upsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}2^{2n}\mathscr{G}_{n-1}.

Returning to (
6.37
) and letting
C_{*}:=(1+C_{S}|\Upsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}})C_{2}
, we have proved (
6.33
).

4.
Conclusion
: Estimate (
6.33
) and the iteration lemma
[
21
, §1, Lemma 4.1]
yield: if

(6.38)

\mathscr{G}_{0}\leq C_{*}^{-1/\epsilon^{2}},

then there holds
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}_{n}=0
. In turn, using (
6.32
), by choosing,
e.g.
,

(6.39)

L=2C_{*}^{1/\epsilon^{2}}\Big(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0}/2,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\xi}f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0}/2,T})}\Big)^{2},

we see that (
6.38
) is satisfied. Using the Monotone Convergence Theorem and (
6.35
),

\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\Upsilon}(g-1)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}t=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|g_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{T_{n},T})}\leq C_{S}|\Upsilon|^{\frac{2}{3}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{G}_{n}=0,

whence
0\leq g\leq 1
a.e.
\Upsilon_{t_{0},T}
. By (
6.30
),

0\leq f\leq L\quad\text{a.e.~}\Upsilon_{t_{0},T},

where

is prescribed by (

∎

7.

7.1.
Regularity bootstrap for first time derivative

The main result of this subsection is as follows.

Proposition 7.1

Lemma 7.2

(

Let
f

. Then, for a.e.

1.1
) in non-divergence form, we obtain (
1.5
), and notice that the terms

have cancelled. Testing with
\Delta f

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\Upsilon}(1-\rho)|\Delta f|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla\partial_{\theta}f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Upsilon}\Delta f\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\xi-\int_{\Upsilon}f\Delta f\Delta\rho\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

whence, arbitrarily fixing a common Lebesgue point

of
f,\rho,{\bf p}
and their derivatives, using the lower bound on

provided by Proposition

, and integrating in time over the interval

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}^{2}+

\displaystyle\|\Delta f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}+\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\nabla f(t_{0},\cdot)

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}\|\Delta\rho\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}\Big).

for some positive constant
C
depending on
t_{0}
, where we used the result of Lemma
6.8
, and we recall
\|\Delta\rho\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}<\infty

6.1
. It follows that, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,
\nabla f\in L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))
,
\Delta f\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t,T})
. Similarly, by testing with
\partial^{2}_{\theta}f
,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|\partial_{\theta}

\displaystyle f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\Upsilon}|\partial^{2}_{\theta}f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Upsilon}(1-\rho)\Delta f\partial^{2}_{\theta}f\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\Upsilon}f\Delta\rho\partial^{2}_{\theta}f\,\mathrm{d}\xi-\int_{\Upsilon}\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))\partial^{2}_{\theta}f\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

whence Young’s inequality and integrating yields
\partial^{2}_{\theta}f\in L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t,T})
,
\partial_{\theta}f\in L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))
.

This argument is only formal, since
a priori
we do not know that
\Delta f
and
\partial^{2}_{\theta}f
belong to
L^{2}
. In turn, we adapt the Galerkin approximation technique employed in the proof of Lemma
6.4
(
cf.
§
6.2
) to this new setting. The only changes are that we now have
\nabla^{2}\rho\in L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})
by Proposition
6.1
.

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

As per the approach in §
6.2
, we recall
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j}
the orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Omega)
of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, and define the Galerkin approximations

f^{n}(t,x,\theta):=\sum^{n}_{j=1}\beta^{n}_{j}(t,\theta)\varphi_{j}(x),

where the coefficients
\{\beta^{n}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,n}
are determined from the linear system of PDEs

(7.2)

\partial_{t}\beta^{n}_{j}-\partial^{2}_{\theta}\beta^{n}_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta^{n}_{k}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\Big((1\!-\!\rho)\nabla\varphi_{k}+\varphi_{k}\nabla\rho\Big)\!\cdot\!\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)=\int_{\Omega}(1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

with corresponding initial data
\beta^{n}_{j}(0,\theta)=\langle f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta),\varphi_{j}\rangle,
chosen such that
f^{n}
solves

(7.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f^{n}+\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))=\dv((1-\rho)\nabla f^{n}+f^{n}\nabla\rho)+\partial^{2}_{\theta}f^{n},\\
&f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot,\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta),\varphi_{j}\rangle\varphi_{j}\end{aligned}\right.

in the weak sense dual to
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
. Notice that
f
, and not
f^{n}
, is on the right-hand side of (
7.2
), for simplicity. The standard theory for such linear systems of uniformly parabolic PDEs implies the existence of
f^{n}\in L^{2}(t_{0},T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(0,2\pi);C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon))
solving the above. Alternatively, one can construct a solution of this problem by doing a further Galerkin approximation in the angle variable;
cf.

e.g.

[
10
, Appendix A]
.

Furthermore, by the orthonormality of the basis functions and standard results on bases,

\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot,\theta)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta),\varphi_{j}\rangle^{2}\longrightarrow\|f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where the limit holds pointwise for all
\theta
. Additionally, by integrating with respect to
\theta
,
\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta),\varphi_{j}\rangle^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,
whence, since the integrands form an increasing sequence of nonnegative quantities, the Monotone Convergence Theorem implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta),\varphi_{j}\rangle^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\|f(t_{0},\cdot,\theta)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

An analogous argument, using similar manipulations to those used to obtain (
6.12
), proves the same convergence result for the space-angle derivatives. We have shown:

(7.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}=\|f(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)},\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla_{\xi}f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}=\|\nabla_{\xi}f(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}.

1.
Consistency of the Galerkin approximations
: Since
f^{n}\in\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
, we may insert it as a test function in the previous weak formulation. We get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\!\!\int_{\Upsilon}\!\!|f^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\!\!\int_{\Upsilon}\!\!\Big((1-\rho)|\nabla f^{n}|^{2}+|\partial_{\theta}f^{n}|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\!=\!\!\int_{\Upsilon}(1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta)\cdot\nabla f^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi-\int_{\Upsilon}f^{n}\nabla f^{n}\cdot\nabla\rho\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Recall
\nabla\rho\in L^{2+\delta}(t_{0},T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))

6.5

\displaystyle\|f^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\xi}f^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},t})}^{2}\leq C\Big(\|

\displaystyle f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|f^{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big),

where

,
f^{n}\in L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}(0,2\pi);C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\Upsilon))

for each

; see also (

\displaystyle\|f^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\leq C\Big(\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}\Big)\exp\Big(C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla

\displaystyle\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big).

The convergence of the initial data (
7.4

\sup_{n}\|f^{n}(t_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}<\infty
, whence we deduce that
\sup_{n}\|f^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}<\infty
,
and, by returning to (
7.5

is uniformly bounded in

:

(7.6)

\sup_{n}\|f^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}+\sup_{n}\|\nabla_{\xi}f^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}<\infty.

Using Alaoglu’s Theorem, we obtain the weak convergence of the sequence

2.
H^{2}

: Since

Relation (
6.9
) implies
\Delta f^{n}
is an admissible test function in (
7.3
). We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla f^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\!\int_{\Upsilon}(1-\rho)|\Delta f^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\xi+\!\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla\partial_{\theta}f^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\Upsilon}\Delta f^{n}\dv((1-\rho)f{\bf e}(\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\xi-\int_{\Upsilon}f_{n}\Delta f_{n}\Delta\rho\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Using the lower bound on

i.e.

Conclusion

t\in(0,T)

∎

Proof of Proposition

7.2.1.

(Equation for

implies that, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\sup_{n}\|\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{4}(t,T)}+\sup_{n}\|\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T;^{2}(\Omega))}+\sup_{n}\|\nabla\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}<\infty.

We perform the second derivative estimate, by testing with
\Delta\dot{p}^{n}\in\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
and using the non-divergence form of the diffusive term, and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\|\nabla\dot{p}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Delta\dot{p}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\dot{p}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\dot{p}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\dv[(1-\rho)\dot{\tilde{{\bf P}}}-\dot{\rho}\tilde{{\bf P}}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\|\dv[-\dot{\rho}\nabla p+p\nabla\dot{\rho}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{4}+\|\Delta\rho\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\Big),

using the lower bound of Proposition
5.2
. The third term on the right is bounded since

\displaystyle\|\dv[-\dot{\rho}\nabla p+p\nabla\dot{\rho}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\Big(\|

\displaystyle\nabla\dot{\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla p\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+\|\dot{\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla p\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\dot{\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+\|p\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\|\Delta\dot{\rho}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big).

By integrating in time, using (
7.24
), Proposition
7.1
, and Lemma
7.6
, we obtain

\|\nabla\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\Delta\dot{p}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq C

for some positive constant
C
depending on
t
, but independent of
n
. It follows from the above and the periodic Calderón–Zygmund inequality of Lemma
2.4
that we have proved the first assertion of the lemma. The boundedness
\nabla\dot{p}\in L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})
follows from the interpolation of Lemma
2.3
. It follows from (
7.18
) that
\partial_{t}\dot{\rho}-\Delta\dot{\rho}\in L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})
. The classical Calderón–Zygmund estimate for the heat equation implies
\partial_{t}\dot{\rho},\nabla^{2}\dot{\rho}\in L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})
a.e.
t\in(0,T)
.

It then follows that
\dot{\rho}\in W^{1,4}(\Omega_{t,T})
, whence Morrey’s embedding yields
\dot{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{t,T})
. We then apply the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality as per (
6.5
) to get

(7.25)

\|\nabla\dot{\rho}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{8}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla^{2}\dot{\rho}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\dot{\rho}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+C\|\dot{\rho}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

and raising to the power
8
and integrating in time yields
\nabla\dot{\rho}\in L^{8}(\Omega_{t,T})
.
∎

-type estimate for

Lemma 7.8

(

-type estimate for
\dot{f}
)

Let

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as that of Lemma

. We construct Galerkin approximations

, each solving the linear approximate problems

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\dot{f}^{n}\!-\!\dv[(1\!-\!\rho)\nabla\dot{f}^{n}\!+\!\dot{f}^{n}\nabla\rho]\!-\!\partial^{2}_{\theta}\dot{f}^{n}\!=\!\overbrace{-\!\dv[\big((1\!-\!\rho)\dot{f}\!-\!\dot{\rho}f\big){\bf e}(\theta)]}^{\in L^{2}}\!+\!\overbrace{\dv[\!-\!\dot{\rho}\nabla f\!+\!f\nabla\dot{\rho}]}^{\in L^{4}},\\

We also have

, there exists
\dot{f}^{n}
sovling the above weakly in duality with

, and uniformly bounded in

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\|\nabla\dot{f}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\Delta\dot{f}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}\dot{f}^{n}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle C\Big(\!\|\!\dv[((1\!-\!\rho)\dot{f}\!-\!\dot{\rho}f){\bf e}(\theta)]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\!+\!\|\!\dv[f\nabla\dot{\rho}\!-\!\dot{\rho}\nabla f]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\!+\!\|\dot{f}^{n}(t)\|^{\frac{10}{3}}_{L^{\frac{10}{3}}(\Upsilon)}\!+\!\|\Delta\rho\|^{5}_{L^{5}(\Upsilon)}\!\Big),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle|f^{(n+1)}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+\int_{\Upsilon}|\nabla_{\xi}f^{(n+1)}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\rho^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{4}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{4}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!+\!\|f^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{4}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{4}(\Upsilon)}^{2}\!+\!\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}^{2}\|\nabla\rho^{(n+1)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle\|p^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla p^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(1+\|p^{(n+1),k}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}\|p^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big),

for a.e.

common to

and all of their first

and

or

provided by Proposition
5.2
. Using an analogous strategy to that used for (

, Grönwall’s Lemma and Lemma

imply

\|p^{(n+1),k}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega_{t,T})}+\|p^{(n+1),k}\|_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla p^{(n+1),k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq C,

with

depending on
t
and
q
but not on

. The uniqueness argument in Step 3 of the proof of Lemma

implies, with Alaoglu’s Theorem, the weak convergence
p^{(n+1),k}\rightharpoonup p^{(n+1)}
in

, and weakly-* in

.

We perform the

estimate for

. Since this function is smooth with respect to

, we expand the diffusions in non-divergence form. Test with

from Proposition

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla p^{(n+1),k}(t,

\displaystyle\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\Delta p^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(

\displaystyle\|\nabla p^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla p^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}+\|\Delta\rho(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\!\|\nabla\rho^{(n+1)}(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\!\!+\!\|\nabla p(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\!\!+\!\|\rho^{(n+1)}(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\!\!+\!\|\Delta p(t,\cdot)\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\bf p}^{(n+1)}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla^{2}{\bf p}^{(n+1)}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{t,T})}\leq C,

whence we also have

) for

It now follows that

H^{2}
-type estimate for

: Our goal in this step is to raise the integrability of
\nabla f^{(n+1)}
and
\nabla^{2}f^{(n+1)}

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}f^{(n+1),k}&-\dv\Big[(1-\rho)\nabla f^{(n+1),k}+f^{(n+1),k}\nabla\rho\Big]-\partial^{2}_{\theta}f^{(n+1),k}\\

weakly in duality with
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\}
. We argue similarly to Step 4. By testing the above with

, we obtain the

estimate

\displaystyle\|f^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla_{\xi}f^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(1+\|f^{(n+1),k}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla\rho(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon)}\|f^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big),

for a.e.
t\in[t_{0},T]
, for any chosen Lebesgue point
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, where the constant on the right-hand side depends on
t_{0}
and
T
, but not on
k
or
t
, and where we used the lower bound on
1-\rho
provided by Proposition
5.2
. Using an analogous strategy to that used for (
7.4
) to control the initial data independently of
k
, Grönwall’s Lemma

\|f^{(n+1),k}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}+\|\nabla_{\xi}f^{(n+1),k}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}\leq C,

where

is independent of

f^{(n+1),k}\rightharpoonup f^{(n+1)}
.

We now perform the
H^{2}
-type estimate. We test the equation with the diffusive term written in non-divergence form against
\Delta f^{(n+1),k}\in\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\}
and obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla f^{(n+1),k}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\Delta f^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|\nabla\partial_{\theta}f^{(n+1),k}(s,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(1+\|\nabla f^{(n+1),k}(t_{0},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Upsilon)}+\|\Delta\rho\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}+\|f^{(n+1),k}\|^{4}_{L^{4}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}\Big),

\|\nabla f^{(n+1),k}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(t_{0},T;L^{2}(\Upsilon))}+\|\Delta f^{(n+1),k}\|_{L^{2}(\Upsilon_{t_{0},T})}\leq C.

Replicating this strategy while testing against

. The weak lower semicontinuity of the norms and Lemma

: We repeat the strategy of the proof of Proposition

. Define

\partial_{t}f^{(n+1)}+\dv_{\xi}(Uf^{(n+1)}+V)=\dv_{\xi}(A\nabla_{\xi}f^{(n+1)}).

Observe that the results of the previous steps imply

a.e.

. In turn, the equation (

7.
Higher integrability for
\partial_{t}f^{(n)}
and
\nabla^{2}_{\xi}f^{(n)}
: We follow the proof of Corollary
7.10
to the letter. Using the boundedness of Steps 5 and 6, we obtain directly from (
7.14

\partial_{t}f^{(n+1)}-A:\nabla^{2}_{\xi}f^{(n+1)}\in L^{4}(\Upsilon_{t,T})\qquad\text{a.e.~}t\in(0,T),

and by the Schauder-type Lemma
2.5

\partial_{t}\dot{f},\nabla^{2}_{\xi}\dot{f}\in L^{4}(\Upsilon_{t,T})

(A.4)

\partial_{t}f^{(n+1)},\nabla^{2}_{\xi}f^{(n+1)}\in L^{2^{m}}(\Upsilon_{t,T})\quad\text{a.e.~}t\in(0,T).

\|\nabla_{\xi}{f}^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{2^{m+1}}(\Upsilon_{t,T})}\leq C\Big(\|\nabla_{\xi}^{2}{f}^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{2^{m}}(\Upsilon_{t,T})}^{2^{m}}\|{f}^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon_{t,T})}^{2^{m}}+\|{f}^{(n+1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Upsilon_{t,T})}^{2^{m+1}}\Big).

and thus the Calderón–Zygmund estimate implies

A.2.
Proof of Lemma
8.2

Recall the expressions (
8.4
). We have the formula
\nabla\Psi(t,x)=-\frac{x}{8\pi t^{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}=-\frac{x}{2t}\Psi(t,x),
as well as the estimate (
1\leq q<\frac{4}{3}
)

(A.5)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{t})}^{q}\leq\|\nabla\Psi\|_{L^{q}((0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{q}\leq C_{q}t^{\frac{4-3q}{2}}\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,\infty),q\in[1,\frac{4}{3}).

with
C_{q}=\frac{4}{4-3q}(4\pi)^{1-q}q^{-\frac{q}{2}-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{q+1}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r
, which are verified by direct computation.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Define
\Phi_{1}(t,x):=\sum_{|n|\leq 2}\Psi(t,x+2\pi n)
and
\Phi_{2}:=\Phi-\Phi_{1}
. Observe

\|\nabla\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{t})}\leq\sum_{|n|\leq 2}\|\nabla\Psi(\cdot,\cdot+2\pi n)\|_{L^{q}((0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}=12\|\nabla\Psi\|_{L^{q}((0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 12C_{q}t^{\frac{4-3q}{2q}},

where we used the translation invariance of Lebesgue measure and the bound (
A.5
). It therefore suffices to estimate
\nabla\Phi_{2}
. For all
x\in\Omega
and
|n|\geq 3
, it is an exercise to show

\displaystyle(8\pi)^{q}t^{2q}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Psi

\displaystyle(t,x+2\pi n)|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{2\pi n_{2}}^{2\pi(n_{2}+1)}\!\!\int_{2\pi n_{1}}^{2\pi(n_{1}+1)}|z|^{q}e^{-\frac{q|z|^{2}}{4t}}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\,\mathrm{d}z_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{2\pi(|n_{2}|-1)}^{2\pi(|n_{2}|+1)}\!\!\int_{2\pi(|n_{1}|-1)}^{2\pi(|n_{1}|+1)}|z|^{q}e^{-\frac{q|z|^{2}}{4t}}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\,\mathrm{d}z_{2}\leq\int_{\{\frac{2\pi}{3}|n|\leq|z|\leq 4\pi|n|\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!|z|^{q}e^{-\frac{q|z|^{2}}{4t}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

In polar coordinates,
\|\nabla\Psi(t,\cdot+2\pi n)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}\leq(8\pi)^{1-q}t^{-2q}\int_{\frac{2\pi}{3}|n|}^{4\pi|n|}r^{q+1}e^{-\frac{qr^{2}}{4t}}\,\mathrm{d}r
, and thus

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi(t,\cdot+2\pi n)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\!\!(4q^{-1})^{1+\frac{q}{2}}(8\pi)^{1-q}t^{1-\frac{3q}{2}}\int_{\frac{\pi\sqrt{q}}{3}\frac{|n|}{\sqrt{t}}}^{\infty}r^{q+1}e^{-r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\!\!(4q^{-1})^{1+\frac{q}{2}}(8\pi)^{1-q}t^{1-\frac{3q}{2}}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}q}{18}\frac{|n|^{2}}{t}}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!r^{q+1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}r^{2}}\!\!\,\mathrm{d}r\!=\!C_{q}^{\prime}t^{1-\frac{3q}{2}}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}q}{18}\frac{|n|^{2}}{t}},

with
C_{q}^{\prime}=C_{q}^{\prime}(q)>0
. Integrating time we get, with
C_{q}
changing from line to line,

\|\nabla\Phi_{2}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{t})}\leq\sum_{|n|\geq 3}\|\Psi(\cdot,\cdot+2\pi n)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{t})}\leq C_{q}t^{\frac{4-3q}{2q}}\sum_{|n|\geq 3}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}\frac{|n|^{2}}{t}}<\infty\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,\infty).

Thus,
\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{t})}\leq C_{q}t^{\frac{4-3q}{2q}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}\frac{|n|^{2}}{t}}\leq C_{q}\Big(\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}\frac{|n|^{2}}{T}}\Big)t^{\frac{4-3q}{2q}}
for all
t\in(0,T)
.
