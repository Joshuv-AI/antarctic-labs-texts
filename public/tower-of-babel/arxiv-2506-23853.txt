Non-local random deposition models for earthquakes and energy propagation

Abstract
In the present paper, we investigate a new class of non-local random deposition models, initially introduced by physicists to investigate the field of mechanical constraints (stress) applied along a line or on a given area located in a seismic zone. The non-local features are twofold. First, the falling objects have random and heavy-tailed dimensions. Second, the locations where the objects are falling are at least for some of the models that we consider, depending on the shape of the surface before the deposition. Let us be more specific, we consider ( h N ) N ∈ ℕ (h_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}} a sequence of random ( d + 1 ) (d+1) -dimensional surfaces defined on [ 0 , D ] d [0,D]^{d} for d ∈ { 1 , 2 } d\in\{1,2\} . The process h N h_{N} is obtained by adding to h N − 1 h_{N-1} an object s ∈[0,D]^d ↦Z_N^ α -1 ψ ( v Y N ( s ) Z N ) , where Z = ( Z i ) i ∈ ℕ Z=(Z_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}} is an i.i.d. sequence of Pareto random variables with parameter β > 0 \beta>0 introduced to tune the vertical and horizontal size of the falling object, where ψ : [ 0 , ∞ ) ↦ ℝ + \psi:[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+} determines the global shape of the object, where v 𝐲 ​ ( 𝐱 ) v_{{\bf y}}({\bf x}) is the distance between 𝐱 {\bf x} and 𝐲 {\bf y} on the torus and where Y = ( Y i ) i ∈ ℕ Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}} are random variables in [ 0 , D ] d [0,D]^{d} that settle the deposition location of each falling object. In the present papers we focus our attention on three variations of this model. First, the rand-model which corresponds to Y Y being a sequence of i.i.d. random variables distributed uniformly on [ 0 , D ] d [0,D]^{d} . Then, the min-model, that introduces an important property of the physics of earthquakes but is also more difficult to tract to the extent that a strong correlation appears between the N N -th falling object and the shape of the profile h N − 1 h_{N-1} . Finally we also consider another variant of the rand-model, called the stellar model, which allows us to study the intensity of the microwaves emitted by stellar clouds and measured at the Earth surface. For those three models, our results identify the limit in law of ( h N ) N ∈ ℕ (h_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}} viewed as a continuous random function and rescaled properly. We also determine the limit in law of the fluctuations of ( h N ) N ∈ ℕ (h_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}} .

Introduction:

Objects that vary greatly in size, and whose distribution can be modelled by a power law, frequently occur in nature.

This is the case of aerosols in the atmosphere
[
8
,
3
]
, or interstellar clouds in space
[
5
]
. In both cases, these objects are formed by collision and fragmentation processes, which can be described by Schmoluchowski-type equations
[
21
,
13
]
. Note that the power-law behavior of the object size distributions is determined by the shape of the reaction rates in these equations.

Earthquakes also have certain power-law properties. At the boundary between two tectonic plates, slow mantle movements accumulate stresses that are suddenly released during an earthquake by the fast movement of a zone at the plate boundary
[
18
]
. The surface of the moving zone fluctuates, and its distribution is known to be a power-law
[
6
,
10
]
. Recent works have proposed an explanation for the origin of these distributions, based on the self-similar nature of the force field at the plate boundary. This provides an explanation for statistical properties of earthquakes such as the Gutenberg-Richter law for the released energy or the Omori-Utsu law for the number of aftershocks
[
15
,
16
]
.

Several situations of interest for the objects introduced above can be described as sums over sets of objects. As we describe in sections 1.2 and 1.3 below, this is the case, for example, with the emission of microwaves by interstellar clouds, or for the absorption by aerosols of radiation of which rays propagate in a straight line. In the case of earthquakes, the stress field results from the accumulation of stresses created by all past events, and can therefore be modeled as the summation of stress variations caused by previous events, see section 1.4.

This summation over sets of objects is akin to considering a deposition process, the particularity of which is that object sizes are power-law distributed. This process forms an interface corresponding to different physical observables, depending on the problem under consideration.
In the case of microwaves emitted by the interstellar medium or in the case of absorption by aerosols, these are the spatial variations in radiation intensity when measured on Earth in different directions towards space.
Finally, for earthquakes, it is the stress field measured as a function of position in the plane separating the two sides of a fault.

We will now describe in more detail how to model these different deposition processes. Variants are considered according to the distribution of object positions and to the scalar or vector nature of the objects.

We call rand-model the one where object positions are sampled uniformly at random and objects are scalar. The stellar model corresponds to vector objects. Finally, the min-model considers that objects are deposited at the minimum of the interface, so at a position that depends on the whole shape of the interface. This last, highly non-local problem is the most complicated, but introduces an effect that is important for earthquake modeling.

1.2
Physical motivations for the rand-model: energy propagation through absorbing objects.

When a wave propagates in a medium that contains absorbing objects, its energy decreases after every encounter with such an absorbing object.

First, we assume that the wave propagates in a straight line perpendicular to a window
[0,D]^{d}
(
d=1
or
d=2
). Second, we assume that the decrease of energy at each encounter is proportional to the width of the absorbing object that is crossed by the straight line and third that the positions of the objects are sampled uniformly at random, then the opposite of the variation of energy is described by the rand-model, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

Remark 1.2.1
.

The standard law for the absorption of energy is that of an exponential decay with rate proportional to a coefficient
a
multiplied by the length of propagation
l
. If the effect is small, we can develop the exponential as
e^{(-al)}\simeq 1-al
, and we recover an effect proportional to the length
l
. If the effect is not small, then we consider the log of the energy instead which also changes by
-al
after propagation over a distance
l
.

This process provides for instance a simple model for the spatial fluctuations of the electromagnetic energy as measured at Earth surface when the electromagnetic radiations have propagated through the atmosphere and interacted with aerosols.

1.3
The stellar-model

A dual situation concerns a medium in which objects radiate energy. This is the case for instance of interstellar clouds in space that radiate microwaves.
The intensity of a cloud radiation is proportional to the
width of the cloud. To adapt our model to the present phenomenon we recall (
1.1.4
) and we choose
d=2
.
The sequence of random variables
Z=(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
provides the size of interstellar clouds indexed in
{\mathbb{N}}
. The observations show that the size of the clouds varies greatly and their distribution is considered to be a power-law as discussed in
[
5
]
. The second sequence of random variables
Y=(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
provides the location of the center of each cloud. Finally, the shape of a cloud being an ellipsoid and the signal measured at the Earth surface being proportional to the width of the ellipsoid projected on the plane normal to the line of sight, we set

\psi(u)=(1-u^{2}){1}_{[0,1]}(u)
for
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The statistical characterization of this interstellar emission is of prime importance to experiments searching the signature of primordial gravitational waves in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) polarization.
These waves are polarized due to the presence of a large scale magnetic field of scale comparable or larger than the one of the cloud. Each cloud thus emits a microwave radiation aligned with a direction that varies from one cloud to another. We assume that these directions are uncorrelated. At the earth surface, measurements are sensitive either to the total energy or to the total field. The former is described by the rand-model whereas the later can be defined as follows. This is the stellar-model.

We let
{\mathcal{S}}_{2}
be the unitary sphere of dimension

We let

\theta_{0}:=E(\widetilde{\Theta}_{1})
. Thus, it suffices to set
\Theta_{i}:=\widetilde{\Theta}_{i}-\theta_{0}

Then, the radiation that is measured at

where

\displaystyle h^{\text{st}}_{N}({\bf x})

1.4
Physical motivations in the context of earthquakes. The min-model.

We consider an area in the vicinity of a subduction zone. Two tectonic plates are moving with respect to each other, raising progressively the mechanical constraints
applied between them. Assuming the boundary between the plates to be linear (
d=1
) or planar (
d=2

t

(T_{N})_{n\geq 0}
the non-decreasing sequence recording the times at which the earthquakes take place successively (

by convention).
Between two consecutive earthquakes, the motions of both plates increase the profile linearly
and uniformly on

with speed

are

\tilde{h}
decreases discontinuously at
T_{N}
.
Thus, for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{T_{N}+t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}_{T_{N}}({\bf x})+C\,t,\quad t\in[0,T_{N+1}-T_{N})

(1.4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{T_{N+1}}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to T_{N+1}^{-}}\tilde{h}_{t}({\bf x})-\phi_{N+1}({\bf x}),

where
\phi_{N+1}:[0,D]^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is a random function that describes the effect of the
(N+1)
-th earthquake on the profile.

In the present paper, we will only consider the evolution of the profile due to earthquakes. Thus, we get rid of the linear growth of the profile
induced by the constant

Moreover, we prefer working with positive modifications of the profile at earthquakes

2.4

Completing our investigation of the min model requires to identify the assymptotics of the fluctuations for

This is more difficult than it is for the rand and stellar models. Indeed, the fact that for every

makes the computations to obtain the tightness of

make some conjectures.

1.

In the case where

the result obtained in Theorem

for the rand model holds true for the min model as well. Indeed, in this case

and Figure

). The indices of those very big variables

and therefore the space between them also tends to

Assuming some mixing features of the growth model we can reasonably expect that the dependence between the

for

2.

3
Preparation: convergence in distribution of random functions in
(C_{d},{\mathcal{C}}_{d})
.

In this Section we describe some strategies to prove the convergence in distribution of a sequence
of random functions.

3.1
Case of real random functions.

We consider
(L_{N})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence of random variables from
(\Omega,{\mathcal{A}},P)
to
(C_{d},{\mathcal{C}}_{d})
.
In other words, for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
we let
L_{N}(\omega):[0,D]^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function
such that
L_{N}:(\Omega,{\mathcal{A}},P)\mapsto(C_{d},{\mathcal{C}}_{d})
is a random variable. We will need several times in this paper to
prove that
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in law towards
\mu
a probability measure on
(C_{d},{\mathcal{C}}_{d})
. The following
allows us to prove such convergences.

Theorem 3.1.1
.

[
1
, Theorem 7.1]

The sequence
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in law towards
\mu
if and only if

(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a tight sequence of random variables
and
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to
\mu
for finite dimensional marginals, i.e.,
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
({\bf s}_{1},\dots,{\bf s}_{k})\in([0,D]^{d})^{k}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}(L_{N}({\bf s}_{1}),\dots,L_{N}({\bf s}_{k}))=_{law}(S({\bf s}_{1}),\dots,S({\bf s}_{k}))

(3.1.1)

with
S
a random function of law
\mu
.

Remark 3.1.2
.

The convergence in probability of
(L_{N}({\bf s}))_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to
S({\bf s})
for every
{\bf s}\in[0,D]^{d}
implies the
convergence of finite dimensional marginals (
3.1.1
).

Proving the tightness of
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
requires to define the continuity modulus of function in
C_{d}
. To that aim, for
h\in C_{d}^{k}
and
\delta>0
we set

w_{h}(\delta):=\sup_{{\bf s},{\bf x}\in[0,D]^{d}\colon\;|{\bf s}-{\bf x}|_{1}\leq\delta}\quad|h({\bf s})-h({\bf x})|.

(3.1.2)

Proposition 3.1.3
.

[
1
, Theorem 7.3]

If
(L_{N}(0))_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a tight sequence of real random variables and if
for every
{\varepsilon}>0

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\,\limsup_{N\to\infty}P\Big(w_{L_{N}}(\delta)\geq{\varepsilon}\Big)=0,

(3.1.3)

then,
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is tight sequence of random functions.

The tightness of
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
may also be proven with the help of Kolmogorov criterion
(see e.g.
[
1
]
) and stated in the following Proposition. This proposition, although well-known
by probabilists is proven in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3.1.4
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of real valued random continuous functions on
[0,D]^{d}
.

•

If there exist
p>1,C>0
and
\gamma>0
such that for all
{\bf x},{\bf s}\in[0,D]^{d}

\underset{N\geq 1}{\sup}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big|L_{N}({\bf x})-L_{N}({\bf s})\Big|^{p}\Big]\leq C|{\bf x}-{\bf s}|_{1}^{d+\gamma},

(3.1.4)

•

if the sequence of real random variables
(L_{N}(0))_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is tight,

then
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a tight sequence of random continuous functions.

3.2
Case of vector random functions

In the framework of the stellar model, we need to take into account sequences of random functions
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}

such that
L_{N}:[0,D]^{2}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. We let
L_{N}^{1},L_{N}^{2},L_{N}^{3}
be the coordinates of
L_{N}
. First, it turns out that checking the tightness of each coordinate
is sufficient to derive the tightness of
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

If
(L^{j}_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a tight sequence of real random functions for every
j\in\{1,2,3\}
,
then
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also a tight sequence of random functions.

Proving this proposition is not difficult, it suffices to extend the definition of the continuity modulus in (
3.1.2
) and to observe that for every
h:=(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\in C_{d}^{3}
,

w_{h}(\delta)\leq w_{h_{1}}(\delta)+w_{h_{2}}(\delta)+w_{h_{3}}(\delta).

The convergence of finite dimensional marginals is defined as in (
3.1.1
). Remark (
3.1.2
)
may be restated here since it suffices that for every
j\in\{1,2,3\}
and every
{\bf s}\in[0,D]^{2}
the sequence of real random variables

(L_{N}^{j}({\bf s}))_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges in probability to
S^{j}({\bf s})
to conclude that
(L_{N})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges for finite dimensional marginals
to
S
.

4.1.1
Case

Remark 4.1.1

For

At this stage, we consider

For every

)

\big[\frac{T_{N+1}}{N}\big]^{{\zeta}}\,\tilde{h}_{N}

The law of large numbers guarantees us that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1+\frac{N}{\log N}}^{\infty}\Big[\frac{1}{T_{i}}\Big]^{{\zeta}}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}||\hat{h}_{N}||_{\infty}=0

By (

{\zeta}>1

\Big|\Big|\bar{h}_{N}-\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{N}{\log N}}\Big[\frac{1}{T_{i}}\Big]^{{\zeta}}\Big|\Big|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{u\in\big[0,\frac{dD}{2}\big]}\bigg|\psi\bigg(u\bigg[\frac{T_{\frac{N}{\log N}}}{T_{N+1}}\bigg]^{{\zeta}}\bigg)-1\bigg|\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big[\frac{1}{T_{i}}\Big]^{{\zeta}}.

(4.1.8)

We conclude that the r.h.s. in (

4.2
Case
{\zeta}<1
for the rand-model

We will apply the strategy displayed in Section
3
. To that aim we write

h_{N}({\bf s}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}^{\alpha-d}\psi\bigg(\frac{v_{Y_{i}}({\bf s})}{Z_{i}}\bigg),\quad{\bf s}\in[0,D]^{d}.

(4.2.1)

The first step consists in proving that the sequence of random continuous processes
(\frac{1}{N}\,h_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is tight in
(C_{d},{\mathcal{C}}_{d})
. To that aim, we will prove that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\,\lim_{N\to\infty}P\Big(\frac{1}{N}w_{h_{N}}(\delta)\geq{\varepsilon}\Big)=0

(4.2.2)

where, for
h\in C_{[0,D]^{d}}
and
\delta>0
,

w_{h}(\delta):=\sup_{{\bf s},{\bf x}\in[0,D]^{d}\colon\;|{\bf s}-{\bf x}|_{1}\leq\delta}\quad|h({\bf s})-h({\bf x})|.

We set
G:[1,\infty)\times[0,dD/2]\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
as
G(z,u)=\psi\big(\frac{u}{z}\big)
. We recall that

\psi
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
, that
\psi(0)=1
and that
\psi(v)=0
for
v\in[1,\infty)
. Thus,
G
is continuous and

\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\ \sup_{u\in[0,dD/2]}\big|G(z,u)-1|=0.

As a consequence,
G
is uniformly continuous on
[1,\infty)\times[0,dD/2]
. Moreover,
\forall{\bf y},{\bf s},{\bf x}\in[0,D]^{d}
it holds that

|v_{{\bf y}}({\bf x})-v_{{\bf y}}({\bf s})|\leq|{\bf x}-{\bf s}|_{1}
so that if
|{\bf x}-{\bf s}|_{1}\leq\delta

\displaystyle\big|h_{N}({\bf s})-h_{N}({\bf x})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}^{\alpha-d}\ \big|G(Z_{i},v_{Y_{i}}({\bf s}))-G(Z_{i},v_{Y_{i}}({\bf x}))\big|

(4.2.3)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}^{\alpha-d}\ w_{G}(\delta).

(4.2.4)

Consequently, for all
{\varepsilon}>0
;

\displaystyle P\Big[\frac{1}{N}w_{h_{N}}(\delta)\geq{\varepsilon}\Big]\leq P\Big[w_{G}(\delta)\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}^{\alpha-1}\geq{\varepsilon}\Big].

Since

P
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega

.
Thus, with the help of the uniform continuity of

It remains to check that
(\frac{1}{N}h_{N}(0))_{N\geq 1}

\eta>0
and we write
for

(4.2.6)

to make sure that the r.h.s. in (

)

. As a consequence, one can choose

For the second step of the proof, we pick

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\frac{1}{N}}\int_{[0,D]^{d}}f_{N}({\bf x})\,d{\bf x}\underset{\text{proba}}{=}0.

{\frac{1}{N}}\big(D^{d}h^{m}_{N}(0)-\int_{[0,D]^{d}}h^{m}_{N}({\bf x})\,d{\bf x}\big)=-{\frac{1}{N}}\int_{[0,D]^{d}}f_{N}({\bf x})\,d{\bf x}.

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\frac{1}{N}}h_{N}(0)\underset{\text{proba}}{=}\gamma_{\alpha,\beta,D}.

We can repeat this last proof for different starting points and

This implies the finite dimensional convergence of the process

, and combined with the tightness of

{\frac{1}{N}}h_{N}\Rightarrow_{N}\gamma_{\alpha,\beta,D}1_{[0,D]^{d}}.

(4.3.7)

Proofs for the stellar model

Lemma A.0.2

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\psi^{(n)}(0)(1+\epsilon(x))

Appendix B
Kolmogorov criterion of tightness

In the present section we prove Proposition
3.1.4
. To that aim, in dimension
d\geq 1
, for
\alpha>0
and
D>0
we let

{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}([0,D]^{d})
be the set of
\alpha-
Hölderian functions on
[0,D]^{d}
. Clearly
{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}([0,D]^{d})\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
. We endow

{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}([0,D]^{d})
with the infinite norm
||\cdot||_{\infty}
and also with the norm
||\cdot||_{\alpha}

which is defined as

||f||_{\alpha}:=|f(0)|+\big[f\big]_{\alpha}:=|f(0)|+\sup_{{\bf x},{\bf s}\in[0,D]^{d},{\bf x}\neq{\bf s}}\frac{|f({\bf x})-f({\bf s})|}{|{\bf x}-{\bf s}|_{1}^{\alpha}}.

(B.0.1)

We also denote by
{\mathcal{B}}_{\alpha}(0,r)
the ball of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}([0,D]^{d})
of radius
r>0
for the
||\cdot||_{\alpha}
-norm
and by
\overline{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\alpha}(0,r)
its closure in
({\mathcal{C}}_{d},||\cdot||_{\infty})
.
Thanks to Arzela-Ascoli Theorem,
\overline{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\alpha}(0,r)
is a compact subset of
({\mathcal{C}}_{d},||\cdot||_{\infty})
.

At this stage we apply
[
20
, exercice 4.3.17]
to deduce from the assumption (
3.1.4
) that for every
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\gamma}{p})
, there exists

\widetilde{C}>0
such that

\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ E\Big[\big[L_{N}\big]_{\alpha}\Big]\leq\widetilde{C}.

(B.0.2)

At this stage, we pick an
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\gamma}{p})
.
Since
(L_{N}(0))_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is tight by assumption, we claim that for every
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists

M_{1}>0
such that for every
N\geq 1
it comes that
P(|L_{N}(0)|>M_{1})\leq{\varepsilon}/2
. Moreover (
B.0.2
)
tells us that for
M_{2}
large enough

\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,P(\big[L_{N}\big]_{\alpha}>M_{2})\leq\frac{E\Big[\big[L_{N}\big]_{\alpha}\Big]}{M_{2}}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}}{M_{2}}\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2},

(B.0.3)

and therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ P\Big(||L_{N}||_{\alpha}>M_{1}+M_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq P\Big(|L_{N}(0)|>M_{1}\Big)+P\Big(\big[L_{N}\big]_{\alpha}>M_{2}\Big)

(B.0.4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}+\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}={\varepsilon}.

We deduce from (

) that

(B.0.5)

Proof of Proposition
2.2.9

We set
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}:=\sigma(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N})
so that
({\mathcal{F}}_{N})_{N\geq n_{1}}
is a filtration adapted to the random variable sequence

(\gamma_{2,N}({\bf x}))_{N\geq n_{1}+1}
. Proposition

will be proven once we show that
(\gamma_{2,N}({\bf x}))_{N\geq n_{1}+1}
is a martingale
({\mathcal{F}}_{N})_{N\geq n_{1}}
-adapted and bounded in
L^{2}
. The fact that it is a martingale is easily checked, i.e.,

\displaystyle E\big[\gamma_{2,N+1}\,\mid\,{\mathcal{F}}_{N}\big]

\displaystyle=E\big[\gamma_{2,N}\,\mid\,{\mathcal{F}}_{N}\big]+E\bigg[\frac{G_{N+1}({\bf x})}{T_{N+1}^{\kappa}}\,\mid\,{\mathcal{F}}_{N}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2,N}+E[G_{N+1}({\bf x})]\,\,E\bigg[\,T_{N+1}^{-\kappa}\,\mid\,{\mathcal{F}}_{N}\bigg]=\gamma_{2,N},

(C.0.1)

where we have used the fact that
G_{N+1}({\bf x})
only depends on
Y_{N+1}
and is therefore independent of
\sigma({\mathcal{F}}_{N},T_{N+1})
and is centered
by a straightforward application of Lemma
A.0.1
.

Moreover for every
N\geq n_{1}+1
we can write

\displaystyle\Big\|\gamma_{2,N}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{G_{i}({\bf x})}{T_{i}^{\kappa}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=E\bigg[\Big(\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{G_{i}({\bf x})}{T_{i}^{\kappa}}\Big)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{N}E\Bigg[\frac{G_{i}({\bf x})^{2}}{T_{i}^{2\kappa}}\Bigg]+2\sum_{n_{1}+1\leq i<j\leq N}E\Bigg[\frac{G_{i}({\bf x})G_{j}({\bf x})}{(T_{i}T_{j})^{\kappa}}\Bigg].

(C.0.2)

We note that
G_{i}({\bf x}),G_{j}({\bf x})
and
\frac{1}{(T_{i}T_{j})^{\kappa}}
are independent and integrable. Moreover,
G_{i}({\bf x})
and
G_{j}({\bf x})
are bounded and centered and therefore the

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{N}\frac{G_{i}({\bf x})}{T_{i}^{\kappa}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

(C.0.3)

Thus, (

Appendix D
A specific example of variables in the domain of attraction
of Normal law

Lemma D.0.1
.

Let
\xi
be a random variable such that

{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\pm\xi>x}\right)}\sim_{x\to+\infty}c_{\pm}x^{-2}.

(D.0.1)

Then if
\mu={{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[{\xi}\right]}
,
\xi,\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n}
are i.i.d., the partial
sum
S_{n}=\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{n}
is in the domain of attraction of a
normal law. More precisely

{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\log n}}}(\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{n}-n\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\longmapsto}\limits^{d}_{n\to\infty}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,c_{+}+c_{-})\,.

(D.0.2)

Proof.

This Lemma is just a straightforward consequence of
Bingham et al. 1987

as we can derive the asymptotics of the truncated variance

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle={{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[{\xi^{2}{{\,\mathbf{1}}_{({{\left|{\xi}\right|}\leq x})}}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\int_{0}^{\infty}}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\xi^{2}>t,{\left|{\xi}\right|}\leq x}\right)}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x^{2}}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\sqrt{t}<\xi\leq x}\right)}\,dt+\int_{0}^{x^{2}}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\sqrt{t}<-\xi\leq x}\right)}\,dt

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du+2\int_{0}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<-\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du\,.

Given
\epsilon>0
, there exists
t_{0}
such that for
t\geq t_{0}
, we have

c_{+}(1-\epsilon)t^{-2}\leq{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\xi>t}\right)}\leq c_{+}(1+\epsilon)t^{-2}

(D.0.3)

Therefore, if
x>t_{0}
, then

\displaystyle 2\int_{t_{0}}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du

\displaystyle=2\int_{t_{0}}^{x}({{\mathbb{P}}\left({\xi>u}\right)}-{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\xi>x}\right)})u\,du

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{+}(1+\epsilon)\int_{t_{0}}^{x}\frac{du}{u}-2c_{+}x^{-2}(1-\epsilon)\int_{t_{0}}^{x}u\,du

\displaystyle=2c_{+}(1+\epsilon)\log(\frac{x}{t_{0}})-c_{+}(1-\epsilon)\frac{x^{2}-t_{0}^{2}}{x^{2}}\,.

Hence,

\limsup_{x\to+\infty}{\frac{1}{\log x}}2\int_{0}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du\leq 2c_{+}(1+\epsilon)\,,

(D.0.4)

and letting
\epsilon\to 0
yields

\limsup_{x\to+\infty}{\frac{1}{\log x}}2\int_{0}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du\leq 2c_{+}\,.

(D.0.5)

It is easy to prove similarly that

\liminf_{x\to+\infty}{\frac{1}{\log x}}2\int_{0}^{x}{{\mathbb{P}}\left({u<\xi\leq x}\right)}u\,du\geq 2c_{+}\,.

(D.0.6)

Eventually, we establish that

\lim_{x\to+\infty}{\frac{1}{\log x}}V(x)=2(c_{+}+c_{-})

(D.0.7)

and therefore
V(x)
is slowly varying.

It is now easy to see that the norming sequence
a_{n}=\sqrt{n\log n}
satisfies
Bingham et al. 1987
:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{n}{a_{n}^{2}}V(a_{n})=c_{+}+c_{-}\,.

Since
{{{\mathbb{E}}}\left[{\xi}\right]}=\mu
has first moment we can choose
b_{n}=n\mu
to
satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(S_{n}-b_{n})\underset{\text{law}}{=}\,{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}(c_{+}+c_{-}))\,.

Transforming
\xi
into
a\xi
for
a>0
, we see that the function

\sigma^{2}(c)
is linear
\sigma^{2}(c)=c\sigma^{2}_{1}
. In the case
\xi=\epsilon U^{-1/2}
with
{{\mathbb{P}}\left({\xi=\pm 1}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}
and
U
uniform
on
(0,1)
, we have
c_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}
and can compute exactly

\sigma^{2}_{1}=1
, by using a Taylor expansion of the caracteristic
function of
\xi
near
0
.
∎
