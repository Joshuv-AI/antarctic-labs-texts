Performance of LFSPRO prediction in TP53 mutation status for prospectively collected probands
Authors: Jessica L Corredor, Ruonan Li, Elissa B Dodd-Eaton, Jacynda Casey, Ashley H Woodson, Nam H Nguyen, Gang Peng, Angelica M Gutierrez, Banu K Arun, Wenyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenetic counseling and testing for germline mutations are essential for identifying individuals at increased risk for cancer. Pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in TP53 are diagnostic of Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS), a highly penetrant disorder with diverse, early-onset tumors. Current clinical guidelines, such as Chompret and classic criteria, provide frameworks for identifying individuals at risk for P/LP TP53 variants; however, genetic counselors often encounter people with features concerning for LFS that do not clearly meet established criteria, creating challenges for risk assessment and testing decisions. We evaluated whether LFSPRO, a Mendelian, family-history-based model that estimates the individual’s probability of harboring a deleterious TP53 variant, improves identification of individuals with LFS relative to guideline criteria. In a prospectively collected cohort of 178 probands who underwent clinical genetic counseling and germline TP53 testing, LFSPRO showed superior discrimination compared with Chompret criteria, with higher sensitivity (81% vs. 33%) and specificity (88% vs. 65%) and improved positive predictive values (PPVs: 0.53 vs. 0.14; negative predictive values [NPVs]: 0.96 vs. 0.85). Receiver operating characteristic analysis confirmed strong discriminatory performance (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.88). Calibration analysis using observed-to-expected ratios indicated good agreement between predicted and observed P/LP variant frequencies (observed/expected = 1.07). These findings demonstrate that LFSPRO outperforms traditional guideline-based criteria for identifying individuals with TP53 mutations in real-world clinical settings. By providing quantitative, well-calibrated TP53 P/LP variant probabilities rather than binary classifications, LFSPRO can enhance genetic counseling and support testing decisions, particularly for individuals who do not clearly meet existing criteria.Keywords: Mendelian models, family history, risk prediction, clinic-based individuals, Bayes calculation, Cancer prevention and early detection

Introduction

Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS [MIM: 151623]) is a rare hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in TP53 (MIM: 191170), a key tumor suppressor gene that safeguards the genome from mutations.1,2,3 Individuals with LFS face an increased risk of developing various cancers throughout their lifetime. The most common cancers seen in LFS families are sarcomas, breast cancer, leukemia, brain tumors, and adrenal cortical carcinoma.4,5 Penetrance estimates from the National Cancer Institute LFS cohort show 50% cumulative cancer risk by age 31 in females and age 46 in males, with lifetime risk approaching 100% by age 70.6 Cancer survivors in this population are at higher risk of developing additional primary cancers and treatment-related secondary cancers,7 making early and accurate identification of people with LFS essential for implementing appropriate surveillance and risk management strategies.

However, effective clinical management of LFS is challenging due to its rare nature, the overlap with other hereditary cancer syndromes, and the variable penetrance and phenotypic expression across families.8 Although early cancer detection, as demonstrated by the 2011 comprehensive Toronto screening protocol, can dramatically improve cancer survival rates in children and adults,9 predicting who will carry a TP53 P/LP variant remains difficult, making it challenging to start screening at the appropriate time. Currently, genetic testing assessment often relies on clinical classification schemes such as classic,10 Birch,5 Eeles,11 and Chompret criteria.12,13,14,15 These schemes include combinations of the proband’s cancer history, age of cancer onset, and limited family cancer history in first- and second-degree relatives to guide recommendations for genetic testing for LFS. However, for some rare cancer types, the above schemes may not appropriately assess a person’s risk, leaving providers to rely on disease-specific publications to make genetic testing recommendations, such as with pediatric osteosarcoma.16 In addition, with increased use of multigene panel testing, many individuals with less striking personal and family histories are found to have P/LP TP53 mutations. These people often do not meet established testing criteria, highlighting the need for supplemental quantitative tools to support clinical decision-making. In cancer-focused genetic counseling sessions, genetic counselors (GCs) use risk prediction models to estimate a person’s lifetime cancer risk, determine eligibility for surveillance and chemoprevention, and assess the likelihood of having other hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes, such as hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (MIM: 604370) and Lynch syndrome (MIM: 120435).17 However, such models did not previously exist for LFS. Given the heterogeneity in cancer presentation of LFS and the significant impact of early identification on personalized surveillance and treatment strategies, a comprehensive risk model is essential to help GCs and other healthcare providers communicate more effectively with individuals and families.

To address this gap, Peng et al.18 developed LFSPRO, a comprehensive statistical framework designed to provide clinicians the ability to determine both the probability of having a TP53 P/LP variant and risk predictions for developing LFS-related cancers. LFSPRO takes the complete family history into consideration (Figure 1A; example input data structure shown in Tables S1 and S2 and output data shown in Table S3), modeling the exact Mendelian inheritance relationship between family members, providing the necessary mathematical foundation for a more refined probability calculation than models based on summary features of the family history.19 While previous studies have demonstrated LFSPRO’s strong performance in both research and retrospective clinical datasets,18,20,21,22 representing an almost complete cycle of risk prediction model development (Figure 1B), its real-world clinical utility to facilitate GCs’ communication and clinical decision-making in the time of expanded referrals is yet to be fully evaluated. In this prospective study, we assessed the accuracy of LFSPRO in predicting TP53 germline mutation status compared to established criteria in a real-time clinical genetic counseling setting. In such settings, the majority of referred individuals will test negative for TP53 mutations, a feature distinct from that observed in retrospective LFS studies.22 To further evaluate its practical value as a decision-support tool, four experienced GCs were invited to use LFSPRO following standard genetic counseling sessions with individuals suspected of having LFS. Counselors reviewed the model’s output alongside their own clinical assessments and completed a structured survey providing their detailed feedback, including the model’s concordance with clinical judgment and its perceived utility.

Methods

Study design and study population

This prospective study was designed to assess LFSPRO’s clinical utility in a real-world genetic counseling setting at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (UT MDACC). This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the UT MDACC (PA14-0039-MDACC and 2020-1214-MDACC). A waiver of informed consent was approved by the UT MDACC IRB because the study did not directly affect participants’ care and did not involve direct contact with participants.

Building on previous validations that demonstrated LFSPRO’s strong performance in research-based (RB) and retrospective clinical datasets, this study aims to evaluate its performance in predicting TP53 P/LP germline variant status compared to established criteria in a prospective clinical cohort and to assess GCs’ experience implementing LFSPRO as part of their standard genetic counseling practice.

From December 2021 to March 2025, probands who underwent genetic counseling at MDACC and were identified as having clinical features “concerning for LFS” were included. Individuals were labeled as concerning for LFS if they (1) met classic and/or Chompret criteria for LFS testing or (2) were deemed concerning for LFS by a certified GC based on their personal/family history, despite not meeting existing criteria. The latter category included individuals with early-onset LFS-spectrum tumors but small or limited family history, somatic testing suggestive of germline TP53 variants, multiple primary cancers (MPCs), and/or extensive family history of childhood and/or rare cancers. At MDACC, GCs practice independently but maintain a collaborative team environment for challenging scenarios. For rare conditions such as LFS, counselors rely on published guidelines to guide testing, but this is also supplemented by clinical judgment to identify at-risk individuals. This study represents a typical GC workflow and testing process at this institution. Notably, many families meeting the classic LFS criteria at MDACC have been previously identified and tested positive for LFS. These families were already followed up routinely for cancer screening and hence were not included in this prospective evaluation. This is a major distinction between our study cohort and any previous LFS cohort studies.6,22,23

Data collection

After receiving standard genetic counseling with an MDACC GC, people identified as concerning for LFS were flagged in Progeny, an internal personal and family history database. GCs on the study team then completed a comprehensive chart review of these individuals and assessed them using LFSPRO. Data collected included LFSPRO-predicted probability of carrying a TP53 mutation, whether the person met Chompret and/or classic criteria, decision to test, and genetic test results when available. Additional data documented for each participant included demographic information (age and sex assigned at birth), personal cancer history (type of cancer, age at diagnosis, and multiple primaries), and family cancer history in first- and second-degree relatives.

Four MDACC GCs who most frequently see individuals for possible LFS were invited to use LFSPRO for those they considered concerning for LFS after completing their standard genetic counseling session and formulating testing recommendations. This ensured that LFSPRO results did not influence clinical testing recommendations or decision-making. After each LFSPRO run, these counselors completed a survey about their experience and assessment of LFSPRO’s clinical utility for that specific individual. Current criteria for LFS genetic testing recommendations

LFS genetic testing recommendations are primarily guided by clinical criteria designed to identify individuals most likely to have LFS. The most widely used criteria in genetic counseling include the classic LFS criteria and the Chompret criteria (see Box 1). The classic criteria, originally defined by Li and Fraumeni in 1988, have high specificity (91.0%–98.1%) but low sensitivity (25.0%–40.0%), making them the most stringent approach.24 Chompret criteria were developed by Chompret et al. and revisited in 2009 and 2015. Clinical cohort studies have demonstrated a sensitivity of 82%–95% while maintaining a reasonable specificity of 47%–58%.14,15,25,26 These criteria guide GCs in evaluating a proband’s personal cancer history, age of cancer onset, and limited cancer patterns in first- and second-degree relatives to determine eligibility for genetic testing. While both frameworks have been valuable for identifying LFS candidates, only one of the probands in our study cohort met the classic LFS criteria, highlighting potential limitations in real-world clinical settings. Consequently, our analysis focused exclusively on comparing LFSPRO performance against the Chompret criteria, which have become the more clinically relevant standard in contemporary practice.

LFSPRO statistical framework

LFSPRO was developed as a Mendelian risk prediction model to estimate the probability that an individual carries a deleterious TP53 mutation based on detailed family cancer history.18 The model adopts a Bayesian formulation in which the TP53 P/LP variant probability is obtained by updating the population prevalence with the likelihood of the observed pedigree and cancer diagnoses:

Here, G0 denotes the genotype of the counselee, H represents the family cancer history, and g∈{0,1} corresponds to the possible genotype states (wild type or mutation positive, including both heterozygous and homozygous mutation genotypes). The prior probability Pr(G0) is set to the population prevalence of TP53 mutations (0.0006, estimated from a Myriad Genetics study reporting 1 in 1,600),18 following the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for homozygotes. The likelihood term Pr(H|G0) represents the probability of observing the family cancer history given the counselee’s genotype, obtained by integrating over all possible genotype configurations of relatives while incorporating Mendelian inheritance and age-specific cancer penetrance. Because the number of genotype configurations grows exponentially with pedigree size, LFSPRO employs the Elston-Stewart or peeling algorithm,27 which recursively factors the pedigree into anterior (ancestral) and posterior (descendant) components conditional on a pivot individual and marginalizes over unobserved genotypes as individuals are peeled off. This recursion enables efficient likelihood evaluation in extended pedigrees without enumerating all possible genotype states. LFSPRO also models de novo TP53 mutations, which arise spontaneously in the germline even when neither parent has the TP53 variant. Such events account for an estimated 7%–20%28 of individuals with LFS and are therefore clinically significant. In the model, they are represented by a transmission probability of 2α, where α is the per-allele mutation rate. By incorporating both inherited and de novo events, LFSPRO yields more accurate variant probability estimates across diverse pedigrees.

LFSPRO was originally developed and externally validated using multiple independent cohorts totaling 867 families with over 1,300 tested individuals, including pediatric and adult sarcoma cohorts and the National Cancer Institute LFS cohort.18 These are RB cohorts (Figure 1B). Building on this foundation, LFSPRO has been enhanced through the integration of refined penetrance models. The cancer-specific (CS)29 and MPC30 models utilize distinct statistical methodologies to estimate age-at-onset penetrance in people with P/LP TP53 mutations. The CS model employs a competing risk approach with cause-specific hazard functions to estimate age-specific penetrance for different cancer types as the first primary tumor, while the MPC model uses recurrent event models based on non-homogeneous Poisson processes to estimate the penetrance of subsequent malignancies. These model extensions were developed and trained using 189 unrelated families (17 TP53 positive and 172 TP53 negative) from MDACC, followed longitudinally from 1944 to 1982. Families were ascertained through probands diagnosed with pediatric sarcoma rather than LFS clinical criteria. While this sampling scheme leads to oversampling of affected individuals, the models explicitly adjust for the resulting ascertainment bias by employing an ascertainment-corrected joint (ACJ) likelihood framework, with an inverse-probability weighting scheme based on the proband’s phenotype. Using Bayesian semiparametric estimation, the CS model estimates age-specific penetrance for breast cancer, sarcoma, and a combined category of other LFS-spectrum malignancies, while the MPC model pools these cancer types to assess the cumulative risk of any subsequent primary tumor. Validation across multiple independent cohorts has demonstrated that LFSPRO achieves better sensitivity and specificity for identifying people with TP53 mutations,20,21 underscoring its clinical utility in LFS family risk assessment and management. By providing individualized, quantitative probabilities rather than binary classifications, LFSPRO facilitates more refined genetic counseling than traditional clinical criteria. Finally, we recently used a retrospective clinical-based (RCB) cohort at MD Anderson to validate the performance of both MPC and CS models.22

Clinical implementation

These statistical models are integrated into LFSPROShiny,31 an interactive R/Shiny application that provides GCs and healthcare providers with a user-friendly interface for completing the probands’ personal and family information and generating individualized TP53 variant probability assessments. LFSPROShiny preserves the underlying statistical algorithms and validated penetrance models from LFSPRO while adding interactive visualization and accessibility features. Examples of the required input data structure for pedigree information and cancer history are provided in Tables S1 and S2, with example outputs shown in Table S3.

For this study, GCs utilized LFSPROShiny to facilitate clinical implementation. The interface allows GCs to efficiently enter individual-level information from MDACC electronic health records to compute the probability of a TP53 mutation and generate individualized risk assessments. While GCs can select between the CS and MPC models depending on the individual being evaluated, the MPC model was set as the default due to its better performance compared to the CS model in prior validation studies.22,31 Furthermore, we used a binary classification threshold of 0.2 for LFSPRO risk scores, as established in prior work,18 where probands scoring ≥ 0.2 were classified as screening positive for a potential TP53 P/LP variant.18

Survey design and administration

To evaluate GCs’ experience using LFSPRO in clinical practice, we developed a structured REDCap (research electronic data capture)32,33 survey instrument addressing four key domains: (1) concordance between LFSPRO predictions and standard clinical assessment, (2) additive value of LFSPRO beyond conventional genetic counseling, (3) comfort level with communicating LFSPRO results to individuals, and (4) practical applications of LFSPRO-derived risk predictions in counseling. GCs taking these surveys could select to complete a survey for (1) an individual undergoing TP53 germline testing, (2) a person with a TP53 germline variant that wanted to utilize LFSPRO’s cancer risk prediction, or (3) a combination of TP53 germline testing, risk assessment, and cancer risk prediction. Each survey contained both structured quantitative measures and open-ended response fields for GCs to provide further feedback on their use of LFSPRO.

Statistical analysis

The performance of LFSPRO and Chompret criteria was evaluated using multiple complementary approaches. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed through sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity measured the proportion of individuals with a TP53 mutation correctly identified by each model, while specificity measured the proportion of individuals without TP53 mutations correctly identified as negative. PPV assessed how often a positive prediction was correct, and NPV assessed how often a negative prediction was correct, providing measures of clinical reliability for each prediction outcome. Discriminatory ability was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) values and 95% CIs calculated for both LFSPRO and Chompret criteria. An AUC of 0.5 represents no discriminatory ability (equivalent to random chance), while 1.0 represents perfect discrimination.

Statistical significance of associations was assessed using two-sided Fisher’s exact test, which was selected due to the limited sample size with cell counts below 5 in some contingency table cells. This non-parametric test was used to evaluate (1) the association between model-based classifications (LFSPRO and Chompret criteria) and actual TP53 mutation status and (2) the association between prediction methods and GCs’ clinical judgment. The null hypothesis in each case was that no association exists between the compared variables, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Confusion matrices were visualized as heatmaps to illustrate the distribution of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives for each prediction method. For the GC survey data, descriptive statistics were calculated as percentages for categorical variables.

Results

Cohort description

From December 2021 to March 2025, a total of 178 probands were included in this concordance study (Table S4). The cohort comprised 115 (64.6%) individuals assigned female at birth and 63 (35.4%) individuals assigned male at birth, with a mean age of 30 years. Among the 148 probands who completed TP53 genetic testing, 23 (15.5%) tested positive for a P/LP TP53 germline variant, including one with a mosaic variant identified in two separate tissue types and one with low variant allele frequency (VAF) suggestive of possible mosaicism. The majority (n = 125, 84.5%) tested negative for germline TP53 variants, though this group included one individual with a TP53 variant at 13% VAF that could not be detected in a second tissue source and another with a variant of uncertain significance in TP53 that was likely mosaic. Thirty individuals were categorized as “no response” to account for those who passively declined testing (e.g., by not returning test kits) or remained undecided, reflecting the practical challenges of genetic testing completion in a clinical setting. Since the Bayesian Mendelian model used in LFSPRO does not account for mosaicism, probands with possible mosaic TP53 variants were excluded from all downstream analysis. This prospectively collected clinical cohort represents a unique dataset of individuals flagged by GCs as concerning for LFS in real-world practice. The 15.5% mutation detection rate and inclusion of complex scenarios with mosaicism and uncertain variants provide an ideal test set for evaluating LFSPRO’s clinical utility.

LFSPRO outperforms existing criteria

Among individuals with germline P/LP TP53 variants (n = 21), 15 (71.4%) had at least one primary cancer, and 6 (28.6%) had at least two primary cancers. Of these individuals, only 7 (33%) met Chompret criteria for genetic testing, while 17 (81.0%) were identified by LFSPRO with a risk score (probability of having a P/LP TP53 variant) ≥ 0.2. In the mutation-negative group (n = 123), 112 (91.1%) had at least one primary cancer, and 9 (7.3%) had at least two primary cancers. Within this group, 43 (35.0%) met Chompret criteria, while only 15 (12.2%) had an LFSPRO risk score ≥ 0.2. Alternatively, 80 (65.0%) individuals did not meet Chompret criteria, and 108 (87.8%) had LFSPRO scores below the 0.2 threshold. See Figure 2A for detailed confusion matrices. Also, a two-sided Fisher’s exact test analysis confirmed LFSPRO’s statistically significant concordance with genetic testing results (p < 0.001), whereas Chompret criteria failed to demonstrate a significant association (p = 1.00). A corresponding Sankey diagram (Figure 2B) further compares the classification flow of probands by LFSPRO and Chompret criteria against genetic testing results.

LFSPRO demonstrated significantly better discriminatory performance over the conventional Chompret criteria for identifying people with TP53 P/LP mutations. LFSPRO achieved a higher sensitivity of 0.810 (95% CI: 0.600–0.923) compared to Chompret’s 0.333 (95% CI: 0.172–0.546) and better specificity of 0.878 (95% CI: 0.809–0.925) vs. 0.650 (95% CI: 0.563–0.730), as illustrated in Figure 2C. LFSPRO demonstrated substantially higher PPV at 0.531 (95% CI: 0.364–0.691) compared to Chompret’s 0.140 (95% CI: 0.070–0.262), indicating greater clinical utility for correctly identifying people with mutations. Similarly, LFSPRO’s NPV of 0.964 (95% CI: 0.912–0.986) outperformed Chompret’s 0.851 (95% CI: 0.765–0.909), providing greater confidence in ruling out TP53 mutations when classified as negative. Specifically, among the 43 individuals who fulfilled Chompret criteria but tested negative for TP53 mutations, LFSPRO correctly predicted 37 (86%) as negative, largely reducing the false positive rate. While LFSPRO introduced 9 additional false positives among individuals who did not meet Chompret criteria, the overall false positive rate remained lower (15 vs. 43). The ROC curve as shown in Figure 2D further demonstrated LFSPRO’s discriminatory ability with AUC scores of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.95) for the MPC model and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.77–0.91) for the CS model. Calibration analyses using observed-to-expected (O/E) ratios further supported model validity. The MPC model, which was used as the default for TP53 mutation probability estimation based on prior validation studies, demonstrated good calibration (O/E: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.80–1.34), with close agreement between predicted and observed TP53 mutation frequencies. The CS model showed modest underestimation of these probabilities (O/E: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.09–1.84).

These findings demonstrate that LFSPRO outperforms Chompret criteria in identifying individuals with TP53 P/LP variants, showing enhanced ability to predict true positives while reducing false positives, and strong alignment with genetic testing results. This suggests LFSPRO’s potential to assist in clinical decision-making and assist individuals’ decisions on genetic testing.

GCs report positive experience with LFSPRO

Beyond comparing LFSPRO with established clinical criteria, we evaluated its concordance with GC’s clinical judgment, the actual decision-making standard in practice. Four experienced GCs completed 64 surveys after counseling sessions. None of the probands in these sessions met classic LFS criteria, and only 20.3% met Chompret criteria. In practice, however, GCs recommended germline testing for 77% of probands, underscoring that current criteria fail to capture many individuals whom clinicians consider appropriate for testing. Within these individuals, LFSPRO provided additional decision support in 16%, highlighting its potential to complement clinical judgment when guideline frameworks alone are insufficient. Furthermore, GCs indicated they were comfortable sharing LFSPRO results with individuals in 75% of sessions, most often providing generalized summaries (39.1%) or specific risk estimates (35.9%), as shown in Figure 3A.

Fisher’s exact test demonstrated a significant association between LFSPRO predictions and GCs’ clinical judgment (p = 0.01), with differential agreement rates when LFSPRO predicted above the cutoff (44.4%) vs. below the cutoff (86.0%). In contrast, Chompret criteria showed no significant association with counselor intuition (p = 1.0), yielding similar agreement rates regardless of whether criteria were met (Figure 3B). GC experience and clinical judgment were not incorporated as features in the LFSPRO model, making this concordance an independent validation of the model’s ability to capture clinically relevant risk patterns through objective variables alone. Together, these findings suggest that LFSPRO is usable, aligns more closely with clinical expertise than guideline criteria, and holds promise for integration into routine practice to support personalized risk assessment in LFS. Discussion

This prospective study provides a clinical validation of LFSPRO, a personalized risk prediction tool for LFS. Compared to the widely used Chompret and classic criteria, LFSPRO demonstrated strong predictive performance for individualized TP53 mutation probability estimates, with significantly higher sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. AUC analysis further confirmed LFSPRO’s strong discriminatory ability, and its predictions were significantly concordant with genetic testing results, in contrast to Chompret criteria, which showed no significant concordance. These findings position LFSPRO as a robust quantitative tool to support GCs and clinicians in identifying individuals at risk for carrying P/LP TP53 variants.

Chompret and classic criteria have long guided genetic testing for LFS and are endorsed in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. However, these criteria rely on specific and limited clinical features and can fail to capture individuals with atypical or limited family histories. As a result, individuals who would benefit from testing may be overlooked, particularly when insurance coverage is tied to satisfying formal guideline criteria. LFSPRO potentially addresses this gap by providing clinicians with individualized, probabilistic estimates of mutation status and specific cancer risk based on detailed family and personal history, offering a more flexible and precise quantitative metric to complement professional judgment, which may facilitate access to testing and lifelong screening protocols. By providing a pedigree-based quantitative assessment, LFSPRO aligns with well-established Mendelian models such as BRCAPRO34 and BOADICEA35 that are already integrated into standard genetic counseling practice.

Beyond assessing diagnostic accuracy, this study evaluated LFSPRO’s impact on clinical decision-making through 64 genetic counseling surveys. In the majority of sessions, counselors reported that the person (77%) did not meet standard testing criteria but were still recommending testing based on clinical judgment, with LFSPRO providing additional decision support in 16% of these sessions. Importantly, LFSPRO predictions showed significant concordance with GC clinical judgment, while current standard criteria did not. These findings underscore LFSPRO’s potential to complement clinical expertise and facilitate more personalized genetic counseling, particularly for individuals who fall outside established testing frameworks.

LFSPRO also offers longitudinal cancer risk estimates at 5-, 10-, and 15-year intervals, as validated in previous retrospective studies, enabling GCs and other providers to deliver more personalized risk counseling. These individualized projections can help individuals better anticipate their likelihood of carrying a TP53 mutation or developing cancer and may assist in making decisions about genetic testing and surveillance. Personalized risk communication has been shown to improve understanding and promote informed decision-making.36 In related clinical contexts, more accurate risk estimates have also been associated with reduced anxiety and cancer worry, particularly in people with high baseline anxiety.37 We expect that LFSPRO has similar potential to improve not only clinical accuracy but also the individual proband’s experience in LFS counseling.

Study limitations

Our study highlights the improved sensitivity and specificity of LFSPRO over existing clinical testing guidelines; however, several limitations remain. LFSPRO produced both false positive and false negative predictions. Among the 15 false positives (Table S5), three involved unaffected family members undergoing predictive testing for known familial TP53 variants, where LFSPRO assigned TP53 mutation probabilities of 20%–70% based on Mendelian inheritance and family cancer history, although these individuals tested negative. These scenarios may inflate the false positive rate and underestimate the model’s true performance in diagnostic testing contexts. One false positive involved a proband with clinical features of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1),38 most likely contributing to a strong family history misattributed to TP53 by LFSPRO. The remaining scenarios featured early-onset LFS-spectrum cancers or strong family histories without identified TP53 mutations. Among the four false negatives (Table S6), one individual carried the c.1010G>A (GenBank: NM_000546.6) (p.Arg337His) variant, a Brazilian founder mutation with reduced penetrance.39,40 The remaining three false negatives had confirmed germline TP53 mutations but limited family cancer history. These examples illustrate another limitation of LFSPRO, as the model relies on individually reported personal and family histories collected in clinical settings. Its accuracy may be limited in scenarios with incomplete family histories or when a de novo TP53 mutation occurs in an individual without prior cancer or sufficient family history. These limitations are shared by current clinical testing guidelines and highlight opportunities for further refinement of LFSPRO to improve its utility in diverse clinical scenarios. Finally, germline status confirmation through additional tissue testing was not uniformly performed for all TP53-mutation-positive results, representing a limitation in definitive variant classification.

Future directions

This study demonstrates that GCs feel comfortable using LFSPRO in clinical practice and find it particularly helpful for guiding testing decisions for families that do not meet standard criteria. To broaden its impact, future work should evaluate its usability among non-genetics providers, particularly in community or resource-limited settings where expertise in LFS may be scarce, and incorporate structured training to support implementation. The results presented in this work, including LFSPRO’s strong performance compared to existing criteria and alignment with clinical judgment, may also help encourage individuals, their family members, and providers to participate in future clinical trials that incorporate LFSPRO.

In parallel, methodological advances continue to expand the underlying statistical framework. A new unified Bayesian semiparametric model jointly accommodates recurrent and competing risks and extends applicability to broader cancer survivor populations.41 Future efforts will focus on validating this model in large independent cohorts and integrating it into LFSPRO, thereby enhancing its clinical utility across diverse settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this prospective study demonstrates LFSPRO’s real-world clinical accuracy in identifying people with P/LP TP53 mutations and strong alignment with GCs’ clinical judgment. The tool’s potential integration into current standard clinical practice can facilitate GCs’ work by providing personalized risk estimates that enhance decision-making and support effective communication with individuals and families, particularly in complex situations where traditional criteria provide limited guidance.
