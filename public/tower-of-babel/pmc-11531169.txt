Intestinal nanoparticle delivery and cellular response: a review of the bidirectional nanoparticle-cell interplay in mucosa based on physiochemical properties
Authors: Yu Wang, Yilei Mo, Yingwei Sun, Jing Li, Yu An, Nianping Feng, Ying Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Orally administered nanocarriers play an important role in improving druggability, promoting intestinal absorption, and enhancing therapeutic applications for the treatment of local and systemic diseases. However, the delivering efficiency and cell response in mucosa to orally administered nanocarriers is affected by the physiological environment and barriers in the gastrointestinal tract, the physicochemical properties of the nanocarriers, and their bidirectional interactions. Goblet cells secrete and form extracellular mucus, which hinders the movement of nanoparticles. Meanwhile, intestinal epithelial cells may absorb the NPs, allowing for their transcytosis or degradation. Conversely, nanoparticle-induced toxicity may occur as a biological response to the nanoparticle exposure. Additionally, immune response and cell functions in secretions such as mucin, peptide, and cytokines may also be altered. In this review, we discuss the bidirectional interactions between nanoparticles and cells focusing on enterocytes and goblet cells, M cells, and immune cells in the mucosa according to the essential role of intestinal epithelial cells and their crosstalk with immune cells. Furthermore, we discuss the recent advances of how the physiochemical properties of nanoparticles influence their interplay, delivery, and fate in intestinal mucosa. Understanding the fate of nanoparticles with different physiochemical properties from the perspective of their interaction with cells in mucosa provides essential support for the development, rational design, potency maximation, and application of advanced oral nanocarrier delivery systems.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The oral route is an important route of administration of drugs for treating various local and systemic diseases with desirable patient compliance [1]. However, the development of oral formulations faces many challenges, including poor druggability, low and variable absorption, and limited therapeutic efficacy. Oral NPs have a carrier function, promote intestinal absorption, improve oral bioavailability, prevent diseases, and have therapeutic applications [2, 3]. Therefore, oral nanoparticles (NPs) development has drawn considerable attention over the past two decades [4, 5]. Various types of orally administered nanocarriers (OAN) have emerged such as organic nanocarrires with lipids or polymers as essential components, inorganic nanoparticles (pure metals, metal oxides, semiconductor materials, calcium phosphates) and hybrid nanoparticles [1, 6]. Investigations on the interaction between OAN and intestinal cells are especially important for understanding their fate in vivo and improving delivery efficiency.

In this review, we discuss our most updated understanding of the bidirectional interactions between OAN and intestinal mucosa cells, focusing on enterocytes as well as goblet, M, and immune cells. Firstly, we summarize the course and fate of NPs in the enteric microenvironment upon encountering mucosa cells. We then summarize the bio-response of cells to NPs in both normal and disease conditions. Furthermore, we comprehensively review the factors influencing the nanoparticle-cell interaction from a physicochemical point of view.

Gastrointestinal tract cells that interact with orally administered nanoparticles

The first point of contact of OAN in the body is the oral cavity. Saliva in the oral cavity contains lysozymes, digestive enzymes, and organic ions, and others, which play a role in food digestion, bacterial reduction, and maintenance of the stability of the oral environment. The stomach is an acidic environment (pH 0.9–1.5) containing various digestive enzymes, such as pepsin, trypsin, and amylase [7]. Desirable NPs should resist these harsh environments and degradation by electrolytes, pH, and digestive enzymes before they can successfully be available at the surface of intestinal mucosal cells. The gastrointestinal tract (GT), the main part of the human body involved in digestion and absorption, consists of enterocytes as well as goblet, Paneth, microfold cells (M cells), and enteroendocrine cells, which constitute the gastrointestinal barrier that protects the human body against pathogenic microorganisms. The GT promotes digestion and absorption, forms a mucus barrier, participates in the immune response, and secretes hormones and mucins [8]. Thus, the different cell types that constitute the GT interact with one another and work in coordination to maintain its normal function and structure, ensuring that the body can digest and absorb nutrients efficiently and maintain the immune balance. From the perspective of the oral NPs delivery system, the different cells in the GT modulate NPs behavior and affect its movement, translocation, targeting, and immune response in different ways [Fig. 1; processed using Adobe Illustrator (Adobe, San Jose, CA)]. Since this review focuses on the bidirectional interaction between OAN and intestinal mucosal cells, the outcome of NP response to the oral cavity and oropharynx environment, including pH, enzymes, and ions, is not discussed in more detail. Readers interested in these aspects are referred to the excellent reviews [9, 10].

Bidirectional interaction between orally administered nanocarriers and intestinal cells

On initial interaction with the mucosa, the NPs may get trapped within the mucus layer secreted by goblet cells and adhere to the components in the mucus. They may also diffuse through the mucus layer and be taken up by the enterocytes. The NPs are then transported through the epithelial cells or effluxed out of the cells by transcytosis or exocytosis, respectively. Some NPs may be toxic to cells, while others might boost the immune response. These processes involve bidirectional interactions between the nanocarriers and intestinal cells. A full understanding of the mechanisms involved in these interactions is beneficial for the rational design of nanocarriers, promotion of delivery efficiency, and reduction of side effects. Nanoparticle fates upon encountering intestinal cells

Nanoparticle endocytosis, exocytosis, and transcytosis by intestinal cells

Nanoparticle endocytosis is a continuous process in which nanocarriers are delivered to vesicles, such as endosomes, lysosomes to initiate different endocytosis pathways [11]. The processes and pathways of endocytosis have been thoroughly reviewed in previous publications [11, 12]. Briefly, the process of endocytosis of OAN by enterocytes can be categorized as (1) clathrin-mediated endocytosis: articulin and clathrin induce cell membrane invagination to form vesicles that encapsulate NPs and then fuse with early endosomes for translocation to lysosomes or the plasma membrane [13]; (2) caveolin-mediated endocytosis: caveolin from the lipid raft structural domains of the plasma membrane forms invaginated fossa vesicles, which in turn are delivered to the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [14]; (3) clathrin- and caveolin-independent endocytosis: the transition from early endosomes to late endosomes is independent of clathrin- and caveolin-mediated direct cell membrane depression [15]; (4) micropinocytosis: receptor tyrosine kinases activate actin, generating plasma membrane folds to form vesicles that are capable of engulfing NPs up to the micrometer scale [16]; and (5) phagocytosis. Depending on the size of clathrin protein vesicles [17], the energy dependence of endocytosed proteins [18], and the role of lysosomal degradation [19, 20], NPs are ingested via different endocytic pathways [17–20]. Typical molecular pathways involved in endocytosis

The intracellular fate of different nanocarriers varies owing to the surface properties of the OAN themselves or the associated ligand modifications. Intracellular transport of OAN often involves multiple pathways. Nanocarriers can be transported to lysosomes or the ER through different pathways, contributing to the complexity of the intracellular transport process. Among the classical pathways involved in the intracellular transport of NPs are the endosome-lysosome, endosome-Golgi, and Golgi-ER pathways [33, 34]. Typically, OAN taken up via the clathrin-mediated pathway target lysosomes and fuse with them to form secondary endosomes, allowing the nanocarriers in the endosomes to be metabolized by enzymatic hydrolysis [12].

As major endomembrane systems in the cell, the ER and Golgi apparatus are involved in the sorting and transportation of intracellular substances [35]. Therefore, OAN that reach the ER and Golgi can be transported out of the cell to accomplish transcellular transport and protect the NPs from hydrolysis [36]. NPs endocytosed by cells are translocated first to the early endosome (pH 6.0), then to the late endosome (pH 5.0), and finally to the lysosome (pH 5.0). Lysosomes contain a variety of degradative enzymes (e.g., nuclease and phosphatase), which may degrade the NPs if they lack the ability to quickly escape from the lysosome. The caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway allows the formation of pH-neutral vesicles that mediate retrograde translocation from the Golgi to the ER, bypassing the endosomal-lysosomal transport pathway and thus preventing the hydrolysis of NPs by lysosomes [37]. These vesicles direct their nanocarriers to the ER and Golgi apparatus.

Intracellular transport following the macropinocytosis uptake pathway involves the ER and Golgi apparatus [38]. For example, Li et al. found that macropinocytosis is the main pathway by which crosslinked α-lactalbumin short nanotubes are absorbed by enterocytes and transported to the ER [38]. Transporter pathways involving the ER and Golgi apparatus enable nanocarriers to avoid lysosomal hydrolysis for translocation out of the cell, which provides the possibility of studying NPs targeting the ER or Golgi apparatus in cells. Zhang et al. found that modifying poly (ethylene glycol)-block-poly (ε-caprolactone) nanomicelles with signal peptides to direct them to the Golgi network resulted in higher transmembrane transport efficiency than directing nanomicelles through the basolateral plasma membrane pathway [39]. Meanwhile, this pathway may further improve drug absorption and transport efficiency in the intestinal epithelial cells (IEC) [39].

In addition to targeting lysosomes, the Golgi apparatus, or the ER, OAN may also escape from endosomes or lysosomes and be transported into the cytoplasm, which usually arises from the formation of pores, rupture, or fusion of membranes occurring in the nanocarriers [40]. In some cases, OAN can avoid hydrolysis by lysosomes through the application of endosomal escape agents. Xu et al. found that the endosomal escape effect of NPs was enhanced by loading their lipid shells with endosomal escape agents, resulting in improved enterocyte absorption of encapsulated insulin [41]. Thus, promoting endosomal escape and overcoming lysosomal degradation of OAN is one strategy to achieve effective drug therapy.

Generally, NPs undergo exocytosis through the following mechanisms: (1) energy-independent passive diffusion, (2) a fast cycling pathway capable of fusing with the cell membrane, (3) lysosomal pathway, and (4) ER-Golgi pathway [42–45] OAN in the form of vesicles take the form of endosomes, and the acidified endosomes may fuse with lysosomes, at which point the vesicle contents are digested and degraded by enzymes. Thus, NPs can bypass lysosomes by exocytosis via the ER-Golgi pathway. Xing et al. found that L-cysteine-modified NPs could bypass lysosomes to promote the exocytosis of PLGA-cored NPs with L-cysteine surface modification during Golgi transport, showing highly efficient transcytosis [46]. In addition, the fusion of components in recovered endosomes with the plasma membrane can be promoted by disrupting lipid rafts, thus enhancing the exocytotic effect of PLGA NPs [47].

Chyle-mediated exocytosis occurs through the fusion of chylomicrons with the basolateral cell membrane and the release of the contained particles into the interstitial space. After basolateral exocytosis, chylomicrons are selectively transported to the lymphatic vessels. Thus, lipid-based NPs coupled with long-chain fatty acid NPs may utilize the chylomicron pathway to transport them to lymphatic vessels and ultimately to systemic circulation [48, 49]. In addition, drugs encapsulated by glycocholic acid-conjugated polystyrene NPs can prevent hepatic first-pass metabolism via the chylomicron pathway, which is accomplished through lymphatic circulation [50].

Notably, NPs undergo exocytosis in the apical and basolateral membranes of IEC [51]. NPs with dominant apical-side exocytosis may result in oral nanocarriers that typically exhibit poor overall trans-epithelial transport efficiency. It has been found that the apical-side exocytosis rate accounts for approximately 80% of the transcellular action of NPs [52]. Therefore, basolateral exocytosis of enterocytes and the inhibition of apical-side exocytosis of enterocytes by OAN would improve their delivery efficiency.

Effect of extracellular mucus secreted by goblet cells on the transferring fate of the nanocarriers

Goblet cells specialize in the secretion and production of mucin to form a mucus layer that covers the GT and defends it against the invasion of foreign microorganisms. Mucus is constantly renewed by the continuous flow of body fluids in the GT, which improves the efficiency of foreign substance removal [53, 54]. This poses a challenge for the systemic administration of oral NPs. Because of the entanglement of mucin, dense fibrous webs with an average diameter of 100–500 nm are formed in the mucin structure, limiting the entry of NPs [55, 56]. Therefore, NPs must resist the electrostatic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen-bonding forces generated when they come into contact with mucus to accomplish mucus penetration and cellular uptake [53]. Generally, a negatively charged mucus layer can trap nanocarriers via electrostatic forces. The mechanisms by which nanocarriers interact with the mucus layer are classified into different types based on their modes of interaction: mucous adhesion systems, mucus permeative systems, and mucolysis systems. The key mechanisms underlying the interaction between OAN and the mucus barrier are summarized in Table 2. These systems alter the interaction between nanocarriers and mucin by changing the surface properties, such as hydrophilicity and charge of specific materials [69]. In addition, biomimetic strategies can alter the interactions between nanocarriers and mucins. Furthermore, the release of mucolytic enzymes has been shown to be effective in facilitating mucus diffusion of OAN. The effect of mucus on the fate of nanocarriers in enterocytes has been comprehensively described [70, 71]. In addition to the above-mentioned interactions of nanocarriers with the mucus barrier, there are some advanced NPs that can shift from utilizing a mucous adhesion system to a mucus permeative system. Furthermore, robotic NPs are applied to cross the mucus barrier, deliver drugs, or examine the GT [72].

Key interaction of nanocarriers with extracellular mucus

Paracellular transport

Nanoparticle translocation within intestinal cells can also occur through the paracellular pathway by entering the cellular space directly through tight junctions between epithelial cells. An important component of the paracellular pathway, tight junctions (TJ), also known as atretic zonules, are regions of TJ between neighboring intestinal cells. The size of the enterocyte paracellular gap is approximately 0.3–1.0 nm [73]. Large solutes with molecular radii exceeding this range are typically excluded. Therefore, the transcellular transport of NPs remains debatable.

Modification of NPs with chitosan, algal polysaccharide persulfates, and cysteine has been reported to enhance NPs paracellular transport by opening TJ [74]. Zhou et al. prepared thiolated polymers of polyacrylic acid-cysteine-6-mercaptonicotinic acid encapsulated in chitosan NPs, which enhanced the ability to trigger the opening of TJ to aid insulin transport via the paracellular pathway, thereby facilitating NPs penetration and enhancing intestinal uptake of insulin, as detected by immunofluorescence staining [62]. In addition to promoting paracellular transport by opening TJ, NPs can also promote paracellular transport by increasing intercellular permeability. Dou et al. prepared chitosan-modified PLGA NPs and found that the chitosan-modified NPs undergoing clathrin-mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis exhibited improved transcellular permeability [27]. The mechanisms by which nanocarriers affect paracellular pathway transport in enterocytes, such as by affecting the distribution of transmembrane proteins and the level of oxidative phosphorylation, have been described by several excellent reviews and, thus, will not be discussed in more detail here [75].

M cells allow nanocarrier transcytosis and transportation into the lymphatic circulation

The M cell pathway allows the uptake and transport of nanocarriers through lymphatic circulation, avoiding hepatic first-pass metabolism [76]. Additionally, M cells participate in the regulation of immune responses and lymphatic circulation as the largest component of gut-associated lymphatic organization (Fig. 3). M cells are characterized by abundant vesicles, short microvilli, and low enzymatic activity. They are capable of the uptake and transcytosis of NPs to selectively present antigens to macrophages or dendritic cells (DC) processing [81, 82]. NPs designed as shown in Fig. 3A induce immune responses by targeting M cells to deliver antigens to DC [77] (Fig. 3A). Therefore, they have received much attention in the field of developing oral vaccines. M cells selectively uptake particles < 1 μm through basal transportation [83]. Targeting M cells is one of the options for avoiding cellular degradation within enterocytes for therapeutic oral vaccines [84]. The physicochemical properties of NPs, such as chitosan modification, hydrophobic ligand modification, and positively charged surface, increase the M cell-mediated transcytosis of NPs to promote antigen delivery [85–87]. Specific ligand-modified NPs can also target M cells to promote NPs uptake and absorption [78, 79] (Fig. 3B-D). Pan et al. used tomato lectin-modified nanoemulsion to target M cells, prolong the interaction between nanoemulsion and the mucus layer, and increase the uptake rate of IEC to deliver vaccines more efficiently to exert immune effects [88]. In addition to the above-mentioned methods of promoting lymphatic circulation by nanocarriers, specific uptake by M cells can be promoted by modification with specific ligands, such as invasins and RGD peptides, to enhance oral cellular uptake of nanocarriers [1]. Additionally, chitosan- and 3-boronobenzoic acid-modified yeast β-glucans as nanocarriers can inhibit inflammatory responses and treat ocular diseases by specifically targeting M cells [80] (Fig. 3E).

Cellular response to nanoparticle stimulation

Induction of intestinal immunity in immune-related cells

The intestinal immune system involves the lamina propria, the intrinsic immune system, which comprises the IEC, intestinal mucosa, and intrinsic lymphocytes, and the adaptive immune system, which comprises the activated and differentiated T and B cells The intestinal immune system is responsible for establishing immune responses and immune tolerance to counteract inflammatory responses in the intestinal tract [89].

Dysregulation of the intestinal immune microenvironment leads to diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, autoimmune disorders, and colorectal cancer (CRC). Amphiphilic Pluronic polymers coated with negatively charged NPs regulate the immune response and mucus barrier microenvironment, and functionalized mulberry leaf lipid NPs can increase mucus volume by increasing the expression of colonic TJ and protecting the colonic epithelial barrier [90]. For example, ulcerative colitis is usually associated with neutrophil and inflammatory factor infiltration [91]. Nanocarriers target intestinal immune cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, and exhibit anti-inflammatory immune responses [92, 93]. In the dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis model, with amphiphilic Escherichia coli membrane mimicry NPs were able to attenuate the inflammatory response by reducing the infiltration of CD4+ T-cells in colonic tissues, by downregulating the release of the pro-inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and interleukin-17 (IL-17), and by upregulating the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor interleukin-10 (IL-10) [92] (Fig. 4A and B). Escherichia coli-derived membrane-coated diselenide-bridged mesoporous silica NPs also decreased the number of immune cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, inhibiting the deterioration of colitis by delivering Asiatic acid and coumaric acid [92, 93] (Fig. 4C and D). Additionally, delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 nucleic acid drugs using mulberry leaf lipid NPs delayed CRC progression in a CRC model by downregulating CD98 expression and polarizing macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [90]. The infiltration of inflammatory factors and overactivation of immune cells are processes of DC development, and nanocarriers exhibit anti-inflammatory effects by modulating immune cells and immune responses. In a study on CRC vaccines, Zu et al. prepared an in situ vaccine nanomedicine, Ce6/R837@Lp127NPs, which significantly aggregated in colorectal tumor tissues, induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), and promoted M1 polarization, in vivo and ex vivo [94].

The intestinal immune barrier system continuously defends the body against invasion by pathogenic microorganisms and maintains intestinal homeostasis by retaining the equilibrium between the intestinal microbial community and the immune cells and immune factors. Thus, it is a key player in regulating the physiological processes of immune homeostasis and metabolism. Conversely, the intestinal microbiome and various metabolites, as part of the intestinal barrier, are involved in the composition, development, and maturation of the immune system and maintenance of the balance of intestinal immunity [95, 96]. They have effects on cellular functions, including maintaining the stability of the intestinal barrier by regulating cellular metabolites and modulating immune responses, such as secretion and expression of inflammatory factors in immune cells [97]. Fu et al. prepared NO-delivered nanogels to modulate IL-17 secretion from immune cells and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway to restore the intestinal barrier by modulating the abundance of pathogenic and probiotic bacteria and increasing the production of intestinal probiotic metabolites (short-chain fatty acids and indole derivatives) [96]. Lipid NPs modified with microbial metabolites can modulate T- and B-cell microbiome metabolites and improve antigen-specific immune responses [98]. In a mouse model of colitis, cerium dioxide nanoparticles prepared by Huang et al. regulated the intestinal microbiota by regulating oxidative stress in colonic epithelial cells and macrophages, maintaining redox homeostasis, reducing the growth of anaerobic bacteria, promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors, inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory factors, and regulating the immune response to treat colitis [99].

A study on oral vaccines found that modifying the physicochemical properties of nanocarriers, such as surface charge, particle size, shape, and surfactants, may protect antigens from hydrolysis by mucosal and intestinal barriers and deliver antigens to trigger an immune response, improving their efficacies [100, 101].

However, OAN may cause damage to the intestinal mucus layer and disrupt the intestinal immune microenvironment. For example, Ag NPs reduce the thickness of the intestinal mucus layer and shorten the intestinal villi, which damage the duodenal lamina propria, resulting in weakened or damaged intestinal defenses. Ag NPs inhibited the differentiation of CD103+ CD11b+ DC, induced the activation of Th17 cells, and inhibited the differentiation of regulatory T-cells, which led to the disruption of the intestinal immune microenvironment [102].

Toxicity to intestinal cells

As an undesirable effect, in some cases, cytotoxicity may be induced during the interaction of nanocarriers with healthy intestinal cells, which involves various mechanisms, such as changes in cell morphology, epigenetic abnormalities, cell-specific maturation, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and DNA alterations [53]. ZnO and Ag NPs have been reported to cause blistering of Caco-2 cell membranes, deterioration of the cytoplasm, affecting cell morphology, and even abnormalities in the nuclear structure and chromatin condensation [103]. Histone modification is an epigenetic mechanism that regulates many cellular physiological processes. Wire Titanium oxide (TiO2) NPs more severe damage to the intestinal barrier compared to spherical and rod-shaped NPs. NPs of all shapes are able to upregulate the levels of H3cit, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, H3K36me3, H3K9ac, and H4K8ac, causing DNA damage and epigenetic cytotoxicity, as evidenced by western blotting and quantitative polymerase chain reaction analyses in Caco-2 cells [104]. The generation of excess ROS and elevated ROS levels caused by ingested NPs can induce apoptosis and necrosis [105]. Ag NPs cause DC apoptosis through ROS generation, leading to abnormal maturation and morphological changes such as increased DC size and lengthening of dendritic tentacles [102].

The cytotoxicity and damage to the intestinal cells have been reported to be related to nanocarrier dose, exposure time, and physicochemical properties. In an in vitro toxicity assay with solid lipid NPs, the NPs affected Caco-2 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner [106]. The cytotoxicity of NPs on intestinal cells is related to the state of the NPs surface modifiers, in addition to the dose. Chitosan NPs were found to show a slight increase in cytotoxicity during gastrointestinal digestion compared to soluble starch-coated NPs and bulk chitosan powder [107]. In addition, Ag and TiO2 NPs, due to their unique antimicrobial effects, may alter the composition of the intestinal microbiota, causing intestinal dysbiosis and even inducing the release of the pro-inflammatory factor interleukin- 7 (IL-7), as well as the infiltration of neutrophils, leading to intestinal inflammation. The influence of physicochemical properties, such as size, charge, and shape of the nanocarriers, on cytotoxicity is discussed in the section “Nanocarrier physicochemical properties contributing to the nanoparticle-cell interaction.”

Altering intestinal cell secretion and function

As a primary function, goblet cells in mucosa secret mucin and form a mucus layer. One of the features of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or ulcerative colitis (UC) is the increased amount goblet cell apoptosis and thinner or the loss of mucus layer. Therefore, increasing mucus secretion or goblet cell abundance is beneficial in treating colonic inflammatory disease. Tian et al. developed a carrier composed of a diethylaminoethyl-dextran hydrochloride/sodium polyphenylene sulfonate coacervates, coated with Eudragit S100, and encapsulated with emodin [108]. This carrier effectively targeted and retained in the colon. Notably, the carrier repaired colonic epithelia by significantly inhibiting goblet cell loss in the colon mucosa, which contributed to the therapeutic effects. Increasing the secretion of mucus has also been used as a strategy for UC treatment using a mulberry biomass-derived NPs [109].

L cells are intestinal secretory cells that produce various peptides to maintain normal physiological functions in the human body. Lipid NPs can change membrane structure fluidity, increase the internalization of lipid NPs by L cells, and induce the secretion of endogenous glucagon-like peptide 1, which regulates glucose uptake by cells to control blood glucose [110].

In addition to altering protein or peptide secretion, cytokine or chemokine secretion by the cells in mucosa also become altered due to the nanoparticle-cell interaction. Details are described in the “Nanoparticle optical property” Sect. [111].

Cellular response in disease lesion

In disease conditions, such as IBD and tumorigenesis, NPs may interact with multiple cells in the microenvironment of lesion location, leading to various cellular responses, such as proliferation of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) [112], increased levels of ROS in intestinal epithelial cells [113], inflammatory cell infiltration [93], apoptosis, ferroptosis, tumor cell ICD [114], secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by immune cells, and macrophage polarization [111]. For example, ISCs play an essential role in maintaining the intestinal epithelium’s homeostasis against injury. However, ISC proliferation is inhibited or depleted by treatments that use irradiation and chemotherapeutic agents. Wei et al. developed oral lipid NPs as carriers to deliver miR-200 [112]. The in vivo study resulted in Ggcr8-deficient mice with damage caused by irradiation, showing that miR-200 loaded NPs attenuated the p53 Pathway and promoted intestinal regeneration. The expression of inflammatory factors and the level of ROS in intestinal cells are one of the key factors in the treatment of IBD. Zero-valent-molybdenum nanodots can inhibit the expression of immune cell inflammatory factors, including IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ, and downregulate the level of ROS in IECs and colonic cells in a mouse IBD model [113]. In addition, pluronic F127-modified gold shell-polymeric core NPs enhance the therapeutic effect on UC by loading curcumin, polarizing pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2-type, modulating redox levels in enterocytes, and inhibiting the NF-kB signaling pathway to modulate immune cells and decrease the degree of inflammation [111]. In CRC models, magnetic mesoporous silicon NPs can target tumor cells to induce ICD, stimulate dendritic cell maturation, contribute to the activation of cytotoxic T cells, trigger apoptosis, and inhibit colorectal tumor growth [114].

Nanoparticle size

The particle size of OAN is one of the factors affecting their interaction with intestinal-associated cells, cellular uptake, transcytosis, and in vivo biopharmaceutical behavior [26, 116, 118].

First, the size of the nanocarrier is related to mucosal transfer. Given that the average grid diameter of the fibrous network in the mucus structure is approximately 100 nm, the nanocarriers with smaller particle sizes tended to penetrate the mucus membrane more easily than those with larger sizes [118]. For example, PLGA NPs with smaller particle sizes are more easily transferred through the mucus layer, and mucus permeability is nearly 1.3 times higher than that of larger NPs [119]. Among the SiO2 NPs with particle sizes ranging from 20 to 200 nm, SiO2 with particle sizes of 20–30 nm has better permeation in the mucus layer. In contrast, the cumulative accumulation of SiO2 with particle sizes > 70 nm in the mucus layer increases with increasing size [118].

The influence of size on the transcytosis of NPs is complex and variable [120]. For example, in general, nanocarriers with a particle size of < 20 nm can undergo endocytosis via the paracellular pathway. In comparison, nanocarriers with a particle size of 25–300 nm may undergo endocytosis via either the clathrin or the caveolin pathways [23]. In some cases of small NPs, endocytosis was inhibited due to the high energy costs associated with membrane bending, which plays a role in cell-nanoparticle interactions and the membrane encapsulation around the NPs [121, 122]. Therefore, smaller nanocarriers often need to be clustered to generate a sufficient driving force for successful cellular internalization. Larger nanocarriers may require more receptors for specific endocytosis to occur, allowing the receptors to diffuse from long distances to the surface of the enterocyte in contact with the nanocarrier during uptake, leading to a decrease in cellular internalization efficiency [123]. According to the theoretical mechanics model proposed by Ding et al., wrapping orally administered NPs increases the tension of the membrane and tensile energy [124]. Therefore, for a given oral drug delivery system, the effect of size on NPs endocytosis and diffusion may vary.

Moreover, the size of the nanocarrier material affects the activation of membrane receptors and the expression of proteins produced by enterocytes [125]. For the inflamed intestinal barrier, size influences the targeting or intracellular targets. The size of nanocarriers also affects the physiological structure of enterocytes, generating an inflammatory response that causes cytotoxicity. In a model of colitis, SiO2 NPs of different sizes according to the range of daily intake showed that smaller-sized NPs could worsen intestinal inflammation through activation of the NLRP3/ASC inflammasome and inducing macrophage secretion of IL-1β [126]. Therefore, size should be considered when designing OAN. For more information on nanocarrier size for mucus permeation, cell targeting, and cellular uptake, the readers can read more specialized and relevant reviews [3, 127].

Nanoparticle shape

To date, NPs of various shapes, including spherical, rod-shaped, disk-shaped, cubic, screw-shaped, three-dimensional, complex-shaped, and biomimetic dendritic or worm-like, have been developed [38, 128]. The effect of NPs shape and features on their interactions with cells is shown in Fig. 6 [processed using Adobe Illustrator (Adobe, San Jose, CA)]. The shape of the nanocarrier plays an important role in influencing the interactions between the NPs and cells. For example, inspired by the conformational changes in influenza viruses and coronaviruses in the GT at different pH values, researchers have developed NPs that can overcome the physiological barrier of the GT and have a unique stretching conformation at acidic pH and a folding conformation at physiological pH, mimicking the adjustable surface of viruses [129].

To overcome the mucus barrier and allow for increased cellular uptake of nanocarriers, the mucus penetration behavior of NPs with different shapes has been explored (Fig. 7). The helical-like NPs can adhere to the intestinal villi owing to their unique biological structure, thus extending the retention time in the intestine and promoting drug effects [130, 131] (Fig. 7A). Rod-shaped NPs with larger aspect ratios are more permeable to mucus than spherical virus-like NPs [132]. Consistent with this, Bao et al. demonstrated that tubular α-lactalbumin can penetrate deeper into the mucus and improve endocytosis than spherical α-lactalbumin, thereby improving the bioavailability of hydrophobic drugs [133]. Zhou et al. found that dendritic NPs could reduce interactions with mucin and enhance mucus penetration ability [134]. The mucus penetration ability of nanocarriers may be related to the size of the contact area with the intestine [29] (Fig. 7B and E). Due to their small cross-sectional area and irregular shape, screw-like nanocarriers have better retention ability in FITC-labeled mouse GT in vitro. Further, screw-like nanocarriers exhibit a stronger mucus permeability in the mucus layer than spherical and rod-shaped NPs [29] (Fig. 7C and D). In rat in situ closed-loop intestinal perfusion experiments, cubic NPs had stronger adhesion capacity and longer retention time in the colonic mucus layer because of their polygonal multilateral structure and larger contact area [135]. In addition, NPs mimicking viruses showed enhanced bioretention capacity in the intestinal mucus layer, as well as a deeper range and wider mucus penetration. Core-shell mesoporous silica NPs mimicking the unique surface configuration of viruses can stably and efficiently cross the intestinal barrier, enhance adsorption at intestinal villi, and prolong retention time in the GT [136].

The cellular uptake pathways of the NPs are also affected by their shape. Computer simulations of the effects of NPs of the same volume but different shapes on their uptake into cells showed that spherical NPs were more readily encapsulated by clathrin endocytosis than rod- and disk-shaped NPs with the same volume and surface properties [24]. Non-spherical NPs were taken up by cells at a faster rate and in more significant amounts than spherical NPs. The effects of spherical, rod-, and disk-shaped polystyrene NPs on their uptake and intestinal transport in enterocytes were investigated using a triple co-culture model of Caco-2 cells, HT-29 cells, and Raji-B lymphocytes. The results showed a more significant degree of uptake and translocation of rod- and disk-shaped NPs than of spherical particles in enterocyte uptake and transport [137]. Nanorods have almost twice the rate of enteric lymphatic transport and a longer retention time in the GT than nanospheres [138]. In addition, rod-shaped NPs were retained longer in the intestine in oral protein-based drug delivery investigations. In contrast, spherical NPs were efficiently transported through the intestinal biological barrier and released into the bloodstream [139]. mPEG-modified covalently grafted mesoporous-silica dendritic NPs promote cellular uptake and mucus penetration, expanding their distribution in the gut [133]. Owing to their three-dimensional chiral topology, improved wettability, and larger contact area with the mucus layer, chiral mesoporous silica nano-screw carriers have a higher uptake rate in Caco-2 cells and enter the cells mainly through the caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway [29].

However, nanocarriers of the same shape with different aspect ratios generate different interactions with cells in the GT. For example, rod-shaped nanocarriers with medium aspect ratios are more likely to be endocytosed by clathrin than rod-shaped nanocarriers with low or high aspect ratios [24]. Similarly, nanoparticles of the same shape but with different surface roughness can differ in their ability to regulate cellular uptake, TJ, and cell permeability [140].

However, it is debatable whether the NPs shape affects transcytosis. For example, non-spherical nanocarriers have a decreased rate of endocytosis via cells as compared to spherical carriers. Wang et al. found that the changes in the potential energy of NPs increased with an increase in the aspect ratio of the NPs, which in turn induced different membrane-coating processes. Moreover, the endocytosis time of NPs increases with the aspect ratio because of the differences in the contact areas with the cell membrane [141]. In addition, the shape of the nanocarrier also affects cell function and toxicity. When the spherical carrier enters the cell, it interferes with cell functions, disrupting the cell cycle, inducing the generation of ROS, and promoting apoptosis. Conversely, the disk-shaped nanocarrier only binds to the cell membrane and does not significantly affect cell viability [142]. Different shapes of TiO2 NPs can cause genotoxicity by breaking the DNA strands of enterocytes. Nanowires are more harmful to enterocytes than rods or spherical NPs. Nanowires disrupt the integrity and permeability of the Caco-2/HT29 barrier by upregulating the expression of genes related to TJ, making nanowire uptake more difficult than other shapes [143].

Under disease conditions, NPs of different shapes may have different fates in vivo. A previous study reported the ability of enterocytes to selectively uptake NPs of various shapes in a model of inflammatory bowel disease. They showed that the intestinal immune cell uptake of worm-like NPs was nearly two times higher than that of spherical NPs, thus having the potential to selectively target sites of intestinal inflammation [128].

Collectively, these studies on the effect of the nanocarrier shape on their uptake contribute to a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of nanocarrier-cell interactions and provide useful information for the design of nanocarriers with improved oral delivery efficiency.

Nanoparticle surface polarity

Nanoparticle surface polarity is a non-negligible factor that affects its transport across the intestinal barrier and drug stability. NPs with hydrophilic surfaces can easily penetrate the mucus barrier because of the difficulty in forming electrostatic interactions with mucus. Zhang et al. found that milk-derived exosome nanocarriers modified with hydrophilic polyethylene glycol improved mucus penetration efficiency and facilitated uptake and absorption by IEC [144]. Hydrophobic surface NPs can access the lipophilic region of the cell membrane and facilitate cellular uptake [145]. In contrast, NPs with lipophilic surfaces can be absorbed via lymphatic uptake, bypass hepatic first-pass metabolic effects, and improve drug stability [146]. Additionally, NPs with lipophilic or amphiphilic components may alter the fluidity of the enterocyte membrane and promote its fusion with the enterocyte membrane, thereby enhancing permeability [147]. Lipophilic NPs were prepared using phospholipids as NP shells to load insulin, forming phospholipid vesicles in intestinal fluid to protect insulin from degradation by trypsin and fusing with cell membranes during endocytosis to facilitate NP transport [148]. To adapt to the intestinal microenvironment for better drug delivery, NPs that can change their surface hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity according to the gastrointestinal environment were constructed. PEG was utilized to hydrophilically coat the surface of the NPs to promote mucus permeation and protect the NPs in crossing the intestinal barrier; PLGA was connected to PEG through a hydrazone bond, and when the NPs reached the jejunum (pH of 5.5), the PEG was rapidly degraded, and the hydrazone bond was disrupted, which transformed the surface of the NPs from hydrophilic to hydrophobic, thus facilitating uptake by cells [149]. However, an increase in hydrophilic components does not necessarily promote mucus permeation, and the balance of NP hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity also affects their transit through the gut. Both hydrophilicity to facilitate mucus permeation and hydrophobicity to cross lipid-rich and relatively hydrophobic cell membranes are required to cross the intestinal barrier. Attar et al. prepared NPs using stearic acid as a hydrophobic lipid and Gantrez as a hydrophilic polymer. NPs with different ratios of hydrophilic/hydrophobic compositions were investigated, and it was found that a hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance enabled the NPs to exhibit optimal intestinal barrier crossing [150].

Nanoparticle mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of nanocarriers, such as rigidity and elasticity, can affect the interaction between NPs and cells and the subsequent cellular response [151]. In a study on cellular uptake, Li et al. found that more rigid α-lactalbumin nanotubes had a greater ability to transiently open TJ and were more likely to promote cellular endocytosis. Therefore, the uptake of harder NPs was more efficient in Caco-2 cells [38]. Stiffness and elastic size of nanocarriers have been evaluated by atomic force microscopy. Xu et al. evaluated the difference between the endocytic abilities of rigid and flexible cyclic graft copolymer NPs by dissipative particle dynamics simulations. They found that rigid self-assembled NPs have a larger spatial skeleton as well as a larger contact surface area than flexible self-assembled NPs during endocytosis and uptake and thus exhibit faster endocytosis wrapping in physiological environments and a more stable structure [151]. NPs with different stiffnesses follow different membrane-wrapping processes inside the cell, which may even damage the cell membrane, thus affecting the endocytosis capacity of the NPs [133]. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations were performed to investigate the effects of nanocarrier stiffness on the transport and uptake by intestinal cells. The results showed that at lower stiffness, the nanocarriers showed evident “worm-like” movement of NPs bending during uptake by Caco-2 cells, which increased the contact area of the NPs with the cells, thus promoting endocytosis. However, when the stiffness exceeds a certain limit, the NPs require a higher bending energy for the cell membrane during endocytosis due to the high stiffness, which causes membrane damage due to the rupture of the cell membrane, resulting in reduced endocytosis efficiency [141].

During endocytosis, nanocarriers directly contact enterocytes, which involves a continuous deformation process and energy change in both the enterocytes and nanocarriers. Therefore, the nanocarrier stiffness affects endocytosis. Softer and moderately rigid NPs of the same size were mainly endocytosed via the caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway. Conversely, harder polyethylene glycol core-shell NPs were endocytosed via the clathrin and macropinocytosis pathways in addition to the caveolin-mediated endocytosis pathway. Harder polyethylene glycol core-shell NPs accumulated more in lysosomes [25]. Therefore, rigidity is a factor that cannot be ignored when studying the endocytosis of OAN by GT cells.

The elasticity of NPs contributes to their transintestinal transport behavior. Highly elastic amphiphilic ionic hydrogel NPs can easily overcome the mucus barrier, thereby facilitating improved uptake efficiency by enterocytes [152]. Therefore, elasticity is an important parameter for consideration during the design of functional OAN that can efficiently overcome the intestinal mucosal epithelial barrier.

Nanoparticle surface charge

The surface charge of nanocarriers can be modulated by conjugated functional groups that affect the interactions between NPs and cells [153]. The surface charge of OAN can affect their diffusion behavior in the mucus layer, as well as their endocytosis and transport in cells. The mucus layer may trap positively charged particles due to the highly negatively charged glycosylation in mucins.

Interestingly, researchers have developed nanocarriers whose surface charge can change according to the environment of the GT [155]. Inspired by the entry of viruses into enterocytes, virus-mimicking NPs capable of carrying a neutral charge on the surface during the mucus permeation process were constructed; for these NPs, there is dissociation of anionic phosphates through intestinal alkaline phosphatase upon entry into the alkaline environment of the intestinal tract, and a conversion of the charge from neutral to positive, promoting cellular NP uptake and absorption of NPs by cells [155]. In endocytosis, nanocarriers with positive charges tend to be more easily taken up by cells than those with negative charges [156]. Ren et al. designed a positively charged NPs whose surface was modified with polyethylene oxide. Positively charged NPs showed stronger mucus affinity in porcine intestinal mucus permeability experiments owing to electrostatic interactions between positively charged NPs and mucin, resulting in a wider distribution of NPs in the mucus layer [157]. In NPs intestinal biodistribution experiments, positively charged NPs had a stronger affinity for M cells than negatively charged NPs and were internalized in greater quantities by M cells [26, 157]. Negatively charged NPs have been reported to be able to facilitate NPs translocation by modulating TJ. Xing et al. used Au NPs with different surface charges to study the effects of interactions with enterocytes. Negatively charged NPs could alter the structure of the TJ by binding to Ca2+ at the TJ and thereby adhering to the calmodulin, that the opening of the TJ facilitated the paracellular transport of the gold NPs [11]. Compared with NPs with unmodified surfaces, negatively charged NPs can not only enhance mucus permeability and promote the passage of NPs through the intestinal barrier [155], but also bind to the positively charged intestinal inflammation site through electrostatic interaction, prolong the retention of NPs at the disease site, and improve the therapeutic effect [158].

Li et al. designed charge-switchable NPs, an amphiphilic ionic polycarboxybetaine with a cationic quaternary ammonium group, and a separate anionic carboxylic acid group, utilizing the protonation of polycarboxybetaine at pH 5.0 to increase the electrostatic interactions between the positive surface charge of the particles and insulin in negatively charged NaOH, and the deprotonation of the carboxylic acid group in the physiological environment at pH 7.4. The negatively charged carboxylic acid groups confer a negative charge on the NPs, and the electrostatic repulsion with insulin released insulin into the bloodstream; the negatively charged NPs also modulated TJ to enhance paracellular pathway transport [159]. To improve the mucus permeability of lipid NPs and cellular uptake and absorption of drugs, NPs were modified by polyphosphates to achieve a negatively charged surface; when the NPs enters the intestine, the polyphosphates are cleaved by intestinal alkaline phosphatase, exposing positively charged polyarginine residues, which interact with the cell membrane to promote endosomal escape [160].Thus, effective regulation of the surface charge may provide a promising strategy for designing and constructing nanocarrier delivery systems with desirable mucus diffusion and transcytosis behaviors. The surfaces of NPs can be modified with different components to possess different charges, such as cruciferin [161], chitosan, and cell-penetrating peptides [27, 162].

The charge properties of NPs are also beneficial in achieving targeted delivery to the inflammatory mucosa and macrophages. The injured colon epithelial surface has a positive charge owing to mucosal mucus depletion as well as the presence of positively charged proteins. The nanoparticle with a negative surface charge, such as sericins modified, targets and adheres to the inflamed tissue via electrostatic adsorption, which promotes colitis treatment [163].

Notably, surface charge is also associated with the safety of NP stimulation. For example, compared to electrically neutral SiO2 NPs, positively or negatively charged NPs induce the upregulation of NO levels and the pro-inflammatory factor IL-8 in Caco-2 cells, leading to enterocyte inflammation [164]. Therefore, surface charge should be taken into consideration to assure safety.

Nanoparticle optical property

Recently, the photothermal effect of nanocarriers was utilized to alter the interaction between cells and NPs by promoting mucus diffusion, uptake, and absorption by enterocytes. In a previous study, NPs capable of producing a photothermal effect under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation were designed and investigated. NIR irradiation leads to a decrease in viscoelasticity between the NPs and the mucus layer, which enhances penetration. It also increases the mechanical movement of pluronic F127-modified gold shell-polymeric core NPs, which promotes lysosomal escape and consequently increases uptake in CT-26 and Raw 264.7 cells. In the treatment of ulcerative colitis, NIR light-irradiated pluronic F127-modified gold shell-polymeric core NPs downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibited inflammatory cell infiltration, alleviating inflammatory responses and promoting the polarization of M1-type macrophages into M2-type macrophages [111] (Fig. 8A). In addition, nanocarriers can be modified by photosensitizers to exert their optical properties and promote cellular uptake and absorption to regulate intestinal immunity. Wu et al. constructed N770-conjugated mesoporous silica NPs coupled with mitochondrial photosensitizer N770 and utilized NIR light irradiation to improve NPs motility in the intestine, enhance cellular uptake, and regulate intestinal immunity against CRC. NIR irradiation also regulates the intestinal microbial community by increasing the diversity and abundance of Lactobacillaceae and Lachnospiraceae in the intestinal tract and inhibiting the development of CRC [165] (Fig. 8B).

Nanoparticle magnetism

The magnetic properties of nanocarriers have been used to apply alternating magnetic fields to treat CRC (Fig. 9). Further, nanocarrier magnetism has been examined using vibrating sample magnetometer. Li et al. constructed natural magnetic mesoporous silicon NPs using alternating magnetic fields (AMF). In colorectal mucus layer diffusion experiments simulated by hydroxyethyl cellulose hydrogel, the application of AMF caused the NPs magneto-thermal effect to lead to an increase in Brownian motion, which enhanced the mucus-penetrating ability of the NPs (Fig. 9A), thus promoting the NPs to pass through the mucus barrier and facilitating lysosomal escape and penetration deep into the colon tumor lesion site [114] (Fig. 9B). Nanoparticle-mediated magneto-thermal effects can also promote macrophage polarization into the M1-type, induce DC maturation, regulate intestinal flora, activate ICD, inhibit the proliferation of CT-26 cells, and treat CRC [114] (Fig. 9C).

Nanoparticle chiral conformation

Chiral conformation is a universal molecular phenomenon in nature, and different chiral structures of the same object produce different physiological effects on the human body [166]. Chiral isomers have unique optical [29] and biorecognition capabilities [167].

Oral nano-delivery systems have been developed to improve the uptake and absorption of OAN and promote efficient drug efficacy by altering the conformation of the NPs [168] (Fig. 10A). Notably, differences in the chiral conformation of the nanocarriers also have different effects on enterocyte bioadhesion.

In the in vitro intestinal penetration experiment, circular dichroism spectrum was used to determine the conformation of NPs. Levorotary-chiral mesoporous silica NPs (Levorotary-CMSN) (tartaric acid-modified mesoporous silica NPs) demonstrated better mucus penetration and penetrated the deepest mucus layer than dextrorotatory-CMSN, racemic DL-CMSN (Fig. 10B), and achiral MSN in ex vivo intestinal permeation experiments [169]. The molecular chiral modification of MSN showed strong bioadhesion in the intestine by increasing the surface roughness, which resulted in a higher affinity and greater adsorption compared to non-chiral MSN [169] (Fig. 10C). Additionally, the design of NPs through a chiral structure can also increase adhesion to the intestinal mucosa to prolong their stay in the intestinal tract and promote cellular uptake and absorption [170] (Fig. 10E and D).

Summary and perspectives

In this review, we have discussed our current understanding of NPs transfer through the intestinal barrier and the bidirectional interaction between OAN and intestinal mucosa cells. The various physiochemical properties of OAN, including size, shape, surface charge, magnetism, chiral conformation, and optical and mechanical properties, which affect the interaction of NPs cells and their fate are summarized. It is important to note that owing to these interactions, NPs may exert their function in drug delivery as well as preventive and therapeutic effects in local and systemic diseases.

Given the complexity of nanoparticle-cell interactions in in vivo enteric microenvironments, further research on this topic is still required before it can be used to design NPs for specific applications that can be translated to the clinic. As discussed in this review, the impact of the physiochemical properties of the NPs on their interactions with the GT cells and the resultant fate of the nanoparticles have been elucidated. Based on these studies, the mechanism underlying the differential fates of the NPs based on their physiochemical properties and the relevant partitioning players in vivo requires further investigation. For example, the corona formed upon NPs exposure to GT fluid and its transfer through the mucus layer, or the nature and type of molecules encountered by the NPs during cellular transfer, remain unknown. Advanced approaches to study the effects of NPs physiochemical properties on their fate in the GT have been developed, and computational approaches are considered powerful tools for analyzing these aspects. Artificial intelligence machine-based systems can be used to screen nanomaterials, predict the cellular uptake and transport of NPs, and simulate the physiological environment of the GT. Deep artificial intelligence can contribute to the study of orally administered nanocarrier delivery systems. Therefore, a more precise computational approach should be explored in future studies.

In addition, utilizing this interaction to achieve improved therapeutic effects or regulate the intestinal immune microenvironment is also an important issue. Future studies should focus on the impact of other physiochemical factors as well as the physiological condition such as the oral cavity on nanoparticle-cell interactions. Safety is a key issue that should not be ignored, and the safety of the nanoparticle itself and the toxicity of OAN to intestinal cells should be considered and investigated.

Understanding the interactions between OAN and the cells residing in the intestinal epithelium and mucosal tissue will aid in the design of smart or advanced oral formulations to improve the bioavailability and efficiency of oral drug delivery.
