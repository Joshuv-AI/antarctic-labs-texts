Deep Micro Solvers for Rough-Wall Stokes Flow in a Heterogeneous Multiscale Method
Emanuel Ström, Anna-Karin Tornberg, Ozan Öktem

Abstract
We propose a learned precomputation for the heterogeneous multiscale method (HMM) for rough-wall Stokes flow. A Fourier neural operator is used to approximate local averages over microscopic subsets of the flow, which allows to compute an effective slip length of the fluid away from the roughness. The network is designed to map from the local wall geometry to the Riesz representors for the corresponding local flow averages. With such a parameterisation, the network only depends on the local wall geometry and as such can be trained independent of boundary conditions. We perform a detailed theoretical analysis of the statistical error propagation, and prove that under suitable regularity and scaling assumptions, a bounded training loss leads to a bounded error in the resulting macroscopic flow. We then demonstrate on a family of test problems that the learned precomputation performs stably with respect to the scale of the roughness. The accuracy in the HMM solution for the macroscopic flow is comparable to when the local (micro) problems are solved using a classical approach, while the computational cost of solving the micro problems is significantly reduced.

Introduction

When devising simulation studies of macroscopic properties of fluid flows over a rough terrain, the large scale separation between terrain structure and the size of the simulation region causes problems for classical solvers. Grids that fully resolve the roughness are typically too costly to use, whereas coarse grids fail to capture the effects of the terrain (see
fig.

2
). A classical approach to remedy the problem of mixed scales is to model them separately; one first approximates the effects of the rough topology on a localized region close to the wall, and translates the effects to a simplified computational region with slightly altered boundary data, called a
wall law

[
13
]
.

Asymptotic homogenization
methods have been used to derive wall-laws for viscous flows over a surface with periodic roughness
[
19
,
18
,
3
]
, as well as random, ergodic roughness
[
5
,
12
]
. For these flows one commonly uses a
Navier-slip
condition, a Robin-type boundary condition that relates the
wall shear stress
to the fluid velocity at the wall by a proportionality constant (the
slip amount
). The slip amount is determined from the solution to a problem on a reference domain that includes the boundary, also called a
unit cell problem
.

Carney and Engquist applied the
heterogeneous multiscale method (HMM)
to
laminar viscous
flow over a non-periodic, non-ergodic rough wall in
[
10
]
.
HMM
is a general framework for multiscale modelling which assumes that the governing equations are known at both the macroscopic and microscopic scales, up to a set of simulation parameters. The key building blocks of
HMM
are a
compression operator
that maps micro scale data to key parameters that govern the macro scale behavior (e.g. slip amount), and a
reconstruction operator
that extracts relevant macroscopic data which modulate the micro scale (e.g. flow rates at the boundary)
[
13
]
.

In the paper by Carney and Enquist, the compression operator extracts pointwise slip amount via not only one (as in asymptotic homogenization), but several microscopic simulations of the linearized flow along the rough wall. Contrary to homogenization, the boundary conditions of the microscopic flow are set by the macroscopic flow. This results in a coupled system that involves both the macroscopic and microscopic variables.

In cases when the microscopic solution, compression and reconstruction operators are all
linear
, one can efficiently precompute the composition of these three operators (or a subset of them) using the
representation theorem
. One important note is that solving multiple microscopic problems can often be more expensive than solving the macroscopic system. Furthermore, wall-laws incur a modelling error which cannot be overcome by
HMM
, even with exact micro solvers. Hence, there is a high tolerance for error in the micro solvers for
HMM
compared to fully resolving methods, and a simultaneous need for reduced computational cost.

One promising idea, which we explore further in this paper, is to
learn
a microscopic solution procedure in
HMM
by training against simulation data
[
14
]
. The key underlying observation motivating this approach is that deep learning based approximations are, once trained, often faster to evaluate than traditional numerical approximations, and that a decrease accuracy compared to classical numerical schemes can be acceptable in the case of
HMM
. As the geometry of the micro domains vary, it is essential that the learned approximation generalizes over the types of geometries that one expect to encounter.

Most work on deep learning for multiscale problems focuses on
sequential methods
where information propagates between the scales in only one direction. An example is the work on
deep potentials
by E et al that leverages deep neural networks to find potentials for atomistic
particle interactions
, trained on
quantum dynamics

[
36
]
. Other examples are prediction of
stress fields

[
7
]
and
crystalline structure

[
31
]
, and homogenized quantities
[
27
]
in microstructures in materials science. See the review by Baek et al for a comprehensive overview of ML methods for multiscale problems
[
30
]
.

In contrast to sequential methods,
concurrent methods
employ a feedback loop in which information flows both from small to large scales and from large to small scales. There is less work on ML for concurrent methods. An example is
[
27
]
, which uses the
PCA-Net
architecture from
[
8
]
to learn solutions to a local
unit-cell problem
in the micro scale variables. The averages feed into a dynamical macro scale
deformation
model, which in turn updates the boundary condition of the unit-cell.

1.1
Outline and Contributions

We consider the setting of rough-wall laminar flow, and propose a learned precomputation for the composition of micro solution and compression operators in the
HMM
approach from
[
10
]
. Our approach uses a
Fourier neural operator (FNO)

[
25
]
, whose input is a parameterization of the wall geometry, and whose output is the
Riesz representor
for the composition of the microscopic solution operator with the compression operator. Leveraging Riesz representation theorem in this way is beneficial for any
HMM
approach, but especially so when the Riesz representor is learned from data. We effectively cut the input dimension in half and subsequently improve sample complexity, since there is no need to incorporate boundary values as a part of the training data. The network architecture makes use of a formulation based on a
boundary integral equation (BIE)
, which enables a significant reduction in dimensionality, and generalizes over a range of geometries.

The paper is structured as follows.
Section

2
presents a mathematical formulation of the rough-wall flow problem along with a review of the
HMM
method from
[
10
]
. There we also formulate a precomputation method based on adjoint calculus. We describe the network architecture and related learning problem in
section

4
. Then, in
section

5
we show that under suitable regularity assumptions, a bounded training loss leads to a bounded error in the homogenized solution. Lastly, in
section

6
we present numerical results that support the theoretical findings. We conclude in
section

7
with a discussion of future work.

2
A Multiscale Flow Problem

We consider rough-wall laminar flow in two dimensions based on the homogeneous Stokes equations, which are obtained by linearizing Navier Stokes equations. Our method relies on
homogeneity
for dimensionality reduction and
linearity
for efficient precomputation. Although the Stokes equations are generally a limiting model, they are good approximations of near-wall flow if surface roughness is small compared to the boundary layer
[
1
]
. In such cases one may use our method for the so-called micro problems presented below, even if the macroscopic flow is non-linear and non-homogeneous. We first state the general setting for which our method can be applied, and then present problem-specific details.

Let

Remark 1
.

In the following, we will denote the

By

2.2
Stokes Flow over a Rough Wall

Consider a bounded two-dimensional open, connected set (
domain
)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary
\partial{\Omega}
. Let
u\in C_{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the velocity field and
p\in C_{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
the pressure field. The
Stokes equations
are given by the following system of
partial differential equations
(PDEs) and
boundary conditions
(BCs):

\displaystyle-\Delta u+\nabla p

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(5)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot u

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(6)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g\quad\text{on }\partial{\Omega},

(7)

where
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a given
external force
and
g\colon\partial{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a given
boundary velocity
. In general (
5
) includes the
viscosity
, but we set it to 1, which is always possible by reduction to dimension free form. We refer to (
5
) as the
momentum equation
, (
6
) as the

and (
7
) as the

. Regularity of

and

\partial{\Omega}

5

6

\mathcal{P}(u,p)=0,

(8)

where
\mathcal{P}
is an operator that maps

onto the pair
(-\Delta u+\nabla p-f,\nabla\cdot u)
.

The central object of study in this work is the
multiscale Stokes problem
, which is to find a solution
(u_{\epsilon},p_{\epsilon})
to the Stokes equations (
5
)-(
7
) in a

\Omega_{\epsilon}
with boundary conditions
g_{\epsilon}
and external force

. We write the full problem for completeness:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}(u_{\epsilon},p_{\epsilon})=0&\text{in $\Omega_{\epsilon}$,}\\
u_{\epsilon}=g_{\epsilon}&\text{on $\partial{\Omega}_{\epsilon}$.}\end{cases}

(9)

The multiscale domain
\Omega_{\epsilon}
has a smooth boundary apart from the
rough wall

\Gamma_{\epsilon}
, a subset that has rapid oscillations with an amplitude and wavelength at some small scale
\epsilon

fig.

1

\Omega_{\epsilon}
. We assume the velocity field satisfies a

at the steady rough wall:

g_{\epsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}g(x)&\text{if $x\in\partial{\Omega}_{\epsilon}\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon}$,}\\
0&\text{if $x\in\Gamma_{\epsilon}$.}\end{cases}

(10)

The problem (
9

since it requires fine discretization near the rough wall. A naive solution is to approximate (
9

, which entirely does away with any dependence on
\epsilon
. However, the surface roughness can contribute to significant changes in surface drag which affects the flow.
Figure

]

2.3
HMM for Rough Wall Flow

Enquist and Carney formulate
HMM
for a non-linear stationary Navier-Stokes problem with a rough wall in
[
10
]
. We adopt the same constitutive model for linearized Stokes flow (
9
). Recall (
section

2.1
) that the
HMM
requires a macro problem (
2
) with unknown quantities
u
and known data
D
, and a micro problem (
3
) with unknown quantities
\chi
and known data
d
, as well as a compression operator
\mathcal{C}
that maps
\chi
to
D
, and a reconstruction operator
\mathcal{R}
that maps
u
to
d
. We present these building blocks as derived by Engquist and Carney. For the remainder of this section we fix an
\epsilon>0
.

2.3.1
Macro Problem

The macroscopic quantity
u
of interest is the macroscopic velocity field. The pressure
p
is treated as an auxilliary variable. The macro problem is stated in (
11
). This is a model for Stokes flow restricted to the smooth domain
\Omega_{0}
; effectively cutting away the rough wall
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
and replacing it with the smooth wall
\Gamma_{0}
. One compensates for the smoothing by imposing a
Robin condition
with a slip amount
\alpha
at
\Gamma_{0}
instead of a non-slip condition (
10
):

\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}(u,p)=0_{\;\;}&\text{in $\Omega_{0}$,}\\
u+(\alpha\tau\cdot\partial_{\nu}u)\tau=0_{\;\;}&\text{on $\Gamma_{0}$,}\\
u=g&\text{on $\partial{\Omega}_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{0}$.}\end{cases}

(11)

Here the operator
\mathcal{P}
was introduced in (
8
),
\nu\colon\partial{\Omega}_{0}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the outward-pointing
unit normal vector
to
\partial{\Omega}_{0}
,
\tau\colon\partial{\Omega}_{0}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
the counter-clockwise
unit tangent vector
,
\partial_{\nu}=\nu\cdot\nabla
is the derivative in the normal direction, and
\alpha\colon\Gamma_{0}\to[0,\infty)
is a positive scalar function (
slip amount
).

In order for the solution to (
11
) to match that of (
9
), one must find a suitable candidate for
\alpha
. Homogenization techniques can approximate
\alpha
in specific settings. For example, when the roughness is a periodic fluctuation in the normal direction of a flat line segment, Enquist and Carney show in
[
10
]
that
\alpha
can be computed from a
unit-cell problem
of Stokes type, posed at the boundary. Basson et al derive a similar method for a
random, homogeneous roughness
in
[
5
]
. In

, we view
\alpha
as the unknown data, which will be determined from the micro scale problem – similar to the unit cell problem in homogenization theory.

2.3.2
Micro Problem

The micro problem consists of a set of
N
Stokes problems on the
micro domains

\{Y_{n,\epsilon}\}_{n=1}^{N}
centered at points
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\Gamma_{0}
along the smooth wall
\Gamma_{0}
and that overlap with the rough boundary

, see

Each domain

defines a solution
\chi_{n,\epsilon}\colon Y_{n,\epsilon}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
to a Stokes problem with pure Dirichlet boundary conditions on the

\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}(\chi_{n,\epsilon},\pi_{n,\epsilon})=0_{\;\;}&\text{in $Y_{n,\epsilon}$}\\
\chi_{n,\epsilon}=h_{n,\epsilon}&\text{on $\partial Y_{n,\epsilon}$}\end{cases}\quad\text{for $n=1,\ldots,N$.}

(12)

Here,

is the microscopic data that agrees with the macro solution

in the interior of
\Omega_{0}
and vanishes at the rough boundary. Once again, we treat the pressure
\pi_{n,\epsilon}\colon Y_{n,\epsilon}\to\mathbb{R}
as an auxiliary variable.

To improve upon readability, we will when referring to solutions of (or quantities related to) (

) and the scale
\epsilon
.

2.3.3
Reconstruction Operator

In our setup, the reconstruction operator is supposed to map the macro flow

, defined on
\Omega_{0}
, to boundary conditions for the micro domains. The resulting boundary value problems on the micro domains should be well-posed and well approximate the fully resolved solution. The macro domain
\Omega_{0}
does not contain the whole of

Setting 1

The extrapolation is given by

(i)

.

(ii)

(iii)

\Omega_{0}\cap\partial Y

(iv)

.

items

2.3.4
Compression Operator

In our setup, the compression operator should map micro solutions

Consider an arbitrary micro solution

Here,

Setting 2

Define

\alpha:=\mathcal{I}\bigl(\bigl\{(x_{n},\tilde{\alpha}_{x_{n}})\bigr\}_{n=1}^{N}\bigr)

3
Computing Riesz Representors

Carney and Enquist use a
finite element method
(FEM) to solve both the micro and micro problems coupled with polynomial interpolation of the slip amount and choose a periodic roughness to avoid extrapolation. We instead use a
boundary integral formulation
of the Stokes equations to solve the micro problem (
12
). We outline the method below.

3.1
Dimensionality Reduction with Boundary Integrals

The
boundary element method (BEM)
and
boundary integral method (BIM)
, are both solution methods based on reformulating PDE systems as integral equations on the boundary of the domain. The main advantage of such methods is
dimensionality reduction
, since one only needs to discretize the boundary
\partial{\Omega}
instead of the entire domain
\Omega
. The main disadvantage is that evaluating the solution often involves computations with nearly singular integral kernels for points close to the boundary. Furthermore, the kernel decays slowly, resulting in dense system matrices. We refer to
[
32
]
for an introduction to BIM.

Let
T
denote a
fundamental solution
for the Stokes equations, known as the
stresslet
:

T_{ijk}(r)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{r_{i}r_{j}r_{k}}{\|r\|^{4}},\quad\text{for $i,j,k\in\{1,2\}$ and $r\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

(14)

The stresslet is related to the gradient of the
Green’s function
for the Stokes equations, known as the
Stokeslet
. The stresslet can be used to construct a
double layer potential

D
that maps vector fields
\rho
on the boundary
\partial{\Omega}
to velocity fields in the interior
\Omega
:

D[\rho]_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\partial{\Omega}}2T_{ijk}(x-y)\nu_{j}(y)\rho_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}s(y),\quad\text{for $x\in\Omega$ and $i\in\{1,2\}$,}

(15)

where
\nu(y)
is the unit vector normal to the wall
\partial{\Omega}_{0}
at the point
y
. Then,
u:=D[\rho]
solves the Stokes equations (
5
)–(
6
) with
f=0
. Next, if
u
solves the full Stokes problem, it has to satisfy the boundary condition (
7
). Taking the limit of the right-hand-side of (
15
) as
x
goes from the interior towards a point
x_{0}
on the boundary an equating with the Dirichlet data from (
7
), we obtain a
BIE

[
32
, p. 110]
:

g(x_{0})=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}u(x)=\mathcal{K}[\rho](x_{0})\quad\text{for $x_{0}\in\partial{\Omega}$
where $\mathcal{K}:=(\tfrac{1}{2}I+D_{\mathrm{PV}})$.}

(16)

In the above,
D_{\mathrm{PV}}
denotes the
Cauchy principal value
of (
15
) evaluated on the boundary
\partial{\Omega}
and
I[\rho]=\rho
is the identity.
Then,
solving the
BIE
(
16
) for
\rho
ensures that
u(x):=D[\rho](x)
in (
15
) solves Stokes equations (
5
)–(
6
) with boundary conditions (
7
).

Due to compactness of
D_{\mathrm{PV}}
when
\partial{\Omega}
is a Lyapunov surface
[
32
]
, the operator
\mathcal{K}
in (
16
) is a
Fredholm integral of the second kind
, which has a bounded inverse.
Furthermore, although the inverse of
\mathcal{K}
is not known analytically, we can formally express the solution
u
and its directional derivatives
\partial_{a}u
in some arbitrary direction
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
in terms the boundary data
h\in C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
:

u=D[\mathcal{K}^{-1}[h]]\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{a}u=\partial_{a}D[\mathcal{K}^{-1}[h]].

(17)

Note that when
u
is evaluated away from the boundary
\partial{\Omega}
, the directional derivative
\partial_{a}D
is given as an integral operator whose kernel is the directional derivative of the stresslet.

3.2
Adjoint Micro Problems

For this section we consider a fixed micro domain
Y
with boundary data
h
and a curve
\ell
over which averages of the velocity field are computed. We drop the subscripts
\epsilon
and
x
. The goal is then to find Riesz representors, which we denote
r_{1},r_{2}
, on the boundary
\partial Y
so that
\langle r_{1},h\rangle_{\partial Y}=\langle\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}
and
\langle r_{2},h\rangle_{\partial Y}=\langle\partial_{\nu}\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}
for all
h
. The Riesz representors provide a shortcut to compute the slip amount (
13
) instead of solving the micro problem (
12
):

\tilde{\alpha}=-\frac{\langle\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}}{\langle\partial_{\nu}\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}}=-\frac{\langle h,r_{1}\rangle_{\partial Y}}{\langle h,r_{2}\rangle_{\partial Y}}.

(18)

The Riesz representors are solutions to the adjoint problem (
4
) that depend on
\ell
and
\partial Y
and can be posed using a variational, classical, or boundary integral formulation of Stokes equations. We consider the latter, and use (
17
) to express the micro solution as
\chi=D\bigl[\mathcal{K}^{-1}[h]\bigr]
and its directional derivative as
\partial_{\nu}\chi=\partial_{\nu}D\bigl[\mathcal{K}^{-1}[h]\bigr]
.

One can derive exact expressions for the Riesz representors
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
directly from the boundary integral formulation (
16
). Define the
adjoint
of the
double layer potential
restricted to a compact subset
K\subset{\kern 0.0pt\Omega}
as
D_{K}^{*}\colon C(K,\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
where

D_{K}^{*}[u]_{i}(y)=2\nu_{j}(y)\int_{K}T_{ijk}(x-y)u_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}s(x)\quad\text{for $y\in\partial{\Omega}$.}

(19)

Boundedness of the adjoint
D_{K}^{*}
follows from the fact that
(x,y)\mapsto T_{ijk}(x-y)\nu_{j}(y)
is in
C(K\times\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
since
\partial{\Omega}
and
K
are separated by a positive minimal distance. Since both
\partial{\Omega}
and
K
are compact, the continuity is also uniform. Similarly, the adjoint of
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
is

\mathcal{K}^{-*}[\rho]=(\tfrac{1}{2}I+D_{\mathrm{PV}}^{*})^{-1}[\rho].

(20)

We can now compute
\langle\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}
and
\langle\partial_{\nu}\chi,\tau\rangle_{\ell}
by the representation formula (
17
) for solutions to the Stokes problem, and then move operations to the tangent function
t
using the adjoint of the double layer potential. The Riesz representor
r_{1}
amounts to solving the
adjoint equations of the first kind:

r_{1}=\mathcal{K}^{-*}[\tilde{r}_{1}],\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{r}_{1}=D^{*}_{\ell}[\tau].

(21)

Hence, one must first compute the adjoint of the
restriction

D_{\ell}
of the double layer potential
D
to the line segment
\ell
, then compute the adjoint of and invert the
double layer potential

\mathcal{K}
. We similarly compute
r_{2}
as the solution to the
adjoint equations of the second kind:

r_{2}=\mathcal{K}^{-*}[\tilde{r}_{2}]\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{r}_{2}:=(\partial_{\nu}D)_{\ell}^{*}[\tau],

(22)

where
(\partial_{\nu}D_{\ell})^{*}
is the adjoint of the composition of the double layer potential and normal derivative operator.

In the following, we consider the special case when
\ell
is a line segment contained in
Y
. Then, the
intermediate representors

\tilde{r}_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}
have closed-form expressions.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\ell
be a straight line segment between two points
a,b
inside
Y
. Let
\nu_{\ell}
denote the normal vector of
\ell
, and similarly let
\nu(x)
denote the normal vector at
x\in\partial Y
. The intermediate Riesz representors
\tilde{r}_{1},\tilde{r}_{2}
can then be expressed as

\tilde{r}_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\Bigg(\frac{iz_{\nu_{\ell}}}{2}\left(\frac{z_{\nu(x)}}{z_{\nu_{\ell}}}\log\left(\frac{z_{x}-z_{a}}{z_{x}-z_{b}}\right)+\left(\frac{z_{\nu(x)}}{z_{\nu_{\ell}}}-2\frac{\overline{z_{\nu(x)}}}{\overline{z_{\nu_{\ell}}}}\right)\overline{\log\left(\frac{z_{x}-z_{a}}{z_{x}-z_{b}}\right)}\right)\\
+\overline{z_{\nu(x)}}\frac{\operatorname{Im}\left[(z_{x}-z_{a})(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})\right]}{(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{a}})(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})}\Bigg),\\
\tilde{r}_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\Bigg(z_{\nu_{\ell}}\operatorname{Im}\left[\frac{z_{\nu(x)}(z_{b}-z_{a})}{(z_{x}-z_{a})(z_{x}-z_{b})}\right]-\frac{(\overline{z_{b}}-\overline{z_{a}})\operatorname{Im}\left[z_{\nu_{\ell}}z_{\nu(x)}\right]}{(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{a}})(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})}\\
-\frac{(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})^{2}\operatorname{Im}\bigl[z_{\nu_{\ell}}(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{a}})\bigr]-(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{a}})^{2}\operatorname{Im}[z_{\nu_{\ell}}(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})]}{(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{b}})^{2}(\overline{z_{x}}-\overline{z_{a}})^{2}}\Bigg),

where
z_{x}:=x\cdot(1,i)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\operatorname{Im},\operatorname{Re}
are the imaginary and real parts, respectively.

Proof.

See Appendix,
section

8.5
.

To find the Riesz representors
r_{1},r_{2}
, we have to approximate
\mathcal{K}^{-*}
in (
20
), e.g., with a Nyström discretisation or Galerkin projection of the spaces.

Remark 2
.

In the general case,
\mathcal{K}^{-*}
is defined on the dual space of
C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, which is the space of functions with bounded variation
BV([0,2\pi],\mathbb{R}^{2})
when
\partial{\Omega}
is 1-dimensional. However,
\mathcal{K}^{*}\colon C_{0}(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is invertible, where
C_{0}(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the space of continuous vector fields on
\partial{\Omega}
with zero net flow over the boundary, i.e.,

C_{0}(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\biggl\{v\in C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2}):\int_{\partial{\Omega}}\!\!v\cdot\nu=0\text{ where $\nu$ is the normal to $\partial{\Omega}$}\biggr\}.

By
[
23
, theorem 1, p. 60]
, we get
C_{0}(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})=\mathrm{span}\{\nu\}^{\perp}
.
Hence, the inverse
\mathcal{K}^{-*}
of the adjoint is well-defined as an operator from the space
C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
to itself. This is sufficient, since we are only concerned with acting with
\mathcal{K}^{-*}
on the continuous functions
\tilde{r}_{1}
and
\tilde{r}_{2}
.

Altogether, the slip amount
\tilde{\alpha}
in (
13
) can be expressed as a function of the Riesz representors as stated in (
18
).
Algorithm

2
in
section

2.1
outlines the improved procedure, which starts with precomputing
r_{1},r_{2}
for each of the
N
micro problems.

3.3
Discretization and Numerical Solution

This section deals with discretising the problems (
21
) and (
22
) to solve for the Riesz representors
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. We discretize each micro domain
\partial Y
into points
(y_{i})_{i=1}^{J}
,
uniformly
distributed in the parameter domain meaning
y_{i}=\phi(t_{i})
, with
t_{i}=2\pi i/J
for
i=1,2,\dots,J
, where
\phi\colon[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a parameterization of the boundary. By
proposition

2
, the intermediate representors
\tilde{r}_{1,i}=\tilde{r}_{1}(y_{i})
and
\tilde{r}_{2,i}=\tilde{r}_{2}(y_{i})
can be expressed analytically. We discretize the boundary integral (
19
) using the
Nyström method
with
trapezoidal rule
, and evaluate at each discretisation point
y_{i}
, i.e., we compute
r_{m,i}
for
m=1,2
by solving

r_{m,i}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{K}_{i,j}[r_{m,j}]\Delta y_{j}=\tilde{r}_{m,i}\quad\text{with $\Delta y_{j}:=\frac{2\pi}{J}\|\phi^{\prime}(t_{j})\|$ and $i=1,\dots,J$,}

(23)

Here
\mathcal{K}_{i,j}
is a
2\times 2
matrix for any fixed
i,j
, and the
n
:th element of the output (
n=1,2
) for a given input
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is computed as:

(\mathcal{K}_{i,j}[v])_{n}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{2}\nu_{l}(y_{j})T_{nlk}(y_{i}-y_{j})v_{k}.

The resulting system matrix is the same for both representors
m=1,2
, but with different right hand sides. The apparently singular
i=j
terms are well defined as continuous limits along the boundary (see Appendix,
section

8.4
). The resulting system of equations is dense but well-conditioned, and we obtain
r_{m}
with high accuracy using only a moderate number of GMRES iterations. Here we benefit from leveraging the
BIE
, since it allows the data dimension to scale as
J
instead of
J^{2}
, which is the dimension of inputs when representing the solution on a 2D
J\times J
grid.

In the case of multiscale Stokes flow, once discretized, the adjoint equations (
21
) and (
22
) result in dense but well-conditioned systems that can be solved efficiently with iterative solvers like
GMRES

[
33
]
.

4
Learning Riesz Representors

It is clear from (
23
) that the representors
r_{m}
depend non-linearly on the boundary shape
\partial Y
.
We propose to approximate the non-linear maps
Y\mapsto r_{m}(Y)
for
m=1,2
with a
FNO

[
25
]
. Approaches based on neural networks are well suited to capture the non-linear behavior of these two maps, but current neural network architectures and training protocols rarely achieve the same accuracy as classical numerical solvers based on FEM or BIM.

Recall that for the multiscale Stokes problem (
9
), one must solve multiple adjoint micro problems of the type (
21
) and (
22
). The goal of this section is to present a unified framework for learning the map
Y\mapsto r_{m}(Y)
for any micro boundary
Y
, where
r_{m}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\partial Y,\mathbb{R}^{M})
. The key idea is to define the map in terms of a
reference domain
, which in our case is the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The geometry can then be represented by a function, and mapping the boundary to data on the boundary corresponds to a map between function spaces.

4.1
The Geometric Learning Problem

We let
\mathcal{J}
be some set of twice differentiable
Jordan curves
that maps the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
to a simple smooth curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\mathcal{J}\subset\bigl\{\phi\in C_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})\mid\phi\;\text{injective}\bigr\}.

Curves in
\mathcal{J}
can now be used to generate domains
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Simply note that for every curve
\phi\in\mathcal{J}
there is a (micro) domain
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
that has this curve as boundary, i.e.,
\partial Y=\phi(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.
Next, formulating a learning problem over such domains involves introducing a
\mathcal{J}
-valued random variable.
This is possible if we can define a probability space that has
\mathcal{J}
as sample space. Then, randomly sampling from
\mathcal{J}
corresponds to randomly sampling domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Remark 3
.

It is non-trivial to rigorously define a notion of probability measure on the set
\mathcal{J}
in cases when it does not admit a finite dimensional parametrisation, and in practice, we restrict ourselves to this case.

To formulate the learning problem, we adopt the notational convention where random variables are typeset in boldface. As an example,
\boldsymbol{u}
is a random variable (or random element) whereas
u
is an element in some vector space.

Definition 3
(Geometric Supervised Operator Learning).

Let
\mathcal{J}
and
{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
,
{\mathrm{H}}_{2}\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
be measurable sets for some
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume now that
(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})
is a
(\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{2})
-valued random variable consisting of a curve
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, input data
\boldsymbol{w}
and output data
\boldsymbol{v}
. Given a loss function
\mathcal{L}\colon{\mathrm{H}}_{2}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define geometric operator learning as the task of learning a measurable map
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}\colon\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\to{\mathrm{H}}_{2}
that maps from the tuple
(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w})
to
\boldsymbol{v}
, by solving the supervised learning problem

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}\in\!\!\!\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathcal{N}\colon\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\to{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}\!\!\!\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{v})}\Bigl[\mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}],\boldsymbol{v}\bigr)\Bigr].

(24)

The geometric learning problem (
24
) was introduced to the SciML community by Li et al in
[
25
]
for solving PDE problems on a 2D geometry with a geometry-aware architecture, called the
geometric Fourier neural operator (geo-
FNO
)
, that parametrised the maps
\mathcal{N}\colon\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\to{\mathrm{H}}_{2}
.
For the loss, we use the relative
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-error:

\mathcal{L}(w,v)=\frac{\|w-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}^{2}}{\|w\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}}+\frac{\|Dw-Dv\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}^{2}}{\|Dw\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}},

(25)

where
Dw
is the derivative of
w
tangent to the boundary. If we take
\boldsymbol{w}
to be the intermediate representors
(\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{4})
and
\boldsymbol{v}
to be the representors
(\boldsymbol{r_{1}}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{r_{2}}\circ\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{4})
, we jointly recover (
21
) and (
22
) by solving (
24
) with
d_{1}=d_{2}=4
.

There are three key challenges that arise in working with the geometric operator learning problem (
24
).
The first relates to computing the expectation, which requires access to the probability distribution for the
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variable
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}],\boldsymbol{v}\right)
. We do not have access to this distribution and
section

4.2
discusses how to address this challenge by means of training data.
Second, the minimisation in (
24
) is over all possible
{\mathrm{H}}_{2}
-valued measurable mappings on
\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}
. This set is far too large to work with from a computational point of view.
Section

4.3
suggests finite dimensional parametrisations of these mappings based in neural network architectures.
Finally, the random varible
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}],\boldsymbol{v}\right)
is defined in terms of random variables
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{w}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}
that take values in infinite dimensional spaces. Hence, numerically working with the latter is only possible after appropriate discretisation.

4.2
Empirical learning problem

Given a finite set of (supervised) training data

\bigl((\phi_{1},w^{1}),v^{1}\bigr),\ldots,\bigl((\phi_{K},w^{K}),v^{K}\bigr)\in(\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}^{d_{1}})\times{\mathrm{H}}^{d_{2}}

consisting of curves
\phi_{k}
, input data
w^{k}
and output data
v^{k}
, and a neural architecture
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\Theta
as in (
29
), we define the
empirical learning problem
as minimizing
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\theta)
for
\theta\in\Theta
where

\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\|\mathcal{N}_{\theta}[\phi_{k},w^{k}]-v^{k}\|^{2}}{\|v^{k}\|^{2}}+\frac{\|D\mathcal{N}_{\theta}[\phi_{k},w^{k}]-Dv^{k}\|^{2}}{\|Dv^{k}\|^{2}}.

(26)

The main differences from (
24
) is that the expectation is w.r.t. the probability distribution that is given by the training data, data and associated operators are discretised, and the minimization in (
24
) is over a finite dimensional space
\Theta
. The norm is computed using the trapezoidal rule, and the differential operator
D
is approximated using FFT.

4.3
Neural Architecture

There is a growing body of research on
neural operators
, which are
neural networks
defined as mappings between function spaces. A common feature of neural operators is

, meaning that the network can handle inputs of varying resolution. Although trained on finite-dimensional data, neural operators are typically designed to approximate operators defined on more general

spaces, like
\mathrm{L}^{2}
. Notable examples include
deep operator networks
(DeepONet)
[
28
]
,
FNO
s
[
26
]
, as well as
BI-TDONet

[
29
]
that was recently proposed for solving

. Additional example are given in
[
9
,
15
,
20
,
16
,
17
]
.

We will use a variant of
FNO
called
geo-
FNO
to represent (discretised) mappings
\mathcal{N}\colon\mathcal{J}\times{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\to{\mathrm{H}}_{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{Z}^{d}=C(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
\mathbb{S}^{1}
is the

. We define the
spectral convolution operator

\mathcal{K}\colon\mathcal{Z}^{d}\to\mathcal{Z}^{d}
(originally referred to as Fourier integral operator in

, we change the name to avoid confusion with the more well-known Fourier integral operator, as defined in PDE analysis) with respect to the kernel
k_{nm}\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
,

:

\mathcal{K}[z]_{n}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{d}\hat{k}_{nm}(\cdot)\hat{z}_{m}(\cdot)\right](x)

(27)

where
z\in\mathcal{Z}^{d}
with components

and

,
\mathcal{F}
is the

on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and

is the Fourier transform of a function

evaluated at the point

. To discretize, truncate

to the
K
:th mode and replace
\mathcal{F}
with the

(DFT). The spectral convolution is combined with a bias function

and a pointwise linear transformation

to form a single
Fourier neural operator block
:

\widetilde{F}_{(W,\hat{k},b)}[z](x)=Wz(x)+\mathcal{K}[z](x)+b(x).

(28)

We write
\theta=(W,\hat{k},b)
for notational compactness. The
Fourier neural operator

F_{\theta}\colon\mathcal{Z}^{d_{1}}\to\mathcal{Z}^{d_{2}}

is the composition of
L
Fourier neural operator blocks
\widetilde{F}_{\theta^{\ell}}\colon\mathcal{Z}^{d}\to\mathcal{Z}^{d}
with parameters
W^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\hat{k}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times d\times d}

\widehat{b}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{K\times d}
and constant

, separated by pointwise-acting non-linear

F_{\theta}=\mathcal{Q}\circ\widetilde{F}_{\theta^{L}}\circ\sigma\circ\widetilde{F}_{\theta^{L-1}}\circ\dots\circ\sigma\circ\widetilde{F}_{\theta^{1}}\circ\mathcal{P}\quad\text{where $\theta=(\mathcal{P},\theta^{1},\theta^{2},\dots,\theta^{L},\mathcal{Q})$.}

(29)

The linear operators
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d}
and
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{2}}
act pointwise similar to
W^{\ell}
. Typically,
\mathcal{P}

lifts
the dimensions of the input to

and
\mathcal{Q}

projects
the output back to the desired dimension.

The geo-
FNO
architecture, originally proposed in
[

]
, is a variant of the

that incorporates a pullback

from arbitrary domains to the
computational reference domain

\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The geo-

replaces initial Fourier transform by a

, defined as
\mathcal{F}_{\phi}[w]:=\mathcal{F}\bigl[|\mathrm{det}D\phi|\cdot(w\circ\phi)\bigr]
. The original work computes

as an integral on the domain of interest

by a change of variables, but we express all integrals on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
– which means that the geo-
FNO

G
is simply a pushforward of the standard
FNO

F_{\theta}
:

\qquad G_{\theta}[\phi,w](x)=F_{\theta}[\phi,w\circ\phi]\bigl(\phi^{-1}(x)\bigr)\quad\text{for $x\in\phi(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\partial Y$ and $\theta\in\Theta$.}

(30)

Here,
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{(d_{1}+d_{2})d+d^{2}L+d(1+d)KL}

4.4
Computational complexity at inference

Given a
J
-point discretization of the micro problem boundary, classical numerical approaches must solve a dense linear
2J\times 2J
system, which has complexity
\mathcal{O}(J^{2}P)
for an iterative solver like GMRES, with
P
iterations. Fast summation methods as the fast multipole method (FMM) or FFT based methods using Ewald decomposition can reduce this cost to
\mathcal{O}(JP)
or
\mathcal{O}(J\log JP)
, but such approaches are advantageous only for sufficiently large systems. For example,
[
35
]
finds conventional direct summation methods advantageous for
J<3000
for FMM applied to BEM in 3D Stokes flow. Methods based on a Fourier decomposition of the problem data and solution have complexity
\mathcal{O}(K_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}P)
where
K_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximal wave number. Importantly, the system matrix of these methods needs to be rebuilt for each new domain, whereas a neural network operates on stored weights.

To compare, the cost of an
FNO
at inference is
\mathcal{O}(DCJ\log J+DK_{\mathrm{max}}C^{2})
, where
C
is the number of channels,
D
is the network depth, and
K_{\mathrm{max}}
the maximal wave number in the Fourier domain. However, estimates for the scaling of
C
and
D
typically rely on proving that the neural network can approximate a spectral solver
[
24
]
, which causes
C
and
D
to scale with the desired accuracy. We will not explore such complexity results in this paper.

5
Error Analysis

The discrepancy between the optimal solution to (
24
) and its empirical counterpart (
26
) can be split into three main terms representing the approximation error, generalization error and discretization error, respectively.

Section

5.1
discusses the
approximation error
that quantifies the difference between the optimal solution and the best possible solution in the search space defined by the neural network architecture. The same section also mentions the
generalization error
, which quantifies the difference between the expected loss (
24
) on the full data distribution (corresponds to having all possible training data) and the empirical loss (
26
) defined by the finite training data. As such, it relates to the expressivity of the search space. However, the emphasis in this work (
section

5.2
) is on assessing how the generalization error for learning the Riesz representors propagates to an error in the point wise estimates for the slip coefficient (
corollary

6
), which after interpolation causes a global error in the macro problem (
theorem

9
).

5.1
Universality and Generality

Work on developing universal approximation theory for neural operators dates back to Chen and Chen in 1995, who showed that continuous operators on compact subsets of Banach spaces can be approximated to arbitrary precision by a shallow neural operator
[
11
]
. The architecture proposed by Chen would later be used under the name “DeepOnet”
[
28
]
. Recently, it was shown that the
FNO
in particular is a universal approximator of continuous operators between some Banach spaces, specifically Sobolev spaces
[
22
]
and the space of continuously differentiable functions
[
24
]
.

The operator
\mathcal{K}_{\partial{\Omega}}^{-*}\colon C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
defined in (
20
) is continuous (see
remark

2
) and can therefore be approximated by the
FNO
to arbitrary precision on a
fixed geometry

\partial{\Omega}
. We aim to learn the operator for any geometry, by considering the map
(\phi,\tilde{r}_{\ell})\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{\phi(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{-*}[\tilde{r}_{\ell}]
, which involves the curve
\phi
as well as the data
\tilde{r}_{\ell}
for
\ell=1,2
. Universality of
FNO
for this type of operator is less obvious, since the set of Jordan curves is not a Banach space.

Luckily, the micro problems in this work can be well approximated by a coarse numerical method, which is a continuous operator on the discretized data. Discretization amounts to evaluation on a finite set of points (see
section

3.3
), and the evaluation operator is continuous on
C(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Hence, the composition of the discretization followed by the numerical routine is itself continuous and therefore, an
FNO
can approximate the numerical routine to arbitrary precision.

5.2
Model and Discretization Error

In the context of
HMM
, there are additional error terms than those mentioned in
section

5.1
that affect the solution. One is the
model error
inherent in the
HMM
, which is the difference between the
HMM
approximation
u_{\mathrm{HMM}}
and the fully resolved solution
u_{\epsilon}
to (
9
). The model error was analyzed in
[
10
]
for a specific toy problem, but so far there is no rigorous analysis of it in the general case.

In this section we study the
approximation error
that is made with the learned approach to compute the slip amount. Our approach learns Riesz representors (that depend only on the geometry) instead of the slip amount itself (that depends also on the boundary conditions), which allows us to train on much less data, but significantly complicates the analysis. We will prove two main results. First, given sufficient alignment conditions on the background flow, we can show (
corollary

6
) that a bounded training error will result in a bound on the expected error of the slip amount
\tilde{\alpha}(x)
, given one arbitrary fixed micro domain centered at some point
x
on the rough boundary.

Second, we show (
theorem

9
) that a bounded expected error of the slip amount leads to a bound on the interpolated slip function
\alpha
on the boundary, given sufficient regularity of the slip amount. This result uses a particular choice of sampling and interpolation strategy and stronger assumptions about the properties of the slip operator (
2
). We include justification for these assumptions in
section

8.2
. Finally, we connect the error bound from
theorem

9
to the error in the macroscopic solution in
proposition

11
, using standard PDE energy norm estimates.

5.2.1
A Pointwise Error Estimate

To relate the training error, which is independent of boundary data, to the error in the slip coefficient, we must pose an additional constraint on the representors
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
– and in particular how well aligned they are to the boundary data
h
in the sense of
definition

4
. We will avoid involving the characteristic micro scale
\epsilon
in this section, but it becomes relevant for
theorem

9
.

Definition 4
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space endowed with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
. Two elements
v
,
w
of
H
are
\eta
–aligned for some
0<\eta<1
if

\frac{\langle v,w\rangle}{\|v\|\|w\|}\geq\eta.

Furthermore, let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
be
H
-valued random variables. Then,
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
are almost surely uniformly
\eta
–aligned if

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\{\boldsymbol{u}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{v}\text{ are not $\eta$--aligned}\}\bigr)=0.

We can now bound the error in the slip coefficient in the statistical sense, given that the correlation between the roughness structure at two different points on the wall decays sufficiently fast with respect to the distance between the points. Our main result in this section relies on the following general Lemma:

Lemma 5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
be random variables such that
\left|\boldsymbol{a}_{1}/\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\right|<C
holds a.s.
Furthermore, assume

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{b}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\delta_{1}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{b}_{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\delta_{2}^{2}.

Then
\mathbb{E}\left[\Bigl|\boldsymbol{a}_{1}/\boldsymbol{a}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b}_{1}/\boldsymbol{b}_{2}\Bigr|\right]<C(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{1}\delta_{2})
.

The main result of this section follows almost immediately from the above lemma:

Corollary 6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}
be a random domain generated by a
\mathcal{J}
-valued random element. We write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}
for the standard
L^{2}
-inner product and norm over
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]
and
\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]
be approximate solutions to the Riesz representors
\boldsymbol{r_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{r_{2}}
on
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}
, all taking values in
C(\boldsymbol{\partial Y},\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Assume
\boldsymbol{r_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]
are both almost surely uniformly
\eta_{1}
- and
\eta_{2}
-aligned with
h
, respectively. Furthermore, assume also that the slip amount
\langle\boldsymbol{r_{1}},h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}/\langle\boldsymbol{r_{2}},h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}
is almost surely bounded by
C
for some
C>0
. Finally, assume that the training losses are bounded by
\delta>0
in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]-\boldsymbol{r_{1}}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}{\|\boldsymbol{r_{1}}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}\right]\leq\delta^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]-\boldsymbol{r_{2}}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}{\|\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}^{2}}\right]\leq\delta^{2}.

(31)

Then, the error in the estimated slip amount is bounded as

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{r_{1}},h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}{\langle\boldsymbol{r_{2}},h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}-\frac{\langle\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}],h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}{\langle\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}],h\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\partial Y}}}\right|\right]\leq C\left(\delta(\eta_{1}^{-1}+\eta_{2}^{-1})+\delta^{2}\eta_{1}^{-1}\eta_{2}^{-1}\right).

Proof.

See
section

8.3
.

Equation (
31
) approximates the training loss when
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}
is sampled from the same distribution that we trained on. Note that even though our construction requires
\boldsymbol{r_{1}},\boldsymbol{r_{2}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}}
to be random variables, they are in practice
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}
-measurable.

The training error in (
31
) is slightly different from the bound used in (
25
). First, we need to control the inverse of the norm of
\mathcal{N}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}},\boldsymbol{\partial Y}]
, which is why it appears in the denominator. Second, we use the
H^{1}
-norm in practice, but this is not a problem for the theory since a bound in the
H^{1}
-norm also yields a bound in the
L_{2}
-norm.

Remark 4
.

We need to assume that training data is
\eta
-aligned. This can be hard to verify in general, but it can be done for some special cases. One is when the micro problem has periodic boundary conditions as shown in
proposition

12
.

5.2.2
An Interpolation Error Estimate

We now aim to show that interpolating over the boundary results in an error that is bounded. For the sake of analysis, it makes sense to model the boundary as a stochastic process. In order to control the interpolation error, we need to assume that the slip amount is bounded and Lipschitz as a function on the smooth surface
\Gamma_{0}
. For the purpose of this section, we assume that the rough wall is parameterized by a normal offset:

\Gamma_{\epsilon}=\bigl\{x+\epsilon\boldsymbol{\varphi}(x_{t}/\epsilon)\nu(x)\mid x\in\Gamma_{0}\bigr\},

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is a random
C^{2}
function.

Definition 7
.

The slip amount
\alpha\colon\Gamma_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
at the point on the smooth surface
x\in\Gamma_{0}
is computed as follows:

•

construct a micro domain
Y_{x}
such that
\partial Y_{x}\cap\Gamma_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(x)\cap\Gamma_{\epsilon}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(x)
is an
\epsilon
-sized ball around
x
.

•

solve the adjoint problems (
21
)–(
22
) on
Y_{x}
, and

•

compute the slip coefficient using (
13
).

Assumption 2
.

The slip amount is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz. Specifically, there exists
C>0
and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{3}>0
such that

\alpha(x)\leq\gamma_{1}C\quad\text{and}\quad|\alpha(x)-\alpha(y)|\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{3}}C|x-y|\quad\text{for any $x,y\in\Gamma_{0}$}

where
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon/\gamma_{1}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{3}=0
.

Remark 5
.

The condition that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is continuous is needed for
x\mapsto\alpha(x)
to be well defined.
2
is motivated by a simple example in
section

8.2
. Roughly,
\gamma_{1}
is the position of the evaluation line
\ell
, and
\gamma_{3}
is the finite width of the micro problem.

Finally, we define an interpolation operator. Although we use a general construction, a simple piecewise linear interpolation is sufficient to satisfy our requirements.

Definition 8
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset K
be a discretization of a set
K
. We define an interpolant as a linear operator
\mathcal{I}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to C(K,\mathbb{R})
that satisfies
\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{N}\bigr](x_{n})=\alpha_{n}
for all
n=1,\dots,N
. We additionally require
\mathcal{I}
to be continuous and such that there is some
C>0
where

\sup_{z\in K}\Bigl|\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{N}\bigr](z)\Bigr|\leq C\max_{n=1,\dots,N}|\alpha_{n}|\quad\text{holds for any $(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}$.}

Finally, we say
\mathcal{I}
tightly interpolates the Lipschitz function
\alpha\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
to the first order if

\Bigl|\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\alpha(x_{j}))_{j=1}^{N}\bigr](z)-\alpha(z)\Bigr|\leq CL\max_{z\in K}\min_{n=1,\dots,N}\|z-x_{n}\|,

holds with
C>0
independent of
L>0
(the Lipschitz constant of the function
\alpha
).

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be as in
definition

7
, and suppose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
satisfies
2
uniformly with
C,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{3}
known. Next assume that we have sample points
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
in
\Gamma_{0}
with

\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\min_{n=1,\dots,N}\|z-x_{n}\|\leq|\Gamma_{0}|/N\quad\text{where $|\Gamma_{0}|$ is the length of the domain.}

Finally, assume
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
estimates
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with an expected error bounded as

\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n})\bigr|\right]\leq\delta\gamma_{2}\quad\text{for some $\delta,\gamma_{2}>0$ and all $n=1,\dots,N$.}

Then, for any interpolant
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)=\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n})_{n=1}^{N}\bigr](z)
with
z\in\Gamma_{0}
that interpolates
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to the first order (
definition

8
), there exists
N
so that the error is bounded over
\Gamma_{0}
as

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(z)\bigr|\right]\leq 2\gamma_{1}C\sqrt{|\Gamma_{0}|\delta\gamma_{2}/(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3})}.

Proof.

The proof is essentially a repeated application of the triangle inequality. Let
z\in\Gamma_{0}
be fixed. Combining linearity, continuity and tightness of
\mathcal{I}
with Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(z)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Big|\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n}))_{n=1}^{N}\bigr](z)+\mathcal{I}\bigl[(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n}))_{n=1}^{N}\bigr](z)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(z)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{n=1,\dots,N}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n})\bigr|+CL\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\min_{n=1,\dots,N}\|z-x_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{n=1,\dots,N}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n})\bigr|+\frac{\gamma_{1}C|\Gamma_{0}|}{\gamma_{3}N}.

Taking the supremum over
z\in\Gamma_{0}
followed by the expectation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(z)\bigr|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}C|\Gamma_{0}|}{\gamma_{3}N}+C\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{n=1,\dots,N}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(x_{n})\bigr|\right]\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}C|\Gamma_{0}|}{\gamma_{3}N}+\gamma_{2}\delta CN.

The above estimate is minimized when
N=\sqrt{|\Gamma_{0}|\delta^{-1}\gamma_{1}/(\gamma_{2}\gamma_{3})}
, giving the result.

A key observation here is that taking the supremum norm results in a loss of accuracy – a point wise error of
\delta
gives rise to a interpolation error
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
. It is possible to improve this result to
\mathcal{O}(\delta\log(1/\delta))
, but that requires strong assumptions on the error distribution (existence of a moment generating function), which are outside the scope of this work.

5.2.3
Combining the Two Estimates

Given
theorem

9
and
corollary

section

8.1
as a motivation for our choices of

and

. For this final step, we need to assume that the distribution of the random micro domains is stationary, which we will do by simply assuming that

Theorem 10
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be as in
definition

7
, and suppose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
satisfies
2
uniformly with

known. Assume the setting in

holds uniformly for all micro problems
\boldsymbol{\partial Y}_{x}
with

, and for all

and
\eta_{2}=1/\gamma_{2}
, and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{2}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{2}/(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3})=0

\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
discretize the smooth boundary
\Gamma_{0}
with resolution

\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}

Then, for any weighted interpolant
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)=\mathcal{I}[(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n})_{n=1}^{N}](z)
that interpolates
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to the first order (
definition

8
), there is a suitable choice of

so that the interpolated slip amount is bounded in error over
\Gamma_{0}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tilde{\alpha}}(z)-\boldsymbol{\alpha}(z)\bigr|\right]\leq 2\gamma_{1}C\sqrt{\delta|\Gamma_{0}|\gamma_{2}/(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3})}.

Proof.

The result follows from combining
corollary

6
and
theorem

9
.

With a bound on the expected error in supremum norm, standard sensitivity analysis for linear elliptic PDE produce an error in the macroscopic solution that is of the same order as the error in the slip coefficient:

Proposition 11
.

Let
u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0},\mathbb{R}^{2})
solve the macro equations (
11
), with the same Dirichlet data
g\in C(\partial{\Omega}_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{0},\mathbb{R}^{2})
and slip functions
\alpha,\beta\in C(\Gamma_{0},\mathbb{R})
, respectively. Assume
\alpha,\beta
are both uniformly bounded from below by some

and from above by

. Then, there is a constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha,\beta
so that

is bounded in the

-seminorm:

\left|u-v\right|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0},\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\alpha_{0}^{-1}C\sup_{x\in\Gamma_{0}}|\alpha(x)-\beta(x)|.

Proof.

See
section

8.6
.

Taking the expectation on both sides, we then obtain a statistical bound on the macroscopic error.

6.1
Data Generation

We generate training data in three steps. First, the micro domain
\boldsymbol{Y}
is defined similarly to the unit cell used in homogenization:

\boldsymbol{Y}:=\mathcal{S}\Bigl(\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\mid\;0\leq x_{1}\leq 1,\;\boldsymbol{\varphi}(x_{1})\leq x_{2}\leq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}\bigr\}\Bigr)

(36)

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a 1D
Gaussian process
with
exponential kernel
that simulates a rough wall,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}>\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\infty}
is a random height and
\mathcal{S}
is a
smoothing operator
that rounds the corners of
\boldsymbol{Y}
so that the maximal curvature of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is also the maximal curvature of
\boldsymbol{Y}
. The smoothing operator ensures that no unnecessary high frequency content is introduced to the micro problem apart from the roughness. From
\boldsymbol{Y}
we approximate the corresponding Jordan curve
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{J}
by uniform discretization. In particular, we use in total eight polynomials of degree 5 (2 coordinates times 4 corners) to ensure that
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is twice differentiable everywhere.

Second, we generate a random line segment
\boldsymbol{\ell}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid 0\leq x_{1}\leq 1,\;x_{2}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}\}
that passes through
Y
at some random height
\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\infty}<\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}<\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}
.
fig.

3

\boldsymbol{Y}
and line segment
\boldsymbol{\ell}

\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{1}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{r}_{2}}
as described in
section

3.1

\boldsymbol{r_{1}},\boldsymbol{r_{2}}
using the boundary integral formulation (

7
Discussion and Outlook

We propose a learning-based precomputation method for microscopic simulations in a
HMM
, assuming a finite-dimensional, linear microscopic model. Applying this to laminar viscous flow over a rough wall, we develop an
FNO
-based neural architecture that leverages the boundary integral formulation of Stokes flow. This micro solver integrates into the
HMM
, and generalizes to arbitrary macroscopic domain and boundary values, provided the roughness distribution aligns with the training data. We show, under regularity assumptions, that a bound on training error leads to a bound on macroscopic error. Our approach achieves the same accuracy as numerical solvers in a third of the computation time on two test cases. Moreover, the error contribution from the learned component of
HMM
is an order of magnitude lower than the total error.

Our work was motivated by two main observations. First, the model error inherent in
HMM
allows for a coarse resolution of micro problems, but the microscopic solver remains the computational bottleneck. Secondly, deep learning surrogates are computationally faster than numerical solvers but have limited accuracy. Our numerical experiments largely support this analysis. Surprisingly,
FNO-HMM
outperforms
BIM-HMM
with discretization adjusted to match the
FNO-HMM
in two categories: inference time and
error variance
on the training data distribution, making it more predictable. We note that, although not done in our implementation,
BIM-HMM
can trade variance in the error for variance in compute times via adaptive discretization.

A crucial design choice is learning the Riesz representors for the two linear functionals involved in computing slip amounts, rather than directly solving micro problems. Methods that do not exploit this structure will always be slower than numerical solvers that compute the representors, since it reduces each
HMM
iteration to computing inner products. A major limitation of our approach is the reliance on linear, homogeneous microscale problems. In some non-linear flows with low Reynolds numbers such an approximation is accurate (for example,
[
10
]
study steady-state Navier Stokes). If the roughness scale is significantly larger than the width of the boundary layer however, another approach is needed.

In realistic settings we expect the roughness distribution to be known, either by design, or by surface scans of the domain. The FNO then likely needs to be trained, or at the very least fine-tuned, on the new roughness distribution. This is a significant limitation, as the training data generation is computationally expensive. One promising direction is adaptive sampling, in which the training data is generated adaptively during training. Additionally, exchanging the
FNO
for an interpretable model such as Gaussian-process regression could enable more accurate error estimates of the slip amount, and potentially allow for a scaling law on the amount of training data required at a given roughness scale.

Results are stated and proved in the special case of a micro domain with constant, horizontal velocity at the upper boundary and periodicity in the

•

•

•

(

We begin by defining the (micro) domain, which is of the form

Y:=\bigl\{(x,y)\colon x\in[0,\gamma_{3}],y\in[\varphi(x)-\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]\bigr\},

where

and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}>0
denote the distance from the evaluation line

to the upper and lower boundary, respectively, and

is the width of the domain. We assume

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{2}=0,\quad\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon/\gamma_{2}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon/\gamma_{1}=0,

(37)

so that asymptotically,
\epsilon\ll\gamma_{1}\ll\gamma_{2}

Next, let
\chi\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}
be a function that is
\gamma_{3}
-periodic in the

\displaystyle\Delta\chi(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla\pi(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=0

(38)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\chi(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=0

for
\boldsymbol{x}\in Y
,

(39)

\displaystyle\chi(x,\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=Ue_{x}

for

(40)

\displaystyle\chi\bigl(x,\varphi(x)-\gamma_{1}\bigr)

\displaystyle=0

for
x\in(0,\gamma_{3})
.

(41)

Here,

is the unit vector in the

\langle\chi_{1}\rangle(y):=\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}\int_{0}^{\gamma_{3}}\chi(x,y)\cdot e_{x}\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\partial_{y}\chi_{1}\rangle(y):=\partial_{y}\langle\chi_{1}\rangle(y).

Specifically, we want to evaluate the above at

.

Assume
\chi
solves (

satisfies (

and

y\mapsto\langle\chi_{1}\rangle(y)
satisfies

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\langle\chi_{1}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}y^{2}}(y)&=\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}\int_{0}^{\gamma_{3}}\partial_{y}^{2}\chi_{1}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}\int_{0}^{\gamma_{3}}-\partial_{x}^{2}\chi_{1}(x,y)+\partial_{x}\pi(x,y)\mathrm{d}x=0\\[7.5pt]

8.2
Lipschitz Continuity of Slip Amount

Here we give a heuristic motivation for why it is natural to assume that the slip coefficient is Lipschitz continuous. The computation is simplified significantly by solving an infinitely wide micro domain of the form

Y:=\bigl\{(x,y)\colon x\in\mathbb{R},y\in[\varphi(x)-\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]\bigr\}.

while we still only average over the interval
[0,\gamma_{3}]
. This avoids the need for more advanced theoretical machinery such as shape calculus. With the above construction and
\epsilon,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
as in
section

8.1
, we write the slip amount
\alpha(z)
on the following form:

\alpha(z)=\dfrac{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\partial_{y}\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}=\dfrac{\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\gamma_{3}}}\chi_{1}(x+z,0)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\gamma_{3}}}\partial_{y}\chi_{1}(x+z,0)\,\mathrm{d}x},

where
S_{z}[u](x,y):=u(x+z,y)
is the shift operator.
We can now characterize the regularity of the slip amount:

Proposition 14
.

For
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, the slip amount
z\mapsto\alpha(z)
is Lipschitz-continuous with a Lipschitz constant bounded by
2\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{3}
.

Proof.

Note first that any function
f
defined by
f(x,z)=g(x+z)
satisfies
\partial_{x}f=\partial_{z}f
. Differentiating
\alpha(z)
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\biggl[\frac{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\partial_{y}\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}\biggr]=\alpha(z)\biggl(\frac{\bigl\langle\partial_{x}S_{z}[\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}-\frac{\bigl\langle\partial_{x}S_{z}[\partial_{y}\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\partial_{y}\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle(0)}\biggr)\\[7.5pt]
=\frac{\alpha(z)}{\gamma_{3}}\biggl(\frac{\chi_{1}(z+\gamma_{3},0)-\chi_{1}(z,0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle}-\frac{\partial_{y}\chi_{1}(z+\gamma_{3},0)-\partial_{y}\chi_{1}(z,0)}{\bigl\langle S_{z}[\partial_{y}\chi_{1}]\bigr\rangle}\biggr).

Next, we evoke the estimates from
corollary

13
and
proposition

12
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{3}}\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon/\gamma_{1}+\epsilon/\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{2})\bigr)\leq 2\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{3}\quad\text{for $\epsilon>0$ small enough.}

(45)

Hence,

\bigl|\alpha(x)-\alpha(y)\bigr|=\left|\int_{x}^{y}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha(z)}{\mathrm{d}z}\mathrm{d}z\right|\leq 2\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{3}}|x-y|,

i.e., the Lipschitz constant is bounded by
2\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{3}
.

Proof of
