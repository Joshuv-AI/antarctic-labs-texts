Clinical Outcomes in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Treated With Non–Vitamin K Oral Anticoagulants Across Varying Body Mass Index
Authors: Astrid Fritz Hansson, Karin Jensevik Eriksson, Christina Christersson, Claes Held, Gorav Batra
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are conflicting data on outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation treated with non–vitamin K oral anticoagulants across varying body mass index (BMI). We investigated cardiovascular and bleeding risk in patients with atrial fibrillation with varying BMI.Methods and ResultsObservational cohort study from the Swedish oral anticoagulation registry between August 2, 2011, and December 31, 2018. Primary outcomes were mortality, ischemic stroke/systemic embolism, and major bleeding. Baseline BMI (kg/m2) was analyzed continuously and categorized: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <25 kg/m2), preobesity (25 to <30 kg/m2), and obesity class I to III (30 to <35, 35 to <40, and ≥40 kg/m2). Adjusted Cox models and nonlinear relationships of BMI were modeled using restricted cubic splines. Non–vitamin K oral anticoagulant–treated patients with atrial fibrillation were included (n=26 047). At baseline, 602 (2.3%) were underweight, 9101 (34.9%) were normal weight, 9970 (38.3%) were preobese, 4280 (16.4%) were obese class I, 1486 (5.7%) were obese class II, and 608 (2.3%) were obese class III. Underweight and obesity class III were in adjusted continuous analysis associated with increased mortality and major bleeding, with lowest risk observed among preobese patients with BMI 28.2 and 26.2 kg/m2, respectively. In adjusted categorical analysis, underweight was associated with increased mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 1.77 [95% CI, 1.57–1.99]) and nonsignificant higher risk of major bleeding (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.95–1.58]). Similarly, obesity class III was associated with increased mortality (HR, 1.67 [95% CI, 1.40–1.99]) and major bleeding (HR, 1.68 [95% CI, 1.29–2.17]). No significant association was observed between higher BMI and ischemic stroke/systemic embolism.ConclusionsIn non–vitamin K oral anticoagulant–treated patients with atrial fibrillation, there appears to be a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and mortality and major bleeding.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, body mass index, non–vitamin K oral anticoagulants, oral anticoagulantsSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Cardiovascular Disease, Obesity, Anticoagulants

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia in adults, with an increasing prevalence. 1 Non–vitamin K oral anticoagulants (NOACs) are often, because of their overall clinical benefit, preferred over vitamin K antagonists for stroke prevention in patients with AF in concordance with clinical practice guidelines. 2 Contemporaneous with the widespread use of NOACs in patients with AF, the prevalence of extreme low and high weight has increased globally during the past decades. 3 Body mass index (BMI) is an easy screening method for standardized weight assessment, and it is an independent risk factor for development and progression of AF. 4 Thus, understanding the prognostic impact of BMI on clinical outcomes among patients with AF is important.

Although obesity is a risk factor associated with a prothrombotic state, various trial data and systematic reviews have suggested favorable outcomes for patients with AF and high BMI: the obesity paradox. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Still, the finding of lower rates of adverse events in preobese and obese patients with AF treated with NOACs is conflicting and has not been confirmed in observational cohort studies. 7 , 9 , 10

Extremely low and high BMI affects the body composition, the thromboembolic tendency, and the function of vital organs such as the heart, liver, and kidney. 11 , 12 Because of body modifications, the absorption, volume distribution, metabolism, and elimination of various drugs can be expected to be affected. 12 Different pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics combined with standardized doses of NOACs may also affect the safety and efficacy of NOACs across varying BMI. As an example, maximum plasma concentrations of apixaban have been shown to be 27% lower and 31% higher in underweight and obese patients, respectively. Still, no continuous relationship between plasma concentration of NOACs and outcomes has been established.

On the basis of the paucity of real‐life data examining NOACs in patients with AF across the range of BMI, the overall objective of this observational cohort study was to investigate the risk of mortality, ischemic stroke or systemic embolism, and major bleedings in patients with AF treated with NOACs across varying BMI. The hypothesis was that extreme BMIs might be associated with a higher risk of mortality, ischemic stroke/systemic embolism, and major bleedings in these patients.

Methods

Data Sources and Patient and Public Involvement

This observational cohort study was based on data from AuriculA, the Swedish national quality register for patients with AF with oral anticoagulation treatment regardless of indication. The study included data from 262 participating centers nationwide, representing approximately half of all patients with AF in Sweden. 13 AuriculA also serves as a dosage/control system for oral anticoagulant therapy. Patient demographics, comorbidities, biochemical markers, and medical treatment were collected from AuriculA. Baseline data from AuriculA were enriched with data from the National Patient Register going back to January 1, 2010, leaving no missing data for comorbidities. The National Patient Register collects International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), codes and dates for all hospitalizations and outpatient visits in Sweden since 1987, and it has high validity. 14 Baseline comedications were obtained from the National Prescribed Drug Register, if prescribed within 6 months before index visit. The National Prescribed Drug Register contains all prescribed medication dispensed at pharmacies in Sweden since 2005. 15 Combined, the information formed the foundation for the variables used in the analysis (Table S1). Data linkage between registries was performed by the National Board of Health and Welfare using the 10‐digit person registration number available to all Swedish citizens.

Patients with AF registered in the AuriculA registry between August 2, 2011, and December 31, 2018, were included. The date of August 2, 2011, marks the day of the first NOAC being approved in Sweden; and December 31, 2018, marks the end of available data. Patients with age <18 years, mechanical heart valves, mitral stenosis, and no NOAC treatment were excluded (Figure 1). Patients with missing or unreasonable BMI (weight <20 kg or >250 kg; height <50 cm or >250 cm) were excluded.

All patients included in AuriculA were informed about their participation in the national quality register and had the possibility to opt out, and as such, no informed consent was required. AuriculA does not allow individual data sharing with third parties. The aggregated data that support the findings of this study are available from the AuriculA steering committee on reasonable request. For contact details, please refer to https://www.ucr.uu.se/auricula. The study was approved by the ethical review board in Sweden (application number 2021‐04509).

Body Mass Index

Height and weight were retrieved from AuriculA. BMI was calculated according to the following formula: BMI=weight (kg)/(height×height) (kg/m2). Patients were stratified into the World Health Organization's (WHO) BMI classification: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to <25 kg/m2), preobesity (25 to <30 kg/m2), obesity class I (30 to <35 kg/m2), obesity class II (35 to <40 kg/m2), and obesity class III (≥40 kg/m2). 16 Continuous BMI was used as the primary independent exposure. Categorical BMI was used as an exposure in secondary analysis.

Outcomes

Outcomes were obtained from the National Patient Register and the Swedish Cause of Death Register using ICD‐10 codes (Table S2). There were 4 outcomes in this study: all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and a composite of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism and major bleeding. The ICD‐10 codes in the National Patient Register corresponding to stroke and major bleeding have previously been validated with high sensitivity and specificity. 14 , 17

Statistical Analysis

This study is reported following the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (Figure S1). Baseline characteristics represented as categorical variables were presented using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were presented using medians with interquartile range. Statistical differences between groups were tested using the Kruskal‐Wallis test or Pearson χ2 tests as appropriate. In the primary analyses, restricted cubic splines with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution of BMI) were used to model nonlinear relationships between BMI as a continuous variable and all outcomes. The median of the patients with normal BMI was set as reference. Cox proportional hazard models were used to adjust for individual factors in the CHA2DS2‐VASc score (heart failure, hypertension, age, diabetes, previous stroke/systemic embolism/transient ischemic attack, prior myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, previous coronary artery bypass graft surgery/percutaneous coronary intervention, and sex) and other established risk factors (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer [within past 3 years], liver disease, chronic kidney disease, and previous major bleeding), medications (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors and NSAIDs), and dosage of NOAC. The proportional hazards' assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals with no significant violations observed. Kaplan‐Meier estimates were plotted to analyze the association between BMI categories and outcomes. In secondary analyses, adjusted Cox proportional hazard models as above were used in the categorical analyses, with normal BMI as reference. Patients were censored at the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2018) or at the time of death. When the outcome was all‐cause mortality, patients were only censored at the end of follow‐up.

In a sensitivity analysis, an on‐treatment analysis was performed, and the end of follow‐up was set as the end of the ordination period, which reflects cessation of treatment or change in oral anticoagulant therapy. To overcome potential reverse causality, a sensitivity analysis removing the first year of follow‐up was performed for all‐cause mortality. As previous studies have reported difference in the effectiveness and bleeding risk of NOACs based on sex, outcomes in relation to BMI were also assessed separately for female and male patients. 10 To establish potential selection bias, the study population was compared with those with missing BMI data using Pearson χ2 tests or the Wilcoxon test, as appropriate. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine whether patients with varying BMI were treated with the full or reduced dosage of NOAC, following recommendations from clinical practice guidelines. 2

Two‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed at Uppsala Clinical Research Center (Uppsala, Sweden) with the use of R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), version 4.1.2.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Comorbidities

In total, 85 892 patients with AF were registered in AuriculA during the studied period. After implementing inclusion and exclusion criteria, 26 047 patients (42.2% women and 57.8% men) were included in the present study (Figure 1). Patients excluded because of missing data on BMI were marginally older, had slightly different baseline medication and comorbidity profiles, and exhibited numerically higher mortality rates compared with the patients included in the present analysis (Table S3 and S4). When stratified according to the WHO's BMI classification, 602 (2.3%) patients were categorized as underweight, 9101 (34.9%) were categorized as normal weight, 9970 (38.3%) were categorized as preobesity, 4280 (16.4%) were categorized as obesity class I, 1486 (5.7%) were categorized as obesity class II, and 608 (2.3%) were categorized as obesity class III. Baseline characteristics are presented in the Table 1. Underweight patients tended to be older, tended to be female sex, and had higher occurrence of heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, and ischemic stroke, resulting in higher median CHA2DS2‐VASc scores. Compared with patients with normal BMI, obese patients were younger, more likely to have hypertension or diabetes, and more likely to be treated with a calcium channel blocker, β‐blocker, renin‐angiotensin system inhibitor, and lipid‐lowering agents.

All‐Cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Mortality

During a median follow‐up of 2.2 years, there were 6052 events of all‐cause mortality. When fitting continuous levels of BMI into adjusted restricted cubic splines, there was a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and all‐cause mortality, with a high variation in risk also observed among patients within BMI categories (Figure 2). In adjusted continuous analysis, preobese patients with BMI 28.2 kg/m2 had the lowest association with risk of all‐cause mortality. Underweight and obesity class III were associated with the highest risk of all‐cause mortality compared with patients with normal BMI (Figure 2). Accordingly, when categorizing BMI according to the WHO classification in secondary analysis, a similar finding was observed with underweight (hazard ratio [HR], 1.77 [95% CI, 1.57–1.99]; P<0.0001) and obesity class III (HR, 1.67 [95% CI, 1.40–1.99]; P<0.0001) being associated with higher risk of all‐cause mortality (Figures 3 and 4). In contrast, preobesity (HR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.76–0.85]; P<0.0001) and obesity class I (HR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.76–0.89]; P<0.0001) were associated with a lower risk of all‐cause mortality in both continuous and categorical analysis. Similar findings were observed for cardiovascular mortality (Figures 2, 3, 4).

Ischemic Stroke or Systemic Embolism

In total, there were 1191 episodes of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism during a median follow‐up of 2.1 years. Although the overall effect of BMI in adjusted continuous analysis was nonsignificant, point estimates for BMI levels indicating underweight were associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism, whereas there was no such association between higher BMI and ischemic stroke or systemic embolism (Figure 2). When categorizing BMI according to the WHO classification, there were no significant associations between BMI and ischemic stroke or systemic embolism (Figures 3 and 4).

Major Bleeding

A total of 2554 patients experienced major bleeding during a median follow‐up of 2.0 years. In the adjusted continuous analysis, a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and major bleeding was observed, with the lowest risk occurring at a BMI of 26.2 kg/m2 (Figure 2). Underweight, obesity class II, and obesity class III were associated with a higher risk of major bleeding (Figure 2). This finding was further supported when BMI was categorized, with obesity class III in multivariable analyses being associated with higher risk of major bleeding (HR, 1.68 [95% CI, 1.29–2.17]; P<0.0001), and although not significant, underweight patients having a numerically higher risk of major bleeding (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.95–1.58]; P=0.12) (Figure 4).

Sensitivity Analysis

In sensitivity analyses, patients were followed up until treatment discontinuation or switch in treatment strategy in an on‐treatment analysis. The median follow‐up for the on‐treatment analysis was 1.1 years. Despite the shorter follow‐up duration, the findings from the on‐treatment analysis remained similar to those in the main analyses (Figure S2 and S3). Likewise, when the first year of follow‐up was excluded, the results remained unchanged (Figure S4). In the sex‐specific analyses, the results mostly aligned with the main findings, except for male patients, where no associations were found between being underweight and the risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism, and major bleeding (Figure S5). This finding could probably be explained by the low number of underweight male patients in the study population (Table 1). Further sensitivity analyses displayed that patients with higher BMI were less likely to have reduced dosage of NOACs compared with normal weight patients. However, when reduced dosage was prescribed, the dose reduction strategy was often not aligned with recommended dose reduction criteria (Tables S5 and S6). Underweight patients were more prone to have a reduced dosage of NOAC compared with normal weight patients (Table S5).

Discussion

This nationwide cohort study examined patients with AF treated with NOACs. The study revealed a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and both mortality and major bleeding, which was supported by analyses of BMI as both a continuous and a categorical exposure. Furthermore, within the categorized BMI cutoffs in the continuous analysis, a high variation in the risk of mortality and major bleeding was observed. There was no significant association observed between higher BMI and ischemic stroke or systemic embolism. However, the continuous analysis of BMI suggested that being underweight was associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism.

The U‐shaped relationship between BMI and all‐cause mortality, with higher risk among underweight and extremely obese patients, observed in this study is similar to findings from a systematic review of prospective studies, including patients treated with NOACs and vitamin K antagonists. 10 , 18 Also, preobesity and obesity have in prior systematic reviews and trial cohorts been associated with favorable outcomes and better survival: the obesity paradox. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 In subgroup analyses from the ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation) trial, preobesity and obesity were associated with a lower risk of mortality. 5 Conversely, observational studies, such as those from the EORP‐AF (EURObservational Research Programme–Atrial Fibrillation) and the FANTASIIA registry, have not found BMI to be independently associated with mortality, therefore not confirming the presence of the obesity paradox in real‐life patients. 9 , 10 However, our study confirms an obesity paradox in patients with AF treated with NOACs, up to a certain level of obesity, which contrasts with prior cohort studies. In this context, patients with extremely low or high BMI have often been excluded from clinical trials. For instance, outcomes data have not been separately reported for patients with a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 or for patients with a BMI ≥30 to 35 kg/m2 in the pivotal NOAC trials. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

In the present study, the overall effect of BMI on the risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism in adjusted continuous analysis was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, point estimates for BMI levels indicating underweight were associated with a higher risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism. These findings are consistent with results from a limited number of studies conducted on underweight patients treated with NOACs. 6 , 8 Data from trial cohorts suggest that obese patients may have a lower risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism. 6 , 7 However, whether a higher BMI is associated with a lower risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism remains unknown based on real‐life studies such as ours, which have shown no such association. 9 , 10 , 23 Nevertheless, it is reassuring to observe that there was no increased risk of stroke or systemic embolism associated with a higher BMI, contrary to our initial hypothesis.

In our study, there was a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and major bleeding, with the lowest risk of bleeding among patients with a BMI of 26.2 kg/m2, a finding that was also supported when BMI was categorized according to the WHO classification. The higher risk of major bleeding associated with obesity contradicts earlier reports from systematic reviews based on categorical data from the NOAC trials in which obesity was associated with lower risk of major bleeding. 7 Few patients in the NOAC trials had obesity class III. 7 However, our findings align with continuous data from the ENGAGE‐AF TIMI 48 (Effective Anticoagulation With Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction 48) trial, in which obesity was associated with higher risk of major bleeding. Also, a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and major bleeding in patients with AF has been suggested in findings from prospective registry studies. 23

Possible explanations for the obesity paradox and the favorable outcomes among preobese and obese patients might be that these patients more frequently have comorbidities (eg, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes) that are earlier and more often aggressively treated with pharmacologic drugs, such as angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, β‐blockers, and statins (as observed in the present study), which might be benefical. 7 , 10 Another explanation could be a greater capacity to manage metabolic stress from chronic illness because of better metabolic reserves. 24 Furthermore, adipose tissue has been found to produce tumor necrosis factor‐α receptors, which alternate arrhythmogenic substrates and reduce inflammation. 5 , 25 Higher BMI has also been associated with lower levels of natriuretic peptides, whereas higher levels of natriuretic peptides have been associated with increased risk of stroke and mortality in patients with AF. 26 , 27 Finally, higher BMI has been associated with lower plasma renin and angiotensin levels during stress, whereas higher levels have been related to unfavorable outcomes. 28 , 29

In our study, patients with underweight and obesity class III had higher risk of mortality and major bleeding. In extremely obese patients, the benefits of high BMI may not exceed the disadvantages of additional adiposity (eg, organ dysfunction, prothrombotic state, and cardiovascular burden). 30 Other possible explanations, probably less pertinent, include loss in efficacy with NOACs because of the lipophilic characteristics of NOAC and altered blood flow to the liver and kidneys, resulting in higher pharmacokinetic clearance and thus lower concentration of NOACs. 11 , 12 , 30 , 31 In reverse, this could also partly explain the higher risk of bleeding associated with underweight. The higher risk of mortality in underweight patients could also be related to residual confounding factors, and underweight is often associated with older age, frailty, comorbidities, and a lack of reserves to handle possible catabolic states. 24 Cardiovascular diseases in underweight patients may also have a different cause than the same disease in patients with obesity and may be attributable to genetic predisposition, which can lead to poorer prognosis. 28 , 32 The limited number of underweight patients (2.3%) and those in obesity class III (2.3%) in this study may suggest that underweight and extreme obesity in the context of AF and NOAC might not be a major clinical concern. Nevertheless, the findings in our study underscore the importance of carefully managing and closely monitoring patients with extremely low or high BMIs. Also, previous studies have shown a lower risk of bleeding complications in obese patients (weight >120 kg or BMI >40 kg/m2) with AF receiving NOACs compared with warfarin. 33 Our study does not contradict these results and does not recommend against prescribing NOACs to patients with low or high BMIs, as our study did not compare different oral anticoagulants.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths and some limitations. The key strength is the large number of participants representing real‐world nationwide patients with broad inclusion criteria and with no loss to follow‐up. The Swedish national registries also provide unique opportunities to collect data and to enrich registries with each other, resulting in minimal missing data. Another strength is that BMI was calculated on the basis of weight and height, rather than being limited to obesity‐related ICD‐10 codes. However, a limited number of patients were excluded because of missing data on height and weight, which could potentially introduce selection bias, as evident by the differences in baseline characteristics. BMI was analyzed both continuously and using the WHO categories, resulting in simpler interpretation of findings. The continuous analysis of BMI also displayed a high variation in risk within BMI cutoffs, information that is often lost when information is categorized. Among the limitations, BMI, despite being the most frequent measurement of adiposity, might not be the most accurate measurement of obesity because it does not take age, sex, and level of fat mass into account. 16 , 30 Other anthropometric measurements (eg, waist circumference and body composition) were not available in AuriculA but could provide more insights. BMI was collected at baseline, notwithstanding that weight might change over time. However, data from prior studies have shown only minor changes in BMI over time. 34 Furthermore, weight or height was not available in AuriculA for patients treated with vitamin K antagonists, therefore preventing a comparison between NOACs and vitamin K antagonists that would have been important to report. Because of the observational nature of the study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, despite adjustment for established confounders. Also, adjustment for further confounding factors not available in our data sample (eg, race and ethnicity, inflammatory biomarkers, and alcohol consumption) may have explained some of the findings. Although an on‐treatment analysis was performed and showed similar findings to the main analysis, compliance to medication over time cannot be guaranteed and is a limitation of this study.

Conclusions

In real‐life patients with AF treated with NOAC, there seems to be a U‐shaped relationship between BMI and adverse outcomes, including mortality and major bleeding. Patients with preobesity and obesity class I had the lowest risk of mortality, a phenomenon known as the obesity paradox. However, underweight and obesity class III were associated with a higher risk of mortality and major bleeding, underscoring the importance of close monitoring for these patients. It was reassuring to observe that there was no association between a higher BMI and the risk of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism.
