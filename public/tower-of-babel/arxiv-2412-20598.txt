Phonon-Induced Effects in Quantum Dot Absorption and Resonance Fluorescence with Hierarchy of Pure States
Sebastian Toivonen, Kimmo Luoma

Abstract
We investigate a quantum dot (QD) system coupled to a vibrational environment with a super-Ohmic spectral density and weakly to a leaky cavity mode, a model relevant for semiconductor-based single-photon sources. The phonon coupling induces dephasing and broadens the absorption and emission line shapes, while the weakly coupled cavity mode leads to effective driving of the QD.To capture non-Markovian effects, we use non-Markovian Quantum State Diffusion and its hierarchical extension the Hierarchy of Pure States to compute multitime correlation functions underlying absorption and resonance fluorescence spectra. We present numerical results for the absorption spectra at strong phonon coupling and finite temperature, as well as for resonance fluorescence spectra at varying phonon coupling strengths and temperatures, and analyse the visibility of the resonance fluorescence spectra to provide insights into how phonon coupling and thermal effects influence the spectral features.

II
Model

In this work we consider a driven semiconductor QD that is placed in a cavity. The exciton, electron-hole pair, is coupled both to the cavity modes and the surrounding lattice vibrations. The QD is modeled as a two-level system with states
\ket{g}
and
\ket{X}
corresponding to the ground and excited states, respectively, and the system Hamiltonian is given by
H_{\text{S}}=\omega_{\text{X}}\ket{X}\bra{X}
. The lattice vibrations, or phonons, are modeled as quantum harmonic oscillators. The free field Hamiltonian of the phonons is given (in the harmonic approximation) by
H_{\text{Ph}}=\sum_{\lambda}\omega_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}a_{\lambda}
, where
a^{\dagger}_{\lambda}
and
a_{\lambda}
are creation and annihilation operators of the mode
\omega_{\lambda}
. We assume a linear coupling between the QD and the environment. The interaction Hamiltonian is in this case given by

\displaystyle H_{\text{I,Ph}}=\ket{X}\bra{X}\sum_{\lambda}g_{\lambda}(a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}+a_{\lambda}).

The coupling strength of each phonon mode is encoded in the coefficients
g_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
. To measure the coupling strength in the continuum limit, the exciton-phonon spectral density is defined by

\displaystyle J_{\text{Ph}}(\omega)=\sum_{\lambda}|g_{\lambda}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\lambda}).

(1)

We assume that the dominant exciton-phonon coupling mechanism is the deformation potential
[
37
]
. As such the spectral density has the explicit form

\displaystyle J_{\text{Ph}}(\omega)=A\omega^{3}e^{-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\xi^{2}}}

(2)

with
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
being the cut-off frequency and
A\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the coupling strength. The influence of the lattice vibrations on the QD is encoded in the bath-correlation function (BCF)

\displaystyle\alpha(t-s)=\mathrm{tr}\left\{\rho_{\text{Ph}}Q(t)Q(s)\right\},

(3)

where
Q(t)=\sum_{\lambda}g_{\lambda}\left(a_{\lambda}e^{-i\omega_{\lambda}t}+a_{\lambda}^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{\lambda}t}\right)
. In the finite temperature case

\rho_{\text{Ph}}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta H_{\text{Ph}}}
and

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\text{d}\omega J_{\text{Ph}}(\omega)\left(\text{coth}\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\cos\omega\tau-i\sin\omega\tau\right),

with
\tau=t-s
and the inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
. At zero temperature this reduces simply to the Fourier integral

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)=\int^{\infty}_{0}\text{d}\omega J_{\text{Ph}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}.

The cavity and it’s associated coupling is incorporated in a quantized manner while the external driving is treated as a classical field. We assume that a single cavity mode
\omega_{c}
is present. The Hamiltonian of the driven cavity is given by

\displaystyle H_{C+D}=

\displaystyle\omega_{c}b^{\dagger}b+g(\ket{X}\bra{g}b+\ket{g}\bra{X}b^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+f(t)b^{\dagger}+f^{*}(t)b,

where
b^{\dagger}
and
b
are the field creation and annihilation operators and
f(t)
is an arbitrary drive and
g
describes the coupling strength between the cavity and the exciton. We eliminate the cavity operators by adiabatic elimination. Detailed calculation is presented in the appendix. This results in the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C+D}=

\displaystyle\omega_{c}|\langle b\rangle|^{2}+g(\ket{X}\bra{g}\langle b\rangle+\ket{g}\bra{X}\langle b^{\dagger}\rangle)

\displaystyle+f(t)\langle b^{\dagger}\rangle+f^{*}(t)\langle b\rangle,

where
\langle b\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\langle b\rangle=\frac{f(t)}{-\omega_{c}+i\frac{\kappa}{2}},

where
\kappa>0
is the cavity damping rate.
In the case of monochromatic continuous wave driving, we set
f(t)=\Omega^{\prime}\cos\omega_{D}t
. Applying the rotating wave approximation and moving to the frame rotating at the drive frequency
\omega_{D}
results in the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{C+D}=\tilde{\Omega}(\omega_{c}\sigma_{x}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sigma_{y}),

(4)

where
\tilde{\Omega}=\frac{-g\Omega^{\prime}}{2\omega_{c}^{2}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}}
. The terms proportional to the identity operator have been omitted since they lead to a global phase only.We also define
\Omega=\Omega^{\prime}\omega_{c}
.

II.1
Hierarchy of pure states

To solve the dynamics of the QD system, we will use the hierarchy of pure states (HOPS) method
[
38
]
. HOPS is a numerically exact method developed to solve the equations emerging from non-Markovian quantum state diffusion (NMQSD)
[
39
,
40
]
. The NMQSD approach aims to solve the dynamics of both the system and its environment for a given open quantum system. The environment is assumed to consist of quantum harmonic oscillators and the total Hamiltonian is assumed to be of the form

\displaystyle H=H_{S}+H_{E}+H_{I},

where
H_{S}
is the system Hamiltonian,
H_{E}=\sum_{\lambda}\omega_{\lambda}a^{\dagger}a
is the environment Hamiltonian and

\displaystyle H_{I}=\sum_{\lambda}(g_{\lambda}La^{\dagger}+g^{*}_{\lambda}L^{\dagger}a)

is the system-environment interaction Hamiltonian. Here
L
is the coupling operator, which is an operator of the system. At zero temperature the initial total state is assumed to be factorized
\ket{\Psi_{0}}=\ket{\psi}\ket{\mathbf{0}}
. The reduced density operator is given by the ensemble average

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t)=\text{tr}_{E}\{\ket{\Psi_{t}}\bra{\Psi_{t}}\}=\mathcal{M}[\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}\bra{\psi_{t}(z)}].

(5)

The average is taken over the stochastic trajectories
\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}
and these obey the linear NMQSD equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}=

\displaystyle-iH_{S}\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}+z_{t}^{*}L\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}

\displaystyle-L^{\dagger}\int^{t}_{0}\text{d}s\alpha(t-s)\frac{\delta}{\delta z_{s}^{*}}\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})},

(6)

where
z_{t}^{*}
is a Gaussian stochastic process with zero mean and correlations

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[z_{t}z_{s}]=0,~\mathcal{M}[z_{t}z_{s}^{*}]=\alpha(t-s).

The norm of the state vector
\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}
is not preserved and as such only a few rare trajectories dominate the ensemble average (
5
). To ensure more rapid convergence, it is desirable to take the ensemble average over normalized trajectories such that each trajectory contributes with equal weight to the average

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t)=\mathcal{M}\left[\frac{\ket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}\bra{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z})}}{||\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})||^{2}}\right].

This requires some modifications to the linear NMQSD equation
[
40
]
. The noise process is replaced by a shifted noise process

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{t}^{*}=z_{t}^{*}+\int^{t}_{0}\text{d}s\alpha^{*}(t-s)\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{s}

(7)

and the operator
L^{\dagger}
is replaced with
L^{\dagger}-\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}
where the expectation value is calculated using the normalized state vector

\displaystyle\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}=\frac{\braket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z})|L^{\dagger}|\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}}{\braket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z})|\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}}.

These replacements lead to the non-linear NMQSD equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}=

\displaystyle-iH_{S}\ket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}+\tilde{z}^{*}_{t}L\ket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}

\displaystyle-(L^{\dagger}-\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t})\int^{t}_{0}\text{d}s\alpha(t-s)\frac{\delta}{\delta\tilde{z}_{s}^{*}}\ket{\psi_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}.

Finite temperature can be incorporated by adding a stochastic potential to the Hamiltonian
[
41
,
42
]

\displaystyle H_{S}\rightarrow H_{S}+V(t),

where
V(t)=\eta^{*}_{t}L+\eta_{t}L^{\dagger}
, with the Gaussian stochastic process
\eta_{t}
obeying the statistics

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[\eta_{t}]=\mathcal{M}[\eta_{t}\eta_{s}]=0,~\mathcal{M}[\eta_{t}\eta_{s}^{*}]=\sum_{\lambda}\bar{n}_{\lambda}|g_{\lambda}|^{2}e^{-i\omega_{\lambda}(t-s)},

where
\bar{n}_{\lambda}=(e^{\beta\omega_{\lambda}}-1)^{-1}
is the Bose-Einstein distribution for the environmental mode
\omega_{\lambda}
.

The difficulty in solving the NMQSD equations arise from the functional derivatives. The HOPS method was developed to solve the NMQSD equations in a numerically exact manner. Essentially the functional derivative is turned into a hierarchy of auxiliary states by assuming that the BCF takes the form of a sum of exponentials

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau)=\sum^{K}_{\mu=1}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}e^{-W_{\mu}\tau},

with
\mathcal{G}_{\mu},W_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}
. The linear HOPS equation corresponding to the linear NMQSD equation (
6
) is given as
[
38
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}}(z^{*})}=

\displaystyle\left(-iH_{S}-\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}k_{\mu}W_{\mu}+z^{*}_{t}L\right)\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}}(z^{*})}

\displaystyle+L\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}k_{\mu}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mu}}(z^{*})}

(8)

\displaystyle-L^{\dagger}\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{e}_{\mu}}(z^{*})},

where
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},...,k_{K})
with
k_{\mu}\in\mathbb{N},k_{\mu}\geq 0
. Note that this is a system, or hierarchy, of coupled stochastic differential equations. Solving this hierarchy corresponds to solving the NMQSD equation for
\ket{\psi_{t}(z^{*})}=\ket{\psi^{\mathbf{0}}_{t}(z^{*})}
. The remaining states
\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}}(z^{*})}
are ’auxiliary states,’ illustrating how the finite memory time of the environment influences the time evolution of the QD. For practical calculations, the hierarchy is truncated based on a specified condition, with the last level set as a terminator. In this work, we employ a triangular truncation condition where auxiliary states for which
|\mathbf{k}|=\sum_{\mu}k_{\mu}>\mathcal{K}
, for some predetermined
\mathcal{K}
, are set to zero. More sophisticated truncation methods have been investigated as well
[
43
]
. The corresponding HOPS for the non-linear NMQSD equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{z}^{*})}=

\displaystyle\left(-iH_{S}-\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}k_{\mu}W_{\mu}+\tilde{z}^{*}_{t}L\right)\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{z}^{*})}

\displaystyle+L\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}k_{\mu}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mu}}(\tilde{z}^{*})}

\displaystyle-(L^{\dagger}-\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t})\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}\ket{\psi_{t}^{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{e}_{\mu}}(\tilde{z}^{*})},

(9)

where
\tilde{z}_{t}^{*}
is the shifted noise process (
7
) and
\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}
is given by the zeroth level state

\displaystyle\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}=\frac{\braket{\psi^{\mathbf{0}}_{t}(\tilde{z})|L^{\dagger}|\psi^{\mathbf{0}}_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}}{\braket{\psi^{\mathbf{0}}_{t}(\tilde{z})|\psi^{\mathbf{0}}_{t}(\tilde{z}^{*})}}.

It is worth noting that the zero-temperature BCF is used for the exponential expansion, and the temperature is incorporated through the stochastic potential without modification to the hierarchy structure.

Independent boson model

The phonons of the environment are modeled as independent bosons. This model, with only the QD and the phonon environment present, is known as the independent boson model (IBM) or pure dephasing model
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
. The IBM admits an exact solution, from which we have for the reduced density matrix
[
48
]

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t)=

\displaystyle~\rho_{gg}\ket{g}\bra{g}+e^{i\omega^{\prime}_{X}t}e^{-\Gamma(t)}\rho_{gX}\ket{g}\bra{X}

\displaystyle+e^{-i\omega^{\prime}_{X}t}e^{-\Gamma^{*}(t)}\rho_{Xg}\ket{X}\bra{g}+\rho_{XX}\ket{X}\bra{X},

where
\omega^{\prime}_{X}=\omega_{X}-\int\text{d}\omega\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}
is the phonon-shifted transition energy of the QD and
\Gamma(t)
is the decoherence function

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=\int\text{d}\omega\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}\left(\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)(1-\cos\omega t)-i\sin\omega t\right).

It is often convenient to approximate the steady state of the system and the environment as the uncorrelated state. To see the validity of this approximation, we calculate the exact correlation function

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{ss}=\text{tr}\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rho_{ss}\}

for both the approximate case and exact case in the frame rotating at
\omega^{\prime}_{X}
. For the approximate case, we find

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle^{\text{approx}}_{ss}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\{(\rho_{S}(\infty)\otimes\rho_{E}^{\beta})\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\}

\displaystyle=\rho_{XX}\exp{\left(\Gamma(\tau)\right)}.

and for the exact case

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{ss}=\rho_{XX}\exp{\left(\Gamma(\tau)+2i\int\text{d}\omega\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}\sin\omega\tau\right)}.

In figures
1
and
2
, the real part of the correlation functions for the approximate case (solid lines) and the exact case (dashed lines) are shown for varying temperatures and coupling strengths. At weak coupling strengths, the correlation functions are in good agreement. However, at stronger coupling strengths, temperature starts to play a significant role in the deviation. This behavior is expected. At low temperatures, the environment is predominantly in its ground state, so its influence on the system is minimal unless the coupling strength is large. In the weak coupling regime, system-environment correlations are negligible, making the uncorrelated steady-state a good approximation. At high temperatures, the environment has a large population of thermal excitations, which introduce significant thermal noise. The system is then primarily subject to thermal fluctuations, and the steady state is dominated
by classical thermal fluctuations rather than
(quantum) correlations between the system and the environment. As a result, even under relatively strong coupling, the system-environment factorization remains a good approximation.

The linear absorption of the QD can be calculated efficiently with HOPS, by needing to consider only a single trajectory. Since HOPS is numerically exact, and NMQSD doesn’t make any assumptions about the strength of the environment coupling, we can simulate spectra in the strong coupling regime very effectively. We consider the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=H^{\prime}+H_{\text{field}}(t),

where
H^{\prime}
is the total Hamiltonian for the system and bath, and
H_{\text{field}}(t)
is the Hamiltonian of an electric field

\displaystyle H_{\text{field}}(t)=-\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{E}(t)=-\mu_{\text{eff}}E(t),

where
\mu_{\text{eff}}=\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}
is the effective dipole operator with the dipole moment operator
\bm{\mu}
and polarization
\bm{\epsilon}
. We assume no permanent dipole moment, as such the dipole moment operator is of the form
[
44
]

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}=\tilde{\bm{\mu}}(\ket{g}\bra{X}+\ket{X}\bra{g}),

where
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}
two-level transition dipole moment, which is assumed to be real. The linear absorption spectrum is given by
[
49
,
50
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\text{Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}te^{i\omega t}M(t),

with the dipole-dipole correlation function

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\{\varrho_{\text{eq}}[\mu_{\text{eff}}(t),\mu_{\text{eff}}]\}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}_{\text{S}}\{\mu_{\text{eff}}\text{tr}_{\text{E}}\{\mathcal{U}_{t}[\mu_{\text{eff}},\varrho_{\text{eq}}]\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{t}\}\}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}_{\text{S}}\{\mu_{\text{eff}}\kappa(t)\}.

The equilibrium state is assumed to be factorized
\varrho_{\text{eq}}=\ket{g}\bra{g}\otimes\rho_{\beta}
. When the cavity is absent, we can calculate the dipole-dipole correlation function by only considering a single trajectory
[
50
]

\displaystyle M(t)\propto\braket{X|\psi_{t}(z^{*}=0)},

where the stochastic process is set to zero.

Near the QD transition frequency, the spectra are dominated by the phonon spectral density, which enables phonon-assisted photon absorption. With the phonon coupling, a clear asymmetry is observed, which is caused by the differing probabilities of phonon absorption and emission
[

. These results are consistent with those reported in Ref.
[

. Without phonon coupling, the spectra would be symmetric Lorentzian peaks and the widths would be determined by radiative broadening.

III.3
Resonance fluorescence spectra

The spectrum of resonance fluorescence for the QD is given by
[
17
,
52
]

\displaystyle S(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau e^{i\omega\tau}\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{ss}},

where the steady state expectation value is given by
\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{ss}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\sigma_{+}(t)\sigma_{-}(t+\tau)\rangle
. The spectrum decomposes into two components

\displaystyle S(\omega)=S_{\text{coh}}(\omega)+S_{\text{inc}}(\omega),

where
S_{\text{coh}}(\omega)
corresponds to coherent scattering and
S_{\text{inc}}(\omega)
corresponds to incoherent scattering which are due to quantum fluctuations
[
17
,
52
]
. These can be explicitly expressed, in the rotating frame of the drive, as

\displaystyle S_{\text{coh}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\sigma_{+}\rangle_{\text{ss}}\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle_{\text{ss}}e^{-i(\omega-\omega_{D})\tau},

\displaystyle S_{\text{inc}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\text{d}\tau\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{+}\tilde{\sigma}_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{\text{ss}}e^{-i(\omega-\omega_{D})\tau},

where the steady state fluctuations are described by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{\pm}(t)=\sigma_{\pm}(t)-\langle\sigma_{\pm}\rangle_{\text{ss}}.

For the simulations we set
\omega_{X}=\omega_{c}=\omega_{D}=1~\mathrm{eV}
,
\kappa=\omega_{c}/10
,
\Omega=0.175~\mathrm{meV}
, and
g=0.01~\mathrm{meV}
.

The spectra are obtained by fitting three Lorentzian peaks to the numerical data. In figure
4
the resonance fluorescence spectra of the QD is shown at different temperatures. As expected a Mollow triplet structure appears for each of the spectra and the side peaks shift towards the center peak, and the width of the peaks broaden as temperature increases. In figure
5
similar behavior is observed but with fixed temperature and varying phonon-coupling strength.

To evaluate the quality of the spectra, we compute their visibility, as well as their spectral resolutions (presented in the appendix). Visibility is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\frac{I_{\text{max}}-I_{\text{min}}}{I_{\text{max}}+I_{\text{min}}},

(10)

where
I_{\text{max}}
is the height of the central peak, and
I_{\text{min}}
is the height of the minimum between the central and sideband peaks. The visiblities of the left and right side peaks are shown in figure
6
as functions of temperature and phonon coupling strength. Both side peaks exhibit nearly identical visibilities. As seen in figures
4
and
5
, increasing the temperature or phonon coupling strength causes the side peaks to shift closed to the central peak. This shift reduces the visibility of the spectrum, which is reflected in our results. The contours are overlaid with analytic curves
T\propto 1/A
, which align closely with the contour trends, possibly highlighting an underlying relationship. The fluctuations visible in the figure are due to noise in the statisical sampling.

IV
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we have applied the HOPS method to calculate the absorption spectrum of QDs with strong phonon coupling, as well as resonance fluorescence spectra at varying temperatures and phonon coupling strengths. For the absorption spectrum, only a single trajectory was required, even at finite temperature and strong phonon coupling. The calculated absorption spectrum is in good agreement with earlier numerical work. This also indicates that, in this particular case, the approximation of the ground state as a factorized state appears to be valid, as the asymmetry in the spectrum appears.

To further assess the applicability of the factorized steady-state approximation, we calculated the correlation function
\langle\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{ss}
for the independent boson model. As expected, at lower temperatures and weak phonon coupling, this approximation works well. However, increasing the phonon coupling strength introduces deviations at short times, after which the correlation functions converge. At higher temperatures, the approximation is less sensitive to increases in phonon coupling strength, maintaining reasonable accuracy.

The resonance fluorescence spectra were calculated for a range of conditions, from weak phonon coupling and low temperatures to strong phonon coupling and high temperatures.
The calculation method is straightforward, as every density matrix propagation can be performed with HOPS without requiring additional computational machinery. From the spectra, we determined their visibility. The contours appear to align closely with curves proportional to
T\propto 1/A
. This suggests that the visibility depends on the characteristics
T+b/A
. Furthermore, this relationship could be used to determine the phonon coupling strength for a given QD system through measurements of resonance fluorescence spectra at different temperatures.

Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. In this work, we adiabatically eliminated the cavity, which restricts our analysis to weak coupling and leaky cavities. To more fully capture the effects of cavity interactions on QD systems, future work must extend the HOPS method to explicitly include the cavity. Additionally, a well-known limitation of trajectory-based methods is the computational cost of long-time propagation. Depending on how quickly the system reaches its steady state, the required resources can become significant. While the ergodic principle can, in some cases, reduce this cost by allowing the steady state to be obtained through time averaging over a single trajectory
[
53
]
, this approach was not applicable in our study. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for expanding the applicability of the HOPS method to more complex systems and longer time scales.

In summary, this study advances the application of the HOPS method to strongly coupled QD systems, providing insights into both absorption and resonance fluorescence spectra. It lays the groundwork for future investigations into more complex systems and experimental validations of theoretical predictions.

Appendix A
Pure state decomposition

In order to propagate the various states and operators encountered in the main text with HOPS, it is necessary to decompose them into pure states. For a hermitian operator, this decomposition can be obtained through the spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\kappa=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\bra{\psi_{\lambda}}.

This also applies to operators that are normal. For other cases we can use the fact that every operator
A
can be decomposed into a hermitian and an anti-hermitian part

\displaystyle A=H+H^{\prime},

where
H=\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{\dagger})
and
H^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(A-A^{\dagger})
. Since anti-hermitian operators can be expressed as
H^{\prime}=iK
where
K
is a hermitian operator, we can express any operator as the sum

\displaystyle A=H+iK,

where
K=-\frac{i}{2}(A-A^{\dagger})
. Since
H
and
K
are hermitian, they can be decomposed into pure states by using their respective spectral decompositions

\displaystyle A=\sum_{\lambda}h_{\lambda}\ket{\psi_{\lambda}^{H}}\bra{\psi_{\lambda}^{H}}+i\sum_{\lambda}k_{\lambda}\ket{\psi_{\lambda}^{K}}\bra{\psi_{\lambda}^{K}}.

Now each of these pure states can be propagated using HOPS

\displaystyle\kappa_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}_{E}\{\mathcal{U}_{t}\kappa\otimes\rho_{E}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\dagger}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\text{tr}_{E}\{\mathcal{U}_{t}\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\bra{\psi_{\lambda}}\otimes\rho_{E}\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\dagger}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\alpha_{\lambda}\mathcal{M}[\ket{\psi_{\lambda,t}(z^{*})}\bra{\psi_{\lambda,t}(z)}],

where
\ket{\psi_{\lambda,0}}=\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}
. For the case of a two-level system, this means that we need to propagate four initial states at most. Note that the propagation of each of the state vectors are performed by using the same noise process.

Appendix B
Adiabatic elimination

The correlation function

The cavity mode can be eliminated in the following way. A model for a continuously monitored cavity mode with heterodyning is given by the following Stratonovich equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\Psi_{t}}=

\displaystyle-i(H_{S}+H_{C}+H_{I})\ket{\Psi_{t}}

\displaystyle+\left(a-\langle a\rangle\right)\left(\xi_{t}^{*}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle\right)\ket{\Psi_{t}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left((a^{\dagger}-\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle)a-\langle(a^{\dagger}-\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle)a\rangle\right)\ket{\Psi_{t}},

(11)

where
H_{S}
is at this point arbitrary,
H_{C}=\omega_{c}a^{\dagger}a+f(t)a^{\dagger}+f(t)a
and
H_{I}=g(La^{\dagger}+L^{\dagger}a)
, where
L
is an arbitrary coupling operator of the system. The Gaussian white noise has zero mean and correlations
\mathcal{M}\left[\xi_{t}\xi_{s}^{*}\right]=\kappa\delta(t-s)
. Other correlations are zero.
Clearly
\langle\Psi|\dot{\Psi}\rangle=0
and the evolution preserves the norm of the state.
The mean field evolution for the system can be found from a variational principle. We use the following variational ansatz

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{t}}=c_{t}\ket{\varphi_{t}}\ket{z_{t}},

(12)

where
\left|c(t)\right|=1
is a phase factor,
\ket{\varphi_{t}}
is an arbitrary state for the system and

\ket{z}=e^{i\phi}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|^{2}}e^{za^{\dagger}}\ket{0}
is a coherent state with a phase factor
\phi
. We thus restrict to a product states where the cavity remains in a coherent state. Using the MacLahlan variational principle we find the following equations of motion

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{t}

\displaystyle=-ic_{t}E_{t},

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\varphi_{t}}

\displaystyle=-i(H_{MF,S}-E_{t})\ket{\varphi_{t}},

(14)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{z_{t}}

\displaystyle=-i(H_{MF,C}-E_{t})\ket{z}-\frac{\kappa}{2}(z_{t}a^{\dagger}-\left|z_{t}\right|)\ket{z},

(15)

where
E_{t}=\bra{\varphi_{t},z_{t}}H\ket{\varphi_{t},z_{t}}
is the average energy and
H_{MF,S}=\bra{z}H\ket{z}
is the mean field Hamiltonian for the system
and
H_{MF,C}=\bra{\varphi}H\ket{\varphi}
is the mean field Hamiltonian for the cavity.
Clearly, state evolution preserves normalization. By plugging in the coherent state we can verify that the coherent state indeed is a solution to the
mean field equations. In particular, we find

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{c}z-\frac{\kappa}{2}z-ig\langle L\rangle-if_{t},

(16)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{c}\left|z_{t}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g\left(\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle_{t}z_{t}+\langle L\rangle_{t}z^{*}_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(f^{*}_{t}z_{t}+f_{t}z_{t}^{*}).

(17)

If the cavity damping
\kappa,\left|f_{t}\right|\gg g
we may adiabatically eliminate the cavity mode by setting
\dot{z}_{t}=0

and approximating

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{f_{t}}{-\omega_{c}+i\frac{\kappa}{2}}.

(18)

This equation states that for strong classical driving and strong cavity damping, the state of the cavity remains coherent and the coherent state
label follows the amplitude of the driving field.
The mean field evolution for the system is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\varphi_{t}}=

\displaystyle-i(H_{S}+gLz^{*}_{t}+gL^{\dagger}z_{t})\ket{\varphi_{t}}

\displaystyle+i(\langle H_{S}\rangle_{t}+g\langle L\rangle_{t}z_{t}^{*}+g\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle z_{t})\ket{\varphi_{t}},

(19)

which is non-linear. The non-linearity emerges as a global phase factor. We can define a new state
\ket{\psi_{t}}=e^{-iu_{t}}\ket{\varphi_{t}}

which satisfies a linear Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\ket{\psi_{t}}=

\displaystyle-i(H_{S}+gLz^{*}_{t}+gL^{\dagger}z_{t})\ket{\psi_{t}},

(20)

when
\dot{u}_{t}=\langle H_{S}\rangle_{t}+g\langle L\rangle_{t}z_{t}^{*}+g\langle L^{\dagger}\rangle z_{t}
.

Appendix C
Spectrum fitting

For each of the resonance fluorescence spectra, the correlation function
\langle\tilde{\sigma}_{+}\tilde{\sigma}_{-}(\tau)\rangle_{ss}
was calculated using at least 60000 trajectories. At low phonon coupling strengths and low temperatures, the spectra were very smooth. However, at stronger coupling and higher temperatures, noticeable noise remained in the spectra. For the figures in the main text and the visibility analysis, we fitted three Lorentzian peaks to the numerically obtained spectra. Increasing the number of trajectories would improve the quality of the spectra from the raw data, and consequently, the fits. Figure
7
shows the correlation function from the numerical data, the corresponding spectrum, and the fit.

Appendix D
Spectral resolution of resonance fluorescence spectra

The spectral resolution is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\frac{\delta}{\Delta\omega},

(21)

where
\delta
is the distance between the central peak and the sideband peak, and
\Delta\omega
is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the central peak. Both visibility and spectral resolution quantify how well the spectral peaks can be distinguished. Higher values of either indicate that the side peaks in the resonance fluorescence spectra can be more easily identified.

The spectral resolutions for the same spectra shown in figure
6
are presented in figure
8
. Similar to the visibilities, the contours follow the analytic curves
T\propto 1/A
. However, a notable difference is that the spectral resolutions vary more rapidly at weak coupling strengths and low temperatures, whereas they exhibit slower variation at high temperatures and strong couplings.
