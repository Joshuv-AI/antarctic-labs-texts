Deep learning-driven super-resolution for cone-beam computed tomography: An ex vivo proof-of-concept study using artificially degraded micro-computed tomography data
Authors: Hossein Mohammad-Rahimi, Konstantinos Verdelis, Rubens Spin-Neto, Bruna Neves de Freitas, Mina Iranparvar Alamdari, S Marjan Arianezhad, Ruben Pauwels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis ex vivo proof-of-concept study aimed to develop deep learning (DL)-based super-resolution (SR) models to enhance simulated cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images.Materials and MethodsMicro-computed tomography data from 51 extracted teeth were artificially degraded to simulate CBCT images. Three DL models, super-resolution convolutional neural network (SRCNN), local texture estimator (LTE), and Swin Transformer for image restoration (SwinIR), were compared with bicubic interpolation. Image quality was assessed using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity index measure (SSIM), learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS), and deep image structure and texture similarity (DISTS). Three dentists evaluated sharpness and noise using a 5-point Likert scale. Eight observers assessed crack visibility in 47 images for LTE and bicubic interpolation using a 5-point Likert scale; scores were binarized using high and low thresholds.ResultsAll models significantly outperformed bicubic interpolation on objective metrics. SwinIR showed the highest PSNR (30.36 ± 2.66), whereas SRCNN achieved the highest SSIM (0.889 ± 0.073). LTE achieved the best LPIPS (0.253 ± 0.101) and DISTS (0.203 ± 0.049). Subjectively, LTE received the highest sharpness ratings (mean. 3.79 ± 0.47), whereas bicubic interpolation received the highest noise ratings (3.97 ± 1.43). LTE significantly improved crack visibility (odds ratio = 1.326, P = 0.006 for the low-threshold analysis; odds ratio = 1.310, P = 0.010 for the high-threshold analysis), with a higher pooled area under the curve (0.81 vs. 0.76, P = 0.063).ConclusionDL-based SR models can enhance simulated CBCT images, with LTE demonstrating superior perceptual sharpness and crack visibility.Keywords: Cone-Beam Computed Tomography, Artificial Intelligence, Image Enhancement, Deep Learning

Introduction

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is widely used as a three-dimensional imaging modality in dentomaxillofacial imaging.1 Despite its widespread use, CBCT has inherent limitations in spatial resolution; a factor affecting this is voxel size, which ranges from 75 to 600 µm.2 Therefore, depending on the CBCT unit and scanning protocol, the ability to visualize fine anatomical structures, as well as overall diagnostic performance, may be compromised. For example, improved spatial resolution has been shown to enhance diagnostic performance for conditions such as vertical root fractures3 and external root resorptions.4 However, even at the highest-resolution settings, CBCT remains unreliable for detailed assessment of fine structures, such as apical root canal anatomy.5

Among the conditions for which CBCT may be useful is the detection of dental cracks and fractures.6 Dental cracks are a common clinical finding and represent the third leading cause of tooth loss, after caries and periodontal disease.7 Voxel size has been reported to be the most important factor in CBCT evaluation of cracked teeth8 and to be associated with better diagnostic performance.9 However, the typical voxel size of CBCT (usually >0.075 mm3), together with other factors that limit the sharpness of clinical scans, makes fine cracks difficult to diagnose.6,9 Therefore, CBCT often fails to reliably reveal fine cracks, especially at early stages, which can lead to uncertain clinical assessments. Although micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is considered the gold standard for crack detection, it is not a clinical imaging modality.10

In recent years, micro-CT has been recognized as a highly reliable tool in dental research for evaluating fine structures.11 Compared with CBCT, the main advantage of micro-CT is its higher spatial resolution, owing to voxel sizes ranging from 1 to 50 µm,12 together with a microfocus spot and a high-resolution detector. In dentistry, micro-CT has been shown to be more reliable than CBCT for several diagnostic tasks, including bone microstructure evaluation,13 dental crack detection,14 and comprehensive assessment of root canal morphology.15 However, all micro-CT studies are ex vivo because the high radiation dose required, long scanning time, and limited form factor make this modality unsuitable for clinical dental applications.16

“Super-resolution” (SR) is a field of computer vision focused on generating high-resolution (HR) images from low-resolution (LR) samples. In medical imaging, SR algorithms are valuable when high-quality image acquisition is limited by hardware constraints, algorithmic limitations, and radiation dose restrictions.17 With advances in deep learning (DL), DL-based SR models have been extensively investigated and have demonstrated superior performance in medical SR tasks.18 In dentistry, DL-based SR models have been shown to successfully increase the resolution of downscaled panoramic radiographs19,20 and dental CBCT images.21,22 However, only a limited number of studies have evaluated the potential of DL to enhance CBCT images toward micro-CT-level quality.23,24 These studies used established legacy DL architectures and relied on exact subvoxel-level image registration between CBCT and micro-CT. Moreover, they lacked perceptual quality assessments by dental experts, which may reveal image-quality properties not captured by technical metrics.

Therefore, this proof-of-concept study developed and tested DL-based SR models to enhance the resolution and sharpness of simulated CBCT images generated by degrading micro-CT data from extracted teeth toward micro-CT-level resolution. It was hypothesized that these models would outperform conventional upscaling methods in restoring diagnostically relevant textures and structures and would improve the visibility of dental cracks.

Materials and Methods

Study design

In this ex vivo study, 3 deep learning models, super-resolution convolutional neural network (SRCNN),25 local texture estimator (LTE),26 and Swin Transformer for image restoration (SwinIR),27 were applied to synthetic two-dimensional CBCT slices (LR images) of extracted teeth to generate SR images. The resulting image quality was then compared with that of the original micro-CT samples (HR images). The best-performing model (LTE), selected on the basis of subjective sharpness assessment, was used for crack visibility assessment. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the “Artificial intelligence in dental research: Checklist for authors, reviewers, readers” guideline.28 Data collection was deemed exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pittsburgh.

Data collection and preprocessing

Two previously collected datasets containing 40 anterior teeth (dataset A) and 11 molars (dataset B) were selected and pooled for this study. The teeth had been extracted at oral surgery practices over a 2-year period for various reasons unrelated to the current study objectives, including non-restorability and lack of periodontal support, and were collected for studies conducted at the School of Dental Medicine, University of Pittsburgh. The teeth were fixed in 5% formaldehyde and stored in a 70% alcohol solution at room temperature.

The first 35 anterior teeth in dataset A were scanned using a Bruker SKYSCAN 1172 system (Bruker MicroCT, Kontich, Belgium) at a voxel size of 21.17 µm. Scanning parameters included a tube voltage of 59 kVp, a current of 167 µA, an exposure time of 500 ms, and 4 projections per frame. The remaining 5 anterior teeth from dataset A, together with all teeth in dataset B, were scanned using a µCT50 system (Scanco Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland) with a tube voltage of 71 kVp, an effective current of 114 µAs, and an exposure time of 1,000 ms per projection. The resulting voxel size for these samples was 20.60 µm. Each tooth was scanned individually in 70% alcohol and stabilized with gauze in a plastic holder provided by the manufacturer. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the projections (raw files) was performed using the system’s proprietary software, which calibrated the resulting volumes through an automatic protocol involving internal standards. The 3D volumes were then exported as a series of Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine files.

For subsequent stages, the datasets were pooled into a single dataset. Before preprocessing, the samples were assigned to the training, validation, and test sets using a stratified sampling approach that kept images from the same tooth together to avoid data leakage. The resulting split was as follows: training set, n = 31 (24 from dataset A and 7 from dataset B); validation set, n = 10 (8 from dataset A and 2 from dataset B); and test set, n = 10 (8 from dataset A and 2 from dataset B).

For data preparation, we used the Fiji open-source software package.29 First, micro-CT samples were exported as two-dimensional image slices at an interval of 20 slices (approximately 412–423 µm, depending on the dataset) along both the axial and sagittal planes. This step produced 3,079 training images, 814 validation images, and 857 test images. Next, the Anisotropic Diffusion 2D function was applied for 20 iterations to reduce noise while preserving edge detail. The smoothing parameter was set to 0, with diffusion coefficients of 0.50 and 0.90 for edge preservation. A time step of 20 and an edge threshold of 3 were used to balance noise reduction and structural integrity.

In the next step, the Otsu thresholding method30 was applied to generate a binary mask for background removal. To refine the segmentation, the mask underwent 10 iterations of dilation to enhance the detected regions. Subsequently, a bounding box was placed around the identified region, and its coordinates and dimensions were used to crop the region of interest. An examiner (H.M.R.) then performed a manual quality assurance step, and images with residual background artifacts were manually cropped to retain only the relevant regions.

To generate synthetic low-resolution dental CBCT images, we used a systematic image-processing pipeline implemented in Python with the PyTorch library. First, images were downscaled by a factor of 5× using bicubic interpolation, resulting in a voxel size of 0.105 mm for dataset A and 0.103 mm for dataset B. Subsequently, a Gaussian blur transformation was applied with a kernel size of 3 and a sigma value of 3 to simulate the blurring characteristics of CBCT relative to micro-CT. The proposed protocol was deemed acceptable after subjective assessment by an oral and maxillofacial radiologist (R.S.N.), who used a grid-search approach with different downsampling and blurring parameters and compared the resulting images with actual CBCT images.

Model architecture and training

SRCNN, SwinIR, and LTE were trained for this study. Each model was modified for the present application and implemented as follows.

SRCNN: For the SRCNN implementation, we used a modified architecture consisting of 4 convolutional layers. Rectified linear unit activation functions were applied after each convolutional layer except the final layer. Before training, input images were upscaled using bicubic interpolation to match the target size. The model was trained on 100 × 100-pixel patches for a maximum of 600 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.0002. A learning rate scheduler was also used to monitor validation loss and reduce the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 if no improvement was observed for 25 consecutive epochs (patience = 25). Training was performed with a batch size of 64, and mean squared error (MSE) was used as the loss function.

LTE: The LTE architecture consisted of an enhanced deep residual network backbone serving as the encoder, which extracted multiscale feature representations from the input images. The extracted features were then processed through a coefficient mapping layer, a frequency modulation layer, and a phase encoding layer to construct an implicit image representation. The coefficient mapping layer consisted of a convolutional layer responsible for learning the modulation coefficients of local textures. The frequency modulation layer, also implemented as a convolutional layer, predicted spatial frequency information, whereas the phase encoding layer used a linear transformation to encode phase variations. Finally, a multilayer perceptron was used as the decoder. Before training, input images were upscaled using nearest-neighbor interpolation. For LTE implementation, data augmentation was applied using random horizontal, vertical, and diagonal flips. The network was trained on random 200 × 200-pixel patches for a maximum of 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0005 and weight decay of 1e-5, with the Charbonnier loss function (ε = 1e-6).

SwinIR: The SwinIR model consisted of initial convolutional layers for shallow feature extraction, followed by deep feature extraction through stacked residual Swin transformer blocks (RSTBs), and final reconstruction through a pixel-shuffle upsampling module. The model used in the current study was configured with a depth of 4 stages, each comprising 6 RSTBs. These blocks used window-based self-attention, with 6 attention heads per stage, to capture multiscale dependencies. Unlike the other models, SwinIR did not require image upscaling before input. The model was trained on 200 × 200-pixel patches for a maximum of 600 epochs with a batch size of 32. The L1 loss function was used for training, together with the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0005 and weight decay of 1e-5.

For hyperparameters such as augmentation, pre-input upscaling approach, and loss function, a grid-search strategy was used, and the optimal settings were selected on the basis of validation-set performance. All models were trained using 3 NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation 48 GB GPUs (NVIDIA Corp, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For all models, an early stopping strategy based on MSE was used, and the best model weights were saved. As a benchmark for assessing the performance of the DL-based SR models, the images were also upsampled to the original size using bicubic interpolation.

Evaluation

The primary evaluation metrics were peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM), both of which quantify the similarity between a given image (which could be an LR image, a model output, or an image upsampled using bicubic interpolation) and a corresponding reference image (here, the HR image). The SSIM value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater visual similarity to the reference image.

In addition to PSNR and SSIM, the learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS)31 and deep image structure and texture similarity (DISTS) were also calculated.32 LPIPS measures perceptual similarity between images by computing distances in deep feature space extracted from pretrained networks. Unlike pixel-based metrics such as PSNR and SSIM, LPIPS is designed to align more closely with human perceptual judgments by leveraging learned representations from deep neural networks trained on large-scale image datasets. This approach is particularly valuable for evaluating SR outputs, in which subtle perceptual differences that are important to human observers may not be adequately captured by traditional pixel-wise comparisons. For implementation, we used the LPIPS Python library (v0.1, open-source Python library) with the AlexNet backbone. The perceptual distance was computed by extracting multiscale deep features from both images and calculating their weighted Euclidean distance in feature space. Lower LPIPS values indicate greater perceptual similarity. DISTS combines structural and textural similarity relative to the reference image using deep features extracted from a pretrained VGG network. Unlike traditional metrics that rely on pixel-level comparisons, DISTS captures perceptual differences in both low-level textures and high-level structural patterns, making it particularly suitable for evaluating SR outputs in which perceptual quality is critical. For implementation, we used the PyTorch DISTS implementation with pretrained weights. Lower DISTS values indicate greater perceptual similarity to the reference image. Both DISTS and LPIPS were calculated for individual images, and the mean and standard deviation for the test dataset are reported.

To assess perceptual image quality, 2 oral and maxillofacial radiologists and 1 dentist with postgraduate training in prosthodontics and oral and maxillofacial radiology performed a subjective evaluation. For this purpose, 100 images measuring 200 × 200 pixels and containing fine structures and crack lines were selected. Each image was enhanced using the 3 models, and benchmark images upsampled using bicubic interpolation were also included. Together with the LR and HR images, a total of 600 images were evaluated in random order.

The observers were asked to score each image on a 5-point Likert scale according to the following criteria.

Sharpness and edge definition: defined as the clarity of edges, the visibility of cracks, and the ability of the image to distinguish between 2 closely spaced objects. The examiners were asked to consider specific factors such as how well cracks were preserved relative to other images, how clearly small radiolucent or radiopaque objects in close proximity could be distinguished, and how well low-contrast tiny objects in radiolucent spaces (such as the root canal space) were preserved.

Noise and artifacts: defined as the level of noise (random variations in brightness or color that are not part of the actual image, such as graininess) and artifacts (unwanted visual effects introduced during image processing) present in the image.

To determine whether the SR model could preserve subtle structures present in the reference standard, we conducted a crack visibility assessment. A subset of 47 micro-CT two-dimensional image samples (200 × 200 pixels) was selected, comprising 21 samples confirmed to be crack-free and 26 samples with visible dental cracks, with micro-CT images serving as the reference standard for crack presence. In accordance with McGuigan et al.,33 observers evaluated crack presence using a 5-point Likert scale, with higher values indicating greater confidence in the presence of a crack, as follows: score 5, there is definitely a crack; score 4, there is probably a crack; score 3, unsure/unable to determine crack presence; score 2, there is probably no crack; and score 1, there is definitely no crack.

The visibility evaluation involved 2 clinician groups: a senior clinician group comprising 3 oral and maxillofacial radiologists and 1 endodontist, and a junior clinician group consisting of 4 general dentists, each with a maximum of 2 years of postgraduate clinical experience.

Before the evaluation session, observers received standardized instructions regarding the assessment protocol and scoring criteria. To avoid potential bias caused by exact duplicates, 1 image from each pair was randomly rotated and cropped. This manipulation ensured that, although the underlying diagnostic information remained unchanged, the images appeared unique to the observers during evaluation. Observers were allowed to adjust screen brightness and zoom levels for detailed examination.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using the SciPy (v1.16.0, open-source Python library)34 and Statsmodels (v0.14.5, open-source Python library)35 libraries. To determine whether parametric tests were appropriate, Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to assess the normality of the metric distributions. Because the normality assumption was not met, nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction were used for all paired model comparisons (after Bonferroni correction by dividing the significance level by the number of comparisons [n = 10], ie, α = 0.005). The 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated empirically from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

For the subjective evaluation, frequency distributions of scores for each model were calculated, together with the mean rank and median score. Consistency between observers was assessed using Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ). In addition, 15% of the samples were used to evaluate intra-observer agreement 2 months after the main assessment. Interpretation of the comparisons followed the classification proposed by Schober et al.,36 in which ρ > 0.90 indicates a very strong correlation, 0.70–0.89 a strong correlation, 0.40–0.69 a moderate correlation, 0.10–0.39 a weak correlation, and <0.10 a negligible correlation. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was then applied to compare subjective scores across models. For post hoc pairwise model comparisons, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used. For all tests, P-values below the corrected significance level (after Bonferroni correction by dividing the significance level by the number of comparisons [n = 10], ie, α = 0.005) were considered statistically significant. The 95% CI was calculated empirically from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the observed data distribution.

For the crack visibility assessment, inter-rater reliability was evaluated using Cohen’s weighted kappa for pairwise agreement within each group and classified according to Landis and Koch.37 For each observer, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score were calculated separately for LTE and bicubic images in 2 ways. In the low-threshold approach, scores ≥3 were classified as positive for crack detection; in the high-threshold approach, only scores ≥4 were considered positive. To compare observer performance between methods, we fitted a marginal regression model using generalized estimating equations (GEE) for a binary outcome at both thresholds. Wald tests were used to compare the LTE and bicubic methods (after Bonferroni correction, which reduced the significance level by a factor of 2, ie, α = 0.025), and effects are reported as odds ratios (ORs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated using pooled scores from all observers. AUCs were compared between enhancement methods using DeLong’s test (α = 0.05).

Results

Objective metrics evaluation

The quantitative outcomes of the trained models are presented in Table 2 and Figure 1. Overall, all trained DL models outperformed the LR and bicubic outputs across all metrics. For PSNR, SwinIR demonstrated the highest value, at 30.36 ± 2.66 (95% CI: 25.55–35.57), closely followed by LTE at 30.34 ± 2.69 (95% CI: 25.57–35.54). For SSIM, SRCNN achieved the highest score, at 0.889 ± 0.073 (95% CI: 0.716–0.980). Finally, LTE showed the best performance for both LPIPS and DISTS (0.253 ± 0.101 [95% CI: 0.095–0.492] and 0.203 ± 0.049 [95% CI: 0.086–0.299], respectively). All pairwise model comparisons showed significant differences for all metrics (P = 0.0035 for LTE vs. SwinIR in SSIM and P < 0.001 for all other comparisons).

Discussion

Although CBCT is widely used as a three-dimensional imaging modality for dentomaxillofacial hard-tissue imaging, its spatial resolution is inherently limited by factors such as focal spot size and voxel size.2 Part of this limitation stems from the need to optimize the radiation dose in clinical CBCT imaging, which constrains total exposure time and, consequently, the number of acquired projections. On the other hand, smaller voxel sizes have been reported to improve diagnostic accuracy in CBCT.4,38 Therefore, we aimed to assess whether DL models could enhance the objective image quality of simulated CBCT images and experts’ perceived image quality, ideally approaching the sharpness of micro-CT. Furthermore, we evaluated whether such enhancement translated into improved dental crack visibility.

All 3 evaluated SR models, SRCNN, LTE, and SwinIR, outperformed conventional bicubic interpolation and the LR images on both objective image-quality metrics and expert evaluations. These findings are consistent with previous research on DL-based SR approaches in dental imaging. Hatvani et al.23 were among the first to apply DL-based SR to dental CBCT, using a two-dimensional U-Net combined with subpixel convolution to enhance ex vivo CBCT slices toward micro-CT quality. They similarly reported that their model outperformed traditional processing methods in terms of PSNR and SSIM. In another study, Ji et al.24 applied the BasicVSR++ model, a neural network architecture originally designed to enhance the quality of video frames by leveraging temporal and spatial information, to CBCT scans of extracted teeth and compared the results with micro-CT images. They reported that SR-enhanced CBCT increased the detection of second mesiobuccal canals in molars from 72% to 92%.

Although the DL models performed similarly on objective evaluation, with LTE showing only slight advantages over the others, LTE outperformed the other models in clinicians’ sharpness ratings. This finding is consistent with a previous study by Mohammad-Rahimi et al.,19 in which LTE outperformed other DL-based SR models in enhancing panoramic radiographs and achieved the highest mean opinion scores among clinicians. In contrast to the sharpness assessment, noise evaluation showed unfavorable agreement, which is consistent with the fundamental relationship between sharpness and noise. In other words, image-processing methods that introduce more blurring also tend to suppress noise, and vice versa. Therefore, this finding was not unexpected, as the primary aim of the proposed SR models was to enhance spatial detail and sharpness rather than denoise the images. Ultimately, the balance between sharpness enhancement and noise suppression should be determined by diagnostic efficacy metrics, which will be the focus of future work. The superior sharpness performance of LTE may be attributable to its architecture. Unlike traditional convolutional neural networks that rely solely on hierarchical feature extraction, LTE incorporates specialized layers, such as frequency modulation and phase encoding, that enable the model to preserve small and intricate structures.26 This emphasis on texture preservation is particularly valuable in dental imaging, where fine features such as cracks, root fractures, or accessory canals may have substantial diagnostic importance. In contrast, models such as SwinIR use global self-attention to capture contextual information.27 This approach is especially powerful in natural-image restoration tasks, in which preservation of global structure and overall image coherence is critical. However, in dentistry, local detail and sharp texture visualization are more clinically relevant.

These differences may also explain the discrepancy between subjective and objective assessment results for PSNR and SSIM. These are global metrics that quantify overall similarity to a reference image without specifically emphasizing high-frequency details, such as cracks, and other diagnostically relevant features. As a result, they may favor smoother reconstructions that minimize pixel-wise error while underestimating the importance of preserving local textures that are critical for clinical interpretation.39,40,41 This limited correlation between these metrics and clinicians’ perceived image quality has been noted previously in dental and medical imaging studies.19,40 Nevertheless, we included PSNR and SSIM because of their standardized use and computational simplicity and because they allow direct comparison with the existing SR literature.42,43 To address their limitations, however, we also included LPIPS31 and DISTS,32 which use deep neural network features to align more closely with human visual perception. Both metrics have been shown to correlate more strongly with subjective assessments of image sharpness and texture preservation than traditional metrics.31,32 This is a key strength of the present study and distinguishes it from previous DL-based SR research in dentistry.

Consistent with the objective and subjective findings, LTE-enhanced images also yielded better crack visibility scores than conventional bicubic upsampling. From an endodontic perspective, improved crack visualization may be clinically relevant because it could influence important decisions, including prognosis assessment, selection of root canal therapy versus extraction, and choice of restoration after endodontic treatment.7,8 Moreover, the moderate inter-rater agreement observed in this study reflects the inherently subjective nature of crack diagnosis and is consistent with previous studies reporting diagnostic variability even among experienced clinicians.44 Notably, LTE enhancement yielded marginally higher inter-rater reliability, suggesting that improved image quality may contribute to more consistent interpretations. However, these results should be interpreted with caution because they are based on two-dimensional simulated images.

In the current study, we used simulated CBCT data generated by degrading micro-CT images rather than a direct conversion approach involving paired CBCT and micro-CT images aligned through registration algorithms, as described by Ji et al.24 Artificial degradation of high-quality images to generate simulated low-quality counterparts is a well-established methodology in medical SR research.17 For example, Hwang et al.22 downscaled HR CBCT images by factors of 2, 3, and 4 to produce LR CBCT images. This simulation approach offers a key advantage over directly paired images because it ensures perfect pixel-wise alignment between the input and target images, whereas perfect pixel-wise registration between different imaging modalities is inherently limited. This limitation can cause the network to learn incorrect mappings or become confounded by anatomical mismatches.

Regarding real-world applications, Çelik et al.20 showed that enhancement of dental radiograph quality could improve diagnosis and treatment planning. According to the hierarchical model of diagnostic imaging efficacy proposed by Fryback and Thornbury,45 the present study would be considered Level 1 and 2, primarily a technical feasibility study evaluating enhancement performance through image-quality metrics (Level 1), with an exploratory assessment of potential diagnostic efficacy related to crack visibility (Level 2). Nonetheless, before clinical implementation, the model must be evaluated more thoroughly at the higher levels, including effects on diagnostic thinking and patient outcomes. In addition to improving diagnostic performance, SR models may reduce the need for advanced equipment and hardware to produce high-quality images. Moreover, Hwang et al.22 highlighted the potential of SR models to reduce radiation dose by allowing lower-resolution acquisitions that can subsequently be enhanced.

Despite these encouraging results, this study has several limitations. First, this was an ex vivo study using extracted teeth and simulated CBCT data, which may not capture the full complexity of clinical imaging. The applied degradation approach was a controlled approximation that facilitated pixel-wise supervised training but did not capture key physical degradation mechanisms inherent to clinical CBCT, such as scatter, beam hardening, detector-related artifacts, or patient motion. Thus, the generalizability of the SR models to clinical CBCT images requires further investigation. Furthermore, sample diversity was limited, and the teeth included may not represent the full range of clinical scenarios, including multirooted teeth and common artifacts such as root-filling materials or crowns that produce streak artifacts. These artifacts are common in clinical CBCT and substantially affect image interpretability, thereby limiting the generalizability of the present findings. In addition, the study focused exclusively on dental hard tissues; therefore, it remains unclear whether the proposed SR approach would perform equally well for bone structures or other anatomical tissues captured in CBCT scans. Although the proposed model could be incorporated into a workflow in which tooth segmentation is performed first, followed by SR enhancement of the teeth alone, global enhancement of an entire CBCT scan would allow broader diagnostic applications. Moreover, evaluations were performed on two-dimensional slices rather than full three-dimensional volumes. Extending SR from isolated two-dimensional slices to coherent three-dimensional volumes poses additional technical challenges that were not addressed in this study, including slice-to-slice consistency in image quality and oversampling between neighboring slices to enable cross-sectional reformatting (ie, coronal and sagittal slices with the same resolution as the enhanced axial slices). In addition, for the deployment of SR on full three-dimensional image stacks, the computational complexity of the model would need to be optimized to allow near-real-time inference. Finally, the true spatial resolution of CBCT is physically constrained by factors such as the focal spot size of the X-ray source and detector characteristics.1 For instance, even with the same nominal voxel size (e.g., 70 µm), micro-CT systems, which use microfocus sources and high-dose, long-exposure protocols, can achieve substantially higher spatial resolution than CBCT systems, which are constrained by clinical dose levels and geometric factors.

In conclusion, this proof-of-concept study demonstrated that DL-based SR models can enhance simulated dental CBCT images. Notably, LTE received the highest clinical ratings for sharpness and yielded greater crack visibility under the controlled experimental conditions. These findings establish technical feasibility and highlight the potential of SR approaches to improve CBCT imaging, potentially enhancing diagnostic capability without requiring hardware upgrades. Future research should validate these models using clinical CBCT images and assess their impact on diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.
