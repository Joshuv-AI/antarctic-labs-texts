A longitudinal mediation model of career self-efficacy, decidedness, and proactive behavior
Authors: Hacı Arif Doğanülkü, Hazel Duru, Amir H Pakpour, Mark D Griffiths
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCareer decidedness, career decision-making self-efficacy, and proactive career behaviors are critical variables in individuals’ vocational development. While cross-sectional research has established associations between these constructs, such designs fail to capture the dynamic, temporal nature of career development or establish clear directional influences. Grounded in Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), the present longitudinal study addressed this gap by examining whether career decidedness mediated the relationship between career decision-making self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors over time. Data were collected in two waves from 390 Turkish university students and analyzed using a cross-lagged panel model. The results demonstrated that initial levels of career decision-making self-efficacy significantly predicted subsequent increases in career decidedness, which in turn predicted higher levels of proactive career behaviors. These findings suggest that career decidedness serves as a temporal bridge, translating self-belief into career-related actions. Practically, the study highlights the importance of university counseling interventions that simultaneously boost students’ self-efficacy and decision-making clarity to foster long-term proactive career engagement.Keywords: Career decision-making self-efficacy, Proactive career behavior, Career decidedness, Longitudinal mediationSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

University students approaching the end of their academic journey are in a critical phase of identity transformation (i.e., the transition from education to employment1). This education-to-work transition can be seen as a formative period during which young individuals strive to balance the need to complete the competencies required in their educational programs with the pressure to integrate into the labor market2. On a global scale, economic volatility and the increasingly non-linear nature of 21st-century career paths pose formidable challenges for university graduates striving to make informed and realistic career choices3,4. According to the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research, approximately 40% of university students experience difficulty in making career decisions, which negatively affects the effective and efficient employment of youth5. Given the current characteristics of the labor market, along with rising unemployment rates and economic stagnation, it has become essential to approach career transitions with caution and strategic planning6.

According to recent Eurostat data, 11.2% of young people aged 15–29 years in the European Union were neither in employment nor in education or training in 2023, underscoring the ongoing challenges in the transition from education to work7. In the context of Türkiye (where the present study was conducted), the Turkish Statistical Institute8 reported that the unemployment rate among the youth population aged 15–24 years was 16.3%. These data underscore that career decision-making, a critical determinant of employment9, has become an increasingly formidable challenge for students nearing the completion of their university education.

Broadly defined as the process of selecting among various professional paths, career decision-making is recognized as one of the most significant and recurring tasks in an individual’s lifetime10,11. These decisions are pivotal because they serve as a core expression of identity and exert a lasting influence across multiple life spheres. Indeed, career choices often dictate an individual’s ability to realize a desired lifestyle, shape their social networks and interpersonal relationships, and ultimately define their overall sense of purpose and well-being12. Consequently, the complexity of managing these interconnected life aspects, combined with the pressure of the labor market, makes the transition to work a psychologically demanding process for emerging adults.

When students nearing the end of their university education lack the competence to make effective career decisions, they may face difficulties in job placement and may be directed toward careers that do not provide personal fulfillment13. At this point in the career decision-making process, an important concept that should be emphasized is career decision-making self-efficacy (CDMSE). According to Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), self-efficacy is a significant factor in the process of career development14.

More specifically, SCCT posits that self-efficacy influences career development primarily through its impact on individuals’ interests, goal setting, and self-regulatory behaviors, rather than exerting a direct effect on decision-related outcomes. Indeed, Paixão and Gamboa15 demonstrated that motivational variables influence decision-making outcomes through behavioral processes such as career exploration. CDMSE reflects individuals’ confidence in their ability to perform well during the career decision-making process16, and can be considered a critical factor enabling university students to make informed decisions during the education-to-work transition and to establish sustainability in their career paths17. Therefore, self-efficacy is expected to operate through intermediary mechanisms that guide individuals toward clearer career decisions, which in turn foster proactive career behaviors. Based on these considerations, the present longitudinal study proposed that CDMSE may serve as an important predictor of career decidedness and subsequent proactivity. Both constructs are considered key factors associated with individuals’ career development.

Behaviors such as career planning, building professional networks, and acquiring career-related skills are described as proactive career behaviors18,19. Individuals who exhibit such behaviors play an active (rather than passive) role in shaping their career development20. Research findings indicate that proactive career behaviors contribute to various career-related outcomes such as employability21,22, career success23, and career satisfaction24,25. Most existing studies have examined proactive career behaviors as antecedents and focused on how they influence constructs related to career development. However, studies that have investigated the antecedents of proactive career behaviors remain limited20. There is a clear need for further research into the antecedents and psychological mechanisms underlying proactive career behaviors20. Based on this need, the present study proposed a model to expand the known antecedents of proactive career behaviors and examined their psychological mechanisms.

Career decision-making self-efficacy and proactive career behavior

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to plan and carry out the actions necessary to achieve success26. Within Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), self-efficacy is identified as a critical component in the career decision-making process27. CDMSE encompasses confidence in tasks such as preparing for a career, making necessary plans and arrangements throughout the career development process, and entering the workforce28.

CDMSE consists of five dimensions: self-appraisal, occupational information gathering, goal selection, planning, and problem-solving29. Moreover, CDMSE plays a prominent role in establishing sustainable careers, setting long-term goals, and forming professional identities30. Individuals with high levels of CDMSE are more likely to regulate their behavior throughout career development and generate effective solutions to the challenges they encounter31,32. Therefore, CDMSE can be considered a concept that supports and enhances several features that positively influence individuals’ career development processes. Another concept that is thought to positively impact career development is proactive career behaviors. A review of the literature shows that only a few studies have investigated the relationship between general self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors33. However, to the best of the present authors’ knowledge, direct longitudinal tests remain scarce. Nevertheless, given the structural features of these concepts, it can be argued that CDMSE has the potential to positively influence proactive career behaviors.

Although proactive career behaviors have been defined in various ways, these definitions generally emphasize self-initiated and future-oriented efforts through which individuals seek to advance their careers and address career-related challenges34. Fundamentally, proactive career behaviors highlight individuals’ initiative during the career exploration process and demonstrate how effort-based behaviors such as information gathering, planning, and coping, contribute to positive career outcomes35. Possessing proactive career behaviors helps individuals cope with rapid changes in the career landscape and supports their career development36.

For university students, who often face an uncertain future regarding their professional lives, it is crucial to generate alternatives, take initiative, and create opportunities even if they cannot fully anticipate the outcomes of their efforts. This initiative can be fostered through a proactive stance37,35. In this context, career decision-making self-efficacy (i.e., individuals’ confidence in their ability to make career-related decisions) may enhance their tendency to act proactively, proceed with greater planning, and exert future-oriented efforts throughout their career development. Based on these considerations, the present study hypothesized that career decision-making self-efficacy would predict proactive career behaviors.

Career decidedness as a mediator

Career decision-making is one of the most challenging and critical turning points individuals face throughout their lives, significantly impacting their overall life course38. Career decidedness, on the other hand, refers to the confidence and certainty individuals exhibit when making decisions regarding their careers39,40. A lack of career decidedness among university students often results in feelings of inadequacy regarding career preparation and goal-setting. Moreover, in the absence of career decidedness, individuals may experience anxiety about university dropout or future employment, which may lead to significant psychological and physiological problems41. CDMSE enables individuals to regulate and apply their career-related skills, and to persist in exploring and engaging in decision-making tasks42. It also serves as a strong and significant predictor of career decidedness42. Following a similar rationale, the present study posited that career decision-making self-efficacy would be a critical predictor of career decidedness.

Individuals with higher levels of career decidedness are more likely to progress in their chosen career fields in a structured and realistic manner41. These characteristics suggest that individuals with strong career decidedness are more likely to exhibit proactive career behaviors. Indeed, Herrmann et al.43 highlighted that individuals who demonstrate proactive career behaviors are more likely to set goals, plan accordingly, and engage in career exploration activities. A review of the literature shows a gap in studies directly examining whether career decidedness predicts proactive career behaviors. However, based on the aforementioned theoretical and empirical insights, the present study hypothesized that young individuals with a high level of career decidedness would display more proactive behaviors in their chosen career paths.

By positioning career decidedness as a mediating mechanism rather than merely an outcome variable, the present study meaningfully extends existing Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) research. While SCCT traditionally emphasizes self-efficacy as a crucial precursor to career actions, the present study’s proposed model argues that during the education-to-work transition – a critical developmental phase44– self-efficacy alone may not be sufficient to directly trigger action. Instead, CDMSE may provide the foundational confidence required for individuals to commit to a specific career path, thereby strengthening career decidedness. This decisiveness may function by reducing career-related uncertainty and internal ambiguity. By minimizing these negative elements, career decidedness can create a state of cognitive clarity that acts as a psychological bridge, enabling students to redirect their mental energy away from the anxiety of choice and toward tangible, proactive career behaviors, such as environmental exploration and networking.

To test this temporal mechanism, the present study utilized a two-wave longitudinal design with a three-month interval between measurement waves. As Taris and Kompier45 emphasize, the choice of a time lag should not be a matter of practical convenience, but must explicitly capture the theoretical process being investigated. Building on this principle, Dormann and Griffin46 advocate for “shortitudinal” study designs, noting that optimal time lags are usually shorter than traditionally assumed and that overly long intervals can lead to the erosion of true effects. In line with these methodological directives, the three-month lag (equivalent to a single academic semester) was deliberately chosen as the optimal “continuous change” window.

From a developmental perspective, this duration provides a sufficient psychological window for cognitive shifts (specifically, the translation of foundational self-efficacy beliefs into a crystallized state of career decidedness) and the subsequent emergence of effort-based proactive behaviors. Moreover, while a single academic semester is long enough to capture this behavioral activation, it is short enough to avoid the aforementioned erosion of effects, minimizing the risk of confounding factors (e.g., actual graduation or sudden macroeconomic shifts) that could wash out the observed relationships. Consequently, it was hypothesized that: (i) career decision-making self-efficacy at Timepoint 1 (T1) would significantly predict career decidedness at Timepoint 2 (T2) (H1); (ii) career decidedness at T1 would significantly predict proactive career behaviors at T2 (H2); and (iii) career decidedness would serve as a longitudinal mediator in the relationship between career decision-making self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors (H3).

Methods

Participant and procedure

Data were collected within a longitudinal design from 390 university students in Türkiye’s Eastern Mediterranean Region, using convenience sampling across two timepoints with a three-month interval. In the first wave, data were collected from 401 students. However, in the second wave, data could not be obtained from 11 of these students. Therefore, the analyses were conducted with data from 390 participants. Of these participants, 245 were female (62.8%) and 145 were male (37.2%). The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 31 years, with a mean age of 20.96 years (SD = 2.19). Regarding their academic level, 59 were in their first academic year (15.1%), 75 in their second academic year (19.2%), 111 in their third academic year (28.5%), and 145 in their fourth academic year (37.2%). Participants were from diverse academic fields, including Education (n = 196; 50.3%), Engineering (n = 80; 20.5%), Economics and Administrative Sciences (n = 63; 16.2%), and Health Sciences (n = 51; 13.1%). Additionally, 221 participants (56.7%) had prior internship experience, while 169 (43.3%) did not.

In both waves, data were collected using a survey hosted on Google Forms. Participants were recruited in classroom settings by sharing the survey link on the WhatsApp application. To ensure data reliability and enable accurate matching of responses across the two waves, participants were asked to generate a unique code. This code included a nickname, followed by the first three letters of the mother’s name, and the last three digits of the school ID number. This coding system allowed for the easy matching of participants’ responses from the first and second wave of data collection.

Prior to data collection, participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study. Additionally, informed consent was obtained from each participant separately in both waves. Participants were also informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point during the data collection process. The study was conducted with the approval of the Çukurova University Scientific Research and Ethics Committee (Reference Number: E.1235027). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants took part in the study voluntarily, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. No financial compensation was provided for participation.

Measures

Demographic information

Demographic data collected from participants included gender and age. No other demographic information was collected.

Career Decision Self-Efficacy Scale-Short Form (CDSES-SF)

The 25-item CDSES-SF47, Turkish version48 was used to assess career decision self-efficacy. The scale comprises five sub-dimensions: self-appraisal, occupational information, goal selection, planning, and problem-solving. Items (e.g., “Make a plan for your goals for the next five years”) are rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 5 (completely confident). Higher scores indicate greater career decision-making self-efficacy. In the present study, the internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92).

Career Decidedness Scale (CDS)

The six-item CDS49; Turkish version50 was used to assess career decidedness. Items (e.g., “I know what kind of jobs I want to do when I graduate”) are rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater career decidedness. In the present study, the internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.80).

Proactive Career Behavior Scale (PCBS)

The 24-item PCBS developed in Turkish51 was used to assess level of proactive career behaviors. It comprises six dimensions: career exploration, goal setting, developing specific plans, networking, mentor support, and participation in skill development activities. Items (e.g., “I meet new people who can support my professional development”) are rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always). Higher scores indicate greater proactive career behavior. In the present study, the internal consistency for the total scale and all subscales were good to excellent (Cronbach’s alphas = 0.83 to 0.91).

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 26 and AMOS 24 software. In the preliminary analyses, correlation coefficients, descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations [SDs], skewness, and kurtosis), and reliability coefficients were examined. To provide a more robust assessment of internal consistency, Guttman’s Lambda-6 (lambda-6) was reported alongside Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. Unlike Cronbach’s alpha, Guttman’s Lambda-6 offers a more conservative estimate of reliability because it accounts for the amount of variance in each item that can be explained by linear regression on all other items, making it less sensitive to the number of items in a scale.

Following established procedures for longitudinal structural equation modeling, longitudinal measurement invariance was assessed for each construct across the two waves to ensure that the instruments assessed the same underlying concepts over time. A hierarchical testing procedure was employed, systematically evaluating configural invariance (same factor structure), metric invariance (equal factor loadings), and scalar invariance (equal item intercepts). Invariance was supported if the changes in fit indices did not exceed the recommended thresholds of ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 ve ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015. To assess potential attrition bias, independent samples t-tests and chi-square analysis were conducted to compare completers and dropouts on demographic and primary study variables.

Before testing the structural model, a series of preliminary analyses were performed to identify potential confounding variables. Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVAs were conducted to examine whether the study variables significantly differed based on academic year, field of study, or prior internship experience at both timepoints. These variables were considered as potential covariates. However, they were excluded from the final structural model to ensure model parsimony because preliminary tests showed no significant group differences (p > .05). Finally, before proceeding to the longitudinal mediation analysis, the psychometric properties of the scales were rigorously evaluated to ensure convergent and discriminant validity. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted for each construct at both timepoints. Convergent validity was assessed using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE), with values exceeding the thresholds of 0.70 and 0.50, respectively52.

Subsequently, an autoregressive cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was utilized to investigate the directional relationships among career decision-making self-efficacy (CDMSE), career decidedness, and proactive career behaviors. This technique evaluates whether the independent variable at T1 predicts change in the mediator at T2, while the mediator at T1 predicts change in the dependent variable at T2. The structural model included proactive career behaviors as a six-factor construct and CDMSE as a five-factor construct. Because career decidedness is a unidimensional construct, it was represented using two item parcels to enhance model parsimony.

An autoregressive cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was utilized to investigate the directional relationships. While acknowledging the potential confounding of between-person and within-person effects in traditional CLPMs, this approach was selected because it is a robust tool for examining temporal precedence in two-wave designs over a relatively short (three-month) interval, ensuring a balance between model parsimony and the examination of cross-lagged associations. In evaluating model fit, stringent modern criteria were adopted: χ2/df < 3, CFI > 0.95, TLI > 0.95, GFI > 0.95 ve RMSEA < 0.06. These thresholds represent rigorous contemporary standards for ensuring a robust model-data fit53,54.

Results

Preliminary analysis

To examine potential attrition bias, the participants who completed both waves (n = 390) were compared with those who dropped out after the first wave (n = 11). Independent samples t-tests showed no significant differences between the two groups regarding T1 career decision-making self-efficacy (t[399] = -0.45, p = .654), career decidedness (t[399] = -1.00, p = .316), proactive career behaviors (t[13.75] = 0.23, p = .821), and age (t[399] = -1.70, p = .091). Moreover, a chi-square test indicated that gender distribution did not differ significantly between completers and dropouts (χ2(1) = 1.37, p = .241). These findings suggest that the attrition was non-systematic and did not bias the study’s results.

To evaluate the potential confounding effects of academic and experiential characteristics, a series of independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVAs were conducted for both Time 1 and Time 2 data. Regarding academic year (four groups), one-way ANOVAs showed no significant differences in CDMSE (T1: F = 0.49, p = .68; T2: F = 0.32, p = .81), career decidedness (T1: F = 0.32, p = .81; T2: F = 0.42, p = .73), or proactive career behaviors (T1: F = 0.37, p = .77; T2: F = 0.28, p = .83). Similarly, for academic field (four groups), no significant differences were found in CDMSE (T1: F = 1.86, p = .13; T2: F = 1.04, p = .37), career decidedness (T1: F = 0.70, p = .54; T2: F = 0.68, p = .56), or proactive career behaviors (T1: F = 0.37, p = .77; T2: F = 0.42, p = .73). Finally, independent samples t-tests showed no significant differences based on prior internship experience (yes/no) for CDMSE (T1: t = 0.18, p = .85; T2: t = 0.82, p = .41), career decidedness (T1: t = − 0.60, p = .54; T2: t = − 0.32, p = .74), or proactive career behaviors (T1: t = − 0.41, p = .67; T2: t = 0.06, p = .95). Because all comparisons remained non-significant across both timepoints, these variables were not included as additional covariates in the final structural model to maintain model parsimony.

Before testing the hypothesized structural model, a series of analyses were conducted to evaluate the psychometric properties and structural integrity of the constructs across the two timepoints. CFAs were performed for each scale to verify their factor structure and convergent validity52. Measurement quality was further substantiated by calculating CR and AVE, with results evaluated against established thresholds (AVE > 0.50 and CR > 0.70)52,53.

Factor loadings for all items were substantial, ranging from 0.66 to 0.91, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50. CR values ranged from 0.93 to 0.97, indicating excellent internal consistency. Longitudinal measurement invariance

To ensure the validity of longitudinal comparisons, a series of hierarchical measurement invariance tests (configural, metric, and scalar) were conducted across the two waves. The results of these analyses are summarized in Table 3. First, the configural invariance model, which imposed no equality constraints on parameters, was tested to evaluate whether the same factor structure held across both timepoints. The model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data (χ2/df = 1.35, CFI = 0.985, RMSEA = 0.030), providing a baseline for subsequent comparisons. Second, metric invariance was assessed by constraining factor loadings to be equal over time. The changes in fit indices between the metric and configural models were negligible (ΔCFI = 0.000; ΔRMSEA = 0.001), well below the recommended thresholds of ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, thereby supporting metric invariance.

Finally, scalar invariance was tested by constraining both factor loadings and item intercepts to be equal across the two waves. Longitudinal mediational model

Structural equation modeling was used to test longitudinal mediation in the study. First, the goodness of fit values of the established model were examined and the values reached were found to be within acceptable limits (χ2 /df = 1.499; CFI = 0.982; GFI = 0.949; TLI = 0.979; RMSEA = 0.036). According to Cole and Maxwell’s55 recommendations, the hypothesis is confirmed if the findings demonstrated significant path a and path b. The findings indicated that career decision-making self-efficacy (at T1) significantly and positively predicted change in career decidedness (at T2), controlling for age, gender, and career decidedness at T1 (path a = β = 0.27, p < .001). Moreover, career decidedness (at T1) significantly and positively predicted change in proactive career behaviors (at T2), controlling for age, gender, and proactive career behaviors at T1 (path b = β = 0.18, p < .001). Therefore, the findings indicated that career decidedness had a mediating effect in this longitudinal relationship between career decision-making self-efficacy and proactive career behaviors. Gender and age were controlled in the model to isolate the unique effects of the focal variables, consistent with the person-input framework of Social Cognitive Career Theory56 and previous longitudinal research (e.g.57,58). Neither gender (all β ≤ 0.02, p > .50) nor age (all β ≤ 0.02, p > .50) yielded significant effects on the outcome variables. These findings demonstrated that the observed cross-lagged relationships were robust and independent of demographic variance, even when controlling for the characteristics of gender and age. The absence of confounding effects was demonstrated by the lack of substantial changes in the estimations when accounting for characteristics such as gender and age.

To formally test the significance of the mediation effect, a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples was performed. The results from the user-defined estimands showed a significant standardized indirect effect of T1 career decision-making self-efficacy on T2 proactive career behaviors through career decidedness (β = 0.03, p = .004, 95% bias-corrected CI [0.008, 0.067]). Because the 95% confidence interval did not include zero, the mediating role of career decidedness was statistically confirmed.

In addition to the statistical significance, the magnitude of the mediation was evaluated by considering the standardized indirect effect in the context of the model’s high autoregressive stability. The results indicated that despite the strong stability paths (ranging from 0.79 to 0.86)—which naturally constrain the remaining variance available for cross-lagged effects—the indirect path accounted for a substantively meaningful transition over time. Following the recommendations of Preacher and Kelley59, the standardized indirect effect (β = 0.03) represented a small-to-medium effect size in this longitudinal context, confirming the substantive role of career decidedness as a developmental bridge between self-efficacy and proactive behaviors. Discussion

University students transitioning from education to working life begin to make choices regarding the careers they wish to pursue, set goals, and develop their competencies. In this context, proactive career behaviors play a critical role in students’ career development. As emphasized by Strauss et al.60, possessing proactive behaviors enhances individuals’ career competencies and increases their employability. Individuals who exhibit proactive behaviors actively manage and develop their own careers58. The present study focused on identifying the factors associated with students’ proactive career behaviors. Accordingly, proactive career behaviors considered pivotal for the career development of graduating students were examined together with career decision-making self-efficacy (CDMSE) and career decidedness. Moreover, the longitudinal associations among these variables were explored through a longitudinal mediation analysis. All three hypotheses tested were supported. Based on these findings, the results provide incremental but meaningful contributions to the literature by enhancing the understanding of the development of proactive career behaviors and the variables that influence them.

The first finding from the present study was that CDMSE significantly predicted career decidedness (H1). This result indicates that individuals with higher levels of career decision-making self-efficacy tend to experience greater career decidedness. Supporting this finding, previous research in the literature has also demonstrated similar relationships. For instance, Karimi et al.38 found a positive and significant relationship between CDMSE and career decidedness among university students. Similarly, Li et al.61 reported that CDMSE significantly predicted career decidedness in a study conducted with university students. Additionally, Sartori et al.62 found that CDMSE was significantly related to career decidedness among high school students. CDMSE reflects individuals’ beliefs in their ability to successfully perform career-related tasks.

Moreover, self-efficacy is not merely a cognitive belief but a critical internal factor that serves as a motivational engine for goal-directed action. According to Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), individuals do not just react to their environment, they proactively influence it through self-reflection and self-regulation. In this context, self-efficacy acts as a primary mediator between an individual’s potential and their actual performance. Recent evidence underscores this mechanism. For instance, Li et al.63 demonstrated that self-efficacy is a fundamental internal determinant that fosters innovative behaviors by increasing an individual’s persistence and resilience when facing professional challenges. When applied to the career domain, this suggests that high self-efficacy reduces the perceived threat of uncertainty, thereby encouraging proactive career behaviors. Individuals with stronger self-belief are more likely to initiate change and persevere in their career development because they perceive their actions as having a direct impact on their future outcomes. By integrating these perspectives, it becomes evident that self-efficacy is the cognitive foundation upon which both innovative professional actions and long-term proactive career strategies are built.

The second finding of the present study was that career decidedness significantly predicted proactive career behaviors (H2). This result suggests that individuals who demonstrate certainty and confidence in their attitudes when making career decisions are more likely to exhibit proactive career behaviors. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous studies directly parallel the present study on this topic. Individuals with higher levels of career decidedness tend to approach career exploration with greater motivation64. Proactive career behaviors are based on individuals’ responsibility during the career exploration process and reflect the positive outcomes of effortful actions such as information gathering, planning, and coping35.

Therefore, it appears that the exhibition of proactive career behaviors is, to some extent, grounded in career decidedness. Individuals who have a clear and definite career choice are likely to be more competent in organizing their cognition and more willing and persistent in exploring their chosen careers. Those who clarify their options with confidence tend to engage in more deliberate and planned actions regarding their career paths. In this context, it can be argued that gaining clarity in career choices appears to be a helpful prerequisite for effectively demonstrating proactive career behaviors. Moreover, it can be argued that gaining clarity in career choices is closely associated with the effective demonstration of proactive career behaviors.

The third and key finding of the study is that career decidedness played a longitudinal mediating role in the relationship between CDMSE and proactive career behaviors (H3). To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous studies have examined these three variables together, nor has career decidedness been investigated as a mediator in this context. Most existing research has treated career decidedness as an outcome variable38,61,42,62 and used other factors as predictors. However, in the present study, proactive career behaviors were uniquely conceptualized as the outcome variable. Although proactive career behaviors are functional by nature, there is a need for a theoretical framework explaining how these behaviors emerge. Individuals’ confidence in their career decision-making process may provide clarity in choosing a career, orienting themselves toward the selected career, and exploring career options. Those who achieve clarity and confidence in their career decisions may exhibit proactive behaviors that guide them toward positive outcomes in their careers.

It is worth noting that while the bivariate correlation between CDMSE and proactive career behaviors was strongly positive (r = .65), the direct path in the structural model was non-significant and slightly negative (β = − 0.06, p = .24). This result suggests a suppression effect or a statistical artifact arising from the high shared variance between CDMSE and career decidedness. This indicates a full mediation model, in which career decidedness accounts for the shared variance of self-efficacy, suggesting that these self-beliefs may operate through decisional clarity in order to translate into proactive career behaviors. While the reported paths are statistically significant, the indirect effect is relatively small in magnitude. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as providing an incremental update to understanding rather than a strong practical effect. From the perspective of Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) and goal-setting paradigms, high self-efficacy may provide the necessary motivation for individuals. However, this potential alone may not be sufficient to initiate action.

Without clear guidance, such as career decidedness, individuals’ proactive efforts may lack a focal point. In this regard, decisional clarity can serve as a cognitive catalyst, converting general self-confidence into tangible and proactive career behaviors. The lack of a direct effect in the study’s findings indicates that merely enhancing students’ self-efficacy is inadequate by itself, and that this potential can only evolve into proactive behaviors when students’ career choices are crystallized. Given that goal selection and planning are fundamental dimensions of CDMSE and play a significant role in setting long-term goals, it is evident that self-efficacy primarily operates through goal-oriented mechanisms. Parallel theoretical and conceptual mechanisms strongly support the study’s findings.

Aligning with this perspective, Lent et al.56 conceptualized elements such as career choices and plans as fundamental goal mechanisms, asserting that establishing goals is instrumental in organizing behavior, directing it, and sustaining it over time to reach intended outcomes. In a complementary manner, Locke and Latham65 emphasized the directive function of goals, noting that they are essentially responsible for channeling individuals’ focus and energy directly toward activities relevant to their objectives.

Implications

The findings of the present study provide a set of recommendations for researchers and practitioners. From a practical perspective, the results offer critical insights for career counseling programs, university career services, and broader policy initiatives aimed at supporting young people during the transition from education to employment. Because career decidedness acts as a crucial mechanism linking self-efficacy to action, university career services and counseling programs should design targeted interventions that go beyond merely boosting students’ self-confidence. They should provide structured guidance-such as goal-setting workshops, alumni mentoring, and career exploration seminars, to help students crystallize their career choices. Moreover, experimental psychoeducational interventions aimed at increasing proactive career behaviors among university students can be designed, explicitly incorporating the decisional variables examined in the present study. On a macro level, policy initiatives focusing on youth employment could benefit from integrating mandatory or elective career decision-making modules directly into university curricula to facilitate a smoother transition to the workforce.

From a research perspective, considering the critical importance of proactive career behaviors in career development, future studies should focus on other positive career-related variables that could contribute to the understanding of proactive career behaviors. Unlike previous studies, proactive career behaviors were conceptualized as the outcome variable, and subsequent research could adopt a similar approach to further clarify the structure of proactive career behaviors. While the present research focused on university students, future studies might explore the development of proactive career behaviors among high school populations. Additionally, future research could investigate the relationships between proactive career behaviors and various positive psychology constructs (e.g., hope, emotional intelligence), as well as how such factors explain proactive career behaviors.

Limitations

Although the present study has important implications, it also has several limitations. First, the results obtained were based on data collected using convenience sampling from students at a single university in Türkiye. As this single-source sample does not cover universities across different regions or tiers in the country, the representativeness and generalizability of the findings to the broader university student population are limited. Moreover, the present study did not include a stratified analysis by major or grade level, which could have provided deeper insights into how these variables differ across academic subgroups. Future research could benefit from employing random or stratified sampling methods across multiple institutions to increase generalizability. Additionally, the results reflect outcomes specific to the Turkish cultural context. Similar research conducted in different cultures may yield different results.

Another important limitation is the exclusive reliance on self-report measures to assess all study variables. This approach introduces the potential for common-method bias, which could artificially inflate the observed relationships among the constructs. To mitigate this risk, future studies should consider incorporating multiple sources of data, such as evaluations from peers or academic advisors, or objective behavioral markers. Moreover, while the present study emphasized the role of CDMSE in establishing the foundations of a sustainable career, it is important to acknowledge that career sustainability is a multidimensional construct influenced by various systemic and contextual factors beyond individual self-efficacy. According to recent frameworks66,67, sustainable careers depend on a complex interplay of person-career fit, institutional support, and evolving labor market demands. Future research could provide a more holistic understanding by integrating these broader dimensions, such as well-being and long-term career productivity, alongside cognitive-motivational variables.

Data collection was conducted at two timepoints three months apart (T1 and T2), therefore future studies could extend the follow-up period and include more data collection timepoints. Finally, the use of the conventional CLPM does not fully disentangle stable between-person differences from intra-individual (within-person) change. Future research with more than two waves of data could utilize alternative approaches, such as the Random-Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM), to more precisely isolate within-person fluctuations and provide a more granular understanding of how these career-related constructs evolve over time.

Conclusion

The present study advances the career development literature by elucidating the mechanisms that drive proactive career behaviors during the critical education-to-work transition. Theoretically, it extends Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) by demonstrating that career decidedness serves as a crucial cognitive catalyst, a psychological bridge necessary to transform career decision-making self-efficacy (CDMSE) into concrete, proactive actions. Methodologically, the two-wave longitudinal design overcomes the limitations of predominant cross-sectional research, providing robust empirical evidence for the temporal sequence and causal direction of these constructs. Practically, the findings underscore that career counseling programs and university policy initiatives must move beyond merely building students’ self-efficacy. To foster sustainable and self-directed career development, institutions must implement targeted interventions that explicitly facilitate decisional clarity, thereby mobilizing young adults’ efficacy into proactive career behaviors.
