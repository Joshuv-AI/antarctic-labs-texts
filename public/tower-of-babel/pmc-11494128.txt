Compound impact of heatwaves on vulnerable groups considering age, income, and disability
Authors: Yeji Kang, Jongchul Park, Dong-Ho Jang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing frequency and intensity of heat waves due to climate change and urbanization have caused serious public health problems, especially in urban areas in which the heat effects are amplified by dense infrastructure and limited green space. This study examined the impact of heatwaves on vulnerable populations in Korean cities, focusing on how age, income, and disability are associated with higher health risks. In our study, we analyzed healthcare big data from 2010 to 2022 for seven major Korean cities. We employed a distributed lag non-linear model to assess the relationship between heat exposure and health outcomes, allowing us to quantify the compounded vulnerabilities due to socioeconomic and physical factors. The results showed that the association of compounded vulnerability was more pronounced in patients hospitalized through the emergency room, a severe health outcome, than in patients with mild health outcome such as outpatient visits for heat-related illnesses. The association of compounded vulnerability was particularly evident in the elderly population. These findings suggest the need for tailored heatwave preparedness strategies for vulnerable groups, contributing to the broader discourse on climate adaptation and public health resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75224-4.Keywords: Urban heat vulnerability, Vulnerable populations, Compound impact, Socioeconomic factors, South KoreaSubject terms: Risk factors, Environmental impact, Environmental health

Introduction

Due to climate change, the average global temperature is rising, and the intensity and frequency of heatwaves are increasing. According to the Copernicus Climate Change Service, 2023 was recorded as the warmest year on record. The global average temperature for that year was 14.98°C, an increase of 1.48°C above pre-industrial levels, as defined by the baseline data from the mid-eighteenth century1. In South Korea, various records related to heat, such as daily maximum and minimum temperatures, number of heatwave days, and tropical nights, were broken because of the extreme heat in 20182. According to the representative concentration pathway scenarios, RCP 4.5 and 8.5, it is expected that the risk of heatwaves will significantly increase in South Korea in the future3.

Numerous studies have reported the negative impacts of heatwaves on health. Heatwaves exacerbate heat-related illnesses4, cardiovascular diseases5–7, respiratory diseases8,9, mental health disorders10, and infectious diseases11–13, and can lead to mortality through various pathways14. In 2018, the extreme heatwave in Korea resulted in more than 1,000 deaths4.

The health impacts of heatwaves vary depending on socioeconomic factors. According to existing research, groups vulnerable to heatwaves include the elderly15,16, outdoor workers17,18, low-income populations19,20, individuals living alone15,21,22, and people with disabilities23–25.

Among these vulnerable groups, information regarding the relative vulnerability of people with disabilities is limited. A recent study reported the relative vulnerability of individuals with disabilities among outpatient and inpatient populations due to heat-related illnesses, based on recent big data analysis in healthcare25. However, most previous studies focused on outpatient populations that represented relatively mild symptoms of heat impacts. Therefore, a need remains to confirm the relative vulnerability of individuals with disabilities, especially regarding severe symptoms leading to emergency room admissions.

Furthermore, previous studies suggested that disabilities might be an important driver of vulnerability to heatwaves compared to age and income-related factors23,25. However, because vulnerability factors were individually addressed in terms of the elderly, low-income, and disabled populations, the exacerbation of vulnerability when disabilities are combined with other vulnerability factors has not been sufficiently explored. Studies have highlighted that the risk of heatwaves may increase when vulnerability factors are compounded. For instance, among outdoor laborers, foreign workers have been found to be at a higher risk of heat exposure26. Similarly, elderly single-person households were observed to have a higher risk of heat-related mortality than the elderly population as a whole27. However, discussions on compound vulnerability are limited, especially regarding whether disabilities exacerbate vulnerability when combined with other vulnerability factors.

Distinguishing the impacts of heatwaves on vulnerable groups as mild and severe is important for developing comprehensive heatwave response policies that consider diversity in heat impacts28. Furthermore, information about compound vulnerability and its exacerbating impacts can help efficiently allocate limited resources and manpower. It can also be crucial for determining policy priorities and tailoring responses to vulnerable groups.

Therefore, this study aims to address two issues. First, it seeks to confirm whether the relative vulnerability of individuals with disabilities is evident not only for mild impacts (outpatient-centered heat-related illnesses), but also for severe impacts (emergency room admissions due to various heat-related illnesses). Second, it aims to utilize healthcare big data to investigate whether disability status can exacerbate vulnerability when combined with other vulnerability factors such as age and low income. Previous studies often addressed vulnerability factors individually among the elderly, low-income, and disabled populations without fully exploring the compounded exacerbation of vulnerability when these factors intersect. This study aims to fill this gap by demonstrating how combined vulnerability factors, especially when disability is involved, can significantly heighten the risk during heatwaves.

Materials and methods

Population

The study area for this research comprises seven major cities in South Korea (Seoul, Busan, Daegu, Incheon, Gwangju, Daejeon, and Ulsan). The health impact data used in this research, obtained from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) big data in healthcare, covers a period of 13 years from 2010 to 2022, with only data from the summer season (June to August) included. This data is divided into two categories (Table 1). First, it includes data on emergency room admissions due to heat-related illnesses. The Inpatients Admitted via Emergency Department (IAED) data encompasses conditions known to worsen or lead to excess mortality during heatwaves, as identified in previous studies, namely cardiovascular diseases5–7,29–32, respiratory diseases8,9,33,34, and mental disorders10. Cardiovascular diseases include arrhythmias, ischemic heart disease, and acute myocardial infarction, while respiratory diseases include asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Mental disorders encompass anxiety, depression, dementia, and insomnia. In addition, emergency room admissions include cases related to falls, drownings, food poisoning, and atopic dermatitis. Second, it includes data on Outpatients and Inpatients of Heat-related Illness (OI-HRI). IAED represents a proxy for severe impacts due to heatwaves, while OI-HRI serves as a proxy for mild impacts.

Weather data

The meteorological data was obtained from the automated synoptic observing system (ASOS) provided by the Korea Meteorological Administration for each metropolitan city. ASOS is a system installed to observe weather conditions over a wide area, focusing on broader regional patterns rather than local phenomena. In large cities, one Synoptic Weather Observation Station is typically installed, and this study was conducted based on data from these stations. This study utilized daily maximum temperature, precipitation, and average humidity as the meteorological variables.

Methods

In this study, we adopted a two-stage approach for the analysis. In the first stage, the health impacts of ambient temperature were analyzed for each city. Distributed lag linear and non-linear model (DLNM) proposed by Gasparrini35 was employed as a statistical model to analyze the relationship between ozone and health outcomes. DLNM can reproduce the nonlinear relationship between temperature and health outcomes and can consider confounders. In particular, this model can consider the delayed effect that occurs in the process of temperature affecting health. The variables used in the DLNM are as follows. For the analysis, we employed the dlnm package in R 4.3.0.

In this context, yt represents the number of IAEA or OI-HRI admissions on specific date t, which follows a Poisson distribution, as these counts represent rare event occurrences in a given time frame. Tmax denotes the daily maximum temperature, and l represents the lag effect, where N is the maximum lag. The lag effect accounts for the delayed impact of temperature on health outcomes. In this analysis, the maximum lag N varies depending on the study population, such as the elderly or individuals with disabilities. The optimal value of N was determined for each target population through a separate optimization process, ensuring that the lag structure appropriately reflects the delayed temperature effect specific to each group. Details about the optimization of the lag can be found in the supplementary material. rhav the daily average relative humidity, and prcp the daily precipitation. sn signifies a serial number, and its degree of freedom was set to 3 times the number of years (the duration of the study data), which is 13 years. doy refers to the Julian Day, representing the day of the year, while weekday denotes the day of the week. To examine the nonlinear relationship between the dependent and independent variables, natural cubic splines (f1 ~ f5) were employed.

The DLNM effectively reproduces the nonlinear relationship between temperature and health impact variables when considering humidity, precipitation, and seasonal variations. We quantified this nonlinear relative risk (RR) concerning the mean RR during heatwaves. In this study, heatwaves were defined as the 90th percentile of daily maximum temperatures in the summer season (June to August) for each region during the period from 2010 to 2022.

In the second stage, a meta-analysis was conducted to derive comprehensive results based on the relative risks of each city. The average effect size in the meta-analysis was calculated using a random-effects model. Since the analysis results for each city were used in the meta-analysis, different populations were considered in the study. The meta-analysis utilized the meta library in R.

Results

In the analysis results, an RR value exceeding 1 signifies that heatwaves have a negative impact on the health of the respective group. The values in the parentheses of RR represent a 95% confidence interval. By examining the RR values and their confidence intervals for each group, the vulnerability to heatwaves can be quantitatively evaluated.

Figure 1 illustrates the RR for each group in a meta-analysis format, indicating that heatwaves are associated with an increase in emergency admissions across all groups. In the case of IAED, the RR for the elderly group is 1.044 (1.010–1.080), indicating that the elderly are vulnerable to heatwaves and have a significantly increased likelihood of being admitted to an emergency room. The RR for the young group is 1.042 (1.021–1.064), showing that this group also experiences health impacts from heatwaves, albeit with a slightly lower risk than the elderly. For the low-income group, the RR is 1.051 (1.028–1.075), indicating higher vulnerability than the elderly and disabled groups. This could be due to difficulties in accessing appropriate cooling facilities or other resources. On the other hand, the RR for the high-income group is 1.034 (1.013–1.055), showing a relatively lower RR than that of the low-income group, possibly due to better environmental conditions and resource accessibility. The RR for the group with disabilities is 1.049 (1.014–1.085), higher than that of the group without disabilities at 1.041 (1.025–1.057).

Meta-analysis of relative risk by age, income, and disability groups during heatwaves from 2010–2022.

In the case of OI-HRI, it is evident that heatwaves also significantly impact the occurrence of outpatient heat-related illnesses. Specifically, the RR values for the group with disabilities are the highest, indicating their extremely high vulnerability. Examining the RR values for each group, the RR for the elderly is 4.290 (3.625–5.076), higher than that of the young group at 3.149 (2.676–3.705). The RR for the low-income group is 3.398 (3.025–3.815), higher than that of the high-income group at 2.441 (1.953–3.051). Similarly, the group with disabilities also exhibits higher vulnerability than the non-disabled group, with RR values of 4.328 (3.372–5.556) and 3.115 (2.737–3.546), respectively.

The common observation in both IAED and OI-HRI is the relative vulnerability of the elderly, low-income, and disabled groups, all of which have higher RR values than the reference groups for both mild and severe health impacts. The difference lies in the most vulnerable group. The disabled group demonstrated high relative vulnerability in OI-HRI, while the low-income group showed high vulnerability in IAED. This complexity suggests that it is difficult to distinguish priority groups among vulnerable populations based on a single vulnerability factor. It also implies that various factors can modulate the outcomes when health impacts from heatwaves occur.

Table 3 presents the RR for groups where vulnerability is compounded, indicating that the impact of heatwaves may be exacerbated when vulnerability factors are combined. In IAED, the RR for individuals who are elderly and disabled is 1.058 (1.024–1.092), higher than for the elderly group at 1.044 (1.010–1.080). However, in OI-HRI, the RR for the elderly and disabled is 3.825 (2.609–5.608), which is lower than for the elderly group at 4.290 (3.625–5.076). The RR for low-income elderly individuals is 1.051 (1.015–1.085), which is slightly higher than for the elderly group alone (1.044: 1.010–1.080). In OI-HRI, the RRs for low-income individuals with additional vulnerabilities (disabled or elderly) are higher than for the low-income group alone. In IAED, the RR for low-income individuals who are disabled is 1.048 (1.012–1.085), slightly lower than that for the low-income group at 1.051(1.028–1.075), but similar to that for individuals who are disabled at 1.044(1.010–1.080).

The results showed that there are differences in the impact of compounded vulnerability between IAED and OI-HRI. The exacerbation of heatwave effects when vulnerability factors are combined was particularly observed in the elderly population. Additionally, the impact of compounded vulnerability was more pronounced in IAED, which reflects more severe effects, than in OI-HRI, which indicates relatively milder effects.

Discussion

One of the objectives of this study was to determine whether the vulnerability of individuals with disabilities is also elevated in severe health outcomes. In IAED, disability was found to be an important factor increasing the relative risk for the elderly, but when combined with low income, it did not significantly raise the relative risk. This was an unexpected result, given the characteristics of low-income areas in large cities as discussed in previous studies.

According to previous studies reported that low-income areas in large cities tend to be densely populated in environmentally disadvantaged areas such as around industrial facilities36,37. These areas often exhibit a lower quality of and accessibility to facilities such as parks and green spaces that can mitigate the effects of heatwaves38–40. In Seoul, vulnerable populations susceptible to heat waves tend to be concentrated in specific areas41,42. In large cities such as Seoul, compound vulnerable groups may be more exposed in spatial inequalities. Nevertheless, this study did not confirm the anticipated increase in risk when low income and disability were combined.

The case of Seoul illustrates how the limitations arising from the optimization process of statistical models and the complexity of translating the effects of heatwaves into health outcomes can influence the risk analysis results for vulnerable groups. The insufficient representation of the relative risk for individuals with disabilities in the IAED analysis for Seoul may be due to the conservative nature of the model’s optimization process. Figure 2 shows the RR curves for vulnerable groups during heatwave periods in Seoul, which has the largest sample size among the study areas. While the relative vulnerability of compound vulnerable groups is partially observed in IAED, the OI-HRI case clearly demonstrates the relative vulnerability of compound vulnerable groups. In the case of IAED, the high-income group had the lowest RR, and the RR for the elderly group increased rapidly at temperatures above the 90th percentile. The RR for elderly individuals with disabilities (E + D) and low-income individuals with disabilities (L + D) did not increase as rapidly at high temperatures as for the elderly group, but these groups showed a pattern of increasing earlier compared to the elderly group. The lower RR increase pattern observed for the compound vulnerable groups (E + D, L + D) compared to the elderly group was also influenced by the characteristics of the optimization process.

The association between temperature and relative risk based on vulnerabilities in Seoul.

Figure 3 show the range of changes in the estimated RR curves is very wide for the L + D group. Many of the estimated curves showed a pattern of rapid increase at temperatures above the 90th percentile, similar to the elderly group, but the optimization process selected the most conservative of these estimates. As a result, the final selected RR estimate did not increase as sharply as in the elderly group, but it is still possible that the relative risk is higher. These results suggest that further research is needed to clearly identify the relative vulnerability of the disabled group with compounded vulnerabilities.

Additionally, the fact that the impact of compounded vulnerabilities manifests differently in emergency admissions and outpatient visits demonstrates that the health effects of heatwaves are not determined by a single factor, but are influenced by a complex interplay of various compounded factors. For example, in IAED, the impact of compounded vulnerabilities was primarily observed in the elderly, while in OI-HRI, it was more pronounced in the low-income group. Furthermore, in OI-HRI, disability was the single most significant vulnerability factor, whereas in IAED, low income had a greater influence. The complexity and disparity of health impacts caused by heatwaves have been noted in previous studies43,44. This suggests that while heatwaves may lead to an increase in hospital visits among disabled and low-income groups, the effects can be mitigated through various adaptive strategies45.

Meanwhile, the RR of OI-HRI in this study showed a clear difference from that of IAED, with values ranging from 1 to 5. This difference can be attributed to two key characteristics of OI-HRI compared to IAED. First, OI-HRI focuses on heat-related illness (HRI), which directly measures the impact of heatwaves. In comparison to IAED, which considers a variety of diseases, the number of HRI cases shows a more significant difference between normal conditions and rising temperatures. This is one reason why the RR of OI-HRI is calculated to be notably higher. Second, the analysis targets outpatients, which are more likely to occur in heat waves than other events such as emergency room visits, admissions via emergency rooms, and deaths. These two characteristics contribute to the high RR of OI-HRI, a possibility that has been corroborated by previous studies on heat-related illnesses46. Additionally, this was confirmed in a study28, which compared the RR of all-cause mortality excluding external causes and the RR of outpatient visits for heat-related illnesses.

Conclusion

This study aimed to analyze the impact of heatwaves on health when vulnerability factors such as disability, old age, and low income are combined, and to investigate how these compounded vulnerabilities manifest differently in mild (outpatient) and severe (emergency admission) health outcomes. However, due to the limitations of the model, there were challenges in confirming the effect of compounded vulnerabilities. The complexity of the health impacts caused by heatwaves also made it difficult to confirm the relatively higher risk associated with compounded vulnerabilities. Additionally, the statistical model used in the study focused more on analyzing correlations rather than establishing causality, which means that further experimental studies or evidence such as temporal precedence are required to confirm that compounded vulnerabilities directly cause health deterioration. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study provide a direction for future research design and are expected to serve as a critical foundation for policy responses targeting vulnerable populations.

This study identified a correlation suggesting that groups with compounded vulnerabilities, such as age, low income, and disability, are more likely to experience higher health risks during heatwaves. When individual factors were analyzed, disability was a relatively greater risk factor for mild cases, while low income was a greater risk factor for severe cases. Compounded vulnerabilities were associated with a higher risk for severe outcomes (emergency admissions) than for mild outcomes (outpatient visits), and this association was particularly pronounced in the elderly population.

The findings of this study indicate that groups with compounded vulnerabilities are likely to be at higher risk during heatwaves. Risk assessment methods that focus solely on single vulnerability factors may underestimate the actual risk environment faced by groups with compounded vulnerabilities. Therefore, long-term and comprehensive studies are needed to further explore the health impacts of compounded vulnerabilities.

In the face of the realities posed by climate change, addressing issues stemming from heatwaves requires a collaborative effort encompassing policy, community engagement, and ongoing research. However, resources are limited, necessitating response policies that focus on the most vulnerable segments of society. Understanding compounded vulnerabilities as revealed in this study could aid in tailoring more effective public health interventions.
