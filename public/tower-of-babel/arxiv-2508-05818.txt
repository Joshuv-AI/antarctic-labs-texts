Validity and Power of Heavy-Tailed Combination Tests under Asymptotic Dependence
Lin Gui Department of Statistics, The University of Chicago Tiantian Mao Department of Statistics and Finance, University of Science and Technology of China Jingshu Wang Department of Statistics, The University of Chicago Ruodu Wang Department of Statistics and Actuarial Science, University of Waterloo

Abstract
Heavy-tailed combination tests, such as the Cauchy combination test and harmonic mean p-value method, are widely used for testing global null hypotheses by aggregating dependent p-values. However, their theoretical guarantees under general dependence structures remain limited. We develop a unified framework using multivariate regularly varying copulas to model the joint behavior of p-values near zero. Within this framework, we show that combination tests remain asymptotically valid when the transformation distribution has a tail index γ ≤ 1 \gamma\leq 1 , with γ = 1 \gamma=1 maximizing power while preserving validity. The Bonferroni test emerges as a limiting case when γ → 0 \gamma\to 0 and becomes overly conservative under asymptotic dependence. Consequently, combination tests with γ = 1 \gamma=1 achieve increasing asymptotic power gains over Bonferroni as p-values exhibit stronger lower-tail dependence and signals are not extremely sparse. Our results provide theoretical support for using truncated Cauchy or Pareto combination tests, offering a principled approach to enhance power while controlling false positives under complex dependence.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Heavy-tailed combination tests, such as the Cauchy combination test
(
Liu and Xie 2020
)
, the harmonic mean p-value method
(
Wilson 2019
)
, and combined p-values based on generalized means
(
Vovk and Wang 2020
)
, have gained increasing popularity for testing global null hypotheses by aggregating p-values under complex and unknown dependence structures. These methods are widely used in empirical studies, including combining p-values from different testing procedures on the same dataset
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
, across genetic variants within a genome region
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, or from repeated data splits
(
Cai et al. 2022
)
. The core idea is to transform p-values via quantile functions of a transformation distribution into heavy-tailed variables and approximate the tail probability of their weighted sum, by treating the components as independent in the approximation. Compared to Bonferroni’s test, which remains valid under arbitrary dependence but is often conservative, heavy-tailed combination tests are viewed as similarly valid in practice but more powerful, especially when signals are dense.

Despite their growing use, the theoretical foundations of these methods remain incomplete. Existing results establish their asymptotic validity—meaning that the limiting ratio of the test’s type-I error to the nominal level
\alpha
becomes at most one as
\alpha\to 0
—only under asymptotic independence of p-values in the lower tail
(
Liu and Xie 2020
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, as when test statistics are pairwise Gaussian and not perfectly correlated. Intuitively, asymptotic independence requires that p-values become nearly independent as they approach zero, a restrictive assumption given that the validity of the combination tests is guaranteed only as
\alpha
vanishes.
While prior work has also shown asymptotic validity in extreme dependence of perfect correlation, little is known theoretically about the tests’ behavior under broader, more realistic dependence structures despite their widespread application in such contexts.
On the other hand, to get full validity for arbitrary dependence, the combination tests need a substantial correction that are often very conservative
(
Vovk and Wang 2020
;
Vovk et al. 2022
)
, and typical dependence assumptions can improve the efficiency of these tests greatly
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Gasparin et al. 2025
)
.

Furthermore, as shown by
(
Gui et al. 2023
)
, under asymptotic independence, heavy-tailed combination tests are asymptotically equivalent to Bonferroni’s test, offering no real power gain.
In contrast, when all base p-values are identical, Bonferroni correction penalizes the number of p-values linearly, whereas the Cauchy and harmonic mean methods maintain the nominal type-I error without a penalty of dimension. This contrast raises key questions: what dependence structures beyond asymptotic independence allow combination tests to offer asymptotic power gains over Bonferroni? How does the extent of dependence among p-values influence the magnitude of such gains? And how can one select the transformation to maximize power while maintaining validity? Addressing these questions is essential for both the theoretical understanding and practical use of heavy-tailed combination tests.

1.2
Our contributions

This paper develops a broad theoretical framework to understand the validity and power of heavy-tailed combination tests under complex dependence among p-values. We introduce the multivariate regularly varying (MRV) copula, a flexible class from extreme value theory that captures the joint behavior of p-values near zero. Intuitively, the probability of the joint distribution of p-values only needs to have a linearly-scaling limit when approaching zero. This class includes many commonly used copulas and allows for various forms of dependence in the tail.

Within this framework, we show that heavy-tailed combination tests are asymptotically valid as long as the transformation distribution used in the test is sufficiently heavy-tailed, specifically when the tail index
\gamma\leq 1
, where a larger
\gamma
represents lighter tail of the transformation distribution. Moreover, both asymptotic type-I error and power increase with
\gamma
, and the choice
\gamma=1
yields the highest power while preserving asymptotic validity across all MRV copulas, making it a practical default.

We also demonstrate that combination tests offer real asymptotic power gains over Bonferroni’s method whenever p-values are not asymptotically independent and signals are not extremely sparse. In fact, Bonferroni can be viewed as a limiting case of combination tests with
\gamma\to 0
. As dependence among p-values increases, the power advantage of combination tests with
\gamma=1
over Bonferroni becomes larger, reaching its maximum under complete lower-tail dependence of the p-values.

In summary, our results establish that heavy-tailed combination tests with
\gamma=1
are the optimal tests to choose that are asymptotically valid under broad and realistic dependence structures, and they outperform Bonferroni in power as dependence grows. For applied use, we recommend using transformations with bounded lower support
(
Gui et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, such as the truncated Cauchy or Pareto combination tests, which satisfy these theoretical properties. Therefore, our results provide a theoretical support for using the specific class
(\gamma=1)
of heavy-tailed combination tests in many areas of scientific practice.

2
Heavy-Tailed Combination Test

We study the global null testing problem based on multiple dependent p-values. Specifically, let
H_{0,1},\dots,H_{0,n}
denote
n
base null hypotheses with corresponding p-values
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
. Our goal is to test the global null hypothesis

H_{0}^{\mathrm{global}}:H_{0,1}\cap\dots\cap H_{0,n}.

To construct the heavy-tailed combination test, we first introduce a class of distributions that form a subset of the regularly varying class:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}^{*}
denote the class of CDFs
F
satisfying:

(i)

F
belongs to the
regularly-varying tailed class

\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}
with tail index
\gamma>0
, i.e.,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\overline{F}(xy)}{\overline{F}(x)}=y^{-\gamma},~~\forall~y>0.

(ii)

F
is strictly increasing and continuous on its support.

(iii)

The support of
F
is left-bounded, i.e.,
\operatorname{supp}(F)=[c,\infty)
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

The class
\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}^{*}
includes many well-known distributions, such as left-truncated
t
distributions, Pareto distributions, and inverse Gamma distributions (see Table 1 of
Gui et al. 2023
for additional examples). Suppose that there is a pre-specified weight
\omega_{i}
for each p-value which satisfies that
\omega_{i}>0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}=n
. Given a distribution
F\in\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}^{*}
, we transform each p-value via its quantile function along with this weight
\omega_{i}
to obtain heavy-tailed statistics:
X_{i,\omega_{i}}=Q_{F}\left((1-P_{i}/\omega_{i})^{+}\right)
. Define the average as follows:

\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i,\omega_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{F}\left((1-P_{i}/\omega_{i})^{+}\right),

(1)

where
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
stands for the vector of weights.
The heavy-tailed combination tests approximate the tail probability
P(\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}}>x)
by
n^{1-\gamma}\overline{F}(x)
for large
x
:

Definition 2.2

(
Weighted Heavy-tailed combination test
)
.

Given p-values
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
, a transformation function
F\in\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}^{*}
and a non-random vector of positive weights
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}=n
, the combined p-value of the heavy-tailed combination test is defined as

P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}=\min\left(1,n^{1-\gamma}\overline{F}(\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}})\right).

At significance level
\alpha
, the corresponding decision function is

\phi_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}=1_{\left\{\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}}>Q_{F}\left(1-\alpha/n^{1-\gamma}\right)\right\}}.

The tail index
\gamma
associated with
F
is referred to as the
transformation parameter
of the test.

Remark 2.1
.

Our formulation of weighting differs slightly from some of the previous work
(
Liu and Xie 2020
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2023
)
in that we apply weights to the base p-values rather than the test statistics, but consistent with
Wilson 2019
;
Vovk and Wang 2020
, where p-values are the primitive objects.
The two formulations are very similar in practice, and our choice is natural because it allows for consistency and comparison across different transformations.
Additionally, instead of using the average statistic in (
1
), one may define a general combination statistic as

S=a\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i,\omega_{i}}
for any fixed
a>0
, with the corresponding combined p-value
\min\left(1,na^{\gamma}\overline{F}(S)\right)
. Setting
a=1/n
corresponds to the average statistics as in
2.2
, while
a=1
corresponds to the sum. Empirically, the choice of
a
has a negligible effect on the power of the combination test.

Example 2.1

(Truncated Cauchy combination test)
.

The Cauchy combination test was originally proposed by
Liu and Xie 2020
. It constructs a combined test statistic

T=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tan\left\{(0.5-P_{i})\pi\right\}.

The corresponding p-value is then computed by approximating the distribution of
T
with a standard Cauchy distribution:

P_{\mathrm{comb}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\arctan T}{\pi}.

Here, the transformation function
F
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard Cauchy distribution, whose survival function is
\overline{F}(x)=1/2-(\arctan x)/\pi
and quantile function is
Q_{F}(x)=\tan[\pi(x-1/2)]
. The transformation parameter is
\gamma=1
and the weights
\omega_{i}=1
.

Since the support of the Cauchy distribution is unbounded, the test can be inefficient when some base p-values are exactly equal to 1, as this leads to infinite transformed values. To address this, researchers have proposed variants of the test based on truncated or half-Cauchy distributions
(
Fang et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
. Adjusted versions of the truncated/half-Cauchy combination test have also been introduced
(
Fang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
to ensure validity under mutual independence of the p-values.

Example 2.2

(Harmonic mean p-values)
.

The harmonic mean p-value (HMP,
Wilson 2019
) is defined as

P_{\mathrm{comb}}=\frac{n}{\sum_{i}1/P_{i}}.

This is a special case of the heavy-tailed combination test, where the transformation function
F
is the CDF of the standard Pareto distribution with survival function
\overline{F}(x)=x^{-\gamma}
for
\gamma=1
. The support of this distribution is
[1,\infty)
. In this case, equal weights
\omega_{i}=1
are assigned to all
i
and the transformed statistics are
X_{i}=1/P_{i}
.
An adjusted version of the HMP was also proposed by
Wilson 2019
to ensure validity under the assumption of mutual independence among p-values and large
n
.

3
Multivariate Regularly Varying Copula

To address general dependence structures and separate their effect from marginal distributions, copulas are the right modeling tools
(
Nelsen 2006
)
.
We consider a broad class of dependent p-values characterized by
multivariate regularly varying (MRV) copula
.
Many of the concepts and results presented in this section are adapted from the theoretical framework in Chapter 8 of
Beirlant et al. 2004
. For clarity and coherence, we present them in an order tailored to the development of the theoretical properties of heavy-tailed combination tests.

3.1
Definition of MRV Copulas

A
copula
is a multivariate distribution function with standard uniform marginals. For a random vector
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
with joint distribution function
F_{\mathbf{Z}}
and marginal distributions
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
, an associated copula
C
satisfies

C(F_{1}(z_{1}),\dots,F_{n}(z_{n}))=F_{\mathbf{Z}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),~~~~(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By Sklar’s theorem
(
Sklar 1959
)
, such a copula always exists and is unique when the marginal distributions are continuous. The copulas of a random vector remain invariant under strictly increasing marginal transformations of the random vector.

In hypothesis testing, type-I error control typically focuses on the behavior of p-values near zero. Therefore, the joint lower tail behavior of p-values plays a central role.
To be consistent with the convention in extreme value theory (where results and models are often formulated for the upper tail), we use the transformation
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
of the p-value vector
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
, which maps lower tail dependence into upper tail dependence. We then characterize the dependence structure by analyzing the upper tail behavior of the associated copula. In particular, we focus on the class of MRV copulas, which are defined via the limiting behavior of the copula in the upper tail.

Definition 3.1

(Tail dependence function and MRV copula)
.

The upper
tail dependence function
of a copula
C
is

D(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}):=1-C(1-u_{1},\dots,1-u_{n}),~\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\right)\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A copula
C
is an
MRV copula
if its tail dependence function
D
satisfies

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}s^{-1}D\left(sv_{1},\dots,sv_{n}\right)=\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),~~\forall v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\geq 0

for some function
\ell
.

The definition of MRV copulas requires that there exists a linear scaling limit as the tail dependence function approaches the origin. The limiting function
\ell
is called the
stable tail dependence function
of the copula
C
and has the property
\ell(sv_{1},\dots,sv_{n})=s\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
for any
s>0
. It also has the following integral representation (Eq. (8.23) of
Beirlant et al. 2004
):

\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{+,\|\cdot\|_{1}}^{n}}\max(v_{1}\theta_{1},\dots,v_{n}\theta_{n})\,H^{*}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}),

(2)

where
H^{*}
is a measure on the positive unit sphere
\mathcal{N}_{+,\|\cdot\|_{1}}^{n}
, known as the
spectral measure
of
C
(for a formal definition of the spectral measure, see Chapter 8.2 of
Beirlant et al. 2004
).

Intuitively, the stable tail dependence function
\ell
captures the joint dependence of the multivariate distribution at the upper tail. For instance, when
n=2
,

\ell(1,1)=2-\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}[F_{1}(Z_{1})>1-s\mid F_{2}(Z_{2})>1-s]

where
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
has copula
C
and marginal CDFs
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. This quantity reflects the limiting conditional tail probability and thus summarizes the strength of tail dependence.

An equivalent characterization of MRV copulas (Chapter 8.3.2 of
Beirlant et al. 2004
) is through the notion of a limiting copula. Specifically,
C
is an
MRV copula
if and only if there exists a copula
C^{*}
such that,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}C(u_{1}^{1/t},\dots,u_{n}^{1/t})^{t}=C^{*}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),\mbox{~for all $u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

(3)

The limiting copula
C^{*}
in (
3
) is also known as the
extreme value copula
. Intuitively,
C^{*}
is the copula of the limiting distribution of the properly normalized component-wise maximum
of independent samples from a multivariate distribution
F
with corresponding copula
C
. The connection between
C^{*}
and the stable tail dependence function
\ell
is given by (Eq. (8.52) of
Beirlant et al. 2004
):

C^{*}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\exp{\left\{-\ell\left(-\log u_{1},\dots,-\log u_{n}\right)\right\}}.

(4)

It has also been established that
C^{*}
,
\ell
and the spectral measure
H^{*}
are in one-to-one correspondence, with each uniquely determining the others. These objects offer complementary representations of the upper-tail dependence structure associated with the MRV copula.

3.2
Examples of MRV Copulas

The class of MRV copulas is quite broad, as its definition involves only the limiting behavior in the tail. As a result, many commonly used dependence structures fall within this class. We present several examples below.

Example 3.1

(Gaussian copula)
.

The
n
-dimensional Gaussian copula is defined as

C_{R}(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})=\Phi_{R}(\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\dots,\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})),

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the quantile function of the standard normal distribution and
\Phi_{R}
is the CDF of the multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and correlation matrix
R
. When
R
is non-degenerate, the limiting copula is

C_{R}^{*}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=u_{1}\dots u_{n}

indicating asymptotic independence (see, e.g.,
McNeil et al. 2015
). Thus, Gaussian copulas are MRV copulas with a mutually independent limiting copula.

Example 3.2

(Multivariate
t
copula)
.

A multivariate
t
copula is defined as the copula of an
n
-dimensional centered
t
distribution
T_{n,\nu,\Sigma}
with degrees of freedom
\nu>0
and scale matrix
\Sigma=(\rho_{ij})_{n\times n}
. The corresponding density is

f_{t}(\mathbf{z};\nu,\Sigma)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+n}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)(\nu\pi)^{n/2}}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\left[1+\frac{1}{\nu}(\mathbf{z}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{z})\right]^{-\frac{\nu+n}{2}},

where
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The multivariate
t
copula exhibits non-trivial tail dependence and is an MRV copula (see Theorem 2.3 of
Nikoloulopoulos et al. 2009
).

Example 3.3

(Survival Archimedean copula)
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty]
be a completely monotone function with
\phi(1)=0
. The associated Archimedean copula is defined as

C^{\phi}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\phi^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})\right),~~u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in[0,1],

and
\phi
is called the
generator
(
Nelsen 2006
). The survival copula captures joint survival probabilities. If
\mathbf{U}=(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
follows
C^{\phi}
, then the survival copula
\overline{C}^{\phi}
corresponds to the distribution of
(1-U_{1},\dots,1-U_{n})
.

If the generator
\phi
is regularly varying at zero with
\theta>0
, i.e.,

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\phi(ty)}{\phi(t)}=y^{-\theta}~\mbox{for any }y>0,

(5)

then the
survival Archimedean copula

\overline{C}^{\phi}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}(-1)^{|I|}\phi^{-1}\left(\sum_{i\in I}\phi(1-u_{i})\right),~~u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in[0,1],

is an MRV copula (see
Embrechts et al. 2009
).

It has been shown that many Archimedean copulas have generators that are regularly varying at zero
(
Weng and Zhang 2012
)
.
A notable example is the survival Clayton copula, which is derived from the Clayton copula. The
Clayton copula
is defined as

C(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-\theta}-(n-1)\right)^{-1/\theta},

(6)

with generator
\phi_{\theta}(t)=t^{-\theta}-1
, which satisfies the regular variation condition in (
5
). Hence, the survival Clayton copula is an MRV copula.

Example 3.4

(Extreme value copula)
.

The extreme value copula itself is an MRV copula as it has the stability property
C(u_{1}^{1/t},\dots,u_{n}^{1/t})^{t}=C(u_{1}\dots,u_{n})
(Eq. (8.53) of
Beirlant et al. 2004
).
Some well-known examples include:

•

A simple example:
\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=(v_{1}^{1/\alpha}+\dots+v_{n}^{1/\alpha})^{\alpha}
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
, and in this case,

C(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\exp\left\{-\ell\left(-\log u_{1},\dots,-\log u_{n}\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\log u_{k})^{1/\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}\right\},

•

The
Galambos copula
or
negative logistic model
has the definition

C_{\theta,\alpha,\beta}^{\text{Gal}}(u_{1},u_{2})=u_{1}u_{2}\exp\left[\left\{(-\alpha\log u_{1})^{-\theta}+(-\beta\log u_{2})^{-\theta}\right\}^{-1/\theta}\right].

•

The
Gumbel copula
is defined as

C_{\theta,\alpha,\beta}^{\text{Gu}}(u_{1},u_{2})=u_{1}^{1-\alpha}u_{2}^{1-\beta}\exp\left\{-\left((-\alpha\log u_{1})^{\theta}+(-\beta\log u_{2})^{\theta}\right)^{1/\theta}\right\},

which is a general version of the Galambos copula.

Example 3.5

(Mixture of MRV copulas)
.

The class of MRV copulas is also convex in the sense that finite mixtures of MRV copulas remain within the class. Specifically, suppose a copula
C
is a finite mixture of MRV copulas
C_{1},\dots,C_{d}
, with non-negative weights
p_{1},\dots,p_{d}
satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}=1
, then

C(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=p_{1}C_{1}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})+\dots+p_{d}C_{d}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})

is still a copula and its tail dependence function is the corresponding convex combination:

D(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}\left(1-C_{k}(1-u_{1},\dots,1-u_{n})\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{k}D_{k}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),

where
D_{k}
is the tail dependence function of
C_{k}
. Since each
C_{k}
is MRV, the limit exists:

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}s^{-1}D(sv_{1},\dots,cv_{n})=p_{1}\ell_{1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})+\dots+p_{d}\ell_{d}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),

for any
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\geq 0
,
where
\ell_{k}
is the stable tail dependence function of
C_{k}
. Thus, a finite mixture of MRV copulas remains an MRV copula.

3.3
Asymptotic dependence structure

We now discuss the asymptotic dependence structure in the upper tail for random variables
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
with an MRV copula, which is characterized by the stable tail dependence function
\ell
, or equivalently, by the limiting copula
C^{*}
or the spectral measure
H^{*}
. These presentations capture how extreme events co-occur across components of a multivariate distribution, and thus can characterize the dependence structure of p-values when they are close to zero.

An important special case is
asymptotic independence
. Intuitively, this condition implies that extreme events in different components of a random vector occur independently at the upper limit. Formally, it is defined as follows:

Definition 3.2

(Asymptotic independence)
.

A random vector
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
with continuous marginals is called
asymptotically independent

if its copula is MRV and its limiting copula
C^{*}
satisfies

C^{*}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=C^{*}_{\mathrm{indep}}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}):=u_{1}\dots u_{n}.

(7)

In this case, the corresponding stable tail dependence function is
\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=v_{1}+\dots+v_{n}
, and the spectral measure
H^{*}
is concentrated on the basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, with unit mass at each point.

Remark 3.1
.

It has been shown that
\mathbf{Z}
with continuous marginals is asymptotically independent if it is pairwise quasi-asympotically independent. That is, for any pair
(i,j)
, the following condition holds (Eq. (8.100) of
Beirlant et al. 2004
)

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}[F_{i}(Z_{i})>1-s\mid F_{j}(Z_{j})>1-s]=0.

This pairwise quasi-asymptotic independence condition is commonly assumed in existing literature on the heavy-tailed combination tests
(
Liu and Xie 2020
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2023
)
.

Another extreme case is asymptotic complete dependence, where all components of the random vector tend to exhibit extreme behavior simultaneously.

Definition 3.3

(Asymptotic complete dependence)
.

A random vector
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
with continuous marginals
is called
asymptotically completely dependent
if its copula is MRV and its limiting copula
C^{*}
satisfies:

C^{*}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=C^{*}_{\mathrm{cdep}}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}):=\min(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}).

(8)

This corresponds to a stable tail dependence function of the form
\ell(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\max(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
and the spectral measure is concentrated on
\mathbf{1}/\|\mathbf{1}\|_{1}
, with a total mass equal to
n
.

Remark 3.2
.

It has also been shown that a sufficient condition for
\mathbf{Z}
to be asymptotically completely dependent is that for any pair
(i,j)
, it satisfies that

\lim_{s\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}[F_{i}(Z_{i})>1-s\mid F_{j}(Z_{j})>1-s]=1.

We now introduce concepts for comparing the strength of asymptotic dependence in the upper tail of MRV copulas. To do so, we begin by defining a partial ordering on copulas.

Definition 3.4

(Pointwise Order)
.

Given two copulas
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, we say that
C_{2}
dominates
C_{1}
in the pointwise order, denoted
C_{1}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}
, if

C_{1}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\leq C_{2}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),\quad\mathrm{for~all~}(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}].

The order is weaker than the commonly used concordance order, which is defined as both
C_{1}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}

and
\overline{C}_{1}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}\overline{C}_{2}
, where
\overline{C}
is the survival copula of a copula
C
.

Intuitively, a higher position in the pointwise order reflects stronger positive dependence. For MRV copulas, this ordering is preserved under the limiting operation, as formalized below:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be MRV copulas with limiting copulas
C_{1}^{*}
and
C_{2}^{*}
, respectively. If
C_{1}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}
, then
C_{1}^{*}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}^{*}
.

This result allows comparisons of asymptotic dependence to be reduced to comparing the MRV copulas themselves. For many commonly used copula families, this pointwise ordering is well-understood and can be inferred directly from parameter values.

Example 3.6

(Dependence ordering of
t
copulas)
.

Let
T=T_{n,\nu,\Sigma}
and
T^{\prime}=T_{n,\nu,\Sigma^{\prime}}
be two centered
n
-dimensional
t
distributions with the same degrees of freedom
\nu
and different scale matrices
\Sigma=(\rho_{ij})
and
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\rho_{ij}^{\prime})
satisfying
\rho_{ii}=\rho^{\prime}_{ii}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. If
\rho_{ij}\leq\rho_{ij}^{\prime}
for all
i\neq j
, then the corresponding
t
copulas satisfy

C_{t,\Sigma}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{t,\Sigma^{\prime}}.

This follows from Theorem 1 of
Ansari and Rüschendorf 2021
, since the supermodular order implies the pointwise order
(
Shaked and Shanthikumar 2007
)
. This result aligns with the intuition that higher correlation leads to stronger positive tail dependence.

Example 3.7

(Dependence ordering of survival Clayton copulas)
.

The dependence level of the survival Clayton copula defined in (
6
) is determined by the generator index
\theta
.
According to Lemma 4.3 of
Embrechts et al. 2009
and (9.A.17) of
Shaked and Shanthikumar 2007
, it holds that for any
0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}
,

C_{\theta_{1}}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{\theta_{2}},

where
C_{\theta_{1}}
and
C_{\theta_{2}}
are survival Clayton copulas with generator index
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
.

Furthermore, all limiting copulas of MRV copulas are naturally bracketed, in the sense of the pointwise order, by the extremal cases of asymptotic independence and asymptotic complete dependence.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
C^{*}
be the limiting copula of an MRV copula, as defined in (
3
). Then the following ordering holds:

C^{*}_{\mathrm{indep}}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C^{*}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C^{*}_{\mathrm{cdep}},

(9)

where
C^{*}_{\mathrm{indep}}
and
C^{*}_{\mathrm{cdep}}
are the limiting copulas corresponding to asymptotic independence and asymptotic complete dependence, as defined in (
7
) and (
8
).

3.2
implies that the limiting upper-tail dependence of MRV copulas is always non-negative in the pointwise sense, as it is bounded below by the case of asymptotic independence.

3.4
Connection between MRV copula and MRV distributions

Finally, we discuss the connection between MRV copulas and MRV distributions.
Intuitively, we will show that if the vector
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
follows an MRV copula, then the heavy-tailed transformed statsitics
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, where each
X_{i}=Q_{F_{i}}(1-P_{i})
for non-negative distribution
F_{i}
with a regularly varying tail, follows an MRV distribution. We will also demonstrate that the tail probability of the average
\bar{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
is analytically tractable.

There are many equivalent definitions of MRV distributions (see, for instance, Theorem 6.1 of
Resnick 2007
). We present an intuitive definition as follows:

Definition 3.5

(MRV distributions (Chapter 8.4 of
Beirlant et al. 2004
))
.

An
n
-dimensional random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with support
[\mathbf{0},\bm{\infty})
has
an
MRV distribution

if there exists a function
\lambda:(\mathbf{0},\bm{\infty})\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that for any
\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbf{0},\bm{\infty})
:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in t[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}]^{c})}{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in t[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]^{c})}=\lambda(\mathbf{x}).

(10)

It can be shown that the limiting function
\lambda
must satisfy
\lambda(s\mathbf{x})=s^{-\gamma}\lambda(\mathbf{x})
for some
\gamma>0
and any
s>0
(See
S3.1
for a proof).
The class of MRV distributions with the parameter
\gamma
is denoted by
\mathbf{X}\in\mathrm{MRV}_{-\gamma}
.

Given an MRV copula
C
and marginal distributions
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
, we can construct an MRV distribution under mild conditions:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
C
be an MRV copula, and let
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}
be marginal distributions supported on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\overline{F}_{i}(t)/\overline{F}_{0}(t)=c_{i}\in[0,\infty),~~i=1,\dots,n,

(11)

for some reference distribution
F_{0}\in\mathscr{R}_{-\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
. If at least one
c_{i}>0
, then the joint distribution with CDF

F_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=C\left(F_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,F_{n}(x_{n})\right)

is an MRV distribution with parameter
\gamma
.

3.3
implies that an MRV distribution can arise from an MRV copula even if only one marginal distribution has a regularly varying tail.
Conversely, the copula of any MRV distribution is itself an MRV copula (see Corollary 5.18 in
Resnick 2008
).
We can then characterize tractable approximations for the tail probability of
\bar{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
in terms of the associated spectral measure
H^{*}
:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in{\rm MRV}_{-\gamma}
,
\gamma>0
, be an
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
-valued random vector.
Then

h(\gamma,H^{*}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\bar{X}_{n}>t)}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>t)}=n^{1-\gamma}\int_{\mathcal{N}_{+,\|\cdot\|_{1}}^{n}}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}\theta_{i})^{1/\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}}H^{*}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}),

(12)

where
H^{*}
is the spectral measure of
\mathbf{X}
, and

c_{i}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>t)}{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\in t[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}]^{c})}\in[0,\infty),~~i=1,\dots,n.

4
Combination Test under MRV Copula

In this section, we investigate the asymptotic validity and power of the weighted heavy-tailed combination test defined in
2.2
, under the setting where the dependence among p-values in the lower tail (their joint behavior as they approach zero) is characterized by an MRV copula. Our analysis focuses on the asymptotic setting where the nominal significance level
\alpha\to 0
and the number of hypotheses
n
is fixed.

4.1
Asymptotic Validity

To build intuition, let us consider the combination test with weights
\omega_{i}=1
for each
i
. If each p-value
P_{i}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
under the global null, then the CDF of the joint distribution of
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
is a copula. If this copula is an MRV copula, then following
3.3
, the heavy-tail-transformed statistics
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, where
X_{i}=Q_{F_{i}}(1-P_{i})
, follows an MRV distribution.
As a result, the tail probability of their average can be characterized using
3.4
, enabling us to analyze type-I error control of the test in the limit
\alpha\to 0
.
More generally,
for a weighted heavy-tailed combination test defined in
2.2
, define the
limiting scaled type-I error
as

q(\gamma)=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{global}}}\left(P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\alpha}.

(13)

We have the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that under the global null, the joint distribution of
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
follows an MRV copula and has standard
uniform marginals. Then the limiting scaled type-I error
q(\gamma)
of the weighted heavy-tailed combination test depends on the transformation function
F
only through its tail index
\gamma
, and is non-decreasing in
\gamma
; that is,

q(\gamma_{1})\geq q(\gamma_{2}),\quad\text{for all }\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2}>0.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
is asymptotically independent.
In particular,
q(1)=1
, and hence the test is asymptotically valid for all
\gamma\leq 1
, i.e.,

q(\gamma)\leq 1,\quad\text{for all }\gamma\leq 1.

4.1

establishes the asymptotic validity of the weighted heavy-tailed combination test for all
\gamma\leq 1
under a broad class of dependence structures, including those that violate asymptotic independence assumptions typically required in earlier literature. In particular, asymptotic validity is guaranteed whenever the joint distribution of the p-values exhibits lower-tail dependence in the limit. Moreover, the limiting type-I error reaches the nominal level, i.e.,
q(1)=1
, when
\gamma=1
, regardless of the specific asymptotic dependence structure among the p-values. In contrast, the test becomes asymptotically conservative for
\gamma<1
and invalid for
\gamma>1
when the
p
-values are not asymptotically independent.

Remark 4.1
.

When the
p
-values are conservative, including discrete
p
-values such as those arising from permutation tests, that is, each base p-value
P_{i}
is stochastically larger than a standard uniform distribution under the null, the asymptotic validity of the weighted heavy-tailed combination test still holds.
Specifically, by Theorems 3.3.5 and 3.3.8 of
Müller and Stoyan 2002
,
there exists a random vector
(\tilde{P}_{1},\dots,\tilde{P}_{n})
with standard uniform marginals and the same copula as
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
such that

P_{i}\geq\tilde{P}_{i}
for all
i
.
Since the combined p-value of the combination test is non-decreasing in each base p-value,
the type-I error of the combination test applied to
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
is no larger than that applied to
(\tilde{P}_{1},\dots,\tilde{P}_{n})
, whose asymptotic validity is guaranteed in
4.1
.

In addition, we characterize the bounds on the limiting scaled type-I error for heavy-tailed combination tests. This limit is determined by the lower-tail dependence structure among the p-values, with the bounds achieved under asymptotic complete dependence and asymptotic independence.

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the same assumptions as in
4.1
, the limiting scaled type-I error
q(\gamma)
as defined in (
13
) satisfies

q(\gamma)\in\begin{cases}\left[\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}^{1/\gamma}\right)^{\gamma},1\right],~~\gamma\leq 1,\\[3.0pt]
\left[1,\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}^{1/\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}\right],~~\gamma\geq 1.\end{cases}

Moreover, the lower (or upper) bound
\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}^{1/\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}
is achieved if and only if
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
is asymptotically completely dependent, while the bound
1
is achieved if and only if
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
is asymptotically independent.

4.2
is closely related to
3.2
,
which establishes that the limiting lower-tail dependence structure among p-values lies between asymptotic independence and asymptotic complete dependence under the MRV copula framework. For
\gamma\leq 1
, where the heavy-tailed combination tests are asymptotically valid, these two extreme cases correspond to the least and most conservative scenarios, respectively.
As a special case, when all the weights are equal (
\omega_{i}=1
), the bound under complete dependence simplifies to
n^{\gamma-1}
.

4.2
Asymptotic Power

Next, we investigate the power difference between heavy-tailed combination tests using different transformation parameters
\gamma
. A key determinant of this power is the strength and distribution of the signals under the alternative.
To formalize this, we define the set of dominating signals as follows:

Definition 4.1

(Dominating signals)
.

Consider the base p-value vector
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
.
For two p-values
P_{i}
and
P_{j}
, we write
P_{i}\succ P_{j}
if

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(P_{i}<t)}{\mathbb{P}(P_{j}<t)}=0.

The set of dominating signals for
\mathbf{P}
is then defined as

I_{\mathbf{P}}=\left\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\nexists j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\text{ such that }P_{i}\succ P_{j}\right\}.

Intuitively,
I_{\mathbf{P}}
consists of the indices of p-values that have the heaviest lower tails under the alternative, which will most likely dominate the combined test statistic.
We also impose the following condition on the joint distribution of the base p-values:

Assumption 1
.

The base p-values
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
under the true configuration of hypotheses satisfy:

(i)

Each
P_{i}
is continuously distributed;

(ii)

The vector
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
has an MRV copula;

(iii)

For any distinct
i,j\in I_{\mathbf{P}}
, the relative tail probabilities are comparable:

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(P_{i}<t)}{\mathbb{P}(P_{j}<t)}\in(0,\infty);

and for at least one
i\in I_{\mathbf{P}}
, there exists an
F\in\mathscr{R}_{-1}
such that
Q_{F}(1-P_{i})\in\mathscr{R}_{-\beta}
for some
\beta\leq 1
.

1
is mild and aligns with typical alternatives in hypothesis testing. Part (i) is a routine continuity assumption. Part (ii) assumes that the limiting lower-tail dependence structure among p-values remains well-defined under any true hypotheses configuration. Part (iii) ensures that the transformed statistics
X_{i,\omega_{i}}
corresponding to dominating signals retain regularly varying tails under the alternative and with any choice of transformation
F
(See
S3.1
for a proof). This is reasonable, as p-values under the alternative are often more concentrated near zero, which results in heavier-tailed transformed statistics than those under the null.

We now present two types of alternatives under which
1
holds:

Example 4.1

(Alternative Types)
.

Define
(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
to follow an MRV copula with standard uniform marginals. Let
\overset{d}{=}
denote equality in distribution. We consider two types of alternative hypotheses:

(a)

Type-A alternative
:

(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})\overset{d}{=}(G_{1}(G^{-1}_{1}(U_{1})+\mu_{1}),\dots,G_{n}(G^{-1}_{n}(U_{n})+\mu_{n})),

where
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\geq 0
and
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
are continuous CDFs satisfying either (i) a tail-shift invariance condition,
\lim_{x\to\infty}\overline{G}_{i}(x-\mu)/\overline{G}_{i}(x)\in(0,\infty)
for all
\mu>0
and all
i
, or (ii) that each
G_{i}
is the standard normal distribution. These conditions are satisfied by many common distributions, including the normal, exponential, and Student’s
t
distributions. Intuitively, the Type-A alternative presents signals through location shifts, such as the common one-sided location test:

H_{0}:\mu_{i}\leq 0~\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n,~~H_{1}:\mu_{i}>0~\text{for some }i=1,\dots,n.

Under condition (i), all coordinates are equally dominant, and
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Under condition (ii), only those indices with the largest
\mu_{i}
values dominate, so
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{i:\mu_{i}=\max_{j}\mu_{j}\}
.

(b)

Type-B alternative
:

(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})\overset{d}{=}((1-U_{1})^{\beta_{1}},\dots,(1-U_{n})^{\beta_{n}}),

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\geq 1
.
Each marginal
P_{i}
then follows a
\text{Beta}(1/\beta_{i},1)
distribution. This model, originally proposed in
Sellke et al. 2001
, serves as a simple parametric form for p-values under the alternative. In this setting, the set of dominating signals is given by
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{i:\beta_{i}=\max_{j}\beta_{j}\}
.

Both types of alternatives satisfy the regularity conditions in
1
. We provide a detailed discussion in
Section

S2
.

With
1
, we can apply
3.4
to analyze the asymptotic power of the heavy-tailed combination test under alternative hypotheses.
Because the power of any test vanishes as
\alpha\to 0
, we compare different tests through a
limiting scaled power
that factors out marginal effects:

\tilde{q}(\gamma)=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(P_{i}\leq{\omega_{i}\alpha}/{n}\right)}.

(14)

The denominator, which is the sum of marginal probabilities, is not affected by the choice of
F
and thus
\gamma
. Hence, any change in
\tilde{q}(\gamma)
with
\gamma
directly reflects a change in power.

We have the following result on the power comparison.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
1
holds.
The limiting scaled power
\tilde{q}(\gamma)
depends on the transformation function
F
only through its tail index
\gamma
, and is non-decreasing in
\gamma
; that is,

\tilde{q}(\gamma_{1})\geq\tilde{q}(\gamma_{2}),\quad\text{for all }\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2}>0.

Moreover, the inequality is strict if and only if
the dominating-signal set
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{S}\}
has at least two elements, and
the subvector
(1-P_{i_{1}},\dots,1-P_{i_{S}})
is not asymptotically independent.

Together,
4.1
and
4.3
explain a key empirical observation
(
Gui et al. 2023
)
: both the type-I error and power of the combination test rises with the transformation index
\gamma
. Based on this insight, we recommend using a transformation with parameter
\gamma=1
in practice, as it offers a favorable trade-off between maximizing power and ensuring asymptotic validity.

4.3
Comparison with the Bonferroni Test

In this subsection, we compare the heavy-tailed combination tests
and the Bonferroni test, which is widely recognized for its validity under arbitrary dependence structures.
Under the MRV framework, we aim to characterize the dependence structures where the combination tests have a real advantage over Bonferroni test.
To facilitate our analysis, we first define the weighted Bonferroni test with pre-specified non-random weights
(
Genovese et al. 2006
)
.

Definition 4.2

(Weighted Bonferroni test)
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
be the p-values and
\bm{\omega}=\left(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
be a non-random weight vector satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}=n
, then the combined p-value of the weighted Bonferroni test is

P_{\mathrm{bon}}^{\bm{\omega}}=\min\left(1,n\min_{i=1,\dots,n}\frac{P_{i}}{\omega_{i}}\right).

We can show that the combination tests are at least as powerful as Bonferroni in the following result.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that
1
holds.
The weighted heavy-tailed combination test is asymptotically at least as powerful as the weighted Bonferroni test:

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\mathrm{bon}}^{\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}\geq 1.

Moreover, the inequality is strict if and only if the dominating-signal set
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{S}\}
has at least two elements, and
the subvector
(1-P_{i_{1}},\dots,1-P_{i_{S}})
is not asymptotically independent.

4.4
confirms that while both the heavy-tailed combination test (with
\gamma\leq 1
) and the Bonferroni test are asymptotically valid under the MRV copula framework, the combination test can be strictly more powerful when the lower tails of the p-values exhibit asymptotic dependence and the signal is not extremely sparse, meaning that the set of dominant signals includes more than one component. Intuitively, when there is only a single strong signal, there is no benefit from aggregation, and the combination test provides no advantage over the Bonferroni method.

We can further show that the Bonferroni test can be viewed as the limiting case of the combination test as
\gamma\to 0
:

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that
1
holds.
We have

\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\mathrm{bon}}^{\bm{\omega}}\leq\alpha\right)}=1.

(15)

Finally, we compare the asymptotic type-I error and power ratio between the proposed heavy-tailed combination test with
\gamma=1
and the Bonferroni test under different dependence structures of the p-values. We demonstrate that the relative advantage of the combination test over Bonferroni increases as the asymptotic lower-tail dependence among the p-values increases.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume the assumptions as in
4.1
. Let
C^{*}
denote the limiting copula of
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
. Then asymptotic type-I error ratio between the weighted the combination test with
\gamma=1
and the weighted Bonferroni test,

r(C^{*})=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{global}}}\left(P^{F,\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{comb}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{global}}}\left(P^{\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{bon}}\leq\alpha\right)},

(16)

is non-decreasing in
C^{*}
under the pointwise order. That is, if
C_{1}^{*}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}^{*}
, then
r(C_{1}^{*})\leq r(C_{2}^{*})
.

This result also extends to the power comparison under alternatives where the tail heaviness of the transformed statistics matches that under the null, i.e.,
\beta=1
in
1
, as is the case for the Type-A alternatives in
4.1
(
S2.1
for the proof).

Corollary 4.1
.

Assume the assumptions as in
4.4
and further
\beta=1
in
1
. Let
C^{*}
denote the limiting copula of
(1-P_{1},\dots,1-P_{n})
. Then asymptotic type-I error ratio between the weighted combination test with
\gamma=1
and the weighted Bonferroni test

\tilde{r}(C^{*})=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(P^{F,\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{comb}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(P^{\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{bon}}\leq\alpha\right)},

(17)

is non-decreasing in
C^{*}
under the pointwise order. That is, if
C_{1}^{*}\preceq_{\mathrm{pw}}C_{2}^{*}
, then
\tilde{r}(C_{1}^{*})\leq\tilde{r}(C_{2}^{*})
.

3.6
and
3.7
illustrate how the pointwise order among
t
and Clayton copulas is governed by their respective parameters, such as correlation or generator index. Since the pointwise order of copulas can be inherited by their liming copulas, in view of
4.6
and
4.1
, this implies that the Bonferroni test becomes increasingly conservative relative to the heavy-tailed combination test with
\gamma=1
. While our theoretical results focus on
\gamma=1
, which we recommend in practice, simulations in
Section

5
will demonstrate that the increasing trend in the power ratio between the combination test and the Bonferroni test with respect to tail dependence persists more broadly for other choices of
\gamma>0
.

Remark 4.2

(One signal)
.

Suppose that we observe that one of the p-values, say
P_{1}=:p
, is close to
0
, and all other p-values
P_{2},\dots,P_{n}
are of moderate size in
(0,1)
. This may happen in the case where only one hypothesis has significant signal and all other null hypotheses are true, that is, an extreme case of sparse signal.
In this case, if
p
is very small, then the p-value
P^{F,\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{comb}}
with any
\gamma>0
and
\bm{\omega}=(1,\dots,1)
is similar, because, as
p\downarrow 0
and keeping the other p-values constant,

\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{F}(1-P_{i})\simeq\frac{1}{n}Q_{F}(1-p),

and hence

P_{\mathrm{comb}}^{F,\bm{\omega}}=n^{1-\gamma}\overline{F}(\bar{X}_{n,\bm{\omega}})=n^{1-\gamma}\overline{F}\left(\frac{1}{n}Q_{F}\left(1-p\right)\right)\simeq n\overline{F}(Q_{F}(1-p))=np.

Therefore, in this special case, we expect all heavy-tailed combination tests perform similarly to the Bonferroni test.

5
Simulations

6
Discussion

We conclude by discussing potential limitations of our results and directions for future research. A primary limitation of our theoretical framework is its focus on asymptotic validity and power as the significance level
\alpha\to 0
, rather than addressing non-asymptotic properties at a fixed
\alpha
. As illustrated in
Section

5
, at conventional significance levels such as
\alpha=0.05
, empirical validity and power can deviate from the asymptotic behavior. Future work could aim to characterize the rate of convergence, providing more practical guidelines for applications.

Another limitation is that our theory focuses on combination tests using heavy-tailed distributions with bounded left supports, thereby excluding the Cauchy distribution and more generally, Student’s
t
distributions. Prior studies
(
Fang et al. 2023
;
Gui et al. 2023
)
have demonstrated that unbounded transformations may lead to inefficient tests when p-values are conservative or negatively correlated. Our results thus reinforce the recommendation to prefer left-bounded transformations in practice.

Although our theoretical results are derived under a fixed number of hypotheses
n
, empirical findings suggest that the methods remain effective for moderate
n
(e.g., a few hundred). We conjecture that the theory can extend to scenarios where
n
increases slowly as
\alpha
decreases, similar to results shown in
Liu and Xie 2020
for special cases.

Lastly, our empirical comparisons with HCCT
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
suggest that adjusting for type-I-error inflation under independence of the p-values is beneficial when
\alpha
is not very small (e.g.,
\alpha=0.05
), especially when the p-values are only weakly dependent. However, such adjustments are less critical for smaller
\alpha
or in settings with moderate to strong dependence among p-values.

S1
Another partial ordering on MRV copulas

In this section, we introduce a new partial ordering on MRV copulas to compare the strength of asymptotic dependence, which enables stronger theoretical results on how dependence impacts the conservativeness and power of heavy-tailed combination tests when
\gamma<1
. Specifically, we define a partial order based on the convex order of the spectral measures associated with MRV copulas:

Definition S1.1

(Convex order)
.

For any two measures
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
on the same space
\Theta
,
H_{1}
dominates
H_{2}
in convex order
, denoted by
H_{1}\geq_{\rm cx}H_{2}
, holds if
\int_{\Theta}\phi({\mathbf{\theta}}){\rm d}H_{1}({\mathbf{\theta}})\geq\int_{\Theta}\phi({\mathbf{\theta}}){\rm d}H_{2}({\mathbf{\theta}})
for any convex function
\phi
.

Definition S1.2

(Partial order between MRV copulas)
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be MRV copulas with spectral measures
H^{*}

and
H^{**}
, respectively. We say that
C^{\prime}

dominates

C
, denoted by
C\preceq C^{\prime}
, if

H^{*}\geq_{\rm cx}H^{**}.

Intuitively,
C\preceq C^{\prime}
corresponds to
C
being less asymptotically positively dependent than
C^{\prime}
. Similar to
3.2
, we can prove that all MRV copulas are between asymptotic independence and asymptotic complete dependence in terms of this new partial order:

Proposition S1.1
.

Let
C_{\mathrm{indep}}
and
C_{\mathrm{cdep}}
be the MRV copulas under asymptotic independence and asymptotic complete dependence, respectively. Then for any MRV copula
C
, we have

C_{\mathrm{indep}}\preceq C\preceq C_{\mathrm{cdep}}.

As established in
3.4
, the asymptotic dependence structure characterized by the MRV copula governs the tail behavior of the
L_{1}
norm of the MRV random vector. Furthermore, the convex ordering of the associated spectral measure and the convexity of
\bm{\theta}\mapsto{{\|(\mathbf{c}\odot\bm{\theta})^{1/\gamma}\|_{1}}^{\gamma}}
for
\gamma\in(0,1]
directly induce this monotonicity:

Proposition S1.2
.

Suppose that the MRV copula
C
has spectral measure
H^{*}

and the MRV copula
C^{\prime}
has spectral measure
H^{**}
.
If
C\succeq C^{\prime}
, then
h(\gamma,H^{*})\leq h(\gamma,H^{**})
for
\gamma\in(0,1].

Proof.

The result follows from (
12
) of
3.4
and that the integral function in the right hand side of (
12
) is convex in
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{N}_{+,\|\cdot\|_{1}}^{n}.

∎

According to the proof of
4.1
,

h(\gamma,H^{*})=n^{1-\gamma}\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}^{\mathrm{global}}}\left(P^{F,\bm{\omega}}_{\mathrm{comb}}\leq\alpha\right)}{\alpha}

characterizes the asymptotic type-I error of the heavy-tailed combination test, with
\bm{\omega}=(1,\dots,1)
. Consequently, a larger partial order of the MRV copula leads to a smaller type-I error, indicating a more conservative test. We can also obtain the same monotonicity property for power, similar to how we establish
4.1
after
4.6
.

Unfortunately, establishing the partial order
C\succeq C^{\prime}
for specific forms of MRV copulas is more challenging than the pointwise order. In low-dimensional cases or certain high-dimensional cases, the order is tractable. For example, when
n=2
, the order
C\succeq C^{\prime}
corresponds to
the pointwise inequality
\int_{0}^{x}H^{*}(t,1){\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{x}H^{**}(t,1){\rm d}t
for
x\in[0,1]
.
1
1

1

Note that when
n=2
,
H^{*}
is a measure on
\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}:x_{1}+x_{2}=1\}
. We have
H^{*}\geq_{\rm cx}H^{**}
if and only if
H_{1}^{*}\geq_{\rm cx}H_{1}^{**}
, where
H_{1}^{*}
is the first marginal distribution of
H^{*}
. Therefore, this is again equivalent to
\int_{0}^{x}H^{*}(t,1){\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{x}H^{**}(t,1){\rm d}t
for
x\in[0,1]
.

For higher dimensions (
n>2
), similar pointwise ordering can hold when independent pairs of components exist. However, general theoretical results on ordering between spectral measures in dimensions
n>2
are limited and technically difficult to obtain (see, e.g.,
Mao and Hu 2015
). For instance, it remains unclear whether increasing the entries of the correlation matrix in a multivariate
t
-copula leads to a spectral measure that is larger in the convex order.

Some special cases nevertheless exhibit the monotonicity described in
S1.2
. For example, the Clayton copula admits this monotonicity property in general dimensions, although the argument does not directly follow from the convex order of spectral measures.

Empirically, our simulations in
Section

5.1
show that
h(\gamma,H^{*})
tends to decrease as dependence increases with a fixed
\gamma<1
, suggesting that the test becomes more conservative. This observation points to a promising direction for future work: developing a more refined order of MRV copulas and its implications for the tail behavior of the MRV random vector.

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\exp\left\{\frac{-2(\mu_{i}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2})\log{t}}{\mu_{i}\sqrt{-2\log{t}}+\mu_{j}\sqrt{-2\log{t}}}\right\}=\begin{cases}0,~~&\mu_{i}<\mu_{j}\\
1,~~&\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}\\
\infty,~~&\mu_{i}>\mu_{j}\end{cases}.

That is,
I_{\mathbf{P}}=\{i:\mu_{i}=\max_{j\in[n]}\mu_{j}\}
. Without loss of generality, we suppose
\mu_{1}=\max_{i\in[n]}\mu_{j}
. To confirm (iii) and
\beta=1
, it suffices to prove that for any
F\in\mathscr{R}_{-1}
,
Q_{F}(1-P_{1})\in\mathscr{R}_{-1}
. The tail probability of
Q_{F}(1-P_{1})
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q_{F}(1-P_{1})>x)=\overline{G}_{1}\left(\overline{G}_{1}^{-1}(\overline{F}(x))-\mu_{1}\right)=\overline{\Phi}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(\overline{F}(x))-\mu_{1}\right)

We check the definition of regularly varying tailed distribution: for all
y>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\overline{\Phi}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(\overline{F}(xy))-\mu_{1}\right)}{\overline{\Phi}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(\overline{F}(x))-\mu_{1}\right)}=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\overline{\Phi}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(y^{-1}t\right)-\mu_{1}\right)}{\overline{\Phi}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(t\right)-\mu_{1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\Phi\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(y^{-1}t\right)\right)}{\Phi\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(t\right)\right)}\times\exp\left\{\mu_{1}\left(\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(y^{-1}t\right)-\overline{\Phi}^{-1}\left(t\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{y^{-1}t}{t}\times\frac{\exp\left[\mu_{1}\sqrt{-2\log(y^{-1})-2\log{t}-\log\log(y^{-1}/t)-\log(4\pi)+o(1)}\right]}{\exp\left[\mu_{1}\sqrt{-2\log{t}-\log\log(1/t)-\log(4\pi)+o(1)}\right]}

\displaystyle=y^{-1}\times\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\exp\left\{\frac{\log{y}}{\mu_{1}\sqrt{-2\log{t}}}\right\}=y^{-1}.

Then the proposition follows.
∎
