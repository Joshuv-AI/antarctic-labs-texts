Spectral-Domain Optical Coherence Tomography Morphological Characteristics in Patients with Cone Dysfunction Disorders: A Retrospective Case Series
Authors: Hassan Khojasteh, Fatemeh Bazvand, Mostafa Haidari, Amir Akhavanrezayat, Alireza Mahmoudi, S Saeed Mohammadi, Hamid Riazi Esfahani, Masoud Mirghorbani, Arefeh Sheikholeslami Salmasi, Afsaneh Azarkish, Jonathan Regenold, Irmak Karaca, Negin Yavari, Mengxi Shen, Gunay Uludag Kirimli, Christopher Or, Hashem H Ghoraba, Quan Dong Nguyen, Loh-Shan B Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo describe spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) morphological characteristics in patients with cone dysfunction disorders.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral university eye hospital between July 2018 and September 2020. Forty-two patients (84 eyes) with cone dysfunction disorders, diagnosed based on clinical findings and electroretinogram evidence of severely reduced or non-recordable cone responses with preserved rod function, were included in our study. SD-OCT imaging was performed, and images were evaluated regarding the integrity and pattern of hyper-reflective outer retinal bands. The relationship between these findings, age, central retinal thickness (CRT), and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was assessed.ResultsSD-OCT images of 82 eyes showed outer retinal abnormalities. Five SD-OCT morphological categories were found, including outer retinal atrophy (24.4%), poorly delineated outer retinal bands (22.0%), ellipsoid zone (EZ) disruption (19.5%), outer foveal defect (17.1%), and prominent outer retinal layers (17.1%). Additionally, five isolated OCT findings were detected, including foveal hypoplasia (14.6%), trans-retinal hyperreflective dots (THD) (29.3%), outer plexiform layer (OPL) schisis (11.0%), subretinal drusenoid-like deposits (9.8%), and EZ bowing (13.4%). Age and CRT were significantly different across the morphological categories (p<0.001). Eyes with prominent outer retinal layers had the best visual acuity, whereas those with outer retinal atrophy had the worst visual acuity; however, differences in BCVA among the morphological categories were not statistically significant in the univariate GEE analysis (p = 0.09) or after multivariable adjustment (p = 0.32).ConclusionSD-OCT imaging demonstrates diverse outer retinal morphological patterns in patients with cone dysfunction disorders. These patterns were associated with age and CRT but were not significantly associated with BCVA. The proposed morphological classification may help characterize disease morphology and warrants prospective evaluation in longitudinal, genetically characterized cohorts to determine its potential utility as a biomarker.

Introduction

Cone dysfunction disorders are a heterogeneous group of inherited retinal diseases characterized by progressive early-onset loss of vision, often accompanied by photophobia and hemeralopia.1,2 Fundoscopy findings of cone dystrophy range from a normal fundus appearance to bull’s eye maculopathy, and the optic disc may show a variable degree of temporal pallor.3,4 Visual field defects include reduced central sensitivity or relative scotoma with intact peripheral visual fields, and color vision might be affected in all three axes.3,5 In electroretinography (ERG), the cone response is reduced while the rod response is preserved, although in more advanced cases, the rod response may also be affected.3 Several genes have been identified as carrying mutations that cause non-syndromic progressive cone degeneration.6 The majority of these cases are autosomal recessive, but they can also be inherited in an autosomal dominant or X-linked fashion.6

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) produces a cross-sectional image of the retina that enables structural evaluation and thickness measurements. By increasing imaging speed compared with time-domain OCT, high-resolution spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) provides a higher axial resolution and less motion artifact.7,8 Additionally, SD-OCT is a widely utilized imaging modality that is neither invasive nor technically challenging to use, making it a practical method for diagnosing and monitoring patients with cone dysfunction disorders.9,10 Nevertheless, few studies have evaluated specific structural changes in cone dystrophy using OCT and their association with visual status. Hood et al reported decreased EZ intensity in 16 patients with cone dystrophy or achromatopsia.11 Cho et al evaluated 15 patients with cone dystrophy and reported four structural OCT categories: no abnormality, foveal EZ loss, foveal thinning with focal foveal EZ loss, and foveal thickening with perifoveal EZ disruption.12 However, in clinical settings, several other types of structural abnormalities may be observed that do not fall into these categories, most likely reflecting the genetic heterogeneity of cone dysfunction disorders.

In this index study, the aim was to characterize the spectrum of SD-OCT morphological abnormalities in cone dysfunction disorders and to classify the major structural patterns observed on OCT. We further sought to evaluate whether these morphological categories were associated with clinical characteristics, including age, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and central retinal thickness (CRT). We hypothesized that distinct SD-OCT morphological patterns would be identifiable in cone dysfunction disorders and would correlate with clinical features, thereby providing a structural framework for disease characterization and a basis for future evaluation as imaging biomarkers in longitudinal, genetically characterized cohorts.

Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary referral university ophthalmology hospital between July 2018 and September 2020. The study protocol was approved by the Tehran University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (approval code of IR.TUMS.FARABIH.REC.1398.035) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki tenets. All participants had provided written informed consent permitting the future use of their clinical data for research. All patients in this study had a history of hemeralopia, visual loss, or reduced color vision and underwent complete ophthalmologic evaluation, full-field standard ERG testing, and SD-OCT imaging. The diagnosis of cone dysfunction disorders was established based on both clinical and electrophysiological findings. Patient inclusion was based primarily on electrophysiological findings, and subjects with severely reduced or non-recordable cone responses and relatively preserved rod function on full-field ERG were included. Clinical findings were used to support the diagnosis, while SD-OCT images of sufficient quality were required for structural analysis. Patients with retinal diseases not fulfilling the above electrophysiological criteria, poor-quality SD-OCT images precluding structural analysis, or incomplete clinical or electrophysiological records were excluded.

Clinical Assessment and SD-OCT Imaging

All eyes were imaged using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). A high-resolution (HR) 20° × 20° macular volume scan centered on the fovea was acquired using 49 B-scans with automatic real-time (ART) averaging of 16 frames per B-scan. The Spectralis system provides an axial resolution of approximately 7 µm and a lateral resolution of approximately 14 µm. CRT was obtained from the device’s automated retinal segmentation software and was not measured manually.

Ophthalmologic examination included BCVA testing, recorded as the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR), manifest and cycloplegic refraction, slit-lamp examination, intraocular pressure measurement, and fundus examination. Full-field standard ERG was performed using the MonPack3 system (Metrovision, Pérenchies, France) in accordance with the 2015 International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) Standard for full-field electroretinography (ffERG), which was the prevailing guideline during the study period (2018–2020).13,14 ERG responses were interpreted using the built-in reference database of the MonPack3 system in conjunction with the ISCEV standard protocol. Subjects were included if ffERG demonstrated severely reduced or non-recordable cone responses with relatively preserved rod function. The dark-adapted (DA) ERGs included responses to flash strengths (in photopic units; phot) of 0.01, 3 and 10 phot cd.s.m-2 (DA 0.01; DA 3; DA 10). The light-adapted (LA) ERGs consisted of responses to a flash strength of 3 phot cd.s.m−2, superimposed on a light-adapting background (luminance 30 cd.m−2) as single flashes (LA 3 ERG) and at a frequency of 30Hz (LA 30 Hz ERG). All ERG tests were performed by an experienced examiner (H.K.). The five major SD-OCT morphological categories were defined based on the predominant outer retinal structural abnormality observed on the foveal B-scan. Diffuse outer retinal atrophy was defined as generalized loss of the outer retinal layers involving the fovea with marked attenuation of the ellipsoid zone (EZ), external limiting membrane (ELM), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Poorly delineated outer retinal bands were defined by reduced distinction of the normal outer retinal hyperreflective bands while these structures remained partially identifiable. Ellipsoid zone disruption was defined as focal or diffuse interruption of the EZ with relative preservation of adjacent outer retinal layers. Outer foveal defect was defined as a localized full-thickness defect involving the outer retinal layers at the foveal center with preservation of the surrounding retinal architecture. Prominent outer retinal layers were qualitatively defined by increased conspicuity of the outer retinal hyperreflective bands relative to the surrounding retina without quantitative reflectivity measurements. SD-OCT images were independently reviewed by two masked retinal sub-specialists (H.K. and F.B.), who were fully masked to patients’ age, BCVA, electrophysiological findings, and clinical diagnosis. Any abnormality in the retinal layers was recorded only after both observers reached a consensus. CRT was defined as the mean retinal thickness of the circular region surrounding the foveola, measuring 1 mm in diameter.

Statistical Analysis

A Q-Q plot and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were utilized to assess the normal distribution of data. To account for the correlation between fellow eyes (within-subject correlation), generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to compare age, mean CRT, and BCVA among the five retinal structural categories. A post hoc Sidak test was used in the GEE analysis for pairwise comparisons between groups. Chi-square analysis was employed to investigate the association between isolated signs and the five categories of retinal structural abnormalities. Finally, a multivariable GEE regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between BCVA and the other study variables while accounting for the inter-eye correlation between fellow eyes.

Statistical analysis was performed using commercial software (SPSS ver. 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Following a comprehensive evaluation, 42 patients were included in the study. 22/42 (52.3%) were female, and the mean age of participants was 21.3 ± 15.3 years (range of 6–59 years). The mean BCVA of patients was 0.80 ± 0.37 LogMAR. The mean CRT was 191.22 ± 52.76 μm. None of the included patients had other neurologic or retinal abnormalities.

SD-OCT demonstrated retinal morphological changes in both eyes of 40 patients, while 2 patients presented with unilateral retinal changes; the normal fellow eyes had no OCT abnormalities. However, the corresponding full-field ERGs of these eyes showed reduced cone function. Analysis of the 82 eyes with OCT abnormalities showed various retinal morphologic changes. Analysis of the four hyperreflective outer retinal bands revealed recurring patterns of abnormalities in multiple eyes. Based on the qualitative concordance of the abnormal features, there were five categories of major retinal changes (Figure 2); A) absence of external limiting membrane (ELM), EZ, and interdigitation zone (IZ) leading to outward distortion of the retina, B) preserved ELM, EZ, and IZ that are not distinguishable or distinct (merging or hatching of layers), C) preserved ELM and IZ with scattered disruptions of the EZ, D) focal disruptions at any layer of ELM, EZ, or IZ at the foveola that create an optically empty space and E) preserved ELM, EZ, and IZ that are prominently hyper-reflective. The five morphological categories, in descending order of frequency, were diffuse outer retinal atrophy (20 eyes, 24.4%), poorly delineated outer retinal bands (18 eyes, 22.0%), EZ disruption (16 eyes, 19.5%), outer foveal defect (14 eyes, 17.1%), and prominent outer retinal layers (14 eyes, 17.1%) (Figure 2). The distribution of morphologies across these five categories was relatively similar (from 17% to 24%; p=0.9). Discussion

In this study, we assessed SD-OCT images of 84 eyes from 42 patients with cone dysfunction disorders. Of this cohort, 82 eyes had OCT abnormalities. We characterized the structural macular abnormalities in these patients to find a correlation between the structural changes and visual function. We classified the observed structural abnormalities into two generalized categories: 1. Abnormalities of outer retina hyper-reflective bands that seemed to be major and were categorized into 5 groups; 2. Isolated OCT abnormality signs. The five major morphological groups include (1) outer retinal atrophy, (2) poorly delineated outer retinal bands, (3) EZ disruption, (4) outer foveal defect, and (5) prominent outer retinal layers. The isolated signs were foveal hypoplasia, THD, OPL schisis, subretinal drusenoid-like deposits, and EZ bowing.

Only a few papers have been published evaluating cone dysfunction disorders using SD-OCT imaging. Cho et al published a large series describing the SD-OCT findings of 15 patients with cone dystrophy.12 They categorized findings into four groups based on the status of EZ, interdigitation zone (IZ), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE): Category 0 included no abnormality, category 1 included foveal EZ loss and obscurity of the border between the EZ band and ELM, category 2 included foveal thinning and focal foveal EZ disruption with an intact ELM, and category 3 included foveal thickening with perifoveal disruption of the EZ. Categories 1–3 alL had in common thickening of RPE and absence of IZ. In the study by Cho et al, 4 out of 15 patients (aged 7, 9, 11, and 13 years) had normal retinal findings on OCT, while in our study only two out of 84 eyes (42 patients) had normal retinal findings on OCT. The absence of structural abnormalities in these two eyes may reflect incomplete penetrance, variable phenotypic expressivity, or an early stage of disease before detectable OCT changes develop. However, because genetic testing and longitudinal follow-up were not available, these possibilities could not be further evaluated. One reason for the lower proportion of structurally normal eyes in our cohort may be that our tertiary referral center predominantly evaluates patients with more advanced disease referred for diagnostic assessment. Additionally, only patients with severely reduced or non-recordable cone responses were included in our study. In contrast, Cho et al included patients with cone dystrophy who recorded photopic and 30-Hz flicker responses that were lower than those of the normal control group (p<0.001) but not severely reduced or non-recordable.

Our findings demonstrate the utility of SD-OCT imaging in the diagnosis of cone dysfunction disorders. In our larger series of 42 patients, some detected SD-OCT abnormalities were difficult to classify into the four categories described by Cho et al12 Additionally, the abnormalities we identified exhibited distinct characteristics and morphological patterns, prompting us to propose the five overarching morphological patterns described above.

Patients in the first category had atrophy of outer retinal layers, resulting in diffuse or outward disfiguration of the outer retinal layers, resembling a waterfall pattern. Outer retinal atrophy is a common structural finding in several retinal diseases and inherited retinal dystrophies. Although it has been associated with visual function in several conditions, its prognostic significance in cone dystrophy remains to be established.

Sergouniotis et al reviewed SD-OCT images of 12 patients with cone dystrophy with supernormal rod electroretinogram due to the KCNV2 gene.15 They found that four of their patients had lost all or nearly all of their outer retinal layers in the foveal region. They observed increased autofluorescence in three patients’ paracentral ring and one patient’s central fovea. The visual acuity of these patients was lower than the other eight patients included in the study. However, statistical analysis was not performed due to the small sample size of patients assessed.15 Mizobuchi et al reported nine patients with cone/cone-rod dystrophy who carried the GUCA1A mutation.16 They found diffuse outer retinal atrophy in four patients, one of whom had outward disfiguration of the retina as we described. Although the VA was lower in patients with atrophy and thinning of outer retinal layers compared to control, the sample size was small and statistical analysis was not performed.15,16

The second category, termed poorly delineated outer retinal bands, was characterized by reduced distinction of outer hyperreflective retinal bands with integration of some or all bands, giving the appearance of a hatched pattern. Hood et al hypothesized that a decrease in the EZ’s intensity is associated with achromatopsia and cone dystrophy.11

The third category we described included patients with scattered EZ disruption. Xu et al reported a cone dystrophy patient with longstanding bilateral vision loss and photophobia who had central loss of the inner segment of EZ.17 Similar findings were discovered in his sister and maternal aunt. In this case, the patient’s cone dystrophy was associated with the KCNV2 mutation. Additionally, his visual acuity remained stable over the two-year follow-up period. Manes et al reported a family carrying the GUCA1A mutation. From adolescence, one of the family members experienced vision loss and photophobia.18 Disease progression continued into adulthood. OCT imaging of that patient demonstrated ellipsoid zone loss with outer nuclear layer (ONL) thinning. Fundoscopic examination showed macular atrophy with granular deposits in the macular region.18 Eight of 12 patients reported by Sergouniotis et al had loss of EZ band in the foveal region, accompanied by a significant decrease in visual acuity.15

The fourth category was outer foveal defect, which was detected at the subfoveal segment of 14 patients. This finding was consistent with the fourth SD-OCT structural category described by Sundaram et al in patients with achromatopsia.19 Furthermore, Hirji et al reported 50 patients with achromatopsia who had retinas that remained structurally stable over a mean follow-up period of 61.6 months.15 However, two patients in this study had focal disruption of EZ that progressed to optically empty spaces in the outer retina.20 Leng et al described this structural abnormality as “foveal cavitation”.16 The authors also reported abnormalities in the desaturated color vision testing of all 8 patients.21

The final category was prominent outer retinal layers, which were found in 14 eyes in our study. Unlike the other four groups with varied degrees of attenuation, loss, disruption, and reduced reflectivity of outer retinal hyperreflective bands, this group manifested with distinct OCT features. The outer retinal layers are preserved and appear to be thickened and more hyperreflective. Duncan et al reported 4 patients with cone-rod dystrophy who had heterozygote point mutations in the peripherin/RDS gene.22 OCT imaging showed extensive loss of the outer nuclear, inner segment, and outer segment layers in the central macula. However, at the fovea, outer retinal structures were relatively well preserved and hyperreflective, but irregular structures were present in all patients’ outer segment-RPE junction layer, giving the appearance of outer retinal layer thickening. This hyperreflective layer had minimal abnormality in patient 1, who had better visual function than others despite having a cone-rod dystrophy phenotype based on full-field ERG testing, and microperimetry showed a 1 to 2 log unit reduction in sensitivity throughout the central 8°.22 In another case report of occult macular dystrophy, OCT imaging of outer retinal layers in the central area demonstrated local thickening. Multifocal ERG testing showed reduced amplitudes in the central ring of both eyes, with normal scotopic and photopic full-field ERG responses.23 However, in our patient, preserved and prominent hyperreflective outer retinal layers were more diffuse, distinct, and did not show disruption of adjacent outer retinal layers. To the best of our knowledge, this feature has not been previously reported in patients with cone dysfunction disorders. The biological basis of this finding remains uncertain. Although it may reflect altered photoreceptor development or organization, alternative explanations include compensatory retinal remodeling, persistence of disorganized outer-segment material, or qualitative differences related to OCT image acquisition parameters. Future studies incorporating age-matched healthy controls, quantitative OCT reflectivity analysis, adaptive optics imaging, and genetic characterization are needed to clarify the underlying mechanism.

In our study, we reported the following isolated OCT features: foveal hypoplasia, THD, OPL schisis, subretinal drusenoid-like deposits, and EZ bowing. While foveal hypoplasia is a common finding in achromatopsia, to our knowledge, the remaining four signs have not been reported.

THD was the most frequent isolated OCT sign in our study. Hyperreflective foci are nonspecific OCT findings that have been reported in numerous acquired and inherited retinal diseases. Proposed mechanisms include activated microglia or macrophages, migration of retinal pigment epithelial cells, and lipid-containing deposits, although their exact biological correlate likely varies among disease processes.24–26 In diabetic macular edema, retinal vein occlusion, and neovascular age-related macular degeneration, hyperreflective foci have been associated with disease activity and treatment response.27–29 However, the pathophysiological basis of THD in cone dysfunction disorders was not investigated in the present study and may differ substantially from that in acquired retinal diseases. Therefore, these comparisons are intended only to provide context rather than imply a shared underlying mechanism. Whether THD has similar clinical significance in cone dysfunction disorders remains unknown. Subretinal drusenoid-like deposits (SDLDs) were also observed in our cohort. Similar OCT findings have been described as subretinal drusenoid deposits in patients with age-related macular degeneration; however, because our observations were based solely on SD-OCT without multimodal imaging confirmation, we use the term “subretinal drusenoid-like deposits” descriptively and did not imply that these lesions are identical to AMD-associated subretinal drusenoid deposits. AMD-associated subretinal drusenoid deposits have been linked to a significantly increased risk of AMD progression, progressive outer retinal degeneration, and reduced retinal sensitivity on microperimetry testing.30 Severe rod dysfunction was also noted in retinal areas covered with pseudodrusen.31 Interestingly, THD and subretinal drusenoid-like deposits were both present in our cone dysfunction disorder cases; however, it is unknown whether the presence of these OCT signs influences the natural history and progression of cone dysfunction disorders.

OPL schisis was detected in 9 (11.0%) eyes in our study. It is a distinct OCT feature that may reflect anatomical variation. EZ bowing was another notable and distinct finding, featuring localized central displacement or bending of the EZ towards the ILM. It may be related to, but is morphologically distinct from, an outer foveal defect with absence of EZ. In our study, EZ bowing was present in 11 (13.4%) eyes. It may reflect altered cone photoreceptor organization.

In this study, the identified major morphological classifications were associated with patients’ ages. EZ disruption and prominent outer retina morphologies were more commonly seen in younger patients (1st and 2nd decades of life), and outer foveal defect was more commonly seen in older patients (3rd through 6th decades of life). Although the EZ disruption group had the lowest mean age and the outer foveal defect group had the highest mean age, these findings, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, cannot be interpreted as evidence of a sequential disease continuum. The observed association between age and morphological category reflects differences between patients rather than within-patient progression. It remains possible that these categories represent distinct phenotypic manifestations, different genetic subtypes, or different stages of disease, but distinguishing among these possibilities requires longitudinal follow-up with serial SD-OCT imaging and genetic characterization. In contrast, Cho et al reported no statistically significant difference between the age categories proposed in their study.12

According to the literature, different genetic variants of cone dystrophy each have distinct mechanisms, pathophysiology, and natural disease courses.1 Hence, it is possible that the various morphological patterns we have seen may be associated with distinct types of cone metabolism pathways and associated dysfunction. Unfortunately, there is currently no information available about the genetic status of the patients in these series.

In univariate and multivariate analyses, there was no correlation between BCVA and the different morphologies. However, morphological classification was associated with CRT; the outer foveal defect group had higher CRT values, and the outer retinal atrophy group had lower CRT values compared with other groups. In contrast to our findings, Cho et al reported that in cases with retinal abnormalities detected using OCT, the CRT and ONL were thinner and the RPE was thicker compared to cases without detectable abnormalities (category 0).12 Additionally, they reported a significant correlation between CRT and BCVA in cone dystrophy patients, whereas no such association was identified in our cohort. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. Our study included only patients with severely reduced or non-recordable cone responses and relatively preserved rod function on full-field ERG, whereas Cho et al included patients with a broader spectrum of cone dysfunction. Moreover, as a tertiary referral center, our cohort may have included patients with more advanced or heterogeneous diseases. Ultimately, differences in SD-OCT acquisition protocols, CRT measurement methodology, and disease-stage distribution between the two studies may also have contributed to the differing results.

The proposed SD-OCT morphological classification may have several potential clinical applications. It provides a standardized framework for describing structural retinal changes in patients with cone dysfunction disorders, which may improve consistency in clinical documentation and facilitate comparisons across studies. When interpreted alongside clinical findings, electrophysiological testing, and genetic data, this classification may also support future genotype-phenotype correlation studies and improve understanding of disease heterogeneity. Although no prognostic conclusions can be drawn from this cross-sectional study, a standardized morphological classification may prove useful for longitudinal monitoring of disease progression and for stratifying patients according to structural phenotype in future investigations. In addition, this framework may help establish imaging-based eligibility criteria and outcome measures for natural history studies and interventional clinical trials. These potential applications require validation in prospective, longitudinal cohorts with comprehensive genetic characterization before implementation in routine clinical practice.

One limitation of this study is that patient inclusion was based primarily on electrophysiological findings without routine genetic confirmation, as genetic testing was not routinely available in this cohort during the study period. Although this approach excluded disorders such as Stargardt disease, which typically do not demonstrate the characteristic electrophysiological pattern of severe cone dysfunction with preserved rod function, inherited retinal disorders with overlapping electrophysiological features, such as achromatopsia, could not be definitively distinguished from cone dysfunction disorders. Consequently, genetic heterogeneity among patients may have influenced the observed SD-OCT morphological patterns, and genotype–phenotype correlations could not be assessed. It should also be noted that our study’s sample size of 82 eyes (42 patients) may still have been insufficient to detect differences statistically, although it was larger than those of previous studies. In addition, the proposed “prominent outer retinal layers” category was based on qualitative SD-OCT assessment. Although this finding was independently identified by two masked retinal specialists and confirmed by consensus, quantitative reflectivity analysis was not performed. Therefore, it remains possible that image acquisition parameters, such as signal strength or gain settings, may have influenced the observed appearance. Moreover, quantitative structural biomarkers such as ellipsoid zone width, outer nuclear layer thickness, and choroidal thickness were not evaluated and should be incorporated. The other limitation of our study was its retrospective design and lack of longitudinal follow-up data. Because many patients were referred to our tertiary center primarily for diagnostic evaluation or a second opinion, follow-up examinations were not consistently available. Consequently, we were unable to assess the rate of visual acuity decline, the longitudinal evolution of retinal structural changes, or symptom duration. In addition, because this was a single-center tertiary referral study, referral bias may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader cone dysfunction disorders population. The absence of an age-matched healthy control group precluded direct comparison of OCT morphology with normal retinal architecture. Although all SD-OCT images were independently reviewed by two masked retinal specialists and discrepancies were resolved by consensus, the proposed morphological classification remains preliminary and should be validated in future studies using formal reproducibility analyses. Of note, a separate inter-eye analysis was not performed because GEE accounted for the inter-eye correlation between fellow eyes. Future prospective studies with long-term follow-up are needed to better characterize disease progression and the relationship between structural changes and visual function in cone dysfunction disorders.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that SD-OCT reveals diverse outer retinal morphological patterns in patients with cone dysfunction disorders, which are associated with age and central retinal thickness but not BCVA. The proposed morphological classification provides a standardized framework for characterizing structural retinal changes and may facilitate future genotype-phenotype studies, longitudinal disease monitoring, and clinical research. Prospective, longitudinal studies incorporating comprehensive genetic characterization and quantitative imaging biomarkers are needed to validate the clinical utility of this classification and determine its potential role in disease stratification and future therapeutic trials.
