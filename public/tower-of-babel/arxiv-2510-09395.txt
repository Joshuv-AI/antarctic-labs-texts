Dark matter production from evaporation of regular primordial black holes
Ngo Phuc Duc Loc

Abstract
We study dark matter (DM) production from evaporation of regular primordial black holes (RPBHs). While there are many available metrics that describe singularity-free RPBHs, we focus on the Hayward metric and the Simpson-Visser metric for illustrative purpose. The framework can be applied straightforwardly to other metrics. RPBHs generally have different Hawking temperature and therefore different lifetime from the standard singular black holes. The resulting modified cosmological constraints for this DM production mechanism and the allowed parameter space to obtain the correct DM abundance are calculated.

II
Evaporation of regular black holes

We consider a class of static, spherically symmetric metrics of the following form:

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{g(r)}+h(r)d\Omega^{2},

(1)

where
d\Omega^{2}
is the usual 2-sphere metric. The metric should be asymptotically flat, meaning that
\{f(r),g(r)\}\xrightarrow{r\rightarrow\infty}1
and
h(r)\xrightarrow{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{2}
. As concrete examples, we choose to work with the Hayward metric
Hayward:2005gi
and the Simpson-Visser metric
Simpson:2018tsi
:

\text{Hayward:}\begin{cases}f_{\text{Hay}}(r)=g_{\text{Hay}}(r)=1-\frac{2Mr^{2}}{r^{3}+2Ml^{2}}\\
h_{\text{Hay}}(r)=r^{2}\end{cases},

(2)

\text{Simpson-Visser:}\begin{cases}f_{\text{SV}}(r)=g_{\text{SV}}(r)=1-\frac{2M}{\sqrt{r^{2}+l^{2}}}\\
h_{\text{SV}}(r)=r^{2}+l^{2}\end{cases}.

(3)

Here,
M>0
can be interpreted as the black hole’s mass and
l>0
is a fundamental length scale known as “regularizing parameter” which ensures the singularity disappear. One can indeed check that the curvature invariants are finite at
r=0
. In the limit
l\rightarrow 0
, the above regular metrics reduce to the singular Schwarzschild metric. It can also be seen from these metrics that there exists a maximum value of
l/M
above which the horizon does not exist:
l/M=\sqrt{16/27}
for Hayward metric and
l/M=2
for Simpson-Visser metric.

We chose the Hayward metric as it is well-known as one of the earliest RBH solutions and is usually used as a good benchmark. However, as mentioned in the introduction, a more rigorous definition of a singularity-free metric is that it must be geodesically complete, which means that the null/timelike geodesics can be analytically extended beyond
r=0
. Although the curvature invariants of the Hayward metric is everywhere finite, the geodesic is incomplete in this spacetime (at least in the original form)
Zhou:2022yio
. This motivates us to also consider another minimal extension of the Schwarzschild solution which is the Simpson-Visser metric. The curvature invariants of this metric are finite and the geodesic is complete.

Just like singular black holes, RBHs also have event horizon and evaporate via Hawking radiation
Hawking:1974rv
;
Hawking:1975vcx
with the temperature defined as
Gibbons:1977mu
:

T_{\text{H}}=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}=\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g(r)}{f(r)}}\Bigg|_{r=r_{\text{H}}},

(4)

where
\kappa
is the surface gravity, the prime denotes derivative with respect to
r
,
r_{\text{H}}
is the radius of the outer event horizon and is identified as the largest root of the equation
f(r)=0
. As different metrics will lead to different Hawking temperature and horizon size, it is useful to define the ratios of these quantities to that of the Schwarzschild black hole:

A(l)\equiv\frac{T_{\text{H}}}{T_{\text{H,Sch}}}\hskip 28.45274pt;\hskip 28.45274ptB(l)\equiv\frac{r_{\text{H}}}{r_{\text{H,Sch}}}.

(5)

The quantities with the extra subscript “Sch” are associated with the Schwarzschild metric and are given by:

T_{\text{H,Sch}}=\frac{m_{\text{pl}}^{2}}{8\pi M}\hskip 28.45274pt;\hskip 28.45274ptr_{\text{H,Sch}}=2GM=\frac{2M}{m_{\text{pl}}^{2}},

(6)

where
m_{\text{pl}}=1.22\times 10^{19}\text{ GeV}=2.18\times 10^{-5}\text{ g}
is the Planck mass.

We have an important remark. The coefficients
A(l)
and
B(l)
are actually functions of
l/M
, but
M
did not appear inside the parentheses as it was implicitly understood to be in units of
M
for a reason that we now explain. There are two approaches when it comes to choosing the regularizing parameter
l
:

•

The first approach is that one can fix
l
and let
M
vary. This way will lead to stable remnants because, as
M
decreases due to evaporation, the ratio
l/M
will increase and eventually reach the extremal value to stop evaporation completely (for example, see Ref.
Davies:2024ysj
or the upper left panel in Fig.
2
below). However, this approach will also violate the standard self-similarity assumption of BH evaporation (i.e., quantities like
T_{\text{H}}M
and
r_{\text{H}}/M
would be time-dependent)
3
3

3

There is a very nice analogy coming from the concept of fractal geometry in mathematics. In fractal geometry, the geometrical pattern at large scale repeats itself at arbitrarily small scales. This is also known as “self-similarity”.
. We call this non-self-similar RBHs. The Hawking temperature of non-self-similar RBHs reaches a maximum value and then drops to zero (see Fig.
1
). We do not follow this approach here. The violation of self-similarity was also criticized in
Khodadi:2025icd
.

•

The second approach that we propose in this paper is that we instead fix the ratio
l/M
. The BH will evaporate more slowly but never actually stop evaporation completely and there is no remnants. This proposal will preserve the standard self-similarity assumption of BH evaporation (i.e., quantities like
T_{\text{H}}M
and
r_{\text{H}}/M
would be constant). This is a standard behavior that we do see in singular black hole as well. We call this self-similar RBHs. The Hawking temperature of self-similar RBHs has a similar behavior to that of the Schwarzschild BH but the scale is adjusted by the choice of the ratio
l/M
(see Fig.
1
). We choose this approach in our paper because our goal is to consider DM as particles, not black hole’s remnants.

III
Dark matter production from evaporation of regular primordial black holes

For the scenario of DM production from evaporation of RPBHs, it is important to determine if there was RPBH domination at early times. As the population of RPBHs dilutes slower than radiation, they may eventually dominate the Universe. In a RD Universe, the fraction of RPBH at the formation time is defined as
\beta\equiv\rho_{\text{RPBH}}/\rho_{\text{rad}}\propto a\propto T^{-1}
. However, there will be no RPBH domination if RPBHs evaporate before the matter-radiation equality time:
t_{\text{eva}}<t_{\text{eq}}\Rightarrow H_{\text{eva}}>H_{\text{eq}}\Rightarrow T_{\text{eva}}>T_{\text{eq}}=\beta T_{i}
. Therefore, the critical population below which there is no RPBH domination is:

\displaystyle\beta_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{\text{eva}}}{T_{i}}

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{*,\text{eva}}^{1/4}\ g_{*,i}^{1/4}m_{\text{pl}}A(l)^{2}B(l)}{32\sqrt{5\pi}M_{i}}

(33)

\displaystyle=1.78\times 10^{-6}A(l)^{2}B(l)\left(\frac{g_{*,\text{eva}}}{106.75}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{g_{*,\text{in}}}{106.75}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{\text{g}}{M_{i}}\right),

(34)

where we used Eqs.

IV
Cosmological constraints

In this section, we show the main cosmological constraints of our scenario.

IV.1
Inflation constraint

In principle, the minimum mass of RPBH can be the Planck mass of order
10^{-5}\text{ g}
as it is the quantum gravity limit below which no classical black hole solution is expected to exist. However, if RPBH forms from inflationary perturbations, the observed upper bound on the energy scale of inflation can be translated into a stronger lower bound on the RPBH mass as follows. The power spectrum of scalar perturbation
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}
and tensor perturbation
\mathcal{P}_{h}
are defined as
Baumann:2022mni
:

\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}(k)\equiv A_{s}\left(\frac{k}{k_{*}}\right)^{n_{s}-1}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{H^{2}}{M_{\text{pl}}^{2}}\Bigg|_{k=aH},

(49)

\mathcal{P}_{h}(k)\equiv A_{t}\left(\frac{k}{k_{*}}\right)^{n_{t}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\frac{H^{2}}{M_{\text{pl}}^{2}}\Bigg|_{k=aH},

(50)

where
\varepsilon
is the first slow-roll parameter,
M_{\text{pl}}
is the reduced Planck mass,
k_{*}
is the pivot reference scale,
(A_{s},A_{t})
and
(n_{s},n_{t})
are the amplitude and tilt of the (scalar,tensor) perturbations respectively. The tensor-to-scalar ratio is defined as
r\equiv A_{t}/A_{s}
which, at the observed CMB scale, leads to:

r\Bigg|_{k=k_{*}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\frac{H_{\text{inf}}^{2}}{M_{\text{pl}}^{2}}\frac{1}{A_{s}}\Bigg|_{k=k_{*}}.

(51)

At
k_{*}=0.05\text{ Mpc}^{-1}
, we have
r<0.1
and
A_{s}\simeq 2.1\times 10^{-9}

Planck:2018jri
. Using
M_{\text{pl}}\simeq 2.43\times 10^{18}
GeV, the upper bound on the energy scale of inflation is

\left(\frac{H_{\text{inf}}}{\text{GeV}}\right)<7.82\times 10^{13}.

(52)

By using the standard Friedmann equation for a RD Universe, this can also be given in terms of the reheating temeperature as:

\left(\frac{T_{\text{reh}}}{\text{GeV}}\right)<7.45\times 10^{15}.

(53)

Eq.
8
then gives a lower bound on the RPBH mass:

\left(\frac{M_{i}}{\text{g}}\right)>1.7

(54)

IV.2
BBN constraint

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) is the moment when temperature is low enough for the formation of light elements’ nuclei. This happens at around 1 second or
T\sim O(1\text{ MeV})

Baumann:2022mni
. From the observation of light elements’ abundances, we know very well the amount of radiation available during this period. Therefore, RPBH must evaporate before BBN to be consistent with observations. From Eq.
20
, the condition
\tau_{\text{RPBH}}<1\text{ sec}
gives an upper bound on the RPBH mass:

\left(\frac{M_{i}}{\text{g}}\right)<8.6\times 10^{8}A(l)^{4/3}B(l)^{2/3}.

(55)

10^{9}\text{ g}

IV.3
Warm dark matter constraint

DM produced from evaporation of RPBHs may have large kinetic energy (warm DM) that could erase the observed small-scale structures such as Lyman-
\alpha
forest or Milky Way satellite. Therefore, we need to put constraint on RPBH such that the DM produced has enough time to lose its momentum due to cosmological redshift. The momentum of DM today is

p_{0}=m_{\chi}v_{0}=p_{\text{eva}}\left(\frac{a_{\text{eva}}}{a_{0}}\right)\simeq T_{\text{H,in}}\left(\frac{a_{\text{eva}}}{a_{0}}\right)=T_{\text{H,in}}\left(\frac{g_{s,0}}{g_{s,\text{eva}}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{\text{eva}}}\right),

(56)

where the average initial momentum of evaporated DM was taken to be the initial Hawking temperature of RPBH. The smallest scale that could be probed is of order
k\sim 10\text{ Mpc}^{-1}
, which corresponds to the photon temperature of order
T_{\gamma}\sim 1\text{ keV}

Lin:2019uvt
. Therefore, thermally produced warm DM must be heavier than 1 keV in order to be non-relativistic by that time. Indeed, it was found in
Villasenor:2022aiy
that thermally produced warm DM must be heavier than 3.5 keV. As entropy is conserved from the decoupling time to now, this lower bound on DM mass can be translated into a universal upper bound on current DM velocity for a generic warm DM candidate
Masina:2020xhk
:

v_{0}=a_{\text{dec}}=\left(\frac{g_{s,0}}{g_{s,\text{dec}}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{\text{dec}}}\right)=\left(\frac{g_{s,0}}{g_{s,\text{dec}}}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{m_{\chi}}\right),

(57)

where
g_{s,0}=3.94
and
g_{s,\text{dec}}\sim 106.75
have been used. So that
m_{\chi}>3.5\text{ keV}
gives
v_{0}<2.19\times 10^{-8}
. By using Eqs.
56
,
5
, and
23
, we obtain an upper bound RPBH mass:

\left(\frac{M_{i}}{\text{g}}\right)\lesssim 2.18\times 10^{5}A(l)^{2}B(l)^{2}\left(\frac{m_{\chi}}{\text{GeV}}\right)^{2}.

(58)

For RPBHs, the constraint is shifted towards lower mass because the RPBHs that we consider have lower temperature and therefore longer lifetime, which means that DM could be produced too late and does not have enough time to become cold enough at the relevant observed small scales.

We first explain the physics behind the parameter space for singular Schwarzschild PBH:

•

The region below the critical line
\beta=\beta_{c}
is where there is no PBH domination because the population is too small. Heavy PBHs live longer so that the required population to have PBH domination gets smaller.

•

The decreasing portion of the solid colored contours describes the case when the initial PBH mass is small enough such that its initial Hawking temperature is greater than DM mass:
T_{\text{H,in}}>m_{\chi}
(see Eq.
39
). In this case, DM production occurs throughout the PBH’s lifetime. More PBH mass leads to more number of DM particles produced per PBH (see Eq.
28
). Thus, PBH population must be reduced to compensate to obtain a fixed observed DM abundance.

•

When the PBH mass is large enough such that
T_{\text{H,in}}<m_{\chi}
, the solid colored contours begin to have an increasing behavior (see Eq.
40
). In this case, DM production occurs at the late stage of PBH evaporation. The number of DM particles produced is independent from PBH mass (see Eq.
31
). The DM abundance is instead controlled by PBH number density (see Eq.
38
). Thus, increasing PBH mass needs increasing PBH energy density to get the same DM abundance.

•

The region above the solid black line is where the population is large enough to have a PBH domination period. The colored contours begin to turn vertical in this region as the DM abundance is independent from PBH population (see Eq.
46
). The origin of this independence comes from the fact PBHs dominated the Universe such that their energy density at the evaporation time controls the Hubble rate at that time, which in turn can be gauged by the decay rate of PBHs that is solely determined by PBHs’ mass (see Eq.
41
and follow-up equations).

For RPBHs, the main physics is the same but the parameter space is shifted. The crucial point to understand these shifts is that a small-mass RPBH would be equivalent to a large-mass singular PBH due to the modification of the Hawking temperature. With that in mind, we can explain the shifts as follows. The critical line
\beta=\beta_{c}
is shifted toward smaller population because, for a given mass, RPBH lives longer than singular PBH so that the population must be smaller in order to not have RPBH domination. Similarly, the decreasing portion of the colored contour is shifted toward smaller population because RPBH lives longer to produce DM so that the population must be smaller to compensate. As a small-mass RPBH would have a similar temperature to a large-mass singular PBH, the turning point at which
T_{\text{H,in}}=m_{\chi}
must be shifted toward smaller RPBH mass. The increasing portion of colored contours is shifted toward larger population because RPBHs have lower temperature and produce less number of DM particles per black hole, so that their population must be enhanced to compensate. Finally, the vertical portion of the colored contours is shifted toward smaller mass since small-mass RPBH is equivalent to large-mass singular PBH. The BBN and warm DM constraints are also shifted toward smaller mass for the same reason (see Sec.
IV
).

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a scenario in which particle DM is produced from evaporation of RPBHs. Assuming self-similarity, RPBHs do not form remnants but evaporate slowly toward zero mass. This proposal is compelling because it assumes the particle nature of DM, which could be tested more easily via direct detection experiments on earth, and it also addresses the singularity problem of black holes while maintaining the standard self-similar evaporation process. We illustrated the idea with some benchmark RBH solutions including the Hayward and the Simpson-Visser metrics to show how the resulting allowed parameter space is modified. Our generic framework can be applied to any other static, spherically symmetric RPBH metric by using the parameterization given in Eq.
5
, together with the analytical formulae for DM abundance in Eqs.
39
,
40
,
47
, and
48
as well as the cosmological constraints in Eqs.
54
,
55
, and
58
. It is also interesting to explore potential observational channels to test our idea such as gravitational waves in future works.
