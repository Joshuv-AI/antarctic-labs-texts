Trend term and noise removal method for blasting vibration signals based on Fourier decomposition
Authors: Yan Zhao, Lujun Yin, Wenzhe Zhang, Jiaxing Tao, He Wang, Ziyi Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe trend term and noise in blasting vibration signals severely affect the accuracy of analysis. However, existing adaptive decomposition methods still have limitations in removing these trend and noise components. This study develops a Fourier decomposition–based (FDM) preprocessing approach for blasting vibration signals by optimizing key parameters, including the cross-correlation threshold, the ultra-low-frequency energy threshold, and the decomposition search path. A comparison between the High-to-Low Frequency Searching (HTL-FS) and Low-to-High Frequency Searching (LTH-FS) search paths indicates that LTH-FS achieves superior decomposition performance when applied to blasting vibration signals. The sensitivity of FDM to different ultra-low-frequency energy thresholds is systematically examined, and a criterion for identifying valid components is established. The results demonstrate that FDM can effectively separate low-frequency trend components, high-frequency noise, and meaningful signal information in blasting vibration records. Compared with Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition with Adaptive Noise (CEEMDAN), and Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD), the pure signal obtained via FDM exhibits the smoothest time-history curve, the highest signal-to-noise ratio (23.2406), and the lowest root-mean-square error (0.0246). At the same time, this paper further confirms the effectiveness of FDM as a high-precision preprocessing method for blasting signals by analyzing the blasting vibration signals in two actual cases: the Chongli Tunnel blasting project and the Caomao Mountain Tunnel blasting project.Keywords: Blasting signal processing, Fourier decomposition, Trend term, Noise removalSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

During blasting operations, the resulting vibration signals inevitably induce dynamic responses in the surrounding environment and nearby structures. Therefore, controlling vibration hazards generated by blasting is particularly important. The analysis and processing of measured blasting vibration signals is one of the key approaches for investigating the characteristics of blasting-induced vibration responses. As a short-duration, impact-type non-stationary nonlinear signal, a blasting vibration record may contain a trend term due to factors such as instrument installation and environmental conditions. Meanwhile, because of the drilling-and-blasting construction process, the original signal may be contaminated by high-frequency noise. In subsequent signal processing, the presence of trend terms, noise, and other interference components will directly affect the accuracy of extracting detailed signal features.

Therefore, removing trend terms and noise interference from blasting vibration signals has become a research hotspot in this field. Li et al.1 processed measured blasting signals using EEMD and wavelet analysis to remove trend terms and noise. However, the accuracy of wavelet analysis is influenced by subjective choices—such as the basis function type, thresholding criterion, and number of decomposition levels—leading to limited processing accuracy. Based on Fourier decomposition2,3, reconstructed the dominant signal components and suppressed high-frequency content in blasting signals using wavelet packet analysis. Fu et al.4 combined CEEMD with sparse baseline estimation to effectively address baseline drift in blasting signals. However, CEEMD does not fundamentally resolve mode mixing during signal decomposition, and their study did not elaborate on the different manifestations of blasting signals under the LTH-FS and HTL-FS conditions. Fei et al.5 proposed a denoising method that combines EEMD with wavelet thresholding. By decomposing the signal into multiscale modal components and then distinguishing noise characteristics via wavelet thresholding, the denoising accuracy is improved. However, the aforementioned methods have not fundamentally solved the modal aliasing problem, and they lack a systematic exploration of the manifestations of blasting signals under different decomposition paths, making it difficult to achieve precise separation of trend components and noise.

Fourier decomposition is a signal processing method based on frequency-domain adaptive filtering, first proposed by Singh et al.6. It was developed to address the limitations of the traditional Fourier transform in handling nonlinear and non-stationary signals, and it has been widely applied in areas such as blasting vibration analysis and mechanical fault diagnosis. Building on this theory, Brincker et al.7 introduced FDM into engineering vibration analysis and proposed a modal-parameter identification method based on the power spectrum matrix. Wang et al.8 systematically compared the performance of three adaptive decomposition methods (FDM, VMD, and EMD) in blasting vibration signal processing and established an evaluation framework for this application. Chen et al.9 combined energy entropy to identify the dominant frequency of blasting signals and used an FDM–energy entropy threshold method to reduce the identification error to within 5%. Zhang et al.10 applied dynamic-bandwidth FDM to blasting signal processing and achieved strong analytical results. Overall, FDM can reduce processing errors and effectively lower the computational cost of blasting signal analysis. Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes an FDM-based preprocessing method for blasting vibration signals, aiming to remove interference information from measured blasting signals that include trend terms and high-frequency noise.

Compared with the time–frequency localization trade-off and the mode-mixing problem in traditional blasting signal preprocessing methods, FDM-based preprocessing offers several advantages. First, FDM provides stronger frequency-domain adaptive decomposition capability: it can dynamically adjust the frequency-band range and decompose the signal into multiple narrowband mode components, thereby enabling accurate band separation. By comparison, EMD also has adaptive decomposition capability, but its bandwidth is essentially determined by the spacing between signal extrema and cannot be dynamically adjusted according to signal characteristics. VMD requires predefining an appropriate number of modes K and the bandwidth-constrained parameter a11. Wavelet transforms rely on fixed basis functions and a predefined framework for band partitioning, and thus cannot adapt dynamically to changes in signal characteristics. Second, under intermittent signals or noisy backgrounds, the physical meaning of EMD decomposition results can be ambiguous, and severe mode mixing may occur12. EEMD cannot eliminate noise-induced mixing simply by ensemble averaging with added noise and may lead to substantial computational cost. VMD can effectively suppress mode mixing only when its parameters are appropriately selected13. In contrast, FDM achieves mode separation via SVD, thereby avoiding mode mixing common in traditional methods14. With frequency-band isolation, it prevents different frequency components from being erroneously merged and significantly improves component purity. Moreover, the core operation of FDM is based on the fast Fourier transform (FFT), which reduces the computational complexity and overall computation to some extent, making it more suitable for blasting signal preprocessing. Finally, FDM also exhibits strong robustness in noise suppression. It can adaptively separate noise from effective components using the intrinsic time–frequency structure of the signal, showing greater stability and effectiveness under different noise types. Compared with EMD, FDM can fundamentally avoid noise propagation caused by mode mixing. Compared with VMD, FDM is more tolerant to parameter mismatch when noise characteristics are unknown, because its “decompose first, then determine” workflow is inherently more robust to such mismatches.

By representing a signal as a superposition of multiple analytical fourier eigenband functions, FDM provides deeper insight into signal analysis. In particular, in the fields of blasting vibration analysis and communication systems, FDM can more accurately extract key information from measured signals. Moreover, its adaptive characteristics can further improve time–frequency analysis accuracy. Meanwhile, FDM can rapidly separate meaningful components, trend terms, and noise from the original signal, thereby effectively reducing mode mixing.

The study (Zhong et al.38) indicates that, when searching for AFIBFs using FDM, either the HTL-FS algorithm or the LTH-FS algorithm can be adopted. Among them, LTH-FS performs better in trend extraction and preservation of effective signal components. Specifically, LTH-FS first identifies and separates the ultra-low-frequency components of the signal and then, on this basis, progressively constructs and isolates higher-frequency oscillatory modes. This strategy effectively avoids the drawback of HTL-FS, in which high-frequency errors are propagated and amplified into low-frequency trend terms, thereby yielding a purer and more reliable trend-term estimate. Therefore, compared with HTL-FS, LTH-FS provides a stronger theoretical basis and more reliable results.

The study2,3 indicates that, based on energy-distribution characteristics16,17, empirical statistical patterns, and noise-suppression requirements, a modal component is typically regarded as noise or a trend term when its cross-correlation coefficient with the original signal is less than 0.1. From the perspective of energy distribution, blasting signals are characterized by abrupt high energy, and their energy amplitude is often significantly higher than that of background noise. In addition, numerous studies (Cao et al.30) have shown that the normalized amplitudes of the principal components of blasting signals are mostly distributed within [0.1, 1.0].

Based on the practical needs of noise suppression in blasting signal processing, the cross-correlation coefficient threshold is set to 0.1, which can effectively distinguish the effective components (> 0.1) and the noise components (< 0.1) contained in the signal. The following provides a detailed explanation of the rationale for choosing 0.1 as the treatment threshold.

First, experimental analyses of a large number of blasting signals from different engineering scenarios (e.g., mine blasting, demolition blasting, and military blasting)18 show that 89.3% of the effective signal components have normalized amplitudes greater than 0.1, whereas only 2.1% of the noise components exceed 0.1. Under a threshold of 0.1, the fidelity of signal reconstruction can exceed 92.7%.

Secondly, from the perspective of the physical meaning of the threshold, a threshold of 0.1 often corresponds to a noise suppression level of − 20 dB and significant events other than 3σ (the symbol σ represents the standard deviation) (assuming Gaussian noise). In addition, engineering practice shows that a threshold of 0.1 is often the effective detection limit of most blasting monitoring equipment (GB 672233).

Meanwhile, to demonstrate the rationality of the 0.1 threshold in practical blasting signal decomposition, we selected five commonly used threshold values (0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25) for comparative analysis. The suitability of the 0.10 threshold was evaluated using four criteria: signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), root-mean-square error (RMSE), signal reconstruction rate, and computational efficiency.

As shown in Table 1, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) reaches its peak within the threshold range of [0.1, 0.15]. If the threshold is set too low, noise in the effective signal cannot be completely separated; if it is set too high, the effective signal will be lost.

If the trend-term amplitude is large, it may produce an artificially high correlation coefficient, thereby affecting the accuracy of the classification. Fortunately, the trend term can also be identified based on the proportion of energy in the ultra-low-frequency band19. Using measured vibration signals as the research object, FDM is employed to obtain the correlation coefficient R(i) between each component and the original signal, as well as the corresponding proportion of energy in the ultra-low-frequency band. These indicators are then used to distinguish trend components from noise components. Finally, the FDM iteration is terminated once the following conditions are satisfied.

(1) Artificially setting the residual energy threshold

By calculating the ratio of the energy of the current residual signal to the initial energy of the original signal, a percentage threshold ε (Boudraa et al. 2007) is set artificially. When the energy ratio is less than or equal to the set threshold ε, the FDM iteration is stopped. This ensures that after passing through FDM, the residual energy of the original signal reaches a level that can be ignored, and it can be considered that the original signal has fully extracted the main components of the signal through FDM.

(2) Set the maximum number of components

Before applying FDM to the original signal, it is necessary to preset the maximum number of components allowed in the iterative decomposition. The iteration is terminated when the number of decomposed components reaches this preset maximum.

(3) Set the maximum number of iterations

By manually specifying a maximum number of iterations, the validity and controllability of the algorithm’s final results can be ensured. During FDM of the original signal, once the preset maximum number of iterations is reached, the iteration is forcibly terminated to prevent the algorithm from entering an infinite loop when other stopping conditions are not satisfied.

As shown in Fig. 1, the FDM framework completes the entire workflow from the original signal to final component separation. First, the original signal x(t) is subjected to a frequency-slice wavelet transform to obtain a two-dimensional time–frequency distribution matrix TFR(t,f). Next, SVD is applied as a preprocessing step to denoise and enhance the time–frequency matrix, thereby improving the clarity of mode separation. Subsequently, following the LTH-FS search strategy, each frequency slice is reconstructed into AFIBFs along the selected search path. The resulting AFIBFs components are then screened based on their physical characteristics to distinguish valid signal components from noise and trend terms. Finally, the selected valid AFIBFs are used to reconstruct the signal.

As shown in Table 5, the decomposition focus of FDM mainly includes the frequency slicing function, the slicing step size, and the setting of termination conditions. The frequency slicing function uses a Gaussian window function with a standard deviation σ of 0.3. If the standard deviation is too small, it will lead to insufficient frequency resolution; if it is too large, it will cause blurred time localization, affecting the precise capture of blasting impact signals. The frequency slicing step size is determined based on the main frequency range of the blasting signal, and a step size of Δf = 2 Hz can achieve a balance between accuracy and efficiency12. If the step size is too small, it will increase the computational burden, while a step size that is too large may miss critical frequencies. Finally, the decomposition termination condition is set so that the residual energy is less than 0.1% of the total energy of the original signal. This threshold ensures that the trend component is fully extracted while avoiding excessive decomposition that produces redundant components.

Blasting vibration signals usually have strong impact and transient characteristics, causing the low-frequency components of the signal to usually concentrate in the very low-frequency band. The trend components in the signal typically appear as relatively smooth, long-term varying elements, and their frequency characteristics are often close to 0 Hz. In this case, selecting a 0–1 Hz frequency band can effectively distinguish and extract the trend components in the signal while avoiding the inadvertent removal of low-frequency vibration components.

In addition, to avoid mistakenly identifying valid signals with a main frequency below 0.5 Hz as trend components, the range of 0–1 Hz is set as the ultra-low frequency band. Studies have shown that the main energy of blasting vibration signals is concentrated between 5 and 100 Hz, while components with frequencies below 5 Hz are mostly noise or baseline drift. Therefore, setting the 0–1 Hz band as the ultra-low frequency band can effectively separate noise and baseline drift while retaining the true trend components in the signal.

In summary, it can be observed that, from a physical definition, the trend component is the slowest-changing part of a signal, with a frequency infinitely close to 0 Hz. At the same time, to avoid mistakenly removing potentially physically meaningful ultra-low-frequency vibrations (such as 2–5 Hz), the 0–1 Hz frequency band is therefore chosen as the very low-frequency band.

Trend term and noise removal effect evaluation

In order to compare and analyze the effect of this method on removing trend terms and noise, we use EMD, CEEMDAN, and VMD to process the same signal respectively.

The blasting vibration meter used in this study was a TC-4850N, with a minimum measurable frequency of 5 Hz.

In Case I, although the dominant frequency of component C1 was 2 Hz—well below the instrument’s minimum measurable frequency—this alone is not sufficient to determine whether it is a trend term. This is because the dominant frequency of blasting vibrations is influenced by factors such as the charge quantity, charging method, distance from the blast center, and the properties of the rock–soil medium26,27. As a result, the dominant frequency varies dynamically and may indeed fall below 5 Hz.

In Case I, although C1 has a cross-correlation coefficient of 0.9336 with the original signal, indicating strong correlation, it is still necessary to determine whether a component is a trend term based on its physical nature. As shown in Fig. 7, the time-domain waveform of C1 is non-oscillatory and does not exhibit clear periodic fluctuations, which is the key physical basis for identifying a trend term. The high cross-correlation coefficient (0.9336) in this case instead reflects that the trend term has a large amplitude and varies slowly, representing substantial interference rather than evidence that it is an effective vibration component. In summary, C1 can be preliminarily regarded as a trend term that should be removed. In Case II, the cross-correlation coefficient of C1 is 0.1437, which does not indicate a good correlation. From this, we can infer that C1 contains interfering components. In summary, it can be preliminarily considered that C1 is a trend term that needs to be removed.

In addition, the proportion of energy in the ultra-low-frequency band can also be used to identify trend terms, so only five low-frequency components are listed here.

Since a trend term is physically defined as the slowest-varying component with the longest period in a signal, its energy in the frequency domain should be highly concentrated in the ultra-low-frequency band. Numerous studies28 have shown that, under an energy-threshold–based criterion for automatic trend-term identification, if the proportion of a component’s energy within the ultra-low-frequency band exceeds a specified threshold, the component’s energy can be considered highly concentrated in the band where the trend term resides, and the component can therefore be identified as a trend-term component.

As mentioned earlier, we calculated the energy proportion in the ultra-low-frequency band for each component. The results of case I are listed in Table 18. It can be observed that, with the ultra-low-frequency energy threshold set to 80%, only C1 reaches 80.93%, exceeding the preset threshold. This indicates that 80.93% of C1’s energy is concentrated in the ultra-low-frequency band, i.e., its energy is highly concentrated in the ultra-low-frequency range. Therefore, C1 can be identified not as an analytic Fourier eigenband function with pronounced oscillatory characteristics, but as a distinct trend component that reflects baseline drift or a long-term trend in the signal. Accordingly, based on this criterion, C1 is removed from the original signal as a trend component, whereas C2, C3, C4, and C5 are retained as effective components. The results of case II are listed in Table 19. It can be observed that under the condition where the energy proportion threshold in the ultra-low frequency band is set to 80%, only C1 has an energy proportion of 83.76% in the ultra-low frequency band, exceeding the preset threshold. This indicates that 83.76% of C1’s energy is concentrated in the ultra-low frequency band, meaning its energy is highly concentrated in the ultra-low frequency range. Therefore, it can be demonstrated that C1 does not belong to a Fourier intrinsic band function with a clear oscillatory characteristic, but is instead a distinct trend component representing signal baseline drift or long-term trends. Based on this criterion, C1 should be removed from the original signal as a trend component.

To further verify the effect of FDM on the preprocessing of blasting vibration signals, a spectral analysis of the blasting signals before and after preprocessing was conducted.

As shown in Fig. 12(a), the original blasting vibration signal exhibits a markedly amplified ultra-low-frequency component near 2 Hz in the spectrum. This component primarily arises from trends such as sensor baseline drift and gradual system response, rather than the dominant frequency of structural vibration induced by blasting. Moreover, this trend increases the energy level in the ultra-low-frequency band. If the original spectrum is interpreted directly, the 2 Hz component could easily be mistaken for the dominant frequency of the blasting vibration, which contradicts the generally accepted engineering frequency range of 5–100 Hz. As shown in Fig. 12(b), after FDM preprocessing, the dominant-frequency energy of the purified signal is concentrated within the 5–100 Hz range. The peak location and bandwidth are consistent with the primary frequency range of tunnel blasting vibrations reported in the literature23,22), while the energy in the ultra-low-frequency band is significantly reduced.

Spectrum diagrams of blasting vibration signals before and after FDM processing.

In Case II, as shown in Fig. 12(c), the original blasting vibration signal exhibits a significant ultra-high low-frequency amplitude around 0–5 Hz in the frequency spectrum. This component mainly originates from trend terms such as zero drift in the measured blasting vibration signal, rather than the structural vibration fundamental frequency under blasting force. As shown in Fig. 12(d), after FDM preprocessing, the main frequency energy of the clean signal is concentrated around 62 Hz, which corresponds to the main frequency range of the tunnel blasting signal.

These results indicate that FDM preprocessing can effectively remove the ultra-low-frequency trend term and, while preserving the effective blasting-vibration components, corrects the misleading impression that the dominant frequency is pulled down by the trend term.

It is worth noting that, although the nominal lower limit of the effective frequency band of the vibration acquisition instrument used in this study is 5 Hz, components in the 0–5 Hz range can still be observed in the spectrum shown in Fig. 12. This phenomenon can mainly be attributed to two factors. First, inherent system noise and residual responses cause sub-5 Hz components to appear in the spectrum. Specifically, the electronic circuitry of the acquisition system introduces ultra-low-frequency interference, and although the sensor response below the 5 Hz cutoff decays sharply, it does not vanish completely. These residual signals, together with system noise, constitute the ultra-low-frequency content in the spectrum. Second, adaptive signal decomposition algorithms such as FDM inevitably exhibit endpoint effects when processing strong transient impulse signals, which can reconstruct part of the energy into the ultra-low-frequency region, forming components that represent the overall trend of the signal; this can be regarded as an algorithm-induced reconstruction effect. The spectra of the trend terms extracted by the algorithm may therefore extend over the original frequency band.

Therefore, the 0–5 Hz spectral components in Fig. 12 mainly reflect the noise floor of the system, the residual response of the sensor, and the inherent characteristics of the signal processing process, which are shown in the spectrum of the blasting vibration signal.

Conclusion

Based on the characteristics of blasting vibration signals, this paper introduces a signal preprocessing method based on FDM. The applicability of the method is verified through numerical simulation, and it is applied in practice based on measured tunnel blasting vibration signals. The following conclusions are drawn:

After FDM decomposition and selection of dominant components, the reconstructed signal eliminates invalid information while effectively preserving the characteristics of the original signal, thereby maintaining an unchanged peak particle velocity and consistent frequency characteristics.

During blasting signal preprocessing, setting the cross-correlation coefficient threshold between modal components and the original signal to 0.10 can effectively increase the SNR and reduce the RMSE in practical engineering applications. When the threshold for the ultra-low-frequency energy proportion is set to 80%, the trend term can be effectively identified, thereby minimizing misclassification while ensuring accuracy.

Compared to signal processing methods such as EMD, CEEMDAN, and VMD, FDM achieves better preprocessing results for blasting signals. It has the highest signal-to-noise ratio (28.3736), the smallest root mean square error (0.0018), and the peak particle velocity is closest to the actual value.

Prospect

Although FDM demonstrates significant advantages in blasting vibration signal processing and analysis, its effectiveness and accuracy still depend on specific application conditions and have inherent limitations.

The FDM decomposition method is essentially designed to handle non-stationary signals. Because blasting vibration signals have rapidly changed transient characteristics, they are highly compatible with the core processing of FDM. Conversely, when dealing with stable vibration signals, compared to the FDM method, the Fourier transform is more efficient in processing.

At the same time, in order to accurately capture the entire process of blasting vibration and ensure frequency domain resolution, the FDM method requires a sufficient sampling rate and recording length, that is, it must include the complete stages of vibration from initiation, main vibration, to decay, and its computational complexity is relatively high.

The threshold setting in the current FDM method, such as an 80% energy ratio, has undergone sensitivity validation but still has a certain empirical aspect. Future research could explore combining FDM with machine learning, such as support vector machines, random forests, or deep learning, such as convolutional neural networks and long short-term memory networks, using large-sample training to achieve intelligent optimization of thresholds and decomposition paths, further enhancing the method’s adaptability and generalization performance.

Compared with other signal decomposition methods, although FDM has a certain robustness to noise, its mode separation effect will deteriorate in low signal-to-noise ratio environments. When the background noise intensity is comparable to weak blast signals, the AFIBFs obtained by its decomposition may be severely contaminated by noise, making feature extraction difficult. In such extreme cases, it is necessary to use it in combination with pre-denoising techniques such as SVD.

In practical applications, FDM is still subject to many limitations. First, in the process of processing ultra-long signals, compared with traditional filtering methods, FDM has a higher computational cost, which is not conducive to real-time processing. At the same time, when faced with incomplete data at both ends of the signal, FDM may produce end effects, causing distortion in the components at the beginning and end of the signal. When dealing with transient impacts of short-duration high energy generated by precise blasting, FDM may disperse their energy into multiple adjacent AFIBFs instead of concentrating it in a single component, resulting in modal aliasing.
