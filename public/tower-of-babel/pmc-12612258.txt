12S rRNA based phylogeny and genetic diversity of Haemaphysalis spinigera, the primary vector for Kyasanur forest disease in India
Authors: Nandhini Perumalsamy, Rohit Sharma, Ayyanar Elango, Shriram Ananganallur Nagarajan, Manju Rahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKyasanur Forest Disease (KFD), also known as Monkey Fever, is an important tick-borne viral infection in India. It is mainly transmitted by the hard tick vector, Haemaphysalis spinigera. This re-emerging viral disease in humans has a mortality rate of 2–10% and was first reported in the Shimoga district of Karnataka in 1957. In recent years, KFD has been reported from various parts of the country, indicating possible geographical expansion and wider distribution of the tick vector. Assessing the distinct populations of H. spinigera and their local adaptations is important. However, the genetic structure of this vector population remains undocumented due to the lack of well-characterized genetic markers. Although the distribution of H. spinigera has been described, information on its genetic diversity and population structure is still limited. In this study, we characterized and validated a mitochondrial marker, 12S rRNA for assessing the genetic diversity of H. spinigera in Eastern and Western Ghats of India. Analysis of ninety individual ticks revealed a genetically diverse population comprising three haplotypes, which formed three distinct clades. These clades included haplotypes from nine geographical populations. Understanding the genetic structure of tick vectors is crucial for studying the transmission dynamics of tick-borne diseases. Our findings suggest that 12S rRNA is a suitable genetic marker for studying intraspecific variation in H. spinigera. Introduction

Ticks are important vectors, second only to mosquitoes in transmitting diseases to humans, and are considered primary ectoparasites of wild and domestic animals1,2. Recent changes in climate and global warming have increased the risk of tick-borne diseases (TBDs), which can be caused by pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and protozo﻿a3–5. Ticks are classified into three families Argasidae (soft ticks), Ixodidae (hard ticks), and Nuttaliellidae (monotypic tick family)4. Among these, hard ticks are the most significant vectors for pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and protozoa3. The genus Haemaphysalis Koch, 1844 is the second-largest genus in the family Ixodidae, with more than 170 described species2. Species of Haemaphysalis are mainly distributed in southern and south-eastern Asia and tropical Africa, with some occurring in Australia, and the Americas6,7.

In India, Haemaphysalis spinigera is the primary vector for Kyasanur forest disease (KFD), also known as monkey fever, caused by a flavivirus named Kyasanur forest disease virus (KFDV)8–14. The first outbreak of KFD was reported in 1957 from Shimoga district, Karnataka. This tick-borne disease is mainly reported in the southern parts of India15. The virus is naturally maintained in a cycle involving ticks, mammals, and birds16. Large-scale deaths of non-human primates (NHPs) have been observed in outbreak areas11. KFDV was first isolated from the dead monkey Semnopithecus entellus (black-faced langur) and later from humans and H. spinigera17. H. spinigera is capable of transmitting KFDV both trans-stadially and transovarially under laboratory conditions8,9.

KFD is endemic in five districts of Karnataka and shows seasonal outbreaks from December to May, coinciding with peak nymphal activity of the vector ticks in forested areas18,19. Since 2012, KFD has been reported from other states such as Tamil Nadu (Nilgiris). Between 2013 and 2017, additional cases occurred in Kerala, Goa, and Maharashtra, raising concerns because these regions share the ecology of the Western Ghats4,20. Over the last 5 decades, an average of 400–500 human cases and monkey deaths have been recorded annually across different forest areas of the Western Ghats17. Global warming and climate changes are expanding the range of tick vectors into naive areas that were previously unsuitable for their survival5,21. Tick dispersal patterns and population structure depend largely on the mobility of their hosts22. Variations in the genetic composition makes different populations within the species (intraspecific variations) and it is studied by the population genetics23. Understanding the genetic diversity of arthropod vectors is important for determining disease transmission dynamics24,25.

Genetic markers are important for molecular taxonomy and assessment of genetic diversity26. Established evolutionary markers such as mitochondrial and ribosomal markers (12S rRNA, 16S rRNA, COI (Cytochrome Oxidase Subunit I), 18S rRNA, ITS-I (Internal Transcribed Spacer-1) and ITS-2 (Internal Transcribed Spacer-2), have been employed to accurately characterize tick species and understanding the population structures26–29. H. spinigera has been described morphologically based on features such as body shape, size, colour, and pattern. It was originally described by Neumann in 1897 from adult specimens collected in southern Maharashtra and Ceylon (now Sri Lanka). These descriptions and illustrations were later expanded by Nuttall and Warburton (1915) and Sharif (1928), including additional geographic and host records10. However, information on the molecular taxonomy, Phylogenetics and population structure of H. spinigera is still limited. Although the phylogenetic structure of H. spinigera has been reported using the 16S rRNA marker30, its genetic diversity has not yet been studied. Therefore, in this study, we have validated 12S rRNA as an intraspecific genetic marker using different population of H. spinigera. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first description of the population structure of H. spinigera from Eastern and Western Ghats of India.

Results

Morphological identification

Based on morphological analysis using a stereomicroscope and established identification keys, the specimens were confirmed to belong to the genus Haemaphysalis. The genus level identification was based on the following morphological characteristics: 1) absence of eyes, 2) laterally flared base of palpal article 2, 3) presence of festoons, 4) a single spur on the first coxa, 5) 4/4 dentition, 6) a ventral retroverted spur on the palpal segment 3, 7) presence of cornua, and (8) blunt, distinctive spurs or ridges on all coxae (9) a scutum longer than wide31–34.

Species-specific keys were utilized for further identification at the species level, confirming H. spinigera at both the nymph and adult stages (Fig. 1). Species level identification was based on the following diagnostic features: a broad salience; a palpal segment 2 that is broader than long; palpal segment 3 with a prominent ventral retroverted spur approximately twice the length of its basal breadth; a median retroverted spur on the dorso-basal margin of palpal segment 3; and a sharp spur on the ventrobasal external margin of palpal segment 2. In males, a prominent long spur is present on coxa 4, while females lack this spur. In addition, males have the scutum covering the entire dorsal surface, whereas females possess a conscutum and alloscutum covering the dorsal side. In H. spinigera, the ventral spur of palpal segment 3 extends halfway to the basal margin of palpal segment 2. The salience is more pronounced in larvae, and the external profile of the palps is less strongly recurved. The ventral basis lacks cornua. In nymphs, the ventral spur of palpal segment 3 is comparatively larger, reaching halfway to the basal margin of palpal segment 2. The ventrobasal external margin of palpal segment 2 forms a sharp spur-like projection, which is clearly visible in all developmental stages of H. spinigera (Fig. 1)31–33.

Molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis of H. spinigera by using 12S rRNA marker

DNA extracted from the morphologically identified ticks was used to amplify the 12S rRNA genetic marker. The designed primers successfully PCR amplified the expected 12S rRNA fragment (~ 339 bps) (Figure S2). The amplified products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. Forward and reverse sequences were aligned, and BLAST analysis on the NCBI platform showed > 90% query cover similarity with various Haemaphysalis species, including Haemaphysalis taiwana (NC_085275.1), Haemaphysalis cornigera (OM_368283.1), Haemaphysalis bancrofti (NC_041076.1), Haemaphysalis novaeguineae (NC_087880.1), Haemaphysalis doenitzi (OM_368278.1), Haemaphysalis qinghaiensis (OM_368295.1) (Figure S3). We found no available 12S rRNA sequence for H. spinigera in NCBI public database. Pairwise sequence alignment was performed between the obtained 12S rRNA sequences of H. spinigera and other Haemaphysalis tick vectors. The results indicated that, H. cornigera and H. taiwana were the closest relatives, showing 96.2% sequence identity with H. spinigera. Other Haemaphysalis tick vectors, known to transmit various diseases globally, also exhibited more than 90% identity with H. spinigera (Table 1)47–49.

For phylogenetic analysis, 12S rRNA sequences from mites (Prostigmata), Argasidae (soft ticks), Nuttaliellidae (monotypic family), and Ixodidae (hard ticks) were obtained and aligned with the sequences of H. spinigera (accession numbers are provided in the tree). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) algorithm, a character-based method that evaluates a large number of hypothetical trees before selecting the optimal tree under a specific evolutionary model35 (Fig. 2). Bootstrap values are indicated on each branch of the phylogenetic tree; higher values represent more robust and reliable groupings of species or sequences, whereas lower values indicate reduced confidence in those relationships36. Additionally, phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) and Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) algorithms (Figures S4 and S5). All three methods produced similar patterns of tick groupings and confirmed the position of H. spinigera excluding mites (Prostigmata) as outgroup (Fig. 2). Haplotypes of H. spinigera clustered in the same clade with H. cornigera and H. taiwana. Other Haemaphysalis species were positioned as sister taxa. Haemaphysalis ticks from China, Australia, and India grouped together, while species from Africa, Europe, and the USA formed a separate branch within the same sub-tree. Other hard tick genera, including Amblyomma, Hyalomma, Rhipicephalus, Dermacentor, Ixodes, and the monotypic genus Nuttalliella, formed a distinct cluster in the phylogenetic tree. All the soft ticks were arranged in separate branch of the sub tree (Fig. 2).

Genetic diversity and population structure and haplotype networking

The obtained sequences (n = 90) were screened initially with the BioEdit software and intraspecific nucleotide variations were observed among the sequences. Variations lead to haplotypes identification in sequences analysed showed three SNPs (Fig. 3). These variations were observed at positions 122nd (T&C), 208th (C & A) and 227th (C & T), and denoted as three haplotypes (Hap_1, Hap_2 & Hap_3). Among studied ticks, 40 ticks showed the SNPs at the positions, 122(T), 208 (C) and 227 (C) (Hap_1), 24 nymphs showed similar nucleotides at 122 (T), 208 (A) and 227 (T) (Hap_2), and 26 nymphs showed similar nucleotides at 122 (C), 208 (A) and 227 (T) (Hap_3) (Table 2). All the sequences (n = 90) were used to construct a phylogenetic tree in MEGA software, which revealed three distinct clades (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Tick population genetics is essential for understanding the genetic diversity and transmission dynamics of TBDs4,37. The population structure and genetic diversity of tick vectors may be influenced by climatic changes driving mobility of hosts towards available survival resources in newer areas, therefore, affecting the transmission dynamics of TBDs4,37,38. Studying the genetic structure of ticks provides valuable insights into their dispersal patterns, helps in assessing the risk of disease transmission, and supports the development of vector control programs25,39. Tick life cycle is complex, involving multiple developmental stages, a variety of hosts, and the ability to adapt different environmental conditions4,40,41. However, genetic diversity associated with different habitats remains poorly documented42. Therefore, characterizing molecular markers for tick vectors of important pathogens is highly desirable.

In this study, we characterized the12S rRNA mitochondrial marker for H. spinigera, the primary vector of KFDV in India. This is the first study to demonstrate the phylogenetic position and population structure of H. spinigera, based on the 12S rRNA, which proved to be a useful intraspecific marker for the understanding the population genetics of this vector. The primer for amplifying the 12S rRNA fragment of H. spinigera was based on conserved regions from available nucleotide sequences of various hard tick species. Morphologically identified H. spinigera specimens were used for molecular characterization. The sequences obtained showed the phylogenetic positioning of H. spinigera alongside other species of the same genus, consistent with previous analyses based on 16S rRNA marker30. In both cases, H. cornigera was identified as the closest relative.

Nucleotide sequences from both nymphs and adults were analyzed for intraspecific variation. Three haplotypes were identified based on nucleotide substitutions at three positions, all of which were transversion mutations. Similar transversion mutations have been reported in Ixodes scapularis based on 16S and 12S rRNA43. Our results indicate that 12S rRNA is a reliable for detecting intraspecific variation in H. spinigera. The ticks used in this study were collected from two different ecologically distinct geographical regions, belonging to Eastern and Western Ghats of India. They are diverse ecosystem due to their land scapes, altitudes, flora, fauna, forests and environmental factors44,45. The Biligirirangan Hills, (BR Hills) serve as a connecting corridor between the Ghats, facilitating the movement of animals, including potential tick hosts45. H. spinigera is associated with a wide range of hosts, including birds and cattle, which may contribute to its localized distribution. Members of the genus Haemaphysalis are well-adapted to various geographical locations and host species46,47. High genetic diversity enables ticks to adapt to new environments, expand their range, and maintain stable populations under changing conditions48. Intraspecific variation in genetic markers is critical for understanding population genetics, as both stochastic processes and natural selection can shape genetic variations49.

The genetic structure analysis of I. scapularis based on mitochondrial genes 12S rRNA and tRNA (Val) genes showed variation among different populations50. Similarly, COI was demonstrated as good population genetic marker for R. microplus from Malaysia, with comparable patterns observed in other regions, including China, the Americas and Africa51. A study on various hard ticks compared four different markers (ITS-2, COI, 12S rDNA and 16S rDNA) and found intraspecific variation in all except ITS-252. Population structure and divergence of Rhipicephalus sanguineus s.l. ticks collected from pet dogs in Colombia were assessed using 12S rRNA, COI and ITS-2, with all mitochondrial genes providing useful for studying intraspecific variation53. In our study the 12 S rRNA was validated as a reliable marker for detecting intraspecific variation in H. spinigera, yielding distinct haplotypes among the tested ticks.

Most Haemaphysalis spp. feed on both avian and mammalian hosts, including domestic and wild animals as well as humans54,55. H. spinigera is the principal vector in KFD-endemic regions, involved in both epizootic and epidemic transmission56. All life stages require different hosts, and these ticks have been reported on tigers, leopards, bears, cattle, buffaloes, and numerous other species. Birds have also been identified as important hosts in India and Sri Lanka57. Humans are mainly infested by the larval and nymphal stages9,10. Movement of monkeys and rodents, particularly the langur (Semnopithecus entellus) and bonnet macaque (Macaca radiata), plays a key role in the spread of KFDV4,8,20,58,59 (Fig. 7).

Gene flow, the movement of genetic material between populations can be inferred from the presence of shared haplotypes across different regions60–62. Such patterns indicate past and ongoing migration, local adaptation, and evolutionary responses to environmental pressures. Population genetic studies can also shed light on acaricide resistance, hybridization events, and species distribution shifts25,63.

In the present study, three haplotypes were identified based on nucleotide variations in the 12S rRNA marker. Haplotype 1 was widespread in both the Eastern and Western Ghats, Haplotype 2 was more restricted to certain locations, and Haplotype 3 was exclusive to the Eastern Ghats. The co-occurrence of different haplotypes in the same ecosystem suggests active gene flow, migration, and local adaptation64,65.

A Tajima’s D value of 2.54433, while not a strict significance threshold, suggests an excess of common alleles, consistent with balancing selection or a recent population bottleneck66. Mismatch distribution analysis indicated both unimodal and bimodal patterns. Unimodal curves suggest recent population expansion, while bimodal or multimodal curves indicate structured populations or admixture events67. This study provides detailed morphological descriptions of H. spinigera validated by molecular characterization. The findings are crucial for understanding the tick’s population structure, geographical adaptation, and potential role in KFD transmission risk assessment. The 12S rRNA marker demonstrates strong potential for future population genetics research to further elucidate the phylogenetic identity of H. spinigera.

This study has certain limitations. The sampling was restricted to selected sites in the Eastern and Western Ghats, which may not capture the full genetic diversity across India. Samples were collected during a single time period, without accounting for seasonal or annual variation in haplotype distribution. Further characterization of molecular marker for H. spinigera may provide more information in future. Moreover, host movement patterns, association of tick distribution in different altitudes and environmental variables were not integrated into the analysis, which may have offered deeper insights.

Conclusion

This study represents the first study on genetic diversity of H. spinigera, the primary vector of KFDV in India, using the 12S rRNA mitochondrial marker. We identified three distinct haplotypes and demonstrated their geographical distribution across the Eastern and Western Ghats. The 12S rRNA marker proved effective for detecting intraspecific variation and can serve as a valuable genetic marker for future molecular and phylogenetic studies. These results contribute to a better understanding of the genetic diversity, dispersal patterns, and transmission dynamics of H. spinigera, providing a foundation for undertaking studies on country wide genetic variations.

Methodology

Study area

In this study H. spinigera ticks (n = 90) were in nine different places from Eastern and Western Ghats of India. The places included in Eastern Ghats were Jawadhu hills (S9), (12°32’43"N 79°01’14"E) (n = 21), Palakuttai (S5), (11°48’20"N 78°27’36"E) (n = 10), Keeripatti (S4), (11°32’18"N 78°29’28"E) (n = 10) of Tamil Nadu, Nalla mala hill range (S3) (15°53’25"N 78°55’24"E) (n = 12), Gujjamammidivalasa (S2) (17°33’59"N 81°39’18"E) (n = 10) of Andhra Pradesh, Gumma (S1), (19°33’01"N 83°12’49"E) (n = 4) of Odisha. In the Western Ghats study sites Masinangudi (S8), (11°34’27"N 76°38’59"E) (n = 4), Nilgiris of Tamil Nadu, Behur Reserve Forest (S7) (11°54’38"N 76°03’35"E) (n = 12) of Kerala and Shimoga (S6) (13°57’27"N 75°30’26"E) (n = 7) of Karnataka (adults and nymphs) (Fig. 8). Field collected ticks were preserved with 80% ethanol and transported to the Indian Council of Medical Research – Vector Control Research Centre (ICMR-VCRC), Puducherry, India, for further analysis.

Morphological identification of ticks

Morphological characteristics of the ticks were examined using a Nikon Stereo Microscope SMZ745/SMZ745T® (Melville, U.S.A.). The ticks were carefully removed from ethanol using tweezers and placed in a cavity block filled with ethanol under the microscope. Preliminary observations were performed to identify the genus based on morphological traits. Subsequently, diagnostic characteristics specific to the genus Haemaphysalis were used to confirm the identification of H. spinigera, following standard dichotomous keys31–34.

DNA extraction from ticks

The ticks were washed with ethanol and distilled water, then dried on tissue paper. They were subsequently transferred to fresh 2 ml tubes, and 5 mm stainless steel beads were added along with ATL buffer. Samples were subjected to bead beating using Qiagen-Tissue lyser II® at a frequency of 25 Hz for 5–7 min68. Total genomic DNA was extracted from both adults and nymphs using the DNAeasy Blood and Tissue kit® (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was quantified using a Thermo NanoDrop Lite UV-Visible Spectrophotometer®.

PCR amplification

For the amplification of expected fragment of 12S rRNA from H. spinigera, we used the primer set previously designed by us using multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of available 12S rRNA sequences from different tick genera carrying conserved regions flanking the variable regions (Figure S1). The designed primer set, forward 5’TTTCCAGTACTTTAACTTTGTTACGA3’ and reverse 5’TGGCGGTATTTTAAGCTTTTC3’ were used for the PCR amplification68. For each PCR reaction, 2 µl of DNA sample was added. PCRs were performed using high-fidelity proofreading Q5 master mix (NEB®, USA). PCR steps include, one cycle of 98°C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98°C for 10 s (denaturation), 56°C for 15 s (annealing), 72°C for 20 s (extension), and a final extension of 72°C for 5 min. The amplified PCR products were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel, under UV light, using the VILBER – Bioprint® gel documentation system.

Sanger sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The PCR products were purified by using the magnetic beads (AMPure XP Bead-Based Reagent-Beckman Coulter) and Sanger-Sequenced at Eurofins Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. (Bengaluru, Karnataka, India). The nucleotide sequences obtained were confirmed as 12S rRNA through an nBLAST search on the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/blast.html). MSA were performed using BioEdit software (https://bioedit.software.informer.com/7.2/). Phylogenetic tree analysis was conducted, and trees were constructed using various algorithms, including Maximum Likelihood (ML), UPGMA, and Neighbour-Joining (NJ), using MEGA 12 software (https://megasoftware.net/dload_win_beta)69.

Analysis of genetic diversity & haplotype network construction

The obtained sequences from H. spinigera (n = 90) were aligned and analysed for haplotypes using software includes BioEdit, MEGA software, Dnasp (http://www.ub.edu/dnasp/index_v5.html), PopART (https://popart.maths.otago.ac.nz/download/)70,71. Initially, all the obtained sequences were aligned with ClustalW algorithm and screened for single nucleotide polymorphisms. A FASTA file generated by using BioEdit software then used for generating a nexus file by MEGA software and subjected to Dnasp5 software for constructing the haplotype file72–74. The software generates genetic parameters such as number of haplotypes, the average number of polymorphic sites, the average number of nucleotide differences, the haplotype diversity, nucleotide diversity, Tajima’s D neutrality test and Fu’s Fs, test75. Tajima’s D neutrality test defines the neutrality of the gene evolution, values of Tajima’ D is in-between − 2 to + 2, above 2 or below − 2 values indicates that non neutrality in evolution of the specific gene66. Fu’s Fs, test is the most powerful tests against background selection76. The haplotype file was utilized for generating trait files of different haplotypes and geotag file was constructed. All these files were used for haplotype networking by using the TCS (Templeton-Crandall-Sanger) statistical parsimony algorithm in PopART tool77,78.
