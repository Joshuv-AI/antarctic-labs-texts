Virtual simulation system in cataract education for medical undergraduates: a prospective quasi-experimental study
Authors: Meng-lu Zhang, Wen Bai, Zhi-peng Yan, Hui-min Ge, Qiu-yang Zhang, Xi-yan Ding, Yan-qing Liu, Xiang Li, Qin Jiang, Ke-ran Li, Wei-hong Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirtual reality (VR) simulation has transformative potential in medical education by allowing risk-free practice of clinical procedures in anatomically accurate, immersive environments. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a virtual reality simulation teaching model for cataract surgery in undergraduate medical education, compared to traditional teaching methods. A prospective study was conducted with 71 undergraduate students majoring in ophthalmology and optometry at Nanjing Medical University, divided into an experimental group (n = 35) and a control group (n = 36). The experimental group received practical training using a VR simulation model, while the control group received traditional offline practical training. Teaching effectiveness was assessed in three modules—clinical reasoning competency, clinical diagnostic proficiency, and surgical technical skills—through dual-mode assessments based on both the VR simulation platform and traditional methods. Results showed that the experimental group scored significantly higher in both traditional and VR assessments (P < 0.05). In the traditional assessment, the experimental group scored higher in Total Score (82.22 ± 4.34 vs. 76.83 ± 5.14), Clinical Reasoning Competency (12.61 ± 0.81 vs. 12.01 ± 0.81), Clinical Diagnostic Proficiency (48.98 ± 3.33 vs. 46.03 ± 3.96), and Surgical Technical Skills (20.64 ± 0.79 vs. 18.79 ± 1.67). This advantage was even more pronounced in the VR assessment, with the experimental group showing substantial superiority in Total Score (84.06 ± 4.85 vs. 71.06 ± 6.84), Clinical Reasoning Competency (10.57 ± 2.19 vs. 9.08 ± 2.06), Clinical Diagnostic Proficiency (52.17 ± 2.83 vs. 43.36 ± 4.01), and Surgical Technical Skills (21.31 ± 2.19 vs. 18.61 ± 2.93). The virtual reality simulation teaching model significantly enhanced medical students’ clinical thinking, clinical diagnosis, and surgical operation skills, outperforming traditional teaching methods.Keywords: Virtual reality simulation, Virtual training, Ophthalmology, Cataract, Undergraduate education, Medical student

Introduction

With the progressive aging of the global population, health concerns such as ocular health are receiving increasing attention. Cataracts, an age-related ocular disease, result in a decline in visual quality due to changes in lens transparency and color [1]. From 1990 to 2019, the global prevalence of cataracts increased by 129.17%, with an even greater rise of 223.54% in China [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, cataracts remain one of the leading causes of blindness and visual impairment worldwide, affecting an estimated 94 million people globally [3]. In 2020, cataracts were responsible for 17 million cases of blindness, accounting for 39.6% of total blindness [4]. Additionally, 83.5 million individuals suffered from moderate to severe visual impairment (MSVI) due to cataracts, making up 28.3% of the total MSVI cases [4]. Over 90% of the disability-adjusted life years (DALY) lost due to cataracts occur in developing countries [5, 6]. Therefore, cataracts pose a significant challenge to global healthcare systems.

The high blindness rate caused by cataracts remains a major issue that needs to be addressed. At the 74th World Health Assembly, the World Health Organization (WHO) set a global goal to increase cataract surgery coverage by 30% by 2030. Most cataract cases can be effectively treated, with phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation being the preferred treatment [7, 8]. However, there is a significant disparity in cataract surgery coverage across countries [9]. In contrast to the large number of cataract patients, there is a severe shortage of trained ophthalmic professionals. Given the inherent complexity of cataract phacoemulsification procedures and the potential for severe perioperative complications [10, 11], phacoemulsification teaching and training are lengthy and challenging processes, further limiting the utilization of cataract surgery. It is crucial to raise awareness about cataracts, promote early detection, and implement health education initiatives to expand surgical skills.

Undergraduate education is a critical stage in the development of medical professionals. Traditional cataract courses heavily rely on textbooks and electronic presentations, which offer limited scope for developing clinical and early practical skills in ophthalmology students [12]. This singular approach is insufficient to meet the growing demand for cataract surgery and the simultaneous shortage of ophthalmologists, necessitating the expansion of curriculum depth and the introduction of diverse learning methods. Surgical simulation plays an essential role in achieving this goal [13]. The integration of virtual reality-based simulation training is transforming educational practices [14–16]. Virtual simulation, combining simulation technology with virtual reality and related technologies, offers students an immersive, realistic, and interactive learning environment [17]. The application of virtual simulation platforms overcomes temporal and spatial constraints, enhances practical, hands-on training opportunities for medical students, and fosters the development and mastery of essential clinical skills.

This study aimed to develop a virtual simulation teaching platform for foundational cataract knowledge education. By comparing student learning outcomes between the virtual simulation experimental teaching model and the traditional practical training teaching model for cataract surgery, the study sought to investigate the efficacy and potential of virtual simulation teaching in the education of ophthalmology professionals.

Methods

Participants

A prospective quasi-experimental study design was adopted after our study was approved by ethics committee of the affiliated eye hospital of nanjing medical university (No.2023015, Clinical trial number: not applicable). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study. Participant confidentiality was maintained, with assurances that no names or identifying information would be disclosed upon publication. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions for medical research involving human subjects. 71 undergraduate students majoring in ophthalmology and optometry at Nanjing Medical University were enrolled in this study. These students, from the 2018 and 2019 entering classes, participated in practical training and assessment related to cataract surgery during their senior year. The student cohort was divided into an experimental group (n = 35) comprised of the 2019 class and a control group (n = 36) comprised of the 2018 class. The experimental group adopted a practical training model based on virtual simulation teaching, conducting cataract diagnosis and surgical operation training through the virtual simulation teaching platform for cataract surgery. The control group adopted the traditional practical training model, conducting cataract diagnosis and surgical operation training through offline practical training (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of Undergraduate Cataract Education

Pre-training Preparation

This included pre-class preparation and theoretical training. (1) Pre-class preparation: Students were asked to consult textbooks such as “Ophthalmology,” “Optometric Instruments,” and “Comprehensive Optometric Practical Training,” and combine these with relevant online course resources to independently review relevant knowledge points of cataract diagnosis and surgical operation; (2) Theoretical training: Senior clinical ophthalmologists specializing in cataracts served as instructors, providing unified training on theoretical knowledge of cataracts, including the diagnosis, clinical manifestations, auxiliary examinations, treatment plans, and other related content.

Implementation of practical training

Experimental Group: This included practical operation and simulated practice (Fig. 2). (1) Practical operation training: Based on the virtual simulation teaching platform for cataract surgery, Unity3D technology was used to allow operators to experience the process of cataract eye examination and surgical treatment on the computer, stimulate learning interest, and enhance the willingness to learn independently. Training was conducted through modules such as medical history collection, doctor-patient communication, auxiliary examination, surgical operation, and prognosis explanation, and a full-process practical operation exercise of cataract diagnosis and surgical operation was carried out; (2) Simulated practice: Quantitative assessment indicators for cataract diagnosis and surgical operation were established. Students used the virtual simulation platform for modular autonomous simulated practice. The virtual simulation practical training system judged the assessment results and provided feedback.

Interface for interactive teaching on the virtual simulation platform

Control Group: This included practical operation training and in-class assessment. (1) Practical operation training: Cataract diagnosis process training was gradually carried out through student pair interaction simulation; surgical operation was observed on-site, and cataract surgery operation training was carried out using an operating microscope on fresh pig eyeballs; (2) In-class assessment: In-class assessments were conducted according to the assessment indicators of cataract diagnosis and surgical operation. The instructors evaluated the assessment results and provided feedback.

Teaching effectiveness evaluation

Both the experimental group and control group underwent practical training followed by dual assessments: a virtual simulation-based evaluation and a traditional assessment, to comprehensively evaluate pedagogical effectiveness.

Assessment under the virtual simulation mode: Conducted via a virtual simulation platform for cataract surgery training, this assessment comprised three weighted modules: (1) Clinical reasoning competency (15%/15 points): Evaluated through medical history collection, doctor-patient communication skills, and clinical case analysis. (2) Clinical diagnostic proficiency (60%/60 points): Evaluated through diagnostic planning, interpretation of ancillary tests, and surgical approach selection. (3) Surgical technical skills (25%/25 points): Evaluated through implementation of surgical steps and handling of surgical complications.

Assessment under the traditional mode: Employing conventional methodologies, this evaluation maintained the same tripartite structure: (1) Clinical reasoning competency (15%/15 points): The evaluation was conducted by means of integrated analysis of complex clinical scenarios, utilizing Standardized Patients (SPs). These SPs delivered an identical patient presentation to every student, guaranteeing an impartial and equitable testing or learning environment. (2) Clinical diagnostic proficiency (60%/60 points): Evaluated through a clinical diagnosis based on symptoms, signs, auxiliary examinations, and other content. (3) Surgical technical skills (25%/25 points): Evaluated through ophthalmic equipment operation and surgical microscope operation.

Statistical analysis

R software (R version 4.3.1) was used for statistical analysis of the data. The reliability of the VR assessment items was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square test. Continuous data were subjected to normality test (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance test. If determined to be normally distributed with equal variance, the independent samples t-test (two-sample t-test) was used; if determined to be non-normally distributed, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Chi-square test was used. P-values for multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study subjects

The baseline characteristics of the study subjects, including both the experimental group and the control group, are presented in Table 1. The experimental group consisted of 35 participants, including 13 males and 22 females, with an average age of 22.40 ± 0.85 years. Their scores in foundational courses, including “Introduction to Optometry”, “Optometry” and “Basic Ophthalmology” were 93.06 ± 1.26, 79.46 ± 8.45, and 85.67 ± 6.08, respectively. The control group consisted of 36 participants, including 11 males and 25 females, with an average age of 22.06 ± 0.71 years. Their scores in the same foundational courses were 93.56 ± 1.08, 78.31 ± 6.91, and 86.03 ± 6.01, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, or scores in foundational courses (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In our study, the virtual simulation teaching platform is adapted from real clinical cases of senile cataracts and adopts an interactive teaching format of 3D scenario simulation. Driven by virtual intelligent standardized patient technology, it presents cases of senile cataracts and simulates real clinical situations. Operators can freely conduct inquiries, auxiliary examinations, surgical and other operations on the 3D virtual standardized senile cataract patient. At the same time, the system can completely record the operator’s operation trajectory and make a comprehensive evaluation.

This study found that ophthalmology undergraduates trained by the cataract diagnosis and treatment virtual simulation platform had higher scores in clinical reasoning competency, clinical diagnostic proficiency, and surgical technical skills in the assessment than the traditional teaching group. Through the training, ophthalmology students can better master cataract diagnosis and related surgical skills. Cataract surgery involves complex steps and tools [11], and further evaluation showed that students trained through traditional methods tend to forget steps, particularly those in the middle of the procedure. Passive learning methods, like textbooks and videos, provide basic knowledge but suffer from low retention due to limited engagement [18]. In contrast, VR allows immersive practice in clinical scenarios, significantly enhancing memory retention. The application of the virtual simulation teaching platform provides a more realistic learning environment compared to traditional medical education. It allows students to experience conditions closer to real-life clinical practice, helping them quickly integrate theoretical knowledge with practical skills.

Despite the increasing use of VR in medical schools worldwide, few studies have reported the integration of VR training into medical school curricula [19]. Williams et al. [20] developed a VR model for pupil examination in the “Simulated Eye Clinic” course at Queen’s University of Belfast. This model simulates a 30–60 s examination of acute glaucoma, an afferent pupillary defect, and a fixed dilated pupil. 96% of participants reported that it enhanced their knowledge and confidence in examining real patients. Additionally, Tajran et al. [21] developed OcuSim, a VR simulator for exploring the eye’s internal structure. At Loyola University Chicago, 52 first-year medical students participated in a one-hour VR session on glaucoma anatomy. They showed improved accuracy in identifying eye anatomy and reported greater confidence in understanding the visual pathway. These studies demonstrate that VR surgical training can improve trainees’ psychological resilience. Given the complexity of the human eye and the precision required in its functional structure, ophthalmic surgeries are inherently complex and carry significant medical risk. Learning surgical skills in ophthalmology is a long and challenging process, requiring strong confidence [22]. By interacting with virtual patients, medical students can practice diagnosing, planning, and implementing treatments in a safe environment, thereby boosting their confidence in their clinical skills and decision-making abilities.

Compared to traditional educational platforms, the ophthalmology virtual simulation platform demonstrates superior comprehensiveness and clinical practice orientation. It moves beyond simple 3D anatomical demonstrations by replicating the entire diagnostic and treatment process, emphasizing experiential learning [23]. This platform creates realistic clinical decision-making scenarios, requiring students to independently diagnose and formulate treatment plans based on simulated patient information, thereby significantly honing their clinical decision-making and thinking skills. For skill acquisition, the platform deeply integrates the structure of deliberate practice, which provides a rigorous framework for mastering complex psychomotor skills [24]. Specifically, it can decompose complex surgeries (such as phacoemulsification) into measurable microtasks with precise goals; it offers real-time statistical analysis and feedback to identify and correct errors immediately; and it encourages trainees to step outside their comfort zone, using feedback-based repetitive learning to enhance proficiency. Furthermore, the platform’s effectiveness is maximized through spaced training—utilizing multiple, short practice sessions—which enhances motor skill acquisition and long-term retention, ultimately leading to deep skill mastery and substantial improvement in clinical competence. However, despite these prominent advantages, virtual simulation platforms still face limitations [25]: these include high initial investment costs, maintenance complexity, the ongoing need to improve the fidelity of haptic feedback, and subtle discrepancies between the virtual visual field and real-world clinical operations, all of which may affect its large-scale adoption and the complete transfer of learned skills to the clinic.

The VR platform is highly suitable for pre-clinical training in medical surgical techniques [26], significantly reducing costs while increasing efficiency. Traditional ophthalmic training requires junior doctors to perform procedures on patients and animals under senior supervision [27]. These programs demand intensive guidance and often involve considerable time and labor costs. Previous studies have highlighted the significant role of ophthalmic surgical simulators in enhancing physician training [28, 29]. Among these simulators, the Eyesi Simulator (VRMAGIC, Mannheim, Germany) [28], HelpMesee Simulator (HelpMesee, New York, NY) [30], and Phacovision (Mererit, Linköping, Sweden) [31] have been used for cataract surgery training for residents and higher-level surgeons. A scoping review suggests that, compared to supervised surgical training on actual patients, virtual reality simulations appear to result in safer outcomes, as evidenced by a reduction in intraoperative complication rates [32]. However, the high cost of commercial simulators limits their use [33]. To address this, we adopt high-fidelity, low-technology, and cost-effective cataract surgery simulation technologies, providing a practical solution that helps undergraduates understand ocular anatomy, cataract theory, and the sequence of surgical procedures. It also offers detailed error feedback, allowing for independent training and reducing time and labor costs. These technologies are easily replicable and accessible to all trainees and instructors.

This study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of these findings is limited as this was a single-center study involving only 71 medical students from one university. We will prioritize a multi-center design in future work, incorporating a larger and more diverse cohort to enhance the external validity of VR training outcomes. Second, in terms of the quasi-experimental design, although the training and assessments were conducted with students at the same academic stage (in their fourth year), the inherent complexities of grade-level differences cannot be entirely excluded as factors influencing the results. Finally, the issue of consistency in evaluation criteria arises. Although the study utilized a dual-mode assessment to comprehensively evaluate clinical reasoning, diagnosis, and surgical skills, it was challenging to achieve complete consistency in the standards across different evaluation modes in practice. As a result, we could only compare the data at a macro level.

Conclusions

The virtual simulation platform for cataract diagnosis and treatment can train the clinical thinking of ophthalmology students, help with knowledge acquisition and mastery, and improve students’ practical abilities, assisting real operations. It is crucial to note that VR simulation is not intended to completely replace traditional wet-lab surgical training but serves as a powerful supplementary tool, providing students with early and safe practice opportunities. In summary, the virtual simulation teaching platform has a high overall acceptance and, when used in ophthalmology teaching, complements traditional teaching methods, making it a good teaching model that is worth promoting and applying in future virtual simulation teaching.
