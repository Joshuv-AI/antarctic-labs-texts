Application of Cutaneous Nerve Block in Balloon Percutaneous Transluminal Angioplasty for Vascular Access Stenosis: A Pain Management Strategy
Authors: Akira Morimoto, Midori Yamamura, Yuko Miki, Ichiro Fujiwara, Akira Yamamoto, Yoshihiro Tsujimoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose: Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty for forearm arteriovenous fistula stenosis is frequently associated with significant pain, which may hinder patient cooperation. Ultrasound-guided cutaneous nerve blocks were evaluated as an alternative analgesic method for percutaneous transluminal angioplasty in forearm arteriovenous fistula, with the aim of reducing pain without motor paralysis or systemic complications.Material and Methods: This retrospective, single-center study included 77 patients who underwent percutaneous transluminal angioplasty for forearm arteriovenous fistula stenosis with ultrasound-guided cutaneous nerve block using lidocaine in October 2024. Target nerves included the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerves, the superficial branch of the radial nerve, and/or the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerves. We retrospectively collected data on block time, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty duration, lidocaine volume, complications, presence or absence of postanesthetic motor paralysis, and analgesic efficacy. Analgesic efficacy was graded as excellent, good, or poor, with “poor” defined as pain necessitating supplemental local infiltration anesthesia for adequate relief.Results: The mean block preparation time was 2.1 ± 0.5 min, and the percutaneous transluminal angioplasty duration was 27.3 ± 9.2 min. The mean volume of 1% lidocaine used was 2.6 ± 1.1 mL. Minor hematomas were observed in eight cases. No patient experienced postanesthetic motor paralysis. Analgesia was rated excellent in 57.1% of patients and good in 29.9%, while poor analgesia occurred in only 12.9%. A superficial branch of the radial nerve block improved outcomes in distal forearm lesions.Conclusions: Ultrasound-guided cutaneous nerve blocks provide rapid, effective, and motor-sparing analgesia for percutaneous transluminal angioplasty of forearm arteriovenous fistula stenosis, making them suitable for outpatient use and warranting further prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease affects nearly 10% of the global population and continues to increase [1]. Most patients with end-stage renal disease undergo hemodialysis, in which vascular access is essential. The 2019 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines recommend individualized access planning to ensure optimal timing and choice [2].

For hemodialysis, AVFs are preferred over catheters owing to their better patency and lower risk [3]. However, AVFs frequently develop dysfunction, with nearly one-third requiring intervention within six months to facilitate maturation or manage complications, and almost half requiring additional procedures within two years to maintain functional patency [4, 5]. Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) is recommended for AVF stenosis or occlusion, which causes dialysis-related problems [2, 6]. Although the initial technical success rate―defined as less than 30% residual stenosis following angioplasty with restoration of adequate flow―is high, restenosis is common, with 1-year primary patency rates of 40%-60% [7]. Adjunctive tools such as drug-coated balloons, high-pressure balloons, cutting balloons, and stents have been introduced to improve outcomes [7, 8].

However, severe pain during vascular dilation is a major issue in PTA and often reduces patient compliance and cooperation [9]. Although local anesthesia is currently the standard method for PTA, it may be insufficient to control procedural pain [10, 11]. While general anesthesia is typically avoided in patients with multiple comorbidities due to cardiopulmonary risks, sedation-based techniques using midazolam or propofol offer reliable sedative effects but carry a higher risk of hypotension and respiratory depression [12, 13]. Ultrasound-guided brachial plexus block (BPB) has been used for PTA of dysfunctional AVF with favorable safety and efficacy profiles [14-18]. Nonetheless, BPB commonly results in temporary motor blockade, as demonstrated by clinical evidence of motor response and paralysis [19]. These findings support the need for caution in contexts such as outpatient procedures, where motor function recovery is essential.

Cutaneous nerve block involves the targeted administration of local anesthetics to the sensory nerves supplying the skin, aiming to provide effective analgesia for procedures involving skin trauma, such as incisions, wound management, or cannulation [20]. Stenosis of the forearm AVF often involves areas innervated by the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, the superficial branch of the radial nerve, and the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Blocking these cutaneous nerves may provide effective pain relief. The aim of this study was to evaluate the technique, safety, and analgesic efficacy of ultrasound-guided cutaneous nerve block during PTA for the treatment of forearm AVF stenosis.

Material and Methods

Study design

The aim of this single-center, retrospective study was to describe the outcomes of PTA with cutaneous nerve block for forearm AVF stenosis. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institute (Approval number 290). Written informed consent to participate in this study was waived owing to its retrospective nature. All patients who underwent PTA for forearm AVF stenosis at our hospital in October 2024 were included in this study. All procedures were performed under ultrasound guidance, and a cutaneous nerve block was administered routinely in all cases. The cutaneous nerve blocks targeted the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, the superficial branch of the radial nerve, and/or the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Exclusion criteria were (1) patients with an arteriovenous graft, (2) patients with AVF stenosis in the upper arm cephalic vein, and (3) patients with central vein stenosis proximal to the axilla.

Ultrasound-guided cutaneous nerve block technique for forearm AVF

Procedures

In cases of AVF stenosis in the forearm, the affected sensory area is typically innervated by the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, the superficial branch of the radial nerve, and the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. This was followed by ventral infiltration of the antebrachial cutaneous nerve (Figure 1). Radial-side stenosis is associated with the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve, while ulnar-side shunt stenosis involves the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. In cases of distal radial-side stenosis where the superficial branch of the radial nerve was confirmed to course adjacent to the stenotic vessel, involvement of the nerve was suspected. Accurate identification of these peripheral cutaneous nerves is essential for a successful cutaneous nerve block [21, 22]. Nerve blocks were performed using an ultrasound system (Aplio a450; Canon Medical Systems) with a linear transducer (PLU-1204BT, frequency range: 5.0-18.0 MHz), using the manufacturer-defined “PV Arterial” preset mode optimized for peripheral vascular imaging. A 25-gauge, 26-mm needle was used for the procedure. Under long-axis ultrasound guidance, the needle was initially advanced along the dorsal aspect of the target nerve, which typically appears as an oval or spindle-shaped hyperechoic structure with a characteristic honeycomb appearance. Notably, commencing the injection from the ventral aspect may result in distal displacement of the nerve, thereby impeding subsequent dorsal access. To mitigate this, 1% lidocaine was first administered dorsally to facilitate elevation of the musculocutaneous nerve from the underlying tissues. This was followed by ventral infiltration, resulting in a circumferential, donut-shaped distribution of local anesthetic around the nerve. The endpoint of the procedure was defined as complete circumferential spread of lidocaine, forming the characteristic “donut sign” under ultrasound guidance. The anesthetic volume was titrated to the minimum required to achieve this pattern, typically about 2 mL per nerve, and it rarely exceeded 5 mL. Although sensory testing, particularly temperature sensation, was initially used to confirm block efficacy, it was later observed that analgesia during PTA was often achieved even without objective sensory changes. Therefore, block success was ultimately defined by ultrasound-guided morphological criteria in combination with clinical analgesic effect. Throughout the procedure, care was taken to avoid adjacent vascular structures and to maintain spatial awareness of nearby major nerves, including the median, ulnar, and radial nerves, so as to minimize unintended spread to off-target neural elements. Following confirmation of the donut sign, the procedure commenced. The time required for the cutaneous nerve block was defined as the interval from the time-out performed after skin disinfection and draping to the point at which the target nerve was identified, lidocaine was injected around the nerve under ultrasound guidance, and the “donut sign” was formed and recorded as an ultrasound image.

The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is the terminal branch of the musculocutaneous nerve. Originating from the lateral cord of the brachial plexus within the axilla [22, 23], the musculocutaneous nerve descends laterally between the biceps brachii and brachialis muscles, advancing toward the lateral aspect of the upper arm. As it emerges subcutaneously proximal to the elbow, it transitions into the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. The nerve continues subcutaneously from the posterior aspect of the distal biceps tendon. Ultrasonography reveals a honeycomb-like hyperechoic structure with a characteristic delta-shaped configuration contiguous with the hypoechoic distal bicep tendon (Figure 2). Distal tracking of the musculocutaneous nerve facilitates identification of the origin of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. However, its continuity may become indistinct posterior to the distal bicep tendon. In such instances, gradual cranial tilting of the ultrasound probe enhances visualization and confirms the anatomical continuity between the musculocutaneous nerve and its terminal branch. Administering a nerve block at the origin of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve provides reliable access while minimizing the risk of injury to adjacent structures such as the cephalic vein.

The superficial branch of the radial nerve originates from the radial nerve in the proximal forearm. It courses distally adjacent to the radial artery from the mid-forearm and enters the subcutaneous layer, approximately five fingerbreadths proximal to the wrist, passing between the radial artery and radial cutaneous vein. A nerve block at this level avoids the surrounding vasculature and provides a safe and effective puncture route.

The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve, a branch of the medial cord of the brachial plexus in the axilla, innervates the ulnar half of the skin of the volar forearm. It branches into multiple smaller nerves distal to the mid-humerus and is, therefore, best blocked proximally (at or above the mid-humerus level). The median nerve runs ulnar to the brachial artery, whereas the ulnar nerve courses dorsally to the ulnar cutaneous vein. Because the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve may cross paths with both the median and ulnar nerves from the axilla to the mid-arm, careful identification and separation of these nerves are critical to avoid anesthetizing unintended nerves.

Variables

The variables assessed in this study included the time required to perform the cutaneous nerve block, duration of the PTA procedure (encompassing both the intervention and sheath hemostasis), volume of 1% lidocaine administered, complications related to the cutaneous nerve block, presence or absence of postanesthetic motor paralysis, and analgesic efficacy of the block. The duration of the cutaneous nerve block was defined as the time between the procedural time-out and the formation of the “donut sign” on ultrasound. Analgesic efficacy was evaluated based on patient-reported pain during PTA and was categorized into three grades: excellent, defined as no pain during the procedure; good, defined as mild pain that did not require additional local infiltration anesthesia; and poor, defined as pain that required supplemental local infiltration anesthesia for relief.

Quantitative variables, specifically the time required for the cutaneous nerve block and PTA procedure time, were summarized as the mean ± standard deviation.

Categorical variables, including the analgesic effect of the cutaneous nerve block (excellent, good, poor) and the presence or absence of associated complications, were presented as counts and percentages.

Results

Participant characteristics

In October 2024, 128 patients underwent PTA for vascular access stenosis. A total of 33 patients were excluded because of an arteriovenous graft, six because of AVF in the cephalic vein of the upper arm, and 12 because of central vein stenosis proximal to the axilla. Finally, 77 patients were included in this study (Figure 3). The average age of the participants was 71.9 years. The study cohort had a median age of 61 years and consisted of 53 males and 24 females. At the time of PTA, the mean dialysis duration was eight years and 11 months. The participants had undergone an average of 6.9 previous PTA procedures. The treatment modalities performed were as follows: PTA in 55 patients, PTA with a drug-coated balloon in 16 patients, and thrombectomy with PTA in six patients. The results are summarized in Table 1. All procedures were performed by three physicians: a radiologist, an internist, and a urologist, with 26, 24, and nine years of experience in vascular access interventions, respectively.

Outcomes of cutaneous nerve block

The treatment modalities performed during this study period were lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve block in 66 patients, lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve plus superficial branch of the radial nerve block in three patients, medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve block in three patients, and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve plus medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve block in three patients. The mean time required for the cutaneous nerve block was 2.1 ± 0.5 min (range: 1-5 min). The mean volume of 1% lidocaine used for the block was 2.6 ± 1.1 mL (range: 1-7 mL). The mean PTA procedure time was 27.3 ± 9.2 min (range: 12-57 min). In all cases, the initial cutaneous nerve block provided sufficient anesthetic coverage for the entire duration of the procedure, and no additional block was required. Minor hematomas due to microvessel puncture were observed in eight patients. All hematomas were mild, detectable by ultrasound but not noticeable as swelling from the body surface, and did not require any additional treatment. Postanesthetic motor paralysis was not observed in any patient (0%). The results are summarized in Table 2.

Analgesic effect of cutaneous nerve block

The analgesic effect was rated as excellent in 44 cases (57.1%) and good in 23 cases (29.9%). The combined proportion of cases rated as excellent or good, in which no additional local anesthesia was required following the cutaneous nerve block, was 87.0%. Ten patients (12.9%) required supplemental anesthesia (Table 3). Of the 10 cases with a poor analgesic effect, seven experienced pain during balloon expansion in the distal forearm or at or immediately above the anastomotic site of the snuffbox AVF. In these cases, supplemental local anesthesia was administered under ultrasound guidance using approximately 1 mL of 1% lidocaine per stenotic site, injected dorsally to the vessel. Two cases involved patients who were unable to maintain a comfortable position due to back pain, requiring only a lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve block to shorten the procedure time without a prior superficial branch of the radial nerve block. One case involved a patient with a thrombotic occlusion who developed vasculitis.

Discussion

This case series highlights that cutaneous nerve block was rapidly administered (mean preparation time, 2.1 min) and achieved effective analgesia. The analgesic effect was rated as excellent or good in 68 of the 78 patients (87.2%), indicating a high rate of satisfactory pain control. This technique achieves analgesia without inducing motor impairment. These findings suggest the potential for efficient and comfortable procedures in this patient population, contributing to improved patient experience and procedural workflows for vascular access management.

While traditional local infiltration of lidocaine around a stenosed vessel is common, its analgesic efficacy can be inconsistent because of insufficient spread of anesthetic to the nerves adjacent to the vessel's inferior wall [10, 11]. Although ultrasound-guided perivascular local anesthetic injection can provide good analgesia, it requires repeated punctures for multiple stenoses and can complicate differentiation between minor perivascular changes caused by local anesthetic and actual vascular injury from balloon PTA. General anesthesia is typically avoided in patients with multiple comorbidities because of the high risk of cardiovascular and respiratory complications [13]. Intravenous moderate conscious sedation also poses risks for patients with end-stage renal disease who frequently present with multiple underlying conditions and systemic comorbidities [24]. In contrast, ultrasound-guided BPB, widely used in upper limb surgeries, provides effective analgesia with a high safety profile and minimal systemic effects [14, 15]. Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility and safety of BPB for dysfunctional AVF during PTA [13, 17, 18]. Although rare, complications, such as hemidiaphragmatic paresis or paralysis, dyspnea or oxygen desaturation, postoperative neurological symptoms, local anesthetic systemic toxicity, and pneumothorax, have been reported in patients receiving BPB. Moreover, BPB frequently induces temporary motor blockade, as evidenced by clinical signs of motor response and paralysis [19], raising concerns about its use in outpatient settings. Musculocutaneous nerve blocks, occasionally performed in conjunction with selected cutaneous nerve blocks, have been reported to offer analgesic efficacy comparable to that of BPB while potentially avoiding some of its severe complications [25, 26]. However, a musculocutaneous nerve block may cause motor blockade of the brachialis and biceps brachii muscles, which could limit elbow flexion. This study demonstrated that a cutaneous nerve block for PTA of forearm AVF stenosis allows rapid preparation and provides effective analgesia without motor impairment. This technique may represent a viable alternative to traditional anesthetic methods.

The findings concerning complications associated with cutaneous nerve blocks suggest a favorable safety profile for this technique. The minor hematomas detected by ultrasound in eight cases were subclinical and did not necessitate additional intervention, indicating that cutaneous nerve blocks are a relatively safe procedure in this context.

This study provides novel insights into the analgesic efficacy of different cutaneous nerve blocks. Notably, none of the five cases in which a superficial branch of the radial nerve block was performed required supplementary local anesthesia during the PTA procedure. Conversely, seven patients who exclusively received a lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve block, particularly those involving the distal forearm radiocephalic or snuffbox AVF, subsequently required additional local anesthetic. This observation strongly suggests that for distal forearm radiocephalic or snuffbox AVF, preprocedural ultrasound examination should consider the potential involvement of the superficial branch of the radial nerve, as its inclusion in the block protocol would likely obviate the need for supplemental local anesthesia during PTA. This finding highlights the importance of selecting a nerve block technique tailored to the anatomical location of the stenosis to optimize pain management.

Although a previous study reported that digital nerve blocks with 2% lidocaine alone provided a mean anesthetic duration of 4.9 hours [27], no published data are available on the duration of analgesia for cutaneous nerve blocks using 1% lidocaine. In our experience, however, the initial cutaneous nerve block consistently provided sufficient anesthesia throughout the PTA procedure, and no cases required a repeat block due to waning analgesia. Chiba et al. [17] reported a mean BPB preparation time of eight minutes; however, a considerable disadvantage of the axillary approach is the requirement for separate injections into individual target nerves. In the present study, the mean block preparation time was 2.1 minutes. This short-duration anesthesia is well-suited for outpatient PTA, as it eliminates the need for an additional waiting period before the procedure.

Using ultrasound guidance for AVF interventions offers several advantages compared with conventional fluoroscopy-guided intervention, including low cost, wide availability, and the avoidance of both radiation exposure and contrast agents, which is particularly important in patients with impaired renal function. Importantly, ultrasound allows immediate detection of vessel rupture and extravascular leakage during balloon dilatation, enabling rapid hemostasis by intravascular balloon compression [28]. Shimizu et al. [29] reported that, compared with infiltration anesthesia alone, the combination of ultrasound-guided infiltration anesthesia with selective cutaneous nerve block significantly improved analgesic effectiveness during PTA. This approach provides a high rate of successful analgesia and a favorable safety profile, with only minor, non-interventional complications observed. In contrast, our study focused on evaluating the clinical utility of cutaneous nerve block for PTA in forearm AVF stenosis. Among 77 cases, additional infiltration anesthesia was required in only 10 cases (13%), while the majority of procedures were successfully managed with cutaneous nerve block alone. Unlike infiltration anesthesia, cutaneous nerve block does not directly affect the stenotic site and, therefore, may not interfere with the early detection of vascular injury during PTA.

This study has some limitations. Despite these promising findings, its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size limited the generalizability of the results and prevented direct comparative analyses. Furthermore, because this study did not include a control group, it remains unclear whether the observed pain control was truly attributable to the cutaneous nerve block. Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts, potentially including randomized controlled trials, to directly compare cutaneous nerve blocks with conventional local infiltration or other regional anesthetic techniques in terms of efficacy, patient satisfaction, and long-term outcomes across various AVF locations. In this study, most patients (87.2%) experienced satisfactory pain control. However, the factors associated with inadequate pain control remain unclear. We encountered a case of AVF occlusion complicated by thrombophlebitis. As previously reported by Fujita et al. [30], when thrombophlebitis associated with AVF occlusion causes severe pain even upon light palpation of the affected area, the analgesic effect of cutaneous nerve block tends to be limited. In our case, subsequent thrombus aspiration led to rapid resolution of phlebitis-associated pain, and no pain was observed during the following PTA.

Conclusion

This case series highlights cutaneous nerve block as an efficient and effective method for pain management during forearm AVF PTA, demonstrating rapid administration, a high rate of successful analgesia, and a favorable safety profile with only minor non-interventional complications. A key insight is the importance of including a superficial branch of the radial nerve block when treating distal forearm radiocephalic or snuffbox AVF to achieve optimal analgesia. Further studies are warranted to establish targeted nerve blocks as a standard of care in interventional radiology for vascular access.
