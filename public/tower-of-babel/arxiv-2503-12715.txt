Renormalization of Schrödinger equation for potentials with inverse-square singularities: Generalized Trigonometric Pöschl-Teller model
U. Camara da Silva

Abstract
We introduce a renormalization procedure necessary for the complete description of the energy spectra of a one-dimensional stationary Schrödinger equation with a potential that exhibits inverse-square singularities. We apply and extend the methods introduced in our recent paper on the hyperbolic Pöschl-Teller potential (with a single singularity) to its trigonometric version. This potential, defined between two singularities, is analyzed across the entire bidimensional coupling space. The fact that the trigonometric Pöschl-Teller potential is supersymmetric and shape-invariant simplifies the analysis and eliminates the need for self-adjoint extensions in certain coupling regions. However, if at least one coupling is strongly attractive, the renormalization is essential to construct a discrete energy spectrum family of one or two parameters. We also investigate the features of a singular symmetric double well obtained by extending the range of the trigonometric Pöschl-Teller potential. It has a non-degenerate energy spectrum and eigenstates with well-defined parity.

Introduction

Physical systems whose properties are described by stationary Schrödinger equations with potentials exhibiting inverse-square singularities present a challenging task. Depending on the values of the parameters, the proper definition of the Schrödinger operator as a self-adjoint one and the proof of the existence of normalizable solutions require the application and further development of analytical methods, including appropriate regularization, the introduction of dynamical scales, and a renormalization procedure. This approach provides a systematic description of the changes in the energy spectrum properties and the corresponding eigenstates across different regions of the coupling space.

The present paper provides a complete solution to all these problems for the generalized trigonometric Pöschl-Teller (TPT) potential

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}V(x)\equiv\mathcal{V}(x)=\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{g_{s}}{\sin^{2}(\alpha x)}+\frac{g_{c}}{\cos^{2}(\alpha x)}\right),\ 0<x<\frac{\pi}{2\alpha},

2.1
Coupling space

The energy eigenstate of the model must be square-integrable, that is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}}\left|\psi_{k;\nu_{s},\nu_{c}}(x)\right|^{2}dx<\infty.

(2.12)

From the asymptotic behaviors (
2.4

2.7

17
]

g=g_{s},g_{c}

i)

Strong-repulsive coupling
-

ii)

Weak-medium coupling
-
-1/4<g<3/4
(

): Both solutions are square-integrable. The condition (
2.12
) does not impose any restrictions, and, a priori, there is no clear criterion to relate

and

. This fact is related to the Hamiltonian (

) not being self-adjoint in this case, although it has a bounded below self-adjoint extension,
[
2
]
.

iii)

Strong-attractive coupling
-
g<-1/4
,
\nu\rightarrow i|\nu|
: The eigenstate strongly oscillates near the singularity without converging to a defined value. This case violates the famous BF bound that delimits the stability of fields in AdS

space
[

,
21
]
, since, in addition to the Hamiltonian not being self-adjoint, it is unbounded below and thus has no positive self-adjoint extension (

]
. On the other hand, the renormalization performed in Section

has nine parts. However, they are equivalent, so the plane is symmetric along the line

2.2
Supersymmetry and boundary conditions

A Hamiltonian is SUSY when it can be rewritten as a product of conjugate operators,
[
24
,
25
]
.

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\hat{H}\equiv\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}+k_{0}^{2},\,k_{0}^{2}=\frac{2mE_{0}}{\hbar^{2}}.

(2.13)

Here,
E_{0}
is the ground state energy, and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\frac{d}{dx}+\mathcal{W}(x),\ \ \hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}=-\frac{d}{dx}+\mathcal{W}(x).

(2.14)

The
superpotential

\mathcal{W}(x)
is a real function related to the potential through the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}(x)=\mathcal{W}^{2}(x)-\frac{d\mathcal{W}(x)}{dx}+k_{0}^{2}.

(2.15)

Defining the
superpotential
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x)=-\alpha\left(\nu_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\cot(\alpha x)+\alpha\left(\nu_{c}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\tan(\alpha x),

(2.16)

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}(x)=\left(\nu_{s}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\alpha x)}+\left(\nu_{c}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\cos^{2}(\alpha x)},

(2.17)

\displaystyle k_{0}^{2}=-\alpha^{2}(\nu_{s}+\nu_{c}+1)^{2},

exactly the potential (
1.1
), see equation (
2.3
).
A SUSY Hamiltonian has a partner defined by reversing the order of the operators
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}
, that is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}=\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}+k_{0}^{2}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\mathcal{V}_{2}(x)=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\mathcal{W}^{2}(x)+\frac{d\mathcal{W}(x)}{dx}+k_{0}^{2}.

(2.18)

A crucial feature of the TPT is that it is
shape invariant
. This means that
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
has the same form as
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, but with shifted parameters. Indeed, a direct calculation shows that the partner potential associated with the superpotential (
2.16
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2}(x)=\left((\nu_{s}+1)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\alpha x)}+\left((\nu_{c}+1)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\cos^{2}(\alpha x)},

(2.19)

which is the potential (
2.17
) with the translations
\nu_{s}\rightarrow\nu_{s}+1
e
\nu_{c}\rightarrow\nu_{c}+1
and
\nu_{c}\rightarrow\nu_{c}+1
. Shape invariance can be used as a criterion to fix the boundary conditions of the problem,
[
26
]
. If the couplings are initially in the pink or brown regions of Figure
2
, the partner model must necessarily be in the red region, where
B_{\nu_{s}+1}(k)=B_{\nu_{c}+1}(k)=0
. On the other hand, SUSY also relates the eigenfunctions through the action of
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{\dagger}
(
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
), for example

\displaystyle\psi_{k;\nu_{s}+1,\nu_{c}+1}(x)\propto\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\psi_{k;\nu_{s},\nu_{c}}(x).

(2.20)

Using this result for the solution near the singularities,
x_{0}=0,\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}
, where
\mathcal{W}(x)\approx-(\nu+1/2)/(x-x_{0})
, the solution with the asymptotic behavior
B_{\nu}(k)|x-x_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}
leads to the undesirable (non-square-integrable) behavior
\sim|x-x_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}-(\nu+1)}
, that is

\displaystyle B_{\nu+1}(k)\propto B_{\nu}(k).

Since
B_{\nu+1}(k)=0
for the eigenfunction to be square-integrable, we have
B_{\nu}(k)=0
. This argument holds for both singularities. The conclusion is that the
shape invariance
of the potential fixes

\displaystyle B_{\nu_{s}}(k)=B_{\nu_{c}}(k)=0,

(2.21)

in the red, pink, and brown regions of Figure
2
. In these cases, starting from
k_{0}
and the
shape invariance
of the potential, the entire spectrum is determined trivially as

\displaystyle k_{n}^{2}=\frac{2mE_{n}}{\hbar^{2}}=\alpha^{2}\left(\nu_{s}+\nu_{c}+2n+1\right)^{2},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

(2.22)

3
The renormalization of the model

A way to make the boundary conditions well-defined at points where the potential has inverse-square singularities is through renormalization
[
10
]
. This approach is well-known for the exact conformal potential
[
10
,
11
,
14
,
27
,
6
]
, with results equivalent to those of a self-adjoint extension, and has also been applied to the hyperbolic Pöschl-Teller potential
[
17
]
. The main appeal of the renormalization process is its ability to relate the problem of boundary conditions to a spontaneous breaking of (asymptotic) conformal symmetry.

3.1
Renormalization procedure

Following the construction presented in
[
17
]
, we will adopt an approach that does not require defining boundary conditions at singular points and does not require explicitly choosing a regulator potential. First, the original potential, singular at
x_{0}
(from the right or left), is regularized by introducing a cutoff at
R=|x-x_{0}|
. A new well-defined potential in the interval
0<|x-x_{0}|<R
is introduced, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{R}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{V}(x)&|x-x_{0}|>R,\\
&\\
\frac{\lambda(R)}{R^{2}}f(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{R})&0<|x-x_{0}|<R.\end{array}\right.

The arbitrary function
f(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{R})
, with
f(1)=1
, is non-singular at the origin - usually chosen as a step function,
[
10
,
27
,
16
,
13
,
14
]
, but it is not necessary to specify it. For the renormalization process to provide
R
-independent physical results, we assume that
R
is a length scale smaller than the others involved in the system, in this case,
1/\alpha
and
1/k
. Thus:

\displaystyle\alpha R\ll 1,\ kR\ll 1\ (k\neq 0).

(3.5)

The first condition is necessary for the potential to be approximated by
\mathcal{V}(x)\approx g/(x-x_{0})^{2}
near the cutoff, and the second condition will be used in the following calculation. Equation (
2.1
) in the regularized region,
0<u=|x-x_{0}|<R
, becomes (the wave function will be denoted as
\psi_{k,<}
)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\psi_{k,<}(u)}{du^{2}}+\left(\frac{(kR)^{2}-\lambda(R)f(u/R)}{R^{2}}\right)\psi_{k,<}(u)=0.

(3.6)

Neglecting the term
kR
, since
kR\ll 1
, we obtain the equation for
k=0
. Therefore, the wave function inside the regularized region does not depend on
k
and coincides with the
k=0
case. Thus

\displaystyle\frac{\psi^{\prime}_{k,<}(u)}{\psi_{k,<}(u)}\Bigg|_{u=R^{-}}=\frac{\psi^{\prime}_{0,<}(u)}{\psi_{0,<}(u)}\Bigg|_{u=R^{-}}\equiv\mathcal{F}(R),

(3.7)

where
\mathcal{F}(R)
is independent of
k
. By the continuity of the wave function and its first derivative, the result must be the same when taken from outside the cutoff,
\psi^{\prime}_{k}(u)/\psi_{k}(u)|_{u=R^{+}}=\mathcal{F}(R)
. That is, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\psi_{k}(u)}{du}-\mathcal{F}(R)\psi_{k}(u)\right)\Bigg|_{u\rightarrow R^{+}}=0,

(3.8)

and the renormalization process effectively consists of imposing a Robin boundary condition at the regularization point
|x-x_{0}|\rightarrow R^{+}
, without the need to specify boundary conditions inside the inaccessible region
|x-x_{0}|<R
. The form of the function
\mathcal{F}(R)
depends on the value of the coupling.

3.2
Weak-medium coupling:
-1/4<g<3/4
(
0<\nu<1

5
Symmetric double-well trigonometric Pöschl-Teller potential

The renormalization of the TPT model introduces families of up to two parameters of energy eigenstates. Since the renormalized Hamiltonian is self-adjoint, each family of eigenstates forms an orthogonal basis of the Hilbert space in the interval
0<x<\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}
. Extending the potential to the interval
-\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}<x<\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}
, we have a symmetric well, so its eigenstates must have well-defined parity around the origin,
[
28
]
. What distinguishes the TPT well from a usual symmetric well is the presence of a singularity at
x=0
, making the construction of the energy spectrum non-trivial. Simply extending an already determined family of eigenfunctions with even and odd parity would only create a duplication with a degenerate spectrum that does not form a basis of the extended Hilbert space. To have a basis in the new interval, we need to renormalize each side of the singularity at the origin independently, i.e., there is a renormalization for the interval
-\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}<x<0^{-}
and another for
0^{+}<x<\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}

Discussion

In this article, we systematically study the renormalization procedure for the one-dimensional stationary Schrödinger equation with a potential exhibiting two inverse-square singularities. The method was applied to the trigonometric Pöschl-Teller potential, defined between two singularities of this type. The renormalized energy spectrum was determined across the entire
g_{s}-g_{c}
coupling plane, generating various regions with distinct properties, as shown in Figure
2
. The fact that the TPT potential is SUSY and shape-invariant simplifies the analysis, and we have to fix Dirichlet boundary conditions at both singularities when
g_{s},g_{c}\geq-1/4
, as illustrated in Figure
3
, showing that there is no need for a self-adjoint extension in this region of the coupling space, as done in
[
2
]
. On the other hand, when at least one of the couplings is in the strong-attractive region,
g<-1/4
, the naive use of the boundary conditions leads to an imaginary spectrum, so the Hamiltonian is not self-adjoint. In these cases, renormalization is necessary and generates a family of eigenstates and eigenvalues dependent on one or two parameters, which introduce anomalous scales via dimensional transmutation, breaking the asymptotic conformal symmetry,
[
30
,
31
]
. The spectrum is discrete but without a lower bound since at least one coupling is on the
strong-attractive
regime. In general, the bound states are determined by complicated transcendental relations with graphical solutions, but for deep states, where
\kappa/\alpha\gg 1
, they always converge to the
Efimov Rule
2
2

2

The exception occurs when
g_{s},g_{c}<-1/4
, where there is competition between the two singularities, and we were unable to identify a pattern in the spectrum.
,
\kappa_{n+1}/\kappa_{n}=e^{\frac{\pi}{|\nu|}}
. That means that deep bound states have wave functions localized near
x=x_{s}
, so the potential is effectively conformal,
\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}V(x)=g/(x-x_{s})^{2}

In our previous work,

17

Thus, the HPT continuous spectrum, anti-bound states, and resonance modes, characterized by at most one parameter, are destroyed, and the TPT model has only bound states. On the other hand, the presence of two singularities implies that the two independent renormalization processes lead to a richer structure with an energy spectrum that can depend on one or two parameters.

The renormalization of the TPT model presented in this article has numerous applications to modern theoretical physics problems. A specific case we are interested in is the dynamics of free scalar, vector, and tensor fields in
