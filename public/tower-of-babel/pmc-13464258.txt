Phenotypic Spectrum of Cardiac Involvement in Eosinophilic Granulomatosis With Polyangiitis
Authors: Zoha Majeed, Alexandra S. Manaila, Bo Xu, Karlee Hoffman, Andres Carmona Rubio, Heba Wassif, Alexandra Villa Forte, Maria Mountis, Arianne Clare C. Agdamag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac involvement in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality and may occur independently of systemic disease activity.Case SummaryWe describe 3 patients with EGPA demonstrating distinct cardiac phenotypes. Patient presentation included: 1) inflammatory cardiomyopathy with severe biventricular dysfunction that improved following escalation of immunosuppression and guideline-directed medical therapy; 2) advanced nonischemic cardiomyopathy and cardiogenic shock requiring temporary and durable mechanical circulatory support, representing an irreversible fibrotic phenotype; and 3) relapsing-remitting eosinophilic perimyocarditis and recurrent ventricular dysfunction despite multiple immunosuppressive regimens. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and hemodynamic assessment were central to phenotypic classification and management.DiscussionEGPA cardiomyopathy exists along a spectrum from reversible inflammatory myocarditis to advanced fibrotic remodeling. Cardiac involvement may progress despite improvement in systemic manifestations and is not reliably predicted by antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody status.Take-Home MessagePhenotype-based assessment and longitudinal cardiac surveillance may guide immunosuppressive strategies and prognosis in EGPA cardiomyopathy.

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), previously known as Churg-Strauss syndrome, is a rare, systemic vasculitis that is typically characterized by asthma, eosinophilia, and small- to medium-vessel inflammation.1 Cardiac-specific involvement in EGPA encompasses a broad spectrum, including eosinophilic myocarditis, chronic inflammatory cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, and coronary vasculitis to name a few.2 Cardiac involvement is more common in patients with antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-negative serologies and high eosinophil counts and is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality of this disease.3Take-Home Message•Phenotype-based assessment and longitudinal cardiac surveillance may guide immunosuppressive strategies and prognosis in EGPA cardiomyopathy.

The spectrum of cardiac involvement in EGPA varies widely and may be asymptomatic initially. Consequently, current guideline-based recommendations include checking cardiac biomarkers (Troponin T and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]), baseline electrocardiography, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) at the time of diagnosis regardless of cardiac symptoms.4 Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) should be considered when abnormalities are detected or when suspicion for myocardial involvement persists, given its high sensitivity for detecting subclinical myocardial disease.5

Based on the 2021 American College of Rheumatology/Vasculitis Foundation guidelines, initial treatment includes high-dose steroids plus a remission induction therapy agent such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or mepolizumab, with evidence favoring rituximab or cyclophosphamide in active severe disease and mepolizumab or methotrexate in active nonsevere disease.1 Remission maintenance therapy includes all of the remission-inducing agents mentioned previously, except for cyclophosphamide, and with the additional option of omalizumab.1

Despite advances in immunosuppressive therapy, the prognosis of EGPA-related cardiomyopathy remains guarded, with acute eosinophilic myocarditis conferring a particularly poor prognosis.6 Early recognition and prompt initiation of multidisciplinary management are critical to improving outcomes in this patient population.

In this case series, we aim to further characterize the clinical spectrum, diagnostic challenges, and outcomes of EGPA cardiomyopathy, contributing to the evolving understanding of this complex disease entity.

Case Series

Patient 1

A 31-year-old man with worsening asthma and sinusitis intermittently responsive to steroids and antibiotics presented with peripheral neuropathy and hemoptysis. Workup revealed normal hemoglobin, marked eosinophilia (16.5%, absolute 3.5 × 109/L), elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP] 19.2 mg/dL and erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR] 30 mm/h), immunoglobulin E (1,639 kU/L), Troponin I (420 ng/L), and NT-proBNP (973 pg/mL). The electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia, right axis deviation, and a new right bundle branch block. Serologies were positive for cytoplasmic ANCA with myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels >8. Urinalysis revealed red blood cells and proteinuria without elevated creatinine. He underwent a biopsy of the petechial rash and transbronchial lung tissue that confirmed EGPA on histopathology.

Initial echocardiogram demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 60% with small to moderate pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology. He was treated with pulse steroids, plasmapheresis, and rituximab induction with improvement in symptoms.

Due to persistent tachycardia with rates in the 110s, a follow-up echocardiogram 6 months later was done that revealed new biventricular dysfunction with LVEF reduction to 20%. During his 4-month follow-up, his LVEF improved to 35% to 40%. He was transitioned to azathioprine maintenance therapy and tapered off steroids. On follow-up echocardiogram 4 months later, his LVEF improved further to 45% to 50%.

Patient 2

A 64-year-old man with history of recurrent deep venous thrombi in 2019 on apixaban therapy, adult-onset asthma, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes, and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (LVEF 12%) with defibrillator placement for primary prevention presented to the cardiology clinic. His heart failure was diagnosed in 2022 with a TTE showing an LVEF of 12% (from 53% in 2016), biventricular dysfunction and dilation along with mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. Left heart catheterization in 2022 revealed no obstructive lesions. He was admitted due to acute decompensated heart failure. Right heart catheterization on admission demonstrated a cardiac index of 1.94 L/min by Fick with elevated left ventricular filling pressures: right atrial pressure 10 mm Hg, pulmonary artery mean pressure 45 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 30 mm Hg. He was admitted to the heart failure intensive care unit with low output cardiogenic shock (NYHA functional class IV, SCAI Stage D shock), and initiated on sodium nitroprusside, dobutamine, and intravenous diuresis.

Admission labs revealed peripheral eosinophilia (17%, absolute 8.4 × 109/L), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 25.6 mg/dL and ESR 46 mm/h), NT-proBNP (9,428 pg/mL), and Troponin T (115 ng/L). He subsequently underwent placement of an intra-aortic balloon pump for temporary mechanical circulatory support, and an evaluation for advanced therapies was opened.

Unfortunately, the course was complicated by development of a large pericardial effusion adjacent to the right atrium measuring 3.0 cm seen on TTE for which he underwent pericardiocentesis with drainage of 750 mL of bloody fluid. The intra-aortic balloon pump was removed 4 days later due to malfunction with a plan for left ventricular assist device (LVAD) placement. In the operating room, median sternotomy revealed a pericardial effusion with hematoma, so the patient underwent partial stripping of inflamed pericardial rind with evacuation of hematoma. LVAD placement was deferred due to the presence of organizing fibrinous exudate and inflammatory pericarditis seen on pathology and an Impella 5.5 was placed for temporary mechanical circulatory support.

In consultation with the hematology/oncology and rheumatology teams, it was noted that the patient had a long-standing history of reactive adenopathy, mediastinal, inguinal, and iliac, since 2014. Urinalysis from 5 months prior revealed proteinuria and hematuria along with the presence of elevated eosinophils in the pleural (21%) and pericardial (13%) fluid. MPO levels were elevated to 822 pmol/L before admission.

After a multidisciplinary team discussion, given the high clinical suspicion of EGPA with cardiac involvement and high mortality of disease, the decision was made to proceed with pulse steroids (1 g intravenous methylprednisolone for 3 days followed by maintenance therapy with prednisone) while awaiting ANCA serologies. CMRI was deferred due to the presence of an Impella 5.5. Lab work revealed a positive cytoplasmic ANCA, and the patient underwent placement of an LVAD with HeartMate III. Myocardial biopsy showed no eosinophils, giant cells, granulomas, vasculitis, or active myocarditis; however, this was determined to be a false negative pathology in the setting of steroid therapy (Figure 3). The patient began to improve clinically. He was weaned off all inotropic therapy, maintained a good urine output, and was working with physical and occupational therapy so was subsequently transferred to the regular nursing floor. Follow-up right heart catheterization and LVAD ramp study revealed a cardiac index of 3.17 L/min by Fick, right atrial mean of 6 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 9 mm Hg.

The patient was discharged to acute rehab with plans for a prolonged prednisone taper and gradual outpatient titration of GDMT. He started mepolizumab therapy, which was later discontinued due to recurrent infections. He was listed for heart transplantation but was later inactivated due to frailty. Follow-up TTEs have shown appropriate left ventricular unloading and right heart catheterization showed preserved cardiac index and reduced left ventricular filling pressures. Titration of GDMT was limited due to hypotension, and he has been maintained on sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist.

This case exemplifies an advanced, fibrotic disease phenotype with pericardial involvement of EGPA-related cardiomyopathy presenting as cardiogenic shock with the need for durable mechanical support. It demonstrates the irreversibility of late-stage nonischemic cardiomyopathy in EGPA despite immunosuppressive therapy and sheds light on hemodynamic parameters in helping diagnose and prognosticate cardiac physiology.

Patient 3

A 72-year-old man with history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, abdominal aortic aneurysm, congestive heart failure (LVEF 53%), atrial fibrillation status post Maze procedure, and left atrial appendage clip, coronary artery disease status post coronary artery bypass grafting, aortic stenosis status post aortic valve replacement, and mitral valve regurgitation status post mitral valve replacement was admitted for evaluation of a petechial rash, generalized weakness, reduced oral intake, and lightheadedness.

The patient exhibited a petechial rash that began on bilateral lower extremities and spread caudally to legs, trunk, hands, and had constitutional symptoms of generalized fatigue and reduced appetite. He also had a new inability to abduct his left eye with associated diplopia on left lateral gaze.

Laboratory evaluation revealed marked leukocytosis with peripheral eosinophilia (56%, absolute 11.8 × 109/L), elevated Troponin T (706 ng/L), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 4.6 mg/dL, ESR 35 mm/h), and immunoglobulin E (2,432 kU/L) as well as positive antineutrophil antibody 1:80 (atypical speckled) and perinuclear ANCA, but negative MPO. Extensive infectious disease workup was unremarkable. A bone marrow biopsy was performed and was negative for malignancy or lymphoproliferative disorder. A skin biopsy was performed showing perivascular inflammatory infiltrate with eosinophilic predominance, consistent with EGPA. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed multiple acute punctate infarcts in a distribution suggestive of embolic events. Magnetic resonance angiography of the brain did not demonstrate vasculitis involving cerebral arteries. The infarcts were attributed to cardioembolic phenomena, likely secondary to intracardiac thrombus formation in the setting of severe hyper-eosinophilia and associated prothrombotic state. This hypothesis was supported by transcranial Doppler ultrasound, which detected 15 micro-embolic events over a 30-minute period, consistent with ongoing cerebral embolization.

The patient was given pulse dose steroids (intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g for 3 days) followed by a steroid taper. On repeat laboratory evaluation, his eosinophil count improved to <0.03. Following steroid initiation, the patient reported significant improvement in his fatigue.

A TEE showed a decrease in LVEF from 53% to 40%, raising suspicion for myocardial involvement with EGPA. CMRI showed LVEF of 32% and epicardial enhancement involving the mid-anterior wall and apical segments appearing to extend to the overlying pericardium suggestive of perimyocarditis (Figure 4). Repeat TTE performed after 5 days of steroids revealed an improvement in LVEF to 45%. The patient continued cyclophosphamide until remission then switched to rituximab. The patient eventually developed progressive left ventricular dysfunction secondary to relapsing eosinophilic myocarditis in the setting of recent prednisone taper and transition to rituximab therapy. TTE showed a new reduction in LVEF to 25%. He was treated with pulse dose steroids and restarted on cyclophosphamide.

Several months later, the patient developed cyclophosphamide toxicity and was subsequently switched to methotrexate. Two years later, he developed worsening rash and EGPA was not well controlled, thus rituximab infusion was resumed. Shortly after, the patient was hospitalized for combined septic and cardiogenic shock. Given his frailty, he was deemed to be a poor candidate for further rituximab infusions and was started on mycophenolate mofetil while methotrexate was discontinued.

This case showcases relapsing-remitting eosinophilic perimyocarditis in EGPA-related cardiomyopathy. Discussion

Cardiac-specific involvement in EGPA has been recognized as a major determinant of morbidity and mortality. Systematic cardiac evaluation is the cornerstone of diagnostic evaluation as clinically silent involvement is common.7,8

Our case series illustrates that EGPA cardiomyopathy does not represent a single clinical entity but rather a spectrum of inflammatory to fibrotic myocardial disease, describing heterogeneous cardiac phenotypes including occult perimyocarditis, inflammatory eosinophilic cardiomyopathy, pericardial disease, and relapsing-remitting disease with variable outcomes depending on disease stage.2,7, 8, 9 EGPA-associated cardiac-specific involvement may be best approached through a phenotype-based framework in which diagnosis and management are guided by the dominant phenotypical pattern rather than systemic disease features alone.2,7, 8, 9 Although no formal phenotype-driven treatment algorithms exist, available data suggest that inflammatory-predominant phenotypes may demonstrate reversibility with early and intensified immunosuppression, whereas advanced fibrotic disease is less responsive and often progresses to refractory heart failure.2,8,9 Our cases support this framework, where our first patient who was identified during an inflammatory phase experienced near-complete recovery following escalation of immunosuppression, whereas chronic eosinophilic inflammation was associated with irreversible remodeling and need for advanced heart failure therapies in the second case. These observations suggest that phenotypic characterization has direct therapeutic relevance, influencing both immunosuppressive strategy and expectations for myocardial recovery.

Cardiac involvement in EGPA may evolve independently of systemic disease activity and is not reliably predicted by ANCA status or eosinophil burden at a single time point. Several studies have reported higher prevalence and severity of cardiac involvement among ANCA-negative patients, suggesting distinct pathogenic mechanisms underlying myocardial injury in EGPA.10,11 However, the patients in our series developed clinically significant cardiomyopathy despite ANCA positivity, highlighting that ANCA status and eosinophilia alone do not reliably stratify cardiac risk. Despite this, ANCA status and eosinophilia remain helpful tools in assessing overall systemic involvement in EGPA.12 In our cohort, myocardial involvement either progressed or relapsed, despite improvement in respiratory symptoms, normalization of eosinophil counts, and initial response to immunosuppression, underscoring the limitations of symptom-driven or laboratory-based cardiac assessment. These observations reinforce the need for dedicated and longitudinal cardiac surveillance in EGPA, particularly in patients with known or suspected cardiac involvement, even when systemic disease appears quiescent.13

CMRI plays an important role in identifying the extent of myocardial involvement in EGPA cardiomyopathy. A prior contemporary systematic review of ANCA-associated vasculitis demonstrated a high prevalence of myocardial fibrosis and inflammatory CMRI abnormalities, including late gadolinium enhancement, even in patients without overt cardiac symptoms or active systemic disease.14 A dedicated CMRI analysis in EGPA identified diverse patterns of myocardial involvement, often dissociated from baseline ventricular function and systemic disease activity.15 In our case series, CMRI proved central to phenotypic classification, identifying inflammatory-predominant disease with reversibility and relapsing eosinophilic perimyocarditis in patients. Collectively, these observations reinforce CMRI as a key tool for detecting subclinical myocardial involvement and informing treatment escalation in EGPA cardiomyopathy.

Conclusions

EGPA cardiomyopathy represents a unique spectrum of disease with varying phenotypic presentations. Our case series sheds light on the various presentations of disease, the utility of CMRI in detecting subclinical disease and guiding escalation of care, and the mismatch between extracardiac manifestations of EGPA and cardiac involvement in those patients. Selected patients may benefit from longitudinal cardiac imaging to detect disease activity and inform further management. Patients with EGPA cardiomyopathy are best served through multidisciplinary care, particularly in the realm of cardio-rheumatology, and our case series highlights the importance of coordination of care between various subspecialties. Future studies should investigate the role of phenotype-based algorithms in the diagnosis and treatment of EGPA cardiomyopathy.
