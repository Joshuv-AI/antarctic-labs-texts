New insights on Bronchial Artery Embolization (BAE) for hemoptysis: a systematic review
Authors: Paolo Ravetta, Marie Bruyneel, Michael Vouche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchial Artery Embolization (BAE) is a well-established, non-surgical treatment for hemoptysis. This systematic review aims to update the current literature on BAE for hemoptysis, focusing on indications, techniques, complications, clinical outcomes, and recurrence rates.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of studies published between May 2016 and July 2025. Eligible studies included patients who underwent BAE for hemoptysis, with more than 50 participants and detailed reporting on indications, techniques, complications, outcomes, and recurrence rates.ResultsThirty-two studies were included. The leading causes of hemoptysis were bronchiectasis (27%), post-tubercular sequelae (17.7%), tuberculosis (15.6%), and lung malignancies (15%).Technical success rates ranged from 64% to 100%, with a median technical success of 98.1% (IQR 91.2–100%). Immediate clinical success ranged from 77.3% to 100%, with a median of 94.0% (IQR 89.8–97.9%).Recurrence rates ranged from 5.2% to 55%. The pooled median recurrence rates were 10.6% (IQR 6.9–19.1%) at 6 months, 23.0% (IQR 19.7–28.3%) at 1 year, 29.8% (IQR 25.2–36.6%) at 2 years, and 34.6% (IQR 34.0–37.3%) at 4–5 years.Complications were predominantly minor (median 10.6%) while major complications occurred in 0–4.8% of cases.ConclusionBAE remains a highly effective and minimally invasive treatment for hemoptysis, with high success and low complication rates. Recurrence can be reduced through optimized imaging, CTA-guided planning, and tailored embolization techniques. A multidisciplinary, patient-centered strategy incorporating radiologic expertise and emerging AI tools may further enhance outcomes and prevent recurrence.Keywords: Hemoptysis, Bronchial artery embolization, Embolization technique, Systematic review, Recurrence, CTA

Introduction

Hemoptysis refers to the expectoration of blood originating from the lower respiratory tract [1, 2]. Since its first description by Rémy et al. in 1974, Bronchial Artery Embolization (BAE) has undergone remarkable evolution in terms of technique, safety, and therapeutic efficacy [3, 4]. It is now recognized as the primary non-surgical treatment for hemoptysis of all severities, particularly in severe or recurrent cases [4, 5].

The first comprehensive systematic review by Panda et al. (2017) summarized studies published between 1976 and May 2016, establishing the initial evidence base for BAE. Although several subsequent reviews have addressed this topic, none have replicated Panda et al.’s inclusion framework.

To expand upon this foundation, the present review systematically analyzes studies published from May 2016 to July 2025, focusing on the indications, procedural techniques, complications, clinical outcomes, and recurrence rates associated with BAE in patients with hemoptysis.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [6]. The PRISMA checklist was followed to ensure transparency and completeness, and the study selection process is illustrated in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) patients with hemoptysis who underwent bronchial artery embolization (BAE) for management; (2) studies including more than 50 patients; (3) reporting on indications, technique, complications, clinical outcomes, and recurrence rates; (4) full-text publications available in English; and (5) studies published from May 2016 onward

The protocol was registered prior to data extraction (PROSPERO ID: CRD42020194119).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients with hemoptysis and undergoing BAE for management; (2) studies with more than 50 patients; (3) indications, technique, complications, clinical outcomes and recurrence rates reported; (4) full-text publications in English available; (5) date of publications from May 2016 to July 2025.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Case reports, studies with less than 50 patients, review articles. (2) Duplicate studies reporting overlapping patient populations (the study with the largest sample size and longest follow-up was selected).

Information sources

Two authors (P.R. and M.V.) independently performed a systematic search of PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus, and Science Direct databases for studies published between May 2016 and July 2025. The reference lists of all retrieved studies were screened for additional eligible articles. The last search was conducted on July 16, 2025.

Search strategy

The databases were searched using the following Boolean string: ((“bronchial artery embolization“[Title/Abstract] OR “bronchial artery embolisation“[Title/Abstract] OR “transcatheter embolization“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“hemoptysis“[Title/Abstract] OR “hemoptysis“[MeSH Terms])) AND (“2016/05/01“[Date - Publication] : “2025/07/31“[Date - Publication]). This strategy combined synonyms for bronchial artery embolization and transcatheter embolization with the term hemoptysis (both as a MeSH term and free text), and limited results to studies published between May 2016 and July 2025.

Selection process

The initial search yielded 531 articles. After removing duplicates and excluding case series or review articles that did not meet inclusion criteria, 44 studies were selected for full-text review. Duplicate reports from the same population were identified by analyzing study period, institution, and authorship. To avoid bias, only the study with the largest sample size and longest follow-up was retained (examples: refs. 13, 56).

After eligibility screening and duplicate removal, 32 studies were included in the final synthesis [6–38]. Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Data collection process

Data extraction was performed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Erasme Hospital, Brussels, Belgium [39]. Extraction was conducted independently by two investigators (PR and MV); discrepancies were resolved by consensus. No automation tools were used.

Outcome definitions and mapping

Hemoptysis grading was based on prior systematic reviews [4]:

Mild: ≤50 ml per episode or < 100 ml per day.

Moderate: 100–300 ml per day.

Severe/massive: >300 ml per day, or associated with a hemoglobin drop > 1 g/dL, hematocrit decrease > 5%, respiratory failure (SpO₂ <60%), or hypotension (systolic < 90 mmHg).

Technical success was defined as successful catheterization and embolization of all identified abnormal arteries.

Immediate clinical success was defined as complete cessation or marked reduction of hemoptysis within 24 h after BAE or during the same hospitalization.

Recurrence was defined as clinically significant hemoptysis requiring medical intervention, hospital readmission, repeat embolization, or surgery. Recurrences were categorized as early (≤ 3 months) or late (> 3 months).

All outcomes were evaluated on a per-patient basis.

Where grading definitions differed, intermediate categories (e.g., “mild–moderate”) were created.

Recurrence was reported either as hemoptysis-free survival (Kaplan–Meier) or as the proportion of patients with recurrence within a specific follow-up period. For comparability, recurrence rates were extracted at fixed time points: 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, and 4–5 years.

Study risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of included observational studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). The overall certainty of evidence was evaluated according to the GRADE approach. Two reviewers (P.R. and M.V.) independently assessed study quality, resolving discrepancies by discussion with all authors.

Synthesis methods

Due to heterogeneity in study populations, etiologies, and follow-up durations, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Results were synthesized narratively and summarized in tabular format.

When aggregating data from all included studies, idiopathic bronchiectasis emerged as the leading cause of hemoptysis, accounting for 27% of cases. Post-tubercular sequelae and tuberculosis, historically predominant worldwide, particularly in Asian studies [1, 2] were the second and third most frequent causes, with incidences of 17.7% and 15.6%, respectively. Lung malignancies accounted for 15.0% of all indications.

Less common causes included pneumoconiosis, cystic fibrosis, sarcoidosis, inflammatory lung disease, and pulmonary infections. Rare etiologies comprised vascular malformations, Wegener’s granulomatosis, Behçet’s disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), iatrogenic causes, embolic events, and congenital cardiac disorders. The etiology was classified as idiopathic or cryptogenic in 5.2% of cases.

The predominance of tuberculosis and bronchiectasis as causes of hemoptysis, particularly in Asian studies, reflects regional variability related to endemic disease patterns. In contrast, in Western countries, lung malignancies, cystic fibrosis, and idiopathic bronchiectasis are more prevalent etiologies, consistent with previous reports [4, 40–43].

When data from studies conducted exclusively in Western countries [18, 19, 23, 26, 32, 34, 36, 38] were pooled, lung malignancies represented 26.4%, making them the leading cause of BAE, followed by lung infections (15.8%) and cystic fibrosis/sarcoidosis (20.6%). In this subgroup, tuberculosis and post-tubercular sequelae were implicated in fewer than 6% of cases.

Grade of hemoptysis

Bronchial Artery Embolization (BAE) was performed to manage massive hemoptysis in approximately 33% of patients. Several studies focused exclusively on individuals presenting with massive or life-threatening hemoptysis [12, 17, 31, 33, 34]. However, the majority of patients experienced mild to moderate hemoptysis, emphasizing the increasingly recognized role of angiographic evaluation and embolization even in less severe cases [16, 27, 44]. This trend highlights the procedure’s proven safety and efficacy in such contexts, as well as its superiority over conservative management in terms of clinical outcomes, survival, and recurrence rates [44].

Pre-procedural evaluation

Fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) and chest computed tomography (CT) are recognized as the most reliable methods for identifying the source and location of bleeding [3, 5, 45, 46].

While most studies included pre-procedural FOB, only two performed it systematically in all patients [12, 21, 37]. In other cases, FOB was used selectively for diagnostic assessment of hemoptysis and to maintain airway patency during selective intubation. Several studies, however, did not mention performing bronchoscopy [7, 8, 11, 14, 20, 22, 24, 25, 30, 34, 35].

Flexible bronchoscopy is recommended when CT findings are inconclusive, in cases of suspected endobronchial lesions, or in massive hemoptysis requiring airway evaluation—ideally within 24–48 h to maximize diagnostic yield [47–49].

Computed tomography (CT) was performed in all studies.

Thirteen studies systematically performed CT angiography (CTA) in all patients [8, 9, 11, 12, 16, 20–22, 25, 26, 28, 33, 34].

The distinction between CT and CTA was not specified in most reports [7, 13–15, 17–19, 23, 24, 27, 29–31, 35], although two specifically compared the diagnostic performance of pre-procedural CT versus CTA [10, 32].

It is important to note that most included studies were retrospective in nature and not specifically focused on pre-procedural assessments, such as CT or bronchoscopy, which may explain the limited data available in this area.

In recent years, updated recommendations have emphasized the use of CTA over non-contrast CT for pre-procedural evaluation [45–47, 50].

An optimal CTA protocol for hemoptysis should include aortic enhancement to identify the origin of bleeding from bronchial arteries (BA) and non-bronchial systemic arteries (NBSA), which together account for approximately 95% of cases. Visualization of the pulmonary arteries, involved in roughly 5% of cases, can be achieved in the same acquisition using split-bolus or extended-contrast techniques [49].

CTA has been shown to more effectively identify both the bleeding source and the responsible vessels compared to direct angiography [51]. This capability assists interventional radiologists in optimizing embolization strategies by precisely delineating the vascular anatomy, improving vessel orientation, and guiding the selection of vascular access, microcatheters, and embolic materials [2, 4, 52, 53].

Furthermore, CTA has been associated with reduced recurrence rates [10, 25, 51] and, although evidence remains limited, may also decrease radiation exposure, procedure time, and contrast use [54, 55].

Dual-energy CT angiography (DECTA) has recently emerged as a valuable tool in the assessment of hemoptysis, particularly for detecting systemic-to-pulmonary shunts (SPS), which play a key role in identifying the bleeding source [56].

In hemodynamically unstable patients with massive hemoptysis, direct embolization or surgery may be performed without prior CT if clinically necessary.

Technique of BAE

The femoral artery was the preferred access route in all reviewed studies. Two studies [11, 25] also reported the use of brachial or radial artery access.

Only three studies [32, 34, 36] mentioned femoral venous access for the evaluation or treatment of pulmonary arteries.

During angiography, a systematic search for non-bronchial systemic arteries (NBSAs)—originating from the subclavian, internal thoracic, or intercostal arteries—was explicitly described in two studies [21, 24], whereas twenty-six others relied on pre-procedural CTA for identification.

Aortography was inconsistently performed and sometimes omitted in the presence of high-quality pre-procedural CTA [8, 10].

The use of cone-beam CT (CBCT) during angiography has gained increasing attention. CBCT enhances the identification of bleeding sources, improves procedural planning, and potentially increases both the safety and efficacy of embolization. Its integration into clinical practice should be considered, especially in complex or inconclusive cases.

Embolizing agents

The selection of embolic agents varied according to operator preference and resource availability.

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles were the most commonly used material, typically ranging from 250 to 900 μm in diameter, with 300–500 μm and 500–700 μm being the most frequent sizes.

Several studies also used microspheres (350–700 μm) [12, 18, 21, 29], which, being hydrophilic and uniform in size, reduce the risk of catheter occlusion compared to PVA.

Gelatin sponge, used either alone [11] or in combination with other agents, was occasionally employed, though its propensity for recanalization may result in inferior long-term outcomes compared to PVA [57].

In BAE, it is customary to preserve the so-called “royal pathway” by avoiding complete occlusion of the bronchial artery origin. Regardless of the underlying etiology, recurrence due to neovascularization or recanalization can occur post-embolization. Therefore, super-selective catheterization and distal embolization—while maintaining proximal arterial patency—are recommended.

Some authors advocate the use of microparticles ≥ 700 μm, although this remains debated. For instance, Sheehan et al. achieved high technical and clinical success rates without major complications using smaller particles (< 300 μm) [36]. In cases involving bronchopulmonary shunts or cervical arterial anastomoses, larger particles (900–1,100 μm) may be required to prevent migration and the risk of pulmonary embolism or stroke.

Bronchial angiograms should be carefully reviewed to identify high-risk shunts or anastomoses. In situations with extensive shunting, marked bronchial angiogenesis, pseudoaneurysms, or arteriovenous malformations, non-particulate embolization techniques may be preferable.

Distal microcoils or liquid embolic agents (e.g., glue) are recommended in these settings.

Super-selective distal catheterization and coil deployment have demonstrated high efficacy with low recurrence rates [15, 28, 58].

The use of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA) has recently gained popularity, showing outcomes comparable to or better than particulate agents [4, 26, 59]. Although NBCA requires greater operator expertise due to the risk of necrosis and non-target embolization, it has shown advantages in reducing recurrence rates [30, 33], shortening procedure times, and lowering radiation exposure [30] compared to PVA or microspheres.

Technical success

Technical success rates ranged from 64% to 100%, with most studies achieving > 95%. The pooled median technical success was 98.1% (IQR 91.2–100%).

The main reasons for technical failure included catheterization difficulties (e.g., acute vessel angulation, tortuosity, dissection, or catheter instability). Some studies also cited arterial spasm, recanalized vessels, or absence of identifiable pathology. In rare cases, procedures were aborted due to patient instability or concerns regarding contrast or embolic agent overdose.

Outcomes of BAE

Immediate clinical success rates ranged from 77.3% to 100%, with most studies reporting success rates above 90%. The pooled median immediate clinical success was 94.0% (IQR 89.8–97.9%).

Predictors of clinical failure were related to patient condition (e.g., hemodynamic instability, elevated PTT >1.5, increased C-reactive protein), whereas embolization of multiple vessels was associated with higher success rates [25, 36].

Recurrence rates varied from 5.2% to 55%, consistent with previous reports [4].

Few studies reported early recurrence (within 2 weeks–3 months), with pooled medians of 10.6% (IQR 8.8–11.1%) at 1 month and 6.8% (IQR 6.0–13.4%) at 3 months.

At defined follow-up intervals, pooled recurrence rates increased over time: 10.6% (IQR 6.9–19.1%) at 6 months, 23.0% (IQR 19.7–28.3%) at 1 year, 29.8% (IQR 25.2–36.6%) at 2 years, and 34.6% (IQR 34.0–37.3%) at 4–5 years.

These findings confirm that while BAE achieves high immediate hemostatic control, the risk of recurrent hemoptysis progressively increases with longer follow-up (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Early recurrence was generally attributed to technical factors, such as incomplete embolization, failure to identify all feeding vessels, or omission of non-bronchial systemic arteries during the initial session.

Late recurrence was most commonly linked to recanalization of previously embolized arteries or recruitment of new vessels due to disease progression [4, 60].

Several clinical and angiographic factors were associated with higher recurrence rates.

Underlying conditions such as malignancy [19, 61, 62], aspergilloma [11, 23, 63–67], cavitary lesions [9, 13, 68], tuberculosis sequelae [27, 57], and sarcoidosis [68] were linked to greater recurrence risk.

Angiographic predictors included: (a) presence of non-bronchial systemic collaterals [13, 24, 27, 69] (associated with early recurrence); (b) bronchopulmonary shunting [13, 31, 67, 70–72]; and (c) incomplete initial embolization [4, 72].

Complications

Complications of BAE were categorized as either minor or major, following the SIR Standards of Practice Committee Classification of Complications by Outcome [50]. and CIRSE classification of complication [73].

Major complications ranged from 0% to 4.8% (median 1.6% IQR 0–4.8%) with the most frequent included stroke or transient ischemic events (Grade 3–4), paraparesis or paraplegia due to spinal cord ischemia (Grade 4–5), and, more rarely, non-fatal embolic events or procedure-related death (Grade 6).

Mean minor complications had a broader range, occurring in 0% to 94% of cases (median 10.6% (IQR 5.2–21.8%), and included symptoms such as chest pain, fatigue, cough or sore throat, shortness of breath, and transient dysphagia. Details regarding complications are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

Bronchial Artery Embolization (BAE) is firmly established as the leading non-surgical treatment for hemoptysis of all severities, achieving high technical and clinical success rates. Most studies report bleeding cessation in over 90% of cases, with a pooled median technical success of 98.1% (IQR 91.2–100%) and immediate clinical success of 94.0% (IQR 89.8–97.9%). These results are consistent with prior large-scale reviews and underline the safety and effectiveness of modern BAE protocols [4, 5]. However, recurrence remains a significant challenge, increasing over time due to disease progression, neovascularization, or incomplete initial embolization, reaching pooled medians of 10.6% at 6 months, 23.0% at 1 year, 29.8% at 2 years, and 34.6% at 4–5 years.

An exhaustive diagnostic work-up to identify the underlying pathology and vascular anatomy, combined with a meticulous embolization technique, is crucial for minimizing complications and achieving durable hemostasis. This review highlights a striking regional contrast: while tuberculosis and post-tubercular sequelae remain dominant etiologies in Asian series, malignancies account for approximately 29% of hemoptysis cases in Western studies, reflecting distinct regional epidemiologic profiles [1, 2, 4, 40–43]. Such patterns mirror broader respiratory disease trends and underscore the importance of region-specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, in large cosmopolitan European cities, a tuberculosis epidemiologic transition is emerging due to migration and population movements resulting in a doubling of prevalence even in low-endemic regions [74].

Pre-procedural imaging: from CT to CTA and DECTA

Pre-procedural computed tomography angiography (CTA) has become indispensable in the management of hemoptysis. It enables precise identification of bleeding sources, characterization of bronchial and non-bronchial systemic arteries (NBSAs), and detailed anatomical mapping that guides safe and effective embolization. The routine use of CTA improves embolization accuracy, reduces procedure time and radiation exposure, and enhances outcomes, particularly in complex vascular anatomies [45–47, 50].

Advanced imaging protocols, such as split-bolus or prolonged contrast administration, allow simultaneous visualization of bronchial, NBSA, and pulmonary arteries, improving detection of the full vascular network responsible for bleeding [49]. Dual-energy CT angiography (DECTA) has further advanced diagnostic capability by detecting systemic-to-pulmonary shunts (SPS), which are critical in identifying bleeding sources and understanding shunt physiology [56].

Cone-beam CT (CBCT) has also gained prominence as an intra-procedural adjunct, providing three-dimensional vascular roadmaps that improve catheter navigation, reduce non-target embolization, and optimize embolic material deployment. In hemodynamically unstable patients, however, direct embolization or surgical management without CT remains appropriate if clinically warranted.

Technical approach and embolic materials

BAE is typically performed via femoral arterial access, with brachial or radial routes occasionally employed. Super-selective distal embolization, while maintaining proximal bronchial patency (“royal pathway”) is widely recognized as a key technical principle to minimize ischemic complications and preserve re-access for future interventions.

The choice of embolic agent plays a pivotal role in both safety and recurrence rates. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles and microspheres remain the most frequently used materials, offering consistent and durable vessel occlusion. However, gelatin sponge, though inexpensive and easy to use, carries a higher risk of recanalization and subsequent rebleeding [57].

Recently, n-butyl cyanoacrylate (N-BCA) has shown superior performance in reducing recurrence rates and procedure times compared to traditional embolic materials [4, 26, 30, 33, 59]. Its rapid polymerization and strong adhesive properties ensure durable occlusion, particularly in high-flow or complex lesions, but require advanced operator skill to mitigate risks such as tissue necrosis or non-target embolization. Coils, vascular plugs, and liquid agents remain valuable adjuncts in the management of pseudoaneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and extensive bronchopulmonary shunts.

Outcomes and predictors of recurrence

Despite excellent immediate outcomes, recurrence remains an inherent limitation of BAE. Early recurrences (within 3 months) are often technical, stemming from incomplete embolization, missed feeders, or failure to address NBSAs, while late recurrences (>3 months) are typically driven by recanalization or disease progression [4, 60].

Multiple risk factors have been linked to recurrence, including malignancy [19, 61, 62], aspergilloma [11, 23, 63–67], cavitary lesions [9, 13, 68], tuberculosis sequelae [27, 57], and sarcoidosis [68]. Angiographic features such as bronchopulmonary shunting [13, 31, 67, 70–72], non-bronchial systemic collaterals [13, 24, 27, 69], and incomplete initial embolization [4, 72] further increase the risk of recurrence.

Comprehensive CTA-guided planning, systematic NBSA mapping, and staged or repeat embolization when required are key strategies to minimize recurrence.

Safety and complications

The safety profile of BAE is favorable, with minor complications, including chest pain, fever, and transient dysphagia, representing the majority of events (median 10.6%, IQR 5.2–21.8%). These typically reflect mild post-embolization syndrome and resolve spontaneously.

Major complications were infrequent (median 1.6%, IQR 0–4.8%) and primarily neurological, comprising stroke or transient ischemic events (CIRSE Grade 3–4) and paraparesis/paraplegia secondary to spinal ischemia (Grade 4–5). Rare cases of non-fatal embolic events or procedure-related death (Grade 6) have been reported [50, 73]. Preventive strategies include meticulous angiographic evaluation, awareness of spinal and cervico-thoracic collaterals, microcatheter positioning, and appropriate particle size selection (≥ 700 μm in shunt-prone anatomy).

Emerging role of artificial intelligence

Recent studies have introduced artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) into the field of BAE, with promising implications for recurrence prediction and procedural planning. Xu et al. developed an artificial neural network model that outperformed a traditional nomogram for predicting post-BAE recurrence [75]. Yan et al. and Duan et al. proposed prediction models and nomograms in non-cancer and bronchiectasis populations, respectively, demonstrating robust calibration and clinical utility [76, 77]. Beyond recurrence prediction, AI-assisted image analysis has been applied to CT and CBCT, improving vessel detection, segmentation, and image quality during BAE procedures [78, 79]. These advances suggest that AI/ML could enhance risk stratification, patient selection, and treatment personalization in future hemoptysis management (Table 6).

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review followed PRISMA guidelines, was registered on PROSPERO, and involved independent data extraction. It integrates data from the largest contemporary cohort of BAE studies (May 2016–July 2025), offering a comprehensive overview of techniques, materials, and outcomes [80]. Nevertheless, the findings are limited by the retrospective design of all included studies, heterogeneity in populations and techniques, and variable follow-up durations. Incomplete reporting of imaging details, complication grading, and recurrence definitions may have introduced bias. Finally, regional differences in tuberculosis prevalence and practice patterns limit the generalizability of pooled outcomes [81].

Conclusion

In conclusion, BAE remains the cornerstone of non-surgical management for hemoptysis, providing rapid and durable hemostasis with high success rates and low complication rates. Recurrence, while expected over time, can be mitigated through comprehensive imaging evaluation, CTA-guided NBSA mapping, and tailored embolization strategies [82]. A multidisciplinary and patient-specific approach, combining radiologic expertise, clinical risk assessment, and AI-assisted tools, may further optimize outcomes and help establish preventive strategies for recurrent hemoptysis in the future.
