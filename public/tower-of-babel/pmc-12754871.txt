Association of stroke with possible sarcopenic obesity and the modified effect from healthy lifestyle: a cross-sectional study based on CHARLS
Authors: Jinhui Song, Danchan Lu, Li Zhang, Yong Wang, Yanfei Wu, Shuchang Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenic obesity (SO), a metabolic disorder, may establish a bidirectional vicious cycle with stroke. However, the relationship between SO and stroke remains unclear. This study aims to analyze the association between these two diseases and to investigate the moderating effect of healthy lifestyle.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study database. Participants meeting the inclusion criteria were classified based on the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) criteria, and their history of stroke was recorded through questionnaires. The lifestyle assessments were based on four dimensions: smoking status, alcohol consumption, social engagement, and sleep duration. Logistic regression models examined the association between possible SO and stroke and the moderating role of a healthy lifestyle. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was performed.ResultsA total of 5,401 individuals were included in the study, 185 of whom were diagnosed with possible SO. Compared to the non-SO group, the SO group had significantly higher rates of diabetes mellitus, cardiac disease, stroke, fall history, and drug use. Logistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between possible SO and stroke (OR = 2.42, 95%CI: 1.43–4.11, P = 0.001). The association was weaker in the high score healthy lifestyle subgroup (OR = 2.38, 95%CI: 1.21–4.70, P = 0.01) than in the low score subgroup (OR = 2.43, 95%CI: 1.02–5.80, P = 0.045). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the results.ConclusionThis study provides evidence that possible SO is significantly associated with stroke. Adhering to a healthy lifestyle, including abstaining from smoking and alcohol consumption, engaging in social activities, and maintaining adequate sleep, might attenuate this association.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

Sarcopenic obesity (SO) is a metabolic disorder. The global prevalence of SO is approximately 10% among individuals over 60 years, increasing to 20% among those over 75 years [1, 2]. It is clinically characterized by decreased muscle strength, reduced physical activity, skin laxity, and visceral fat deposition. Moreover, it is often associated with insulin resistance and dyslipidemia [3, 4]. The development of SO is linked to the decline in muscle anabolism, chronic physical inactivity, insufficient protein intake, chronic inflammation, and endocrine disorders [5–7]. Notably, patients with SO have a significantly higher risk of cerebrovascular disease than others, as the considerable overlap in causative factors.

Stroke, the second leading cause of death globally, is potentially comorbid with SO [8]. Firstly, obesity is an important risk factor for ischemic stroke, as overweight individuals have a 22%−40% increased risk of stroke. Secondly, reduced muscle mass in SO patients leads to decreased glucose uptake capacity, further exacerbating hyperglycemia-induced vascular endothelial damage [9, 10]. Thirdly, SO patients often exhibit traditional risk factors such as hypertension and hyperlipidemia, forming a complex pathogenic network [11, 12]. Furthermore, numerous stroke patients exhibit long term paralysis and dysphagia, resulting in muscle atrophy and obesity [13, 14]. 28% of elderly stroke patients in the recovery phase exhibit SO [15]. These two disorders exacerbate each other, forming a vicious cycle that severely impairs life quality of patients. Clinical data demonstrate that SO patients exhibit limited recovery of motor function post-stroke, a 30% higher incidence of complications (e.g., pressure sores, dysphagia), and an increased risk of rehospitalization [16]. Therefore exploring the co-morbid links between these two diseases and identifying effective interventional treatments is essential.

In epidemiological studies of SO, the diagnosis of definite sarcopenia requires the concurrent presence of reduced muscle mass, decreased muscle strength, and impaired physical performance, as recommended by clinical guidelines. However, body composition assessments—essential for quantifying muscle mass—pose substantial challenges in large-scale community-based cohorts, primarily due to their high cost and operational complexity, which hinder widespread implementation. To address this limitation, the concept of "possible sarcopenia" has been proposed as a simplified screening metric. Its core lies in evaluating muscle function solely through muscle strength (e.g., handgrip strength), an approach explicitly supported by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) [17]. Notably, this definition of possible sarcopenia has been widely adopted in epidemiological research focusing on SO. For instance, Yu et al. [8]. and Jiang et al. [18]. explored the potential association of "possible SO" with cardiometabolic morbidity or cardiovascular disease, respectively, demonstrating the feasibility of this simplified metric in large-scale cohort studies. Consistent with these prior studies, the present research defines "possible SO" as the co-occurrence of obesity and possible sarcopenia (assessed via handgrip strength). This approach aims to strike a balance between the operational practicality required for large-scale community cohort studies and the validity necessary for robust health risk assessment.

Research has shown that cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption are independent risk factors of severe coronary artery calcification, which in turn increases the risk of cardiovascular events, including stroke [19]. Regarding social engagement, studies indicate that individuals with stronger social connections exhibit a significantly lower risk of stroke, while those experiencing social isolation or persistent loneliness face a markedly elevated risk [20]. Furthermore, poor sleep quality may augment the risk of SO and, either directly or indirectly, increase stroke risk by disrupting metabolic homeostasis and exacerbating chronic low-grade inflammation [21]. Therefore, adherence to a healthy lifestyle appears to be a promising strategy for mitigating SO and attenuating stroke risk. However, current evidence remains limited, and large-scale population-based studies investigating the association between SO and stroke are absent.

Against this backdrop, the present study sought to elucidate the association between possible SO and stroke and to assess the extent to which healthy lifestyles modify this association. The findings offer evidence-based, lifestyle-oriented recommendations for mitigating the co-occurrence of SO and stroke.

Methods

Data sources and study population

The data for this cross-sectional study were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database. This nationally representative health status tracking survey was led by Peking University and involved biennial or triennial interviews. CHARLS employed multistage stratified probability sampling and covered middle-aged and older populations aged 45 years and older across 28 provinces in China. The survey was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Specific data from CHARLS can be accessed at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en.

In the CHARLS 2015, 15,637 were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete information regarding body mass index (BMI), handgrip strength and stroke; (2) missing data on healthy lifestyle scores (including smoking, alcohol consumption, social activities, and sleep duration); (3) absence of basic demographic information; and (4) missing data on comorbidities. The study population screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Assessment of possible SO status

SO was defined as the coexistence of obesity and sarcopenia. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 according to the WHO criteria for Asian populations [22]. While skeletal muscle strength and mass are still considered the basis for a definitive clinical diagnosis, the AWGS introduced "probable sarcopenia" in 2019, defined as low muscle strength or low physical functioning, to allow for early lifestyle interventions in community settings [17]. So, for screening convenience, this study assessed muscle strength based on the handgrip strength. Participants stood while their maximum handgrip strength was measured twice with their dominant hand, and the mean value was recorded. Handgrip strength < 28 kg in men and < 18 kg in women was defined as possible sarcopenia. This definition adheres to the AWGS 2019 screening framework while adapting to the data availability of the CHARLS database (which lacks body composition measurements).

Participants were categorized into two groups based on the aforementioned criteria. The SO group met the criteria for both obesity and possible sarcopenia, while the control group did not meet the criteria for either obesity or possible sarcopenia.

Assessment of stroke events

Stroke events were identified based on questionnaire responses, including cerebral infarction and cerebral hemorrhage. Participants were asked, “Have you been diagnosed with Stroke by a doctor?” Those who answered “yes” were classified as having a history of stroke, whereas those who answered “no” were not.

Healthy lifestyle score

In this study, a healthy lifestyle was operationalized as not smoking, not drinking, engaging in social activities, and maintaining sufficient sleep. Non-smoking was defined as never having smoked or having quit smoking. Non-drinking of alcohol was defined as consuming alcohol less than once in the past year. Visiting friends, playing mahjong, cards, or attending community events in the past month was considered social activity participation. Sufficient sleep was defined as sleeping for a duration of at least 6 h per night. One point was assigned for each criterion met, with a total of four points possible, where higher scores indicated a healthier lifestyle. In this study, a healthy lifestyle score of 3–4 was classified as the high group, whereas a score of 0–2 was classified as the low group.

Covariates

Covariates that may affect SO and stroke were also included in this study. Covariates included age (> 60, ≤ 60), sex (male, female), residential (city, village), marital status (married living, married nonliving, never married, divorced, and widowed), level of education (incomplete primary, elementary, middle, high school and above), income, diabetes mellitus (yes, no), cancer (yes, no), cardiac disease (yes, no), gastropathy (yes, no), history of falls (yes, no), and drug use (yes, no).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%), and group differences were assessed using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group comparisons were performed using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. The association between possible SO and stroke was analyzed using logistic regression models, with stroke events as the outcome. Logistic regression models were then fitted to evaluate the effect of lifestyle on the SO-stroke relationship, stratified by healthy lifestyle scores into high (3–4) and low (0–2) subgroups. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 further adjusted for marital status, residential, income, medical history (diabetes, cancer, cardiac disease, and gastric disease), drug use, and fall down, building on model 2. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Finally, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the stability of the results. Missing values in continuous and categorical variables were imputed via Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), yielding five complete datasets. Subsequently, the identical multivariable-adjusted logistic regression model was fitted to each imputed dataset to estimate the association between sarcopenic obesity (SO) and stroke, which adjusted for age, sex, residence, marital status, education level, income, diabetes, cancer, cardiac disease, gastric disease, history of falls, and medication use. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1), with two-sided P < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Using CHARLS data, this study represented systematic investigation of the association between possible SO and stroke, as well as the moderating role of healthy lifestyle factors. The results revealed a significant co-morbid association between possible SO and stroke. Meanwhile, the healthy lifestyle exerted a modifying effect on the SO-stroke association. The link between the two diseases was reduced within individuals who adhered to non-smoking, non-alcohol consumption, active socialization, and sufficient sleep. Comprehensive lifestyle interventions may partially alleviate the pathological effects of possible SO and stroke.

The results of this study are consistent with previous evidence of SO-related metabolic risk. Studies indicated that the serum triglyceride-glucose index was higher in patients with SO than in those with obesity or sarcopenia alone [22]. SO may increase the risk of cerebrovascular endothelial injury by aggravating insulin resistance and lipid metabolism dysregulation [22, 23]. A cohort study further confirmed that patients with SO had a 28%−39% higher risk of cardiovascular events compared to the non-SO population [18]. Similarly, reduced limb mobility due to neurologic impairment exacerbated SO development in stroke patients. Long-term follow-up of acute stroke patients revealed that SO incidence was elevated 3 years post-onset compared to healthy controls and was positively correlated with the severity of neurologic impairment. Improving activities of daily living and addressing dysphagia during the recovery period effectively reduced SO incidence in stroke patients [15, 24, 25].

The association between possible SO and stroke may be mediated through multiple pathologic pathways. Firstly, obesity-induced visceral fat accumulation promotes vascular endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerotic plaque formation, and increased platelet aggregation [26]. Evidence indicates that individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 have a 59% increased risk of stroke, with higher risks observed in abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 85 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women) [27, 28]. Second, the increased cardiac output and decreased vascular compliance in SO patients amplify the effects of blood pressure fluctuations on cerebral micro circulation, predisposing them to cerebral hemorrhage [29]. Furthermore, impaired muscle pump function in the lower limbs of SO patients results in venous stasis, increasing the risk of stroke due to deep vein thrombosis dislodgement [30]. Similarly, prolonged bed rest results in muscle atrophy in stroke patients. The over activation of the ubiquitin—proteasome system accelerates muscle proteolysis [31]. Besides, activation of the hypothalamic—pituitary—adrenal axis and elevated cortisol levels contribute to ectopic adipose tissue deposition in stroke patients [32]. Post-stroke swallowing disorders and gastrointestinal dysfunction commonly result in inadequate protein intake. Stress hyperglycemia exacerbates insulin resistance, creating a vicious cycle of “muscle loss—fat accumulation” that ultimately results in SO [33, 34].

The results of this study demonstrated that healthy lifestyle practices attenuate the co-morbid association between possible SO and stroke. In the first place, smoking stands as an independent risk factor for stroke. In patients with possible SO, smoking interacts with muscle mass loss and fat deposition. Smoking cessation can mitigate metabolic disorders and aid SO patients in controlling BMI [35]. Secondly, excessive alcohol consumption correlates with hypertension, hypercoagulability, and diminished cerebral blood flow. Abstaining from alcohol also indirectly lowers stroke risk in SO patients by reducing inflammatory factors and enhancing vitamin D levels [36]. Moreover, social activity confers protective effects through behavioral and psychological mechanisms. Socially active individuals exhibit increased physical activity energy expenditure, directly promoting muscle maintenance and lipolysis. Simultaneously, social interaction diminishes the risk of loneliness—induced binge eating and ameliorates oxidative stress and chronic inflammatory states [37]. Lastly, sleep deprivation or poor quality (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea) is significantly linked to both SO and stroke. Adequate sleep preserves growth hormone secretion rhythms, promotes muscle protein synthesis, and regulates appetite. Sleep interventions also effectively reduce cortisol secretion and maintain blood pressure stability, thereby controlling stroke events [21, 38]. Given that SO and stroke predominantly affect middle-aged and older populations with elevated basal metabolic disorders, the benefits of lifestyle improvements may manifest more readily. Comprehensive lifestyle management exhibits greater clinical relevance in moderating possible SO and stroke compared to single-dimensional interventions.

Using a large-scale sample spanning urban and rural middle-aged and elderly populations, this study provides a new viewpoint for the synergistic management of possible SO and stroke. In clinical practice, emphasis should be placed on assessing muscle strength and obesity indices in stroke patients to facilitate the early identification and prevention of SO. Patients with possible SO should be encouraged to adopt a healthy lifestyle incorporating resistance training to remain vigilant regarding stroke events.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, owing to the cross-sectional observational design, the study precludes causal inferences. The self-reported questionnaires may engender recall bias. Consequently, prospective intervention trials that utilize objective medical records instead of self-reported questionnaires are warranted to address these limitations. Secondly, the absence of physical-performance metrics and direct quantification of skeletal muscle mass may have resulted in an underestimated prevalence of SO. Moreover, individuals who succumbed to severe stroke were likely under-represented. Consequently, future investigations should utilise comprehensive, objective assessments to corroborate these results. Thirdly, the analysis failed to explicitly adjust for established SO and stroke risk factors, including malnutrition, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and dyslipidemia. These omissions potentionally compromise the external validity of the findings and warrant cautious interpretation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes a significant correlation between possible SO and stroke. A healthy lifestyle, characterized by non-smoking, non-alcohol consumption, active socialization, and adequate sleep, may partially attenuate this association. These findings offer novel insights for preventing possible SO and stroke and guide clinical identification of high-risk comorbidity groups at an early stage.
