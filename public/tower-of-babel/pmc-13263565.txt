Interlaminar versus sublaminar lumbar decompression: a retrospective study on patient satisfaction as measured by PROMIS outcomes
Authors: Joseph P. Cavataio, Nadeed Sadique, Ali Mehaidli, Obed B. Barkus, Alqasim Elnaggar, Elizabeth Turner, Ryan Berger, Kevin Taliaferro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterlaminar and sublaminar decompressions are evolving alternatives to traditional lumbar laminectomy for managing symptomatic lumbar stenosis, but comparative patient-reported outcomes remain underexplored. This study evaluates differences in Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) pain interference and physical function (PF) scores between interlaminar and sublaminar lumbar decompression.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 74 patients (18–85 years) who underwent lumbar decompression without fusion at a Level I Trauma Center from January 2022 to March 2024. Patients were categorized by decompression type per operative reports. PROMIS pain and PF scores were collected preoperatively and at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Linear mixed-effects models assessed changes from baseline, adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), surgical levels, and operative time.ResultsSublaminar decompression demonstrated greater improvements from baseline in PROMIS PF scores compared to interlaminar decompression at 2 weeks (mean difference: +7.84), 4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. Similarly, PROMIS pain scores improved more in sublaminar patients at 2 weeks and 6 months, with interlaminar patients showing a smaller reduction [mean difference in pain improvement: −4.27; standard error (SE): 1.99; P=0.03]. Absolute PROMIS pain and PF scores, however, did not significantly differ between the two techniques (pain: P=0.93; PF: P=0.23). A significant interaction was observed between BMI and surgical type for pain outcomes: in sublaminar decompression, every 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI above the cohort mean (29.3 kg/m2) was associated with a +0.79-point increase in PROMIS pain score (P=0.002), while interlaminar decompression demonstrated no BMI dependency.ConclusionsSublaminar decompression was associated with greater improvements in PROMIS pain and PF scores compared to interlaminar decompression, although absolute postoperative scores were similar. BMI was a significant modifier of pain outcomes in sublaminar patients. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Key findings

• Sublaminar decompression was associated with greater improvements from baseline in Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System pain and physical function scores compared to interlaminar decompression.

• Body mass index (BMI) was a significant modifier of pain after sublaminar decompression, with higher BMI leading to higher pain scores.

• Absolute postoperative scores were similar.

What is known and what is new?

• Sublaminar and interlaminar decompressions can both provide adequate enlargement in thecal sac cross-sectional area.

• Differences in patient-reported outcomes are present between sublaminar and interlaminar decompression techniques for lumbar spinal stenosis.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Sublaminar decompression, along with lower patient BMI, can result in greater improvements in post-operative pain and function.

• Decompression technique and patient demographics are important determinants of patient-reported outcomes when treating lumbar spinal stenosis.

Introduction

Historically, lumbar laminectomies have been the most common approach to managing symptomatic lumbar stenosis. Surgical techniques have evolved from full laminectomies to more conservative decompressions, such as interlaminar decompression or sublaminar decompression (1-3). In a traditional lumbar laminectomy, the entire lamina is removed along with any other structures causing compression on the spinal cord or nerves. Full decompression is achieved, but there is risk for damage to paraspinal muscles, the posterior columns, and the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments (2,4,5). The rates of these procedures continue to rise in the United States, with a study reporting a 387.1% increase in lumbar decompression with fusion and a 79.6% increase in lumbar decompression alone (6).

The more conservative approaches, sublaminar and interlaminar decompressions, while being less invasive, carry potential risks as well. Interlaminar decompression will remove the lamina and ligamentum flavum to increase space in the spinal canal (7). A sublaminar decompression involves taking down the interspinous ligament and using a laminar spreader to remove the thickened ligamentum flavum with greater access (8). There are hypothesized benefits and drawbacks to both types of decompression. While an interlaminar decompression keeps the interspinous ligament intact, there is a smaller window through which to perform the decompression. This could lead to an increased rate of dural tear or inadequate decompression (9). The sublaminar approach could introduce instability at the operative segment when releasing the interlaminar ligaments. However, its larger window may be of benefit in avoiding dural tear or inadequate decompression.

Prior studies have investigated the amount of decompression from a technical perspective and based on imaging findings, yet no previous head-to-head patient outcome studies comparing interlaminar decompression to sublaminar decompression have been performed (2,10).

The objective of this study is to determine the difference in patient-reported outcomes between those who underwent sublaminar versus interlaminar decompression without fusion of the lumbar spine for treatment of symptomatic lumbar stenosis. Our primary objective was to compare patient-reported outcomes, specifically Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) pain interference and physical function (PF) scores, following interlaminar versus sublaminar lumbar decompression without fusion. As a secondary objective, this study analyzed the effect of the number of levels decompressed during surgery on postoperative outcomes. The hypothesis is that there will be a significant difference in patient outcomes between interlaminar decompression and sublaminar decompression. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-159/rc).

Methods

This study is a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent elective, one- or two-level lumbar decompression with fusion at a Level I Trauma Center in the United States from January 2022 to March 2024. The Henry Ford Health Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol (No. N17283-01), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Patients between the ages of 18–85 years were included if they had both preoperative and postoperative PROMIS pain and PF scores relevant to their lumbar disease within eight weeks before and one year after surgery. Exclusion criteria included prior lumbar spinal surgery, surgery for trauma, tumor, or infections, more than four levels of decompression, incomplete PROMIS surveys, urgent surgery based on neurological deficit, and concomitant inflammatory arthritis.

A total of 101 charts were reviewed; six patients were excluded due to prior lumbar surgeries, four patients were excluded due to decompression with concomitant fusion, one patient was excluded due to more than five levels of laminectomies, and 20 patients were excluded due to incomplete PROMIS scores. A total of 70 patients were included in the final analysis. Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Demographics and baseline characteristics

Demographic data collected included age, sex, body mass index (BMI) and smoking history. Baseline characteristics consisted of the presence/absence of comorbidities. Co-morbidities recorded include hypertension, diabetes, depression, osteoarthritis, anxiety, sleep apnea, osteoporosis/osteopenia. To further investigate the relationship between diabetes and the surgical outcomes, pre-op glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was also noted.

Preoperative assessment

Preoperative assessment included PROMIS pain and PF scores within eight weeks of the day of surgery. Preoperative visual analog scale (VAS) pain score overall for back pain was also noted.

Operative data and characteristics

Operative details reviewed included surgical time, spinal levels decompressed, and surgical complications. Operative notes were reviewed to categorize patients into interlaminar decompression or sublaminar decompression types. It is important to note that nearly all interlaminar decompression surgeries were completed by one senior spine surgeon and nearly all sublaminar decompression surgeries were completed by another senior spine surgeon.

Interlaminar decompression surgical technique

A midline incision is made centered over the lumbar interspace, sharply dissecting down to the fascia with a scalpel. The fascia is then split in the midline over the rostral and caudal spinous processes and subsequently reflected bilaterally. A cobb elevator is used to bluntly strip subperiosteal attachments. The supraspinous and interspinous ligaments in the midline and the capsule laterally are carefully protected to maintain the posterior tension band (Figure 1A). Self-retaining retractors are then placed.

Intraoperative photos of an interlaminar decompression of the lumbar spine. (A) Supraspinous and interspinous ligamentous structures (posterior tension band structures, arrow) are left intact and protected. (B) Removal of the deeper lamina and ligamentum flavum (arrow) allows free passing of a Woodson over the thecal sac.

A microscope to assist with illumination and microdissection of the neural elements is used for the remainder of the case. To begin the decompression, a high-speed bur is used to remove the caudal edge of the rostral lamina. Subsequently, the rostral edge of the caudal vertebral lamina is removed to reveal the most caudal attachments of the ligamentum flavum. A curette and Kerrison rongeur are then used to dissect and remove ligamentum flavum attachments, respectively. Removal of the deeper lamina and ligamentum flavum allows free passage of the Woodson, marking completion of the interlaminar decompression (Figure 1B).

Removing both segments of the lamina also allows for better visualization. A partial medial facetectomy and widening of the hemi-laminotomy is performed using a high-speed bur, curettes and Kerrison rongeurs. The decompression is continued until the shoulder of the descending nerve root can be visualized and mobilized medially to inspect the disc. If needed, a Woodson elevator and nerve hook are used to free any compressive disc material. The pituitary rongeur is then used to remove the disc en-bloc. If indicated by clinical and radiopathologic findings, the same steps and procedures for a contralateral interlaminar decompression with medial facetectomy can be performed at the same spinal level.

Sublaminar decompression surgical technique

A midline incision is made with a scalpel, sharply dissecting down to the fascia. Bovie electrocautery is used to split the fascia midline, and a Cobb elevator and electrocautery are then used to dissect laterally to the facet joint in a subperiosteal fashion bilaterally. The supraspinous and interspinous ligaments are removed with a rongeur and self-retaining retractors are placed (Figure 2A).

Intraoperative photos of a sublaminar decompression of the lumbar spine. (A) Posterior tension band removed with rongeur (arrow). (B) A high-speed bur removes the lamina (arrow). (C) Ligamentum flavum (arrow) is mobilized and removed with a Kerrison rongeur. (D) A medial facetectomy allows the nerve root shoulder (arrow) to mobilize. (E) Complete sublaminar decompression.

The microscope is brought in for illumination and microdissection of neural elements. A rongeur is used to remove the caudal edge of the rostral spinous process and the rostral edge of the caudal spinous process and intervening ligaments. A high-speed bur is used to remove the caudal edge of the rostral lamina and the rostral edge of the caudal lamina to allow space for placement of the Kerrison (Figure 2B). Curettes are used to mobilize ligamentum flavum, and Kerrison rongeurs are used to cut and remove flavum rostrally and caudally underneath intact lamina (Figure 2C). After completing midline to lateral and rostral to caudal removal of the ligamentum flavum attachments, the midline, sublaminar decompression is considered complete.

Similar to the interlaminar decompression technique, medial facetectomy with a high-speed bur is performed. The shoulder of the L4 nerve root is mobilized, and a Woodson elevator is passed around it (Figure 2D). The root and thecal sac are freely and easily retracted medially to allow inspection of the disc, which did not demonstrate any compressive material. If indicated by clinical and radiopathologic findings, the same steps and procedures for contralateral sublaminar decompression with medial facetectomy can be performed at the same spinal level (Figure 2E).

Follow up

This study used post-surgical time points of 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year. PROMIS pain and PROMIS PF were recorded at these respective time points within similar clinic settings and utilizing the same PROMIS survey tools. Patients without PROMIS scores completed through 6 months were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis involved fitting the PROMIS pain and PF patient data separately to two versions of linear mixed-effects models using the R package: lmerTest (RRID:SCR_015656). Use of this model addresses missing scores by contributing each patient’s likelihood based on their visits with scores to the overall likelihood of the model. The primary model was created utilizing PROMIS score differences between pre-operative and post-operative interval scores. Analyzing baseline-adjusted outcomes quantifies between-group differences in mean change from baseline, accounting for bias and variability in initial pain and PF scores across patients. This provided interval-specific differences and accounted for variations in pre-operative PROMIS scores, shown to be a determinant in outcomes in this surgery (11,12). A secondary model was created to estimate differences between the two procedures’ absolute scores, varying with post-operative interval and BMI. Both models adjusted for covariates, including BMI, age, legal gender, and surgical time. Patient’s % HbA1c was not used due to absent measurements in 18% of the cohort. Table S1 contains a descriptive summary of the adjusted covariates for each patient cohort analyzed in PROMIS pain and PF mixed-effects models, including patient % HbA1c and the percentage of active smokers. Kenward-Roger calculation for degrees of freedom provided t-tests for fixed effects. For interval-specific comparisons, Holm-adjusted pairwise contrasts were used. Model fit and sensitivity were assessed with R2 and intraclass correlation coefficients, respectively. All analyses were conducted using the R Project for Statistical Computing (RRID:SCR_001905). The confidence level used for all statistical tests was 0.95, where P values of <0.05 are considered statistically significant, unless otherwise stated.

The final analysis included 70 patients. From this total, pre- and post-operative PROMIS pain scores were present in 68 patients and in 62 patients for PROMIS PF scores. Similar demographics were present across patient groups used for PROMIS PF and pain analyses in both sublaminar and interlaminar groups (Table 1). The mean (± standard deviation) follow-up time was 141.1 (±30.7) days.

Change in pre- to post-operative PROMIS score

Sublaminar decompression patients had greater improvements from baseline in PROMIS PF scores than interlaminar counterparts, with significance or approaching significance at the 2-week, 4-week, 3-month, and 6-month post-operative time points (Table 2). However, at the 1-year post-operative time point, patients’ PROMIS PF scores were similar between decompression types. This linear mixed-effects model had 138 observations from 58 patients with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.74. Similarly, PROMIS pain scores in sublaminar patients also had greater improvements from baseline compared to interlaminar patients at the 2-week and 6-month post-operative time points, and a difference at three months that approached significance (Table 3). Again, 1-year post-operative scores were similar between the two types of decompression. This model demonstrated interlaminar patients to have a higher average increase from their baseline pain score compared to sublaminar patients [β: −4.5, standard error (SE): 2.04, P=0.03]. This model had 174 observations from 67 patients with an ICC of 0.39. Notably, sample sizes were limited at the 1-year time point, with just six PROMIS PF scores and seven PROMIS pain scores per decompression type recorded.

Absolute PROMIS score

The linear mixed-effect models considering absolute PROMIS scores demonstrate no difference between sublaminar and interlaminar decompression procedures in either pain or PF scores (P=0.91; P=0.16, respectively). Yet, the PROMIS pain score model suggests that sublaminar patients, not interlaminar, have BMI-dependent absolute pain scores. Every one kg/m2 higher than our cohort average, 29.4 (±5.7) kg/m2, increases the pain score by an estimated 0.83 points (β: 0.80, SE: 0.25, P=0.003). At an average BMI or lower, effects on PROMIS pain scores are negligible. Interlaminar patients demonstrate no such relationship with BMI. This model had 175 observations from 68 patients with an ICC of 0.17. The PROMIS PF absolute score model did not demonstrate BMI-dependence for either procedure type. In both models, a higher age correlated with worse outcomes for this study cohort. PROMIS Pain scores were estimated to increase by 1.33 points for each decade of life added to the mean of the cohort, 66.2 (±11.8) years old (β: 0.133, SE: 0.07, P=0.07). PF scores were estimated to decrease by 3.13 points for each decade of life, higher than the mean age of the cohort (β: −0.313, SE: 0.10, P=0.003).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study is the first to directly compare patient-reported outcomes between sublaminar and interlaminar decompression in lumbar surgery. Among 70 patients in the final analysis, sublaminar decompression patients demonstrated greater improvements from baseline in PROMIS PF scores at the 2-week, 4-week, 3-month, and 6-month intervals, with statistical significance or a trend toward significance at each of these time points. Similar patterns were observed for PROMIS pain interference, with greater improvements in the sublaminar group at the 2-week and 6-month time points, and a near-significant difference at three months. At the 1-year follow-up, both PF and pain scores were similar between groups, though sample sizes were small. PROMIS captured early postoperative differences between decompression techniques despite no statistically significant differences in absolute PF or pain scores. The mixed-effects models were based on 138 PF observations from 58 patients (ICC =0.74) and 174 pain observations from 67 patients (ICC =0.39), supporting the robustness of these estimates.

PROMIS as an outcome measure

PROMIS Computer Adaptive Testing (CAT) allowed efficient tracking of functional and pain recovery over multiple time points while minimizing patient survey burden. Prior studies assessing PROMIS outcomes in lumbar spine surgery have validated its sensitivity in detecting changes in pain and function, particularly in populations undergoing decompression procedures (12). The use of PROMIS CAT provides high precision with minimal survey burden, allowing for efficient monitoring of patient recovery (13). Unlike legacy instruments such as the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) or SF-36, PROMIS offers a standardized metric applicable across multiple conditions, which is crucial for comparative effectiveness research (14,15). Further studies in spine surgery, joint replacement, and trauma have demonstrated consistent baseline and postoperative PROMIS trends like those observed (16).

Strengths and limitations of PROMIS

Despite its advantages, PROMIS has limitations in the surgical context. Ceiling or floor effects may limit sensitivity when patients score near maximum or minimum values preoperatively (17-19). The disconnect between true functional gains and a patient’s perceived recovery can delay PROMIS improvements, affecting score interpretation. Additionally, the general PROMIS domains may not capture all condition-specific nuances compared to specialized tools, and patient self-reporting is inherently vulnerable to bias (20,21).

Comparing sublaminar and interlaminar decompression

Both sublaminar and interlaminar decompression have been shown to achieve adequate decompression, with documented increases in thecal sac cross-sectional area (22,23). Interlaminar decompression involves a midline or paramedian approach through the lamina, with removal of part of the lamina and ligamentum flavum while largely preserving the spinous process, interspinous ligaments, and facet joints (24,25). In contrast, sublaminar decompression undercuts the lamina from beneath and removes ligamentum flavum but preserves much of the dorsal laminar surface (26).

The smaller surgical window in sublaminar decompression may limit visualization of dura and nerve roots, potentially increasing risks of dural tear or nerve injury (26). However, because sublaminar decompression preserves more of the lamina, it may be more suitable for fusion procedures (27). Fusion is generally indicated in cases of significant spinal instability and deformity (1,28). In this cohort, patients with decompression and fusion were excluded to allow comparison of decompression alone.

Sublaminar decompression may have resulted in better PROMIS PF and pain interference scores compared to interlaminar decompression in this study because the greater degree of dissection in the sublaminar approach may lead to a greater increase in thecal cross-sectional area for more decompression. However, additional studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis. Another possible explanation suggests that interlaminar decompression, creating access directly through the lamina, may result in postoperative radiculopathy. Soliman et al. in 2019 suggested a greater increase in postoperative radiculopathy after bilateral interlaminar decompression compared to classic laminectomy (23). To date, there are no studies in the literature to suggest increased radiculopathy postoperatively after sublaminar decompression.

Impact of BMI on outcomes

BMI emerged as a significant modifier of outcomes in this study, particularly in interaction with decompression type at the 3-month time point. Higher BMI was associated with worse absolute PROMIS pain scores in sublaminar decompression patients, a relationship not observed in the interlaminar group. This suggests that sublaminar decompression and the resultant instability may be more susceptible to biomechanical stresses imposed by increased body mass, potentially affecting postoperative pain perception (29). The additional soft tissue bulk in higher BMI patients may limit surgical exposure and visualization, possibly compromising the extent of decompression despite the theoretically larger sublaminar window (30,31). Conversely, the interlaminar approach, which preserves midline structures and avoids extensive ligamentous release, may maintain consistent outcomes across BMI strata due to a less invasive corridor and less instability. Previous literature has identified elevated BMI as a predictor of worse postoperative pain and function after lumbar decompression procedures (32). The findings of this study suggest that decompression technique selection should consider patient-specific anatomical factors, including BMI, to optimize postoperative outcomes and minimize technique-related disadvantages in higher BMI individuals.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective study design precludes causal inference and introduces potential selection bias, as surgical approach selection was based on surgeon discretion rather than randomization. Selection bias was minimized by applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to include all eligible patients with adequate demographic, health, and PROMIS information recorded. To address information bias, the decompression technique type was determined by a thorough review of operative reports by two independent authors rather than the use of coding data. The 6-month follow-up period may be insufficient to assess long-term pain and functional outcomes. Additionally, many later follow-up intervals were underpowered, with only seven patients per cohort remaining at the 1-year mark, limiting the ability to detect significant differences. Although PROMIS is a validated instrument, it remains a subjective measure susceptible to psychosocial influences that were not controlled for in this study (20,21). Additionally, ceiling and floor effects, the timing of assessments in relation to recovery milestones, and the broad scope of PROMIS domains may limit its sensitivity in detecting all clinically meaningful changes.

A significant limitation of this study is the lack of patient-reported symptoms on the level of neurogenic claudication, radicular symptoms, and back pain to distinguish. PROMIS pain interference and PF scores are unable to distinguish between these symptoms, and thus, the ability to compare improvements in these symptoms by decompression type was not possible.

Clinical and research implications

PROMIS outcomes can meaningfully inform surgical decision-making by clarifying patient-centered differences between decompression techniques. They support shared decision-making by setting realistic expectations for recovery. Additionally, PROMIS data have potential applications in health system quality improvement and value-based reimbursement models. The ability to stratify risk, counsel patients preoperatively, and monitor recovery longitudinally makes PROMIS a valuable tool for both clinicians and researchers.

Future directions

Future research should include prospective, multicenter trials comparing sublaminar and interlaminar decompression with fusion. Such studies should include objective outcomes, such as radiographic fusion rates, gait analysis, and quantitative assessments of decompression. Additionally, comparing each technique’s ability to alleviate clinically important symptoms such as neurogenic claudication, radicular symptoms, and back pain should be evaluated. Biomechanical studies on ligament preservation versus resection within lumbar fusion could clarify the structural mechanisms underlying functional differences observed, potentially guiding surgical technique refinement. A study directly measuring and comparing decompression volume and its relation to PROMIS outcomes would add critical insight.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that sublaminar decompression with fusion yields greater short-term improvements in PROMIS pain interference and PF scores compared to interlaminar decompression. These differences, which exceed established MCIDs, suggest surgical technique selection can affect patient-centered outcomes after lumbar spine surgery. BMI was identified as a significant modifier of pain outcomes in sublaminar decompression patients, and individualized surgical planning may enhance recovery trajectories.
