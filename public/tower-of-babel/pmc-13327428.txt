The Reliability of Intraoperative Frozen Section for Glottic Lesions Suspected of Early Malignancy
Authors: Gal Levi, Yaniv Hamzany, Nir Tsur, Yulia Strenov, Daniel Ben‐Ner, Hagit Shoffel‐Havakuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGlottic lesions suspected of malignancy may be treated by laser cordectomy. Although obviating the preoperative biopsy can expedite treatment and preserve the surgical planes, it poses a risk of mistreatment. This study sought to determine if intraoperative frozen section (FS) prior to cordectomy can reliably guide lesion management.Study DesignRetrospective chart review, 2017 to 2024.SettingTwo tertiary university‐affiliated referral centers.MethodsPatients who underwent intraoperative FS sampling of glottic lesions suspected of early malignancy were identified. In all cases, samples were obtained from the area with the most suspicious characteristics using cold instruments. To determine accuracy, the FS results were compared with the final pathology report using 2 binary categorization systems: invasiveness (SCC present/absent) and malignant potential (lesion warrants/does not warrant curative cordectomy). Sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV) were calculated.ResultsNinety‐one vocal‐fold lesions obtained from 70 patients in 81 surgeries were included. FS analysis detected invasive carcinoma with 94.5% accuracy; there were no false‐positives. While examining malignant potential, the accuracy was 94.5%, with 37 true‐positives, 49 true‐negatives, 2 false‐positives, and 3 false‐negatives. The calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were 92%, 96%, 95% and 94%, respectively.ConclusionsIntraoperative FS is a reliable tool for the identification of early malignancy in suspicious glottic lesions, especially invasive carcinoma. The high PPV and NPV for high malignant potential suggest that intraoperative FS, combined with clinical judgment, may guide surgeons' decision‐making.Keywords: dysplasia, frozen section, glottic carcinoma, sensitivity, specificity

Glottic lesions suspected of malignancy may be treated by laser cordectomy. Before proceeding to surgical resection, the surgeon should decide whether or not to perform a preoperative biopsy to confirm the diagnosis of malignancy. Besides expediting treatment, obviating preoperative biopsy will preserve the surgical planes by avoiding tissue damage and inflammation, potentially allowing for a more superficial resection. Therefore, some surgeons opt for a single curative cordectomy procedure during which a specimen is taken for diagnostic histopathology. 1 , 2 , 3 However, the benefits of this management strategy must be weighed against the risks of overtreating a nonmalignant lesion or undertreating a malignant one.

Histopathological analysis of a formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) specimen is the gold standard for diagnosis of malignancy. However, because the process is very time‐intensive (preparation of the tissue alone can take several hours), it cannot be used to guide intraoperative decisions. As a result, surgeons often rely on rapid microscopic cryosectioning, or frozen section (FS), of a specimen. Nevertheless, since the quality of the slides produced by FS is lower and the analysis is done quickly, the final diagnosis should still be confirmed by FFPE tissue histopathology.

In this context of suspicious glottic lesions, while intraoperative FS might provide histopathological evidence supporting the decision to operate, the evidence regarding its diagnostic accuracy, particularly with extremely small glottic samples, is sparse. Most of the available publications that addressed the yield of FS in glottic squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) focused on its reliability for achieving clear oncological margins. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Additionally, in some of the studies, FS samples were obtained with CO2 laser, which is known to induce artifacts on histopathology. 4 , 5 , 6 , 9 Thus, the results to date have been inconsistent. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of intraoperative of FS analysis of samples obtained from glottic lesions suspected of early cancer using cold instruments. We hypothesized that FS is a reliable tool for supporting the decision to operate in the absence of a preoperative biopsy. To test this hypothesis, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive values (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) were calculated with reference to the final pathology by the standard FFPE protocol.

Methods

Patients and Setting

A retrospective chart‐review study was performed in 2 tertiary university‐affiliated referral centers, between October 2017 and October 2024 in one and August 2019 to October 2024 in the other. The cohort consisted of patients with suspicious lesions limited to the glottis who underwent direct laryngoscopy with intraoperative FS sampling using cold instruments. The study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Rabin Medical Center institutional review board.

Preoperative Assessment Protocol

In all cases, the preoperative laryngoscopic and videostroboscopic evaluation was performed by a fellowship‐trained laryngologist (HSH or YH) using a rigid endoscope (Hopkins Strobolaryngoscope 70° 7.2 × 9.3 mm; Karl Storz Se & Co. KG) and/or a flexible distal‐chip nasolaryngoscope (Olympus ENF‐V3 video rhinolaryngoscope; Olympus Corp.), with and without narrow‐band imaging. Lesions demonstrating features considered indicative of possible early glottic SCC (stage Tis‐T1) or dysplasia were selected for the study protocol, as follows: keratosis, irregularity, infiltration, vascularity, pathological intraepithelial papillary capillary loops (IPCL), ulceration, endophytic growth, and stiffness of the vocal fold on stroboscopy (Figure 1). In cases in which clinical judgment or intraoperative evaluation did not support a suspicion for dysplasia or malignancy, or cordectomy could not be performed in the same procedure (e.g., due to exposure difficulties), FS samples were not taken, and the patient was not included in the study.

Surgical Technique

FS samples were obtained by suspension microlaryngoscopy under an operating microscope. The suspicious appearance of the lesion was reassessed and confirmed, followed by its inspection and palpation by the surgeon (HSH or YH). The area of the lesion most suspicious for malignancy was selected for FS sampling.

Tissue Sampling for FS Biopsy

Particular attention was directed at obtaining a small yet high‐quality representative specimen for processing and evaluation. Tissue sampling was performed exclusively with cold‐steel instrumentation in order to avoid charring and thermal effects on the tissue, thereby minimizing artifacts. The sample was handled gently with a microscopic grasper to prevent the risk of crushing injury associated with cupped biopsy forceps, and dissection was performed with a microscopic sickle knife and scissors. The fresh samples were immediately transported to the pathology laboratory for cryosectioning and analysis. The FS tissue‐sampling technique is demonstrated in the supporting information video S1.

Cryosectioning

Frozen tissue blocks were sectioned using a Leica CM1950 cryostat at a temperature of −20°C to −25°C. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 µm and carefully transferred onto positively charged glass slides and allowed to adhere. Immediately prior to hematoxylin and eosin staining, sections were submerged in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a few seconds. The fixed sections were evaluated by a senior pathologist.

Histology Reports

Both FS and FFPE sections were reviewed by senior pathologists; however, the evaluating pathologist was not necessarily the same for both methods. The distribution of pathologists varied by institution: at RMC, cases were managed by 2 specialists with extensive head and neck experience, whereas at AMC, cases were randomly assigned to a larger pool of pathologists across the department.

Data Collection

Data for the study were collected from the computerized medical and surgical charts, as follows: demographics (age, sex), prior glottic biopsy, prior radiation therapy, prior glottic surgery, intraoperative data, FS report, and final pathology (FFPE) report. Dysplasia was classified as mild, moderate, or severe according to the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation. 10 (The 2017 two‐tiered system of the WHO was not used to ensure consistency throughout the term of the study. 11 ) The FS and FFPE results were categorized as follows: (1) no malignancy, (2) severe dysplasia, (3) carcinoma in situ (CIS), (4) invasive SCC, (5) malignancy cannot be ruled out, and (6) nondiagnostic.

FS Accuracy Analysis

Since CIS is often considered synonymous with severe dysplasia, 12 the 2 categories were grouped together for the analysis. To determine the accuracy of FS, the results were compared with the final FFPE pathology report using 2 binary categorization systems: invasiveness of the lesion, that is, whether or not the pathologic evaluation demonstrated invasive SCC; and malignant potential of the lesion, that is, whether or not curative management of the lesion requires cordectomy. High malignant potential (requiring curative cordectomy) was defined as severe dysplasia, CIS, dysplasia that cannot rule out malignancy/invasiveness, and invasive SCC; low malignant potential was defined as hyperplasia, inflammation, reactive changes, and mild to moderate dysplasia. A FS sample that was too superficial (non‐diagnostic) was considered negative for both invasiveness and malignant potential.

Statistical Analysis

Data organization and analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel (Build 19045 version). Categorical variables were described by prevalence and percentage, and continuous variables, by mean and range. The final pathology results served as the reference to determine the reliability of FS analysis. The number of true‐ and false‐positive and true‐ and false‐negative cases was determined, and sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy were calculated.

Results

FS specimens were obtained from 91 vocal folds from 70 patients who underwent 81 surgeries with glottic FS biopsies. The cohort included 68 male and 23 in female patients of median age 65 years (range: 24‐86 years).

The final (FFPE) pathologic analysis yielded SCC in 27 vocal folds (29.7%), CIS in 8 (8.8%), severe dysplasia in 5 (5.5%), mild to moderate dysplasia in 15 (16.5%), and benign findings in 36 (39.5%) (Table 1). Thus, according to the binary classification system, there were 28 cases of invasiveness and 40 cases of high malignant potential. Type I cordectomy was performed in 77 cases (84.6%), type II cordectomy in 6 (6.6%), and more extensive cordectomy in 8 (8.8%) (Table 1). The mean duration of surgery was 86 ± 50.9 minutes.

Using the FFPE results as the reference, the accuracy of FS analysis for the detection of lesion invasiveness was 94.5%, with very high specificity and PPV of 100%, but lower sensitivity of 82.1% and NPV of 92.6%. There were 6 cases of incorrect identification of invasiveness (false negatives), of whom 4 were falsely identified on FS as severe dysplasia/CIS, and 2 as moderate dysplasia. Nevertheless, there were no false positives of invasiveness (Table 3). The accuracy of FS analysis for the detection of malignant potential was 94.5%, with high specificity of 96.1% and PPV of 94.9%, in addition to high sensitivity of 92.5% and NPV of 94.2%. There were 5 cases of incorrect identification of malignant potential: 3 false negatives and 2 false positives (Table 3), these incorrect identification cases will be later described in detail. Separate analysis of the findings for each institution involved in the study yielded comparable rates for invasiveness (specificity, NPV) and malignant potential (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV) (Supporting information S2: Table S1).

Under the study management protocol, all patients diagnosed with a benign lesion or dysplasia (including those with discrepancies between FS and FFPE findings) underwent subepithelial (type 1) cordectomy. Type I cordectomy was performed in the vast majority of lesions with low malignant potential (Table 4 and Supporting information S2: Table S2). The 2 lesions misidentified on FS as having high malignant potential (CIS or inflammation/cannot rule out malignancy) were diagnosed by FFPE analysis as sialometaplasia and pyogenic granuloma, respectively. All 3 lesions falsely classified as having low malignant potential on FS were initially diagnosed as moderate dysplasia. The corresponding FFPE diagnosis was CIS in one and SCC in the others. The latter cases also accounted for 2/5 false‐negative cases for invasiveness. In the remaining 3 false‐negative cases for invasiveness, the final FFPE diagnosis was CIS in 2 and high‐grade dysplasia in one (Table 4). Overall, none of the false‐negative and false‐positive cases in our series resulted in inappropriate surgical management (under‐ or over‐resection). The SCC cases were treated by cordectomy types I‐III, while the 2 benign cases (sialometaplasia and pyogenic granuloma) were treated by type I cordectomy. None of these discrepancy cases required a revision procedure based on final pathology.

Analysis of the characteristics of these cases did not reveal any factors predictive of incorrect FS results. None of the 8 patients with an incorrect FS result underwent prior irradiation to the larynx or had evidence of active fungal infection. Additionally, smoking history, current smoking status, and prior glottic surgery, did not significantly affect the rate of incorrect FS results.

Discussion

This study showed that intraoperative FS is extremely reliable for the diagnosis of glottic lesions suspected of being SCC, and for deciding whether or not to perform curative cordectomy (malignant potential). In a series of 91 samples, using the final (FFPE) pathology as a reference, FS analysis proved highly accurate in identifying invasive SCC, with a specificity and PPV of 100%. In each of the 23 cases in which FS identified SCC, the finding correlated with the FFPE pathology report. However, sensitivity was only 82%. In terms of identifying malignant potential (at least severe dysplasia/CIS) in order to guide the management approach (curative cordectomy), FS proved highly effective, with accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV rates all greater than 90%.

Intraoperative FS analysis offers several advantages over routine biopsy. It is significantly faster and makes single‐stage cordectomy possible by providing simultaneous diagnosis and treatment, thereby improving prognosis. It also eliminates the need for prior in‐office biopsy and an operating room for preliminary tissue sampling, potentially reducing costs and improving efficiency. However, FS analysis is not without shortcomings, as it requires an experienced pathologist on standby and causes delays while the surgeon waits for the results. Moreover, it harbors a risk of false negatives and false positives, which could impact the surgical outcome. In addition, intraoperative interpretation of FS specimens is challenging because of the need for rapid reportage of results and speedy sample processing which can introduce artifacts such as freezing distortion, inconsistent section thickness, and suboptimal staining, potentially obscuring crucial cellular details. Furthermore, the limited tissue sample obtained during surgery may not fully represent the entire lesion, especially when reactive changes or unusual tumor variants are present. This is particularly true in the narrow glottis, where samples might be compromised by crushing, charring, or thermal spread with the use of such surgical instruments as graspers or lasers. Our FS sampling protocol sought to mitigate these surgeon‐dependent issues for the pathologist.

Our review of the literature revealed only sparse research on the reliability of intraoperative FS analysis for the diagnosis and treatment of suspicious glottic lesions. Remacle et al 4 evaluated the use of intraoperative FS for determining clear margins in a series of 97 samples of glottic lesions treated by CO2 laser cordectomy. The accuracy rate was 94%, and the NPV, 95%. In their previous work, the same group addressed the problems of laser‐induced tissue damage and artifacts in histopathological analysis. 13 The NPV rate in the present study was similar to that of Remacle et al, 4 although we focused on intraoperative diagnosis, not margin involvement, and used cold‐steel instruments exclusively. Nerurkar et al, 5 in a study of 164 glottic samples, assessed the accuracy of FS performed during CO2 laser surgeries in terms of both margin involvement and primary diagnosis. The results were impressive, with sensitivity 98.4%, specificity 95.1%, PPV 95.2%, and NPV 98.3%. However, the sample consisted of many lesion types, including non‐malignancies such as amyloidosis (with 100% accuracy for FS), confounding the ability to analyze the FS study accuracy for identifying malignant potential and malignancy.

Jin et al 9 evaluated intraoperative FS accuracy in 119 glottic lesions, reporting a malignancy PPV of 88.9%—comparable to our 100% PPV for carcinoma. However, they reported significantly lower PPVs for dysplasia (18.2%) and CIS (16.7%), which they attributed to superficial sampling and mixed pathology. We propose that these discrepancies may also stem from differences in FS sampling techniques and the distinct classification systems employed in each study.

A 2024 study by Aires et al 6 of 113 samples aimed, much like our study, to assess the reliability of FS in identifying suspicious glottic lesions, albeit somewhat different results. Accuracy was 80.5%, specificity 86.9%, and PPV 95.9%, but sensitivity and NPV were very low at 78.9% and 51.3%, respectively. Aires et al focused only on the reliability of FS for the diagnosis of malignancy, 6 whereas we examined FS prediction of invasiveness and malignant potential, strengthening our capability to draw clinical conclusions. Although specificity exceeded sensitivity also in our study, all indicators showed higher rates for malignant potential, supported by exceptionally high detection rates of invasiveness. In addition, the earlier study used electrocautery as well as CO2 laser instrumentation, similar to the other reports reviewed, and categorized the FS results as benign, dysplasia (mild, moderate, and severe dysplasia or CIS), and malignant (including microinvasive and invasive SCC) as opposed to the binary system applied in the present study, which might explain our better results. Notably, none of the previously mentioned publications detailed the specific technique used to obtain FS. Consequently, it was not possible to assess how factors such as crushing, charring, or thermal damage may have influenced their FS reports in comparison to our findings.

The findings of the present study offer practical insights into the use of FS in intraoperative decision‐making which can be directly applied in clinical practice. Avoiding preoperative biopsy conserves vocal‐fold tissue, prevents the development of granulation tissue, and maintains the surgical planes, thereby facilitating superficial cordectomy. Classifying a lesion as benign during surgery will spare patients unnecessarily wide resection and preserve their voice. Identifying a lesion as malignant during surgery will indicate the need for wider tumor resection, allow for an appropriate single‐stage cordectomy, and expedite treatment.

The binary classification systems described in this work are easy to apply and therefore can facilitate intraoperative decisions. A finding of invasive SCC by FS was very reliable, with a 100% likelihood that the diagnosis will be confirmed by FFPE. In supporting intraoperative decision‐making regarding the need for curative cordectomy, FS was extremely accurate, identifying lesions with high malignant potential (at least severe dysplasia/CIS), with high sensitivity (92.5%) and specificity (96.1%). It is also noteworthy that there were very few false‐negative or false‐positive cases in our series, and none resulted in inappropriate surgical management (under‐ or over‐resection). This may suggest that together with careful intraoperative inspection, FS analysis may further augment surgeons' clinical judgment and prevent the need for repeated procedures.

Our study was strategically conducted across 2 institutions, RMC and AMC, offering a diverse look at pathology expertise. While pathologists at RMC possess highly specialized and homogeneous training, the team at AMC represents a more heterogeneous range of experience levels. Despite these differences, Supporting information S2: Table S1 demonstrates that outcomes remained comparable across both sites. These findings suggest that achieving high accuracy in glottic FS is reproducible in varied clinical settings, provided that high‐quality biopsies are obtained and close interdisciplinary collaboration with the pathologists is maintained.

The main limitations of this study are the retrospective design, which harbors several biases, and the two‐centers setting. Further prospective studies are needed to support our findings, in addition to multicenter studies across different countries to broaden the dataset and enhance the generalizability of the findings. Yet, since our study involved several pathologists in 2 different centers and yielded comparable results, the reproducibility of our outcomes in other institutes is suggested. Another limitation of our study is the lack of long‐term follow‐up, which precluded the assessment of local recurrence rates necessary to further validate our proposed approach. We therefore suggest that future studies should also incorporate long‐term follow‐up. While these future directions may yield more conclusive results, we believe our study already has the potential to affect the everyday practice of laryngologists treating early glottic cancer and dysplasia.

Conclusion

Intraoperative FS is a valuable tool to support decision‐making regarding the need for and extent of cordectomy for glottic lesions suspected of malignancy. Samples are best obtained from the section with features most suggestive of malignancy using cold‐steel instruments, and should be handled gently. In the present study, FS predicted both malignant potential and invasiveness with very high accuracy, specificity, and PPV. Improvement in sensitivity and NPV can be achieved by using the FS prediction of malignant potential compared to invasiveness. In general, findings on FS analysis correlated highly with the final pathology. By combining clinical judgment and FS results, surgeons can achieve a better treatment strategy tailored to the individual patient.
