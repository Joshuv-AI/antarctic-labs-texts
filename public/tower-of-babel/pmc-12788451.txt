Assessment of ECG Changes in Hypothyroid Patients and Their Correlation with Serum TSH Levels
Authors: Mahi J. Khiloshiya, Tirth H. Vyas, Ashka Priyadarshi, Sanket R. Patel, Anuj S. Modi, Swati Mahajan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypothyroidism, a common endocrine disorder, is known to influence cardiovascular physiology, particularly through its effects on cardiac electrophysiology. Electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities, such as bradycardia, low-voltage complexes, and prolonged QT intervals, are frequently observed. The relationship between the severity of hypothyroidism, often measured by serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels, and the extent of ECG changes remains a subject of clinical relevance.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional observational study was conducted over a period of six months at a tertiary care hospital. A total of 100 hypothyroid patients (TSH > 5.0 µIU/mL) aged between 20 and 60 years were enrolled. Patients with known cardiac diseases, electrolyte imbalances, or on medications affecting cardiac conduction were excluded. ECG parameters, including heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, and presence of low-voltage complexes, were recorded. Serum TSH levels were measured using chemiluminescence immunoassay. Statistical analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation and Chi-square tests to assess the relationship between ECG findings and TSH levels.Results:Out of the 100 patients, 70% were females and 30% were males. The mean age was 38.4 ± 9.6 years. Bradycardia (heart rate < 60 bpm) was observed in 48% of patients, prolonged QTc in 36%, and low-voltage complexes in 22%. A significant positive correlation was found between serum TSH levels and QTc interval (r = 0.41, P = 0.002), and a negative correlation with heart rate (r = −0.53, P < 0.001). The incidence of ECG changes was significantly higher in patients with TSH levels above 10 µIU/mL (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Hypothyroidism is associated with distinct ECG abnormalities, the severity of which correlates with rising TSH levels. Regular ECG monitoring in hypothyroid patients, especially those with elevated TSH, is essential for early detection of potential cardiac complications and timely intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Hypothyroidism, a condition characterized by decreased production of thyroid hormones, has widespread systemic implications, with notable effects on the cardiovascular system. The thyroid hormones, thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), play an essential role in regulating myocardial contractility, vascular resistance, and cardiac electrophysiology. Consequently, alterations in their levels can significantly impact cardiac rhythm and function, which can be readily identified using electrocardiographic (ECG) evaluation.[1]

Electrophysiological manifestations of hypothyroidism may include sinus bradycardia, low-voltage QRS complexes, ST-T wave changes, and prolonged QT intervals, among others. These changes are often subtle in subclinical cases but become more pronounced with the severity of hormonal deficiency. Clinical studies have also suggested that serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels, a sensitive marker for hypothyroid status, may be predictive of the extent of ECG abnormalities, particularly in overt hypothyroidism.[23]

The potential cardiovascular morbidity associated with untreated or poorly managed hypothyroidism underscores the importance of routine cardiac assessment. As ECG is a simple, noninvasive, and cost-effective diagnostic tool, it offers a practical means to monitor cardiac status in hypothyroid patients, especially in resource-constrained settings.[4]

Despite extensive research on the systemic effects of hypothyroidism, the correlation between serum TSH levels and specific ECG alterations remains underexplored in certain populations. Hence, this study aims to evaluate the ECG changes in patients diagnosed with hypothyroidism and assess their correlation with serum TSH levels, thereby contributing to more comprehensive clinical management strategies.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted over a period of six months at a tertiary care center. A total of 100 adult patients aged 20–60 years, diagnosed with primary hypothyroidism (TSH >5.0 µIU/mL), were enrolled after obtaining informed consent. Patients with known cardiac disorders, electrolyte imbalances, diabetes, or those on medications affecting cardiac conduction were excluded.

For each participant, a 12-lead resting electrocardiogram (ECG) was recorded using a standard ECG machine. ECG parameters assessed included heart rate, PR interval, QRS duration, QT and corrected QT (QTc) interval, and the presence of low-voltage complexes. Serum TSH levels were measured using a chemiluminescence immunoassay method from fasting blood samples.

Data were compiled and analyzed using SPSS software version 25.0. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to evaluate associations between TSH levels and ECG parameters, with P values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 100 patients with hypothyroidism were evaluated, comprising 70 females and 30 males. The mean age of participants was 38.4 ± 9.6 years. ECG analysis revealed several abnormalities across the study population.

Table 1 presents the distribution of ECG findings among the study group. Sinus bradycardia (heart rate <60 bpm) was observed in 48% of patients. Prolonged QTc interval (>440 ms in males and >460 ms in females) was seen in 36%, while low-voltage QRS complexes were present in 22%. Prolongation of PR interval (>200 ms) and QRS duration (>110 ms) was noted in 15% and 10% of cases, respectively.

DISCUSSION

The present study assessed electrocardiographic alterations in hypothyroid patients and explored their correlation with serum TSH levels. The findings reveal that hypothyroidism is associated with notable ECG abnormalities, with a clear trend indicating worsening cardiac electrophysiology as TSH levels increase.

Bradycardia was the most frequently observed change, present in nearly half of the patients. This can be attributed to the reduced sympathetic activity and diminished metabolic demand characteristic of hypothyroid states, which directly impact sinoatrial node activity.[1] Our findings align with those reported by Klein and Danzi, who emphasized that hypothyroidism leads to decreased heart rate and impaired diastolic function.[2]

QTc prolongation, seen in 36% of cases, is a clinically significant observation, as it may predispose patients to ventricular arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes.[3] Prolongation of the QT interval is believed to result from altered repolarization due to decreased expression of cardiac potassium channels under hypothyroid conditions.[4] A similar trend was noted in the study by Bhakat et al., which demonstrated a positive correlation between elevated TSH levels and QTc prolongation.[5]

Low-voltage complexes, identified in 22% of patients, may be related to pericardial effusion or myxedematous infiltration, both of which are recognized features of severe hypothyroidism.[6] Though not as common, PR and QRS interval prolongation in a subset of our patients further supports the hypothesis that thyroid hormone deficiency slows atrioventricular conduction and reduces myocardial contractility.[7]

The statistically significant inverse correlation between TSH levels and heart rate, along with the positive association with QTc duration, underscores the importance of early diagnosis and thyroid hormone replacement therapy to prevent cardiovascular complications. These results are consistent with those of Biondi and Cooper, who emphasized the cardiovascular risks associated with untreated hypothyroidism, even in its subclinical forms.[8]

CONCLUSION

In summary, our study confirms that hypothyroidism is associated with distinct ECG changes, and their severity increases with higher TSH levels. This highlights the need for routine ECG monitoring and TSH assessment in hypothyroid patients to guide timely therapeutic interventions.
