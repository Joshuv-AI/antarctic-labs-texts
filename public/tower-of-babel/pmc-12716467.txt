Associations of accelerated biological aging with intraoperative hypotension in major surgery: a multicenter cohort study of 116,996 patients
Authors: Jihong Jiang, Xuan Yin, Siyu Kong, Xinchi Li, Fan Yang, Xuesheng Liu, Yali Ge, Jie Sun, Lihai Chen, Hongwei Shi, Jifang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative hypotension (IOH) is a prevalent hemodynamic complication associated with adverse postoperative outcomes. Identifying patients at high risk for IOH remains a challenge. Accelerated biological aging, reflecting cumulative physiological decline, may heighten susceptibility to hypotensive events by impairing cardiovascular homeostasis and autonomic regulation, making it a potent yet underutilized predictor in perioperative medicine.Materials and methodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study analyzed 116,996 adults undergoing major cardiac and non-cardiac surgery across three tertiary academic medical centers in Eastern China. Accelerated biological aging was measured using Phenotypic Age Acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel). Outcomes included incidence of IOH (Mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 60 mmHg), cumulative duration, and area under curve (AUC) below this threshold. AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal integration method to quantify the integrated area where MAP < 60 mmHg. Associations were assessed using generalized linear regression, adjusting for key confounders.ResultsAccelerated biological aging was significantly associated with elevated IOH risk (adjusted odds ratio per unit increase 1.04, 95% confidence intervals 1.02–1.06, p < 0.001), with risk increasing progressively across acceleration groups. Accelerated biological aging also correlated with prolonged IOH duration and increased AUC. Subgroup analyses suggested increased IOH susceptibility associated with accelerated biological aging across most subgroups except patients of 40–79 years and those underwent cardiac surgery. Several estimates had wide confidence intervals, indicating variability and uncertainty in the subgroup-specific effects.ConclusionsAccelerated biological aging was significantly associated with both increased risk and greater severity of intraoperative hypotension. These findings support the integration of biological aging metrics, such as PhenoAgeAccel, into preoperative risk assessment to optimize hemodynamic management in vulnerable surgical populations.Keywords: Biological age, intraoperative hypotension, accelerated biological aging, surgery

Introduction

Intraoperative hypotension (IOH) represents a common hemodynamic challenge in surgical patients, most commonly characterized by reductions in systolic or mean arterial pressure below absolute or relative thresholds during anesthesia delivery [1–3]. IOH was strongly associated with major adverse outcomes, including myocardial injury, acute kidney injury, and 30-day mortality [4–7].

The pathophysiology of IOH involves intricate interactions between pharmacological effects and physiological reserve. General anesthetics produce dose-dependent depression of myocardial function while simultaneously impairing vascular autoregulation through direct vasodilation and attenuation of sympathetic tone [8–10]. These effects are normally counterbalanced by compensatory mechanisms in healthy individuals; however, this physiological reserve becomes progressively compromised with advancing age and comorbid conditions. Of particular relevance, the aging process fundamentally alters cardiovascular homeostasis through multiple pathways: reduced β-adrenergic responsiveness, impaired baroreflex sensitivity, and diminished vascular compliance collectively predispose older patients to hemodynamic instability [11]. The additional frailty burden marked by multisystem physiological decline and reduced functional reserve, further compounds this vulnerability [12,13].

While chronological age and comorbidity burden remain well-established risk factors for IOH [14], their predictive utility is limited. Chronological age fails to capture the marked interindividual variability in physiological aging trajectories, while comorbidity indices often catalog diseases without fully quantifying the underlying systemic physiological decline. This heterogeneity has spurred the development of biological age algorithms designed to quantify systemic aging more precisely than chronological time alone [15,16]. These models integrate diverse data, from molecular epigenetic markers to clinical biomarkers, to estimate an individual’s overall physiological functional reserve. Among these, we chose to employ Phenotypic Age Acceleration (PhenoAgeAccel) for this study. This algorithm incorporates a composite of routinely available clinical biomarkers (including albumin, creatinine, glucose, and C-reactive protein) that collectively reflect the integrity of multiple organ systems [17–19]. Its basis in standard clinical chemistry makes it highly practical for translational application, and it has demonstrated superior prognostic value for mortality and morbidity across diverse clinical contexts [20–25], suggesting it is well-suited to capture the multisystem physiological vulnerability relevant to hemodynamic instability. However, its potential application in perioperative medicine, particularly for predicting anesthesia-related hemodynamics instability, remains unexplored.

Therefore, this multicenter cohort study aimed to investigate the association between accelerated biological aging (quantified by PhenoAgeAccel) and susceptibility to IOH. We hypothesized that surgical patients exhibiting accelerated biological aging would demonstrate a substantially increased risk of IOH, attributable to compromised organ functional reserve.

Materials and methods

Study design

This multicenter retrospective cohort study leveraged integrated electronic health records (EHR) from three tertiary hospitals in eastern China: Nanjing First Hospital (2016.04–2023.09), Nanjing Zhongda Hospital (2019.01–2023.04), and First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (2022.01–2022.12). Ethical approval for this study (protocol KY20240924-09) was provided by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital, Nanjing, China on 24 September 2024 (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07080086), adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent waiver was granted for de-identified data. Methodology strictly adhered to STROBE guidelines (Supplementary Table S1).

Study population

Patients undergoing major cardiac or non-cardiac surgery under general anesthesia with anticipated postoperative hospitalization ≥48 h between January 2015, and April 2023, were included. For individuals with multiple eligible surgeries, only data from the first procedure were analyzed. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) incomplete surgical documentation or unverified sex information; (2) inadequate intraoperative mean arterial pressure (MAP) records for hypotension assessment.

Data collection

The multicenter retrospective cohort included eligible subjects from three tertiary academic medical centers. Preoperative demographic characteristics, comorbidities, the type of surgical intervention and laboratory test results-were extracted from EHRs and administrative databases. Intraoperative physiological parameters including MAP and surgery duration were automatically captured through standardized anesthesia management systems [26]. Arterial catheters were placed at the fourth intercostal space along the midaxillary line upon hospital admission. Continuous blood pressure monitoring followed instrument calibration (zeroed against atmospheric pressure, Supplementary Figure S1). MAP values were interpolated when missing using MAP = (2 × diastolic blood pressure + systolic blood pressure)/3. Unique patients ensured across-databases linkage integrity.

Exposure of interest

The primary exposure was biological age measured via Phenotypic Age (PhenoAge), computed using a validated algorithm that integrates chronological age with nine biomarkers: albumin, creatinine, glucose, C-reactive protein (CRP), lymphocyte percent, mean cell volume, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), alkaline phosphatase, and white blood cell count. For each biomarker, we selected the most recent measurement within 30 days before surgery to best capture the patient’s immediate preoperative physiological and biological status [17,27]. The secondary exposure was PhenoAgeAccel, derived as the residual from a linear regression of PhenoAge on chronological age to assess physiological deviation from expected aging trajectories. PhenoAgeAccel values >0 indicate accelerated biological aging (biologically older than chronological age) while values <0 indicate decelerated biological aging (biologically younger). PhenoAgeAccel was analyzed as both a continuous variable and categorically by standard deviation (SD) thresholds, specifically: ≤0 SD, 0–1 SD, 1–2 SD, and >2 SD.

Missing data handling

For biomarkers with missingness under 20% (including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, glucose, lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, red blood cell distribution width, serum creatinine, and white blood cell), we implemented multiple imputations to predict values based on existing data patterns, thereby preserving intrinsic variability and variable relationships. For CRP with missingness exceeding 20%, missing values were substituted with cohort-specific arithmetic means to provide stable estimates (Supplementary Table S2). To validate imputation integrity, we repeated analyses exclusively on patients without any imputed parameters, confirming result robustness [28].

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the incidence of IOH. IOH was defined as the presence of a period during surgery where the MAP fell below 60 mmHg, consistent with established criteria [29,30]. To evaluate the potential dose-response relationship between accelerated biological aging and the severity of IOH, we constructed a threshold-time plot using continuously measured intraoperative MAP data. In patients meeting IOH criteria, we quantified severity using dual metrics: (1) cumulative duration (minutes, spent below absolute MAP thresholds), and (2) total area under the curve (AUC) (mmHg*min, area under threshold-specific curves). Specifically, AUC was calculated as the integrated area where MAP values fell below 60 mmHg during surgery, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S2. The AUC was computed numerically using the trapezoidal integration method implemented in R.

Potential confounders

Potential confounders influencing both biological aging biomarkers and study outcomes were identified through triangulation of published literature, expert consensus, and clinical relevance assessments. Adjusted covariates encompassed demographics (chronological age and sex), baseline comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease), and perioperative characteristics (surgery classification and duration).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were evaluated using descriptive statistics and compared between patients with different PhenoAgeAccel. Continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) and median (interquartile range, IQR), and compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) and median (IQR) and compared by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The distribution of PhenoAge across patients with and without IOH was visualized using histogram and boxplots. Correlation analyses between chronological age and biological age were conducted using density heatmap with Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) and mean absolute error (MAE). We also tested the correlation between PhenoAgeAccel and chronological age.

The outcomes were described using number of events and incidence rates (IR). Generalized linear models were employed to evaluate the linear association between accelerated biological aging and IOH related outcomes with adjustment for chronological age, sex, duration of surgery, type of surgery, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. The associations were quantified in terms of absolute risk, risk difference, adjusted odds ratios (aOR) or adjusted coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P-values. Natural splines were used to evaluate the non-linear association between accelerated biological aging and outcomes, with three knots placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the exposure distribution. Statistical significance was established at a two-side P-value <0.05. The statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted in stratified patient groups by chronological age, sex, duration of surgery, and type of surgery. Specifically, age was categorized into five groups: 0–39 years, 40–59 years, 60–69 years, 70–79 years, and 80 years and older. Surgical procedures were classified according to duration shorter than 120 min, equal or longer than 120 min, cardiac surgery, and noncardiac surgery. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure to control for potential inflation of type I error in the subgroup analyses. Interaction tests were further performed by including cross-product terms between PhenoAgeAccel and each stratification variable in the regression models, and likelihood ratio tests were used to obtain P-values for interaction.

Due to the high proportion of missing CRP values, four sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robust of our results. First, analyses were repeated in patients without any missing biomarker variables (‘Restricted cohort’ analysis). Second, PhenoAge and PhenoAgeAccel were recalculated after excluding CRP from the formula (‘Excluding CRP’ analysis). Third, CRP values were multiply imputed and PhenoAge and PhenoAgeAccel were recalculated based on the imputed datasets (‘Multiple-imputed CRP’ analysis). Fourth, to minimize the potential influence of brief or transient episodes, incident IOH was redefined as cumulative duration of MAP < 60 mmHg exceeding 10 min during surgery. Generalized linear models were applied to examine the association between PhenoAgeAccel and hypotensive outcomes.

PhenoAge demonstrated a strong correlation with chronological age in the overall population (PCC = 0.86; MAE = 7.08) and all subgroups (Supplementary Figure S3). PhenoAgeAccel showed minimal correlation with chronological age (PCC < 0.01), confirming its independence from chronological aging as an indicator of biological aging dynamics (Supplementary Figure S4).

Baseline characteristics significantly differed across PhenoAgeAccel categories (all p < 0.001). Biological age progressively increased across groups: 50.9 y (≤0 SD), 59.1 y (0–1 SD), 71.5 y (1–2 SD), 87.6 y (>2 SD). Surgery duration similarly increased: 96.99 min, 106.18 min, 108.21 min, 108.70 min. Comorbidity burdens showed consistent escalation: hypertension (2.2, 2.7, 2.9, and 3.0, p < 0.001), anemia (0.0, 0.2, 0.5, and 1.1, p < 0.001), diabetes (0.6, 1.3, 2.3, and 2.7, p < 0.001), stroke (0.7, 1.1, 1.6, and 1.7, p < 0.001), coronary kidney disease (0.0, 0.0, 0.1, and 4.2, p < 0.001), and chronic respiratory disease (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4, p < 0.001). Patients with SD ≤ 0 had higher noncardiac surgery rates (96.9 vs other groups 94.4-94.7%), lower cardiac morbidity (congestive heart failure: 0.4 vs 0.6–0.7; coronary artery disease: 1.4 vs 2.3–2.6), but elevated cancer incidence (10.7 vs 9.0-9.6%) (Table 1).

Association between accelerated biological aging and IOH

IOH was identified in 15,960 (13.6%) patients. After adjusting for sex, chronological age, duration of surgery, type of surgery, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, generalized linear regression analysis demonstrated that accelerated biological aging significantly increased IOH risk (overall aOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06, p < 0.001), with stratified analysis revealing progressive risk elevation across acceleration groups (vs. SD ≤ 0: 0–1 SD aOR 1.05, 1–2 SD aOR 1.13, >2 SD aOR 1.15) (Table 2). Accelerated biological aging was additionally associated with prolonged IOH duration (aOR 2.93, 95% CI 2.46–3.39; p < 0.001) and increased AUC (aOR 34.22, 95% CI 28.18–40.26; p < 0.001), with natural spline regression confirming inverted U-shaped relationships for all IOH metrics (Figure 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analysis demonstrated significant positive associations between accelerated biological aging and incident IOH among young adults (0–39 years, aOR 1.09, 95% CI 1.05–1.13, p < 0.001), elderly (≥80 years: aOR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.16, p = 0.020), males (aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02–1.08, p = 0.001), females (aOR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, p = 0.016), non-cardiac surgery patients (aOR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.08, p < 0.001), and both surgical duration (≥120 min: aOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.07, p = 0.002; <120 min: aOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.11, p = 0.049). Conversely, cardiac surgery patients showed significant negative association (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98, p = 0.011). PhenoAgeAccel maintained positive associations with IOH duration and AUC across all subgroups except ≥80 years, consistent with primary analyses (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4), while natural spline regression revealed inverted U-shaped trajectories in most subgroups that mirrored main findings; however, cardiac surgery patients displayed divergent patterns: decreasing IOH incidence but increasing duration and AUC with PhenoAgeAccel elevation (Supplementary Figure S5). The results of all sensitivity analyses were consistent with those of the primary analysis. (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that patients with accelerated biological aging would demonstrate a significantly greater risk of experiencing IOH due to compromised organ functional reserve. Our findings suggest that PhenoAgeAccel is an independent predictor of IOH, though the observed associations warrant careful interpretation. Furthermore, our results highlight the potential importance of integrating biological aging assessments into perioperative risk stratification, potentially guiding tailored interventions to mitigate hemodynamic instability in surgical patients.

Advanced chronological age is one of the most important risk factors for IOH [14,31]. Attributable to age-related cardiovascular changes such as significantly reduced cardiovascular reserve, the older surgical candidates were observed to have an exaggerated blood pressure response during induction of anesthesia [32]. Our analysis revealed that biological age often exceeded chronological age, especially among male patients, those undergoing cardiac surgeries, with longer surgical durations, or who experienced IOH. This suggests a potential trend align with previous studies [33,34], that accelerated biological aging may be a more relevant predictor of adverse health conditions such as hypertension, anemia, diabetes, stroke, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and chronic respiratory disease, further supporting the concept that biological age reflects a broader decline in physiological resilience [35–39]. However, it is important to note that several subgroup estimates were imprecise, as indicated by wide confidence intervals, reflecting uncertainty in these specific effects. Furthermore, while PhenoAgeAccel incorporates several biomarkers, it may not capture all aspects of physiological decline that contribute to hemodynamic vulnerability. The variable effect sizes and overlapping distributions for the severity metrics (duration and AUC of IOH) also suggest that the relationship is complex and may be influenced by other perioperative factors not fully accounted for in our model.

Interestingly, our analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between PhenoAgeAccel and the severity of hypotensive events, suggesting that at elevated levels of biological aging, clinicians are more attuned to the signs of extreme aging and are likely to implement preventive measures to mitigate the incidence of IOH. This finding underscores the importance of considering biological aging metrics in clinical assessments to avoid selection bias; patients with advanced biological age may not be adequately represented in typical evaluations. Conversely, when PhenoAgeAccel is low, the implications of accelerated aging may be overlooked by anesthesiologists, who might proceed with induction and maintenance under the assumption that the patient is within the expected chronological age range. This could inadvertently heighten the risk of IOH.

Notably, we observed an inverse association between PhenoAgeAccel and IOH incidence among cardiac surgery patients, a finding that contrasts with the general surgical population. This divergence may reflect the distinct pathophysiology and intensive management inherent to cardiac surgical settings. Such patients frequently present with altered cardiovascular homeostasis and are routinely managed under enhanced hemodynamic monitoring, individualized anesthetic regimens, and goal-directed fluid and vasopressor strategies. These standardized interventions likely attenuate the risk of IOH, irrespective of phenotypic aging trajectories. Consequently, while PhenoAgeAccel may serve as a useful marker of physiological reserve in broader surgical contexts, its relationship with intraoperative blood pressure stability appears modulated by procedure-specific factors and perioperative care intensity.

Previous research has linked accelerated biological aging to various adverse health outcomes, but its role in perioperative hypotension has been less explored. The identification of accelerated biological aging as a risk factor for IOH underscores the potential of incorporating biological aging metrics into preoperative assessments. Although the effect sizes for severity metrics were variable and distributions overlapped, such integration could hypothetically improve the prediction of perioperative complications, allowing for tailored anesthetic management and potentially reducing the incidence of adverse events linked to hypotension. Strategies may include closer hemodynamics monitoring, personalized anesthetic depth adjustments, and prophylactic measures in patients with accelerated biological aging. Our results support this paradigm, suggesting that preoperative assessment of biological age could help identify patients who would benefit most from a tailored, protective hemodynamic management approach aimed at maintaining cardiovascular stability while avoiding the risks of overaggressive intervention.

Our findings should also be interpreted within the context of the ongoing debate surrounding intraoperative blood pressure management. While observational studies consistently demonstrate associations between IOH and adverse postoperative outcomes, randomized trials have failed to establish a clear causal relationship [40]. This discrepancy suggests that patient-specific vulnerability, rather than hypotension itself, may be the primary determinant of poor outcomes. In this regard, accelerated biological aging may represent an underlying state of physiological frailty that predisposes patients both to IOH and to postoperative morbidity. This interpretation aligns with the emerging paradigm of protective hemodynamics, which advocates for individualized, physiology-based blood pressure targets aimed at minimizing iatrogenic harm [41,42]. Our results therefore provide a potential mechanistic link that reconciles prior inconsistencies between observational and interventional evidence and support a tailored hemodynamic management strategy guided by biological age.

A major strength of this study is its large sample size and multicenter design, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the use of a validated and quantifiable measure of biological aging provides a novel approach to understanding perioperative risk. However, this study is subject to several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, retrospective design inherently limits the ability to establish causality between accelerated biological aging and IOH. While our analyses were adjusted for a comprehensive set of clinical covariates, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors, such as subtle differences in frailty, nutritional status, or specific comorbidities, might partly explain the observed associations. Second, CRP had a high proportion of missing values due to medical insurance reimbursement policy, and imputation using the mean value may introduce bias in the estimation of PhenoAgeAccel and its association with clinical outcomes. Third, our definition of IOH incidence, based on any recorded MAP < 60 mmHg, may have led to misclassification by capturing brief or transient episodes that are not clinically meaningful. However, to better characterize the severity of hypotension, we additionally quantified the cumulative duration and AUC below this threshold. Fourth, the lack of external validation may limit the robustness of our findings and precludes confirmation of their reproducibility in other independent datasets. Fifth, the study population was extracted from three tertiary centers in Eastern China, which may not fully represent the broader surgical population. Extrapolation of our findings to Western cohorts with distinct demographic characteristics (e.g. genetic backgrounds, comorbidity profiles) and perioperative practices (e.g. anesthetic drug choices, fluid management strategies, blood pressure intervention thresholds, and adherence to enhanced recovery protocols) should be made with caution. Nonetheless, the generalizability of our findings should not be overlooked. The rapidly aging population in Asia highlights the global relevance of our study, as older adults are particularly vulnerable to hypotension due to reduced baroreflex sensitivity and other physiological changes [43,44]. Furthermore, the ease of measuring biological age supports its potential integration into preoperative risk stratification across diverse populations, thereby improving surgical outcomes worldwide. Future research should aim to validate these findings in diverse, multinational cohorts, as well as explore the mechanistic pathways through which accelerated biological aging influences hemodynamic stability during surgical procedures.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates a clear association between accelerated biological aging and increased risk of IOH in a large, predominantly major surgical population. These findings underscore the importance of considering biological aging in perioperative assessments and developing tailored anesthetic strategies to improve hemodynamic stability and surgical outcomes for biologically older patients. The integration of biological aging metrics, such as PhenoAgeAccel, into preoperative evaluation could help identify patients at high risk for hemodynamic instability, enabling targeted monitoring and preemptive strategies to mitigate intraoperative hypotension.
