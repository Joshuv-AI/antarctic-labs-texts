Quantum kinetic theory of quadratic responses
M. Mehraeen

Abstract
Recent work has revealed a general procedure for incorporating disorder into the semiclassical model of carrier transport, whereby the predictions of quantum linear response theory can be recovered within a quantum kinetic approach based on a disorder-averaged density-matrix formalism. Here, we present a comprehensive generalization of this framework to the nonlinear response regime. In the presence of an electrostatic potential and random impurities, we solve the quantum Liouville equation to second order in an applied electric field and derive the carrier densities and equations of motion. In addition to the anomalous velocity arising from the Berry curvature and the Levi-Civita connection of the quantum metric tensor, a host of extrinsic velocities emerge in the equations of motion, reflecting the various possibilities for random interband walks of the carriers in this transport regime. Furthermore, several scattering and conduction channels arise, which can be classified in terms of distinct physical processes, revealing numerous unexplored mechanisms for generating nonlinear responses in disordered condensed matter systems.

III
Linear density matrix

First, we revisit the quantum kinetic equation at linear order in the applied electric field. This transport regime has been extensively studied in the literature and the discussion presented in this section is not new. However, since the contributions to the linear-response densities and their physical interpretations are required for obtaining and classifying quadratic-response quantities, for completeness–as well as for notational uniformity–we briefly review here the main results. IV
Quadratic density matrix

We now extend this framework to nonlinear responses. In the quadratic response regime, wherein one is also interested in the second-order deviation of an electron distribution from equilibrium, there are also
O(\tau^{2})
contributions to the density operator. Thus, the appropriate expansion to consider is

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}=n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, where we retain sub-subleading disorder corrections as well. This is an essential formal inclusion, partly due to the general expectation that intrinsic effects should appear at any order in the field expansion in the absence of disorder. Similar to the linear response regime–where band-off-diagonal elements do not appear at leading order in the disorder–for steady-state solutions, one can readily verify that
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)}
will not contribute to the density operator. Thus, the field-quadratic decomposition of the quantum kinetic equation results in the five general equations

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)}\right)=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)},

(14a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}+\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H^{0},S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right]=D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)},

(14b)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(f_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right)+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(f_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right)=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbf{k}}f_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right)^{a},

(14c)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}+\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H^{0},S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]&=D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}+D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\\
&+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)},\end{split}

(14d)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}\left(f_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right)+J_{E\mathbf{k}}\left(f_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right)+J_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}\left(f_{0\mathbf{k}}\right)=0.

(14e)

The solution to Eq. (
14a
) for the nonlinear density is simply

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)}=\left(\frac{e}{\hbar}\right)^{2}E_{\mu}E_{\nu}\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}\partial_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mu}(\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}\partial_{\mathbf{k}}^{\nu}f_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}),

(15)

which quantifies the ordinary scattering of electrons at second order in the field expansion. Below, we discuss in detail the solutions of the remaining four equations and their associated physical processes.

IV.1
Off-diagonal elements

Consider first the off-diagonal elements. The evolution equation for
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
, Eq. (
14b
), is quite similar to that of
S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, given by Eq. (
9b
), and may be regarded as its quadratic-response counterpart, with the analogous higher-order driving terms

D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)ab}=\frac{ie}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}(n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}-n_{E\mathbf{k}b}^{(-1)})
and

I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)ab}=-J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{ab}(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)})
and the solution

S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}=\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}G^{R}(\varepsilon)\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right]G^{A}(\varepsilon),

(16)

A quick comparison between Eq. (
74
) and Eq. (
75
) reveals that
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
simply corresponds to
S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
with the densities replaced by their higher-order counterparts in the electric field. The situation is different for the other off-diagonal component,
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, which includes terms that have no linear-response counterparts. As indicated in Eq. (
14d
), its evolution is determined by five distinct driving terms, which consist of two covariant-derivative-type terms and three collision integral-type terms, given by

\displaystyle D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{ie}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\left[n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(0)}-n_{E\mathbf{k}b}^{(0)}\right],

(17a)

\displaystyle D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)ab}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbf{k}}S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]^{ab},

(17b)

\displaystyle I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)ab}

\displaystyle=-J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right),

(17c)

\displaystyle I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)ab}

\displaystyle=-J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\left(n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right),

(17d)

\displaystyle I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)ab}

\displaystyle=-J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right),

(17e)

with the familiar formal solution

\begin{split}S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}&=\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}G^{R}(\varepsilon)\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}+D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right.\\
&\left.+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}\right]G^{A}(\varepsilon).\end{split}

(18)

It is worth noting that the two driving terms
D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}
and
I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}
are themselves functions of off-diagonal density matrix elements and can therefore be further decomposed as

\displaystyle S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}\right]

\displaystyle=S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}[D_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}]\right]+S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}[I_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}]\right],

(19a)

\displaystyle S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}\right]

\displaystyle=S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\right]+S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\right],

(19b)

We thus observe that, in comparison to the relatively simple picture in the linear response regime, there are a large number of terms which contribute to the off-diagonal density matrix elements in the quadratic response regime, reflecting a wealth of interconnected scattering processes that are illustrated schematically in Fig.
1
. We next discuss the derivation of the carrier densities and the ensuing physical classification of these processes.

IV.2
Mixed scattering

Consider Eq. (
14c
), which must be solved for
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
. Based on the previously stated correspondence between
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
and
S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, it is straightforward to conclude that this equation is essentially the field-quadratic counterpart to Eq. (
9c
) and thus describes the special (skew, side-jump) scattering of ordinary-scattered electrons. A difference, however, with the linear response is the additional covariant-derivative term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
14c
), which includes terms that physically describe the ordinary scattering of electrons having previously undergone side jump or skew scattering at linear order in the electric field [see Eqs. (
84
) and (
85
)]. The conclusion, therefore, is that
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
measures the density of electrons which undergo a mixture of consecutive special and ordinary scatterings and thus consists of the four distinct contributions

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}=n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{sj,o})}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{sk,o})}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{o,sj})}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{o,sk})},

(20)

where

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{sp,o})}
is the density of ordinary-scattered electrons which subsequently experience special scattering (sp=sj,sk), and
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{o,sp})}
is the density of special-scattered electrons which then undergo ordinary scattering. As a result, Eq. (
14c
) is also decomposed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{sj,o})}\right)+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right]\right)+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right)\\
&=-\frac{ie}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}},S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]^{a},\end{split}

(21a)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{sk,o})}\right)+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}\right]\right)=0,

(21b)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{o,sj})}\right)=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{sj})},

(21c)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{o,sk})}\right)=\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{sk})},

(21d)

the solutions of which–presented in Appendix
D
–yield the mixed-scattering carrier densities.

IV.3
Zeroth-order scattering

We next turn to Eq. (
14e
), the last of the five main equations arising from the quantum kinetic equation in the quadratic response regime, which must be solved for
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
. Similar to the special scattering processes in the linear regime, this includes the density matrix elements which are formally of
O(n_{I}^{0})
in the disorder expansion, but can nevertheless arise from the concerted actions of disorder and the geometry of Bloch states. A relatively large number of collision integrals contribute to the density matrix at this order, leading to a multitude of scattering processes. Upon inspection, these are classified as five physically distinct processes, which we label as

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}=n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(ssj)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(qsj)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(tsk)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(csk)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(ask)}},

(22)

resulting in the decomposition

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{ssj})}\right)+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]\right)+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right)=0,

(23a)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{csk})}\right)+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\right]\right)=0,

(23b)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{tsk})}\right)+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]\right)=0,

(23c)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{ask})}\right)+\mathcal{I}_{1}=0,

(23d)

\displaystyle J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{qsj})}\right)+\mathcal{I}_{2}=0,

(23e)

with the lengthier expressions
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
presented in Appendix
C
. Below, we elucidate this classification and elaborate on the physical significance of each term.

IV.3.1
secondary side jump

Consider first Eq. (
23a
). Note that this equation is structurally analogous to Eq. (
13a
) and thus describes a side-jump process, with the difference that it acts on
n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
. It is then clear that
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(\text{ssj})}
measures the density the electrons that are first subjected to a side-jump or skew scattering process and then undergo an additional side jump. This secondary side-jump density is thus itself comprised of two terms

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(ssj)}}=n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sj,sj)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sj,sk)}},

(24)

each of which is obtained by solving the relevant contribution to Eq. (
23a
).

IV.3.2
tertiary skew scattering

Given the presence of secondary side-jump scatterings, it is natural to ask whether a special scattering event could be proceeded by a skew-scattering process as well. To answer this in the affirmative, consider next Eq. (
23c
). Based on the form of the collision integral

J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]\right)
given in Appendix
A
, it is evident that this measures the skew scattering of electrons which have initially experienced mixed scattering. Thus, as illustrated in Fig.
1
, overall, this describes a three-stage scattering process at least, which we refer to as tertiary skew scattering. From the fourfold decomposition of
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
given by Eq. (
20
), it is clear that
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(tsk)}}
also decomposes as

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(tsk)}}=n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sk,sj,o)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sk,o,sj)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sk,sk,o)}}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{\text{(sk,o,sk)}},

(25)

yielding four distinct contributions.

IV.3.3
quadratic side jump

In the quadratic response regime, there is yet another source of side-jump scattering. In addition to the secondary side jump, which arises from collision integrals containing corrections to
n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
that are linear in the electric field, one must also take into consideration field-quadratic corrections to the equilibrium distribution function itself. These quadratic side-jump contributions are obtained by solving Eq. (
23e
) for
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(qsj)}}
, which reads

\begin{split}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{qsj})}&=-\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left\{J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}[D_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}]\right]\right)\right.\\
&\left.+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]\right)+J_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(f_{0\mathbf{k}}\right)\right\}.\end{split}

(26)

The explicit form of
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\text{(qsj)}}
is rather lengthy and can be found in Appendix
D
. It is interesting to note, however, the appearance of the quantum metric in Eq. (
103
), which then compels one to interpret the quadratic side jump as being partly driven by the quantum metric tensor. This is in line with the findings of Ref.
[
134
]
, which relates the quantum metric to the field-induced positional shift of the carriers.

IV.3.4
cubic skew scattering

In addition to the tertiary skew scattering, the carrier density of which contains
\braket{UU}^{2}
-type terms, there is another source of skew scattering, which consists of terms that are cubic in
\braket{UU}
and acts on the density of ordinary-scattered electrons,
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)}
, guaranteeing that the resultant density is of O(
n_{I}^{0}
). The carrier density from this cubic skew scattering is obtained by solving Eq. (
23b
), yielding

n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{csk})}=-\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\right]\right),

(27)

where, once again, we relegate the lengthy explicit form to Appendix
D
.

IV.3.5
anomalous skew scattering

Lastly, we consider Eq. (
23d
), the solution of which is

\begin{split}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(\text{ask})}&=-\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left\{J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}[I_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}]\right]\right)\right.\\
&\left.+J_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}]\right]\right)\right.\\
&\left.+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}\right]\right)+J_{E\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\left[I_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]\right)\right\}.\end{split}

(28)

This describes an anomalous skew scattering process, whereby electrons which scatter ordinarily at linear order in the electric field,
n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
, subsequently undergo a field-dependent scattering with a skew scattering disorder profile. As is clear from the relevant expressions in Appendix
D
, the resultant carrier density is thus comprised of terms proportional to the product of the covariant derivative and the antisymmetric parts of
\braket{UU}\braket{UU}
-type terms.

IV.4
General remarks on the formalism

Having presented the derivation of the density matrix, we note that a feature of the density-matrix formalism that becomes evident in the kinetic approach is the ability to provide a detailed mapping of the scattering channels that arise through the various processes and the interconnections between the various carrier densities. One could argue that this feature is slightly obscured in other methods, such as diagrammatic considerations, which typically do not deal directly with carrier densities. An alternative distinction scheme, which complements the viewpoint presented here, is to classify all non-ordinary scatterings as either side jump or skew scattering, where the former process would involve contributions from the conduction band only, while the latter receives contributions from both the valence and conduction bands. Despite this, as we illustrate for a specific model in Section
VI
, one could argue for the merits of a more detailed distinction between the various scatterings, as processes which would otherwise fall within the same classification subtype turn out to have noticeably different scaling signatures in the response of the system.

In closing this section, it is also useful to briefly compare the formalism presented in this work to another recent quantum kinetic approach discussed in Ref.
[
22
]
. There, the collision integral is approximated as being proportional to the density matrix via a constant relaxation time such that scatterings are accounted for by introducing intraband and interband relaxation times for the diagonal and off-diagonal components of the quantum kinetic equation, respectively. In the present approach, we assume the diagonal elements of the collision integral are proportional to a general momentum-dependent transport time, the solution of which is obtained from extracting the carrier density from the collision integral. This results in a transport time that is physically equivalent to including self-energy and vertex corrections in its derivation
[
15
]
. Furthermore, the solution of the off-diagonal quantum kinetic equation at different field and impurity orders emerges once the relevant carrier densities are known. Therefore, the transport time manifests in the off-diagonal density matrix as well through the carrier densities. Nevertheless, as is evident in both approaches, at higher orders beyond linear responses, intraband and interband processes are intricately connected, leading to a variety of physical phenomena in the nonlinear response regime.

V
Emergent Velocities and Modified Semiclassics

Having delved into the solution of the quantum Liouville equation, we may now proceed to evaluate the modified semiclassical equations of motion and, subsequently, the disorder-dressed quadratic current density. The former is found by adopting the proposed prescription outlined in Ref.
[
14
]
, the essential idea of which is to reexpress the ensemble average of the velocity operator entirely in terms of the diagonal elements of the density matrix,
i.e.
, the carrier densities. That is, we solve the equation

\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\dot{\mathbf{r}}^{a}n_{\mathbf{k}a}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}f_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab},

(29)

for the time evolution of the carrier position

\dot{\mathbf{r}}^{a}
, where

\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv(1/\hbar)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbf{k}}H_{\mathbf{k}}^{0}
is the velocity operator. Using the results from the previous section, the right-hand side of Eq. (
29
) reads

\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}f_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{\mathbf{k}a}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}S_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}.

(30)

The first term on the right, which is already diagonal, describes the ordinary group velocity experienced by all the various carriers. Our goal then is to band-diagonalize the second term. For the linear-response term, inserting Eq. (
12
) leads to contributions from the anomalous and extrinsic velocities

\begin{split}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}\right)\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)ab}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}f_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\right.\\
&\left.+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}\right],\end{split}

(31)

where the extrinsic velocity is defined as
[
14
]

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Braket{\left[U,G^{A}(\varepsilon)\left[U,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]G^{R}(\varepsilon)\right]}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a},

(32)

with the prime indicating that only off-diagonal elements of the Berry connection are evaluated–reflecting the gauge invariance of the extrinsic velocity. Explicitly, this reads

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=\frac{2}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}b}\text{Im}\left(\frac{\Braket{U_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}\left[U,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{ba}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}-i\delta}\right).

(33)

We now extend this analysis to the quadratic response contributions. The first term to consider is

S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
. Based on the previously stated analogy with its linear response counterpart
S_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, it is straightforward to verify that

\begin{split}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}\right)\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)ab}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}\right.\\
&\left.+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)}\right].\end{split}

(34)

Thus the effect of
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
is to simply describe the contributions of the anomalous and extrinsic velocities in the quadratic response regime, where we note that the anomalous contribution is that of the familar Berry curvature dipole
[
93
]
, albeit with the full disorder dependence of the transport time. Similarly, for the
S_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
contributions from

D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
and

I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
, inserting the relevant terms of Eq. (
18
) into Eq. (
30
) yields

\begin{split}-\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ba}\left[D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}+I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}\right]^{ab}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[\frac{e}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(0)}\right.\\
&\left.+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}\right],\end{split}

(35)

showing that the higher-order carriers also experience these two velocities.

We next turn to the contributions from Eq. (
18
) which have no counterparts in the linear response regime. The first term to consider is

D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}
, given by Eq. (
17b
). After a little algebra, it can be shown that the contribution of this term to Eq. (
30
) is also comprised of an intrinsic part and an extrinsic part as

\begin{split}-\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\mathcal{A}_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{ba}D_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)ab}&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}E^{\nu}E^{\rho}\sum_{b}M_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu\mathbf{k}}^{ba}f_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}\right.\\
&\left.+\beta_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)},\right],\end{split}

(36)

where

M_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\equiv 2\hbar/(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b})
with
a\neq b
. We thus see that from the off-diagonal density matrix, the geodesic term naturally emerges through this approach in the equations of motion and carrier transport, which is quite reasonable, given the Riemannian structure on the underlying manifold of quantum states
[
129
]
.

The remaining term in Eq. (
36
) arises from a new extrinsic velocity,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}
, given by

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\\
&\times\Braket{\left[U,G^{A}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\left[U,G^{A}(\varepsilon)\left[V,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]G^{R}(\varepsilon)\right]G^{R}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a},\end{split}

(37)

or explicitly as

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=\frac{2e}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}b}\text{Re}\left(\frac{\Braket{U_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}\left[U,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\right]_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{ba}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}-i\delta}\right),

(38)

with

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}=-\left[\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}/(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}+i\delta)
,
which is linear in the applied electric field and thus appears only in the nonlinear transport regime, in contrast to the field-independent
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
, which manifests in both linear and nonlinear responses. This then suggests a general pattern, in which extrinsic velocities that are at most of
O(E^{n-1})
contribute to the
n
-th order response, in agreement with a recent study based on a semiclassical Boltzmann transport analysis
[
126
]
. This is indeed the case for the remaining quadratic-response velocities, as we show below.

Moving on to Eq. (
17d
), the contribution from

I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)}
is also that of an extrinsic velocity, which reads

-\sum_{\mathbf{k}ab}\mathcal{A}_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{ba}I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime(0)ab}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\gamma_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)},

(39)

with

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\\
&\times\Braket{\left[U,G^{A}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\left[V,G^{A}(\varepsilon)\left[U,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]G^{R}(\varepsilon)\right]G^{R}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a},\end{split}

(40)

or

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=\frac{2e}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}b}\text{Re}\left(\frac{\Braket{U_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}\left[\left(\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\right]_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{ba}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}-i\delta}\right),

(41)

with

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}=-\left[U,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\right]_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}/(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}+i\delta)
. As can be seen, this differs from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}
by an exchange in the orders of the electrostatic and disorder potential in the formal definition.

Lastly, the contribution arising from
I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)}
can be expressed as two separate extrinsic velocities acting on the linear and quadratic densities

-\sum_{\mathbf{k}aa^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a^{\prime}a}I_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{\prime\prime(0)aa^{\prime}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[\kappa_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}+\chi_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)}\right],

(42)

with

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=ie\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\left[G^{R}(\varepsilon)\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}G^{A}(\varepsilon),\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right]_{\mathbf{k}}^{a},

(43)

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\\
&\times\Braket{\left[UG^{A}(\varepsilon^{\prime})G^{A}(\varepsilon),\boldsymbol{\alpha}G^{R}(\varepsilon)UG^{R}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]+\text{h.c.}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a},\end{split}

(44)

which explicitly read

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=2e\sum_{b}\text{Re}\left(\frac{\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime ab}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime ba}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}-i\delta}\right),

(45)

and

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}&=2\sum_{bc}\text{Re}\left[\frac{\braket{U_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{ab}U_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{ca}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{bc}}{(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}-i\delta)(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{b}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{c}+i\delta)}\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{\braket{U_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{bc}U_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}^{ca}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}}{(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{c}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-i\delta)(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{b}+i\delta)}\right].\end{split}

(46)

It is perhaps interesting to note from these terms that the off-diagonal elements of the linear-response velocity
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
also contribute to the transport, albeit as higher-order extrinsic terms.

Gathering all the above terms, and using the equation of motion for the carrier momentum,

\dot{\mathbf{k}}^{a}=-e\mathbf{E}/\hbar
,
we ultimately arrive at the equation of motion for the carrier position given by Eq. (
1
). And at second order in the electric field, the total current density–defined as

\mathbf{j}\equiv-e\text{Tr}(\mathbf{v}\braket{\rho})
–is expressed as

\begin{split}j_{\mu E^{2}}&=-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\left[v_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}E_{\nu}\Omega_{\rho\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}+\left(\frac{e}{\hbar}\right)^{2}E_{\nu}E_{\rho}\sum_{b}M_{\mathbf{k}}^{ab}\Gamma_{\nu\rho\mu\mathbf{k}}^{ba}f_{0\mathbf{k}}^{a}+\alpha_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}\left(n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)}+n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}\right)\right.\\
&\left.\hskip 189.75105pt+\left(\beta_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}+\gamma_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}+\kappa_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}\right)n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}+\chi_{\mu\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)},\right].\end{split}

(47)

Several remarks regarding these results are in order. First, as one would expect, the leading-order disorder contribution to the quadratic current is

\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(-2)}=-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)},

(48)

which arises from the group velocity of ordinary-scattered carriers. At next order in the impurity density, three terms contribute, which are related to the group velocity of mixed-scattered carriers, the Berry curvature dipole
[
93
]
and the leading order contribution from the (linear-response) extrinsic velocity as

\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(-1)}=\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{mix})}+\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{ex})}+\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{BC})},

(49)

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{mix})}=-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)},

(50a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{ex})}=-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)},

(50b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}_{E^{2}}^{(\text{BC})}=-\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\mathbf{E}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}.

(50c)

The remaining terms are then of
O(n_{I}^{0})
. One such term is the anomalous velocity experienced by
n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(0)}
carriers, leading to anomalous nonlinear currents arising from side-jump and skew scattering processes. Interestingly, such nonlinear responses have also been recently proposed in Refs.
[
113
,
23
]
. Another
O(n_{I}^{0})
term in Eq. (
47
) is that of the quantum metric, which has recently garnered significant interest as an intrinsic nonlinear response
[
105
,
20
,
109
,
110
,
112
,
135
,
114
,
115
]
and makes a natural appearance in the density-matrix formalism. The remaining
O(n_{I}^{0})
terms are related to the group velocity of the carriers due to zeroth-order scattering and the various extrinsic velocities experienced by the carriers, revealing the intricate interplay between disorder and quantum geometry at this order.

In order to gain better insight into the emergence of extrinsic velocities in the carrier dynamics and transport in the nonlinear response regime, it is helpful to first revisit the linear response of the system, in which the nonequilibrium Fermi-surface carriers
n_{E\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
experience the extrinsic velocity
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
. In the presence of disorder, the electrons undergo random scatterings off the impurities, which leads to fluctuations in their velocities as they scatter between different energy bands. The net effect of this process is an overall change in the average velocity of the electrons within each energy band, which is captured by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}
and contributes to the transport at the Fermi level through the nonequilibrium carriers.

Extending this picture to the quadratic responses, it is clear that Eq. (
50b
) describes a similar effect of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
on the field-corrected distribution
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-2)}
. The effect at the Fermi surface can be understood through an integration by parts

-e\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}a}^{(-2)}=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}a}n_{E\mathbf{k}a}^{(-1)}\mathbf{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}^{a},

(51)

where we have introduced the extrinsic transport length vector

\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\alpha\mathbf{k}}^{a}=\tau_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{a}
, which measures the average displacement the carriers experience over the transport time by the random interband walks on the Fermi surface. From Eq. (
51
), we see that this average displacement receives a correction from the electric field–absent in the linear response–which results in a nonlinear current contribution at
O(n_{I}^{-1})
. At next order in the impurity density, a similar argument can be applied for the contribution of
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\mathbf{k}}
as well as for that of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with the difference that in the latter case, the mixed-scattering carriers
n_{E^{2}\mathbf{k}}^{(-1)}
come into play, indicating additional intermediate side-jump and skew-scattering processes.

Furthermore, the emergence of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\mathbf{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\mathbf{k}}
can be attributed to the fact that the electric field itself can also induce interband coherence effects through its coupling with the position operator in the electrostatic interaction. In the absence of disorder, this field-mediated interband mixing can be understood as giving rise to the intrinsic Berry curvature and quantum metric contributions in Eq. (
1
). And when disorder is present, the combined effect of the two interactions can give rise to new means of interband mixing such that the resulting extrinsic velocities acquire field dependencies. This is in contrast to the linear response regime, where–within the weak-disorder limit–the electrostatic and scattering corrections to the Bloch states can be separated, resulting in only independent corrections to physical observables
[
12
]
.

Broken time reversal symmetry

To illustrate the leading and subleading responses that emerge from the theory, consider a minimal model of 2D tilted massive Dirac fermions

VI.2
Restoring time reversal symmetry

In order to better explore the contributions of the subleading terms and their different scalings with the system parameters, it is useful to also consider the time-reversal symmetric counterpart of the model given by Eq. (
52
), which can be obtained by extending the system to include the transformed Hamiltonian with negative tilt and mass,
(\mathbf{t},m)\rightarrow-(\mathbf{t},m)
. Note that negating the mass corresponds to the transformation
\theta\rightarrow\pi-\theta
in the angular functions
\mathcal{S}_{\mu xx}(\theta)
given by Eqs. (
156
-
162
) in Appendix

\sigma_{\mu xx}^{(\text{o})}(-\mathbf{t},-m)=-\sigma_{\mu xx}^{(\text{o})}(\mathbf{t},m).

(70)

Therefore, the subleading terms in the broken time reversal system are the leading-order terms in the extended time-reversal-invariant system and will dominate the transport. And their contributions to the transport are obtained by simply doubling the broken time reversal expressions of the conductivities presented above.

In Fig.

As can be seen, there are noticeable qualitative and quantitative differences between the contributions, such that the sum of the side-jump terms alone can give rise to a sign change of the conductivity (see the solid blue curve). VII
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we have presented a nonlinear response theory based on a recently-developed density-matrix formalism in the linear response regime
[
10
,
11
,
14
]
, whereby the density matrix is decomposed into disorder-averaged and fluctuating parts. By treating the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the density matrix in band space on an equal footing, the full contribution of the disorder interaction can be taken into account in a systematic manner. And through the perturbative solution of the quantum Liouville equation in the presence of electrostatic and weak disorder potentials, we have shown how the combined actions of the electric field and random impurities give rise to a multitude of scattering channels in the quadratic response regime, resulting in a classification of carrier densities in terms of distinct physical processes.

Moreover, in addition to the previously known linear-response extrinsic velocity, several extrinsic velocities have been identified, which only appear in the nonlinear response of the system, revealing the rich structure of interband coherence effects in this transport regime. As an application of this theory, we have studied the quadratic response in a prototypical model of tilted Dirac fermions, and have derived the leading and subleading disorder contributions to the nonlinear conductivities.

This work suggests several follow-up studies. One possible direction would be to extend the analysis to nonlinear spin currents, which would allow for a detailed classification of spin-orbit torques in the nonlinear response regime. Another direction would be to apply the theory to more complicated disorder profiles, which have recently been shown to play a role in generating nonlinear responses
[
85
,
77
,
91
]
. Yet another possible direction would be to explore the zeroth-order contributions in specific materials–as has recently been initiated through other approaches as well
[
126
,
138
]
–in order to develop a better understanding of the contributions of the quadratic-response extrinsic velocities and their interplay with quantum geometry in this transport regime.

In closing, we note some physically relevant limits that can be relaxed in future studies based on the present approach. First, we have focussed our analysis on DC responses. Generalizing this to the AC regime, which is particularly significant in the study of nonlinear optical responses, will allow for the continued exploration of the resonance structures of nonlinear responses. This can have interesting signatures in quantum materials
[
19
]
and can be utilized as useful probes of quantum geometry
[
20
]
. In addition, in this initial study, we have limited our analysis to low temperatures. Relaxing this enables further analysis of finite-temperature effects via the quantum kinetic approach, including the effects of thermal magnons and nonlinear thermoelectric responses
[
24
]
. Finally, while it is customary to include the effect of a magnetic field in semiclassical transport studies of spin-orbit-coupled materials via the Zeeman interaction–similar to the exchange coupling in magnetic systems, a thorough analysis would also require the inclusion of the magnetic field in the derivation of the equations of motion. This leads to additional contributions, such as the coupling to the Berry curvature and modifying the density of states, which can then manifest in the nonlinear response of the system
[
134
]
.
