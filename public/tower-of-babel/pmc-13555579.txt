Discovery of TCR-like antibodies to the KRAS G12D neoantigen via in silico-in vitro workflow
Authors: SangPhil Ahn, Tae-Sung Oh, Seonghyuk Suh, Joon-Young Jeon, Sangjoon Lah, Kyeongmin Ryu, Sanghyeon Choi, Hyeonkyeong Kim, Eun Gyo Lee, Hyejin Lee, JooYeon Lee, Yoonji Kim, Sanggil Kim, So Ra Park, Hojin Yoo, So-Young Choi, Joo Eon Lee, Dong-Kyun Kim, Bo Mi Ku, Wooram Jung, Myung-Ju Ahn, Jae U. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibodies that recognize peptide-loaded class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules could enable therapeutic targeting of intracellular oncogenic proteins, yet their discovery has been hampered by the small size of peptide antigens and the need for allele-specific recognition. Here, we describe an integrated in silico-in vitro workflow for generating high-affinity, selective antibodies to Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) G12D10 presented by human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-C∗08:02, a clinically validated cancer neoantigen. In the in silico stage, multiple human antibody-derived variable fragments optimally docked to the target were generated, followed by limited sequence design of complementarity-determining regions (CDRs). In the in vitro stage, limited CDR diversity was introduced to construct a yeast surface-displayed library, which was subjected to iterative selection. This workflow yielded KRAS G12D10/HLA-C∗08:02-specific antibodies with high affinity. Comprehensive specificity profiling using a phage display library confirmed the absence of human off-target reactivity, and in silico analysis predicted low immunogenicity. When reformatted as chimeric antigen receptors or bispecific T cell engagers, these antibodies mediated selective cytotoxicity against target-positive cells. Together, these findings establish a practical design-to-function pipeline for T cell receptor (TCR)-like antibody discovery and demonstrate the feasibility of therapeutically targeting KRAS G12D-driven malignancies.

Introduction

The Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) is one of the most frequently mutated proto-oncogenes in human cancers, with activating mutations at glycine 12 (G12X), common in pancreatic, colorectal, and non-small cell lung cancers.1,2 Although a range of small-molecule inhibitors, including sotorasib and adagrasib, have been developed for select alleles such as KRAS G12C, these therapies are limited by allele specificity and often exhibit transient clinical benefits due to pathway reactivation or resistance.3,4,5 Additionally, broader strategies targeting pan-KRAS or other common variants, such as G12D and G12V, remain under intensive investigation but face challenges of specificity and therapeutic index.6,7

KRAS cannot be reached by conventional antibodies because it is intracellular, but its cleaved peptide is loaded onto and presented by class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) composed of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) and β2-microglobulin (β2M) at the cell surface, providing a surface-exposed alternative target.8 Leveraging this antigen-presentation axis, multiple therapeutic strategies have emerged, including cancer vaccines, T cell receptor (TCR)-engineered therapies, and peptide-loaded class I MHC (pMHC)-directed TCR-like antibodies.9,10,11 Currently, NT-112, a TCR-engineered T cell therapy targeting the KRAS G12D decamer peptide (KRAS G12D10; GADGVGKSAL) presented by HLA-C∗08:02/β2M (hereafter called KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08), is in a phase 1/2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06218914), building on reports of durable responses in two patients with advanced pancreatic cancer treated under compassionate use.12,13 Despite these precedents, therapeutic targeting of pMHCs remains technically demanding. Soluble TCRs require laborious refolding and stabilization, and TCR-engineered T cells rely on ex vivo cell engineering, together imposing manufacturing burdens and limiting patient access.14,15 On the other hand, cancer vaccines rely on the endogenous T cell repertoire, which in advanced disease is often sub-optimally primed or functionally exhausted.16

TCR-like antibodies, which combine TCR-level specificity with the manufacturability and stability of antibody scaffolds, represent an alternative modality amenable to broad deployment. However, the development of antibodies that specifically recognize short peptides, typically 8–11 amino acids in length, presented on MHC remains a considerable challenge.17 Discrimination must be achieved on highly similar HLA scaffolds, where informative contacts are largely confined to solvent-exposed peptide side chains. Thus, conventional screening often fails to recover such rare binders. Recent progress in computational design has yielded de novo binders against specified pMHC epitopes using diffusion-based generative models and structure-guided docking pipelines.18,19,20,21

The KRAS G12D10 epitope qualifies as a public antigen, a shared, tumor-specific peptide generated by recurrent driver mutations and displayed by a common HLA allele, and is therefore broadly targetable in defined patient subsets.22,23 To date, there have been no reports of a TCR-like antibody targeting KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08. Here, we report an integrated in silico-in vitro approach to discover and develop high-affinity TCR-like antibodies that selectively target the KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08. This approach integrates epitope-centric in silico design with focused complementarity-determining region (CDR) library screening to discover and optimize antibodies with strong binding affinity and stringent specificity. Structural analysis by X-ray crystallography delineates the epitope-focused binding mode, while orthogonal specificity is confirmed across related pMHCs. These antibodies exhibit selective cytotoxic activity when reformatted as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) or single-chain diabody (scDb) bispecific T cell engager molecules. Our work demonstrates the translational relevance of these antibodies in targeting the KRAS G12D10 neoantigen in HLA-C∗08-positive tumors, and the integrated in silico-in vitro workflow would be broadly applicable to the development of TCR-like antibodies.

Results

Integrated in silico-in vitro approach to discover antibodies to KRAS G12D10/HLA-C∗08:02 complex

Antibody-antigen interactions occur primarily through CDRs, which form flexible loops lacking defined secondary structures. This inherent structural plasticity poses challenges for computational antibody design, limiting generalizability and reducing success rates with current methods.24,25 To address these limitations, we applied a previously developed pipeline that maintains the canonical backbone conformation of CDR loops while constraining residue design to structurally compatible positions, guided by position-specific consensus residue preferences.26

We first aligned available TCR-like antibody/pMHC complexes to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08.27 The original pMHC in each complex was then replaced with KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08, resulting in various docking orientations of the TCR-like antibodies relative to the target. We then superposed fragment variables (Fvs) from TCR-like and human antibodies onto the aligned TCR-like antibodies. Candidate Fvs were ranked using multiple metrics and visually inspected to identify models with sterically feasible docking geometry and peptide accessibility (Figure 1A). Next, for sequence design and optimization, we used an in-house version of ProteinMPNN28 trained for antibody structures; residue positions proximal to the peptide were identified based on structural analyses, including Cα-Cβ vector orientation and loop flexibility, while residue positions essential for preserving loop conformation were excluded from the design. Additionally, multiple sequence alignment-based amino acid frequency profiles were applied to bias sampling toward evolutionarily preferred residues at each position.29,30 Designed pMHCs were generated and relaxed by using Rosetta FastRelax.31 Based on multiple filtering criteria in Rosetta, we finally selected nine Fvs for in vitro discovery steps.Figure 1Integrated computational design and experimental screening(A) Three-step computational workflow. Three to four residues within the CDR-H3 and CDR-L3 loops of each candidate were randomized to construct a pooled single-chain Fv (scFv) yeast surface-displayed (YSD) library. The library was screened against KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 tetramers or monomers, and antigen-positive clones were enriched by iterative fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). To ensure specificity, counter-selection was performed with SARSpep/HLA-C∗08:02 (hereafter SARS/HLA-C∗08) during intermediate rounds. SARSpep is a 9-mer peptide (ITDAVDCAL) derived from SARS-CoV-2. After several enrichment cycles, a hit clone, C8K10D, was isolated (Figures 1B and S1). Sequence analysis indicated that this clone has an Fv sequence similar to the antibody in a pMHC (PDB: 1W72) but adopts an orientation resembling another pMHC (PDB: 7BBG) according to the predicted design model. Biolayer interferometry (BLI) confirmed binding of the C8K10D Fab to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of ∼1.36 μM (Figure 1C, left). No binding was detected to SARS/HLA-C∗08 or p53(R175H) (HMTEVVRHC) bound to HLA-A∗02:01 (here after, p53(R175H)/HLA-A∗02), supporting specificity (Figure 1C, right). Together, these results establish C8K10D as a viable starting point for affinity maturation.

Affinity maturation

To enhance target-binding affinity of C8K10D, we constructed a secondary YSD library by randomizing the CDR-H1 and CDR-H2 regions, which were not diversified in the primary library. To remove avidity effects, we performed the FACS rounds with the monomeric KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 complex (Figures 2A and S2). Following iterative rounds of enrichment, seven distinct clones (C8K10D1–C8K10D7) were isolated. Binding evaluation using BLI confirmed enhanced target binding across the panel, with several clones exhibiting nanomolar-range affinity to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 (Figure 2B, left). In particular, three variants, C8K10D3, C8K10D5, and C8K10D6, exhibited minimal binding to the control SARS/HLA-C∗08 (Figure 2B, right). Non-specific cell binding and its removal

To assess cell-surface binding, we produced C8K10D3, C8K10D5, and C8K10D6 clones as full-length IgG and tested them on the K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cell line, which expresses HLA-C∗08:02 but lacks the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), allowing surface display of exogenously loaded peptides. Cells were pulsed with KRAS G12D10 or SARSpep or left unpulsed and then incubated with each of the three antibodies. Flow cytometry showed that all three antibodies bound to KRAS G12D10-pulsed cells. Low-level binding was also detected on SARSpep-pulsed and non-pulsed cells, above the background level measured with anti-p53(R175H)/HLA-A∗02 antibody, which served as a negative control (Figures 3A and S3B). Based on favorable expression yield and biophysical properties, we selected C8K10D5 as the lead candidate. We first assessed whether C8K10D5 scFv expressed on yeast mediates binding to K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cells pulsed with the KRAS G12D10 peptide, which might be strong enough to allow cell-to-cell FACS sorting. Microscopic imaging revealed obvious yeast binding to peptide-pulsed cells but not to non-pulsed cells (Figures 3B and S4). Following these observations, we constructed a YSD sub-library of the C8K10D5 scFv by randomizing the CDR-L2 loop, which was predicted to contribute less to antigen recognition (Figure 3C, left). Library screening was performed for negative selection for K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cells pulsed with the SARSpep peptide or left unpulsed and for positive selection with the same cells pulsed with the KRAS G12D10 peptide (Figure S5). After sorting, yeast binding to non-pulsed cells decreased from 5.5% to 3.4%, whereas binding to KRAS G12D10-pulsed cells increased from 21.1% to 37.2% (Figures 3C, middle, and S6). Two enriched clones were isolated and designated as C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18. BLI-based kinetic analysis further revealed dissociation constants of 5 and 27 nM for C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18, respectively, with no measurable binding to SARS/HLA-C∗08 (Figure 3D, left).

To test whether the observed specificity and pMHC selectivity extend beyond K562 (human CML, blast-crisis origin), we generated 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP–) cells of B-lymphoblastoid origin using the same protocol as for K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP–) and evaluated Fab binding on this cell line. As a positive control, we included a recombinant TCR ectodomain previously reported to bind selectively to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08.27 Both C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 Fabs bound selectively to KRAS G12D10-pulsed 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP–) cells, showing no detectable binding to non-pulsed or SARSpep-pulsed cells (Figure 3D, right). Together, these results demonstrate that C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 are devoid of detectable non-specific binding to cell-surface molecules and thus highly target selective.

Peptide-centric binding and fine epitope specificity

For structural validation, we produced C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 Fabs. Whereas the C8K10D5-18 Fab itself precipitated upon concentration, the C8K10D5-1 Fab was highly soluble, and its complex with KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 was crystallized. The structure of C8K10D5-1 Fab/KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 was determined at 4.5 Å resolution and revealed a peptide-centric binding of the Fab to the pMHC, which is closely similar to the design model (Table 1; Figure 4A). Despite the low resolution, the structural model was well supported by the electron density map, which allowed for credible registration of the neoantigen and individual CDR loops (Figure S7A). We assessed fine epitope specificity of C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 using both K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP–) and 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP–) cells pulsed with peptides harboring alanine substitutions at each residue position of KRAS G12D10 or pulsed with peptides with other commonly found G12 mutations (G12V, G12C, and G12R). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated strong binding of both Fabs to cells pulsed with the KRAS G12D10 peptide but no detectable interaction with the cells pulsed with the other G12 mutations or alanine-substituted peptides at position 3, 7, or 10 (Figure 4D). Since C8K10D5-18 shares high sequence identity and a near-identical paratope with C8K10D5-1, the molecular interactions of C8K10D5-18 were inferred from the C8K10D5-1 crystal structure. Within this structural context, Asp3p of the peptide, which corresponds to the KRAS G12D mutation, does not directly contact the Fab. Instead, its side chain lies below the peptide plane and forms ionic interactions with Arg156HLA (Figures 4B and S7B). Asp3p is known to be a key determinant of KRAS G12D10 binding to the HLA, and Leu10p likewise contributes critically to pMHC stability,27 explaining why their alanine substitutions abrogate the Fab interaction. Lys7p makes ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions with Asp31L and Tyr29L of the Fab light chain, respectively (Figure 4C). In addition, Lys7p is surrounded by the side chains of Met106H of the Fab heavy chain and Gln155HLA of the HLA, further reinforcing the Fab-pMHC interaction. This binding footprint explains the loss of Fab interaction upon K7A substitution of the peptide.

Notably, a structural comparison with an AlphaFold 2 (AF2)32 prediction revealed significant discrepancies at the binding interface (Figure S7C). The relatively low predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores for the peptide and CDR regions in the AF2 model underscore the limitations of computational predictions and highlight the importance of experimental structural validation for these complex targets (Figure S7C).

Comprehensive specificity profiling and HLA-C restriction analysis

For a more rigorous evaluation of the potential off-target landscape, we constructed a phage-displayed library in a single-chain trimer (SCT) format, a well-established scaffold for stable pMHC presentation.33 In this library, the primary anchor residues at positions P1, P2, and P10 were maintained to ensure stable loading onto HLA-C∗08:02, while the remaining residues (P3–P9) were fully randomized using NNK degenerate codons, yielding a total library size of 3.3 × 1010 transformants. We conducted four rounds of biopanning against both C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 Fabs, followed by deep sequencing to track the enrichment trajectories of binding peptides (Figure 5B). A total of 156 unique peptide sequences, including the target KRAS G12D10 and the consistently dominant “GACAFTNTIL” sequence (Pepdominant), were identified as being progressively enriched across the selection rounds (Table S1). A sequence search of these 156 sequences against the human proteome (UniProt: UP000005640) revealed no identical matches, suggesting a minimal risk of cross-reactivity with endogenous self-antigens. To assess the integrity of the screening process, we synthesized the seven sequences from the enriched pool and tested their binding to C8K10D5-1 Fab using a peptide-pulsing assay. Four sequences showed strong Fab binding, supporting the validity of the screen, whereas the other three showed no detectable binding (Figures 5C and S8A). We speculate that these non-binding sequences were enriched as artifacts of the SCT format, which may stabilize peptide presentation in the HLA groove, whereas the corresponding isolated peptides may not be stably presented in the peptide-pulsing assay. Notably, all four binding sequences contained an aspartate at position 3, a residue critical for KRAS G12D10 binding to HLA-C∗08:02, while all three non-binding sequences carried a different residue at this position (Figure 5C). This variation in binding among enriched sequences suggests that the biopanning process successfully selected for peptides with structural characteristics specific to the KRAS G12D10 epitope rather than resulting in indiscriminate enrichment of hydrophobic or non-specific binders.

In addition to peptide specificity, we evaluated the potential for HLA alloreactivity, which is a critical safety parameter for TCR-like therapeutics. HLA-C alleles are generally categorized into C1 and C2 groups based on a key dimorphism at residues 77 and 80 of the α1 helix.34 We established a panel of TAP-competent 721.221 cell lines individually expressing representative alleles from both groups: HLA-C∗03:04 and C∗07:02 (C1 group) and HLA-C∗04:01 and C∗05:01 (C2 group), alongside the target HLA-C∗08:02. These cells were tested for C8K10D5-1 Fab binding both in the presence and absence of exogenous KRAS G12D10 peptide. The antibody demonstrated strict HLA-C∗08:02 restriction, binding to peptide-pulsed HLA-C∗08:02-expressing cells exclusively, with no detectable interaction with any other HLA-C alleles regardless of peptide presence (Figures 5D and S8B).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our lead candidates possess an exceptional specificity profile, with no significant non-specific binding across a diverse repertoire of HLA-C molecules and endogenous peptides.

Structure-guided improvement of productivity and affinity

C8K10D5-1 shows higher target-binding affinity, production yield, and solubility than C8K10D5-18. The two variants differ at six residues (Figures 6A and 6B). Inspection of the crystal structure suggested that three substitutions are unlikely to affect physicochemical properties, whereas the other three residues of Asp31L, Gln55L, and Glu102H in C8K10D5-1 and Tyr31L, Leu55L, and Asp102H in C8K10D5-18 are positioned to do so. Because Asp31L and Gln55L in C8K10D5-1 are solvent exposed (Figure 6A), the corresponding hydrophobic residues Tyr31L and Leu55L in C8K10D5-18 would also be solvent exposed, which likely negatively affects protein folding and protein solubility. In particular, Tyr31L would impair target binding, as Asp31L is a residue that forms an ionic interaction with Lys7p of the bound peptide (Figure 4C). We generated single and combinatorial variants at the three positions. Among these, C8K10D5-18-DQE with triple substitutions (Y31DL/L55QL/D102EH) and C8K10D5-18-DE with double substitutions (Y31DL/D102EH) exhibited markedly improved production, from barely detectable levels to 340 and 244 mg/L, respectively (Figure 6B). We next evaluated the specificity of the two variants by flow cytometry using K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) and 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cells. Both variants stained KRAS G12D10-pulsed cells more strongly than the parental clone, whereas staining of non-pulsed or SARSpep-pulsed cells was minimal and comparable to the anti-p53(R175H)/HLA-A∗02 antibody negative control (Figures 6D and S9). Collectively, these data indicate that structure-guided optimization of C8K10D5-18 improved physicochemical properties and binding affinity without compromising specificity and pMHC selectivity.

Given the CDR redesign and the introduction of non-germline residues in these optimized variants, we further evaluated their potential immunogenicity using an in silico HLA class II binding prediction approach.35 Overall, the distribution of predicted HLA class II binding affinities remained comparable between the parental template (PDB: 1W72) and the engineered antibodies, C8K10D5-18-DE and C8K10D5-18-DQE, across a broad range of HLA alleles (Figure S10). While modest shifts in percentile rank were observed for a subset of mutation-containing core peptides, particularly within the light chain, these changes were not consistently observed across all peptides or HLA alleles. Importantly, the frequency and breadth of high-affinity HLA binders remained comparable to the template, suggesting that the introduced modifications did not significantly increase the predicted immunogenic risk.

Functional validation in CAR-T and bispecific T cell engager formats

To assess whether the discovered antibodies enable target-specific activation of T cells, we generated second-generation CAR constructs incorporating each scFv of C8K10D5-1, C8K10D5-18, C8K10D5-18-DE, and C8K10D5-18-DQE fused to a signaling cassette (Figure 7A, top). Primary human T cells were engineered with these CAR constructs (Figure S11A), and the resulting CAR-T cells were co-cultured with K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) or 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−). All CAR-T variants showed specific lysis of KRAS G12D10-pulsed cells, whereas SARSpep-pulsed or non-pulsed cells exhibited minimal lysis (Figure 7A, bottom). Furthermore, no significant cytotoxicity was observed against cells pulsed with the wild-type KRAS decamer peptide (KRAS WT10; GAGGVGKSAL) or the Pepdominant peptide identified in the phage display screening (Figure S11B). To further evaluate the antibodies as an alternative therapeutic modality, we generated a bispecific scDb T cell engager. This construct incorporated the Fv of C8K10D5-18-DQE and the anti-human CD3 Fv from SP3436 (Figure 7B, top). Discussion

TCR-based therapeutics have emerged as a powerful class of cancer immunotherapies, with several modalities demonstrating clinical success. Tebentafusp, a bispecific T cell engager that combines a recombinant TCR with an anti-CD3 single-chain variable fragment, has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of uveal melanoma.38 More recently, the TCR-engineered T cell therapy afami-cel has also received FDA approval for synovial sarcoma.39 Recently, de-novo-designed proteins targeting pMHC complexes emerged, enabled by advances in artificial-intelligence-driven protein design.18,19,20,21 These include highly stable α-helical bundle binders generated by integrating generative backbone design with deep-learning-based sequence optimization.

Discovering antibodies to pMHC targets is technically challenging because the antigenic surface is dominated by pMHC helices, whereas the relevant discriminating features lie within the bound peptide.40 For neoantigens, these features may involve a single mutated residue among a short 9–10 amino acid sequence, making the epitope exceptionally demanding in terms of selectivity.22 Purely in silico antibody discovery suffers from low success rates, while large-pool experimental validation is costly and labor-intensive. CDR loop conformational plasticity further limits discovery by undermining the accuracy of CDR loop design and structural prediction.24 Previous strategies leveraged TCR-like antibodies, introducing limited randomization within CDRs to generate new binders against peptides presented by the same HLA subtype. Although effective in some cases, such an approach is inherently narrow and cannot be generalized to different HLA types.41,42

To improve efficiency and reduce cost, we combined a computational approach in the early design stage with powerful conventional in vitro screening in the later stage. Because flexible loop design performs poorly, we reduced conformational uncertainty by restricting mutations to structurally compatible positions, preserving canonical backbones and favoring consensus amino acids.26 Early experimental screening against both target and non-target antigens allowed us to discover binders with the desired selectivity and affinity but with detectable off-target binding. To overcome non-specific cell binding, we implemented a cell-based sorting scheme41,42 that co-cultured Fv-displaying yeast with mammalian target cells and applied negative selection on non-pulsed cells followed by positive selection on peptide-pulsed cells under stringent controls. Finally, the crystal structure of the lead antibody-antigen complex validated the design model, explained its high selectivity and provided a structural basis for improving physicochemical properties without compromising target selectivity and specificity.

The TCR-like antibodies developed in this study exhibit markedly higher affinity than typical TCRs. While TCRs generally have a KD in the micromolar range, our lead candidate demonstrated a KD value in the low nanomolar range and maintained strict specificity for KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08, without detectable cross-reactivity to the tested KRAS G12X variant pMHCs. Notably, the corresponding WT 10-mer peptide cannot form a stable complex with HLA-C∗08:02, and therefore the potential cross-reactive antigen is unlikely to be displayed by normal cells, which is an advantageous feature when targeting KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08.

Our CAR-T functional data suggest that these antibodies could be transferable to TCR-proximal formats such as synthetic TCR and antigen receptor T cells (STAR-T) and TCR fusion construct (TruC).43,44 These formats, embedding antibody recognition within chimeric receptors, enable direct engagement of native TCR signaling and heightened recognition of low-abundance antigens. Moreover, bispecific scDb T cell engager data showing strict antigen-dependent activation support expansion beyond adoptive cell therapy. Reformatted as bispecifics, our antibodies could be able to redirect endogenous cytotoxic T cells to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08-positive tumors, enabling off-the-shelf dosing. Together, these results substantiate the potential of our antibodies for both adoptive cell therapies and off-the-shelf bispecific T cell engagers. However, while our optimized in vitro assays demonstrate potent and specific activity, it should be noted that these evaluations were primarily conducted using overexpression systems, which may not fully reflect the antigen densities of native tumor cells. Therefore, subsequent studies incorporating patient-derived tumor models with endogenous KRAS(G12D) expression and in vivo efficacy evaluations will be essential to fully validate the clinical translation of these engineered candidates.

In conclusion, the presented study reports a generalizable design-to-function pipeline for generating high-affinity, high-specificity pMHC-targeting antibodies. When applied to the clinically validated KRAS G12D10 neoantigen, this approach yielded a promising lead for HLA-restricted cancer immunotherapy. This efficient and cost-effective workflow would accelerate the development of precision biologics against a broad range of intracellular oncogenic targets presented by class I MHC molecules.

Materials and methods

Computational design of TCR-like antibody

Ten structures of TCR-like antibody-pMHC I complexes (PDB: 1W72, 3CVH, 3GJF, 3HAE, 4WUU, 6UJ9, 6W51, 7BBG, 7RE7, and 7TR4) were aligned to the structure of KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08:02 (PDB: 6ULK) using PyMOL (Schrödinger). Fv domains from 100 human antibodies were then superposed onto each framework by backbone alignment, and candidates were ranked by steric clash counts, peptide accessibility, and geometry consistency.

Sequences were designed with a custom ProteinMPNN model optimized for CDRs.28 Designable sites were pre-selected based on side-chain vector orientation toward the bound peptide and loop stability (backbone torsion angles). Positions critical for maintaining canonical CDR conformations were excluded. Residue choices were weighted by position-specific frequencies from human antibody repertoires. Designs with ProteinMPNN scores >0.828 were retained, modeled, and relaxed with Rosetta FastRelax.31 Nine designs were finally selected for library construction based on the highest composite ranking across all metrics, including buried solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and unsatisfied polar atoms at the antigen-antibody interface.

Yeast library construction and induction

Yeast scFv libraries were constructed by overlap extension PCR using synthetic DNA oligonucleotides. For each sub-library, selected CDR residues were diversified by incorporating NNM codons at the designated positions, where “N” denotes any nucleotide and “M” denotes adenine or cytosine. The VH and VL domains were joined by a (G4S)4 linker to form the scFv coding sequence. Following PCR assembly, 6 μg of the resulting scFv library DNA and 2 μg of linearized pCTCON2 vector (New England Biolabs) containing a Trp selectable marker were co-transformed into S. cerevisiae EBY100 using an electroporation-based method (MicroPulser, Bio-Rad) as previously described.

Transformed yeast were cultured in Trp-deficient SD-CAA medium at 30°C, harvested, and resuspended at 3 × 106 cells/mL in galactose-containing SG-CAA medium to induce surface display. Cultures were incubated for 20 h at 20°C to express Aga2p-scFv fusions with a C-terminal Myc tag prior to selection.

Yeast surface display library construction and screening

Antibody Fv libraries were displayed on the surface of S. cerevisiae EBY100 strain and screened against KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08:02/β2M using sequential magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and FACS. For all selections, cells were washed and resuspended in PBSF buffer (1% BSA in PBS [pH 7.4]). MACS enrichment was performed with approximately 1 × 109 induced yeast cells, and FACS rounds were performed with 1 × 107 cells.

Biotinylated pMHC I complexes were used for all selection steps. For tetramer-based selections, pMHC I monomers were incrementally added to streptavidin-phycoerythrin (SA-PE) at a defined molar ratio, with the pMHC I in slight molar excess to ensure complete tetramerization. For monomer-based selections, biotinylated pMHC I was first incubated with yeast, followed by two washes to remove unbound antigen, after which SA-PE was added to label bound complexes.

The first library was constructed from nine designed Fv variants, with three to four residues in the CDR3 loops randomized to generate diversity. For the clone that yielded a binding hit, diversification targeted one residue in CDR-H3 and two residues in CDR-L3. The library was first enriched by MACS with 100 nM tetramerized target pMHC I, followed by three rounds of FACS positive selection. Upon detection of a distinct binding population, negative selection against SARS/HLA-C∗08 was incorporated before positive sorting with the target complex.

A second library, based on the C8K10D framework, incorporated simultaneous diversification of four residues in CDR-H2 and two in CDR-H3. Screening was performed similarly but extended to four FACS rounds, with later rounds using mainly monomeric pMHC I to increase selection stringency.

The third library, derived from C8K10D5, randomized five residues in CDR-L2 and included a mammalian cell co-culture step for negative selection. In this approach, yeast cells were incubated with K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−/mScarlet-H+) cells pulsed with SARSpep, and non-binding yeast cells were recovered for subsequent positive selection with the target monomer. Later rounds incorporated dual-color competition assays in which SARS/HLA-C∗08 and KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 were labeled with different fluorophores, enabling isolation of clones that bound exclusively to the target. Final enrichment steps used <1 nM monomeric target pMHC I to preferentially isolate high-affinity binders.

Antibody production

For Fab production, VH and VL DNAs (Twist Bioscience) were cloned into pTT5-derived mammalian expression vectors (Addgene), encoding CH1-(His)×6 or CL. The amino acid sequences for all antibody clones used in this study are listed in Table S2. CHO-S cells (6 × 106 cells/mL; Gibco) were co-transfected and cultured in ExpiCHO medium (Gibco) for 10 days. Supernatants were clarified (12,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C), diluted 1:1 with PBS, purified by HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Scientific), followed by size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 75; Cytiva). Significant precipitation of C8K10D5-18 Fab was observed during concentration. Attempts to improve solubility by varying buffer conditions, such as using low-salt formulations (10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.0]), were unsuccessful.

For full-length IgG production, the VH domain was subcloned into an IgG heavy-chain vector containing the CH1-CH2-CH3 domains and co-transfected with the same light-chain construct used for Fab expression. Clarified supernatants were captured on MabSelect Sure (Protein A; Cytiva), washed with PBS, eluted with 0.1 M glycine (pH 3.0), immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), and further purified by using a HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 200 column equilibrated in PBS.

Production of pMHCs

Peptide-MHC I complexes were produced by refolding from denatured HLA heavy chain and β2M in buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 400 mM L-arginine, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM oxidized glutathione, 5 mM reduced glutathione, and 0.2 mM PMSF) as described previously.45 Refolding proceeded at 18°C for 48 h, after which the mixture was concentrated to 7.5 mL and buffer exchanged on a PD-10 column (30 mM NaCl and 20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0]). Complexes were purified by using a HiPrep 26/60 Sephacryl S-300 HR column (Cytiva), HisPur Ni-NTA resin, and a Capto HiRes Q 5/50 column (Cytiva).

BLI

Biotinylated pMHCs were immobilized onto SA biosensor tips (Sartorius) at 2.5 nM in Kinetics Buffer (Sartorius). Association and dissociation were run for 120–480 s, adjusted to apparent affinity on an Octet R8 system (Sartorius). Signals were processed using double-reference subtraction (ligand-free sensor and analyte-free well) to remove non-specific/background responses. Kinetics were analyzed in Octet BLI Analysis v.12.2.2.4. (Sartorius).

Mammalian cell culture

The human B-lymphoblastoid cell line 721.221 (Merck, SCC275) cells were maintained in RPMI1640 (Gibco, 72400047) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 12483020). The human erythromyeloid leukemia cell line K562 (ATCC, CRL-243) was maintained in IMDM medium (Gibco, 12440053) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.

Generation of stable cell line

Stable 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) and K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cell lines were generated by sequential lentiviral knockin and lentiviral CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, as previously described.45 The stable K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−/mScarlet-H+) cell line was generated by transducing pLV-mScarlet-IRES-Puro into K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−).

Genes encoding HLA-C∗08:02, HLA-C∗03:04, HLA-C∗07:02, HLA-C∗05:01, and HLA-C∗04:01 were synthesized and individually cloned into the pLV-mScarlet-IRES-Puro backbone to replace the mScarlet-IRES-Puro sequence with an E2A self-cleaving peptide-LNGFR fusion tag. Stable 721.221 cell lines expressing each allele were generated via lentiviral transduction and subsequently isolated by FACS using CD271-FITC Antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-112-605).

HLA-C∗08:02-IRES-KRAS(WT) or HLA-C∗08:02-IRES-KRAS(G12D) cassettes were assembled by fusing HLA-C∗08:02 with an IRES and full-length KRAS(WT) or KRAS(G12D), then cloned into the pLV-EF1a-IRES-Puro vector and used to transduce 721.221 cells to yield 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+-IRES-KRAS(WT)) and 721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+-IRES-KRAS(G12D)).

Peptide pulsing

721.221(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) and K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−) cells were washed with serum-free RPMI 1640 or IMDM, respectively, and seeded in low-attachment plates (SPL Life Sciences, 32006) at 1–2 × 106 cells/mL in the presence of synthetic peptide (10 μM) and human β2M (10 μg/mL). Control wells received β2M only. Plates were incubated for 20–24 h at 26°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator before downstream functional assays.

Cell-binding assay

Peptide-pulsed cells were washed once in ice-cold PBSF and resuspended at 2–5 × 105 cells per 100 μL. Cells were stained for 1 h at 4°C with 50 nM Fab or IgG, washed twice in PBSF, then incubated 1 h at 4°C (dark) with either FITC anti-6×His (Abcam ab1206, 50 nM) or Protein A-Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen P11047, 50 nM). After a final wash, cells were resuspended in 200 μL PBSF and analyzed on a CytoFLEX SRT (Beckman Coulter). Binding was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity within a live-cell gate.

Confocal imaging

Peptide-pulsed K562(HLA-C∗08:02+/TAP−/mScarlet-H+) cells (2 × 105 cells) and yeast cells (20 × 105 cells) were resuspended in 200 μL PBSF and co-incubated for 10 min. Cell mixes were diluted to 1.6 mL PBSF and plated on 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (SPL 200350). Live imaging was performed on a Nikon A1 HD25 mounted on an Eclipse Ti2 inverted microscope with a PLAN APO lambda ×40/0.95 NA objective. Images were acquired using NIS-Elements AR 5.30 with digital zoom.

Structure determination

C8K10D5-1 Fab (see results section) bound to KRAS(G12D)10/HLA-C∗08 (16 mg/mL) was crystallized in a solution composed of 1,200 mM NaH2PO4, 200 mM (NH4)2SO4, 200 mM Li2SO4, and 100 mM CAPS buffer (pH 10.5) by hanging-drop vapor diffusion at 20°C. The crystals were cryoprotected by the same solution supplemented with 15% (v/v) glycerol. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the beamline 5C of Pohang Accelerator Laboratory and processed with HKL2000. Molecular replacement, model building, and structure refinement were performed using COOT and PHENIX.46,47

Generation of single-chain pMHC libraries

SCT libraries were displayed on the surface of phage as disulfide-trapped constructs comprising KRAS G12D10 peptide-(G4S)3-β2M-(G4S)3-HLA-C∗08:02(Y84C, A139C). The HLA-C∗08:02 Y84C and A139C mutations introduce an engineered disulfide bond at the C-terminal end of the peptide-binding cleft between the α1 and α2 helices, thereby enhancing peptide-MHC stability.

To generate pMHC libraries with randomized KRAS G12D10 sequence (GADGVGKSAL), positions 3–9 of the peptide were randomized while the anchor residues at positions 1, 2, and 10 were preserved. The libraries were generated by PCR amplification of the pMHC construct using primers containing NNK degenerate codons (N = any base; K = G or T) to introduce seven randomized amino acid positions (forward primer: 5′-ACTCGCGGCCCAGCCGGCCATGGCTGGTGCA(NNK)7TTAGGTGGTGGTGGTAGCG-3′; reverse primer: 5′-CCGCACGGATATCTTTGCTG-3′). The resulting PCR products were cloned into a modified pComb3X vector, using Sfi1 and electroporated into electrocompetent E. coli TG1 cells. The total library size was determined to be 3.3 × 1010 colony-forming units by colony counting after limiting dilutions.

The phagemid library was introduced into E. coli TG1 cells by electroporation and grown in 2 × YT medium containing 2% glucose to an OD600 of 0.4, followed by infection with VCSM13 helper phage, a derivative of M13KO7 helper phage (provided by Hyo Jeong Hong from Kangwon National University, Republic of Korea). Cultures were mixed by inversion, incubated for 30 min at 37°C, pelleted, and resuspended in 2 × YT medium, then incubated at 26°C for 16 h with shaking. Phage particles were harvested by polyethylene glycol precipitation and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane.

Screening of single-chain HLA libraries

Two biotinylated Fabs of TCR-like antibodies, C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18, were captured on SA-coated magnetic beads (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, CA) and incubated at room temperature with gentle rotation with ∼8 × 1012 phage particles per round that had been pre-blocked in 5% skim milk in PBS. Binding was carried out for 1 h at room temperature with gentle rotation. The beads were then magnetically collected and washed three times with 3% skim milk in PBS and twice with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20. Bound phage particles were eluted with 0.2 M glycine-HCl (pH 2.2), used to infect early log-phase E. coli TG1 cells, and plated onto 2 × YT medium containing 2% glucose at 30°C for 16 h. Colonies were resuspended in 2 × YT medium containing 2% glucose and 100 mg/mL ampicillin for the next round of panning. Four rounds of selection were performed.

High-throughput sequencing analysis of pHLA libraries

After each round of selection, as well as from the unselected library, plasmid DNA was extracted from ∼109 E. coli TG1 cells using a QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen; Hilden, Germany). Amplicons for sequencing were generated by PCR using the purified plasmids. Sequencing libraries were prepared by random DNA fragmentation, followed by adapter ligation and PCR amplification, according to standard Illumina protocols, and were sequenced on an Illumina platform using sequencing-by-synthesis chemistry.48 Sequence logos were generated using the WebLogo web server.49

Deep sequencing analysis of enriched peptide sequences

Deep sequencing datasets obtained from four rounds of sorting (R1–R4) were used to analyze peptide enrichment. To avoid bias toward sequences presented in earlier rounds, all peptide sequences detected in any round were included. For each round, peptide abundance was normalized to the total number of reads, and the normalized frequency (fr) was defined as the read count of a given peptide divided by the total reads in that round. Peptide enrichment was calculated as the log2 fold change in normalized frequency between R1 and R4.

Enrichment trajectories were further evaluated by counting the number of consecutive round-to-round transitions (R1→R2, R2→R3, and R3→R4) in which normalized peptide frequency increased. Peptides were ranked according to their log2 enrichment, and only those represented by at least 200 reads in the final round (R4) were retained for downstream analysis. The peptide “CAFTNTI,” which showed dominant enrichment in both C8K10D5-1 and C8K10D5-18 datasets, was selected as a common candidate. In addition, the three highest-ranked peptides from each dataset were selected. These peptides were synthesized for further experimental validation.

In silico immunogenicity prediction

The potential immunogenicity of the Fv regions was assessed using the IEDB MHC II Binding Prediction tool. Amino acid sequences of the Fv regions from C8K10D5-1, C8K10D5-18DE, C8K10D5-18DQE, and the parental template (PDB: 1W72) were analyzed using the NetMHCIIpan 4.1 EL prediction method.35 To ensure comprehensive coverage across diverse populations, predictions were performed against the full HLA reference set, including all available HLA class II alleles. The peptide length was set to 15-mer, and the predicted binding affinities were expressed as percentile ranks. For comparative analysis, core peptides containing amino acid substitutions relative to the parental template were identified. The distribution of percentile ranks for these mutation-containing core peptides across all evaluated HLA alleles was plotted to evaluate the relative immunogenic liability of the engineered variants.

Production of CAR-T cells

Primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were purchased from Cellular Technology Limited (C.T.L., CTL-UP1). According to the manufacturer's specifications, these cells were ethically isolated from healthy donors under Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved protocols with written informed consent. PBMCs were activated with Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Scientific) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 300 IU/mL IL-2. Second-generation CARs were constructed by linking the scFv via a Gly-Ser (GS) linker to the CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains, followed by the intracellular signaling domains of 4-1BB and CD3ζ. A truncated LNGFR (ΔLNGFR, CD271) served as a selection marker. The CAR cassette was inserted into the pLV-EF1a-IRES-Puro lentiviral vector.50 Activated T cells were transduced, expanded for 7–10 days in the presence of IL-2, and assessed for CAR expression by flow cytometry using anti-LNGFR staining.

Bispecific scDb T cell engager production

Bispecific scDb T cell engagers were generated by linking the VH and VL domains of SP34 and C8K10D5-18-DQE (see results) with Gly4Ser linkers and cloning into a pTT5-derived expression vector (Addgene). CHO-S cells (Gibco) were transfected at 6 × 106 cells/mL using ExpiFectamine CHO (Gibco) and cultured in ExpiCHO Expression Medium for 10 days. Supernatants were clarified, mixed 1:1 with PBS, and purified using HisPur Ni-NTA resin and a HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 200 pg column equilibrated with PBS.

Cytotoxicity assay

For CAR-T cytotoxicity assays, target cells were washed and labeled with 5 μM calcein-AM (Thermo Scientific) for 1 h at 37°C. Labeled cells were washed twice and co-cultured with CAR-T cells at E:T ratios of 0.5:1, 1:1, or 1:5 in 96-well round-bottom plates for 4 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. Supernatants were transferred to black-walled 96-well plates, and fluorescence was measured at 494/535 nm using a microplate reader. Supernatants were transferred to black-walled 96-well plates, and fluorescence (F) was measured at 494/535 nm. Spontaneous and maximum release were defined using target cells without effectors or with lysis solution, respectively.(Equation 1)SpecificLysis(%)=F(Samplerelease)−F(Spontaneousrelease)F(Maximumrelease)−F(Spontaneousrelease)×100

For the bispecific scDb T-cell engager cytotoxicity assays, target cells were 721.221 cell lines stably expressing firefly luciferase via lentiviral transduction. Primary human T cells were co-cultured with these target cells at an E:T ratio of 1:1 in the presence of scDb at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1 nM. After 16 h, target cell viability was quantified by measuring luciferase activity using a Steady-Glo firefly luciferase assay kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific lysis (%) was determined by subtracting the average T-cell only luminescence signal from the treated well signal, dividing this value by the difference between the untreated control and the average T-cell only signals, and then subtracting the resulting fraction from 1 before multiplying by 100.

Experiments were performed in at least triplicate and analyzed with GraphPad Prism.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance in CAR-T cytotoxicity was assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests. Cytotoxicity data for the bispecific scDb T cell engagers were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.
