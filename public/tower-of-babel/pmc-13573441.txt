Dietary fiber source-dependent modulation of pregnant sow fecal microbiota, gas profiles, and short-chain fatty acids in vitro
Authors: Weikang Huangfu, Yujie Zheng, Boshuai Liu, Yalei Cui, Zhichang Wang, Yinghua Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence indicates that dietary fiber (DF) modulates gut microbiota and microbial fermentation metabolites, but the dynamic fermentation outcomes of different DF sources remain difficult to monitor directly under physiological conditions. This study used an in vitro batch fermentation model inoculated with pregnant sow fecal microbiota to compare the effects of a fiber-free control (CON), commercial concentrated fiber (CCF), beet pulp (BP), and alfalfa meal (AM) on microbial composition, gas concentration profiles, and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations at 8, 12, 24, and 36 h.ResultsDifferent fiber substrates generated distinct fermentation profiles. CO₂ was the predominant gas throughout fermentation, whereas H₂ showed clearer substrate-dependent differences. AM generally resulted in higher total measured gas concentration, H₂ concentration, and total SCFA concentration than the other treatments. 16 S rRNA sequencing showed that different fiber substrates shaped the overall fecal microbial community structure, with selected genus-level taxa, including Lactobacillus, norank_f__Muribaculaceae, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Holdemanella, and Fusobacterium, exhibiting substrate-associated variation patterns. Correlation analysis further linked H₂ with SCFAs and selected fermentation-associated taxa. PICRUSt2 prediction suggested that AM fermentation was associated with differences in predicted microbial functional potential.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that different DF sources generate substrate-specific fecal microbial fermentation patterns in vitro. Among the tested substrates, AM produced a stronger SCFA- and H₂-associated fermentation response and was linked with selected microbial response patterns. Integrated correlation analysis suggested microbiota-associated co-variation between H₂ and SCFAs during DF fermentation, which requires further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05568-6.

Introduction

Dietary fiber (DF) is an important substrate for hindgut microbial fermentation and can shape gut microbial composition and fermentation metabolite profiles [1]. In the hindgut, DF is degraded by anaerobic microorganisms to generate short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), mainly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, as well as fermentation gases, including carbon dioxide (CO₂), hydrogen (H₂), methane (CH₄), and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) [2–4]. These fermentation products provide complementary information on microbial substrate utilization and fermentation direction [3]. Therefore, simultaneous evaluation of fecal microbiota, SCFAs, and gas concentration profiles may help clarify how different DF sources shape microbial fermentation.

In pregnant sows, gut microbial composition and fermentation metabolite profiles are responsive to dietary fiber supply. Previous studies have shown that DF supplementation during gestation can alter gut microbial composition and fermentation metabolites in sows and their offspring [5–7]. However, the effects of DF depend strongly on its source and chemical composition. Different DF sources contain different proportions of soluble and insoluble fiber and may therefore support distinct microbial communities and fermentation outputs [8]. Thus, evaluating the fermentation characteristics of different DF sources using fecal microbiota from pregnant sows may provide useful information on substrate-specific microbial fermentation patterns.

In the present study, commercial concentrated fiber (CCF), beet pulp (BP), and alfalfa meal (AM) were selected to represent fiber sources with distinct compositional characteristics. CCF is a commercial concentrated fiber source with a high total dietary fiber content and a high proportion of insoluble fiber. BP is a commonly used fermentable fiber ingredient in pig diets and generally contains a relatively high soluble fiber fraction, including pectin-rich components [8, 9]. In contrast, AM is a forage-derived fiber ingredient mainly composed of insoluble fiber components, such as cellulose and lignin, and also contains non-fiber nutrients, including crude protein [5, 10]. These differences in fiber composition, solubility, and substrate matrix may support different microbial communities and lead to distinct fermentation outputs, including gas concentration profiles and SCFA concentrations.

Although in vivo studies are essential for evaluating physiological outcomes, they are less suited for isolating the direct fermentation characteristics of specific DF sources under controlled conditions [11]. In particular, dynamic changes in microbial gas concentration profiles and SCFA concentrations are difficult to monitor directly in living animals. In vitro batch fermentation provides a useful approach to evaluate the fermentation characteristics of specific substrates by fecal microbiota and allows time-dependent assessment of microbiota-associated metabolites, including SCFAs and gases [12–14]. Therefore, the present study used an in vitro batch fermentation model inoculated with fecal microbiota from pregnant sows to compare the effects of CCF, BP, and AM, with a fiber-free control (CON). We hypothesized that these fiber sources would differentially shape fecal microbial communities and generate distinct gas concentration and SCFA profiles. Correlation analysis and PICRUSt2-based functional prediction were further used to explore potential associations among fiber-associated microbiota, fermentation gases, SCFAs, and predicted microbial functions.

Materials and methods

Fiber sample preparation

Three fiber substrates were used for in vitro fermentation, including commercial concentrated fiber (CCF), beet pulp (BP), and alfalfa meal (AM), with a fiber-free group as the control (CON). CCF was obtained from Beijing Jiuzhou Hulian Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), whereas BP and AM were obtained from the Yuanyang Experimental Base of Henan Agricultural University, China. All fiber sources were ground to pass through a 1.0-mm mesh screen. The nutrient composition and dietary fiber fractions of CCF, BP, and AM, including total dietary fiber (TDF), soluble dietary fiber (SDF), insoluble dietary fiber (IDF), and crude protein, are summarized in Table S1.

Different substrate weights were used to provide broadly comparable total fiber input among fiber sources. As shown in Table S1, CCF had a substantially higher TDF content than BP and AM; therefore, 40 mg CCF and 80 mg BP or AM were weighed into sterilized fermentation bottles. This design allowed comparison of fermentation characteristics among different fiber sources under a similar total fiber supply. In addition, AM contained a higher crude protein level than BP, indicating that non-fiber nutrients in AM may also contribute to fermentation outcomes.

In vitro batch culture fermentation

Growth conditions of fecal microbiota

The fermentation medium was prepared according to Pi et al. [14], with minor modifications. Briefly, 1 L of medium contained 13.00 g soy peptone, 4.50 g yeast extract, 4.00 g mucin, 0.80 g L-cysteine HCl monohydrate, 0.40 g porcine bile extract, 0.05 g KH₂PO₄, 1.50 g NaHCO₃, 4.50 g NaCl, 4.50 g KCl, 0.64 g MgSO₄, 0.15 g CaCl₂·2 H₂O, 0.20 g MnCl₂·4 H₂O, 0.05 g hemin solution, and 1.00 g Tween 80. The medium was dissolved by heating and boiled to remove dissolved oxygen. Nitrogen was immediately introduced to maintain anaerobic conditions. The medium was then dispensed into sterilized fermentation bottles containing the corresponding substrates, with 5 mL medium added to each bottle. The bottles were sealed with pressure-resistant caps and sterilized by autoclaving.

The same basal fermentation medium was used for all groups to maintain fecal microbial activity. Therefore, the added fiber substrate was the main experimental variable rather than the exclusive carbon source. The CON group contained the same basal medium and fecal inoculum but no added fiber substrate.

Fecal sample collection and pre-treatment

Fresh fecal samples were collected from three healthy pregnant sows maintained at the Experimental Base of Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. The sows had similar litter sizes, backfat thicknesses, body weights, and physiological conditions, with an average gestation day of 94.8 ± 0.84 days. All sows were fed a standard corn–soybean meal-based diet, and no antibiotics were administered during the three months before fecal sample collection. Prior informed consent was obtained from the animal owner for fecal sample collection and research use.

Fresh fecal samples were collected immediately after natural defecation and transferred into sterile anaerobic containers. No invasive procedures, anesthesia, or euthanasia were involved. A 10% fecal suspension was prepared using anaerobic phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), homogenized for 5 min, and filtered through three layers of sterile gauze. In an anaerobic workstation, 500 µL of inoculum was added to each sterilized fermentation bottle containing 5 mL of medium and the corresponding substrate. All procedures were performed under anaerobic conditions.

In vitro batch culture fermentation of fecal microbes

In vitro fecal fermentation was performed according to the method described by Liu et al. [15], with minor modifications. The experiment included four treatments: CON, CCF, BP, and AM. Independent sealed anaerobic fermentation bottles were prepared for each treatment and time point. After inoculation, the bottles were incubated at 39 ± 0.5 °C for 8, 12, 24, or 36 h. This temperature was selected to approximate the physiological temperature of the porcine hindgut and is consistent with previous in vitro fermentation models using porcine fecal microbiota [14]. At each time point, fermentation was terminated by placing the bottles in liquid nitrogen.

Headspace gases were analyzed using a fermentation gas analyzer produced by Hangzhou Hailu Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), as described in Sect. "Fermentation gas analysis". Fermentation broth was collected at each indicated time point and separated into supernatant and microbial pellet. The supernatant was used for SCFA determination. To characterize the microbial community after fermentation, microbial pellets collected after 36 h of fermentation were used for DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene high-throughput sequencing. Three independent fermentation replicates were prepared for each treatment and time point.

Fermentation gas analysis

At each fermentation time point, the bottles were equilibrated to room temperature, and headspace gas concentrations were automatically analyzed using a fermentation gas analyzer produced by Hangzhou Hailu Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The analyzer was used to detect CO₂, H₂, CH₄, and H₂S [16].

Gas concentrations were recorded according to the output units of the analyzer and are presented as ppm in this study. The original CO₂ output was recorded as volume percentage (%Vol) and was converted to ppm using the equation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{ppm}=\%\mathrm{Vol}\times10,000$$\end{document}. Therefore, the gas data reported in this study represent headspace gas concentrations rather than gas volumes.

Determination of fermented short-chain fatty acids

SCFA concentrations in fermentation supernatants were determined by gas chromatography. Briefly, 500 µL of fermentation supernatant was mixed with 100 µL of crotonic acid/metaphosphoric acid solution and stored at − 40 °C for 24 h for acidification and deproteinization. The crotonic acid/metaphosphoric acid solution was prepared by dissolving 2.5 g metaphosphoric acid in 100 mL deionized water supplemented with 0.6464 g crotonic acid. After acidification, samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were filtered through a 0.22-µm hydrophilic membrane. Then, 150 µL of filtered supernatant was transferred into an injection vial for analysis.

SCFAs were determined using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a DB-FFAP column (0.32 mm × 30 m × 0.5 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a flame ionization detector.

Determination of microbial composition in fermentation broth

Microbial samples collected after 36 h of fermentation were used for bacterial DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using the primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were examined by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform according to standard protocols. High-quality sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity, and taxonomic classification was performed against the SILVA 138 database. Subsequent α-diversity, β-diversity, taxonomic composition, differential abundance, correlation, and functional prediction analyses were performed based on the OTU table.

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

Gas and SCFA data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.0.1. Data are presented as means ± SEM. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Microbial sequencing data were analyzed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). Microbial community analyses were performed at the OTU level. α-diversity was evaluated using the Shannon and Chao indices, and β-diversity was assessed based on OTU-level unweighted UniFrac distances and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Differences in bacterial relative abundance among groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test implemented on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. P-values from taxon-level differential abundance analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and FDR-adjusted P-values were reported as q-values. LEfSe analysis was performed to identify differentially enriched taxa, with an LDA score > 4.0 and P < 0.05 considered significant.

Microbial functional potential was predicted using PICRUSt2 implemented on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. OTU representative sequences and OTU-level abundance profiles were used as input data. Predicted functions were annotated against the KEGG database and summarized at the KEGG Level 3 pathway level. Differences in predicted KEGG pathways among groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test.

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations among fecal microbiota, fermentation gases, SCFAs, and predicted KEGG Level 3 pathways. For network construction, the top 30 genera ranked by mean relative abundance were selected, and only significant correlations with |r| > 0.5 and P < 0.05 were retained. Correlation networks were generated using the Majorbio Cloud Platform and further visualized and refined using Cytoscape software.

Results

Gas concentration profiles during fecal microbiota fermentation

The workflow of the in vitro batch fermentation experiment is summarized in Supplementary Figure S1. As shown in Fig. 1, different fiber substrates resulted in distinct headspace gas concentration profiles during fermentation. Total measured gas concentration increased rapidly during the early fermentation stage and tended to plateau from 24 to 36 h. Compared with CON, fiber-supplemented groups generally showed higher total measured gas concentrations, with AM showing the most pronounced increase (Fig. 1A, P < 0.05). Among the individual gases, CO₂ showed the highest absolute concentration and increased continuously from 8 to 36 h, indicating that CO₂ was the major contributor to the total measured gas concentration. However, no significant difference in CO₂ concentration was detected among treatments at the same time point (Fig. 1B). In contrast, H₂ showed a clearer substrate-dependent response, with AM and CCF generally showing higher H₂ concentrations than CON and BP during fermentation (Fig. 1C, P < 0.05). CH₄ increased mainly during the early fermentation stage and remained relatively stable from 24 to 36 h, with higher concentrations observed in AM and CCF at 8–24 h (Fig. 1D, P < 0.05). H₂S peaked during the early fermentation stage and then decreased over time, and fiber-supplemented groups generally showed lower H₂S concentrations than CON, especially CCF and BP (Fig. 1E, P < 0.05). Overall, these results indicate that different fiber substrates differentially modulated headspace gas concentration profiles during in vitro fermentation, with CO₂ as the dominant gas and H₂ showing a distinct fiber-responsive pattern.

SCFA concentration profiles during in vitro fermentation

SCFA concentrations during in vitro fermentation are shown in Fig. 2. Total SCFAs increased progressively over time in all groups, with AM showing the highest concentration, particularly at the late fermentation stage (Fig. 2A, P < 0.05). Among individual SCFAs, acetate was the predominant component and increased continuously from 8 to 36 h. Fiber-supplemented groups, especially AM and BP, generally showed higher acetate concentrations than CON (Fig. 2B, P < 0.05). Propionate also increased markedly during fermentation, with AM and BP showing greater concentrations than CON and CCF at 24–36 h (Fig. 2C, P < 0.05). Butyrate concentration increased over time in all groups, and AM showed the highest level at 36 h (Fig. 2D, P < 0.05). Valerate, isobutyrate, and isovalerate remained relatively low during the early fermentation stage but increased after 24 h, with AM generally showing higher concentrations at 36 h (Fig. 2E–G, P < 0.05). Overall, these results indicate that different fiber substrates differentially modulated SCFA concentration profiles during in vitro fermentation, with AM promoting a greater accumulation of total SCFAs and several major SCFA components.

Fiber substrates shaped fecal microbial composition after fermentation

Microbial community composition was analyzed using 16 S rRNA sequencing of samples collected after 36 h of fermentation. Overall, different fiber substrates altered fecal microbial community structure, as reflected by OTU distribution, α-diversity, β-diversity, and taxonomic composition analyses (Supplementary Fig. S2). Compared with CON, AM showed higher microbial diversity, as indicated by the Shannon and Chao indices (Supplementary Fig. S2B–C, P < 0.05). PCoA and NMDS analyses further showed separation between CON and fiber-supplemented groups, suggesting that different fiber substrates reshaped the overall microbial community structure (Supplementary Fig. S2D–E).

At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, and Actinobacteriota were the dominant bacterial phyla after fermentation (Supplementary Fig. S2F). Fiber supplementation was associated with changes in the relative abundance of several dominant phyla, most notably a lower relative abundance of Fusobacteriota compared with CON, whereas AM showed a higher relative abundance of Firmicutes (Supplementary Fig. S3A–B). At the genus level, selected taxa exhibited substrate-associated variation patterns after fermentation (Fig. 3). AM showed relatively higher abundances of Lactobacillus, norank_f__Muribaculaceae, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, and Holdemanella, whereas BP showed relatively higher abundances of Collinsella and Olsenella. In contrast, Fusobacterium showed lower relative abundance in fiber-supplemented groups than in CON. Desulfovibrio also showed different abundance patterns among fiber treatments (Fig. 3A–H). However, after FDR correction for taxon-level multiple comparisons, these selected genus-level differences were interpreted as exploratory microbial response patterns rather than definitive differentially abundant genera.

LEfSe, co-occurrence network, and ternary analyses further supported substrate-associated microbial responses (Supplementary Fig. S4). LEfSe analysis identified taxa contributing to group separation, including Fusobacterium and Allisonella in CON, Desulfovibrio and Cloacibacillus in CCF, Collinsella, Olsenella, and Dialister in BP, and norank_f__Muribaculaceae and Holdemanella in AM (Supplementary Fig. S4A; LDA score > 4.0, P < 0.05 ). Correlations among fecal microbiota, gases, and SCFAs during fermentation

Because fermentation gases and SCFAs are both microbial fermentation products and reflect complementary aspects of substrate degradation, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore whether changes in gas concentration profiles were associated with SCFA accumulation during dietary fiber fermentation. Total gas, CO₂, H₂, and CH₄ were generally positively associated with total SCFAs and major SCFA components, especially acetate, propionate, and butyrate, whereas H₂S showed an opposite association pattern (Fig. 4A). Among these gases, H₂ exhibited the strongest and most consistent positive correlations with SCFAs.

Predicted microbial functions and their associations with fermentation metabolites

PICRUSt2 analysis was performed to predict the functional potential of fecal microbiota after fermentation. KEGG Level 3 prediction showed that different fiber substrates altered several microbial functional pathways, including purine metabolism, ribosome, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 5A). Among these predicted pathways, purine metabolism and ribosome were higher in AM than in CON, whereas biosynthesis of secondary metabolites was higher in fiber-supplemented groups than in CON (Fig. 5A, P < 0.05).

To link predicted microbial functions with measured fermentation metabolites, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between differential KEGG Level 3 pathways and gas and SCFA concentrations. Discussion

The fermentation characteristics of DF are not determined simply by total fiber content, but are strongly influenced by fiber source, solubility, structural complexity, and accompanying non-fiber components [17, 18]. These substrate properties can affect microbial access to fermentable carbohydrates, the microbial groups favored during fermentation, and the balance of fermentation end products, including gases and SCFAs [12, 19]. Therefore, comparing different DF sources under controlled conditions is important for clarifying substrate-specific microbial fermentation patterns. Compared with in vivo studies, in vitro batch fermentation reduces host-related confounding factors and allows dynamic assessment of gas concentration profiles and SCFA accumulation across fermentation time points [20, 21]. In the present study, fecal microbiota from pregnant sows were used as the inoculum because they provide a relevant microbial source for evaluating the fermentability of fiber ingredients used in sow nutrition. We compared CCF, BP, and AM to determine how fiber source influences fecal microbial composition, gas concentration profiles, SCFA concentrations, and predicted microbial functions. These findings should therefore be interpreted as substrate-specific in vitro fermentation responses rather than direct evidence of in vivo physiological outcomes.

Gas concentration profiles first indicated that the tested substrates differed not only in fermentation intensity but also in fermentation route. Previous in vitro studies have shown that gas formation is closely related to substrate fermentability, but is also affected by fiber composition, substrate accessibility, microbial populations, and fermentation conditions [14, 18]. Therefore, gas profiles and SCFA accumulation provide complementary but not identical information on DF fermentation [22]. In the present study, CO₂ was the predominant gas throughout fermentation, reflecting microbial organic matter degradation and decarboxylation processes, whereas H₂ showed clearer substrate-dependent differences. The relatively higher H₂ response in AM may reflect more active carbohydrate-oriented fermentation and reducing-equivalent disposal during substrate degradation [19, 23]. Although the tested substrates differed in measured fiber fractions, including SDF, IDF, and TDF, the distinct gas responses among AM, BP, and CCF suggest that gas formation cannot be explained by fiber solubility or total fiber content alone. Instead, substrate accessibility, microbial utilization capacity, and fermentation route may jointly shape gas concentration profiles during in vitro fermentation [18]. H₂S showed a pattern distinct from H₂ and was relatively lower in several fiber-supplemented groups than in CON, especially CCF and BP, suggesting that fermentable fiber substrates may reduce sulfur-related fermentation and favor carbohydrate-oriented fermentation [24, 25]. Thus, the positive association between H₂ and major SCFAs observed in this study likely reflects coordinated carbohydrate-oriented fermentation rather than a direct causal effect of H₂ on SCFA accumulation.

SCFA concentrations provided a complementary liquid-phase readout of the same fermentation process. SCFAs are major end products of microbial carbohydrate fermentation and therefore provide an important liquid-phase readout of substrate utilization by fecal microbiota [26, 27]. However, their accumulation does not necessarily parallel gas concentration profiles because organic acid profiles represent the net balance among substrate hydrolysis, microbial conversion of carbohydrate-derived intermediates, cross-feeding, and possible utilization of SCFAs by other microorganisms [27, 28]. In the present study, AM showed stronger total SCFA accumulation than BP and CCF, which was generally consistent with its higher H₂ response and may reflect more sustained carbohydrate-oriented fermentation [29]. However, because monosaccharide composition, molecular structure, viscosity, water-holding capacity, particle characteristics, and dry matter disappearance were not directly measured in this study, this response should be interpreted as a substrate matrix-associated fermentation phenotype rather than evidence for a specific physicochemical mechanism. The relatively higher SDF fraction in BP did not translate into the strongest SCFA response, further suggesting that soluble fiber content alone cannot fully predict SCFA accumulation [1, 30]. Conversely, the high TDF and IDF content of CCF may provide structural carbohydrate substrates but may also limit rapid microbial access and hydrolysis. Therefore, the differences in SCFA profiles among AM, BP, and CCF likely reflect the combined effects of measured fiber fractions, substrate accessibility, microbial utilization capacity, and cross-feeding interactions during in vitro fermentation [27, 30].

These substrate-dependent fermentation outputs were accompanied by changes in fecal microbial community structure. At the phylum level, AM showed a relatively higher abundance of Firmicutes, a phylum that includes several saccharolytic and SCFA-producing bacterial groups and may therefore be associated with altered fermentation potential [5, 28]. At the genus level, selected taxa exhibited substrate-associated variation patterns, including Lactobacillus, norank_f__Muribaculaceae, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Holdemanella, Collinsella, Olsenella, Fusobacterium, and Desulfovibrio. Several of these taxa have been associated with carbohydrate utilization, lactate-related metabolism, cross-feeding, or SCFA-related fermentation. For example, Lactobacillus may contribute to lactate-related fermentation, and lactate can subsequently serve as an intermediate substrate for propionate- or butyrate-producing bacteria through cross-feeding [27, 31]. Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group and Holdemanella may also be involved in polysaccharide degradation or SCFA-related fermentation [32, 33]. In addition, the BP-associated patterns involving Collinsella and Olsenella may reflect carbohydrate- or organic acid-related fermentation [34, 35], whereas the CCF-associated pattern involving Desulfovibrio may be more closely related to sulfate reduction, H₂ utilization, or sulfur-associated fermentation [36]. Together, these microbial patterns may provide microbial context for the distinct SCFA accumulation and H₂-related fermentation responses among substrates, particularly in the AM group. However, because these selected genus-level findings did not remain significant after FDR correction, they should be interpreted as exploratory microbial response patterns rather than definitive differentially abundant taxa.

Correlation network analysis further linked substrate-associated microbial patterns with metabolite outputs, as commonly used in in vitro fiber fermentation studies [14, 18]. In this analysis, the top 30 genera ranked by relative abundance were selected to reduce network complexity. Several selected taxa, including Acidaminococcus, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Holdemanella, Lactobacillus, Collinsella, Olsenella, and norank_f__Muribaculaceae, were positively correlated with H₂ and major SCFAs. These associations suggest that, under the present fermentation conditions, H₂ was closely linked with SCFA accumulation and may serve as an associated indicator of carbohydrate-oriented fermentation, because dietary fiber fermentation can generate SCFAs, H₂, and CO₂ as major microbial end products [23]. Notably, the positive association of Acidaminococcus with H₂ is consistent with reports that Acidaminococcus fermentans can generate CO₂ and H₂ together with organic acids during anaerobic fermentation, although this should be interpreted cautiously at the genus level [37]. Conversely, Fusobacterium, Desulfovibrio, and Cloacibacillus were negatively associated with several SCFA components, suggesting that these taxa may occupy microbial niches less closely aligned with SCFA-accumulating carbohydrate fermentation. This interpretation is compatible with the known role of sulfate-reducing bacteria, including Desulfovibrio, in H₂ utilization and sulfur-associated metabolism [23, 36]. Together, these results support a putative microbiota–gas–SCFA association during DF fermentation, particularly involving the co-variation of H₂ and major SCFAs. However, these findings remain correlation-based and do not prove that H₂ or specific microbial taxa causally mediate SCFA formation.

PICRUSt2-based prediction provided functional context for the substrate-dependent fermentation patterns observed in this study. Because DF fermentation is primarily driven by microbial carbohydrate degradation, functional interpretation was focused on predicted carbon metabolism and carbohydrate-related pathways [30]. The substrate-related changes in carbon metabolism and transport-related pathways may reflect differences in microbial substrate utilization capacity, consistent with the observed variation in gas concentration profiles, SCFA accumulation, and microbial community structure. In the AM group, higher predicted purine metabolism, ribosome, amino acid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites may indicate broader microbial biosynthetic and growth-associated potential during active fermentation rather than direct evidence of enhanced fiber-degrading enzyme activity [38]. However, because PICRUSt2 infers functional potential from 16 S rRNA gene profiles, these results should be interpreted as supplementary and hypothesis-generating evidence rather than direct proof of gene abundance, gene expression, enzyme activity, CAZyme profiles, or metabolite flux [39]. Further shotgun metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, CAZyme-focused, culture-based, and targeted metabolomic or metabolic flux analyses are needed to validate the microbial pathways underlying fiber source-dependent fermentation.

Conclusions

Different dietary fiber sources generated distinct fecal microbial fermentation profiles during in vitro fermentation with fecal microbiota from pregnant sows, as reflected by changes in microbial composition, gas concentration profiles, and SCFA accumulation. Among the tested substrates, AM was more strongly associated with SCFA accumulation, H₂-related gas responses, and selected exploratory microbial response patterns than BP and CCF. Integrated correlation analysis further suggested a putative microbiota–gas–SCFA association during dietary fiber fermentation, with H₂ co-varying with SCFA accumulation. These findings highlight substrate-specific in vitro fermentation responses to different fiber sources, while further in vivo and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the microbial and metabolic processes underlying fiber source-dependent fermentation.
