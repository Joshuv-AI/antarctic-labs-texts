Fluctuation-Response Theory of Non-Equilibrium Complex Fluids Preprint: APS/123-QED
Ryota Takaki, Frank Jülicher

Abstract
A fundamental challenge in soft matter physics is to describe materials, such as the living cytoplasm and tissues, that are simultaneously active, chemically driven, and exhibit long-lasting memory of mechanical stresses. Here, we construct a generalized hydrodynamic framework at finite wavevectors and frequencies that can be applicable to non-equilibrium fluids with memory. Our approach is based on a non-equilibrium fluctuation-response relation in a steady state using correlation function identities. This approach provides a general formalism to derive hydrodynamic constitutive equations that is distinct from Mori-Zwanzig projection formalism. As a corollary, we obtain a generalized fluctuation-response relation in non-equilibrium steady states similar to the relation obtained by Harada-Sasa. Applying our theory to chemically driven active fluids reveals Active Viscoelastic Memory, whereby chemical reaction cycles renormalize the system’s viscous response. We find that this active viscoelastic memory can produce a negative storage modulus at finite frequency, behavior absent in ordinary viscoelastic fluids. Our first-principles framework provides a general basis for understanding memory-dependent dynamics across a wide range of biological and synthetic active systems.

II
Fluctuation-response relation for steady states

In this section, we derive a fluctuation–response relation applicable to steady states, including non-equilibrium, which serves as the foundation for the subsequent sections for the constitutive equations of non-equilibrium fluids.

We consider steady state correlation functions in ergodic systems.
Under the assumption of ergodicity, steady state correlation functions can be defined equivalently as averages over a single long-time trajectory or over a phase-space ensemble, see Appendix
B.1
.
This framework is applicable to systems that relax within a finite time scale but allows for slow relaxation processes.

Consider a set of dynamical variables which in general take complex values,
{\underline{A}}=(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)^{\top}
, where
⊤
denotes the transpose. We denote the steady state dynamic correlation matrix of the variables using dyadic product (see Appendix
A.2
),

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t) \vec{A}^\dagger}\quad,\end{split}

(1)

where we use the notation
{\underline{A}}\equiv{\underline{A}}(t=0)
such that the absence of the time argument implies time
t=0
, and the dagger,
\dagger
, denotes hermitian conjugate, i.e. transposed complex conjugate.
The time translational invariance of the steady state correlation function implies (See Appendix.
B.2
),

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t) \vec{A}^\dagger}=-\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{A}}(t) \vec{\dot{A}}^\dagger}\quad,\end{split}

(2)

where the dots denote time derivatives.
For notational convenience, we define

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\phi}}(t)\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{{\dot{A}}}(t) \vec{\dot{A}}^\dagger}\quad.\end{split}

(3)

We also introduce the static correlation functions

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{g}}\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{A} \, \vec{A}^\dagger}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{\underline{\omega}}\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{A}} \, \vec{A}^\dagger}\quad.\end{split}

(4)

The static correlation matrix
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
, which we term the reactive frequency matrix, is an anti-Hermitian matrix. When the time reversal signature of all components of
{\underline{A}}
is identical, then
\underline{\underline{\omega}}=0
by time reversal symmetry, see Appendix
B.3
, therefore non-vanishing
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
signifies a non-equilibrium steady state with broken time reversal symmetry. When
{\underline{A}}
contains variables with opposite time-reversal signatures, non-vanishing
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
is possible at equilibrium.

We would like to obtain a dynamical equation for the time dependent correlation function
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
. With this aim, we use the Laplace transform of Eq. (
2
), which reads

\begin{split}s^{2}\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)-s\underline{\underline{g}}-\underline{\underline{\omega}}=-\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)\quad,\end{split}

(5)

where the hat denotes the Laplace transform and
s
is the Laplace variable, as shown in Appendix
A.1
.
The following identity holds for general correlation functions:

\begin{split}\left[\underline{\underline{g}}+\frac{1}{s}\underline{\underline{\omega}}+\frac{1}{s}\left(s^{2}\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)-s\underline{\underline{g}}-\underline{\underline{\omega}}\right)\right]\cdot\left(s\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)-\underline{\underline{g}}\right)\\
=\left(s^{2}\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)-s\underline{\underline{g}}-\underline{\underline{\omega}}\right)\cdot\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)+\underline{\underline{\omega}}\cdot\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)+s[\underline{\underline{g}},\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)]\,,\end{split}

(6)

where
[\underline{\underline{A}},\underline{\underline{B}}]=\underline{\underline{A}}\cdot\underline{\underline{B}}-\underline{\underline{B}}\cdot\underline{\underline{A}}
denotes the commutator. This can be readily checked, noting that both sides of Eq. (
6
) are equal to
s\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)\cdot\quantity(s \mat{\hat{\psi}}(s)-\mat{g})
.
Combining Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
6
), we obtain

\begin{split}s\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)-\underline{\underline{g}}=&-\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) - \mat{\omega})\right]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) - \mat{\omega})\cdot\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)\\
&+\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) - \mat{\omega})\right]^{-1}s\cdot[\underline{\underline{g}},\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)]\quad.\end{split}

(7)

Retuning to the time domain, we obtain an exact non-linear relation for the dynamical evolution of the correlation function,

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)=&-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\underline{\underline{K}}(t-t^{\prime})\cdot\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t^{\prime})\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\underline{\underline{M}}(t-t^{\prime})\cdot\quantity[ \mat{g}, \mat{\psi} (t')]\quad.\end{split}

(8)

The matrices
\underline{\underline{K}}
and
\underline{\underline{M}}
represent the memory kernels. The Laplace-space representation of
\underline{\underline{K}}
and
\underline{\underline{M}}
read

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)\equiv\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) -\mat{\omega})\right]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s)-\mat{\omega})\quad,\end{split}

(9)

and

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{M}}}(s)\equiv\left[\frac{1}{s}\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) -\mat{\omega})\right]^{-1}\quad,\end{split}

(10)

respectively. This formulation generalizes the approach of Ref.
Berne et al. 1966
, derived for equilibrium systems, to multiple variables and to non-equilibrium steady states. From Eq. (
5
), it follows that

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s=0)=\underline{\underline{\omega}}\quad,\end{split}

(11)

provided that the correlation
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
remains finite at long time.
Therefore we can write the memory kernel of Eq. (
9
) as

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)=\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)\right]^{-1}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)\quad,\end{split}

(12)

defining the transient part of
\hat{\phi}(s)
,

\begin{split}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)-\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(0)\quad.\end{split}

(13)

When all components of
{\underline{A}}
have the same time-reversal signature, a non-vanishing value of
\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(0)
is an indicator of a non-equilibrium steady state.

When the matrices
\underline{\underline{g}}
and
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
commute, the commutator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
8
) vanishes. In this study, we focus on isotropic systems for which the matrix
\underline{\underline{g}}=C\underline{\underline{I}}
, where
\underline{\underline{I}}
is the identity matrix and
C
is the magnitude of static correlation. Consequently, the commutator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
8
) vanishes in the following.

From Eq. (
8
) we can derive the dynamics of the average response to a small perturbation at the initial condition, see Appendix
C.1
. To linear order it obeys

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t)}=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\underline{\underline{K}}(t-t^{\prime})\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t')}\quad.\end{split}

(14)

Here we use without loss of generality
\expectationvalue{\vec{A}}=0
for the unperturbed state; a premise we follow throughout the subsequent sections without stating explicitly.
The memory kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}
, which is evaluated at the reference unperturbed state, governs how this perturbation relaxes over time.
Note that we also derive in Appendix
C.2
a generalized, model independent form of a fluctuation-dissipation relation in non-equilibrium steady states using Eq. (
14
), which takes a form similar to the Harada-Sasa relation
Harada and Sasa 2005
, see Eq. (
100
).

In Appendix
C.3
, we illustrate the formalism developed in this section with a two-dimensional rotating harmonic oscillator.
In the subsequent sections, we employ Eq. (
14
) with the memory kernel Eq. (
12
) to derive the constitutive equations for isotropic complex fluids in non-equilibrium steady states, fluctuations in chemical reactions, and, finally, non-equilibrium complex fluids driven by chemical reactions.

III
Constitutive equation for isotropic complex fluids

In this section, we derive the constitutive equation for the stress in non-equilibrium isotropic complex fluids. The detailed derivation of this section is found in the Appendix
D
.
We adopt index-free notation for tensor contractions to simplify the presentation. Because this can obscure the order of contractions, Appendix
A.3
summarizes the conventions we use. For completeness, the detailed derivations in the Appendices present the contractions explicitly using index.

III.1
Momentum density and conservation law

We begin by defining the relevant dynamical variable and its associated conservation law.
We introduce the momentum density for a fluid composed of
N
particles,

\begin{split}{\underline{j}}({\underline{r}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\,{{\underline{v_{i}}}}\,\delta({\underline{r}}-{\underline{r_{i}}}(t))\quad,\end{split}

(15)

where
m_{i}
,
{\underline{r_{i}}}
, and
{\underline{v_{i}}}
denote the mass, position, and velocity of particle
i
, respectively.
Eq. (
15
) is written in Fourier space,

\begin{split}{\underline{j}}({\underline{q}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\,{\underline{v_{i}}}\,\exp(i \vec{q}\cdot\vec{r_i}(t))\quad.\end{split}

(16)

The momentum density follows the conservation law,

\begin{split}\partial_{t}{\underline{j}}({\underline{q}},t)=-i\,\underline{\underline{\sigma}}({\underline{q}},t)\cdot{\underline{q}}\quad,\end{split}

(17)

where
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}({\underline{q}},t)
is stress tensor given by the Irving–Kirkwood expression
Irving and Kirkwood 1950
,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\sigma}}({\underline{q}},t)=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}{\underline{v_{i}}}\,{\underline{v_{i}}}&-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}{\underline{r_{ij}}}\,{\underline{F_{ij}}}\,g(i{\underline{q}}\cdot{\underline{r_{ij}}})\exp(i \vec{q} \cdot\vec{r_i}).\end{split}

(18)

Here
{\underline{r_{ij}}}={\underline{r_{i}}}-{\underline{r_{j}}}
is the relative position vector and
{\underline{F_{ij}}}
is the pair-wise force acting between particles. The function
g(x)
is defined as
g(x)=(e^{x}-1)/x
.

The momentum density is related to the macroscopic strain rate to linear order by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\gamma}}({\underline{q}},t)=-i{\underline{q}}\,{\underline{j}}({\underline{q}},t)/\rho_{0}\quad,\end{split}

(19)

where
\rho_{0}
is the average mass density of the system.

III.2
Constitutive equation of stress

We derive the constitutive equation of stress using Eq. (
14
) by choosing the normalized dynamical variable,

\begin{split}{\underline{A}}={\underline{j}}({\underline{q}},t)/\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})}\quad,\end{split}

(20)

where the normalization factor
\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})}
is determined by the isotropic steady state fluctuation,

\begin{split}C({\underline{q}})\underline{\underline{I}}=\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q}) \, \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}\quad.\end{split}

(21)

For equilibrium systems, the equi-partition principle implies,

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q}) \, \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}_{\rm eq}=\rho_{0}Vk_{B}T\underline{\underline{I}}\quad,\end{split}

(22)

where
V
is the volume of the system,
k_{B}
is the Boltzman constant, and
T
is temperature. Therefore,
C({\underline{q}})
can be understood as defining a non-equilibrium effective wavelength dependent temperature, which reduces to the thermodynamic temperature at equilibrium.

We first compute the static correlation
\underline{\underline{g}}
and dynamic correlation
\underline{\underline{{{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},t)
in the memory kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}
[Eq. (
12
)]. Due to the normalization, we have
\underline{\underline{g}}=\underline{\underline{I}}
. Using Eq. (
20
) in Eq. (
3
), together with momentum density conservation Eq. (
17
), we obtain,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{{{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},t)={\underline{q}}\cdot{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},t)\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}\quad,\end{split}

(23)

where the stress correlation tensor is defined as

\begin{split}{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},t)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{{\sigma}}(\vec{q},t)\, \mat{\sigma}^\dagger(\vec{q}) }}{C({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}\quad.\end{split}

(24)

Using Eq. (
12
) we obtain
\underline{\underline{K}}
, and finally using Eq. (
14
), we derive the following equality involving the stress perturbation in Fourier-Laplace space:

\begin{split}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\mat{\sigma}}}(\vec{q},s)} -\qty(\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q})^{-1} \cdot\Delta\Tfor{\hat{N}}(\vec{q},s):\expval{{\hat{\gamma}}(\vec{q},s)} )\cdot{\underline{q}}=0\quad,\end{split}

(25)

where we have again used momentum conservation.
Here we have defined the transient part of the stress correlation function, analogously to Eq. (
13
),

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)-\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)\quad.\end{split}

(26)

Eq. (
25
) determines the linear response function up to a divergence-free contribution if the correlation functions are known. Specifically, it dictates the relationship between the stress response and stress correlation functions for the longitudinal and shear components of the stress tensor. These components are parallel to the wavevector
{\underline{q}}
, or coupling the parallel and perpendicular directions. They are the only components that affect the evolution of the momentum density, see Eq. (
17
), and are therefore fully determined by Eq. (
25
). The stress components perpendicular to
{\underline{q}}
(the transverse direction) do not affect the evolution of the momentum density and are therefore left undetermined by Eq. (
25
). This ambiguity is analogous to the gauge freedom in electromagnetism; while the linear response function for the hydrodynamic components is well-defined, the components associated with the purely transverse stress cannot be unambiguously determined.
The related aspects to address the ambiguity between the stress correlation and elastic modulus has been discussed in
Semenov and Baschnagel 2024
;
Grimm et al. 2025
.

To obtain a constitutive relation for all components, we fix the gauge. As detailed in Appendix
D.2
, this leads to
the constitutive equation

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)}=\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\eta}}}}}({\underline{q}},s):\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\gamma}} (\vec{q},s)}\ ,\end{split}

(27)

with the generalized viscosity tensor,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\eta}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\quantity(\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} )^{-1}\cdot\Delta\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\ .\end{split}

(28)

This approach allows all components of this viscosity tensor to be determined if all components of the stress correlation tensor are known from either simulation or experiment.

Eqs. (
27
)–(
28
) represent the constitutive equation of stress for isotropic fluids in finite wave length and frequency. This applies to non-equilibrium steady states as well as to equilibrium.
The generalized viscosity in Eq. (
28
) extends the expression for the frequency and wave-dependent shear viscosity for equilibrium fluids, obtained by Evans
J Evans and P Morriss 2007
;
Evans 1981
, to non-equilibrium steady states.

We note that the stress correlation function at
s=0
at equilibrium satisfies
\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)=0
for finite wave length
J Evans and P Morriss 2007
, reflecting time reversal symmetry. For systems in a non-equilibrium steady state, however,
\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)\neq 0
in general. This can be understood by noting that
\underline{\underline{{\hat{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},0)=\underline{\underline{\omega}}
with

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\omega}}={\underline{q}}\cdot\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}\quad,\end{split}

(29)

which implies
\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)=0
for
{\underline{q}}\neq 0
by time reversal symmetry at equilibrium.

The connection to conventional viscous transport coefficient is recovered in the hydrodynamic limit in equilibrium. The Green-Kubo relation for viscosity is obtained by taking the long-wavelength limit (
q\to 0
) of the correlation function, followed by the zero-frequency limit of its Laplace transform (
s\to 0
), which yields a finite result. This is detailed in Appendix
D.3
.

III.3
Isotropic fluids in a two-dimensions

The generalized viscosity in Eq. (
28
) is computed from stress-stress correlation functions. These correlations can be obtained from simulation or experiment. Alternatively, one could determine them self‐consistently using a mode‐coupling–type approach
Götze 2009
or using microscopic models which allow analytical computation. Here as an example and for the comparison purpose to the chemically driven fluids in the later section (Sec.
V
), we compute the generalized viscosity for isotropic achiral fluids in two dimensions using a specific form of the correlation function. We consider in two dimensions, without the loss of generality, the
y
-axis to be aligned with the direction of
{\underline{q}}
, i.e.,
{\underline{q}}=(0,q)
.
In this reference frame, the isotropic and absence of chirality impose strong constraints: any tensor component with an odd number of a given index must vanish by symmetry
Wittmer et al. 2023
; for example,
T_{xy}=0
,
T_{xyyy}=0
. Using these symmetry constraints and noting that
\hat{\gamma}_{yx}({\underline{q}},s)=\hat{\gamma}_{xx}({\underline{q}},s)=0
in the reference frame chosen, we express the constitutive equation Eq. (
27
) as

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{xy}(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\eta}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\hat{\gamma}_{xy}(\vec{q},s)}\quad;\end{split}

(30)

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{yy}(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\zeta}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\hat{\gamma}_{yy}(\vec{q},s)}\quad,\end{split}

where the generalized viscosities are given by

\begin{split}\hat{\eta}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\Delta\hat{N}_{xyxy}\rho_{0}s}{\rho_{0}s-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}_{xyxy}}\quad;\end{split}

(31)

and

\begin{split}\hat{\zeta}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\Delta\hat{N}_{yyyy}\rho_{0}s}{\rho_{0}s-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}_{yyyy}}\quad.\end{split}

(32)

Motivated by Refs.
Klochko et al. 2018
;
Maier et al. 2018
;
Maier et al. 2017
where the stress correlation functions were calculated by a projection operator method and by the fluctuation dissipation relation at equilibrium, we consider the transient parts of the stress of the simple form

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{N}_{xyxy}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\eta^{\perp}\rho_{0}s}{\eta^{\perp}q^{2}+\rho_{0}s}\quad,\end{split}

(33)

and

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{N}_{yyyy}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\eta^{\parallel}\rho_{0}s}{\eta^{\parallel}q^{2}+\rho_{0}s}\quad.\end{split}

(34)

We thus obtain the rheology of a Newtonian fluid with the constant shear and bulk viscosities
\eta^{\perp}
and
\eta^{\parallel}
, respectively, such that we have in the time domain

{\eta}({\underline{q}},t)=\eta^{\perp}\delta(t)\quad,\qquad{\zeta}({\underline{q}},t)=\eta^{\parallel}\delta(t)\quad.

(35)

This minimal scenario will serve as a reference for the chemically driven case analyzed in Sec.
V.2
-
V.3
.

IV
Constitutive equation for chemical reactions

Chemical reactions generate thermodynamic driving forces for various biological processes, including the active stresses produced by molecular motors. In this section, we apply Eq. (
14
) to the chemical potential differences arising from such reactions. We begin this section by introducing the fundamental concepts of chemical reactions such as chemical potentials and reaction rates.

IV.1
Chemical potentials and reactions rates

We consider a system of mixture of
M
chemical species. The chemical potential of species
a
at position
{\underline{r}}
and time
t
,
\mu_{a}({\underline{r}},t)
, is defined using the number density of species
a
,
n_{a}
, and free energy density
f(n_{1},...,n_{M})
,

\begin{split}\mu_{a}({\underline{r}},t)\equiv\frac{\partial f}{\partial n_{a}({\underline{r}},t)}\quad.\end{split}

(36)

We consider chemical reactions
I=1,...,I_{\rm max}
which are described by the stoichiometric coefficient
\nu^{I}_{a}
. Here
\nu^{I}_{a}
is the number of molecule species of
a
removed by one event of the reaction
I
. When
\nu^{I}_{a}>0
, species
a
acts as a substrate and its number decreases; conversely, when
\nu_{a}<0
, species
a
is a product and its number increases.
We define the chemical potential difference between reactants and products in the chemical reaction
I
:

\begin{split}\Delta\mu_{I}({\underline{r}},t)\equiv\nu^{I}_{a}\mu_{a}({\underline{r}},t)\quad.\end{split}

(37)

The repeated indices imply the summation. Here
\Delta\mu_{I}
is also called reaction Gibbs free energy. The rate of number density change for species
a
is denoted
r_{a}=dn_{a}/dt
, which satisfies

r_{a}=-\nu_{a}^{I}r_{I}
, where
r_{I}
is the net reaction rate of the reaction
I
. Taking the time derivative of Eq. (
37
) leads to

\begin{split}\frac{d\Delta\mu_{I}({\underline{r}},t)}{dt}=\nu_{a}^{I}\quantity( \frac{\partial n_b}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\mu_a}{\partial n_b})=-r_{J}\nu_{a}^{I}\frac{\partial\mu_{a}}{\partial n_{b}}\nu_{b}^{J}\ .\end{split}

(38)

We define

\begin{split}R_{I}({\underline{r}},t)\equiv r_{J}({\underline{r}},t)\kappa_{IJ}({\underline{r}},t)\quad,\end{split}

(39)

with the susceptibility
\kappa_{IJ}
,

\begin{split}\kappa_{IJ}({\underline{r}},t)\equiv\nu_{a}^{I}\frac{\partial\mu_{a}({\underline{r}},t)}{\partial n_{b}({\underline{r}},t)}\nu_{b}^{J}\quad.\end{split}

(40)

Therefore Eq. (
38
) can be written as

\begin{split}\frac{d\Delta\mu_{I}({\underline{r}},t)}{dt}&=-R_{I}({\underline{r}},t)\quad.\end{split}

(41)

This evolution equation for
\Delta\mu_{I}({\underline{r}},t)
plays a role for chemical reactions analogous to the momentum conservation law [Eq. (
17
)] for fluids.

For simplicity, we consider an isotropic mixture of chemical species and fast diffusion of the chemical species, such that
\underline{\underline{\kappa}}
can be treated as a constant matrix in space and time. As we show in Appendix
E.1
, the susceptibility and the static fluctuation of chemical potentials is related in equilibrium as

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q})\, \Delta\vec{\mu}^\dagger(\vec{q})}_{\rm eq}=Vk_{B}T\underline{\underline{\kappa}}\quad.\end{split}

(42)

Eq. (
42
) represents the static Kubo relation for the chemical potential fluctuations, analogous to Eq. (
22
) for isotropic fluids.

IV.2
Constitutive equation for chemical reaction rates

We now derive the constitutive equation for the reaction rates. Similar to the previous section, we begin from Eq. (
14
). In this case, we consider normalized dynamical variables using the chemical potential differences,
\Delta{\underline{\mu}}\equiv(\Delta\mu_{1},\Delta\mu_{2},....)^{\top}
,

\begin{split}{\underline{A}}=\Delta{\underline{\mu}}({\underline{q}},t)/\sqrt{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}\quad.\end{split}

(43)

For the purpose of this work to illustrate the basic principles, we consider the simple scenario where the steady-state fluctuations of chemical potentials are identical and uncorrelated across different reactions. This assumption is justified when each reaction is independently driven by a reservoir with fixed chemical potential, for example. Note that a special case is a single chemical reaction, such as ATP hydrolysis, in the system. The normalization is thus given by the following steady state correlation,

\begin{split}C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})\underline{\underline{I}}=\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q}) \, \Delta\vec{\mu}^\dagger(\vec{q})}\quad.\end{split}

(44)

We obtain
\underline{\underline{g}}=\underline{\underline{I}}
due to this normalization and,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},t)=\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{{{R}}}(\vec{q},t)\, \vec{{R}}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}\quad.\end{split}

(45)

Applying Eq. (
41
) to the left-hand side of Eq. (
14
) and substituting the expressions for
\underline{\underline{g}}
and
\underline{\underline{\phi}}
into the memory kernel, we obtain the constitutive equation in Fourier-Laplace space

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{R}}(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\underline{\underline{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\hat{\mu}}(\vec{q},s)}\quad.\\
\end{split}

(46)

This can be transformed to the constitutive equation for the chemical reaction rate
r
,

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{r}}(\vec{q},s)}=\underline{\underline{\kappa}}^{-1}\cdot\hat{\underline{\underline{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\hat{\mu}}(\vec{q},s)}\quad,\\
\end{split}

(47)

with the kernel given by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)&=\left[\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{s}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)\right]^{-1}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)\quad,\end{split}

(48)

where
\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)=\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},0)
.
Eqs. (
47
)–(
48
) form the constitutive equation for chemical reaction rates, analogous to the constitutive equation for the stress obtained in Eqs. (
27
)-(
28
).

V
Non-equilibrium complex fluids driven by chemical reactions

In this section, we consider complex fluids whose behavior is governed by internal chemical reactions. Such systems are driven into non-equilibrium steady states by the continuous energy consumption of their individual constituents. Our formalism unifies the mechanical response of a fluid with the chemical reactions that drive its activity, systematically yielding the constitutive equations for chemically driven active fluids. The detail derivations in this section may be found in Appendix
F
.

V.1
General form of constitutive equation

Building upon the previous considerations, we now derive the constitutive equation for an isotropic active fluid driven by chemical reactions, beginning with the selection of the appropriate dynamical variables:

\begin{split}{\underline{A}}=\quantity(\vec{j}(\vec{q},t)/\sqrt{C(\vec{q})},\ \Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q},t)/\sqrt{C_\mu(\vec{q})})^{\top}.\end{split}

(49)

We again consider for simplicity an isotropic system, where the chemical reactions does not induce a directional current in the steady state. This implies their steady-state cross-correlation vanishes,

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q})\, \Delta{\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}=\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q})\, \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}=0\quad.\end{split}

(50)

The full steady-state correlation matrix then simplifies to

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{g}}({\underline{q}})=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\underline{I}}&\underline{\underline{0}}\\
\underline{\underline{0}}&\underline{\underline{I}}\end{pmatrix}\quad.\end{split}

(51)

Using the momentum density conservation, the matrix
\underline{\underline{\phi}}({\underline{q}},t)
can be written as,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\phi}}({\underline{q}},t)=\begin{pmatrix}{\underline{q}}\cdot\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}&i{\underline{q}}\cdot\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{\sigma R}({\underline{q}},t)/\rho_{0}\\
\\
-\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)\cdot i{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}&\underline{\underline{N}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},t)/\rho_{0}\end{pmatrix}\quad.\end{split}

(52)

Here the correlation matrices are defined as

\begin{split}&\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{\sigma}(\vec{q},t)\, \mat{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}\quad;\quad\\
\\
&\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{\sigma R}({\underline{q}},t)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{\sigma}(\vec{q},t)\, \vec{R}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}/\rho_{0}}\quad;\\
\\
&\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{R}(\vec{q},t)\, \mat{\sigma}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{\sqrt{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})C({\underline{q}})}/\rho_{0}}\quad;\quad\\
\\
&\underline{\underline{N}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},t)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{R}(\vec{q},t)\, \vec{R}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}\quad.\end{split}

(53)

The correlations
\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}
and
\underline{\underline{N}}^{RR}
are analogous to Eq. (
24
) and Eq. (
45
), respectively. The correlations
\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{\sigma R}
and
\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{R\sigma}
describe the chemo-mechanical couplings due to the concurrent presence of mechanical processes and chemical processes.
The transient part of the correlation functions are defined analogously to those in the previous sections, as in Eq. (
26
):

\begin{split}&\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}({\underline{q}},0)\quad;\quad\\
\\
&\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}({\underline{q}},0)\quad;\\
\\
&\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},0)\quad;\quad\\
\\
&\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},0)\quad.\end{split}

(54)

Delegating the detailed derivation to the Appendix
F.1
, a procedure analogous to that in the previous sections leads to the constitutive equation in Fourier-Laplace space:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)}&=\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Pi}}}}}({\underline{q}},s):\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\gamma}}(\vec{q},s) }+\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Lambda}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\hat{\mu}}(\vec{q},s)};\\
\\
\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{r}}(\vec{q},s)}&=\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Xi}}}}({\underline{q}},s):\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\gamma}}(\vec{q},s)}+\underline{\underline{\hat{\Upsilon}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\hat{\mu}} (\vec{q},s)},\end{split}

(55)

where the generalized transport coefficients are given by

\begin{split}&\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Pi}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\\
&\underline{\underline{\hat{Y}}}\cdot\left(\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}\cdot\underline{\underline{X}}^{-1}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}\right);\end{split}

(56)

\begin{split}&\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Lambda}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\\
&\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{C}{C_{\mu}}}\underline{\underline{\hat{Y}}}\cdot\left(\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}+\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}\cdot\underline{\underline{X}}^{-1}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR}\right);\end{split}

\begin{split}&\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Xi}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\\
&\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mu}}{C}}\underline{\underline{\kappa}}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\underline{Z}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}\cdot{\underline{q}}\cdot\underline{\underline{U}}^{-1}\cdot{\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}+\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}\right);\end{split}

\begin{split}&\underline{\underline{\hat{\Upsilon}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\\
&\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\underline{\underline{\kappa}}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\underline{Z}}\cdot\quantity(\frac{1}{s \rho_0} \Delta\Tthr{\hat{N}}^{R\sigma}\cdot\vec{q} \cdot\mat{U}^{-1}\cdot{\vec{q}} \cdot\Delta\Tthr{\hat{N}}^{\sigma R} + \Delta\mat{\hat{N}}^{RR}),\end{split}

using the matrices
\underline{\underline{U}}
,
\underline{\underline{X}}
,
\underline{\underline{Y}}
, and
\underline{\underline{Z}}
:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{U}}}\equiv\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}{\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}\cdot{\underline{q}}\ ;\quad\underline{\underline{\hat{V}}}\equiv-\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}i{\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}\ ;\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{W}}}\equiv\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}\cdot i{{\underline{q}}}\ ;\quad\underline{\underline{\hat{X}}}\equiv\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{s\rho_{0}}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR};\end{split}

(57)

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{Y}}}\equiv\quantity(\mat{\hat{U}}^{}-\mat{\hat{V}}^{} \cdot\mat{\hat{X}}^{-1}\cdot\mat{\hat{W}}^{})^{-1};\\
\\
\underline{\underline{\hat{Z}}}\equiv\quantity(\mat{\hat{X}}^{}-\mat{\hat{W}}^{}\cdot\mat{\hat{U}}^{-1}\cdot\mat{\hat{V}}^{})^{-1}.\end{split}

Eqs. (
55
)–(
57
) provide general set of constitutive relations for isotropic active fluids driven by chemical reactions. In the constitutive equations, Eq. (
55
), the contribution to the stress tensor that is proportional to the chemical potential difference,
\Delta{\underline{\mu}}
, is known as the active stress
Jülicher et al. 2018
. The active stress originates from the chemo-mechanical coupling coefficient
\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Lambda}}}}
, which is mediated by the tensors,
\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}
and
\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}
. In the limit where this coupling is absent, i.e.,
\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}=\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}=0
, it follows that
\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Lambda}}}}=\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Xi}}}}=0
, and the active stress consequently vanishes. The system then becomes decoupled, and the equations revert to the separate forms for stress and chemical reaction rates derived previously in Sections
III
and
IV
, respectively.

We find that the effective viscosity,
\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{\Pi}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)
, is itself modified by chemo-mechanical coupling. This implies that the memory kernel governing the viscous stress relaxation depends directly on the active chemical processes. We identify this coupling-dependent relaxation, which we call Active Viscoelastic Memory. Consequently, the rheology of fluid can be fundamentally changed in the presence of chemical processes.

V.2
Active isotropic fluids driven by single chemical reaction in a two-dimensions

In this section we derive explicit expressions for the constitutive Eqs.(
55
)–(
56
) for achiral systems in a two‐dimensions with a single chemical reaction—a situation often relevant to biological materials powered by ATP hydrolysis. We denote this chemical reaction as
I
. To simplify further, we use the coordinate frame where the
y
-axis is aligned with the direction of
{\underline{q}}
, i.e.
{\underline{q}}=(0,q)
. In this frame, we obtain simplified constitutive equations:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{xy}(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\eta}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\hat{\gamma}_{xy}(\vec{q},s)};\end{split}

(58)

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{yy}(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\zeta}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\hat{\gamma}_{yy}(\vec{q},s)}+\hat{\Lambda}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\Delta\hat{\mu}_{I}(\vec{q},s)};\end{split}

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\hat{r}_I(\vec{q},s)}=\hat{\Xi}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\hat{\gamma}_{yy}(\vec{q},s)}+\hat{\Upsilon}({\underline{q}},s)\expectationvalue{\Delta\hat{\mu}_{I} (\vec{q},s)}.\end{split}

The generalized transport coefficients are given by

\begin{split}\hat{\eta}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{xyxy}\rho_{0}s}{\rho_{0}s-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{xyxy}};\end{split}

(59)

\begin{split}&\hat{\zeta}({\underline{q}},s)=\\
&\frac{\rho_{0}s(\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}-\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}+\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}\rho_{0}s)}{(\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}-\rho_{0}s)(\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}q^{2}-\rho_{0}s)-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}};\end{split}

\begin{split}&\hat{\Lambda}({\underline{q}},s)=\\
&\frac{\hat{\chi}_{yy,I}\rho^{2}_{0}s^{2}}{(\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}-\rho_{0}s)(\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}q^{2}-\rho_{0}s)-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}};\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}&\hat{\Xi}({\underline{q}},s)=\\
&\frac{\hat{\chi}_{I,yy}\rho^{2}_{0}s^{2}}{(\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}-\rho_{0}s)(\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}q^{2}-\rho_{0}s)-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}};\end{split}

\begin{split}&\hat{\Upsilon}({\underline{q}},s)=\\
&\frac{s}{\kappa_{II}}\frac{(\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}-\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy})q^{2}+\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}\rho_{0}s}{(\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}-\rho_{0}s)(\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}q^{2}-\rho_{0}s)-q^{2}\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}};\end{split}

with the cross coupling terms,

\begin{split}\hat{\chi}_{yy,I}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\frac{\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}({\underline{q}},s)}{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{C({\underline{q}})}{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}}\ ;\end{split}

(60)

and

\begin{split}\hat{\chi}_{I,yy}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\frac{\Delta\hat{N}_{I,yy}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},s)}{\kappa_{II}}\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}{C({\underline{q}})}}\ .\end{split}

(61)

These results should be compared to Eq. (
30
)–(
32
) for the isotropic fluids without chemical reactions.

Linear irreversible thermodynamics dictates reciprocity relations between cross-coupling transport coefficients, known as Onsager reciprocity
Onsager 1931a
;
Onsager 1931b
. In this simplified form, we can readily confirm the Onsager reciprocity in equilibrium:
\hat{\Lambda}=-\hat{\Xi}
.
In equilibrium, we have
C({\underline{q}})=\rho_{0}Vk_{B}T
[Eq. (
22
)] and
C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})=Vk_{B}T\kappa_{II}
[Eq. (
42
)]. Furthermore, we have
N^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}({\underline{q}},t)=-N_{I,yy}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)
due to the time reversal symmetry in equilibrium, see Appendix
B.3
. Substituting these conditions into the definitions of the coupling coefficients [Eqs. (
60
)-(
61
)] directly yields the reciprocity relation; see Appendix
F.3
for the detail. In a non-equilibrium steady-state, however, time-reversal symmetry is broken, and these relations do not hold, thus allowing for non-reciprocal coupling.

In this coordinate frame, the chemical reaction couples only to the longitudinal stress
\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{yy}}
, not the transverse stress
\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{xy}}
. We therefore focus on
\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{yy}}
for the specific example in the following section.

V.3
Simple example of correlation functions

The generalized transport coefficients in Eq. (
59
) involve correlation functions that must be specified. For the stress-stress correlation functions, we use again the form of Eq. (
34
).
For the correlation functions involving chemical reactions, we choose functions that satisfy the following conditions: for
s=0
, Eq. (
54
) requires
\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}({\underline{q}},0)=\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}({\underline{q}},0)=\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}({\underline{q}},0)=\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}({\underline{q}},0)=0
. Because the chemical reaction rates are not hydrodynamic variables that becomes slow in the small
q
limit, we choose chemical correlations that are independent on
q
. The cross correlations between mechanical and chemical degree of freedom contain
q
. We thus have

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma\sigma}_{yyyy}(q,s)=\frac{\eta^{\parallel}\rho_{0}s}{\eta^{\parallel}q^{2}+\rho_{0}s}\quad;\quad\Delta\hat{N}^{RR}_{II}(q,s)=\lambda\frac{\rho_{0}s}{\lambda+s}\,;\end{split}

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{N}^{\sigma R}_{yy,I}(q,s)=\nu_{\sigma R}\frac{\rho_{0}s^{2}}{(s+\lambda)(s+\lambda\,q^{2}\xi_{\mu}^{2})}\quad;\end{split}

(62)

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{N}^{R\sigma}_{I,yy}(q,s)=\nu_{R\sigma}\frac{\rho_{0}s^{2}}{(s+\lambda)(s+\lambda\,q^{2}\xi_{\mu}^{2})}\quad.\end{split}

Here
\lambda
is the relaxation rate of the chemical process,
\nu_{\sigma R}
and
\nu_{R\sigma}
characterize the chemo-mechanical coupling (with units of velocity), and
\xi_{\mu}
is chemo-mechanical coupling length.

For an equilibrium system with time-reversal symmetry, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem and microscopic reversibility holds. From this, Kubo relation [Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
42
)] and Onsager reciprocity (
\hat{\Lambda}=-\hat{\Xi}
) follow. In the following, we set
\nu\equiv\nu_{\sigma R}=-\nu_{R\sigma}/\varepsilon
to model the reciprocity breaking where
\varepsilon=1
respects the reciprocity and
\varepsilon\neq 1
otherwise. The chemo-mechanical coupling length is set as
\xi_{\mu}=\absolutevalue{\nu}/\lambda
.
We substitute these forms of the correlation functions into Eq. (
59
) to obtain the generalized transport coefficients. The resulting transport coefficients in Laplace space are shown in Eq. (
184
) in Appendix
G
.

For large

,
\hat{\zeta}(q,s)
and

\begin{split}&\hat{\zeta}(q,s)=\eta^{\parallel}+\frac{\zeta_{c}(\varepsilon)}{s}+O(s^{-2})\quad;\\
\\
&\zeta_{c}(\varepsilon)\equiv-\varepsilon\rho_{0}\lambda^{2}\xi_{\mu}^{2}\quad,\end{split}

(63)

and

\begin{split}&\hat{\Lambda}(q,s)=\sqrt{\frac{C}{C_{\mu}}}\lambda\xi_{\mu}+\frac{\Lambda_{c}(q)}{s}+O(s^{-2})\quad;\\
\\
&\Lambda_{c}(q)\equiv-\frac{\lambda\,\xi_{\mu}}{\rho_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{C}{C_{\mu}}}(\lambda\rho_{0}q^{2}\xi_{\mu}^{2}-\eta^{\parallel}q^{2})\quad,\end{split}

(64)

respectively. The first terms in
\hat{\zeta}(q,s)
and
\hat{\Lambda}(q,s)

\delta(t)

VI
Discussion

The evolution equation for the correlation function, Eq. (
8
), is built upon the property of time-translational invariance and a general identity for the steady state correlation functions. The procedure is an exact equivalence transformation of correlation functions, a principle that is shared with the Mori-Zwanzig projection operator formalism
Zwanzig 2001
.
Our formulation provides a direct link between transport coefficients and correlation functions, consistent with the established relations at equilibrium. Namely, the memory kernel, Eq. (
9
), is expressed explicitly in terms of the flux-flux correlation function,
\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}(s)
. These are precisely the correlations that determine transport coefficients via the Green-Kubo relations at equilibrium, thus creating a transparent link between the system’s memory, its transport properties, and the underlying microscopic fluxes.

The equation governing the response of dynamical variables, Eq. (
14
), can be viewed as a general linear fluctuation–response relation valid in non-equilibrium steady states with non-Markovian dynamics. Starting from Eq. (
14
), we can readily derive the fluctuation–dissipation relation for non-equilibrium steady states, as detailed in Appendix
C.2
. The resulting expression is model-independent and can be applied to quantify deviations from equilibrium across a broad class of systems, in particular biological systems such as cytoskeleton
Mizuno et al. 2007
. Related advances include the generalized fluctuation-dissipation theorem for Markovian dynamics derived by Prost, Joanny, and Parrondo
Prost et al. 2009
from the Hatano–Sasa identity
Hatano and Sasa 2001
. Seifert and Speck derived a generalized fluctuation–dissipation relation for non-equilibrium steady states in Markovian systems by explicitly incorporating entropy production
Seifert and Speck 2010
. More recent advances in nonequilibrium fluctuation–response relations for Markovian networks include Refs.
Aslyamov et al. 2025
;
Bao and Liang 2024
. In the context of active matter, equilibrium-like Green–Kubo relations have been shown to hold in certain non-equilibrium steady states and perturbations in hydrodynamic limit
Han et al. 2021
;
Chun et al. 2021
. Finally, linear-response and Green–Kubo formulations based on the integration-through-transients framework
Fuchs and Cates 2002
;
Fuchs and Cates 2009
have been developed and discussed in detail
Dal Cengio et al. 2019
.

Hydrodynamic descriptions of active matter have been successful in capturing a wide range of biological phenomena
Marchetti et al. 2013
. Our formulation extends the applicability of active matter theory to far-from-equilibrium, memory-rich complex fluids. Furthermore, it makes explicit the connection between fluid transport coefficients and broken time-reversal symmetry: in non-equilibrium fluids, the transport coefficients are renormalized in a manner controlled by the degree of time-reversal breaking, see Eqs. (
26
) and (
28
). This result helps to bridge active matter physics and the recent progress on non-equilibrium thermodynamics such as stochastic thermodynamics
Falasco and Esposito 2025
;
Seifert 2012
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021
;
Shiraishi 2023
.

In this study, we restricted our analysis to isotropic systems. For anisotropic cases, such as nematic phases, the matrix
\underline{\underline{g}}
in Eq. (
8
) becomes anisotropic and the commutator does not vanish, therefore the fluctuation–response relation of Eq. (
14
) has to be generalized to take into account the commutator.
While our framework can be extended to treat anisotropic systems, we will explore this generalization in future work. We likewise leave for future study the explicit treatment of
chiral fluids, whose influence is expected to appear as signatures in the reactive frequency matrix
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
.

Our theory yields several testable predictions. (i) Length-scale crossover. The viscous kernel
\zeta(q,t)
crosses over from monotonic relaxation at long wavelengths to oscillatory relaxation at short wavelengths (Fig.
1
). Spatially resolved microrheology, for example, tracking embedded tracer particles, should therefore find simple decay at large scales but oscillatory stress relaxation at small scales. (ii) Metabolic control of the crossover. Modulating the colony’s metabolic rate (e.g., via nutrient levels) changes
\xi_{\mu}
, shifting the characteristic length scale of the transition between the monotonic and oscillatory relaxations. (iii) Negative storage modulus. We predict regimes where the storage modulus
\tilde{G}^{\prime}(q,\omega)
becomes negative at finite frequency due to reactive (chemo–mechanical) couplings.
Verifying these effects would provide a direct experimental probe of Active Viscoelastic Memory.

To conclude, we have developed a general framework for describing non-equilibrium systems, starting from a fundamental identity for steady-state correlation functions. With this approach, we systematically derived the constitutive equations for complex fluids driven by internal chemical reactions. Our analysis uncovers a mechanism beyond the conventional notion of active stress: a direct contribution of chemo–mechanical coupling to the viscous transport coefficients. We believe that this first-principles framework provides a broadly applicable tool for non-equilibrium systems, and in particular for dense active matter, where the interplay of memory and activity gives rise to rich emergent phenomena.

Appendix B
Correlation functions

B.1
Definition of the correlation functions

We define the correlation of a set of dynamical variables
{\underline{A}}(t)=(A_{1}(t),A_{2}(t),\ldots)^{\top}
, using the time average,

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{{A}_i(t'') {A}^*_j(t')}\equiv\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}{A}_{i}(t^{\prime\prime}+t){A}_{j}^{*}(t^{\prime}+t)dt.\end{split}

(77)

Here
*
is complex conjugation.
Alternatively, the correlation function can be defined through a phase space average as

\begin{split}&\expectationvalue{{A}_i(t'') {A}^*_j(t')}\equiv\int d{{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}}p({\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}){A}_{j}^{*}({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}})\int d{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}}p({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}}|{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}){A}_{i}({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}}),\end{split}

(78)

where
{\underline{X}}
denotes the full set of phase space variables. The joint probability distribution,
p({\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}},{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime\prime}})=p({\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}})p({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}}|{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}})
, in Eq. (
78
) is a steady state distribution. Assuming ergodicity in the limit
\tau\rightarrow\infty
, the two definitions yield the same correlation function.

By choosing
t^{\prime\prime}=t+s
and
t^{\prime}=s
, and invoking time translational invariance, we may set
s=0
and define the correlation matrix,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t) \vec{A}^\dagger},\end{split}

(79)

where we used the notation
{\underline{A}}\equiv{\underline{A}}(t=0)
.

B.2
Time derivatives of correlation functions

We derive general properties of the steady-state correlation functions using the definition in Eq. (
77
)
Hansen and McDonald 2013
. Taking the time derivative of the correlation function with respect to
t’
, we find

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t+t') \vec{A}^\dagger(t')}=\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{A}}(t+t') \vec{A}^\dagger(t')}+\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t+t') \vec{\dot{A}}^\dagger(t')}=0.\end{split}

(80)

Evaluating this expression at
t’=0
gives

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{A}}(t) \vec{A}^\dagger}=-\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t) {\vec{\dot{A}}^\dagger}}.\end{split}

(81)

Choosing
t=0
in Eq. (
81

is anti-Harmitian.
Taking the second derivative of
\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t+t') \vec{A}^\dagger(t')}
with respect to

and using Eq. (
81
), we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t) \vec{A}^\dagger}=-\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{A}}(t) \vec{\dot{A}}^\dagger}.\end{split}

(82)

B.3
Symmetry property of correlation functions

When the system possesses time-reversal symmetry, the correlation function satisfies

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{A_i(t)A^*_j}=\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_i(-t)A^*_j}=\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_i A^*_j(t)}=\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_j(t)A^*_i}^{*}.\end{split}

(83)

Here,
\epsilon_{i}(\epsilon_{j})
denotes the time-reversal signature of
A_{i}(A_{j})
. In deriving the above, we have used time translation invariance. This relation implies that, at equilibrium, equal-time (
t=0
) correlation functions between variables with opposite time-reversal signatures vanish.

When the dynamical variables depend on space and the system exhibits inversion symmetry, the correlation function in Fourier space satisfies

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{A_i(\vec{q},t)A^*_j(\vec{q})}=p_{i}p_{j}\expectationvalue{A_i(-\vec{q},t)A^*_j(-\vec{q})}.\end{split}

(84)

Here,
p_{i}
(
p_{j}
) denotes the inversion parity of
A_{i}
(
A_{j}
) under the transformation
{\underline{q}}\rightarrow-{\underline{q}}
.

When the dynamical variables exhibit both time-reversal and spatial inversion symmetry, the correlation function in Fourier space satisfies

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{A_i(\vec{q},t)A^*_j(\vec{q})}=\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_j(\vec{q},t)A^*_i(\vec{q})}^{*}=\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_j(-\vec{q},t)A^*_i(-\vec{q})}=p_{i}p_{j}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\expectationvalue{A_j(\vec{q},t)A^*_i(\vec{q})}.\end{split}

(85)

Appendix C
Evolution of the average response

C.1
Derivation of the fluctuation-response relation

To derive the equation for the average response of dynamical variables, we use the definition of the correlation function, Eq. (
78
), applied to Eq. (
8
) in the absence of the commutator term.
In element-wise notation, we have

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{A_i(t)A_j^*}=-\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K_{im}(t-t^{\prime})\expectationvalue{A_m(t')A_j^*}.\end{split}

(86)

Using the definition Eq. (
C.1
) we can write this as

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\int d{{{\underline{X}}_{0}}}p({\underline{X}}_{0})A_{j}^{*}({{\underline{X}}_{0}})\int d{{\underline{X}}_{t}}p({{\underline{X}}_{t}}|{{\underline{X}}_{0}})A_{i}({{\underline{X}}_{t}})\\
&=-\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K_{im}(t-t^{\prime})\int d{{{\underline{X}}_{0}}}p({\underline{X}}_{0})A_{j}^{*}({{\underline{X}}_{0}})\int d{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}p({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}|{{\underline{X}}_{0}})A_{m}({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}).\end{split}

(87)

The average over the initial distribution involving
A_{j}^{*}({{\underline{X}}_{0}})
appears on both sides and can thus be factored out, leaving only the conditional average for a given initial condition
{\underline{X}}_{0}
:

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\int d{{\underline{X}}_{t}}p({{\underline{X}}_{t}}|{{\underline{X}}_{0}})A_{i}({{\underline{X}}_{t}})=-\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K_{im}(t-t^{\prime})\int d{{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}p({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}|{{\underline{X}}_{0}})A_{m}({{\underline{X}}_{t^{\prime}}}).\end{split}

(88)

Multiplying
p({\underline{X}}_{0})
for the both sides and integrating over
{\underline{X}}_{0}
yields

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{A_i(t)}=-\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}K_{im}(t-t^{\prime})\expectationvalue{A_m(t')}.\end{split}

(89)

In matrix notation, this becomes Eq. (
14
).

In the above derivation, we assumed that the form of the kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}(t)
remains in the functional form of the steady state while the average of
{\underline{A}}(t)
is the deviation from the steady state. This implies Eq. (
89
) is correct in the linear order because the next order would involve the correction to
\underline{\underline{K}}(t)
.

C.2
Fluctuation-response relation in non-equilibrium steady states

In this section, we derive the fluctuation-response relation in non-equilibrium steady states similar to Harada-Sasa
Harada and Sasa 2005
using Eq. (
14
). We introduce external forces
{\underline{f}}(t)
into Eq. (
14
) to prove the response,

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t)}=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\underline{\underline{K}}(t-t^{\prime})\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(t')}+{\underline{f}}(t).\end{split}

(90)

By taking the Laplace transform we obtain

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{A}}(s)}=\quantity( s \mat{I} + \mat{\hat{K}}(s))^{-1}\cdot{\underline{\hat{f}}}(s),\end{split}

(91)

where we set
\expectationvalue{\vec{A}(0)}=0
.
Eq. (
91
) defines the response matrix:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{\Gamma}}}(s)\equiv\quantity( s \mat{I} + \mat{\hat{K}}(s))^{-1}.\end{split}

(92)

Using Eq. (
5
), the kernel
\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)
can be expressed as

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)&=\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s) -\mat{\omega})\right]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(\mat{\hat{\phi}}(s)-\mat{\omega})\\
&=-s\underline{\underline{I}}+\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}^{-1}(s)\cdot\underline{\underline{g}}\quad.\end{split}

(93)

Therefore, we obtain the relation

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)=\underline{\underline{g}}\cdot\underline{\underline{\hat{\Gamma}}}(s).\end{split}

(94)

In thermal equilibrium,
\underline{\underline{g}}
relates to the thermal energy via the static Kubo formula, see Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
42
), for example.

We define the symmetric (
\rm S
) and anti-symmetric (
\rm A
) part of
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
upon the time reversal as

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{\rm S}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\psi}(t) + \mat{\psi}(-t) );\quad\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{\rm A}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\psi}(t) - \mat{\psi}(-t) ).\end{split}

(95)

Similarly, we define the symmetric and anti-symmetric part of
\Gamma(t)
as

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{\rm S}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\Gamma}(t) + \mat{\Gamma}(-t) );\quad\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{\rm A}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\Gamma}(t) - \mat{\Gamma}(-t) ).\end{split}

(96)

The Fourier transform of
\psi_{ij}(t)
, denoted as
\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\omega)
, reads

\begin{split}\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\omega)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi_{ij}(t)dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm S}(t)dt+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm A}(t)dt\\
&=\quantity(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm S}(t)dt
+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm S}(t)dt)+\quantity(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm A}(t)dt
-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\psi_{ij}^{\rm A}(t)dt )\\
&=2\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl[\hat{\psi}^{\rm S}_{ij}(s=i\omega)\bigr]+2i\,\mathrm{Im}\bigl[\hat{\psi}^{\rm A}_{ij}(s=i\omega)\bigr]\\
&=2g_{ik}\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl[\hat{\Gamma}^{\rm S}_{kj}(s=i\omega)\bigr]+2ig_{ik}\,\mathrm{Im}\bigl[\hat{\Gamma}^{\rm A}_{kj}(s=i\omega)\bigr].\end{split}

(97)

The final line of Eq. (
97
) follows from Eq. (
94
) and the summation over repeated indices is implied.
We define the causal response functions,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{R}}(t)\equiv\theta(t)\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{\rm S}(t)\end{split}

(98)

and

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{S}}(t)\equiv\theta(t)\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{\rm A}(t),\end{split}

(99)

where
\theta(t)
represents the Heaviside step function.
Using these response functions, we can rewrite Eq. (
97
) in the Fourier space,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\tilde{\psi}}}(\omega)-2\underline{\underline{g}}\cdot\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl[\underline{\underline{\tilde{R}}}(\omega)\bigr]=2i\underline{\underline{g}}\cdot\,\mathrm{Im}\bigl[\underline{\underline{\tilde{S}}}(\omega)\bigr].\end{split}

(100)

In equilibrium, the correlation function
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
is purely even when all the constituent variables have identical time-reversal signature (see Appendix
B.3
). Consequently, its odd component vanishes, as does the anti-symmetric part of the response function, i.e.,
\underline{\underline{S}}(t)=0
. A non-zero
\underline{\underline{S}}(t)
is therefore a signature of the non-equilibrium steady states. Eq. (
100
) provides a generalization of the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem for non-equilibrium states by connecting the correlation function
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(t)
to both the symmetric response
\underline{\underline{R}}(t)
and the anti-symmetric response
\underline{\underline{S}}(t)
. The left-hand side of this equation is analogous to the Harada-Sasa relation
Harada and Sasa 2005
, which quantifies the violation of the equilibrium Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem. Our result shows this violation is directly encoded in the anti-symmetric response,
\underline{\underline{S}}(t)
.

C.3
Example: The 2D Rotating Harmonic Oscillator

In this section, we illustrate the method presented in Sec.
II
using the model of a two-dimensional rotating harmonic oscillator. We consider the stochastic differential equation:

\begin{split}d{\underline{r}}(t)=(-\kappa\,\underline{\underline{I}}+\Omega\,\underline{\underline{J}})\cdot{\underline{r}}(t)\,dt+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\underline{I}}\cdot d{\underline{W_{t}}}\quad,\end{split}

(101)

where
{\underline{dW_{t}}}
represents a Wiener process. The matrices
\underline{\underline{I}}
and
\underline{\underline{J}}
are the symmetric identity matrix and the anti-symmetric rotation matrix, respectively, given by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{I}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad;\quad\underline{\underline{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\end{split}\quad.

(102)

Here,
\kappa
,
\Omega
, and
D>0
are the trap stiffness, rotation frequency, and diffusion constant, respectively.
For notational convenience, we define:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{L}}\equiv-\kappa\,\underline{\underline{I}}+\Omega\,\underline{\underline{J}}\quad;\quad\underline{\underline{B}}\equiv\sqrt{2D}\underline{\underline{I}}\quad.\end{split}

(103)

Computation of the static correlation matrix
\underline{\underline{g}}
.

First, we compute the equal-time (static) correlation function. Using Itô’s Lemma, we obtain:

\begin{split}d\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}&=\expectationvalue{d\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}+\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, d\vec{r}(t)^\top}+\expectationvalue{d\vec{r}(t)\, d\vec{r}(t)^\top}\\
&=\underline{\underline{L}}\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}dt+\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}\cdot\underline{\underline{L}}^{\top}dt+B\cdot B^{\top}dt\quad.\end{split}

(104)

Therefore, the time evolution of the correlation matrix is given by:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}&=\underline{\underline{L}}\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}+\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}\cdot\underline{\underline{L}}^{\top}+B\cdot B^{\top}=0\quad,\end{split}

(105)

where we used the property that for a stationary state, the time derivative of the equal-time correlation function vanishes. Therefore Eq. (
105
) leads to the isotropic static correlation,

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{g}}\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}&=\frac{D}{\kappa}\underline{\underline{I}}\quad.\end{split}

(106)

Dynamical correlation matrix
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(\tau)
.

Using Eq. (
101
), the equation of motion for the dynamical correlation function is

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\tau}\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t+\tau)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}=\underline{\underline{L}}\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t+\tau)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}\quad,\end{split}

(107)

which leads to the solution:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t+\tau)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}=\exp(\mat{L}\, \tau)\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}\quad.\end{split}

(108)

Thus, the dynamical correlation function
\underline{\underline{\psi}}(\tau)\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t+\tau)\, \vec{r}(t)^\top}
is given by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\psi}}(\tau)=\exp(\mat{L}\, \tau)\cdot\underline{\underline{g}}\quad(\tau\geq 0)\quad.\end{split}

(109)

We note that time-translational invariance leads to the relation:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\psi}}(-\tau)=\underline{\underline{\psi}}(\tau)^{\top}\quad.\end{split}

(110)

Furthermore, differentiating with respect to
\tau
gives

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\dot{\psi}}}(-\tau)=-\underline{\underline{\dot{\psi}}}(\tau)^{\top}\quad.\end{split}

(111)

Computation of the reactive frequency matrix
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
.

The static correlation matrix
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
is computed symmetrically at
\tau=0
to account for the discontinuity of the derivative at the time origin:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\omega}}&\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\dot{\psi}}(\tau=0^+) + \mat{\dot{\psi}}(\tau=0^-))\quad.\end{split}

(112)

Using Eq. (
111
) and Eq. (
109
), this can be rewritten as:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\omega}}&=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{\dot{\psi}}(\tau=0^+) - \mat{\dot{\psi}}^\top(\tau=0^+) )\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mat{L}\cdot\mat{g} - \qty(\mat{L}\cdot\mat{g})^\top)\\
&=\frac{\Omega D}{\kappa}\underline{\underline{J}}\quad.\end{split}

(113)

Thus, we find that
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
is an anti-symmetric matrix proportional to
\Omega
:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\omega}}&=\frac{\Omega D}{\kappa}\underline{\underline{J}}\quad.\end{split}

(114)

We consider, in this example, the time reversal signatures of the dynamical variables in
{\underline{r}}=(x,y)^{\top}
to be the same, therefore
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
vanishes when the system is in equilibrium (
\Omega=0
);
\underline{\underline{\omega}}
describe a non-reciprocal coupling out of equilibrium.

Computation of the kernel,
\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)
.

In Laplace space, we have the following relationship for a steady state:

\begin{split}\Delta\hat{\phi}(s)\equiv\hat{\phi}(s)-\underline{\underline{\omega}}=-s^{2}\underline{\underline{\hat{\psi}}}(s)+s\underline{\underline{g}}\quad.\end{split}

(115)

By substituting
\Delta\hat{\phi}(s)
into the expression for the kernel
\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)
[Eq. (
12
)], we obtain:

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}(s)&=\quantity[\mat{g} - \frac{1}{s}\Delta\hat{\phi}(s) ]^{-1}\cdot\Delta\hat{\phi}(s)\\
&=-\quantity( -\kappa\, \mat{I} + \Omega\, \mat{J})\quad.\end{split}

(116)

Using Eq. (
14
) from the main text then leads to:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)}=\quantity( -\kappa\, \mat{I} + \Omega\, \mat{J})\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{r}(t)}\quad,\end{split}

(117)

which is consistent with the deterministic part of Eq. (
101
).

Therefore, by using this formulation, the linear coefficient matrix can be computed from the correlation functions without prior knowledge of the underlying equation of motion.

Appendix D
Constitutive equation for isotropic non-equilibrium complex fluids

D.1
Properties of memory kernels and response functions

In this section, we derive the identity in Eq. (
25
). In the following subsection, we obtain the constitutive equation, Eq. (
27
), by fixing the gauge.

We first evaluate Eq. (
14
) for the choice of the dynamical variable,

\begin{split}{\underline{A}}=\frac{{\underline{j}}({\underline{q}},t)}{\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})}},\end{split}

(118)

where
\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})}
is a normalization factor defined through

\begin{split}C({\underline{q}})\underline{\underline{I}}=\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q}) \, \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}.\end{split}

(119)

We have assumed isotropic fluctuations of the momentum density in the steady state.

We now evaluate the equation of motion for the momentum density:

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q},t)}=-\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\underline{\underline{{K}}}({\underline{q}},t-t^{\prime})\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q},t')},\end{split}

(120)

where the memory kernel in Laplace space is given by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)=\left[\underline{\underline{g}}-\frac{1}{s}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)\right]^{-1}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s);\end{split}

(121)

with

\begin{split}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)=\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},0).\end{split}

(122)

We begin by evaluating the memory kernel
\underline{\underline{K}}({\underline{q}},t)
. Due to the normalization of the momentum density, we have

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{g}}({\underline{q}})=\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q}) \ \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})}=\underline{\underline{I}}\quad.\end{split}

(123)

The matrix
\underline{\underline{\phi}}
is given by

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},t)=\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{\dot{j}}(\vec{q},t) \ \vec{\dot{j}}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})}=\frac{\expectationvalue{\qty(\mat{\sigma}(\vec{q},t) \cdot\vec{q} ) \qty(\vec{q}^\top\cdot\mat{\sigma}^\dagger(\vec{q}) ) }}{C({\underline{q}})}\quad,\end{split}

(124)

which in Laplace space is

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{{\hat{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)\cdot\vec{q}) \qty(\vec{q}^{\top} \cdot\mat{\sigma}^\dagger(\vec{q})) }}{C({\underline{q}})}\quad.\end{split}

(125)

In the index notation, this reads

\begin{split}\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},s)=q_{\gamma}\frac{\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\gamma}(\vec{q},s) {\sigma}_{\beta\epsilon}^*(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})}q_{\epsilon}\quad.\end{split}

(126)

We define the stress correlation tensor

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)\, \mat{\sigma}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}\quad,\end{split}

(127)

by which
\underline{\underline{{\hat{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},s)
can be expressed as

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{{\hat{\phi}}}}({\underline{q}},s)={\underline{q}}\cdot\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}\quad.\end{split}

(128)

Then we can write the transient part of
\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)
as

\begin{split}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)={\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}\quad,\end{split}

(129)

where we have defined the transient part of the stress correlation tensor,

\begin{split}\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)-\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},0)\quad.\end{split}

(130)

Substituting Eq. (
123
) and Eq. (
129
) into Eq. (
121
), we obtain

\begin{split}\hat{K}({\underline{q}},s)=\quantity[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} ]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} /\rho_0)\quad.\end{split}

(131)

Using the momentum conservation [Eq. (
17
)] and Eq. (
131
), Eq. (
120
) can be written in the Laplace space as

\begin{split}-i\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)}\cdot{\underline{q}}&=-\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{j}}(\vec{q},s)}\\
&=-\quantity[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} ]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{ \Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} /\rho_0)\cdot\expectationvalue{\vec{\hat{j}}(\vec{q},s)}\\
&=-\quantity[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} ]^{-1}\cdot\quantity(i\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s)):\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\gamma}}(\vec{q},s)}\quad.\end{split}

(132)

Using index notation, Eq. (
132
) can be written as

\begin{split}q_{\beta}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}}(\vec{q},s)} -\qty[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q}]^{-1}_{\alpha\xi}\Delta\hat{N}_{\xi\beta\gamma\epsilon}(\vec{q},s)\expval{{\hat{\gamma}_{\gamma\epsilon}}(\vec{q},s)} )=0\quad.\end{split}

(133)

We write Eq. (
133
) using the index-free notation, as given in Eq. (
25
),

\begin{split}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\mat{\sigma}}}(\vec{q},s)} -\qty[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q}]^{-1} \cdot\Delta\Tfor{\hat{N}}(\vec{q},s):\expval{{\hat{\gamma}}(\vec{q},s)} )\cdot{\underline{q}}=0\quad.\end{split}

(134)

D.2
Gauge freedom in momentum density conservation

Our derived expression for the stress response, given in Eq. (
25
), does not uniquely determine the stress tensor. This result contains a fundamental ambiguity which stems from the underlying physical constraint that the stress tensor must satisfy: the conservation of momentum, Eq. (
17
). This ambiguity can be understood as a gauge freedom in the definitions of the stress and momentum density that leaves the form of the conservation law invariant.

Suppose the stress
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\circ}
and the momentum density
{\underline{j}}^{\circ}
satisfy the conservation law,

\begin{split}\partial_{t}{j}^{\circ}_{\alpha}({\underline{q}},t)=-iq_{\beta}\sigma^{\circ}_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},t)\quad.\end{split}

(135)

The form of this law is preserved under the general transformation:

j_{\alpha}({\underline{q}},t)=j^{\circ}_{\alpha}({\underline{q}},t)+\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}({\underline{q}},t)\quad,\quad\sigma_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},t)=\sigma^{\circ}_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},t)+\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},t)\quad,

(136)

where the arbitrary fields
{\underline{\mathcal{A}}}
and
\underline{\underline{\mathcal{B}}}
are linked by the constraint:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}({\underline{q}},t)=-iq_{\beta}\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}({\underline{q}},t)\quad.

(137)

To formulate a well-defined constitutive relation, we must first fix this gauge. We adopt the specific choice where the momentum density is not transformed, i.e.,
{\underline{\mathcal{A}}}=0
. The constraint in Eq. (
137
) then requires the change in the stress tensor,
\underline{\underline{\mathcal{B}}}
, to be divergence-free:
q_{\beta}\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}=0
. With this gauge choice, the constitutive equations are given by Eq. (
27
)-(
28
).

We demonstrate that Eq. (
25
) is gauge invariant.
Since
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\circ}
and
{\underline{j}}^{\circ}
satisfy the momentum density conservation, Eq. (
135
), they satisfy Eq. (
25
),

\displaystyle q_{\beta}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}^\circ}} -\qty[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}^\circ\cdot\vec{q}]^{-1}_{\alpha\xi}\Delta\hat{N}_{\xi\beta\gamma\epsilon}^\circ\expval{{\hat{\gamma}_{\gamma\epsilon}^\circ}} )=0\quad.

(138)

Here
\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\circ}
and
\underline{\underline{\hat{\gamma}}}^{\circ}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\Delta{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}}^{\circ}({\underline{q}},s)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{{\hat{\sigma}}}^\circ(\vec{q},s)\, \mat{\sigma}^{\circ\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{C^{\circ}({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}-\frac{\expectationvalue{\mat{{\hat{\sigma}}}^\circ(\vec{q},0)\, \mat{\sigma}^{\circ\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{C^{\circ}({\underline{q}})/\rho_{0}}\quad;

(139)

\displaystyle C^{\circ}({\underline{q}})\underline{\underline{I}}

\displaystyle\equiv\expectationvalue{\vec{j}^\circ(\vec{q}) \, \vec{j}^{\circ\dagger}(\vec{q})}\quad;\quad\underline{\underline{\hat{\gamma}}}^{\circ}({\underline{q}},s)\equiv-i\,{\underline{q}}\,{\underline{\hat{j}}}^{\circ}({\underline{q}},s)/\rho_{0}\quad.

(140)

Using the transformed
j_{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
, we have the constitutive equation,

\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\sigma}}(\vec{q},s)}=\quantity(\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}(\vec{q},s) \cdot\vec{q} )^{-1}\cdot\Delta\hat{\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s):\expectationvalue{\mat{\hat{\gamma}} (\vec{q},s)}\quad.

(141)

We can directly confirm that Eq. (
25
) is gauge invariant by first contracting Eq. (
141
) with
{\underline{q}}
, and then substituting the transform Eq. (
136
). Since the divergence of the stress is invariant under our chosen gauge transformation, we obtain:

\displaystyle q_{\beta}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}}} -\qty[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}} \cdot\vec{q}]^{-1}_{\alpha\xi}\Delta\hat{N}_{\xi\beta\gamma\epsilon} \expval{{\hat{\gamma}_{\gamma\epsilon}}} )=q_{\beta}\quantity( \expval{{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}^\circ}} -\qty[\mat{I} - \frac{1}{\rho_0 s}\vec{q}\cdot\Delta\hat{\Tfor{N}}^\circ\cdot\vec{q}]^{-1}_{\alpha\xi}\Delta\hat{N}_{\xi\beta\gamma\epsilon}^\circ\expval{{\hat{\gamma}_{\gamma\epsilon}^\circ}} ).

(142)

Since the right-hand side is zero by Eq. (
138
), the left-hand side must also be zero. Therefore, Eq. (
25
) is gauge invariant and
the constitutive relation Eq. (
141
) satisfies Eq. (
25
).

We can also choose a gauge that is different from
{\underline{\mathcal{A}}}=0
. This gauge is fixed by choosing

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\beta}S_{\alpha\beta}\quad,\end{split}

(143)

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}\dot{S}_{\alpha\beta}-\partial_{\gamma}f_{\alpha\beta\gamma}+\partial_{\gamma}f_{\alpha\gamma\beta}+\partial_{\gamma}f_{\beta\gamma\alpha}\quad.\end{split}

(144)

This gauge was analyzed in the context of general relativity by Belinfante
Belinfante 1940
and Rosenfeld
Rosenfeld 1940
and later applied to fluids by Martin et al.
Martin et al. 1972
.
Here
S_{\alpha\beta}
is the spin angular momentum density tensor which is anti-symmetric (
S_{\alpha\beta}=-S_{\beta\alpha}
) and
f_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
is defined using the total angular momentum flux
M_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
as,

\begin{split}f_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\quantity(M_{\alpha\beta\gamma} - r_\beta\sigma^\circ_{\alpha\gamma} + r_\alpha\sigma^\circ_{\beta\gamma} )\quad.\end{split}

(145)

f_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
is anti-symmetric for the exchange of the first two indices:
f_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=-f_{\beta\alpha\gamma}
.

As detailed in the Appendix A of Martin et al.
Martin et al. 1972
the transformation for the stress in Eq. (
144
) is chosen to make the stress tensor symmetric:
{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}={\sigma}_{\beta\alpha}
. This was used for equilibrium fluids to effectively eliminate the spin angular momentum and attribute all angular momentum to its orbital component. However, for non-equilibrium systems like active chiral fluids, the intrinsic spin angular momentum is a crucial physical feature. Therefore, this gauge choice is not suitable for non-equilibrium fluids, which would otherwise conceal the important physics by symmetrizing the stress tensor.

D.3
Green-Kubo relation for viscosity

The Green–Kubo formula for viscosity in equilibrium can be recovered from the generalized viscosity tensor, Eq. (
28
), in the hydrodynamic limit. First, we consider the static momentum density correlation function,

\begin{split}C({\underline{q}})&={\expectationvalue{ \frac{1}{d}\sum_{ij} m_i m_j v_\alpha^i v_\alpha^j e^{i\vec{q} \cdot(\vec{r_i} - \vec{r_j})}}}=\frac{1}{d}{\sum_{i}m_{i}^{2}\expectationvalue{(v^i_\alpha)^2}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}m_{i}m_{j}\expectationvalue{v^i_\alpha v^{j}_\alpha e^{i\vec{q} \cdot(\vec{r_i} - \vec{r_j})}}}\quad.\end{split}

(146)

Here
d
is the spatial dimension.
In a simple equilibrium fluid the positions and velocities are uncorrelated as well as the velocities of distinct particles, therefore

\begin{split}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}m_{i}m_{j}\expectationvalue{v^i_\alpha v^{j}_\alpha e^{i\vec{q} \cdot(\vec{r_i} - \vec{r_j})}}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}m_{i}m_{j}\expectationvalue{v^i_\alpha v^{j}_\alpha}\expectationvalue{ e^{i\vec{q} \cdot(\vec{r_i} - \vec{r_j})}}=0\quad.\end{split}

(147)

Thus, we obtain

\begin{split}C({\underline{q}})&=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}m_{i}^{2}\expectationvalue{(v^i_\alpha)^2}={\sum_{i}m_{i}k_{B}T}={\rho_{0}Vk_{B}T}\quad.\end{split}

(148)

We have used the equipartition theorem.
Therefore, we obtain the following expression for the stress correlation function:

\begin{split}\hat{N}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\epsilon}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{\expectationvalue{\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{q},s) \sigma^*_{\gamma\epsilon}(\vec{q})}}{Vk_{B}T}\quad.\end{split}

(149)

Noting that
\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)=\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}({\underline{q}},s)
in equilibrium, the generalized viscosity tensor, Eq. (
28
), in the hydrodynamic limit (
{\underline{q}}\rightarrow 0
) can be written as:

\begin{split}\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\hat{\eta}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\epsilon}({\underline{q}},s)&=\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\hat{N}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\epsilon}({\underline{q}},s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{N}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\epsilon}({\underline{q}}=0,t)e^{-st}dt\quad.\end{split}

(150)

Taking the limit
s\rightarrow 0
subsequently, we obtain

\begin{split}\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\hat{\eta}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\epsilon}({\underline{q}},s)=\frac{1}{Vk_{B}T}\int_{0}^{\infty}\expectationvalue{{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{q}=0,t) \sigma^*_{\gamma\epsilon}(\vec{q}=0)}dt\quad.\end{split}

(151)

This is the Green–Kubo formula for the viscosity tensor, which relates the transport coefficients to equilibrium time-correlation functions of the stress tensor.

Appendix E
Constitutive equations for chemical reactions

E.1
Kubo formula for chemical potential differences

In equilibrium, we can derive a relationship between the fluctuations of chemical potential differences,
\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q})\, \Delta\vec{\mu}^\dagger(\vec{q})}
, and the susceptibility
\underline{\underline{\kappa}}
. We begin by expanding the free energy functional
\mathcal{F}
to second order around its equilibrium state:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\equiv\int dr^{d}f({\underline{r}})=F_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\int dr^{d}n_{a}({\underline{r}})k_{ab}n_{b}({\underline{r}})\quad,\end{split}

(152)

where

\begin{split}k_{ab}=\frac{\partial^{2}f({\underline{r}})}{\partial n_{a}({\underline{r}})\partial n_{b}({\underline{r}})}\end{split}

(153)

is the local curvature matrix in composition space.
In Fourier space, the quadratic part of the free energy in Eq. (
152
) reads:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\underline{q}}n_{a}({\underline{q}})k_{ab}n_{b}^{*}({\underline{q}})\quad.\end{split}

(154)

Applying the equipartition theorem to each modes, we obtain:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{n_a(\vec{q})n_b^*(\vec{q}) }=k^{-1}_{ab}Vk_{B}T\quad.\end{split}

(155)

The chemical potential is given by
\mu_{a}({\underline{q}})=k_{ab}n_{b}({\underline{q}})
in the quadratic approximation, therefore we find the fluctuations of chemical potential differences as:

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\Delta\mu_I(\vec{q})\Delta\mu^*_J(\vec{q})}=\nu_{a}^{I}\nu_{b}^{J}k_{ac}k_{bd}\expectationvalue{n_c(\vec{q})n_d^*(\vec{q}) }=\kappa_{IJ}Vk_{B}T\quad.\end{split}

(156)

This establishes the static Kubo formula for chemical potential differences in equilibrium.

Appendix F
Constitutive equations for chemically driven complex fluids

F.1
General form of constitutive equations

In this section, we derive the constitutive equation for isotropic, chemically driven active fluids. Again, our aim is to evaluate Eq. (
14
) for a choice of dynamical variables. We consider the set of dynamical variables given in Eq. (
49
):

\begin{split}{\underline{A}}=\quantity(\frac{\vec{j}(\vec{q},t)}{\sqrt{C(\vec{q})}}, \frac{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q},t)}{\sqrt{C_\mu(\vec{q})}})^{\top}.\end{split}

(157)

We consider the non-equilibrium steady state where the chemical driving fuels local activity without generating a macroscopic, directional current. Namely,

\begin{split}\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q})\ \Delta{\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}=\expectationvalue{\Delta\vec{\mu}(\vec{q})\ \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}=0\quad.\end{split}

(158)

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{g}}({\underline{q}})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q})\, \vec{j}^\dagger(\vec{q})}}{C{}({\underline{q}})}&\frac{\expectationvalue{\vec{j}(\vec{q})\, \Delta{\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}}\\

(159)

Next, we evaluate the time-dependent correlation matrix

\begin{split}\underline{\underline{\phi}}({\underline{q}},t)&=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\expectationvalue{\dot{\vec{j}}(\vec{q},t)\, \dot{\vec{j}}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{C({\underline{q}})}&\frac{\expectationvalue{\dot{\vec{j}}(\vec{q},t)\, \Delta\dot{\vec{\mu}}^{\dagger}(\vec{q})}}{\sqrt{C({\underline{q}})C_{\mu}({\underline{q}})}}\\

\\
-\underline{\underline{\underline{N}}}^{R\sigma}({\underline{q}},t)\cdot i{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}&\underline{\underline{N}}^{RR}({\underline{q}},t)/\rho_{0}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(160)

We compute the contraction
\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot{\underline{\hat{A}}}({\underline{q}},s)
, which appears inside
\underline{\underline{\hat{K}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot{{\underline{\hat{A}}}}({\underline{q}},s)
:

\begin{split}\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{\phi}}}({\underline{q}},s)\cdot{\underline{\hat{A}}}({\underline{q}},s)&=\begin{pmatrix}{\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}}^{\sigma\sigma}\cdot{\underline{q}}/\rho_{0}&i{\underline{q}}\cdot\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{\sigma R}/\rho_{0}\\
\\
-\Delta\underline{\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}}^{R\sigma}\cdot i{{\underline{q}}}/\rho_{0}&\Delta\underline{\underline{\hat{N}}}^{RR}/\rho_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\underline{\hat{j}}}/\sqrt{C}\\
\\

F.3
Onsager reciprocity

A fundamental consequence of microscopic time-reversal symmetry is the Onsager reciprocal relations, which state that the matrix of phenomenological coefficients linking thermodynamic fluxes to forces must be related near equilibrium. Here we show that, in equilibrium, the chemo-mechanical coupling coefficients in Eq. (
58
) follows the Onsager reciprocal relation:
\hat{\Lambda}({\underline{q}},s)=-\hat{\Xi}({\underline{q}},s)
. Note that the minus sign stems from the distinct time reversal signatures for the momentum density and chemical potential difference (see Appendix
B.3
).
Using the Kubo formulas in equilibrium, Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
42
), to the cross-coupling coefficients, Eqs. (
60
)-(
61
),
we obtain

(181)

B.3
