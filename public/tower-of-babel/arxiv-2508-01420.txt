The space of Dirac-minimal metrics is connected in dimensions 2 2 and 4 4
Bernd Ammann, Mattias Dahl

Abstract
. Let M M be a closed connected spin manifold. Index theory provides a topological lower bound on the dimension of the kernel of the Dirac operator which depends on the choice of Riemannian metric. Riemannian metrics for which this bound is attained are called Dirac-minimal. We show that the space of Dirac-minimal metrics on M M is connected if M M is of dimension 2 2 or 4 4 .



1.
Introduction

On a closed connected spin manifold
M
, index theory for the Dirac operator, in particular the family Atiyah-Singer index theorem is a strong and interesting tool to determine non-trivial topology of the space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
of Riemannian metrics with positive scalar curvature (psc metrics). However, when the index theoretical information vanishes, it remains a notoriously difficult problem to prove connectedness properties for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
. For
\dim M=3
a recent result by Bamler and Kleiner
[
6
]
building on previous work by Marques
[
19
]
shows that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
is either empty or contractible.

The above mentioned applications of index theory rely on the fact that the Dirac operator for a psc metric is an invertible operator. Thus, the space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
of metrics whose Dirac operator is invertible contains
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
. As a consequence, non-trivial homotopy groups in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
, that are detected by (untwisted) index theoretical methods remain non-trivial in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
. For example, if the index
\alpha(M)\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})
,
m=\dim M
, does not vanish, we have
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)\subset\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)=\emptyset
. Similarly, spectral flow of the Dirac operator, or equivalently a family index theorem, can be used to show that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
have infinitely many components if
m\equiv 3\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,4
,
m\geq 7
, and at least two components, if
m\equiv 0,1\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
. Such results use the fact that
{\operatorname{KO}}_{m+1}(\mathrm{pt})
is non-trivial in these dimensions
m
.

However, if the
{\operatorname{KO}}_{m+1}(\mathrm{pt})
-valued spectral flow between Riemannian metrics
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
is zero, it is difficult to show that
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are in the same connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
. The main result of this article is a proof of the connectedness of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
in the cases
m=2
and
m=4
. Note that this is the first such connectedness result for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
. Our result is in contrast to the setting of metrics with positive scalar curvature in the case
m=4
: Seiberg-Witten theory tells us that on many connected closed oriented
4
-manifolds
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{psc}}(M)
is not connected
[
25
, Section 5 and 6]
.

If the index
\alpha(M)
does not vanish, the space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
is empty, and our setting has to be adapted. Let us recall the Atiyah-Singer index theorem for a closed spin manifold
M
with its extension using Hitchin’s
\alpha
-invariant in
{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})
. Let
\mathcal{R}(M)
be the space of Riemannian metrics on
M
.
For any
g\in\mathcal{R}(M)
we consider the spinor bundle
\Sigma M=M\times_{\rho}\Sigma_{m}
that arises by considering a real irreducible representations
\rho\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}_{m}\to\operatorname{End}(\Sigma_{m})
of the
m
-dimensional real Clifford algebra,
m=\dim M
. Let us summarize some classical facts about such representations, as explained and proven e. g., in
[
17
, Theorem I.5.8]
. In dimensions
m\not\equiv 3\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,4
the choice of irreducible representation is unique up to isomorphism (in the sense of real representations).
The representation
\Sigma_{m}
carries a complex structure, commuting with the
\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}_{m}
-action if
m\equiv 1,2,3,4,5\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
, and this complex structure is unique (up to isomorphisms in the sense of complex representations) for
m\equiv 2,4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
. This implies for
m\equiv 1,2,3,4,5\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
that
\Sigma M
can be obtained from the classical definition of the complex spinor bundle by forgetting the complex structure, and we will identify
\Sigma M
with the classical spinor bundle in this case. In the cases
m\equiv 2,3,4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
there is even a quaternionic structure on
\Sigma_{m}
, again unique up to isomorphisms in the sense of quaternionic representations. Thus
\Sigma M
is a quaternionic vector bundle. We write
\mathbb{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}
for
m\equiv 2,3,4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
,
\mathbb{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}
for
m\equiv 1,5\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
, and
\mathbb{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}
for
m\equiv 0,6,7\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
.

The Dirac operator
\not{D}^{g}
is an unbounded
\mathbb{K}
-linear operator
\Gamma(\Sigma M)\to\Gamma(\Sigma M)
with discrete spectrum. Thus all eigenspaces are (sub-)vector spaces over
\mathbb{K}
.
The
{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})
-valued Atiyah-Singer index theorem says that

\alpha(M)=\begin{cases}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}&\text{if }m\equiv 0\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker\not{D}^{g}\!\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,2\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2&\text{if }m\equiv 1\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}\!\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,2\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2&\text{if }m\equiv 2\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}-\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}&\text{if }m\equiv 4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
0\in{\operatorname{KO}}_{m}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\{0\}&\text{if }m\equiv 3,5,6,7\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\,.\end{cases}

The value of
\alpha(M)
does not depend on the Riemannian metric and is thus a “topological invariant”. More precisely it only depends on the oriented homeomorphism type of
M
for
m\equiv 0,4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
, and it also depends on the differential structure and the spin structure if
m\equiv 1,2\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
, but it is always independent of the Riemannian metric
g
.
In terms of the classical
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
-genus we have
\alpha(M)=\hat{\mathcal{A}}(M)
for
m\equiv 0\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
and
\alpha(M)=2\hat{\mathcal{A}}(M)
for
m\equiv 4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
.

In all cases the index theorem provides a lower bound on
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\ker\not{D}^{g}
.
In fact, let us define

|\alpha(M)|\coloneqq\begin{cases}\bigl|\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}\bigr|&\text{if }m\equiv 0\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
1&\text{if }m\equiv 1,2\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\text{ and }\alpha(M)\neq 0\\
0&\text{if }m\equiv 1,2\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\text{ and }\alpha(M)=0\\
\bigl|\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}-\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}\bigr|&\text{if }m\equiv 4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8\\
0&\text{if }m\equiv 3,5,6,7,\end{cases}

then the Atiyah-Singer index theorem implies
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\ker\not{D}^{g}\geq|\alpha(M)|
.
We define the space

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)\coloneqq\bigl\{g\in\mathcal{R}(M)\mid\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\ker\not{D}^{g}=|\alpha(M)|\bigr\}.

of metrics for which the kernel of the Dirac operator attains this lower bound. In particular
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M)
in the case
\alpha(M)=0
.

From now one let us assume that
M
is connected. Then the set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
is open and dense in
\mathcal{R}(M)
, see
[
2
]
. Metrics in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
are called Dirac-minimal or
\not{D}
-minimal, metrics in the complement
\mathcal{R}(M)\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
are called non-
\not{D}
-minimal.

The main result of this article is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a closed connected spin manifold of dimension
m=2
or
m=4
. Then
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
is connected.

Remarks 1.2
.

(1)

Note that a corresponding statement in many other dimensions does not hold. For example, for
\alpha(M)=0
and
m\equiv 1,3,7,0\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
,

the second author of this article

used the spectral flow for proving that

This proof of disconnectedness cannot be extended to the case

(2)

Consider

-minimal Riemannian metrics exist, if and only if

•

\alpha(M)=0

Recall that Hitchin proved

11

In

It follows from their calculation that for any integer

-minimal metrics exists, if the above conditions are satisfied.

In some cases a more geometric description can be obtained from the theory of minimal surfaces and the spinorial Weierstrass representation. We refer to work by Weiß, Witt and the first author

-minimal.
In the cases

•

\alpha(M)=0

•

\gamma\geq 5

embedded minimal surfaces in flat

.
As the historical origin of this representation had attracted some interest, let us remark here that for the purpose of this remark the original literature by Weierstrass already gives a local description in terms of holomorphic functions which can be easily seen to glue together to a harmonic spinor.

(3)

If

More precisely,

In the remaining part of the introduction we assume

We define the space
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)
as follows. If

we define

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)\coloneqq\{u\cdot g\mid u\in C^{\ell}(M;\mathbb{R}_{>0})\text{ and $g$ is a flat metric}\}

and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)=\emptyset

is not diffeomorphic to

.
The set

is a closed subset of
\mathcal{R}(M)
of infinite codimension, see Proposition
2.3
.

Note that
\ker\not{D}^{g}
is a quaternionic vector space. In the following we write
\omega\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(TM)
for the
real volume element
, i. e., if
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of some

, then
\omega\mathchoice{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.39993pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle x$}}{\lower 2.20001pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 4.49997pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle x$}}{\lower 1.99997pt\hbox{|}\lower 4.10004pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}{\lower 1.79993pt\hbox{|}\lower 3.80005pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}=e_{1}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{m}
. The action of
\omega
on spinors anticommutes with
\not{D}
as
m
is even.

For
m=4
we have
\omega\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\omega=1
and thus the action of
\omega
on spinors induces a decomposition
\Sigma M=\Sigma_{+}M\oplus\Sigma_{-}M
into quaternionic subspaces for the eigenvalues
\pm 1
of the idempotent
\omega
. Be aware that the standard conventions imply that

\Sigma_{+}M
and

\Sigma_{-}M
.
This yields a decomposition

and we have

\hat{\mathcal{A}}(M)/2=\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}-\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}.

Thus, using
|\alpha(M)|\coloneqq|\hat{\mathcal{A}}(M)/2|
we see that for

and

we have
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}=|\alpha(M)|+2d
.

For
m=2
we have
\omega\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\omega=-1
, and thus the map

is however no longer invariant under multiplication with

. In fact, one easily checks that multiplication with

defines antilinear isomorphisms
\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}\to\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}
and
\ker\not{D}^{g}_{-}\to\ker\not{D}^{g}_{+}

and it turns out that

can be identified with the index
\alpha(M)
of

in
{\operatorname{KO}}_{2}(\mathrm{pt})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. We write

if this index is nontrivial and

, such that

.

In both cases
m=2
and

we define

\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)\coloneqq\bigl\{g\in\mathcal{R}(M)\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)\mid\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g}=|\alpha(M)|+2d\bigr\}.

In particular
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{min}}(M)=\mathcal{R}_{0}(M)
and we have the disjoint union

\mathcal{R}(M)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}\amalg\coprod_{d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{R}_{d}(M).

Note that
\mathcal{R}_{\leq d}(M)\coloneqq\bigcup_{0\leq l\leq d}\mathcal{R}_{l}(M)
is an open subset of
\mathcal{R}(M)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
be a closed connected spin manifold of dimension
m=2
or
m=4
. Then for
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d>0
,
\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
has codimension at least
2
in
\mathcal{R}_{\leq d}(M)
. Furthermore
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)
has infinite codimension in
\mathcal{R}(M)
.

Note that we do not expect the codimension estimate for
\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
to be sharp except in the case
m=2
,

and in the case that we have
m=4
,

is “almost” a product of surfaces.
Improved estimates in the other cases shall be the subject of a future article.
However, the codimension
2

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

2.1.
Scalar products on quaternionic vector spaces

See also
[
15
, Chapter 2]
.

We follow the convention that vectors in quaternionic vector spaces are multiplied by quaternionic scalars from right. However we allow real scalars to be multiplied both from the left and from the right.

Let
V
be a quaternionic vector space. An
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear map
g\colon V\times V\to\mathbb{H}
is
\mathbb{H}
-sesquilinear
if we have

g(v\lambda,w)=\bar{\lambda}\cdot g(v,w),\quad g(v,w\lambda)=g(v,w)\lambda.

for every
v,w\in V
and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{H}
. We then also say that
g
is an
\mathbb{H}
-sesquilinear form
.
Such a form is called
\mathbb{H}
-hermitian
if for every
v,w\in V

g(w,v)=\overline{g(v,w)},

(1)

and
positive definite
if for every
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
we have
g(v,v)>0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

A positive definite
\mathbb{H}
-sesquilinear form is
\mathbb{H}
-hermitian.

Proof.

Let
I\in\spann\{i,j,k\}
be one of the complex structures in
\mathbb{H}
, i. e.,
I^{2}=-1
.
Let
\pi_{I}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
be the orthogonal projection with
\pi_{I}(1)=1
,
\pi_{I}(I)=i
.
Then
\pi_{I}\circ g
is a positive definite sesquilinear form on
(V,I)
in the complex sense.
From classical linear algebra over
\mathbb{C}
we know that
\pi_{I}\circ g
is hermitian, i. e., (
1
) is satisfied for
\pi_{I}\circ g
instead of
g
.
Applying this for
I\in\{i,j,k\}
implies (
1
).
∎

An
\mathbb{H}
-scalar product
or a
quaternionic scalar product
is defined as a positive definite
\mathbb{H}
-sesquilinear form.

Example 2.2
.

We write
X^{*}=\overline{X^{T}}=\overline{X}^{T}
for
X\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
. On
\mathbb{H}^{n}
we have the standard scalar product

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=X^{*}Y.

If
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
is a quaternionic scalar product on
V
, then
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle\coloneqq\Re\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
is a real scalar product and it satisfies

\langle{\varphi},\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle{\varphi},\psi\rangle-\langle{\varphi},\psi\cdot i\rangle\cdot i-\langle{\varphi},\psi\cdot j\rangle\cdot j-\langle{\varphi},\psi\cdot k\rangle\cdot k

(2)

for any
{\varphi},\psi\in V
. Further, multiplications by
i
,
j
and
k
are isometries with respect to

\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle
.

Conversely, if
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle
is a real scalar product such that
i
,
j
and
k
are isometries, then we may use (
2
) to define a quaternionic scalar product with
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle=\Re\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
In particular,
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
is
\mathbb{H}
-sequilinear.

Note that in both settings we then also have

\langle{\varphi},\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle{\varphi},\psi\rangle+\langle{\varphi}\cdot i,\psi\rangle\cdot i+\langle{\varphi}\cdot j,\psi\rangle\cdot j+\langle{\varphi}\cdot k,\psi\rangle\cdot k.

Let
V
be a quaternionic line.
For
\psi\in V
with
|\psi|=1
one checks that
(\psi,\psi\cdot i,\psi\cdot j,\psi\cdot k)
is an orthonormal real basis of the quaternionic line
V
. As a consequence we get

\begin{split}|\psi|^{2}{\varphi}&=\psi\cdot\langle\psi,{\varphi}\rangle+\psi\cdot i\langle\psi\cdot i,{\varphi}\rangle+\psi\cdot j\langle\psi\cdot j,{\varphi}\rangle+\psi\cdot k\langle\psi\cdot k,{\varphi}\rangle\\
&=\psi\cdot\langle\psi,{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\end{split}

(3)

for any
{\varphi},\psi\in V
.

2.2.
Spinors and quaternionic structure

We want to recall some facts about spin structures and spinors on oriented manifolds
M
of dimension
m
.

In the literature several equivalent definitions of spin structures may be found.
In differential geometry one often defines a spin structure by considering the principal
{\operatorname{SO}}(m)
-bundle
P_{\operatorname{SO}}(M)
of positively oriented orthonormal frames, and then one defines a spin structure as an equivariant double cover
P_{\operatorname{Spin}}(M)\to P_{\operatorname{SO}}(M)
,
[
12
,
17
]
.
This type of spin structure, called a
metric spin structure
, has the drawback that it depends on the choice of a Riemannian metric
g
.
An alternative way is to consider the non-trivial double cover
\widetilde{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}(m,\mathbb{R})\to{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}(m,\mathbb{R})
instead and to define a spin structure as an equivariant double cover
P_{\widetilde{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}}(M)\to P_{{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}}(M)
, where
P_{{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}}(M)
is the principal
{\operatorname{GL}}_{+}(m,\mathbb{R})
-bundle of positively oriented frames. This type of definition allows us to define a spin structure on an oriented manifold, in a way independent of a Riemannian metric, and will be called a
topological spin structure
or later simply a
spin structure
.
It is classically known since many decades that there is a 1-to-1 relation between isomorphism classes of metric spin structures and isomorphism classes of topological spin structures, but as this occasionally led to misunderstandings, it is helpful that details of this construction were worked out e. g., in
[
26
]
and
[
3
, Sec. 2.2]

Let us also briefly mention that in topology spin structures are often defined as a lift of the classifying map
M\to B{\operatorname{SO}}
for
TM
to a map
M\to B{\operatorname{Spin}}
whose homotopy classes again provide an equivalent definition; this point of view will not be used in this article.

Thus we will assume that
M
carries a (topological) spin structure in this sense, and that it defines – for each Riemannian metric
g
– a spin structure
P_{\operatorname{Spin}}^{g}(M)\to P_{\operatorname{SO}}^{g}(M)
in the usual Riemannian sense. Using a representation

\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)\to{\operatorname{GL}}(V)
on a vector space
V

we define an associated bundle
V^{g}M\coloneqq P_{\operatorname{Spin}}^{g}(M)\times_{\rho}V
.

We will use this construction for spinor bundles, where
V=\Sigma_{m}
is an irreducible linear Clifford module.
An
irreducible linear Clifford module
is a representation
\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)\to{\operatorname{GL}}(\Sigma_{m})
that extends to an irreducible representation of the Clifford algebra
\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
This definition comes in different flavors, in particular one may choose different structures on
\Sigma_{m}
. For example one may consider
\Sigma_{m}
as a real vector space, and then one may show that
\Sigma_{m}
has a scalar product for which Clifford-multiplication is skew and thus
\rho
acts isometrically.
The scalar product on
\Sigma_{m}
is then uniquely determined up to scaling, thus we obtain an equivalent definition if we view
\Sigma_{m}
as a Euclidean vector space and if we consider representation
\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)\to{\operatorname{O}}(\Sigma_{m})
instead. Similarly, connectedness of
{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)
allows to reduce to representations
\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)\to{\operatorname{SO}}(\Sigma_{m})
.

For each representation
\rho\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\operatorname{End}(\Sigma_{m})
the associated bundle construction provides an associated
spinor bundle

\Sigma^{g,\rho}M\coloneqq P^{g}_{\operatorname{Spin}}(M)\times_{\rho}\Sigma_{m}
. The
{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)
-equivariance of the map
\rho
leads to an action of
\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(TM,g)
on
\Sigma^{g,\rho}M
, see again
[
12
,
17
]
for details. For
p\in M
, the action of
X\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(T_{p}M,g)
on
{\varphi}\in\Sigma_{p}^{g,\rho}M
is denoted by
X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,{\varphi}
, and we also use
X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,Y
to denote the multiplication of
X,Y\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(TM,g)
.

For a given Riemannian metric
g
, we obtain the real spinor bundle
\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{R}}M\to M
.
Note that the isomorphism type of the real representation
\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(M)\to{\operatorname{GL}}(\Sigma_{m})
, of real orthogonal representation
\rho\colon{\operatorname{Spin}}(M)\to{\operatorname{O}}(\Sigma_{m})
is not unique for
m\equiv 3\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,4
, but it is unique for our main cases of interest, namely
m\in\{2,4\}
. We refer to
[
17
, I §5]
for a discussion of such representations.

Similarly, we may require that
\Sigma_{m}
is a vector space over the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
or over the quaternionic numbers
\mathbb{H}
,
and we obtain
\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{C}}M
and
\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{H}}M

in the same way by replacing real, bilinear scalar products by complex or quaternionic sesquilinear scalar products.
We denote the result real, complex or quaternionic fiberwise scalar product as
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
,
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
, or
\langle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
By integrating with respect to the Riemannian volume element we obtain for
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}\}

the
\mathbb{K}
-sesquilinear
L^{2}
-scalar product on the space of
L^{2}
-sctions
L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{K}}M)
, denoted by
\llangle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rrangle_{\mathbb{K}}
. In the notation of formulas, we suppress the Riemannian volume element in integrals for the sake of readability.

Now we specialize to the cases
m\equiv 2,4\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,8
in which there is up to isomorphism one irreducible real (resp. complex, resp. quaternionic) representation in
\Sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{m}
(resp.
\Sigma^{\mathbb{C}}_{m}
, resp.
\Sigma^{\mathbb{H}}_{m}
) of
\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, see
[
17
, I §5]
and
[
12
, Section 1.7]
. As these spaces have the same real dimension, there is a complex isomorphism
\Sigma^{\mathbb{H}}_{m}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{C}}_{m}
and a real isomorphism
\Sigma^{\mathbb{C}}_{m}\to\Sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{m}
. When passing to the associated bundles we obtain isomorphisms
\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{H}}M\to\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{C}}M\to\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{R}}M
,
“forgetting” multiplication with
j
and then with
i
, which we use to identify these bundles.

The
real volume element
in the Clifford algebra is defined as
\omega\coloneqq e_{1}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{2}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{m}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(T_{p}M)
, for a positively oriented orthonormal basis
(e_{a})
. For
m
even, the
complex volume element
is defined as
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}\coloneqq i^{m/2}\omega\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}(T_{p}M)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
, for a positively oriented orthonormal basis
(e_{a})
. Because of
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=1
, it defines a grading
\Sigma^{g}_{\mathbb{C}}M=\Sigma^{g}_{+}M\oplus\Sigma^{g}_{-}M
in the sense of complex bundles with scalar product.

For
m=4
, quaternionic multiplication commutes with
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
, and thus this splitting also holds in the sense of quaternionic bundles with
\mathbb{H}
-sesquilinear scalar product. In particular, this implies that
\llangle\Phi,\Psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0
for
\Phi\in\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}_{+}M)
and
\Psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}_{-}M)
.

For
m=2
, quaternionic multiplication by
j
anti-commutes with
\omega_{\mathbb{C}}
; thus
\Sigma^{g}_{+}M
and
\Sigma^{g}_{-}M
are no longer quaternionic bundles, and multiplication by
j
defines complex anti-linear isomorphisms
\Sigma^{g}_{+}M\to\Sigma^{g}_{-}M
and
\Sigma^{g}_{-}M\to\Sigma^{g}_{+}M
. In this dimension
\Phi\in\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}_{+}M)
and
\Psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}_{-}M)
satisfy
\llangle\Phi,\Psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{C}}=0
, but e. g., for
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot j
we have
\llangle\Phi,\Psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\|\Phi\|^{2}\cdot j
.

2.3.
The Dirac operator

The Riemannian metric
g
on
M
gives the Levi–Civita connection
\nabla^{g}
on
TM
, and an associated connection-1-form on
P_{\operatorname{SO}}^{g}(M)
. This connection-1-form lifts to a connection-1-form on any spin structure
P_{\operatorname{Spin}}^{g}(M)
and finally induces a connection, denoted by
\nabla^{g}
as well, on any associated spinor bundle
\Sigma^{g}M
, see
[
17
, II §4]
and
[
12
, Section 3.1]
.

The
Dirac operator

\not{D}^{g}\colon\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}M)\to\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}M)
is defined by

\not{D}^{g}{\varphi}\coloneqq\sum_{a=1}^{n}e_{a}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla^{g}_{e_{a}}{\varphi}

where
(e_{a})
is a locally defined orthonormal frame.
The Dirac operator is a self-adjoint elliptic densely defined unbounded operator
\not{D}^{g}\colon L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}M)\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}M)
.
As such, since
M
is closed, the Dirac operator is a Fredholm operator with unbounded real spectrum consisting of eigenvalues with finite-dimensional eigenspaces, see
[
17
, III §5]
and
[
12
, Chapter 4]
. In particular, the kernel
\ker\not{D}^{g}
is a finite-dimensional vector space. A
harmonic spinor
is an element of
\ker\not{D}^{g}
.

When we use the
C^{1}
-topology on
\mathcal{R}(M)
, any metric has a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
such that the eigenvalues
of
\not{D}^{g}
for
g\in\mathcal{U}
can be numbered by functions that are continuous in
g
, see for example
[
22
]
.

2.4.
Identifying spinor bundles and Dirac operators over spaces of Riemannian metrics

On a spin manifold
M
with a fixed (topological) spin structure,
not only the connection and the Dirac operator but even the construction of the spinor bundle
depends on the choice of a Riemannian metric on
M
in a subtle way. The dependence of the choice of metric was investigated by Bourguignon and Gauduchon in
[
9
]
.
Given two metrics
g,h
, there is an endomorphism
b^{g}_{h}
of
TM

such that
h(b^{g}_{h}\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,b^{g}_{h}\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,)=g(\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,)
, and
b^{g}_{h}
is symmetric
and positive definite (with respect to both metrics).
The endomorphism
b^{g}_{h}
is uniquely determined by these properties and
gives a map
P_{\operatorname{SO}}^{g}(M)\to P_{\operatorname{SO}}^{h}(M)
.
Further, this map lifts to a map
\beta^{g}_{h}\colon P_{\operatorname{Spin}}^{g}(M)\to P_{\operatorname{Spin}}^{h}(M)
between the
{\operatorname{Spin}}(m)
-principal bundles defined by the same topological spin structure.
This, in turn induces linear maps

\beta^{g}_{h}\colon\Sigma^{g}M\to\Sigma^{h}M,\qquad\beta^{g}_{h}\colon\Gamma(\Sigma^{g}M)\to\Gamma(\Sigma^{h}M)

of the associated spinor bundles and their spaces of smooth sections.
The map
\beta^{g}_{h}
is by construction a fiberwise isometry of the Hermitian metrics. We recalibrate this map by considering the volume elements
\,\mathrm{d}V^{g}
and
\,\mathrm{d}V^{h}=(f^{g}_{h})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V^{g}
of
g
and
h
, where
f^{g}_{h}
is a positive function.
Following
[
18
]
we define the rescaled map
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}=(f^{g}_{h})^{-1}\beta^{g}_{h}
,
and we get another vector bundle isomorphism
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}\colon\Sigma^{g}M\to\Sigma^{h}M
, which is not a fiberwise isometry, and an isometry

\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}\colon L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}M)\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{h}M)

of the corresponding Hilbert spaces of
L^{2}
-sections. We have
\beta^{h}_{g}\circ\beta^{g}_{h}=\id
and
\hat{\beta}^{h}_{g}\circ\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}=\id
.
However, one has to be aware of the effect that in general for three Riemannian metrics
g,h,k
we have
\beta^{h}_{k}\circ\beta^{g}_{h}\neq\beta^{g}_{k}
and
\hat{\beta}^{h}_{k}\circ\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}\neq\hat{\beta}^{g}_{k}
, so the identification given by
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}
is not transitive.

Having fixed a Riemannian metric
g_{0}
, the map
g\mapsto\hat{\beta}_{g_{0}}^{g}
defines trivializations
\Sigma^{\univ}_{p}M\coloneqq\coprod_{g\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}_{p}M}\Sigma^{g}_{p}M\to\Sigma^{g_{0}}_{p}M\times\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}_{p}M
for any
p\in M
and
L\coloneqq\coprod_{g\in\mathcal{R}(M)}L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}M)\to L^{2}(\Sigma^{g_{0}}M)\times\mathcal{R}(M)
, and the latter map is a fiberwise isometry of Hilbert spaces from the bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
to the trivial
L^{2}(\Sigma^{g_{0}}M)
-bundle over
\mathcal{R}(M)
.
Because of the lacking transitivity for
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}
, this identification depends on the choice of
g_{0}
. This way to trivialize the Hilbert bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
is worked out by Maier
[
18
]
, relying on
[
9
]
.

For understanding the results of this article, it is sufficient to work in Maier’s trivialization.
However, let us briefly sketch in the next subsection a more conceptual picture,
which may turn out to be important when considering, for instance,
higher order terms in a perturbative expansion.

2.5.
The universal spinor bundle

In this subsection we describe a natural connection
\hat{\nabla}
on the Hilbert bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
. As the concepts of this sections are not required for the logical structure of the article, we will only sketch this connection. However this connection will motivate our notation used later on.

In the following, let
\Sigma^{g}M\to M
be the real, complex or quaternionic spinor bundle over a Riemannian spin manifold
(M,g)
.
Note that for
p\in M
, the fiber
\Sigma^{g}_{p}M
, viewed as a Clifford module with compatible scalar product only depends on
g_{p}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}_{p}M
and thus one may define the
universal spinor bundle

\Sigma^{\univ}M\coloneqq\coprod_{p\in M}\coprod_{g\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}_{p}M}\Sigma^{g}_{p}M\to\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}M\,,

see
[
4
,
5
,
20
]
for details.
This bundle carries a natural partial connection, where “partial” means that only differentiation along the fibers of the bundle
\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}M\to M
is defined. Now, the Hilbert bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
inherits a connection
\nabla
from the partial connection on
\Sigma^{\univ}M
. This connection is tightly related to the maps
\beta^{g}_{h}
discussed above from
[
9
]
.
On the one hand, if we have
{\varphi}\in\Gamma(L)
(i. e.,
{\varphi}_{h}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{h}M)
, depending differentiably on
h\in\mathcal{R}(M)
) and
k\in T_{g}\mathcal{R}(M)\cong\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)
, then we have the formula

\nabla_{k}{\varphi}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}\beta^{g+tk}_{g}{\varphi}_{g+tk}\,.

On the other hand,
\beta^{g}_{h}
is the parallel transport along
t\mapsto(1-t)g+th
with respect to
\nabla
.
Note that this parallel transport does
not
preserve the
L^{2}
-scalar product on
L
,
thus
\nabla
is not compatible with the
L^{2}
-metric.

Recall from the preceding subsection, that Maier recalibrated the map
\beta^{g}_{h}
to
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}
in order to preserve the fiberwise
L^{2}
-scalar product
\llangle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rrangle
on
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
.
In the same spirit we define a
recalibrated connection

\hat{\nabla}
on
L
by the formula

\hat{\nabla}_{k}{\varphi}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}\hat{\beta}^{g+tk}_{g}{\varphi}_{g+tk}\,.

This connection also arises from a recalibrated connection on

\Sigma^{\univ}M\to\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}_{+}T^{*}M
.
As
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}
preserves the
L^{2}
-scalar product, the connection
\hat{\nabla}
is compatible with the fiberwise
L^{2}
-metric on
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
, i. e.,

\partial_{k}\llangle{\varphi},\psi\rrangle=\llangle\hat{\nabla}_{k}{\varphi},\psi\rrangle+\llangle{\varphi},\hat{\nabla}_{k}\psi\rrangle

for
{\varphi},\psi\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{g}M)
and
k\in\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)\cong T_{g}\mathcal{R}(M)
.

This construction may be done for real, complex and quaternionic spinor bundles
and we get forgetful isomorphisms

\Sigma^{\univ}_{\mathbb{H}}M\to\Sigma^{\univ}_{\mathbb{C}}M\to\Sigma^{\univ}_{\mathbb{R}}M

by forgetting multiplication with
j
and
i
respectively. In dimension
2
and
4
these are isomorphisms of complex vector bundles and real vector bundles, respectively.

At the end of this excursion on the universal spinor bundle,
let us mention that for a given background metric
g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(M)
we have four connections on the bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
.
The first is defined by the Bourguignon-Gauduchon trivialization
\beta_{g_{0}}^{g}
, the second by Maier’s trivialization
\hat{\beta}_{g_{0}}^{g}
, the third and the fourth are the connections
\nabla
and
\hat{\nabla}
.
We will prove a transversality statement for a section of a related bundle over
\mathcal{R}(M)
at a metric
g_{0}
where the section vanishes.
This transversality statement is unaffected by the choice of connection.

2.6.
Variation of the Dirac operator with respect to the metric

From now we specialize to dimension
m=\dim M\in\{2,4\}
for which the spinor bundles are quaternionic line bundles.

For the bundle
L\to\mathcal{R}(M)
defined above, let
\operatorname{End}(L)\to\mathcal{R}(M)
be the bundle of densely defined fiberwise
\mathcal{H}
-linear operators.
The Dirac operator
\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\colon g\mapsto\not{D}^{g}
is a section of this bundle, and due to its symmetries it is even a section of the subbundle
\operatorname{End}_{\omega\text{-ac}}^{\text{SA}}(L)\to\mathcal{R}(M)
of self-adjoint operators anti-commuting with the real volume element
\omega
.

It is a classical fact, see
[
18
, Prop 2.4]
for a reference, that
\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}
is differentiable in the sense that we may derive
\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}
with respect to the induced connection
\hat{\nabla}
in any direction
k\in T_{g_{0}}\mathcal{R}(M)\cong\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)
, and the derivative is given by

\frac{d}{dt}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t=0$}}\left(\hat{\beta}^{g_{0}+tk}_{g_{0}}\circ\not{D}^{g_{0}+tk}\circ\hat{\beta}_{g_{0}+tk}^{g_{0}}\right)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{m}k(e_{a},e_{b})e_{a}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla^{g_{0}}_{e_{b}}-\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\operatorname{div}^{g_{0}}k\right)\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\,,

where
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{m})
is a locally defined orthonormal frame, for
k\in\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)
.
In view of the interpretation of
\hat{\beta}^{g}_{h}
as the parallel transport for the connection
\hat{\nabla}
we write the left hand side of this equation as

\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}\Bigr)(k)
,
so we obtain

\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g=g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}\Bigr)(k)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{m}k(e_{a},e_{b})e_{a}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla^{g_{0}}_{e_{b}}-\tfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{div}^{g_{0}}k\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\,.

(4)

The locally defined orthonormal frame
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{m})
allows taking traces by contraction, e. g., for
A\in\operatorname{End}(TM)
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^{1}(M)
we have

\tr(A)=\sum_{a=1}^{m}g(A(e_{a}),e_{a}),\quad\tr^{g}(\alpha\otimes\beta)=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\alpha(e_{a})\beta(e_{a})\,.

In particular, the right hand side does not depend on the choice of the frame. However, the right hand side of these equations is only defined on the domain of the corresponding frame. In the following we abuse notation by interpreting the formulas of the right hand sense as formulas on
M
.
The above expression then means that for any
p\in M
and any frame
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{m})
defined on an open neighborhood
U_{p}
of
p
, the formula holds on
U_{p}
. Obviously, in order to check this, it is sufficient to prove that for any
p\in M
this holds for some frame defined on some
U_{p}\ni p
.

2.7.
Codimension of conformally flat metrics

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
M
be a manifold of dimension at least 4. Then the set of locally conformally flat metrics has infinite codimension in
\mathcal{R}(M)
.

Proof.

In dimensions at least 4, a Riemannian metric is locally conformally flat if and only if its Weyl curvature tensor vanishes identically.
This fact, which is usually formulated for smooth metrics, easily extends to metrics of regularity
C^{\ell}
for
\ell\geq 2
, an even holds in some weak sense for
\ell=1
.

Let
g^{0}
be locally conformally flat. Let
p\in M
. In a neighborhood
U
of
p
there are coordinates
x^{a}
centered at
p
in which
g^{0}_{ab}=u\delta_{ab}
for some positive
C^{\ell}
-function
u
. Let
w_{acdb}
be a constant tensor with all the pointwise symmetries of a Weyl tensor, and set

\gamma^{t}_{ab}\coloneqq\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{3}t\cdot\chi\sum_{c,d=1}^{n}w_{acdb}x^{c}x^{d}

where
\chi
is a cut-off function with support in
U
and
\chi=1
near
p
. Set
g^{t}_{ab}=u\gamma^{t}_{ab}
. Then the
(4,0)
-Weyl curvature tensor of
g^{t}
is
W^{g^{t}}=uW^{\gamma^{t}}
and at the center of coordinates we have
W^{\gamma^{t}}(p)_{acdb}=tw_{acdb}
. Let
\mathcal{W}_{n}
be the dimension of the space of pointwise Weyl tensors in dimension
n
. Then the above construction provides a
\mathcal{W}_{n}
-dimensional space of perturbations
g^{t}
which deform
g^{0}
away from being conformally flat, so the space of locally conformally flat metrics has codimension at least
\mathcal{W}_{n}
. Repeating the argument with any finite number points
p
with disjoint neighborhoods
U
we see that the codimension is unbounded and thus infinite.
∎

3.
Deformations of harmonic spinors

In the preceding section we discussed the variation of the Dirac operator with respect to the Riemannian metric. We will now define the variation of the projection of the Dirac operator to the bundle spanned by small eigenspinors.

3.1.
The kernel projection

Let
M
be a closed connected spin manifold of dimension
m=2
or
m=4
.
Let
g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(M)
have
\dim_{\mathbb{H}}\ker\not{D}^{g_{0}}=|\alpha(M)|+2d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, thus
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker\not{D}^{g_{0}}=2|\alpha(M)|+4d
.
We choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\spec\bigl(\not{D}^{g_{0}}\bigr)\cap(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subset\{0\}
.

Further, we consider an open star-shaped
C^{1}
-neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
in the space of Riemannian metrics with respect to the
C^{1}
-topology,
such that
\varepsilon\notin\spec\bigl(\not{D}^{g}\bigr)
for all
g\in\mathcal{U}
.
We take
\mathcal{U}
small enough, so that for all
g\in\mathcal{U}
the Dirac operator
\not{D}^{g}
has precisely
2|\alpha(M)|+4d
complex eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. For
g\in\mathcal{U}
let

K_{g}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\ker\bigl(\not{D}^{g}-\lambda\bigr)\,.

Then
K\coloneqq\bigcup_{g\in\mathcal{U}}K_{g}
is a quaternionic vector bundle over
\mathcal{U}
of quaternionic rank
|\alpha(M)|+2d
, see e. g.,
[
23
, Theorem 4.5.2]
.
The bundle
K\to\mathcal{U}
is a subbundle of finite rank of the bundle
L\mathchoice{\mbox{\lower 1.49994pt\hbox{{|}}}_{\mathcal{U}}}{\mbox{\lower 1.19995pt\hbox{{|}}}_{\mathcal{U}}}{\mbox{\lower 1.69998pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle|$}}_{\mathcal{U}}}{\mbox{\lower 0.89996pt\hbox{{|}}}_{\mathcal{U}}}\to\mathcal{U}
, introduced in the previous section, and the
L^{2}
-orthonormal projection
L\to K
is given by the spectral projection
\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g}
projection to the sum of eigenspinors of
\not{D}^{g}
with eigenvalues in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.
The
L^{2}
-norm on
L
gives a fiberwise real scalar product
\llangle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rrangle
on
K
that is invariant under multiplication by
a\in\mathbb{H}
with
|a|=1
, and it extends to a quaternionic scalar product
\llangle\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,,\,\raisebox{1.00006pt}{{\scalebox{0.3}{$\bullet$}}}\,\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Be aware that
K
is not a parallel subbundle of
L
, but – as usual – we obtain an induced connection on the bundle
K
by projecting to
K
, i.e.,
\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g}\circ\hat{\nabla}
is the induced connection on
K
. Then the inclusion
\iota_{K}\colon K\to L
is not parallel, its derivative at
g_{0}
, applied to
{\varphi}\in K
is given by

\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\iota\Bigr)\;{\varphi}=\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\varphi}\;\;-\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\varphi}=\pi_{(-\infty,-\varepsilon]\cup[\varepsilon,\infty)}^{g_{0}}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\varphi}\,,

thus, the covariant derivative of
\iota_{K}
at
g_{0}
is a bilinear map from
\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)\times K
to the orthogonal complement of
K
in
L
.

The splitting
\Sigma^{g}M=\Sigma^{g}_{+}M\oplus\Sigma^{g}_{-}M
and the metric
g\in\mathcal{U}
induce a splitting
K=K_{+}\oplus K_{-}
. These summands are quaternionic subbundles for
m=4
, but only complex subbundles for
m=2
. In the case
m=2
multiplication by

maps
K_{\pm}
to
K_{\mp}
.

We now define the bundle
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{U}
by

\mathcal{E}_{g}\coloneqq\operatorname{End}_{\omega\text{-ac}}^{\text{SA}}(K_{g})\,,

that is the fiber
\mathcal{E}_{g}
at
g
is the space of self-adjoint endomorphisms of
K_{g}
that anti-commute with the real volume element
\omega\coloneqq e_{1}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{2}
or
\omega\coloneqq e_{1}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{2}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{3}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,e_{4}
respectively, where
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
is a locally defined positively oriented orthonormal frame.
Then
\mathcal{E}
inherits a connection, also denoted by
\hat{\nabla}
, from the connection on
K
, defined above.
When we restrict the Dirac operator
\not{D}^{g}
for each
g\in\mathcal{U}
to
K_{g}
, we obtain an endomorphism of
K_{g}
, and due to its self-adjointness and its anti-commutativity with the volume element, restricting to
K_{g}
for each

yields a section of
\mathcal{E}
. Thus, for the inclusions
\iota_{K}\colon K\to L
and
\iota_{K}^{g}\colon K_{g}\to L_{g}

\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\mathchoice{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.39993pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle K$}}{\lower 2.20001pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 4.49997pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle K$}}{\lower 1.99997pt\hbox{|}\lower 4.10004pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle K$}}{\lower 1.79993pt\hbox{|}\lower 3.80005pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle K$}}=\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\circ\iota_{K}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{E},\quad g\mapsto\not{D}^{g}\mathchoice{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.39993pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle K_{g}$}}{\lower 2.20001pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 4.49997pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle K_{g}$}}{\lower 1.99997pt\hbox{|}\lower 4.10004pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle K_{g}$}}{\lower 1.79993pt\hbox{|}\lower 3.80005pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle K_{g}$}}=\not{D}^{g}\circ\iota_{K}^{g},

is a section of
\mathcal{E}
. The zero locus of this section is
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
. We denote the covariant linearization at
g_{0}
of this map by

P\coloneqq\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\bigl(\not{D}^{g}\circ\iota_{K}^{g}\bigr)\colon\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M)\to\mathcal{E}_{g_{0}}.

(5)

In order to study the regularity and codimension of the zero level set
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
of

\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}
we will show that

\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\coloneqq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{\mathrm{image}}P\leq 1
at
g_{0}
implies that the metric
g_{0}
is conformal to a flat torus, so otherwise

\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\not{D}^{\,{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\geq 2
which implies that
\operatorname{codim}(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{R}_{d}(M))\geq 2
, unless
g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cf}}(M)
.
This implies that the codimension of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(M)
in
\mathcal{R}(M)
is at least
2
.

3.2.
The energy-momentum tensor

We define
Q_{{\varphi},\psi}\in\Gamma(\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}\nolimits^{2}T^{*}M\otimes\mathbb{H})
by the formula

Q_{{\varphi},\psi}(X,Y)\coloneqq-\tfrac{1}{8}\Bigl(\bigl\langle X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{Y}{\varphi},\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\bigl\langle Y\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{X}{\varphi},\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\bigl\langle{\varphi},X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{Y}\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\bigl\langle{\varphi},Y\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{X}\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Bigr).

For
p\in M
,
X,Y\in T_{p}M
,
{\varphi},\psi\in\ker\not{D}
, and a quaternion
\lambda
we have

Q_{{\varphi}\cdot\lambda,\psi}(X,Y)=\bar{\lambda}\cdot Q_{{\varphi},\psi}(X,Y)\text{ and }Q_{{\varphi},\psi\cdot\lambda}(X,Y)=Q_{{\varphi},\psi}(X,Y)\cdot\lambda.

The tensor
Q_{{\varphi},{\varphi}}
is obtained by differentiating
\int_{M}\langle\not{D}^{g}{\varphi},{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to the Riemannian metric, see
[
7
, Sec. 6]
for details.
Such functionals often arise in action functionals in physics, and the differential is thus called an
energy-momentum tensor
.
For instance, it provides an energy-momentum term in Einstein’s field equations arising from fermions.
We extend this terminology to the unsymmetrized variant
Q_{{\varphi},\psi}
used in this article.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
{\varphi}
and
\psi
are harmonic spinors, then
\tr Q_{{\varphi},\psi}=0
.

Proof.

From the definition of the map
Q
we have

Q_{{\varphi},\psi}(X,X)=-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\langle X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{X}{\varphi},\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\langle{\varphi},X\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{X}\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\bigr).

For an orthonormal basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{m})
of the tangent space we calculate

\tr Q_{{\varphi},\psi}=\sum_{a=1}^{m}Q_{{\varphi},\psi}(e_{a},e_{a})=-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\langle\not{D}{\varphi},\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\langle{\varphi},\not{D}\psi\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\bigr)=0.\qed

3.3.
Variational formula

We will now use the variational formula from Subsection
2.6
in order to express the operator
P
defined in (
5
) in terms of the energy-momentum tensor
Q_{{\varphi},\psi}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
{\varphi},\psi\in\ker\not{D}^{g_{0}}
and

we have

\begin{split}\llangle P(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}&=-\tfrac{1}{8}\int_{M}\sum_{a,b=1}^{m}\Bigl(\bigl\langle e_{a}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{e_{b}}{\varphi},\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\bigl\langle e_{b}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{e_{a}}{\varphi},\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\bigl\langle{\varphi},e_{a}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{e_{b}}\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\bigl\langle{\varphi},e_{b}\,\raisebox{1.79993pt}{{\scalebox{0.5}{$\bullet$}}}\,\nabla_{e_{a}}\psi\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Bigr)\cdot h(e_{a},e_{b})\,\mathrm{d}V^{g_{0}}\\
&=\llangle Q_{{\varphi},\psi},h\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}.\end{split}

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\Bigl(\not{D}^{g}\circ\iota_{K}^{g}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}\Bigr)\circ\iota_{K}^{g_{0}}+\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\not{D}^{g_{0}}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\iota_{k}^{g}

\displaystyle=\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}^{g_{0}}\circ\Bigl(\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}\Bigr)\circ\iota_{K}^{g_{0}}\,.

In the last equality we used the fact that

.
Thus

we obtain

.

Since
{\varphi},\psi\in\ker\not{D}^{g_{0}}
this gives

\llangle P(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\llangle\pi_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\circ\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\llangle\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,.

\begin{split}\llangle P(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\llangle\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}(h){\varphi},\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\llangle{\varphi},\frac{\hat{\nabla}}{dg}\mathchoice{\lower 3.9pt\hbox{\Big|}\lower 7.40005pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.30002pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.60004pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 3.00003pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 6.00006pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}{\lower 2.5pt\hbox{\big|}\lower 5.09995pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle g_{0}$}}\not{D}^{g}(h)\psi\rrangle_{\mathbb{H}}\Bigr)\\

3.4.
Calculations with the energy-momentum tensor

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Z
be a (locally defined) vector field with
