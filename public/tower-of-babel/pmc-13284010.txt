Clinical effect of percutaneous endoscopic visual trephine decompression combined with pedicle screw fixation in the treatment of lumbar vertebral burst fracture with secondary spinal canal stenosis
Authors: Kepeng Li, Bo Jiang, Ye Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the feasibility and preliminary clinical outcomes of percutaneous endoscopic visual trephine decompression (PEVTD) in combination with pedicle screw fixation for the treatment of lumbar burst fractures with secondary spinal stenosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 21 patients with lumbar burst fractures and secondary spinal stenosis treated from December 2022 to December 2024. All patients underwent PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw internal fixation. The duration of endoscopic decompression surgery, blood loss, the incidence of intraoperative dural tearing, the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain score, the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade, and the MRI dural sac cross-sectional area were recorded to assess the effectiveness of decompression.ResultsThe mean operative time for endoscopic decompression was 49.3 ± 10.2 min (41–60 min) with an estimated blood loss of approximately 160 ml. No intraoperative dural tearing occurred. Compared with the preoperative values, the postoperative VAS score and ASIA grade at 1 week and 1 year significantly improved(P < 0.05). The dural sac cross-sectional area increased significantly postoperatively (P < 0.01).ConclusionPEVTD appears to be a feasible minimally invasive technique for treating lumbar burst fractures with secondary spinal canal stenosis in selected patients (those without posterior column injury and with ≤ 50% canal compromise). The preliminary results suggest that PEVTD is a feasible minimally invasive technique with short-term outcomes that appear acceptable in this selected cohort, although these findings are exploratory and require confirmation in prospective comparative studies.Keywords: Percutaneous endoscopy, Visual trephine decompression technique, Lumbar burst fracture, Secondary spinal stenosis, Percutaneous pedicle screw fixation

Background

Lumbar burst fracture is a common type of spinal injury that often results from high-energy trauma. Approximately 30% of patients present with neurological damage, primarily due to the presence of bone fragments in the spinal canal [8]. Early surgical intervention is critical for restoring spinal alignment, decompressing neural structures, and facilitating neurological recovery [9]. Open decompression and internal fixation surgery, while considered the conventional approach for managing such fractures, is associated with significant surgical trauma and substantial intraoperative blood loss, in conflict with the principles of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) [4]. In recent years, with developments in surgical techniques and medical devices, spinal surgery has gradually become less invasive. Percutaneous pedicle screw fixation can effectively reduce paraspinal muscle damage, which is beneficial for early postoperative recovery. However, percutaneous pedicle screw fixation alone cannot expand the spinal canal volume and is therefore suitable for only patients without spinal canal occupation or symptoms of neurological compression. For patients with lumbar burst fractures with associated neurological injuries, open decompression and internal fixation surgery remains the primary treatment option [11].

With the increasing popularity of percutaneous endoscopic techniques, minimally invasive treatment methods for spinal burst fracture-induced secondary spinal canal stenosis have been explored [5, 15, 17, 23]. In recent years, spinal surgeons have focused on the treatment of spinal canal stenosis caused by burst fracture through the combination of percutaneous endoscopic decompression and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation [17]. Unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) techniques have been chosen by some physicians for spinal canal decompression because these techniques reduce iatrogenic damage to the posterior ligamentous complex [22]. With advances in medical devices, the working channel of a single-channel endoscope can be expanded to 1.3 cm, providing a larger space for decompression. The application of visual trephine systems under single-channel endoscopy offers a novel approach for posttraumatic spinal canal decompression in patients with lumbar fracture.

The application of percutaneous endoscopic visual trephine decompression (PEVTD) combined with pedicle screw fixation for the treatment of secondary lumbar spinal canal stenosis following lumbar fracture has not yet been reported. We retrospectively analysed 21 cases of lumbar burst fractures with neurological injury treated in recent years using PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation. We hypothesized that the combination of PEVTD and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation can achieve favourable clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Liangping District People's Hospital (2024KXSC20), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. We reviewed consecutive patients with lumbar burst fractures and secondary spinal canal stenosis treated between December 2022 and December 2024. All patients underwent PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) single-segment lumbar burst fracture with neurological deficits requiring decompression, (2) bone fragments occupying ≤ 50% of the sagittal diameter of the spinal canal, and (3) intact interspinous and supraspinous ligaments (no fusion required). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) osteoporotic or pathological fracture, (2) multisegment fracture, (3) lamina fracture with potential dural rupture, (4) bone fragments occupying > 50% of the sagittal diameter of the spinal canal, (5) ligamentous rupture requiring fusion, and (6) incomplete follow-up data.

Surgical methods

Under general anaesthesia, patients were placed prone with their abdomen free. Percutaneous pedicle screws were inserted into the fractured vertebra and adjacent segments under fluoroscopic guidance. To avoid obstructing the endoscopic working channel, the screws on the decompression side were temporarily left without rod placement.

A 1—cm incision was made at the target interlaminar space. Sequential dilators were inserted, followed by a working cannula (1.3 cm outer diameter). A spinal endoscope was introduced, and soft tissues were cleared using radiofrequency ablation to expose the inferior lamina.

Using a visual trephine under direct visualization, partial inferior laminectomy and medial facetectomy were performed. The ligamentum flavum was resected to expose the dural sac. The tongue-shaped cannula tip was positioned between the dura and lamina for neural protection during bone removal.

For bilateral decompression, the cannula was tilted medially, and the “over-the-top” technique was performed. The visual trephine was used to remove the ventral spinous process base and contralateral lamina until the contralateral dural sac margin was visualized. Adequate decompression was confirmed by dural sac pulsation. Haemostasis was achieved with bone wax and radiofrequency ablation. No drainage tube was placed. On the first postoperative day, patients were allowed to move with lumbar support, and normal activities were resumed after 3 months. The internal fixation devices were removed after 1 year.

Clinical and radiological outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at 1 week and 1yearafter operation. Pain intensity was evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), which ranged from 0 (no pain) to 10 (severe pain). Neurological function was graded according to the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale (grades A–E). Decompression efficacy was quantified by measuring the dural sac cross-sectional area on axial T2-weighted MR images at the fracture level using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Construct stability was assessed by measuring the local kyphosis angle between the superior endplate of the vertebra above the fracture level and the inferior endplate of the vertebra below the fracture level on standing lateral radiographs using the Cobb method; loss of correction was calculated as the difference between the immediate postoperative and final follow-up angles. Intraoperative blood loss was estimated using the Gross formula on the basis of preoperative and postoperative day 1 haematocrit values; preoperative Hct was measured within 24 h prior to surgery, while postoperative Hct was measured on the first postoperative day (POD 1) before intravenous fluid administration. Total intraoperative fluid administration (including crystalloids and colloids) and operative time were recorded for all patients to account for potential haemodilution effects.

Correlation analysis

To evaluate the relationship between radiographic decompression and clinical improvement, we calculated changes in the dural sac cross-sectional area (CSA) (ΔCSA = postoperative CSA – preoperative CSA), VAS score (ΔVAS = preoperative VAS score – postoperative VAS score), and ASIA grade (ΔASIA = postoperative ASIA grade – preoperative ASIA grade). Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the associations between ΔCSA and ΔVAS (at 1 week and 1 year) and between ΔCSA and ΔASIA (at 1 week and 1 year). Correlation coefficients (r) and significance levels (P) were recorded, with P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Statistical methods

The data were analysed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables (VAS score, kyphosis angle, and dural sac area) are presented as means ± SDs and were compared using repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. ASIA grades are presented as medians (IQRs) and were compared using the Friedman test with post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 21 patients who met the inclusion criteria underwent PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation. The cohort included 12 males and 9 females, with a mean age of 51.8 ± 7.6 years (range, 44–60 years). Fractures were at the level of L2 in 10 patients, L3 in 6 patients, and L4 in 5 patients. According to the AO classification, there were 6 cases of type A3 fracture and 15 cases of type A4 fracture. The mean time from injury to surgery was 4.3 ± 3.6 days (range, 2–8 days) (Table 1).

Surgical and perioperative outcomes

The mean operative time for endoscopic decompression was 49.3 ± 10.2 min (range, 41–60 min). The total operative time, including percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, was 90.3 ± 20.4 min. The mean intraoperative fluid volume (crystalloids and colloids) was 425 ± 175 ml. The estimated blood loss ranged from approximately 100 to 225 ml, with a mean of approximately 160 ml.

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 1. The mean VAS score significantly improved from 8.3 ± 1.3 preoperation to 2.7 ± 1.3 at 1 week postoperation and 1.5 ± 0.5 at the 1-year follow-up (F = 58.87, P < 0.05). Post-hoc analysis revealed significant differences in VAS score between all time points (P < 0.05 for each comparison).

Neurological function, assessed by the ASIA grade, significantly improved over time (χ2 = 24.6; P < 0.001). The median ASIA grade improved from 4 (IQR, 3–4) preoperation to 4 (IQR, 4–5) at 1 week and 5 (IQR, 5–5) at 1 year postoperation. Post-hoc Wilcoxon tests with Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.017) demonstrated significant improvements between preoperation and 1 week postoperation (P = 0.002), preoperation and 1 year postoperation (P < 0.001), and 1 week and 1 year postoperation (P = 0.003). At the 1-year follow-up, 15 patients (71.4%) achieved ASIA grade E, and 6 patients (28.6%) achieved ASIA grade D.

Radiological outcomes

The mean dural sac cross-sectional area increased significantly from 73.68 ± 18.36 mm2 preoperation to 167.22 ± 27.73 mm2 postoperation (t = 6.78, P < 0.01), representing a mean increase of 93.54 mm2.

The local kyphosis angle decreased from 15.2° ± 4.3° preoperation to 4.1° ± 1.8° immediately postoperation (P < 0.01). At the 1-year follow-up, the mean kyphosis angle was 5.6° ± 1.9°, with a mean loss of correction of 1.5° ± 0.8° (range, 0.5°–2.8°), indicating the satisfactory maintenance of construct stability (Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis suggested that the change in dural sac cross-sectional area (ΔCSA) was significantly positively correlated with improvements in the VAS score at 1 year (r = 0.54, P = 0.012) and the ASIA grade at 1 year (r = 0.47, P = 0.032). The correlations between ΔCSA and early postoperative outcomes approached significance for the VAS score at 1 week (r = 0.43, P = 0.051) but did not reach significance for the ASIA grade at 1 week (r = 0.36, P = 0.108). Notably, that these correlation analyses were exploratory; the lack of significance at 1 week and the moderate strength of the observed associations (r = 0.47–0.54) preclude definitive conclusions about causality or clinical relevance. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only.

Complications

No intraoperative complications, including dural tearing and nerve root injury, were observed. At the 1-year follow-up, no cases of screw loosening, breakage, or implant failure were detected on radiographs. No postoperative wound infections or neurological deterioration occurred.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation is a technically feasible minimally invasive option for selected patients with lumbar burst fracture and secondary spinal stenosis, with short-term clinical and radiological outcomes that appear promising in this exploratory cohort. Posterior surgical approaches for treating thoracolumbar burst fractures are well established and provide adequate decompression and stabilization while avoiding the morbidity of anterior surgery [2, 3, 10, 13, 16, 19]. The posterior approach preserves physiological lordosis and allows indirect dural sac decompression, reducing the risk of dural tearing associated with direct ventral manipulation [24].

The PEVTD technique combines single-channel endoscopy with a visual trephine, enabling precise “over-the-top” bilateral decompression through a 1—cm incision. In our cohort, the mean decompression time was 49.3 ± 10.2 min, with estimated blood loss of 162.4 ± 61.6 mL and no dural tearing. The cross-sectional area of the dural sac increased significantly from 73.68 ± 18.36 mm2 to 167.22 ± 27.73 mm2 (P < 0.01), confirming adequate decompression. The VAS score improved from 8.3 ± 1.3 preoperation to 1.5 ± 0.5 at 1 year postoperation (P < 0.05), and the ASIA grade significantly improved(P < 0.001). The local kyphosis angle decreased from 15.2° ± 4.3° to 4.1° ± 1.8° postoperation, with minimal loss of correction (1.5° ± 0.8°) at 1 year, indicating satisfactory construct stability. These outcomes align with the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery principles [1, 4, 7, 20], and the postoperative dural sac area exceeded the threshold in mm2 commonly cited for adequate decompression [14].

Correlation analysis suggested an exploratory association between the degree of dural sac expansion (ΔCSA) and long-term improvements in the VAS score (r = 0.54, P = 0.012) and ASIA grade (r = 0.47, P = 0.032). However, these correlations were moderate in strength, did not establish causality, and were not significant at 1 week postoperation (VAS score: r = 0.43, P = 0.051; ASIA grade: r = 0.36, P = 0.108). These findings indicate that early clinical recovery may be influenced by factors other than radiographic decompression alone. Therefore, the findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and prospective validation is required before any clinical conclusions can be drawn [18].

The necessity of direct fragment reduction remains debated. While some studies report adequate spinal canal remodelling with posterior stabilization alone [12, 21], our strict selection criteria (≤ 50% canal compromise, intact posterior ligaments) ensured that patients who were most likely to benefit from indirect reduction were included. For fractures with > 50% compromise or laminar fractures, open surgery with direct decompression remains necessary [6].

Our findings align with recent literature on minimally invasive surgery for the treatment of spinal trauma. Yang et al. conducted a large retrospective cohort study of 231 patients with AO Spine A3 and A4 thoracolumbar fracture and neurological deficits and demonstrated that endoscopic decompression combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation achieves clinical and radiographic outcomes comparable to those of open surgery, with significantly less surgical trauma [23]. While both studies support the feasibility of MISS, the techniques differ in their execution. Yang et al. performed endoscopic decompression via unilateral or bilateral laminectomy using standard instruments (burs and Kerrison punches), with the approach guided by the laterality of symptoms and imaging findings. In contrast, we employed the PEVTD technique, which uses a specialized visual trephine to perform controlled, “over-the-top” bilateral decompression from a unilateral approach. Patient selection also differed: Yang et al. included patients with A3 and A4 fracture with neurological deficits (ASIA A–D), representing a broader cohort with potentially more severe injury, whereas we applied stricter criteria (≤ 50% canal compromise and intact posterior ligamentous complexes). This comparison contextualizes our PEVTD technique within the evolving landscape of MISS approach for spinal trauma.

Key technical considerations for PEVTD include(1) maintaining the trephine depth within 50% of capacity to visualize deeper structures,(2) preserving the deep cortical layer during bone removal, (3) extending decompression to the medial pedicle edge, and (4) positioning the cannula tip between the dura and lamina for neural protection [25].

This study has several limitations. The use of a retrospective single-arm design without a control group precludes definitive conclusions about comparative efficacy. Furthermore, the small sample size (n = 21) and short follow-up (1 year) limit generalizability. Blood loss was estimated indirectly using the Gross formula, which can be influenced by haemodilution. The application of strict inclusion criteria (intact posterior ligaments and ≤ 50% canal compromise) resulted in a highly select population; therefore, our findings cannot be extrapolated to patients with more severe injury. Furthermore, the correlation analyses were exploratory, with only moderate correlation coefficients and nonsignificant findings at early follow-up; the results of these analyses do not demonstrate causality and should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate these preliminary findings.

Conclusion

This study suggests the procedural feasibility of PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for selected patients with lumbar burst fracture and secondary spinal canal stenosis and provides preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence regarding its short-term clinical outcomes. No claim of clinical effectiveness can be made from this retrospective single-arm study. In this small case series, PEVTD combined with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation was associated with an increased dural sac cross-sectional area and improved neurological outcomes, with no observed intraoperative dural tearing. Estimated blood loss, derived indirectly using the Gross formula, ranged from approximately 100 to 225 ml; these values should be interpreted with caution because of the limitations of the estimation method and potential effects of haemodilution. Owing to the retrospective, single-arm design and absence of a comparator group (e.g., open surgery or UBE) in this study, no claims of the superiority of this technique over others can be made. The favourable outcomes observed may be partially attributable to the strict patient selection criteria (intact posterior ligamentous complex, ≤ 50% canal compromise). Therefore, these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation through prospective comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods.
