Artificial intelligence with deep learning driven entropy-curvature attention mechanism for detection and segmentation of skin lesions using biomedical images
Authors: Tawfiq Hasanin, Mona Almofarreh, Hend Khalid Alkahtani, Mashael M. Asiri, Faisal Mohammed Nafie, Mohammed Alahmadi, Adel Albshri, Abdulsamad Ebrahim Yahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In medical image analysis, skin lesion diagnosis remains a complicated task. Skin lesions are a prevalent form of skin disease that exists globally. A malignant skin cancer, otherwise called melanoma, usually starts after melanocyte cells begin to grow uncontrollably. Among several forms of cancer, malignant melanoma is the deadliest form as it has a high vulnerability to spread into deeper tissues and because of its high death rate as well. Early identification of skin tumours is essential to permit advanced treatment. In general, the manual inspection of dermatologists is time-consuming, subjective, and tedious, resulting in dissimilar recognition precision based on their experience. In recent times, many investigators have studied the application of deep learning (DL) methods to skin lesion segmentation. In this study, the DL-driven entropy–curvature attention mechanism for enhanced segmentation and classification of skin lesions (DLECAM-ESCSL) model is proposed in medical imaging techniques. The primary purpose of the DLECAM-ESCSL model is to develop an effective approach for precise skin lesion segmentation to assist in early and reliable skin disease diagnosis using advanced techniques. At first, the image pre-processing step involves various levels, such as image resizing, hair removal, and noise removal, to improve the quality of raw images by eliminating the noise. Furthermore, the attention mechanism-based entropy-curvature (ECA) method is employed for the segmentation process. For the feature extraction process, the DLECAM-ESCSL model utilises the vision transformer (ViT) model to recognise and isolate the most relevant information from raw data. Finally, the Wasserstein autoencoder (WAE) model is used for classification. The performance valuation of the DLECAM-ESCSL approach portrayed a superior accuracy value of 99.16% over existing methods under the Skin Cancer ISIC dataset.

Introduction

Skin cancer is a commonly researched subject because of the increased death rates and confirmed cases. Cancer is deadly if the growth of abnormal cells isn’t managed1. Skin carcinoma is an abnormal development of skin cells that often develops on the skin when exposed to direct ultraviolet rays or sunlight. Skin cancer is a deadly condition that is categorised into two kinds: benign and melanoma. Benign is continually responsive to medications and barely spreads to other cells. Melanoma is a serious kind of skin tumour that originates in the melanocytes2. Melanoma is treated if identified at an initial phase through lesion removal, whereas late identification is linked with a high level of mortality. Therefore, timely identification and correct diagnosis of melanoma are essential for the patient. Histologic analysis of a skin lesion is the best practice for identification and prediction3. However, it is an expensive, invasive, and labour-intensive process. The diagnosis procedure begins with a visual examination of abnormal lesions by examining the patterns of skin lesions. But, even with dermatoscopes, the diagnostic accuracy relates to the knowledge of the dermatologist4. Dermoscopic images created worldwide are presently examined by dermatologists almost thoroughly by visual examination that necessitates higher expertise and focus, is a longer process, and is likely to have subjectivity in interpretation5. Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) is progressively researched to help dermatologists bypass these problems and advance diagnostic precision, efficacy, and fairness6. Detecting the boundaries and locations of lesions comes under Segmentation, whereas diagnosing the categories comes under classification, such as seborrheic keratosis, nevus, melanoma, etc. Various imaging methods, namely confocal microscopy and multi-spectral imaging, are applied to solve the problems faced in detecting melanoma. Though those imaging tools are expensive and large, the dermatologist has to be trained in such imaging modalities7. It is proven that dermoscopic tests by qualified and skilled dermatologists are effective in skin lesion identification. Thus, an automated approach for strong examination of the dermoscopic dataset is useful to doctors.

At present, DL approaches have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in several pattern recognition and medical imaging applications8. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are one of the commonly employed DL methodologies that are influenced by the human visual cortex. CNNs are efficiently implemented for several imaging applications. A CNN configuration is presented to detect salient regions in images, wherein every superpixel is labelled depending on its global as well as local context9. With the swift development of DL models, a lot of scholars have suggested employing Deep CNN for detecting and segmenting skin cancer. The automated CAD system relies on DL techniques, which have provided an opportunity to identify skin disease types more precisely and rapidly10.

In this study, the DL-Driven Entropy–Curvature Attention Mechanism for Enhanced Segmentation and Classification of Skin Lesions (DLECAM-ESCSL) model is proposed in medical imaging techniques. The main contributions of this article are summarised as follows:

Existing works

Ali et al.11focused on enhancing convergence and expediting training by classifying initial-stage skin tumours. Integrating the network-in-network (NIN) and VGG19 frameworks. The remarkable non-linearity of this framework makes extracting features and patterns tasks simpler. Yang and Pan12proposed a skin lesion segmentation method, which attains advanced segmentation precision by integrating current CNN techniques. Initially, this method utilises a Multiscale Residual Block (MRB). Next, the skip connections are improved with a Bidirectional Information Fusion Module (BFM) to improve features by mutually combining semantic data from higher-level spatial data and mapping features from lower-level feature maps. Mustafa et al.13presented an innovative hybrid DL to segment and classify skin lesions. To perform that, a morphology-based method removes hair to boost the segmentation accuracy for the pre-processing phase. Segmentation detaches the lesion from the nearby skin, providing the classification process with a concentration and the capability to remove background noise, which impacts classification precision. Yadav et al.14introduced the application of a new Sailfish-based Gradient Boosting Framework (SbGBF) to recognise and segment the SL region with a higher precision. Next, the sailfish fitness function (FF) is processed to trace the region features in pre-processed SL images, and segmentation is implemented. Thapar et al.15examined skin cancer identification through dermoscopy images, improving experts’ proficiency. The SI paradigm utilised dermoscopy images for locating skin area lesions. The Grasshopper Optimiser Algorithm (GOA) method gives better segmentation results. Xu et al.16developed a two-step encoder, which integrates transformers and CNNs. The feature fusion model in the network incorporates global information and local features, highlights significant channel features by channel attention mechanism (AM), and removes unrelated feature expressions. Alenezi et al.17proposed a multi-task learning methodology for melanoma detection using dermoscopy imaging. Initially, an effective pre-processing method is employed for hair removal and image enhancement processes. Following that, the lesion region was segmented by a VGGNet al.gorithm-driven FCN Layer utilising improved images. At last, a cropping procedure was implemented for the identified cancer. Reddy et al.18recommended a novel architecture by leveraging skin lesion classification processes for the automatic identification of numerous skin disorders. Next, the skin lesion segmentation takes place through optimised U-Net segmentation, wherein the enhancement is achieved by suggesting a hybrid optimiser approach. Deepa and Madhavan19developed an automated framework by utilising an Adaptive Layer-based Visual Transformer with UNet (AL-VTransUNet) methodology for segmentation and a Dilated DenseNet with Multi-Head Attention (DD-MHA) model for classification. The Improved Random Parameter-based Galactic Swarm Optimisation (IRP-GSO) is utilised for tuning.

Mahmoud and Soliman20developed an automated system by employing Adaptive Snake (AS) and Region Growing (RG) algorithms for segmentation, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) for classification. Fatima et al.21reviewed and analysed the DL methods comprising CNN and Transformer-based models, for medical image segmentation across multiple modalities such as MRI, CT, ultrasound, X-ray, dermoscopy, and histopathology. Sarwar et al.22improved skin lesion classification by utilising a Hybrid Residual Network with UNet (ResUNet) for segmentation and feature extraction, incorporated with Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) for tuning. Arshad et al.23improved classification and localisation of skin lesions by employing a network-level fusion of a 5-block Inverted Residual Network (IRN) and a 6-block Inverted Bottleneck Network (IBN). The approach also utilises Bayesian Optimisation (BO) for hyperparameter tuning and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) to improve model interpretability and lesion localisation. Yuan, Zhao, and Agaian24developed an efficient and lightweight model, UCM-Net, by synergising Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP) and CNN. Al-Waisy et al.25proposed an automated framework, Skin-DeepNet, by utilising Mask Region-based CNN (Mask R-CNN) and GrabCut for segmentation, High-Resolution Network (HRNet) with attention blocks for dual-feature extraction, and Deep Belief Networks (DBN) for classification. Claret, Dharmian, and Manokar26improved the accuracy and efficiency of skin cancer classification by utilising CNNs for learning discriminative features and Discrete Wavelet Transformation (DWT) for robust feature extraction from dermoscopic images. Masood, Naseer, and Saeed27developed a model by utilising Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling-based Deeplabv3 (ASPP-Deeplabv3) along with UNET-based models, utilising VGG-16, VGG-19, and DenseNet as encoders. Also, Generative Adversarial Network-based UNET (GAN-UNET) is evaluated to explore the potential of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAN) in generating high-quality segmented lesion images. Gupta et al.28developed an automated expert system, MPXCN-Net, by utilising feature fusion from three pre-trained CNNs, such as MobileNetV2, DarkNet19, and ResNet18, integrated with classifiers such as K-Nearest Neighbour (KNN), SVM, and ensemble methods. Wang et al.29proposed the Global-View Weakly Supervised Network (GloW-VSNet) model to accurately segment vitiligo lesions while preserving spatial continuity. The framework also utilized scribble-guided learning, differentiable feature clustering, and spatial attention mechanisms (SAM). ThangaPurni and Braveen30proposed a hybrid (ARP-ViT-CNN) model by incorporating Angular Radial Partitioning (ARP), CNN, and ViT. Liu et al.31presented the Multiscale Input Fusion Residual Attention Pyramid Convolution Network (MRP-UNet) approach. The study also utilized Multiscale Input Fusion (MIF), Res2Net with Squeeze-and-Excitation (Res2-SE), and Pyramid Dilated Convolution (PDC) models. Akram et al.32proposed a model by employing Mask Region-based Convolutional Neural Network (Mask R-CNN / MRCNN). The study also utilized lesion boundary delineation and Residual Network-50 (ResNet50) for robust lesion detection. Thwin and Park33proposed a method by employing ensemble DL models, integrating U-Net, SegNet, and DeepLabV3 for segmentation, and Visual Geometry Group-16 (VGG16), ResNet-50, and Inception-V3 for classification. Despite crucial improvements, various limitations can be seen in the presented studies, such as high computational cost, reliance on large annotated datasets, and challenges in handling low-contrast or artefact-affected images. Also, suboptimal end-to-end performance can be seen as various models concentrate on either segmentation or classification, and few methods, such as NIN+VGG19 or MRB + BFM, improve convergence or segmentation precision but lack robustness across diverse datasets. Hybrid and optimisation-based methods additionally improve accuracy but are often complex and time-consuming. Transformer-CNN encoders enhance feature integration but require heavy computational resources. The research gap is in developing lightweight, efficient models that can simultaneously handle segmentation and classification while maintaining high accuracy across heterogeneous datasets. Addressing it can improve accessibility and applicability in clinical settings.

The primary purpose of the DLECAM-ESCSL model is to develop an effective approach for accurate skin lesion segmentation to assist in early and reliable skin disease diagnosis using advanced techniques. Image pre-processing technique

At first, the image pre-processing step involves various levels, such as image resizing, hair removal, and noise removal, to improve the quality of raw images by eliminating the noise. Converting the image into a more valuable and advantageous form is called image pre-processing6. The skin images might comprise unnecessary noise, hair, or deformation. This model’s performance depends on the image quality. The skin images required handling to get an improved solution. It enhances the quality of the image for additional processing, improves precision, and decreases complexity. This pre-processing stage contains the succeeding subdivisions, which are explained below:

Image resizing

The images of dissimilar sizes could not always acquire the same feature counts. Then, the input images decrease or increase in dimensions to solve this issue. ECA-based segmentation process

Besides, the ECA method is employed for the segmentation process34. This process efficiently captures the fine-grained lesion boundaries while also conserving structural information. The entropy and curvature information is integrated by this model, which enables it to emphasise regions with high informational content and significant geometric changes. Furthermore, AM additionally improves this by concentrating on the most relevant lesion features, mitigating the influence of background noise and artefacts like hair or illumination variations. Also, the accuracy of delineating irregular and intrinsic lesion shapes is improved compared to standard CNN-based segmentation models. Moreover, ECA exhibits robustness to discrepancies, thus making it appropriate for heterogeneous biomedical image datasets. Its computational efficiency and ability to incorporate local and global context data make it an ideal choice for automated skin lesion analysis.

Proposed a segmentation structure and embedded ECA into this structure that contains a hierarchical decoder and encoder. ECA is applied in every skip connection and encoding layer. Every ECA block includes three components, such as CFA, TSP, and EGA, which are arranged successively to increase inference efficacy and lessen memory consumption while learning richer feature representation.

Tri-axial strip pooling (TSP)

At present, the frequently applied pooling models in NNs consist of spatial pyramid, max pooling, and average pooling. Nevertheless, max pooling concentrates only on important information that can result in complete data loss. Spatial pyramid pooling can process inputs of dissimilar dimensions; however, it raises the processing capability. To primarily decrease memory usage in the segmentation, TSP is presented. Curvature filter attention (CFA)

In the tasks of image segmentation, global and local features are significant. At last, to incorporate local and global data, the mapping feature gained from the three planes is combined, and the output and input of the ECA are designed for a similar size, attaining a plug‐and‐play feature. WAE-based classification model

This model is considered for its ability in learning robust latent representations while reducing the distance between the encoded distribution and a prior distribution. WAE utilises the Wasserstein distance to achieve better alignment between the latent space and the true data distribution, and also improves generalisation on unseen or heterogeneous lesion images. WAE also exhibit efficiency in handling discrepancies in lesion shape, size, and texture, which are common in biomedical datasets. Furthermore, the model enables more reliable classification even with restricted labelled data. Its integration of reconstruction and distribution alignment ensures that subtle features critical for differentiating lesion types are preserved, resulting in higher accuracy and robustness compared to conventional classification methods. Conclusion

In this study, the DLECAM-ESCSL model is proposed for medical imaging techniques. The primary purpose of the DLECAM-ESCSL model is to develop an effective approach for accurate skin lesion segmentation to assist in early and reliable skin disease diagnosis using advanced techniques. At first, the image pre-processing step involves various levels, such as image resizing, hair removal, and noise removal, to improve the quality of raw images by eliminating the noise. Besides, the ECA method is used for the segmentation process. For the extraction of the feature process, the DLECAM-ESCSL approach utilises the ViT model to recognise and isolate the most relevant information from raw data. Finally, the WAE model is employed for the classification process. The performance valuation of the DLECAM-ESCSL approach portrayed a superior accuracy value of 99.16% over existing methods under the Skin Cancer ISIC dataset. The limitations include the dependence on 2D images, which may not capture the full depth and texture of skin lesions. The small dataset size may also restrict the exposure to diverse cases, and the performance may be reduced when applied to images from various sources or with varying lighting conditions. The segmentation and classification quality may affect the manual annotation errors in the dataset. Computational cost for processing high-resolution images remains a threat to real-time applications.
