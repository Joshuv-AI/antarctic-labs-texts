Letter to the Editor: “Association between drinking water type and kidney stone risk in U.S. adults: a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2009–2016 data”
Authors: Haiying Lv, Qinghua Hu, Yue Li
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Qin et al, entitled “Association between drinking water type and kidney stone risk in U.S. adults: a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2009–2016 data”[1]. This cross-sectional study shows an association between tap water consumption and lower kidney stone prevalence, while bottled water shows no significant correlation among U.S. adults. These findings offer valuable insights for public health discussions, though we suggest caution in interpreting them as causal relationships. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the TITAN guidelines[2].

Kidney stones have pathophysiological heterogeneity. For example, uric acid stones form in acidic urine (pH < 5.5), while calcium phosphate stones precipitate in alkaline conditions (pH > 6.5)[3]. Given that both bottled water and regional tap water vary considerably in pH, the same water source may simultaneously reduce risk for one stone type while increasing it for another. However, the NHANES database lacks stone composition data, preventing subtype-specific analysis. Consequently, by treating all kidney stones as a homogeneous outcome, the study may have obscured these opposing associations, potentially explaining the modest overall effect size.

Regarding residual confounding, while the authors have made substantial adjustments for various demographic and lifestyle factors, two potential confounders merit further attention. On one hand, socioeconomic status is likely associated with drinking water choices (e.g., bottled water due to safety or convenience) and, in turn, with kidney stone risk through its impact on diet quality and healthcare access. On the other hand, objective biomarkers such as estimated glomerular filtration rate and serum uric acid are directly related to stone formation and may also influence drinking behavior based on health status or medical advice, contributing to residual confounding[3]. Including these physiological markers in the model would provide a more thorough adjustment for potential confounders.

We appreciate the authors’ focus on mineral content as a plausible mechanism. To further advance this compelling line of inquiry, future studies might benefit from adopting the paradigm of “environmental nephrology”[4]. This approach would enable investigators to simultaneously account for both mineral compositions and non-mineral contaminants. A pertinent example includes disinfection by-products (e.g., trihalomethanes) in tap water, which are associated with chronic kidney disease risk[5]. Conversely, microplastics from bottled water have demonstrated the potential to induce renal oxidative stress and inflammation in preclinical studies[6]. Developing multidimensional “water quality-health” assessment models represents a critical next step for establishing a robust evidence base to inform precise, region-specific public health recommendations.
