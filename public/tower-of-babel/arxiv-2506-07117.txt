Abstract
. The p rimal- d ual h ybrid g radient (PDHG) method is one of the most popular algorithms for solving saddle point problems. However, when applying the PDHG method and its many variants to some real-world models commonly encountered in signal processing, imaging sciences, and statistical learning, there often exists an imbalance between the two subproblems, with the dual subproblem typically being easier to solve than the primal one. In this paper, we propose a flexible t riple- B regman balanced primal- d ual a lgorithm (TBDA) to solve a class of (not necessarily smooth) convex-concave saddle point problems with a bilinear coupling term. Specifically, our TBDA mainly consists of two dual subproblems and one primal subproblem. Moreover, three Bregman proximal terms, each one with an individual Bregman kernel function, are embedded into the respective subproblems. In this way, it effectively enables us to strike a practical balance between the primal and dual subproblems. More interestingly, it provides us a flexible algorithmic framework to understand some existing iterative schemes and to produce customized structure-exploiting algorithms for applications. Theoretically, we first establish the global convergence and ergodic convergence rate of the TBDA under some mild conditions. In particular, our TBDA allows larger step sizes than the PDHG method under appropriate parameter settings. Then, when the requirements on objective functions are further strengthened, we accordingly introduce two improved versions with better convergence rates than the original TBDA. Some numerical experiments on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate that our TBDA performs better than the PDHG method and some other efficient variants in practice.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are concerned with a class of (not necessarily smooth) convex-concave saddle point problems, in which a bilinear term couples the primal and dual variables. Mathematically, the problem under consideration reads as

(1.1)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}~{\mathscr{L}}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle Ax,y\rangle-g(y),

where
\mathbb{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are two nonempty, closed and convex sets,
f:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
and
g:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
are proper closed convex functions,
A:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{Y}
is a bounded linear operator,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the standard inner product with induced Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|\equiv\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}
for vectors. Under certain conditions, (
1.1
) reduces to the well-studied composite optimization problem:

(1.2)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}f(x)+g^{*}(Ax)

where
g^{*}
corresponds to the conjugate function of
g
. Also, it provides an equivalent way to handle the following linearly constrained convex minimization problem:

(1.3)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)\;|\;Ax=b\right\},

where
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a given vector. Therefore, although
we will restrict the later discussion to the case of (
1.1
) with vector variables, all of our results are
applicable to the case with matrix variables. In recent years, we have observed the successful and diverse applications of saddle point problems in the fields of game theory, signal processing, imaging sciences, machine learning, and statistical learning, e.g., see
[
15
,
20
,
21
,
38
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
55
]
, to name a few.

In the literature, one of the most popular solvers for (
1.1
) is the first-order primal-dual algorithm
[
14
,
16
]
, which is also named as the primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) method in the imaging community
[
9
,
21
,
27
,
55
]
. Given the
k
-th iterate
(x^{k},y^{k})
, the iterative scheme of the PDHG method
[
14
]
reads as

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,y^{k}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),

\displaystyle y^{k+1}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}\left\{-g(y)+\langle A\tilde{x}^{k+1},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},

where
\mu>0
and
\gamma>0
are proximal parameters serving as step sizes, and
\sigma\in[0,1]
is an extrapolation parameter. Particularly, (
1
) reduces to the Arrow-Hurwicz Primal-Dual (AHPD) method
[
1
]
when taking
\sigma=0
. Unfortunately, the AHPD method is not necessarily convergent with fixed
\mu
and
\gamma
under the standard convexity conditions of
f
and
g

[
29
]
, even it also has global convergence and sublinear convergence rate under relatively strong assumptions
[
21
,
29
,
30
,
44
,
46
]
. Comparatively, the version with
\sigma=1
requires weaker convergence-guaranteeing conditions than the AHPD method.
Specifically, it was documented in
[
14
,
16
]
that the PDHG method equipped with
\sigma=1
is globally convergent under generic convex conditions as long as

(1.5)

\|A^{\top}A\|<\mu\gamma,

where
\|AA^{\top}\|
is the spectrum of
AA^{\top}
. Actually, as reported in
[
12
,
31
]
, the extrapolation parameter
\sigma
plays a crucial role in weakening the convergence-guaranteeing condition and accelerating the PDHG method. Therefore, He and Yuan
[
31
]
enlarged the range of
\sigma
as
\sigma\in[-1,1]
with the help of an extra correction step. Later, some more improved variants of the PDHG method with relaxed step sizes and extrapolation parameter were developed in recent papers, e.g., see
[
2
,
12
,
17
,
26
,
37
,
39
,
40
,
42
,
53
]
. Furthermore, some researchers introduced dynamic strategy to adjust the step sizes in
[
8
,
10
,
13
,
18
,
43
]
.
As shown in
[
23
]
, when
\mathbb{X}
and/or
\mathbb{Y}
are not the whole spaces, the
x
- and/or
y
-subproblems in (
1
) possibly lose their closed-form solutions. In this case, it usually takes much computing time to find approximate solutions of the subproblems. Therefore, some inexact variants of the PDHG method (e.g., see
[
35
,
36
,
48
,
54
]
) were introduced for the purpose of reducing the computational cost of exactly solving subproblems.

When applying the PDHG method (
1
) and its variants to some specific models (e.g., (
1.3
) and its concrete forms in Section
5
), it is noteworthy that the dual subproblem often is easier than its primal subproblem in the sense that the dual one has lower computational cost. Interestingly, as shown in
[
32
]
, the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM, which is also a primal-dual method) can be greatly improved by using a so-named balancing strategy to increase the computational cost of the dual subproblem (which corresponds to the Lagrangian multiplier).
To some extend, the newly introduced balancing strategy in
[
32
]
can be regarded as one of the “dual stabilization” techniques mentioned in
[
34
]
to speed up primal-dual algorithms for solving (
1.3
). More recently, He et al.
[
33
]
proposed a so-called symmetric primal-dual algorithm (SPIDA) for (
1.1
), where they introduced another type balancing strategy by calculating the dual subproblem twice to achieve a balance between the primal and dual subproblems. Actually, their SPIDA shares the similar idea used in the alternating extragradient method
[
8
,
10
]
and the primal-dual fixed point algorithm
[
19
]
. We can easily observe from the reported experiments in these papers that one more computation on the dual subproblem is also able to improve the performance of primal-dual algorithms on solving (
1.1
) (including (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)). Therefore, these results also encourage us to further design efficient algorithms for (
1.1
), with a focus on balancing the complexity of the primal and dual subproblems.

In this paper, we are particularly interested in (
1.1
) with the case that the dual subproblem is assumed to be easier than the primal one. Accordingly, we design a
t
riple-
B
regman primal-
d
ual
a
lgorithm (TBDA), which is able to exploit the unbalanced structure to achieve a flexible balance between the primal and dual problems. Specifically, we first follow the symmetric spirit used in
[
33
]
to update the dual variable twice, where the first one is only employed in the update of the primal variable. Here, we should emphasize that all subproblems involve Bregman proximal terms and each one has an individual Bregman kernel function, which makes our algorithm flexible to achieve a friendly balance between the primal and dual problems. Under appropriate parameter settings, our TBDA allows larger stepsizes than the PDHG method for the primal and dual subproblem. Furthermore, we incorporate an extrapolation step used in (
1
) to enhance our TBDA, yielding improved numerical performance and a better theoretical convergence rate. In this way, the proposed TBDA enjoys a versatile algorithmic framework to produce some customized algorithms, and in particular to cover the iterative schemes of some classical first-order methods, including the (linearized and balanced) ALM and SPIDA. Under mild conditions, we show theoretically that our TBDA is globally convergence and exhibits an ergodic convergence rate of
O({1}/{N})
, where
N
is the iteration counter. When
f
or/and
g
are further assumed to be strongly convex relative to their Bregman kernel functions, we introduce two improved versions of the original TBDA, and one of them achieves a promising convergence rate of
O(1/\omega^{N})
, where
\omega>1
is a given algorithmic constant. Finally, we apply our algorithm to quadratic optimization problems and RPCA. Some numerical results clearly show that our TBDA works better than the classical PDHG method and some other efficient variants.

This paper is divided into six sections. In Section
2
, we summarize some basic concepts and definitions that will be used in the theoretical analysis. In Section
3
, we first introduce the algorithmic framework of the new TBDA. Then, we discuss the connection between the TBDA and several other first-order algorithms. Under appropriate conditions, we establish some convergence results of the TBDA. In Section
4
, we propose two improved versions of the TBDA under some stronger conditions. To support the idea of this paper, we report some computational results in Section
5
. Finally, we complete this paper with some concluding remarks in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

This section summarizes some notations, definitions, and inequalities that will be used in subsequent analysis.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space. The superscript
\top
denotes the transpose of vectors and matrices. For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote
\|x\|_{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|
as the
\ell_{1}
norm of
x
, where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th component of vector
x
. For a symmetric and positive definite matrix
G
(denoted by
G\succ 0
), we define
\langle x,y\rangle_{G}:=\langle x,Gy\rangle
, where
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Consequently, we further denote
\|x\|_{G}:=\sqrt{\langle x,Gx\rangle}
as the
G
-norm of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Given a matrix
A:=(a_{ij})_{m\times n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote its nuclear norm by
\|A\|_{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{m,n\}}\sigma_{i}(A)
, where
\sigma_{i}(A)
is the
i
-th largest singular value of
A
. Moreover, we use
\|A\|
to represent the spectrum of
A
, which refers to the square root of the maximum eigenvalue of
A^{\top}A
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be an extended real-valued function, and denote the domain of
f
by

\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}:=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\;|\;f(x)<\infty\right\}
. Then, we say that function
f
is

(i)

proper if
f(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}\neq\emptyset
.

(ii)

convex if
f\left(tx+(1-t)y\right)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)
for any
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
and
t\in(0,1)
.

(iii)

\varrho
-strongly convex with a given
\varrho>0
if
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
is convex and the following inequality holds for any
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
and
t\in(0,1)
:

f(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)-\frac{\varrho}{2}t(1-t)\|x-y\|^{2}.

Given a proper closed convex function
f
, the subdifferential of
f
at
x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
is defined by

\partial f(x)=\left\{\;\xi\,|\,f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle y-x,\xi\right\rangle,\,\forall\,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}\;\right\}.

In what follows, we denote
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\;|\;\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\}
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex function, finite at
x
and
y
, and differentiable at
y
. Then, the Bregman distance
[
11
]
between
x
and
y
associated with the kernel function
\phi
is defined as

\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(x)-\phi(y)-\langle\nabla\phi(y),x-y\rangle,

where
\nabla\phi(y)
represents the gradient of
\phi
at
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For simplicity, we here refer the reader to
[
4
,
7
]
for some widely used Bregman kernel functions. It is easy to see that the Bregman distance covers the standard Euclidean distance as its special case when
\phi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
. However, the Bregman distance does not always share the symmetry and the triangle inequality property with the Euclidean distance. Below, we recall two fundamental properties of the Bregman distance
[
6
]
that will be useful in the convergence analysis.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

Let
x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\phi)}
and
a,b\in\mathrm{\bf int}\,{(\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\phi)})}
. Then, we have

(i)

\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(a,b)+\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(b,a)=\langle\nabla\phi(a)-\nabla\phi(b),a-b\rangle
.

(ii)

\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,a)-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,b)-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(b,a)=\langle\nabla\phi(b)-\nabla\phi(a),x-b\rangle
.

Note that
\mathrm{\bf int}\,{(\mathbb{X})}
denotes the interior of
\mathbb{X}
. In the case where
\mathbb{X}
has no interior, we can instead use the relative interior of
\mathbb{X}
.
According to
[
41
]
, we define the strong convexity of
f
relative to
\psi
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable convex function. We say that the function
f
is
\varrho
-strongly convex relative to the function
\psi
on
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)}
, if for any
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf int}\,{(\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)})}
and
\nu\in\partial f(x)
, there exists a scalar
\varrho\geq 0
for which

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nu,y-x\rangle+\varrho\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(y,x).

Given a proper closed convex function
f:\mathbb{X}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, the proximal operator of
f
(see
[
45
,
47
]
), denoted by
\mathrm{\bf prox}_{\lambda f}(\cdot)
, is given by

\mathrm{\bf prox}_{\lambda f}(a)=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|x-a\|^{2}\right\},\quad\lambda>0,\;\;a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

With the definition of Bregman distance, we define the so-named Bregman proximity operator
[
3
]
associated with
\phi
as follows:

\mathrm{\bf Bprox}_{\lambda f}^{\phi}(a)=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,a)\right\},\quad\lambda>0,\;\;a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

which, by denoting
x^{\star}\equiv\mathrm{\bf Bprox}_{\lambda f}^{\phi}(a)
, implies the following important property:

\frac{1}{\lambda}\langle\nabla\phi(x^{\star})-\nabla\phi(a),x-x^{\star}\rangle\geq f(x^{\star})-f(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{X},

or equivalently,

\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,a)-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x,x^{\star})-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(x^{\star},a)\right)\geq f(x^{\star})-f(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{X}.

Below, we present the first-order optimality condition of (
1.1
). The pair
(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
is called a saddle point of (
1.1
) if it satisfies the following inequalities

{\mathscr{L}}(\widehat{x},y)\leq{\mathscr{L}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})\leq{\mathscr{L}}(x,\widehat{y}),\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{X},\;\forall\,y\in\mathbb{Y},

which means that

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{P}(x):={\mathscr{L}}(x,\widehat{y})-{\mathscr{L}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})=f(x)-f(\widehat{x})+\left\langle x-\widehat{x},A^{\top}\widehat{y}\right\rangle\geq 0,\;\;\forall x\in\mathbb{X},\\
&\mathcal{D}(y):={\mathscr{L}}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})-{\mathscr{L}}(\widehat{x},y)=g(y)-g(\widehat{y})+\left\langle y-\widehat{y},-A\widehat{x}\right\rangle\geq 0,\;\;\forall y\in\mathbb{Y}.\end{aligned}\right.

In what follows, the primal–dual gap is defined by

\mathcal{G}(x,y):=\mathcal{P}(x)+\mathcal{D}(y)\geq 0.

It is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
in above are convex functions.

3.
The Triple-Bregman Balanced Primal-Dual Algorithm

In this section, we first introduce the basic framework of the triple-Bregman balanced primal-dual algorithm (TBDA) for (
1.1
). Then, we discuss its connections with some existing first-order algorithms for (
1.1
) and (
1.3
). Finally, we show its global convergence under some mild conditions.

3.1.
Algorithmic framework: TBDA

Recall the assumption that the saddle point problem (
1.1
) has an unbalanced structure in the sense that the primal subproblem is more difficult than the dual one. Therefore, we first follow the ways used in
[
8
,
10
,
33
]
to make a prediction
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
on the dual variable
y
. Then, we update the primal one
x^{k+1}
by absorbing the
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
. Hereafter, we follow the extrapolation step in the PDHG method (
1
) to generate
\bar{x}^{k+1}
for numerical acceleration. Finally, we update the dual variable with the information of
\bar{x}^{k+1}
and
y^{k}
. Note that the three primal and dual subproblems are equipped with Bregman proximal terms, which make our algorithm flexible to balance the computational cost of updating primal and dual variables (see Sections
3.2
and
5
). The details of the algorithm are summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

The Triple-Bregman Balanced Primal-Dual Algorithm for (
1.1
).

1:

Choose starting points
x^{0}\in\mathbb{X}
and
y^{0}\in\mathbb{Y}
, and parameters
\sigma
,
\gamma
,
\mu
and
\tau>0
.

2:

repeat

3:

Compute
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
,
x^{k+1}
,
\bar{x}^{k+1}
and
y^{k+1}
, respectively, via

(3.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}\left\{g(y)-\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\},

(3.2)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k})\right\},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{Y}}\left\{g(y)-\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},y\rangle+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,y^{k})\right\}.

4:

until
some stopping criterion is satisfied.

5:

return
an approximate saddle point
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
.

It has been documented in
[
29
,
30
]
that the AHPD algorithm (i.e., taking
\sigma=0
in (
1
)) is not necessarily convergent in practice. Here, we consider a toy example constructed in
[
29
]
to illustrate the faster behavior of Algorithm
1
than the PDHG method (with setting
\sigma=1
) and SPIDA
[
33
]
. Specifically, we consider the following linear programming problem:

(3.5)

\min_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left\{\;2x_{1}+x_{2}\;|\;x_{1}+x_{2}=1,\;x_{1}\geq 0,\;x_{2}\geq 0\;\right\},

whose the dual form is
\max_{y}\left\{\;y\;|\;y\leq 1,\;y\leq 2\;\right\}
.
It is clear that the optimal solution of (
3.5
) is
(x_{1}^{\star},x_{2}^{\star})=(0,1)
and
y^{\star}=1
is the optimal solution of the dual problem. When applying Algorithm
1
to (
3.5
), by taking parameters
\sigma=1
and
\gamma=\mu=\tau
and setting the Bregman kernel function as
\phi(w)=\psi(w)=\varphi(w)=\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}
for simplicity, the specific iterative schemes of Algorithm
1
become

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\tilde{y}^{k+1}=y^{k}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(x^{k}_{1}+x^{k}_{2}-1\right),\\
x^{k+1}_{1}=\max\left\{\left(-\frac{2}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\tilde{y}^{k+1}+x_{1}^{k}\right),0\right\},\\
x^{k+1}_{2}=\max\left\{\left(-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\tilde{y}^{k+1}+x_{2}^{k}\right),0\right\},\\
\bar{x}^{k+1}_{1}=2x^{k+1}_{1}-x^{k}_{1},\\
\bar{x}^{k+1}_{2}=2x^{k+1}_{2}-x^{k}_{2},\\
y^{k+1}=y^{k}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\bar{x}^{k+1}_{1}+\bar{x}^{k+1}_{2}-1\right).\end{cases}

We can see from (
3.6

\tilde{y}^{k+1}
and

) is simpler than the primal variable

without projection operations (i.e.,

Now, we consider two different groups of proximal parameters to conduct the performance of PDHG, SPIDA, and TBDA. All the algorithms start with

3.2.
Understanding some first-order methods by TBDA

In this subsection, by choosing the Bregman kernel functions and the extrapolation parameter, we demonstrate the versatility of TBDA to produce many variants. Particularly, we focus on applying TBDA to the linearly constrained convex optimization problem (
1.3
) for understanding the iterative schemes of some first-order methods in the framework of our TBDA.

Firstly, it is clear that our TBDA immediately reduces to the SPIDA
[
33
]
by removing the extrapolation step (
3.3
) and taking the same Bregman kernel function in (
3.1
) and (
3.4
). Below, we discuss its connections with some other first-order algorithms for the linearly constrained convex optimization problem (
1.3
). We now formulate (
1.3
) as a saddle point problem:

(3.7)

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}{\mathscr{L}}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle Ax,y\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle,

which is a special case of (
1.1
) with specifying
g(y)=\langle b,y\rangle
. Consequently, applying TBDA to (
3.7
) yields

(3.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{y}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{\langle b,y\rangle-\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\},\\
&x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k})\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{\langle b,y\rangle-\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},y\rangle+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,y^{k})\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Hereafter, we illustrate the versatility of (
3.8
) to produce first-order algorithms for (
1.3
).

3.2.1.
Understanding the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM)

With the iterative scheme (
3.8
), we take the Bregman kernel functions as
\phi(y)=\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{A^{\top}A}^{2}
. Then, the Bregman proximal terms in (
3.8
) immediately are specified as:

\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})=\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,y^{k})=\frac{1}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k})=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|_{A^{\top}A}^{2}.

Consequently, (
3.8
) becomes

(3.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{y}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{\langle b,y\rangle-\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
&x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|_{A^{\top}A}^{2}\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{\langle b,y\rangle-\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},y\rangle+\frac{\tau}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that both
y
-subproblems are unconstrained optimization problems. Therefore, by invoking the first-order optimality conditions, (
3.9
) can be further simplified as

(3.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{y}^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(Ax^{k}-b\right),\\
&x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|Ax-Ax^{k}+\frac{1}{\mu}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\|^{2}\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(A\bar{x}^{k+1}-b\right).\end{aligned}\right.

Observing that
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
essentially serves as an intermediate iterate, we plug it into the update of
x^{k+1}
with setting
\mu=\frac{1}{\gamma}
. Then, (
3.10
) reads as

(3.11)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|Ax-b+\gamma y^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\tau}(A\bar{x}^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

As a result, when taking
\gamma=\tau
and
\sigma=0
in (
3.11
), we obtain the iterative scheme of the standard ALM. Additionally, (
3.11
) produces some other variants by setting
\gamma\neq\tau
and
\sigma\neq 0
.

3.2.2.
Understanding the linearized ALM

With the discussions in Section
3.2.1
, we only change the Bregman kernel function
\psi
in the
x
-subproblem as
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
. Then, we specify (
3.8
) as

(3.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{y}^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k}-b),\\
&x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}+\frac{1}{\mu}A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\|^{2}\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\tau}(A\bar{x}^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

As a consequence, when taking
\gamma=\tau
and
\sigma=0
, the iterative scheme (
3.12
) recovers the linearized ALM. Of course, we can also take
\gamma\neq\tau
and
\sigma\neq 0
to produce some other variants of (
3.12
).

3.2.3.
Understanding the balanced ALM

Unlike the choices discussed in Sections
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
, we take two different Bregman kernel functions for the two
y
-subproblems. Specifically, we set
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
,
\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}_{(AA^{\top}+\kappa I)}
, and
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, where
\kappa>0
and
I
stands for an identity matrix which always automatically
matches the size for operations. Then, we have

(3.13)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{y}^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k}-b),\\
&x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}+\frac{1}{\mu}A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\|^{2}\right\},\\
&\bar{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\sigma(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),\\
&y^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{\langle b,y\rangle-\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},y\rangle+\frac{\tau}{2}\left\|y-y^{k}\right\|^{2}_{(AA^{\top}+\kappa I)}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

where the last
y
-subproblem amounts to

y^{k+1}=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\tau}(AA^{\top}+\kappa I)^{-1}(A\bar{x}^{k+1}-b).

Consequently, by plugging
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
into the update of
x^{k+1}
and taking
\mu=\frac{1}{\gamma}
and
\sigma=\tau=1
, we immediately obtain the balanced ALM introduced in
[
32
]
. Moreover, when taking
\phi(y)=\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}_{(AA^{\top}+\kappa I)}
and
\sigma=0
, we can recover the doubly balanced ALM mentioned in
[
33
]
. Also, we can produce some extrapolated variants with
\sigma\neq 0
.

3.2.4.
Understanding ALM-based multi-block splitting methods

In recent years, the multi-block structure frequently appears in those models arising from imaging sciences and statistical learning. Here, we consider a multi-block form of the saddle point problem (
1.1
), i.e.,

(3.14)

\min_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{X}_{i}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(x_{i})+\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i},y\right\rangle-g(y)\right\},

where each
f_{i}:\mathbb{X}_{i}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
is a proper closed convex function and
A_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n_{i}}
is a given matrix. In particular, when
g(y)=\langle b,y\rangle
, the problem (
3.14
) is equivalent to the multi-block linearly constrained convex optimization:

(3.15)

\min_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{X}_{i}}\left\{\;\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(x_{i})\;\Big|\;\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}=b\;\right\}.

By denoting
{\bm{x}}:=(x_{1}^{\top},x_{2}^{\top},\ldots,x_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
,
{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(x_{i})
,
{\bm{A}}:=[A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{p}]
, and
\mathbb{X}:=\mathbb{X}_{1}\times\mathbb{X}_{2}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{X}_{p}
, we can rewrite (
3.14
) as the following compact form:

(3.16)

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{X}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}{\mathscr{L}}({\bm{x}},y):={\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})+\left\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},y\right\rangle-g(y).

Consequently, applying Algorithm
1
to (
3.16
) and using the separability of objective function, we have

(3.17)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{g(y)-\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k},y\right\rangle+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\},\\
x_{i}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{X}_{i}}\left\{f(x_{i})+\left\langle A_{i}x_{i},\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{i}}(x_{i},x_{i}^{k})\right\},\;i=1,\ldots,p,\\
\bar{x}_{i}^{k+1}&=x_{i}^{k+1}+\sigma(x_{i}^{k+1}-x_{i}^{k}),\;i=1,\ldots,p,\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{g(y)-\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}\bar{x}_{i}^{k+1},y\right\rangle+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,y^{k})\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

It is noteworthy that all
x_{i}
-subproblems are separable. Then, we can implement (
3.17
) in a parallel way. When setting
\phi(y)=\varphi(y)
and
\sigma=0
, it has been shown in
[
33
]
that (
3.17
) recovers the iterative schemes of many ALM-based multi-block splitting methods, e.g.,
[
22
,
24
,
25
,
28
,
52
]
. More generally, we can take
\phi(y)\neq\varphi(y)
and
\sigma\neq 0
to generate more implementable variants for (
3.15
). In Section
5
, we will demonstrate the extrapolated step
\bar{x}_{i}^{k+1}
with
\sigma\neq 0
is able to improve the performance of (
3.17
).

3.3.
Convergence analysis

In this subsection, we demonstrate that the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
converges to a solution of (
1.1
) under relatively mild conditions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{(\hat{x},\hat{y})\}
be a saddle point of (
1.1
), and let
\{(\tilde{y}^{k},x^{k},y^{k})\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Then, we have

(3.18)

\displaystyle\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})+\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(\sigma+1)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\mu\sigma\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\;\;\left.+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-(1+\sigma)\left\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle\right).

Proof.

First, by invoking the first-order optimality conditions of (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), it holds, for all
y\in\mathbb{Y}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
, that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma\left(\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k})\right)-Ax^{k},y-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(y),

(3.20)

\displaystyle\left\langle\tau\left(\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k})\right)-A\bar{x}^{k+1},y-y^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq g(y^{k+1})-g(y),

(3.21)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu\left(\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k})\right)+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1},x-x^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq f(x^{k+1})-f(x).

Due to the arbitrariness of
y
in (
3.19
) and (
3.20
), we let
y:=y^{k+1}
in (
3.19
) and
y:=\hat{y}
in (
3.20
), respectively. Then, we arrive at

(3.22)

\left\langle\gamma\left(\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k})\right)-Ax^{k},y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(y^{k+1})

and

(3.23)

\left\langle\tau\left(\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k})\right)-A\bar{x}^{k+1},\hat{y}-y^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq g(y^{k+1})-g(\hat{y}).

By adding (
3.22
) and (
3.23
), we have

(3.24)

\displaystyle\gamma\left\langle\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle+\tau\left\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k}),\hat{y}-y^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\left\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},\hat{y}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle A(x^{k}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(\hat{y}).

Similarly, by the arbitrariness of
x
in (
3.21
),
we take
x:=\hat{x}
and let
x:=x^{k}
with multiplying both sides by
\sigma
. Then, we have

(3.25)

\left\langle\mu(\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}))+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1},\hat{x}-x^{k+1}\right\rangle\geq f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})

and

(3.26)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu\left(\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k})\right)+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1},\sigma(x^{k}-x^{k+1})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 184.9429pt\geq\sigma\left(f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{k})\right).

Combining (
3.24
), (
3.25
) and (
3.26
), we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle\gamma\langle\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\tau\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k}),\hat{y}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\hat{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle+\langle A(x^{k}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\sigma(x^{k}-x^{k+1})\rangle-\langle A\bar{x}^{k+1},\hat{y}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1},\hat{x}-\bar{x}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(\hat{y})+f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})+\sigma\left(f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{k})\right).

Note that

\langle A(\hat{x}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-\hat{y}\rangle-\langle\hat{x}-\bar{x}^{k+1},A^{\top}(\tilde{y}^{k+1}-\hat{y})\rangle=0,

which, together with (
3.27
), leads to

(3.28)

\displaystyle\gamma\langle\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\tau\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k}),\hat{y}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\hat{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle+\langle A(x^{k}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\sigma(x^{k}-x^{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle\geq f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})+\sigma\left(f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{k})\right)+\langle A^{\top}\hat{y},\bar{x}^{k+1}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(\hat{y})-\langle A\hat{x},\tilde{y}^{k+1}-\hat{y}\rangle.

By invoking the definitions of
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
in (
2.1
), the right-hand side of (
3.28
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})+\sigma\left(f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{k})\right)+\langle A^{\top}\hat{y},\bar{x}^{k+1}-\hat{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(\hat{y})-\langle A\hat{x},\tilde{y}^{k+1}-\hat{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma\left[\left(f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})+\langle A^{\top}\hat{y},x^{k+1}-\hat{x}\rangle\right)-\left(f(x^{k})-f(\hat{x})+\langle A^{\top}\hat{y},x^{k}-\hat{x}\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+f(x^{k+1})-f(\hat{x})+\langle A^{\top}\hat{y},x^{k+1}-\hat{x}\rangle+g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(\hat{y})-\langle A\hat{x},\tilde{y}^{k+1}-\hat{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})+\sigma(\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\mathcal{P}(x^{k}))+\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1}),

which, together with the three-point inequality of Bregman distance and (
3.28
), implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})+\sigma(\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\mathcal{P}(x^{k}))

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\langle\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\tau\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k}),\hat{y}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\hat{x}-x^{k+1}\rangle+\langle A(x^{k}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),\sigma(x^{k}-x^{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\gamma(\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k}))

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\left(\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mu(\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k}))

\displaystyle\quad+\langle A(x^{k}-\bar{x}^{k+1}),\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\rangle-\mu\sigma\left(\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})+\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\tau(\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1}))-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\left(\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})\right)-\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})-\sigma\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\sigma)\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right),

which, by further rearranging terms, concludes that

\displaystyle\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})+\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(\sigma+1)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\mu\sigma\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})-(1+\sigma)\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\{(\hat{x},\hat{y})\}
be a saddle point of (
1.1
) and
\{(\tilde{y}^{k},x^{k},y^{k})\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. For given
\gamma
,
\mu
,
\tau>0
, if there exist positive constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}>0
such that the following inequality

\displaystyle\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\mu\sigma\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})-(1+\sigma)\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})

(3.29)

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+c_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})+c_{3}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k}).

Then the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}
is bounded in
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
, and all its cluster points are solutions of the saddle point problem (
1.1
).

Proof.

It first follows from (
3.2
) and Lemma
3.1
that

\displaystyle\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})+\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(\sigma+1)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-c_{1}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})

\displaystyle\quad-c_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-c_{3}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k}),

which can be rewritten as

(3.30)

\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})+{\bm{a}}_{k+1}\leq{\bm{a}}_{k}-{\bm{b}}_{k}

with the notations

(3.31)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\bm{a}}_{k}:=\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k}),\\
&{\bm{b}}_{k}:=c_{1}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+c_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})+c_{3}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k}).\end{aligned}\right.

By recalling the updating schemes of
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
and
x^{k+1}
, it is clear from the definitions of
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
that

\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})\geq 0
and
\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})\geq 0
. Moreover, using the definition of Bregman distance yields
{\bm{a}}_{k}\geq 0
and
{\bm{b}}_{k}\geq 0
. Therefore, it follows from (
3.30
) that

{\bm{a}}_{k+1}\leq{\bm{a}}_{k}-{\bm{b}}_{k}\leq{\bm{a}}_{0}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}{\bm{b}}_{i}\leq{\bm{a}}_{0},

which further implies that

(3.32)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})=0,

and the sequence
\{(x^{k},\tilde{y}^{k},y^{k})\}
is bounded. Moreover,
\{{\bm{a}}_{k}\}
is also a bounded and monotonically decreasing sequence.

Let
\{(x^{k_{j}},\tilde{y}^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})\}
be a subsequence converging to a cluster point
(x^{\infty},y^{\infty},y^{\infty})
.
It follows from (
3.19
) and (
3.21
) that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x)-f(x^{k_{j}+1})+\left\langle\mu\left(\nabla\psi(x^{k_{j}+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k_{j}})\right)+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k_{j}+1},x-x^{k_{j}+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,\\
&g(y)-g(y^{k_{j}+1})+\left\langle\gamma\left(\nabla\phi(y^{k_{j}+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k_{j}})\right)-Ax^{k_{j}+1},y-y^{k_{j}+1}\right\rangle\geq 0\end{aligned}\right.

hold for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
y\in\mathbb{Y}
. By taking
j\rightarrow\infty
in the above inequalities, it holds, for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
y\in\mathbb{Y}
, that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x)-f(x^{\infty})+\left\langle A^{\top}y^{\infty},x-x^{\infty}\right\rangle\geq 0,\\
&g(y)-g(y^{\infty})+\left\langle-Ax^{\infty},y-y^{\infty}\right\rangle\geq 0,\end{aligned}\right.

which, together with the optimality condition in (
2.1
), that
(x^{\infty},y^{\infty})
is a saddle point of (
1.1
). Using the boundedness and monotonically decreasing property of the sequence
\{{\bm{a}}_{k}\}
implies that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\bm{a}}_{k}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\bm{a}}_{k_{j}}=0.

Therefore, we have
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}x^{k}=x^{\infty}
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}y^{k}=y^{\infty}
, which means that the whole sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}
converges to a solution of (
1.1
). The proof is complete.
∎

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu(1+2\sigma)-\alpha}{2}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\{(\hat{x},\hat{y})\}
be a saddle point of (
1.1
), and let
\{(x^{k},\tilde{y}^{k},{y}^{k})\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
from any initial point
(x^{0},y^{0})
. Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
y\in\mathbb{Y}
, it holds that

(3.43)

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right),

where
N
is a positive integer,
\mathbf{x}^{N}:=\frac{1}{\sigma+N}\left(\sigma x^{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}x^{k}\right)
and
\mathbf{y}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{y}^{k}
.

Proof.

Summing up (
3.18
) from
0
to
N-1
, we get

(3.44)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\{\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(1+\sigma)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{N}))+\tau(\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{N}))

\displaystyle\leq\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0}).

Since
\mathcal{P}(x)
and
\mathcal{D}(y)
are convex with respect to
x
and
y
, respectively, it follows from the left-hand side of (
3.44
) and the Jesen inequality that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\{\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(1+\sigma)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\{\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+\sigma(\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\mathcal{P}(x^{k}))+\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})\right\}

\displaystyle=-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{N})+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\{\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k})+\mathcal{P}(x^{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq(\sigma+N)\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\sigma x^{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}x^{k}}{\sigma+N}\right)+N\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{y}^{k}}{N}\right)-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

\displaystyle\geq N\left(\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\sigma x^{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}x^{k}}{\sigma+N}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\tilde{y}^{k}}{N}\right)\right)-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

(3.45)

\displaystyle=N(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N}))-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0}).

Substituting (
3.3

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right).

The proof is complete.

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}(1+\sigma)-\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\sigma\right)\mathcal{P}(x^{k})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\right)\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma x^{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\bar{x}^{k+1}}{\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\right)\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\tilde{y}^{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}}\right)-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)\left(\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\sigma x^{0}+\beta_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}\bar{x}^{k}}{\sigma+\beta_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}\tilde{y}^{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k-1}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

(4.10)

\displaystyle=t_{N}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N}))-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0}).

Consequently, substituting (
4.1
) into (
4.9
) yields

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{t_{N}}\left(\beta_{0}\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Since
\frac{1}{\beta_{k+1}}=\frac{\mu+\varrho_{1}}{\mu}\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}
and
\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}<2
, we can conclude through induction that the value of
\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}
does not change after a finite number of
k
iterations. Thus,
t_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta_{k+1}}=O(N)
. Then, it follow from (
4.3
) that there exists some constant
C>0
such that

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{C}{N},

which means that Algorithm
2
also enjoys
O(1/N)
convergence rate. In this situation, it is possible to obtain a tighter upper bound than Theorem
3.2
under an appropriate setting on the
\beta_{k}
.

) with strongly convex

and and

Below, we establish the similar result to Theorems
3.2
and
4.1
for Algorithm
3
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\{(\hat{x},\hat{y})\}
be a saddle point of (
1.1
), and let
\{(\tilde{y}^{k+1},x^{k+1},{y}^{k+1})\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
3
from any initial point
(x^{0},y^{0})
. If there exist positive constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+(\sigma\mu+\varrho_{1}\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\varrho_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})+\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})

\displaystyle\quad-(1+\sigma)\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+c_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})+c_{3}\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k}).

Then, we have

(4.11)

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{\omega^{N-2}}\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right).

where
N
is a positive integer,

\mathbf{x}^{N}:=\frac{\sigma x^{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}\bar{x}^{k}}{\sigma+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{y}^{N}:=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}\tilde{y}^{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}}

with a given constant
\omega>1
.

Proof.

Similar to (
3.19
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), under the conditions that
f
is
\varrho_{1}
-strongly convex relative to
\psi
and
g
is
\varrho_{2}
-strongly convex relative to
\varphi
, it follows from the iterative schemes of Algorithm
3
that

(4.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\langle\gamma\left(\nabla\phi(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\nabla\phi(y^{k})\right)-Ax^{k},y-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\geq g(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-g(y)+\varrho_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,\tilde{y}^{k+1}),\\
&\langle\tau\left(\nabla\varphi(y^{k+1})-\nabla\varphi(y^{k})\right)-A\bar{x}^{k+1},y-y^{k+1}\rangle\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\geq g(y^{k+1})-g(y)+\varrho_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y,y^{k+1}),\\
&\langle\mu\left(\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k})\right)+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1},x-x^{k+1}\rangle\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\geq f(x^{k+1})-f(x)+\varrho_{1}\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

holds for all
y\in\mathbb{Y}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
. By following the analysis of Lemma
3.1
, it follows from (
4.12
) that

\displaystyle(\tau+\varrho_{2})\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+(\mu+\varrho_{1})\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})+\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(\sigma+1)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle=\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})+\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})-(\sigma\mu+\varrho_{1}\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k},x^{k+1})-\mu(1+\sigma)\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\varrho_{2}\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})+(1+\sigma)\langle A(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k}),

where, by recalling
\mu=\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\omega-1}
and
\tau=\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\omega-1}
, its first two terms imply that

\displaystyle(\tau+\varrho_{2})\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+(\mu+\varrho_{1})\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k+1})\right)

(4.13)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k})\right)+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-(1+\sigma)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1}).

To simplify the notation, we let

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{k}:=\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{k}),\\
&\widetilde{\bm{c}}_{k}:=\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})-\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})-(\sigma+1)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1}).\end{aligned}\right.

As a consequence, by multiplying
\omega^{k}
on both sides of (
4.2
), we immediately have

(4.14)

\omega^{k+1}\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{k+1}\leq\omega^{k}\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{k}+\omega^{k}\widetilde{\bm{c}}_{k}.

Hence, summing up (
4.14
) from
0
to
N-1
, we obtain

(4.15)

\omega^{N}\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{N}-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\widetilde{\bm{c}}_{k}\leq\omega^{0}\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{0}\equiv\widetilde{\bm{a}}_{0}.

Similar to (
3.3
), we get

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\widetilde{\bm{c}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})+\omega^{k}(1+\sigma)\mathcal{P}(x^{k+1})-\omega^{k}\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{k})

\displaystyle=-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})+\frac{1}{\omega^{N-1}}(1+\sigma)\mathcal{P}(x^{N})+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\mathcal{D}(\tilde{y}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(\omega^{k-1}(1+\sigma)-\omega^{k}\sigma\right)\mathcal{P}(x^{k})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sigma+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\right)\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sigma x^{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\bar{x}^{k+1}}{\sigma+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\right)\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}\tilde{y}^{k+1}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega^{k}}\right)-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}\right)\left(\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\sigma x^{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}\bar{x}^{k}}{\sigma+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}}\right)+\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}\tilde{y}^{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega^{k-1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{N}-1}{\omega-1}\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N})\right)-\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0}).

By substituting (
4.2
) into (
4.15
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}^{N})+\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}^{N})

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\omega-1}{\omega^{N}-1}\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right).

By utilizing the fact that
\omega>1
, it is easy to see that

\frac{\omega-1}{\omega^{N}-1}\leq\frac{\omega}{\omega^{N}-1}\leq\frac{\omega}{\omega^{N}-\omega}\leq\frac{1}{\omega^{N-1}-1}\leq\frac{1}{\omega^{N-2}},

which, together with (
4.2
), implies that

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}^{N},\mathbf{y}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{\omega^{N-2}}\left(\tau\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}(\hat{y},y^{0})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\hat{x},x^{0})+\sigma\mathcal{P}(x^{0})\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

5.2.
RPCA

In this subsection, we show the applicability of Algorithm

to multi-block separable convex optimization problems as discussed in Section

. More concretely, we consider the classical RPCA problem, which reads as

(5.2)

\min_{X,Z}\;\left\{\;\|X\|_{*}+\lambda\|Z\|_{1}\;|\;X+Z=H\;\right\},

where
\|X\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm (i.e., the sum of the singular values of
X
) and
\|Z\|_{1}
represents the sum of the absolute value of
Z

H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a given matrix. Obviously, the RPCA model (
5.2
) can be reformulated as the following separable saddle point problem:

(5.3)

\min_{X,Z}\max_{Y}\;\left\{\;{\mathscr{L}}(X,Z,Y)=\|X\|_{*}+\lambda\|Z\|_{1}+\langle X+Z,Y\rangle-\langle H,Y\rangle\;\right\}.

Given the complexity of (
5.3
), in this subsection, we compare TBDA with PDHG and SPIDA while also examining the sensitivity of TBDA to its parameters. In our experiments, we consider some synthetic and real datasets, respectively.

We first conduct the numerical performance of TBDA on synthetic datasets. To generate the data, we let
X^{\star}=UV
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
are independent random matrices whose entries come from Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Clearly, the preset
r
controls the rank of
X^{\star}
to satisfy the low-rank property. Then, to generate the sparse matrix
Z^{\star}
, we first randomly select a support set
\Omega
of size
0.15\times mn
(i.e.,
15\%
non-zero components) so that all elements are independently sampled from a uniform distribution in
[-30,30]
. Finally, we let
H=X^{\star}+Z^{\star}
be the observation matrix. In this way, we can guarantee that
(X^{\star},Z^{\star})
is the optimal solution of (
5.2
). Here, when implementing the SPIDA, we take the Bregman functions
\psi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{F}^{2}
for the
X
- and
Z
-subproblems, and
\phi(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\|Y\|_{F}^{2}
the
Y
-subproblem. In our experiments, we take the same step size for PDHG and SPIDA, i.e.,
(\gamma_{0},\mu_{0})=(\|A\|,\|A\|)
. To investigate the sensitivity of TBDA’s step sizes, we first fix
\sigma=1
and
\tau=\gamma
, then TBDA with
(\gamma,\mu)
=
(p_{1}\gamma_{0},p_{2}\mu_{0})
is denoted by “TBDA
(p_{1},p_{2})
” for simplicity, where we consider three groups of
(p_{1},p_{2})
with
\{(0.91,0.91),(0.83,1.00),(1.00,0.83)\}
. Besides, our experiments employ the following stopping criterion

(5.4)

\text{Tol}:=\frac{\|(X^{k+1},Z^{k+1},Y^{k+1})-(X^{k},Z^{k},Y^{k})\|_{F}}{\|(X^{k},Z^{k},Y^{k})\|_{F}}\leq\epsilon

and set
\epsilon=10^{-5}
for all algorithms. In the numerical simulations, we consider four scenarios on
(m,n)=(256i,512i)
and
r=0.15*256i
(by Matlab script “
r = round(0.15*min(m,n))
”) with
i=1,2,3,4
. All results are summarized in Table
2
, where we report the rank of the recovered low-rank matrix
\hat{X}
(denoted by
\mathrm{rank}(\hat{X})
), the number of nonzero components of the obtained sparse matrix
\hat{Z}
(denoted by
\|\hat{Z}\|_{0}
), and the relative error defined as

\text{Rerr}=\frac{\|\hat{X}+\hat{Z}-X^{\star}-Z^{\star}\|_{F}}{\|X^{\star}+Z^{\star}\|_{F}},

the number of iterations (Iter.), and computing time in seconds (Time). It can be seen from Table

6.
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced a Triple-Bregman balanced primal-Dual Algorithm (TBDA) for solving a class of structured saddle point problems. The proposed TBDA offers a flexible and user-friendly algorithmic framework that not only help us understand the iterative schemes of some augmented Lagrangian-based algorithms, but also enables the design of customized variants with larger step sizes through appropriate choices of Bregman kernel functions and extrapolation parameters. Under relatively strong assumptions on the objective functions, we further developed two improved versions of TBDA. Computational results showed that TBDA outperforms the benchmark PDHG on both synthetic and real datasets, particularly when the dual subproblem is easier to solve than the primal one.
