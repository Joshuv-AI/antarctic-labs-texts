Does freezing impede the growth of random recursive trees? Thanks: Department of Mathematics and Statistics, McGill University, robin.khanfir@mcgill.ca
Anna Brandenberger, Simon Briend, Hannah Cairns, Igor Kortchemski

Abstract
Uniform attachment with freezing is an extension of the classical model of random recursive trees, in which trees are recursively built by attaching new vertices to old ones. In the model of uniform attachment with freezing, vertices are allowed to freeze, in the sense that new vertices cannot be attached to already frozen ones. We study the impact of removing attachment and/or freezing steps on the height of the trees. We show in particular that removing an attachment step can increase the expected height, and that freezing cannot substantially decrease the height of random recursive trees. Our methods are based on coupling arguments.

Introduction

We are interested in the height of random recursive trees built using uniform attachment with freezing, which is a model recently introduced by
Bellin et al. [2]
. The motivation of the model stems from contact tracing in SIR epidemic models. More generally, the impact of freezing on dynamically-built random graph models is a natural mathematical question.
The work of
Bellin et al. [3]

studies scaling limits
in the specific regime where the number of non-frozen vertices roughly evolves as the total number of
vertices to a given power. Based on results of
Bellin et al. [2]
,
Kammerer et al. [7]
completes the study of the height of the infection tree of a SIR epidemic on the complete graph using uniform attachment with freezing.

This model is defined as follows. Given a
choice sequence

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}
, we set
S_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1
and for every
j\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m\mathclose{\rrbracket}
, let

S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mathrm{x}_{i}\,;\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\coloneqq\min\{j\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m\mathclose{\rrbracket}:S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=0\}

(1.1)

with the convention
\min\varnothing=\infty
.
Starting from a unique active vertex, defined to be the root, we recursively build random rooted trees by reading the elements of the sequence one after the other, by applying a “uniform freezing” step when reading
-1
(which amounts to freezing an active vertex chosen uniformly at random) and a “uniform attachment” step when reading
+1
(which amounts to attaching a new vertex to an active vertex chosen uniformly at random). Note that
\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, if finite, represents the first time at which all vertices in the tree are frozen. For every
j\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m\mathclose{\rrbracket}
we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
the random rooted tree built in this fashion after reading the first
j
elements of
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
(see
Section

2.1
for a precise definition) and let
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{T}_{m}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
be the last tree. We denote by
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
the height of
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
and
\mathsf{h}(v)
the height of a vertex
v\in\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
.

Finally, we define

\mathbb{X}_{n}=\bigcup_{m\geq n}\{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}:\#\{i\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m\mathclose{\rrbracket}:\mathrm{x}_{i}=+1\}=n\textrm{ and }\tau(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\geq m\},

so that trees of the form
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
with
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
are all possible
n
-edge trees built by uniform attachment with freezing. To simplify notation, we write
(+1)^{m}=(+1,+1,\ldots,+1)
where
+1
appears
m
times and similarly define
(-1)^{m}
. Also, we write
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}
for the concatenation of
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}
.

In this note, we address a question of
Bellin et al. [2]
on whether
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
always stochastically dominates the height of the uniform attachment tree, also known as the random recursive tree.
Our main result (
Theorem

1.2
below) states that, roughly speaking, the asymptotic order of magnitude of
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
for any
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
is at least
e\log(n)
with high probability, which is the order of magnitude of the height of a random recursive tree
\mathcal{R}_{n}
with
n
edges. This can informally be rephrased by saying that freezing random trees doesn’t asymptotically shrink them.

At first glance this may seem quite obvious in light of the results of
Bellin et al. [3]
: there it is shown (under some technical assumptions) that if the number of active vertices evolves as
n^{\alpha}
, where
n
is the total number of vertices and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then the height of the tree is of order
n^{1-\alpha}
, thus indicating that adding more freezing tends to increase the height. However this is specific to this regime, and not true in general. Indeed, it is
not
true that the height of random recursive tree with
n
edges is stochastically dominated by
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
for any
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
(see
Section

2.2
). More generally, there is no obvious coupling that would exhibit a stochastic ordering when modifying the choice sequence. Indeed, the expected height of the tree can increase under both removal of a freezing step or removal of an attachment step, as emphasized in the following result.

Proposition 1.1
.

The following assertions hold.

(i)

Removing a freezing step immediately followed by an attachment step can increase the expected height of the tree. More precisely, for every
A>0
, there exist
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that for

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(-1,+1)(+1)^{n}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(+1)^{n},

(1.2)

we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]+A
.

In particular, if
\bar{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(+1)^{n+1}
then we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\bar{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]+A
, so removing a freezing step can increase the expected height of the tree.

(ii)

Removing an attachment step immediately followed by a freezing step can increase the expected height of the tree. More precisely, for every
A>0
, there exist
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that for

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(+1,-1)(+1)^{n}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(+1)^{n},

(1.3)

we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]+A
.

(iii)

Removing an attachment step can increase the expected height of the tree. More precisely, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(+1,+1,-1)(+1)^{n}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(+1,-1)(+1)^{n},

(1.4)

we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]
.

Despite the lack of stochastic domination under the above two natural operations on the choice sequence, we are able to show that for any
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}^{n}
,
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
is asymptotically at least of the same order as
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
, so that adding freezing cannot substantially decrease the height of random recursive trees.

For random recursive trees, it is well known (see
Addario-Berry and Ford [1, Corollary 1.3]
and
Pain and Sénizergues [9, Eq. (1.3)]
) that as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})=e\log n-\frac{3}{2}\log\log n+O_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})\right]=e\log n-\frac{3}{2}\log\log n+O(1).

(1.5)

The following statement formalizes our claim that the asymptotic lower bound for
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
matches
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
to leading order.

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

\min_{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\geq e\log(n)-5\log\log n\right)\rightarrow 1.

as
n\to\infty
. Also, for every
n
sufficiently large,

\min_{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]\geq e\log n-5\log\log n.

The key to proving the result is the following. Recall that the random recursive tree with
n
edges corresponds to the sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(+1)^{n}
without any freezing steps, so we can write
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\mathcal{T}((+1)^{n})
. We make use of the following coupling which

does
stochastically decrease the height of the tree.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}
such that
S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})>0
for every
j\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m-1\mathclose{\rrbracket}
. Suppose there exists
k\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m-1\mathclose{\rrbracket}
such that
\mathrm{x}_{1}=\mathrm{x}_{2}=\cdots=\mathrm{x}_{k}=+1
and
\mathrm{x}_{k+1}=-1
. Let
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(\mathrm{x}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{x}_{k-1},\mathrm{x}_{k+2},\dots,\mathrm{x}_{m})
be the sequence obtained from
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
by removing
\mathrm{x}_{k}
and
\mathrm{x}_{k+1}
. Then

\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})).

We establish this stochastic domination via a coupling between the construction of the two random trees, which is relatively general and may be of independent interest.
Using it recursively allows us to compare the heights of a tree with freezing and of a random recursive tree.
The proof of
Theorem

1.3
is based on an alternative construction of uniform attachment trees with freezing of
Bellin et al. [2]
, based on time-reversal, through
a growth-coalescent process of rooted forests.

. In

and

). The proof of

Examples and counter-examples

2.1
The recursive construction of uniform attachment with freezing

Consider a choice sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}
such that
S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})>0
for every
j\in\mathopen{\llbracket}1\mathclose{}\mathpunct{},m-1\mathclose{\rrbracket}
. We now formally construct the random trees
(\mathcal{T}_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))_{0\leq j\leq m}
. These trees will be rooted and vertex-labelled by either “
a
” (if the vertex is active) or “
f
” (if the vertex is frozen).

Algorithm 1.

\bullet

Start with the tree
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
made of a single root vertex labelled
a
.

\bullet

For every
1\leq j\leq m
, let
V_{j}
be a random uniform active vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{j-1}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, chosen independently from all previous ones. Then,

–

if
\mathrm{x}_{j}=-1
, build
\mathcal{T}_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
from
\mathcal{T}_{j-1}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
simply by replacing the label
a
of
V_{j}
with label
f
;

–

if
\mathrm{x}_{j}=1
, build
\mathcal{T}_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
from
\mathcal{T}_{j-1}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
by adding an edge between
V_{j}
and a new vertex labelled
a
.

We let
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{T}_{m}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
be the last tree. Observe that for
0\leq j\leq m
,
S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
represents the number of active vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
.

2.2
The height of the frozen tree does not stochastically dominate the height of the random recursive tree

Here we make the observation that it is not true in general that adding freezing to a random recursive tree stochastically increases the height. Recall that we write
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\mathcal{T}((+1)^{n})
, where
(+1)^{n}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
is the sequence of length
n
without any freezing steps. We also write
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{T}((+1,-1)^{n}))
, where
(+1,-1)^{n}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
is the alternating sequence
(+1,-1,+1,-1,\ldots,+1,-1)
of length
2n
.

Observation 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 3
we have
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})\not\succeq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
.

This simply comes from the fact that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})=1\right)<\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})=1\right)
. Indeed, we clearly have
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})=1\right)={1}/n!
and
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})=1\right)={1}/{2^{n-1}}
.

It is interesting to note that although the probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})=1\right)
is asymptotically much smaller than
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})=1\right)
, the random variable
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
is typically much larger than
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
. Indeed,

\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{A}_{n})\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\frac{1}{\log n}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})\rightarrow e

in probability as
n\to\infty
, respectively by
Bellin et al. [2, Theorem 3 (3)]
and (
1.5
).

2.3
Removing a freezing step immediately followed by an attachment step can increase the expected height

Here we establish
Proposition

1.1
(i). Recall that we take

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(-1,+1)(+1)^{n}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}=(+1)^{m}(-1)^{m-1}(+1)^{n},

(2.1)

so that
\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}
is
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
with a consecutive freezing and attachment step removed.

We first need some technical estimates. Let
(\mathcal{R}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
,
(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
and
(\mathcal{R}_{i}^{2})_{i\geq 0}
be three independent random recursive trees without freezing (independent from all the other random variables), indexed in such a way that
\mathcal{R}_{i}
,
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{i}
and
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{i}
have
i
edges. Finally, let
I_{n}
be an independent random variable uniformly distributed on
\{0,\ldots,n\}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exist two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the following assertions hold:

(i)

for
n
sufficiently large,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})\geq\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\right)\leq 2c_{1}n^{-\frac{1}{8}}
;

(ii)

for every
n,k\geq 1
,
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{I_{n}})-\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{n-I_{n}})|\geq k\right)\leq 4c_{1}e^{-c_{2}k/4}
.

The proof of
Lemma

2.2
is deferred to the end of
Section

2.3
.

Proof of
Proposition

1.1
(i).

We set
m=\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor
in (
2.1
).

We may first assume that
(\mathcal{T}_{k}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))_{0\leq k\leq 2m-1}=(\mathcal{T}_{k}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))_{0\leq k\leq 2m-1}
. Observe that
\mathcal{T}_{2m-1}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{T}_{2m-1}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}})
is a random recursive tree with
m
edges, where
2
uniform vertices are active and the other
m-1
are frozen. We denote by
U_{m}
and
{V}_{m}
these two active vertices. Without loss of generality, we may assume that in
\mathcal{T}_{2m}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
,
V_{m}
is frozen and
U_{m}
is the only active vertex. Finally, denote by
U^{\prime}_{m}
the new active vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{2m+1}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, which is a child of
U_{m}
.

We can then naturally couple
(\mathcal{T}_{k+2}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))_{2m-1\leq k\leq 2m-1+n}
and
(\mathcal{T}_{k}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))_{2m-1\leq k\leq 2m-1+n}
so that the following holds (see
Figure

1
for an illustration):

–

\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
is obtained by grafting
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}}
on
U_{m}
and
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}
on
U^{\prime}_{m}
,

–

\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}})
is obtained by grafting
\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}}
on
V_{m}
and
\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}
on
U_{m}
.

This follows from the well-known fact that in a random recursive tree with
n+1
edges, when removing the first added edge, the number of edges of the connected component containing the root is uniformly distributed on
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

Figure 1:
An illustration of the coupling of
\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
and
\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}})
in the proof of
Proposition

1.1
(i). The empty vertex is frozen.

This coupling implies that the following equalities in distribution hold jointly:

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\max\Big(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m}),\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+1+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}),\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})\Big),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\max\Big(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m}),\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}),\mathsf{h}(V_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})\Big).

The intuition behind the remainder of the proof is that
\max(\mathsf{h}(U_{m}),\mathsf{h}(V_{m}))
is typically larger than
\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+1
. Formally, we let
\Delta_{n}=\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})-\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}})
. Using the identity
\max(a,b)=a+\max(0,b-a)
, we decompose the maxima by writing

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}})+\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\max(1,\Delta_{n})+\max(0,{Z}_{m,n})

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}})+\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\max\big(0,\mathsf{h}(V_{m})-\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\Delta_{n}\big)+\max\big(0,\hat{Z}_{m,n}\big),

where

\displaystyle Z_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m})-\max\big(\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+1+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}),\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})\big)\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m})-\max\big(\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}),\mathsf{h}(V_{m})+\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})\big).

We claim that both
\mathbb{E}\left[\max(0,{Z}_{m,n})\right]\rightarrow 0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\max(0,\hat{Z}_{m,n})\right]\rightarrow 0
. Indeed, observe that

\max\big(0,{Z}_{m,n}\big)\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}m\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\geq\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\max\big(0,\hat{Z}_{m,n}\big)\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}m\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\geq\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})}.

since
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\leq m
and

\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}\max\big(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{I_{n}}),\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{2}_{n-I_{n}})\big)
.
The fact that
\mathbb{E}\left[\max(0,{Z}_{m,n})\right]\rightarrow 0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\max(0,\hat{Z}_{m,n})\right]\rightarrow 0
then follows from
Lemma

2.2
(i), using
m=\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor
.

Now observe that
\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta_{n}|\right]=O(1)
by
Lemma

2.2
(ii). Using the bounds

\max\Big(0,\mathsf{h}(V_{m})-\mathsf{h}(U_{m})\Big)-|\Delta_{n}|\leq\max\Big(0,\mathsf{h}(V_{m})-\mathsf{h}(U_{m})+\Delta_{n}\Big)\leq\max\Big(0,\mathsf{h}(V_{m})-\mathsf{h}(U_{m})\Big)+|\Delta_{n}|

combined with (
2.3
), it follows
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max\Big(0,\mathsf{h}(V_{m})-\mathsf{h}(U_{m})\Big)\right]+O(1)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\big|\mathsf{h}(U_{m})-\mathsf{h}(V_{m})\big|\right]+O(1),

where the final equality is due to the exchangeability of
(U_{m},V_{m})
.

To conclude, it suffices to check that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\big|\mathsf{h}(U_{m})-\mathsf{h}(V_{m})\big|\right]=+\infty
. This is a direct consequence of
Lodewijks [8, Theorem 2.4]
applied with
k=2
and
a_{1}=a_{2}=0
, which implies that
(\mathsf{h}(U_{m})-\mathsf{h}(V_{m}))/\sqrt{\ln(m)}
converges in distribution to a
\mathcal{N}(0,2)
random variable. This
implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]\to\infty

(2.4)

as
n\to\infty
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

2.2
.

We use
Addario-Berry and Ford [1, Corollary 1.3]
, which gives the existence of a constant
c>0
such that for every
n,k\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{n})\right]\right|\geq k\right)\leq ce^{-\frac{1}{3e}k}.

(2.5)

For the first assertion, we bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})\geq\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\right)
from above by

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})\geq\frac{e}{2}\log(n)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}^{1}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\leq\frac{e}{2}\log(n)\right).

By (
1.5
), for
n
sufficiently large we have

\left\{\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})\geq\frac{e}{2}\log(n)\right\}\subset\left\{\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor n^{1/9}\rfloor})\right]\geq\frac{3e}{8}\log(n)\right\}

and

Proof of

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

For
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
, we distinguish two cases depending on
\max_{1\leq j\leq m}S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, which represents the maximum number of active vertices reached at some point. Set
r_{n}=\lfloor n/(3\log n)\rfloor
for
n
large enough and

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{1}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}:\max_{1\leq j\leq m}S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})\geq r_{n}\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}:\max_{1\leq j\leq m}S_{j}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})<r_{n}\}.

High values.

Take
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\mathbb{X}^{1}_{n}
. By iteratively removing the first consecutive
+1,-1
’s we can transform
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
into a sequence
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}
starting with
r_{n}
times
+1
. By
Theorem

1.3
, this shows that

\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\succeq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{r_{n}}).

(3.1)

Then by (
1.5
) we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]-e\log n\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{r_{n}})\right]-e\log n=e\log\left(\frac{r_{n}}{n}\right)-\frac{3}{2}\log\log r_{n}+O(1)\geq-5\log\log n

for
n
sufficiently large.
Similarly,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\geq e\log(n)-5\log\log n\right)\\
\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{R}_{r_{n}})-e\log r_{n}+\frac{3}{2}\log\log r_{n}\geq e\log(n)-5\log\log n-e\log r_{n}+\frac{3}{2}\log\log r_{n}\right),

which by (
1.5
) goes to
1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
uniformly in
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}^{1}_{n}
, since
e\log(n)-5\log\log n-e\log r_{n}+\frac{3}{2}\log\log r_{n}\rightarrow-\infty
.

Low values.

Take
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathrm{x}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in\mathbb{X}^{2}_{n}
. In this case, we lower bound
\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))
by the height of a uniform active vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{m}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}})
, which by
Bellin et al. [2, Eq. (5)]
has the same law as

\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=1}
,
where
(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
are independent Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(1/S_{i}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))_{1\leq i\leq m}
.
Since
\#\{1\leq i\leq m:\mathrm{x}_{i}=1\}=n
by definition of
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, it follows that

\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(x))\succeq_{\mathrm{st}}\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/r_{n}),

where
\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/r_{n})
is a Binomial random variable with
n
trials and success probability
1/r_{n}
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))\right]-e\log n\geq{n/r_{n}-e\log n\geq}(3-e)\log(n)\geq-5\log\log n.

Next, we use the following concentration inequality for Bernoulli random variables due to Bennett;
see e.g.
Boucheron et al. [4, Theorem 2.9]
. We state it below, tailored for our purpose.

Proposition 3.1

(Bennett’s inequality)
.

Let
(Y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be independent Bernoulli random variables of respective parameters
(p_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Set
m_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
and assume that
m_{n}>0
. Then for every
t>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}>m_{n}+t\right)\leq\exp\left(-m_{n}g\left(\frac{t}{m_{n}}\right)\right)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}<m_{n}-t\right)\leq\exp\left(-m_{n}g\left(\frac{t}{m_{n}}\right)\right),

where
g(u)=(1+u)\ln(1+u)-u
for
u>0
.

Hence, setting
s_{n}=n/r_{n}
, Bennett’s inequality gives that for every
t>0
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/r_{n})<s_{n}-t\right)\leq\exp(-s_{n}g(t/s_{n}))
with
g(u)=(1+u)\ln(1+u)-u
. Applying this inequality with
t={s_{n}-e\log n}\geq(3-e)\log n
yields that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))<e\log(n)-5\log\log n\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathsf{H}(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}))<e\log(n)\right)\leq\exp(-3\log(n)g(1-e/3)),

which goes to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
uniformly in
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{X}^{2}_{n}
.
∎
