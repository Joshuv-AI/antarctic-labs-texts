Effect of sprint interval cycling with blood flow restriction on capillary blood lactate, cognitive function and perceptual response: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Bryan J H Lee, Joshua Y Z Ng, Pei Ling Choo, Xiang Ren Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegular participation in physical activity can slow age-related functional and cognitive decline but many have cited a lack of time and energy as key barriers to exercise adherence. Hence, exercises with lower time commitment may appeal to sedentary individuals or working adults who perform insufficient physical activity. Sprint interval exercise with blood flow restriction (BFR-SIE) is a time-efficient exercise modality that may augment cognitive gains through enhanced metabolic stimulation and cerebrovascular alterations. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the effect of BFR-SIE on capillary blood lactate, cognition and perceptual response in healthy young adults. This study involved randomized controlled trials with unblinded crossover design. 18 participants (10 males; 8 females; age 26.4 ± 3.3 years; body mass 60.5 ± 12.6 kg; height 1.65 ± 0.07 m) completed sprint interval cycling involving six sets of 10 s all-out sprints with 1-min rest intervals, performed (1) with BFR applied at 50% occlusion pressure on both thighs (BFR-SIE), and (2) without BFR (SIE). Before and after each session, capillary blood lactate level was assessed, and cognitive assessments (Digit Span and Stroop test) were performed to evaluate working memory, selective attention, and executive function. Cycling power output was measured during sprints. Both BFR-SIE and SIE groups displayed comparable improvements in Stroop reaction times while task accuracies were largely unaltered for Stroop and Digit Span tests (p > 0.05). Both groups experienced similar increases in blood lactate levels despite a higher peak power output observed in SIE (366 ± 118 W vs. 336 ± 119 W; p < 0.001). Despite a lower power output during BFR-SIE, participants reported a higher mean rating of perceived exertion (5.3 ± 1.9 vs. 4.9 ± 1.8; p = 0.003). A single bout of sprint interval exercise with BFR did not further increase capillary blood lactate levels nor benefit cognition, likely due to the counteracting effect of higher perceived exertion. While BFR remains a promising tool to amplify the physiological responses to exercise, it may be suboptimal to implement for self-paced exercise, maximal exercise or exercise to exhaustion.ISRCTN registration: ISRCTN16365146; retrospectively registered on 07 Oct 2025.Keywords: Kaatsu training, Occlusion training, High-intensity interval training, Cognition, Brain healthSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology, Neurophysiology

Introduction

Participation in regular exercise can slow down age-related functional and cognitive decline through downstream mechanisms relating to the regulation of brain neuroplasticity and neuronal signaling1,2. However, a recent global status report on physical activity in 2022 highlighted that more than 80% of adolescents and 27% of adults did not attain adequate levels of physical activity as recommended by the World Health Organization3. A lack of time and energy have been commonly self-reported as the key barriers to participation in physical activity4. This underscores the need to explore alternative modes of exercise with lower time commitment that appeal to sedentary individuals or working adults who perform insufficient physical activity. Importantly, there have been growing evidence that participating in shorter duration exercise programs5 or even a single bout of physical exercise can enhance cognitive performance such as attention or executive functioning6.

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been gaining popularity in recent years due to its time efficiency in improving cardiovascular health. A meta-analysis on 37 studies found that HIIT resulted in a significant improvement in VO2max by 0.51 L/min7. While some have argued that HIIT may be perceived as too strenuous by sedentary individuals8, it has been shown that HIIT interventions are tolerable and acceptable, exhibiting lower dropout rates than traditional exercise programs9. Apart from its established effects on improving aerobic capacity, HIIT has been found to enhance cognitive function through the increased production of circulating metabolites which mediate exercise-induced neuroplasticity10. Exercising at high intensities elevates the level of blood lactate, along with other neurotrophic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and insulin-like growth factor 1 which play key roles in maintaining brain health and cognition11. Notably, peripheral blood lactate can cross the blood-brain barrier to function as neuronal signaling molecules to increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression2,12. Additionally, lactate can act as an alternative source of energy to meet the metabolic demands of the brain, especially in the prefrontal cortex responsible for executive function1. Therefore, a higher intensity exercise may promote greater benefits for cognitive gain or neuroprotective effects as compared to low or moderate-intensity exercise.

In recent years, there has been increasing research on a novel training strategy for cognitive gain which involves the coupling of exercise with blood flow restriction (BFR) technique. BFR training involves fully occluding venous blood flow while partially restricting arterial inflow to exercising muscles13. The addition of BFR to low-intensity aerobic exercises is shown to induce higher metabolic stress and increase the level of circulating blood lactate14. Furthermore, a previous study has demonstrated that high-intensity sprint interval cycling with BFR induced greater blood lactate levels compared to the same exercise without BFR15, and a higher exercise load with BFR can generate progressively higher levels of lactate accumulation16. Moreover, a study investigating the effects of dual-task training on cognition reported an elevated BDNF level with the addition of BFR to treadmill walking exercise17. The increase in neurotrophic factors and other metabolites after BFR training, akin to high-intensity aerobic exercise, has been proposed to improve cognitive function2.

However, existing studies investigating the effect of BFR training on the brain function primarily focused on BFR with low intensity resistance training18,19, with none examining the effects of combining BFR with high intensity aerobic exercise on cognition. Importantly, high intensity exercise coupled with BFR may provoke greater pulsatile blood flow and shear stress stimulus, which promote cerebral blood flow and beneficial vascular adaptations20. It has been shown that BFR exercise elevates oxyhemoglobin concentration in the motor cortex, indicating a positive effect on brain blood flow19. Concurrently, the added metabolic stress from BFR can trigger an amplified increase in blood lactate production during high intensity exercises, thus acutely improving the cognitive function. Hence, there is growing interest to explore whether BFR can be applied during HIIT protocols such as sprint interval exercise (SIE) to elicit cognitive and physiological benefits. SIE involves short bursts of “all-out” sprinting at supramaximal intensities alternating with rest intervals. Due to the short duration required, SIE may elicit lower perceptions of effort and commitment compared to continuous exercise, which can improve the adherence to regular exercise routine.

While there have been studies21–23 on the effects of sprint cycling exercise with BFR on muscle function and performance, no studies have explored its impact on cognition function. Given the high intensity nature, it is vital to first evaluate the feasibility and efficacy of an acute session of SIE coupled with BFR (BFR-SIE) and gain mechanistic insights before exploring its use as a new training modality. Therefore, our study aimed to investigate the effect of a single bout of BFR-SIE on the capillary blood lactate levels and cognition of healthy young adults as compared to SIE alone. In addition, we profiled their perceptual strain as this can modulate exercise adherence and motivation. We hypothesized that BFR-SIE promotes greater blood lactate production which enhances brain metabolism and function, leading to improved cognitive performance post-exercise.

Methods

Participants

Healthy young adults who are aged between 18 and 35 and are recreationally active (≥ 2 times of physical activity per week) with no prior experience of BFR-related training were recruited for this study. Participants who had cardiovascular or clotting disorders, nerve entrapments in the hip area, any condition(s) contraindicated to cycling, or taking medications that influenced heart rate or blood pressure were excluded from the study. After attending an informed consent session, all participants completed a Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q+) and BFR pre-screening questionnaire to assess their eligibility to participate in the study before they provided their written informed consent. Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Singapore Institute of Technology (SIT-IRB Ref no: 2022118) and the study was registered in ISRCTN following analysis (ISRCTN16365146; retrospectively registered on 07 Oct 2025). The conduct of this study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and local regulations (Human Biomedical Research Act). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of experiment.

Experimental design

The study was conducted as randomized controlled trials with a crossover design. Due to the physical nature of the intervention involving physical compression of the muscles during BFR, there was no blinding involved. The trial adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2025) for reporting guidelines. After screening, all eligible participants attended three separate sessions including a familiarization session and two exercise sessions at the Singapore Institute of Technology campus. Participants were randomly assigned by the study team to two study trials (Trial A or B) using an online random generator tool (https://www.randomlists.com/team-generator), with participants starting with either the BFR-SIE or SIE session (Fig. 1). After the completion of the first exercise session, participants returned for the second exercise session after a washout period of at least 7 days. Participants were advised not to engage in any form of strenuous physical activity between experimental sessions to avoid any confounding effect.

CONSORT flow diagram for this randomized crossover trial. A total of 19 participants were randomized to Trial A or Trial B where they performed sprint interval cycling with blood flow restriction (BFR-SIE) or without BFR, before crossing over to complete both experiments. 18 participants completed both sessions and were included for analysis while one participant (from Trial B sequence) did not complete the SIE session due to discomfort experienced during exercise.

Familiarization session

During the familiarization session, the participants’ height and weight were assessed with a stadiometer (SECA 213, Hamburg, Germany) and a portable weighing scale (Tanita HD-382, Tokyo, Japan) while resting heart rate (HR), blood pressure, and blood oxygen saturation level (SpO2) were recorded via vitals monitor cart with a pulse oximeter (Spot Vital Signs, Welch Ally, New York, USA). Subsequently, the total limb occlusion pressure (LOP) was assessed for both thighs in all participants using the pressure occlusion cuffs (Occlusion Cuff Elite, Belfast, Northern Ireland) and a vascular doppler (SonoTrax, Edan, San Diego, CA). To determine the LOP, occlusion cuffs were applied on participants’ thigh muscles and inflated until the auscultatory pulse was no longer audible. The recorded LOP value was then used to determine the personalized relative occlusion pressure (50% LOP) applied on each thigh during BFR-SIE. Based on pilot studies performed by the authors, it was found that BFR at 70–80% LOP during low-intensity exercise (weighted back-squats at 30% of 1-repetition maximum) led to severe lower limb numbness in a few participants. Therefore, an occlusion pressure at 50% LOP was selected in this study to avoid potential limb discomfort with supramaximal exertion.

To familiarize with the sprint cycling protocol on the cycle ergometer (Monark 939E, Monark Sports & Medical, Sweden), participants began with a 4-minute warm-up at 40 W and maintained a cadence between 70 and 80 rpm. Subsequently, they performed two 10 s all-out sprints with 1 min of self-paced active recovery intervals between sprints, followed by a 5-minute self-paced cool-down. Participants were not exposed to cognitive assessments during the familiarization trial.

Experimental sessions

Before each cycling session, participants performed two cognitive assessments (Fig. 2) - Digit Span and Stroop test. Digit span involves repeating a sequence of numbers in the exact order they were presented, which assesses one’s working memory as part of executive function24. On the other hand, the Stroop test involves processing conflicting information between colors and words, which assesses selective attention (orienting to a target stimulus), cognitive flexibility (switching between different tasks or thoughts) and inhibitory control (suppressing automatic responses in favor of goal-directed behavior), with the latter two as part of executive function25. Participants wore disposable earplugs during the tests to minimize external distractions. Baseline HR, blood pressure and SpO2 were measured before exercise, and the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) at rest was assessed using a Borg CR10 scale (ranged from 0 to 10). A HR chest strap (Polar H10 HR Sensor, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) was worn for continuous monitoring throughout the exercise.

Experimental design involving pre- and post-assessments on cognition and capillary blood lactate, and the sprint interval cycling intervention with or without blood flow restriction (BFR).

After mounting the cycle ergometer, participants wore a mouthpiece connected to a calibrated breath-by-breath metabolic cart (Quark COSMED, Rome, Italy) for continuous respiratory gas analysis. The resting volumes of oxygen consumption (VO2, ml/kg/min) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2, ml/kg/min) were assessed while participants remained seated on the cycle ergometer for 3 min. Participants then performed a standardized warm-up (similar to familiarization trial) for 4 min. After which, capillary blood lactate concentration was assessed via a finger prick test using a handheld lactate analyzer (Lactate Plus Meter, Nova Biomedical, Massachusetts, United States).

Thereafter, participants performed six sets of 10 s all-out sprints with flywheel resistance set at 0.05 kg/kg of body mass, alternated with 1 min of active rest intervals cycling at set resistance of 5 N. Their RPE was assessed after each sprint interval. Power output (Watts) was recorded every second via the Monark Bike software. After the six sprints, post-trial capillary blood lactate was immediately measured. A 5-minute self-paced cooldown (5 N resistance) was performed post-exercise followed by an extended cool-down of self-paced ambulation for an additional 5 min, before performing the post-session cognitive tests. Overall, there was a 10-minute rest period between the cessation of SIE and the cognitive tests. Post-session vitals (HR, blood pressure, SpO2), and RPE were also recorded.

For BFR-SIE, participants performed the exercise with BFR cuffs applied at 50% LOP on both thighs, and the cuffs were maintained throughout the exercise (total duration spans approximately 7 min). Whereas for SIE, participants performed the same protocol without BFR. Standardized encouragement (e.g., “Come on, [number of seconds left]”) was provided to all participants and they were instructed to try their best to complete the sprint.

Cognitive assessments (Digit span and Stroop test)

The cognitive assessments were performed on a computer via a software platform (Inquisit 5 Lab, Millisecond, Seattle, Washington). Digit Span consisted of two tasks − (1) forwards and (2) backwards. In Digit Span forwards task, the software displayed a series of digits (ranging from 2 to 9 numbers), at a rate of one digit per second. Participants were required to input the same order of the digits. In Digit Span backwards task, the software displayed a series of digits, but participants had to input the series of digits in reverse. Each task includes a total of 14 trials, and the number of correct trials before failing two consecutive trials at any time was recorded to indicate their task performance. The number of correctly recalled trials provides an index of working memory capacity.

The Stroop test consists of neutral, congruent, and incongruent stimuli. Participants were required to press the respective key on the keyboard which corresponds with the color of the words appearing on the screen. Participants were instructed to respond correctly as quickly as possible. For the neutral stimuli, only a colored box is displayed. For congruent stimuli, the word and the color match each other (e.g., the word “green” is displayed in green) whereas for incongruent stimuli, the word and color do not match each other (e.g., the word “green” is displayed in red). Reaction time and accuracy towards the congruent and incongruent stimuli were recorded and analyzed. Comparison of the reaction times towards the stimuli between groups allows for the assessment of selective attention and quantifies the response delay due to Stroop interference while the comparison of task accuracy allows for the assessment of effective conflict resolution and inhibitory control.

Statistical analysis

Estimation of the required sample size was performed using G*Power application (G*Power 3.1.9.7, gpower.hhu.de). Sample size was determined a priori to detect significant change based on a previous study with improved free recall performance (effect size = 0.8) in the high-intensity interval training group compared to control rest26. Power analysis indicated a sample of 15 participants was sufficient to provide 0.8 power for two-tailed t-tests with ⍺ of 0.05 for significance.

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to compute all data. Shapiro-Wilk Test was performed to assess data normality. Paired t-test was used to compare the mean/peak HR, RPE and power output between BFR-SIE and SIE groups. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA test was used to compare mean HR, power output, RPE, VO2, VCO2, respiratory exchange ratio (RER) changes across sprints between BFR-SIE and SIE groups. For non-normal data, the Wilcoxon Signed-rank test was used to compare between groups for parameters including the Stroop test reaction time and accuracy, Digit Span accuracy and capillary blood lactate levels. Significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05. Data are presented as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) for normal data, and median with interquartile range in parentheses for non-normal data.

Results

Demographics

A total of 18 participants (10 males; 8 females; mean age 26.4 ± 3.3 years; body mass 60.5 ± 12.6 kg; height 1.65 ± 0.07 m) completed the exercise trials. One participant dropped out of the study due to experiencing discomfort during the sprint cycling exercise without the application of BFR cuffs (SIE session), leading to non-completion of the experiment.

Heart rate

Main effects analysis showed that the mean HR profiles were similar between the BFR-SIE and SIE groups (F1,204 = 0.197, p = 0.658, η2p = 0.001), with a main effect of sprints on HR (F5,204 = 4.870, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.107) and no interaction effect (F5,204 = 0.299, p = 0.913, η2p = 0.007). The average mean HR across sprints (BFR-SIE: 151 ± 23 beats/min; SIE: 153 ± 23 beats/min; p = 0.548) and average peak HR across sprints (BFR-SIE: 154 ± 21 beats/min; SIE: 156 ± 20 beats/min; p = 0.288) were comparable between both groups.

Capillary blood lactate level

There was an increase in blood lactate levels from pre- to post-session in both BFR-SIE [Pre: 2.7 mmol/L (1.9–3.3); Post: 12.0 mmol/L (9.0–12.8); p < 0.001] and SIE [Pre: 2.9 mmol/L (1.9–4.0); Post: 11.6 mmol/L (9.1–13.8); p < 0.001] groups (Fig. 3). However, there was no difference in the magnitude of increase (calculated as fold changes from baseline) between BFR-SIE and SIE [3.7-fold (2.8–4.9) vs. 4.1-fold (2.6–6.1); p = 0.267].

Capillary blood lactate concentration (mmol/L) during pre-session and post-session assessments in the BFR-SIE and SIE groups (n = 18). The open circles represent each participant’s data. Each boxplot represents the median (line within the box), interquartile range (box), and the whiskers denote the range from minimum to maximum values. Significance denoted by *** p < 0.001.

Power output

The mean power output across six sprints was higher in the SIE group than the BFR-SIE group (F1,204 = 3.822, p = 0.052, η2p = 0.018), with no main effect of sprints or interaction effect (p > 0.05). Moreover, the SIE group showed a higher average mean power (296 ± 88 W vs. 270 ± 96 W; p = 0.010) and average peak power (366 ± 118 W vs. 336 ± 119 W; p < 0.001) output across sprints than BFR-SIE, indicating that the SIE group generated greater force during the sprints.

Rating of perceived exertion

The mean RPE across six sprints was higher in the SIE group than the BFR-SIE group (F1,204 = 3.639, p = 0.058, η2p = 0.018), with a main effect of sprints on RPE (F5,204 = 34.218, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.456) and no interaction effect (F5,204 = 0.119, p = 0.998, η2p = 0.003). Comparing sprint 1 to 6, there was a progressive increase in RPE reported by participants in both BFR-SIE and SIE groups (Table 1). BFR-SIE group displayed a higher average mean RPE across six sprints when compared to SIE (BFR-SIE: 5.3 ± 1.9; SIE: 4.9 ± 1.8; p = 0.003). Respiratory parameters

One participant’s gas exchange data was excluded due to low recorded values caused by air leakage from an ill-fitted facemask. The mean VO2 across six sprints (Table 1) were comparable between the BFR-SIE and SIE groups (F1,192 = 0.123, p = 0.726, η2p = 0.001), with a main effect of sprints on VO2 (F5,192 = 9.218, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.194) and no interaction effect (F5,192 = 0.256, p = 0.936, η2p = 0.007). The mean VCO2 across six sprints (Table 1) were comparable between the BFR-SIE and SIE groups (F1,192 = 1.321, p = 0.252, η2p = 0.007), with a main effect of sprints on VCO2 (F5,192 = 13.846, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.265) and no interaction effect (F5,192 = 0.083, p = 0.995, η2p = 0.002).

Aligned with the results observed from power output and RPE, the mean RER across six sprints (Table 1) was found to be higher in the SIE group than the BFR-SIE group (F1,192 = 3.617, p = 0.059, η2p = 0.018), with a main effect of sprints on RER (F5,192 = 10.734, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.218) and no interaction effect (F5,192 = 0.114, p = 0.989, η2p = 0.003).

Digit span accuracy

In the Digit Span forwards task, the number of correct trials (out of 14 trials) was comparable between BFR-SIE and SIE groups for pre-exercise session [BFR-SIE: 8 (7–9); SIE: 8 (7–8); p = 0.630] and post-exercise session [BFR-SIE: 8 (7–8); SIE: 8 (7–8); p = 0.280]. This was similar for the Digit Span backwards task where the number of correct trials was comparable between BFR-SIE and SIE groups for pre-exercise session [BFR-SIE: 8 (7–8); SIE: 8 (7–9); p = 0.080] and post-exercise session [BFR-SIE: 8 (7–9); SIE: 8 (7–8); p = 0.620]. Therefore, both groups demonstrated similar task accuracies (57%) in the forwards and the backwards tasks during post-exercise sessions.

Stroop reaction time and accuracy

From pre- to post-session, both groups experienced a decrease in Stroop reaction time for both congruent (BFR-SIE: p < 0.001; SIE: p = 0.001) and incongruent stimuli (BFR-SIE: p = 0.008; SIE: p = 0.004) (Fig. 4A). However, the decrease in reaction time was similar in both groups for the congruent [BFR-SIE: 10% (5–18); SIE: 13% (11–19); p = 0.755] and incongruent stimuli [BFR-SIE: 8% (3–22); SIE: 19% (6–24); p = 0.741]. Task accuracy was largely similar across both groups except that there was a decrease in accuracy for incongruent stimuli in the SIE group [Pre: 96.4% (92.9–100.0); Post: 92.9% (89.3–97.3); p = 0.016] (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the acute effect of a single session of BFR-SIE on the blood lactate levels and cognitive performance of healthy young adults. Our findings demonstrated that (1) capillary blood lactate levels were comparable with or without BFR application, (2) both BFR-SIE and SIE did not influence Digit Span task accuracies, (3) both BFR-SIE and SIE led to improved reaction times while SIE group had decreased accuracy for incongruent stimuli during Stroop test and lastly, (4) BFR-SIE was associated with higher ratings of perceived exertion when compared to SIE.

In this study, working memory was found to be unaltered by the short bout of sprint cycling exercise with or without BFR applied as the task accuracies in both Digit Span forwards and backwards tasks were not improved. According to the Baddeley’s model27, working memory comprises three components including visuospatial sketchpad, phonological loop, and central executive function. The Digit span test utilised in our study primarily assesses the phonological loop and thus, any functional changes pertaining to the visuospatial sketchpad and central executive function components may not be observed in this study. Furthermore, it is plausible that the enhancement of working memory function may be a gradual process and requires more time to manifest, due to the complex processing of information and dependence on brain connectivity27. Hence, meaningful changes to memory function may not be apparent after a single bout of exercise. This corroborates with the finding from another study28 where working memory in healthy young adults was not influenced by six bouts of 6-seconds ‘all-out’ sprints. Therefore, an acute exercise bout may exert varying effects on different cognitive domains where there could be changes in one cognitive domain (e.g., executive function) but not necessarily in other domains.

While working memory performance remained unaltered, the Stroop test results demonstrated that both BFR-SIE and SIE groups showed improvements in reaction time with preserved accuracy from pre- to post-session. The increased blood lactate levels during the sprint cycling may have contributed to cognitive benefits where lactate can cross the blood-brain barrier to be utilised by the brain for enhanced neural metabolism29. Furthermore, the high-intensity exercise could have elevated arousal levels, thus improving cognitive function acutely30. However, it is prudent to note that the improvement in reaction time in BFR-SIE could be solely attributed to the effects of SIE where the BFR application exerted no influence, since similar improvements have been observed in both conditions. Inter-subject variability in Stroop reaction times influenced by various factors including fatigue, sleep quality, varying levels of stress, anxiety, and focus could have hindered the detection of minute differences, if any, between the improvement observed in BFR-SIE and SIE31,32.

Importantly, BFR-SIE did not benefit executive function as observed from the similar task accuracy between both groups. In Stroop test, the evaluation of executive function, including inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility, can be derived from the participants’ responses to incongruent stimuli involving Stroop interference. The reduced task accuracy for incongruent stimuli exclusive to SIE could indicate the adverse effect of high intensity exertion on executive function, albeit not observed in BFR-SIE. Alternatively, this could also suggest that executive function was preserved after performing BFR-SIE when compared to SIE. On the other hand, the lack of improvement in executive function with BFR-SIE could be related to the additional perceptual strain from use of BFR. The BFR exercise could have engendered greater perceived fatigue among participants, resulting in the impairment in physical performance with lower power output, and potentially culminated in mental fatigue which limits executive functioning. We postulate that the reduced capacity to exercise harder in BFR-SIE could also have hindered the augmentation of blood lactate production which is the proposed metabolic driver for cognitive benefits. However, the reciprocal relationship between exercise intensity, blood lactate and its influence on cognitive performance remains to be further investigated in future studies.

Metabolite accumulation in exercising muscles is a crucial training stimulus for maximizing the cognitive benefits of SIE33. Since BFR occludes venous blood flow away from exercising muscles, it is expected that BFR increases the level of metabolite (i.e., lactate and hydrogen ions) accumulation when compared to non-BFR exercises performed at similar intensities. As aforementioned, it is important to consider that lactate production is positively correlated with power output34, regardless of the use of BFR in interval cycling trials35. The lower power output in the BFR-SIE group could have been attributed to increased stimulation of group III muscle afferents by the constriction pressure of the cuffs. The neural feedback by these sensory afferents could have resulted in lowered central motor drive and transiently reduced the force-generating capacity of the exercising musculature36. Nonetheless, despite the lower power output in BFR-SIE, we found that both groups showed similar circulating levels of blood lactate post-exercise. Collectively, these findings suggest that the addition of BFR cuffs during sprint cycling did not promote a greater production of blood lactate, likely due to an undesired reduction in power output during the cycling sprints.

Given that both the application of BFR technique and the SIE protocol have been ascribed relatively high levels of discomfort35, the profiling of the perceptual responses to BFR-SIE is vital to determine its feasibility as a new training method. BFR-SIE reported a higher mean RPE over the 6 sprints compared to SIE, indicating an overall higher perceptual strain. Apart from influencing power output, stimulation of group III muscle afferents may also cause higher perceptual strain37. A commonly reported reason for the greater perception of efforts across varying intensities of intermittent cycling exercise with BFR was the increased discomfort from wearing BFR cuffs35,38. Stimulation of group III afferents due to the mechanical constriction by BFR cuffs has been shown to transmit nociceptive stimuli to structures of the brain associated with motivation and goal-oriented behavior such as the thalamus and prefrontal cortex39. Increased stimulation of these afferents could have accentuated sensations of discomfort and elicited a higher perceived strain in BFR-SIE. This suggests that employing BFR techniques for self-paced exercise, maximal exercise or exercise to exhaustion may be suboptimal since the added perceptual strain limits motivation and performance.

The usage of different BFR cuff pressures can also influence perceptual responses. The 50% LOP used in our study could be too high to strike the balance between increasing metabolic stress from blood flow reduction and causing excessive discomfort. Utilizing a LOP below 50% may alleviate the discomfort associated with BFR, and result in more favorable perceptual responses38. Secondly, intermittent cycling protocols with BFR found that deflating the BFR cuffs during recovery periods led to lower levels of perceptual discomfort14,40. As our protocol involved BFR cuffs remaining inflated during recovery periods to minimize delays from repeated BFR cuff adjustments, there is longer exposure of the exercising muscles to local hypoxia and constrictive discomfort, thus causing a greater perceived strain. Thus, it may be necessary to introduce longer rest periods with deflating of BFR cuffs in SIE protocol to circumvent the high perceived strain stemming from prolonged occlusion.

Limitations

Several limitations in this study should be considered. Firstly, the Digit Span test used in our study did not assess all components of working memory (only assessed the phonological loop). Secondly, the Stroop test accuracy may have been limited by a ceiling effect41, with a high median accuracy above 90% during the pre-session tests. The Stroop test may be deemed less challenging by younger participants, which could explain the lack of differences in their cognitive performances. Therefore, future research could employ more sensitive and comprehensive cognitive tests such as reading span task and flanker task to assess working memory and executive function respectively. Another limitation is the absence of practice trials for the cognitive tasks, which would have allowed participants to familiarize themselves with the tasks, thereby minimizing any potential learning effects. In addition, potential cognitive gains may only manifest after long-term training with BFR due to adaptive responses such as lowering of discomfort with BFR cuffs over time42 and/or cerebrovascular changes with increased BDNF levels43, which may be explored in future studies. It is also pertinent to note that other factors influencing cognitive performance including circulating BDNF levels, cerebral blood flow, glucose metabolism and cortical excitability were not assessed in this study. Finally, our findings are limited to healthy young adults and may not be generalizable to all individuals. The feasibility and safety of BFR exercises remain to be verified in other populations such as older adults who are most likely to benefit from this exercise due to age-related cognitive decline.

Conclusion

The coupling of BFR with sprint interval cycling presents a novel modality to minimize the time spent in exercise and to reap potential benefits in enhancing cognition in healthy young adults. However, our findings have shown that the addition of BFR did not confer further benefits, likely due to its counteracting impairment on force production during sprint cycling. This hindered the ability to augment beneficial metabolite production. While BFR remains a promising tool to amplify the physiological responses to exercise, healthcare professionals and athletes should note that it may be suboptimal to employ BFR for self-paced exercise, maximal exercise, or exercise to exhaustion since the added perceptual strain limits motivation and performance which offset the intended benefits. Future research should explore potential cognitive gains from long-term BFR exercise training while including a broader array of cognitive and neurophysiological outcome measures.
