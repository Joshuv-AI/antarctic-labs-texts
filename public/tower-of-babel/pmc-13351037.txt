Chromosome-level genome assembly of the bird cherry-oat aphid, Rhopalosiphum padi (Hemiptera: Aphididae)
Authors: Bin Zhu, Juncheng Yan, Meixi Liu, Ningning Wu, Pei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bird cherry-oat aphid (Rhopalosiphum padi) is a globally destructive pest of gramineous crops, threatening food security via direct feeding damage and barley yellow dwarf virus (BYDV) transmission. However, its genetic adaptability mechanisms remain poorly understood due to limited genomic resources. Here, we present the first high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for R. padi. The assembled genome spans 322.52 Mb, with 95.81% successfully anchored to four pseudochromosomes. It exhibits high continuity, with scaffold and contig N50 values of 88.62 Mb and 13.67 Mb, respectively, and a BUSCO completeness of 99.9%. Genome annotation using NR, Swiss-Prot, eggnog, GO and KEGG database has identified 18,981 protein-coding genes. This high-quality genome provides a critical resource for dissecting R. padi’s adaptability and developing sustainable pest control strategies.

Background & Summary

Gramineous crops, including wheat (Triticum aestivum), barley (Hordeum vulgare), oats (Avena sativa) and corn (Zea mays), serve as the backbone of global food security, providing over 50% of humanity’s caloric intake and acting as the primary feed source for livestock1. However, their productivity is severely threatened by the bird cherry-oat aphid, Rhopalosiphum padi (Hemiptera: Aphididae)-a cosmopolitan pest with a millennia-long history of damaging cereal crops. Native to Eurasia, R. padi has now spread to all major cereal-producing regions2,3. It specializes in feeding on the phloem sap of gramineous plants through its piercing-sucking mouthparts, causing direct physiological damage such as leaf chlorosis, stunted tillering, and reduced grain weight. Additionally, it induces indirect losses by transmitting Barley Yellow Dwarf Virus (BYDV), a pathogen that can lead to yield reductions of up to 40% in severely infected fields4,5. Despite its significant economic impact, the control of R. padi still relies heavily on broad-spectrum insecticides. This strategy is increasingly challenged by evolving insecticide resistance (e.g., resistance to neonicotinoids and pyrethroids) and ecological side effects, including declines in pollinators and soil contamination6,7.

As a member of the Aphididae family, R. padi exemplifies the ecological and genetic diversity of aphids-a group comprising over 5,000 species with diverse host associations and life strategies8. While genomic sequencing has advanced our understanding of aphid biology, only 24 of these have achieved chromosome-level resolution. Moreover, most cereal-feeding aphids lack comprehensive genomic datasets necessary for functional gene discovery9,10. The unique life cycle of R. padi, which involves seasonal host alternation (overwintering on Prunus spp. and colonizing gramineous crops in summer), adds complexity to its genetic adaptation, requiring specialized mechanisms for host recognition, cold tolerance, and reproductive switching11. However, the absence of a high-quality genome has hindered efforts to identify key genes involved in detoxification, virus transmission, and environmental stress responses, thereby limiting the development of targeted, sustainable control strategies.

We present a chromosome-scale genome assembly of R. padi generated by integrating multiple sequencing technologies: PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing for accuracy, Illumina short-read sequencing for error correction, and Hi-C chromatin conformation capture for chromosomal scaffolding (Table 1). The finally assembled genome spans 322.52 Mb, comprising 272 contigs with a N50 length of 13.67 Mb, 35 scaffolds with a N50 length of 88.62 Mb, and 95.81% of the sequences are anchored to 4 chromosomes (Fig. 1, Table 2). 18,981 protein-coding geneswith a mean CDS length of 7,608 bp were identified from the R. padi genome using de novo, homology- and RNA sequencing-based methods (Table 3). Functional annotation found that 16,755 (88.27%), 9,248 (48.72%), and 12,937 (68.16%) genes had significant hits with proteins cataloged in NR, SwissProt and eggNOG, respectively. There were 9,065 (47.76%) and 6,306 (33.22%) genes annotated to GO terms and KEGG pathways, respectively (Table 5). Comparative genomic analyses between R. padi and its congener Rhopalosiphum maidis reveal striking chromosomal synteny conservation, reflecting their close evolutionary relationship (Fig. 2). Phylogenetic reconstruction (with 10 Hemiptera species and Drosophila melanogaster as outgroup) indicates R. padi diverged from other aphids ~19 million years ago (MYA), from non-Aphidoidea Hemiptera ~216 MYA, and from D. melanogaster ~273 MYA (Fig. 3A). Orthology analysis revealed the presence of approximately 7,082 aphid-specific genes and 1,053 species-specific genes in R. padi, which may involve in host adaptation, plant-insect interactions, or other aphid-specific biological processes. Gene family expansion analyses show enriched KEGG pathways (e.g., Ascorbate & aldarate metabolism, metabolism of xenobiotics, cAMP signaling) (Fig. 3B) and GO terms (e.g., membrane integral components, nucleic acid binding) (Fig. 3C), corresponding to adaptive strategies for host plant utilization and environmental stress response.

Methods

Sample preparation

The R. padi population used in this study was originally collected in 2018 from Xinxiang, Henan Province, China, and has since been maintained continuously on wheat seedlings in our laboratory. Cultures were maintained under controlled conditions: 22 ± 2 °C, 60% relative humidity, and a 16 h light:8 h dark photoperiod, with continuous propagation for over 5 years. Isogenic colonies were initiated from a single parthenogenetic female of R. padi and maintained individually on wheat seedlings until collection for sequencing. For genomic DNA extraction and genome sequencing, approximately 200 mg of fresh R. padi individuals were collected, including first- to fourth-instar nymphs, as well as winged and wingless adults. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use. The frozen samples were fully homogenized in tissue lysis buffer, and total genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Genome sequencing

For short-read sequencing, a paired-end library (2 × 150 bp) with an average insert size of 400 bp was constructed using the VAHTSTM Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA). For long-read sequencing, a PacBio SMRTbell library with an average insert size of 15 kb was prepared using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA), and subjected to circular consensus sequencing (CCS) on a PacBio Sequel II system with 8 M SMRT Cells.

To achieve chromosome-level genome assembly, Hi-C technology was employed to capture genome-wide chromatin interactions. Briefly, approximately 200 mg of fresh mixed R. padi individuals (including first- to fourth-instar nymphs, as well as winged and wingless adults) were ground in 2% formaldehyde for cross-linking of cellular proteins, followed by extraction of ~100 μg of genomic DNA. Chromatin integrity and residual cross-linked proteins were subsequently evaluated. Chromatin was digested with the restriction enzyme Mbo I, biotinylated residues were incorporated during sticky-end repair, and the resulting blunt-end fragments were ligated under dilute conditions12,13. The genomic DNA was then randomly sheared into 300–500 bp fragments, and biotin-labeled fragments were isolated using magnetic beads. Subsequent steps, including end repair, dA tailing, adapter ligation, and DNA purification, were performed sequentially. Library quality was assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and quantitative PCR (qPCR). The Hi-C library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads, generating approximately 45.4 Gb of clean data (Table 1).

For PacBio full-length transcriptome sequencing, total RNA was isolated from fresh mixed R. padi individuals (with equal proportions of first- to fourth-instar nymphs, as well as winged and wingless adults) using the EASYspin Plus Cell/Tissue RNA Isolation Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies). Ten micrograms of total RNA were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SMARTer PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara) in accordance with the manufacturer’s standard protocol. A SMRTbell library was constructed with the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit (Takara) and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform using SMRT Cell 8 M. Full-length consensus isoforms were generated using SMRTlink software. After quality control, 76.48 Gb of Subread Bases were retained, with a Subread N50 length of 2.13 kb.

Genome survey and assembly

To estimate genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content, k-mer distribution of Illumina paired-end reads was analyzed using Jellyfish and GenomeScope with k-mer size 1714. Illumina genomic sequencing yielded 36.5 Gb of high-quality paired-end reads (Table 1). The genome size of R. padi was estimated to be 343.37 Mb via k-mer counting, with k-mer analysis revealing moderate heterozygosity (0.311%) and high repeat content (53.75%) (Fig. 1A). The estimated genome size is close to the final assembled size. It is comparable to R. maidis15, and smaller than Acyrthosiphon pisum16, Schizaphis graminum17 and Megoura crassicauda9. Scaffold N50 and gene count also show consistency across species (Table 3).

For HiFi read generation, raw polymerase reads were directly processed via the pbccs pipeline (default parameters; https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) for filtering and error correction. The resulting HiFi reads were then subjected to de novo assembly using hifiasm software (https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm). From the raw sequence reads, 19.7 Gb of filtered PacBio HiFi reads were obtained, with an average read length of 16,635 bp (Table 1).

Chromosome assembly using Hi-C

For Hi-C-based chromosome-level assembly, Hi-C reads were aligned to the draft genome using Juicer v1.6.2 (https://github.com/aidenlab/juicer). After filtering to retain uniquely mapped valid interaction pairs, the data were processed using the 3D-DNA pipeline (https://github.com/theaidenlab/3d-dna)18: its misjoin correction module eliminated erroneously assembled junctions, the scaffolding algorithm ordered and oriented contigs into chromosome-scale scaffolds, and the “seal” function recovered scaffolds that had been incorrectly discarded in previous steps. Interchromosomal interaction intensity heatmaps were generated using Juicer v1.6.2 to visually quantify chromosomal contact frequencies. Following Hi-C integration, 95.81% of the genome sequence was anchored to chromosomes, resulting in 4 final pseudochromosomes, and the assembly continuity was significantly improved.

Genome annotation

The R. padi genome was comprehensively annotated for repetitive sequences, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), protein-coding genes, and their functions via a standardized multi-faceted computational pipeline. Repetitive elements were identified using specialized tools: Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v4.09 (http://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf409.legacylinux64.download.html)19 for tandem repeats, and RepeatModeler v2.0 (http://www.repeatmasker.org/RepeatModeler/)20 for interspersed repeats (e.g., transposons). De novo predicted and database-derived repetitive sequences were masked with RepeatMasker v4.1.0 (http://www.repeatmasker.org/RMDownload)21 to minimize interference with downstream gene prediction (Table 4). Functionally conserved ncRNAs were annotated: tRNAs with tRNAscan-SE v1.4alpha22, and other ncRNAs (e.g., rRNAs, miRNAs, snRNAs) by searching Rfam v11.023 with Infernal v1.1.3 (http://eddylab.org/) and a strict E-value cutoff of 1e-5 to filter false positives.

To optimize protein-coding gene model accuracy and completeness, three complementary prediction strategies were integrated: Homology-based prediction: Protein sequences from six related aphid species (A. pisum16, R. maidis15, Diuraphis noxia24, Aphis gossypii25, Aphis glycines26, Myzus persicae27) retrieved from NCBI were aligned to the R. padi genome using GeMoMa v1.6.1 (http://www.jstacs.de/index.php/GeMoMa) to refine intron-exon boundaries. RNA-seq-assisted prediction: PacBio full-length transcriptomic data was used to predict ORFs via PASA (https://sourceforge.net/projects/pasa/files/stats/timeline) with default parameters, leveraging transcriptional evidence for gene structure validation. Ab initio prediction: Augustus v3.2.2 (http://augustus.gobics.de/binaries/) and SNAP (http://snap.stanford.edu/snap/download.html) were applied to the repeat-masked genome to identify gene models based on intrinsic sequence features.

Gene models from the three approaches were integrated using EVidenceModeler (EVM; https://sourceforge.net/projects/evidencemodeler/) to generate a high-confidence protein-coding gene set. PASA was further used to annotate UTRs and predict alternative splicing events. Functional characterization of the final gene set was performed by BLAST alignment against five databases (NR, Swiss-Prot, eggNOG, GO, KEGG) with an E-value cutoff of ≤1e-5 for meaningful matches (Table 5).

Genome synteny and phylogeny analysis

MUMmer (https://github.com/mummer4/mummer) was applied for the detailed collinearity analysis between R. padi and R. maidis15 genomes. The results reveal striking conservation of linear synteny between the four chromosomes of R. padi and R. maidis, with minimal structural disruptions. Specifically, the homology of chromosomes 1–4 between R. padi and R. maidis reaches 91.30%, 92.26%, 88.08%, and 90.23%, respectively (Fig. 2).

Ten additional Hemiptera species, Apolygus lucorum28, Nilaparvata lugens29, Bemisia tabaci30, S. graminum17, R. maidis15, Melanaphis sacchari31, Metopolophium dirhodum5, A. pisum16, M. persicae27 and D. noxia24 were used to infer orthologous genes, and D. melanogaster was selected as outgroup. Orthologous gene clusters were identified via the OrthoFinder v 3.1.3 (https://github.com/OrthoFinder/OrthoFinder)32 with “-M msa”. Following this, species phylogenetic trees were constructed using FastTree (v2.2)33 with the JTT + CAT model, based on conserved single-copy gene sequences. Subsequently, divergence times were estimated, and analyses of gene family expansion and contraction were performed.

Calibration points for divergence time estimation were established based on data retrieved from TimeTree34. For R. padi, the divergence node from other aphid species corresponds to a timeline of approximately 19 million years ago (19 MYA). Its divergence time from other Hemipteran species outside the Aphidoidea superfamily aligns near 216 million years ago (216 MYA), while its divergence time from D. melanogaster is around 273 million years ago (273 MYA) (Fig. 3A).

Through the analysis of gene family expansion and contraction, we identified that the KEGG pathways enriched with expanded genes in aphid species mainly include: Ascorbate & aldarate metabolism, Metabolism of xenobiotics, Cardiac muscle contraction and cAMP signaling pathway (Fig. 3B). It should be pointed out that the enrichment of the “cardiac muscle contraction” pathway highlights the importance of conserved signaling components, such as calcium-handling proteins and ion channels, in fundamental insect physiological processes like circulatory and visceral muscle activity, rather than reflecting a vertebrate-type heart function. GO enrichment analysis of the expanded gene families revealed significant enrichment in Integral component of membrane, Nucleic acid binding and ATP binding (Fig. 3C). The functions of these expanded genes (such as signal transduction, environmental response, and metabolic regulation) typically represent the evolutionary strategies of aphid species for adapting to host plants and complex environments.

Technical Validation

Validations were employed to assess the contiguity, accuracy and completeness of the genome assembly. First, clean Illumina reads were aligned against the contigs assembled using BWA v0.7.15 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bio-bwa/files/)37, and SAMtools v1.4 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/samtools/files/samtools/)38, respectively. Genome assembly and protein-coding gene set completeness were evaluated using BUSCO v6.0.0 (https://busco.ezlab.org/) with the hemiptera_odb12 lineage dataset39. The genome assembly showed that 99.9% of BUSCOs were complete, of which 99.6% were single-copy and 0.3% were duplicated. Fragmented and missing BUSCOs accounted for 0.0% and 0.1%, respectively. For the protein-coding gene set, 97.5% of BUSCOs were complete (82.4% single-copy, 15.2% duplicated), with 1.1% fragmented and 1.4% missing. These results, particularly the near-complete assembly and high gene set completeness, demonstrate the high quality of the R. padi genome assembly and annotation (Table 2).
