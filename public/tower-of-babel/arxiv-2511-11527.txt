Evaluation of Audio Compression Codecs
Thien T. Duong, Jan P. Springer

Abstract
Perceptual quality of audio is the combination of aural accuracy and listener-perceived sound fidelity. It is how humans respond to the accuracy, intelligibility, and fidelity of aural media. Today this fidelity is also heavily influenced by the use of audio compression codecs for storing aural media in digital form. We argue that, when choosing an audio compression codec, users should not only look at compression efficiency but also consider the sonic perceptual quality properties of available audio compression codecs. We evaluate several commonly used audio compression codecs in terms of compression performance as well as their sonic perceptual quality via codec performance measurements, visualizations, and PEAQ scores. We demonstrate how perceptual quality is affected by digital audio compression techniques, providing insights for users in the process of choosing a digital audio compression scheme.

1
Introduction

Perceptual quality of audio is the combination of aural accuracy and listener-perceived sound fidelity
[
14
]
. How listeners experience digital audio depends on
sound fidelity
, i.e. the accuracy of a digital signal representing the reproduction of sound.
High-fidelity
or hi-fi digital audio ideally should have low levels of noise and distortion in its signal to provide a high perceptual quality.

Our goal is to assess widely used audio compression codecs such as
[
FLACWebsite
]
or AAC and MP3
[
7
]
with respect to compression efficiency as well as how the compression techniques in these audio codecs affect the perceptual quality of the signal reconstruction. In addition, we use visual analytics derived techniques to present the perceptual impact of compression techniques via spectrograms, sound field diagrams, and objective perceptual scoring using
Perceptual Evaluation of Audio Quality
(PEAQ)
[
32
]
.

We argue that in choosing an audio compression codec, users should not only look at the compression efficiency but also consider the sonic perceptual quality of an audio compression codec.

2
Related Work

Audio codecs use general compression techniques to reduce the size of digital audio file. Traditional audio compression techniques include Huffman coding
[
6
]
, Linear Predictive Coding (LPC)
[
23
]
, and psychoacoustic-based perceptual encoding
[
8
]
.

Audio codecs are categorized into
lossy
or
lossless
depending on the compression scheme. Lossy audio codecs, such as MP3 and AAC
[
7
]
or Vorbis
[
Moffitt:OVOFA:2001
]
, compress digital audio by removing
unimportant
data, i.e. parts of the digital signal that can be difficult to perceive by a listener because it is not within the human hearing threshold. Lossless audio codecs such as
[
FLACWebsite
]
compress digital audio by only removing redundancies and compressing repeated patterns in the audio signal while maintaining all of the original signal’s data.

Assessing digital audio codecs requires comparing compression efficiency as well as evaluating the resulting perceptual quality. Compression metrics include compression ratio, encoding speed, and decoding speed. Evaluating perceptual quality requires a more complex assessment. Classically, perceptual quality assessments require trained listeners to perform listening tests
[
24
]
. However, listening tests are a rather subjective assessment with inherent listener bias as well as reproducibility problems. We present perceptual-quality assessment using visualizations as well as objective scoring. Tools such as frequency spectra
[
5
]
, spectrograms
[
30
]
, and sound-field diagrams
[
20
]
show the impact of compression artifacts to the perceptual quality of encoded digital audio. In addition,
Perceptual Evaluation of Audio Quality
(PEAQ) is used to quantify the perceptual quality by generating a single
Objective Difference Grade
(ODG) score of encoded digital audio
[
32
]
.

In recent years, experiments with AI-driven audio compression techniques have been conducted. These techniques are based on well-known neural network architectures such as
Residual Vector Quantized Generative Adversarial Networks
(RVQGAN)
[
22
]
. We also compare RVQGAN audio compression technique with traditional audio compression techniques in our evaluation.

3.2
Audio Codecs and Settings

Audio-compression ratio in percentage is calculated as shown in
equation

1
.

\text{Compression ratio}=\frac{\text{Uncompressed file size}}{\text{Compressed file size}}\cdot 100

(1)

The lower the compression ratio, the more storage space a digital audio file will occupy. Encoding speed and decoding speed are expressed in samples per microsecond and calculated as shown in
equation

2
.

\text{Speed}=\frac{2\cdot\text{Sample size}}{\text{Recorded time}\cdot 1,000,000}

(2)

Sample size in
equation

2
is multiplied by two because audio tracks (usually) contain stereo signals in a left and a right channel. In addition, calculated speeds are divided by 1,000,000 in
equation

2
to convert from samples per second to samples per microseconds.

The most commonly used audio codecs were chosen for our experiments: FLAC, MP3, AAC, and Vorbis. These audio codecs have different settings, allowing users to choose different compression levels. To keep our experiments close to real life scenarios, the most common settings were chosen for each audio codec.

5
Discussion

5.1
Audio-Codec Performance

In our audio-codec performance tests (cf.
table

4
), FLAC exhibited the lowest compression ratio of all tested audio codecs, meaning that lossless audio files will take up more storage space than files encoded using a lossy compression technique. On the other hand, FLAC also achieved the highest encoding speed. These two average values for FLAC are expected because lossless compression techniques preserve most data in an audio file while only removing redundancies and repeated patterns.

All lossy audio codecs have similar compression ratio, with MP3 (128 kbps) achieving the highest compression ratio. Both MP3 quality settings exhibit lower encoding speeds than other lossy audio codecs. On the other hand, they show significantly higher decoding speeds than other lossy audio codecs with MP3 at 128 kbps being the fastest to decode. It can be argued that MP3 is a strong contender for the best lossy audio codec due to its high decoding speeds, making it the ideal choice for audio playback on low-power devices.

Among lossy audio encoders, Vorbis exhibits above average compression ratios, the fastest encoding speeds, and decent decoding speeds. In our audio-codec performance evaluation, Vorbis is the runner up for lossy audio-codecs, providing decent compression ratios and fastest lossy encoding speeds.

5.2
Perceptual Quality

Our perceptual quality evaluation shows that lossy compression algorithms sacrifice high-frequency aural details to reduce storage size. Audio encoding using psycho-acoustic principles determines these region are mostly inaudible to the average listener and the audio data for these regions can simply be removed. The differing graphs in
figure

6
Conclusions

We evaluated several of the most common audio-compression codecs providing insights in how audio compression techniques affect sonic perceptual quality of digital music. Our tests indicate that lossy audio-compression codecs do provide better compression performance than lossless audio compression codecs. This stems mainly from the fact that lossy audio-compression algorithms sacrifice aural perceptual quality for higher compression speeds and lower final storage size. However, the Vorbis audio codec emerged as the exception to this rule. It exhibits acceptable compression efficiency when compared to its competitors while at the same time achieving perceptual quality nearly imperceptibly different compared to respective uncompressed digital audio signal.

We also evaluated RVQGAN, an AI-based audio compression scheme. While RVQGAN’s codec performance in speed and size was impressive, its perceptual quality is severely lacking. We are sure to see more advancement in this field, especially improving the perceptual quality of future AI-driven audio compression codec developments.

Our evaluation strengthens our argument that, when choosing a digital audio compression codec, focus should not only be on the audio compression performance. Users should also consider the perceptual quality of the encoded audio signal to ensure the best possible listening experience. We compliment Vorbis as an excellent lossy audio-compression codec also able to maintain high-fidelity perceptual quality.
