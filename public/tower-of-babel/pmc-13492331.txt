Introduction

Secundum atrial septal defects (ASDs) account for approximately 10% of congenital cardiac malformations.1 Most ASDs occur sporadically; however, familial forms have been described and are enriched for pathogenic variants in genes involved in cardiac development. Among these, NKX2-5, a cardiac-specific homeobox transcription factor essential for atrial septation and conduction system formation, is the most consistently implicated gene.2 Pathogenic variants in NKX2-5 are identified in a small proportion of sporadic ASDs and in approximately 8%–12% of familial cases.3 Beyond septal defects, NKX2-5 variants are associated with a characteristic cardiac phenotype that includes AV conduction disease, atrial arrhythmias, and, in rare cases, ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death.4, 5, 6

Arrhythmic manifestations in NKX2-5 mutation carriers may occur in the absence of overt structural heart disease or left ventricular dysfunction.4,7 While missense variants affecting the NKX2-5 homeodomain have been shown to impair DNA binding and transcriptional activity, providing mechanistic insight into phenotypic heterogeneity, the arrhythmogenic implications of missense and truncating variants remain incompletely understood.8 As a result, clinical decision making regarding sudden cardiac death prevention remains challenging. Current guidelines for primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) do not specifically address arrhythmic risk associated with developmental gene mutations such as NKX2-5.9 Herein, we report a case of a young woman with familial ASD due to a novel truncating NKX2-5 variant who developed progressive conduction disease, atrial arrhythmias, and symptomatic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, illustrating how genotype-informed risk assessment influenced the decision to pursue ICD therapy.

Case summary

A 42-year-old woman with a history of a secundum ASD, surgically repaired at age 17, was referred for evaluation of recurrent atrial arrhythmias and prodromic syncope. Her family history was notable for an autosomal dominant pattern of congenital heart disease, with ASD documented in multiple first- and second-degree relatives across 3 generations, some of whom also required permanent pacemaker implantation for conduction system disease.

She presented with recurrent palpitations, sometimes associated with dizziness followed by syncope, and episodes of documented paroxysmal atrial flutter. Her baseline electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (PR interval 278 ms) and a QRS duration of 106 ms (Figure 1). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% with septal hypokinesia. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed mild left ventricular dilation with inferoseptal hypokinesis, preserved right ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 56%), no atrial enlargement, and no late gadolinium enhancement. On electrophysiological testing, baseline conduction assessment revealed a normal HV interval (41 ms) and an anterograde Wenckebach cycle length of 590 ms. The AV node and atrial effective refractory periods were 420 and 220 ms, respectively, at a drive train of 700 ms. No retrograde conduction was present, and programmed ventricular stimulation was not performed. Typical isthmus-dependent atrial flutter was induced and ablated, along with a focal atrial tachycardia anterior to the ostium of the coronary sinus (Figure 2). The latter arrhythmia was inducible and terminable with pacing but was not entrainable, consistent with a nonautomatic focal mechanism. Bouts of irregular left-sided self-terminating atrial arrhythmias were noted, and atrial fibrillation was reproducibly induced with single premature atrial beats, prompting electrical cardioversion.

Given the strong family clustering of ASD and conduction abnormalities in addition to multiple inducible atrial arrhythmias, genetic testing was pursued. This identified a pathogenic frameshift variant in NKX2-5 resulting in premature protein termination (NM_004387.3:c.688_690del, p.Leu223ProfsTer21), which has not been previously reported and is absent from the population included in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0). Loss-of-function variants in NKX2-5 are a recognized cause of familial ASDs, conduction disease, and arrhythmias.

After ablation, the patient continued to experience bouts of palpitations with presyncope. Further evaluation demonstrated no evidence of inducible ischemia on rubidium positron emission tomography. An implantable cardiac monitor was placed. Device interrogation revealed brief bouts of atrial fibrillation, blocked premature atrial beats, and second-degree Mobitz I AV block. In addition, episodes of nonsustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia of multiple morphologies were recorded. The longest episode lasted 28 beats, had a maximum rate of 240 beats/min, and was associated with presyncope (Figure 3). After multidisciplinary case review and patient-centered shared decision making, a dual-chamber ICD was implanted to mitigate progressive bradyarrhythmia and potential ventricular arrhythmic risk. Metoprolol 25 mg twice daily was initiated postimplantation. Over 1 year of follow-up, device interrogation demonstrated recurrent episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, with rates up to 227 beats/min and durations of up to 12 seconds, 30% of the time spent in atrial fibrillation, and 29% ventricular pacing (minimum programmed rate 50 beats/min). No appropriate or inappropriate device therapies were delivered. All tested relatives with ASDs carried the same NKX2-5 likely pathogenic variant, whereas unaffected family members did not.

Discussion

Pathogenic variants in NKX2-5 are a well-established cause of familial ASDs and are frequently accompanied by progressive conduction system disease and atrial arrhythmias.7,8,10 Beyond these recognized manifestations, accumulating evidence indicates that NKX2-5–associated disease may also confer susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias and, in rare cases, sudden cardiac death. A few family-based case series and individual reports have described malignant ventricular arrhythmias, unexplained syncope, or sudden death in NKX2-5 mutation carriers, sometimes occurring in the absence of severe structural heart disease or marked ventricular dysfunction.5,6

The present case underscores the clinical complexity of risk stratification in NKX2-5–associated disease. Our patient exhibited a classic NKX2-5 phenotype, including familial ASD, progressive AV conduction abnormalities, and complex atrial arrhythmias, but also developed symptomatic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. Importantly, ventricular arrhythmias occurred in the setting of only mild ventricular remodeling, preserved right ventricular function, and no myocardial scar on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. This constellation challenges conventional paradigms that rely primarily on ventricular systolic dysfunction or ischemic substrate to guide sudden cardiac death prevention.

From a mechanistic perspective, NKX2-5 encodes a cardiac transcription factor critical for both structural cardiogenesis and the development and maintenance of the specialized conduction system. Loss-of-function variants, including frameshift and nonsense mutations, are thought to act predominantly through haploinsufficiency, resulting in impaired transcriptional regulation of downstream targets involved in conduction system integrity and myocardial electrophysiology.7,8 While AV block and atrial arrhythmias are well-recognized consequences, the mechanisms underlying ventricular arrhythmogenesis remain incompletely defined. In the absence of overt structural abnormalities or myocardial scar, ventricular arrhythmias in NKX2-5–associated disease likely arise from an intrinsic electrical substrate related to abnormal cardiac development and conduction system organization. This may promote reentry through nonuniform conduction and conduction delay, and potentially abnormal automaticity via altered ion channel and pacemaker current expression. Although the precise mechanism cannot be established, triggered activity remains a possible contributor.

Device therapy in NKX2-5 mutation carriers, therefore, presents a particular challenge. Permanent pacemaker implantation for progressive AV block is well-documented and widely accepted in this population.7 In contrast, indications for ICD therapy, particularly for primary prevention, are not clearly defined.9 In this context, the occurrence of symptomatic rapid nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, a pathogenic NKX2-5 frameshift variant, and underlying potentially progressive conduction disease supported a proactive approach to dual-chamber ICD implantation. This case highlights how genotype-informed risk assessment can meaningfully influence device selection in carefully selected patients.

Finally, this report reinforces the importance of genetic evaluation in patients with familial ASD and arrhythmias. For this patient’s family, recognition of NKX2-5 disease triggered long-term follow-up despite the absence of residual shunts, given the risk of progressive conduction system disease. Contemporary international consensus statements increasingly recognize the role of genetic testing in congenital heart disease and inherited arrhythmia syndromes, both to clarify diagnosis and to inform clinical management.11 As the spectrum of NKX2-5–associated phenotypes continues to expand, additional data will be needed to better define ventricular arrhythmic risk and to guide evidence-based recommendations for device therapy in this population.

Conclusion

This case illustrates how identification of a pathogenic NKX2-5 variant can influence arrhythmic risk assessment and device selection in patients with familial ASD and arrhythmias. In the presence of symptomatic nonsustained ventricular tachycardia and progressive conduction disease, genotype-informed evaluation supported implantation of a dual-chamber ICD despite the absence of conventional guideline indications. These findings highlight the value of integrating genetic data into individualized management strategies for congenital heart disease–associated arrhythmias.
