Lp(a) levels and ischemic stroke recurrence in type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Aichun Cheng, Fangyuan Zhang, Aoming Jin, Jing Xue, Zhiyuan Feng, Xia Meng, Yongjun Wang, Jie Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe investigated the association between lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] levels and stroke recurrence in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients with recent acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA).MethodsThis study included 3,311 T2DM patients with recent acute ischemic stroke or TIA and complete Lp(a) data from the Third China National Stroke Registry. The patients were categorized into three groups based on the 40th and 70th percentiles of the Lp(a): ≤13.1, 13.1 to 29.2 and ≥ 29.2 mg/dL. The primary outcome was stroke recurrence within one year, with incident cases further classified as either ischemic or hemorrhagic. Cox proportional hazards regression and restricted cubic splines were used to evaluate these associations.ResultsA total of 3311 patients (2142 men, 64.69%, median age 63) were analyzed. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between Lp(a) levels and the risk of stroke recurrence. After adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors, patients with Lp(a) levels ≤ 13.1 mg/dL or ≥ 29.2 mg/dL had hazard ratios of 1.34 (95% confidence interval (CI), 1.02–1.76) and 1.35 (95% CI, 1.01–1.79), respectively, for total stroke compared to those with Lp(a) levels between 13.1 and 29.2 mg/dL. The corresponding hazard ratios were 1.36 (95% CI, 1.02–1.81) and 1.36 (95% CI, 1.01–1.83) for ischemic stroke and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.37–2.09) and 0.77 (95% CI, 0.31–1.94) for hemorrhagic stroke, respectively.ConclusionsBoth low and high levels of Lp(a) are associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence in T2DM patients with a recent history of acute ischemic stroke or TIA, demonstrating a U-shaped relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02005-y.

Background

Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) exhibit a distinct metabolic profile, placing them at a significantly higher risk for arteriosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD) compared to non-diabetic individuals [1]. T2DM is often accompanied by multiple comorbidities, and comprehensive cardiovascular risk management has been recognized as a key strategy for improving long-term outcomes over the past few decades [2].However, despite advances in controlling risk factors, patients with T2DM and ASCVD continue to experience a high residual risk of recurrent events in clinical practice [3]. Therefore, identifying additional risk factors contributing to the residual cardiovascular risk in T2DM patients is crucial.

In recent years, increasing attention has been given to the inverse relationship between Lp(a) and T2DM, as studies have reported lower Lp(a) levels in individuals with a higher prevalence of T2DM [4–6]. However, mendelian randomization studies have yielded highly heterogeneous findings regarding the causal relationships between Lp(a) and T2DM [7–10]. Lp(a), a complex lipoprotein composed of apolipoprotein (a) and covalently bound to apolipoprotein B of modified low-density lipoprotein (LDL) [11], has been identified as an independent and causal risk factor for ASCVD [12–14]. Notably, recent advancements in Lp(a)-lowering therapies have provided robust clinical and experimental evidence supporting the efficacy of novel therapeutics in reducing Lp(a) levels [15]. These developments have raised growing interest in whether lowering Lp(a) could reduce recurrent cardiovascular events in T2DM patients with a history of ASCVD. However, evidence remains limited, particularly in the context of acute ischemic stroke or TIA. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between Lp(a) levels and stroke recurrence in T2DM patients with a history of acute ischemic stroke or TIA, using data from the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III).

Methods

Study populations

We utilized data from the CNSR-III, a nationwide prospective registry designed to collect comprehensive clinical data on patients with acute ischemic cerebrovascular events in China. The detailed study design and methodology have been previously described [16]. In brief, CNSR-III enrolled 15,166 patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke or TIA within seven days of symptom onset between August 2015 and March 2018. The study was conducted across 201 hospitals spanning 22 provinces and 4 municipalities. Among these, 171 sites participated in a prespecified biomarker sub-study, which involved the collection of blood and urine samples for further analysis. The study was approved by the ethics committees of the Beijing Tiantan Hospital and all participating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives prior to enrollment.

Baseline data collection

Baseline information included demographic information(age and sex), body mass index (BMI, calculated as kg/m2), systolic blood pressure(SBP), diastolic blood pressure(DBP), current smoking status, heavy drinking (defined as ≥ 2 standard alcohol consumption per day), medical history(coronary heart disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia and stroke), type of index event (TIA or ischemic stroke), etiology subtypes were classified according to the TOAST criteria (large-artery atherosclerosis [LAA], cardioembolism, small vessel occlusion [SAO], other determined cause, or undetermined cause) [17], stroke severity on admission (assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale[NIHSS] score), and laboratory test results. All clinical information was collected through the electronic data capture system.

Lp(a) measurement

Ethylene Diamine Tetraacetic Acid(EDTA) fasting blood samples were obtained within 24 h of admission from 171 study sites participating in the biomarker sub-study. All the blood samples were transported to the center laboratory in Beijing Tiantan Hospital in a maintained cold chain and stored at − 80 °C until testing. Lp(a) concentrations were determined by using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Mercodia AB, Uppsala, Sweden). The Mercodia Lp(a) ELISA is based on the direct sandwich technique where two monoclonal antibodies are directed against separate antigenic determinants on the Apo(a) molecule. This assay has been extensively validated and demonstrates high reproducibility (with a coefficient of variation ranging from 5.52% to 6.98%), which yields robust results and is generally unaffected by apolipoprotein(a) size heterogeneity.

Patients follow-up and outcome evaluation

The follow-up procedure of CNSR-III has been described previously in detail [16]. Patients were followed up at 3 months, 6 months and 1–5 year annually, Patients were interviewed face-to-face at 3 months and contacted over the telephone by trained research coordinators at 6 months and 1–5 year annually. The primary outcome was stroke recurrence (time-to-first event) within one year, including both new ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke events. The secondary outcomes included ischemic stroke recurrence and hemorrhagic stroke events, analyzed separately. All cerebrovascular events were confirmed by the treating hospital, and suspected recurrent cerebrovascular events without hospitalization were judged by an independent end point judgment committee.

Statistical analyses

Participants were categorized into three groups based on the 40th and 70th percentiles of Lp(a) levels: ≤13.1 mg/dL (Q1), 13.1–29.2 mg/dL (Q2), and ≥ 29.2 mg/dL (Q3). Given prior evidence suggesting a potential non-linear association between Lp(a) and vascular risk in patients with cerebrovascular disease and type 2 diabetes [18], the second quartile of Lp(a) (Q2), corresponding to the interval with the lowest incidence rate of stroke recurrence, was defined as the reference group. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the χ² test for categorical variables and the nonparametric Wilcoxon test for continuous variables.

The time to all outcome events of each group was presented using the Kaplan-Meier curves, and the differences between groups were tested by the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to calculate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all outcome events during the 1-year follow-up period. Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs were first calculated on univariable (unadjusted) Cox regression models. Adjusted HRs and their 95% CIs were calculated on multivariable Cox regression model and was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, TOAST subtype, medical history (coronary heart disease, stroke), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)and NIHSS at admission.

Subgroup analysis was pre-specified to examine the association between Lp(a) levels and stroke recurrence according to glycemic control status, defined by HbA1c levels with a cutoff of 6.5%. The interaction between the Lp(a) groups and the HbA1c subgroup was tested by introducing a multiplicative interaction term into the Cox regression model.

To assess potential nonlinear relationships, the association between Lp(a) and stroke recurrence risk was modeled on a continuous scale using restricted cubic splines, with the optimal degrees of freedom (df) selected by minimizing the model’s Akaike information criterion (AIC). A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Patients characteristics

A total of 15,166 patients with ischemic stroke or TIA from CNSR-III were screened for eligibility. We first excluded 1,342 participants who did not have blood samples sent to the central laboratory, followed by 7,777 participants without a diagnosis of T2DM. Additionally, 173 participants with missing baseline Lp(a) values were excluded. After applying these criteria, a total of 3,311 T2DM patients with a history of acute ischemic stroke or TIA were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1, Table S1).

Association between Lp(a) levels and risk of recurrent stroke

Within one year, 357 (10.78%) participants experienced recurrent stroke events, including 331 (10.00%) recurrent ischemic strokes and 31 (0.09%) hemorrhagic strokes. Compared to individuals with Lp(a) levels between 13.1 and 29.2 mg/dL, the crude HR for total stroke was 1.34 (95% CI, 1.03–1.75) in those with Lp(a) ≤ 13.1 mg/dL and 1.46 (95% CI, 1.11–1.93) in those with Lp(a) ≥ 29.2 mg/dL. The corresponding crude HRs (95% CI) for ischemic stroke were 1.35 (1.02–1.78) and 1.49 (1.11–1.98). The number of events was also relatively high (Q1: 139, Q2: 78, Q3: 114), further supporting a potential pathogenic role of Lp(a) in ischemic stroke recurrence. In contrast, for hemorrhagic stroke recurrence, the number of events was low across all groups (Q1: 12, Q2: 10, Q3: 9), and the HRs were 0.90 (0.39–2.08) and 0.90 (0.37–2.22), respectively (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 2, the cumulative incidence of total and ischemic stroke was significantly higher in individuals with Lp(a) levels ≤ 13.1or ≥ 29.2 mg/L (P = 0.0215, P = 0.0210, respectively). After adjusting for baseline covariates, Lp(a) levels ≤ 13.1 or ≥ 29.2 mg/dL remained independently associated with an increased risk of total stroke (aHR, 1.34; 95%CI, 1.02–1.76; P = 0.037; aHR, 1.35; 95%CI, 1.01–1.79; P = 0.0423; respectively) and ischemic stroke (aHR, 1.36; 95%CI, 1.02–1.81; P = 0.0351; aHR, 1.36; 95%CI, 1.01–1.83; P = 0.0427; respectively) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

We further examined the association between Lp(a) levels and outcomes stratified by HbA1c levels. In the HbA1c < 6.5% group, there were 28 (6.85%) recurrent events, with 26 (6.37%) recurrent ischemic stroke events within 1 year. Despite numerical increases in the crude incidence of recurrent stroke and ischemic stroke in patients with Lp(a) ≤ 13.1 mg/dL or ≥ 29.2 mg/dL, compared to those with Lp(a) levels between 13.1 and 29.2 mg/dL, neither low nor high Lp(a) levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of total recurrent stroke or recurrent ischemic stroke (Table 3). In contrast, in the HbA1c ≥ 6.5% group, there were 202 (11.17%) recurrent events, with 189 (10.45%) recurrent ischemic stroke events within 1 year. Compared to individuals with Lp(a) levels between 13.1 and 29.2 mg/dL, the crude HR for total stroke was 1.46(95% CI, 1.02–2.11) for those with Lp(a) ≤ 13.1 mg/dL and 1.80 (95% CI, 1.24–2.63) for those with Lp(a) ≥ 29.2 mg/dL. For ischemic stroke, the crude HR (95% CI) was 1.47(1.00–2.16.00.16) and 1.81(1.23–2.67), respectively. After adjusting for baseline covariates, both low (≤ 13.1 mg/dL) and high (≥ 29.2 mg/dL) Lp(a) levels remained significantly associated with an increased risk of total stroke (aHR, 1.50; 95%CI, 1.03–2.18; P = 0.0358; aHR, 1.58; 95%CI, 1.07–2.32; P = 0.0213; respectively) and ischemic stroke (aHR, 1.54; 95%CI, 1.04–2.27; P = 0.0302; aHR, 1.56; 95%CI, 1.04–2.33; P = 0.0304; respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cohort study utilizing data from the CNSR-III, we investigated the association between Lp(a) levels and the risk of stroke recurrence in T2DM patients who had recently experienced an acute ischemic stroke or TIA. Our findings revealed a U-shaped relationship, with both low (≤ 13.1 mg/dL) and high (≥ 29.2 mg/dL) Lp(a) levels linked to an increased risk of total and ischemic stroke recurrence. After adjusting for baseline covariates, patients with Lp(a) ≤ 13.1 or ≥ 29.2 mg/dL had a 34% and 35% increased risk of total stroke, respectively, and both groups faced a 36% increased risk of ischemic stroke.

ASCVD remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in T2DM. Coexisting conditions common in T2DM, such as dyslipidemia-which affects up to 85% of patients-significantly contribute to the heightened ASCVD risk [19–21]. Effective management of cardiovascular risk factors is essential for improving long-term outcomes in T2DM. Despite clear LDL-C targets [22], residual cardiovascular risk remains higher in T2DM patients with a prior stroke. Growing evidence suggests that beyond traditional lipid parameters, Lp(a) plays a significant role in cardiovascular risk, and lowering Lp(a) has shown potential benefits [23–25]. However, in T2DM patients, this relationship appears more complex, as lower blood lipid levels, including Lp(a) levels may be linked to an increased risk of developing T2DM [26]. Therefore, the benefits of lowering Lp(a) in T2DM patients with a history of ASCVD remain uncertain. Zhang et al. identified Lp(a) as an independent predictor of recurrent cardiovascular events (CVEs) in T2DM patients with a history of CVEs [27]. A prospective study of 1,018 myocardial infraction patients showing that Lp(a) levels were linked to recurrent myocardial infraction and all-cause mortality in those without T2DM but not in those with T2DM [28]. In contrast, Schwartz GG et al. analyzed 969 patients who had recently experienced acute coronary syndrome and were receiving statin therapy, finding no significant association between Lp(a) levels and adverse CVEs [29]. Building upon these findings, we were the first to explore the impact of Lp(a) levels on stroke recurrence in T2DM patients with recent ischemic stroke. Our analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between Lp(a) levels and risk of stroke recurrence. This U-shaped pattern aligns with the dual-pathophysiological model of Lp(a) recently summarized by Panagiotopoulos et al., which posits that while elevated Lp(a) contributes to atherothrombosis, abnormally low levels may impair vascular repair mechanisms, thereby increasing stroke risk from both ends of the concentration spectrum [18]. The observed pattern may reflect a specific interaction between T2DM and Lp(a), potentially diminishing the protective role of lower Lp(a) levels in reducing stroke recurrence.

Our study found that elevated Lp(a) levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence; however, this association was mainly observed for ischemic stroke recurrence, with no significant relationship identified for hemorrhagic stroke recurrence. This finding is consistent with the results of Holmes et al., who reported that lipoprotein particles and their lipid constituents were robustly associated with myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, but not with intracerebral hemorrhage [30]. These observations suggest that ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction share a common atherosclerotic and lipid-driven pathophysiological basis, whereas hemorrhagic stroke is more strongly related to hypertensive small vessel disease and vascular fragility rather than lipid accumulation [30]. Moreover, Holmes et al. noted that although lipids were not associated with intracerebral hemorrhage, certain non-lipid metabolites-such as glycoprotein acetyls and glucose-were linked to all three vascular diseases, implying that systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation may represent shared underlying pathways. Taken together, our findings support the role of Lp(a) as a potential biomarker for ischemic stroke recurrence, while its clinical relevance in hemorrhagic stroke appears limited.

Some Mendelian randomization studies have confirmed that low Lp(a) levels are associated with an increased risk of developing T2DM [31]. However, the impact of low Lp(a) levels on stroke recurrence in patients already diagnosed with T2DM remains unclear. Emerging evidence suggests that low Lp(a) may adversely affect glycaemic control, with lower plasma Lp(a) concentrations being linked to poorer glycaemic regulation and a greater likelihood of requiring insulin therapy in individuals with T2DM [32]. These findings raise the question of whether the impact of Lp(a) on stroke recurrence varies by glycaemic status. To address this, we further conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by HbA1c levels. Among T2DM patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, indicative of poor glycaemic control, both low and high Lp(a) levels were associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence. In contrast, this U-shaped association was not observed in patients with HbA1c < 6.5%, suggesting that glycaemic status may modify the relationship between Lp(a) and stroke outcomes. Building on previous findings that low Lp(a) levels may interfere with insulin signaling in target cells and contribute to insulin resistance [33], a possible explanation for our results is that, in the context of poor glycaemic control, this adverse effect may be amplified. Under such conditions, Lp(a) may further exacerbate metabolic disturbances, including chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress. These compounded effects could increase the risk of stroke recurrence at both low and high Lp(a) levels, resulting in a U-shaped association. In contrast, in patients with well-controlled blood glucose, the overall metabolic burden is relatively limited, and the influence of Lp(a) may be more direct and linear, thereby attenuating the U-shaped pattern observed in those with poor glycaemic control.

Nevertheless, our study also had several limitations. Firstly, this study measured Lp(a) levels only at admission, without assessing Lp(a) concentrations prior to or during the pathological progression. Additionally, LPA genotypes were not evaluated in this study. Although Lp(a) levels are genetically determined, the risk of cardiovascular disease is primarily influenced by elevated plasma Lp(a) concentrations, rather than the independent effects of LPA genotypes [34]. Secondly, this is a study among Chinese population with T2DM and prior acute ischemic stroke or TIA, and whether the data applied to other populations need to be testified. Finally, as the study was an observational study, further investigations are needed to clarify the underlying mechanism of the associations.

Conclusions

In summary, both low and high levels of Lp(a) were associated with an increased risk of recurrent stroke in T2DM patients with prior acute ischemic stroke or TIA. Further studies are required to validate these findings and explore the underlying biological mechanisms.
