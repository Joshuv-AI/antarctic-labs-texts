Limited impact of autochthonous bioaugmentation used for the bioremediation of the oily desert soil at elevated temperatures
Authors: Dina M Al-Mailem, Mayada K Kansour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo investigate the potential of autochthonous bioaugmentation to enhance the bioremediation of oil-contaminated soils at high temperatures, pristine Kuwaiti desert soil samples were contaminated with oil and bioaugmented with four autochthonous thermotolerant/thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria, individually and as consortia. Even without bioaugmentation, all treated soil samples had similar cultivable communities after bioremediation. Throughout the year-long period, none of the four inoculated bacterial strains could be cultivated from the bioaugmented soils. In contrast, two of the inoculants, Amycolatopsis and Isoptericola, were molecularly detected at trace levels in all soils studied. Incubation time and season, rather than treatments primarily, shaped the composition of these communities. Oil degradation rates in bioaugmented and unbioaugmented soils were not statistically different. This study illuminated the diversity of Kuwaiti desert hydrocarbonoclastic microbial populations, enhancing their high-temperature oil-degradation capacity. However, the dynamic nature of these populations creates serious challenges for any introduced members seeking acceptance.Subject areas: Soil science, Environmental science, Environmental management, Pollution, Soil ecology

Introduction

Soil oil contamination is a major environmental issue that substantially affects local economies, human health, and ecosystems.1,2 The problem is more severe in places such as Kuwait, which has extensive oil extraction and processing operations. Kuwait, one of the world’s top oil producers, has faced significant contamination issues, particularly during the 1991 Gulf War, when massive oil spills and fires caused severe environmental damage.

Effective remediation methods are necessary to address oil-contaminated soil, mitigate health concerns, and restore ecological functions. Due to its affordability and environmental sustainability, bioremediation has become one of the more promising remediation technologies,3,4 unlike physicochemical techniques, such as skimmers, dispersants, oil booms, sorbents, incineration, and landfilling, which have several disadvantages, including their high cost and potential to cause new pollution.5,6 One bioremediation method is bioaugmentation, which treats contaminated environments with autochthonous, allochthonous, or genetically engineered microbial strains.7,8 Due to the intense competition between introduced and native strains, autochthonous bioaugmentation, which uses only native species to the contaminated area as inoculants, is an effective bioremediation technique. In this case, microorganisms are isolated from a polluted site and grown in highly controlled environments. The pre-adapted pure microbial strains are then reseeded in the exact location.9

In Kuwait, where summer temperatures can reach 60°C, thermotolerant and thermophilic bacteria are highly relevant, particularly in the context of oil spills. These heat-tolerant bacteria are the only ones that can survive and function in the region’s extreme temperatures, making them particularly beneficial in bioremediation.10,11 According to research, numerous thermotolerant and thermophilic bacterial strains have been isolated and identified as oil degraders12,13,14; however, up to this point, very few studies have been conducted on utilizing these microorganisms in bioaugmentation as a technique for oil bioremediation in high-temperature desert environments. Our research group investigated the use of such organisms for bioremediation; however, those studies focused exclusively on biostimulation rather than bioaugmentation. It was reported that the addition of Ca2+ and dipicolinic acid (two key compounds in the endospores of the genus Bacillus) biostimulated oil bioremediation at 50°C.12,15 Perfumo et al. (2007)16 reported substantial hexadecane breakdown at 60°C resulting from bioaugmentation with the thermophilic bacterium Geobacillus thermoleovorans. In another recent study, 81.5% of the total petroleum hydrocarbons in petroleum-contaminated soil were degraded in 14 days by a thermophilic petroleum-degrading consortium containing Caldibacillus, Geobacillus, Mycolicibacterium, Bacillus, Chelatococcus, and Aeribacillus spp.17 These bioaugmentation investigations were carried out in controlled bench-scale settings over short periods. In this study, hydrocarbonoclastic thermotolerant/thermophilic bacteria native to Kuwaiti desert soil were investigated for the bioremediation of the same oil-contaminated soil in an open area over an extended period to mimic natural environmental conditions. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of inoculating oil-contaminated soils with autochthonous bacteria, known for degrading oil at elevated temperatures, on the bioremediation of these soils. Additionally, the study aimed to monitor the growth and dissemination of these bacteria, their interactions with native soil bacteria, and their contributions to bioremediation over 1 year.

Results and discussion

Number of culturable mesophilic/thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria during bioremediation

Fewer hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria can live and propagate at 50°C than at 30°C (Figure 1). Counts at 30°C ranged from 2.9 × 104 to 888 × 104 CFUs per gram of soil, whereas those harvested at 50°C ranged from 0.12 × 104 to 27.0 × 104 CFUs per gram of soil. The second half of the year saw a notable increase in CFU numbers at both temperatures. This increase can be attributed to the fact that when a pristine ecosystem is freshly contaminated with oil, it takes considerable time for the allochthonous oil-utilizing microflora to adapt and use the hydrocarbon contaminants as their primary carbon and energy source.18

Examining Figure 1A, we can see that in the ninth month of the bioremediation process, the number of culturable mesophilic hydrocarbonoclastics was significantly highest in all treated soil samples, including the control (ANOVA, p < 0.05). From that point on, the counts continued to decrease until the end of the experiment. The ninth month of the experiment was September, during which the temperature in Kuwait usually fluctuates between 25°C and 35°C, the optimum temperature for mesophiles (Table 1), which could explain the rise in CFU counts over this period. Nutrient loss and the accumulation of hazardous metabolites in the final quarter of the bioremediation process may have contributed to the subsequent decline in CFU numbers, along with seasonal temperature fluctuations and other factors. These dynamic shifts in the numbers indicate the involvement of various bacterial populations in the oil-degradation process.

Referring to Figure 1A, the soil sample inoculated with a mixture of the four bacterial strains produced the highest number (ANOVA, p < 0.05) of culturable mesophilic hydrocarbonoclastics throughout the bioremediation year, regardless of the season. Although none of the inoculated bacterial strains could be cultivated during the one-year bioremediation, as described later, enriching the treated soil with a mixed bacterial population appears to promote the flourishing of indigenous microflora through synergistic interactions.19 The same treated soil also harvested the highest number of thermophilic bacteria in the first three seasons of the bioremediation process (Figure 1B). At the end of the year, the CFU numbers of culturable thermophiles increased in some soil samples while dropping in others, indicating that each treated soil has distinct circumstances that influence microbial growth.

Dynamics of culturable bacterial communities during bioremediation

The experiment involved isolating bacterial strains using a plate dilution counting method that relies on oil vapor as the only carbon source. The oil vapor-based plate dilution method selectively enriches aerobic, cultivable hydrocarbonoclastic microorganisms that can utilize volatile and semi-volatile petroleum fractions, without attempting to capture the complete range of hydrocarbon-degrading taxa in the soil. However, it does have the critical benefit of capturing only hydrocarbonoclastic microorganisms. Therefore, the isolates reported in this study are considered the most notable members of the hydrocarbonoclastic bacterial populations that could be cultivated from the treated soil samples (Figure 2). During the year-long bioremediation at 30°C and 50°C, 49 bacterial strains were identified from all treated soil samples (Table 2). Mesophiles were the most diverse, with 33 strains growing at 30°C, whereas only 10 thermophiles grew at 50°C. Furthermore, six thermotolerant bacterial strains thrived and reproduced at both temperatures.

Dynamics of mesophilic community

Figure 2A depicts the distribution of 39 hydrocarbonoclastic bacterial strains isolated at 30°C during a year of bioremediation in all treated soil samples. The isolates primarily belonged to the genera Streptomyces, Arthrobacter, Microbacterium, Nocardioides, Dietzia, and others, which are considered indigenous soil hydrocarbonoclastics.20,21 The diversity of hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria observed at time 0 (immediately after oil addition) suggests that these microorganisms are present even in pristine soils and obtain their nutrients from conventional carbon sources (e.g., sugars and amino acids)22 or from nonhydrocarbon sources (e.g., intermediate metabolites, viz., pyruvic acid, lactic acid, and citric acid).23 When oil is added, the less hydrophobic soil core releases bacterial species with hydrophobic cell surfaces. Numerous hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria possess hydrophobic cell envelopes that facilitate their adhesion to hydrophobic substrates, such as oil, allowing them to access and metabolize the substance.24,25

The diversity of the bacterial population reduced over time (Figure 2A). After six months of bioremediation, fewer bacterial species could be cultivated, with Nocardioides agrisoli and Nocardioides vastitatis predominating in most treated soil samples. The predominance of these two species persisted into the ninth month in all soil samples, including the unbioaugmented control; however, a different Nocardioides species, N. kandeliae, became predominant at the end of the bioremediation process, along with Microbacterium paludicola. Changes in bacterial communities over time reflect the complex composition of crude oil, which provides a wide range of substrates for microbial populations to vary. Most hydrocarbonoclastic microorganisms are better at degrading low-molecular-weight hydrocarbon fractions, which are consumed first, which can explain why bacterial diversity decreased over time as this carbon source was depleted.4,26 Most cultivated taxa that dominated the bioaugmented soil samples also dominated the unbioaugmented ones. However, the dominant bacterial community structure varied by season, indicating that soil microorganisms respond to changes in biotic and abiotic variables by selectively developing specific microbial groups.

Dynamics of the thermophilic community

Bacterial diversity was substantially lower at 50°C, with most bacterial species falling into the Actinobacteria and Bacilli classes (Figure 2B). The predominance of thermotolerant/thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic taxa changed with seasons. Initially (at time 0), mainly Aeribacillus composti and Chelativorans intermedius predominated the treated soil samples. After 6 months of bioremediation, only one strain, Amycolatopsis thermalba, could be cultured from most of the treated soil samples. At the same time, the new taxa Dietzia papillomatosis and Krasilnikoviella muralis became predominant in the ninth month. At the end of the bioremediation process, the genus Nocardioides, represented by the two species N. kandeliae and N. zeicaulis, predominated in all soil samples, along with other strains, albeit in smaller proportions, such as Microbacterium petrolearium, Georgenia sediminis, Amycolatopsis tucumanensis, and others.

Notably, Actinobacteria members predominated in all treated soils in both mesophilic and thermophilic communities during the second half of the bioremediation year, particularly Nocardioides spp. at the final time point. According to reports, the genus Nocardioides harbors several genes encoding aromatic compound-degrading pathways,27 which explains its prevalence in environments where high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons persist at the end of the bioremediation process. Interestingly, Nocardioides spp. have been shown to play a crucial role in hydrocarbon bioremediation, even in hypersaline soils.28

Like the mesophilic community, the thermotolerant/thermophilic community is influenced by various oil substrates and environmental conditions, leading to a continuous shift in bacterial populations.29 The chronology of biodegradation of these substrates and the bioproducts resulting from the process controls when and which bacterial strains can grow and propagate. Establishing consortia comprising several bacterial strains capable of metabolizing a broad spectrum of pollutants enhances their tolerance to physicochemical parameters, such as temperature, pH, and salinity, through the wide range of degradative enzymes that break down different hydrocarbon components.30,31

Figure 2 and Table 2 reveal that none of the four bacterial strains employed as inoculants could be cultured from the bioaugmented soil throughout the bioremediation process, despite being indigenous and previously isolated from the same soil. The failure to isolate the injected strains by cultivation may be partly due to technical constraints in selective plating. The oil vapor-based plate dilution method successfully enriches hydrocarbonoclastic microorganisms but shows a bias toward rapidly growing, physiologically active taxa, potentially missing species at low abundance or in a dormant or stressed state. Consequently, the absence of introduced strains in culture should not be construed as their total eradication from the soil system. This conclusion aligns with DNA investigations that identified certain infected taxa at trace quantities, indicating persistence beneath cultivation detection thresholds, as will be detailed later. Nonetheless, the absence of cultivable recovery suggests that these strains did not proliferate adequately to significantly impact the predominant hydrocarbonoclastic population during bioremediation. It has been reported that bacterial communities are dynamic, meaning that new members may emerge and current members may decline, making it highly challenging for autochthonous inoculants to survive. The growth and dissemination of the introduced microbes may also be influenced by a wide range of other factors, including soil characteristics, availability of hydrocarbons and nutrients, temperature, moisture, pH, and salinity.32

At each stage of the bioremediation process, predominant strains possess traits that confer a selective advantage in environments with complex oil. In addition to their metabolic adaptability, these indigenous taxa are presumably better able to survive the harsh, variable conditions of the desert, characterized by prolonged high temperatures, limited moisture, and unpredictable nutrient availability. Prolonged environmental adaptation, along with functional redundancy within the indigenous community, may have reduced the ecological niche available to the introduced strains and constrained their role in overall hydrocarbon degradation. The findings emphasize the significance of native microbial communities as the principal catalysts of petroleum biodegradation in desert soils.

Table 2 presents 16S rDNA sequencing data and GenBank accession numbers for all hydrocarbonoclastic bacterial strains isolated in this study. The taxonomic classifications in the table were derived from the closest matches in the GenBank database. Partial 16S rRNA gene sequencing was sufficient to identify the dominant hydrocarbonoclastic isolates at the genus level, whereas accurate species-level identification requires more sophisticated methods, such as whole-genome sequencing. Nevertheless, genus-level identification sufficed for the ecological and functional assessments presented in this research.

Molecular analysis of bacterial communities

Only two of the inoculant genera, Amycolatopsis and Isoptericola, were detected at any treatment or time point in this study, even though all four were autochthonous. While all soils in this study contained trace amounts of Amycolatopsis and Isoptericola, Amycolatopsis in the Amycolatopsis-treated soils started with an initial 0.5% relative abundance and increased to >4.5% by 12 months, while the soils treated with a mixture of cultures increased from trace amounts of Amycolatopsis to 2.5% over the 12 months.

Overall, the soil communities were represented by three major bacterial phyla: Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria (Figure 3). Together, these three highly diverse and ubiquitous lineages accounted for an average of 89% (sd = 11%) of the microbes detected in the samples. The number of taxa (ASVs) in the communities reached its highest value at time 0. It decreased by an average of 46% over the 12-month period, confirming observations from the cultivation effort. Interestingly, the control group showed the slightest decrease in observed taxa (25%). The only communities that defied this downward trend were those treated with Chelativorans multitrophicus (Che), with a slight increase in the sixth month compared to the initial state (4,415 vs. 3,883 ASVs observed). A comparison of community composition using non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS) indicated that the incubation period was the most prominent factor shaping the communities, rather than the treatments. Considering seasonal variation at each interval will clarify the rationale for the configuration of bacterial communities (Figure 4). In other words, communities subjected to different treatments but sharing the same incubation duration and season exhibited greater similarity to each other than to those with varying incubation lengths and seasons. The dispersal of the clustering pattern with incubation time suggests that the communities may gradually diverge (i.e., become more dissimilar) over time. The finding that microbial communities in these soils are strongly clustered by incubation time and seasonal conditions, rather than by bioaugmentation treatment, suggests that environmental and temporal factors have a greater impact on community assembly. Successional changes in microbial populations are likely driven by seasonal variations in temperature and moisture, as well as by gradual modifications in hydrocarbon composition during biodegradation. These dynamics imply that inoculated strains encounter constantly shifting competitive environments, which limit their ability to establish and survive. As a result, the timing of bioaugmentation relative to seasonal conditions and substrate availability may be just as important as inoculum selection. Such temporal limitations may restrict the long-term influence of inoculation strategies in highly dynamic desert systems by overriding treatment effects.

Oil degradation during bioremediation

Figure 5 illustrates how, over a year of bioremediation, the crude oil that had been mixed with the treated soil samples was dramatically attenuated in all soil samples (ANOVA, n = 3, p < 0.05), including the unbioaugmented (control) sample. Approximately 50% of the additional oil was consumed after six months of bioremediation. Oil consumption ranged between 35.3% in the soil sample inoculated with Amycolatopsis thermoflava and 56.8% in the soil sample inoculated with Chelativorans multitrophicus. The fact that this much oil was consumed in just six months suggests that many favorable conditions favored the oil degraders. First, with respect to environmental factors, bacteria typically require sufficient carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and other trace elements to grow and propagate. However, since carbon and hydrogen are the primary components of petroleum hydrocarbons, the environment requires additional essential elements to support the growth of oil-degrading bacteria.29 Table 1 shows that these elements were readily available and sufficient for the bacteria to bioremediate oil in situ, provided suitable humidity, temperature, and pH conditions were present.

Another critical issue that must be considered as a reason for the high oil consumption rate in the first six months is that oil is a complex mixture of organic components that range in biodegradability from the simplest to the most complicated, as follows: linear alkanes, branched alkanes, low molecular weight alkyl aromatics, monoaromatics, cyclic alkanes, polyaromatics, and asphaltenes.33 Most hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria can completely mineralize chemically simple petroleum hydrocarbons, such as linear alkanes, which comprise 40–60% of oil content,34 as these bacteria possess all the necessary enzymes for the targeted substrate.35,36 Mineralization of this fraction of simple hydrocarbons causes a decline in the amount of oil added.

Figure 6 and Table 3 evaluate the soil sample inoculated with Chelativorans multitrophicus, highlighting differences in its gas chromatography profile between the start and end of the bioremediation process. A decline in resolved n-alkanes (C12–C28) was observed, along with the absence of light fractions after 12 months. Additionally, a significant accumulation of branched alkanes, cycloalkanes, alkyl-naphthene, isoprenoids, and resins resulted in an unresolved complex mixture (UCM) that is not readily biodegradable and accumulates disproportionately after bacteria have depleted more accessible carbon sources. All these factors signify extensive biodegradation.

Although the number of mesophilic and thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria increased in the second half of the bioremediation year (Figure 1), the rate of oil attenuation continued to decline. At the end of the bioremediation process, oil consumption ranged from 41.5% in soil inoculated with the bacterial mixture to 58.6% in soil inoculated with Chelativorans multitrophicus. In the late stages of the bioremediation process, residual complex hydrocarbons, such as polyaromatics and asphaltenes, slow oil consumption, as few bacterial strains can completely mineralize these compounds due to the lack of certain required enzymes.33 It was previously mentioned that Nocardioides spp., which harbor several genes encoding diverse aromatic degradation pathways, predominated the culturable mesophilic and thermophilic communities at the end of the bioremediation process (Figure 2).

In the context of bioremediation research in desert and arid environments, the total petroleum hydrocarbon degradation observed in this study (41.5–58.6% over one year) aligns with the range reported under comparable harsh conditions. Benyahia & Embaby (2016)37 documented a 23% natural attenuation in desert soil over 156 days, while enhanced systems (biostimulation/bioaugmentation) achieved a 77% reduction in TPH during the same period, illustrating the challenges of petroleum remediation in arid environments with limited moisture and nutrient availability. According to other research, native desert soils can effectively self-clean, removing 50–73% of the oil over 6 months of incubation without intensive treatment. This shows that native microbial communities can biodegrade even under high oil loads.20 These comparisons support the conclusion that natural attenuation plays a dominant role under the studied conditions and indicate that the degradation efficiency in the current desert system reflects a relatively strong self-cleaning capacity, especially given the lack of significant environmental manipulation.

Figure 5 shows the mean similarity in oil degradation rates across the six treatments over one year of bioremediation (ANOVA, p > 0.05). This suggests that there is no statistically significant difference in oil degradation rate between bioaugmented and non-bioaugmented soils (StataIC 16). Together with the earlier finding that no inoculants could be cultivated and trace amounts were detected by molecular analysis, these data allow us to conclude that the existing hydrocarbonoclastics played a significant part in the oil biodegradation process rather than the introduced ones.

The absence of established inoculation strains likely illustrates fundamental concepts of ecological competition and niche theory. The competitive exclusion concept posits that organisms occupying identical or significantly overlapping niches, competing for the same limited resources, cannot live eternally; dominant competitors will supplant subordinate ones.38 Niche differentiation and competitive interactions significantly shape soil microbial community structure, with indigenous hydrocarbonoclastic populations adeptly utilizing available substrates and ambient conditions, likely outcompeting foreign strains.39 Moreover, significant functional redundancy among indigenous microorganisms allows numerous native species to perform analogous metabolic processes (e.g., hydrocarbon degradation), hence lowering the ecological niche available for the development of introduced consortia.40 Microbial dominance patterns are further shaped by competitive phenotypes and resource exploitation strategies, with locally adapted taxa frequently predominating in complex communities.41 These dynamics correspond to microbial succession models in which early colonists and established guilds limit niche opportunities for subsequent arrivals.42 These considerations collectively indicate that the dynamic and robust nature of indigenous microbial communities poses a significant obstacle to effective bioaugmentation, even with the use of autochthonous thermotolerant/thermophilic inoculants. In these circumstances, enhancing indigenous microbial populations may be a more efficacious remediation approach than introducing selected isolates. This work verifies the results of a prior study, which employed autochthonous halotolerant/halophilic organisms as inoculants to bioremediate oil-contaminated hypersaline soils.28 Experimental evidence has demonstrated that autochthonous bioaugmentation has a limited impact on oil bioremediation.

Conclusions

Kuwaiti desert soil, despite enduring harsh temperatures for most of the year, is rich in hydrocarbonoclastic microbial communities that can withstand high heat. These communities constantly change both quantitatively and qualitatively, particularly when exposed to complex hydrocarbon mixtures, such as oil. Enhancing the oil-degrading capacity of such soil through indigenous bioaugmentation is challenging. This study showed that indigenous bacterial strains introduced into contaminated desert soils faced significant challenges in surviving and assimilating into pre-existing communities. Changes in microbial community succession during bioaugmentation deplete the introduced inoculum. Nonetheless, oil degradation in all examined soil samples increased, regardless of bioaugmentation, thereby underscoring the importance of natural attenuation and the inherent self-cleaning capacity of damaged ecosystems. The finding that microbial community structure was predominantly influenced by incubation duration and seasonal fluctuations, rather than by bioaugmentation therapy, underscores a significant limitation of bioaugmentation in high-temperature desert environments. The data indicate that temporal dynamics and environmental variability may outweigh treatment effects, reducing the likelihood of sustained inoculum establishment. Therefore, to increase the competitiveness of introduced strains, future bioaugmentation strategies may need to include precise timing of inoculation, environmental modification (such as nutrient or moisture control), or staged application in line with early biodegradation stages. On the other hand, in such temporally dynamic systems, methods that encourage natural microbial populations might be more successful than injection.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations that should be considered are presented in this study. This study was conducted in open desert field conditions, which constrained the implementation of strict abiotic controls and complicated the differentiation between biodegradation and environmental weathering processes, including volatilization and photooxidation. Moreover, the microbial analysis relied partly on culture-dependent methods and partial 16S rRNA gene sequencing, potentially underestimating uncultured hydrocarbon degraders and limiting taxonomic resolution mainly to the genus level. Despite these limitations, the study provides realistic insight into microbial responses and hydrocarbon attenuation under natural desert conditions.

Method details

Sample collection

In winter (January), pristine desert soil samples were collected in sterile containers from the Wafra area, 117.8 km south of Kuwait City (28°38′10.4″N 48°04′31.1″ E). Five sampling points were used to capture the natural variability of microbial ecosystems. Samples were then sent to the laboratory for processing on the same day. pH, moisture, and temperature were measured on-site. In the lab, additional chemical parameters were determined using an ion chromatograph model 850 Professional IC (Metrohm, Switzerland) for anions and a CHNS elemental analyzer (Elementar Unicube, Germany) for other elements.

Inoculant preparation

Based on our previous experiments, four autochthonous thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria were selected as inoculants: Isoptericola variabilis, Chelativorans multitrophicus, Geobacillus kaustophilus, and Amycolatopsis thermoflava.12 Before being included in our private culture collection, these bacterial strains were isolated from the same desert soil and identified via their 16S rRNA gene sequences. Results from previous experiments showed that the selected isolates consumed a significant proportion of the crude oil in the medium after 2 weeks of incubation at 50°C.15 The bacterial strains were cultured in the nutrient broth at 50°C to establish the inoculants. The concentration of the cultures was quantified by cell counting to ensure that 109 cells were added per kilogram of treated soil.

Experimental setup

The five soil samples were mixed and artificially contaminated with 5% (w/w) weathered Kuwaiti light crude oil (WKLCO) from the Kuwait Oil Company (KOC). The mixture was then distributed into six 40 × 40 cm wooden boxes with a 20 cm depth and treated as follows: As a control, one box was maintained without bioaugmentation, four boxes underwent bioaugmentation with individual bacterial strains in a quantity of 109 cells/kg soil, and the last box had bioaugmentation with a combination of the four individual cultures, one-fourth each, to reach the same concentration. The soil boxes were exposed to the open air and kept in a sheltered section of the Faculty of Science’s Botanical Garden for a year. They were watered with distilled water to ensure the soil’s moisture content was at least 30%, as determined by ongoing assessments. The residual oil content and microbial populations were assessed over a one-year period by harvesting three replicates from each box at the start of the experiment and after 6, 9, and 12 months. Upon collecting each sample, the soil in each container was meticulously blended to ensure homogeneity.

Microbial analysis

Counting and identification of culturable mesophilic/thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastics

The dilution-plating method was used to count and isolate mesophilic and thermophilic hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria from the treated soil. Solid mineral media described by Sorkhoh et al. (1990)47 was prepared, and a filter paper soaked with 2 mL (WKLCO) was placed on the plate lid to provide oil vapor as the sole carbon and energy source. At the beginning of the bioaugmentation experiment (Time 0) and after 6, 9, and 12 months, three soil samples were taken from each box, evenly mixed, and serially diluted (10−1-10−5). 0.1 mL aliquots of these sample dilutions were used to inoculate mineral medium plates. For every dilution, three replicates were created. Two sets of plates were prepared for each treated soil, and the plates were incubated for 12 days after being well sealed. One set was incubated at 30°C and the other at 50°C to isolate mesophiles and thermophiles, respectively. For colony-forming unit (CFU) counting, plates with a dilution yielding an acceptable number of colonies (25–250) were selected, and CFUs per gram of soil were determined. Gram staining was used as a preliminary screening method to categorize colonies by Gram reaction and fundamental cellular morphology (e.g., cell shape and organization), following initial selection based on colony appearance. Representative colonies from each morphological category were subsequently purified for additional study.

500 mg of fresh 48-h bacterial biomass was homogenized in 100 μL of PrepMan Ultra Sample Preparation Reagent (Applied Biosystems, USA) and 200 μL of molecular water (Sigma, UK) to extract complete genomic DNA from the isolates for identification. The mixture was then incubated in a water bath at 100°C for 10 min, cooled for 2 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 3 min to collect the DNA-containing supernatant. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to amplify the 16S rRNA genes. The reaction mixture contained PuReTaq Ready-To-Go PCR Beads (cytiva, UK), 1 μL (25 ng) of DNA template, and 1 μL (20 pM/μL) each of the universal primer combinations of the forward primer, GM5F (5′-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′), and the reverse primer, 907 R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTGAGTTT-3′).48 The volume was then made up to 25 μL with molecular water. Amplification was performed using a Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA) via touchdown PCR, with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min. The annealing temperature was set to 65°C and decreased by 1°C per cycle to 55°C, with 15 additional cycles conducted at this temperature. A QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, USA) was used to remove Taq polymerase, primers, and dNTPs from the PCR product. Partial sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene was done using a BigDye version Terminator Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA); 20 ng of the DNA template was added to 2 μL of a Big Dye v 3.1 terminator and 2 μL of Big Dye Terminator v 1.1, v 3.1 5X sequencing buffer; 1 μL of either 907 R or GM5F was added to the mixture, and the final volume was brought up to 10 μL with molecular water. Labeling was completed in a Veriti Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA) with one cycle at 96°C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles of 1 min at 96°C, 5 s at 50°C, and 4 min at 60°C. The pure template DNA samples were processed in a 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) after being purified using the BigDye Xterminator Purification Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). Sequencing analysis (SeqA 7 software) was used to analyze the results. The resulting reads were compared against known sequences in the GenBank database49 using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Website (Bethesda, MD, USA).

Molecular analysis of bacterial communities and bioinformatics

During the 1-year bioaugmentation period, three replicate soil samples were collected from each soil box at the beginning and at 3-month intervals to analyze soil bacterial populations using molecular signatures. Using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP, USA), the total genomic DNA of the soil samples was extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA extracts from each box were pooled, and DNA purity was checked using a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for quantification. Following the manufacturer’s protocols at Mr. DNA (MRDNA, Molecular Research LP, Shallowater, TX, USA), DNA samples were sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq platform with 16S rRNA gene primers and data processing protocols as previously described.50,51 Briefly, libraries were processed with cutadapt v2.7,43 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using dada2 v1.14,44 and taxonomic classification was performed using the SILVA v138 database.52 Community statistics and visualization were performed using the R packages phyloseq and ggplot2.45,46

Extraction and analysis of residual oil

Five grams of soil were taken from each soil box in triplicate. Three separate extractions with 15 mL of methylene chloride (dichloromethane) were used to recover the residual oil. The extracts were combined, and the volume was adjusted to 50 mL with methylene chloride. The extracts were dried by passing them through an anhydrous sodium sulfate column, then carefully evaporated under nitrogen gas to approximately 2 mL, taking care not to dry them completely.

Subsequently, 1 μL of the extract was analyzed by Gas–Liquid Chromatography (GLC) using an Agilent 7890A GLC system (USA) equipped with FID, a DB-5 capillary column (Agilent Technologies, USA), and helium as the carrier gas. The oven temperature was set to 50°C for 3 min, then increased at 3°C/min to 80°C, then at 8°C/min to 256°C, and finally at 30°C/min to 330°C, where it was maintained for 11 min. The percentage of hydrocarbon consumption was calculated as the reduction in total peak area in the samples relative to the controls (at the initial time point in each box). Hopane was used as a stable internal standard to quantify hydrocarbon consumption.53

Quantification and statistical analysis

All determined chemical parameters are the mean of 5 samples, ±SD. Oil consumption values are means of 3 replicates, ±SD; these were mentioned in the figure legend. Sequences of isolated strains were analyzed using the SeqA 7 software for identification. Community statistics and visualization were performed using the R packages phyloseq and ggplot2.
