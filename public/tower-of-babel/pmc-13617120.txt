Case Report: Primary ovarian pulmonary-type small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma mimicking benign adnexal torsion: a diagnostic challenge
Authors: Sijia Xu, Pinqiu Lv, Na Li, Bin Wang, Xuan Du, Zhitao Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell carcinoma of the ovary, pulmonary type (SCCOPT), is a rare and aggressive ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma. Because its clinical and radiological features overlap with benign adnexal conditions and metastatic neuroendocrine tumors, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. Introduction

Ovarian neuroendocrine neoplasms (O-NENs) are rare tumors, accounting for less than 1% of ovarian malignancies (1). Small cell carcinoma of the ovary, pulmonary type (SCCOPT), is an exceptionally rare and aggressive subtype of ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma, with only a limited number of reported cases and generally poor outcomes (2–4).

The current WHO classification recognizes small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type (SCCOHT), as a distinct SMARCA4-deficient ovarian tumor entity rather than an ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma (5). SCCOHT typically occurs in younger women and is characterized by SMARCA4 inactivation, whereas SCCOPT is an exceptionally rare ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma that predominantly occurs in older, postmenopausal women (4, 6). Histologically, SCCOPT resembles small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) (7, 8). Diagnosis requires exclusion of metastatic extra-ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma and distinction from other ovarian small cell tumors, particularly SCCOHT (9).

Because SCCOPT lacks specific clinical and radiological features, preoperative diagnosis remains difficult. Most patients present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal distension, pelvic pain, or vaginal bleeding (7). In contrast, acute abdomen caused by adnexal torsion, tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum is uncommon in ovarian malignancies. Malignant tumors are generally considered less prone to torsion because adhesions and infiltrative growth may limit ovarian mobility (10, 11). Thus, the coexistence of torsion, rupture, and hemoperitoneum may obscure the malignant nature of the lesion and delay recognition of the underlying tumor. Although acute abdomen is a well-recognized presentation of benign adnexal disorders, it is rarely reported as the initial manifestation of SCCOPT. Only a limited number of SCCOPT cases presenting with acute abdomen have been described (12, 13). This unusual presentation represents a diagnostic pitfall, as emergency clinical scenarios may divert attention from a possible ovarian malignancy.

Given its rarity, no standardized treatment has been established for SCCOPT. Current management is largely based on strategies used for SCLC and other high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas, with surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy commonly adopted, although the optimal regimen remains uncertain.

Herein, we report a case of primary ovarian SCCOPT presenting as acute abdomen caused by adnexal torsion, tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum. This case emphasizes that ovarian malignancy should be considered even when the initial presentation resembles a benign gynecologic emergency. The diagnosis was established through integrated evaluation of imaging findings, pathological examination, molecular profiling, and comprehensive exclusion of an extra-ovarian primary tumor.

Case presentation

Patient history and clinical findings

A 61-year-old postmenopausal woman (G1P1) presented to the emergency department on January 30, 2026, with acute abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and one episode of syncope. She reported a 1-week history of intermittent lower abdominal discomfort, which had significantly worsened over the preceding 5 h. She denied abnormal vaginal bleeding, vaginal discharge, fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, or urinary complaints. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, gynecological disease, or malignancy. There was no personal or family history of ovarian, breast, colorectal, or other malignancies.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed mild abdominal distension and lower abdominal tenderness without rebound tenderness or guarding. Laboratory and imaging findings

Initial laboratory evaluation showed no evidence of anemia. Serum CA-125 and HE4 levels were mildly elevated, while other tumor markers were within normal limits.

Transvaginal ultrasonography revealed a cystic-solid mass in the left adnexal region measuring approximately 8.2 × 6.0 cm, with internal vascularity. Moderate pelvic free fluid was observed, with a maximal depth of approximately 5.9 cm. Given the acute abdominal symptoms and episode of syncope, intra-abdominal bleeding was suspected, and hemoperitoneum was subsequently confirmed during surgery (Figures 2A,B).

The lesion measured approximately 8.2 × 6.0 cm and was accompanied by pelvic free fluid. (C–F) Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a multiloculated cystic-solid mass arising from the left adnexa. The lesion showed heterogeneous signal intensity, restricted diffusion of the solid component, and marked enhancement of the cyst wall, septa, and solid portions, producing a honeycomb-like appearance.

Contrast-enhanced pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a multiloculated cystic-solid mass arising from the left adnexa, measuring approximately 64 × 63 × 90 mm. The lesion showed multiple cystic components and a relatively well-defined margin. The solid components exhibited restricted diffusion with high signal intensity on diffusion-weighted imaging. After contrast administration, the cyst wall, septa, and solid components showed marked enhancement, resulting in a honeycomb-like appearance. Based on the multiloculated cystic-solid morphology and enhancement pattern, an adult granulosa cell tumor was considered the leading differential diagnosis; however, ovarian malignancy could not be excluded (Figures 2C–F).

Surgical findings and management

Given the acute abdominal presentation, adnexal mass, and suspected hemoperitoneum, urgent surgical intervention was indicated. As the patient remained hemodynamically stable without evidence of ongoing massive hemorrhage, pelvic MRI was performed before surgery to further characterize the lesion and facilitate surgical planning.

Exploratory laparoscopy was performed on February 2, 2026. Upon entry into the peritoneal cavity, approximately 550 mL of hemoperitoneum was identified and evacuated. The left ovarian tumor was found to be torsed approximately 180 at the pedicle, with evidence of preoperative capsular rupture and active bleeding (Figures 3A,B). The uterus and contralateral adnexa were grossly unremarkable, and no obvious extra-ovarian lesions were identified within the abdominal or pelvic cavity.

Intraoperative laparoscopic findings. (A) Laparoscopic exploration revealed an enlarged hemorrhagic left ovarian tumor associated with hemoperitoneum. (B) A capsular defect with active bleeding was identified on the tumor surface, consistent with preoperative tumor rupture.

Peritoneal fluid and lavage cytology showed no malignant cells. Intraoperative frozen section examination suggested a malignant ovarian tumor. Therefore, comprehensive laparoscopic staging surgery was performed, including total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy, peritoneal lavage, and systematic exploration of the abdominal and pelvic cavity.

The procedure was completed laparoscopically without intraoperative complications. Estimated blood loss was approximately 200 mL, and no allogeneic blood transfusion was required. According to the FIGO staging system, the tumor was classified as stage IC2 based on preoperative tumor rupture. Peritoneal cytology was negative, and pelvic lymph node dissection showed no metastatic involvement (0/13). No gross extra-ovarian disease was identified.

Pathological and immunohistochemical findings

Gross examination revealed a left adnexal tumor measuring approximately 12 × 8 × 5 cm with a cystic-solid appearance, accompanied by extensive hemorrhage and necrosis.

Microscopically, the tumor was composed of high-grade small round cells arranged in diffuse sheets and nests. The tumor cells exhibited scant cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei, nuclear molding, frequent mitotic activity, and necrosis (Figure 4A).

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated diffuse expression of neuroendocrine markers, including synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56, with a high Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 70% (Figures 4B–E). Focal nuclear positivity for PAX8 was identified, supporting Müllerian differentiation. TTF-1 was negative in tumor cells, arguing against a pulmonary primary origin (Figure 4F). Complete loss of Rb expression and aberrant p53 overexpression were observed. SMARCA4 immunostaining showed retained nuclear expression, helping to exclude SCCOHT (Figures 4G–I).

Taken together, the morphological, immunohistochemical, and clinical findings supported the diagnosis of small cell carcinoma of the ovary, pulmonary type (SCCOPT).

Molecular findings

Paired tumor–blood next-generation sequencing revealed an HRD-negative profile, with a genomic instability score (GIS) of 37, below the predefined HRD positivity threshold of 43. No pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in BRCA1 or BRCA2.

A somatic TP53 p.M237I mutation was identified, with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 86.78%, consistent with the mutant-type p53 overexpression observed by immunohistochemistry. In addition, a germline heterozygous MSH2 p.T933I variant was detected and classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). No pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variants were identified.

The sequencing panel included MMR-related genes; however, the analysis was limited to DNA-level alterations. Mismatch repair (MMR) protein immunohistochemistry and microsatellite instability (MSI) testing were not performed; therefore, the functional significance of the MSH2 p.T933I variant remains uncertain.

Postoperative workup for exclusion of extra-ovarian primary tumors

Postoperative ^18F-FDG PET-CT was performed on February 12, 2026, to evaluate the possibility of an extra-ovarian primary tumor. No definite extra-ovarian primary lesion was identified. Mild FDG uptake (SUVmax 1.55) was observed in the gastric pylorus, whereas a 3-mm right upper lobe pulmonary nodule showed no abnormal FDG uptake.

Subsequent gastroscopy and colonoscopy were performed on March 19, 2026. Gastric biopsy demonstrated chronic inflammatory changes and benign fundic gland polyps without evidence of malignancy. Colonoscopic examination was unremarkable.

Overall, these investigations identified no evidence of an extra-ovarian primary tumor. Although an occult primary site cannot be completely excluded, the comprehensive postoperative evaluation, together with the clinical, pathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular findings, strongly supported the diagnosis of primary ovarian pulmonary-type small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCCOPT).

Postoperative course and oncologic management

The postoperative course was uneventful. Following multidisciplinary team discussion, adjuvant systemic chemotherapy was initiated with carboplatin (AUC 5, day 1) and etoposide (100 mg/m2, days 1–3), administered every three weeks.

At the latest follow-up (June 2026), the patient had completed six cycles of chemotherapy without experiencing grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT performed before the sixth cycle (June 11, 2026) demonstrated postoperative changes at the ovarian resection site without abnormal enhancement and no radiological evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis. The patient remained alive without clinically evident disease recurrence at the latest follow-up.

Discussion

Small cell carcinoma of the ovary, pulmonary type (SCCOPT), is an exceptionally rare and aggressive ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma, for which clinical experience and comprehensive molecular characterization remain limited (1, 14). In a population-based study of 166 patients with ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma, Zhu et al. reported aggressive clinical behavior and poor survival outcomes compared with most epithelial ovarian cancer subtypes (15). We report a case of primary ovarian SCCOPT presenting as acute abdomen caused by adnexal torsion, preoperative tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum, thereby mimicking a benign gynecological emergency. Although adnexal torsion and spontaneous tumor rupture have been reported in ovarian neoplasms, the simultaneous occurrence of adnexal torsion, tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum is uncommon in malignant ovarian tumors and has rarely been described in SCCOPT. Currently, no well-established risk factors have been identified for SCCOPT. Advanced age and postmenopausal status are frequently reported characteristics, although their role as true risk factors remains uncertain (4, 7). Unlike some ovarian malignancies with known hereditary or reproductive associations, no specific predisposing factors have been established for SCCOPT.

Due to its extreme rarity, SCCOPT has been described mainly in individual case reports and small series. Reported patients are typically older, postmenopausal women (4, 7). Histologically, SCCOPT resembles small cell lung carcinoma, showing small hyperchromatic cells, nuclear molding, frequent mitotic activity, and necrosis (8). Immunohistochemically, neuroendocrine markers such as synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56 are commonly expressed, whereas exclusion of metastatic extra-ovarian neuroendocrine carcinoma remains essential (16). Molecular data remain limited, and current treatment strategies are largely based on surgical resection followed by platinum-based chemotherapy; however, optimal management and prognostic factors remain unclear (1–3). Malignant ovarian tumors are generally considered less susceptible to torsion because infiltrative growth, pelvic adhesions, and reduced tumor mobility may limit adnexal rotation. Adnexal torsion is more frequently associated with benign ovarian lesions; however, torsion in postmenopausal women remains clinically important because malignant pathology, although uncommon, may occasionally be identified in this population (17). Consequently, when torsion is accompanied by hemorrhagic changes and acute abdominal symptoms, the underlying malignant nature of the lesion may be obscured by an apparently benign emergency presentation. Furthermore, the imaging manifestations of adnexal torsion are variable and may overlap with those of other complex adnexal lesions, contributing to preoperative diagnostic uncertainty (18). In the present case, although preoperative MRI demonstrated a complex cystic-solid adnexal mass with suspicious enhancing solid components, the coexistence of torsion, tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum initially favored the diagnosis of a benign adnexal emergency rather than an aggressive ovarian malignancy. This case highlights the importance of maintaining ovarian malignancy in the differential diagnosis of postmenopausal women presenting with acute abdominal pain, adnexal masses, and pelvic free fluid, even when the initial clinical and radiological impression favors benign adnexal torsion.

The major diagnostic challenge in this case was establishing the primary ovarian origin of the neuroendocrine carcinoma. Histologically, SCCOPT shares substantial morphological overlap with small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and other high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas; therefore, exclusion of metastatic disease is essential before assigning an ovarian primary diagnosis (7, 8, 16). In our patient, the diagnosis was established through integration of the clinical presentation, radiological findings, histopathological features, immunophenotype, molecular profile, and comprehensive postoperative investigations.

Histopathologically, the tumor exhibited classic high-grade small-cell features, including diffuse sheets and nests of tumor cells, nuclear molding, frequent mitotic activity, and extensive necrosis. Immunohistochemically, diffuse expression of synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and CD56 confirmed neuroendocrine differentiation, while the high Ki-67 proliferation index supported the highly proliferative nature of the tumor. Focal PAX8 expression supported Müllerian differentiation, a finding compatible with an ovarian primary in the appropriate clinicopathological context (19). In contrast, negative TTF-1 expression, together with the absence of a detectable pulmonary primary lesion on postoperative PET–CT, argued against metastatic SCLC, although neither marker alone is sufficient to determine the site of origin. Although focal CK20 and SATB2 expression raised consideration of a gastrointestinal primary tumor, subsequent gastroscopy, colonoscopy, and imaging evaluation revealed no evidence of an extra-ovarian primary malignancy. Furthermore, retained SMARCA4 expression helped distinguish this tumor from small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type (SCCOHT), in which SMARCA4 inactivation is characteristic (6, 20). Taken together, these clinicopathological, immunophenotypic, and radiological findings strongly supported the diagnosis of primary ovarian SCCOPT. The comprehensive postoperative evaluation—including PET–CT, gastroscopy, and colonoscopy—further supported an ovarian primary origin by excluding clinically detectable pulmonary, gastrointestinal, and other common primary sites, although an occult extra-ovarian primary cannot be completely excluded.

The genomic architecture of SCCOPT remains largely uncharacterized, with comprehensive molecular profiling reported in only a limited number of cases (1, 2). Recent molecular data suggest that SCCOPT shares some biological features with small cell lung carcinoma, although the basis of these similarities remains unclear (21). Molecular testing may complement histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation by providing additional insights into diagnostic assessment, molecular characterization, and treatment considerations. In particular, SMARCA4 status may provide additional support in distinguishing SCCOPT from SCCOHT, which is characterized by SMARCA4 inactivation (5, 6). TP53 pathway alterations have been described in ovarian pulmonary-type neuroendocrine carcinomas, although their biological and prognostic significance remains uncertain (1, 7). In our patient, paired tumor–blood next-generation sequencing demonstrated an HRD-negative, BRCA1/2 wild-type profile. The principal somatic alteration was a TP53 p.M237I mutation with a high variant allele frequency, which correlated with the mutant-type p53 overexpression observed by immunohistochemistry. Complete loss of Rb expression was also identified. This concurrent disruption of TP53 and Rb, a molecular feature frequently observed in SCLC, may partly contribute to the aggressive biological behavior of SCCOPT (22). However, whether these alterations have independent prognostic significance remains unknown and will require additional molecularly characterized cases with long-term follow-up.

Because SCCOPT is exceedingly rare, treatment recommendations remain largely empirical, and no standard therapeutic strategy has been established (1, 3). Current management is generally extrapolated from SCLC and other high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas, with complete surgical resection followed by platinum-based chemotherapy representing the most widely adopted approach (1, 3). Following intraoperative suspicion of malignancy, our patient underwent comprehensive surgical staging and subsequently received six cycles of carboplatin plus etoposide. Treatment was well tolerated, with no grade 3 or higher adverse events, and follow-up imaging demonstrated no evidence of recurrence or distant metastasis. Carboplatin was selected instead of cisplatin to improve postoperative tolerability while preserving the established platinum–etoposide backbone. In this patient, the HRD-negative and BRCA1/2 wild-type profile did not provide a molecular rationale for considering PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This report describes a single patient with relatively short follow-up, precluding any firm conclusions regarding long-term prognosis. Next-generation sequencing identified a germline MSH2 variant of uncertain significance. However, because MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI testing were not performed, the clinical relevance of this variant cannot be fully determined. Nevertheless, this case contributes additional clinicopathological and molecular evidence to the limited literature on SCCOPT and underscores an important diagnostic pitfall: this rare tumor may present as a benign-appearing surgical emergency.

This case underscores that ovarian malignancy should remain in the differential diagnosis of postmenopausal women presenting with acute abdominal pain, an adnexal mass, and pelvic free fluid, even when the initial clinical impression favors benign adnexal torsion. Accurate diagnosis of SCCOPT requires integration of the clinical presentation, imaging findings, histopathology, immunophenotype, molecular profile, and systematic exclusion of an extra-ovarian primary tumor. Increased awareness of this diagnostic pitfall may facilitate earlier recognition of this rare malignancy in emergency settings.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of primary ovarian SCCOPT presenting with acute pelvic pain, adnexal torsion, tumor rupture, and hemoperitoneum, mimicking a benign gynecological emergency. This case highlights that malignancy should remain an important consideration in postmenopausal women with adnexal masses and acute abdominal symptoms, even when torsion is initially suspected.

Accurate diagnosis requires an integrated approach combining imaging, histopathology, immunohistochemistry, molecular profiling, and comprehensive exclusion of extra-ovarian primary tumors. Given the extreme rarity of SCCOPT, standardized treatment strategies have not yet been established. Additional well-characterized cases with long-term follow-up are needed to better define its biological behavior and guide clinical management.
