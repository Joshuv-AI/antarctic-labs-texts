Alleviation of lower-extremity venous symptoms with ablation of truncal superficial veins is not dependent upon junctional reflux
Authors: Harold J. Welch
Published: 2026

The American Venous Forum (AVF) has sponsored another excellent trial meant to answer important clinical questions. The current trial, JURY-2 (Effect of junctional reflux on the Venous Clinical Severity Score in patients with insufficiency of the great saphenous Vein), is a follow-up to the 2024 JURY study (Effect of JUnctional Reflux on the Venous Clinical SeveritY Score), which determined that the presence or absence of junctional reflux had no bearing on the clinical status of a leg with varicose veins.1 This separate follow-up study has shown that endovenous ablation of the great saphenous vein (GSV) (thermal and nonthermal) provides similar excellent improvement in the Venous Clinical Severity Score whether there is junctional reflux or not.

The authors' subtlety pointed out in their introduction that patients with isolated GSV reflux were often not able to receive optimal treatment—that is, ablation of an incompetent GSV. They were not able to receive such treatment because payers determined that junctional reflux had to be present (without evidence) to provide coverage. A number of physicians who are experts in the treatment of venous disease have had conversations in the past with payers, and were told that, for payer's policies to change, they needed evidence. Well, again, they have it. Just as in the original JURY trial, junctional reflux has no bearing on clinical symptoms or treatment results. To their credit, several payers have already eliminated the criterion of junctional reflux for coverage of endovenous ablation, but all the rest need to eliminate it as well, and stop this as a barrier to optimal care.

This study, like the previous JURY study, has once again illustrated how the AVF, through its leadership and Research Committee, and the obtainment of research grants, can inspire, develop, and deliver outstanding studies that provide evidence to clinical questions that allow practitioners to provide the best care possible to their patients. The AVF is to be congratulated.

Of course, one cannot read a study and not have additional questions. In my opinion, the addition of concomitant procedures (phlebectomy, sclerotherapy) to endovenous ablation can cloud the results of the ablation procedure itself. Twenty percent of patients in the GSV reflux group and 45% of the GSV + junctional reflux group underwent concomitant procedures. Although I suspect the results would not be particularly different, it would be interesting to see how the patients who underwent ablation alone compared. And, because the authors have the patient data, it would be a nice follow-up study to see if the patients with junctional reflux at the time of ablation develop more recurrences and/or anterior saphenous vein incompetence in the future. I am looking forward to more clinically relevant studies from the AVF.
