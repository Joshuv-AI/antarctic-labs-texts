CXCL10 promotes non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) progression by modulating the phosphate to urate ratio (PUR)
Authors: Yonglun Zhang, Qian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to explore the causal relationships and mechanisms between NSCLC-related genes, metabolites, and NSCLC using Mendelian Randomization (MR). The study employed differential gene expression analysis, two-sample MR analysis, and mediation analysis, utilizing NSCLC Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) data from the Finngen database, gene data from the eQTLGen and pQTL databases, and NSCLC expression data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. First, two-sample MR analysis was performed to evaluate the causal relationships between expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) genes and NSCLC. Violin plots were then used to validate the expression levels of these genes in NSCLC. Finally, mediation analysis was conducted to explore the mediating role of metabolites in the relationship between gene expression and NSCLC development. Our study found a significant positive association between high expression of the CXCL10 gene and increased NSCLC risk. Further mediation analysis revealed that CXCL10 promotes NSCLC development by regulating the PUR. Specifically, the mediation effect was 0.039, with a mediation proportion of 25.4% and a P-value of 0.012. This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of CXCL10 in NSCLC and suggests its potential as a biomarker for future targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04151-1.Keywords: Metabolites, Non-small cell lung cancer, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) ranks as one of the top causes of cancer-related mortality globally, contributing to approximately 12.9% of all cancer deaths, with an estimated 1.3 million fatalities each year [1–3]. Among these cases, about 80% to 85% are diagnosed as NSCLC [3–5]. LC is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, which greatly limits treatment options. The median survival time for patients is approximately 18 months, with the five-year survival rate ranging from 15% to 21%, depending on gender [6, 7]. Unfortunately, approximately 75% of NSCLC patients are diagnosed at a late stage or after metastasis has occurred, resulting in a five-year survival rate of less than 5% [1]. Despite significant progress in understanding the genetic, molecular, and biological mechanisms of LC, current treatments are still insufficient. Metabolites are gaining attention for their involvement in LC development, and numerous studies have been carried out to identify potential biomarkers in bodily fluids such as blood and urine. These biomarkers could aid in early LC detection [8–14], tumor characterization [15, 16], and tracking disease progression [8, 11]. However, there is a lack of relevant research on how genes influence the mechanism of NSCLC by regulating metabolites. This study aims to fill this gap.

Metabolites, which are the byproducts of various metabolic processes, are increasingly recognized as valuable in disease research and clinical diagnosis, reflecting both the physiological and pathological conditions of organisms [17]. By measuring and analyzing metabolite levels and their fluctuations in body fluids or tissues, we can uncover the molecular mechanisms driving disease onset and progression, and identify potential biomarkers for early detection and prognosis [18, 19]. Metabolomics has proven to be vital in studying a range of diseases, including cancer, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions [20–22]. Although metabolite identification and validation have a long history, a major challenge in biomarker validation lies in accounting for the variations in metabolite levels among individuals, caused by genetic and environmental factors [23, 24]. Identifying metabolites and exploring their biological roles is crucial in cancer research, with many studies emphasizing the significant potential of metabolomics in this field [25, 26].

MR is a novel analytical method that utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as natural randomization tools to examine potential causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. It has been widely applied in studies of various diseases to identify risk factors and underlying pathogenic mechanisms [27–29]. Recent advances have emphasized that incorporating eQTL information into MR greatly enhances causal inference by linking genetic variants to gene expression, thereby elucidating the transcriptional mechanisms underlying complex traits and diseases [30, 31]. This integration improves the interpretability of mediation analyses and provides a deeper mechanistic understanding of how specific genes influence disease development through downstream metabolic pathways. In this study, MR methods were applied for the first time to integrate GWAS, eQTL, and pQTL data together with 1,400 metabolites, aiming to identify novel therapeutic targets and their potential metabolic pathways in NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Differential expression analysis

We downloaded microarray gene expression data for NSCLC from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE18842 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The differential expression grouping condition was: diseased tissue vs. normal tissue (control). This dataset includes 46 diseased tissue samples, 45 normal tissue samples, and a total of 91 samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package, with genes considered differentially expressed if they met the following criteria: absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) greater than 0.5 and a p-value less than 0.05.

eQTL data set

In this study, summary-level cis-eQTL data for all genes were downloaded from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://www.eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html) [32]. All data are cis-eQTLs, derived from blood samples. We filtered the instrumental variables (IVs) according to the following parameters: p-value < 5e-8, clumping window: 10,000 kb, r² < 0.1. After applying these filters, a total of 15,695 genes with cis-eQTL data remained.

pQTL data set

pQTL summary statistics were obtained from the deCODE genetics plasma proteomics resource (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/) [33]. A total of 4907 raw files were downloaded, all from the Icelandic population. We then extracted cis-pQTLs according to the following criteria: (1) SNP and gene are located on the same chromosome; (2) SNP is within a ± 1 Mb range of the gene. This resulted in 4706 cis-pQTL files. These 4,706 cis-pQTL files were further filtered to select IVs based on the following parameters: p-value < 5e-8, clumping window: 10,000 kb, r² < 0.1. After filtering, 1614 cis-pQTL data files remained.

Outcome dataset

GWAS summary statistics for NSCLC were downloaded from the FinnGen Study, Data Freeze 12 (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results), and used as the outcome dataset. The dataset includes a total of 38,519 participants, with 6,446 in the disease group and 37,874 in the control group. All participants are of European descent.

Two-sample MR analysis

This study used the TwoSampleMR package (R 4.3.2) to perform a two-sample MR analysis. The analysis was divided into three main parts. In the first part, cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data corresponding to differential genes were used as exposures, with NSCLC as the outcome. In the second part, 978 filtered metabolites were used as exposures, and NSCLC was the outcome. In the third part, the target gene was used as the exposure, and metabolites were used as the outcome. To reduce bias from weak IVs, variables with an F-value less than 10 were excluded before further analysis. In this study, inverse variance weighted (IVW) was used as the main method, and exposures that could not calculate IVW results were excluded. The MR-Egger intercept test was employed to identify pleiotropy, while Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.All of our analyses adhered to the three fundamental assumptions of MR, as illustrated in Fig. 1A.

Mediation analysis

A two-step MR mediation analysis was performed to explore whether target genes influence the promotion or inhibition of NSCLC through the regulation of metabolites. 1400 metabolite data were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) with dataset IDs GCST90199621 to GCST90201020 [34]. SNPs were selected based on the following criteria: p-value < 5e-8, clumping window: 10,000 kb, r² < 0.1, resulting in 978 metabolites. The effect of the target gene eQTL on NSCLC was calculated as beta_all, the effect of the target gene eQTL on metabolites as beta1, and the effect of metabolites on NSCLC as beta2. The mediation effect (beta12) was then computed as beta1 × beta2, and the mediation proportion (beta12_p) was calculated as (beta12/beta_all) × 100% (Fig. 1B). Mediators were selected based on the criteria: p-value < 0.05, mediation proportion > 10%, and consistent direction of the mediation effect with the total effect.

Results

Screening and selection of target genes

We intersected the differential genes with cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data, resulting in 437 intersecting genes (Fig. 2A). The cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data corresponding to the differential genes were used as exposures, and NSCLC was used as the outcome for two-sample MR analysis. The results are shown in S1 and S2 Table. We then intersected the cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL genes associated with NSCLC with the differential genes, and the results are shown in Fig. 2B and C. Among these, CTSS, NAAA, and SECTM1 were found to inhibit the development of NSCLC, while CBR1 and CXCL10 were found to promote NSCLC progression. Ultimately, we selected CXCL10 as the target gene. To validate our results, the expression of CXCL10 in the GSE18842 dataset and the GEPIA2 online analysis platform (GEPIA2 web server, http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) is shown in Fig. 3, which demonstrates that CXCL10 is upregulated in the disease group (p < 0.05), consistent with our MR analysis results.

The mechanism of action of CXCL10

We divided this analysis into three parts. In the first step, 978 filtered metabolites were used as exposures, and NSCLC was used as the outcome for two-sample MR bulk analysis. The results showed that 82 metabolites were associated with the disease (S3 Table). In the second step, CXCL10 eQTL was used as the exposure, and the 82 metabolites associated with NSCLC were used as the outcome for two-sample MR analysis. The results showed that CXCL10 eQTL was associated with 15 metabolites (S4 Table). In the third step, a mediation analysis was conducted, and the results showed that the PUR served as a significant mediator in the process by which CXCL10 eQTL promotes the development of NSCLC. The results were as follows: beta12_p = 25.4%, p = 0.026, beta1 = 0.165, beta2 = 0.239, beta12 = 0.040 (Table 1). The MR analysis results for CXCL10 eQTL and NSCLC, the MR analysis results for CXCL10 eQTL and PUR, and the analysis results for PUR and NSCLC are shown in Fig. 4. The related forest plots, scatter plots, and leave-one-out analysis are shown in S1 Fig. Sensitivity analysis, including pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and leave-one-out analysis, demonstrated the robustness of the results S5 Table and S1 Fig. In conclusion, we ultimately identified that CXCL10 eQTL promotes the development of NSCLC through the PUR.

Discussion

Chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 10 (CXCL10), also referred to as interferon γ-induced protein 10 (IP-10), is a secreted protein produced by various cell types, including monocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and cancer cells [35]. CXCL10 is a member of the CXC chemokine family and serves as a ligand for the CXCR3 receptor [36]. It also interacts with and activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [37]. Recent research has demonstrated a strong link between the CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling pathway and inflammation as well as tumor development [38]. CXCL10 not only activates effector Th1 cells but also attracts CXCR3 + CD8 + T cells to tumor sites, triggering granzyme-B production in these cells to enhance the antitumor response [39]. Studies have shown that the CXCL10 ligand contributes to the development and progression of various inflammatory diseases and cancers by recruiting CXCR3-expressing T cells, B cells, monocytes/macrophages, and NK cells. CXCL10 is highly expressed in the tissues of gastric, breast, lung, and osteosarcoma tumors and is strongly linked to the malignant characteristics of these cancers [40]. Notably, CXCL10 can exhibit context-dependent effects in cancer [40]. On the one hand, transient or localized CXCL10 production enhances immune surveillance by recruiting CXCR3 + effector T cells and NK cells to tumors and augmenting antitumor responses [41]. On the other hand, sustained or dysregulated CXCL10–CXCR3 signaling can propagate tumor-promoting programs—e.g., activating MAPK/p38 or PI3K–Akt cascades in myeloid and/or tumor compartments—thereby fostering proliferation, immune suppression, and angiogenesis [42]. This duality highlights the need to interpret CXCL10 within its spatial and temporal microenvironmental context.

Recent studies have highlighted CXCL10’s significant role in advancing lung cancer (LC) progression. One study showed that tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE) induces Arginase-1 (Arg-1) M2 polarization of macrophages through CXCL9/CXCL10, which in turn promotes LC progression via autophagy/E-cadherin signaling [43]. Another study suggests that CXCL10 may act as a negative prognostic factor in NSCLC patients [44]. Our findings provide a mechanistic complement to these observations, suggesting that CXCL10 contributes to NSCLC progression not only through immune modulation but also through metabolic regulation.

Our study found that CXCL10 promotes the onset of NSCLC by regulating the increase in the PUR. This is the first time we have explored the downstream mechanism of CXCL10 on NSCLC through metabolites as intermediaries. Inorganic phosphate (Pi) is essential for all living organisms, supporting various cellular functions such as mineral metabolism, energy production from membrane phospholipids and nucleotides, and acting as a substrate for phosphorylated intermediates in cell signaling [45]. Recent studies indicate that abnormal Pi intake is strongly associated with the development of several types of cancer [46]. Pi plays a vital role in gene regulation during the cell cycle, as well as in signal transduction and transcriptional control in vitro [47]. Moreover, a high-phosphate diet (HPD) has been shown to enhance lung tumorigenesis through altered Akt signaling [48]. Another study suggests that the lungs exhibit a homeostatic response to elevated Pi levels. While excessive Pi intake initially promoted LC progression, the subsequent decline in tumorigenesis was likely due to metabolic adaptation, leading to tumor cell quiescence. This adaptation may eventually contribute to more severe cancer later on [49]. The link between metabolic changes and cancer has gained significant attention in recent years [50]. Uric acid (UA), a product of purine metabolism, serves as a potent antioxidant in the body [51]. It controls the activity of superoxide dismutase, neutralizes free radicals, and binds transition metals, thereby safeguarding cells from oxidative harm [52]. Purine bases are transformed into uric acid (UA) by xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR), which also generates other antioxidants that play a role in the innate immune system [53]. A previous MR analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between UA levels and LC risk, suggesting that UA may act as a protective factor against LC development and is associated with better prognosis in LC patients with higher blood UA levels [54]. Although previous studies have explored the roles of phosphate and urate in LC, the causal relationship and underlying mechanisms of the PUR in LC have not been studied. This study is the first to propose that the PUR plays a promoting role in the development of NSCLC and is regulated by CXCL10.

Taken together, these findings suggest that CXCL10 may act as a context-dependent regulator linking immune signaling and metabolic reprogramming. Therapeutically, modulation of the CXCL10/PUR axis could offer a novel strategy to suppress tumor-promoting inflammation while preserving its beneficial immune functions. Future studies integrating metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics, and single-cell sequencing could help delineate how CXCL10 signaling shapes the tumor microenvironment and identify potential combination therapies targeting both metabolic and immunologic pathways.

Limitations

The analysis in this study involves a two-sample MR method, using data from large GWAS cohorts with 1,400 metabolites, and combined with eQTLGen and pQTL databases for multiple validations. The study demonstrates considerable statistical power and consistency. However, our study also has certain limitations. Firstly, despite our efforts to identify and remove outlier variants, the potential influence of horizontal pleiotropy on the results cannot be ruled out. Secondly, the use of summary-level statistics rather than individual-level data limits our ability to explore causal relationships among subgroups, such as females and males. Thirdly, as all participants in the FINNGEN cohort are of European ancestry, potential population heterogeneity should be considered when generalizing our findings. Validation in diverse and independent cohorts (e.g., TCGA or UK Biobank) is warranted. Finally, our findings represent an overall estimate for non-small cell lung cancer. Future studies are warranted to conduct subtype-specific analyses in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) to further elucidate the subtype-dependent mechanisms of CXCL10.

Conclusion

This article reveals how CXCL10 regulates the PUR to influence the occurrence of NSCLC through MR and mediation analysis. These findings provide new insights into the pathogenesis of NSCLC and offer ideas for new therapeutic targets and future research directions.
