Comparison of accuracy and axial length acquisition success rate by 3 types of SS-OCT–based biometers
Authors: Yukihito Kato, Masahiko Ayaki, Yoshiki Tanaka, Akeno Tamaoki, Yukihiro Sakai, Kei Ichikawa, Kazuo Ichikawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An AL acquisition success rate by ARGOS, IOLMaster 700, and Anterion was compared, and the results of ARGOS were highest even with mature cataract and very dense nuclear sclerosis.

Modern cataract surgery has become a relevant option for refractive surgery. Importantly, refraction after surgery should be matched to the patient's expectations. To determine the power of the optimal intraocular lens (IOL), accurate preoperative biometric measurements are necessary. Axial length (AL) measurement has evolved from ultrasonic to optical methods, and since the launch of the first optical biometer using the time-domain method in 1999, the prevalence of postoperative refractive errors caused by AL has decreased.1,2 The most remarkable advantage of ultrasonic measurement is that AL is measurable in any eye; however, although both the immersion and contact ultrasonic methods are considered accurate, the measurement values may depend on the skill of examiner.

Optical AL measurement is noncontact and allows for stable measurements without variation due to the examiner's skill, leading to fewer postoperative refractive errors.3–5 Since 2014, biometers have emerged that are equipped with a swept-source (SS) light source with a main wavelength of 1060 nm, making efficient optical interferometry possible in a short time compared with the time-domain method using near-infrared light. These biometers are less affected by intermediate transmitters such as posterior subcapsular cataract, improving the reproducibility and AL acquisition success rate.6–9 The ARGOS (Alcon Laboratories, Inc.) equipped with SS-optical coherence tomography (OCT) (OCTB1) has a high AL acquisition success rate even in dense nuclear sclerosis.10 The Anterion (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH) (AS-OCTB) mainly functions as a 3D image analysis of the anterior segment and uses a main wavelength of 1300 nm for its light source. By combining it with optional cataract application, it becomes possible to measure AL and calculate IOL power. Previous reports have compared the IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG) (OCTB2), the first AL measurement device equipped with an SS light source, and the AS-OCTB and have determined that the AL acquisition success rate for patients excluding mature cataracts was 100% for OCTB2 and 95.2% for AS-OCTB.11 In this study, we compared the AL acquisition success rate and the relationship between various ocular parameters on the 3 devices, OCTB1, OCTB2, and AS-OCTB, including in patients with mature cataract and nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher.

METHODS

Participants and Ethics

We enrolled patients undergoing preoperative biometrical examinations with OCTB1, OCTB2, and AS-OCTB before cataract surgery at Chukyo Eye Clinic from September 2019 to June 2022. The 3 devices were used in random order, and the same certified orthoptist performed the measurements in each case. The IOL implanted was SN60WF (Alcon Laboratories, Inc.). We excluded patients with keratoconus, vitreous hemorrhage, corneal opacity, or any other condition that could affect biometric measurements before surgery, those with a history of eye surgery including laser refractive surgery, those with complications during or after surgery, and those in whom we were unable to obtain test data due to poor fixation. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Chukyo Eye Clinic Ethics Committee (approval date June 1, 2023; approval number: 20230601054). As this was a retrospective study, the ethics committee allowed the opt-out method instead of consent forms.

Description of Biometers

The central wavelengths of OCTB1, OCTB2, and AS-OCTB are 1060 nm, 1055 nm, and 1300 nm, respectively. All 3 devices are equipped with SS light sources. The measurable range of AL is 14 to 38 mm for OCTB1 and OCTB2, and 14 to 32 mm for AS-OCTB. The characteristic of the calculated AL is that OCTB1 measures the sum-of-segments AL (ALsos), while OCTB2 and AS-OCTB measure the traditional AL (ALtrad). OCTB1 is equipped with an enhanced retina visualization (ERV) mode that allows the measurement of AL by changing the sensitivity position of the OCT to the retina even under compromised light energy due to advanced cataract. The measurement positions for the corneal curvature radius are Φ2.2 mm (16 points) for OCTB1, Φ2.5 mm (6 points) for OCTB2, and Φ3 mm (62-radial scan) for AS-OCTB. The high reproducibility of the parameters obtained with the 3 biometers used in this study has been confirmed previously.6,12–14 The AL acquisition was determined by whether the OCT image of the retina could be obtained in the ERV mode for OCTB1. For OCTB2, incomplete measurement in all 6 meridian directions was considered a measurement failure. For AS-OCTB, measurement failures were considered to have occurred when the measurement waveform was not displayed. The OCT image of the retina is not displayed if the difference in AL between 3 datasets obtained in a single measurement exceeds 0.06 mm.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure was the AL acquisition success rate. As secondary outcomes, in eyes where AL could be measured by all devices, we compared AL, keratometric power, anterior chamber depth (ACD), lens thickness (LT), and corneal horizontal diameter (CD), and the predicted refractive error (subjective refraction value − predicted refraction value) was examined at 1 month postoperatively. The IOL power calculation formula used was Barrett Universal II, and we used a lens factor of 1.94.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v. 27, and the Cochran Q test was used to test the AL acquisition success rate. Bland Altman plots were used to compare ALs between devices using GraphPad Prism v. 8. The difference in AL before and after cataract surgery was tested using the Wilcoxon test. The Friedman test was conducted for comparisons of each parameter, and the Dunn multiple comparison test was used for the arithmetic mean and absolute value of the predicted refractive error. The significance level was set at less than 5%.

Sample Size Calculation

The AL acquisition success rate for OCTB1 in a previous report was 97.6% in all cases, whereas for OCTB2, it was 92.6%.10 In cases with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher, the rate was 93.4% for OCTB1 and 61.5% for OCTB2. Based on these results, the sample size was set at 235 eyes for all patients and 35 eyes for those patients with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher, resulting in a detection power of 81% for all patients and 95% for those with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher. The biometers used this study have different central wavelengths; however, OCTB1 and OCTB2 use similar wavelengths. Therefore, we focused on comparing these biometers and calculated the sample size based on OCTB1 and OCTB2.

RESULTS

AL Acquisition Success Rate

There were 361 patients (361 eyes) with a mean age of 72.6 ± 6.4 years, 166 of whom were men and 195 were women (Figures 1 and 2). The grades of nuclear sclerosis are grade 1 (4 eyes), grade 2 (41 eyes), grade 3 (275 eyes), grade 4 (33 eyes), grade 5 (5 eyes), and other (white cataract) (3 eyes). The types of cataracts are nuclear (100 eyes), cortical (208 eyes), posterior subcapsular (48 eyes), anterior subcapsular (2 eyes), and white cataract (3 eyes). Measured parameters are given in Table 1. Preoperative AL measured with OCTB1 was 22 mm or shorter in 33 eyes (9%), between 22 and 26 mm in 233 eyes (65%), and 26 mm or longer in 95 eyes (26%). The AL acquisition success rate for all patients was significantly different between devices with 100.0% (n = 361) for OCTB1 (ERV mode used in 5 eyes [1.4%]), 98.6% (n = 356) for OCTB2, and 96.7% (n = 349) for AS-OCTB. AS-OCTB produced significantly lower rates than the other 2 devices (P < .0001; Figure 1, A). Similarly, in patients with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher, the AL acquisition success rate differed significantly with 100.0% (n = 41) for OCTB1 (ERV mode used in 5 eyes), 87.8% (n = 36) for OCTB2, and 87.8% (n = 36) for AS-OCTB, with OCTB1 giving significantly higher rates than the other 2 devices (P = .007; Figure 1, B). Of the 5 eyes measured in the ERV mode, 2 eyes had grade 5 nuclear sclerosis, while 3 eyes had white cataract. These 5 eyes were unmeasurable by both OCTB2 and AS-OCTB. Among 12 patients who were not suitable for AS-OCTB measurements, 3 eyes had white cataract and 8 had nuclear sclerosis of grade 3 or higher. The AL of these 8 eyes with nuclear sclerosis was measurable by both OCTB1 and OCTB2. Postoperatively, their ALs ranged from 26.24 mm to 32.00 mm, indicating that AS-OCTB might be disadvantaged in measuring long ALs, even in patients with dense nuclear sclerosis. The Bland-Altman plot results for the preoperative and postoperative AL measured by OCTB1 for all patients showed an average difference of −0.006 mm (95% limits of agreement [LoA], −0.08 to 0.06 mm; and 95% CI, −0.01 to −0.002 mm; Figure 2, A). The Bland-Altman plot results for the preoperative and postoperative AL of OCTB1 for 41 eyes with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or more showed an average difference of 0.00 mm (95% LoA, −0.08 to 0.08 mm; and 95% CI, −0.01 to 0.02 mm; Figure 2, B).

Differences in AL Between Biometers

A total of 349 eyes with measurable AL across all 3 devices were included in this analysis (Figure 3). The mean AL was 24.34 ± 1.88 mm with OCTB1, 24.35 ± 1.93 mm with OCTB2, and 24.37 ± 1.96 mm with AS-OCTB, without significant differences among the devices. Bland-Altman plots comparing the differences between OCTB1 and OCTB2 showed an average difference of −0.007 mm, with a 95% LoA ranging from −0.14 to 0.12 mm and a 95% CI from −0.01 to 0.00 mm. For OCTB1 and AS-OCTB, the average difference was −0.02 mm, with a 95% LoA from −0.21 to 0.17 mm and a 95% CI from −0.03 to −0.01 mm. The difference between OCTB2 and AS-OCTB averaged −0.01 mm, with a 95% LoA from −0.11 to 0.09 mm and a 95% CI from −0.02 to 0.00 mm (Figure 3). Bland-Altman analysis revealed AL measured by OCTB1 tended to be shorter for short eyes and longer for long eyes compared with the other 2 devices as reported previously.6,10

Changes in AL Before and After Cataract Surgery

A total of 349 eyes with measurable AL across all 3 devices were included in this analysis. The preoperative mean AL for OCTB1 was 24.34 ± 1.88 mm, and the postoperative AL remained unchanged at 24.34 ± 1.88 mm. For OCTB2 and AS-OCTB, the preoperative mean Als were 24.35 ± 1.93 mm and 24.37 ± 1.96 mm, respectively. By contrast, the postoperative ALs were significantly shorter: 24.28 ± 1.93 mm for OCTB2 (P < .0001) and 24.33 ± 1.94 mm for AS-OCTB (P < .0001). Among the 36 eyes with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher, there were no significant differences in preoperative and postoperative AL for OCTB1 (preop, 24.40 ± 2.15 mm; postop, 24.40 ± 2.16 mm), while both OCTB2 and AS-OCTB showed significantly shorter postoperative ALs (OCTB2: preop, 24.39 ± 2.24 mm, postop, 24.34 ± 2.22 mm, P < .0001; AS-OCTB: preop, 24.46 ± 2.25 mm, postop, 24.43 ± 2.22 mm, P = .0004). For the 5 eyes measured using the ERV mode, the average difference between preoperative and postoperative AL was 0.014 ± 0.10 mm. The maximum difference was 0.16 mm, with a longer preoperative AL of 22.07 mm and a shorter postoperative AL of 21.91 mm. For the other 4 eyes, the differences ranged from 0.07 to −0.10 mm. The postoperative AL was 21.78 mm with OCTB2 and 21.83 mm with AS-OCTB.

Comparison of Other Ocular Parameters

The mean keratometric power was strongest at 44.43 ± 1.68 diopters (D) with OCTB1 (P < .0001), followed by 44.32 ± 1.67 D with OCTB2 and 44.35 ± 1.64 D with AS-OCTB (Figure 4). ACD was deepest at 3.24 ± 0.47 mm using OCTB1 (P < .0001), followed by 3.12 ± 0.47 mm with OCTB2 and 3.19 ± 0.47 mm with AS-OCTB. LT was measured at 4.64 ± 0.41 mm with OCTB1, 4.61 ± 0.42 mm with OCTB2, and 4.67 ± 0.41 mm with AS-OCTB, with the latter obtaining the thickest results (P < .0001). The CD was 11.68 ± 0.47 mm with OCTB1, 11.85 ± 0.45 mm with OCTB2, and 11.65 ± 0.49 mm with AS-OCTB, with OCTB2 obtaining the widest diameter (P < .0001).

Predicted Refractive Error

The arithmetic mean of the prediction error (mean prediction error) was not statistically significant between the devices at 0.17 ± 0.37 D for OCTB1, 0.14 ± 0.36 D for OCTB2, and 0.15 ± 0.39 D for AS-OCTB (Figure 5). The mean absolute error and median absolute error of the absolute values of prediction errors did not differ significantly at 0.32 ± 0.24 D and 0.28 D with OCTB1, 0.30 ± 0.25 D and 0.25 D with OCTB2, and 0.32 ± 0.27 D and 0.26 D with AS-OCTB, respectively.

Accuracy of AL Measurements With OCTB1 Using the ERV Mode

Among the 5 eyes measured with OCTB1 using the ERV mode, the maximum difference in AL before and after surgery was 0.16 mm, resulting in a refractive error of 1.48 D of hyperopia. In this particular case, the preoperative AL was 22.07 mm, postoperative AL was 21.91 mm, mean keratometric power remained constant at 45.4 D, and LT was 4.1 mm, with an ACD of 3.45 mm. The preoperative predicted refractive error based on AL was −0.48 D, while the postoperative predicted refractive error was −0.01 D. The measurement error in AL using the ERV mode contributed to a predicted refractive error of 0.47 D. For the other 4 cases using the ERV mode, the predicted refractive error ranged from −0.16 to 0.75 D.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared the AL acquisition success rates, various ocular parameters, and postoperative refractive errors among 3 types of biometers with varying central wavelengths and methods of calculating AL. The AL acquisition success rate was excellent for OCTB1 (100%), closely followed by OCTB2 and AS-OCTB. The OCTB1 with an ERV mode allowed measurement even in cases where other devices failed. We previously reported that OCTB1 had a higher AL acquisition success rate than the other 2 devices, although measurement failed in mature or white cataracts and vitreous hemorrhage.15 In this study, AL was measurable in all eyes with ERV mode, although patients with vitreous hemorrhage were not included. We further confirmed the accuracy of preoperative AL obtained by OCTB1 using the ERV mode by comparing them with postoperative measurements. Bland-Altman plots comparing preoperative and postoperative AL showed small differences in both the total cohort and the subgroup with nuclear sclerosis of grade 4 or higher. ERV mode is reportedly useful for dense cataracts, providing an advantage in AL measurement compared with other devices.15 Uniquely, the wavelength used by the AS-OCTB (1300 nm) decreases light transmission through the vitreous body, according to the International Commission on Illumination Technical Report.16 This wavelength is applied primarily for anterior segment OCT and affects the AL acquisition success rate.

The mean values of the overall AL obtained with the 3 devices did not differ significantly. OCTB1 calculates ALsos based on the refractive indices of various tissues, and the proportion of the highest refractive index in the lens affects the measured AL.10,17 In addition, when comparing the differences in AL before and after surgery, OCTB1 using ALsos showed no significant difference, while both OCTB2 and AS-OCTB using ALtrad measured shorter ALs postoperatively. This difference may be attributed to the method of calculating the AL. The present results further confirm the accuracy of OCTB1 as indicated in previous investigations.18,19

The Barrett Universal II formula was used for IOL power calculation for predicting the refractive error. This formula is integrated in all 3 biometers, facilitating comparisons of all measured parameters. In addition, previous reports indicated that this formula was less affected by parameter variations and provided minimal predicted refractive errors compared with other formulas.20 Regarding the ocular parameters obtained with the 3 devices, OCTB1 produced significantly higher mean keratometric power, likely due to differences in measurement location. ACD, LT, and CD also differed among the devices, indicating a lack of compatibility. However, the mean prediction error, the mean absolute error, and the median absolute error of the prediction errors were similar among the 3 devices. Therefore, the parametric differences do not affect the calculated prediction errors for each device. However, the OCTB1 has the Barrett True AL formula, which incorporates the AL obtained from the segmented refractive index.

This study has several strengths. The assessment of postoperative refractive error caused by AL is a new aspect of this study. The results of AL acquisition success rate were consistent with a recent study; however, postoperative AL was not evaluated in that study, and the accuracy of the preoperative measurements was not determined.21 In addition, their study lacked an evaluation of the refractive error. Second, the predicted refractive error was assessed with a single IOL leading to precise comparison of biometers. Third, all patients were examined by a skilled certified orthoptist. Finally, a detailed classification of cataract type was used in the analysis. A major limitation of this study, however, is the exclusion of patients with poor fixation. In mature cataracts, unstable fixation during measurements due to visual impairment makes it difficult to obtain reliable results. It should be determined whether the inability to measure AL in these patients is due to cataract opacity or unstable fixation. Moreover, a well-designed multicenter study could explore additional advantages of biometers because AL differs among races and geographical regions. Further studies would also be warranted to evaluate an AL acquisition success rate in unhealthy eyes, including those with vitreous opacity, keratoconus, or after corneal refractive surgeries.

In conclusion, 3 types of SS-OCT–based biometers were compared and the OCTB1 equipped with an ERV mode allowed for an excellent AL acquisition success rate even in cases of mature cataracts.
