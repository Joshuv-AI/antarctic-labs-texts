Association between health literacy and the need for hemodialysis in patients with type 2 diabetes: a case-control study
Authors: Amir Hossein Shakarami, Shiva Borzouei, Zahra Cheraghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the high prevalence of type 2 diabetes and its significant renal complications, which can ultimately lead to renal failure and require patients to undergo hemodialysis, this study was designed and conducted to examine the association between health literacy and hemodialysis in diabetic patients. This case-control study examined 291 patients: 97 were diabetic hemodialysis patients, and 194 were non-hemodialysis diabetic patients. Data were collected using a standard adult health literacy questionnaire. Additionally, a 19-item demographic questionnaire was used to collect information on the patients’ personal characteristics. Independent t-tests and analyses of variance at the 95% level of statistical significance were used to compare mean health literacy scores based on demographic variables. A multivariable logistic regression model at a 5% significance level was used to determine the relationship between health literacy scores and factors associated with type 2 diabetes requiring hemodialysis. All analyses were performed using Stata 17 statistical software. The mean age of the patients in this study was 62.3 ± 9.72 years. Health literacy had an inverse association with hemodialysis outcomes for diabetic patients, and for each unit increase in health literacy score, the odds of hemodialysis for diabetic patients (HFDP) decreased by 15% (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.82, 0.88, P-value < 0.01).Income level also had an inverse association with hemodialysis outcomes in diabetic patients; those with middle socioeconomic status had 77% lower odds of requiring hemodialysis compared to those from low socioeconomic backgrounds (odds ratio: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.09, 0.56, p = 0.001). Physical activity was also inversely associated with hemodialysis outcomes, i.e. as weekly physical activity increased, the odds of HFDP decreased by 23%. (Odds Ratio: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.64, 0.94, p = 0.011). The results indicated that health literacy, income level, and physical activity are inversely associated with hemodialysis dependence. In addition, underlying health conditions are a contributing factor to the increased likelihood of diabetic patients requiring hemodialysis.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a complex metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from impaired insulin secretion, action, or both1.The global prevalence of diabetes has risen alarmingly since the 1990s, with projections estimating 300 million cases by 20252,3. In Iran, 15% of adults have diabetes, and 25.4% exhibit prediabetes, yet only 79.6% of affected individuals are aware of their diagnosis—a critical barrier to effective management4,5. Health literacy—the ability to access, understand, and apply health information—is increasingly recognized as a determinant of self-care and outcomes in chronic diseases like diabetes6.

Robust evidence links higher health literacy to better glycemic control and reduced complications7–9, though most studies focus on general diabetic populations. Notably, while prior research has examined health literacy’s role in diabetic nephropathy10,11, its specific association with progression to hemodialysis dependence remains unexplored.

Type 2 diabetes (T2DM) accounts for 90% of cases and often leads to microvascular complications, including end-stage renal disease (ESRD)12.

In Iran, hemodialysis is the primary renal replacement therapy for diabetes-related ESRD16,17, imposing significant clinical and economic burdens. Although antihyperglycemic medications and self-care (e.g., diet, monitoring) mitigate complications13,14, the role of health literacy in preventing hemodialysis dependence is unclear.

This study aims to bridge this gap by evaluating whether health literacy, alongside socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, influences hemodialysis risk in Iranian T2DM patients.

Methods

Study Design and Setting: This was a classical case-control study conducted at Shahid Beheshti Hospital in Hamadan, Iran. The study population comprised two groups: patients with type 2 diabetes who were receiving regular hemodialysis (the case group) and patients with type 2 diabetes not requiring renal replacement therapy (the control group).

Sampling and Participants: Participants were selected using a random sampling method from the available patient pools attending the hospital’s nephrology/dialysis clinic (for cases) and the general diabetes/endocrine outpatient clinics (for controls) during the study period. We employed a 1:2 case-to-control ratio to enhance the study’s power. The final sample included 97 cases and 194 controls (total n = 291), with the sample size calculated based on a comparative proportion formula referenced from prior work (Tajdar et al., 2021).

The HELIA questionnaire is composed of 33 items, which are grouped into five domains: access (six items), reading (four items), comprehension (seven items), evaluation (four items), and decision making and application of health information (twelve items). Participants were instructed to respond to each item using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “very easy” to “very difficult,” resulting in a total score between one and five for each response15. The demographic questionnaire contained 19 items designed to assess patients’ personal characteristics. The validation of HELIA in diabetic populations was performed in Iran15.

The total sample size was determined based on a comparative proportion test in independent samples with reference to the study by Tajdar and colleagues16, resulting in a final number of 291 participants — 97 hemodialysis patients and 194 non-dialysis control patients. The inclusion criteria for the study stipulated that participants must be adults (over 18 years of age), residents of Hamadan, and diagnosed with type 2 diabetes for a minimum of one year. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy and lactation. The collection of data was executed through the distribution of paper questionnaires to patients attending outpatient clinics and dialysis units in a face-to-face manner. The analysis of the collected data incorporated independent t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare mean health literacy scores across demographic variables, with statistical significance set at 95%.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the association between health literacy scores and the presence of type 2 diabetes on hemodialysis, with a 5% significance level. The backward stepwise selection method for final variable selection. In this approach, the significance and removal levels were set at 0.1 and 0.2, respectively.

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test as a statistical test for goodness of fit and calibration for logistic regression models was used. The list of all variables adjusted for in the logistic regression including: age, gender, residence (city/rural), marital status (single/married), household members, income (poor/average/good), smoking (present/past), education (illiteracy, primary/high school, high school, university), family history of chronic diseases, physical activities per week, total health literacy score.

All analyses were performed using Stata 17 statistical software. All analyses were conducted using Stata 17 software. The ethical considerations that were strictly adhered to are documented as follows: participant information was reported in aggregate form without individual identifiers, and confidentiality and integrity were maintained throughout the analysis process. Addressing challenges, such as the lack of cooperation from some participants, necessitated an emphasis on the confidentiality of the collected data.

Results

This case-control study examined the baseline characteristics of diabetic patients on and not on dialysis. Of the 291 participants in the total sample, 97 (33.33%) were in the dialysis group, while 194 (66.67%) were in the non-dialysis group. The mean age was 61.80 ± 10.06 years in the non-dialysis group and 63.30 ± 8.95 years in the dialysis group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.107). Approximately half of the patients in both groups were female and half were male, with no statistically significant difference observed (P = 0.868). Furthermore, the majority of patients in both groups lived in urban areas, and there was no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.170).

With respect to marital status, the majority of patients in both groups were married; however, the proportion of unmarried patients was higher in the dialysis group (P = 0.078). A subsequent analysis of the data revealed that household size did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.150). However, a disparity emerged in terms of income, with dialysis patients exhibiting a lower income compared to non-dialysis patients (61.86% vs. 36.60%, P < 0.001). The educational attainment of the dialysis group was found to be lower, with a higher percentage of university graduates in the non-dialysis diabetic group (29.90% vs. 19.59%) (P < 0.001).

Subsequent analysis of family histories of chronic diseases revealed no significant differences between the two groups (P = 0.130). However, a significant disparity in the frequency of physical activity per week was observed, with the dialysis group exhibiting lower activity levels (1.73 ± 0.16 vs. 2.16 ± 0.26; P = 0.009). The dialysis group had significantly lower overall health literacy scores and lower scores in all dimensions (access, reading comprehension, evaluation, and decision-making) compared to the non-dialysis group (P < 0.001) (see Table 1).

The logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between several factors and the occurrence of dialysis in diabetic patients: A higher total health literacy score was strongly associated with a reduced likelihood of dialysis (AOR = 0.85 per 1-point increase, 95% CI [0.82–0.88], p < 0.001). The presence of comorbidities significantly increased the odds of dialysis (AOR = 2.64, 95% CI [1.22–5.70], p = 0.013). A higher frequency of physical activity per week was protective against dialysis (AOR = 0.77 per additional session, 95% CI [0.64–0.94], p = 0.011). The number of family members did not show a statistically significant association with dialysis risk (AOR = 1.29 per person, 95% CI [0.88–1.89], p = 0.177) (See Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, a logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the factors associated with the progression to dialysis in diabetic patients. The findings indicated the role of specific factors in increasing or decreasing the risk of dialysis dependence within this population. In the following section, a thorough examination and scientific interpretation of these findings will be provided.

A number of studies have previously investigated the relationship between health literacy and the development of chronic complications in diabetic patients, including renal complications such as nephropathy10,11. However, the present study is the first to explore this relationship in the context of diabetes. Nevertheless, the present study is the first to specifically examine risk factors for hemodialysis dependence, including health literacy and its domains, in diabetic patients. The findings indicate that health literacy, income level, and comorbidities are significant factors that substantially influence the likelihood of requiring dialysis in diabetic individuals.

The mean health literacy scores were 42.6 and 66.7 for the dialysis and non-dialysis groups, respectively, demonstrating a significant difference between the two groups. The mean score for dialysis-dependent patients fell within the “inadequate” range, while the mean score for non-dialysis patients fell within the “adequate” range. One prior study reported health literacy levels in diabetic patients undergoing hemodialysis using a different assessment instrument with a potential score range of 1–4. The mean health literacy score in that study was 2.9917. A body of research has been conducted on the general diabetic population, and the results of these studies have indicated that the prevalence of inadequate or marginal health literacy ranges from 16% to 76%. This finding underscores the necessity for interventions to enhance health literacy in this demographic10,18,19.

The principal finding of this study is the significant and robust association between health literacy and dialysis dependence in diabetic patients. The findings of this study indicate that health literacy functions as a protective factor against hemodialysis in type 2 diabetic patients, with an increase of one unit in the health literacy score resulting in a 15% reduction in the likelihood of dialysis dependence. This phenomenon may be attributed to the observation that patients with higher health literacy demonstrate superior health-related behaviors20, enhanced treatment adherence21, and more effective disease management. However, a paucity of studies has yielded a consistent correlation between health literacy and renal complications in diabetic patients. For instance, Schillinger et al. (2002) and Osborn et al. (2018) found no significant association between health literacy and nephropathy10,22. In contrast, Cavanaugh et al. reported a significant relationship between health literacy and chronic complications in diabetic patients. However, it should be noted that only 12% of the subjects in their “chronic complications” group had nephropathy, while the rest exhibited other complications, such as retinopathy and neuropathy23. The observed discrepancies between the present study and those referenced may be attributed to methodological differences. Specifically, while the present The study employed a case-control design; the aforementioned studies used a cross-sectional design, which limits their ability to establish causality. Additionally, the study by Osborn et al. focused on type 1 diabetes, which differs from type 2 diabetes in terms of its pathophysiology, treatment, and prognosis. Another distinction lies in the assessment instruments used: Schillinger et al. used the TOFHLA questionnaire, which evaluates functional literacy through reading and numeracy tasks. In contrast, our study used the HELIA questionnaire, a self-report instrument that provides a more comprehensive evaluation of health literacy by assessing higher-level competencies, such as evaluation and decision-making15,24.

In the present study, among lifestyle-related variables, income level and physical activity exhibited an inverse, protective relationship with dialysis dependence in type 2 diabetic patients. Lower income levels were found to be associated with a 77% increase in the likelihood of developing dialysis dependence, a finding that aligns with the conclusions of previous studies on the relationship between income and diabetic nephropathy23,25,26.This phenomenon may be attributed to enhanced access to quality healthcare, medications, and nutritious diets among individuals with higher incomes27,28. Furthermore, an increase in weekly physical activity by one hour was associated with a 23% decrease in the probability of requiring dialysis treatment. A substantial body of research has underscored the pivotal function of physical activity in mitigating the development of nephropathy and decelerating its progression to end-stage renal disease in diabetic patients29,30. A body of research has demonstrated a correlation between physical activity and enhanced renal function markers, including glomerular filtration rate, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and serum creatinine levels31,32. The aforementioned benefits may be attributable to enhanced glycemic control, diminished blood pressure, and mitigated inflammation and oxidative stress.

The presence of comorbidities has been demonstrated to significantly increase the likelihood of dialysis dependence in diabetic patients. Individuals afflicted with additional chronic conditions exhibited a 1.5-fold elevated probability of necessitating dialysis treatment. This finding is well-supported by prior research, which has demonstrated that comorbidities substantially elevate the risk of diabetic nephropathy, end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and eventual hemodialysis31,33. This underscores the challenges associated with the management of multiple chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension, cardiovascular diseases) in conjunction with diabetes, which can complicate disease control and accelerate renal decline34.

In univariate analysis, family size was associated with an odds ratio of 1.35, indicating that each additional family member increased the likelihood of dialysis dependence by 35%. While this association did not attain statistical significance in multivariable analysis, it does suggest the potential influence of household dynamics on health outcomes. Larger families may encounter heightened financial and healthcare management challenges, which could potentially compromise the management of chronic diseases. However, the impact of family support on self-care and diabetic outcomes is not uniform. While some studies have reported a positive effect, others have not observed a significant impact35–37. Further research with larger samples is necessary to elucidate this relationship.

Conclusion

The present study identified health literacy, income level, and comorbidities as key determinants of hemodialysis dependence in patients with type 2 diabetes. Higher health literacy and income were protective factors, while comorbidities significantly increased risk. These results highlight the importance of improving health literacy and financial access to optimize diabetes management and prevent renal complications. Implementing targeted interventions in these areas could reduce dialysis dependence and improve patients’ quality of life.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its use of the validated HELIA questionnaire, which assesses health literacy across five domains: reading, access, comprehension, evaluation, and decision-making. Its validation in an Iranian population enhances result accuracy. However, self-reporting biases and limitations such as a small sample size and geographic restriction (Hamadan, Iran) may affect generalizability. Future multicenter studies with larger samples are recommended. Unmeasured confounders (e.g., healthcare access, psychosocial factors) could also influence outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into health literacy’s role in dialysis dependence among diabetic patients. Key constraints include the cross-sectional design (limiting causal inference), potential residual confounding (e.g., diet, medication adherence), and limited external validity beyond Iran. The HELIA tool’s multidimensional assessment is a notable advantage over alternatives like TOFHLA (Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults). Lastly, while case-control studies are efficient for rare diseases, resource constraints, or rapid results, their retrospective nature introduces biases. For stronger causal conclusions, findings should be corroborated by prospective cohort or experimental studies. Such as It is plausible that progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and initiation of hemodialysis negatively influence health literacy. The physical and cognitive demands of dialysis, coupled with the psychological stress of managing a severe chronic illness, could gradually impair a patient’s capacity to access, comprehend, and apply health information. Consequently, the lower health literacy observed in diabetic patients on hemodialysis may be a result of their advanced disease stage and complex treatment requirements, rather than a precursor to them. Furthermore, we couldn’t account for key clinical factors like diabetes duration, long-term HbA1c, blood pressure, or use of renoprotective drugs like SGLT2 inhibitors. This creates a risk of residual confounding. So, the association we found with health literacy might partly reflect underlying differences in disease severity or quality of clinical management, which we couldn’t measure. Also Selecting controls from a general diabetes clinic and cases from dialysis units may introduce spectrum bias, as controls likely encompass various stages of kidney disease. Furthermore, survivor bias is possible, as only patients who lived to require dialysis were included, potentially inflating the average health literacy in the case group and underestimating its true effect. Crucially, the lack of data on diabetes duration and detailed clinical history (e.g., long-term glycemic control, use of renal protective drugs) are major unmeasured confounders. Therefore, our results demonstrate an important association but cannot confirm causality, highlighting the need for prospective studies that address these biases. Given these limitations, the findings demonstrate a robust and clinically important association but cannot definitively establish causality. Future research should employ prospective cohort designs following a well-characterized population of diabetic patients from diagnosis, with regular assessments of kidney function, health literacy, and key clinical confounders.
