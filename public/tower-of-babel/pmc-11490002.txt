International medical students in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study on their reasons for choosing Türkiye and their demographic and socioeconomic characteristics
Authors: Engin Karadag, Esra Tekel, Ahmet Su, Metin Kocaturk, Hatice Ergin-Kocaturk, Murat Polat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study is to investigate the reasons why international medical students choose Türkiye, which has one of the largest higher education capacities in Europe and is also among the top 10 most preferred countries in the world by international students, and their demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. The data of this cross-sectional study were collected through an online survey that included questions such as students’ gender, age, ethnicity, monthly income, pre-school education, parental education level and professions, number of siblings, rural status of the region where they grew up, and reasons for choosing Türkiye. 482 students at various grade levels from 30 medical schools participated in the study. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square, and ANOVA tests. The results revealed that there are significant differences in the participants’ parents’ education levels, occupations and economic levels according to their ethnicity. Besides, the participants’ reasons to choose Türkiye significantly differ according to their ethnicity while the value of medical education offered in Turkish higher education institutions is emphasized.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06200-9.

Introduction

The globalization of higher education refers to the increasing interconnectedness of educational institutions and systems worldwide, allowing for cross-border education, academic mobility, and international collaboration. In Türkiye, this trend has gained significant momentum in recent years as the country seeks to position itself as a regional hub for higher education and has become an attractive destination for international students, particularly from the Middle East, Africa, and Central Asia, due to its affordable education, diverse programs, and strategic geographic location. Government initiatives like the “Türkiye Scholarships” program have further promoted the country as a global education destination. To appeal to a broader international audience, many Turkish universities have introduced programs taught in English, especially in fields like engineering, medicine, and business. Besides, Turkish universities are expanding their partnerships with global institutions, participating in student and faculty exchange programs, and engaging in collaborative research projects.

These trends show Türkiye’s commitment to integrating its higher education system into the global academic landscape while attracting international talent. However, medical education offered in Türkiye is getting more internationally attractive. In Türkiye, starting from the 1980s, the number of medical faculties has approximately doubled every decade, accordingly, the number of students admitted to medical faculties has constantly increased [16]. The medical school admission process in Türkiye is based on the examination system. After completing high school, Türkiye-based students take the Higher Education Transition Exam and the Undergraduate Placement Exam conducted by Assessment, Selection and Placement Centre. Higher education placement scores are calculated by adding secondary education success scores to the scores they receive from this exam [6]. Students make choices based on their scores from these exams and are placed in the medical faculties of the universities they choose. However, a different process is followed for international students. The principles regarding the admission of students from abroad, including the minimum scores of methods such as countries’ own national exams, international exams, secondary education (high school) grade point averages, exams conducted by the university, etc., are regulated in the form of directives or regulations accepted by the university senates [14]. Not only the admission processes to medical schools, but also the tuition fees of Turkish citizen students and international students differ (Law No 2547).

Although Turkish citizens’ education at a state university is free, foreign students are required to pay a certain amount of tuition fee to study in Türkiye. The Council of Ministers determines the minimum and maximum amount of tuition fees to be collected each year from students admitted from abroad to public universities, without exception. In the 1st paragraph of the 3rd article of the Presidential Decree no. 32,249, it is stated that the tuition fee to be collected from the students who will enroll within the international student admission quotas is determined by the universities, provided that it is not less than twice the student current service cost determined by the presidency. When the student current service cost determined by the Council of Ministers is examined, the highest cost belongs to the medical faculty [15]. Therefore, foreign students in Türkiye pay the most tuition fees to medical faculties.

The process is not complete even after graduating from medical school. Physicians who successfully complete their medical school education in Türkiye find themselves at two crossroads. The first one is to start working as a general practitioner after graduation in public hospitals, the second one is to take the Medical Specialization Exam (MSE) to be placed in any field of specialization and to start specialization studies without performing compulsory service. MSE is hold twice a year by the Assessment, Selection and Placement Center with a central exam, multiple choice [7].

In short, it can be said that medical education in Türkiye differs from other faculties because it is a long, costly and labor-requiring process. Accordingly, studies suggest that medical school students are different from the whole society in terms of background and socio-economic status. This difference determines the society that medical school students want to serve when they become doctors. Because doctors prefer to serve society close to their own background. This is not a desired situation, because health care is a service that all segments of society should have equal access to. In this respect, it is of great importance to investigate doctors’ backgrounds and take precautions accordingly [10, 13].

Medical graduates in Türkiye follow a highly structured path toward employment after completing their education. The process is characterized by compulsory state service, specialization opportunities, and employment in both the public and private healthcare sectors. Türkiye’s healthcare system offers medical graduates a high likelihood of employment shortly after graduation. The employment rate for medical graduates is nearly 100%, largely due to the compulsory state service, which requires newly graduated doctors to serve in underserved or rural areas. This system helps meet the healthcare needs of regions that experience shortages of healthcare professionals, while ensuring that new doctors gain practical experience. Around 70–80% of medical graduates work in the public healthcare system. This includes positions in public hospitals, family health centers, and rural healthcare facilities. The remaining 20–30% of medical professionals find employment in the private sector, which is growing, especially in urban areas where the demand for specialized healthcare services and medical tourism is increasing. One of the key career paths for medical graduates in Türkiye is specialization. To enter a residency program in their chosen field, graduates must pass the MSE, a highly competitive exam. Each year, 16,000–20,000 medical graduates take the MSE, vying for around 5,000–6,000 residency positions. The acceptance rate into residency programs is around 25–30%, meaning a significant number of graduates must retake the exam or work as general practitioners (GPs) before securing a residency position. Depending on the field of specialization, residency programs last between 3 and 6 years. Specialties such as cardiology, surgery, and dermatology are among the most competitive, with limited spots and higher cut-off scores. Due to the competitive nature of the MSE, many graduates dedicate 1–2 years to preparation, which can delay their entry into residency programs and their eventual specialization.

In this context, this study describes the reasons why international medical students choose to study in Türkiye, which has one of the largest higher education capacities in Europe and is also among the top 10 most preferred countries in the world by international students. As a descriptive one, this study focuses on demographic backgrounds of international students studying in Türkiye. Based on their ethnicity and socio-economic background, it is aimed to figure out value of medical education in Türkiye when compared to its counterparts to contribute to a better understanding of the needs and expectations of this group of students and to the development of a more sustainable educational policy. Within this respect, it is hypothesized that why international medical students choose Türkiye as a destination differ significantly based on their ethnicity.

Method

Population and sample

The population of our study consists of 13,513 international students studying at 128 medical faculties (91 public, 37 non-profit foundation universities) in Türkiye according to statistics by Council of Higher Education [5]. Considering the distribution differences in the number of international medical students in our study, we decided to conduct stratified sampling. First, we divided the universities with medical faculties into two separate layers: (i) public and (ii) non-profit foundation universities. We drew a frequency diagram of the universe based on the number of international students in medical faculties. From the frequency diagram, we created faculties into ten separate layers (0-0.99%; 1-1.99%; 2-2.99% …10% and above). In this case, we have achieved a distribution that approaches normal for each layer. After listing the international student rates of the faculties that make up the universe from smallest to largest, we created ten new columns by moving the data for each layer into separate columns. After this process, we calculated the determinants of each layer (mean, standard deviation, variance, minimum and maximum values, etc.). Since the differences between the stratum averages and the population averages were large, we decided to apply the proportional stratified random sampling method. According to this method, the sample size for each layer was calculated through the proportional distribution formula, and two universities for state universities and one university for foundation universities were randomly selected from each subgroup. Thus, a total of 30 universities were determined. Then, how many international students would be included in the sample from these universities (from each group) was calculated by accepting the confidence interval as 0.95 and the margin of error as 0.05. As a result of the procedures, the minimum sample size to represent the international students consisting of 13,513 units was calculated as 374 students based on Hamburg’s [9] model. 482 students from the specified universities who participated in the study voluntarily constituted the sample of the study.

Data collection tools

Data collection tool was developed by the researchers based on Theory of Planned Behavior [1, 2]. This theory explains how human behavior is driven by three key factors: Attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. It expands on the Theory of Reasoned Action by including perceived control over the behavior. According to TPB, an individual’s intention to perform a behavior is influenced by their positive or negative attitude toward it, societal pressures (subjective norms), and the degree of control they believe they have (perceived behavioral control). Together, these factors shape behavioral intentions and, consequently, actual behavior. To group major motives to study abroad, first we reviewed the related literature and then coded each motive or reason under the related factor. Additionally, as this study focuses on socioeconomic status of participants, we also collected data about the participants themselves and their families. This section included questions on gender, age, year in medical school, length of stay in Türkiye, ethnicity, perceived Turkish and English language proficiency, average monthly income in Türkiye, pre-school education, political leanings, parents’ education level and professions, parents’ income level, number of siblings, rural situation of the region where they grew up, and the country they want to live in after graduation.

Data collection process

After receiving the ethics committee approval for the research from the “Social and Human Sciences Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Board”. We collected informed consent forms and informed participants about the confidentiality of data, voluntariness, and anonymity of participation. Data collection process was conducted online between 2022 and 2023. Participants were reminded to complete the form in the language they felt most comfortable with-Turkish, English, French and Arabic. They were invited via e-mail individually.

Analysis

We created a unique data set by performing data cleaning before data analysis. We used descriptive statistics to summarize students’ demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. We used chi-square tests to detect differences in the characteristics of the participants. ANOVA was used for differences in students’ reasons for choosing Türkiye according to their ethnic origins; then Scheffe analysis was used as a post-hoc tests to reveal differences between subgroups after ANOVA. In the data analysis conducted in this study, we determined the significance level as p < 0.05.

Findings

Student characteristics

Nearly half of the international medical students in Türkiye (49%) are female. The students were mostly born in 2000–2005 (70%), 9% were preparatory, 21 were first grade, 18 were second grade, 18 were third grade, 16 were fourth grade, 18 were fifth grade and 8 were sixth grade students. Their ages ranged between 29 and 19 and 72% of the participants received pre-school education.

When the time spent in Türkiye is examined, it is seen that almost all of them do not have any pre-university experience in Türkiye. While the participants’ perceived Turkish language proficiency is at an advanced level, their English language proficiency is mostly at an intermediate level. Participants were asked about their average monthly income in Türkiye. Responses were grouped into four levels, with 37% of respondents saying their monthly income was less than 2000 Turkish Lira (approximately $62); 32% stated that it was 2000–4999 Turkish Lira, 16% stated that it was 5000–7999 Turkish Lira and 15% stated that it was 8000 Turkish Lira and above.

In the study, students were asked about their political tendencies. A total of 312 (65%) of our participants answered the question about their political tendencies (they were able to select more than one option). The proportion of participants who describe themselves as liberal/humanist and nationalist is 22%. These were followed by conservative/religious (19%) and feminists (8%).

We discussed the countries where the participants wanted to live after graduation based on continents. Only 18% of the participants want to continue living in their country after graduation. On the other hand, 54% of the students stated that they wanted to continue their post-graduation in European countries (mainly Northern European Countries), and 17% stated that they wanted to continue their lives in North American countries (mostly Canada) (Table 1).

Characteristics of participants

Ethnicity

We considered the ethnic origins of the participants based on their home countries (Table 2). In this context, we grouped the participants’ countries as (i) Middle East (Syria, Iraq, Iran, etc.), (ii) Turkic Republics (Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, etc.), (iii) South Asia (India, Pakistan, Afghanistan, etc.), (iv) European, and (v) African countries. Nearly half (47%) of international medical school students in Türkiye are of Middle Eastern origin. Students of Middle Eastern origin are followed by students from the Turkic Republics (16%), Europe (15%), Africa (14%) and South Asia (9%). The five highest countries are; Syria (16%), Iraq (15%), Azerbaijan (11%), Iran (10%) and Pakistan (6%). It differs significantly between the ethnicities of international medical school students and general international students in Türkiye (p < 0.05, χ 2 = 169, df = 5). International students in medical school are more likely to be of Middle Eastern, European and South Asian origin, and less likely to be of Turkic Republic and African Asian origin, compared to other international undergraduate students in Türkiye.

Family characteristics

Education and professions of parents

While the mothers of 45% of the participants are university graduates, only 12% of them have primary school education or lower (Table 3). On the other hand, while the primary school or lower education level rate of fathers is only 3%, the university graduation rate is 59% (Table 3). A significant difference was found in terms of education levels of both mothers (X2 = 48.31, p < 0.01) and fathers (X2 = 65.93, p < 0.05) according to their ethnicity. While the mothers of students of European origin are mostly high school graduates (53%), the mothers of students of Turkic Republic origin are university graduates (67%). On the other hand, the mothers of students of Middle Eastern and African origin are mostly primary and secondary school graduates. A similar distribution is valid for father’s education level.

For the occupations of the participants’ parents, we used the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) classification created by The International Labor Organization (ILO), which is widely used in the literature. Half of the participants (49%) have working mothers and most of the employees do unskilled jobs (22%). The students’ fathers mostly work in unskilled jobs (34%), as professionals (22%) and as managers (12%). As with the level of education, a significant difference was found in terms of the education levels of both mothers (X2 = 87.82, p < 0.05) and fathers (X2 = 162.29, p < 0.05) according to their ethnicity. While the fathers of students of European origin are predominantly farmers, foresters and fishermen (25%) and crafts and related tradesmen (17%), the fathers of students of African and Middle Eastern origin work in unskilled jobs.

Number of siblings, rurality and economic status

Nearly half of the participants come from families with four or more children (48%). A significant difference was found in the number of siblings according to their ethnicity (X 2 = 140.36, p < 0.05). Students of Middle Eastern and African origin come from larger families than students of other origins. Especially students from European and Turkic Republics originate from families with two or three children.

The proportion of participants who spent their early childhood (0–7 years) in large-scale cities (100,000 or more population) is 58%. The rate of those who grew up in small-scale rural areas with a population of less than 1,000 is 19%, and 14% grew up in large-scale rural areas (with a population of 1,000 and 2-999). A significant difference was found in terms of rurality according to ethnicity (X 2 = 134.37, p < 0.05). Especially students from European and Turkic Republics are more likely to come from rural areas than other origins.

Participants were asked to answer the economic levels of their families in six groups according to their country. In this context, 44% of the participants come from families with middle, 19% from upper-middle, 15% from low-middle, 11% from low, 9% from high and 2% from very high economic level families. A significant difference was found in the economic levels of the participants’ families according to their ethnic origins (X 2 = 91.89, p < 0.05). Students of South Asian, Middle Eastern and African origin are more likely to come from economically higher families than other origins (Table 4).

Reasons for medical Faculty students to choose Türkiye

We examined the reasons why international students choose Türkiye within a 23-factor survey. Among the reasons why medical school students prefer Türkiye, “cheap country”, “geographical location”, “religious closeness”, “cultural closeness” and “the value of education received in a foreign country” stand out as the most important factors. On the other hand, “part-time work opportunities while studying”, “art and music”, “experiencing Turkish culture”, “career opportunities in Türkiye” and “gender equality” are not among the priority factors for international students in Türkiye (Table 5).

The reasons why students prefer Türkiye differ significantly according to their ethnic origins (p < 0.01). The most important reasons for students from Turkic Republics are “educational comparison with my own country”, “value of education received in a foreign country”, “geographical location”, “religious proximity” and “cultural proximity”, while students of Middle Eastern origin choose Türkiye because of “geographical location”, “cultural proximity”, “religious proximity”, “peaceful and safe society” and “lifestyle”. The reasons for the students of European origin are “cultural closeness”, “cheap country”, “religious closeness”, “geographic location”, “family friends or partners living in Türkiye.” In addition, reasons for students of African origin are “scholarships”, “cheap country”, “the value of education received in a foreign country”, “improving career opportunities in my own country”, “peaceful and safe society”, “reputation of the university” and “religious proximity”. Finally, students of South Asian origin choose Türkiye for “experiencing Turkish culture”, “scholarships”, “peaceful and safe society”, “cultural closeness”, and “family, friends or partners living in Türkiye”.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we focused on international students, who have been partially neglected in the medical education literature. In our study, the reasons why international medical students in Türkiye, which has one of the largest higher education capacities in Europe and is also among the top 10 most preferred countries in the world by international students, and their family structures were examined according to their ethnic origins.

According to the findings of the study, the highest number of students to study at the faculty of medicine in Türkiye come from Middle Eastern countries, and among these, Syria comes first. It can be said that one of the most important reasons for this is the agreement made between Türkiye and Syria. An agreement was made between the two countries due to the high tuition fees of international students and the internal turmoil in Syria. In Article 3 of the decision numbered 7384 published in the official gazette numbered 32,249 published by the Presidency, it is clearly stated that Syrian citizens are expected to receive scholarships from the Higher Education Institution [15]. It can be said that the majority of Syrian students come to Türkiye have their education on scholarship.

According to the findings, after the Middle East, the majority of the participants come from the Turkic Republics, relying on cultural proximity, language and history. This situation can be explained by the concept of cultural distance. The concept of cultural distance refers to the similarity between two cultures [8]. According to Babiker et al. [3], international students can measure the distance between their own culture and the new culture using 10 basic parameters. These are climate, clothing, language, education level, food, religion, financial well-being, entertainment activities, family structure, and dating/marriage. If the similarities between two cultures are evaluated in terms of these parameters, there is a decrease in the anxiety and culture shock experienced by international students. However, another striking finding in this research is that the distribution of ethnic origins of international medical faculty students differs from the ethnic origins of international students in other faculties. International students from Europe and South Asia prefer to study medicine in Türkiye more than other majors. It would be more meaningful to discuss this finding together with another finding of the study. According to another finding of the research, approximately 70% of international medical school students want to live in Europe or North America after graduation. When these two findings are evaluated together, it is seen that students from Europe want to return to Europe or North America after completing their education in Türkiye, in other words, they preferred Türkiye only for education. This situation highlights two major motives to choose Türkiye, which are value of education and low costs. When Turkish higher education institutions are compared with their European and American counterparts based on international rankings, only a few universities take place top 500 and most of them do not offer medical education. This indicated that even though rankings are not the best, medical education is more valuable than other majors.

In this study, the family structures of international medical faculty students were discussed from different perspectives and examined according to their ethnic origins. Accordingly, 45% of the students’ mothers and 59%’s fathers are university graduates. A striking point in the findings of the research is that the graduation rates of the parents of international students differ according to their ethnic origins. Accordingly, while European mothers and fathers are more likely to be high school graduates, Turkic Republic mothers and fathers are more likely to be university graduates. This situation is related to the policies implemented by European countries and Turkic republic countries regarding education and labor market. It implements the young and fast policy in the European labor market. Some European governments are concerned about the issue of prolonged university studies. They think that students waste too much time before higher education graduation thus they are not at the labor market’s disposal. Besides, European governments fear that talented youth may stay too long outside the labor force, thus reducing both individual and social life-long returns to higher education [12]. Therefore, European individuals are encouraged to join the workforce quickly and while they are young. This finding also coincides with the finding regarding the professions of the parents in the study. Accordingly, European parents have occupations such as farming, forestry, fishing or crafts or tradesmen that require direct participation in the workforce, rather than professional occupations that require higher education graduation. It is seen that the families of students coming from the Turkic republics are mostly university graduates and work in professional occupations. Due to the tuition fees of students from the Turkic republics, it is not surprising that their families have professional occupations. Because receiving medical education requires a certain economic burden [4]. Considering that the salaries of individuals with university degrees are higher than those with high school degrees or less [11], it is understandable that the economic level of the parents of students from the Turkic republics is higher than those of other ethnic origins.

According to the findings, only 11% of international students perceive the economic level of their families as low, while the others perceive it as medium and above. In short, although the family structures of international students vary according to their ethnic origins, we can say that their economic levels are at a level that can afford the education of their children. According to the 1st paragraph of the 3rd article of the presidential decree no. 32,249 published in the Official Gazette, the tuition fees that international students must pay are at least around 82.800 TL annually [15]. Considering that medical education in Türkiye lasts 6 years, it can be said that the economic status of their families is critical for international students studying at medical school. While a European family get this wage by doing crafts, fishing, forestry and tradesmanship, a Turkic family gets it by working in a professional occupation, an African family works in unskilled jobs, and a Middle Eastern family pays it through scholarships. Therefore, it can be said that the family structures of international medical faculty students in Türkiye vary considerably according to their ethnic origins.

Finally, in this study, the reasons why international medical faculty students prefer Türkiye were examined according to their ethnic origins and it was found that the reasons of preference differed according to their ethnic origins. Accordingly, while religious proximity and cultural proximity come to the fore for students coming from the Turkic Republics, being a peaceful society is also added to these reasons for students coming from the Middle East. The major reason for this is that, in addition to the actively ongoing Palestinian-Israeli conflict and the Syrian conflict, the region is also surrounded by other long-term conflict zones such as Iran-Iraq and the region is also surrounded by other long-term conflict in the conflict zones such as Afghanistan, the Caucasus, the Horn of Africa, and Sudan [17]. Therefore, it is understandable that students in the Middle East see Türkiye as a safe and peaceful environment for education. In addition, it is noteworthy that scholarships are among the reasons why students from South Asian and African countries choose Türkiye. It can be said that they cover their education expenses with scholarships, especially due to the low income level in these countries. For this reason, scholarships, which do not appear as a reason for preference in other ethnicities but appear in these two ethnicities, are actually the main reason why these students can study in Türkiye.

In short, this research tried to reveal the ethnic origins and family structures of international medical students studying medicine in Türkiye and their reasons for choosing Türkiye. The demographic structures of the students vary greatly, and this diversity shows statistically significant differences according to their ethnic origins. In sum it can be said that children from families with middle and lower middle socio-economic levels from Europe, children from families with middle and upper middle socio-economic levels from the Turkic Republics, and children from families with low socio-economic levels from Africa, Middle East and South Asia choose to study medicine in Türkiye.

This study aimed to contribute to a better understanding of international medical school students’ needs and expectations when they choose Türkiye as a destination. Results showed that many of the participants believe the value of medical education offered in Türkiye in addition to low costs and proximity in several areas. These findings indicate that Türkiye has been able to offer affordable and quality medical education for many years and contributed to overcome potential inequality in access to health care in the future, resulting from doctors’ preferences to work in environments similar to their own origins. Also, this could be evaluated as a significant starting point while building medical school curriculum and medical education policies. However, there are several limitations of the study. As it is a descriptive nature, we could not deduce inner motivation of participants to choose Türkiye. Future studies on this issue could be developed in a more holistic way to understand why and why not prospective students choose Türkiye and any other country.
