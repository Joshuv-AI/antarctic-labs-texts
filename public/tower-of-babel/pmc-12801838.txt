Resilience and its determinants among family caregivers of palliative care patients in Nepal
Authors: Raj Kumar Gajmer, Kedar Prasad Baral, Paras Kumar Acharya, Kiweta Bista, Rashik Prajapati, Jeevan Thapa, Rajesh Nath Gongal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing incidence of patients requiring palliative care warrants holistic care approaches for patients where caregivers play vital roles. Understanding caregiver resilience and its determinants is crucial for developing targeted interventions to strengthen care for both patients and their caregivers.ObjectiveTo assess resilience levels among caregivers of palliative care patients and identify associated factors using the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in five palliative care centers in Nepal from 2023 to 2024 including 422 family caregivers of palliative care patients identified using the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicator Tool for Low-Income Settings (SPICT-LIS). Data were collected via demographic questionnaires and the validated Nepalese version of the CD-RISC. Resilience scores were reported as median and interquartile range. Associations were examined using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H), and effect sizes (epsilon-squared) were calculated for significant findings. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals and data analysis were performed in SPSS version 20 with effect size calculation in R-software.ResultsThe caregivers had a mean age of 38.03 years (SD ± 12.3), with 51.2% being male. The resilience scores ranged from 13 to 100 (median 70.5). Caregiver resilience was significantly associated with perceived health status (p < 0.001) and patient diagnosis (p = 0.002). Caregivers with “very good” or “good” perceived health demonstrated higher resilience scores (median 74 and 73, respectively). Those caring for patients with cardiopulmonary conditions presented the highest resilience (median 75), whereas chronic kidney disease caregivers presented the greatest variability in resilience scores.ConclusionsThe patient’s diagnosis and the caregivers’ perceived health status were important determinants of caregiver resilience in Nepal’s palliative care context. These results emphasize that routine palliative treatment in Nepal and other low and middle income countries should incorporate routine caregiver health assesments and support. Educational initiatives and support groups tailored to a particular diagnosis can assist caregivers in overcoming the burden of care and build resilience. In addition to community based programs that honor cultural caregiving roles, training healthcare staff to recognize and support caregiver needs may reduce caregiver burden and improve the sustainability of care.Keywords: Caregivers, CD-RISC, LMIC, Nepal, Palliative care, Resilience

Introduction

The global burden of chronic noncommunicable diseases and aging populations has led to an unprecedented increase in palliative care needs. Approximately 56.8 million people worldwide require palliative care, including 31.1 million people before the end of life and 25.7 million people near death. These patients present complex needs requiring prolonged care, creating substantial physical, financial, and psychosocial/emotional burdens on their caregivers [1–4].

Caregivers are those individuals in close supportive roles who share the illness experience and undertake vital care work and emotional management, who may or may not be family members [2–7]. For sustainable long-term care, caregivers require resilience—an internal psychological resource that helps reduce emotional distress and maintain or restore mental health [8].

Resilience varies across cultures, societies, and geographical contexts, with different impacts on individuals. Caregiver resilience enables care recipients to remain within their social environments, reducing formal resource utilization and delaying or preventing institutional admissions. The World Health Organization has classified Nepal as having isolated palliative [9–12]care services (Level 3a) (WHO 2024), facing unique challenges due to cultural diversity, limited healthcare infrastructure, and socioeconomic disparities [11–13].

Recent systematic reviews underscore the necessity of investigating factors contributing to caregiver burden within local community settings, thereby supporting the appropriateness of culturally specific research methodologies [13]. While there is increasing literature on caregiver resilience in western contexts, research in south Asian settings, particularly Nepal, remains limited. Existing studies from neighboring countries such as India and Bangladesh suggest that cultural norms around family obligations and limited existing health support systems may influence caregiver resilience differently than in high income countries [14–16]. However, Nepal’s unique socio-cultural structure characterized by ethnic diversity (more than 125 ethnic groups), geographical challenges and evolving healthcare infrastructure necessitates context specific research. The intersection of mandatory caregiving roles, limited formal healthcare support, and diverse cultural practices contributes to distinct caregiving environment that may not be adequately understood through findings from other countries with different contexts. Furthermore, no published studies have systematically examined the factors influencing resilience among family caregivers in Nepal’s Palliative care settings.

This study addresses this research gap by assessing the level of resilience among caregivers and identifying factors associated with it which will inform evidence-based program strategies for supporting caregivers and enhancing holistic patient care.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from 2023 to 2024 across five palliative care centers in Nepal: Patan Academy of Health Sciences (Lalitpur), Green Pasture Hospital (Pokhara), Hospice Nepal, Bhaktapur Cancer Hospital and Makawanpur Community-based Palliative Care program area. These sites provide formal palliative care services, with Makawanpur representing a rural geographic setting ensuring diverse representation. Though the predominance of facility-based recruitment may under represent caregivers with limited access to formal healthcare services, this functional approach was necessary given resource limitations and limited palliative care services in Nepal (Table 1).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee, Patan Academy of Health Sciences (Reference No. FLW2309051795). Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted adhering to the ethical principles outlined in Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants and sampling

Family caregivers of patients needing palliative care composed the study population. The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, providing care for ≥1 month, and the ability to read and write in Nepali. Caregivers with mental health disorders or a history of psychiatric conditions were excluded.

The sample size for the study was calculated using an assumed prevalence of 0.5, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence interval, resulting in sample size of 384 participants. To address possible non-response, the number was increased by 10%, yielding the final sample size of 422. Caregivers were recruited parallelly in all participating sites through a pragmatic, convenience-based approach. Those participants who were interested and met eligibility criteria were enrolled after informed consent. This recruitment method was chosen because it allowed the team to efficiently include participants from diverse sites despite limited resources.

Data collection

Patient identification

Patients needing Palliative care were identified using Nepalese version of Supportive and Palliative Care Indicator Tool for Low-Income Settings (SPICT-LIS) [17, 18]. Patient performance status was assessed via the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale [19].

Caregiver assessment

The Nepalese version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) was administered to assess the resilience of caregiver [18]. The CD-RISC is a 25-item instrument grounded in stress, coping, and adaptation research. The CD-RISC has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse populations and cultural contexts worldwide. The items are scored on a 5-point scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating greater resilience [7, 20–22]. The Nepalese version has been validated in individuals with chronic pain, demonstrating good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.93) and test-retest reliability [23, 24]. While this tool was not specifically validated in caregivers prior to this study in Nepal, the high reliability coefficient, and validation across diverse populations and cultural contexts worldwide support its appropriateness for Nepal.

Data collectors were trained research assistants who received a two-day training session covering: study objectives and ethical considerations, standardized administration of the CD-RISC and demographic questionnaires, techniques for minimizing interviewer bias, and procedures for handling participant questions or distress. Regular supervision and quality checks were conducted throughout the data collection period to ensure consistency.

Demographic data

Comprehensive demographic information, including age, sex, education, marital status, health status, and duration of caregiving, was collected for both patients and caregivers.

Statistical analysis

The categorical variables were presented with frequency and percentage, while the continuous variable were summarized using mean and standard deviation. The resilience score was summarized using the median and inter-quartile range due to non-normal distribution assessed by Kolmogorov Smirnov test (p-value < 0.001), and its association was assessed using non-parametric test (Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal Wallis H test). The analysis was done via SPSS version 20. For the significant association of resilience score with diagnosis and perceived health status, the effect size was further assessed by computing epsilon-squared(ε²) from the H statistics using R software. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Caregiver characteristics

The study included 422 caregivers with a mean age of 38.03 years (SD ± 12.3). Males comprised 51.2% of the participants. The duration of care ranged from 1 month to 27 years. Most caregivers were Hindu (82.7%), married (76.5%), and had secondary education (44.5%). The majority lived in joint families (55.9%) and had no chronic diseases (89.3%). Blood relatives (parents, children, grandparents) constituted 64% of the caregivers.

Patient characteristics

The patients had a mean age of 58 years (SD ± 20), with 53.1% being older than 60 years. The age range was 5–98 years, with 54.5% being female. The most common conditions requiring palliative care were cancer (44.8%), followed by neurological diseases (17.3%) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Most patients (97.4%) had single conditions, and 65.4% had a performance status of ECOG 0–2.

Resilience scores

The CD-RISC scores ranged from 13 to 100, with a median of 70.5(IQR:15), indicating substantial heterogeneity in resilience capacity among caregivers.

Factors associated with caregiver resilience

Caregiver factors

The perceived health status of the caregiver was strongly associated with resilience (p < 0.001). Caregivers who rated their health as “very good” or “good” demonstrated significantly higher resilience scores (median 74 and 73, respectively) than those who reported “average” (67), “poor” (65), or “very poor” (45) health, indicating a graded relationship. The effect size (ε²) was estimated to be 0.014, indicating a small effect size.

No other caregiver demographic characteristics (age, sex, education, marital status, religion, family type, caregiving history, or relationship with the patient) were significantly associated with resilience scores (Table 2).

Patient factors

A significant association was observed between patient diagnosis and caregiver resilience (p < 0.001). Caregivers of patients with cardiopulmonary conditions presented the highest resilience scores (median 75) with low variability. Those caring for patients with chronic kidney disease presented the greatest variability in resilience scores, whereas those caring for patients with cancer and neurological conditions presented intermediate levels. A small effect size (ε² = 0.043) was estimated for the association.

Patient age, sex, number of conditions, and performance status were not significantly associated with caregiver resilience (Table 3).

Discussion

Caregiving in Nepal

Evaluation of 422 caregivers of patients needing palliative care via the Nepali version of the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale revealed substantial heterogeneity in resilience capacity (range 13–100, median 70.5). Similar finding was found in the study done by Connor KM et al. and Huerzeler and et al. [7, 25]. The CD-RISC has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse populations and cultural contexts in multiple Western studies. This finding corresponds with recent evidence from Cao et al. and Levasseur et al., which highlights that in resource-limited settings with culturally mandated caregiving, contextual factors may be more predictive than individual demographics [26, 27]. In Nepalese society, family caregivers function as informal healthcare providers, managing complex medical, physical, and psychological care responsibilities within a resource-limited healthcare system. In Nepal, the healthcare system has limited palliative care services, a challenging geographic area, and cultural expectations that position caregiving as a moral obligation.

Primary determinants of resilience

Self-perceived health status

In this study, the most significant association with caregiver resilience was self-perceived health status (p < 0.001, ε²=0.043). Caregivers who rated their health as “very good” or “good” demonstrated significantly higher resilience scores (median 74 and 73, respectively) than those who reported “average,” “poor,” or “very poor” health, indicating a clear dose‒response relationship.

This relationship operates through several mechanisms: healthy caregivers are better able to handle physical demands, maintain their energy levels, and solve problems efficiently. According to the stress process model, the link between caring stressors and outcomes is influenced by caregiver health resources [28]. It is important to note that this relationship may be bidirectional while better health may enable greater resilience, higher resilience may also contribute to better health maintenance through more effective stress management and self-care behaviors. Recent studies have confirmed that greater resilience is correlated with lower caregiver burden and perceived stress, whereas caregiver health indicators have shown notable changes during healthcare crises [29–31]. This finding is consistent with Aaron Antonovsky’s Salutogenesis framework, which emphasizes on factors that promote human health and supports well-being [32]. In Nepal, where healthcare resources are limited and preventive and curative services are not readily available with cultural expectations of caregiver self-sacrifice, these findings highlight caregiver health as a key intervention point. This finding is consistent with the biopsychosocial model of health, which suggests that subjective health perceptions can be more predictive of outcomes than objective measures [33].

Patient diagnosis

Patient diagnosis was significantly associated with caregiver resilience (p value < 0.001, ε²= 0.014), with distinct patterns across conditions:

Cardiopulmonary conditions presented the highest resilience scores (median 75) with low variability, likely reflecting disease normalization as “natural aging” and predictable symptom patterns enabling effective management routines. Clear management protocols of these conditions provide clear and concrete action plans for caregivers making them more confident in caring [34–36].

The lower resilience scores associated with neurological conditions might be due to unique challenges, including progressive cognitive decline, behavioral symptoms requiring 24-hour vigilance, and unpredictable disease trajectories, leading to chronic stress [37]. A Systemic review on older adults with chronic illness done by Chandrani Isac et al. showed that in conditions such as dementia, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and multi-morbidity, caregivers experience heightened demands (high burden indicating lower resilience) due to care recipients’ dependence, behavioral challenges (e.g., memory issues, agitation), and unpredictable health trajectories [16]. This lower score can be correlated with the concept of ambiguous loss, where caregivers grieve for someone who is physically present but psychologically absent. This is particularly correlated with the caregivers of dementia patients [38, 39].

Chronic kidney disease exhibited the greatest resilience variability, likely reflecting heterogeneous responses to complex treatment regimens ranging from dietary management to dialysis, with outcomes varying by treatment access and financial resources [40, 41]. The mix-method cross-sectional study done by Ramasamy et al. demonstrates that nearly all caregivers of hemodialysis patients in central India experience moderate to severe caregiving burden, which significantly impairs their quality of life [42]. Though they used other tool for assessment of burden (Zarit Burden Index) and quality of life (World Health organization Quality of life Brief Version) the result shows high caregiver burden signifying lower resilience. A recent comprehensive cross-sectional study by Abdullah et al. on caregivers of CKD patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis found that 91.1% of caregivers experienced significant burden indicating lower resilience [43]. In Nepal, where dialysis access is limited, concentrated in urban centers, high-cost and troublesome to acquire appointment timesfor dialysis, this variability may be more pronounced than in settings with better healthcare infrastructure.

Within the study population, caregivers looking after patients with cancer exhibited significantly higher levels of resilience compared to those caring for patients with other non-cancer conditions. Similar findings were found in the systemic review done by Mollaei and et al [44, 45]. Caregivers of cancer patients face stigma-related challenges, with cultural beliefs associating cancer with karma or inevitable death. This concept of inevitable death may play a role in developing resilience.

Though effect size is small for both significantly associated determinants, they represent clinically meaningful differences in resilience capacity that may influence caregiver functioning and patient care outcomes.

Implications for policy and practice in Nepal and similar contexts

Our findings closely support Nepal’s National Strategy for Palliative Care 2025–2035 and offer clear direction for improving caregiver support. Since caregivers’ own health strongly influences their resilience, routine checks that cover both physical and mental well-being should be part of palliative care services. Different illnesses also call for different types of support system. For example, caregivers of patients with neurological conditions may need dementia-focused training, respite care, and counseling; those supporting people with kidney disease may benefit from financial advice and nutrition guidance; and cancer caregivers can gain from peer support and efforts to reduce stigma.

Training healthcare workers to understand what caregivers need, offer practical timely advice, and link families to useful local services is essential especially in rural Nepal, where access is limited and support can be hard to find. Community-focused solutions that draw strength from local customs and involve health volunteers like those seen in Makawanpur show that it’s possible to reach even remote villages. Medical expenses being unaffordable for many families, programs should also point the way towards financial help and existing subsidies. All of these steps suggest realistic ways forward as Nepal works to put its palliative care strategy into action, while also helping officials plan for future needs.

Study limitations

As this was a cross-sectional study, causal relationships cannot be established. Additionally, the convenience sampling approach drawn from healthcare facilities may have resulted in an overrepresentation of caregivers with relatively better access to services. The CD-RISC tool may not capture all culturally relevant aspects of resilience such as religious adjustments, extended family support, caring obligations. We did not measure other cofounders like household income, social support networks, religious and spiritual coping mechanisms, all of which may influence resilience. Future studies could employ longitudinal designs to asses resilience trajectories over the caregiving course. Mix-methods research can be adopted to explore culturally incorporated resilience processes and indigenous coping mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study found that the primary determinants of factors affecting family caregivers’ resilience are patients’ diagnosis and the caregiver’s self-perceived health status. This emphasizes the need to incorporate caregiver health assessments into holistic patient care rather than considering it as second thought. These findings have important implications for Nepal’s continued palliative care growth and are comparable to those of low and middle income countries. Diagnosis-based support, caregiver health assessments and specific interventions in routine healthcare services, specific training for healthcare professionals to recognize and meet caregiver requirements, and the application of community-based, culturally adopted interventions are advised. Such interventions are critical for affordable and durable healthcare delivery in resource limited settings like ours and many LMICs. Furthermore, to better understand resilience of caregivers, design focused interventions, and guide the development of support models that are completely integrated into Nepal’s complex cultural and caring environment, longitudinal and qualitative research is also needed.
