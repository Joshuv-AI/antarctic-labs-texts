Advances in Magnesium‐Based Thermoelectrics: A Critical Review
Authors: Li‐Min Zhang, Li Zhang, Nan‐Hai Li, Yan‐Ling Yang, Xiao‐Lei Shi, Zhi‐Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials have emerged as a key research direction toward large‐scale applications, owing to their elemental abundance, low material cost, good environmental compatibility, and competitive thermoelectric performance in the low‐to‐mid temperature range. Given the rapid progress achieved in this field, this review systematically surveys the latest advances in three representative classes of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials: Mg3X2 (X = Sb, Bi), MgAgSb, and Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn). Emphasis is placed on their crystal structures and electronic band features, phonon transport behavior, and carrier scattering mechanisms, together with a discussion of bulk and thin‐film fabrication strategies and their impacts on thermoelectric performance. Commonalities and distinct characteristics of performance optimization through carrier concentration tuning, band engineering, microstructural design, and interface engineering are summarized. Furthermore, key challenges associated with chemical and thermal stability as well as device integration are critically assessed, and device‐level design principles such as electrode contacting, diffusion barrier layers, and substrate selection are systematically reviewed. Finally, considering the current physical and engineering bottlenecks, several critical directions for the future development of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials are proposed, highlighting the importance of a paradigm shift from “material‐level performance optimization” toward “material‐device‐system co‐design” to accelerate their practical deployment.Keywords: application, device, magnesium, materials, thermoelectric

Introduction

With the rapid growth of global energy demand and the increasing severity of environmental challenges, the development of efficient and environmentally benign energy conversion technologies has become an urgent task in the modern energy landscape. Thermoelectric materials, which directly convert heat into electricity, have attracted widespread interest owing to their unique advantages, including the absence of moving parts, high reliability, and environmental friendliness, and have found broad applications in waste‐heat recovery, solid‐state refrigeration, and flexible devices [1, 2]. The thermoelectric effect is fundamentally manifested through the Seebeck, Peltier, and Thomson effects, among which the Seebeck effect is one of the most widely exploited principles in thermoelectric materials. It enables the generation of an electrical voltage driven by a temperature gradient (ΔT), thereby realizing the conversion of thermal energy into electrical energy. The performance of thermoelectric materials is commonly evaluated by the dimensionless figure of merit, ZT, defined as ZT = S 2 σT/κ, where S is the Seebeck coefficient, σ is the electrical conductivity, T is the operating temperature, and κ is the thermal conductivity. The κ consists of two components: the electronic contribution (κ e) and the lattice contribution (κ l) [3]. However, the three key thermoelectric parameters (S, σ, and κ) are intrinsically coupled, as they are all governed by the carrier concentration (n) and the electronic band structure of the material. As a result of this inherent interdependence, targeted optimization of any single parameter is often achieved at the expense of the others. It is precisely this trade‐off among the fundamental transport parameters that has made the substantial enhancement of the ZT a longstanding bottleneck and central challenge in the field [4].

In general, different thermoelectric materials exhibit their maximum ZT (ZT max) values over distinct temperature ranges [5]. For example, GeTe‐based materials are typically classified as mid‐temperature thermoelectrics, with their ZT max occurring in the range of approximately 500–800 K [6, 7], whereas oxide thermoelectrics [8] or alloy‐based systems such as Half‐Heusler compounds [9] are representative high‐temperature thermoelectric materials, whose high‐ZT regime is usually above ∼900 K. In contrast, near‐room‐temperature thermoelectric materials display their optimal ZT values in the vicinity of room temperature (from room temperature to ∼400 K) [10]. Owing to their high thermoelectric performance near ambient conditions, these materials are particularly well suited for near‐room‐temperature thermoelectric energy conversion applications, such as wearable sustainable power supply and wearable cooling, and thus hold considerable application and commercial potential [11]. Conventional near‐room‐temperature thermoelectric materials, such as Bi2Te3 and its alloys, have long demonstrated excellent thermoelectric performance in the low‐ and mid‐temperature ranges [12]. However, practical issues remain, including the high cost associated with the Te element, which leads to a relatively low cost‐performance ratio [13]. These limitations have stimulated increasing interest in sustainable and environmentally benign alternative materials. In recent years, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials have emerged as a research hotspot owing to their low cost, elemental abundance, and high thermoelectric potential, making them suitable for a broad range of low‐ to mid‐temperature thermoelectric applications [14, 15]. Throughout the development of Mg‐based thermoelectrics, the key milestones summarized in Figure 1 have laid the foundation for their remarkable performance improvements (Figure 1). In 2012, band convergence in Mg2Si1− x Sn x unlocked a new route to enhance the S without compromising carrier mobility (µ), achieving ZT ≈ 1.3 [16]. For MgAgSb, the identification of the room‐temperature α‐phase in 2012 paved the way for subsequent nanocomposite engineering [17], culminating in a record power factor (S 2 σ) of 27.2 µW cm−1 K−2 in 2024 through the introduction of MgCuSb nanoprecipitates [18]. The most transformative progress has occurred in n‐type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2. Following the initial demonstration of ZT ≈ 1.5 in 2016 [19], phase boundary mapping in 2018 revealed Mg vacancies (VMg) as the intrinsic defect governing p‐type behavior, establishing Mg‐excess as the essential thermodynamic state for n‐type performance [20]. Most recently, a “lattice repair” strategy in 2026 elevated the VMg formation energy while introducing hierarchical phonon scattering centers, delivering a record peak ZT ≈ 2.1 [21]. These achievements are driven by the urgent demand for sustainable and environmentally friendly thermoelectric materials, while simultaneously underscoring the rapid rise of Mg‐based thermoelectrics as a compelling platform for sustainable waste‐heat recovery. As evidenced by the table, achieving high conversion efficiency (η) requires not only the selection of high‐performance n‐type and p‐type materials with optimal ZT values across broad ΔTs, but also the sophisticated engineering of modular architectures. Among these materials, the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 system, as one of the most representative magnesium‐based thermoelectric families, has been extensively studied due to its high σ, low κ, and favorable ZT. In recent years, researchers have significantly enhanced its thermoelectric performance by effectively tuning the n, electronic band structure, and crystal structure through strategies such as doping, alloying, and defect engineering [22, 23, 24, 25]. In addition, the MgAgSb system has also demonstrated outstanding thermoelectric performance. Owing to its high band degeneracy and relatively low κ e, MgAgSb exhibits a distinct advantage in p‐type thermoelectric performance. Recent studies have shown that, by optimizing dopant species and crystal growth conditions, the ZT of MgAgSb can reach notably high values [26]. Furthermore, the Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn) family, as another class of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials with great potential, has emerged as a promising research direction due to its favorable thermoelectric performance and thermal stability in the mid‐ to high‐temperature range [27].

In view of the rapid development of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials and their associated devices, this article, drawing on the authors’ recent research experience in magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials and devices, reviews the recent advances in magnesium‐based thermoelectric systems, including Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, MgAgSb, and Mg2X. Particular emphasis is placed on their structural characteristics, performance optimization strategies, and application prospects. We focus on the latest progress in understanding structure‐property relationships, performance enhancement approaches, and device integration and applications, while also summarizing the key challenges and future research directions identified in existing studies. Through a comprehensive synthesis and critical analysis of recent achievements, this review aims to provide both theoretical insights and practical guidance to support the continued development of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials.

Three Representative Classes of Magnesium‐Based Thermoelectric Materials

Mg3X2 (X = Sb, Bi) Systems

Material Structure

Mg3Sb2 and Mg3Bi2 are stable at room temperature in a trigonal crystal structure with the space group P 3¯ m1. This structure is commonly described as the CaAl2Si2‐type and is also classified as the inverse α‐La2O3‐type structure. As typical Zintl phases, their unique bonding characteristics and electronic band structures provide a favorable foundation for thermoelectric performance optimization. As shown in Figure 3a, Mg3Sb2 crystallizes in a trigonal system (space group P 3¯ m1), and its structure can be described as a layered architecture alternately stacked along the c‐axis. On the one hand, from the perspective of atomic composition and arrangement, the structure consists of Mg2+ cation layers (occupying the Mg1 sites) and covalently bonded anionic framework layers [Mg2Sb2]2− (comprising Mg2 and Sb sites), which are periodically arranged along the c‐axis. The interlayer interactions are dominated by ionic bonding [19, 177]. On the other hand, in terms of chemical bonding and coordination environments, the [Mg2Sb2]2− (X = Sb, Bi) polyanionic layers are constructed from tetrahedrally coordinated Mg and Sb atoms interconnected by strong covalent bonds, forming a robust two‐dimensional covalent framework. In contrast, the octahedrally coordinated Mg2+ cation layers are inserted between these frameworks and interact with the adjacent polyanionic layers through typical ionic bonds [178, 179, 180]. Essentially, this structure arises from the spatially ordered alternation of a covalently bonded anionic framework and ionically bonded cation layers. Such an “alternating covalent‐ionic layered” structural motif underpins the coexistence of moderate σ and intrinsically low κ in these materials.

As illustrated in Figure 3b, the thermoelectric performance of the Mg3X2 (X = Sb, Bi) system is fundamentally governed by its distinctive electronic band structure. As a typical Zintl‐phase semiconductor, this system combines the advantages of a relatively light effective mass (m*) with high valley degeneracy, providing a solid electronic‐structure basis for achieving an excellent S 2 σ. Mg3Sb2 is an indirect‐band‐gap semiconductor, and both theoretical and experimental studies have shown that its conduction‐band minimum (CBM) is located near the L point of the Brillouin zone and exhibits pronounced multivalley degeneracy [181, 182]. First‐principles calculations reveal that the conduction band (CB) consists of multiple equivalent electron valleys, with an effective valley degeneracy exceeding six. This feature significantly enhances the density‐of‐states m*, thereby increasing the S without markedly compromising µ [68]. Meanwhile, the valence‐band maximum (VBM) of Mg3Sb2 is relatively flat, resulting in a large hole m*, which makes the intrinsic electronic structure more favorable for n‐type transport. This characteristic constitutes one of the key electronic‐structure origins underlying the generally superior n‐type performance compared with p‐type behavior in the Mg3Sb2 system. In comparison, Mg3Bi2 exhibits more complex band‐structure characteristics. Owing to the strong spin‐orbit coupling (SOC) associated with Bi atoms, its band gap (E g) is substantially narrowed. Some studies have suggested that Mg3Bi2 approaches a semimetallic state in the ideal crystalline limit and may even display weak band inversion features [183]. Importantly, an appropriate Sb/Bi ratio enables synergistic optimization of “high band degeneracy‐high µ‐suppressed bipolar effect” in the low‐ to mid‐temperature range, which constitutes the core electronic‐structure foundation for achieving high ZT values in n‐type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 materials. Overall, the excellent thermoelectric performance of Mg3X2 (X = Sb, Bi) compounds originates from their highly tunable electronic band structures, characterized by multivalley CBs, adjustable E g, and strong coupling between defect chemistry and electronic transport. These attributes make Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 a prototypical material system for band‐engineering‐driven thermoelectric optimization.

The phonon spectra of the Mg3X2 (X = Sb, Bi) system play a crucial role in reducing κ and enhancing the ZT [184, 185]. Figure 3c presents the low‐frequency phonon dispersion of Mg3Sb2, where the most pronounced compositional variations are observed. In this representation, the magnitude and sign of the Grüneisen parameter are reflected by the thickness and color of the phonon branches, clearly revealing the strong anharmonicity of the phonons [186]. Recent studies indicate that the phonon behavior of Mg3X2 materials exhibits pronounced structural dependence, providing an important theoretical basis for further thermoelectric performance optimization. The phonon spectrum of Mg3Sb2 is characterized by a high density of low‐frequency phonon modes, which is essential for suppressing κ l. Specifically, low‐frequency phonons (primarily associated with lattice vibrations) dominate the phonon transport in the Mg3Sb2 system. These modes typically exhibit strong phonon–phonon interactions, which significantly reduce phonon group velocities and thereby effectively suppress κ [187]. Compared with Mg3Sb2, Mg3Bi2 displays a relatively simpler phonon spectrum, yet it still exhibits low κ and strong phonon scattering, particularly at elevated temperatures. Overall, the phonon spectra of Mg3X2 materials play a vital role in reducing κ and improving thermoelectric performance. Phonon‐carrier coupling effects and strategies for optimizing phonon scattering are therefore expected to remain key focuses of future research.

Although the crystal structure and electronic band features of the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 system have been systematically elucidated and established as a classic example of band‐engineering‐driven thermoelectric optimization, it should be noted that most existing discussions of the “multivalley degeneracy‐high µ” advantage are based on assumptions of ideal crystals or near‐equilibrium defect distributions. In practice, under conditions of heavy doping and multiphase regulation, local compositional fluctuations, defect correlations, and non‐ideal grain‐boundary potential barriers can substantially reshape the effective band structure near the Fermi level (E F), causing experimental systems to deviate from the applicability of single‐parabolic‐band or simple multivalley models. Moreover, although band‐gap narrowing and enhanced band degeneracy are beneficial for electrical transport in the mid‐ to high‐temperature range, they inevitably compress the safe operating window with respect to bipolar effects, resulting in a reduced tolerance of the optimal composition to variations in temperature and composition. This implies that reliance on band‐structure advantages alone is insufficient to guarantee stable high performance at the engineering scale, and their practical value must be further validated under realistic conditions involving complex defect chemistries and service environments.

Synthesis and Fabrication

The synthesis routes of the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 system have a profound impact on its thermoelectric performance, as the choice of fabrication method directly determines the microstructure, dopant homogeneity, and defect intensity of the material. In recent years, a variety of synthesis strategies have been developed, among which mechanical alloying, conventional melting, and the Bridgman method are the three most commonly employed approaches. These methods not only enable effective control over composition and crystal structure but also facilitate optimization of thermoelectric properties. A detailed discussion of these three synthesis routes is presented below.

Mechanical alloying is a solid‐state powder processing technique in which intense mechanical grinding induces repeated cold welding, fracturing, and re‐welding of powder particles through high‐energy collisions and friction, thereby enabling material synthesis and grain refinement [189]. High‐energy ball milling is particularly well suited for the doping and alloying of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, as it significantly enhances compositional homogeneity and microstructural refinement. Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 materials synthesized by ball milling typically exhibit reduced grain sizes, which strengthens grain‐boundary scattering, suppresses κ, and consequently improves thermoelectric performance. As illustrated in Figure 4a, this process is carried out under an argon atmosphere to suppress Mg oxidation. After precise weighing of the starting materials, high‐energy ball milling is employed to achieve uniform elemental dispersion and pre‐refinement of grains, followed by densification of the resulting powders via hot pressing (HP) [119] The mechanical forces generated during high‐energy ball milling not only shorten elemental diffusion paths but also introduce nanocrystalline grain boundaries and lattice distortions, providing a structural basis for enhanced phonon scattering and reduced κ l. Meanwhile, the sealed argon environment effectively prevents Mg evaporation and oxidation during milling, making this approach an efficient route for the scalable preparation of polycrystalline Mg3X2 powders. Furthermore, combining ball milling with post‐processing techniques such as HP or spark plasma sintering (SPS) enables further optimization of the microstructure and enhancement of thermoelectric properties. Bhardwaj et al. reported that bulk nanostructured Mg3Sb2 and Mg3Sb1.8Bi0.2 samples prepared by high‐energy ball milling followed by SPS exhibited a significant increase in the overall ZT compared with their conventionally prepared bulk counterparts [190]. Similarly, Yu et al. employed mechanical alloying in combination with SPS and co‐doping with Te and Y to optimize the thermoelectric performance of Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5, achieving a ZT max exceeding 1.7 at 703 K [61]. Despite its advantages in achieving excellent dopant homogeneity and microstructural refinement, mechanical alloying also presents certain challenges. The high mechanical stresses introduced during high‐energy ball milling can lead to non‐uniform defect formation or even phase segregation, which may adversely affect thermoelectric performance. Consequently, precise control over milling time and milling speed to avoid excessive deformation and over‐milling remains a critical factor in optimizing this synthesis route.

The conventional melting method is a traditional high‐temperature synthesis technique in which starting materials are reacted through melting at elevated temperatures to form alloys. Owing to its operational simplicity, readily available raw materials, and suitability for large‐scale synthesis, this method has been widely employed for the preparation of thermoelectric materials [191]. As illustrated in Figure 4b, the raw materials are first loaded into graphite crucibles (or carbon‐coated quartz tubes) and sealed in evacuated quartz tubes, followed by high‐temperature melting in a muffle furnace for a prescribed duration to achieve atomic‐scale compositional homogenization. The resulting ingots are subsequently densified by SPS, yielding dense, high‐performance alloy products [174]. In addition, the conventional melting approach allows precise tuning of phase purity and microstructure through careful control of processing parameters such as temperature, holding time, and heating rate. A key aspect of this strategy lies in compensating for Mg volatilization losses during melting and sintering by introducing excess Mg (e.g., ∼5 wt%) [76]. Meanwhile, the rapid heating and pressurization characteristics of SPS effectively suppress grain coarsening and secondary‐phase formation, thereby balancing high bulk density with microstructural integrity. As a result, this route is commonly used to fabricate thermoelectric materials with excellent electrical transport properties. Kihou et al. successfully synthesized Mg3+ δ Y x Sb1.5Bi0.5 samples under Mg‐rich conditions using the melting method, achieving a maximum n of 9.59×1019 cm−3 [95]. Lei employed a rare‐earth element, Gd, as an effective dopant to regulate the n of Mg3Sb1.3Bi0.7 alloys, and, by combining the melting method with SPS, realized a high n of up to 8.9 × 1019 cm−3 and a ZT max of 1.55 at 700 K [79]. However, a major challenge of this method lies in the requirement for prolonged reaction times and high processing temperatures, which can cause severe Mg volatilization and result in discrepancies between the actual and nominal compositions. Consequently, further optimization of the synthesis process is necessary for practical applications.

Beyond the commonly used melting‐sintering routes, crystal growth techniques based on the Bridgman method have attracted increasing attention because of their advantages in controlling grain orientation and reducing defect intensity. Among these, the vertical Bridgman technique and in situ loaded Bridgman growth are two representative approaches. The vertical Bridgman technique is a classical single‐crystal growth method, primarily employed to obtain high‐quality, large‐sized single crystals. As shown in Figure 4c, high‐purity starting materials are typically sealed in quartz ampoules with a conical tip, which are then slowly translated through a furnace with a steep axial ΔT [184, 192, 193, 194, 195, 196]. Nucleation initiates at the tip of the ampoule, and as the solid‐liquid interface advances, a single grain grows continuously to form a bulk single crystal. This process proceeds without external mechanical constraints, allowing free crystal growth and thereby yielding extremely low defect intensities and excellent crystalline integrity. Consequently, it is well suited for fundamental studies and optoelectronic applications where lattice perfection is critically required. In contrast, in situ loaded Bridgman growth is a modified technique developed to meet the device‐oriented requirements of functional materials, particularly thermoelectric materials. As illustrated in Figure 4d, the raw materials are first loaded into a crucible and sealed within a stainless‐steel enclosure, followed by high‐temperature homogenization and quenching. The assembly is then inverted and placed into a Bridgman furnace, where bulk single‐crystal growth is achieved under a maintained ΔT at the solid‐liquid interface [107]. The defining feature of this approach is the “loaded” configuration, in which solidification occurs under applied pressure. The applied pressure effectively suppresses the volatilization of easily evaporating elements, enhances the stability of the solid‐liquid interface, and promotes preferential grain growth along the heat‐flow direction, resulting in a strong texture extending throughout the sample. Moreover, the “in situ” nature of this process implies that the crystal grows and densifies directly within the final mold, enabling the one‐step fabrication of dense, high‐performance bulk materials with controlled shapes, thereby eliminating subsequent grinding, shaping, and sintering steps. This technique is particularly well suited for volatile Zintl‐phase materials such as Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, which exhibit intrinsically anisotropic thermoelectric properties, as it maximizes orientation‐dependent electrical transport while effectively preserving stoichiometric composition. Nevertheless, a major limitation of the Bridgman method lies in its relatively high energy consumption and slow growth rates, which may lead to low production efficiency in industrial‐scale applications. Balancing growth rate and material quality, therefore, remains a critical challenge for the broader application of Bridgman‐based techniques.

It should be emphasized that, although a variety of synthesis and fabrication strategies for Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 have been demonstrated at the laboratory scale to deliver excellent thermoelectric performance, significant challenges remain with respect to reproducibility and engineering scalability. Mechanical alloying combined with SPS can achieve fine‐grained microstructures and high densification; however, the resulting properties are highly sensitive to milling conditions, oxygen content in the powders, and the sintering history, and batch‐to‐batch performance variations are often underestimated in the literature. Conventional melting routes, while more straightforward in terms of compositional control, suffer from increasingly severe Mg volatilization and oxidation issues during scale‐up. Bridgman‐type single‐crystal growth can markedly enhance intrinsic transport properties, yet its high energy consumption, low growth rates, and complex equipment requirements limit its applicability in large‐scale TED manufacturing. Consequently, achieving an optimal balance among performance, stability, and manufacturing cost remains a central challenge that has yet to be fully addressed in the preparation of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 materials.

Strategies to Improve the Thermoelectric Performance

The performance regulation of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials is closely associated with defects in the lattice, among which VMg plays a pivotal role in modifying the thermoelectric properties of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2. VMg not only determines the n and conduction type but also influences the ZT through its impact on defect formation energies [76]. However, a fundamental challenge arises from the high saturated vapor pressure of Mg, which leads to the formation of uncontrollable VMg (so‐called electronic “killers”) in Mg3Sb2 [19, 24, 197]. As shown in Figure 5a, Ohno et al. investigated the effects of VMg on Mg3Sb2 using a phase‐boundary mapping approach and identified VMg as electronic “killers” responsible for inducing p‐type behavior, thereby limiting n‐type conduction. They demonstrated that maintaining Mg‐rich conditions effectively suppresses the formation of VMg, enabling n‐type electrical transport. In contrast, under Sb‐rich conditions, the presence of VMg pins the E F away from the CB, resulting in persistent p‐type behavior [20]. This finding provides a theoretical basis for optimizing the thermoelectric performance of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 through precise control of Mg stoichiometry. Zhang et al. further revealed that Mg loss in bulk samples can be divided into two major contributions. One arises from surface‐related losses due to evaporation, sublimation, or oxidation to form MgO, while the other originates from internal Mg depletion within the bulk. As visualized by the multistep migration mechanism in Figure 5b, Mg‐loss‐induced VMg formation proceeds via a sequential process: Mg atoms first diffuse from the grain interior to grain boundaries (Step 1), then rapidly migrate along the grain boundaries toward the sample surface (Step 2), and finally leave the system through surface evaporation or precipitation (Step 3) [198]. This dynamic process is a key driving factor for the continuous accumulation of VMg under Sb‐rich conditions and explains the spatiotemporal evolution of defect concentrations under non‐equilibrium conditions. At the atomic scale, Liu et al. further clarified the structure‐property relationship between VMg and carrier transport through defect chemistry analysis, as illustrated in Figure 5c. The deintercalation of Mg atoms is accompanied by the generation of VMg, which acts as an acceptor defect by capturing electrons and directly inducing p‐type conduction. Conversely, excess Mg introduces interstitial Mg species that act as donor defects, supplying free electrons and driving the system toward n‐type behavior [174]. This defect‐carrier coupling mechanism constitutes a central target for subsequent microstructural and defect‐engineering strategies.

Regarding the regulation of VMg in n‐type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, recent research has evolved from the conventional strategy of “excess Mg compensation” toward a multidimensional engineering paradigm that integrates thermodynamic suppression, kinetic filling, and grain‐boundary engineering. This shift represents a significant methodological innovation. Specifically, Fan et al. proposed a dynamic repair mechanism based on “in situ vacancy filling”. As illustrated in Figure 5d, they leveraged the selective reaction between transition metal Ti and Sb within the matrix to generate Mg in situ during the SPS process. Exploiting the inherently low diffusion barrier of Mg2+ in the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 lattice (∼0.2–0.45 eV), they achieved global diffusion of Mg from grain boundaries into the grain interiors, effectively filling pre‐existing VMg. This “local reaction‐global diffusion” strategy successfully eliminated Mg‐depleted regions at grain boundaries, boosting µ to over 231 cm2 V−1 s−1, a value substantially exceeding that of traditionally sintered samples [66]. Beyond dynamic defect repair, the structural integrity of grain boundaries represents another critical frontier. In a complementary approach, Zhang et al. shifted the focus toward the chemical environment modification of grain boundaries. By introducing ZrSb1− x compounds, they demonstrated that the Zr‐rich ZrSb3/5 phase is more effective than the Sb‐rich ZrSb2 in stabilizing the Mg/(Sb, Bi) stoichiometry at grain boundaries. This stabilization effectively suppresses the precipitation of Bi‐rich phases and the sustained volatilization of Mg, while concurrently mitigating grain‐boundary potential barriers. Furthermore, the synergistic integration of uniformly dispersed ZrSb3/5 nanoparticles and strain‐induced twin structures enhances phonon scattering across the entire frequency spectrum. Consequently, this multi‐scale structural engineering allows for a high µ while simultaneously achieving a ∼20% improvement in room‐temperature ZT [104]. Figure 5e summarizes the phonon‐related response to bonding softening during the alloying of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, as elucidated by Wu et al. Local internal stresses, induced by VMg and their derivative defects, result in lattice softening and a consequent weakening of chemical bond stiffness. This atomic‐scale strain field, acting in synergy with nano‐scale defects, restricts the phonon mean free path to the lattice constant level, thereby driving the κ l toward the theoretical limit (∼0.3 W m−1 K−1) without significantly compromising electrical performance. This strategy culminated in a ZT max of 2.06 and a ZT ave of 1.58 (300–723 K) [37]. Addressing the problem from a thermodynamic perspective, Jiang et al. proposed a “lattice repair” strategy. By introducing alkaline‐earth metals (Ba, Ca, Sr) with lower electronegativities, they effectively strengthened the local metal‐Sb bonding, increasing the formation energy of VMg from ∼0.97 eV to ∼2.42 eV. This thermodynamically suppresses the generation of intrinsic VMg and eliminates major scattering centers, resulting in a ∼35% improvement in µ. Meanwhile, the self‐compensation mechanism ensures an optimal n. Additionally, Ba doping flattens the CBM, increasing the m* and further enhancing the S. Concurrently, the mass and ionic radius mismatch between Ba and Mg induces atomic‐scale mass fluctuations and local strain fields, while simultaneously facilitating the formation of mesoscale defects such as nano‐twins and dislocations. This builds a hierarchical, multi‐scale phonon scattering architecture that effectively scatters phonons across the entire frequency spectrum, reducing κ l to ∼0.4 W m−1 K−1 at 773 K. Through the synergistic restoration of electronic transport and suppression of thermal transport, a record‐high ZT of ∼2.1 was achieved at 773 K, with a single‐leg device efficiency of approximately 14% [21]. This work highlights defect energetics as a powerful and universal paradigm for transcending the performance limits of Zintl‐phase thermoelectrics. Significantly, whether these multi‐dimensional engineering strategies (encompassing thermodynamic suppression, kinetic filling, and grain‐boundary synergy) can be extended to other Zintl phases or chalcogenide thermoelectric systems characterized by volatile elements remains an intriguing and pivotal direction for future research.

After mitigating the detrimental effects of VMg, one of the key strategies for further enhancing the performance of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 materials lies in controlling the grain size. Achieving high ZT values over a wide temperature range has long been a central goal in thermoelectric research, and HP at elevated temperatures (e.g., ∼800°C) can increase the average grain size and thereby improve the ZT of the material [199]. However, the high saturated vapor pressure of Mg leads to significant Mg loss, which hinders effective n‐type doping. As a result, further grain growth achieved through prolonged thermal treatment is severely constrained [20]. To address this issue, Wood et al. developed a novel annealing process under Mg vapor, as illustrated in Figure 5f. In this approach, MgO is used as the crucible and placed on a graphite holder, and the sample is heated to the target temperature using an induction heater. This process promotes grain growth and increases the average grain size, thereby significantly improving the low‐temperature thermoelectric performance [84]. Zhang et al. investigated potential strategies to suppress performance degradation in magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials and found that Mg vapor annealing is a direct and effective method to enhance thermal stability. By saturating grains and grain boundaries with elemental Mg, this treatment effectively suppresses Mg migration and loss [198]. Masato et al. also successfully synthesized n‐type Te‐doped Mg3Sb2 single crystals by combining Mg vapor‐saturated annealing with an Sb‐flux method. Even after 16 h of saturated annealing, the samples retained their single‐crystalline nature, resulting in substantially improved thermoelectric performance [194]. Similarly, Jin et al. achieved high‐performance thermoelectric materials through post‐annealing under Mg vapor pressure [184]. In addition, Liu et al. explored the optimization of grain size in Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 by adjusting the sintering temperature. By tailoring the sintering temperature and introducing a small amount of Cu, they rationally engineered the microstructure to maximize the thermoelectric performance of n‐type materials, ultimately achieving ZT values exceeding 1 over a wide temperature range from 323 to 423 K. Figure 5g schematically illustrates the microstructural evolution with increasing sintering temperature, showing that higher temperatures lead to larger grain sizes and reduced porosity, thereby diminishing the influence of grain boundaries [99]. Chen et al. further demonstrated that optimizing the sintering temperature can introduce lattice strain, resulting in a ∼30% reduction in κ l, while simultaneously promoting grain growth that increased σ and µ by ∼300% and ∼200%, respectively. Benefiting from the synergistic enhancement of thermal and electrical properties, the material achieved a ZT of 1.7 at 750 K, corresponding to a maximum theoretical η of 12.8%. This strategy provides a new design paradigm for the development of high‐efficiency n‐type Mg3Sb2‐based thermoelectric materials [62]. More recently, researchers have discovered that certain metallic additives can play a previously underappreciated role in promoting grain growth. For example, Lou et al. found that the addition of metallic Nb improves grain‐boundary wetting, thereby facilitating grain growth and reducing grain‐boundary electrical resistivity [200]. Zhai et al. also experimentally demonstrated that trace Cu doping can significantly increase grain size, leading to an ZT ave as high as 1.35 [59].

Although a stable microstructural framework can be established by suppressing grain coarsening and regulating intrinsic VMg, precise control of doping remains a central element in the thermoelectric optimization pathway and is intricately intertwined with grain size and microstructural evolution. In thermoelectric materials, the primary role of doping is to finely tune the n to optimize electrical transport (S 2 σ), while simultaneously introducing point defects that scatter phonons and reduce κ, thereby synergistically enhancing η [201]. The dopant species, substitutional sites, and doping concentrations all exert distinct influences on material properties [202]. In general, an effective n‐type dopant should simultaneously exhibit a low formation energy and an appropriate defect transition level. To this end, Zhang et al. systematically investigated the n‐type doping effects of all rare‐earth elements. As shown in Figures 6a,b, they compared the defect formation energy of Nd substitution at the Mg site with those of rare‐earth substitutions at the Mg1 site under Mg‐rich conditions, as well as with extrinsic defects such as TeMg1, ScMg1, and YMg1 in Mg3Sb2. This comprehensive computational screening elucidated structure‐property relationships for various dopant groups in Mg3Sb2‐based systems and identified several promising n‐type dopants [109]. Figure 6c illustrates a synergistic enhancement strategy induced by Cu doping. A small amount of Cu promotes grain coarsening, thereby increasing µ, while interstitial Cu atoms introduce lattice distortions that suppress phonon transport. The combined effects of these mechanisms enable a ZT value of 1.73 at 723 K [59]. Huang et al. employed a three‐step approach to introduce Cr doping into Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, achieving simultaneous interfacial electronic structure modulation and enhanced lattice phonon scattering. This strategy markedly improved the thermoelectric performance of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2‐based materials, delivering a ZT max of 1.9 at 573 K and enabling the fabrication of high‐performance TEDs with a η of 10.5% [44]. In addition, Hu et al. reported a strategy based on Zn alloying to achieve electronic orbital alignment. In Na‐doped p‐type Mg3Sb2 matrices, the introduction of Zn modified the crystal‐field environment by reducing the lattice parameter ratio c/a, thereby decreasing the energy separation between the px , y and pz orbitals at the VBM. This orbital degeneracy significantly increased the density‐of‐states m* and yielded a high S. As a result, a ZT value of 0.87 was achieved in Mg1.95Na0.01Zn1Sb2, offering a new avenue for performance optimization in p‐type magnesium‐antimony‐based thermoelectric materials [120].

It is worth noting that within the landscape of Mg‐based Zintl thermoelectrics, there exists a distinct subclass of materials centered on cation substitution at the Mg site. A representative strategy involves the incorporation of isovalent divalent cations (particularly Zn2+, as well as Cd2+) into Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 to form Mg3− x A x (Sb, Bi)2 solid solutions, or the introduction of other divalent/multivalent cations to achieve synergistic regulation. In contrast to the conventional “anion‐site (Sb/Bi) alloying combined with electron doping (Te/Se)” approach, Mg‐site substitution typically does not aim at direct modulation of n as Zn2+ serves as an isovalent dopant for Mg2+. Instead, it functions by modulating local chemical bonding and defect chemistry to “indirectly” dictate the achievable E F position and the upper limit of µ. On one hand, Mg‐site substitution tunes the formation energy of intrinsic defects (such as VMg) and charge compensation pathways, thereby mitigating the accumulation of acceptor defects triggered by Mg migration and volatilization during high‐temperature processing and service, which effectively broadens the stable n‐type window. On the other hand, the mass and size fluctuations, along with local strain fields introduced by Zn (or similar cations), enhance phonon scattering and suppress κ l. This, coupled with the potential reduction of grain‐boundary potential barriers and the refinement of the grain‐boundary chemical environment, leads to a significant enhancement in µ [203]. This concept extends to other p‐type Mg‐based Zintl phases as well. For instance, Song et al. demonstrated a sophisticated dual‐doping strategy in Mg2ZnSb2 by co‐substituting Li+ at the Zn2+ site and Ge4− at the Sb3− site. This synergistic approach effectively decoupled the electrical and thermal transport properties: the electrical properties remained robust and comparable to those of single‐doped samples, while the κ l was significantly reduced due to intensified point‐defect scattering from the Ge dopant. This decoupling enabled an optimized Mg2.06Zn1.995Li0.005Sb1.975Ge0.025 composition to achieve a high peak ZT of 1.14 at 625 K, showcasing the power of combined cation/anion engineering to selectively tune transport parameters [204]. Recently, Mehrotra et al. systematically investigated the influence of partial Mg substitution by Zn in the YbMg2− x Zn x Bi0.2Sb1.8 system, achieving a low κ l of 0.58 W m−1 K−1 and a peak ZT ≈ 0.9 at 773 K. By pairing this p‐type material with an Mg3(Sb, Bi)2‐based n‐type leg, a two‐couple TED was fabricated, demonstrating a η of 8.5% under a ΔT of 420 K. Notably, this system is free of toxic elements such as Cd, Pb and Te, highlighting its superior environmental compatibility and practical application potential [167]. Consequently, Mg‐site cation engineering can be regarded as an essential supplementary avenue that bridges “defect controllability, microstructural evolution, and synergistic thermal/electrical transport”, providing additional degrees of freedom to achieve higher reproducibility and enhanced engineering robustness.

However, as the dopant concentration is further increased, single‐dopant strategies often encounter the ceiling imposed by the “solid solubility limit”. As schematically illustrated in Figure 6d, the n does not increase linearly and indefinitely with dopant content. In the lightly doped regime, the electron concentration rises approximately linearly with increasing dopant level; once the heavily doped regime is reached, shifts in the internal chemical potential trigger pronounced self‐compensation effects, causing the n to saturate or even decrease [205, 206]. Although electrical transport is constrained under heavy doping, this regime unexpectedly opens a new window for tuning thermal transport. Excess dopants often precipitate in the form of nanoscale secondary phases or second‐phase inclusions. As exemplified by the heavily NiTe2‐doped Mg3.2Bi1.5Sb0.5 system shown in Figure 6e, heavy doping introduces a high intensity of point defects, dislocations, and nanoscale precipitates. These multiscale microstructural features act as broadband phonon‐scattering centers, effectively impeding heat flow. Consequently, despite some sacrifice in electrical transport efficiency, a net gain in the ZT can be achieved through a substantial reduction in κ l [97].

To overcome the self‐compensation bottleneck associated with single‐element doping and to further decouple electrical and thermal transport, dual‐doping or co‐doping strategies have been developed [207]. Rather than being confined to the regulation of a single lattice site, these approaches exploit differences in lattice‐site preference and chemical complementarity between multiple dopants to achieve synergistic effects in which “1 + 1 > 2”. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward “dual‐donor co‐doping” strategies involving both anion and cation sites. For example, co‐doping with Y and Te has been demonstrated to effectively suppress the formation of VMg. Compared with singly doped systems, dual‐doped materials can accommodate a higher electron concentration, enabling S 2 σ exceeding 20 µW cm−1 K−2 and achieving a ZT max above 1.7 at 703 K in Y0.01Mg3.19Sb1.5Bi0.47Te0.03 [61]. More sophisticated strategies further involve the introduction of 3d transition‐metal elements (such as Mn, Co, and Zn) as “auxiliary dopants”. Unlike conventional electron donors, these elements often play multiple roles in Mg‐based matrices. For instance, in co‐doped systems combining Mn with chalcogen elements (Te/Se), transition metals can not only induce electron and phonon spin scattering through the introduction of magnetic moments but also modulate transport properties from a crystal‐chemical perspective. Liang et al. reported that co‐doping Mn at the Mg site together with Se, exploiting the electronegativity difference between Mn and Mg (1.55 vs 1.31), successfully induced chemical bond softening. Remarkably, this effect simultaneously enhanced both n and µ, and, when combined with strong phonon scattering induced by point defects, resulted in a ZT as high as 1.6. These findings demonstrate that transition‐metal‐assisted dual‐doping strategies offer substantial potential for decoupling and optimizing electrical and phonon transport in magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials [75]. Figure 6f schematically summarizes the representative physical picture of such dual‑doping strategies, highlighting the cooperative role of substitutional defects, vacancies, and pores in regulating phonon transport and enabling effective decoupling of electrical and thermal transport [120]. The thermoelectric performance of p‐type Mg3Sb2 (a typical Zintl phase) has long been constrained by its suboptimal electrical transport properties [208]. To address this, Tang et al. implemented a Li/Cd co‐doping strategy: Li acts as a hole dopant to optimize n, while Cd synchronously suppresses ionized impurity scattering, thereby enhancing µ. Simultaneously, the introduction of atomic‐scale mass contrast effectively suppresses κ l. This synergistic strategy yielded a ZT of 0.68 at 773 K, establishing a highly efficient paradigm for the optimization of p‐type Mg3Sb2 [209].

The defect‐engineering strategies discussed above are, in essence, closely intertwined with band engineering, which constitutes the core approach for optimizing the thermoelectric performance of Mg3Sb2‐based materials. Mg3Sb2 is a wide‐band‐gap semiconductor, whereas its isostructural counterpart Mg3Bi2 is a semimetal or a narrow‐band‐gap semiconductor. The formation of an unlimited solid solution, Mg3Sb2− x Bi x , therefore provides broad latitude for continuous tuning of the electronic band structure. Predictions based on the single parabolic band (SPB) model (Figure 6g) further quantify the impact of such band engineering on the ZT [210]. The calculations indicate that the optimal n window shifts with increasing Bi content. At elevated temperatures (700 K, dashed line), Bi‐containing compositions exhibit the potential to surpass the ZT peak of pure Mg3Sb2 owing to their higher µ and optimized band structures. It should be noted, however, that excessive Bi incorporation, while enhancing σ, leads to an overly narrowed E g that readily triggers bipolar diffusion at high temperatures, thereby degrading both the S and κ. Consequently, most experimental studies currently focus on compositions near an intermediate Bi content, where an optimal balance between carrier activation and bipolar suppression can be achieved. By adjusting the Bi/Sb ratio, the band features near the E F can be precisely tailored. As shown in Figure 6h, with increasing Bi content (from blue Mg3Sb2 to green Mg3Bi2), the CBM shifts markedly downward, resulting in a progressive narrowing of the E g [211]. This band‐gap narrowing not only modifies the m* but also strongly influences transport properties. During the transition from a semiconductor to a semimetal, both the multivalley degeneracy and the curvature of the bands evolve accordingly, providing a physical basis for balancing the S and σ. Importantly, the introduction of Bi plays a critical role in regulating impurity energy levels. As schematically illustrated in Figure 6i, increasing Bi content dramatically reduces the E g from 0.516 eV for pure Mg3Sb2 to 0.179 eV (theoretical values) [211]. More critically, the downward shift of the CBM renders donor defects that were originally deep‐level states (such as interstitial Mg or extrinsic dopants) relatively shallow, substantially lowering their ionization energies. As a result, donor electrons can be more readily thermally excited into the CB, significantly increasing the n at room temperature. This mechanism fundamentally explains why pure Mg3Sb2 is difficult to dope n‐type, whereas Mg3Sb2− x Bi x alloys can exhibit excellent n‐type thermoelectric performance.

In polycrystalline Mg3Sb2‐based thermoelectric materials, grain boundaries play a dual role. On the one hand, they effectively scatter phonons and reduce κ l; on the other hand, intrinsically high grain‐boundary potential barriers severely impede carrier transport, and grain boundaries often serve as fast diffusion pathways for Mg volatilization at elevated temperatures. Consequently, precise modification of grain‐boundary characteristics and the introduction of functional secondary phases are key to further enhancing device performance and stability. Researchers have found that introducing elemental metals directly during synthesis not only enables n tuning, but that excess elemental additives can also segregate at grain boundaries or form secondary phases, thereby achieving simultaneous optimization of electrical/phonon transport and material stability. The behavior of certain dopants in the matrix exhibits strong concentration dependence. Taking Mn as an example (Figure 7a), at low concentrations Mn primarily acts as a substitutional dopant that improves electrical properties; at higher concentrations, Mn redistributes and segregates at grain boundaries, forming Mn‐rich secondary phases [58]. As shown in Figure 7b, such Mn segregation at grain boundaries not only serves as an effective phonon‐scattering center but also establishes a Mg‐rich chemical environment that suppresses the formation and migration of VMg [212]. To alleviate the restriction of grain boundaries on µ, the incorporation of high‐work‐function elemental metals, such as Nb or Ta, has proven effective [213, 214]. As illustrated in Figure 7c, these metallic nanoinclusions form “metal‐semiconductor” interfaces at grain boundaries, transforming originally high and uniform potential barriers into interface barriers with an energy‐filtering effect. This design allows high‐energy carriers to pass while scattering low‐energy carriers, thereby significantly improving the S 2 σ while maintaining a high S [30].

To address the issue of Mg volatilization at high temperatures, the introduction of elemental Zr provides a physical‐barrier solution. As shown in Figure 7d, Zr reacts with the matrix to form an in situ secondary phase, ZrSb1− x , at grain boundaries. These finely dispersed nanophases act as physical barriers that block Mg diffusion pathways. From a thermodynamic perspective (Figure 7e), this process also consumes excess Sb at grain boundaries, reducing the chemical potential gradient of Mg and fundamentally weakening the driving force for Mg migration [215, 216]. On this basis, the introduction of Ag (Figure 7f) represents another class of grain‐boundary engineering strategy that achieves synergistic regulation through secondary‐phase formation. In addition to inducing point defects, excess Ag precipitates as Ag‐rich phases at grain boundaries. These interfacial phases cooperatively enhance phonon scattering while stabilizing the local crystal structure, thereby further improving the overall thermoelectric performance without severely degrading electrical transport [217]. Beyond the direct addition of elemental metals, a more recent strategy involves introducing compounds (such as oxides) and exploiting their in situ reactions with the matrix to generate functional grain‐boundary phases. As shown in Figure 7g, non‐stoichiometric TiO2− x is introduced into the matrix, and its high reactivity during sintering induces the in situ formation of highly conductive Ti3Sb intermetallic compounds [218, 219], which are uniformly distributed along grain boundaries. The core advantage of this strategy lies in band‐structure reconstruction. According to the density functional theory (DFT) calculations and band schematics in Figure 7h, compared with conventional insulating or high‐resistance grain boundaries, the introduction of the highly conductive Ti3Sb phase dramatically reduces interfacial band bending and can even flatten the bands. This “conductive bridge” effect eliminates grain‐boundary barriers to electron transport, allowing carriers to traverse grain boundaries with minimal scattering and enabling ultrahigh µ in polycrystalline materials approaching that of single crystals. As a result, Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 achieves a ZT max of 2.02 at 798 K and a ZT ave of 1.49 over the temperature range of 300–798 K. The fabricated single‐leg devices exhibit a high η of approximately 15% together with excellent thermal stability [32].

In summary, defect engineering, heavy doping, and multiscale microstructural regulation have substantially driven the continuous increase of ZT values in the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 system. However, it must be emphasized that high performance often comes with hidden trade‐offs. First, although heavy doping and the introduction of multiphase structures are effective in reducing κ l, they may trigger elemental redistribution and phase instability during long‐term operation or thermal cycling, ultimately leading to degradation of electrical properties. Second, some optimization strategies rely on highly delicate defect equilibria or narrow compositional windows, such that their performance advantages diminish rapidly once the system deviates from the optimal conditions. Moreover, most reported performance enhancements are still based on short‐term measurements, while systematic evaluations of long‐term stability and device‐scale effects remain insufficient. Therefore, the currently reported “high ZT” values should be regarded primarily as demonstrations of intrinsic material potential, rather than being directly equated with engineering‐ready performance.

Thin Films

With the growing demand for miniaturized TEDs in the Internet of Things and wearable electronics, the fabrication of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2‐based thin films has emerged as a recent research hotspot [220, 221]. However, owing to the large disparity in saturated vapor pressures between Mg and Sb/Bi, precise control over film stoichiometry and the achievement of high crystallinity remain the most critical challenges in this field. As illustrated in Figure 8, current strategies primarily focus on multi‐target co‐sputtering, deposition‐kinetics regulation, and layered thermal evaporation techniques. Magnetron sputtering, in particular, has become the mainstream approach for thin‐film fabrication because of its high deposition rate, dense film morphology, and excellent uniformity [222]. To address Mg deficiency, multi‐target co‐sputtering is widely employed. As shown in Figure 8a, co‐sputtering from three independent targets(Bi, Mg, and Mg3Sb2) enables atomic‐level fine control over Mg and Bi contents by independently adjusting the sputtering power of each target. This approach not only compensates for Mg loss during sputtering but also promotes effective Bi incorporation. As depicted in the structural evolution schematic in Figure 8b, subsequent annealing further drives Bi atoms into the lattice, either filling VMg or substituting Sb sites, thereby forming solid‐solution films with precise stoichiometry and enhanced structural stability [223]. Beyond target composition, kinetic parameters during deposition, particularly substrate temperature, play a crucial role in thin‐film growth. Figure 8c illustrates the processes of particle collision, surface migration, and nucleation during radio‐frequency magnetron sputtering. Experimental results show that tuning the deposition temperature effectively controls the relative fractions of the Mg3Sb2 primary phase and the Sb secondary phase in the films (Figure 8d) [224]. An appropriate substrate temperature not only facilitates the formation of highly textured crystalline structures but also establishes electronic transport pathways between Mg3Sb2 grains and precipitated Sb phases. Such phase‐separated architectures are beneficial for optimizing carrier transport properties in thin films.

As another classical physical vapor deposition technique, thermal evaporation offers unique advantages for producing large‐area, uniform films. To address the high volatility and oxidation susceptibility of Mg, a single‐source thermal evaporation strategy based on “layer‐by‐layer growth followed by post‐annealing” has been developed. As shown in Figure 8e, this method involves the alternate deposition of multiple Mg and Sb/Bi precursor layers onto c‐Al2O3 (sapphire) substrates. The multilayer architecture provides uniform diffusion sources for subsequent reactions. Upon annealing under a controlled atmosphere, solid‐state interdiffusion reactions are induced between the layers, ultimately yielding highly crystalline, phase‐pure Mg3Sb2 thin films on insulating substrates. Moreover, the lattice matching between the sapphire substrate and the film promotes improved crystallographic orientation and alignment [225].

Chemical and Thermal Stability

Although Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 materials have achieved significant breakthroughs in thermoelectric performance, their operational stability at elevated temperatures (>600 K) remains a major obstacle to commercialization. The exceptionally high saturated vapor pressure and chemical reactivity of Mg give rise to three fundamental challenges under high‐temperature conditions: Mg volatilization and VMg formation within the lattice, surface oxidation and corrosion, and interfacial reactions between metallization layers [226, 227]. In response to these issues, the present review addresses stabilization strategies primarily from three perspectives: thermodynamic equilibrium control, kinetic limitation, and macroscopic interface engineering.

Mg3Sb2 is a typical Zintl‐phase compound with a relatively narrow thermodynamic stability window. Early studies have shown that Mg volatilization induces the formation of acceptor‐type VMg, leading to a substantial reduction in n and even a transition from n‐type to p‐type conduction [20]. A conventional mitigation strategy is to introduce excess Mg during synthesis to maintain a Mg‐rich environment based on phase‐boundary control principles. To achieve long‐term thermodynamic stability without external Mg addition, incorporating an internal solid‐state Mg source represents a promising strategy. As illustrated in Figure 9a, in conventional polycrystalline materials, Mg readily diffuses along grain boundaries and evaporates, resulting in the formation of Mg concentration gradients that accelerate Mg depletion [228]. In contrast, Geng et al. proposed an innovative approach by introducing MgB2 as a “magnesium reservoir” (Figure 9b). MgB2 exhibits a moderate decomposition pressure at elevated temperatures, enabling it to thermodynamically buffer the Mg vapor pressure of the host matrix. When VMg forms in the matrix due to volatilization, Mg atoms released from the decomposition of MgB2 can rapidly refill these VMg, while the MgB2 phase itself simultaneously acts as a physical barrier that suppresses long‐range Mg diffusion. This mechanism is markedly more effective than the simple addition of excess Mg, allowing devices to maintain high power output during prolonged operation (Figure 9c,d) [85]. Similarly, related studies have reported strategies that employ Mg‐rich nanoprecipitates to suppress Mg loss, further confirming the effectiveness of second‐phase compensation mechanisms [76].

From a kinetic perspective, excess Mg inevitably becomes depleted during prolonged high‐temperature service; therefore, increasing the migration barrier of Mg atoms is the fundamental pathway to enhance intrinsic stability. Although Mg‐Sb bonding exhibits a certain degree of ionic character, its bonding strength is insufficient to confine the thermal vibration of Mg atoms at elevated temperatures. Introducing transition‐metal atoms (such as Co, Mn, and Zn) into interstitial sites can effectively modulate the local chemical environment. As shown in Figure 9e, DFT calculations reveal that interstitial Mn atoms form strong Mn‐Mg interactions with surrounding Mg atoms. Integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) analysis confirms that these impurity‐induced bonds are significantly stronger than the intrinsic Mg–Mg or Mg–Sb bonds. This so‐called “lattice anchoring effect” increases both the formation energy and migration barrier of VMg. The effectiveness of this strategy has been verified through long‐term aging tests (Figure 9f): while the electrical resistivity of undoped samples drifts rapidly at 673 K, Mn‐doped samples exhibit remarkably stable electrical performance [82]. This finding echoes the mechanisms reported by Jiang et al. for various dopants that enhance the thermal stability of Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5, indicating that reinforcing lattice rigidity via reconstruction of cation sites or interstitial sites constitutes a general and effective modification principle [50].

Device‐level stability depends not only on the intrinsic properties of the material, but also critically on its interfacial interactions with the environment and with electrodes. Mg is highly susceptible to oxidation, readily forming insulating MgO and volatile Sb, which leads to surface degradation and powdering. As shown in Figure 9g, conventional single‐layer Mg coatings are unable to effectively block oxygen ingress. To address this issue, the development of composite protective coatings such as ME20M is particularly important. Owing to its dense structural design, this coating successfully isolates oxygen during in situ tests at 400°C, effectively suppresses interdiffusion, and reduces Mg evaporation, thereby providing a general technological route to improve the high‐temperature thermal stability of thermoelectric generators (TEGs) [34]. This strategy is consistent with the approach reported by Shuang et al., who enhanced the thermal stability of Mg3Sb2− x Bi x alloys by encapsulating them with boron nitride coatings, likewise aiming to construct a physical barrier against degradation [229]. In addition, during metallization for TED integration, the choice of diffusion‐barrier materials must simultaneously satisfy the requirements of low contact resistance and chemical inertness [34]. Common Ni or Cu electrodes tend to react vigorously with Mg at elevated temperatures, forming brittle intermetallic compounds or inducing severe Kirkendall effects. As shown in Figure 9h, Qu et al. employed Fe powder as the barrier‐layer material. Fe is thermodynamically immiscible with Mg and possesses a coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) close to that of Mg3Sb2, which effectively avoids delamination caused by thermal stress. SEM‐EDS analysis (Figure 9i) confirms that the Fe/Mg3Sb2 interface remains sharp and well defined after high‐temperature sintering, with no obvious elemental interdiffusion. More importantly, a one‐step sintering process based on Fe powder achieves an ultralow contact resistivity (< 5 µΩ cm2) (Figure 9j) [171]. This performance surpasses that of widely used interfacial layers reported in the literature, such as 304 stainless steel [230], Mg2Cu [231], and Mg4.3Sb3Ni [232], providing an optimal solution for the fabrication of highly reliable all‐magnesium‐based TEDs.

Beyond high‐temperature thermal stability, the intrinsic electrochemical susceptibility of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 to moisture‐induced corrosion represents another critical bottleneck impeding its commercial deployment. Given the highly negative equilibrium potential of Mg (approximately −0.8 to −0.9 V), the material is prone to hydrogen‐evolution corrosion when exposed to condensed aqueous films. The resulting loose and porous MgO/Mg (OH)2 corrosion products fail to provide an effective barrier against water and oxygen, leading to rapid degradation of thermoelectric performance. To address this, a “self‐defensive” strategy based on the concept of sacrificial anode cathodic protection (SACP) has recently been reported by Yu et al. (Figure 9k). By introducing Al into the Mg3.2Sb0.5Bi1.49Te0.01 matrix, the authors successfully triggered the in situ precipitation of a secondary Mg17Al12 phase, which possesses a significantly lower equilibrium potential (−1.99 V). This phase acts as a sacrificial anode, forming micro‐galvanic couples that electrochemically protect the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 matrix. Concurrently, the dissolution products of this phase, namely Mg2+ and Al3+ ions, promote the in situ formation of a dense, multi‐layered Mg/Al oxide/hydroxide passivation film, effectively sealing off moisture‐ingress pathways. Experiments demonstrate that this strategy achieves a 92% reduction in the average corrosion rate in air (down to 95 µm/year) and an 86% reduction in water (to 0.36 µm/h). Furthermore, integrating Mg17Al12 as an interfacial layer in thermoelectric cooling modules (TEC) enables exceptional reliability; the devices maintain stable internal resistance with a maximum temperature difference (ΔT max) of 88.1 K, thereby surpassing conventional Bi2Te3‐based modules. This paradigm of constructing micro‐scale sacrificial interfaces not only mitigates corrosion throughout the entire lifecycle (from storage and processing to service) but also provides a generalizable design philosophy for protecting other moisture‐sensitive functional materials.

In summary, while substantial progress has been made in stabilizing Mg3(Sb, Bi)2‐based materials through kinetic lattice engineering and thermodynamic reservoir strategies, the long‐term operational durability remains a multifaceted challenge. Current approaches, such as excessive Mg loading or Mg‐reservoir integration, are inherently limited by the finite consumption of the sacrificial source over prolonged service. Simultaneously, while the introduction of second phases or heavy‐element doping effectively enhances atomic migration barriers, it often introduces interfacial complexities and structural brittleness, posing potential trade‐offs in mechanical reliability. Crucially, the transition from material‐level stability to device‐level performance is not always straightforward; interfacial degradation, thermal stress accumulation, and encapsulation failure frequently emerge as primary bottlenecks that compromise the operational lifespan of the entire system. Therefore, it is increasingly evident that the stability of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 is not merely an intrinsic materials issue, but a hierarchical challenge coupling materials, interfaces, and device architectures. To bridge the gap between laboratory‐scale successes and practical deployment, future efforts must shift towards a holistic design strategy—one that validates material integrity under realistic, integrated service conditions while simultaneously optimizing the synergistic stability of thermal, mechanical, and electrochemical interfaces within the device.

MgAgSb

Structures

The MgAgSb compound exhibits three temperature‐driven crystallographic phases, namely the low‐temperature α phase, the intermediate‐temperature β phase, and the high‐temperature γ phase. Among them, the low‐temperature α‐MgAgSb phase has been identified as the most promising thermoelectric phase owing to its excellent semiconducting properties. As shown in Figure 10a, α‐MgAgSb crystallizes in a tetragonal structure with the space group I 4¯ c2 [17, 188]. From the perspective of atomic arrangement, its crystal structure can be described as a complex ternary system composed of a distorted Ag‐Sb rocksalt‐like sublattice with Mg atoms occupying the interstitial voids [17, 234]. In this structure, Ag and Sb atoms form a covalently bonded network that constitutes the primary pathway for electronic transport, while Mg atoms reside in the cage‐like cavities created by the Ag–Sb framework and interact with surrounding atoms predominantly through ionic bonding [140]. This distinctive “distorted rocksalt framework plus filler atoms” structural motif not only breaks inversion symmetry but also gives rise to a large unit cell containing 16 formula units. Such features provide a structural basis for strong phonon scattering induced by lattice distortions, thereby enabling an intrinsically low κ [2, 235, 236].

(b) Electronic band structures of MgAgSb [175]. Copyright 2017, John Wiley and Sons. (c) Phonon dispersion relations of MgAgSb [241]. Copyright 2017, Elsevier.

As illustrated in Figure 10b, the thermoelectric performance of α‐MgAgSb is fundamentally governed by its distinctive electronic band structure, which underpins its excellent p‐type transport behavior at room temperature and in the intermediate temperature range [237]. As a narrow‐band‐gap semiconductor, an accurate description of its band‐edge characteristics is essential for understanding its transport mechanisms. The figure compares band structures calculated using the PBE functional and the modified Becke–Johnson (MBJ) potential. It is evident that the PBE approach tends to underestimate the E g and can even predict metallic behavior [238], whereas MBJ calculations correct this deficiency and reveal a realistic E g of approximately 0.2–0.3 eV, consistent with experimentally observed semiconducting transport behavior [175, 239, 240]. Notably, both the VBM and CBM of MgAgSb exhibit Dirac‐cone‐like linear dispersion in the Brillouin zone, implying extremely low m* and correspondingly high µ. At the same time, the band structure displays pronounced multivalley degeneracy. This combination of “low m*‐high band degeneracy” enables MgAgSb to maintain high σ while preserving a relatively large S, thereby delivering an outstanding S 2 σ [175].

The exceptional thermoelectric performance of the MgAgSb system is largely dictated by its intrinsically ultralow κ l, which is intimately linked to its phonon spectral characteristics. Figure 10c presents the phonon dispersion of α‐MgAgSb, revealing the key lattice‐dynamical mechanisms at play [241]. Owing to the large number of atoms in the unit cell (48 atoms), the phonon spectrum features a rich manifold of optical branches. Inspection of the dispersion relations shows pronounced overlap and hybridization between low‐frequency acoustic branches (LA/TA) and low‐lying optical branches (LO/TO) [242]. This strong acoustic‐optical phonon coupling, together with the pronounced anharmonicity introduced by the distorted Ag‐Sb framework, substantially enhances phonon–phonon scattering and shortens the phonon mean free path [243]. In addition, the Mg atoms occupying the interstitial voids are relatively weakly bound and exhibit localized vibrational modes that act as a damping effect on lattice vibrations, further suppressing heat transport [175, 234, 244, 245]. Consequently, the synergistic interplay between the complex crystal structure of MgAgSb and its distinctive phonon dispersion behavior constitutes the microscopic physical origin of its glass‐like κ l [242, 246].

Strategies to Improve Thermoelectric Performance

Although α‐MgAgSb possesses strong intrinsic thermoelectric potential, further performance enhancement is still constrained by the well‐known coupling among transport parameters: optimization of electrical transport (n, µ, and band degeneracy) is often accompanied by changes in thermal transport (particularly κ l and bipolar effects), which ultimately limits the attainable ZT [176]. Consequently, current research has increasingly adopted a “divide‐and‐conquer, then synergize” strategy. In this approach, κ l is first minimized as much as possible on the thermal‐transport side, while the S 2 σ is enhanced on the electrical‐transport side, and a compatible microstructural framework is subsequently constructed through interface and phase‐structure engineering. In the MgAgSb system, further reduction of κ l is typically identified as the primary breakthrough, as it most directly enhances ZT without necessarily compromising electrical performance. As shown in Figure 11a, Li doping introduces multiscale phonon‐scattering centers ranging from point defects to nanoprecipitates, dislocations, and stacking faults, thereby achieving broadband scattering of short‐, medium‐, and long‐wavelength phonons. This “full‐spectrum” phonon scattering effectively suppresses heat transport while preserving a continuous conductive framework for charge carriers [176]. Pristine MgAgSb generally behaves as a low‐carrier‐concentration p‐type semiconductor, which limits its σ. Optimizing the Fermi‐level (E F) position via acceptor doping is therefore critical for enhancing the S 2 σ. As schematically illustrated in Figure 11b, the E F of undoped samples lies deep within the E g, resulting in insufficient carrier excitation. Zheng et al. demonstrated that doping with the rare‐earth element Yb can precisely shift E F deeper into the valence band (VB), thereby significantly increasing the hole concentration. Notably, this doping strategy delivers a synergistic “two‐birds‐with‐one‐stone” effect: as shown in Figure 11c, the introduction of heavy atoms generates strong mass fluctuation and strain‐field point defects, which, while optimizing electrical transport, also act as additional phonon‐scattering centers that further suppress heat transport [241]. Similar electrically and thermally synergistic optimization strategies have been validated in other doped systems, such as those involving Zn and Cu [26, 139].

In the pursuit of high σ, heavy doping often leads to a sacrifice in µ due to impurity scattering. To overcome this trade‐off, the introduction of high‐µ secondary phases to construct composite materials has emerged as a promising strategy. As shown in Figure 11d, recent studies have proposed incorporating high‐µ InSb nanocrystals into the MgAgSb matrix [39]. InSb possesses an intrinsically high µ, and its VBM aligns well with that of MgAgSb. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that Ag under Ag‐rich conditions does not significantly deteriorate the semiconducting properties of InSb [247]. As a result, a conductive network resembling a “carrier highway” is formed within the composite, where holes preferentially travel through the low‐resistance InSb phase, leading to a pronounced recovery of the macroscopic µ. This “energy‐filtering” or “conductivity‐bridging” mechanism effectively compensates for grain‐boundary scattering losses and substantially enhances the S 2 σ.

Finally, a deep understanding of the phase‐transition mechanisms in MgAgSb is essential for improving its operational stability. As shown in Figure 11e and f, the low‐temperature α phase and the high‐temperature γ phase exhibit fundamentally different crystal structures: the α phase features a distorted atomic arrangement, whereas the γ phase adopts a highly symmetric cubic fluorite structure. Comparative analyses of differential charge‐density maps (Figure 11g,h) reveal that Ag‐Sb bonds in the α phase display stronger covalent character, with more pronounced electron‐density overlap. This robust covalent network not only underpins the high µ of the α phase but also endows it with superior mechanical stability at room temperature. In contrast, the γ phase exhibits more delocalized electronic distributions and weaker bonding directionality [240]. These insights highlight the necessity of strictly controlling thermal‐treatment processes during material synthesis to avoid residual metastable phases, thereby ensuring reliable performance under long‐term thermal cycling.

Although the MgAgSb system has attracted considerable attention because of its outstanding thermoelectric performance near room temperature and is widely regarded as a potential alternative to conventional Bi2Te3‐based materials, several non‐negligible limitations remain at both the materials‐physics and engineering‐application levels. First, MgAgSb exhibits pronounced temperature‐induced phase transitions, with a relatively narrow multiphase coexistence window and significant differences in thermodynamic stability among the various crystal phases. As a result, its optimal performance is often confined to a limited operating temperature range. Under practical device service conditions, ΔTs, thermal cycling, and electrical loading can trigger phase rearrangements, leading to irreversible degradation of electrical transport properties. Second, many performance‐optimization strategies commonly reported in the literature, such as off‐stoichiometric regulation or multielement doping, rely on precise control within narrow compositional windows. The MgAgSb system is highly sensitive to compositional deviations, and even a slight stoichiometric imbalance can markedly alter the carrier type and n distribution. This “narrow‐window” optimization characteristic, while capable of delivering high ZT values at the laboratory scale, poses substantial challenges for scale‐up synthesis and batch‐to‐batch reproducibility. In addition, the relatively low mechanical strength of MgAgSb and its potential interfacial reactivity impose further demands on long‐term reliability in modular device configurations. Collectively, these issues indicate that future research on MgAgSb should gradually shift from an exclusive focus on maximizing single performance metrics toward a more comprehensive evaluation framework that emphasizes phase‐stability control, expansion of compositional tolerance, and performance retention under realistic device operating conditions.

Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn)

Structures

Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn) compounds remain stable in a cubic anti‐fluorite structure over the temperature range from room temperature to the mid–high‐temperature regime, crystallizing in the space group Fm 3¯ m. This structure can be regarded as an “anion–cation inverted” variant of the CaF2 structure and represents one of the prototypical simple, high‐symmetry semiconductor crystal frameworks. As shown in Figure 12a, the X (Si/Ge/Sn) atoms form a face‐centered cubic (FCC) anionic sublattice, while Mg atoms occupy all tetrahedral interstitial sites, giving rise to a three‐dimensional, isotropic bonding network with mixed ionic–covalent character [188]. From the coordination perspective, each Mg atom resides at the center of a tetrahedron formed by X atoms (fourfold Mg‐X coordination), whereas each X atom is surrounded by eight nearest‐neighbor Mg atoms in a cubic coordination environment [248]. This structural motif, characterized by a highly symmetric three‐dimensional framework together with well‐defined electron donation from Mg and electron acceptance by X, renders the Mg2X system amenable to wide‐range n tuning via doping and provides a robust parent lattice for continuous Si/Ge/Sn solid solutions. Consequently, performance variations among Mg2X compounds are more commonly governed by band‐edge responses and the evolution of scattering mechanisms under isostructural solid‐solution conditions, rather than by non‐comparable changes arising from structural phase transitions.

(b) Orbital‐projected electronic band structure of Mg2Si [249]. Copyright 2018, American Physical Society. As shown in Figure 12b, the orbital‐projected band structure of Mg2Si reveals the characteristic band‐edge composition of the Mg2X system: the VBM is dominated by X p orbitals, whereas the CBM exhibits significant contributions from Mg s/p states. This reflects a bonding nature primarily governed by Mg → X charge transfer with an appreciable degree of covalent hybridization [249]. Within this electronic‐structure framework, Mg2X compounds generally behave as indirect‐band‐gap semiconductors. Their CB valleys are located near equivalent high‐symmetry points in the Brillouin zone and intrinsically exhibit multivalley characteristics (arising from valley degeneracy in the cubic structure). This provides an electronic basis for enhancing the density‐of‐states m* and increasing the S with only a modest sacrifice in µ. In particular, in solid‐solution systems such as Mg2 (Si, Sn), lattice‐parameter variations and relative shifts of band edges induced by different X elements can drive CB valleys closer in energy (band convergence), thereby further increasing the effective valley degeneracy and optimizing the S 2 σ [16, 250]. Meanwhile, the incorporation of heavier elements such as Sn introduces stronger spin‐orbit coupling and a tendency toward band‐gap narrowing, which in turn affects the onset temperature and magnitude of bipolar transport. This necessitates coordinated optimization of composition and n [250]. Overall, the electronic‐transport advantages of Mg2X do not arise from complex structural motifs, but rather from a tunable yet well‐controlled band structure defined by a “multivalley CB imposed by the high‐symmetry anti‐fluorite framework, together with well‐defined orbital characters at the band edges”.

Beyond electronic structure, lattice thermal transport in Mg2X is likewise highly sensitive to structural and compositional perturbations. Compared with layered Zintl phases, the three‐dimensional, high‐symmetry framework of Mg2X generally exhibits a relatively high intrinsic κ l in its pristine state [251]. Consequently, reducing κ l relies more critically on the introduction of alloy disorder and point‐defect scattering. As shown in Figure 12c, the room‐temperature κ l of Mg2 (Si, Ge, Sn) solid solutions follows a characteristic U‐shaped dependence on composition x, with a minimum at intermediate compositions [252]. In particular, Sn‐containing solid solutions display stronger suppression of κ l in the mid‐x range. This behavior can be attributed to several concurrent factors: (i) pronounced mass contrast among Si, Ge, and Sn, which enhances mass‐fluctuation scattering; (ii) differences in atomic radii that generate local strain fields and further increase phonon‐scattering cross sections; and (iii) solid‐solution disorder that compresses the phonon mean free path over a broad frequency range, thereby effectively reducing κ l [253]. Accordingly, a clear optimization logic emerges for the Mg2X system at the structural level: while maintaining the stability of the anti‐fluorite parent phase, strong phonon scattering is introduced via isostructural Si–Ge–Sn solid solutions to suppress κ l. Combined with the intrinsic multivalley CBs and tunable E g of Mg2X, this approach establishes a reproducible and parameterizable structural platform for subsequent doping and band‐engineering strategies.

Strategies to Improve Thermoelectric Performance

Performance enhancement in the Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn) system typically follows a synergistic optimization along three intertwined axes: band structure, carrier transport, and thermal transport. First, ternary solid solutions provide continuous tunability of the CB‐edge structure. As shown in Figure 13a, within the Mg2Sn‐Mg2Ge‐Mg2Si compositional space there exists a narrow “sweet‐spot” region that can be regarded as the compositional projection of band crossing/band convergence conditions. Within this window, the energy separation between the light and heavy CBs (CL and CH), as illustrated in Figures 13b–d, is reduced to a scale comparable to thermal excitation, thereby effectively enhancing the equivalent valley degeneracy and density‐of‐states m* and yielding a structural gain in the S 2 σ [250]. This indicates that compositional design in Mg2X is better viewed as a targeted search along a band‐convergence pathway rather than a simple end‐member substitution or single‐variable extrapolation [29]. Second, optimization of electrical transport relies on the coupled regulation of donor doping and defect chemistry. X‐site donors (such as Sb) are commonly employed to establish stable n‐type conduction and tune the n into the optimal operating range; however, their benefits are constrained by the combined effects of ionized‐impurity scattering and alloy‐disorder scattering. Consequently, the doping level must be optimized in concert with the degree of solid‐solution disorder (Si/Ge/Sn ratios) to prevent µ losses from offsetting gains in band degeneracy [254]. In addition, Mg volatilization and intrinsic defects can induce carrier compensation and batch‐to‐batch variability. Thus, controlling Mg activity and stoichiometry to stabilize n and µ is generally regarded as a prerequisite for achieving reproducible, high S 2 σ. Finally, the key to thermal‐transport optimization lies in extending point‐defect scattering from ternary solid solutions into a broader phonon‐scattering spectrum. The mass contrast and size mismatch among Si, Ge, and Sn can substantially suppress κ l; building upon this, the introduction of multiscale structural features such as phase separation or nanoprecipitates, dislocations, and grain‐boundary networks can further compress the phonon mean free path and drive κ l toward the alloy limit [255]. For Sn‐rich compositions with relatively narrowed E g, high‐temperature bipolar transport must also be treated as a design constraint. Coordinated tuning of composition and n is required to delay the onset of bipolar effects, thereby preserving the S and effective κ at elevated temperatures. Overall, the high‐performance design paradigm for Mg2X can be summarized as follows: lock in the band‐convergence window via ternary solid solutions, calibrate optimal n‐type doping while suppressing compensation, synergistically reduce κ l through alloying and multiscale microstructural engineering, and simultaneously manage bipolar effects under Sn‐rich conditions.

As one of the earliest magnesium‐based thermoelectric systems to be systematically investigated, Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn) has largely shaped the conventional optimization paradigm that combines alloying with n control. However, it is important to recognize that further improvements in the thermoelectric performance of this system have increasingly exhibited a pattern of “diminishing returns” in recent years. After the superposition of multiple strategies, such as extensive alloying, heavy doping, and microstructural engineering, the incremental gains in ZT have become markedly constrained. From a fundamental perspective, the band structure of Mg2X is relatively simple, with limited CB degeneracy, and it is difficult to simultaneously achieve substantial enhancement of both m* and µ. As a result, the theoretical upper bound of the S 2 σ is comparatively well defined. Moreover, to suppress the intrinsically high κ l, a large intensity of point defects and alloy‐scattering centers is commonly introduced. While effective in reducing κ l, these approaches inevitably introduce additional carrier‐scattering mechanisms that degrade electrical transport, giving rise to a classic “thermo–electric trade‐off” constraint. At the engineering level, persistent challenges remain, including Mg volatilization, oxidation sensitivity, and interfacial reactions under mid‐ to high‐temperature operating conditions. Although various coating and atmosphere‐control strategies have been proposed, their long‐term stability and reproducibility have yet to be systematically validated. Consequently, future development of the Mg2X system may be better positioned as a theoretical model platform and a benchmark material for the mid‐temperature regime, rather than as the primary vehicle for pursuing record‐breaking ZT values. Its practical potential is more likely to be realized through deep integration with novel band‐engineering concepts or composite‐structure strategies.

Deviceization and Application

Structural Design and Modeling

Although magnesium‐based thermoelectric systems exhibit promising performance in the mid‐temperature regime, device‐level output capability is not a simple “scaled‐up” reflection of material properties. Instead, it is jointly shaped by structural parameters, interfacial states, and boundary conditions. On the one hand, the quality of the electrical contact between electrodes and thermoelectric legs introduces additional electrical losses and may degrade during long‐term operation due to interdiffusion or interfacial reactions [256]. On the other hand, substrates and filler materials can form parallel heat‐conduction pathways, reducing the effective ΔT applied across the thermoelectric legs and, during thermal cycling, inducing stress concentration and mechanical failure [257]. Given these considerations, before discussing specific strategies involving electrodes, diffusion barriers, fillers, and encapsulation, it is essential to establish a clear structural description and modeling framework. Such a framework enables different device designs to be evaluated on a consistent basis and provides an interpretable foundation for parameter optimization.

Figure 14a illustrates the structural hierarchy of a typical TEG module: p‐type and n‐type thermoelectric legs are sandwiched between the top and bottom substrates and electrically connected in series and thermally in parallel via metal electrodes, such that a ΔT between the hot and cold sides drives electrical power generation [258]. For magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials (typically targeting mid‐temperature applications), device performance depends not only on the intrinsic thermoelectric properties of the materials but is also strongly constrained by structural dimensions, interfacial quality, and parasitic heat losses. Consequently, device design and evaluation must be conducted within a coupled “materials‐structure‐boundary conditions” framework. From an engineering perspective, a single thermocouple pair is often treated as a representative unit for analysis. The leg height, cross‐sectional area, fill factor, number of thermocouple pairs, and the structural parameters of electrodes and solder layers collectively determine the module's electrical resistance and thermal conduction pathways, thereby governing the output voltage, current, and power. Figure 14b presents an equivalent modeling approach based on an electrical resistance network. Electrically, a thermocouple pair can be represented as a temperature‐difference‐driven voltage source accompanied by an unavoidable internal resistance. When an external load is connected, the circuit current is driven by the thermoelectric voltage and constrained jointly by the internal resistance and the load resistance, with the output power delivered to the load depending on the degree of load matching [258]. Thermally, heat transfer from the hot side to the cold side does not occur exclusively through the thermoelectric legs; parallel heat‐leakage pathways also exist through the substrates, solder layers/electrodes, and structural gaps. These parasitic paths reduce the effective ΔT actually applied across the thermoelectric legs compared with the externally imposed ΔT. Meanwhile, Joule heating induced by electrical current and heat absorption/release associated with thermoelectric effects modify the internal temperature distribution, giving rise to coupled thermal–electrical interactions. Therefore, modeling approaches suitable for magnesium‐based TEDs must simultaneously incorporate temperature‐dependent transport properties, interfacial thermal and electrical contact resistances, and the thermal resistances of external heat sources and heat sinks. Only with such comprehensive treatment can the power output and efficiency of modules under realistic operating conditions be reliably predicted, providing a sound basis for the joint optimization of geometric parameters and module‐level architectures, such as leg height, fill factor, electrode layout, and interfacial processing.

Optimization of Device Components

Electrodes and Soldering

When translating thermoelectric materials from “intrinsic material performance” to “device‐level output, ” electrodes and interfacial connections are often among the first bottlenecks to emerge [259]. They not only determine whether charge carriers can be injected and extracted with minimal loss (i.e., low contact resistance and near‐ohmic behavior), but also govern whether a module can maintain chemical and mechanical stability during long‐term mid‐temperature operation and thermal cycling [256]. Consequently, electrode and interconnection design must simultaneously satisfy multiple constraints (chemical compatibility, process feasibility, efficient electrical injection, and long‐term stability) and converge through closed‐loop validation at the device level.

Figure 15a presents the isothermal section of the Mg–Ni–Sb ternary system at 773 K, illustrating a contact‐layer screening strategy guided by CALPHAD phase‐diagram calculations. By making the potential interfacial phase formation, reaction pathways, and thermodynamic stability “predictable” at the design stage, this approach avoids interface mismatch and uncontrolled reaction‐layer growth that often arise from empirical trial‐and‐error methods [42]. Based on this framework, Yin et al. identified Mg2Ni as a compatible contact layer for n‐type Mg3Sb2‐based materials and established “phase stability and controllability of reactions at the contact/transition layer” as one of the core criteria in device design [42]. For Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 devices, the key implication of this strategy is that electrode selection should not be based solely on σ and processability. Instead, candidate combinations that form high‐resistance or brittle interfacial phases within the target operating temperature range should be eliminated at the outset, and interfacial evolution should be constrained as much as possible to acceptable and reproducible phase compositions and thickness scales. Once the contact layer is determined, the joining process becomes critical in determining whether performance and reliability can be simultaneously achieved under the practical constraints of “low‐temperature processing and mid‐temperature operation”. Figure 15b summarizes the multilayer structure extending from the thermoelectric leg to the substrate, where excessively high joining temperatures at any layer can trigger thermally sensitive degradation of Zintl phases or accelerate interfacial reactions. Figure 15c directly compares conventional soldering with low‐temperature sintering of Ag nanoparticles: the former relies on relatively high peak processing temperatures, whereas Ag nanoparticle sintering enables dense, electrically conductive joints to be formed at substantially lower temperatures. This decouples the “processing temperature” from the “service‐temperature requirement” [42]. Such decoupling is particularly important for Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, as a reduced thermal history during joining not only lowers the risk of thermally induced degradation of material properties, but also suppresses the rapid growth of interfacial reaction layers and the accumulation of residual thermal stresses, thereby providing a more tolerant processing window for achieving stable, low‐parasitic‐loss interconnections.

Figure 15d further summarizes the generality of the Ag nanoparticle strategy from the perspective of the correspondence between joining temperature and the melting point/stability window of thermoelectric materials. By exploiting nanoscale sintering and densification, this approach enables a unified joining methodology applicable to thermoelectric materials and module architectures across multiple temperature regimes [260]. From a device‐engineering standpoint, this means that joining layers no longer need to rely on low‐melting‐point solders to achieve processability. As a result, the material selection space for electrodes, transition layers, and diffusion barriers is significantly expanded, facilitating the simultaneous optimization of electrical losses, thermal losses, and thermal‐expansion matching within structural design. Whether an interfacial design is truly effective must ultimately be validated through device‐level performance and thermal‐cycling stability. Figure 15e presents the output retention of Zintl‐phase devices under hot‐side temperature (T h) cycling, demonstrating that, with matched contact layers and low‐temperature joining, device efficiency and output can be maintained with minimal degradation over multiple thermal cycles. This behavior supports the reliability requirements for mid‐temperature waste‐heat recovery applications. Importantly, it also underscores a shift in evaluation priorities: beyond static metrics such as initial contact resistance and shear strength, the evolution of interfacial resistance after thermal cycling, the morphology of reaction layers, and the retention of device output should be treated as essential criteria. Otherwise, there is a risk of misinterpreting “initially low resistance” as “long‐term low resistance” [42, 260].

Beyond chemical compatibility and processing considerations, the interfacial electronic structure is equally critical in determining whether a contact can approach ohmic behavior. Figure 15f uses a metal/Mg2Si system as an illustrative example, showing that the formation of specific interfacial or electrode phases (such as Mg2SiNi3) can tune the work function and band alignment, reduce the interfacial barrier, and improve charge injection, thereby achieving lower contact resistance and more robust interconnections [261, 262]. Although this example does not involve a Zintl phase, the underlying insight can be directly transferred to Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 systems: contact layers and interfacial phases should not be regarded merely as structural elements for diffusion blocking or mechanical bonding. Instead, they should be treated as components that actively define the electrical boundary conditions of the device. In this context, it is essential that stable band alignment and low‐barrier carrier transport channels be maintained within the service temperature window, so as to prevent additional losses arising from barrier drift caused by interdiffusion or phase transformations at the interface [42, 261, 262].

Current interfacial challenges in TEDs are shifting from simply “minimizing contact resistance” toward achieving long‐term reliability and batch‐to‐batch consistency [164]. Although low‐temperature joining can mitigate thermal damage, large‐scale manufacturing remains constrained by processing windows, porosity, and creep issues, as well as cost considerations. Similarly, contact layers selected via phase‐diagram screening can reduce the risk of interfacial mismatch reactions, yet under prolonged aging and thermal cycling, they may still develop secondary reaction layers through slow interdiffusion, leading to contact‐resistance drift and performance variability. A more viable future direction lies in the adoption of manufacturable multilayer or graded interfacial architectures, in which diffusion blocking, mechanical buffering, compliance, and electrical matching are co‐designed. Such approaches should be complemented by standardized accelerated lifetime testing protocols that explicitly decouple and quantify “initial low resistance” from “lifetime low resistance” [164]. In parallel, tighter integration of CALPHAD and computational tools with in situ characterization and multiphysics modeling is needed to enable predictive interface design and to shorten the iteration cycle for interfacial solutions in a systematic manner [263].

Diffusion Barriers

The design of transition layers and diffusion barrier layers can be viewed as imposing dual constraints on interfacial reaction pathways and diffusion kinetics. Their purpose is not merely to isolate the metal electrode from the thermoelectric material, but to transform uncontrolled, rapid growth of reaction layers into predictable, slow diffusion processes, while simultaneously maintaining low specific contact resistance throughout the service window [264] together with sufficient interfacial bonding strength [230]. Figures 16a–c present a high‐throughput screening perspective, showing that different candidate interfacial materials exhibit strongly material‐dependent interfacial reaction or diffusion‐layer thicknesses when contacted with Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5, with these differences becoming even more pronounced after aging at 400°C for 7 days. More importantly, the temporal evolution of the interfacial reaction layer (IRL) thickness can be described by diffusion‐controlled kinetics, displaying an approximately linear dependence on the square root of aging time. This enables interfacial thermal stability to be assessed not merely by whether a reaction occurs, but through extrapolatable growth‐rate parameters that are directly useful for engineering evaluation. Within this kinetic framework, Wu et al. extrapolated the IRL growth behavior for Fe/Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5 interfaces and showed that, even over long durations at 400°C, the IRL thickness can remain at the micrometer scale, thereby providing a clear upper‐bound criterion for preliminary screening of barrier‐layer materials [265]. However, a thin IRL alone is insufficient to guarantee device reliability. Figure 16d further places shear strength and specific contact resistance within the same coordinate system, directly illustrating the multi‐objective constraints inherent to interface engineering. For n‐type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, Mg depletion and elemental interdiffusion near the interface can induce electrical losses and performance drift; thus, an effective barrier layer must simultaneously achieve diffusion passivation and electrical‐contact optimization. Wu et al. proposed an Fe‐based, alloying‐enabled multi‐criterion coupling strategy incorporating strong interfacial affinity, thermal expansion coefficient matching, diffusion passivation, and dopant deactivation. This approach yielded interfacial materials such as Fe7Mg2Cr and Fe7Mg2Ti, which exhibit initial shear strengths exceeding 40 MPa and specific contact resistances below 5 µΩ cm2, while retaining acceptable strength and low resistance after aging at 400°C for 15 days [265]. These results underscore that the essential role of transition layers lies in providing chemical buffering and electrical matching while permitting moderate, controllable metallurgical interdiffusion to establish stable ohmic contacts rather than striving for absolute interfacial inertness.

Figure 16e illustrates another equally effective approach, in which the interfacial reaction product itself is converted into a stable diffusion barrier, while a graded structure is employed to jointly manage stress and diffusion. Wang et al. utilized Mg4.3Sb3Ni formed by the reaction between Ni and Mg3.2Sb2Y0.05 as the barrier layer, constructing a graded junction of Mg4.3Sb3Ni/Mg3.2Sb2Y0.05/Mg4.3Sb3Ni. Through continuous transitions in composition and physical properties, this graded architecture mitigates interfacial stress concentration driven by thermal expansion mismatch and simultaneously suppresses detrimental elemental interdiffusion [232]. After aging at 673 K for 20 days, the device efficiency retained nearly 90% of its initial peak value, demonstrating the transferability of barrier‐layer design concepts from materials to the device level. Figure 16f presents the temperature‐dependent linear CTE for various candidate transition/barrier materials, enabling evaluation of their thermal expansion matching with the thermoelectric material side (denoted as TEcM in the legend) [34]. Overall, the CTE of all materials increases monotonically with temperature; however, the TEcM curve remains the highest across the entire testing range, indicating that the thermoelectric material exhibits the most “active” thermal expansion behavior. This highlights that interfacial design must prioritize the mitigation of thermal mismatch. A comparison among the alloys shows that 304 stainless steel and Fe7Mg2Ti possess relatively low and similar CTE values, Fe7Mg7Cr lies at an intermediate level, whereas the multicomponent alloy FeCrTiMnMg exhibits a higher CTE that more closely approaches that of TEcM at elevated temperatures. Consequently, Figure 16f directly supports the “CTE‐matching” design principle proposed by Wu et al.: by alloying (particularly through the incorporation of Mg and multiple elements) to raise the CTE of Fe‐based interfacial materials toward that of the thermoelectric material, interfacial thermal stresses and the risks of cracking or delamination induced by ΔCTE during thermal cycling can be reduced. This provides a foundation for achieving higher shear strength and long‐term reliability [265].

For a broader range of material systems, the trends illustrated in Figure 16g–i reflect a shift in the selection of barrier and transition layers from empirical trial‐and‐error toward phase‐diagram‐ and database‐driven, computable design. In Figure 16g, multicomponent phase equilibrium is imposed as a constraint: by constructing phase diagrams of target systems based on first‐principles calculations and screening candidate interfacial phases that are in two‐phase equilibrium with the thermoelectric material, the thermodynamic driving force for the continuous formation of secondary reaction layers can be fundamentally reduced. As shown in Figure 16h, Xie et al. further combined this thermodynamic screening with secondary filtering based on engineering parameters of the candidate phases, such as electrical resistivity and melting point. In this way, long‐term stability and manufacturability are effectively embedded at the materials‐selection stage. Using this methodology, semimetallic MgCuSb was identified as an effective transition layer compatible with MgAgSb. This paradigm has since been applied to the systematic screening of thermoelectric interfacial materials and validated at the device level, and has been summarized as a generalizable engineering roadmap [266, 267] Furthermore, Figure 16i presents the variation of η with ΔT for TEDs comprising two and seven thermocouple pairs. In both cases, η increases with increasing ΔT. Notably, the two‐pair MgAgSb/Mg3.2Bi1.5Sb0.5 TED achieves a η of 9.25% at a ΔT of 300 K, demonstrating favorable module scalability and low performance loss upon upscaling [267].

Filling Materials

For magnesium‐based TEDs, the “non‐functional volume” within the inter‐leg gaps often governs the ability to maintain an effective ΔT: convective and radiative heat leakage through these gaps can markedly reduce the cross‐leg ΔT and amplify temperature drops caused by interfacial thermal contact resistance, thereby preventing high material‐level ZT from translating into device efficiency [268]. Accordingly, the primary objective of insulating fillers is to suppress convection/radiation and bypass heat conduction as much as possible (without introducing electrical leakage or chemical degradation) while also providing the necessary structural support for assembly and service. An ideal filler must balance low κ, dielectric strength, thermal stability, and mechanical support, yet a single‐material optimum rarely exists. Vacuum is the ideal thermal insulator, but is difficult to maintain over long periods [1]. Air has low κ, but for Mg‐based materials (e.g., Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, Mg2Si, MgAgSb), it poses risks of oxidation and moisture‐induced degradation and failure. Polymers/resins offer low κ but are limited by heat resistance and long‐term creep. Inorganic fillers (SiO2, glass fibers, ceramic/glassy materials) provide superior structural stability but may introduce higher solid‐state thermal conduction and thermal‐stress concentrations. Models and experiments that account for heat losses consistently show that reducing filler κ and increasing an appropriate fill factor can effectively enhance device efficiency η; for example, aerogel fillers can deliver >8% efficiency improvement relative to unfilled devices, and η gains can exceed 9% as insulation approaches ideal conditions [269]. For magnesium‐based devices, practical engineering guidance is as follows: for mid‐temperature power generation (∼400–800 K), prioritize thermally robust inorganic insulation systems (e.g., aerogel composite felts, glass/ceramic fiber felts with inorganic binders), and reduce oxygen/moisture ingress through coordinated encapsulation and filling. In compact modules targeting high power density, leg spacing and fill factor should be co‐optimized to minimize bypass heat paths, while preventing fillers from intruding into electrode/diffusion‐barrier joints where they could cause wetting or diffusion impediments.

Protect Layers

Key environmental failure modes of magnesium‐based thermoelectric legs include oxidation and moisture‐induced corrosion, Mg volatilization leading to compositional drift and pore formation, and increases in contact resistance caused by interfacial interdiffusion [270]. The role of protective coatings extends beyond simply “shielding the environment”; more importantly, they stabilize chemical potential gradients across materials and interfaces during thermal cycling and long‐term operation, thereby suppressing elemental migration and preserving reproducible electrical and thermal boundary conditions. For the Mg2Si system, SiO2 anti‐oxidation coatings prepared via an improved sol‐gel process have been shown to maintain thermoelectric performance without noticeable degradation after aging for 200 h in air at 823 K, highlighting the critical importance of thin, dense oxygen‐diffusion barriers for Mg‐based compounds [271]. For Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5devices, coordinated interface and surface engineering has been demonstrated to simultaneously enhance efficiency and thermal stability: by combining appropriate interfacial transition/diffusion barrier materials with protective coatings to suppress interdiffusion and Mg evaporation, device efficiencies of ∼13% can be achieved while maintaining stable output for over 100 h under operating conditions [34]. These results underscore that “coating design” for magnesium‐based devices should be optimized as part of a coupled system together with electrodes, diffusion barriers, and joining materials, rather than as an isolated selection of a single coating material. In terms of material selection, coatings should satisfy several criteria: low oxygen and moisture permeability, strong adhesion to the Mg‐based substrate, thermal expansion compatibility, resistance to cracking under thermal cycling, and minimal added thermal contact resistance. In practice, multilayer architectures (e.g., a dense barrier layer combined with a compliant buffer layer) or synergistic combinations of “protective coating + interfacial diffusion barrier” can be employed to jointly reduce chemical and mechanical driving forces, thereby expanding the reliability window of magnesium‐based TEDs.

Leg Geometry

In magnesium‐based modules, leg geometry, specifically the height (H), cross‐sectional area (A), shape, and inter‐leg spacing, directly determines the internal electrical resistance, thermal conductance, and thermal‐stress distribution of the device, and thus serves as the central tuning knob for balancing efficiency‐power density‐reliability [272, 273]. For typical n‐type Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 paired with compatible p‐type materials, σ and κ are often asymmetric. Accordingly, different A and H values should be adopted for p‐ and n‐legs to achieve electrical and thermal matching, thereby preventing either side from becoming an electrical or thermal bottleneck. Meanwhile, as H decreases, device efficiency becomes increasingly sensitive to contact resistance and interfacial thermal resistance, further amplifying the importance of interface engineering. Beyond dimensional matching, non‐rectangular cross sections and variable‐area leg designs can tailor heat‐flux lines and current‐density distributions without increasing device volume, thereby enhancing the effective ΔT and alleviating localized thermal‐stress concentrations. However, for brittle polycrystalline Mg‐based legs, the processing defects and strength degradation associated with complex geometries cannot be ignored. Consequently, a more practical pathway for magnesium‐based devices is manufacturable geometric optimization: using coupled finite‐element simulations (under specified hot‐side/cold‐side heat‐transfer conditions, packaging constraints, and temperature‐dependent material parameters) to jointly optimize H, A, inter‐leg spacing, and fill factor. Priority should be given to parameter sets that are scalable and reproducible in manufacturing, rather than to intricate shapes that are difficult to mass‐produce consistently. From a manuscript‐level perspective, it is worth emphasizing that for mid‐temperature magnesium‐based materials such as Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, the benefits of geometric optimization typically arise from two factors: (i) increasing the effective cross‐leg ΔT under given heat‐dissipation conditions; and (ii) reducing peak thermal stresses that drive crack initiation during thermal cycling. Together, these factors determine whether efficiency and lifetime can be preserved as modules are scaled up.

Substrate

Substrates are often underestimated in TEDs, yet their importance is even more pronounced for magnesium‐based modules. On the one hand, the substrate provides electrical insulation and mechanical support while participating in heat spreading; on the other hand, under mid‐temperature operating conditions, thermal‐expansion mismatch between the substrate and the metal electrodes, joining layers, and thermoelectric legs often governs thermal‐cycling reliability. Conventional solid‐state modules typically employ ceramic substrates (e.g., Al2O3 and AlN) to provide electrical insulation and thermal robustness. Among these, AlN is advantageous due to its relatively high κ, which helps reduce substrate thermal resistance and improve temperature uniformity at the hot and cold sides [274]. However, a high κ can, in certain architectures, exacerbate parasitic heat dissipation, necessitating coordinated trade‐offs with heat‐sink design. Moreover, the substrate's heat capacity and thickness significantly influence temperature response and output fluctuations under transient thermal loads [275], thereby affecting the effective energy conversion of magnesium‐based devices in realistic operating scenarios (e.g., start‐stop operation, pulsed heat sources, and intermittent cooling). This conclusion has been repeatedly demonstrated in both device simulations and experimental validations. Accordingly, in the design of mid‐temperature magnesium‐based power‐generation modules, substrate selection should not be based solely on “temperature tolerance and electrical insulation”. Instead, heat capacity, thermal diffusivity, CTE matching with electrodes and encapsulation, and compatibility with joining processes (sintering, soldering, diffusion bonding) should be incorporated into a unified optimization framework.

Topology

Device geometry defines heat‐flow directions, assembly schemes, heat‐dissipation pathways, and system‐integration forms, thereby setting the application boundaries of magnesium‐based TEDs [276]. For mid‐temperature power generation centered on bulk legs such as Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, the classic π‐type configuration remains the dominant choice. Its advantages include predominantly one‐dimensional heat flow, relatively mature device modeling and parameter calibration, and good compatibility with high‐reliability electrode and diffusion‐barrier technologies. By contrast, for large‐area, low heat‐flux‐density scenarios (e.g., extended waste‐heat surfaces or requirements for conformal attachment), thin‐film or corrugated architectures [277] can provide wide‐area coverage while maintaining lateral heat flux and manufacturability. However, magnesium‐based materials are still constrained by processing windows and environmental stability when fabricated as thin films or integrated into flexible packages. More critically, geometric topology and thermo‐mechanical coupling analyses must be brought forward to the design stage. Magnesium‐based legs are susceptible to tensile stresses and interfacial delamination during thermal cycling, and the arrangement of legs within a module (number along the X/Y directions, spacing, and constraint conditions) can markedly alter the overall stress and deformation distributions [278]. Parametric scanning of module topologies via coupled thermo‐mechanical finite‐element simulations enables reduction of average internal stresses and extension of device lifetime without changing the material system, which is particularly essential for the engineering‐scale deployment of magnesium‐based modules.

Hierarchical and Graded Structural Design

The advantageous operating temperature range of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials is typically concentrated in the mid‐temperature regime, while the optimal working window of any single material is inherently limited. The essence of hierarchical and graded design lies in “redistributing material performance” at the device scale to enhance the full‐temperature‐range ZT ave and to reduce irreversible losses [279]. One approach is the segmented or cascaded strategy [280], in which materials optimized for different temperature ranges are connected in series along the heat‐flow direction, allowing each segment to operate near its own optimal temperature window and thereby improving overall efficiency. However, the additional interfaces introduced by segmentation inevitably accumulate electrical and thermal contact resistances and increase the risks of thermal‐expansion mismatch and interdiffusion. As a result, such designs place greater demands on the systematic integration of diffusion barriers, transition layers, and joining processes. Experimental studies in magnesium‐based systems have already demonstrated the potential of segmented modules; for example, segmented Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 (n‐type)/cubic GeTe (p‐type) TEDs have achieved high device efficiencies under optimized designs, validating their engineering feasibility [163]. A second route is the extension of the “graded” concept [281, 282]. Beyond material segmentation, gradients in composition or doping along the leg direction, as well as gradients in porosity, texture, or interfacial functionality, can be employed to simultaneously satisfy electrical, thermal, and mechanical constraints, for instance, prioritizing low contact resistance and high µ at the cold end, while emphasizing oxidation resistance and suppression of Mg volatilization at the hot end. For magnesium‐based systems, the value of such gradient designs is particularly evident in terms of reliability: by smoothing abrupt changes in chemical potential and CTE, the driving forces for interdiffusion and thermal stress are reduced, enabling modules to transition from “laboratory efficiency” to “long‐term serviceability”. Overall, hierarchical and graded structures in magnesium‐based devices are not a simple stacking of materials. Rather, they represent a reverse‐design philosophy that explicitly addresses the losses and failure modes associated with increased interfacial counts, trading a more controllable level of structural complexity for a larger effective ΔT, better full‐temperature‐range matching, and more stable long‐term output. This is precisely the challenge that must be addressed for magnesium‐based thermoelectrics to evolve from material‐level breakthroughs to robust device‐level engineering.

Applications

The core value of thermoelectric technology lies in achieving efficient, direct conversion between thermal and electrical energy through the synergistic optimization of material properties and device architectures, thereby offering irreplaceable application potential in mid‐ to low‐temperature power generation, solid‐state cooling, and flexible thermal management [283]. Owing to their high ZT values enabled by low‐dimensional structure engineering, materials‐level advantages such as Te‐free/low‐toxicity and lightweight characteristics [284], and intrinsically designable mechanical flexibility, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials have emerged as one of the leading candidates for next‐generation high‐performance TEDs. Correspondingly, their device‐oriented exploration is progressively advancing from laboratory‐scale prototypes toward practical application scenarios.

Power Generation

Low‐ to mid‐temperature waste heat (100–600 K) is ubiquitous in industrial exhaust streams, automotive tailpipes, and distributed energy systems [285]. It is typically characterized by a temperature that decays along the flow direction, complex thermal‐resistance networks, small usable ΔTs, and pronounced temporal fluctuations [286]. Owing to their high η within this temperature range, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials are particularly well suited for harvesting such low‐grade thermal energy. For example, Shi et al. addressed waste‐heat recovery from exhaust pipelines by developing a steady‐state three‐dimensional multiphysics model that couples fluid flow, heat transfer, and thermoelectric conversion. Based on this model, they inversely optimized the thicknesses of ceramic plates, electrodes, and thermoelectric legs, as well as array layouts, and adopted a zoned design strategy in which the effective area is gradually increased along the flow direction. This approach mitigates the current limitation caused by downstream T h decay and ultimately yields an engineering optimization pathway consistent with the real temperature distribution [75]. The same design philosophy is directly applicable to magnesium‐based systems: the optimal operating temperature windows of Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 and α‐MgAgSb align well with low‐grade heat sources, yet their device‐level output is highly dependent on the ability of external heat exchange and geometric parameters (leg height, fill factor, series/parallel configuration) to “amplify” the effective ΔT. Accordingly, for non‐uniform heat sources such as vehicles or pipelines, Mg‐based TEGs are better implemented using zonal matching strategies (varying A, leg height, or series/parallel connections) together with integrated, modular heat‐exchanger designs, so as to enhance average system power density and operational stability under real‐world conditions. In addition, thermoelectrics offer a particularly practical entry point in distributed self‐powered sensing applications, which typically involve small ΔTs, low power consumption, and stringent reliability requirements. Representative examples include condition monitoring of industrial pipelines and valves, waste‐heat sensing on rotating or reciprocating machinery surfaces, temperature‐gradient harvesting on heat exchangers and motor housings, and energy collection from ΔTs in buildings and outdoor infrastructure. These scenarios share common features: available ΔTs are often only a few to several tens of kelvin, and power demand is dominated by intermittent sampling and low‐duty‐cycle wireless transmission. In such cases, the key system‐design objective is not to pursue record η, but rather to convert small ΔTs into usable electrical power and deliver it with stable, reliable output.

Cooling and Thermal Management

Solid‐state cooling technologies, benefiting from the absence of moving parts and refrigerant emissions, are in strong demand for electronic thermal management and precision temperature control [287]. For magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials, cooling via the Peltier effect fundamentally relies on the synergy between a high S 2 σ and low κ. At the device level, however, the primary objective is not merely to maximize the attainable ΔT, but rather to maintain a stable cold‐side temperature (T c) and an acceptable coefficient of performance (COP) under constrained heat‐rejection conditions [288]. Accordingly, for Mg‐based materials to penetrate near‐room‐temperature thermal management applications, a more viable entry point lies in thermal management modules rather than direct one‐to‐one replacement of conventional cooling chips. Representative concepts include coupling with phase‐change or flexible heat‐spreading layers for peak shaving and load leveling, integration with high‐thermal‐conductivity spreaders for hotspot temperature homogenization, or combination with structurally engineered packaging to realize lightweight, conformal temperature control. In TEC systems, insufficient heat dissipation on the cold side can drive the device away from its optimal operating point, leading to increased input power consumption and reduced net cooling capacity. Meanwhile, interfacial thermal resistance directly erodes the usable ΔT and can trigger localized thermal‐stress concentrations [289]. Therefore, the engineering priorities for Mg‐based TEC and thermal‐management units should focus on: low‐thermal‐resistance yet electrically insulating heat‐spreading layers; compliant, thermally conductive gap‐filling and bonding strategies; electrode and diffusion‐barrier systems capable of suppressing elemental interdiffusion; and co‐design with external heat sinks (radiative, evaporative, phase‐change‐based, or lightweight porous heat‐dissipation structures). In other words, materials selection is only the starting point (system‐level thermal‐resistance management ultimately determines whether Mg‐based solutions can deliver competitive efficiency and reliability). In practical environments, the heightened sensitivity of Mg‐based materials to oxidation and elemental diffusion further elevates the importance of interfaces and packaging in cooling and thermal‐management scenarios. For realistic deployment, it is advisable to establish performance‐retention and failure‐mode databases under combined thermal cycling, humid‐thermal exposure, and mechanical loading, and to form reusable Mg‐based device engineering solutions through the coordinated optimization of interfacial architectures (transition layers, bilayer metals, diffusion barriers) and protective encapsulation layers.

Flexible and Wearable Devices

With the rapid development of wearable electronics, electronic skin, and human‐body energy harvesting technologies, flexible TEDs capable of stable operation under small ΔTs (<10 K), repeated deformation, and complex service environments have become a major research focus [222, 290]. Compared with conventional rigid TEDs, flexible and wearable devices impose much more stringent and comprehensive requirements on materials: in addition to a high S 2 σ or ZT near room temperature, they must simultaneously offer mechanical compliance, interfacial reliability, environmental stability, and low‐cost, scalable manufacturability [291]. In this context, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials, particularly the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 and Mg3Bi2 systems, have gradually demonstrated distinctive advantages over conventional Bi2Te3‐based materials [292]. As shown in Figure 17a, representative flexible thermoelectric thin films are compared in terms of room‐temperature ZT, fracture strain (ε s), and tensile strength (σ s) [221]. Notably, Mg3Sb0.5Bi1.49Te0.002 thin films retain thermoelectric performance comparable to that of Bi2Te3‐based materials while exhibiting markedly higher mechanical ductility and strength. This rare coexistence of “high performance and high flexibility” positions Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 as a compelling alternative to traditional brittle inorganic thermoelectric materials. At the same time, it highlights a fundamental trade‐off in flexible thermoelectrics: achieving simultaneously extreme thermoelectric performance and high ductility/strength remains intrinsically challenging. Based on Mg3Sb0.5Bi1.49Te0.002 thin films, Li et al. constructed both in‐plane and out‐of‐plane flexible TEDs (Figure 17b,c) [221]. In the in‐plane configuration, thermoelectric legs are arranged parallel to the flexible substrate, making it suitable for low‐ΔT energy harvesting from skin or curved heat sources. In contrast, the out‐of‐plane configuration employs a vertical heat‐flow pathway to enhance effective temperature‐difference utilization, which is more favorable for increasing output voltage and power density. The successful implementation of both architectures demonstrates the excellent adaptability of Mg‐based thin‐film materials to diverse device geometries. Figure 17d presents a schematic of a modular flexible TED based on Mg3Bi2 thin films. By introducing Ag electrodes and Ag gel interconnect layers between n‐type and p‐type thermoelectric legs, low contact resistance and good mechanical compatibility are achieved. This modular design provides a clear pathway toward scalable integration of flexible devices and underscores the critical role of interface engineering in wearable thermoelectric systems [293]. To meet the demands for environmental adaptability and mechanical robustness in wearable applications, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was employed as a flexible filler and encapsulation material (Figure 17e). Supported by a PDMS/polyimide (PI) composite structure, the double‐arm TED maintains stable output under repeated bending and external disturbances [223]. As shown in Figure 17f, the output voltage exhibits a good linear dependence on the ΔT, and no noticeable performance degradation is observed over multiple testing cycles, indicating reliable electrical stability of Mg‐based flexible devices under small‐temperature‐difference wearable conditions [223].

Beyond the Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 system, MgAgSb‐based flexible TEDs also show promising application potential. Figure 17g illustrates the equivalent output testing setup of an Ag2Se/MgAgSb flexible Y‐type TED. By incorporating aluminum heat sinks and flexible electrode structures at the hot and cold ends, an effective ΔT can be established under conformal heat‐source conditions, validating the feasibility of the MgAgSb system for flexible thermoelectric power generation [294]. To further address the requirements of lightweight design and thermal management in wearable devices, researchers have proposed integrating Mg‐based thermoelectric thin films with highly porous honeycomb support structures (Figure 17h). Through Mg vapor annealing, functional structures such as Mg2Si are formed within silica‐coated polybutadiene honeycomb templates, yielding an ultralight, low‐thermal‐conductivity support system with good mechanical stability. The SEM image in Figure 17i clearly reveals the hierarchical pore architecture of this honeycomb structure, offering new insights into future structure–thermal–mechanical co‐design strategies for Mg‐based flexible TEDs [295].

In summary, although magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials have achieved remarkable improvements in ZT at the laboratory scale in recent years, it must be emphasized that advantages in intrinsic material performance cannot be linearly translated into device‐level η. In practical TEDs, non‐ideal factors such as contact resistance, interfacial thermal resistance, electrode reactions, and the degradation or failure of diffusion barrier layers often substantially diminish the contribution of intrinsic material properties, resulting in a pronounced gap between measured device output power and theoretical predictions [296]. Moreover, most device‐oriented studies still focus on short‐term performance under steady‐state conditions, while systematic evaluations of thermal cycling, mechanical stress, and long‐term service reliability remain relatively limited. For magnesium‐based materials in particular, their high chemical reactivity and the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients with commonly used electrode materials are further amplified at the device scale, becoming major obstacles to engineering implementation. Equally important, many current device optimization strategies continue to follow design paradigms developed for conventional Bi2Te3‐ or PbTe‐based systems, without fully accounting for the unique characteristics of magnesium‐based materials in terms of thermal stability windows, optimal operating temperature ranges, and interfacial chemical behavior. Therefore, future progress in magnesium‐based TEDs urgently requires the establishment of a coordinated, materials‐centric design framework that integrates materials properties, interfaces, and device architectures, rather than relying on the straightforward transplantation of performance concepts from other thermoelectric systems.

Conclusion and Outlooks

Magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials, benefiting from high elemental abundance, low density, environmental friendliness, and outstanding thermoelectric potential in the low‐to‐mid temperature range, have in recent years emerged as one of the most representative and competitive material systems in the thermoelectric field. This article systematically reviews three typical classes of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials, namely Mg3(Sb, Bi)2, MgAgSb, and Mg2X (X = Si, Ge, Sn), summarizing recent advances in their crystal and electronic band structures, phonon transport regulation, synthesis and fabrication routes, performance optimization strategies, as well as device integration and applications. Particular emphasis is placed on emerging trends of magnesium‐based materials toward thin‐film architectures, mechanical flexibility, and device‐level implementations. As illustrated in Figure 18, the current development logic of magnesium‐based thermoelectrics has evolved into a closed‐loop, synergistic paradigm spanning “structure‐processing‐performance‐device‐modeling”. Starting from the crystal structure and intrinsic transport properties at the core, processing routes are used to encode microstructures and defect states into the material. These material‐level advantages are then translated into system‐level output at the device scale through leg geometry design, interfacial interconnections, and thermal management. Throughout this entire workflow, multiscale modeling and data‐driven approaches serve as the critical foundation for shortening iteration cycles and enhancing predictive capability. The emergence of this paradigm indicates that future competition will shift from isolated material metrics toward the ability to achieve coordinated optimization across multiple length and time scales.

Prospects of typical magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials and their device designs.

Despite the significant progress achieved, and the clear performance potential as well as cost and environmental advantages demonstrated by magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials in the low‐ and mid‐temperature regime, their transition from laboratory research to practical device‐ and system‐level applications still faces a series of cross‐scale physical and engineering challenges. Future studies should therefore build upon a deeper understanding of intrinsic transport mechanisms while further strengthening application‐oriented design principles, enabling a shift from “performance‐demonstration‐driven research” toward integrable and service‐ready material systems suitable for real‐world operation:

First, at the level of materials physics, it is essential to move beyond the current simplified understanding of carrier transport and phonon scattering. Most existing performance‐optimization strategies still rely on band models under quasi‐equilibrium conditions and independent‐scattering approximations. However, in magnesium‐based systems featuring heavy doping, multiphase coexistence, or strong defect engineering, the coupled interactions among carriers, defects, and grain boundaries are often substantially underestimated. Future efforts should therefore integrate first‐principles calculations, nonequilibrium transport theories, and in situ characterization techniques to systematically elucidate the cooperative roles of band‐structure reconstruction, the evolution of m*, and multiscale scattering mechanisms under realistic operating conditions. Such an approach is crucial for providing a sound physical basis for rationally assessing performance limits.

For systems such as Mg3(Sb, Bi)2 and MgAgSb, many of the reported high performances rely on extremely narrow composition/defect windows and highly sensitive processing histories, which often lead to pronounced batch‐to‐batch variations and significant challenges in scale‐up manufacturing. A more engineering‐oriented pathway should emphasize a wider processing window, more stable carrier control, reduced performance dispersion, and more predictable microstructural evolution. In this context, strategies such as high‐entropy or multi‐principal‐element design, deliberate engineering of phase‐boundary networks, and the construction of robust transport pathways (rather than single‐point optimization) are likely to be key enablers for improving reproducibility and large‐scale manufacturability.

Third, stability and service reliability must be elevated from “short‐term performance retention” to a closed‐loop understanding of failure mechanisms. Owing to their high chemical reactivity, strong interfacial reactivity, and thermodynamic metastability, magnesium‐based materials are prone to elemental migration, phase decomposition, interfacial embrittlement, and contact degradation under thermal cycling, long‐term current loading, mechanical stress, and complex atmospheres. Future efforts should establish a unified failure taxonomy that clearly identifies dominant degradation modes, such as oxidation/evaporation, diffusion‐driven reactions, thermal fatigue, creep/cracking, and interfacial electromigration, and develop reproducible accelerated lifetime testing protocols and lifetime models that explicitly link material parameters, interconnect architectures, and operating conditions. In this way, serviceability can become as quantifiable, comparable, and optimizable as ZT. Beyond the material level, research on magnesium‐based thermoelectrics must move away from a single‐metric, materials‐centric mindset toward materials‐device‐system co‐optimization. In practice, many high‐ZT magnesium‐based materials fail to deliver their theoretically expected η once integrated into devices, primarily due to the combined effects of contact resistance, interfacial reactions, thermal expansion mismatch, and suboptimal module architectures. Accordingly, future work should strengthen the systematic design and modeling of electrode/diffusion‐barrier/substrate systems and evaluate device performance and stability under realistic operating conditions, thereby enabling the effective translation of material‐level advantages into device‐level efficiency gains.

Fourth, device development must transition from a purely “materials‐driven” approach to a synergistic design framework integrating materials, interfaces, structures, and thermal management. A high ZT value does not inherently guarantee high device efficiency; contact resistance, diffusion barrier reactions, thermal expansion mismatch, parasitic heat losses, and module architecture can all substantially reduce the realizable performance. For magnesium‐based systems, key priorities in device engineering should include: systematic materials screening of electrode/barrier/solder/substrate combinations with integrated thermos‐electro‐chemical compatibility assessment; construction of interfaces that simultaneously deliver low contact resistance and long‐term stability; optimization of leg geometry and gradient/hierarchical architectures to match heat flux and current distribution; and ensuring process uniformity and packaging reliability when scaling from single‐leg devices to multi‐leg modules. Consistent with the “leg design‐modeling” framework illustrated in Figure 18, it is recommended that device efficiency, power density, areal output power, and lifetime be treated as primary performance metrics alongside ZT, thereby advancing toward truly device‐deliverable performance. Overall, the future progress of magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials will depend on deeper fundamental understanding of transport physics, a transition in materials design paradigms, and the coordinated resolution of engineering and device‐level challenges. Only through coordinated advances across multiple length scales and performance dimensions can magnesium‐based thermoelectrics truly evolve from “promising materials” into “practically deployable materials”.

Fifth, thin‐film fabrication, flexibility, and miniaturization represent differentiated opportunity windows for magnesium‐based materials, but these advances must be underpinned by manufacturing science. Thin‐film deposition techniques (sputtering, evaporation, pulsed laser deposition, etc.) combined with post‐deposition annealing provide new degrees of freedom for precise control of composition and stress, while enabling flexible energy harvesting and microsystem integration. However, these approaches also introduce new challenges, including compositional segregation, grain texture evolution, interfacial diffusion, and residual stress accumulation. Future priorities should include developing low‐temperature process windows compatible with flexible substrates; designing stretchable interconnect and encapsulation strategies; establishing accurate measurement protocols for thin‐film κ and thermal contact resistance; and creating device architectures compatible with microfabrication. The goal should be to deliver manufacturable thin‐film performance, rather than relying on idealized thin‐film ZT values that are difficult to reproduce at scale.

Finally, data infrastructure and standardization will become the “invisible foundation” for accelerating iteration. Variations across research groups in magnesium‐based systems often originate from differences in sample density, compositional deviation, measurement methodology, and interface processing details. We therefore advocate for further standardization of reporting practices, including key transport properties (S, σ, κ, along with uncertainty); microstructural statistics; processing parameters; and device testing conditions. Establishing shared datasets and benchmark samples, combined with high‐throughput computation/experimentation and machine learning, will enable rapid identification of “robust optimum regions” within the composition‐process‐performance space, shifting development from empirically driven exploration toward predictive design.

From an application roadmap perspective, magnesium‐based thermoelectric materials do not need to replace conventional systems across all temperature ranges. Their most strategic positioning lies in mid‐to‐low‐temperature waste heat recovery, distributed energy harvesting, and flexible/wearable and microdevice applications, where their advantages in cost, resource availability, and environmental compatibility can be fully leveraged, while complementing high‐temperature or ultra‐high‐performance thermoelectric systems. Ultimately, whether magnesium‐based thermoelectrics can transition from “promising materials” to “service‐ready materials” will depend on three pillars: (i) deep understanding of transport and failure mechanisms under realistic operating conditions; (ii) materials design oriented toward engineering tolerance rather than peak performance alone; and (iii) coordinated optimization across materials, interfaces, devices, and system levels. By advancing along the closed‐loop paradigm illustrated in Figure 18, magnesium‐based thermoelectric systems are well positioned to occupy a stable and irreplaceable role in the future thermoelectric technology landscape.
