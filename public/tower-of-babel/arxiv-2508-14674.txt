Contributions to the Theory of Clifford-Cyclotomic Circuits
Linh Dinh & Neil J. Ross

Abstract
Let n n be a positive integer divisible by 8. The Clifford-cyclotomic gate set 𝒢 n \mathcal{G}_{n} consists of the Clifford gates, together with a z z -rotation of order n n . It is easy to show that, if a circuit over 𝒢 n \mathcal{G}_{n} represents a unitary matrix U U , then the entries of U U must lie in ℛ n \mathcal{R}_{n} , the smallest subring of ℂ \mathbb{C} containing 1 / 2 1/2 and exp ⁡ ( 2 ​ π ​ i / n ) \mathrm{exp}(2\pi i/n) . The converse implication, that every unitary U U with entries in ℛ n \mathcal{R}_{n} can be represented by a circuit over 𝒢 n \mathcal{G}_{n} , is harder to show, but it was recently proved to be true when n = 2 k n=2^{k} . In that case, k − 2 k-2 ancillas suffice to synthesize a circuit for U U , which is known to be minimal for k = 3 k=3 , but not for larger values of k k . In the present paper, we make two contributions to the theory of Clifford-cyclotomic circuits. Firstly, we improve the existing synthesis algorithm by showing that, when n = 2 k n=2^{k} and k ≥ 4 k\geq 4 , only k − 3 k-3 ancillas are needed to synthesize a circuit for U U , which is minimal for k = 4 k=4 . Secondly, we extend the existing synthesis algorithm to the case of n = 3 ⋅ 2 k n=3\cdot 2^{k} with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 .

Introduction

1.1
Background

Let
n
be a positive integer divisible by 8. The

Clifford-cyclotomic gate set of degree

,
8
,

,

An important question in the theory of Clifford-cyclotomic circuits is
that of precisely characterizing the matrices that can be exactly
represented by a circuit over
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}
be
the smallest subring of
\mathbb{C}
containing
1/2
and
\zeta_{n}

is easy to see that, if a circuit over

unitary matrix

must lie in

\mathcal{R}_{n}

with

\mathcal{R}_{n}
can be represented by a circuit over

only known to be true for the Clifford+

as well as for a handful of adjacent gate sets

[
4
,
9

. Recently, however, it was shown that this

is a power of 2
[

]
. In
that case,

questions. Firstly, when

ancillas needed to synthesize a Clifford-cyclotomic circuit of degree

n
be made smaller than
\log(n)-2
? Secondly, can one prove an exact
synthesis result for Clifford-cyclotomic circuits whose degree is not
a power of 2? In the present paper, we contribute to the theory of
Clifford-cyclotomic circuits by answering both questions positively.

Contributions

4
Determinants

The
determinant
is an important function on matrices. We will
be interested in the determinant of certain block matrices.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
M
be an
m\times m
matrix
with entries in
R
. The
determinant of
M
with respect to

R
, denoted by
\det\nolimits_{R}(M)
, is defined as

\det\nolimits_{R}(M)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}M_{j,\sigma(j)},

where
S_{m}
is the symmetric group of degree
m
and
\sgn(\sigma)
is
the sign of the permutation
\sigma\in S_{m}
. If
R
is a subring of
some ring
R^{\prime}
and
M
is a matrix with entries in
R
, we have

\det\nolimits_{R}(M)=\det\nolimits_{R^{\prime}}(M)
. For simplicity, when the
ring
R
in which the determinant is to be computed is clear from
context, we sometimes write
\det(M)
rather than

\det\nolimits_{R}(M)
. The determinant is multiplicative, in the sense
that for matrices
M
and
M^{\prime}
over
R
of compatible dimensions, we
have
\det\nolimits_{R}(MM^{\prime})=\det\nolimits_{R}(M)\det\nolimits_{R}(M^{\prime})
. Moreover, if
M
is a complex
unitary matrix, then
|\det\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}(M)|=1
.

If
\phi:R\to R^{\prime}
is a ring homomorphism, then the entrywise
application of
\phi
is a ring homomorphism
\mathrm{M}_{m}(R)\to\mathrm{M}_{m}(R^{\prime})
. By a slight abuse of notation, we use the symbol

\phi
to denote both the homomorphism
R\to R^{\prime}
and its entrywise
extension
\mathrm{M}(R)\to\mathrm{M}(R^{\prime})
. Now consider a matrix
M

over
R
. Since
\phi
is a ring homomorphism and the determinant is a
polynomial in the matrix entries, we have

\phi(\det\nolimits_{R}(M))=\phi\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}M_{j,\sigma(j)}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\phi\left(M\right)_{j,\sigma(j)}=\det\nolimits_{R^{\prime}}(\phi(M)).

(5)

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
R^{\prime}
be a commutative subring of

\mathrm{M}_{m}(R)
. Now consider a matrix
M
in

\mathrm{M}_{m^{\prime}}(R^{\prime})
. The matrix
M
is an
m^{\prime}\times m^{\prime}
block matrix
whose blocks are
m\times m
matrices over
R
. We can therefore
compute the determinant of
M
with respect to
R^{\prime}
, and then the
determinant of the resulting matrix with respect to

R
. Alternatively, we can “open” the blocks and think of
M
as an

mm^{\prime}\times mm^{\prime}
matrix over
R
to directly compute the determinant of

M
with respect to
R
. The following theorem, whose proof can be
found in
[

, states that these two
quantities are equal.

Theorem 4.1

(Silvester)
.

Let

be a commutative ring, let
R^{\prime}
be a commutative subring
of
\mathrm{M}_{m}(R)
, and let

. Then

\det\nolimits_{R}M=\det\nolimits_{R}(\det\nolimits_{R^{\prime}}M).

5
Catalytic embeddings

We now introduce
catalytic embeddings

[
2
,
3
,
10
]
. Let
\mathcal{U}
and

\mathcal{V}
be two sets of unitary matrices. An

m
-
dimensional catalytic embedding
from
\mathcal{U}
into

\mathcal{V}
is a pair
(\phi,\textbf{c})
where
\phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
is a function and
\textbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a
unit vector such that

1.

If
U\in\mathcal{U}
has dimension
d
, then
\phi(U)\in\mathcal{V}
has dimension
md
, and

2.

For any
\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}

\phi(U)(\textbf{u}\otimes\textbf{c})=(U\textbf{u})\otimes\textbf{c}.

We often write
(\phi,\textbf{c}):\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
to indicate that
(\phi,\textbf{c})
is a catalytic
embedding from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{V}
. The composition of an

m
-dimensional catalytic embedding
(\phi,\textbf{c}):\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and an
m^{\prime}
-dimensional
catalytic embedding
(\phi^{\prime},\textbf{c}^{\prime}):\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{W}

is the
mm^{\prime}
-dimensional catalytic embedding
(\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi,\textbf{c}\otimes\textbf{c}^{\prime}):\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{W}
. The
catalytic embedding
(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{U}},1):\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
acts as the identity for this notion of
composition.

We now discuss two specific catalytic embeddings which will be
important in the rest of this paper. Let

and define the
matrix
\Lambda_{k}
and the vector
\textbf{c}_{k}
by

\Lambda_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
\zeta_{2^{k-1}}&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\textbf{c}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\zeta_{2^{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

We can use
\Lambda_{k}
and

to define a catalytic
embedding, following

2
,
3
]
. Consider a matrix

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(U)(\textbf{u}\otimes\textbf{c}_{k})

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
a
and

be integers, at least one of which is

, and equality is achieved
exactly when
(a,b)
is one of

(0,\pm 1)

(\pm 1,\mp 1)

Proof.

First notice that if

and

. A similar argument applies when
a=0
and

exactly when
a=\pm 1
and
b=0
, while equality in the second of
these cases happens exactly when
a=0
and
b=\pm 1
. Now suppose
that
a
and
b
are both nonzero, and assume, without loss of

\absolutevalue{a}\geq\absolutevalue{b}
. We then have

, so that

. Hence,

a^{2}+ab+b^{2}\geq b^{2}\geq 1,

since

. Equality in this case happens exactly when
b^{2}=1
and
a^{2}+ab=0
which, in turn, happens exactly when
b=\pm 1
and
a=\mp 1
.
∎

Conclusion

We now know that, for
n=2^{k}
and

,
m
-qubit circuits
with ancillas over
\mathcal{G}_{n}
correspond precisely to matrices in

\mathrm{U}_{2^{m}}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
. Yet, many questions in the theory
of Clifford-cyclotomic circuits remain unanswered. Two natural open
problems are the following.

1.

For which values of
n
does the correspondence between circuits
over
\mathcal{G}_{n}
and matrices in
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{R}_{n})

hold?

2.

For the values of
n
for which the correspondence holds, what
is the smallest number of ancillas required to synthesize circuits
in the worst case?

A natural initial step in addressing the first of these open problems
is to consider values of
n
of the form
p\cdot 2^{k}
, for

prime. While it stands to reason that our results might generalize to
such cases, it gets progressively harder to analyze the relevant

We start by recalling two well known facts, before establishing a

Let

Lemma A.3

We have
