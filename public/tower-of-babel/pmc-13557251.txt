Interplay of Biological Treatment and Psychological Features in Patients with Psoriasis: A Real-Life Prospective Study
Authors: Gaia Moretta, Tonia Samela, Alessia Provini, Gianluca Pagnanelli, Dario Graceffa, Diego Orsini, Viviana Lora, Antonio Giovanni Richetta, Annunziata Dattola, Simone Amato, Nicoletta Bernardini, Paola Tribuzi, Arianna Zangrilli, Martina Vultaggio, Luca Bianchi, Giacomo Caldarola, Eleonora De Luca, Clara De Simone, Domenico Giordano, Cinzia Masini, Mauro Bavetta, Stefania Lechiancole, Vincenzo Panasiti, Damiano Abeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmotional dysregulation (ED), common among patients with psoriasis, leads to psychopathology and impairs treatment adherence.ObjectiveThis study examined the changes in prevalence of ED in patients and its effects on illness perception, mental health, and quality of life (QoL) during one year of treatment with risankizumab.MethodED, illness perception, general health, and QoL were measured at baseline, and at four, 16, and 52 weeks posttreatment in 58 patients with psoriasis.ResultsHigher ED was associated with greater psychological distress and poorer QoL. Patients with higher levels of ED reported greater psychological distress, worse QoL, and higher symptom-related distress throughout treatment as compared to those with lower ones. Although both groups improved in terms of illness perception and clinical severity outcomes, patients with high ED consistently experienced worse subjective outcomes.ConclusionED is frequent in psoriasis patients and negatively affects mental health and QoL, despite clinical improvements in skin symptoms. Targeted psychological interventions may be necessary for patients who exhibit higher levels of emotional dysregulation to ensure comprehensive care and better long-term outcomes.Keywords: Psychodermatology, Psoriasis, Emotional dysregulation, Patient-reported outcomes, Quality of life

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder driven by a polygenic predisposition and is characterized by significant dermatological manifestations with a high impact on quality of life [1].

Numerous studies emphasize that individuals with psoriasis frequently experience severe psychological symptoms linked to their condition, making them more susceptible to depression, suicidal ideation [2], anxiety, worry, and social phobia and are prone to use unhealthy coping habits [3]. In fact, studies have found a prevalence of depression in patients with psoriasis of 74%, with an incidence of 91.9 per 1000 person-years [4,5]. Emotional stress is thought to play a significant role in both the onset and exacerbation of psoriasis [6–8].

Emotional regulation is a construct that refers to the processes by which individuals appraise, label, regulate, display, and experience their emotions [9]. The absence of any of these skills may indicate difficulties in emotional regulation [10]. Recent studies have identified, with different methods, a high risk of emotional dysregulation [11–15] and low ability to process emotional information (i.e., emotional awareness) in patients with psoriasis [13,16]. These psychological difficulties in processing emotion could be risk factors for exacerbation and maintenance of psychopathology as well as an obstacle to therapeutic adherence [17]. Moreover, emotional dysregulation may affect the patient’s illness perception, which, in turn, may contribute to the mismanagement of chronic diseases [18–21]. The perception of overwhelming emotional demands drains the psychological resources needed for everyday self-care management of chronic diseases such as psoriasis, contributing to poor health outcomes [22], and may lead to an increase in perceived illness severity in these patients [11].

Patients with psoriasis can have a cumulative life course impairment due to the effect of skin signs and physical and psychological comorbidities [23]. However, the cumulative impact can vary from patient to patient according to their psychological condition and their personality [24]; higher values of emotional dysregulation can have a higher cumulative impact.

New biologic drugs can effectively treat psoriasis, achieving Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [25] 90–100 (90–100% improvement from baseline). An early intervention with new targeted therapy to obtain complete disease control may prevent psychological distress, depression, and cumulative impact on QoL [23].

While the clinical effectiveness of biological treatments has been established, few studies have investigated the interplay of pharmacological interventions and psychological features in patients with psoriasis. Thus, we hypothesized that outpatients may have difficulties in understanding and regulating their inner states and emotions, with serious consequences on their mental health and treatment satisfaction. Consistently with this hypothesis we aimed: i) to describe the presence of difficulties in emotion regulation among outpatients with plaque psoriasis; ii) to assess the impact of emotional dysregulation on disease perception, mental health, and quality of life (QoL) over the course of one year of treatment with risankizumab.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants, Ethics Compliance

This work was a longitudinal multicenter descriptive study approved by the Ethics Committee of the IDI-IRCCS (protocol number: 682/1) with the aim of assessing emotional dysregulation, QoL, and disease perception of people who suffer from psoriasis over the course of one year of treatment with risankizumab. From October 2022 to April 2023, we recruited consecutives patients with a diagnosis of psoriasis, admitted in 10 dermatological centers in Lazio, Italy. In order to participate in the study, the inclusion criteria were: i) age ≥18 years, both sexes; ii) candidate for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, eligible for systemic therapy; iii) able to understand Italian, having signed the informed consent, and having accepted to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: i) the presence of diagnosed past or current psychiatric disorders; ii) the use of psychoactive medications in the preceding 12 months; iii) having undergone psychological or psychotherapeutic treatments in the preceding 12 months.

At baseline, each patient filled out a standardized sociodemographic data collection form concerning age, sex, weight and height in order to calculate BMI, comorbidity (i.e., a disease or medical condition that is simultaneously present with psoriasis), and smoking habit. Complete sociodemographic information is presented in Table 1. Each patient was then asked to complete a series of self-reported measures (see Measures section below). During the visit, the dermatologist assessed the clinical severity of psoriasis (i.e., PASI) and the prescribed dosage of risankizumab. At 4-, 16- and 52-week follow-up (i.e., T0, T4, T16, T52), patients completed a new questionnaire containing self-reported measurements, and the dermatologist re-scored the PASI.

Measures

In this study the extent and severity of psoriasis was evaluated by PASI. The PASI score is a composite measure that evaluates multiple aspects of psoriasis, focusing on four regions: the head, arms, trunk, and legs. In each area, three plaque characteristics—erythema (redness), induration (thickness), and desquamation (scaling)—are assessed. The scores for these clinical signs are summed for each region and then weighted based on the proportion of the body that each area represents. This calculation yields a final score, ranging from 0 to a maximum of 72. In this study, PASI was evaluated at baseline and at 4, 16, and 52 weeks of treatment.

Difficulties in emotional regulation were assessed by the Difficulties in Emotional Regulation Scale (DERS) [26] in its Italian adaptation. It is a self-report questionnaire developed on an integrated and comprehensive theoretical model that measures clinically relevant difficulties in regulating negative emotions [27]. This instrument measures potential difficulties in the following areas: i) Nonacceptance, the tendency to feel negative secondary emotions or reject one’s discomfort; ii) Goals, difficulty in maintaining focus and task performance during negative emotions; iii) Impulse, trouble controlling behavior when upset; iv) Awareness, lack of attention to and recognition of emotions (scored by reversing responses); v) Strategies, limited belief in the ability to effectively regulate emotions; vi) Clarity, the extent to which individuals understand their emotions clearly. Difficulties in emotional regulation were assessed in this study at baseline. The illness perception was evaluated with the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (Brief IPQ) [29]. The questionnaire assesses how individuals tend to perceive and accommodate the emotional and practical consequences associated with their disease. This measure is based on the theoretical assumption that a disease can generate both cognitive and emotional representations (i.e., illness) in patient’s mind. The individual would first form a mental representation of his or her illness, then adopt a set of behaviors to cope with it, and finally, evaluate the effectiveness of these behaviors within his or her environment [29]. The questionnaire consists of nine items that can be rated on a Likert scale (0–10) that yields a single total score. Higher scores are indicative of worse patient adaptation to the disease.

The Skindex-17 is an instrument derived from the Skindex-29 [30]. It is an instrument designed to measure the effects of skin disease on patients’ lives, which has already been applied in different populations of dermatology patients [31]. Patients are asked to rate their health status over the preceding week, marking the corresponding score on a Likert scale (never; rarely/sometimes; often/always), with a total of 17 items. The Skindex17 has two subscales: the first measures how dermatological symptoms affect the patient’s health-related QoL; the second, instead, investigates the psychosocial distress experienced. The scores of the psychosocial subscale can be categorized in three levels of severity: mild (≤20.82), moderate (20.83–37.50), severe (≥37.51) [30]. The General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12) [32,33] is a self-administered questionnaire designed to measure the degree of psychological distress and the presence of nonpsychotic psychiatric symptoms. It is a widely used instrument in a variety of patient populations, including those with dermatological diseases [32]. The GHQ-12 is not an instrument designed to provide a diagnosis but to identify a probable case of a disorder of psychiatric significance in patients who obtain relevant scores. Higher scores on the GHQ-12 indicate a higher level of psychological distress [32]. GHQ-12 can be scored in two different ways: the first consists in a continuous score; the second is dichotomous. This first scoring mode is known as “bimodal,” whereby the so-called “GHQ-case” is defined by a profile of responses worthy of clinical attention and corresponds, for each item, to the scores of 3 or 4, which are coded as “1”; scores 1 and 2 are instead coded as “0.” The second GHQ-12 scoring mode is the one most frequently used in a variety of contexts clinical settings [34]. In this study, the specific cutoff for bimodal mode scoring was set for scores ≥7.

The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) is a validated questionnaire that measures Health Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) in patients with dermatological diseases. It consists of 10 questions and assesses the impact of the skin disease on daily life, work, and social relationships in the preceding seven days. This tooll provides a score between 0 (no impact) to 30 (maximum impact on HRQoL). The higher the score, the greater the impairment of QoL.

In this study the Brief IPQ, the GHQ, the Skindex-17, and the DLQI were administered at baseline and at 4, 16, and 52 weeks.

Data Collection Procedures and Statistical Analysis

The study sample corresponds to the actual number of outpatients seen during the study period who agreed to participate in the study; no sample size calculation was performed. Sociodemographic information was summarized in frequency distributions and the patient-reported outcomes as means and standard error (SE) of the mean. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to evaluate the reliability and internal consistency of self-reported instruments. The proportion of patients reaching PASI 75 and PASI 90 was calculated at the three follow-up time points. Differences between baseline (i.e., T0) and T4, T16, and T52, respectively, were obtained both for self-reported measures and clinical severity scores. Subsequently, we dichotomized the DERS scores, assigning a value of ‘0’ if the patient scored below the mean proposed by Sighinolfi, Pala, Chiri, Marchetti and Sica [27] in the validation study of the instrument (i.e., normative data from the Italian population), or ‘1’ if the score was above this threshold. We then calculated the mean change in PASI, GHQ-12, Brief IPQ, Skindex-17, and DLQI scores from T0 to T16 separately for patients scoring above or below the DERS cutoff. To compare such changes, a t-test was used. All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0.1.0) [36].

Results

The characteristics of the study population, for the main variables of interest, are summarized in Table 1. The study population comprised 58 participants, with a mean PASI score of 15.4 (SE=1.2) and a mean DERS total score of 75.9 (SE=3.2). Males, representing 62.1% of the sample, had a higher mean PASI (17.2 vs 12.9) but a lower DERS score (73.5 vs 80.0) compared to females, though the differences were not statistically significant (PASI P=0.076; DERS P=0.332). Age groups (i.e., <55 and 55+) and BMI categories showed similar PASI and DERS scores, with no significant difference across levels (all p-values >0.5). Education, marital status, smoking, alcohol use, and the presence of comorbidities like diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia did not significantly affect PASI or DERS scores (all p-values > 0.2). However, individuals with arthritis tended to have a higher PASI (17.3 vs 14.5) and DERS score (82.3 vs 72.8) than those without arthritis, although these differences were not statistically significant (PASI P=0.288; DERS P=0.158).

Table 1 also shows the mean baseline PASI and total DERS scores. No significant difference was observed for such measures in the different levels of the considered variables, with the only comparison approaching statistical significance for PASI between males (17.2) and females (12.9).

When we compared the T0-T16 differences for PASI and the PROs separately for patients below and above the threshold for the total DERS score, we observed that the individuals above the cutoff constantly had larger improvements than did those below the cutoff. However, while some of these differences were large (e.g., 5.9 vs 2.7 for the change in GHQ-12 scores), the only statistically significant difference was seen for the psychosocial scale of the Skindex-17, with a 34.3 mean improvement among patients with above cutoff DERS total scores vs 3.9 for those below cutoff (P= = 0.007).

The proportion of patients who reached PASI 75 was 33.3% at T4, 88.6% at T16, and 89.2% at T52. For PASI 90, these proportions were 19.4%, 80.0%, and 81.1%, respectively.

Discussion

The present study investigated the presence and impact of emotional dysregulation in patients with plaque psoriasis undergoing treatment with risankizumab, with a focus on how difficulties in emotion regulation affect disease perception, mental health, and QoL over one year of treatment (i.e., 52 weeks). Psoriasis, a chronic inflammatory skin disorder, not only presents physical symptoms but also deeply impacts psychological well-being, leading to higher rates of emotional distress, depression, and anxiety symptomatology [2]. This study highlights that patients with psoriasis seem to struggle with emotional regulation, which may worsen their mental health and decrease treatment satisfaction [11].

Our findings suggest that problems in emotional regulation, as measured by the DERS scale, seemed to be frequent among the sample, though no significant difference in baseline PASI and DERS scores was observed across sex, age, or BMI. However, individuals with comorbidities such as psoriatic arthritis tended to exhibit higher levels of emotional dysregulation, consistent with the understanding that physical discomfort can exacerbate psychological distress [37]. This aligns with the literature linking stress and emotional challenges to the exacerbation of psoriasis symptoms [8]. Interestingly, while the study did not observe statistically significant differences in PASI scores among patients with higher emotional dysregulation, these individuals reported more considerable improvements in mental health and QoL during treatment, particularly regarding psychosocial distress, as suggested by changes over time of the Skindex-17 psychosocial subscale mean scores, suggesting that addressing emotional regulation may play a critical role in overall well-being of these patients. Despite some large changes, statistical significance was only reached for the Skindex-17 psychosocial subscale, indicating that the burden of emotional dysregulation may most strongly impact social and psychological aspects of living with psoriasis.

To further explore the relationship between problems in emotional regulation and treatment outcomes in patients who suffer from psoriasis, we analyzed differences in mean scores across patient-reported outcomes (PROs) based on DERS scores. These analyses revealed trends in how emotional regulation difficulties impact patients’ mental health, QoL, and disease perception over the course of treatment. For example, regarding the GHQ-12 (whcih measures psychological distress and the likelihood of psychiatric symptoms), patients with higher emotional dysregulation (i.e., DERS total: 21) consistently reported higher psychological distress across all time points compared to those with lower dysregulation (i.e., DERS total: 0). At baseline (T0), 50% of patients with high emotional dysregulation met the GHQ-12 case threshold, compared to only 14% of those with lower dysregulation. Although the gap narrowed over time, significant differences persisted through week 52. This indicates that problems of emotional regulation may heighten susceptibility to psychological distress throughout the treatment period. Also, patients with high emotional dysregulation at baseline had a mean DLQI score of 12.37, significantly higher than those with low dysregulation (mean DLQI: 8.00). Although both groups experienced substantial improvements over time, patients with higher emotional dysregulation still reported worse QoL scores at week 16 (mean DLQI: 2.67 vs 0.70) and week 52 (mean DLQI: 2.45 vs 0.86). The difference in scores at week 4 was statistically significant (P=0.006), indicating that emotional dysregulation significantly affects perceived QoL early in treatment.

Illness perception, measured using the IPQ, captures how patients perceive the severity and impact of their condition on everyday life from a cognitive and emotional point of view. At baseline, patients with higher emotional dysregulation reported worse illness perception (mean IPQ: 53.37) compared to those with lower dysregulation (mean IPQ: 46.60), approaching statistical significance (P=0.066). However, both groups showed improvements in illness perception over time, with the differences between groups becoming smaller over time. This suggests that while emotional dysregulation affects initial illness perception, it may become less influenced by emotional factors as treatment progresses. Symptom-related distress was significantly higher among patients with higher emotional dysregulation at baseline (mean SYM: 68.03) compared to those with lower dysregulation (mean SYM: 51.43), with a statistically significant difference (P=0.021). While both groups showed improvements, patients with higher dysregulation continued to report greater symptom-related distress at all follow-up time points. This suggests that emotional dysregulation may exacerbate patients’ subjective experience of psoriasis symptoms, even as clinical severity improves.

Finally, regarding the clinical measures of psoriasis severity (i.e., the PASI score), at baseline, patients with higher emotional dysregulation had a slightly higher mean PASI score compared to those with lower dysregulation. By week 16, both groups showed significant improvements, with PASI scores nearing 0, indicating successful disease management with risankizumab. Notably, by week 52, both groups sustained high rates of clinical response (with 81.1% of patients achieving PASI 90), indicating that while emotional dysregulation influences subjective and psychological outcomes, it does not impair the therapeutic efficacy of risankizumab on skin symptoms.

Our results indicate that emotional dysregulation seems to play a significant role in shaping the psychological and QoL outcomes of patients with psoriasis, even though it does not seem to affect the clinical severity of the disease (as measured by PASI). Specifically, patients with higher levels of emotional dysregulation seems to exhibit greater psychological distress, worse illness perception, and more symptom-related distress, especially at the early stages of treatment. These factors, in fact, seems to contribute to the reduced QoL observed in these patients.

It is important to note some issues limiting the generalizability of our findings. The study sample may not permit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of patients who suffer from psoriasis, candidate for systemic treatments. Larger sample sizes are needed to corroborate the observed associations between emotional dysregulation and clinical/psychological outcomes. Another limitation is that several key variables, such as psychological distress, illness perception, and QoL, were measured using only self-reported questionnaires. Self-reported data are subject to biases such as social desirability and recall bias, which may affect the accuracy of the results. Moreover, potential confounding variables, such as undeclared comorbid mental health conditions and medication adherence, were not controlled in the analysis. These factors could have influenced the relationship between emotional dysregulation and patient outcomes.

While the study included a follow-up period of up to 52 weeks, this may still be relatively too short to fully capture the long-term effects of emotional dysregulation on quality of life and psychological distress. Psoriasis is a chronic condition, and the emotional burden may fluctuate over a longer period.

The study design was observational, making it difficult to establish causal relationships between emotional dysregulation and changes in clinical or psychological outcomes. Randomized controlled trials are necessary to confirm whether emotional dysregulation directly impacts these variables. Finally, the study primarily focused on emotional dysregulation, without considering other emotional or psychological factors, such as coping mechanisms, which could also play significant roles in the observed outcomes. Participants self-reported the absence of any prior diagnosis of psychopathology, current psychotherapeutic treatment, and psychoactive medication use via the questionnaire. Notably, no preliminary psychiatric screening interviews were conducted to corroborate these self-reported declarations. Further study may consider a broader psychological assessment to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the psychosocial aspects of psoriasis.

Conclusions

The significant differences in PRO measures like the DLQI and psychosocial distress scores of the Skindex-17 suggest that emotional regulation strategies should be integrated into the management of psoriasis to address problems in emotional regulation, which could improve patients’ perceptions of their disease, adherence to treatment, and overall well-being. Furthermore, the consistent psychological improvements seen in both groups highlight the effectiveness of risankizumab in managing not only the physical symptoms but also the broader psychosocial impacts of psoriasis. However, more targeted psychological interventions may be necessary for patients who exhibit higher levels of emotional dysregulation to ensure comprehensive care and better long-term outcomes. Overall, the findings emphasize the importance of addressing emotional health in patients who are undergoing psoriasis treatment, as improving emotional regulation may not only enhance mental health but also positively influence patients’ perception of their disease and treatment outcomes. The integration of psychological support, particularly concerning emotional regulation strategies, could optimize treatment outcomes and improve the overall QoL of patients with psoriasis.
