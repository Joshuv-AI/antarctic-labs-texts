The cost-effectiveness of motivational interviewing to activate pain support in veterans seeking compensation for musculoskeletal conditions: multisite randomised clinical trial
Authors: Paul G Barnett, Kathryn Gilstad-Hayden, Christina M Lazar, Steve Martino, Marc I Rosen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe evaluated the cost and cost-effectiveness of brief motivational interviews conducted to facilitate pain and substance use treatment.DesignThis two-arm parallel group 36-week multi-site randomised pragmatic clinical trial compared the motivational interviewing for pain activation (MAPS) intervention to usual care. Cost was assessed from administrative databases and participant questionnaires. Activity limitations and quality of life were also assessed. Assessment staff were blinded to treatment group assignment.SettingEight medical centres of the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) in the six states in the New England region of the US.ParticipantsVeterans with a New England address who had filed a claim for a service-related musculoskeletal injury reported at least moderately severe pain and could be reached by telephone were invited to participate but were excluded if they were enrolled in another trial, had received more than two types of VA pain services in the prior 12 weeks or were not available for follow-up. There were 1101 participants. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated in 945 trial participants (85.8%) with at least one follow-up assessment.InterventionA 1-hour long telephone-delivered motivational interview designed to engage participants in treatment was followed by up to four 20-min follow-up sessions.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were change in pain intensity and substance use. This paper reports findings from the secondary outcomes of cost-effectiveness and quality of life for the intervention relative to usual care.ResultsThe intervention cost $300 per participant, resulted in significantly greater use of physical therapy, spinal manipulation, acupuncture and other outpatient visits, and increased the cost for the care of musculoskeletal conditions by $US1,508 (p<0.001). From the societal perspective, MAPS had no significant effect on total costs (point estimate of $US1,258 higher, p=0.52). Formal healthcare system costs accounted for only 5.8% of societal costs. Other societal costs included the value of patient time (12.8%) and lost productivity (81.4%). In the short-term, the intervention was not cost-effective over the range of credible critical values of willingness to pay for a quality-adjusted life year.ConclusionsThe intervention increased use of care for musculoskeletal services, increasing cost from the perspective of the healthcare system. There was no significant difference in cost from the societal perspective. It resulted in a slight reduction in pain but had no significant effect on preference-rated quality of life. MAPS was not cost-effective over the short 36-week time horizon of the trial, but its long-term cost-effectiveness is unknown.Trial registration numberNCT04062214.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Motivational interviewing, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Health care costs, Productivity costs, Quality of life, Veterans

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a leading cause of pain and disability. Most US adults report having felt pain in the previous 3 months, mostly from MSDs.1 MSDs are the leading cause of years of life spent with disability in the world population.2 Non-pharmacologic treatments are effective but are underused.3

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a patient-centred approach to communication originally developed for the treatment of alcohol use disorders.4 It has been applied to other substance use disorders, physical activity, care for chronic illness, pain and musculoskeletal conditions.5 Randomised trials have found short-term benefit from MI and other patient-centred communication interventions for the treatment of MSDs 5–8 and chronic pain,9 with limitations from small sample size and weaknesses in trial design.5 9 10

Evidence for the cost-effectiveness of MI for musculoskeletal conditions is more limited. MI was found cost-effective for workers on sick leave because of musculoskeletal conditions,11 12 but an intervention to improve activity and diet in patients with chronic disease was not cost-effective.13 Behavioural interventions other than MI, including advice and education, have been found to be cost-effective additions to the treatment of low-back pain,14–17 with exceptions.18 19

Motivational interviewing to activate pain support (MAPS) is a telephone-delivered MI intervention developed to improve engagement in pain treatment while reducing alcohol use. It was tested in veterans with MSDs as one of the pragmatic trials of the Pain Management Collaboratory group.20 Primary outcomes were change in pain intensity and substance use.21 After 36 weeks of follow-up, participants randomised to MAPS had 54% more outpatient encounters for musculoskeletal conditions and an average of 0.25 greater reduction in the pain severity subscale of the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI). This greater reduction in pain was statistically significant but was less than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) adopted by the trial, a 15% reduction in BPI severity. There was no significant difference between treatment groups in substance use outcomes.

We now consider the secondary outcomes of the trial: cost and cost-effectiveness. We hypothesised that MAPS was a cost-effective use of healthcare resources, delivering sufficient value to justify its cost. We give special attention to costs incurred by patients and the effect of care on productivity within and outside of the workplace. Guidelines recommend that cost-effectiveness analysis from the societal perspective include these costs,22 23 but they have been ignored by most economic studies.23–26 This is especially problematic in economic evaluations of care for musculoskeletal disease. Chronic musculoskeletal pain has been identified as the condition in which productivity losses account for the greatest proportion of societal costs.23

Methods

Trial procedures

This two-arm parallel group 36-week multi-site randomised pragmatic clinical trial compared MAPS, a telephone-delivered motivational interview intervention, to treatment as usual (TAU). Sites included eight medical centres in the six states in the New England region of the US. Veterans who served in the US military after 11 September 2001 with a New England address who had filed a claim for a service-related back, neck, knee or shoulder condition with the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) were invited to participate and included if they had access to a phone and had at least moderately severe pain as assessed by the BPI. The trial excluded those who were unable to participate in follow-up and those who were enrolled in another pain trial and those who had used more than two types of VA pain services in the prior 12 weeks.

The trial enrolled 1101 participants between 23 October 2019 and 22 June 2023. Randomisation to treatment groups was balanced within study sites by urn randomisation27 based on sex, race (white, black, other), ethnicity (Hispanic or not Hispanic) and use of illicit drugs. Participants were assessed at baseline and 12 and 36 weeks after baseline by research assistants blinded to group assignment.

The trial protocol was approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board and VA Central Institutional Review Board and pre-registered on 13 August 2019 with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04062214) https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04062214 where the study protocol may be found, including the plan for economic analysis. Trial procedures were approved and periodically reviewed by a Data Safety and Monitoring Board convened by the National Centre for Complementary and Integrated Health. The follow-up of the last patient concluded 4 March 2024. Other study procedures required by research checklists, including protocol revisions, patient and public involvement in study design, calculation of sample size, randomisation and blinding, safety and data monitoring, reported adverse events and plan for data sharing, are found in the supplement to this paper online supplemental additional file 1. The study protocol registered with ClinicalTrials.gov is found as online supplemental additional material (SBIRT study protocol version 3 (Prot_SAP_002).pdf). A description of the protocol was published.28

Patient and public involvement in study. Study design and results were presented to the May/June 2019 meeting of the Veterans Engagement Group of the Pain Research, Informatics, Multi-morbidities, and Education Centre. This group made recommendations on how to engage veterans and providers in the intervention and ways to disseminate results. The study design and results were also presented to the Veteran Engagement Panel of the VA Pain/Opioid Consortium. That panel provided feedback about study result interpretation and impact for the VA and veterans receiving care.

Overview of economic methods

We follow recommendations to conduct cost-effectiveness analysis from both the health system perspective and from the societal perspective.29 From the perspective of the health system, cost includes the intervention and services sponsored by VA. The societal perspective adds the cost of health services received outside of VA and costs incurred by participants, including the value of time spent seeking health services and obtaining self-directed care, and the value of the effect of disability on productivity within and outside of employment. Health system costs were limited to care for MSDs. Benefits were assessed as preference-rated quality of life. All costs were adjusted to $US2024 using the urban Consumer Price Index for all items.30 Discounting was not applied because of the short 36-week time horizon. Methodologic details are found in the supplement.

Intervention

MAPS is an MI-based individual intervention delivered by telephone. The intervention was provided by counsellors with master’s degrees who were trained by a psychologist (SM) member of the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers. Training included mock sessions with actors portraying study participants.

Counsellors delivered an initial 1-hour motivational interview to engage each participant in treatment for pain and substance use. In this first session, counsellors asked participants about their compensation claim and prior pain treatments, nurtured motivation and provided information about available pain care. Participants who screened positive for substance misuse received additional MI addressing this condition. The initial session was followed by up to three 20-min sessions during the first 12 weeks and a final 20-min booster session up to 32 weeks following randomisation. These subsequent sessions addressed participants’ motivation for pain and addiction care. Counsellors alerted primary care providers of participants’ interest in pain and addiction services via the VA electronic health record. Counsellors received ongoing supervision, including review of recorded sessions, with monthly feedback and coaching. Adherence and competence were verified by independent assessment using the Independent Tape Rater Scale applied to five skill areas.21 31 Integrity was good with similar performance across counsellors.

The cost of the intervention was estimated with micro-costing methods. Counsellors recorded the time spent on behalf of each participant on scheduling and preparing for calls, telephone interaction with the participant and follow-up activities, including documentation of interaction in medical records. Time spent by staff in training and supervision was also logged. The cost to train and supervise counsellors was distributed in proportion to the time counsellors spent for each participant randomised to MAPS.

Screening would be required to realise the same effectiveness in clinical practice: identification of participants who were available, willing to participate, with sufficient current pain, but currently not using more than two modes of pain treatment. The cost of screening participants assigned to TAU was considered a research expense and was excluded.

Cost was based on the direct labour cost, including benefits. Overhead cost (space, utilities, telephone, information technology and administrative support) was set at 33% of direct labour expense. This was the median value of the overhead rate in five cost studies that estimated overhead as a percentage of labour cost, including fringe benefits (cited in the supplement), and the rate used for a comparable telephone counselling intervention.32 Overhead cost was included because it is part of the opportunity cost, the marginal cost of the intervention in the long run.22

VA provided health services

VA-provided care was from the managerial cost accounting (MCA) system, an activity-based allocation system used by all VA health facilities.33 Community care sponsored by VA was from paid claims in the Integrated Veteran Care Consolidated Data Sets. Care for MSD was identified by diagnosis code. The cost of inpatient care included professional services rendered during the stay. Outpatient encounters were defined as all services provided in a single day at a location and exclude claims for laboratory tests and medical supplies. Prescription medications dispensed outside the hospital were identified in the MCA pharmacy extract. We used medication class and active ingredients to classify pain-specific medications into mutually exclusive categories.

Value of participant time

We used guideline-recommended methods to estimate the value of participant time.22 These specify different valuations of time while employed and time outside employment. The value of time during employment was estimated as the marginal pre-tax wage rate plus benefits. We used the national average wage paid by private US employers in 202434 plus 46.2% benefit cost, resulting in a rate of $51.27 /hour. The value of time spent outside of employment was estimated as the post-tax compensation rate, the value of employed time less the average tax rate of 9.2%.22 This was $48.05 /hour.

Time seeking care

Since the goal of MAPS was to engage participants in additional care, it was important to include the value of time spent obtaining care to account for costs from the societal perspective. Adult Americans spend an average of 121 min to receive an outpatient visit, including time spent on travel, waiting and receiving the care.35 This cost was 121 min of time for each outpatient encounter valued at the rate for time outside of employment.

Self-reported pain services

Participants were asked to report on the quantity of 25 types of treatment for musculoskeletal pain obtained outside of VA or obtained at home. The cost of pain treatment services provided outside of VA was the product of the quantity of service reported and a service-specific unit cost. Depending on the service, the unit cost was estimated based on trial cost data, the VA fee schedule, Medicare reimbursement rates or the peer-reviewed literature, as documented in the supplement. Time on self-directed therapy was assigned the hourly rate for time outside of employment. The duration of each self-directed service was from estimates in the peer-reviewed literature, also documented in the supplement.

Value of losses due to disability

If successful, MAPS might increase productivity in the workplace or at home. Measurement of preference-based quality of life is unlikely to capture this effect. The recommended practice is to include productivity losses as a cost.36

Participants completed the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire (WPAIQ). This instrument determines time missed from work (absenteeism), impairment of work (presenteeism) and impairment of regular activities outside of work.37 WPAIQ asks about the effect of disability on productivity while employed on a scale of zero to ten, usually interpreted as percentage impairment in 10% intervals. Losses due to presenteeism were estimated as the time employed multiplied by this percentage disability and valued using the hourly rate for time while employed.

WPAIQ also asks about the effect of disability on normal activities on a scale of zero to ten. These percentages were applied to the estimated time spent on unpaid household and caregiving work given the employment status, gender and age of each participant, using national means from the American Time Use Survey.38 39 Losses were estimated using the hourly rate for time outside employment. The value of productivity losses at baseline and 12 and 36 weeks was combined into a time-weighted average via straight-line interpolation.

Quality of life

Quality of life was assessed with the five-dimension, five-level, European Quality of Life instrument (EQ-5D-5L),40 a preference-based measure that was scored using US norms.41 Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were estimated as the time-weighted average of the values assessed at baseline and 12 and 36 weeks via straight-line interpolation, the usual method used to summarise quality of life.42

Statistical methods

Unadjusted differences between MAPS and TAU were based on regressions that included the indicator of the treatment group as the sole covariate. Adjusted estimates also controlled for baseline EQ-5D-5L, length of follow-up and urn randomisation variables: study site, sex, race, ethnicity and use of illicit drugs. Inclusion of baseline quality of life as an independent variable avoids biases that come from estimating change from baseline or from ignoring baseline values altogether.42–44 Length of follow-up was included because follow-up intervals did not exactly conform to the dates specified in the protocol, creating differences in the time in which health services were received and quality adjusted life years were accumulated. Inclusion of stratification variables as covariates is the best practice for statistical analysis of clinical trials.45 46 All regressions were estimated with robust standard errors to account for potential heteroscedasticity.47

We used a complete case analysis, removing participants who did not have values for all analytic variables. We thus excluded participants who did not complete even one post-randomisation assessment. In randomised clinical trials with missing outcomes, a complete case analysis that controls for baseline covariates can yield estimates of treatment effect that are as unbiased but more efficient than multiple imputation.48

Generalised linear regression models were employed, as cost is not normally distributed and regression errors are frequently heteroscedastic.49 A Box-Cox regression identified the link function50 and a modified Park test to specify the distribution.51 Goodness of fit statistics were also evaluated, and the link function and distribution that provided the best goodness of fit statistics (lowest value of Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria) were specified.47 Utilisation regressions used negative binomial rather than Poisson regression. For all utilisation variables, the overdispersion parameter was significantly greater than zero, demonstrating that variance exceeded the mean and that the assumption of Poisson regression, that variance equals the mean, was not appropriate.

Employment and disability were assessed with the WPAIQ and quality of life with the EQ-5D-5L at baseline and at 12 weeks and 36 weeks after randomisation. Repeated measures regression was used to compare differences in responses by treatment group over time. Logistic regression was used for employment, negative binomial regression for hours missed from work and identity normal specification for continuous variables, the EQ-5D-5L and the severity of disability on a scale from zero to ten. Models accounted for the correlation of responses from the same participant and controlled for the baseline value of the measure and urn randomisation variables.

Assessment of cost-effectiveness

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was determined as the ratio of the difference in cost (adjusted mean cost with MAPS less adjusted mean cost with TAU) divided by the difference in outcomes (adjusted mean QALYs with MAPS less adjusted mean QALYs with TAU). Guidelines recommend a life-long time horizon.29 We considered cost-effectiveness only for the time horizon of the trial, as there was no information about cost or outcomes beyond the 36-week follow-up period.

We estimated the uncertainty of the ICER with net benefits regression.52 The net benefit is the monetary value of the QALYs enjoyed by the trial participant less the cost that they incurred. The decision-maker wants to know if the intervention is cost-effective, that is, if it yields a net monetary benefit. The intervention is cost-effective if the coefficient for the treatment group from the net benefit regression is positive. The uncertainty is tested by the one-sided statistical test that the coefficient is significantly greater than zero. Since there is no universally accepted monetary value of a QALY, a set of regressions was estimated using the range of plausible values for the decision-maker’s willingness to pay for a QALY. Net benefit regressions were adjusted with the same independent variables used to adjust treatment group effects in all other regressions.

Results from the net benefits regressions were plotted on a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC). The plot provides the probability that the intervention is cost-effective at different monetary values of a QALY.53 The decision-maker can read from the curve the probability that the intervention is cost-effective at any chosen threshold of willingness to pay per gain in QALY.

Results

Study recruitment and randomisation

The study contacted 13 816 potential participants and randomised 1101 (8.0%) (see study flow diagram, figure 1). Most potential participants declined to participate, could not be contacted, had left the area or were not available for follow-up. Clinical data were available on the results of screening the remaining 2242 potential participants. Among these, 54.4% met clinical criteria for eligibility, 37.9% did not have pain that was at least moderately severe, and 7.8% were already receiving three or more modes of pain treatment. Among those with data, the clinical criteria for study participation were met in slightly more than half of the target population.

Study flow diagram EQ5D: The EQ-5D-5L is the EuroQol health state questionnaire with 5 dimensions and five levels per dimension. MAPS, motivational interviewing to activate pain support; EQ-5D-5L, five-dimension, five-level, European Quality of Life instrument.

There were 558 randomised to MAPS and 543 to TAU. All enrolled participants completed baseline assessments; 80.7% completed the week 12 assessment, and 76.6% of participants completed the week 36 assessment.

Economic findings are based on 945 participants (85.8%) who completed at least one follow-up assessment (487 randomised to MAPS and 458 to TAU). In this cohort, 82.9% responded to both assessments, 11.1% responded exclusively to the 12-week assessment and 6.0% exclusively to the 36-week assessment. There were three deaths in the study, one participant who was randomised to MAPS and two randomised to TAU. We treated these deaths as loss to follow-up and conducted sensitivity analysis to consider the potential for their inclusion to affect cost-effectiveness findings.

Our approach to missing data was to employ a complete case analysis. We excluded the 156 (14.2%) randomised participants who did not complete even one follow-up visit. Most outcome data were absent for these excluded participants. The proportion of participants that were excluded did not differ by treatment group assignment (12.7% for MAPS and 15.7% for TAU, p=0.16). This suggests that missingness was not related to intent to treat. In addition, the baseline score for preference-rated quality of life, a measure of severity of illness, was not different in those excluded. Baseline scores were not significantly different between those who did and did not complete their 12-week follow-up visit (p=0.63) nor between those who did and did not complete their 36-week follow-up visit (p=0.96).

Baseline characteristics of participants (table 1) were similar for the two treatment groups. Because the trial recruited more recent veterans, participants were younger (mean age 39.7 years) and more likely to be female (14%) than is typical of veterans enrolled to use VA healthcare.54 The mean of pain severity and interference scores was moderate. The mean score in response to the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) represented moderate depression. The principal substances reported in response to the Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test screening test included tobacco, cannabis and alcohol; 3.5% of participants reported risky use for each of the other drugs, including opioids. At baseline, participants reported having used an average of 2.7 different non-pharmacological pain treatment modalities in the prior 3 months. About 9% had received substance use treatment, principally counselling and nicotine replacement therapy.

The screening of 13 816 potential participants required 809.2 hours of research assistant effort at a cost of $39 586, or $35.96 for each of 1101 participants. The cost of screening MAPS participants was included because screening would be needed to replicate the intervention in clinical practice. Screening TAU participants was regarded as a research cost and was therefore excluded. The costs of training (including practice cases) added $18.30 to the mean cost of MAPS; supervision to ensure the fidelity of MAPS added $50.73.

Effect of MAPS on employment and disability

Employment and disability were assessed with the WPAIQ. Differences between MAPS and TAU at 12-week and 36-week assessments were estimated with repeated measures regressions. At baseline, 73.1% of participants were employed. There were no significant differences between treatment groups at either assessment and no significant changes relative to baseline. At baseline, participants reported missing 1.6 hours of employment in the prior week due to health problems. Again, there were no significant differences between treatment groups at either follow-up time and no significant changes in hours of lost work from baseline.

Respondents reported the extent to which health problems affected paid employment on a scale from 0 to 10. The baseline mean was 4.48 (or 44.8% disabled during working hours). After 36 weeks of follow-up, the MAPS treatment group reported a mean value of 3.94 (or 39.4% disabled). This value was 0.44 less disabled than reported by TAU participants (p=0.022, 95% CI −0.82 to −0.06). In other words, after 36 weeks, the MAPS participants had an absolute value of disability that was 4.4% less than TAU participants. The adjusted estimate at 36 weeks for MAPS participants was 0.53 lower than their baseline value (p<0.002, 95% CI −0.824 to −0.252). Over the course of the trial, the effect of this reduced disability was mitigated by variation in hours worked, and there was no significant difference between treatment groups in the effect of disability on working hours (cost of presenteeism, table 2).

The extent to which health problems affected normal activities outside of work was also reported on a scale from 0 to 10. The mean disability at baseline was 5.62 (or 56.2% disability in normal activities). Both treatment groups reported significant decreases at both follow-up assessments. Treatment groups did not differ at 12 weeks. At the 36-week follow-up, the adjusted mean disability with MAPS was 4.81, significantly less than the mean of 5.25 reported with TAU, an adjusted difference of 0.34 less disability with MAPS (p=0.005, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.13). Although fewer health problems were reported at 36 weeks, there were no significant differences between groups in the value of productivity from unpaid work over the course of the trial (cost of unpaid work, table 2).

Effect of MAPS on quality of life

Cost-effectiveness analysis assesses the benefits of an intervention in terms of morbidity-adjusted survival, expressed as QALYs. The adjustment of years of survival for quality of life is made with a preference-rated scale anchored at values of zero (for death) and one (for perfect health).36

Quality of life was assessed with the EQ-5D-5L. The differences between MAPS and TAU at the 12-week and 36-week assessments were estimated with repeated measures regression. At baseline, the mean response to the EQ-5D-5L was 0.547 (SD 0.254). Preference-rated quality of life increased slightly over the course of the trial. EQ-5D-5L scores at week 12 were 0.048 higher than at baseline (p<0.001). At week 36, they were 0.053 higher than baseline (p<0.001). There were no significant differences between treatment groups at follow-up; the score of MAPS participants was 0.024 lower at week 12 (p=0.07) and 0.002 lower at week 36 (p=0.90).

Linear interpolation was used to estimate the QALYs enjoyed by the economic cohort (n=945). The incremental effect of MAPS on QALYs was estimated using ordinary least squares regression with QALY as the dependent variable, with explanatory variables treatment group, baseline quality of life, length of follow-up, and urn randomisation variables. Baseline EQ-5D-5L was included as a covariate because of bias that can result from its exclusion.42

The adjusted mean of QALY by treatment group was 0.401 for TAU and 0.392 for MAPS. Participants randomised to MAPS realised 0.009 fewer QALYs during the trial, a difference that was not statistically significant (p=0.188, 95% CI −0.022 to +0.004) (table 4). Parameters for covariates were consistent with expectations. The regression coefficient for baseline EQ-5D-5L was positive and significant (p<0.001). Participants with the highest quality of life at baseline enjoyed the highest cumulative quality of life during the trial. The parameter for weeks of follow-up was also positive and significant (p<0.001). Participants with longer follow-up had greater time to accumulate QALYs.

MAPS incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

A point estimate for the ICER was found by dividing the incremental effect of MAPS on cost by its incremental effectiveness (in QALYs). These incremental effects were based on the adjusted difference in means of cost and adjusted difference in QALYs reported above. The point estimate for the ICER was -$210,466/QALY from the VA perspective and -$144,731 from the societal perspective (table 5). A negative ICER can result when there is a negative difference in cost divided by a positive difference in QALYs. Decision-makers will consider this cost-effective, approving any intervention that reduces costs and improves outcomes. A negative ICER can also occur if there is a positive difference in cost divided by a negative difference in QALYs. The decision-maker will not regard this as cost-effective, as costs increased and outcomes worsened. This was the situation that was observed for MAPS.

Sensitivity analyses

The cost of counsellor training was distributed to MAPS participants with the assumption that no additional patients would benefit. Had trained counsellors conducted MI with additional patients the per-patient cost of training would be reduced. As training costs were less than $20 per participant, this would result in only a slight reduction in the cost of the intervention.

We tested the sensitivity of findings to regression adjustment. Comparison of unadjusted to adjusted differences reported in tables 2 and 3 generally shows that estimated differences between treatment groups were larger with adjustment. Adjustment did not change which cost variables had a significant difference between groups. Adjustment resulted in the treatment groups' differences in four utilisation variables changing from not significant to significant with p<0.05 (VA provided surgery, imaging and spinal manipulation and other self-directed complementary and alternative care).

We considered how inclusion of participants who died during the trial would have affected estimates of QALY. Two deaths (one MAPS and one TAU) occurred before the 12 week follow-up, and one death (TAU) occurred before the 36 week follow-up. The sensitivity analysis considered the effect of assuming a zero value for utility on the date of death and thereafter, up to week 36. Including these values reduced the mean QALY of each treatment group by 0.0008 or by 0.002%. The difference in the mean QALYs by treatment group is unaffected. The short follow-up period attenuated the impact of deaths on the estimated QALY.

We modelled an alternate estimate of the ICER based on the small but statistically significant 0.25 unit (95% CI −0.44 to −0.06) greater reduction in pain severity on the BPI from MAPS compared with TAU participants.21 Among those with severe pain, a one-unit decrease in BPI is associated with as much as a 0.1 increase in utility (preference-rated quality of life).55 56 Thus, an estimate of QALYs based on the BPI scores in people with severe pain would suggest that MAPS increased utility by 0.025 (0.25 units times 0.1 utilities per unit of BPI). The trial lasted 36 weeks, or 69% of a year, so this represents a gain of 0.017 QALYs (69% of 0.025). From the perspective of the health system, the extra cost of MAPS ($1,868) divided by this gain represents an ICER of $108 000/QALY ($1,868/0.017 QALYs). For the perspective of society, the extra cost of MAPS represents an ICER of $74 220 ($1,285/0.017 QALYs). This alternate estimate suggests that MAPS approached the threshold of being cost-effective. Because the cited studies did not provide a complete description of the relationship between BPI and preference-rated utility, strong assumptions were needed for this sensitivity analysis. The true ICER based on BPI responses is unknown.

Discussion

MI was tested as a method for engaging patients with MSD in pain treatment. MAPS was not cost-effective within the 36 weeks of the trial follow-up period. There is insufficient information to know if it might be cost-effective over the long term. Previous trials have found that MI13 and other behavioural interventions for MSD18 19 were not cost-effective, but other trials have found MI11 12 and other interventions were cost-effective.14–17 This trial is distinct from these previous efforts because of its much larger number of trial participants, the focus of its intervention on improving engagement in care and its comprehensive measurement of costs from the societal perspective.

Cost and utilisation

Although the $300 cost of the MAPS intervention was modest, the motivational interviews resulted in significantly greater use of physical therapy, spinal manipulation, acupuncture and other outpatient visits for musculoskeletal care, resulting in $1,508 additional cost of care for MSDs (p<0.001).

Cost from societal perspective

The economic evaluation of this trial differed from previous studies because of the more comprehensive measurement of costs from the societal perspective, the approach recommended by US and most other guidelines. We included the value of participant time and the effect of disability on productivity both within and outside of the workplace. These costs are excluded from most economic evaluations.

Many interventions require patients to undertake self-directed care or to travel and receive services from a provider. The cost of the time involved is usually neglected with the potential to bias cost effectiveness analysis in favour of interventions that impose additional burdens on patients.57 Only 10% of cost-effectiveness studies have examined productivity loss.23 Most studies that have included workplace productivity only considered absence from work and ignored lowered productivity while at work (presenteeism).23 24 58 59 Unpaid labour (productivity within the household) has been rarely considered, probably in fewer than 1% of cost-effectiveness analyses.23 26

The heterogeneity in methods reflects variation in cost-effectiveness guidelines over time and between locales. Measurement of complete societal costs was not included in US guidelines until 2016.22 Only half of the guidelines of other countries recommend the inclusion of productivity losses23 and a minority of guidelines recommend inclusion of the costs of unpaid labour.

Cost documented in the MAPS trial show that costs outside the formal healthcare system are large and thus have the potential to influence cost-effectiveness findings. Just 5.8% of societal costs in this trial were incurred in the formal healthcare system. Other costs included: the value of time seeking formal care (2.1%), the time engaging in self-directed services (10.7%), workplace absenteeism (9.7%) and presenteeism (30.5%) and unpaid labour (41.1%). These proportions are consistent with studies that have considered these costs. When they are measured, the cost of diminished workplace productivity in general11 60 61 and absenteeism in particular usually exceed health system cost.19 62–67 Studies that consider workplace productivity losses usually exclude presenteeism, but it can be more costly than absenteeism, often by a factor of two or more.68–71 In the MAPS trial, the cost of presenteeism was three times that of absenteeism. A few studies have found the cost of presenteeism to be equal to that of absenteeism72 or even less.73

The effect of disability on productivity outside the workplace—household work and caregiving—is even more rarely considered. Losses in unpaid labour can exceed losses from paid work73 74 but may be less.12 75 76 In the MAPS trial, we found losses from unpaid work were slightly less than those from paid work.

The diversity of approaches used in cost-effectiveness analysis threatens the consistency of economic analysis. Reviews of cost-effectiveness analyses from the societal perspective found that exclusion of productivity costs can affect qualitative24 and quantitative58 findings. This was not the case in the MAPS trial. Excluding productivity losses and the value of patient time did not affect the conclusion that the MAPS intervention was not cost-effective over the 36 weeks of the trial. Nevertheless, 94% of the costs from the societal perspective were incurred outside of the healthcare system, suggesting the importance of patient time and of losses in productivity in conducting cost-effectiveness analysis.

Quality of life

The baseline response to the EQ-5D-5L was 0.547. This is much worse than the quality of life in the general population. A random sample of the US population found that 75% had an EQ-5D-5L score of at least 0.80.77

There was no significant effect of MAPS on quality of life, as assessed by the EQ-5D-5L. The trial assessed patient pain with the BPI and found that reduction in pain severity was significantly greater among participants randomised to MAPS. This reduction in pain was less than the MCID set in the trial protocol. The small reduction in pain was not captured in the responses to the EQ-5D-5L. This may reflect a lack of response of the EQ-5D-5L to the modest improvements in chronic musculoskeletal pain, rather than a lack of significant clinical benefit. This quality-of-life instrument has been found less responsive than instruments designed to measure severity of back pain56 and has been unable to differentiate minimally clinically important changes in shoulder pain.78 The EQ-5D-5L is less responsive than instruments specifically designed for pain measurement because it has more limited gradations in severity and it is designed to measure dimensions of quality of life other than pain.56 The greater variance in preference ratings reflects individual differences other than the state of health. We employed the EQ-5D-5L to assess quality of life because this pragmatic trial had two primary outcomes: pain and substance abuse. A pain-related instrument alone would not have fully captured preference-rated outcomes. However, for studies in which pain is the sole primary outcome, knowledge of the relationship between preference-rated quality of life and responses to the BPI could allow BPI responses to be the basis of cost effectiveness analysis. So far, only selected parameters relating BPI to quality of life have been reported.55 56 In a sensitivity analysis, we used those parameters and BPI values from the trial to estimate the incremental effects of MAPS on quality of life and the resulting cost-effectiveness. The resulting ICER was more favourable, but the analysis did not resolve whether MAPS was a cost-effective use of healthcare resources.

Limitations

The MAPS trial focused on post-9/11 veterans who had at least moderately severe pain and were receiving two or fewer modes of pain treatment. Participants may not be typical of people with MSD. Even so, trial results may be generalisable beyond the select group studied. MSD are a prevalent problem in primary care and there is inconsistent application of evidence-based treatment.3 Because of its focus on younger patients with pain from MSD incurred in military service that is undertreated, MAPS may be generalisable to a much larger group that might benefit from MI—non-veterans with moderately severe pain from occupationally related MSD that is undertreated.

Healthcare costs were limited to care for the treatment for MSD. Information on self-directed care and care received outside of the VA could be feasibly obtained only by self-report. Since MAPS was directed at engagement in MSD care, it was decided to limit the burden of self-report to services for MSD, and to limit measurement of VA costs to MSD care to be consistent.

It is recommended that cost-effectiveness analysis employ a life-time horizon.79 This cost-effectiveness analysis is limited to the 36-week follow-up period of the trial. Effects of the intervention on costs and benefits over the long term are unknown. Some effects are likely to have been realised after the end of follow-up: the full effect of additional treatment on pain and the effect of additional care resulting from relationships with new providers resulting from study referrals. It should be acknowledged that the effects of motivational interviews decrease with time.80 We did not use multiple imputation but relied on a complete case analysis, with the possibility that results may be sensitive to our treatment of missing data.

Quantity of non-VA services and self-directed care were assessed at two points in time. Employment, disability and quality of life were assessed at three points. Infrequent self-reports are inherently subject to recall bias which can be addressed by greater frequency of assessment, participant diary or other methods.81 Recall bias could also affect the accuracy of self-reported employment, disability and quality of life. More frequent assessment, direct observation, employer data and other possible reporting enhancements were infeasible in this pragmatic trial.

Guidelines do not specify how losses related to unpaid labour should be determined.26 The MAPS trial employed WPAIQ, the instrument most frequently used to measure productivity loss in economic evaluations.82 83 The WPAIQ characterises the degree of disability outside of the workplace but does not ask about hours of unpaid work lost to disability. Other studies have described the extent of non-workplace disability measured by WPAIQ without estimating its monetary value.84 85 We estimated its monetary value by multiplying the degree of disability by the national mean hours of unpaid work for the participant’s employment status, gender and age reported in the American Time Use Survey, and using national wage rates as the value of patient time. We acknowledge that this is a novel approach and that better methods have become available. More recently developed instruments have been designed to measure this loss directly.86 87

Strong assumptions were made to value participant time and lost productivity. We relied on the value of patient time established by US cost-effectiveness guidelines.22 We assumed that 10-level responses to WPAIQ items be interpreted as 10% increments in level of disability, an assumption made by many studies. We assumed participants spent the national average time obtaining formal outpatient care. For each type of self-directed care, we assumed participants used the average time spent on each reported use. Each of these assumptions has the potential to bias estimates. These assumptions were applied to all trial participants, regardless of treatment group assignment. Alternate assumptions would multiply participant-specific responses by a different set of constants. This is unlikely to affect the finding that treatment groups did not differ in the cost of patient-directed treatment or the value of lost productivity.

Our estimate of societal costs is based on self-report. Limitations to our method are acknowledged, but we did not want to ignore societal costs altogether. An increasing number of guidelines (and now the majority) recommend that costs outside formal healthcare system be included in cost-effectiveness analysis.23 As yet, only a minority of guidelines recommend that the value of unpaid labour also be included. Its exclusion has the potential to bias results against those who have no formal employment, including people with disabilities from MSD.

Conclusions

MAPS was a low-cost intervention that increased use of care for musculoskeletal conditions. From the perspective of the healthcare system, it increased costs. From the societal perspective, it resulted in no additional cost. Patient-incurred cost and lost productivity accounted for 94% of costs from the societal perspective, suggesting that choice of analytic perspective may have a strong influence on the cost-effectiveness analysis of care for musculoskeletal conditions.

The MAPS intervention resulted in a slight reduction in pain but there was no significant improvement in preference-rated quality of life, as measured by the EQ-5D-5L. MAPS was not cost-effective over the short 36-week time horizon of the trial, but its long-term cost-effectiveness is unknown.
