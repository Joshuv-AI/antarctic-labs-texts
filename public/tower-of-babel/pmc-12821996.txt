Effects of interprofessional education on dental hygiene students: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Eun-Mi Choi, Su-Kyung Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterprofessional education has become increasingly important in preparing dental hygiene students for collaborative healthcare practice. However, despite its growing implementation, comprehensive evidence of the effectiveness of interprofessional education for dental hygiene students remains limited. Therefore, this study conducted a systematic review and synthesized evidence on the educational effects of interprofessional education for dental hygiene students, including impacts on their competencies, attitudes, readiness, knowledge, and satisfaction.MethodsA systematic review was conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Five electronic databases (MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, PubMed, and Scopus) were searched from inception through April 2025 without language restrictions. Studies involving dental hygiene students participating in interprofessional education with measurable outcomes were included. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed their quality using appropriate tools. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251040678).ResultsAmong 888 initially retrieved records, 33 studies published between 1997 and 2025 met the inclusion criteria. Most included studies demonstrated statistically significant effectiveness of interprofessional education. The Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale was the most frequently utilized assessment instrument. A meta-analysis of measurements with this scale (n = 275) revealed significant improvement in readiness for interprofessional learning. In contrast, an analysis of measurements using the Interprofessional Socialization and Valuing Scale (n = 104) showed no significant changes in interprofessional socialization. However, the overall evidence should be interpreted cautiously due to the predominance of non-randomized study designs and substantial risk of bias across included studies.ConclusionsThis systematic review indicates that interprofessional education has the potential to be effective for dental hygiene students. However, the quality of evidence is limited by methodological constraints. These findings support integrating interprofessional education into dental hygiene curricula to enhance preparation for collaborative healthcare practice.

Background

The transition toward patient-centered, team-based healthcare delivery has created new collaborative care models that require dental hygienists to assume expanded clinical responsibilities and interprofessional roles. The legal and professional scope of practice for dental hygienists includes preventive oral healthcare, patient education, and periodontal therapy, and their job definitions continue to evolve according to national regulations. These expanded responsibilities underscore the need for additional competencies to ensure effective interprofessional collaboration. Given their roles as providers of preventive oral healthcare and patient education, dental hygienists are strategically positioned to serve as integral members of interprofessional teams. Accordingly, interprofessional education (IPE) is essential for equipping dental hygiene students with the collaborative skills necessary to integrate oral health into overall health promotion and to improve patient outcomes across diverse healthcare settings [1, 2]. This broadened scope of practice requires enhanced educational preparation, particularly interprofessional education (IPE), to develop the collaborative competencies necessary for effective team-based care. The World Health Organization’s Framework for Action on Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice emphasizes that effective healthcare delivery requires professionals who can work collaboratively across disciplinary boundaries to improve patient outcomes [3]. The theoretical foundation of IPE draws on multiple frameworks, such as the Canadian Interprofessional Health Collaborative (CIHC) framework and the World Health Organization’s recommendations, in addition to the Interprofessional Education Collaborative (IPEC) Core Competencies. Among these, the IPEC framework, updated in 2023, was selected in this review due to its global adoption, validation across multiple health professions, and comprehensive coverage of four essential domains: values and ethics for interprofessional practice, roles and responsibilities for collaborative practice, interprofessional communication practices, and interprofessional teamwork and team-based practices [4]. The growing recognition of oral-systemic health connections necessitates that dental hygienists understand the roles and responsibilities of other healthcare professionals in managing conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease (e.g., hypertension, heart failure), and respiratory disorders (e.g., COPD, pneumonia). Evidence highlights the bidirectional links between these systemic diseases and oral health, underscoring the contribution of dental hygienists within interprofessional teams for chronic disease management.

Current dental hygiene education must prepare students to work effectively in interprofessional healthcare teams. Accreditation standards consistently emphasize interprofessional competence. For example, Standard 2–15 of the Commission on Dental Accreditation (CODA, United States) requires that ‘graduates must be competent in interprofessional communication, collaboration and interaction with other members of the health care team to support comprehensive patient care.’ Similarly, the Canadian accreditation standards, the Australian Dental Council, and European education frameworks also highlight interprofessional communication and teamwork as core requirements, underscoring the global importance of these competencies [5–8]. This competency encompasses the expanded roles of dental hygienists, including increased autonomy in patient care, chronic disease management, prescriptive authorities, and collaboration across multiple practice settings. Dental hygienists’ legal and professional scope of practice encompasses preventive oral healthcare, patient education, and periodontal therapy, while their responsibilities continue to evolve across jurisdictions. These expanded responsibilities highlight the need for interprofessional collaboration competencies [9]. For example, collaboration between dental hygienists and physicians in diabetic care has been associated with reductions in HbA1c levels [10], while periodontal therapy has been linked to improved management of cardiovascular and respiratory conditions [11]. These findings require dental hygienists to possess interprofessional competencies for effective communication and coordination with medical professionals.

Previous systematic reviews have demonstrated the positive effects of IPE on healthcare students, including improvements in collaborative attitudes, interprofessional knowledge, and teamwork skills. Nursing and pharmacy education studies have reported gains in teamwork attitudes, clinical communication, and patient care outcomes. Moreover, recent domestic and international studies have reported the implementation of IPE in dental hygiene curricula, and our findings are compared with these studies to highlight both common outcomes and unique contributions of dental hygiene education [12, 13]. Despite the growing implementation of IPE in dental hygiene curricula, few studies have synthesized evidence regarding the effects of IPE on dental hygiene students. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively evaluate the effects of IPE on learning among dental hygiene students’ collaborative competencies, attitudes, and practice readiness.

Methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14] and was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251040678).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were studies that (1) involved dental hygiene students or undergraduate healthcare students with IPE experience; (2) implemented educational, teaching, or learning strategies incorporating collaboration with students from two or more healthcare professions; (3) reported measurable pre–post outcomes related to students’ competencies, attitudes, readiness, knowledge, or satisfaction; and (4) employed quantitative or mixed-methods study designs.

The exclusion criteria were (1) studies that involved only dental hygiene students without interprofessional components; (2) studies that did not include student populations; (3) conference abstracts, letters, editorials, protocols, commentaries, or systematic reviews; and (4) articles that examined multiple professions but did not report specific outcomes for dental hygiene students.

Search strategy

The search included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, and pre–post intervention studies that evaluated educational outcomes of IPE interventions. We searched the MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE (via Ovid), Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases. The final search of all databases was performed on April 15, 2025. No restrictions were imposed on language or publication year.

The following Ovid MEDLINE search strategy was employed: (“dental hygiene”:ab, ti OR “dental hygienist students”:ab, ti OR “dental hygiene student”:ab, ti OR “dental hygiene education”:ab, ti OR “dental hygiene curriculum”:ab, ti OR “dental hygiene undergraduate”:ab, ti OR “dental hygien* student*” OR “dental hygien* educat*” OR “dental hygien* school*” OR “dental hygien* trainee*” OR “dental hygien* undergrad*” OR “dental hygien* curricul*”) AND (“interprofessional education”/exp OR “interprofessional education”:ab, ti OR “inter-professional education”:ab, ti OR “interdisciplinary education”:ab, ti OR “inter-disciplinary education”:ab, ti OR “interprofessional learning”:ab, ti OR “inter-professional learning”:ab, ti OR “collaborative learning”:ab, ti) NOT ((Meta-Analysis) OR (Review) OR (Systematic Review)).

Study selection

EndNote reference-management software was used to detect and remove duplicate publications. The Rayyan web-based platform was employed for the initial screening of titles and abstracts to ensure efficiency and transparency. After this automated phase, manual mapping was conducted to align duplicate records and ensure accurate classification. This two-step process was applied to enhance methodological rigor rather than to merely fulfill a procedural requirement. Two independent reviewers assessed the titles and/or abstracts of the remaining publications to determine their eligibility for full-text evaluation. Subsequently, the reviewers examined the complete manuscripts of potentially relevant studies; mapped records to the corresponding studies; and categorized them as included, excluded, or ongoing. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1 using a modified PRISMA flow diagram. Only minor discrepancies were identified between the reviewers in the risk of bias assessment, which were resolved through discussion and consensus. This process enhanced transparency and ensured independent judgment.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two authors, who systematically documented pertinent information in a standardized extraction form. The collected data comprised the first author, publication year, study design, sample size, participating disciplines, core content, teaching method, teaching duration, and outcomes (measurement).

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias for each included study was independently assessed by two authors using Cochrane’s ROBINS-I V2 tool [15]. This assessment examined seven domains: confounding, classification of intervention, selection into the study, deviations from intended intervention, missing data, and selection of reported result. Figure 2 presents a comprehensive visualization of the risk of bias domains. Any evaluative discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through a collaborative discussion.

Data synthesis

Studies reporting sufficient quantitative data with comparable IPE interventions and outcome measures of educational effectiveness were included in the meta-analysis. A fixed-effect model was employed for all meta-analyses given the minimal heterogeneity observed (I² ≤ 40%). Effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated where appropriate, and statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic. We interpreted I² values following the Cochrane Handbook guidelines, which stipulate that values of 0% to 40% might not indicate excessive variability between studies. We did not conduct sensitivity or publication bias analyses due to the limited number of studies per outcome. Publication bias assessment using funnel plots was not feasible with fewer than 10 studies per outcome, as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Sensitivity analyses were not performed given the small number of pooled studies.

Results

Study selection

The initial database search yielded 888 results. After removing duplicates, we screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining 327 publications, of which 66 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 33 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review (Table 1) [16–47].

Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 1997 and 2025, with the majority published after 2010, reflecting growing interest in research on IPE for dental hygiene students. The included studies employed a variety of research designs, with the majority being pre–post intervention studies (n = 18, 54.5%). This was followed by quasi-experimental studies (n = 7, 21.2%) and cross-sectional studies (n = 4, 12.1%). A smaller number of studies applied more rigorous approaches, including randomized controlled trials (n = 2, 6.1%) and mixed-methods studies (n = 2, 6.1%), reflecting methodological diversity and rigor. The most frequent interprofessional partners for dental hygiene students were dentistry, nursing, medicine, and pharmacy students. Other participating disciplines were physical therapy, occupational therapy, dental therapy, and various other health professions, such as social work, physician assistant studies, public health, audiology, speech pathology, and nutrition/dietetics. Sample sizes ranged from 12 to 334 participants.

Risk of bias

The quality of the included studies varied, with most showing serious or critical risk of bias. The predominance of non-randomized designs and methodological shortcomings severely limit the generalizability and reliability of our findings [13, 49]. Thus, while this review provides a synthesis of current evidence, the conclusions must be interpreted with caution, and there is a clear need for future high-quality randomized controlled trials. Domain 1 emerged as the most problematic area, with 60.6% of studies exhibiting a serious or critical risk of bias due to unmeasured confounding factors, lack of control groups in pre–post studies, and inadequate adjustment for baseline differences. Domain 2 (intervention classification) showed the best performance, with 84.8% achieving low risk. The remaining domains showed mixed results, with moderate concerns predominantly concerning participant selection bias, missing data, and different dropout rates across professional groups.

IPE interventions

Teaching methods varied across studies and included simulation-based learning [19, 23, 25, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 41], case-based learning [18, 21, 26, 39], clinical practicum experiences [16, 20, 31], and classroom-based sessions [42, 45, 48]. Intervention duration ranged from single-day sessions to longitudinal programs and included short-term (≤ one day) [18, 29, 30], medium-term (between one week and one semester) [16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 24–28, 33–36, 38–47] and long-term interventions (>one semester) [17, 20, 31, 37].

Core content consisted of IPEC core competencies and clinical practice topics. IPEC comprised teams and teamwork [22, 24, 44, 45], communication [30, 33, 35], roles and responsibilities [24, 26, 35], and values and ethics [28]. Clinical practice content comprised oral health assessment and care [23, 31, 36, 41]; patient safety [25] and hospital hazard identification; chronic disease management focusing on diabetes and oral health connections [43, 48]; management of older patients [25, 41]; and specialized communication with diverse populations [43, 45], including patients with limited English proficiency and hearing-impaired individuals.

Effectiveness of IPE

The results revealed that 28 of 33 studies demonstrated statistically significant IPE effectiveness. Knowledge and skill outcomes were effective in all studies. The Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) was the most frequently used measurement instrument, with its scores indicating consistent benefits of IPE across multiple healthcare disciplines and educational contexts.

Attitudes and readiness

Various evaluation scales, such as the RIPLS [16, 20, 28, 42], Modified RIPLS [39], Interprofessional Socialization and Valuing Scale (ISVS-9 A/9B) [21, 26, 29], Extended Professional Identity Scale (EPIS) [24], Student Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education instrument version 2 (SPICE-R2) [22, 35], Interdisciplinary Education Perception Scale (IEPS) [42], and University of West England Interprofessional Questionnaire (UWEIQ) [45], were used.

The RIPLS [16, 28, 32, 39, 42, 48] was the most frequently used tool. The results revealed that IPE enhanced students’ readiness for interprofessional learning and significantly increased positive attitudes toward collaboration. Based on RIPLS results, dental hygiene students exhibited more positive teamwork attitudes than dentistry students in a one-year follow-up [20]. Students reported improvements in teamwork attitudes (p < 0.05), and increased scores on the Team STEPPS Teamwork Attitudes Questionnaire (T-TAQ) or RIPLS were observed [48]. In contrast, several studies showed that dental hygiene students exhibited no significant changes [16, 28] or negative changes in RIPLS scores [42]. Three studies [21, 26, 29] assessed interprofessional socialization using the ISVS-9 A/9B and demonstrated significant improvements. Students exhibited improved self-perception, leadership comfort, and collaboration values [26].

Interprofessional competencies

Two studies [23, 32] evaluated IPEC Core Competencies and revealed mixed results. Both studies demonstrated significant improvement in interprofessional interaction but showed no changes in interprofessional values. The Interprofessional Collaborative Competency Attainment Survey (ICCAS) showed significant improvements in most of the domain statements following an interprofessional activity [18, 31]. Several studies examined team-related competencies and demonstrated positive outcomes. Students improved on all items of the Team Skills Scale (TSS) [17]. Significant improvements were observed in team efficacy and attitudes toward care for older individuals [25]. The Team Objective Standardized Clinical Examination (TOSCE) was used to evaluate behavioral assessment [19, 45]. The TOSCE assessment demonstrated statistically significant improvements in health literacy communication confidence and interprofessional teamwork understanding [19]. However, another study found no significant differences between learning formats, although students in blended learning demonstrated enhanced perceptions of learning outcomes [45].

Knowledge and skills

Six studies [30, 34, 39, 43, 46, 47] evaluated healthcare-specific knowledge and demonstrated significant improvements. Audiology knowledge increased from 4% to 90%, hearing assessment knowledge increased from 14% to 76%, and communication knowledge increased from 8% to 67% [30, 34]. Menopause and diabetes care knowledge improved significantly [39, 43]. Rural healthcare confidence and multidisciplinary team knowledge showed significant improvements [46, 47]. Studies examining communication skills and health literacy in diverse populations revealed consistent improvements [19, 27, 33]. Provider familiarity with best practices, interpreter practices [33], health literacy communication skills [19], and competencies in caring for patients with limited English proficiency [48] exhibited significant improvements. In specialized clinical areas, attitudes toward care for older individuals [25] and role security for substance abuse screening improved significantly [38].

Meta-analysis of outcomes

The meta-analysis examined five studies with available raw data that enabled conversion to mean ± standard deviation (SD) and employed identical outcome measures. A meta-analysis of RIPLS [16, 20] measurements from two studies (n = 275) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in readiness for interprofessional learning following the intervention (mean difference [MD] = -0.05; 95% CI: [-0.07, -0.03]; p < 0.00001). The negative mean difference indicates improvement, as lower RIPLS scores represent greater readiness for interprofessional learning. The analysis revealed low heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 38%, p = 0.20), indicating reasonable consistency between the study results (Fig. 3). However, regarding clinical significance, the small effect size (MD = -0.05) represents a minimal change on the RIPLS scale that may not translate to meaningful educational or professional outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effectiveness of IPE in dental hygiene education. The consistency of positive findings across diverse educational methods suggests potential benefits of IPE for developing interprofessional competencies among dental hygiene students. However, the substantial methodological limitations identified across included studies necessitate cautious interpretation of these results.

This study identified dentistry, nursing, medicine, and pharmacy as primary IPE partners, reflecting logical educational synergies rooted in complementary professional strengths and shared patient populations. The partnership between dental hygiene and dentistry represents a natural clinical relationship, with evidence suggesting that early informal interactions in undergraduate settings enhance formal clinical collaborations [50]. However, the effectiveness of partnerships varied considerably across studies, suggesting that successful IPE implementation requires careful attention to curriculum design, facilitator preparation, and organizational support, rather than simply bringing different professions together.

IPE research indicates that dental hygiene and dentistry students benefit from early informal interactions in undergraduate settings prior to formal clinical collaborations. McComas et al. [50] demonstrated that increased shared learning between dentistry and dental hygiene students resulted in greater collaborative engagement in clinical patient care. Additionally, corroborating evidence from another investigation revealed that extended interpersonal contact among students, such as shared living arrangements during rotations, enhanced perceptions of the effectiveness of clinical collaboration among dental hygiene students [51]. These partnerships help clarify evolving scopes of practice and promote mutual respect, which is essential for effective team-based oral healthcare delivery.

Dental hygienists and nurses serve as primary health educators, coordinate care between providers, and focus on addressing social determinants of health for vulnerable populations. Coan et al. [31] found that collaboration between dental hygiene and nursing students in hospital units resulted in significant improvements in ICCAS assessments. This finding reflects the importance of authentic practice environments for students’ meaningful interprofessional learning in hospital units. Given the well-established relationship between oral and chronic diseases, IPE between dental hygiene and nursing students is essential for comprehensive patient care.

IPE programs for dental hygiene students have demonstrated educational efficacy across multiple pedagogical modalities, encompassing simulation-based methodologies, case-based collaborative interventions, team-based learning frameworks, and authentic clinical practicum experiences. A recent systematic review of IPE identified simulation-based education and problem-based learning as the most prevalent undergraduate-level teaching and learning approaches [52]. The present findings suggest that simulation-based learning is a potentially effective pedagogical strategy for dental hygiene IPE, with evidence of improvements in clinical competencies and collaborative attitudes. Reutter et al. [23] and Karpa et al. [32] implemented simulation training interventions with healthcare students from dental hygiene, occupational therapy, medical, and nursing programs and demonstrated significant improvements in interprofessional interaction, measured using validated assessment instruments, including IPEC competency assessments.

Research on IPE in dental hygiene has employed various validated instruments aligned with IPEC core competencies, such as the RIPLS, ISVS-9 A/9B, TOSCE, ICCAS, and TSS. The RIPLS was the most frequently utilized instrument in the present study. The RIPLS, developed by Parsell and Bligh [53], is a self-report instrument designed to assess healthcare students’ attitudes toward interprofessional learning across dimensions of teamwork and collaboration, professional identity, and roles and responsibilities. As the most frequently employed measurement tool in IPE research [54], the RIPLS has achieved widespread global adoption with validations in multiple languages and cultural contexts, facilitating pre–post intervention assessments and curriculum evaluation across healthcare disciplines.

RIPLS assessments in this study demonstrated remarkable consistency, with five of six studies reporting significant improvements. Meta-analysis of RIPLS data revealed statistically significant improvements in readiness for interprofessional learning. Among long-term interventions (≥ one year), a three-year clinical practicum showed significant increases among 331 participants [16], and a one-year student-run clinic demonstrated superior collaborative attitudes among dental hygiene students [32]. Clinical environments consistently outperformed classroom approaches, with mixed classroom-clinical sessions achieving highly significant results [48]. Even short-term interventions proved effective, with a four-week blended learning program showing significant enhancements among 23 students [28].

Although the meta-analysis demonstrated overall significant improvement in readiness for interprofessional learning among dental hygiene students, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the high proportion of included studies with serious or critical risk of bias. This divergence may be explained by the fact that readiness measures capture short-term attitudinal changes, whereas interprofessional socialization requires sustained and longitudinal engagement. Therefore, while IPE may foster immediate readiness, the development of interprofessional socialization may necessitate longer-term interventions and curricular integration. This limitation underscores the urgent need for more rigorous research to strengthen the evidence base. Nevertheless, this review represents the first systematic attempt to synthesize the available evidence on interprofessional education for dental hygiene students. Our findings provide valuable insights into current trends, highlight critical gaps in the literature, and establish a foundation for future rigorous research to advance dental hygiene education. Although the findings indicate the effectiveness of IPE for developing interprofessional competencies, persistent implementation barriers require systematic interventions to optimize educational outcomes. Undergraduate IPE enables students to learn with, from, and about multiple healthcare professions from program inception, fostering understanding of interprofessional roles and establishing effective collaborative relationships essential for healthcare practice [13]. Key implementation barriers include scheduling coordination difficulties, curricular integration challenges, resource constraints, insufficient administrative support, inadequate faculty training, and organizational limitations [55, 56]. Success factors encompass meaningful professional participation, quality facilitation, facilitator preparation, and organizational support [49]. Therefore, addressing these barriers through strategic curriculum design, faculty development, and enhanced organizational support is essential to maximize the effectiveness of IPE and achieve optimal interprofessional learning outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. The predominant limitation stems from the substantial risk of bias identified across the included studies, with 78.8% of non-randomized studies demonstrating serious or critical overall risk of bias and no studies achieving low risk ratings [57]. Most critically, the majority of studies employed single-group pre–post designs without adequate control for confounding factors, thereby limiting causal inference about IPE effectiveness. Furthermore, the predominant reliance on self-reported attitudinal measures introduced measurement bias, while different dropout rates across professional groups and missing data further compromise the validity of findings.

The inadequate number of studies pooled for each outcome measurement limited quantitative synthesis, while heterogeneity in outcome measurement instruments across studies further restricted the robustness of pooled effect estimates. Future research should prioritize methodologically rigorous designs including adequately powered randomized controlled trials where feasible, standardized outcome measures with extended follow-up periods, and validated IPE-specific assessment instruments in diverse educational contexts. Despite these limitations, this systematic review and meta-analysis contributes valuable evidence that IPE interventions effectively improve student attitudes toward interprofessional collaboration within dental hygiene curricula. Nevertheless, this review provides valuable insights into current trends in interprofessional education (IPE) for dental hygiene students, identifies key gaps in the literature, and establishes a foundation for future rigorous research to advance dental hygiene education. Based on these findings, several practical implications can be drawn for dental hygiene curricula and faculty development. Integrating IPE longitudinally throughout the curriculum may strengthen students’ readiness for collaborative practice and promote sustained interprofessional socialization. Faculty development programs should focus on enhancing facilitation competencies that enable effective interprofessional learning and on cultivating teaching skills that foster mutual respect and psychological safety among learners. In addition, institutional support—including flexible scheduling, interdepartmental collaboration systems, and shared educational resources—should be reinforced to ensure sustainable implementation. Collectively, these multidimensional efforts may enhance not only student learning outcomes but also the overall culture of collaboration within dental hygiene education.

Conclusions

This systematic review suggests that IPE may be beneficial for dental hygiene students by improving knowledge, clinical skills, and collaborative attitudes. These outcomes were reported in studies that utilized validated instruments such as RIPLS, ICCAS, and ISVS, although methodological heterogeneity and limited evidence quality should be acknowledged. The meta-analysis indicated a positive trend toward increased readiness for interprofessional learning. Teaching methods such as simulation- and case-based learning showed promise across diverse educational contexts. Overall, while the findings are encouraging, they should be interpreted with caution, as the current evidence base remains preliminary.
