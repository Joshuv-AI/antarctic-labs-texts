Integrating GAN-based machine learning with nonlinear Kalman filtering for enhanced state estimation
Authors: Lior Tobaly, Eyal Yaniv, Zeev Zalevsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study introduces a novel approach for enhancing state estimation in non-linear dynamic systems by integrating Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) with the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF). While the UKF improves upon traditional Kalman Filters by using sigma points to estimate the mean and covariance in non-linear transformations, its effectiveness is limited by static parameters - specifically, the process noise covariance (Q), measurement noise covariance (R), and scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β). We propose a dynamic framework in which a GAN predicts and updates these parameters in real-time, based on the UKF’s recent performance, allowing the filter to better adapt to rapidly changing system dynamics. This method is validated on real-world aircraft navigation data containing time-stamped records of position, velocity, heading, and environmental variables. Results show that the GAN-enhanced UKF significantly reduces state estimation errors compared to conventional static models. The proposed framework is generalizable and can be applied to other domains such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and smart cities, where accurate real-time state estimation under uncertainty is critical.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26339-9.

Introduction

Accurate state estimation in dynamic systems is essential for a wide range of real-world applications. Kalman filtering has long been regarded as the gold standard for such tasks, especially in linear systems. However, most real-world systems exhibit non-linear and time-varying behaviours, which limit the effectiveness of traditional Kalman filters.

The Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) provides a robust alternative for non-linear systems. It improves upon the limitations of linear models by using sigma points to capture the mean and covariance through non-linear transformations. Despite its strengths, the UKF still depends heavily on predefined parameters: the process noise covariance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q$$\end{document}), measurement noise covariance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R$$\end{document}), and the scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β). These parameters influence how sigma points are distributed and weighted, and therefore play a critical role in estimating higher-order statistics. In practice, these values are often fixed or chosen heuristically, which limits the filter’s ability to adapt to dynamic environments.

In this work, we introduce a data-driven, adaptive approach to parameter tuning in the UKF by leveraging Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). The proposed method enables the UKF to predict and update \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q,\:R$$\end{document} and the scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β) dynamically, using feedback from the filter’s own performance. The goal is to enhance the filter’s responsiveness to rapidly changing system conditions, improving overall state estimation accuracy.

To evaluate the effectiveness of this approach, we apply it to a real-world dataset of aircraft navigation data, which includes time-stamped records of position, velocity, heading, and relevant environmental variables. Our results demonstrate that the GAN-UKF outperforms traditional static-parameter UKF models in terms of error reduction and tracking consistency.

This work lays the foundation for a generalizable, adaptive filtering framework suitable for use in complex domains such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and smart cities - contexts where accurate and real-time state estimation is critical.

In evaluating filtering methods for tracking highly manoeuvring aircraft, the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) demonstrates superior alignment with the true trajectory compared to both the standard Kalman Filter (KF) and the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). While the EKF improves upon the KF by incorporating local linearization, it struggles in scenarios with abrupt directional changes, where the UKF consistently provides more accurate state estimation. This advantage stems from the UKF’s use of sigma points, which allow it to more effectively handle nonlinear dynamics and high manoeuvrability. It should be noted, however, that this improvement comes at the cost of greater computational complexity, since the UKF must propagate a larger set of sigma points through nonlinear functions at each time step. Despite this added burden, the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost favours the UKF in applications where precision and robustness are paramount, such as aircraft navigation.

Building upon this foundation, recent advances in machine learning - particularly Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) - offer promising avenues for further enhancing UKF performance. By dynamically adjusting key parameters, including the process noise covariance matrix (Q), measurement noise covariance matrix (R), and scaling factors (α, κ, β), GANs enable the UKF to adapt continuously to evolving system dynamics. In this approach, the GAN’s generator proposes parameter updates based on historical performance, while the discriminator evaluates their effectiveness in reducing prediction errors. This integration transforms the UKF into an adaptive model capable of real-time parameter tuning, thereby improving estimation accuracy for critical state variables such as velocity, latitude, and longitude.

Related work - literature review

Kalman filtering in non-linear systems

The Kalman filter, introduced by R.E. Kalman in 1960, revolutionized linear state estimation by providing optimal recursive estimates for linear Gaussian1. However, practical systems (e.g. aerospace vehicles and robots) are often non-linear, violating Kalman’s assumptions2. Early extensions like the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) linearize non-linear dynamics but can suffer from instability if nonlinearities are severe2. This motivated sigma-point approaches such as the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF)2. The UKF uses deterministic sampling (unscented transform) to propagate means and covariances through non-linear transforms, achieving accuracy up to second-order without explicit linearization2. UKF’s efficacy led to its wide adoption in aerospace navigation and robotic localization throughout the 2000s3. Further advances included the Cubature Kalman Filter (CKF)4, which selects integration points from a spherical-radial rule to improve numerical stability in higher dimensions4. For highly non-Gaussian or multi-modal scenarios, particle filters emerged, using Monte Carlo samples to represent arbitrary5.

In domains like aerospace and autonomous vehicles, UKF became a standard for sensor fusion and navigation due to its balance of accuracy and computational load2. For instance, UKF is employed in satellite attitude estimation systems, providing robust performance even with complex quaternion kinematics and intermittent sensor faults6. In automotive systems, UKF is used for vehicle dynamics estimation (e.g. tire slip angles and velocities) under non-linear tire-road interaction7. Alshawi et al. (2024) propose an adaptive UKF for extreme driving maneuvers, which adjusts process and measurement noise covariances online to maintain accuracy during sudden dynamics7. Such adaptive variants address time-varying noise and unmodeled dynamics by tuning filter gains in real time. Recent work by Tong et al. (2022) introduced an improved UKF to handle random communication delays in smart grid state estimation, demonstrating robust tracking despite latency and packet loss8.

Despite these successes, classical UKF/EKF frameworks rely on accurate process models and noise statistics. Mismatches in system modeling (e.g. unmodeled flight dynamics or load changes in power grids) can degrade filter performance3. There is a need for filters to adapt or learn unknown model aspects on the fly. This challenge has catalyzed research into data-driven and hybrid approaches that combine domain knowledge with machine learning to handle non-linearities and uncertainties beyond the reach of conventional filters.

Machine learning in state estimation

The past decade has seen rapid growth in applying machine learning (ML) to state estimation, motivated by the ability of ML models (especially deep neural networks) to approximate complex non-linear mappings. Traditional Kalman filters are model-based, errors arise if the assumed physics are incomplete. Machine learning offers a model-free or model-enhanced avenue: either replace the physics-based estimator with a learned model or augment the estimator with learned components9. For example, a neural network can learn to predict system state directly from raw sensor data, or estimate unmodeled dynamics to be fed into a Kalman filter. Researchers have increasingly explored hybrid Kalman filters and neural networks to leverage both data-driven flexibility and probabilistic filtering10.

Preliminary efforts in the 2010s integrated simple neural networks with Kalman filters for specific tasks like sensor bias correction and fault detection. For instance, in aerospace, Chen et al. (2024) combine a neural network with a Kalman filter to detect and recover from gyroscope faults in satellite attitude estimation6. The neural network monitors residuals and adaptively adjusts filter estimates during faults, improving reliability of the attitude solution. In robotics, Urrea & Agramonte (2021) provide a comprehensive overview of Kalman filtering in robotic systems and note that neural networks have been used to assist in non-linear mapping problems such as vision-based localization11. One approach is using a neural network to learn the observation function (e.g. mapping image features to robot pose), while a Kalman filter handles the sequential estimation, combining learned measurements with dynamic models.

With the deep learning revolution, researchers began applying advanced architectures (CNNs, RNNs, etc.) to state estimation. A notable direction is recurrent neural networks (RNNs), like LSTMs, for sequential sensor data. Recurrent nets can serve as standalone estimators or be embedded in filtering frameworks. Kim & Lee (2021) compared several ML models for aviation wind prediction and found an LSTM network best captured short-term wind dynamics12. They then augmented the LSTM with a UKF (termed “UKF-LSTM”), which treated the LSTM’s prediction as a process model within a Kalman filtering loop12. This hybrid improved forecasting accuracy and provided confidence intervals via the UKF’s covariance estimation. Similarly, in intelligent vehicles, deep learning has been used to estimate vehicle states that are difficult to model. Yang et al. (2024) propose a hybrid deep neural network-UKF algorithm to estimate a bus’s roll and sideslip angles in real time13. A deep network first learns to predict these angles from IMU and GPS data, its output is then fused by an UKF with a vehicle motion model, yielding an estimator that is both accurate and physically consistent14. The hybrid outperformed purely model-based and purely data-driven methods, highlighting the benefit of combining neural networks with Kalman filtering.

By 2023, the synergy of ML and state estimation had become a significant research trend. Bai et al. (2023) published an extensive survey on “Kalman filter driven by machine learning”, documenting the evolution from EKF to modern hybrid schemes15. They conclude that learning-based methods have achieved notable success in autonomous navigation, power grid monitoring, and industrial IoT, by addressing model uncertainties and non-linearities that classical filters struggle with15. Feng et al. (2023) similarly review dozens of Kalman-neural network hybrid models, affirming that such approaches often outperform either method alone across aerospace, automotive, and robotics applications16. The literature indicates a clear trajectory: towards “learning Kalman filters” that can adjust their models using data, rather than relying on fixed equations. This sets the stage for the latest innovation - incorporating generative models, like GANs, to further enhance state estimation adaptivity.

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) in adaptive filtering

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), first introduced by Goodfellow et al. in 2014, are a class of deep learning models that learn data distributions via a two-player game between a Generator and a Discriminator17. GANs excel at producing realistic samples from complex, high-dimensional distributions. In state estimation contexts, GANs can model uncertainties or generate synthetic sensor data that mimic real-world variations. This capability is valuable for adaptive filtering, where understanding and adapting to changing noise or system dynamics is crucial. By 2020, GANs had been successfully applied in various domains to create realistic data, for instance, Goodfellow et al. (2020) overview their success in generating high-resolution images and note their potential for wider applications18. Researchers have begun leveraging GANs to address two key challenges in filtering: data scarcity (by augmenting training or calibration data) and non-Gaussian noise modeling (by learning complex noise distributions).

One line of work uses GANs to produce synthetic time-series data that improve filter training or resiliency. In battery management systems (power systems domain), obtaining extensive labeled data for state-of-charge (SOC) estimation is difficult due to long testing times. Hu et al. (2024) propose a GAN-based augmentation technique to generate realistic battery voltage and current trajectories for training a state estimator19. They develop a Time-Series DCGAN (TS-DCGAN) that learns from limited experimental data and produces additional “pseudo-experimental” battery discharge profiles, including realistic noise and aging effects19. By training an extended Kalman filter on this enriched dataset, the authors achieved higher SOC estimation accuracy and robustness to sensor noise19. The GAN essentially serves as a data generator capturing the stochastic dynamics of battery behavior under varied conditions, thereby improving the filter’s ability to generalize. Similarly, in smart grid state estimation, conditional GANs have been used to simulate plausible grid measurement vectors under different load conditions, augmenting data for training neural network observers20. These studies show that GANs can address data insufficiency, a common issue in developing data-driven filters.

Another contribution of GANs is modeling complex error distributions that challenge traditional filters. Standard Kalman filters assume Gaussian noise; in reality, noise can be multi-modal or heavy-tailed (e.g. due to sporadic outliers or mode switches). GANs provide a non-parametric way to learn such distributions. Tian et al. (2022) tackled urban traffic state estimation with sparse and multi-modal data using a pattern-adaptive GAN (PA-GAN)21. Traffic flow and speed measurements in a city can be sporadic (due to sensor outages) and exhibit multi-modal patterns (e.g. rush-hour vs. off-hour behaviors). PA-GAN learned to generate realistic traffic state vectors (speed distributions across a network) conditioned on time-of-day and sparse sensor inputs21. By imbuing a particle filter with these GAN-generated samples as priors, the authors achieved more accurate and stable traffic state estimates under sparse data scenarios, compared to filters with simple Gaussian assumptions21. The GAN effectively adapted the filter’s prior to the current traffic pattern, enabling multimodal state representation (e.g. multiple plausible congestion scenarios) until disambiguated by new data. In power systems, researchers have trained Wasserstein GANs to learn the distribution of meter measurements under normal conditions, these GANs are then used within a chi-square test or filter framework to detect anomalies and adapt state estimates when adversarial readings occur22. By learning the true distribution of measurement noise and load variability, a GAN can help a state estimator distinguish nominal fluctuations from abnormal deviations, enhancing resilience to attacks or sensor faults.

The above examples illustrate GANs enabling adaptive filtering in ways not possible with conventional methods. By providing rich stochastic information (through generated samples), GAN-augmented filters can adapt to regime changes: e.g. a traffic estimator can seamlessly shift its state predictions when the GAN indicates a transition from free-flow to congested traffic pattern. Moreover, GANs can operate in high-dimensional observation spaces (images, lidar scans) by learning lower-dimensional representations for filtering - an approach being explored in autonomous driving for visual-inertial odometry, where GAN-like generators simulate sensor readings for unseen conditions to update filter predictions. One challenge is ensuring the stability of the combined GAN-filter system. Since GAN outputs are probabilistic, integrating them into recursive estimation requires careful design to avoid divergence. Techniques like GAN ensemble averaging (to quantify uncertainty) and regularizing the generator with physical laws (to maintain consistency) have been proposed9. Overall, GANs bring a powerful data-driven adaptation mechanism, and initial studies in smart cities, power grids, and autonomous systems show improved estimation accuracy, especially in edge cases or sparse-data regimes. This paves the way for more holistic hybrid ML-Kalman filtering frameworks, discussed next.

Hybrid machine learning–Kalman filtering approaches

Hybrid approaches tightly integrate machine learning models with Kalman filter algorithms to exploit their complementary strengths. The Kalman filter provides recursive optimal estimation with uncertainty quantification, while ML models (e.g. deep neural networks or GANs) contribute flexibility in modeling complex phenomena and learning from data. The literature in recent years reveals a spectrum of hybrid architectures. Shuo Feng et al. (2023) categorize these into series (filters and networks in sequence) and parallel (filters and networks exchanging information iteratively) hybrids16. An example of a series hybrid is the UKF-LSTM mentioned earlier: the LSTM produces a preliminary estimate that is then refined by a Kalman filter12. A parallel hybrid might involve a network that continuously updates filter parameters (like noise covariances) during operation, essentially learning to “steer” the Kalman filter.

KalmanNet (2021–2022) and related approaches treat the Kalman filtering algorithm as a trainable architecture. In KalmanNet, neural networks learn to approximate the filter gain or model parameters from data, allowing operation even with partially known system models23. Yanjie Yu et al. (2023) developed an Unscented Trainable Kalman Filter (UTKF) where a deep neural network learns to predict the process dynamics, and the UKF framework then propagates and updates the state24. This was demonstrated in power system state estimation with missing data: the network learned the generator dynamics and the UTKF robustly estimated voltages even when certain sensors failed, outperforming static UKF assumptions24. Such approaches blur the line between model-based and data-driven - the filter becomes a scaffold within which learning happens.

The PINN-UKF framework by de Curto et al. (2024) is one example9, but similar physics-informed deep filters have appeared in traffic and robotics. Zhao & Yu (2023) introduced an Observer-Informed Deep Learning (OIDL) scheme for traffic networks, where a PDE-based traffic flow observer runs in parallel with a deep neural network25. The observer (analogous to a model-based filter) ensures physical consistency (e.g. conservation of cars), while the deep network, guided by the observer’s feedback, learns to estimate the traffic state from sparse boundary measurements25. The two components iterate, resulting in a self-correcting hybrid that significantly improved highway traffic density estimation under real sensor data25. This parallel architecture highlights how classical filters can regularize deep learning, and conversely how deep learning can augment observers in unobserved regions.

An emerging theme is enhancing filter robustness using ML. RKFNet introduced by Hao et al. (2025) combines a heavy-tailed noise Kalman filter with deep learning26. The base filter (RKF) assumes noise follows a heavy-tailed distribution to better handle outliers. A neural network is then trained to adapt the tail parameters and identify outlier-contaminated measurements26. In experiments on autonomous vehicle tracking with occasional GPS spikes, RKFNet automatically down-weighted the spikes and retained accuracy, whereas a standard KF would diverge. This demonstrates a powerful synergy: the deep network learns abnormal patterns, and the filter provides the principled mathematical framework to incorporate that knowledge for robustness.

Across aerospace, automotive, power, and smart cities, these hybrids have set new performance benchmarks. In autonomous vehicles, the fusion of camera/LiDAR deep perception with filtering yields accurate and stable state estimates for navigation. For example, a recent vehicle sideslip estimator combines a vision-based CNN (giving a pseudo-measurement of slip angle) with a vehicle-model UKF27. The UKF cross-checks the CNN output against physical vehicle dynamics, resulting in an estimator that can trust the CNN when it is confident but fallback to model predictions when the CNN is uncertain27. This adaptive trust mechanism solved a long-standing issue in tire-slip estimation under varying road conditions. In aerospace, Chen et al. (2024) report a hybrid framework for spacecraft attitude: a small CNN processes star-camera images to provide coarse attitude quaternions, which an Unscented Kalman Filter then fuses with gyroscope data for high-rate attitude tracking6. The result is sub-arcsecond pointing accuracy, even during periods when the star camera is blinded (the UKF propagates using gyro bias estimates learned during nominal conditions). In power systems, hybrid schemes (like Yu’s UTKF and others) are enabling dynamic state estimation even under low-observability (few sensors) by learning system dynamics from historical data28. A comparative study by Qiu et al. (2025) in an energy microgrid showed that a hybrid UKF-ANN estimator tracked load voltages with 40% lower error than a pure ANN and 25% lower error than a tuned UKF, especially during abrupt load changes29. The ANN learned the nonlinear relationship between loads and voltage drops, while the UKF ensured the estimates respected circuit equations and filtered out measurement noise.

The integration of GANs with UKF - the focus of our manuscript - can be viewed as part of this broader hybrid trend. GANs provide a powerful means to simulate and adapt to new operating conditions on the fly. Combined with UKF’s principled estimation, a GAN-UKF hybrid can continually improve state estimates even as the environment or system behavior shifts. For example, one could imagine an UKF that uses a GAN-generated ensemble of predicted sensor readings as the sigma-points for its unscented transform, effectively learning the measurement distribution in real-time. Early explorations of this idea in traffic and power systems are promising21. As research advances, we expect to see GAN-augmented UKFs enabling autonomous vehicles to handle novel road scenarios, smart grids to estimate state under cyber-attack by learning attack patterns, robots to localize in unseen terrains by simulating sensor data, and more.

Generative adversarial networks (GANs)

Generative models were notably advanced in 2014 when Ian Goodfellow and colleagues introduced Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)30. GANs consist of two neural networks - the Generator (G) and the Discriminator (D) - that are trained simultaneously in an adversarial process. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Generator receives random noise vectors as input and produces synthetic data samples, while the Discriminator evaluates both real and generated data, classifying them as either authentic or fake. During training, the Discriminator learns to distinguish real from generated data, while the Generator learns to improve its output so that it increasingly resembles the real data. This adversarial setup, driven by classification loss for the Discriminator and reconstruction loss for the Generator, leads to progressively more realistic outputs from the Generator over time.

Integrating GANs with UKF

While the UKF provides strong performance in nonlinear systems, its accuracy depends heavily on fixed parameters such as the process noise covariance Q, measurement noise covariance R, and sigma point scaling factors (α, β, κ). In static UKF formulations, these parameters are predefined and remain constant, leading to suboptimal performance in dynamic environments where noise levels or system nonlinearities evolve.

To address this limitation, we propose integrating a GAN framework with the UKF to enable adaptive parameter tuning. Specifically:

The generator proposes updated values for Q, R, α, β, κ at each cycle, based on historical state estimation performance and prediction errors.The discriminator evaluates these proposals by comparing predicted versus observed errors, rewarding parameter sets that reduce estimation inaccuracies.Through iterative training, the GAN learns to produce parameter adjustments that optimize UKF performance under varying conditions.

Dataset description

Dataset description

This study utilizes the TrajAir dataset31, a unique and comprehensive collection of real aircraft trajectory data curated specifically for state estimation and trajectory prediction tasks. The dataset was collected around Pittsburgh-Butler Regional Airport (ICAO: KBTP) - a non-towered general aviation airport located approximately 10 miles north of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA. The airport’s constrained airspace and adherence to strict FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) traffic regulations provide a valuable environment for capturing detailed and structured aircraft motion data. The dataset is publicly available at https://theairlab.org/trajair/#download.

Dataset origin

Data were collected using an Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) receiver installed at the airport. This receiver used satellite navigation to determine aircraft positions and timestamps, continuously logging broadcasts from aircraft equipped with ADS-B Out technology. To include aircraft lacking this capability, additional data were retrieved via the Traffic Information Service–Broadcast (TIS-B), which retransmits radar data in an ADS-B-compatible format.

The collection spanned 111 days, from September 18, 2020, to April 23, 2021, covering operations between 1:00 AM and 11:00 PM local time. Days impacted by equipment repairs or extreme weather were excluded to maintain data quality.

Meteorological integration

In addition to flight trajectories, the dataset includes environmental data sourced from METeorological Aerodrome Reports (METAR), generated by the airport’s Automated Weather Observing System (AWOS). These meteorological readings - such as temperature, pressure, wind speed, and wind direction - were aligned post hoc with flight data using timestamp matching from the Iowa State METAR repository. This integration enables a richer analysis by contextualizing aircraft dynamics within varying atmospheric conditions.

Purpose and scope

The TrajAir dataset was designed as a benchmark resource for testing advanced trajectory prediction and dynamic state estimation models, particularly in non-towered terminal airspace where conventional air traffic control is minimal. Its broad applicability includes research into socially aware trajectory models, adaptive filtering, and multi-agent interaction modelling in constrained environments.

Methodology

To enable real-time adaptation of critical parameters such as the process noise covariance (Q), measurement noise covariance (R), and scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β), this research introduces a dynamic Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) enhanced by Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). The primary contribution of this work lies in the mathematical formulation of a dynamic UKF and its integration with a GAN-based learning mechanism.

GAN-UKF collaboration

The integration of a UKF and a GAN, illustrated in Fig. 2, results in a dynamic and adaptive filtering system. By continuously learning from the filter’s performance, the GAN module dynamically adjusts the UKF parameters, thereby improving state estimation accuracy over time.

GAN-enhanced UKF architecture

The GAN consists of two fully connected neural networks:

Generator: Receives a 100-dimensional input vector representing past UKF performance metrics. This vector is processed through two hidden layers with 128 and 256 neurons, using ReLU activations. The output layer has 10 neurons: the first five represent the diagonal elements of the Q matrix, and the next five correspond to R. In extended configurations, the first three elements may be interpreted as scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β).Discriminator: Accepts the generated (or real) 10-dimensional parameter vector as input and processes it through two hidden layers (256 and 128 neurons), outputting a binary classification via a sigmoid activation to indicate whether the parameters are realistic. The GAN is trained using binary cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0002.

During each UKF cycle, the Generator produces candidate parameters using recent performance data. These parameters are applied in the UKF update. The Discriminator assesses the effect on estimation accuracy by comparing the filter’s predicted states with ground-truth values. This adversarial feedback loop allows the system to adaptively tune UKF parameters in real time.

This dynamic, performance-driven interaction between the UKF and GAN enables more accurate tracking of complex, nonlinear processes. By continuously optimizing its internal parameters based on performance feedback, the UKF becomes a self-improving filter, ideal for applications in uncertain and rapidly changing environments. Unlike static filtering methods, this framework evolves over time, maintaining optimal performance under varying operating conditions.

Two conditions were evaluated per trial: Clean (no outliers) and Outliers (2% of time steps receive injected spikes).

Comparative analysis and robustness

The sensitivity analysis shows that the proposed GAN-UKF approach consistently outperforms the static UKF, regardless of the initial values of Q and R. This highlights the effectiveness of the adaptive mechanism, which dynamically tunes filter parameters in response to real-time feedback, thus ensuring optimal performance even when initial estimates are not ideal. Static UKF models, by comparison, can suffer large and persistent errors if Q and R are not tuned precisely for the given environment.

Computational overhead and real-time feasibility

The proposed GAN-UKF framework introduces only modest computational overhead relative to traditional Kalman Filters, primarily due to the integration of a generator network that dynamically predicts the UKF parameters (Q, R, and optionally α, β, and κ). However, the generator is a lightweight feedforward neural network with two hidden layers (128 and 256 neurons), and only a single forward pass is required during each UKF cycle.

Performance profiling shows that parameter generation completes in under 10 milliseconds on a GPU (NVIDIA RTX 3080) and under 50 milliseconds on modern CPUs (e.g., Intel i7-12700 H). These runtimes fall well within the latency constraints of real-time systems operating at 1 Hz or lower, such as those in aviation navigation.

Additionally, complete UKF-GAN cycle times across test segments—including prediction, update, and parameter adaptation—remained under 3 s per segment.

Therefore, the proposed architecture is well-suited for real-time and embedded applications where computational resources and latency are constrained. The component-level runtimes and per-cycle totals (including framework/IO overheads) are summarized in Table 6 that follow:

We report aggregate results for both conditions - clean and outlier-injected - in the Monte Carlo results. For each filter we compute mean errors across all segments and runs.

Across conditions, introducing outliers increases error for every method, as expected, but to markedly different degrees. Under outliers, the GAN-UKF retains the lowest aggregate error (lowest RMSE total) among the tested filters, followed by the best of the adaptive baselines. The static UKF and PF show the largest increases, particularly in the position channels (Lat/Lon) and altitude, indicating higher sensitivity to spiky measurements.

GAN-UKF provides the strongest robustness trade-off across conditions. It attains the lowest total error on clean data and remains the top performer when outliers are injected, showing a smaller increase in error than UKF, EKF, and PF. Methods like UKF-IAE, PF, KalmanNet, and UKF-LSTM either start from much higher error levels or display instability, so their small changes under outliers are not indicative of true robustness. This aligns with our sensitivity analysis where independent tuning of Q and R away from the diagonal (Q ≠ R) improved resilience to measurement anomalies.

Comparative analysis with intelligent filters

The additional results confirm that while non-classical filters reduce error relative to the UKF baseline, their performance remains well below that of the GAN-UKF. For example, UKF-IAE achieved improvements of ~ 20–30% over UKF in latitude and longitude estimation, but the GAN-UKF delivered > 90% error reduction in the same dimensions. Particle filters also offered moderate improvements but suffered from instability and higher variance, particularly in altitude estimation.

Overall, the GAN-UKF consistently outperformed all tested baselines across every state variable, demonstrating its robustness and generalization capability. This validates that the observed gains are not merely incremental over the UKF, but represent a substantial advance beyond existing “intelligent” filters. These performance gains are especially evident in challenging scenarios such as abrupt manoeuvres or unpredictable weather, where traditional, static models often underperform. The findings strongly support the adoption of adaptive filtering techniques, like the GAN-UKF, in domains that demand high-fidelity real-time estimation.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel approach for enhancing state estimation in non-linear dynamic systems by integrating Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) with the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF). The core innovation lies in leveraging the generative capabilities of GANs to dynamically adjust key UKF parameters - including the process noise covariance (Q), measurement noise covariance (R), and the scaling factors (primary, secondary, and tertiary; α, κ, and β) - all of which are essential for accurate state prediction in nonlinear environments.

Unlike existing adaptive approaches such as IAE or IMM, which focus on heuristic tuning of measurement noise, the GAN-UKF jointly adapts both the process noise (Q), measurement noise (R), and the scaling parameters (α, β, κ). This enables holistic, data-driven adaptation of the filter dynamics. To our knowledge, this is the first approach that integrates a generative adversarial model with a UKF to simultaneously adjust multiple parameters sets in real time, leading to superior accuracy and robustness. Empirical evaluations using real-world aircraft navigation data demonstrated that the dynamically tuned UKF significantly outperformed its conventional, static counterpart. The improvements were most notable in Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for critical state variables such as latitude, longitude, altitude, heading, and speed. For example, RMSE in spatial positioning (latitude and longitude) was reduced from several degrees to fractions of a degree, highlighting the model’s ability to adapt and maintain precision.

While this study focused on aircraft navigation, the proposed framework has broad applicability. The GAN-enhanced UKF architecture can be extended to any domain that requires accurate, real-time state estimation under uncertainty - including robotics, autonomous vehicles, environmental monitoring, and smart city systems.

In summary, this work establishes a foundation for flexible, adaptive state estimation in complex systems. It demonstrates that integrating machine learning techniques - specifically GANs - into classical filtering frameworks such as the UKF can significantly improve estimation performance. The approach offers a powerful path forward for advancing state estimation in modern, data-rich, and dynamic environments.
