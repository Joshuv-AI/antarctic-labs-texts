Machine learning for identifying antibacterial agents derived from yam (Dioscorea) and elucidating antibacterial mechanisms based on metabolomics
Authors: Jianjun Ding, Yulun Chen, Tao Jiang, Yajun Gong, Shaofeng Yuan, Yahui Guo, Yuliang Cheng, Yuhang Du, Hang Yu, Weirong Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaponins from edible plants are promising natural antibacterial agents because of their structural diversity, abundance, and safety. To rapidly identify antibacterial saponins, this study developed a machine learning-based screening strategy using multimodal early fusion of 1D&2D, 3D, and MACCS molecular features. Predictive models were built and systematically evaluated, and the fusion model showed excellent performance on an independent test set. Guided by this model, hecogenin, a potent antibacterial compound from yam, was identified. In a lettuce-based food application system, hecogenin significantly reduced bacterial contamination. Metabolomic analysis showed that it inhibited Staphylococcus aureus by disrupting tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine metabolism. Molecular simulations further indicated that its antibacterial activity was linked to binding the key enzymes AroA and AroB in the amino acid biosynthesis pathway. This study provides an effective strategy for discovering antibacterial agents from edible plants and highlights the potential of dietary saponins as food preservatives.Keywords: Feature fusion, Machine learning, Yam, Antibacterial, Food preservatives, Metabolomic, Molecular simulations, Yam (Dioscorea).

Introduction

Food contamination caused by pathogenic bacteria can occur at multiple stages of the food production, distribution, and consumption chain, resulting in hundreds of millions of cases of illness worldwide each year (Cui et al., 2025; Pires et al., 2021; Yang, Du, et al., 2025). As one of the most common foodborne pathogenic bacteria, S. aureus can cause various severe infections, including sepsis and gastroenteritis (Li, Pan, et al., 2022). Conventional chemical preservatives, such as sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate, and sodium nitrite, have been widely used in the food industry because they can effectively inhibit the growth of foodborne pathogenic bacteria (Yang et al., 2023). However, the long-term overuse of antibiotics and chemical preservatives has promoted the rapid spread of multiple drug-resistant (MDR) bacterial pathogens (Yang, Li, et al., 2025). In particular, with the continued global spread of MRSA, the resulting food contamination will further intensify the already severe challenge of preventing and controlling foodborne diseases (Fri et al., 2020). Given the increasingly prominent issue of bacterial resistance, there is an urgent need to develop novel antimicrobial agents that can be safely applied in the food industry.

Saponins derived from edible plants (dietary saponins) have attracted considerable attention from researchers because of their high abundance and structural diversity, and because they exhibit a variety of health-beneficial properties for humans, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-aging activities, while being generally regarded as harmless to human health (Guo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Saponins are widely distributed in legumes (such as soybean, chickpea, mung bean, peanut, broad bean, kidney bean, and lentil), and are also found in oats, plants of the family Liliaceae (such as chives and garlic), asparagus, tea, spinach, beet, and cassava, as well as in medicinal and edible plants such as ginseng, Polygonatum sibiricum, and Platycodon grandiflorus (Güçlü-Üstündag & Mazza, 2007; Guo et al., 2025). In recent years, studies have shown that some dietary saponins can effectively inhibit the growth of foodborne pathogens. For example, ginsenosides Rg1 and 20R-Rg2 can effectively inhibit multiple foodborne pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Bacillus cereus (Xue et al., 2020). Tea saponins from Camellia oleifera also showed good antimicrobial activity against Escherichia coli and S. aureus, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 1 and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively (Zhao et al., 2020). Our previous study also found that saponins derived from Sapindus mukorossi could effectively inhibit the activity of foodborne pathogens such as S. aureus and Micrococcus luteus (Wei et al., 2021). Despite these encouraging findings, the complex structures and large diversity of saponins mean that traditional manual extraction and screening are not only labor-intensive and time-consuming, but also result in substantial consumption of human and material resources. Therefore, the use of machine learning techniques to identify saponins and their derivatives with potential antibacterial activity is of great scientific significance and practical value.

Machine learning (ML)-based approaches have gradually been applied to the discovery of natural bioactive ingredients, significantly accelerating research progress in bioactive ingredient discovery in the food industry (Chang et al., 2025). In particular, ML-based approaches have been widely used to mine health-beneficial functional peptides, such as antihypertensive and antioxidant peptides, from enzymatic hydrolysates of dietary proteins (Bao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025). In recent years, research has further expanded to the use of machine learning to screen natural antimicrobial bioactive ingredients from nature. For example, one study employed partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) to identify novel antimicrobial natural bioactive ingredients and discovered three new antimicrobial peptides from calf whey by-products (Sanchez-Reinoso et al., 2024). In addition, regression-based machine learning methods have also been used to discover new antimicrobial peptides from chicken blood and to explore their potential as food preservatives (Rahimi et al., 2025). However, few studies have yet used machine learning to mine small-molecule antimicrobial bioactive ingredients derived from edible plants.

Yam (Dioscorea) is a widely distributed plant genus comprising numerous species across Africa, Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Oceania. Plants in this genus are rich in various saponin compounds (Obidiegwu et al., 2020). Against this backdrop, this study aimed to use machine learning methods to construct a saponin antibacterial activity prediction model by integrating 1D&2D features, 3D structural features, and molecular fingerprint features through a multimodal feature fusion strategy, and to apply this model to mine dietary saponins with antibacterial potential from Yam (Dioscorea). To achieve this goal, multiple rounds of feature screening were first performed on saponin features, and a multimodal early fusion strategy was then adopted to concatenate the screened features. Subsequently, multiple machine learning and machine learning methods were used to build the saponin antibacterial activity prediction model and evaluate its performance. An ablation study was conducted to assess the contribution of different feature types to the predictive performance of the fusion model. Meanwhile, two feature importance analysis methods were employed to identify the key features associated with antibacterial activity. Using the best-performing saponin antibacterial activity prediction model, dietary saponins with antibacterial activity were further mined from Yam, and their antibacterial activity was validated by the broth microdilution method. In addition, lettuce was used as an application carrier to evaluate its potential in practical scenarios. Finally, metabolomics analysis was combined to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the inhibitory effect of antibacterial saponins against S. aureus, and molecular simulation was performed to verify the antibacterial mechanism.

Materials and methods

Materials

S. aureus ATCC 29213 was provided by the Food Safety and Quality Control Research Center, School of Food Science and Technology, Jiangnan University. Fresh lettuce was purchased from a local market in Wuxi. The Epoch2 microplate reader was purchased from BioTek (USA); the PYX-DHS-50 × 65-BS water-jacketed constant-temperature incubator was purchased from Shanghai Yuejin Medical Instrument Factory; hecogenin (He) and ursolic acid (UA) were purchased from Chengdu Puruikang Technology Co., Ltd.; and all other compounds and chemical reagents were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China.

Dataset and preprocessing

A dataset of 155 plant-derived saponins from different plant species was collected from PubChem, SciFinder, and the literature. The dataset was subsequently preprocessed using RDKit (http://www.rdkit.org/), including removal of compounds lacking activity data, deletion of duplicate compounds, and removal of salts while retaining the parent structures. The inhibitory activity against S. aureus was encoded as a binary value of “1” or “0”, where “1” indicated that the molecule exhibited growth-inhibitory activity against S. aureus, and “0” indicated no inhibitory activity (Choo et al., 2023).

Calculation and filtering of molecular descriptors

1D and 2D features were generated using PADEL, yielding a total of 663 descriptors, including structural information such as molecular weight (MW), lipophilicity (XLogP), and the number of acidic atoms (nAcid) (Yap, 2011). Molecular fingerprint features were generated using the MACCS fingerprints produced by PADEL, comprising 166 features; different features reflect information from different structural scaffold fragments. The 3D features were generated using QikProp software developed by Schrödinger, yielding a total of 52 descriptors that provide structural information related to the spatial conformation and stereochemical properties of compounds, such as solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), molecular volume (Volume), and the number of rotatable bonds (Rotor) (Laoui & Polyakov, 2011). This study employed a rigorous, multi-step feature screening strategy to effectively identify anomalous features and eliminate low-variance ones. First, the raw data were cleaned before feature selection. Next, features with excessively high correlations were removed to reduce multicollinearity. Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to further screen the descriptors and retain those with greater contributions.

Development and evaluation of multi-feature fusion model

By integrating 1D and 2D features, 3D features, and MACCS molecular fingerprint features, a more effective and robust feature vector representation was constructed. Six algorithms were employed to investigate the relationship between saponin structures and their inhibitory activity against S. aureus, support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and random forest (RF), as well as three machine learning methods, multilayer perceptron (MLP), convolutional neural network (CNN), and residual neural network (ResNet). Eighty percent of the compounds were randomly assigned to the training set, and the remaining 20% were used as the test set. The experiment was performed for five iterations. Among these models, CNN, MLP, and ResNet were implemented based on PyTorch, and all models were completed in the Python environment.

Model performance was rigorously evaluated on an independent test set and comprehensively assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) (Yan et al., 2025).

Bayesian hyperparameter tuning

This study employed the Optuna library for Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, which has demonstrated strong performance in tuning hyperparameters of classification models (Pang et al., 2025). Optuna features an efficient search strategy and effective resource management, enabling rapid identification of the optimal parameter combinations for regression and classification models with relatively low computational cost. To further improve optimization efficiency, callback functions and an early-stopping mechanism were introduced; when model performance stabilized across consecutive iterations, the training and validation process was automatically terminated. In this study, a Bayesian optimization framework was constructed to systematically evaluate 50 different hyperparameter combinations, thereby determining the optimal parameter configuration to maximize model predictive performance.

Construction of the saponin database for yam

The COCONUT natural products database (https://coconut.naturalproducts.net/), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/), and SciFinder (https://scifinder-n.cas.org/) were jointly used for the identification of saponin types. Species retrieval was performed using “Dioscorea” and “yam” as the keyword, resulting in the identification of 239 saponin structures derived from yam. Subsequently, their 1D & 2D features, 3D features, and MACCS molecular fingerprint features were generated for subsequent model screening.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of saponin compounds

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the broth microdilution method (Cui et al., 2025). S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was washed with PBS (0.1 M, pH 7.2) and resuspended to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. The plant-derived saponins were then serially diluted and added to the bacterial suspension, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, 20 μL of 0.5% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) aqueous solution was added to observe bacterial growth (Selles et al., 2020). Ursolic acid (UA) was included as the positive control. The inhibitory effect of saponins on bacterial growth was evaluated by measuring OD600, and the lowest concentration corresponding to a significant decrease in absorbance was defined as the MIC (Li et al., 2014).

Antibacterial effect of plant-derived saponins on lettuce

Following the experimental protocol reported by Tian et al. (2024) with minor modifications, fresh lettuce purchased from a local market in Wuxi was cut into squares of approximately 3 cm × 3 cm, with each piece weighing 0.3 ± 0.01 g. The samples were divided into three groups, A, B, and C, among which A and B served as control groups and C served as the experimental group. Group A was used as the negative control without bacterial inoculation. Groups B and C were inoculated on the lettuce surface with a suspension of S. aureus at an inoculation level of 106 CFU/g. Subsequently, the surfaces of samples in groups A and B were uniformly sprayed with 200 μL of sterile PBS, whereas the surfaces of samples in group C were uniformly sprayed with 200 μL of plant-derived saponin solution (1 mg/mL). Immediately after treatment, the samples were wrapped with plastic wrap to simulate the packaging environment of vegetable products and to avoid cross-contamination between samples from different groups. The samples were then kept at room temperature overnight. Thereafter, the lettuce samples were immersed in 4 mL of sterile PBS and vortexed for 2 min, followed by serial dilution and spread plating. Each group included three replicates.

Extraction of intracellular metabolites

S. aureus samples were treated with plant-derived saponins at 1 × MIC, and metabolites were extracted using a methanol-water solution (4, 1, v/v). The samples were subjected to ice-bath ultrasonication at 1620 W for 10 min (6 s on, 4 s off), followed by an additional 20 min of ultrasonication in an ice-water bath, and then left to stand overnight at −40 °C. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and 400 μL of the supernatant was transferred to glass derivatization vials and evaporated to dryness. The dried residue was then reconstituted in 200 μL methanol-water (1, 4, v/v), vortexed for 30 s, ultrasonicated again in an ice-water bath for 3 min, and left overnight at −40 °C. After centrifugation under the same conditions, 150 μL of the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Finally, the supernatant was carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS/MS analysis. Equal volumes of metabolites from each sample were pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples for online detection.

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for bacterial metabolism

Metabolite chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo UHPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The solvent gradient was programmed as follows: from 0 to 4 min, 95% A/5% B to 70% A/30% B; from 4 to 10 min, 70% A/30% B to 20% A/80% B; from 10 to 14 min, 20% A/80% B to 0% A/100% B; and from 14 to 16 min, 0% A/100% B to 95% A/5% B for system equilibration. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C. All samples were kept at 4 °C during analysis. Mass spectrometric data were acquired using a Thermo UHPLC-Q active mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, operating in either positive or negative ion mode. The optimal conditions were set as follows: auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 35 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 8 Arb; spray voltage, −3200 V in negative ion mode and 3800 V in positive ion mode; electron ionization (EI), ion source temperature, 230 °C; quadrupole temperature, 150 °C; and electron energy, 70 eV. The scan mode was full scan (SCAN), with a mass range of 50–500 m/z.

Bioinformatics analysis

Compound identification was based on multiple dimensions, including retention time (RT), accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and isotopic distribution, with reference to the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (http://www.hmdb.ca/), LipidMaps (version 2.3), the METLIN database (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and the local LuMet-Animal database. Pairwise comparisons of metabolites were performed using the R package ropls (version 1.6.2) for principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and permutation testing (200 iterations). In addition, Student's t-test and differential abundance analysis were conducted. Significantly different metabolites (DMs) were screened based on variable importance in projection scores from the OPLS-DA model and P values from the t-test, and the metabolic pathways involving these DMs were annotated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). The Python package scipy.stats was used for pathway enrichment analysis, and Fisher's exact test was applied to identify biological pathways most relevant to the experimental treatment. Corrected P values were calculated by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were considered DEMs. Each experiment included six biological replicates.

Molecular simulation study

Molecular docking was used to investigate the interactions between plant-derived saponins and 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (AroA) as well as 3-dehydroquinate synthase (AroB). The protein structures of AroA and AroB were generated using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/) and preprocessed, including bond correction, residue completion, hydrogen addition, removal of water molecules, and removal of all heteroatoms except the ligand (Ye et al., 2023). In addition, the plant-derived saponin ligands were prepared using LigPrep (version 3.4), including 3D conformer generation, protonation, and energy minimization based on the OPLS2005 force field to obtain the optimal conformations. Subsequently, molecular docking of the ligands to the proteins was performed using the standard precision (SP) docking algorithm in Schrödinger software. Finally, the interactions between the ligands and proteins were visualized using the Maestro graphical interface program (version 10.2) (Yang, Du, et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software LLC, Boston, MA, USA) was used for graph plotting and statistical analysis. Differences among groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc tests. Except for metabolomics analysis, which was performed in six replicates, all other experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Feature selection and fusion

The 1D and 2D features of plant-derived saponins (such as MW, XLogP, and nAcid) are mainly used to characterize structural information from the perspectives of molecular composition, physicochemical properties, topological structure, and atomic connectivity. The MACCS molecular fingerprint features further reflect the compositional characteristics of different structural scaffold fragments. In contrast, the 3D features (such as ATSC7v and AATSC7p) describe the spatial arrangement and stereochemical properties of the compounds based on their three-dimensional molecular conformations (Laoui & Polyakov, 2011).

This study adopted a strict multi-step feature screening strategy to effectively identify anomalous features and eliminate low-variance features. First, the raw data were cleaned before feature selection, including the removal of missing values, the treatment of infinite values, and the deletion of features with all-zero values. Anomalous features may lead to a large number of blank blocks in the feature matrix (Yan et al., 2025). After data cleaning, 579 1D&2D features, 29 3D features, and 107 MACCS fingerprint features were retained (Fig. 1.A, C, and E). Subsequently, pairwise correlations among the features were calculated, and highly similar features with correlation coefficients greater than 0.9 were removed. Highly correlated features may cause unstable parameter estimation in the model; therefore, removing features with excessively high correlation helps reduce multicollinearity issues (Tian et al., 2025). Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was further applied to screen the descriptors and retain features with higher contributions (Wang et al., 2020). After the above screening steps, 23 1D&2D features, 13 3D features, and 7 MACCS features were ultimately retained (Fig. 1). Subsequently, a multimodal early-fusion strategy was used to concatenate the selected 1D&2D, 3D, and MACCS features, thereby constructing a new high-dimensional vector containing 43 features (Zamani & Baleghi, 2023).

Machine learning model construction and performance evaluation

This study integrated multiple screened features, including 1D&2D features, 3D features, and MACCS features, and employed six classic machine learning and deep learning algorithms, namely random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), convolutional neural network (CNN), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and residual network (ResNet), to construct models for predicting the antibacterial activity of plant-derived saponins, thereby screening dietary saponins with antibacterial effects (Fig. 2). The area under the curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were used as core evaluation metrics to systematically compare the performance of each model (Han et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025).

Framework of the multimodal feature fusion machine learning model used in this study to identify saponins and their derivatives with antibacterial activity.

AUC can effectively measure a model's ability to distinguish positive and negative samples across different thresholds, and a value closer to 1 indicates stronger discriminative performance (Ma et al., 2024). The AUC values of the six models were all higher than 0.89, indicating that they all exhibited good discriminative ability and could effectively distinguish between antibacterial and non-antibacterial saponin structures. However, only the RF and SVM models achieved both ACC and F1-score values above 0.85, suggesting that, in terms of overall predictive accuracy as well as the balance between precision and recall, RF and SVM performed significantly better than the other four models (Fig. 3).

(D) Convolutional neural network (CNN). (E) Multilayer perceptron (MLP). (F) Residual neural network (ResNet). Solid lines represent the mean ROC curves across the five folds, and shaded areas represent the variability of the true-positive rates across folds, corresponding to one standard deviation above and below the mean. AUC values are reported as the mean ± standard deviation across the five folds.

Notably, although the KNN model achieved a relatively high AUC value, all other metrics were the lowest, particularly the MCC value, which was only 0.60, indicating poor performance in terms of classification reliability and balance. As a more robust classification metric, MCC comprehensively considers the four outcomes in the confusion matrix-true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives-and can obtain a high score only when an algorithm performs well across all classes; therefore, it more comprehensively reflects the model's true predictive ability (Oppen et al., 2023). The two machine learning models, CNN and MLP, showed similar performance, with AUC values of 0.91 and 0.90, ACC values of 0.83 and 0.82, respectively, and comparable F1-Score and MCC values. However, their overall performance was still lower than that of the RF, SVM, and ResNet models. The fusion model based on RF performed the best, with all evaluation metrics reaching the highest levels (Fig. 3A): the AUC value was 0.96, the ACC value was 0.88, the F1-Score was 0.89, and the MCC value was 0.77, all of which were significantly superior to those of the other models.

Feature importance analysis

To better understand and interpret the factors influencing the antibacterial activity of dietary saponins, we implemented two complementary feature analysis methods. First, feature contributions were evaluated using permutation importance analysis. The core principle is to randomly shuffle the values of an individual feature and quantify its importance by measuring the decrease in model prediction performance (Vigneau et al., 2018). Subsequently, the game theory-based SHAP feature analysis method was applied to further assess the contribution of descriptors to model predictions (Wang et al., 2025). By calculating each feature's marginal contribution to the prediction outcome, this method provides an intuitive and consistent explanation of both the global and local predictive behavior of the model (Fan et al., 2022).

Both feature-importance analysis methods consistently identified several key descriptors, including FISA, QPPCaco, QPlogKp, AATSC1P, AATSC7v, and #rotor (Fig. 4). Among them, the 1D&2D descriptors FISA, QPPCaco, and QPlogKp, which are related to compound polarity and permeability, indicate that the antibacterial activity of dietary saponins is closely associated with their lipophilicity and hydrophilicity balance. Consistent with this, Xue et al. (2020) also reported that the antibacterial activity of dietary saponins is related to the hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) of their structures. AATSC1P, AATSC7v, and #rotor reflect structural characteristics such as molecular flexibility and van der Waals volume. Previous studies reported that the antibacterial efficacy of antimicrobial agents is related to their structural flexibility and van der Waals volume (Zaman et al., 2021). In addition, features such as ATSC1m, ATSC7v, ATSC7p, and ATSC8m are associated with 3D structural properties, suggesting that the antibacterial activity of dietary saponins is also highly correlated with their three-dimensional structural characteristics.

Ablation study of multi-modal fusion model

Further, ablation experiments were conducted to assess the superiority of the model integrating three types of features (Pang et al., 2025). To evaluate the contribution of each feature type to model performance, experiments were designed in which one feature type was ablated at a time, as well as experiments in which two feature types were ablated simultaneously (Table S3). AUC, ACC, and F1-score were used to compare the impact of ablation on the accuracy of the fusion model. For single-feature ablation, removing the 3D features had the greatest impact on model performance. When only one type of feature was ablated, the model accuracy decreased by no more than 10%, indicating that features from different molecular dimensions may be somewhat interchangeable in the model. However, compared with single-feature ablation, simultaneously removing two types of features had a greater impact on model performance. In particular, when both 1D&2D and 3D features were ablated, the model almost completely lost its predictive ability (AUC = 0.50, ACC = 0.53, F1-score = 0.60). This decline can be attributed to the neglect of differences in molecular topological parameters and intrinsic physicochemical properties, which are crucial for accurately predicting the bioactivity of structures (Li, Yu, et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023).

In summary, the ablation study showed that the AUC, ACC, and F1 values of all six feature combinations were lower than those of the original fusion model, indicating that ablating any one type of feature reduced model accuracy. This suggests that each feature type is important in the multimodal feature fusion model. It also indicates that multimodal models can capture the complex and intertwined interactions and relationships among different modalities, thereby achieving more accurate molecular property prediction (Cai et al., 2022; Pang et al., 2025).

Based on the multimodal fusion model, antibacterial saponins were mined

Dietary saponins in edible plants are mainly classified into five major types: spirostanol, furostanol, oleanane, ursane, and lupane saponins (Fig. 5). Yam is a well-known vegetable in East Asia, especially in China, and has been used as both food and traditional medicine for thousands of years. It contains large amounts of spirostanol and furostanol saponins (Epping & Laibach, 2020).In the yam saponin database constructed in this study, 239 saponins were included, of which 5 were predicted to have a high likelihood of antibacterial activity (Table S1).

Database of edible plant-derived saponins, including yam-derived saponins and derivatives.

The compound with the highest antibacterial potential in yam was hecogenin (He), which has previously been identified in Dioscorea as a bioactive component beneficial to the gut microbiota. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of hecogenin against S. aureus was determined using the conventional broth dilution method (Fig. 6B). Based on changes in OD600 absorbance, hecogenin showed a marked decrease at 256 μg/mL, accompanied by a reduction in the red particulate precipitate at the bottom of the 96-well plate (Aksic et al., 2023; Stojanovic-Radic et al., 2012). The results of the conventional broth dilution assay indicated that the MIC of hecogenin was 256 μg/mL. Compared with certain food-grade flavonoids and phenolic acids that exert antibacterial effects at relatively low concentrations, pure hecogenin exhibits moderate antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, with an MIC of 256 μg/mL. Nevertheless, hecogenin showed stronger inhibitory activity than eugenol, with an MIC of 500 μg/mL (Wang et al., 2026), which has been widely studied in food science. It also demonstrated better antibacterial performance than tea saponin, with an MIC of 500 μg/mL (Zhao et al., 2020), a representative antibacterial saponin. Therefore, despite its moderate individual antibacterial potency, the development strategy based on processing by-product resources retains research value. However, hecogenin is not suitable for use as a standalone food preservative and may be more promising as a component of compound preservative formulations. A comprehensive economic feasibility assessment requires further validation through pilot-scale extraction and scale-up experiments.

Application of dietary saponin antibacterial agents in food preservation

Over the past decade, numerous studies worldwide have reported the detection of S. aureus and MRSA strains at various stages of food production and the supply chain (Dos Santos et al., 2026; Khalifa et al., 2025). Among retail vegetable samples, lettuce has shown the highest prevalence of S. aureus (Wu et al., 2018). Plant-based food matrices are increasingly being explored as practical platforms for applying functional ingredients, probiotics, and plant-derived bioactive compounds, while also enabling the evaluation of their effects under realistic food conditions (Praia et al., 2022; Suzuki et al., 2024). In this context, the lettuce model used in this study provides a practically relevant preliminary food application platform for evaluating the potential antibacterial application of hecogenin in fresh fruits and vegetables.

This study systematically evaluated the inhibitory effect of hecogenin on the growth of S. aureus on lettuce by simulating bacterial contamination and antimicrobial treatment during food preservation. Artificially contaminated lettuce was sprayed with PBS (Control group) or hecogenin solution, wrapped with plastic film, and left at room temperature overnight. The treated samples showed no visible effect on food appearance (Fig. 6C). Analysis of the bacterial suspensions collected from the contaminated lettuce showed that the wild-type background level in the sterile negative control group of lettuce was relatively high (2.40 log CFU/g), which may be due to the complex microenvironment on the lettuce surface, such as folds, waxy layers, and natural microbial colonization (Jackson et al., 2013). However, after hecogenin treatment, the residual S. aureus count in the artificially inoculated lettuce decreased from 6.83 to 3.93 log CFU/g (Fig. 6D). This indicates that hecogenin can still effectively eliminate pathogens in practical application scenarios and keep the total bacterial count at a relatively low level, thereby avoiding microbial imbalance caused by antimicrobial treatment. The microbial balance on vegetable surfaces is crucial for maintaining freshness (Aruscavage et al., 2008). In addition, because hecogenin is a natural component found in yam and has relatively high safety for human health, it may serve as an ideal candidate to replace traditional chemical preservatives.

Metabolomics explanation mechanism

In recent years, integrated omics strategies have increasingly been applied in food-related systems to associate compositional characteristics with microbial responses, thereby providing methodological support for evaluating the mechanisms of bioactive compounds (Herkenhoff et al., 2023; Suzuki et al., 2025). To elucidate the inhibitory mechanism of the dietary saponin antibacterial agent against S. aureus, LC-MS/MS was employed to analyze differences in intracellular metabolites between the hecogenin-treated group and the control group. As shown in the PCA score plot (Fig. 7A), the hecogenin-treated group was clearly separated from the control samples, indicating a marked difference between the two groups. Subsequently, OPLS-DA analysis (Fig. 7B) also revealed significant separation between the two groups, suggesting that the saponin antibacterial agent induced bacterial metabolic disruption (Sun et al., 2023). To further visualize the differences in metabolite expression between the hecogenin-treated group and the control group, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed based on the differential metabolites. The hierarchical clustering results (Fig. S2) showed complete separation of the two groups, indicating significant differences in their metabolic profiles.

(D) Metabolic pathway enrichment plot for S. aureus treated with hecogenin. (E) Four significantly differential metabolites.

Further volcano plot analysis was performed to examine metabolite changes among different groups, and a total of 2901 metabolites were identified. After hecogenin treatment, 1878 metabolites were downregulated and 252 metabolites were upregulated in S. aureus (Fig. 7C). Overall, the major marker metabolites showed a downward trend following hecogenin treatment, further confirming the above results. The key downregulated metabolites included L-Phenylalanine, L-Tryptophan, L-Tyrosine, and L-Glutamate (Fig. 7E). To further analyze the functions of these metabolites, these compounds were mapped to the KEGG database, based on which the top 20 significantly enriched metabolic pathways were identified (Fig. 7D).

The antibacterial mechanism of dietary saponins

Amino acids are essential for protein synthesis and serve not only as important nutrients but also as key components for microbial environmental adaptation and metabolic regulation. Among them, amino acid metabolism is considered one of the most important metabolic pathways (He et al., 2024). After hecogenin treatment, several amino acid metabolism pathways were significantly altered, including phenylalanine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, lysine degradation, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Fig. 8).

Overview of metabolic changes in S. aureus after hecogenin treatment. Blue indicates significantly downregulated metabolites (P < 0.05), and red indicates significantly upregulated metabolites (P < 0.05). (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

L-Phenylalanine is a rigid amino acid that shares an upstream biosynthetic pathway with L-Tryptophan and can serve as a key precursor involved in the biosynthesis of various bacterial metabolites. In the phenylalanine metabolic pathway, L-Phenylalanine, phenylethylamine, 3-hydroxy-phenylacetate, and fumarate all showed a downward trend. Disruption of phenylalanine metabolism may impair bacterial protein synthesis (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, the disturbance of the phenylalanine metabolic system in S. aureus after hecogenin treatment suggests that its protein synthesis may have been affected. Liang et al. (2024) found that amino acid metabolism in essential oil-treated Streptococcus agalactiae was severely disrupted, which was attributed to the downregulation of phenylalanine and L-tryptophan. Meanwhile, the downregulation of fumarate can significantly inhibit the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby directly affecting energy metabolism and normal bacterial growth (Yuan et al., 2023).

As an important metabolite in microorganisms, glutamate is crucial for resisting oxidative stress and maintaining the redox balance within bacterial cells (Xie et al., 2019). In S. aureus treated with hecogenin, glutamate showed a downward trend. This may be attributed to insufficient intracellular energy supply, which makes it difficult to maintain normal transmembrane transport function, thereby accelerating glutamate oxidative decarboxylation and generating more 2-oxoglutarate, which then enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to restore energy supply (Yuan et al., 2023). In addition, the downregulation of glutamate may also lead to decreases in UMP and FGAR, thereby indirectly regulating pyrimidine metabolism and purine metabolism (Fig. 8).

Molecular simulation verified the antibacterial mechanism

Metabolomics results indicated that the metabolism of tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine was affected (Fig. 7E and Fig. 8). 5-Enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (AroA) and 3-dehydroquinate synthase (AroB) are key enzymes in the biosynthetic pathway of aromatic amino acids and are responsible for the synthesis of aromatic amino acids such as tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine (Shi et al., 2025). Previous studies have highlighted the important roles of AroA and AroB in various bacteria; reduced expression of these enzymes can significantly disrupt aromatic amino acid biosynthesis, thereby impairing bacterial growth and reducing virulence (Buzzola et al., 2006).

Molecular docking results showed that hecogenin could stably bind within the active pockets of AroA and AroB and exhibited good spatial complementarity (Fig.S1A and B). CHEMBL1383715 and IMB-T130, two known inhibitors, were employed as positive controls for AroA and AroB, respectively. The docking scores of hecogenin against AroA and AroB were − 6.471 kcal/mol and − 8.082 kcal/mol, which were slightly superior to those of the two reference inhibitors (−5.583 kcal/mol for CHEMBL1383715 and − 6.215 kcal/mol for IMB-T130). Hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions constituted the major binding forces between hecogenin and the two target enzymes. In the AroA-hecogenin complex, hydrogen bonds were formed with ASN258, ALA288, and HIE203, along with hydrophobic interactions involving residues such as PRO233 and PHE230. In contrast, the binding of hecogenin to AroB was mainly mediated by hydrogen bonds with GLN171 and PHE163. Previous studies have shown that hydrogen-bond interactions can significantly enhance the binding strength between antibacterial agents and bacterial enzymes (Bai et al., 2023; Noshad et al., 2022). These results suggest that the inhibitory activity of hecogenin against S. aureus may be associated with its action on two key enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of aromatic amino acids such as tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that a machine learning approach based on multimodal feature fusion can be effectively used to mine dietary saponins with antibacterial activity from edible plants, providing a feasible strategy for the rapid discovery of novel antibacterial agents in the food industry and helping address the increasingly severe problem of bacterial resistance. Based on this approach, we successfully screened and identified a potent antibacterial saponin, hecogenin, from Yam. Derived from an edible food source, this saponin offers good dietary safety and thus has considerable potential for practical development. Further validation in a lettuce model showed that hecogenin could still significantly reduce foodborne bacterial contamination in real application scenarios, demonstrating good potential for food preservation. Metabolomic analysis revealed that hecogenin could inhibit bacterial growth by interfering with tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine metabolism in S. aureus; molecular simulation results further indicated that its antibacterial effect may be related to hecogenin acting on the key enzymes AroA and AroB in the aromatic amino acid biosynthesis pathway. In summary, as a natural bioactive compound derived from edible plants, hecogenin exhibits excellent antibacterial performance and promising potential for food preservation applications. Meanwhile, the multimodal feature-fusion machine-learning screening system established in this study also provides a new research paradigm and technical reference for the efficient mining and development of plant-derived antibacterial bioactive compounds.
