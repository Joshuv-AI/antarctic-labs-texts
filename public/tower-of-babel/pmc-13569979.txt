The Impact of Upfront DihydroPyrimidine Dehydrogenase (DPD) Testing on the Therapeutic Management of Patients Scheduled for 5‐FU
Authors: Govind Kallee, Sébastien Salas, Gérard Milano, Florence Duffaud, Laetitia Dahan, Joseph Ciccolini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) is catabolized by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD). DPD deficiency can trigger life‐threatening toxicities, and pretesting DPD is increasingly recommended. Here, we investigated the impact of upfront DPD testing on treatment strategy, that is, whether DPD status influences physicians' choice to use 5‐FU or not, and if so, whether to maintain standard dosage or reduce it.MethodsThis is a non‐interventional, observational study of 525 adult patients likely to be treated with 5‐FU. Upfront DPD status was determined by measuring plasma uracil. Multivariate logistic regression was then used to investigate the factors influencing treatment strategies. A further analysis was then performed on a subset of 419 fit patients eligible for 5‐FU. Finally, we studied the impact of adaptive dosing on clinical outcomes (efficacy and toxicity) in the 374 patients who received 5‐FU eventually.ResultsDPD testing identified 392 extensive (EM; 74.7%), 78 poor (PM; 14.8%), and 55 ultra‐rapid metabolizers (UM; 10.5%). Multivariate analyses identified performance status (p = 0.001), DPD status (p = 0.006), age (p = 0.004), metastatic stage (p = 0.04) and history of prior 5‐FU treatment (p = 0.002) as factors influencing treatment strategy. PM were more likely to be treated without 5‐FU (22%) than EM (3%) or UM (10%) patients (p = 0.002). Similarly, of those treated with 5‐FU, 100% of PM had a dose reduction, compared to 9% and 25% of EM and UM patients, respectively (p < 0.001). No difference in efficacy (response rate: 51.6%, progression‐free survival: 14.7 months) and safety (severe toxicities: 4.3%) was found, regardless of the DPD status and subsequent dose adaptation, but reduced survival was observed in patients for whom 5‐FU was excluded.ConclusionUpfront DPD testing has a strong impact on treatment strategies, and subsequent adaptive dosing limits the risk for severe toxicities without compromising efficacy in real‐world patients.

Introduction

5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) is a pivotal drug in oncology, in both its injectable and oral prodrug form (i.e., capecitabine) [1, 2]. DPD deficiency is a pharmacogenetics syndrome possibly leading to severe or fatal toxicity after standard doses of 5‐FU or capecitabine [3]. Several methods based upon genotyping or phenotyping strategies have been proposed to determine a priori the DPD status in patients to avoid adverse events [4, 5, 6, 7]. As the conversion of endogenous uracil (U) to dihydrouracil (UH2) is catalyzed too by liver DPD, and despite technical drawbacks requiring rapid sample shipping and processing, measurement of plasma U levels can be used as a cheap and convenient surrogate marker of DPD activity. In 2018, French Health Authorities made the detection of DPD deficiency mandatory before starting 5‐FU or capecitabine treatment [8]. The threshold for DPD deficiency is set at U > 16 ng/mL with a recommendation for dose reduction for patients with Poor Metabolizer profile (PM) and contraindication of use for patients with complete deficiency (i.e., U > 150 ng/mL) [8, 9]. However, no precise guidelines have been issued regarding the extent to which dosing should be reduced in patients with intermediate (i.e., mild to severe) DPD deficiency. Other institutions, such as the European Medical Agency, the UK National Health Service (NHS) and the Dutch Pharmacological Genetics Working Group, have issued recommendations concerning DPD screening, mostly by genotyping DPYD, with DPYD genotype being next converted into an activity score. However, these recommendations have not yet been made mandatory, as in France [10]. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC), guidelines suggest that patients with gene activity score of 1 or 1.5 are to start therapy with 50% of the standard dose of fluoropyrimidine drugs [11]. In a previous single‐center study on 352 patients scheduled for 5‐FU with upfront DPD testing [12], we showed that DPD ultra‐rapid metabolizer status was not associated with lack of efficacy and found that PM patients with reduced dosing of 5‐FU had a similar toxicity/efficacy ratio than other patients [12]. However, we did not study in detail how the DPD PM status was used by the prescribers and what kind of therapeutic decision was undertaken (i.e., reduction in 5‐FU dosing or selection for alternative therapy). The aim of this new study was thus to further investigate now, on an extended number of patients, the impact of upfront DPD testing on the selection of treatment modalities for routine cancer patients scheduled for fluoropyrimidine drugs.

Patients and Methods

This non‐interventional observational study was based on an analysis of DPD tests conducted at the Marseille University Hospital (France) between January 2020 and June 2022. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Cellule d'Evaluation de la Direction de la Recherche Clinique et de l'Innovation (DRCI), APHM Marseille, France) and was conducted in accordance with the European recommendations concerning the regulation of clinical trials (EU) 536/2014 and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR; EU) 2016/679. The study has been assigned French legal MR004 status for non‐interventional studies and registered as PADS#25‐201. Informed consent was waived by the DRCI of the APHM due to the non‐interventional nature of this study, as all patients were treated following standard guidelines, with no additional sampling or examination. For that reason, no therapeutic drug monitoring of 5‐FU was undertaken, as this is not a standard practice per French national guidelines.

DPD status determination: DPD was assessed by measuring plasma U levels at baseline [13]. Uracilemia was measured by UPLC‐UV using a technique validated according to European Medical Agency and ISO 15189 guidelines, with a limit of quantification of 5 ng/mL and precision and accuracy both below 15% [14]. Patients were then classified as follows: ultra‐rapid metabolizer (UM, U < 5 ng/mL), extensive metabolizer (EM, 5 ng/mL ≤ U ≤ 16 ng/mL), poor Metabolizer (PM: 16 ng/mL < U < 150 ng/mL) and with complete DPD deficiency (U ≥ 150 ng/mL). For PM patients, the reduction in dose ranged from −15% to −75% of the standard dose, depending on the level of deficiency plus other clinical or biological parameters, based upon physician's choice. Patients with complete deficiency (i.e., U > 150 ng/mL) were formally contraindicated per French guidelines.

The data were collected retrospectively from patient medical records in our institution's database. Those consisted of demographic, clinical, and biological information's. All patients were treated in accordance to each validated protocol specific to their disease. However, prescribers could perform additional dose adjustments, due for instance to comorbidities or poor performance status.

The primary objective of this study was to determine how the DPD status influences the prescriber's therapeutic strategy in patients possibly scheduled for a regimen with 5‐FU. We thus focused on cancers for which the pivotal drug was 5‐FU, namely: colorectal, pancreatic, head and neck, gastric and esophageal cancers [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

The primary endpoint was the relationship between DPD status (i.e., PM, EM, UM) and treatment strategy as determined by the observation among patients of a significantly different adjusted odd ratio (OR) at the 95% confidence interval in prescriber's decision to use, or not, 5‐FU.

The secondary endpoint was the impact of DPD status on subsequent dose reductions from the standard dose in the subset of patients treated with 5‐FU.

Finally, the last endpoints were related to clinical outcome: toxicity (CTCAE version 4.0 grading) and efficacy (Complete Response (CR), Partial Response (PR), Stable disease (SD), Progressive disease (PD), Recist criteria version 1.1; Progression‐Free Survival (PFS) and survival rate at 18 months) were analyzed with respect to the DPD phenotypes (i.e., PM, EM, UM) with subsequent dose adjustment, and with respect to keep, or not, 5‐FU.

Statistical analysis: Multivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the relationship between initial treatment choice (i.e., 5‐FU vs. no 5‐FU) and the explanatory variables, including age, gender, World Health Organization (WHO) score, DPD status, renal impairment, cancer stage and pretreatment. Multicollinearity was checked using the Belsley–Kuhn–Welsch technique. The impact of DPD status on subsequent dose reductions of 5‐FU from the standard dose, and on the clinical outcome, was assessed with an analysis of variance (ANOVA), a Welch ANOVA and chi‐square test or for low cardinality outcome or Fisher exact test (depending on the number of patients) for low cardinality outcome. The alpha risk was set at 5% and two‐tailed tests were used. The analyses were performed using the module Stat of SciPy library in Python version 3.11.0.

Results

Of the 647 patients initially available in the institution database, 40 individuals (6.2%) with cancers for which 5‐FU was not the reference treatment were excluded. For 23 (3.6%) patients, no follow‐up information was retrieved in their medical files, 39 (6%) other patients died before treatment had started, in 7 (1.1%) cases acute renal failure prevented the start of treatment, and 13 (2%) patients refused all treatment, and those patients were excluded too. A total of 525 (81.1%) cases were therefore finally analyzed to determine to what extent DPD status influences the treatment strategy.

Male/female sex ratio (%) was 57.8/42.2, median age 67.3 (range: 20–97, mean 67.1), 18.5% of whom were 75 or over. Sixty‐one percent of patients had preserved quality of life (WHO score: 0–1).

Out of those 525 patients, upfront DPD testing identified 392 EMs (74.7%), 78 PMs (14.8%) and 55 UMs patients (10.5%). No patient with complete DPD deficiency (i.e., U > 150 ng/mL) was found during the observation period.

The primary site of cancer was predominantly gastrointestinal (90.3%), with colorectal cancer accounting for the majority (40.6%), followed by pancreatic cancer (28.6%), gastric cancer (8.6%) and esophageal cancer (7.8%). Metastases were observed in 49.7% of cases. Almost 11% of patients (10.6%, 55 patients) were already receiving chemotherapy for their cancer at the time of uracilemia measurement, including 33 patients (6.3%) who had previously received 5‐FU and were treated for relapsing disease. Three hundred and seventy‐four patients received 5‐FU (71.2%), 76 (14.5%) received treatment without 5‐FU (other chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy with or without targeted therapy), 42 received palliative treatment (8.0%), and systemic treatment was deemed unnecessary in the remaining 33 patients (6.3%). The mean follow‐up period was 19 months (range: 0–51). The results of the univariate and multivariate analyses are given in Table 1.

The DPD status was a predictive factor in the decision‐making process. Next, further analysis was conducted exclusively among eligible patients who were scheduled for 5‐FU treatment without any relative contraindications irrespective of the DPD status (such as age, performance status, prior therapeutic interventions, or severe comorbidities). This analysis was undertaken to identify potential confounding factors that had not been adequately addressed in the prior regression logistic analysis.

A total of 419 fit patients were thus identified as fit and considered eligible for 5‐FU‐based therapy (318 EM, 58 PM and 43 UM). Of these, 175 (41.8%) had colorectal cancer, 113 (27%) had pancreatic cancer, 57 (13.6%) had head and neck cancer, 41 (9.8%) had stomach cancer, and 33 (7.8%) had esophageal cancer. As in the previous overall analysis, we found that a lower proportion of PM individuals were treated with 5‐FU (77.6% vs. 91.1%, p=0.002), particularly those with pancreatic (p = 0.02) or head and neck (p = 0.03) cancers (Figure 2).

The vast majority (95.4%) of patients with colorectal cancer received 5‐FU, regardless of their DPD status (Table 1). However, when considering all tumors, PM patients had either 5‐FU excluded (22.4%) or had a dose reduction (77.6%). When 5‐FU was administered to PM patients, the dose was reduced by an average mean of 40% (range: 20%–75%). Conversely, patients with reduced dosing of 5‐FU were only 9.5% and 25% in UM and EM patients, respectively (p < 0.001, Table 1; Figure 3).

Finally, when focusing on the subset of 374 patients treated by 5‐FU, no statistical difference was found in the clinical outcome, regardless of their DPD status and subsequent adjustments in dosing. Regarding efficacy, the therapeutic response (PR + CR) was observed in 57.8% of patients (60% PM, 56.8% EM, 61.9% UM, p = 0.817 n.s.). PFS was 19.8 months, 13.1 months and 16.7 months in PM, EM and UM patients, respectively (p = 0.715, n.s.). Survival rate at 18 months was 71.1%, 64.1%, 71.5% in PM, EM and UM patients, respectively (p = 0,357, n.s.) (Table 2).

A total of 16 cases of severe toxicity were documented, including 7 cases of acute renal failure, 5 cases of bone marrow failure, 3 cases of pulmonary embolism and one case of intestinal obstruction. The incidence rates were 4.7%, 4.4% and 4.6% for UM, EM and PM patients, respectively (p = 0.561 n.s.). Overall, we observed that the incidence of severe toxicities (i.e., CTCAE grading ≥ 3) were below 5%, regardless of the DPD status (Table 2).

Due to the small number of patients (i.e., 37 patients selected for other treatment modalities, mostly with pancreatic and head and neck cancer), it was not possible to statistically compare the clinical outcomes of patients for whom 5‐FU was precluded with those of patients on a standard or reduced 5‐FU regimen. However, a downward trend in the survival rate at 18 months was observed in patients with pancreatic or head and neck tumors who had not received 5‐FU therapy in comparison to patients who had received 5‐FU (38.8% vs. 50.4%).

Discussion

Determining the most effective way to identify patients with DPD deficiency is still an ongoing question. Over the last 3 decades, several strategies have been proposed, mostly based on genotyping DPYD to detect variants associated with reduced activity score, or on direct or surrogate functional testing. Both approaches have pros and cons in terms of cost (and subsequently, reimbursement), specificity, sensitivity and suitability for large‐scale screening and implementation in routine clinical practice [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Subsequent guidelines for adaptive dosing strategy may differ as well. In France, assessing DPD deficiency is part of the standard pretreatment evaluation for cancers that can be treated with 5‐FU or capecitabine since 2018. Although complete DPD deficiency formally contraindicates treatment with these drugs, the current recommendations for patients with intermediate deficiency are unclear and simply advocate a reduced dose, at physician's discretion [8]. A recent study based upon the CPIC guidelines in patients with gastro‐intestinal cancer suggested that 50% dose reduction in patients bearing DPYD allelic variants could be over‐conservative and associated with under‐exposure next [21]. A similar concern has been raised by the ALPE2U study based on U measurement, which involved reducing the dose by 50% for PM patients [22].

Many studies have already described the implementation of DPD testing at the bedside to improve their safety. However, it is not yet clear to what extent patients with intermediate DPD deficiency have their treatment modified in real‐world settings. Indeed, most of the studies focus on logistical and practical considerations [23, 24]. However, one meta‐analysis [25] examined the relationship between genotype‐guided testing and subsequent dose reduction in fluoropyrimidines but did not consider shifts toward alternative treatment regimens. For instance, genotype‐guided dosing of capecitabine or 5‐FU has been demonstrated to be equally safe and effective [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. According to [25], severe toxicity occurred in 18%–50% of patients with DPYD allelic variants who received a reduced dose compared with 14%–38% of patients with the wild type who received a standard dose. Taken together, these findings support the routine implementation of DPYD genotyping at the outset and align with CPIC recommendations.

The aim of our study was therefore to provide further information on the impact of determining DPD status on therapeutic shifts, beyond dose reduction.

Our study, although monocentric, indicates that patients with PM DPD status are more likely to be switched to alternative therapeutics that do not contain 5‐FU, when available. Notably, in patients with colorectal cancer, for whom 5‐FU is the backbone of all regimens, most DPD‐deficient patients continued to receive this drug, albeit at reduced dosages in 100% of PM patients. The same observation was made in patients with rectal or anal cancer, for whom 5‐FU is often the primary treatment [19, 20].

Our findings suggest that DPD PM status has a greater impact on other treatment options for pancreatic and head and neck cancers. Specifically, 100% of UM patients, 91% of EM patients, and 70% of eligible PM patients with pancreatic tumors received 5‐FU therapy (primarily mFolfirinox). The remaining patients received alternative therapeutic regimens that were considered less toxic, such as a gemcitabine‐based therapy [15, 30]. Of the 85 patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer, 64 were eligible for treatment with 5‐FU. Ultimately, 67.2% of patients received 5‐FU, with a notable distinction based on their DPD status: 83.3% of UM patients, 71.4% of EM patients, but only 33.3% of PM patients. Similarly, of the 57 head and neck patients eligible for treatment with a fluoropyrimidine drug, 51 received 5‐FU (100% of UMs, 92.5% of EMs, and 62.5% of PMs).

It must be highlighted that while 5‐FU was more likely to be discarded in patients with PM compared to other patients (excluding those with colorectal cancer), our data also indicate that PM patients were systematically treated with a reduced dose. Importantly, in a previous study on a smaller cohort, we already reported that clinical outcomes (i.e., efficacy and toxicity) were not statistically different in patients regardless of their DPD status (i.e., UM, EM or PM) [12]. Here again, we found that similar survival rate at 18 months and PFS were observed among our patients despite the differences in dosing strategies, whereas severe toxicities were always kept below 5%. Considering our analysis of 5‐FU dosing variations depending on the DPD status, it suggests that reducing 5‐FU dosages in PM patients can effectively mitigate the risk of severe toxicities without compromising treatment efficacy. It has to be highlighted that other studies [22] have questioned the relevance of using uracil as a reliable surrogate marker to customize 5‐FU dosing, on the basis that it could lead to under‐exposure in PM patients, although no data on actual lack of efficacy have been reported yet. Of note, in [31], unilateral dose reduction of 50% was decided in DPYD wt/Uhigh patients, whereas in our PM patients, dose reduction was more dynamic and spanned a 20%–75% range. In our study, no PK data were available in our cohort to check whether 5‐FU exposure remained in the target range (i.e., AUC comprised between 20 and 30 mg·h/L) or not, because this was not part of standard routine care in our institute. However, both 18 months survival and PFS figures suggest that reducing dosing in PM patients did not alter treatment efficacy, while improving safety.

Consequently, and despite the inherent limitations of a study conducted in a single institute, our findings suggest that determining the DPD status upfront using plasma U as a surrogate could be clinically beneficial in routine patients—an observation that is reinforced by the real‐world nature of this work reflecting the routine care in routine patients.

Importantly, in our study when other treatment modalities were available (such as for pancreatic, head and neck, or esophageal cancer), less toxic treatment options for PM patients were preferred to 5‐FU. This conservative approach is guided by previous reports suggesting that even a reduced dose of 5‐FU could lead to severe toxicities in certain circumstances [32]. Due to the small number of fit patients for whom 5‐FU was excluded because of their DPD PM status, it was not possible to perform statistical analysis regarding possible loss of efficacy. However, a numerical discrepancy was observed in the survival rate at 18 months. This suggests that the alternative regimen could be less effective than the 5‐FU‐based regimen. This hypothesis is fully consistent with the fact that, in both pancreatic and head and neck cancers, 5‐FU remains the backbone of first‐line therapy and removing it could thus be detrimental. Overall, improving the toxicity‐efficacy ratio of 5‐FU through upfront DPD testing is still a matter of debate because this drug is rarely administered as a single agent, and several other parameters such as performance status are probably also at play in adverse events [7, 10, 11, 13]. Therefore, focusing only on DPD status, as determined by genotyping or phenotyping, could be insufficient, and recent clinical reports have questioned its clinical relevance indeed [21, 22, 33]. Although our real‐world data show that current DPD testing contributes to reducing the risk of severe or lethal toxicities without compromising efficacy, there is still room for improving the practices at bedside, especially in the way dose reduction is performed in PM patients. A possible way to further improve the administration of 5‐FU would be thus to develop an algorithm that identifies its Maximum Tolerated Dose according to DPD phenotype and other factors such as sex, performance status, age, and associated cytotoxics. This could help maintain a finely tuned dosage of 5‐FU, rather than excluding the drug, which, as our data suggest, could have a negative impact on survival eventually. This could improve current empirical practices and be the next step in enhancing routine practice further.

Conclusion

This observational study shows that implementing upfront DPD testing as part of routine practice has a strong, independent influence on treatment choice: PM patients are less likely to receive a 5‐FU regimen than EM or UM patients. Regarding dose reduction, our survey shows that 5‐FU is always administered at a reduced dose to PM patients. The absence of any difference in clinical outcomes (efficacy or toxicity) suggests that reducing the 5‐FU dose in PM individuals helps to maintain an optimal toxicity‐efficacy ratio in cancer patients.
