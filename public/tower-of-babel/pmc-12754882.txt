Sustainable AI in medicine: navigating innovation, challenges, and environmental impact
Authors: Elena Bignami, Luigino Jalale Darhour, Valentina Bellini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

However, alongside these benefits, AI brings considerable environmental costs due to its high computational demands. The study by Pariso et al. on AI-driven energy management in Italian hospitals illustrates the dual nature of this transformation, highlighting both the potential for increased efficiency and the structural challenges involved. These requirements result in significant energy consumption, CO₂ emissions, freshwater use, and electronic waste. Data centers, essential to AI functionality, contribute notably to global electricity use and water stress, especially in areas already facing environmental constraints. To address these concerns, healthcare institutions should adopt strategies such as energy monitoring tools, lifecycle assessments, and low-carbon infrastructures. By aligning innovation, fairness, and environmental responsibility, AI can fulfill its promise of advancing medical science without compromising planetary health.

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming medicine, unlocking new possibilities to improve healthcare systems worldwide. Cutting-edge AI tools offer transformative potential to revolutionize clinical efficiency and patient care while reducing errors and optimizing resource allocation. However, this technological revolution entails inherent costs, necessitating rigorous evaluation to ensure its long-term viability and sustainability. In this context, recent studies, such as Pariso et al.‘s investigation into AI integration for energy management in Italian hospitals, highlight both the opportunities and the structural challenges involved in applying AI to resource-intensive healthcare environments [1]. At the same time, Green AI is emerging as a paradigm that emphasizes energy efficiency and sustainable approaches in the design, deployment, and application of AI technologies. It advocates for reducing the computational intensity of AI models through optimized algorithms, low-carbon infrastructures, and transparency in energy usage, ensuring that advancements in AI not only enhance healthcare but also align with global sustainability goals [2]. This discussion builds on those insights, exploring AI’s potential in medicine, the environmental challenges it poses, and innovative strategies to address them within a framework of sustainable digital transformation.

AI and its environmental footprint

As AI-driven solutions become progressively embedded in healthcare, it is crucial to consider the often-overlooked environmental implications associated with these technological advancements. While AI holds the promise of optimizing energy use and boosting efficiency, its implementation comes with substantial computational demands. These demands translate into significant environmental challenges, including greenhouse gas emissions, freshwater depletion, and electronic waste generation. Recent estimates quantify the healthcare sector’s climate footprint at approximately 4.4% of global net emissions, with hospitals ranking among the most energy-intensive public infrastructures. In this context, the integration of AI into healthcare systems must be critically evaluated not only in terms of clinical utility but also regarding its environmental and economic sustainability. A lifecycle perspective shows that 17% of healthcare emissions arise from direct activities (e.g., facilities and fleets), 12% from indirect energy use (e.g., heating and electricity), and a substantial 71% from the supply chain, including pharmaceuticals, devices, and IT systems all areas directly or indirectly impacted by AI integration [3]. The exponential growth in the computational power required to develop and deploy advanced AI models has risen concerns, making sustainability a critical consideration in healthcare innovation. Training and inference processes for AI models are particularly resource-intensive, with energy requirements influenced by factors such as dataset size, model complexity, and training duration. Research estimates suggest that AI servers consume between 85 and 134 terawatt-hours annually, accounting for approximately 1% of global electricity usage. This considerable energy consumption is further compounded by the frequent reliance on non-renewable energy sources, underscoring the urgent need for sustainable practices in AI development and deployment [4]. The environmental impact of AI in healthcare is particularly evident in radiology, where the development and deployment of AI models generate significant greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Radiology accounts for up to 1% of global GHG emissions, with data centers and computational efforts becoming major contributors due to the exponential growth of AI applications. For instance, training a single AI model in radiology can emit over 626,000 kg of carbon dioxide equivalents (CO2e), nearly five times the lifetime emissions of an average car [5]. Importantly, the environmental impact of AI extends beyond energy use. Data centers, the backbone of AI operations, require extensive cooling systems to prevent overheating. These systems often rely on freshwater, placing additional strain on local water resources, particularly in regions already facing water scarcity. The cooling process not only increases water consumption but also illustrates the complex and interconnected challenges of resource management inherent in AI infrastructure [6]. Moreover, CO₂ emissions from data centers vary depending on infrastructure design, energy mix, and cooling technologies employed. The absence of standardized reporting frameworks further complicates efforts to quantify and mitigate these emissions, posing a barrier to environmental accountability. Considering these issues, the findings of Pariso et al. gain further relevance: their study on AI-driven energy management in Italian hospitals emphasizes the need for structured approaches and monitoring tools to ensure both operational efficiency and sustainability [1]. Healthcare institutions can adopt a range of strategies inspired by industry best practices to address environmental risks. Implementing standardized tools to measure energy consumption, CO₂ emissions, and water usage is a critical first step. Transparent reporting mechanisms and lifecycle assessments of AI systems can promote accountability and guide targeted improvements. For example, integrating emission calculators and sustainability dashboards into hospital operations, especially in energy-intensive facilities can help organizations monitor and actively reduce their environmental footprint [7]. Adopting a circular approach to resource utilization offers another promising avenue for minimizing waste. Heat generated during AI operations can be repurposed for hospital heating systems, transforming a byproduct into an asset. Likewise, materials from decommissioned hardware can be recycled, extending their lifecycle, and reducing the demand for new raw materials. Innovations in algorithm design, such as energy-efficient models and data compression techniques can help reduce computational costs, lowering the environmental burden of AI applications. Cloud-based solutions, particularly those powered by renewable energy, offer scalable and centralized infrastructure with reduced impact. By consolidating systems and eliminating redundancies, cloud computing enhances efficiency while limiting emissions. Furthermore, advanced cooling technologies, such as liquid immersion cooling, and the retrofitting of existing data centers with renewable energy sources and waste heat recovery systems can amplify these sustainability benefits, ensuring that AI operations align with broader environmental goals [8]. A concrete example comes from the SickKids Hospital’s emergency department AI model: while the on-premise version consumes 2,947 kWh/year (1.2 tonnes CO₂), migrating it to cloud infrastructure reduced energy use to just 20 kWh/year (0.1 tonnes CO₂), achieving a 92% reduction in emissions [4]. This demonstrates how infrastructure choices can dramatically alter AI’s environmental footprint in clinical settings. Notably, regulatory frameworks are increasingly addressing the environmental impact of AI, providing guidelines to align technological advancements with sustainability goals. For instance, the European Union’s AI Act emphasizes transparency and accountability in high-risk AI systems, including those used in healthcare, and encourages energy-efficient practices in AI development [9]. Similarly, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has proposed guidelines for data center sustainability, targeting reductions in energy consumption and water use [10]. These policies underscore the importance of integrating environmental considerations into AI deployment, offering a structured pathway for healthcare institutions to adopt compliant and sustainable practices. By adhering to such regulations, stakeholders can ensure that AI innovations in medicine not only enhance clinical outcomes but also align with global environmental standards.

Economic implications of sustainable AI in healthcare systems

The integration of AI into healthcare systems, with the aim of reducing the climate footprint, has significant economic implications that deserve in-depth analysis. As highlighted by Kaushik et al. [3], the adoption of Green AI models such as Tiny AI can significantly reduce the energy costs associated with healthcare operations by optimising the use of computational resources and reducing processing times. These lightweight architectures are designed to deliver adequate performance with substantially lower energy demand, offering a promising strategy for healthcare systems seeking to balance innovation with sustainability. However, while Tiny AI and similar models may reduce operational costs and carbon intensity, their deployment in high-stakes clinical contexts requires careful assessment of potential performance limitations, particularly regarding diagnostic accuracy and robustness. Ensuring that energy efficiency does not come at the expense of clinical reliability is therefore a crucial component of the economic evaluation of Green AI. The initial implementation of these technologies requires substantial investment in IT infrastructure, staff training and upgrading of existing facilities. For example, their article points out that the adoption of smart meters and IoT sensors can identify anomalies in energy consumption, but this requires a significant initial expenditure for the installation and maintenance of such devices. Beyond model design, the economic sustainability of AI adoption depends on a complex interplay between upfront investment and long-term efficiency gains. Another relevant economic aspect is the trade-off between immediate costs and long-term benefits. Pariso et al. [1] highlight how, despite delays in the adoption of AI in Italian hospitals, the potential savings from predictive maintenance and energy consumption optimization can justify the initial investments. For example, reducing medical equipment downtime and energy waste can translate into significant economic savings in the medium to long term. However, the lack of a clear regulatory framework and financial incentives, as highlighted in the study, represents a critical barrier for many healthcare facilities, especially in contexts with limited resources.

AI as a tool for environmental sustainability in healthcare

While the environmental challenges posed by AI are significant, AI itself can serve as a powerful instrument to mitigate and in some cases even resolve the very issues it creates. In healthcare settings, AI can be strategically leveraged to optimize hospital energy consumption by analyzing usage patterns and identifying inefficiencies. Predictive models can help ensure optimal staffing, reduce the overuse of medical devices, and streamline supply chain logistics. These capabilities align closely with the objectives outlined by Pariso et al., who emphasize how AI can support energy managers in achieving greater operational efficiency and environmental sustainability within Italian hospitals [1]. AI can also play a crucial role in monitoring and optimizing water usage within healthcare facilities. Using sensors and predictive models, AI can detect consumption patterns and pinpoint inefficiencies, such as leaks or excessive usage in non-critical activities. For instance, real-time water consumption analytics can help optimize the management of routine operations like washing and sanitation, ensuring more responsible and efficient use of water resources. Furthermore, intelligent water management systems can alert hospital staff to areas where conservation practices could be implemented, such as water recycling systems thereby reducing the overall water footprint of the institution. In parallel, AI has the potential to enhance the management of medical waste, particularly in the sorting and recycling of healthcare materials. AI systems can accurately classify and segregate different types of waste, minimizing contamination risks and ensuring proper disposal or recycling. Continuous monitoring of waste streams by AI-driven platforms can also help identify opportunities to reduce unnecessary waste production and improve disposal efficiency-actions that can significantly lower both environmental impact and operational costs. Moreover, AI can play a pivotal role in designing circular production cycles, ensuring that waste generated in one process becomes a resource for another. This approach directly supports the broader shift toward sustainable resource utilization in hospitals. AI-powered platforms can also support the training of healthcare professionals in sustainable practices through gamified learning and interactive modules, encouraging behaviors such as reducing single-use items or optimizing energy usage-initiatives that can reinforce a culture of environmental responsibility [11]. Additionally, AI-driven chatbots and telemedicine platforms can contribute to sustainability by promoting personalized patient education and encouraging adherence to treatment plans and healthy behaviors, while supporting remote monitoring and home-based care. For example, wearable devices integrated with AI can notify both patients and clinicians of early warning signs, enabling timely interventions and reducing the need for hospital visits. This integrated approach not only optimizes resource use and reduces environmental impact but also ensures continuous, tailored support for patients in their home environments, further aligning clinical innovation with sustainability objectives [12]. Moreover, the environmental benefits of telemedicine are particularly notable. Systematic reviews report savings ranging from 0.7 to 372 kg CO₂e per consultation, depending on the transport method avoided, with real-world implementation in the UK leading to annual reductions of over 500,000 kg CO₂e by shifting just 15% of follow-up appointments online. These examples underscore how AI-enabled interventions can yield tangible environmental and economic benefits when applied within structured healthcare contexts [13].

Future perspectives

Future research should extend beyond the current environmental assessment of AI in healthcare to encompass its broader economic and policy dimensions. A rigorous evaluation of the cost-effectiveness and return on investment (ROI) of sustainable AI infrastructures is particularly needed. While the transition toward Green AI may entail high initial expenditures for digital infrastructure, workforce training, and system integration, long-term gains in operational efficiency, energy savings, and resource optimization could offset these costs over time. Developing economic models and cost–benefit frameworks capable of quantifying these trade-offs would provide healthcare administrators and policymakers with a clearer basis for decision-making. In this context, the adoption of methodologies such as lifecycle cost analysis or social cost–benefit analysis could illuminate how investments in sustainable AI yield both financial and societal returns. Equally important is the exploration of policy instruments that can render sustainable AI adoption more viable. Governments and health authorities could play a pivotal role through “green” incentives, subsidies, or tax benefits supporting hospitals that invest in low-carbon digital technologies and energy-efficient computational infrastructure. Furthermore, integrating environmental performance metrics into procurement criteria and accreditation standards could foster accountability and encourage institutional alignment with sustainability goals. Collaborative public–private funding models may also help distribute the financial burden of transitioning toward greener AI solutions, particularly in low-resource healthcare systems. A further line of inquiry concerns the trade-offs between environmental sustainability and clinical performance. While energy-efficient architectures such as “Tiny AI” promise lower emissions and reduced computational costs, their potential limitations in diagnostic accuracy, scalability, or robustness in high-stakes medical applications warrant careful scrutiny. Future empirical studies should aim to systematically compare the environmental and clinical performance of different AI models to ensure that gains in sustainability do not inadvertently compromise patient safety or quality of care. Finally, the integration of these dimensions calls for a conceptual framework linking Green AI, Fair AI, and Effective AI, illustrating how environmental efficiency, ethical responsibility, and clinical utility can coexist within a unified model of sustainable innovation. Such a framework could guide both research and policy toward achieving a form of digital medicine that is not only technologically advanced but also economically viable, ethically sound, and environmentally responsible. By aligning analytical rigor with pragmatic policy and design strategies, future work can ensure that the sustainability agenda in AI moves from principle to practice, reinforcing the dual commitment to human and planetary health (Fig. 1).

Conclusions

AI and emerging technologies offer transformative potential in healthcare, enhancing diagnostics, decision-making, and patient outcomes. However, the environmental costs of these innovations demand urgent attention. By adopting standardized measurement tools, promoting circular resource use, and leveraging AI for sustainability, healthcare systems can mitigate their ecological impact. Future research should investigate the environmental and resource implications of clinical workflows with and without AI. This includes analyzing energy consumption, material usage, and personnel efficiency. Such studies will provide insights into the trade-offs between the clinical benefits of AI and its environmental costs, guiding the development of sustainable healthcare practices. At the same time, future research should quantify the specific environmental impacts of AI in healthcare through life cycle analyses, explore potential rebound effects in clinical workflows, and build ethical and regulatory frameworks for the equitable distribution of the benefits of sustainable AI. The development of Fair AI is equally essential, focusing on ensuring equitable access to high-quality care and advancing global health equity by addressing social disparities. The WHO (2021) guidance on AI ethics emphasizes equitable access to AI-driven healthcare tools, particularly in low-resource settings. Current regulatory frameworks like the EU AI Act are beginning to address environmental impacts, requiring transparency about energy use for high-risk AI systems. Healthcare institutions should consider both the clinical efficacy and environmental justice implications of AI deployment, ensuring solutions don’t exacerbate existing disparities in healthcare access or create new environmental burdens for vulnerable populations [14].

Only through a joint commitment to innovation, justice, and ecological responsibility will it be possible to achieve digitally advanced medicine that does not compromise the health of the planet.
