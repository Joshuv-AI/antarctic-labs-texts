Potential of green tea-type processed tea from Eurya alata Kobuski leaves: a comprehensive analysis of major nutrients and bioactive components
Authors: Bin Wang, Ishtiaq Ahmad, Danhua Lin, Shiguo Chen, Xingqian Ye, Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLingmu tea, produced from Eurya alata Kobuski leaves, is a traditional non-Camellia sinensis tea, yet the chemical basis underlying its quality formation during green tea–type processing remains unclear. This study systematically investigated the effects of processing on sensory quality, chemical composition, and antioxidant capacity of Lingmu tea. Processed tea exhibited a bright orange–yellow infusion, a mellow and umami-dominant taste, and a mature, harmonious aroma characterized by chestnut-like, sweet, woody, and floral notes. Green tea–type processing increased total phenolic content from 183.20 to 224.59 mg/g and total flavonoid content from 87.31 to 120.11 mg/g. Metabolomic analysis revealed significant remodeling of non-volatile metabolites, characterized by flavonoid enrichment and catechin reduction. The major aroma contributors (ROAV > 1), included linalool, 1-octen-3-one, dimethyl sulfide, nonanal, and ethanol, with their dominant contributors differing markedly between fresh leaves and processed tea. Antioxidant capacity was functionally redistributed after processing: DPPH and superoxide anion scavenging activities decreased, whereas total antioxidant capacity increased substantially (from 208.31 to 346.77 μmol/g), while hydroxyl radical scavenging activity showed no significant difference. Overall, this study elucidates the chemical basis of quality formation in Lingmu tea and supports its evaluation, development, and standardization as a functional specialty tea.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

Tea (Camellia sinensis) is one of the most popular non-alcoholic beverages worldwide because of its unique flavor and diverse health benefits1. In parallel with the growing demand for functional beverages, non-Camellia teas derived from region-specific plant resources have attracted increasing attention for their distinctive sensory properties and potential health functions2. Unlike traditional Camellia sinensis teas, these products are typically produced from leaves, flowers, fruits, or roots of non-Camellia plants and often exhibit unique flavor profiles shaped by specific raw materials and processing practices. Although previous studies on representative non-Camellia teas have demonstrated that their flavor quality and bioactivity are closely associated with chemical composition3–5, systematic investigations into processing-induced chemical transformations and their impact on aroma formation remain limited.

Lingmu belongs to the family Pentaphylacaceae and is an evergreen shrub widely distributed in eastern China, Korea, and Japan6. The young leaves have long been harvested and processed by local residents to produce a traditional non-Camellia tea known as “Shenxian tea.” It has been traditionally consumed for over 1000 years, with records tracing its use back to the Tang Dynasty (7th–10th century), and it remains part of local dietary practices today. According to traditional medical theory, Lingmu tea is believed to dispel wind and clear heat, promote diuresis, reduce swelling, and support wound healing, and it is commonly used for physical conditioning and inflammation-related symptoms7. Compared with traditional Camellia sinensis teas, non-Camellia functional teas—including those derived from Eurya species—have been reported to exhibit markedly different chemical compositions and sensory characteristics. For example, tea produced from Eurya alata (“Qianlin tea”) has been reported to display an orange–yellow liquor color, high astringency, and intense floral–fruity aroma, clearly distinguishing it from conventional green tea8. In addition, studies on Eurya japonica have shown that although its leaves can synthesize small amounts of caffeine and theobromine, the activity of key N-methyltransferases involved in alkaloid biosynthesis is substantially lower than that in tea plants, indicating pronounced metabolic differences among Eurya species and between Eurya and Camellia9. These findings suggest that Eurya-derived teas may possess genus- and species-specific metabolic pathways and aroma formation mechanisms. However, despite these reports on related Eurya species and other non-Camellia teas, systematic studies on Lingmu tea itself remain scarce. In particular, comprehensive characterization of its chemical composition and the dynamic changes of key functional and aroma-related compounds during processing are still lacking, which limits scientific quality evaluation and further industrial development.

We hypothesized that processing induces metabolic reprogramming in Lingmu leaves, resulting in coordinated alterations in non-volatile metabolites and volatile compounds, which subsequently modulate aroma characteristics and antioxidant-related behavior. To test this hypothesis, this study compared fresh leaves (FL) and corresponding processed tea leaves (TL) of Lingmu. Specifically, this study examined processing-induced shifts in key metabolites, including non-volatile components and volatile compounds, to characterize their transformation patterns during processing. Changes in total amino acids, organic acids, mineral elements, polyphenols, and volatile components were investigated to reveal their transformation patterns during processing. Correlation analysis and multivariate statistical methods were applied to identify key differential compounds that showed significant changes during processing. In addition, changes in antioxidant-related components were analyzed to reveal their redistribution during processing and their contribution to functional properties. Together, these analyses clarify how metabolite shifts, aroma reconstruction, and antioxidant redistribution collectively contribute to the flavor formation and functional characteristics of Lingmu tea.

Results

Processing technology and sensory quality characteristics of Lingmu leaves

Lingmu fresh leaves were harvested and subsequently processed using a green tea-type manufacturing process (Fig. 1A, B). The sensory evaluation results are presented in Fig. 1C. Sensory color parameters were used to characterize the visual properties of the tea infusion. The infusion of TL exhibited a bright orange–yellow appearance. Compared with the water control, the ΔL* value was positive, indicating a higher brightness of the tea liquor, while both Δa* and Δb* values were also positive, suggesting a color shift toward red and yellow hues, respectively, which together contributed to a characteristic yellow–orange tone. And the ΔE value was as high as 74.51, far exceeding the threshold of visual perception, demonstrating that the color change was readily and unambiguously distinguishable by the human eye. Radar plot analysis further illustrated the overall sensory performance of the samples across multiple aroma and taste dimensions. In terms of aroma attributes (Fig. 1D), relatively high scores were observed for Strength and Persistence, indicating a strong initial aroma release and good retention of volatile compounds10. Chestnut and Harmony also showed prominent scores, reflecting the formation of a characteristic pan-fired green tea aroma profile. With respect to taste attributes (Fig. 1E), the radar plot revealed that Umami and Sweet were the dominant sensory characteristics of the tea infusion, accompanied by a high Harmony score. In contrast, Bitter and Astringent received low scores, suggesting a relatively mild bitterness and astringency in the final infusion. Overall, TL infusion is characterized by a high and persistent aroma, a mellow and refreshing taste, and a well-balanced sensory profile.

Differences in physicochemical properties of Lingmu leaves before and after processing

The physicochemical composition of tea leaves constitutes the material basis for their flavor characteristics and potential functional activities11. To clarify the effects of processing on the intrinsic quality of Lingmu leaves, the major physicochemical parameters of FL and TL were systematically compared (Table 1). Regarding the basic physicochemical indices, the ash content of FL was 5.03%, which decreased to 4.16% after processing. The most significant change was observed in moisture content, which markedly decreased from 73.09% in FL to 2.21% in TL. In contrast, the crude fiber content remained essentially unchanged before and after processing, indicating limited variation in structural polysaccharide components. Water extract content, an indicator closely related to liquor strength and flavor fullness, was 50.78% in FL and 47.69% in TL, remaining at a relatively high level in both samples. Regarding sugars, the soluble sugar content decreased from 54.69 mg/g in FL to 44.08 mg/g after processing. Glucose and fructose showed marked reductions, with glucose decreasing from 27.41 to 4.25 mg/g and fructose from 6.11 to 2.86 mg/g. Notably, sucrose was not detected in FL but was present at 3.28 mg/g in TL. Protein content increased from 107.72 mg/g in FL to 115.51 mg/g after processing, whereas total amino acid content showed no significant difference between FL and TL. Regarding functional components, total phenolic content increased from 183.20 to 224.59 mg/g, and total flavonoid content increased from 87.31 to 120.11 mg/g after processing. Caffeine was not detected under the applied extraction and analytical conditions in either FL or TL. Taken together, processing of Lingmu leaves was characterized by substantial moisture reduction, sugar redistribution, and increased levels of phenolic and flavonoid compounds, while maintaining relatively stable amino acid content.

Differences in amino acids, fatty acids, and mineral elements before and after Lingmu leaves processing

Amino acids are important endogenous nitrogen-containing constituents in tea leaves and represent a fundamental nutritional and biochemical component of tea12. To evaluate the effect of processing on amino acid composition, a total of 17 amino acids were quantified in Lingmu leaves based on acid hydrolysis followed by amino acid analysis (Fig. 2A). No significant differences were observed in either total amino acid content or relative compositional profiles between FL and TL, indicating that the applied green tea-type processing procedure largely preserved the overall amino acid pool. Among the quantified amino acids, histidine (His) was the most abundant, reaching 8.96 mg/g in FL and 7.71 mg/g in TL. Glutamic acid (Glu), aspartic acid (Asp), leucine (Leu), and lysine (Lys) were also present at relatively high levels. The similar abundance patterns of these amino acids before and after processing indicate that the amino acid composition remained relatively stable during manufacturing. In short, green tea-type processing did not result in marked quantitative changes in total amino acid levels or their relative distribution in Lingmu leaves.

Fatty acids are important precursors for tea aroma formation. To characterize the fatty acid profile of Lingmu leaves before and after processing, a total of 23 fatty acids were identified in FL and TL, including 11 saturated fatty acids and 12 unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 2B). The overall fatty acid profile showed that α-linolenic acid (C18:3, ω-3) and linoleic acid (C18:2, ω-6) were the dominant components in both sample, accounting for approximately 80.40% (FL) and 81.90% (TL) of the total fatty acid content, respectively. This confirms that unsaturated fatty acids were more abundant than saturated fatty acids in both samples. Among fatty acids with relatively high abundance, palmitic acid (C16:0) was the predominant saturated fatty acid, with comparable contents in FL (36.52 ± 1.50 μg/g) and TL (36.28 ± 0.81 μg/g). Whereas oleic acid (C18:1, ω-9) was the main monounsaturated fatty acid, with contents of 26.01 ± 0.79 μg/g in FL and 26.50 ± 0.50 μg/g in TL. Several fatty acids showed significant changes after processing. The content of α-linolenic acid (C18:3, ω-3) increased from 256.25 ± 8.44 μg/g in FL to 286.43 ± 5.71 μg/g in TL, corresponding to an approximate 1.12-fold increase (p < 0.05). Heneicosanoic acid (C21:0) was not detected in FL but reached 0.40 ± 0.05 μg/g in TL. In contrast, arachidic acid (C20:0) decreased from 1.37 ± 0.06 μg/g in FL to 1.18 ± 0.01 μg/g in TL (p < 0.05). A more pronounced decrease was observed for linolelaidic acid (trans-C18:2), which declined from 2.97 ± 0.06 μg/g to 1.45 ± 0.08 μg/g, corresponding to a 51.2% reduction (p < 0.01). In words, processing selectively altered the fatty acid composition of Lingmu leaves, while the dominant unsaturated fatty acid framework was largely retained.

Chemical elements play a key role in tea plant growth, quality formation, and nutritional value13. In this study, a total of 30 elements were detected in FL and TL of Lingmu leaves. These elements included 7 macroelements, 10 trace elements, 7 ultra-trace elements, and 6 heavy metals, reflecting a typical mineral enrichment pattern of tea plants (Fig. 2C). Among the macroelements, potassium (K) showed the highest content, with 12,869.84 mg/kg in FL and 12,591.69 mg/kg in TL, followed by calcium. Among the trace elements, manganese (Mn) was the most abundant, reaching 365.64 mg/kg in FL and 339.75 mg/kg in TL. Iron was the second most abundant trace element, and its content showed little change during processing, indicating good stability. Among ultra-trace elements, barium (Ba) showed a relatively high level and remained stable before and after processing. Regarding safety, the contents of all detected heavy metals were far below the maximum limits defined in the national food safety standard GB 2762-2017, indicating good food safety of Lingmu leaves. Taken together, most elements remained relatively stable after processing, suggesting that the processing procedure did not markedly alter the mineral profile. However, several elements showed clear processing-related responses.

Differences in polyphenolic compounds in Lingmu leaves after processing

Non-volatile metabolites, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, form the chemical basis that determines tea flavor and functional properties14. In this study, 1,766 polyphenolic compounds were identified in both FL and TL of Lingmu leaves (Supplementary Data Table S1). Among them, flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, coumarins, and tannins accounted for 54.6, 29.3, 9.9, and 5.2%, respectively (Fig. 3A). These results indicate that flavonoids and phenolic acids dominate the polyphenolic composition of Lingmu leaves. PCA revealed a clear separation between FL and TL along the first principal component (PC1, 68.38%) and the second principal component (PC2, 9.93%) (Fig. 3B), indicating that processing significantly reshapes the polyphenol metabolic profile. OPLS-DA further confirmed the clear distinction between the two sample groups (Fig. S1A), while hierarchical cluster analysis also revealed a distinct separation between FL and TL samples (Fig. S1B). Based on the combined criteria of VIP ≥ 1, |Log₂FC| ≥ 1, and −log₁₀(p) ≥ 1.3, a total of 719 differential polyphenols were identified, including 354 significantly upregulated and 365 significantly downregulated compounds (Figs. 3C and S1C, D). Notably, substituted and glycosylated flavonoids, including 6″-O-acetyl-pratensein 7-O-β-D-glucopyranoside, Rubusin A, and 3-O-Methylellagic acid 4′-sulfate, accumulated markedly after processing. In contrast, several polyphenols with higher structural reactivity, such as Peonidin-3-O-rutinoside and Proanthocyanidin A2, decreased significantly after processing (Fig. 3D). Pathway enrichment analysis showed that processing-related changes were mainly concentrated in flavonoid biosynthesis (Figs. 3E and S1E, F). Structural classification further revealed that Flavones and Flavonols accounted for the majority of processing-responsive polyphenols, whereas other subclasses, including Flavanols and Isoflavones, exhibited more limited and selective responses (Fig. 3F, G). Typical flavanol catechins, such as epigallocatechin gallate and gallocatechin derivatives, decreased after processing (Fig. 3H). In summary, these results demonstrate that processing induces extensive but structured changes in the polyphenolic profile of Lingmu leaves.

Changes in organic acids in Lingmu leaves after processing

To characterize the effects of processing on organic acid composition, organic acids in Lingmu leaves before and after processing were analyzed. In this study, 23 organic acids were detected (Fig. 4A), with gallic acid showing the highest content, followed by lactic acid. This indicates that the organic acid profile of Lingmu leaves is dominated by phenolic acids and small aliphatic acids. Processing significantly reshaped the organic acid profile of Lingmu leaves. Succinic acid and protocatechuic acid increased markedly in the TL, whereas salicylic acid, gallic acid, ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, quinic acid, tartaric acid, sinapic acid, lactic acid, vanillic acid, cinnamic acid, and ursolic acid showed clear decreases. Notably, the concurrent reduction of gallic acid and quinic acid was particularly significant. Further enrichment analysis of phenolic acid-related pathways revealed distinct metabolic alterations after processing (Fig. 4B). Processing significantly enhanced the biosynthesis of other gallic acid derivatives, protocatechuic acid derivatives, and phenylacetic acid derivatives, while the biosynthesis of p-coumaroyl sugars and caffeoylquinic acid derivatives was markedly suppressed. The simultaneous upregulation of protocatechuic acid-related derivatives and downregulation of p-coumaric acid and caffeoylquinic acid derivatives together form a characteristic phenolic acid metabolic remodeling pattern during Lingmu leaves processing. Taken together, these results indicate that processing induces a pronounced reconfiguration of organic acids and phenolic acid derivatives in Lingmu leaves.

Changes in flavor differences of Lingmu leaves after processing

Volatile metabolites represent another key indicator for evaluating tea aroma quality15. In this study, headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) was used to profile volatile compounds in FL and TL, resulting in the identification of 661 compounds (Supplementary Data Table S2). Classified by chemical type, these included terpenes (75 compounds, 13.09%), hydrocarbons (69 compounds, 12.04%), esters (60 compounds, 10.47%), heterocyclic compounds (60 compounds, 10.47%), alcohols (52 compounds, 9.08%), ketones (40 compounds, 6.98%), acids (36 compounds, 6.28%), aldehydes (28 compounds, 4.89%), phenols (27 compounds, 4.71%), aromatic hydrocarbons (13 compounds, 2.27%), and ethers (13 compounds, 2.27%) (Fig. 5A), indicating a highly complex and multilayered volatile metabolite profile in Lingmu leaves. PCA showed a clear separation between FL and TL in the volatile metabolite profiles, with the first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) explaining 78.46 and 9.24% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 5B). This suggests that processing markedly reshapes the overall aroma composition. Volcano plot analysis further revealed that, using a threshold of |Log₂Fold change| > 1 and −log₁₀(p value) > 1.3, 172 volatile metabolites were significantly upregulated while 149 were significantly downregulated between FL and TL (Fig. 5C). Statistical analysis of differential metabolites across volatile compound classes showed that processing significantly promoted the accumulation of organoheterocyclic compounds, ketones, and aromatic hydrocarbons, while reducing both the diversity and abundance of hydrocarbons and alcohols (Figs. 5D and S2). At the individual compound level (Fig. 5E), β-phellandrene, phenylethyl alcohol, 2,3-butanedione, and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural were significantly increased after processing, whereas β-myrcene and sulfur-containing compounds (such as dimethyl disulfide) showed a marked decrease.

Considering that tea aroma is influenced not only by volatile compound abundance but also by their olfactory thresholds16. Compounds with ROAV ≥ 1, indicating odor contributions equal to or exceeding that of the reference compound within the same aroma system, are regarded as substantially contributing to overall tea aroma, 20 and 23 key aroma-active compounds were identified in FL and TL, respectively (Fig. 5F, G, and Table 2). In FL, Linalool was the primary contributor (ROAV = 100), followed by several lipid oxidation-related compounds, including 1-Octen-3-one (ROAV = 25.71), 1-Penten-3-one (ROAV = 56.03), Nonanal (ROAV = 19.69), and Heptanal (ROAV = 26.98). In TL, 1-Octen-3-one became the dominant aroma compound (ROAV = 100), while Dimethyl sulfide (ROAV = 98.65), Linalool (ROAV = 39.86), Ethanol (ROAV = 38.58), and Nonanal (ROAV = 29.96) remained as backbone compounds. Flavor wheel analysis indicated that FL aroma was dominated by Green, Sweet, Citrus, and Fruity notes, whereas TL showed reduced Green, Sweet, Fruity, Apple, and Balsamic attributes and increased Ethereal and Almond-related notes (Fig. 5H, I). Sankey diagram analysis further illustrated the correspondence between differential volatile compounds and aroma traits, highlighting notable changes in butanal and dimethyl disulfide during processing (Fig. 5J). Overall, these results demonstrate that processing induces pronounced alterations in both the composition and relative contribution of volatile metabolites in Lingmu leaves.

Differences in antioxidant capacity of Lingmu leaves after processing

The antioxidant activity of tea primarily derives from its abundant phenolic compounds and related secondary metabolites17. Results from DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays showed that FL exhibited significantly higher scavenging activity than TL, with DPPH scavenging rates of 81.78% in FL compared with 37.24% in TL, and ABTS scavenging activities of 55.48 and 44.13%, respectively (Fig. 6A, B). In contrast, T-AOC measurements revealed the opposite trend, with TL showing significantly higher T-AOC than FL (346.77 vs. 208.31 μmol/g, Fig. 6C). Additionally, FL displayed stronger superoxide anion (O₂−·) scavenging activity compared to TL (35.92 vs. 17.92%, Fig. 6D), whereas hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging capacity did not differ significantly between the two (7.70% in FL and 9.63% in TL, Fig. 6E). These results indicate that different antioxidant assays reveal distinct response patterns between FL and TL, rather than a uniform change in antioxidant capacity after processing.

To further explore the intrinsic relationship between phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity, correlation analysis revealed distinct patterns between total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and various antioxidant indices (Fig. 6F). DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion scavenging activities were significantly negatively correlated with TFC, whereas T-AOC showed a significant positive correlation with TFC. Meanwhile, hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was significantly positively correlated with TPC. Taken together, these results indicate that different antioxidant assays reveal distinct response patterns between FL and TL, rather than a uniform change in antioxidant capacity after processing.

Discussion

The Lingmu tea infusion exhibited a bright yellow–orange appearance, as reflected by relatively high L*, a*, and b* values and a large ΔE value, indicating a visually distinctive liquor color that is readily perceivable by consumers. Such color characteristics are consistent with those typically observed in green tea-type products and may contribute positively to consumer perception and market acceptance. Sensory evaluation revealed high scores for aroma Strength and Persistence, suggesting that the tea infusion contains abundant and stable aroma-active compounds10. In addition, prominent scores for Chestnut and Harmony reflected the formation of a typical pan-fired green tea aroma profile, characterized by Maillard-derived nitrogen-containing heterocycles providing the core chestnut-like notes, together with lipid-derived thermal oxidation products modulating overall aroma harmony. Similar associations between chestnut-like aroma notes and thermal processing have been widely reported in green tea manufacturing. In terms of taste, the dominance of Umami and Sweet attributes, together with a high Harmony score, indicates a coordinated taste profile characterized by smoothness and balance. Such sensory features are commonly attributed to the presence of soluble amino acids and certain soluble sugars in the tea infusion. In contrast, Bitter and Astringent received low scores, suggesting a relatively mild bitterness and astringency in the final infusion. These sensory properties are consistent with the compositional features typically observed in processed green teas, as reported in the literature18. Overall, the sensory characteristics of Lingmu tea, including its bright liquor color, persistent chestnut-like aroma, and mellow, harmonious taste, reflect the integrated expression of multiple flavor-active components. These features are consistent with the typical quality attributes of green tea-type products and support the positioning of Lingmu tea as a well-balanced specialty tea.

From a physicochemical perspective, the reduction in ash content after processing suggests that certain mineral components may be partially lost. However, the ash level of the final product remains within the acceptable range for green tea and complies with relevant quality standards19. The pronounced decrease in moisture content is a key outcome of processing and is essential for enhancing storage stability and preventing microbial growth. In addition to improving shelf life, moisture removal promotes the relative concentration of soluble constituents within the tea matrix. This concentration effect, together with thermal-induced tissue disruption, facilitates the enrichment and release of aroma- and taste-related compounds, thereby supporting the development of a more intense sensory profile20. The observed decrease in soluble sugars, particularly glucose and fructose, indicates that these sugars were either degraded or involved in thermal reactions during fixation and drying, such as caramelization and Maillard-type pathways, which are known to contribute to flavor complexity. The appearance of sucrose exclusively in TL suggests dynamic interconversion or rearrangement of sugar fractions during processing, reflecting active carbohydrate metabolism21. Meanwhile, the increase in protein content accompanied by stable total amino acid levels implies that protein hydrolysis and amino acid consumption may occur simultaneously, resulting in no net change in the total amino acid pool. This observation is consistent with the relatively mild green tea-type processing applied in this study. In addition, the significant increases in total phenolic and flavonoid contents after processing are likely associated with the release of bound phenolic and flavonoid forms induced by thermal processing, as well as processing-related disruption of cellular structures and phenolic–matrix interactions, which together improve the extractability and apparent abundance of these compounds22. Furthermore, the absence of detectable caffeine suggests that Lingmu tea may serve as a potential low-caffeine tea alternative for caffeine-sensitive consumers. Taken together, the physicochemical reorganization of Lingmu leaves during processing—characterized by moisture loss, sugar transformation, and phenolic enrichment—creates favorable conditions for aroma formation, taste harmonization, and functional enhancement.

The overall stability of amino acid composition suggests that amino acid metabolism in Lingmu leaves is comparatively conserved under the applied green tea-type processing conditions. It should be noted that the apparent stability of total amino acid levels may result from a dynamic balance between protein hydrolysis and subsequent amino acid degradation or utilization during processing, with these concurrent biochemical processes offsetting each other at the total amino acid level. Fatty acid analysis further revealed selective metabolic remodeling during processing. The dominance of α-linolenic acid and linoleic acid in both FL and TL is consistent with the typical lipid composition of tea leaves and previously reported lipidomic profiles23. In addition to maintaining cell membrane structure, these unsaturated fatty acids constitute an important biochemical reservoir for aroma precursor formation24. The processing-induced increase in α-linolenic acid suggests enhanced availability of substrates for the lipoxygenase–hydroperoxide lyase pathway, promoting the generation of lipid-derived volatile compounds, particularly C8 and C9 compounds associated with fresh, green, and mushroom-like aromas25. In contrast, the reduction in arachidic acid and the marked decrease in linolelaidic acid indicate that certain fatty acids are more susceptible to thermal degradation or oxidative transformation under high-temperature conditions. The selective reshaping of the fatty acid spectrum thus provides a favorable biochemical basis for aroma evolution during Lingmu tea processing and is closely linked to the accumulation of lipid-derived aroma-active compounds such as 1-octen-3-one. Mineral element analysis showed that potassium remained the dominant macroelement, consistent with its role in osmotic regulation, enzyme activity, and transport of taste-related compounds26. The stability of calcium supports the maintenance of leaf tissue integrity during processing. Among trace and ultra-trace elements, manganese and barium remained relatively stable, while minimal changes in iron content indicate good elemental stability. The consistently low levels of heavy metals confirm the good food safety of Lingmu leaves and indicate that processing did not introduce contamination risks. Taken together, the stability of most mineral elements suggests that processing primarily affects organic metabolites while leaving the inorganic elemental framework largely intact, with observed changes mainly attributable to moisture loss and relative concentration effects.

Polyphenolic profiling revealed that processing is the primary driver of polyphenolic differentiation in Lingmu leaves. The accumulation of substituted and glycosylated flavonoids suggests enhanced resistance to thermal stress and oxidative conditions, highlighting their importance as functional markers of processing adaptation. In contrast, the reduction of highly reactive polyphenols, such as anthocyanin derivatives and proanthocyanidins, is consistent with their susceptibility to thermal degradation. Enrichment of differential metabolites in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, together with a structural shift from flavanols toward flavones and flavonols, underlies the coordinated metabolic reprogramming of polyphenols. This shift contributes to the attenuation of green, bitter, and astringent sensory attributes and supports the modulation of antioxidant behavior and flavor characteristics in processed Lingmu tea. Organic acid metabolism was also systematically remodeled during processing. The decreases in gallic acid and quinic acid, both closely associated with acidity, astringency, and polyphenol stability, suggest moderated acidity and reduced astringency through pH adjustment27. In contrast, the accumulation of succinic acid represents a distinct processing-related feature and may constitute a key chemical basis for improved freshness and flavor harmony28. Metabolic enrichment analysis further supports coordinated remodeling of phenolic acid derivatives, indicating that processing reorganizes organic acid metabolism as an integrated response rather than altering individual compounds in isolation.

Finally, volatile metabolite analysis demonstrated that processing exerts a broad and systematic influence on the aroma system of Lingmu tea. The redistribution of volatile compound classes and the accumulation of structurally complex compounds contribute to the attenuation of sharp green notes and the development of a more mature and balanced aroma profile. At the compound level, enrichment of β-phellandrene, phenylethyl alcohol, 2,3-butanedione, and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural enhances citrus, floral, creamy, and roasted notes29–31, while the reduction of β-myrcene and dimethyl disulfide suppresses raw-green and sulfurous odors32. Sulfur-containing compounds (such as dimethyl disulfide) typically impart a pungent, sulfurous note to tea infusions and may cause unpleasant off-odors when present at high levels. In this study, such compounds were found to be significantly reduced in Lingmu tea, which may be attributed to the relatively poor thermal stability of volatile sulfur compounds such as dimethyl disulfide, making them prone to volatilization, degradation, and secondary reactions during the roasting process33. In addition, their interactions with other thermally generated compounds and tea polyphenols may further reduce their accumulation or sensory impact, thereby contributing to the suppression of sulfur-related off-odors34–36. ROAV analysis further reveals a transition from a linalool-dominated fresh aroma in FL to a 1-octen-3-one-dominated mature aroma in TL. Linalool, a terpene compound with significant floral and citrus notes, is recognized as a core aroma component in many flower teas, green teas, and oolong teas37. 1-Octen-3-one imparts herbal, earthy, and mushroom-like notes and serves as a marker for mature and complex aromas in tea38. Together, these changes drive the transformation of aroma perception and establish key volatile markers reflecting the aroma-modulating effects of tea processing.

The divergent antioxidant behaviors observed between FL and TL suggest that processing does not simply alter the overall antioxidant intensity but instead reprograms antioxidant behavior across different radical systems. The higher DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion scavenging activities in FL indicate a stronger capacity for rapid electron or hydrogen donation, which is characteristic of radical scavenging mechanisms dominated by highly reactive phenolic structures39. This behavior is consistent with the enrichment of flavanols and catechin-type compounds in FL, which are characterized by multiple free hydroxyl groups and B-ring catechol or galloyl structures that favor fast electron or hydrogen transfer reactions. Such structural features are particularly effective in assays such as DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion scavenging, which rely on direct radical quenching40. In contrast, the significantly higher T-AOC value observed in TL reflects enhanced cumulative and system-level reducing capacity rather than fast radical neutralization. The T-AOC assay captures the synergistic reducing effects of multiple antioxidant components, suggesting that processing promoted the accumulation of structurally more stable flavone and flavonol glycosides. Their extended conjugation, reduced chemical reactivity, and glycosylation patterns favor sustained and cooperative antioxidant effects rather than rapid radical scavenging41. Correlation analysis further supports this interpretation. The negative correlations between TFC and fast radical scavenging assays (DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion) indicate that flavonoid subclasses enriched after processing preferentially contribute to integrated antioxidant capacity rather than immediate radical quenching. Conversely, the positive correlation between T-AOC and TFC highlights the role of these flavonoid subclasses in enhancing overall reducing potential. Meanwhile, the positive association between hydroxyl radical scavenging activity and TPC suggests that total phenolics exert a more direct influence on highly reactive radicals. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that processing reshapes the structural composition and relative abundance of phenolic compounds in Lingmu leaves, leading to a functional differentiation among phenolic subgroups across multiple antioxidant systems. The observed shift from rapid radical scavenging toward enhanced integrated reducing capacity underlies the contrasting antioxidant performance between FL and TL and aligns with the processing-induced transition from flavanol-dominated profiles toward substituted and glycosylated flavones and flavonols. This establishes a clear structure–antioxidant behavior relationship in Lingmu tea.

This study elucidated the processing-induced quality formation of Lingmu leaves through integrated sensory, physicochemical, multi-omics, and antioxidant analyses. Green tea-type processing markedly reshaped chemical composition and flavor characteristics while maintaining the nutritional basis. Processed Lingmu leaves showed a bright orange–yellow infusion, a mellow umami-dominant taste with minimal bitterness and a coordinated chestnut-like aroma. Metabolomic profiling revealed selective remodeling of non-volatile metabolites, characterized by increased substituted and glycosylated flavonoids and reduced catechins, together with shifts in organic acids toward flavor-softening components. Volatile analysis demonstrated aroma reconstruction driven by reduced green and sulfurous compounds and enrichment of key aroma-active constituents. Antioxidant activity displayed functional redistribution rather than uniform change. Overall, these results provide a mechanistic understanding of how green tea-type processing modulates chemical composition, flavor attributes, and antioxidant behavior in Lingmu leaves, offering a scientific basis for further optimization of processing strategies. However, the processing conditions investigated in this study were established at the laboratory scale, and additional studies are required to evaluate process robustness, scalability, and batch-to-batch consistency before practical standardization can be achieved.

Methods

Plant materials and treatments

Fresh Lingmu leaves (Eurya alata Kobuski) were harvested in May 2025 from Dinghai District, Zhoushan City, Zhejiang Province, China (29°55′–30°15′N, 121°38′–122°15′E) (Fig. 1A). Fresh leaves (FL) were collected as the raw material and processed following a typical green tea manufacturing procedure to obtain Lingmu tea (TL). Briefly, the leaves were withered at room temperature for 8–10 h to reduce moisture content and allow initial metabolic adjustment. This was followed by fixation at 240–260 °C for 6 min, a condition selected to rapidly inactivate oxidative enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase, thereby preventing excessive enzymatic oxidation while permitting controlled thermal reactions related to aroma precursor transformation. The fixed leaves were then rolled for 30 min to disrupt cellular structures and facilitate component redistribution, and subsequently dried at 110–120 °C for 20 min, followed by a second drying step at 100–110 °C for 50 min. These drying conditions were designed to gradually remove residual moisture while stabilizing thermally sensitive metabolites and volatile precursors, resulting in the final TL product (Fig. 1B). Both FL and TL samples were immediately stored at −80 °C to prevent chemical changes prior to analysis. Before subsequent analyses, all samples were freeze-dried to a constant weight, ground into fine powder, and passed through a 0.35 mm sieve for further physicochemical and metabolomic analyses.

Chemicals

Distilled water was prepared using a Milli-Q ultrapure water system from Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA). LC–MS grade methanol, formic acid, and acetonitrile were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Caffeine (purity ≥99%), glucose (purity ≥99%), fructose (purity ≥99%), and sucrose (purity ≥99%) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). A mixed standard solution of 17 amino acids was purchased from Whatman (Maidstone, UK). A mixed standard solution of 37 fatty acid methyl esters was obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). 27 organic acid standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Analysis of basic chemical components

Soluble sugar content was determined using a plant soluble sugar assay kit (BC0030, Solarbio, China) after water-based extraction of powdered tea samples, followed by heating in a boiling water bath and centrifugation. Total amino acid content was measured using an amino acid assay kit (BC1575, Solarbio, China) after ice-bath homogenization of samples with the extraction reagent, subsequent boiling water extraction, and centrifugation. Total flavonoid and total phenolic contents were quantified using commercial assay kits (BC1335 and BC1345, Solarbio, China, respectively) following ultrasonic-assisted extraction of dried and powdered samples with the corresponding extraction solutions and subsequent centrifugation. All sample preparation and measurements were performed in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Sensory evaluation

TL infusions were prepared according to the Chinese standard GB/T 23776-2018 (Methodology for sensory evaluation of tea) using the cylindrical cup method for green tea, with minor modifications. Briefly, TL samples (5 g, processed whole tea leaves) were infused with 250 mL of purified water at 100 °C for 4 min. Sensory evaluation was conducted by a trained panel of eight assessors (three males and five females, aged 26–40 years). All panelists held official tea quality evaluation certificates issued by the China Tea Science Society, including three senior and five intermediate evaluators. Aroma attributes, including Tender, Green, Chestnut, Sweet, Flowery, Persistence, Harmony, and Strength (eight factors), and taste attributes including Umami, Sweet, Bitter, Astringent, Strong, Thick, and Harmony (seven factors) were evaluated (The definitions of each sensory attribute and the corresponding reference standards are provided in Supplementary Data Table S3). Scores of the eight aroma sub-attributes and seven taste sub-attributes were used as variables to construct radar plots, generating aroma and taste profiles for different tea samples. All attributes were evaluated using a 7-point intensity scale ranging from 0 to 3 with 0.5-unit increments (0 = not perceptible, 0.5 = extremely weak, 1 = just perceptible, 1.5 = weak, 2 = moderate, 2.5 = strong, 3 = very strong), following quantitative descriptive analysis principles. Each sample was evaluated in three independent sessions, with intervals of at least 1 h between sessions to minimize sensory fatigue. All samples were labeled with random three-digit codes and evaluated under blind conditions, and the serving order was fully randomized to reduce order effects. All participants provided written informed consent prior to the evaluation, and the sensory study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Approval No. 2025090153), in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis of basic physicochemical components

Test samples were prepared according to the preparation of test solutions in the Chinese national standard GB/T 8313-2018. Ash content was determined by the ignition and gravimetric method following GB 5009.4-2016. Moisture content was measured by the direct drying method according to GB 5009.3-2016. Crude fiber content was analyzed using the acid-alkali digestion method based on GB/T 8310-2013. Water extract content was determined by the boiling water method following GB/T 8305-2013. Monosaccharide content was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography according to GB 5009.8-2023. Protein content was determined based on GB 5009.5-2025. Caffeine content was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography following GB 5009.139-2014.

Color difference measurement

The color characteristics of tea samples were evaluated using Hunter L* (lightness), a* (red–green), and b* (yellow–blue) parameters. Measurements were performed with a 10° standard observer angle and D65 standard illuminant. Color values were recorded using a colorimeter (CR-5, Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan). For analysis, 5 mL of tea infusion sample (TL) was placed in a rectangular glass cuvette with dimensions of 50 × 38 mm and measured under constant temperature conditions. The instrument was calibrated with a standard black plate before measurement. The total color difference (ΔE) of the infusion was calculated by Eq. (1)42.

Where obvious color differences can be distinguished by the human eye when ΔE is greater than 3; color differences are difficult to perceive when ΔE ranges between 1 and 3; and no perceptible color difference is observed when ΔE is less than 1.

Analysis of amino acid composition and content

Total amino acids in tea samples were determined using a high-speed amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), following previously reported methods with little modified43. Briefly, 2 g of tea powder was placed into a hydrolysis tube and mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid. The tube was frozen for 5 min, flushed with nitrogen to remove air, sealed, and hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 22 h. After hydrolysis, the sample was cooled to room temperature. The hydrolysate was filtered, transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask, diluted with deionized water, and mixed well. An aliquot of 1 mL was dried under reduced pressure, then redissolved in 2 mL of sodium citrate buffer at pH 2.2. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous membrane and transferred to vials for analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using buffer solutions with different pH values and ionic strengths as mobile phases. Detection was carried out with a UV–Vis detector at 570 nm and 440 nm. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.40 mL/min, and the derivatization reagent flow rate was 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 38 °C, and the post-column reactor was maintained at 135 °C. The autosampler temperature was kept at 4 °C, with an injection volume of 20 μL. Amino acid contents were quantified by comparing peak areas with those of standard amino acid solutions. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Analysis of fatty acid composition and content

Fatty acid composition and content were determined based on published methods with minor modifications44. Briefly, 50 mg of freeze-dried tea powder was weighed into a digestion tube. Then, 1 mL of 5% sulfuric acid-methanol solution (v/v) and 200 μL of butylated hydroxytoluene methanol solution (0.2 vol%) were added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s to ensure homogenization. Samples were then incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 1 h to complete fatty acid methyl ester formation. After cooling to room temperature, 2 mL of 0.9% (w/w) NaCl aqueous solution and 1 mL of n-hexane were added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min. The upper organic phase was collected and extracted once again. Combined extracts were gently evaporated to dryness under nitrogen. The residue was redissolved in 400 μL of n-hexane and transferred to vials for analysis. Gas chromatography analysis was performed under the following conditions. High-purity nitrogen (purity ≥99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injector temperature was set at 270 °C, and the detector temperature was set at 280 °C. The split ratio was 10:1, and the injection volume was 1.0 μL. Separation was achieved using an HP-88 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm). The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature at 100 °C held for 3 min, increased to 180 °C at 10 °C/min and held for 6 min, increased to 200 °C at 1 °C/min and held for 20 min, and finally increased to 230 °C at 4 °C/min and held for 5.5 min. Fatty acids were identified by comparing retention times with those of authentic fatty acid standards analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions. Quantification was performed using an external calibration method, and absolute concentrations of individual fatty acids were calculated from standard calibration curves constructed with corresponding fatty acid standards. Results are expressed as μg/g dry weight.

Determination of chemical element contents

Chemical element contents were determined based on published methods with minor modifications45. 0.30 g of tea sample was weighed into a quartz digestion tube. Then, 2.00 mL of ultrapure water and 3.00 mL of nitric acid were added. Samples were pre-digested in a fume hood for 12 h. Next, 150.00 mL of ultrapure water and 5.00 mL of nitric acid were added to the outer vessel of the microwave digestion system and mixed well. Microwave digestion was carried out according to the instrument protocol. After digestion, the tubes were opened, and the digest was heated on a hot plate at 120 °C for 2 h to remove residual acid. After cooling to room temperature, the solution was diluted to 50.00 mL with ultrapure water. Before analysis, operating parameters and stability of the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP–MS) were optimized. The system vacuum was maintained below 6.0 × 10−⁸ kPa, and argon pressure was controlled at 0.60–0.70 MPa. After ignition, a 1 ng/mL tuning solution was used for mass calibration and sensitivity optimization. Nebulizer pressure was set at 150–250 kPa, radiofrequency power at 1200–1300 W, plasma gas flow at 14.0–15.0 L/min, and nebulizer gas flow at 0.8–1.2 L/min. Prior to measurement, the sample introduction system was rinsed with 1.0% (v/v) nitric acid for 20 min. Detection was performed in reaction cell mode using peak-hopping scanning, with 100 scans per analysis. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and mean values were used for subsequent analysis.

Determination of organic acid profiles and contents

The profiles and contents of organic acids were determined based on published methods with minor modifications46. Briefly, 0.100 g of sample was weighed and mixed with 5 mL of 3 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution. The mixture was ultrasonically extracted at 50 °C for 30 min. The extract was adjusted to pH about 1.8 using 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid, followed by back-extraction twice with 10 mL of ethyl acetate. Each extraction was shaken for 15 min and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The ethyl acetate phases were combined and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 50 °C. The residue was redissolved in 5 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol–water solution and filtered through a PTFE membrane to obtain the sample solution. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis was performed using a C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm × 1.7 μm) maintained at 30 °C. The flow rate was 0.2 mL/min, injection volume was 5 μL, and the autosampler temperature was set at 15 °C. Detection was carried out with an electrospray ionization source in both positive and negative modes (ESI⁺/ESI−) using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The capillary voltage was set at 2.5 kV. Source temperature was 150 °C, and desolvation temperature was 500 °C. Nitrogen was used as both desolvation gas at 1000 L/min and cone gas at 50 L/min. Chromatographic separation was performed using a ternary mobile phase system consisting of mobile phase A (water), mobile phase B (0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water), and mobile phase C (methanol). The gradient elution program was set as follows: mobile phase B was maintained at 1% throughout the entire run. Mobile phase C was held at 10% from 0 to 1 min, increased from 10 to 40% between 1 and 2 min, further increased from 40% to 100% between 2 and 3 min, and maintained at 100% from 3 to 8 min. The composition was then returned from 100% to 10% methanol between 8.0 and 8.1 min, followed by equilibration at 10% methanol from 8.1 to 13 min. The remaining proportion was mobile phase A.

Targeted polyphenol metabolomics

Tea polyphenols were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–ESI–MS/MS). The procedure was based on a previous report with minor modifications25. Briefly, 30 mg of tea powder was accurately weighed and extracted with 1.5 mL of precooled (−20 °C) 70% (v/v) methanol–water solution. Extraction was carried out at 4 °C, with vortex mixing for 30 s every 30 min, repeated six times. The extract was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred to sample vials for analysis. L-2-chlorophenylalanine was added as an internal standard.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 0.2 mL of supernatant from each sample to validate the analytical method and monitor system stability. During LC–MS data acquisition, the injection sequence included solvent blanks, experimental samples, and QC samples. Three consecutive solvent blank injections were performed at the beginning of the run to check and stabilize the UPLC–QTOF–MS system. All experimental samples were randomized and analyzed with six technical replicates. Solvent blanks and QC samples were injected once every 12 experimental samples to monitor background signals and system stability. At the end of the analytical sequence, ten solvent blank injections were performed. This injection sequence was based on a previously reported protocol with minor modifications47.

UPLC–ESI–MS/MS analysis was performed using an ExionLC™ AD system coupled with a QTRAP tandem mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phases consisted of water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (B). The gradient program started at 95% A and 5% B, linearly changed to 5% A and 95% B over 9 min, held for 1 min, then returned to 95% A and 5% B within 1.1 min, followed by equilibration for 2.9 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, column temperature was 40 °C, and injection volume was 2 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted using an electrospray ionization source coupled to a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass analyzer. Source temperature was set at 500 °C. Ion spray voltage was +5500 V in positive mode and −4500 V in negative mode. Ion source gas I, gas II, and curtain gas pressures were 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively. Collision-activated dissociation was set to high. Data acquisition was performed in multiple reaction monitoring mode using nitrogen as the collision gas at medium intensity. Declustering potential and collision energy were optimized for each transition, and dynamic monitoring was applied according to metabolite retention times. Polyphenols were annotated using a targeted UPLC–ESI–MS/MS approach with database matching and classified into three confidence levels based on MS/MS spectral quality and retention time consistency (Supplementary Data Table S1): L1 (score ≥ 0.7, full MS/MS and RT match), L2 (score 0.5–0.7, moderate MS/MS and RT agreement), and L3 (tentative identification based on Q1/Q3 transitions, retention time, declustering potential, and collision energy).

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using MultiQuant software for chromatographic peak integration and correction, and peak areas were used to represent the relative abundances of metabolites. The resulting peak area matrix was exported for downstream analysis. Missing values were imputed using a minimum value-based approach, in which missing entries were replaced with one-fifth of the minimum detected value for each metabolite across all samples. Quality control was performed using QC samples, and metabolites with a coefficient of variation (CV) ≥ 0.5 were excluded to ensure data robustness. For multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap visualization, and k-means clustering, data were normalized and scaled using unit variance (UV) scaling. Specifically, data were mean-centered and divided by the standard deviation of each variable to achieve comparable variance across metabolites.

Flavoromics analysis

Tea aroma compounds were extracted using solid-phase extraction, following a published method with minor modifications48. The extraction solvent was prepared by mixing pentane and methyl tert-butyl ether at a volume ratio of 4:1. Tea leaf powder was used for extraction, and samples were extracted three times with a total of 60 mL solvent. The combined organic phase was dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate and concentrated to about 2 mL using a TurboVap concentrator (Biotage, Sweden). The extract was further reduced to 0.5 mL under a gentle nitrogen stream. Prior to use, the SPE cartridge was conditioned with 6 mL of pentane and methyl tert-butyl ether (1:1, v/v). The concentrated extract was loaded onto the cartridge and eluted with 10 mL of the same solvent mixture. The eluate was concentrated under nitrogen to approximately 100 μL for analysis.

For headspace analysis, samples were transferred into 20 mL headspace vials, and 10 μL of 2-octanol (10 mg/L stock solution prepared in deionized water) was added as an internal standard. Solid-phase microextraction was performed using a PAL autosampler. Samples were incubated at 60 °C for 15 min, extracted for 30 min, and desorbed for 4 min. Volatile compounds were analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry using an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph coupled with a 5977B mass selective detector and a DB-Wax capillary column. Injection was carried out in splitless mode with helium as the carrier gas. The purge flow was 3 mL/min, and the column flow was 1 mL/min. The oven temperature program started at 40 °C for 4 min, increased to 245 °C at 5 °C/min, and held for 5 min. The injector, transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole temperatures were set at 250, 250, 230, and 150 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in electron ionization mode at 70 eV, with a scan range of m/z 20–400 and a solvent delay of 2.37 min. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using Chroma TOF software and the NIST mass spectral database for peak extraction, deconvolution, alignment, and compound identification.

Relative odor activity value calculation

Volatile compound data were filtered and calculated as follows. First, the detection frequency of each compound in each sample group was calculated, and only compounds with a detection rate higher than 50% were retained. Second, the coefficient of variation of peak areas was calculated, and compounds with CV values lower than 30% were selected. After filtering, the mean peak area of each compound in each group was calculated and converted into relative percentage content, expressed as C%. Only compounds with clearly reported odor threshold values were retained. Odor thresholds of volatile compounds in water were obtained from published literature49–52. Relative odor activity values (ROAV) were then calculated by combining relative content with perception thresholds53. The compound with the greatest contribution to overall aroma, as indicated by the highest ratio of relative content to odor threshold, was defined as the reference compound and assigned an ROAVstan value of 100, following common practice in ROAV-based aroma evaluation to enable relative comparison within the same aroma system. Analysis of antioxidant activity

Tea samples were ground into fine powder prior to analysis. Antioxidant activities were evaluated using five commercial assay kits obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China), including ABTS radical scavenging activity assay kit (BC1325), DPPH radical scavenging activity assay kit (BC4750), FRAP total antioxidant capacity assay kit (BC1310), superoxide anion scavenging activity assay kit (BC1410), and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity assay kit (BC1320). For the ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging assays, powdered tea samples were extracted and reacted with the corresponding reagents provided in the kits, followed by incubation in a 40 °C water bath for 30 min according to the manufacturers’ instructions. After incubation, the reaction mixtures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for absorbance measurement. For the FRAP total antioxidant capacity assay, superoxide anion scavenging activity assay, and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity assay, the powdered samples were homogenized in the extraction buffer supplied with each kit under ice-bath conditions to minimize oxidative degradation. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent antioxidant activity determination. All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers’ protocols, and absorbance values were measured using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Biological replication was conducted at the harvest level. Fresh Lingmu leaves were collected from three independent biological replicates, each consisting of leaves harvested from different plants within the same cultivation area. Each biological replicate was processed independently to obtain the corresponding tea leaves. All subsequent chemical analyses were performed using these independent biological replicates, with analytical measurements conducted in triplicate. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test with SPSS software, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and statistical significance was defined as FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, corresponding to −log₁₀(p) > 1.30. Multivariate statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate global metabolic differences among samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models were constructed using the OmicShare online platform (https://www.omicshare.com/) and SIMCA software (version 14.1), respectively. Differential metabolites were screened based on the combined criteria of VIP > 1 from the OPLS-DA model, |log₂ fold change (FC)| ≥ 1.0, and −log₁₀(p) > 1.30. Finally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the OmicShare online platform (https://www.omicshare.com/). The enrichment background set consisted of all metabolites identified in this study, and pathway overrepresentation was assessed using a hypergeometric test. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and pathways with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.
