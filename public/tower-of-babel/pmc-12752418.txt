Towards greener care: understanding sustainability awareness among nursing students through qualitative inquiry
Authors: Wdad Aanazy, Amira Yahia Boshra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvironmental sustainability is increasingly important in healthcare due to the sector’s substantial impact on energy use and waste generation. Nurses play a crucial role in promoting sustainable practices to protect both public health and the environment. This study aims to explore nursing students’ awareness and perceptions of sustainability in healthcare.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was employed using semi-structured interviews with 15 undergraduate and graduate nursing students. Data were analyzed thematically to identify key patterns.ResultsFifteen nursing students—both male and female—from undergraduate and postgraduate levels—participated in the interviews. Thematic analysis identified four overarching themes encompassing nine subthemes: (1) understanding of sustainability, (2) personal motivations and values, (3) institutional and educational contexts, and (4) professional vision for sustainable nursing practice. Overall, participants demonstrated a general awareness of sustainability but reported limited integration within academic curricula and inadequate institutional support in clinical settings.ConclusionEnhancing nursing curricula and strengthening institutional support are essential to empower future nurses to effectively integrate sustainability into healthcare practice.Clinical trialNot applicable. This study is not a clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04134-1.

Introduction and background

Environmental sustainability has become a global priority, with increasing recognition that human activities significantly impact the natural environment. The healthcare sector, in particular, is a major contributor to energy consumption, waste generation, and the use of hazardous materials [1, 2]. Hospitals and other healthcare facilities consume large amounts of electricity and water, produce substantial volumes of solid and medical waste, and rely on products that may be harmful to ecosystems. These activities contribute to climate change, environmental pollution, and depletion of natural resources, highlighting the urgent need for sustainable healthcare practices that minimize environmental harm while maintaining safe, high-quality patient care [3–5]. Addressing these challenges requires integrating environmentally responsible practices at both systemic and individual levels within healthcare delivery.

Nurses are increasingly recognized as key agents in advancing environmental sustainability within healthcare systems. Due to their continuous interaction with patients, families, and clinical processes, nurses are well-positioned to implement practical sustainability measures, such as reducing waste, optimizing resource use, and advocating for eco-conscious decision-making in their daily practice [6]. In addition to clinical responsibilities, nurses can influence institutional policies, participate in sustainability committees, and promote environmental awareness among colleagues and patients. Consequently, embedding sustainability principles into nursing education is critical. Nursing curricula that incorporate environmental knowledge, values, and skills prepare future professionals to enact sustainable practices, ensuring that environmental considerations are integrated into patient care and organizational processes [7]. Despite the recognized importance of sustainability education, challenges persist, including limited formal curricular content, inadequate faculty training, and insufficient institutional support to foster sustainable practices in clinical settings [7].

In Saudi Arabia, the healthcare sector is undergoing rapid transformation under Vision 2030, which emphasizes modernization, quality improvement, and long-term sustainability in health services [8] These reforms create a unique opportunity to integrate environmental sustainability into professional education and clinical practice. Nursing students, as future healthcare leaders, are a pivotal group for promoting sustainable care and shaping environmentally responsible practices in healthcare institutions [9]. However, evidence on how nursing students perceive, understand, and enact sustainability within Saudi healthcare settings remains limited. Few studies have explored their awareness, attitudes, and educational experiences regarding sustainable healthcare, making it difficult to develop tailored strategies to embed sustainability into curricula and clinical training [10, 11]. Understanding these perspectives is essential to guide educational planning, strengthen professional competencies, and align nursing training with national healthcare priorities and global sustainability goals.

By focusing on nursing students in Saudi Arabia, this study addresses the existing knowledge gap, exploring how students perceive and enact sustainability within their education and early clinical practice. Findings are expected to inform nursing curricula, educational policies, and institutional initiatives aimed at fostering environmentally responsible healthcare practices nationwide.

Research aim

To explore how nursing students at Majmaah University perceive and enact sustainability in their education and early clinical practice.

Research question

How do nursing students at Majmaah University perceive and enact sustainability within their education and early clinical experiences?

Methodology

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design to explore nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of sustainability in healthcare education and early clinical practice. Qualitative descriptive research emphasizes capturing participants’ perspectives in their own words, providing rich, practical insights, and generating findings directly applicable to educational policy and practice [12, 13]. This approach is particularly suitable for under-researched areas, where understanding experiences in detail can inform curriculum development, professional training, and institutional strategies [14, 15]

While qualitative descriptive studies focus on describing participants’ experiences, this study applied a structured thematic analysis approach guided by Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework to organize and interpret data systematically. The framework provided a clear process for moving from familiarization with the data to the development of coherent themes, while remaining close to participants’ own words. This approach ensured transparency and rigor in analysis without relying on the reflexive interpretation that characterizes reflexive thematic analysis.

Data collection

Data were collected from April to June 2024 using semi-structured interviews, designed to elicit rich, in-depth accounts of student nurses’ experiences and perceptions of sustainability. The interview guide included open-ended questions and probes to encourage reflection on personal, educational, and clinical experiences [16]. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English where required. All participants were asked the core questions, with probes and follow-ups used flexibly depending on the flow of the interview.

Throughout the analysis, negative and divergent cases—participants whose views contradicted dominant patterns—were deliberately identified and examined. These cases were used to refine theme boundaries and ensure that the final thematic structure captured the full spectrum of perspectives rather than only majority viewpoints. This systematic and collaborative approach enhanced the credibility, transparency, and analytical rigor of the study.

Transcription, translation, and audit

Data analysis began immediately after each interview to keep details fresh in the researcher’s mind [12]. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Arabic, preserving participants’ original expressions and nuances. The transcripts were reviewed multiple times for accuracy and completeness [17].

The primary researcher, bilingual in Arabic and English and experienced in qualitative research, conducted all interviews. The interviewer had no teaching, grading, or supervisory relationship with any participant, minimizing the risk of coercion and ensuring voluntary participation. Interviews were conducted in a confidential setting, and participants were reminded that they could withdraw at any time.

The primary researcher translated all transcripts into English, preserving literal meaning wherever possible and conveying intended meaning when exact wording could not be retained. To ensure accuracy, transcripts were back-translated into Arabic by the same researcher, and any discrepancies were resolved collaboratively [18]. For additional confirmability, 10% of the transcripts were independently reviewed by two bilingual Associate Professors in Nursing who had no teaching, grading, or supervisory role with participants. This independent review minimized bias and ensured objective verification of translation and interpretation.

All transcripts were kept verbatim rather than cleaned, maintaining linguistic authenticity and contextual nuance. Selected original Arabic excerpts corresponding to translated quotations are provided in Appendix 1 to illustrate translation fidelity and transparency.

This systematic process of verbatim transcription, forward- and back-translation, independent bilingual verification, and separation between interviewer and participants strengthened the trustworthiness, credibility, and confirmability of the data. A theme was defined as a coherent and meaningful pattern in the data that captured important insights related to the research question, regardless of frequency of occurrence. Themes were identified based on their ability to represent shared meanings, reveal tensions, and highlight critical insights across participants’ accounts (see Table 2).

Rigor and trustworthiness

Rigor was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, iterative coding using a structured codebook, peer debriefing with a qualitative research expert, and the use of illustrative quotes to substantiate themes. Dependability was supported by maintaining a detailed audit trail documenting all phases of data collection, codebook development, and analytic meetings. Confirmability was strengthened by grounding analytic decisions firmly in the data, validating translations, and independently reviewing 10% of transcripts through bilingual auditors. These strategies ensured the transparency and trustworthiness of the findings.

Setting

Majmaah University –Nursing College.

Sampling approach

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit nursing students who could provide rich insights into sustainability and environmental awareness within nursing education. This approach allowed for the deliberate inclusion of participants with relevant educational backgrounds and experiences [19].

Participant selection

A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit participants who could provide rich and diverse perspectives on sustainability awareness in nursing education and early clinical practice. Although a general invitation was distributed to all nursing students at Majmaah University via mass email explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature, selection was guided by purposive criteria to ensure variation in gender (female and male), academic level (undergraduate and postgraduate), and campus site. Students who expressed interest were contacted individually to schedule interviews at convenient times.

This process continued sequentially until thematic saturation was achieved—defined as the point at which no new codes, categories, or concepts emerged from consecutive interviews. Saturation was documented in a saturation memo (Appendix 2), maintained throughout data collection. After the 13th interview, no new codes were identified, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm redundancy.

In total, 17 students were approached; 15 consented and completed interviews (12 females, 3 males), while two declined participations due to scheduling conflicts. The final sample represented both undergraduate (n = 10) and postgraduate (n = 5) programs, providing a balanced range of experiences across different educational stages.

The sample size was supported by the principle of information power, as participants possessed specific knowledge and experience relevant to the study’s aim, enabling sufficient analytical depth and thematic richness within the achieved sample.

Result

Fifteen nursing students, both male and female, from undergraduate and postgraduate levels, participated in the interviews. Thematic analysis generated four overarching themes encompassing nine subthemes. The themes included: (1) understanding of sustainability (definitions and relevance in nursing practice), (2) personal motivations (values, attitudes, and future intentions), (3) institutional and educational contexts (curriculum, resources, and faculty support), and (4) professional vision (patient care, environmental responsibility, and long-term impact). Collectively, these findings highlight the opportunities and challenges students encounter in integrating sustainability into their education and future clinical practice.

Theme One: Understanding and Awareness

Participants expressed varied understandings of sustainability and its relevance to nursing. Many recognized its importance for environmental protection and health promotion, while others viewed it primarily as an administrative or institutional concern. Awareness often stemmed from limited coursework or personal interest, revealing notable gaps in formal education.

Subtheme 1.1 – Understanding of Sustainability

Participants’ conceptualizations of sustainability varied widely, reflecting differences in depth and scope of understanding. Most described it as a multidimensional concept encompassing environmental, social, and economic well-being.

As one undergraduate explained, “Sustainability affects not just health, but also the economy and society. If we waste resources, it can lead to economic losses and social challenges” (UG-F-10). Another added, “It’s about making choices that protect the environment while improving health outcomes” (UG-M-14).

Yet, a few participants expressed a limited or externalized understanding, perceiving sustainability as unrelated to nursing practice: “I never really thought about sustainability in nursing before; it seems more like a hospital administration issue” (PG-M-2, divergent case). These contrasting perspectives helped refine the theme boundaries by illustrating that, while many students recognize the concept’s relevance to healthcare, others still regard it as peripheral to their professional identity.

Subtheme 1.2 – Sustainability Awareness in Nursing Education

Awareness of sustainability concepts within nursing curricula appeared inconsistent. Several participants reported limited exposure through brief lectures or self-directed learning efforts: “It’s very important for nursing students to learn about sustainability because we can have a big impact on the environment through our practices” (UG-F-8).

However, others highlighted insufficient curricular coverage: “We have only one lecture on environmental issues, which isn’t enough to change behaviours” (PG-F-13, divergent case). Some students compensated through independent learning: “I read about waste reduction in nursing journals, and it made me more conscious of my daily actions” (UG-M-7).

Integrating these divergent views clarified that while a general awareness of sustainability exists, its conceptual and educational integration within nursing remains uneven and dependent on individual initiative.

Theme 2: Practical Application and Motivation

Participants described differing levels of engagement in applying sustainability principles in their academic and clinical contexts. While some actively incorporated eco-friendly practices, others felt constrained by institutional barriers or a lack of motivation. These variations highlighted how both environmental structures and individual agency influence sustainable behaviour.

Subtheme 2.1 – Practical Application of Sustainability

Students demonstrated varying capacities to translate sustainability knowledge into daily practice. Many identified institutional and logistical barriers—such as the absence of recycling infrastructure or clear guidance—as key obstacles: “At the university, there were no containers for recycling in certain areas, and people weren’t sure about what materials to separate” (UG-F-3).

Others showed initiative despite systemic constraints: “I try to minimize single-use items when caring for patients during clinical training, but sometimes it’s difficult due to hospital protocols” (PG-F-1).

A few participants acknowledged minimal engagement, citing uncertainty about how to act: “Honestly, I haven’t done much because there’s no guidance on how to apply sustainability practically” (UG-F-15, divergent case).

These divergent accounts refined the theme boundaries by illustrating that students’ sustainable practices depend heavily on institutional leadership and the clarity of procedural guidance.

Subtheme 2.2 – Personal Motivation and Commitment

Participants’ personal values and sense of professional responsibility strongly influenced their motivation to engage in sustainability-related behaviours. Several expressed intrinsic motivations grounded in ethical duty: “Sometimes it feels like the systems in place don’t support sustainable practices, but I know that if I take small steps, I can still make a difference” (UG-F-9).

Others, however, felt discouraged by limited peer engagement or institutional reinforcement: “I don’t really feel motivated because no one else in my team seems to care about sustainability” (PG-F-4, divergent case).

Conversely, some displayed high levels of personal commitment: “I actively look for ways to reduce waste in my daily practice because it aligns with my values” (UG-M-14).

Together, these contrasting views reveal that while individual motivation can spark sustainable action, a supportive social and institutional culture is essential for maintaining such behaviours over time.

Theme 3: Institutional Context

Participants emphasized that institutional structures and educational systems play a decisive role in shaping sustainable nursing practice. Their accounts revealed that while awareness and motivation exist at the individual level, institutional environments often determine whether sustainability is enacted or constrained.

Subtheme 3.1 – Institutional Arrangements for Sustainability

Participants highlighted that institutional policies, infrastructure, and leadership are essential enablers of sustainability in nursing practice. As one student noted, “Nurses need to know how to manage medical waste, reduce energy consumption in healthcare settings, and incorporate sustainability into their daily practices” (UG-M-7).

However, several participants observed a persistent gap between policy and implementation: “There’s policy on paper, but in practice, the hospital doesn’t enforce sustainable measures” (PG-F-4, divergent case). Another student suggested, “Clear protocols for recycling and energy use would make it easier for staff to act sustainably” (UG-F-13).

These divergent accounts refined the theme by illustrating that institutional support must move beyond symbolic commitment to ensure operational enforcement and visible leadership engagement.

Subtheme 3.2 – Role of Educational Institutions

Students consistently underscored the role of educational institutions in cultivating sustainability competence. One undergraduate proposed, “It would be great if nursing students had the chance to work on sustainability-focused projects—like waste management initiatives or studies on the environmental impact of healthcare practices” (UG-F-12).

In contrast, a postgraduate student reflected, “Our curriculum doesn’t really prepare us to think about sustainability; it’s mostly theory without practice” (PG-F-15, divergent case). Others advocated experiential learning: “Workshops on sustainable nursing could help students translate knowledge into action” (UG-F-3).

Together, these perspectives clarified the boundary between theoretical instruction and applied practice, revealing that curricular reforms emphasizing experiential and project-based learning are critical for embedding sustainability in nursing education.

Subtheme 3.3 – Support Systems and Incentives

Many participants believed that mentorship, recognition, and institutional encouragement are vital for maintaining engagement in sustainability initiatives. As one student suggested, “It would be really helpful if the university provided more resources and awards for applying sustainability—like workshops, guest lectures, or incentives for attending” (UG-F-8).

Conversely, others expressed disappointment over the lack of acknowledgment: “I don’t feel encouraged because there’s no recognition for sustainable practices in our university” (UG-M-7, divergent case). Some emphasized the importance of professional modelling: “Mentorship from experienced nurses who model sustainable practice would be motivating” (UG-M-14).

These contrasting accounts refined the theme by showing that while individual awareness is present, structured mentorship and reward systems are necessary to institutionalize sustainability and promote a culture of environmental accountability.

Theme 4: Patient Care and Professional Vision

Participants discussed the relationship between sustainability and professional nursing practice, emphasizing its implications for patient outcomes and future professional identity. Their reflections revealed a growing awareness that sustainability is not only an environmental issue but also a component of safe, ethical, and high-quality care.

Subtheme 4.1 – Impact on Patient Care

Participants widely acknowledged that environmental conditions and sustainable practices directly influence patient health outcomes. As one undergraduate explained, “Environmental cleanliness, air quality, and waste management are crucial for patient recovery” (UG-F-13).

However, others described tension between sustainability and immediate clinical priorities: “Sometimes we ignore sustainability because patient care comes first, even if it’s not the best for the environment” (PG-M-2, divergent case). At the same time, several participants emphasized that sustainability and safety can coexist: “Using eco-friendly materials can still meet patient care standards if implemented carefully” (UG-F-6).

These divergent perspectives refined the theme by illustrating that sustainable practice in healthcare requires balancing ecological responsibility with clinical safety, supported by evidence-based policies and institutional guidance.

Subtheme 4.2 – Future Vision for Sustainability

Most participants expressed a strong forward-looking commitment to promoting sustainability within nursing practice. As one student reflected, “I want to be part of the shift toward greener healthcare by promoting sustainable practices like reducing waste in hospital settings” (UG-F-5).

In contrast, some participants admitted uncertainty about their role: “I’m not sure how I can contribute to sustainability in my future workplace; it feels too complicated” (PG-M-2, divergent case). Others envisioned taking on leadership responsibilities: “In the long run, I hope to lead projects that integrate eco-friendly practices in nursing care” (UG-F-11).

These varied accounts refined the theme by showing that while enthusiasm for sustainability is evident among nursing students, translating this vision into practice will require structured mentorship, role modelling, and institutional support.

Synthesis of findings

Students’ understanding of sustainability shapes both their actions in clinical practice and their professional vision, but the extent to which these intentions are realized is influenced by institutional arrangements, available support systems, and curriculum structure. When structural and institutional support is limited, intrinsic motivation alone is often insufficient to translate knowledge into consistent practice, highlighting the importance of integrated educational and policy interventions.

The four overarching themes—Understanding and Awareness, Practical Application and Motivation, Institutional Context, and Patient Care and Professional Vision—are closely interconnected. For instance, students’ personal motivation and commitment is strongly influenced by the institutional context and the availability of support systems. Likewise, their understanding and awareness of sustainability informs both their practical application in clinical settings and their professional aspirations for future nursing practice.

Several key dynamics emerged from the analysis. While students generally demonstrate strong intrinsic motivation, structural and institutional barriers frequently impede the implementation of sustainable practices. Curriculum gaps mean that students often rely on personal initiative to acquire knowledge about sustainability, leading to variability in awareness and competence. Students consistently linked environmental practices to patient care, reinforcing the relevance of sustainability within nursing education. Furthermore, institutional support, guidance, and incentives were identified as crucial enablers, enhancing engagement and helping to embed sustainable practices more fully into both education and clinical practice.

Overall, this integrated synthesis moves beyond a mere listing of themes, illustrating how individual, educational, and institutional factors interact to shape students’ experiences and behaviors. It provides a solid foundation for actionable recommendations to enhance curriculum design, clinical practice, and institutional policies aimed at fostering sustainability in nursing.

Discussion

This study offers the first qualitative understanding of sustainability awareness among Saudi Arabian nursing students, demonstrating how institutional support systems and curriculum design affect the adoption of sustainable practices. The results provide practical advice for incorporating sustainability into nursing education through student-led projects, faculty involvement, and curriculum reform.

The findings of this study highlight the complex interactions that shape nursing students’ engagement with sustainability between curriculum design, institutional structures, and individual motivation. Although students demonstrate an admirable degree of internal drive and a thorough comprehension of the connection between sustainability and patient care, external circumstances frequently limit their capacity to continuously implement sustainable practices in clinical settings. Understanding and Awareness, Practical Application and Motivation, Institutional Context, and Patient Care and Professional Vision were the four interconnected themes that surfaced. As a substitute of functioning independently, these themes create a dynamic structure in which each component influences and reinforces the others. For instance, the institutional setting has a direct impact on students’ motivation, and their understanding of sustainability influences both their clinical judgements and their goals for future practice. This aligns with established definitions and indicates a baseline comprehension among nursing students of the intergenerational dimensions of sustainable development. Participants’ narratives revealed that their conceptualization of sustainability encompassed critical components such as climate change, environmental degradation, accountability, and adaptability. This recognition reflects a convergence with the literature that positions sustainability as a dynamic construct requiring proactive engagement [20]. Other finding revealed that one important factor that has been noted is the curriculum gap, which makes it necessary for students to learn about sustainability. This causes the students to have varying degrees of knowledge and competency. Although these challenges, students continuously link better patient care to sustainable practices, highlighting the importance of sustainability as a fundamental aspect of nursing education. The awareness of the environmental and health implications of sustainability also emerged strongly from the data. Students identified sustainability as intersecting with public health, economic stability, and overall societal well-being—reinforcing the notion that sustainability in nursing transcends environmental stewardship and is central to holistic patient care. Embedding this understanding in nursing education and professional standards can enhance the profession’s role in mitigating environmental harm and promoting health equity [20]. Participants emphasized that education significantly influences attitudes toward sustainability. Several noted that sustainability training fosters environmentally conscious behaviors, not only within hospital settings but also in the wider community. Although many reported a heightened awareness of unsustainable practices during clinical placements, they also expressed uncertainty regarding how to translate this awareness into practical action. These findings echo [21], who found that while students recognized the links between healthcare practices, resource use, and climate change, they struggled to operationalize sustainable practices due to limited institutional support and guidance. The application of sustainable principles in clinical practice was discussed with cautious optimism. While participants identified existing efforts such as waste reduction and resource management, they also pointed to barriers, including inadequate infrastructure and unclear policy frameworks. These limitations hinder the widespread implementation of sustainable practices, despite nurses’ willingness to contribute. As Mead et al. [22] noted, addressing the contextual mechanisms that facilitate or impede sustainability is essential to embedding environmental considerations in healthcare improvement initiatives. Significant barriers to implementation were noted, including the scarcity of eco-friendly resources, institutional inertia, and insufficient support from leadership. Participants described feelings of powerlessness and invisibility in efforts to champion sustainability within rigid systems, echoing sentiments found in Fields et al. [23]. These findings reinforce the necessity of systemic change—both at the educational and policy levels—to empower nurses and nursing students to engage meaningfully in sustainability efforts. Barriers are also multifactorial, involving individual, institutional, infrastructural, political, and societal dimensions [24, 25]. Similarly, a study conducted by Zoromba (2025) found that nurses saw sustainability as an ethical duty associated with resource conservation and waste reduction. Waste management and energy conservation were examples of sustainability behaviors, but there were major obstacles due to uneven institutional policies and a lack of official training. Organizational difficulties, conflicting priorities, and a lack of drive were major obstacles, but teamwork, education, training, and leadership support were found to be essential enablers of sustainable practices [26]. Another study a line to this study barriers conducted by Aboueid(2023) found that individual factors (such as knowledge, skills, and attitude), institutional factors (such as budget, strategy, and preparedness), geographic and infrastructure factors (such as infrastructure and public awareness), political factors (such as incentives and regulations), and other factors (such as patient awareness and knowledge) were all considered barriers and enablers [27]. Crucially, the data revealed a shared belief in the positive impact of sustainability on patient care. Participants acknowledged that sustainable healthcare practices could improve care quality, reduce health disparities, and enhance patient satisfaction. This supports findings by Knagg et al. [28], who emphasized the role of patient education and empowerment in promoting sustainable healthcare delivery. Similarly, Asabea Addo et al. reported a positive correlation between environmental responsibility and patient satisfaction, suggesting that sustainability may serve as a quality indicator in healthcare provision [29]. Personal values and moral commitment emerged as strong motivators for sustainability engagement. Many participants reported taking individual actions toward environmental sustainability despite facing institutional constraints. This highlights the role of intrinsic motivation in driving change and aligns with theoretical perspectives on voluntary sustainability behaviors [24, 30]. These findings suggest that fostering personal responsibility and ethical engagement may be as important as structural interventions. Participants expressed a clear and forward-looking vision for the future of sustainability in nursing. Their aspirations included leadership in promoting sustainable technologies, interdisciplinary collaboration, and institutional reform. Sustainability was framed as a core nursing value, essential to the profession’s evolving identity. Rosa et al. similarly emphasized the role of health professionals in achieving global Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), advocating for proactive engagement from the nursing profession in environmental and social justice [31]. The study also revealed a consensus on the critical role of educational institutions in shaping sustainable nursing practice. Participants suggested that universities and healthcare facilities collaborate to provide project-based learning opportunities focused on environmental health. These might include initiatives in waste management, sustainability audits, or eco-conscious policy development. This recommendation is supported by Berchin et al. and Leal et al., who argue that higher education institutions must lead by example in sustainability education, capacity building, and community engagement [32, 33]. In addition to curriculum integration, participants called for supportive institutional structures such as mentorship programs, dedicated resources, and incentive schemes to foster sustained engagement. These findings reinforce the need for holistic educational reform that not only informs but also empowers students. As noted by Chen et al., collaboration between professional education and innovation can enhance sustainable outcomes [34]. Similarly, Nguyen-Van et al. highlight the importance of social networks and relationships in facilitating pro-environmental behavior [35].

Conclusions

This study concludes that students’ perceptions of how nursing students handle sustainability in their education and clinical practice vary. It emphasises how important it is to balance individual motivation with curricular and institutional support to produce a generation of nurses who are prepared to spearhead sustainable healthcare practices. The findings emphasise the importance of including environmental health into nursing education to develop future nurses who are both clinically capable and environmentally sensitive.

Study limitations and implications for education, practice, and policy

Despite the valuable insights generated, this study presents several limitations. The qualitative design, while effective in exploring nursing students’ perceptions, limits the generalizability of findings beyond the specific institutional context. Although the sample size reached data saturation, it may not reflect the full diversity of views across academic levels, regions, or cultural backgrounds. Additionally, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias, prompting participants to present more favourable views on sustainability than they might practice. The reliance on self-reported data also poses challenges in verifying whether expressed beliefs translate into actual clinical behaviours. Moreover, the dynamic nature of sustainability discourse is itself a limitation. As environmental concerns and healthcare policies evolve, so too may students’ perceptions, necessitating continuous evaluation to ensure relevance. The study also excluded faculty perspectives, which could have enriched the understanding of structural and curricular barriers to integrating sustainability in nursing education.
