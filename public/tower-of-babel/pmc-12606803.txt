Acupuncture improves migraine and quality of life in patients with migraine: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Tingting Lu, Yang Yang, Jieyun Li, Liangping Lu, Jie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveA systematic review (SR) with meta-analysis (MA) was conducted to investigate acupuncture's efficacy in treating migraine and its impact on quality of life.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Chinese Biomedical Databases, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP, and Wanfang were searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from database inception to May 31, 2024. Statistical analyses were performed with Review Manager 5.4.1.ResultsWe included 23 RCTs comprising 2295 patients. Compared with sham acupuncture, acupuncture reduced migraine duration (MD: − 4.36 h; 95% CI: − 8.07, − 0.64), the number of mean migraine attacks (MD: − 0.82; 95% CI: − 1.57, − 0.08), days with migraine per 4 weeks (MD: − 1.38; 95% CI: − 2.16, − 0.60), improved MSQ restrictive subscale (MD: 13.73; 95% CI: 5.60, 21.87) and MSQ emotional function (MD: 9.93; 95% CI: 2.64, 17.22). Compared with pharmacological treatment, acupuncture improved MSQ restrictive subscale (MD: 5.69; 95% CI: 2.15, 9.23) and MSQ emotional function (MD: 7.14; 95% CI: 1.62, 12.66). Compared with waiting lists, acupuncture reduced the number of mean migraine attacks (MD: − 0.80; 95% CI: − 1.12, − 0.48), days with migraine per 4 weeks (MD: − 2.10; 95% CI: − 2.74, − 1.46), and improved MSQ restrictive subscale (MD: 9.40; 95% CI: 4.82, 13.98) and MSQ emotional function (MD: 9.00; 95% CI: 4.52, 13.48).ConclusionsAcupuncture was significantly associated with reduced migraine duration, number of migraine attacks, number of migraine days, and improved quality of life. However, due to language restrictions and potential bias, further rigorous trials are needed to identify its efficacy and relevant exploratory trials are also warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02977-y.Keywords: Acupuncture, Migraine, Quality of life, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Migraine is one of the most prevalent neurologic disorders worldwide. Migraine is mainly characterized by recurrent unilateral pulsating headaches and transient focal neurological manifestations [1]. In 2021, migraine ranked third among the top 10 nervous system diseases with the highest age-standardized disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) [2], which are calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs) and represent the combined non-fatal and fatal burden of each condition [3]. Although a full and clear pathogenesis of migraine has not yet been established, several studies have demonstrated that the regulation of migraine attacks is related to a specific signaling molecule pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide [4]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen are first-line treatments for migraines, while triptans, 5-hydroxytryptamine type 1F (5-HT1F) receptor agonists, and Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor antagonists are specific options for acute migraine relief [5, 6]. However, long-term treatment of migraine with western drugs can cause adverse reactions and drug dependence, and their high costs and restricted availability have limited their use [7]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore valid complementary and alternative therapies for the treatment of migraine.

Acupuncture is inserting needles into specific acupoints on the human meridians (paths through which the vital energy known as “qi” flows) to modulate body physiology [8]. Acupuncture originated in China more than 3000 years ago and has been recognized and used worldwide [9]. The British National Health System recommends acupuncture for headaches, migraines, back pain, etc. [10, 11]. Manual acupuncture was superior to sham acupuncture and usual care in the prophylaxis of episodic migraine without aura [12], and acupuncture may be safer and more effective than topiramate for chronic migraine [13]. Although several studies have shown that acupuncture may be more effective than sham acupuncture and Western medicine in improving the quality of life of migraine patients [12, 14, 15], its efficacy needs to be confirmed by further research.

Therefore, this systematic review (SR) with meta-analysis (MA) of RCTs was designed to investigate the efficacy of acupuncture for treating migraine and explore its impact on patients' quality of life, to generate reliable evidence for clinical practice.

Methods

This SR with MA was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [16].

Search strategy

The PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Chinese Biomedical Databases (CBM), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP (China Science and Technology Journal Database), and Wanfang Database were searched systematically using a comprehensive search strategy for identifying potentially available RCTs from database inception to May 31, 2024. Two investigators screened the reference lists of the included RCTs and relevant SRs to identify potentially eligible studies. The detailed search strategy is provided in Appendix (Text S1).

Selection criteria

Participants were specified as having migraine (with or without aura) by the International Headache Society (IHS), the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD), or other criteria, without age restriction [1]. The interventions were acupuncture, and the control interventions involved sham acupuncture, pharmacological treatment (e.g., flunarizine, metoprolol, venlafaxine), usual care, and waiting lists. RCTs published in both English and Chinese were included.

The primary outcomes of interest were migraine duration, the number of mean migraine attacks, and days with migraine per 4 weeks. The secondary outcomes were the migraine-specific quality-of-life questionnaire (MSQ) and the emotional functional and restrictive subscales and change from baseline in the total score of the Migraine Disability Assessment Score (MIDAS). The MSQ questionnaire comprises three scales: restrictive subscale, role prevention, and emotional functional, which are used to assess three quality-of-life domains [17]. The MIDAS questionnaire assesses the work and daily life activities of migraine patients in five dimensions: the sum of days missed at work or school, days missed at housework, days missed at non-work activities, and days at work or school plus days of housework where productivity was reduced by half or more in the last 3 months [18].

Studies that used the same acupuncture method but compared different acupoints were excluded. Studies that combined the intervention or control measure with other therapies, were also excluded. Duplicate studies, those with unavailable data or full-text, and editors, comments, and conference abstracts were excluded. Studies that only reported the outcome of effectiveness, and sample sizes of less than 10 were also excluded.

All studies identified from the databases were imported into EndnoteX9. Two independent investigators screened the studies according to the abovementioned selection criteria. Any disagreement in the process was resolved by consulting a third investigator.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Two independent investigators extracted the following items: study characteristics (first author, year of publication, and country), baseline characteristics (sample size, age, and details of interventions), study design, and data on outcomes of interest [19]. All extracted data were checked for accuracy and consistency by a third investigator.

Two of the investigators assessed the risk of bias (RoB) of eligible RCTs independently using the Cochrane RoB tool, which includes random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias [20]. Each domain could be classified as low, unclear, or high RoB. Any disagreement in the process was resolved through discussion with a third investigator.

Statistical analysis

We adopted the meta-analysis to inform the direct estimates using Review Manager 5.4.1. Heterogeneity across different RCTs was identified by the χ2 test and reported as I2 value. If there was no statistical heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model was used. If an I2 value > 50% was considered as highly statistical heterogeneity, the random-effects model was used. In addition, sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The continuous outcome with the same unit was calculated as mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For studies with more than one control group, such as acupuncture vs sham acupuncture vs waitlist control vs usual care, the results were split into pairwise comparisons by the different comparison groups. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on the type of control interventions (including sham acupuncture, pharmacological treatment, usual care, and waiting lists) and treatment duration periods. When 10 or more studies were available for comparison, funnel plots were used to assess publication bias. The threshold of statistical significance is set at p < 0.05, and all statistical tests are two-sided.

Results

Study selection

We identified a total of 3131 potential studies, and 1471 duplicates were removed using Endnote X9. The independent investigators screened the titles and abstracts to find possible eligible studies. The full text of 141 studies was read, and eventually, a total of 23 RCTs [12, 21–42] (Fig. 1) were included for further analysis.

Study characteristics

We included 23 RCTs [12, 21–42] comprising 2295 patients, conducted in six countries. The publication years ranged from 2005 to 2024. Thirteen RCTs were based on different versions of the ICHD [12, 23–26, 28, 31, 32, 35, 37–39, 42], seven were based on IHS criteria [21, 22, 27, 30, 33, 34, 40], and the remaining three did not mention the criteria used [29, 36, 41]. Among the 23 RCTs included, 20 (87%) were 2-group design, of which 11 compared acupuncture with sham acupuncture [23, 24, 26–30, 32, 36, 37, 42]. The remaining 3 were 3-group studies (13%), two of which compared acupuncture with sham acupuncture or waiting lists [21, 33], and one compared acupuncture with sham acupuncture or usual care [12]. Furthermore, five RCTs (22%) compared acupuncture with flunarizine [25, 31, 35, 39, 41], and three RCT (13%) compared acupuncture with pharmacological treatment [40], venlafaxine [34], or metoprolol [22], respectively. The follow-up duration was from four weeks to one year. Characteristics of the included RCTs are shown in Table 1.

Risk of bias

In the domain of randomization sequence generation, 21 RCTs (91%) provided a random allocation scheme with low RoB, whereas two RCTs (9%) did not mention it, with unclear RoB. In allocation concealment, 12 RCTs (52%) provided an allocation concealment scheme, which was considered to have a low RoB, whereas 11 RCTs (48%) did not mention it, with unclear RoB. In the blinding of participants and personnel, nine RCTs (39%) blinded participants with low RoB, 13 RCTs (57%) did not mention it, with unclear RoB and one RCT (4%) did not blind participants with high RoB. In the blinding of outcome assessment, nine RCTs (39%) blinded outcome measures and processors with low RoB, and 14 RCTs (61%) were not provided with unclear RoB. All RCTs were considered to have a low RoB in presenting incomplete outcome data and an unclear RoB in selective reporting. In addition, 22 RCTs (96%) had low RoB, whereas one RCT (4%) had implementation bias with high RoB in other sources of bias. The results of the RoB assessment of RCTs are shown in Table 2.

The results of meta-analysis

With regard to migraine duration (Fig. 2), we pooled the results from eight studies [28, 30–32, 34, 37, 39, 42]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture (MD: − 4.36 h; 95% CI: − 8.07, − 0.64; I2 = 94%; P = 0.02), suggesting that acupuncture can effectively shorten the duration of migraine. Whereas there was no significant difference between acupuncture and pharmacological treatment groups (MD: − 9.90 h; 95% CI: − 27.43, − 7.62; I2 = 98%; P = 0.27), indicating that both treatments may have similar effects on migraine duration.

Forest plot of migraine duration for acupuncture versus the control group

With regard to the number of mean migraine attacks (Fig. 3), we pooled the results from nine studies [21, 22, 25, 28, 29, 34, 35, 40, 42]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture (MD: − 0.82; 95% CI: − 1.57, − 0.08; I2 = 87%; P = 0.03), suggesting that acupuncture can reduce the frequency of migraine attacks. However, there was no significant difference between acupuncture and pharmacological treatment groups (MD: − 0.77; 95% CI: − 1.87, 0.33; I2 = 93%; P = 0.17), indicating that acupuncture may be comparable to pharmacological treatment. Additionally, acupuncture exhibited a better effect than waiting lists (MD: − 0.80; 95% CI: − 1.12, − 0.48; P < 0.00001), further highlighting its effectiveness.

Forest plot of the number of mean migraine attacks for acupuncture versus the control group

With regard to days with migraine per 4 weeks (Fig. 4), we pooled the results from five studies [27, 30, 32, 33, 37]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture (MD: − 1.38; 95% CI: − 2.16, − 0.60; I2 = 80%; P = 0.0005), and acupuncture exhibited a better effect than waiting lists (MD: − 2.10; 95% CI: − 2.74, − 1.46; P < 0.00001). These findings suggest that acupuncture can effectively reduce the number of days with migraine.

Forest plot of days with migraine per 4 weeks for acupuncture versus the control group

With regard to the MSQ restrictive subscale (Fig. 5), we pooled the results from six studies [23, 24, 26, 33, 36, 41]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture (MD: 13.73; 95% CI: 5.60, 21.87; I2 = 92%; P = 0.0009), acupuncture also exhibited a better effect than pharmacological treatment groups (MD: 5.69; 95% CI: 2.15, 9.23; P = 0.002), and waiting lists (MD: 9.40; 95% CI: 4.82, 13.98; P < 0.0001). These findings suggest that acupuncture can significantly improve the restrictive aspects of migraine.

Forest plot of the MSQ restrictive subscale for acupuncture versus the control group

With regard to the MSQ emotional function (Fig. 6), we pooled the results from six studies [23, 24, 26, 33, 36, 41]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture (MD: 9.93; 95% CI: 2.64, 17.22; I2 = 90%; P = 0.008), acupuncture also exhibited a better effect than pharmacological treatment groups (MD: 7.14; 95% CI: 1.62, 12.66; P = 0.01), and waiting lists (MD: 9.00; 95% CI: 4.52, 13.48; P < 0.0001). These findings suggest that acupuncture can significantly improve the emotional aspects of migraine.

Forest plot of the MSQ emotional function for acupuncture versus the control group

With regard to the change from baseline in the score of MIDAS (Fig. 7), we pooled the results from two studies [12, 38]. The result showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and control group (including sham acupuncture, waiting lists, usual care) (MD: − 10.28; 95% CI: − 17.15, − 3.41; I2 = 0%; P = 0.003). The finding suggests that acupuncture can reduce disability associated with migraine.

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis, we performed by eliminating one study in each turn. For migraine duration, excluding Ma et al. showed a statistically significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture, with a significant reduction in heterogeneity (MD: − 1.59 h; 95% CI: − 2.16, − 1.01; I2 = 28%; P < 0.00001) [37]. For the number of mean migraine attacks, excluding Linde et al. revealed a significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture, with a significant reduction in heterogeneity (MD: − 1.13; 95% CI: − 1.74, − 0.52; I2 = 61%; P = 0.0003) [21]. For days with migraine per 4 weeks, the heterogeneity was not significantly reduced by excluding each study. For the MSQ restrictive subscale, excluding Chang et al. showed a significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture, with a reduction in heterogeneity (MD: 9.30; 95% CI: 3.73, 14.88; I2 = 79%; P = 0.001) [24]. Similarly, for the MSQ emotional function, excluding Chang et al. showed a significant difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture, with a significant reduction in heterogeneity (MD: 4.00; 95% CI: 3.24, 4.75; I2 = 0%; P < 0.00001) [24].

Subgroup analysis

For migraine duration, acupuncture showed a significant difference over sham acupuncture for the period of ≤ 4 weeks (MD: −10.22; 95% CI: −18.81, −1.63; P = 0.02), ≥ 1 year (MD: −8.84; 95% CI: −10.60, −7.08; P < 0.00001) and not mentioned (NM) (MD: −1.55; 95% CI: −2.12, −0.97; I2 = 0%; P < 0.00001). The results of chi-square test (Chi2 = 62.76, P < 0.00001) showed significant differences between subgroups. Additionally, acupuncture was significantly superior to pharmacological treatments for ≤ 4 weeks (MD: −33.30; 95% CI: −39.16, −27.44; P < 0.00001). The chi-square test results (Chi2 = 124.69, P < 0.00001) also showed significant differences between subgroups.

For the number of mean migraine attacks, acupuncture was significantly better than sham acupuncture for the period of ≤ 4 weeks (MD: −1.00; 95% CI: −1.45, −0.55; P < 0.0001). The results of chi-square test (Chi2 = 11.10, P = 0.004) showed significant differences between subgroups. Additionally, there was no significant difference between acupuncture and pharmacological treatment groups for ≤ 4 weeks and NM. For days with migraine per 4 weeks, acupuncture was significantly better than sham acupuncture for the period of ≤ 4 weeks (MD: −1.29; 95% CI: −1.80, −0.78; I2 = 17%; P < 0.00001) and ≥ 1 year (MD: −1.94; 95% CI: −2.23, −1.65; P < 0.00001), but not for NM. The results of chi-square test (Chi2 = 1.19, P = 0.28) showed no significant differences between subgroups.

For the MSQ restrictive subscale, acupuncture showed a significant improvement over sham acupuncture for 9–12 weeks (MD: 26.78; 95% CI: 20.89, 32.67; P < 0.00001) and NM (MD: 12.51; 95% CI: 2.62, 22.39; I2 = 90%; P = 0.01). The results of chi-square test (Chi2 = 37.65, P < 0.00001) showed significant differences between subgroups. For the MSQ emotional function, acupuncture showed a significant improvement over sham acupuncture for the period of ≤ 4 weeks (MD: 6.00; 95% CI: 1.52, 10.48; P = 0.009), 9–12 weeks (MD: 22.22; 95% CI: 16.36, 28.08; P < 0.00001), and NM (MD: 4.72; 95% CI: 1.18, 8.26; I2 = 34%; P = 0.009). The results of chi-square test (Chi2 = 26.63, P < 0.00001) showed significant differences between subgroups.

Overall, acupuncture showed significantly better than sham acupuncture over multiple treatment duration periods for migraine duration, number of migraine attacks, migraine days per 4 weeks, and quality of life (MSQ restrictive subscale and MSQ emotional function). In addition, acupuncture was significantly better than pharmacological treatment in reducing migraine duration in the short term (≤ 4 weeks) and may be as comparable as pharmacological treatment in the number of migraine attacks. These results suggest that acupuncture is an effective option for migraine management, especially in both short and long-term treatments showing significant efficacy. Subgroup analysis results are presented in Appendix (Text S3).

Discussion

This SR with MA included 23 RCTs involving 2295 patients and evaluated the efficacy and quality of life of acupuncture among migraine patients. The present study indicated that acupuncture could reduce migraine duration, the number of mean migraine attacks, days with migraine per 4 weeks more than sham acupuncture, and the number of mean migraine attacks, days with migraine per 4 weeks than waiting lists. In terms of quality of life, acupuncture has an improvement in the MSQ restrictive subscale, MSQ emotional function than sham acupuncture, pharmacological treatment and waiting lists, and the score of MIDAS than usual care.

Acupuncture (compared with sham acupuncture) was associated with a reduction in migraine duration. Moreover, we found that acupuncture was more effective than sham acupuncture and waiting lists in terms of the number of mean migraine attacks and days with migraine per 4 weeks. Several meta-analyses have revealed that acupuncture may be more effective than sham acupuncture in reducing migraine intensity [14], the frequency of migraine attacks [15], and migraine days [43]. We found that acupuncture showed no significant advantage over pharmacological treatment in reducing the number of mean migraine attacks. However, acupuncture was significantly superior to pharmacological treatments for ≤ 4 weeks. The previous meta-analysis also revealed that acupuncture is better than or comparable to medication [14, 44].

In terms of the change from baseline in the score of MIDAS, we found that acupuncture was superior to usual care, however acupuncture showed no significant differences between sham acupuncture and waiting lists. The results are similar to those of previous studies [43], which indicate that sham acupuncture has a significant placebo effect of sham acupuncture in terms of migraine symptoms, emotional condition, and quality of life. It is usually related to moderate non-specific effects or a placebo effect of frequent interactions between patients and acupuncturists [45]. Moreover, compared with sham acupuncture, pharmacological treatment, and waiting lists, acupuncture improved MSQ restrictive subscale and MSQ emotional function. The previous meta-analysis also revealed that acupuncture had a higher MSQ score than sham acupuncture and pharmacological treatment regarding function-restrictive and emotional function after treatment [46], which is consistent with our finding.

The findings from our SR with MA demonstrated that acupuncture was significantly superior to sham acupuncture in treating migraine and improving quality of life. Through sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis, we observed that treatment duration periods contributed to the heterogeneity of the study and found that acupuncture had significant efficacy in the treatment of migraine, especially in short and long-term treatment. Furthermore, acupuncture was significantly better than pharmacological treatment in reducing migraine duration in the short term, and acupuncture was comparable to pharmacological treatment in some respects. The neural mechanism of the immediate effect of acupuncture in the treatment of migraine remains unclear, and sustained acupuncture may be related to the regulation of pain-related brain networks [47]. Acupuncture causes a stimulus reflex that activates peripheral nerves and transmits sensory information from the spinal cord to the brain, activating peripheral autonomic pathways [48]. An imaging study indicated that acupuncture could activate brain regions with pain perception, emotional processing and was closely associated with improved functional connectivity in migraine patients [49]. Acupuncture may also inhibit neurotransmitter release, reduce neuroinflammatory responses, modulate central analgesic mechanisms, and normalize abnormal brain activity, thereby blocking the transmission of pain signals [50]. A cost-effective study also revealed that acupuncture could offer clinical benefits and cost savings in the treatment of migraine [51]. Therefore, acupuncture remains a valid treatment option for migraine.

The present MA has some strengths. First, this MA was conducted to compare acupuncture therapies and sham acupuncture, pharmacological treatment, usual care, and waiting lists for the improvement of symptoms and quality of life in patients with migraine. Second, we performed subgroup and sensitivity analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity. The results provided guidance for clinical practice by revealing differences in efficacy across treatment duration periods, validating the robustness of the results, and enhancing their credibility. There are also several limitations in this MA. First, this MA only included studies published in English and Chinese; this may restrict the generalizability of the results. Second, acupuncture treatment periods among participants within each study and between studies may contribute to heterogeneity; it is necessary to standardize acupuncture treatments, harmonize measurement tools and methods, and reduce heterogeneity between studies. Third, because of the limited number of trials included in each outcome, funnel plots were not feasible. Therefore, we could not fully evaluate publication bias. Future research in the areas is needed to increase sample size and long-term follow-up to provide more reliable evidence to verify results.

Conclusions

Acupuncture was significantly associated with a reduction in migraine duration, number of migraine attacks, number of migraine days, and an improvement in quality of life (especially for MSQ restrictive subscale and MSQ emotional function). Acupuncture was not always significantly superior to pharmacologic treatment in terms of migraine duration and number of migraine attacks. However, due to the limitations of the study, more rigorous and multi-center RCTs are needed to further identify the effectiveness of acupuncture in treating migraines. Further clinical trials with long-term follow-up and comparisons with new pharmacologic treatments are also essential.
