Extensions of integral orthoregular sets and icubes
Márton Erdélyi, Péter Maga, Gergely Zábrádi

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the question when a (rational or Gaussian) integral vector can be extended to an integral orthogonal basis consisting of vectors of equal length. We also study when a set of integral vectors has such an extension. Some necessary conditions are given which are proven to be sufficient in dimensions 3 3 and 4 4 .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11E25, 11R52, 52C07

1.
Introduction

Given an integral vector
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-y\\
x\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is also integral, has the same length as the original one, and they are orthogonal to each other. This motivates the question in higher dimension: given an integral vector, can we find orthogonal integral vectors of the same length, or, most optimistically, can we complete our first vector to an orthogonal basis consisting of integral vectors of equal length?
The answer in this generality is negative, as one can readily check that if
n
is odd and all the coordinates of
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are odd, then for any
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
we have
|w|^{2}=w^{T}w\equiv v^{T}w\penalty\ (\mathrm{mod}\penalty\ 2)
showing that if
w
is orthogonal to
v
of then
|w|^{2}
is even, thus there is no integral vector which is orthogonal to
v
of the same length.

The more refined question is then of course
when
a given vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
can be continued to an orthogonal system consisting of vectors of the same length. This turns out to be surprisingly challenging even in low dimension. Before describing the history of the problem and our new results, we introduce the main notions following

[
GKMS12
]
. (All along the paper, vectors are thought of as column vectors.)

Definition

(
k
-icube in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
)
.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, a matrix
(v_{1}|\dotsc|v_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times k}
is called a
k
-icube (in
\mathbb{Z}
, of norm
\lambda>0
),
if

v_{i}^{T}v_{j}=\begin{cases}\lambda,\qquad&\text{if $i=j$},\\
0,&\text{if $i\neq j$.}\end{cases}

In particular, a nonzero integral vector
v
alone is a
1
-icube of norm
|v|^{2}
. For
1\leq\ell<k
, we say that an
\ell
-icube
A
can be extended to a
k
-icube, if some
\ell
columns of some
k
-icube form
A
. An icube is a
k
-icube for some
1\leq k\leq n
.

In this paper, we present our new findings in this area, which we group into four topics. The first one collects some observations about the extendability of vectors (
1
-cubes) to
n
-cubes both over
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. The second one solves completely the question of extendability of icubes in dimensions
3
and
4
over the Gaussian integers (whose counterpart over the rational integers had previously been solved by others). The third one is the
2
-dimensional setup (both over the rationals and quadratic number fields) for arbitrary hermitian forms in place of the standard inner product of the
n
-space, revealing a delicate connection to divisibility relations in orders of certain quaternion algebras. Lastly, we provide an application in the sup-norm problem of automorphic forms – which in fact had been our main motivation to investigate the extendability of Gaussian integral icubes.

1.1.
Extension of vectors to
n
-icubes

The analysis of rational integral
3
-icubes has been started by Sárközy
[
Sár61
]
using Euler matrices (see also the work of Horváth
[
Hor24
]

[

]

[

]
, and Chamizo and Jiménez-Urroz
[

]

]

For

every integral vector can be extended to an
n
-icube and the construction uses the algebraic properties of complex numbers, quaternions and octonions respectively. This is based on the existence of Hurwitz algebras, so we have no such constructions for other values of

(see for example the first page of
[

]

Proposition 1.1

(
[
GKMS12
, Proposition 1.3]
, already proven in
[

, attributed to Kárteszi)
.

If

is odd, then the norm of any

-icube in

is a square.

Our first result is

Theorem 1

If

and

is contained in an
n
-icube, then
|v|^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
is the sum of two squares.

Note that this is a generalization of
[

, Proposition 18]
.

One can synthesize Proposition
1.1
and Theorem

as follows:

Corollary 1.2

If

is contained in an
n
-icube, then
|v|^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
is the sum of
(4,n)
squares.

The converse is not true in general, for example the vector
(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{9}
has square norm but the argument of the first paragraph shows that it cannot be extended to a
9
-icube. On the other hand

is also of norm 9, and it has an obvious extension to a

However for

(
[

, already proven in

If

Thus

Theorem 3
.

Assume

We also have

Theorem 4

Any vector in

Note that while one can extend any vector

For

Let

(1)

\mu=\Delta\varepsilon,

is an absolute square

For example if

(integral

If

After fixing

Finding a 3-icube containing a fixed vector

1.4.
An application to automorphic forms

We also present an application from the analytic theory of automorphic forms. Assume
G
is a reductive group defined over a number field
K
such that
G(K_{\infty})
is not compact for at least one archimedean place
K_{\infty}
of
K
. Consider then the quotient space

X:=G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),

where
\mathbb{A}
stands for the adele ring of
K
, and assume that
X
has finite volume with respect to the
G(\mathbb{A})
-invariant measure (regarding the theory, this is quite natural to do). The sup-norm problem of automorphic forms asks for pointwise bounds on smooth,
L^{2}
-normalized functions
\phi:X\to\mathbb{C}
which generate irredubible
G(\mathbb{A})
-representations under the right action of
G(\mathbb{A})
. We further assume that
\phi
is spherical, i.e. it is fixed under the action of a maximal compact subgroup of
G(\mathbb{A})
. The baseline bound is due to Sarnak
[
Sar04
]
, and it says the following. For any compact set
\Omega\subseteq X
, we have that
1
1

1

Here and below, we follow the notation of Vinogradov:
A\ll_{D}B
means that
|A|\leq CB
for some constant
C
depending only on
D
.

\|\phi|_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}\ll_{\Omega}\lambda_{\phi}^{\frac{\dim-\mathrm{rk}}{4}}

holds for all
\phi
, where
\lambda_{\phi}
is the Laplace eigenvalue of
\phi
, while
\dim
and
\mathrm{rk}
stand for the dimension and the rank, respectively, of the real symmetric space corresponding to
X
. This general bound relies only on the analytic properties of
\phi
, in particular, it holds for eigenfunctions on more general symmetric spaces, and the exponent
\frac{\dim-\mathrm{rk}}{4}
is known to be sharp in the broad generality. The arithmetic sup-norm problem asks if it can be improved to
\frac{\dim-\mathrm{rk}}{4}-\delta(G)
with some
\delta(G)>0
depending only on
G
in the arithmetic situation presented here. For
G=\mathrm{PGL}_{n}
(a prominent class of groups in the theory), the answer is positive, see
[
BM16
]
for
K=\mathbb{Q}
and
[
MZ24
]
for
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
. More general classes of groups are treated in the unpublished preprint of Marshall
[
Mar
]
, in particular, all real quasi-split simple groups except for those being isogenous to
\mathrm{SU}_{n,n-1}
,
n\geq 2
. This exceptional class seems to be untreatable with the current technology, and our investigations on icubes give a quantitative explanation to this obstruction, at least, for some small values of
n
. We describe this phenomenon briefly, noting that in fact, the sup-norm problem of automorphic forms on
\mathrm{SU}_{n,n-1}
had been the original motivation to our research on icubes.

The method towards all known results in the sup-norm problem (at least, in high rank) starts with applying (some version of) the amplification method as pioneered by Iwaniec and Sarnak
[
IS95
]
and estimating the so-called spherical transform. A careful analysis leads then to the heart of the matter: a delicate matrix-counting problem whose exact form depends a lot on what
G
and
K
exactly are.

For the group
G=\mathrm{SU}_{n,n-1}
over
K=\mathbb{Q}
, this matrix-counting problem is the following (after some simplifications), see e.g.
[
BM15
]
for more details, which describes the setup of the analogous matrix-counting problem for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}
. Assume that
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are Gaussian primes lying above split rational primes such that
|\ell_{1}|^{2},|\ell_{2}|^{2}
are about size
L
, a large parameter, and consider

S_{n}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):=\left\{\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i])\ \mathrm{diag}(1,\ell_{1}\ell_{2},\dotsc,\ell_{1}\ell_{2},|\ell_{1}\ell_{2}|^{2})\ \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}[i])\mid\gamma^{*}\gamma=|\ell_{1}\ell_{2}|^{2}\cdot I\right\},

where
I
stands for the identity matrix.
Note that the set
S_{n}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
is exactly the set of
n
-icubes with Smith normal form

\mathrm{diag}(1,\ell_{1}\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{1}\ell_{2},|\ell_{1}\ell_{2}|^{2}).

To solve the sup-norm problem, it would be sufficient to prove that

\#S_{n}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\ll_{n}L^{2(n-1)-\eta}

holds for some
\eta>0
(which might depend on
n
but nothing else).

Unfortunately, this is not the case even for
n=2
. One can easily see that

\#S_{2}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=\{(a,b,c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}\mid a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}=|\ell_{1}\ell_{2}|^{2},\ \gcd(a^{2}+b^{2},|\ell_{1}\ell_{2}|^{2})=1\},

Lemma 2.1

Assume

Since

Let

\pi^{2r}\mid\pi^{r}\overline{\pi}^{r}\mid\alpha\overline{\alpha}=a\mid b=\beta\overline{\beta},

Now assume that

Now if

then we define

For this section let

and put

for the ring of integers in

Observe that if

More generally,

where

is an absolute square in

and the only absolute square in

which divides

First we prove a rational version of Proposition
1.8
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
(a_{1},a_{2})\in K^{2}\times K^{2}
is a

\lambda
, then
a_{2}=(Ma_{1})_{\perp}/\delta
for some
\delta\in K

Proof.

Note that for any

we have

(5)

\alpha Q((x,y)^{T})=|\alpha x+\beta y|^{2}+(\alpha\gamma-|\beta|^{2})|y|^{2}=|\alpha x+\beta y|^{2}+\mu|y|^{2}.

If

and

form a

-orthobalanced basis,
then

, which implies that

a_{2}=(Ma_{1})_{\perp}/\delta=\left(-(\overline{\beta}x+\gamma y),\alpha x+\beta y\right)^{*}/\delta

for some

. Applying (

\begin{split}\alpha Q((Ma_{1})_{\perp})&=|-\alpha\cdot(\overline{\overline{\beta}x+\gamma y})+\beta\cdot(\overline{\alpha x+\beta y})|^{2}+\mu|\overline{\alpha x+\beta y}|^{2}\\

Finally, we obtain that

Lemma 3.6
.

If
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}[i]
and
\varepsilon=1
then

S=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}[j],&\text{ if }R=\mathbb{Z}\\
\mathbb{A}=\{r+sj\mid r,s\in\mathbb{Z}[i]\},&\text{ if }R=\mathbb{Z}[i].\end{cases}

In both cases
S
has enough divisors.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
S
is as in the statement. We are left with checking that the condition in the definition of “
S
has enough divisors” holds. Assume hence that
|y|^{2}

divides
|t|^{2}
for some
y,t\in S
, our goal is to factorize
t
to
uv
such that
|u|^{2}=|y|^{2}
.

The case
S=\mathbb{Z}[j]
follows from Lemma
2.1
, hence we focus on
S=\mathbb{A}
. Since the case
|y|^{2}=1
is trivial, a simple induction argument shows that it suffices to treat the situation when
|y|^{2}=p
is a rational prime number. We go by cases.

Case 1:
p=2
. Then for
t=t_{\mathbb{R}}+t_{i}i+t_{j}j+t_{k}k
(with
t_{\mathbb{R}},t_{i},t_{j},t_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
), we have that
2\mid|t|^{2}=t_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}+t_{i}^{2}+t_{j}^{2}+t_{k}^{2}
, hence
t_{\mathbb{R}}
has the same parity as odd many among
t_{i},t_{j},t_{k}
. If, say,
t_{\mathbb{R}}\equiv t_{i}\bmod 2
, then
t_{j}\equiv t_{k}\bmod 2
, and one can easily check that

(1+i)^{-1}t=\frac{t_{\mathbb{R}}+t_{i}}{2}+\frac{-t_{\mathbb{R}}+t_{i}}{2}i+\frac{t_{j}+t_{k}}{2}j+\frac{-t_{j}+t_{k}}{2}k\in S,

hence
u=1+i
and
v=(1+i)^{-1}t
give an appropriate factorization admitting
|u|^{2}=2
. If
t_{\mathbb{R}}\equiv t_{j}\bmod 2
, then similarly
u=1+j
,
v=(1+j)^{-1}t
, while if
t_{\mathbb{R}}\equiv t_{k}\bmod 2
, then
u=1+k
,
v=(1+k)^{-1}t
are appropriate.

Case 2:
p\neq 2
and
|t|^{2}<p^{2}
. Let
S^{\prime}:=S\cup(S+\frac{1+i+j+k}{2})
be the order of Hurwitz quaternions. It is known that every nonzero right ideal of
S^{\prime}
is a principal right ideal (in fact, there is a “non-commutative euclidean division”, see e.g.
[
CS03
, Section 5.1]
). This implies that, for some
u^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
,

pS^{\prime}+tS^{\prime}=u^{\prime}S^{\prime}.

Then
p=u^{\prime}c
for some
c\in S^{\prime}
, hence
|u^{\prime}|^{2}\mid p^{2}
(in
\mathbb{Z}
). Similarly,
t=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
for some
v^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
, hence
|u^{\prime}|^{2}\mid|t|^{2}
(in
\mathbb{Z}
). Recalling we are under the assumption
|t|^{2}<p^{2}
, these altogether imply that
|u^{\prime}|^{2}\in\{1,p\}
. On the other hand, we have that

u^{\prime}=pa+tb
with some
a,b\in S^{\prime}
, hence

|u^{\prime}|^{2}=p^{2}|a|^{2}+p(a\overline{tb}+tb\overline{a})+|t|^{2}|b|^{2}.

By assumption,
p\mid|t|^{2}
, hence
|u^{\prime}|^{2}=p
.

If
u^{\prime}\in S
, then we set
u:=u^{\prime},v:=v^{\prime}
.

If
u^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
, then choose
\omega=\frac{\pm 1\pm i\pm j\pm k}{2}
in such a way that
(u^{\prime}+\omega)/2\in S
(i.e.
u^{\prime}+\omega
has even integer coordinates in the basis
1,i,j,k
). Let then

u:=u^{\prime}\overline{\omega}=(u^{\prime}+\omega)\overline{\omega}-1=\frac{u^{\prime}+\omega}{2}\cdot 2\overline{\omega}-1.

Then on the one hand,
|u|^{2}=|u^{\prime}|^{2}=p
, while on the other hand,
u\in S
. Correspondingly, let
v:=\omega v^{\prime}
.

In either case,
uv=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}=t
is clear. Further,
u
has odd many odd coordinates (in the standard basis
1,i,j,k
), since
|u|^{2}=p
is odd. This implies that
v\notin S^{\prime}\setminus S
(for if
v\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
, then
t=uv\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
, a contradiction). Then
uv=t
,
u,v\in S
and
|u|^{2}=p
.

Case 3:
p\neq 2
and
|t|^{2}\geq p
. Choose
m\in S
such that

t^{\prime}:=t-pm\in\left(-\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2}\right)+\left(-\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2}\right)i+\left(-\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2}\right)j+\left(-\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2}\right)k,

in particular,
|t^{\prime}|^{2}<p^{2}
. Then

|t^{\prime}|^{2}=|t-pm|^{2}=(t-pm)(\overline{t}-p\overline{m})\equiv|t|^{2}\equiv 0\bmod p.

We know from Case 2 that
t^{\prime}=uv
with some
u,v\in S
,
|u|^{2}=u\overline{u}=p
. Then
t=uv+pm=u(v+\overline{u}m)
is an appropriate factorization, since
v+\overline{u}m\in S
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}
and

be a positive definite hermitian form with matrix

(2)

4.

From now on let

or

or

again.

Let

be an icube of norm

. We will need the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

(1)

The
R
-module

\Lambda=\{w\in R^{n}\mid a_{j}^{*}w=0\text{ for }1\leq j\leq k\}

is free of rank
n-k
.

(2)

If

is the restriction of the standard inner product to
\Lambda
, then

\mathrm{disc}(Q)=\frac{\lambda^{k}}{|d_{k}(A_{0})|^{2}},

where

is the determinant of the Gram matrix of

with respect to a free basis as in (1) and

is the

-th determinantal divisor of

.

The case
R=\mathbb{Z}
,

and
a_{1}\in R^{n}
is primitive (

) is
[
Hor24
, Proposition 2]
.

Proof.

(1)

As
R
is a principal ideal domain,
\Lambda
is free. On the other hand, we have

\dim(\Lambda\otimes K)=\dim(\mathrm{Span}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})^{\perp})=n-k,

so
\Lambda
has rank
n-k
.

(2)

Fix a basis
b_{k+1},b_{k+2},\dots,b_{n}
of
\Lambda
and let

\Lambda^{\prime}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k},b_{k+1},\dots,b_{n}\rangle\leq R^{n},

a free module of rank
n
and put
B:=(a_{1}|a_{2}|\dots|a_{k}|b_{k+1}|\dots|b_{n})
. Then
B^{*}B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda I&\\
&M\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where

is the identity matrix and
M=M^{*}\in R^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
is the matrix of
Q
in the basis
b_{k+1},b_{k+2},\dots,b_{n}
. Thus

(11)

\mathrm{disc}(Q)=\det(M)=|\det(B)|^{2}/\lambda^{k}.

The
R
-index
[R^{n}:\Lambda^{\prime}]_{R}
is defined (
[
Con
]
, Definition 5.14) as the

and equals (
[

]

[R^{n}:\Lambda^{\prime}]_{R}=(\det(B)).

Consider the map

\varphi:R^{n}\to R^{k},\quad w\mapsto A_{0}^{*}w=(a_{1}^{*}w,a_{2}^{*}w,\dots,a_{k}^{*}w)^{T}.

Its kernel is
\Lambda
by definition and its image

is the

\varphi(e_{i})=A_{0}^{*}e_{i}
of the standard basis vectors for

. As

for

\varphi(\Lambda^{\prime})=(\lambda R)^{k}\leq\varphi(R^{n}).

Thus
\varphi
restricts to an isomorphism

. Using this and
[

]
, Theorem 5.18 we get

(12)

(\lambda^{k})=[R^{k}:(\lambda R)^{k}]_{R}=[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}[(\varphi(R^{n}):(\lambda R)^{k}]_{R}=[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}[R^{n}:\Lambda^{\prime}]_{R}.

We claim
[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}=(\overline{d_{k}(A_{0})})
. Indeed if
b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{k}\in R^{k}
are free generators of
\varphi(R^{n})
then they are

-linear combinations of the images
\varphi(e_{i})=A_{0}^{*}e_{i}
of the standard basis vectors

. Using
[

]
, Theorem 5.22 again we get
[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}=\det(b^{\prime}_{1}|\dots|b^{\prime}_{k})
and by the multilinearity of the determinant it is an

-linear combination of

. Hence
(d_{k}(A_{0}^{*}))=(\overline{d_{k}(A_{0})})
divides

[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}

On the other hand any column

of

can be expressed as an

-linear combination of the

-s, so the determinant of any

minor is divisible by
\det(b^{\prime}_{1}|b^{\prime}_{2}|\dots|b^{\prime}_{k})
, thus

[R^{k}:\varphi(R^{n})]_{R}=(\det(b^{\prime}_{1}|b^{\prime}_{2}|\dots|b^{\prime}_{k}))
also divides
(d_{k}(A_{0}^{*}))=(\overline{d_{k}(A_{0})})
and our claim follows.

Plugging this into (

[R^{n}:\Lambda^{\prime}]_{R}=(\lambda^{k}/\overline{d_{k}(A_{0})})
whence

\det(B)=\omega\lambda^{k}/\overline{d_{k}(A_{0})}

for some unit

Lemma 4.7
