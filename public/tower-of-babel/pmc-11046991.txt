Opportunistic optimisation of inhaler technique in hospitalised adults with asthma: a two-phase quality improvement project
Authors: Mrinalini Dey, Koushan Kouranloo, Joseph Hanna, Ananya Singh, Alice Rafferty, Stephen Scott
Published: 2023

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic respiratory condition, responsible for approximately 1 in 250 deaths worldwide. Inhaled medications remain the mainstay of chronic stage treatment. However, disease control is rarely optimised in practice due to poor inhaler technique. We undertook a quality improvement project to opportunistically optimise inhaler technique in adults with asthma. We aimed to investigate effectiveness of two different educational methods to improve inhaler technique in patients diagnosed with asthma, hospitalised with a non-asthma-related diagnosis.

Materials and methods

We undertook a real-world, opportunistic quality improvement project. Inhaler technique in hospitalised patients, previously diagnosed with asthma, was assessed in two cohorts over two 12-week cycles using a standardised device-specific proforma of seven-step inhaler technique, classed: 'good’ if six of seven steps were achieved; 'fair’ if five of seven steps were compliant; and 'poor’ for others. Baseline demographics and comorbidity data were collected in both cycles. Cycle 1 involved face-to-face education by a healthcare professional; cycle 2 involved additional use of an electronic device to show device-specific videos (asthma.org.uk). In both cycles, patients were reassessed within 2 days for improvements and the two methods were compared for effectiveness (Fig 1). Results

During cycle 1, 32/40 patients were reassessed within 48 hours; eight were lost to follow-up. During cycle 2, 38/40 patients were reassessed within 48 hours; two were lost to follow-up.

During cycle 1, two and 12 patients had good/fair baseline inhaler technique respectively, and 26 had poor technique. Most commonly missed steps were no expiry check / not rinsing mouth after steroid use. On reassessment, 17% of patients improved from poor to fair/good.

During cycle 2, initial assessment identified: 23 poor; 12 fair; five good. Post-videos, 35% of patients improved from poor to fair/good. The proportion of patients improving from poor to fair, or poor/fair to good increased in cycle 2 vs 1 (52.5% vs 33%).

With regards to patient comments, there was a demonstrable preference for videos in cycle 2. Patients described them as 'helpful,’'easy to understand,’'informative’. Patients reported that they were able to learn specific steps of inhaler technique that they had previously missed, eg 'I sometimes wonder if the medicine is going in, but I will remember to tilt my head back and hold my breath after taking my inhaler.’ Most patients agreed that an acute admission is a good opportunity to check inhaler technique, due to being quick and feasible if the patient is not too unwell and has capacity.

Conclusion

We have shown, in an opportunistically recruited cohort of patients admitted to hospital with non-asthma-related conditions, that there are deficiencies in inhaler technique among patients with asthma. We have demonstrated that visual instruction (with the use of specialised videos) is associated with improved inhaler technique compared with verbal instruction alone. Consideration of comorbidities and involvement of the multidisciplinary team, including carers and family members, is vital in ensuring optimised use of inhalers and subsequent good asthma control.

Opportunistic assessment and use of online videos are associated with optimised inhaler technique in patients with asthma, and this is a user-friendly and cost-effective approach to patient education.
