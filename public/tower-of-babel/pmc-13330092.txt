Association of pericardial fat volume with risk of incident type 2 diabetes mellitus or major adverse cardiovascular events: Evidence from UK Biobank cohort study
Authors: Feng Hu, Jing Wang, Xinjie Zeng, Hui Chen, Chaoyang Lin, Fayuan Fu, Lin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study seeks to investigate the relationship between pericardial fat volume and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsThe analysis included a cohort of 39, 125 participants from the UK Biobank. The associations between the mean estimate pericardial fat area (MEPFA), as measured by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and the incidence of T2DM and MACE were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models and Kaplan–Meier survival curves.ResultsThere were 343 occurrences of new-onset T2DM and 1, 894 occurrences of new-onset MACE over a median follow-up period of 55 months. Compared with patients with MEPFA ≤ 13.80 cm2, there was a significantly higher risk of new-onset T2DM (adjusted-HR: 2.09, 95% CI 1.38 to 3.18, P < 0.001) and MACE (adjusted-HR: 1.19, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.39, P = 0.027) in the highest MEPFA quartiles. The survival analysis further substantiated this discrepancy, with a log-rank test yielding P < 0.001. Participants exhibiting higher levels of MEPFA demonstrated poorer left ventricular morphology, systolic function, and global strain.ConclusionsThe findings indicated that elevated MEPFA levels were significantly and independently associated with the onset of T2DM and MACE. Preliminary results suggested that increased levels of pericardial fat might enhance the predictive capability for cardio-metabolic risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01127-y.

Introduction

Cardiac adipose tissue is comprised of both epicardial and pericardial adipose tissue. Both epicardial and pericardial fat volume (EFV and PFV) have been linked to previous cardiovascular incidents, subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, the severity of coronary artery disease (CAD), and the probability of future major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [1–6]. In a prospective study involving 200 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and elevated urinary albumin excretion rates, the median total cardiac fat volume, rather than EFV or PFV alone, was independently associated with an increased risk of incident cardiovascular disease or all-cause mortality [3]. A correlation was observed between elevated levels of cardiac adipose tissue, EFV, and both coronary artery calcium and interleukin-8 [3]. In a prospective cohort study of 1, 030 patients with T2DM, EFV, as opposed to PFV measured by echocardiography, was associated with MACE and all-cause mortality after a 4.7-year follow-up period, particularly among men [4]. EFV was found to independently predict long-term MACE in T2DM patients without prior coronary events [5].

In a case-control study involving 2, 751 asymptomatic patients who subsequently experienced MACE over a four-year follow-up period, PFV assessed via non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scans was found to be significantly associated with the risk of MACE [7]. Individuals with pre-diabetes and diabetes had a notably greater amount of EFV or PFV compared to those without these conditions [8]. The accumulation of visceral fat adjacent to the thoracic aorta appeared to exert a substantial impact on insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, suggesting its potential utility as supplementary information for cardiovascular risk stratification in the early stages of glucose dysfunction [8].

Although several retrospective studies have explored the association between EFV and T2DM [9, 10] or MACE [3–6], the relationship between PFV and the occurrence of future MACE remained a subject of debate [3, 4, 7]. This study sought to investigate the association between PFV and the risk of future incident T2DM and MACE from the UK Biobank cohort.

Methods

Data sources

The UK Biobank dataset comprised over 500,000 participants aged over 40 years, recruited between 2006 and 2010 [11, 12]. Ethical approval for the UK Biobank was granted by the National Research Ethics Service North West (Approval No.: 21/NW/0157), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The study initially included a total of 502, 131 participants. From this cohort, we excluded individuals based on the following criteria: those with missing follow-up information (n = 1, 297), pregnancy (n = 2), lacking data on mean estimate pericardial fat area (MEPFA, n = 455, 424) measured by cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) examination, and a history of CAD (n = 2, 785) or stroke (n = 642) or atrial fibrillation (n = 1, 027) or heart failure (n = 48) or T2DM (n = 1, 781). Consequently, the final analysis comprised 39, 125 participants without type 1 diabetes mellitus (Fig. 1).

Data collection

This study incorporated four principal categories of covariates: (1) Demographic characteristics, which included variables such as age (Field-ID 21003), sex (Field-ID 31), ethnicity (Field-ID 21000), household income (Field-ID 22189), educational attainment (Field-ID 6138), smoking (Field-ID 20116) and alcohol (Field-ID 20117) consumption status (2). Anthropometric indices, which encompassed systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse rate (Field-ID 102), waist circumference (Field-ID 48), hip circumference (Field-ID 49), pulse wave velocity (Field-ID 21021) and body mass index (BMI, Field-ID 21001). SBP and DBP (Field ID 4079, 4080) were measured twice at the assessment center using an automated blood pressure device (Omron 705 IT electronic blood pressure monitor), or manually (Field ID 9394) using a sphygmomanometer if the blood pressure device failed to measure the blood pressure or if the largest inflatable cuff of the device did not fit around the participant’s arm. The mean values of multiple automated or manual measurements were used. For mean arterial pressure (MAP) we used the following algorithm: (SBP + 2×DBP)/3 (3). Laboratory parameters, including serum albumin (Field ID 30600), total cholesterol (Field ID 30693), total triglycerides (Field ID 23407), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, Field ID 23526), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, Field ID 23526), glycosylated hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C, Field ID 30750), serum creatinine (Field ID 30700), fasting blood glucose (FBG, Field ID 30740), C-reactive protein (CRP, Field ID 30710), white blood cell count (Field ID 30000) and hemoglobin (Field ID 30020) (4). Chronic diseases and medication usage. Hypertension was defined as a diagnosis by a physician, blood pressure readings of 140/90 mmHg or higher, or the ongoing use of antihypertensive medications [13]. Dyslipidemia was characterized by total cholesterol levels of 200 mg/dL or higher, triglyceride levels of 150 mg/dL or more, LDL-C levels of 130 mg/dL or higher, or the use of lipid-lowering medications. T2DM was identified based on FBG levels of at least 126 mg/dL, HbA1C levels of 6.5% or higher, self-reported diabetes, or the use of hypoglycemic agents [12].

CMR protocol and pericardial fat quantification

A clinical 1.5 Tesla Magnetom Aera tomograph, manufactured by Siemens Healthcare in Erlangen, Germany, was utilized for conducting CMR imaging. The UK Biobank CMR protocol has been comprehensively detailed in a specific publication [14]. Cardiac function evaluation was achieved by integrating multiple cine series, including long-axis cines, vertical long-axis, left ventricular outflow tract, and a complete short-axis stack encompassing both the left and right ventricles. The CMR sequences were assessed using the cvi42 software suite from Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., located in Calgary, Canada [14].

An automated neural network, equipped with a built-in quality-controlled algorithm, was employed to segment the area of pericardial fat using the end-diastole phase of a CMR four-chamber balanced steady-state free precession (BTFE) cine sequence in a long-axis heart image [15]. In this analysis, PFV, as defined by the measurement of MEPFA (Field ID 31085), was characterized as the adipose tissue located within the visceral pericardium, including fat that directly surrounds the coronary arteries.

Patient follow-up and clinical outcomes

The mean follow-up period was 1650.0 days. The primary endpoint of the study was the long-term incidence of T2DM or MACE. MACE was defined CAD (angor pectoris or myocardial infarction, I20-I25), stroke (I60-I64), atrial fibrillation (I48), heart failure (I50), or sudden cardiac arrest (I46), or cardiovascular death. Cardiovascular death was defined according to the ICD-10 codes (I00– I99, excluding infection codes: I32.0, I32.1, I33.0, I33.9, I38, I39, I40.0, I41.0–I41.2, I43.0, I52.0–I52.1, I68.1, I98.1) [16]. Mortality data were obtained through linkage with the UK National Health Service Central Registry. The follow-up period began on the date of the first imaging visit (from April 30th, 2014 to March 13th, 2020) and concluded either at the time of the participant’s death or at the end of the follow-up period (December 19, 2022).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, while continuous variables with normal distribution were summarized by means and standard deviations. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were represented by medians and interquartile ranges, analyzed across four categories of MEPFA. The distribution of characteristics across these categories was assessed using the Chi-square test, ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis test.

To minimize reverse causality in this study, individuals with pre-existing conditions such as CAD, stroke, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, or diabetes were excluded from the analyses. Furthermore, the relationships between the MEPFA and the incidence of T2DM and MACE were assessed using three multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Model I was adjusted for age, sex, and ethnic group, while Model II, the final confounder model, included adjustments for age, sex, ethnicity, education level, BMI group, MBP, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Kaplan-Meier curves were employed to evaluate the impact of various categories on survival free from new-onset T2DM or MACE events. In addition, generalized linear regression models and restricted cubic splines were employed to evaluate the association between MEPFA and the risk of incident T2DM and MACE. The robustness of these associations was further assessed through subgroup analyses using stratified regression and interaction tests. Statistical analysis were conducted using R (http://www.rproject.org, version 4.2.2) and Empower 2.0 (R) (http://www.empowerstats. com) software, with a two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics at baseline

Table 1 showed the baseline characteristics of the enrolled subjects stratified by four categories of MEPFA. A general trend was observed across the four categories of MEPFA, with patients in the high MEPFA group more likely to be male and exhibit a higher prevalence of current or former smoking, heavy alcohol consumption, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, and use of medications for chronic conditions compared to those in the low MEPFA group (all P values ≤ 0.001). Additionally, individuals in the high MEPFA group demonstrated elevated levels of age, SBP, DBP, MBP, pulse rate, waist or hip circumference, BMI, pulse wave velocity, CRP, creatinine, total triglyceride, HbA1C, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI). Conversely, the high MEPFA group was associated with lower levels of HDL-C and educational attainment compared to those in the lower-scoring group (all P values < 0.001). The characteristics of participants grouped by aging or sex were also assessed in Supplemental Table 1. Additionally, a partial correlation analysis was performed between MEPFA and various continuous variables in Supplemental Table 2. MEPFA showed a strong association with obesity indices (waistline or BMI) and visceral adipose tissue measured by abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Association of MEPFA with new-onset T2DM or MACE

The Cox proportional hazards models demonstrated that, in the final confounder-adjusted model, the MEPFA index, when treated as a continuous variable, showed a significantly positive correlation with the incidence of both new-onset T2DM and MACE (adjusted-HR per SD increase: 1.27, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.15 to 1.40, P < 0.001; adjusted-HR per SD increase: 1.06, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.12, P = 0.012, respectively; Table 3). When analyzed as a categorical variable, patients in the highest quartiles of MEPFA, compared to those with MEPFA ≤ 13.80 cm², exhibited a significantly elevated risk of developing new-onset T2DM (adjusted-HR: 2.09, 95% CI 1.38 to 3.18, P < 0.001; P for trend < 0.001) and experiencing MACE (adjusted-HR: 1.19, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.39, P = 0.027; P for trend = 0.034) (Table 3).

Characteristics of CMR and abdominal MRI

The unadjusted cardiac characteristics are detailed in Table 4, highlighting differences in left ventricular morphology, function, and strain measures, as well as right ventricular and left atrial morphology measures. Participants in the higher MEPFA quartiles exhibited significantly increased levels of left ventricular mass index or mass-to-volume ratio, right ventricular end-systolic or stroke volume, and left atrial biplanar volume compared to those in the lower quartiles (all P values < 0.001). In contrast, the higher MEPFA group was associated with significantly lower levels of left ventricular global function index, stroke volume index, global circumferential or radial strain, and cardiac output index (all P values < 0.001). Additionally, participants in the higher MEPFA quartiles showed increased levels of abdominal visceral adipose tissue as measured by MRI (all P values < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses by potential effect modifiers

Considering the potential impact of significant covariates, we conducted stratified and interaction analyses of the MEPFA indices across various subgroups, including age (< 65 or ≥ 65 years), sex, MBP dichotomy, BMI categories, educational attainment, smoking and drinking status, presence of metabolic syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cancer. However, no significant interactions were observed between MEPFA levels and the incidence of T2DM or MACE within any of these subgroups (refer to Supplemental Fig. 2 and Supplemental Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the impact of the MEPFA indices on the incidence of T2DM and MACE from the UK Biobank cohort. Our results demonstrated that individuals with higher MEPFA levels were at an increased risk for both new-onset T2DM and MACE, even after controlling for socio-demographic and metabolic factors. Participants with greater MEPFA level demonstrated the worse left ventricular morphology, systolic function and global strain. To our knowledge, this study was the first to illustrate the added predictive value of PFV, as measured by CMR, for both new-onset T2DM and MACE in a large general population.

Our research provided significant insights into the existing evidence linking cardiac adipose tissue with cardiovascular risk markers. In patients with T2DM, EFV showed limited utility as an independent marker for coronary artery plaque and was only weakly associated with adipokine expression [4]. However, EFV was identified as an independent predictor of MACE in patients with T2DM [4, 5] or in individuals with a low Framingham risk score [6]. Furthermore, children with a family history of T2DM demonstrated significantly reduced flow-mediated dilation and cardiac diastolic dysfunction, which were associated with increased epicardial fat thickness [17]. Epicardial adipose tissue attenuation observed through CT scan could serve as a predictor for MACE in individuals with aortic stenosis following transcatheter aortic valve implantation [18]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis, encompassing 83 observational studies with a total of 73, 934 patients, revealed a significant association between PFV and cardiovascular diseases. The findings indicated that PFV was significantly correlated with CAD, ventricular dysfunction, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, MACE, and coronary artery calcifications [19].

Research suggested that adipose tissue adjacent to the right coronary artery might promote atherogenesis through the initiation of inflammatory signals [20]. The association between increased PFV and ischemia was potentially linked to the paracrine effects of pericardial and pericoronary artery fat [20]. Notably, the volume of local pericoronary fat exhibited a strong and independent correlation with the extent of atherosclerotic plaque [21]. Among 333 without a prior history of CAD, a significant relationship was observed between elevated EFV and coronary artery calcium scores, as well as their progression, indicating a paracrine influence of this fat depot on the coronary arteries [22]. In our research, participants with greater MEPFA quartiles demonstrated elevated levels of abdominal composition of visceral adipose tissue from MRI. MEPFA showed a strong correlation with obesity indices and abdominal visceral adipose tissue.

The independent prognostic value of PFV for T2DM and MACE might provide a clinically significant complement to previous findings of heightened inflammation in pericardial fat deposits. Asymptomatic patients who experienced MACE demonstrated higher PFV compared to matched controls without such events [7]. Unlike intra-thoracic fat, PFV was associated with the burden of prior cardiovascular disease, even after accounting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors [2]. In a cohort of 1, 777 consecutive patients without a prior diagnosis of coronary artery disease, elevated PFV observed on non-contrast CT was significantly associated with myocardial ischemia, as assessed by single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) [23].

Our results, as presented in Supplemental Table 2, demonstrated a significant association between MEPFA and both obesity indices and visceral adipose tissue. This finding suggested the need to further explore its relationship with obesity and its potential additive effect to previously established risk factors, in order to assess the feasibility of integrating it into cardiovascular risk scores [19]. Additionally, our study identified that pericardial fat accumulation was significantly and independently associated with the onset of T2DM and MACE. Quantifying pericardial fat levels might enhance the predictive accuracy for adverse health outcomes.

EFV has been suggested as a valuable marker for identifying increased cardio-metabolic risk. Among patients with CAD undergoing high-intensity statin therapy, increased epicardial adipose tissue thickness consistently emerged as an independent predictor of new-onset T2DM [9]. Furthermore, according to the Finnish Diabetes Risk Score, epicardial adipose tissue thickness was independently associated with a heightened risk of T2DM [10]. Our findings also demonstrated that increased PFV was the most significant risk factor for T2DM and could play a critical role in risk assessment and monitoring.

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of this study was its extended median follow-up period and considerable sample size, which facilitated an in-depth examination of the impact of PFV on the incidence of new-onset T2DM and MACE. The robustness of these associations was further supported by the results of sensitivity analyses conducted alongside multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. However, the study was not without limitations. Firstly, the participants of the UK Biobank exhibited lower levels of socioeconomic deprivation and possessed fewer lifestyle risk factors relative to the general UK population. Therefore, they demonstrated a reduced prevalence of long-term chronic diseases. Consequently, it was necessary to ascertain whether our findings could be generalized to other ethnic groups or geographical regions. Additionally, the study did not track the dynamics of individual MEPFA indices, precluding the possibility of conducting a time-varying analysis for the cohort. Furthermore, the evaluation of EFV was absent from the database, which could have provided more comprehensive insights. Finally, it was inherently unable to establish causality as an observational study. Larger prospective studies were necessary to determine whether a linear relationship existed between PFV and cardiometabolic risk and mortality.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrated that elevated MEPFA levels, as assessed by CMR, were significantly and independently associated with the onset of T2DM and MACE from the UK Biobank cohort. Preliminary results suggested that increased levels of pericardial fat might enhance the predictive capability for adverse cardiovascular events. Comprehensive longitudinal studies should be conducted to verify and enhance the findings of this research.
