The Influence of Media Type and Posting Time on Altmetric Engagement of Orthopaedic Surgery Journals on X
Authors: Firas Moussa, Thomas Leonard, Mitchell J. Christiansen, Mary K. Mulcahey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Digital promotion of orthopaedic research is increasingly associated with scholarly visibility yet the influence of specific social media posting characteristics on online attention remains unclear. The Altmetric Attention Score (AAS) is a weighted score that measures the amount of attention a study has received. The score is based on three factors: the volume of mentions, the medium the mention came from, and the author of the mention. In orthopaedic research, a higher AAS is correlated with higher citation rates. The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between AAS and the presence of various types of multimedia in posts on X by orthopaedic surgery journals and the time posts were made.Methods:Data were collected from the X accounts of the British Journal of Sports Medicine (BJSM), Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery (JBJS), Arthroscopy, Journal of Arthroplasty, American Orthopedic Society for Sports Medicine Journals (AOSSM), and Journal of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (JAAOS) over two separate periods, January 1, 2023, to June 31, 2023, and July 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024. Media types were classified into the following categories: no media, text only, simple media, infographics, and videos. To analyze the effect of post time on AAS score, posts were grouped into three different time groups: 10 pm to 5:59 am, 6 am to 1:59 pm, and 2 pm to 9:59 pm. Statistical analysis for data sets with three or more groups was done with an Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with post hoc t-tests performed when statistical significance was found. For data sets with only two groups, t-tests were done.Results:One-way ANOVA results showed a significant difference in the average AAS between media types for the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery (JBJS) in the 2024 period (P < 0.01) and in Arthroscopy for both the 2023 and 2024 periods (P < 0.0001 and P < 0.05, respectively). Post hoc t-tests showed a significant difference in average AAS between simple media and infographics for JBJS in 2024 with average AAS scores of 2.81 and 11, respectively (P < 0.001). In 2023, Arthroscopy showed significant differences in the average AAS of simple media vs infographics, simple media vs text only, and infographics vs no media (P < 0.0001, 0.0001, and 0.05, respectively). In 2024, Arthroscopy showed significant differences in average AAS for simple media vs infographics, videos vs infographics, and videos vs text only with P < 0.05, 0.005, and 0.05, respectively. For the American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine (AOSSM) journals, t-tests showed a statistically significant difference in average AAS between posts made from 6 am to 1:59 pm and posts made from 2 pm to 9:59 pm with a P < 0.05 and average AAS of 12.9 and 74.5, respectively.Conclusion:Although results were not consistent across all journals, one trend was the increased AAS of infographics. This repeated success suggests a promising avenue for orthopaedic journals to improve the reach of their studies by increasing the utilization of infographics. Time posted did not seem to have a notable effect on AAS, but for the AOSSM Sports Medicine journals, the time frame of 2 pm to 9:59 pm may be an optimal time to maximize reach. Future studies with larger data sets and the development of an objective media classification system may provide an avenue toward strengthening the findings of this study and developing more precise strategies for orthopaedic journals to optimize their social media presence.

The use of social media has increased substantially over the past several years, with more than five billion users worldwide in 2024, up from 3.5 billion in 2019 and projected to rise to more than six billion by 2028.1 In addition, more than half of US adults report using social media as a source of news.2 This shift in news consumption extends beyond the general public. A study from Gross et al.3 showed that orthopaedic surgeons aged 52 and younger are almost twice as likely as their older colleagues to use social media to stay up to date with new publications.

This growing association between social media and the dissemination of research is reflected in the relationship between sharing studies on social media and citation count. In 2023, Halvorsen et al. demonstrated that studies that were mentioned in 10, 100, and 1000 tweets showed 1.1-, 1.7-, and 245-fold increases in citation count, respectively.4 In addition, a 2020 study from Zhang et al. demonstrated that there is a positive correlation between the average number of tweets and retweets per article and the number of academic citations in orthopaedic surgery articles.5 A separate study from Sudah et al.6 found that articles published in shoulder and elbow surgery journals had an increased citation count within 3 years if they were tweeted versus those that were not tweeted. Together, these studies suggest that journals can increase the reach of research by sharing articles on social media.

On a broader scale, the impact factor of orthopaedic journals has been shown to be correlated with the number of social media followers a journal has, specifically on Facebook and X.7 In addition, journal impact factor has been shown to be strongly correlated with a higher average number of monthly tweets.8 Tweeted articles received 8.51 citations on average compared with 2.56 citations on average for nontweeted articles.

One interesting metric that can be used to measure an article or journal's social media reach is the Altmetric Attention Score (AAS). The AAS is a weighted score that measures the amount of attention a research article has received.9 The score is based on three factors: the number of mentions, the medium (newspaper article, blog post, social media, etc.) the mention came from, and the author of the mention.9 In orthopaedic research, a higher AAS has been shown to correlate with higher citation rates.10,11 Examining orthopaedic journals with the highest average AAS may help identify effective social media strategies that enhance research visibility and impact.

The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between AAS and the presence of various types of multimedia in posts on X by orthopaedic surgery journals and the time posts were made. We hypothesize that posts with more visually engaging media types such as infographics or videos will have higher AAS than posts with simple and/or no media.

Methods

Orthopaedic journal social media accounts analyzed were selected based on the presence of the following factors: follower count of at least 10,000, the account creates original posts (i.e., not including reposts) of studies greater than 10 times per month and the account creates posts containing more than 1 distinct kind of media. This led to the inclusion of the following accounts: @AOSSMjournals, @OfficialJAAOS, @jbjs, @ArthroscopyJ, @BJSM_BMJ, and @JArthroplasty. The X account @AOSSMjournals includes journal articles from the AOSSM family of journals including The American Journal of Sports Medicine (AJSM), Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine (OJSM), and Sports Health and Video Journal of Sports Medicine (VJSM). Only original posts that included links to journal articles were included as part of the analysis. Data were collected from these accounts manually and included the following information: date and time of the post, the type of media associated, links to the post, and links to the articles. Media types were classified into the following categories: no media, text only, simple media, infographics, and videos. No media was defined as a post with only a link to the article, while text only was defined as a post that had media attached that only contained text. Simple media was defined to include imaging (i.e., radiograph and MRI), photos, graphs, and charts. Infographics were defined as media that displayed conclusions or results from the study in a visual format. An AAS score for each article was obtained using the Altmetric Bookmarklet tool. Times were recorded because they were displayed on X without normalization for time zones. Data collection was done for posts from January 1, 2023, to June 31, 2023, and from July 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024. These two 6-month intervals were selected to capture unique posting periods. The earlier time interval was chosen so that AAS had a notable amount of time to accumulate, while the later time interval was used to evaluate for consistency across multiple periods.

Statistical analysis was done using Microsoft Excel. Primarily, a single-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to look for notable differences in AAS score between types of media. Not all included journals had each type of media, so analyses were conducted within each journal rather than across journals. Significance was defined as P < 0.05. If significance was met in the ANOVA, post hoc t-tests were done between each media type to see which specific media types had notable differences in AAS score. This process was done twice for each account, once for the 2023 data and once for the 2024 data to account for differences in AAS score due to accumulation over time. To analyze the effect of post time on AAS score, posts were grouped into three different time groups: 10 pm to 5:59 am, 6 am to 1:59 pm, and 2 pm to 9:59 pm. These periods were chosen to reflect three different periods of social media use, overnight, early morning-afternoon, and afternoon-evening. The specific times were chosen to evenly split up the hours of the day into 8-hour blocks. ANOVA analysis was again done to look for significant differences between AAS with significance again defined as P < 0.05. The proportions of each media type were determined for each 6-month period to see whether there was a difference in the types of media posts included over the 2-year period.

Results

One-way ANOVA results demonstrated a significantly different average AAS between media types for JBJS in the 2024 period (P < 0.01), and post hoc t-tests revealed this difference to be between simple media and infographics with average AAS of 2.81 and 11, respectively (P < 0.005). For Arthroscopy in both the 2023 and 2024 periods, ANOVA testing showed significant differences in average AAS between media types with P-values of <0.0005 and <0.01, respectively. For Arthroscopy in 2023, post hoc t-tests showed that infographics outperformed simple media, text only outperformed simple media, and infographics outperformed no media with P < 0.0001, <0.0001, and <0.05, respectively. For Arthroscopy in 2024, post hoc t-tests showed infographics > simple media, infographics > video, and text only > video with P < 0.05, <0.01, and <0.05, respectively. One-way ANOVA of all other periods of all other journals had P > 0.05, so no post hoc testing was done. In statistical testing about time posted, only AOSSM in 2023 showed a statistically significant difference in average AAS. As the data set only included posts from 6 am to 1:59 pm and 2 pm to 9:59 pm, only a t-test was done which demonstrated a P-value of <0.05 with average AAS of 12.9 and 74.5, respectively. All other statistical tests about time posted showed no notable difference. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between Altmetric Attention Score and the presence of various types of multimedia in posts on X by orthopaedic surgery journals and the time posts were made. Arthroscopy's X posts in the 2023 data set demonstrated that simple media underperformed both infographics and text only posts. In the 2024 data set, infographics outperformed simple media with text only posts newly outperforming video posts. These relationships suggest a potential opportunity to additional viewer engagement through favoring informative promotional pieces. JBJS's X posts in the 2024 data set similarly demonstrating a favorability for infographics over simple media echoes the informational advantage. The AOSSM journal's X posts from the 2023 data set garnered more engagement if posted from 2 pm to 9:59 pm vs 6 am to 1:59 pm. In 2024, the same trend was seen without statistical significance possibly due to low statistical power. For all other journals included, there were no notable relationships determined possibly because of the nonchronologic feed on X. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the time a post is published is less likely to affect how often users view it.

Although the results of this study varied across the different journals analyzed, the findings were in agreement with previously published studies. A 2018 randomized controlled trial from Huang et al.12 demonstrated that when articles from an emergency medicine journal were promoted with an infographic versus a screen capture of the abstract, there were notable increases in Altmetric score and abstract views. This mirrors the findings observed here for Arthroscopy and JBJS and provides more evidence for the utility of infographics as a way to increase the dissemination of medical research. In 2021, Kunze et al.13 compared the AAS of infographics that were included in electronic versions of Arthroscopy to topic matched research articles. Although these infographics and articles were not necessarily posted to social media, the authors found that infographics had a nearly fivefold increase in average AAS and markedly increased X and Facebook mentions. This once again agrees with the trends seen in this study of infographics generating higher average AAS.

Although infographics outperformed other types of media for some journals in this study, in other journals, there were no notable differences identified. Results vary dramatically across multiple studies with Bennett and Slattery finding decreased AAS for sports science infographics, Aggarwal finding no relationship for visual abstracts in general medical journals such as the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA), the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM), and the British Medical Journal, and Kim et al. finding a positive association for graphical abstracts in gastroenterology journals.14-16 These studies were done in journals from different medical specialties, with varying methodologies and any number of factors could have led to the differences in results. This lack of consensus highlights the need for more granular analysis that can provide strong evidence and specific recommendations to help guide resource efficient and widespread dissemination of research.

In 2023, Bonnevie et al.17 compared studies published in the NEJM that were promoted through a video abstract versus ones that were not promoted at all and found notable increases in views and AAS for studies that were promoted with video abstracts. This is an interesting difference from our study where videos did not show an increased AAS when compared with other media types in any journal and even demonstrated a markedly lower AAS compared with text only for Arthroscopy for 2024. This may have been due to multiple reasons. In this study, videos were not stratified into the type of video, so the relative strength of video abstracts may have been diluted because of other types of videos such as procedure videos. Future studies should compare online attention between different types of videos as a promotional material.

There are several limitations to this study. First was the inability to create an entirely objective system of classifying the media type. Although posts within the category of text only and no media were straightforward to classify, posts within the infographic category may have been more at risk of classification bias. Another limitation was the impact of confounding variables on AAS. The average AAS of any type of media may have been affected by any number of unrelated factors such as posts from other X accounts or on other article sharing mediums, the strength, topic, or clinical importance of the study or the prominence of the author. In addition, only articles posted on X were analyzed; therefore, articles that were not shared on X that may have achieved notable scholarly attention through other pathways were not included. In addition, by limiting the timeframe of posts to include only the first 6 months of 2023 and the last 6 months of 2024, we may have excluded posts that could have helped to show significance in more of the journals. Another limitation specific to the 2024 data is the shorter amount of time to accumulate a representative AAS score. Although posts made in early 2023 had almost 2 years to reach a relatively stable AAS score, posts made in December 2024 had only a few weeks to gain attention and may end up having higher AAS scores than were observed. This was accounted for in the decision to analyze 2023 vs 2024 data separately but may have led to statistically significant differences in the 2024 data that were not. Finally, posting times were not normalized to a single time zone. The journals examined may have readers across a wide variety of geographical areas, and therefore, differences in time zone may have diminished any true association between posting time and AAS.

Conclusion

Although results were not consistent across all journals, one trend was the increased AAS of infographics. This repeated success suggests a promising avenue for orthopaedic journals to improve the reach of their studies by increasing their utilization of infographics. Time posted did not seem to have a notable effect on AAS, but for the AOSSM Sports Medicine journals, the time frame of 2 pm to 9:59 pm may be an optimal time to maximize reach. Future studies with larger data sets and the development of an objective media classification system may provide an avenue toward strengthening the findings of this study and developing more precise strategies for orthopaedic journals to optimize their social media presence.
