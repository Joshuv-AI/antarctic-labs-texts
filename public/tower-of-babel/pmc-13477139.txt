Paying the toll: Transfusion and outcomes of patients bridged with mechanical circulatory support to heart transplantation
Authors: Swaroop Bommareddi, Chen Chia Wang, Walter Navid, Esha Chawla, John Trahanas, Brian Lima, Awab Ahmad, Kevin C. McGann, Mark Petrovic, Stephen Devries, Joshua Lowman, Tarek Absi, Eric Quintana, Kelly Schlendorf, Matthew Bacchetta, Ashish S. Shah, Aaron M. Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveVarious mechanical circulatory support (MCS) strategies exist to bridge patients with heart failure to heart transplant. This study provides a comprehensive analysis comparing perioperative transfusion requirements and posttransplant outcomes between recipients with various pretransplant MCS types.MethodsAll adult heart transplants from January 2020 to June 2025 from a single-center, minus multiorgan transplants or adult congenital heart disease cases, were included. Patients were stratified as: no MCS, pretransplant intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), Impella, durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD), and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).ResultsA total of 600 adult heart transplants were included, of which 263 (43.8%) had no MCS, 91 (15.2%) an IABP, 47 (7.8%) an Impella, 179 (29.8%) a durable LVAD, and 20 (3.3%) had ECMO as pretransplant support. Intraoperative transfusion requirements were greater in the Impella, LVAD, and ECMO cohorts compared with the no-MCS cohort. Postoperatively, both LVAD and ECMO groups had greater transfusions relative to those without MCS. Transfusion requirements were similar between the LVAD and ECMO cohorts. The IABP, Impella, and LVAD groups had comparable posttransplant outcomes relative to the no-MCS group. The ECMO cohort had greater 30-day and 1-year mortality rates compared with those without pretransplant MCS.ConclusionsPerioperative transfusion requirements were comparable between those with no pretransplant MCS and those with IABP or Impella and was greater in those with pretransplant LVAD or ECMO. Survival in patients with IABP, Impella, and LVAD was similar to those with no MCS and worse in patients with ECMO compared with no MCS.

Different bridging strategies to heart transplant have different perioperative outcomes. Central MessagePatients bridged to heart transplant with durable LVADs or ECMO have greater perioperative transfusion requirements compared with those bridged with IABP or Impella or those with no support. PerspectivePretransplant support with IABP and Impella yield comparable perioperative transfusion needs and posttransplant outcomes compared with those without pretransplant support. Pretransplant durable LVAD and ECMO were associated with greater perioperative transfusion requirements. These insights may help guide clinical decision-making during the care of patients with advanced heart failure.

Heart transplantation remains the gold standard treatment for end-stage heart failure refractory to medical management. Although the donor pool has increased in recent years, the increase in demand continues to outpace the donor supply.1 Given these challenges, various mechanical circulatory support (MCS) bridging strategies exist to support recipients until a donor heart becomes available. However, insight on how various pretransplant MCS strategies affect the perioperative and posttransplant course is crucial to ensure the approach is selected. Specifically, perioperative transfusion requirements are an important outcome that may be directly influenced by pretransplant MCS technique.

Previous studies have demonstrated patients with durable left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) undergoing heart transplantation are associated with greater perioperative transfusion requirements compared with those receiving non-LVAD-explant heart transplants.2 It has also been shown that temporary support with a microaxial left ventricular assist device, or Impella 5.5 (Abiomed), was associated with lower perioperative transfusion requirements compared with those bridged with a durable LVAD.3,4 Other studies have examined posttransplant survival with other forms of MCS bridging, such as intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), but perioperative transfusions and other outcomes in these groups remain lacking.5,6

This study addresses this gap by performing a comprehensive comparison of perioperative transfusion requirements and posttransplant outcomes among recipients of heart transplants with no pretransplant MCS, and those with pretransplant MCS including IABP, Impella, durable LVAD, or ECMO. We hypothesized that bridging with a durable LVAD or ECMO would have the greatest transfusion requirements and worse clinical outcomes compared with the other groups.

Methods

Study Population and Data Definitions

All adult heart transplants at a single institution from January 2020 to June 2025 were included. Patients receiving multiorgan transplants or those with congenital heart disease were excluded. Patients were stratified into cohorts on the basis of the presence or lack of pretransplant MCS: no MCS, IABP, microaxial left ventricular assist device (Impella), durable LVADs, or ECMO. Those with ECMO plus either an IABP or Impella were placed in the ECMO cohort. Durable LVAD included HeartWare HVAD (Medtronic), HeartMate II, and HeartMate 3 (both Abbott). Both donation after brain death and donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors were included. All donation after brain death donors were recovered in a similar fashion, whereas DCD recovery strategies included direct procurement and perfusion, thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion, and rapid recovery with extended ultra-oxygenated preservation.7 Preservation types included static cold storage or machine perfusion. Machine perfusion includes normothermic machine perfusion (Transmedics Inc) and hypothermic machine perfusion (XVIVO Perfusion Inc), and modes of static storage included storage on ice, storage at 10 °C (Traferox Technologies Inc), storage at 4 °C to 8 °C (Paragonix Technologies), and storage at 10 °C using a commercially available cooler (TTI Consumer Power Tools Inc).

Outcomes of interest included perioperative transfusion requirements, which included intraoperative and postoperative, takeback for bleeding, severe primary graft dysfunction, defined by the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation consensus statement in 2014,8 other clinical outcomes reflecting early graft function and index hospitalization course, and survival up to 1-year posttransplant. Perioperative transfusion included both intraoperative and postoperative (within 48 hours) packed red blood cells (pRBCs), platelets, fresh-frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate. Postoperative vasoactive inotrope scores were calculated as dopamine (μg/kg/min) + dobutamine (μg/kg/min) + 100 ∗ epinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 100 ∗ norepinephrine (μg/kg/min) + 15 ∗ milrinone (μg/kg/min). Perioperatively, protocols for transfusion include transfusions when hemoglobin <8 g/dL, platelets <75,000/μL, and fibrinogen <200 mg/dL. Transfusions may also be necessary when there is concern of clinical bleeding.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are reported as number (percentage) and compared using the Fisher exact test. Continuous variables are reported as median (quartile 1 to quartile 3) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. For perioperative transfusion requirements, post-hoc pairwise comparisons with the Dunn test and Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons were performed. Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank test were used to compare survival between the study groups. Multivariate logistic (categorical outcomes) and linear regression models (continuous outcomes), and Cox proportional hazards models (survival) were used to evaluate the association of each pretransplant MCS group on posttransplant outcomes relative to the no MCS group while adjusting for potential confounders. For the adjusted survival analysis, the proportional hazards assumption was verified for the primary predictor of interest (MCS group) using Schoenfeld residuals, where a P > .05 indicated no violation of the proportional hazards assumption. All analysis was conducted using R 4.5.1 (R Core Team).

The institutional review board or equivalent ethics committee of the Vanderbilt University Medical Center approved the study protocol and publication of data, under protocol no. 241909, approved on December 30, 2025. Patient written consent for the publication of the study data was waived by the institutional review board because of the retrospective, chart review nature of the study.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 600 adult heart transplantations met inclusion criteria, of which 263 (43.8%) had no MCS, 91 (15.2%) had an IABP, 47 (7.8%) had an Impella 5.5, 179 (29.8%) had a durable LVAD, and 20 (3.3%) had ECMO as pretransplant support. The proportion of DCD transplants, DCD recovery strategies, and static cold storage technique used were different between the study groups (Table 1). The total allograft ischemic time was also different between the cohorts (median 217 minutes, no MCS vs 213 minutes, IABP vs 245 minutes, Impella vs 244 minutes, LVAD vs 213 minutes, ECMO, P = .002). Recipient age, body mass index, and proportion with ischemic cardiomyopathy were also different between the study groups. Unadjusted Outcomes

There were significant differences in both intraoperative and postoperative transfusion requirements across the study cohorts (Table 2). Ventilation duration was different, with the EMCO cohort having the longest ventilatory times (median 11.4 hours no MCS vs 13.3 hours IABP vs 9.3 hours Impella vs 13.7 hours LVAD vs 19.4 hours ECMO, P = .001). Postoperative cardiac indices and vasoactive inotrope score within first 24 hours posttransplant were also different, as well as the total hospital length of stay (median 17 days no MCS vs 17 days IABP vs 18 days Impella vs 18 days LVAD vs 26 days ECMO, P = .001). Discussion

This study represents the largest single-center experience with a comprehensive comparison of perioperative transfusions and postoperative clinical outcomes between all common MCS bridging strategies to heart transplant over 5 years at a high-volume single center. Our main findings were (1) intraoperatively, both durable LVAD and ECMO cohorts had greater transfusion requirements compared with the no-MCS, IABP, or Impella 5.5 groups; (2) postoperatively, both the durable LVAD and ECMO cohorts had greater transfusion requirements compared with the no-MCS group; and (3) the ECMO cohort was associated with increased mortality, whereas the IABP, Impella 5.5, and LVAD groups had similar postoperative outcomes compared with the no-MCS group. These findings provide valuable insight for all involved in the care of patients with advanced heart failure and heart transplant, especially for patient prognostication and patient counseling.

Our study reiterates findings of previous studies comparing transfusion requirements between recipients of heart transplant bridged with Impella 5.5 versus durable LVAD but also provides novel findings. Alvarez and colleagues3 found that bridging with Impella 5.5 was associated with lower postoperative transfusion needs compared with durable LVADs. However, they did not include a group with no MCS as the reference and did not capture intraoperative transfusions. Shapiro and colleagues4 found that patients bridged to transplant with an Impella 5.5 had lower intraoperative transfusions than those bridged with a durable LVAD; however, this study also lacked a no-MCS group as the reference, and conversely did not include postoperative transfusions. Our study found a similar greater total intraoperative transfusion requirements in the durable LVAD group compared with not only the Impella 5.5 group but also the IABP and no-MCS groups. We also found that those bridged to ECMO also had greater transfusions than those bridged with Impella 5.5, IABP, or no MCS. Interestingly, our ECMO and durable LVAD cohorts had similar intraoperative transfusion needs. These findings are likely attributable to the hemolysis and coagulopathic state associated with durable LVAD or ECMO use, and adhesions inherent to a redo sternotomy in patients with LVAD. Postoperatively, our study only observed differences in transfusions between the durable LVAD or ECMO groups compared with the no-MCS group. However, we believe that this is a result of inadequate power to detect a true difference and the need to adjust for multiple comparisons in our study. Furthermore, Interestingly, there are several novel findings from this study that have not previously been reported. This includes that pretransplant IABP has comparable intraoperative and postoperative transfusion requirements compared with no MCS, and the ECMO cohort having increased transfusion needs similar to the durable LVAD cohort. Notably, we also found a significantly greater need for intraoperative pRBC and platelet transfusions in the Impella 5.5 group compared with the no-MCS group. This could be partially attributable to hemolysis, a known complication of Impella use or acquired von Willebrand deficiency.9, 10, 11 These insights could better inform the pretransplant MCS choice, such as in cases in which both IABP and Impella 5.5 can adequately support a patient. Although we recognize that most patients’ clinical status determines their MCS choice and we cannot avoid using greater-risk options because of these findings, we believe that our results are most informative when helping set expectations so care teams can better coordinate and planning for escalating care after the transplant.

Our study also provides both comparable findings with previous studies regarding posttransplant outcomes of patients bridged with MCS. Many previous studies have demonstrated that patients requiring pretransplant ECMO had worse posttransplant survival and outcomes.5,12,13 Similarly, we found that our ECMO cohort had longer ventilation durations and lengths of stay as well as greater mortality compared with those without pretransplant MCS. Previous studies have also demonstrated comparable survival between those with a durable LVAD compared with those with temporary MCS5,13 or those without pretransplant MCS.6,12,14 This finding was reiterated in our adjusted multivariate analyses demonstrating comparable 1-year survival between the LVAD and no-MCS cohorts. Lastly, Nordan and colleagues15 demonstrated comparable survival between transplant recipients bridged with IABP versus Impella 5.5, a finding that this study also reiterated.

The results of this study could help guide the clinical management of patients with heart failure awaiting transplantation. As briefly mentioned, both IABP and Impella 5.5 are acceptable options for status 2 patients requiring hemodynamic support in a hospitalized setting.9 In this case, our finding of Impella 5.5 use being associated with greater intraoperative pRBCs may inform the decision to use an IABP if the degree of hemodynamic support needed can be met. However, escalation to ECMO may not be avoidable in patients in whom IABP or Impella 5.5 has failed to provide adequate support. Similarly, the need for a durable LVAD is not avoidable in those with current contraindications to heart transplant or those recipients with an especially long wait-list time. Therefore, further work into strategies to reduce transfusion requirements and improve posttransplant outcomes in the LVAD and ECMO cohorts are needed in the future. For example, partial coverage of the LVAD during the initial implant has been shown to reduce perioperative transfusion requirements during the LVAD explant-transplant process.2 This study provides valuable information to ensure prudent clinical decision-making for patients requiring a bridge to heart transplant and better help teams coordinate care by setting expectations early when the patient is undergoing the transplant. These findings could also help perioperative patient counseling and expectations setting. Future efforts are needed to help improve outcomes in those requiring greater-risk MCS options.

This study has limitations inherent to single-center, retrospective studies with low sample sizes in the Impella and ECMO groups. The greatest limitation of this study is that patients within each MCS group may be inherently different. For example, differences in pretransplant hemodynamic stability likely determined the need for IABP/Impella 5.5 versus ECMO, and these differences may be a possible for differences in posttransplant outcomes. We also acknowledge that all patients in the LVAD cohort had a previous sternotomy compared with the majority of patients in the other cohorts having no previous sternotomy which is a confounder for increased transfusion requirements. Similarly, ECMO support is associated with more significant coagulation disturbance compared with other support options. Although we conducted adjusted analyses to adjust for some baseline differences, we were unable to adjust for pretransplant hemodynamic and coagulation status. However, we believe that the comprehensive analysis we conducted, that also included intraoperative and postoperative transfusion requirements, provides valuable insight for the heart transplant community. Another limitation is that transfusion decisions may vary by transplant surgeon, anesthesiologist, and critical care team. Although this is unavoidable, we believe that future studies with larger cohorts would reduce the impact of this variability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that heart transplantation bridging with durable LVAD or ECMO was associated with greater intraoperative transfusion requirements compared with those bridged with IABP or Impella 5.5, and those without pretransplant MCS. Although postoperative transfusion requirements were also greater in both LVAD and ECMO groups, there was no difference between the durable LVAD and ECMO groups. Although further studies are required, these results may help provide insight into preoperative MCS strategy and help prognosticate recipients of heart transplantation on the basis of MCS platform at the time of surgery.
