Arithmetic holonomy bounds and effective Diophantine approximation Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 11J68, 11J72
Frank Calegari, Vesselin Dimitrov, Yunqing Tang

Abstract
In this paper, we explore several threads arising from our recent joint work on arithmetic holonomy bounds, which were originally devised to prove new irrationality results based on the method of Apéry limits. We propose a new method to address effective Diophantine approximation on the projective line and the multiplicative group. This method, and all our other results in the paper, emerged from quantifying our holonomy bounds in a way that directly yields effective measures of irrationality and linear independence. Applying these to a dihedral algebraic construction, we derive good effective irrationality measures for high order roots of an algebraic number, in an approach that might be considered a multivalent continuation of the classical hypergeometric method of Thue, Siegel, and Baker. A well-known Dirichlet approximation argument of Bombieri allows one to derive from this the classical effective Diophantine theorems, hitherto only approachable by Baker’s linear forms in logarithms or by Bombieri’s equivariant Thue–Siegel method. These include the algorithmic resolution of the two-variable S S -unit equation, the Thue–Mahler equation, and the hyperelliptic and superelliptic equations, as well as the Baker–Feldman effective power sharpening of Liouville’s theorem. We also give some other applications, including irrationality measures for the classical L ⁡ ( 2 , χ − 3 ) L(2,\chi_{-3}) and the 2 2 -adic ζ ⁡ ( 5 ) \zeta(5) , and a new proof of the transcendence of π \pi . Due to space limitations, a full development of these ideas will be deferred to future work.

2.1
Holonomy bounds and irrationality.

Let us recall the framework in
[

\eta

We start (typically) with

Consider two positive integers

\mathbf{Q}(x)
-span of power series

A dihedral method.

We begin by recalling the classical hypergeometric method
[
54

of the equation
aX^{r}-bY^{r}=c
for
r\geq 3
and
a,b,c\in\mathbf{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}

The hypergeometric equation with three parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
is the second-order linear ODE

\displaystyle x(x-1)\frac{d^{2}F}{dx^{2}}+\left((\alpha+\beta+1)x-\gamma\right)\frac{dF}{dx}+\alpha\beta F=0,

with one solution, regular at the singular point
x=0
, being given by the power series

\displaystyle F(x)={}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha, \beta}{\gamma};x\right]}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+k-1)\cdot\beta(\beta+1)\cdots(\beta+k-1)}{\gamma(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+k-1)}\,\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.

A well-known fact going back to Gauss and Jacobi
[
48
, page 160]
is that the Hermite–Padé approximants to the hypergeometric functions of the special form
{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha, 1}{\gamma};x\right]}

are all explicitly given in terms of hypergeometric polynomials themselves. This applies in particular to the logarithm function
\log(1-x)=-x\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1, 1}{2};x\right]}
as well as the binomial functions
(1-x)^{\nu}={}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-\nu, 1}{1};x\right]}
, and we have:

6\leq m\leq\frac{\max\log|\varphi(z)|+\sum_{p}\log R_{p}}{\log|\varphi^{\prime}(0)|+\sum_{p}\log R_{p}-\tau}=\frac{\log\left((1/5)\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+(1/5)^{n})^{24}\right)+12\log 2}{-\log 3+12\log 2-5}=5.52667\ldots<6.

We proved
such bounds (together with the application to
\zeta_{2}(5)
) in 2020
(see
[
34
, §15.7]
), but we put
the argument aside in order to work towards the main theorems of
[
34
]
; we apologize
for the delay!
Quite recently,
Lai, Sprang, and Zudilin
[
50
]
have found a completely new proof
of the irrationality of
\zeta_{2}(5)
, more in the spirit of Apéry’s original
argument, by working with a differential equation with better convergence properties. The corresponding local system in their case appears to have a genuinely different
geometric origin, coming from a moduli
space of Calabi–Yau
3
-folds rather than a symmetric power of the Picard–Fuchs equation
of a modular curve.
Neither of these results, however, have any direct bearing on the (ir)rationality
of the classical
\zeta(5)
.

One may wonder
why one can prove the irrationality of
\zeta_{2}(5)\in\mathbf{Q}_{2}
but not the irrationality of
\zeta(5)\in\mathbf{R}
.
We give a heuristic explanation why the first problem is easier. Let
\mathcal{MT}(\mathbf{Z})
denote the Tannakian category of mixed Tate motives over
\mathbf{Z}
.
Associated to
\mathcal{MT}(\mathbf{Z})
is a
\mathbf{Q}
-algebra of real (fixed by complex conjugation) motivic periods
\mathcal{P}

[
27
]
.
The comparison theorem between Betti cohomology and de Rham cohomology (over
\mathbf{C}
) induces a period map
\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
,
and Grothendieck’s period conjecture implies that this map is injective
[
3
]
.
A consequence of this conjecture is that the
\mathbf{Q}
-algebra
generated by
\zeta(2)
and the odd values
\zeta(2k+1)
for
k\geq 1

is free on these generators; equivalently, that these values are all
transcendental and moreover algebraically independent.
What is perhaps surprising at first is that the
p
-adic analogue of this story
behaves somewhat differently
[
14
,
45
]
.
One of the most basic manifestations of this difference is
that the
p
-adic comparison theorem naturally places the
p
-adic
analogue of
2\pi i
as an element in
B^{+}_{\mathrm{dR}}
which maps to zero in
\mathbf{C}_{p}
.
In particular, the only sensible definition of
\zeta_{p}(2)

returns
\zeta_{p}(2)=0
(
[
63
, Rem 3.7]
). One nonetheless conjectures
[
64
]

that the
\mathbf{Q}
-algebra
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p}(3),\zeta_{p}(5),\zeta_{p}(7),\ldots)

is free (on the obvious generators).
Let us consider
how the vanishing of
\zeta_{p}(2)
affects approaches to irrationality.
The first step in any Apéry type scheme is to produce a motivic local system
where the corresponding
L
-value arises as a period.
One issue, when trying to construct such local systems
is that various periods get “mixed” together and it is hard to separate
the period one is particularly interested in.
(One interesting discussion of these issues
from a geometric point of view
can be found in
[
28
]
.)
The point is now that,
p
-adically, the vanishing of
\zeta_{p}(2)

makes it easier to realize the odd zeta values on their own.
Relevantly for us, the construction in
[
33
]

directly generalizes to produce an ODE over
\mathbf{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,-1/2^{6},\infty\}

associated to
\zeta_{2}(2k+1)
for any
k
; we know of no simple analogous construction
for
\zeta(2k+1)\in\mathbf{R}
.
As another example; the ODE for the
2
-adic Catalan’s
constant in
[
33
]
has the form
\mathcal{L}F=0
over
\mathbf{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,-1/2^{4},\infty\}
where

\mathcal{L}=x(1+16x)^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+(1+16x)^{2}\frac{d}{dx}-4.

Up to scaling this is a hypergeometric equation which one can solve explicitly, namely, with:

(5.9)

A(x)=(1+16x)^{1/2}\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2,1/2}{1};-16x\right]},\qquad C(x)=(1+16x)^{1/2}\cdot{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1/2,1/2}{1};1+16x\right]}.

The holomorphic solution
B(x)\in\mathbf{Q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
to the non-homogenous equation
\mathcal{L}F=1+16x

with
B(0)=0
and
B^{\prime}(0)=1
is of denominator type
\tau=[1,2,\ldots,n]^{2}
.
Another solution to this equation
\mathcal{L}F=1+16x
is given explicitly by

D(x)=-\frac{1+16x}{4}\cdot{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{1,1,1}{3/2,3/2};1+16x\right]}.

Over the real numbers,
D(x)
is singular at
x=0
, and we have the equality

(5.10)

D(x)=B(x)-\frac{G}{2}\cdot A(x)-\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}\cdot C(x),

where
G\in\mathbf{R}
is now the usual Catalan’s constant.
In contrast,
2
-adically,
we see that
D(x)
converges for
|1+16x|_{2}<2^{4}
,
or for
|x|_{2}<2^{8}
, and so it is certainly regular at
x=0
. Now the
2
-adic avatar of
Eq.

5.10
simplifies to the formula

(5.11)

D(x)=B(x)-\frac{G_{2}}{2}\cdot A(x),

in which
G_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}_{2}
is the
2
-adic Catalan constant. Thus
Eq.

5.11

realizes
G_{2}
as an Apéry limit.
The key point is now that
Eq.

5.10
is
precisely the “same” equation as
Eq.

5.11
as long as we
interpret the
2
-adic meaning of
(2\pi i)^{2}
to be zero, but Apéry’s
method only allows one to deduce that
G_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}_{2}\smallsetminus\mathbf{Q}
, but not that
G\in\mathbf{R}\smallsetminus\mathbf{Q}
.
That said, while this is an argument to explain why the
p
-adic zeta
values of small weight should be easier to study than their Archimedean counterparts,
there is also the contrasting remark that we can’t as yet even rule
out the possibility that
\zeta_{p}(3)=0
for all sufficiently large
p
,
let alone prove that all those values are irrational.
