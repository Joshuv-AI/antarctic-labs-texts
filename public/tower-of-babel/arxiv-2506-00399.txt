Evolution of wind-generated shallow water waves in a Benney-Luke equation
Montri Maleewong, Roger Grimshaw

Abstract
In recent papers, denoted by MG24, MG25 in this text, we used the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation and its two-dimensional extension, the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation to describe the evolution of wind-driven water wave packets in shallow water. Both equations were modified to include the effect of wind forcing, modelled using the Miles critical level instability theory. In this paper that is extended to a Benney-Luke (BL) equation, similarly modified for wind forcing. The motivation is that the BL equation is isotropic in the horizontal space variables, unlike the KP model, and noting that the KdV model is one-dimensional. The modified BL equation is studied using wave modulation theory as in MG24, MG25, and with comprehensive numerical simulations. Despite the very different spatial structure the results show that under the right initial conditions and parameter settings, solitary wave trains emerge as in MG24, MG25.

1
Introduction

The evolution of wind-generated water waves is a highly studied topic, for the overlapping
aims of scientific research and operational forecasting, see
Janssen, 2004
;
Osborne, 2010
;
Grimshaw et al., 2018
for instance.
For the most part the emphasis has been on deep-water waves for the valid reason that in
the ocean and in many experimental wave flumes, wind waves do not feel the water depth.
Nevertheless it is somewhat surprising that studies of wind-driven waves in shallow water are
relatively sparse until quite recently, see
Costa et al., 2014
;
Zdyrski and Feddersen, 2021
;
Manna and Latifi, 2023
and our recent work

Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
. Not surprisingly these were based on the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation,
which is well established as a model for shallow water waves, and widely known as a
canonical integrable nonlinear wave equation. The inclusion of wind forcing terms, especially
the crtical layer mechanism of
Miles, 1957
leads to an (unstable) Burgers type of term
and we call that outcome a KdVB equation. Since the KdV equation is uni-directional,
with only one horizontal space coordinate,
x
, in
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
we examined a similar forcing
extension to the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation which, while still uni-directional,
has a weak dependence on the spatial variable
y
, transverse to the wind direction (
x
);
that outcome we call the KPB equation. Both the KdV and KPB models exhibit the
formation of many solitary waves, a soliton gas, see the review by
El, 2021
.

The KP equation is not isotropic in the spatial variables
x,y
as the equation contains a
scaling
y\sim x^{2}
, unlike the full Euler equations which are isotropic in
x,y
.
This feature was remedied by
Benney and Luke, 1964
who derived the Benney-Luke (BL) equation
for shallow water waves, which unlike the KP equation is isotropic in
x,y
.
Here we add the same wind forcing term to the BL equation, which is again a Burgers
type of term. As shown by
Benney and Luke, 1964
, see also
Ablowitz and Curtis, 2011
, the BL equation has the
same type of travelling waves, cnoidal and solitary, as the KdV and KP equations.

The BL equation is an asymptotic reduction of the full Euler equations based on a balance two
small parameters,
\epsilon
measuring wave amplitude, and
\beta
measuring long wave dispersion.
At leading order it is the two-dimensional linear wave equation, and importantly has two small
perturbative terms, weak nonlinearity and slow long-wave linear dispersion, made equal with

\beta=\epsilon
. Consequently, initially the solution is close to that of the linear long wave equation,
and the appearance of nonlinear and dispersive effects, such as the formation of solitary waves,
takes a very long time, and occurs in a reference frame moving with the linear long wave speed,
requiring very long-time numerical simulations. Importantly here the dispersion term is regularised
to remove spurious high wavenumber instability generated by the asymptotic reduction, see

Whitham, 1974
;
El et al., 2016
for instance.

In the next section
2
, we present the asymptotic derivation of the forced regularised
BL equation. Then in section
3
we describe the cnoidal and solitary wave solutions,
3.1
,
followed by their modulation theory,
3.2
, and in particular the modulation theory for a solitary
wave train,
3.3
. Our numerical simulations of the BL equation is described in section
4
.
We conclude with a summary and discussion in section
5
.

2
Benney-Luke equation

Benney and Luke, 1964
derived the BL equation as a shallow-water, long-wave asymptotic reduction
of the full Euler equations. Here we outline that asymptotic theory, following the original derivation
and adding to it wind forcing due to the critical level instability theory mechanism of
Miles, 1957
,
as introduced by
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024a
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
into the full Euler equations. The water is an
inviscid, incompressible fluid of constant density in the domain
-h<z<\zeta

where
z=-h
is a rigid fixed bottom and
z=\zeta(x,y,t)
is the free surface.
For irrotational flow the governing equations are expressed in terms of a
velocity potential
\phi(x,y,z,t)
so that the fluid velocity
{\bf u}=\nabla\phi
.

\nabla^{2}\phi+\phi_{zz}=0\,,\quad\nabla^{2}\phi=\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}\,,\quad-h<z<\zeta\,.

(1)

The bottom boundary condition is

\phi_{z}\,=\,0\,,\quad\hbox{on}\quad z=-h\,.

(2)

At the free surface the kinematic and dynamic boundary conditions are

\zeta_{t}+\phi_{x}\zeta_{x}+\phi_{y}\zeta_{y}-\phi_{z}=0\,,\quad z=\zeta\,,

(3)

\phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2}+\phi_{z}^{2})+g\zeta=-\frac{P_{a}}{\rho_{w}}\,,\quad z=\zeta\,.

(4)

Here
\rho_{w}
is the constant water density, and
P_{a}(x,y,t)
is the surface air
pressure containing the wind forcing term to be specified later.
Next, we introduce scaled variables, denoted by
[\bar{\cdot}]
, and two
dimensionless small parameters
\epsilon,\beta
,

\begin{split}&\phi=CL\beta\bar{\phi}\,,\,\,\zeta=A_{0}\bar{\zeta}\,,\,\,(x,y)=L(\bar{x},\bar{y}),\,\,z=h\bar{z},\,\,t=\frac{L}{C}\bar{t}\,,\,\,C=(gh)^{1/2}\,,\\
&\qquad\epsilon=\frac{h^{2}}{L^{2}}\,,\quad\beta=\frac{A_{0}}{h}\,,\quad\frac{P_{a}}{\rho_{w}}=\beta C^{2}\bar{P_{a}}\,.\end{split}

(5)

where
A_{0},L
are measures of wave amplitude and horizontal wave length scale respectively.

The equations (
1
-
4
) become, omitting the
[\bar{\cdot}]

and putting
\beta=\epsilon
as in
Benney and Luke, 1964
,

\displaystyle\epsilon\nabla^{2}\phi+\phi_{zz}=0\,,\quad-1<z<\epsilon\zeta\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\phi_{z}=0,\quad z=-1\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\epsilon\zeta_{t}+\epsilon^{2}(\phi_{x}\zeta_{x}+\phi_{y}\zeta_{y})-\phi_{z}=0\,,\quad z=\epsilon\zeta\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\epsilon(\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2})+\phi_{z}^{2})+\zeta=-P_{a}\,,\quad z=\epsilon\zeta\,.

(9)

An asymptotic solution of (
6
,
7
) for
\epsilon\ll 1
is

\phi=f(x,y,t)-\epsilon\nabla^{2}f\frac{(z+1)^{2}}{2}+\epsilon^{2}\nabla^{4}f\frac{(z+1)^{4}}{24}+O(\epsilon^{3})\,.

(10)

Then (
9
) yields

\zeta=-f_{t}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2}-\nabla^{2}f_{t})-P_{a}.

(11)

The next step is to use equations (
10
,
11
) in (
8
) to get the
regularised Benney-Luke equation,

f_{tt}-\nabla^{2}f+\epsilon[-\frac{1}{3}\nabla^{2}f_{tt}+f_{t}\nabla^{2}f+2f_{x}f_{xt}+2f_{y}f_{yt}]+P_{at}=0\,.\\

(12)

This agrees with
Benney and Luke, 1964
after using the leading order term to regularise and omitting
P_{a}
.

We assume that the wind forcing acts in the
x
-direction, so that, following
Miles, 1957
,
see also
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
and the references therein, the air pressure is linked to the
surface slope; in non-dimensional variables,

P_{a}=\alpha\zeta_{x},\quad\alpha=\epsilon^{1/2}\beta^{\prime},\quad\beta^{\prime}=\beta_{M}\frac{\rho_{a}}{\rho_{w}}\frac{W_{r}^{2}}{C^{2}}\,.

(13)

\rho_{a,w}
are the air, water densities,
\beta_{M}
is a dimensionless parameter
introduced by
Miles, 1957
, and
W_{r}
is a reference velocity. As anticipated
P_{a}

is a small perturbation because
\rho_{a}\ll\rho_{w}
and
W_{r}\ll C
. It can now be checked from (
12
)
that
\beta_{M}\gtrless 0
leads to instability or decay for a periodic wave.

Equation (
12
) contains a small parameter
\epsilon
and initially to the lowest order in
\epsilon
,
the solutions are those of the linear long wave equation. Nevertheless, since it is the role of the

O(\epsilon)
terms which is the main interest, long-time simulations are needed.
A suitable value of
\epsilon
is in the range
0.1-0.5
, large enough to explore nonlinear and
dispersive effects, but well below large amplitude dynamical wave instability.
The BL equation (
12
) has an asymptotic reduction for
\epsilon\to 0
to the KP equation
expressed in the variables
X=x-t,Y=\epsilon^{1/2}y,T=\epsilon t

see
Ablowitz and Curtis, 2011
;
Ablowitz and Curtis, 2013
and below.

For the numerical simulations, the BL equation (
12
) is written as a coupled system of two
first order in time equations for
f,g=-f_{t}
,

f_{t}=-g,\quad g_{t}=-\nabla^{2}f+\epsilon[\frac{1}{3}\nabla^{2}g_{t}-g\nabla^{2}f-2f_{x}g_{x}-2f_{y}g_{y}]+\alpha g_{xt}\,.\\

(14)

The surface displacement (
11
) is then given by

\zeta=g-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2}+\nabla^{2}g)-\alpha g_{x}\,.

(15)

The BL equation can also be converted to a conventional Boussinesq-type system for

\zeta,U=f_{x},V=f_{y}
, where
U,V
are the leading order horizontal velocity field,
but we will not give details here.

The BL equation (
12
) is isotropic in
(x,y)
unlike the KPB equation of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
in which

y\sim x^{2}
in scaling. As shown by
Ablowitz and Curtis, 2011
;
Ablowitz and Curtis, 2013
and mentioned above, the BL equation
has an asymptotic reduction to the KPB equation as
\epsilon\to 0
, expressed to the leading order
in
\epsilon
for a wave moving in the positive
x
direction with a phase speed approximately unity,

(2\zeta_{T}+\frac{1}{3}\zeta_{XXX}+3\,\zeta\,\zeta_{X})_{X}+\zeta_{YY}+\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon}\zeta_{XXX}=0.\quad X=x-t,Y=\epsilon^{1/2}y,T=\epsilon t.

(16)

With some numerical rescaling this is the KPB equation of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
.

The initial conditions are specification of
f=f_{0}(x,y),g=g_{0}(x,y)
. In general these can be
specified independently, but here we will link them to assist in the generation of waves
moving in the positive
x
-direction. Two main cases A, B are considered,

\begin{split}&A:\,\,f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{A}{\omega_{0}}\sin{(k_{0}x+l_{0}y)},\,\,g_{0}(x,y)=A\,\cos{(k_{0}x+l_{0}y)},\\
&B:\,\,f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{A}{\omega_{0}}\sin{(k_{0}x)}\cos{(l_{0}y)},\,\,g_{0}(x,y)=A\,\cos{(k_{0}x)}\cos{(l_{0}y)},\\
&\omega_{0}=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{(1+\epsilon\kappa_{0}^{2}/3)^{1/2}},\,\,\kappa_{0}=(k_{0}^{2}+l_{0}^{2})^{1/2}.\end{split}

(17)

(f_{0},g_{0})(x,y)
are sinusoidal functions and
\omega_{0}
is the linear frequency for a wave
in the positive
k_{0},l_{0}
direction.
A
is the initial wave amplitude, and
k_{0},l_{0}
are the
initial wavenumbers, in case A generating a wave initially travelling in the
k_{0},l_{0}
direction.
In case B,
g_{0}(x,y)
is the initial elevation used in the KPB simulation of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
,
but here in the BL equation (
14
) it will generate waves in both positive and negative
x
-directions.
We choose
f_{0}(x,y)
to correspond to linear waves moving in the positive
x
-direction
and use an upstream sponge layer to absorb waves moving in the negative
x
-direction.
These initial conditions are designed to produce wave packets, with many solitary waves,
resembling a soliton gas as found in the KdVB and KPB simulations of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
.
As time increases the nonlinear terms in (
14
) come into play, generating solitary waves as
the dispersive and nonlinear effects come into balance. The numerical simulations are on a
periodic domain
[-P_{X}<x<P_{X},\,-P_{Y}<y<P_{Y}]
. The initial condition (
17
) is
combined with envelopes
ENV(x),ENV(y)
with long length scales
L_{X,Y}

such that
2\pi/k_{0}\ll L_{X}\ll P_{X}
,
2\pi/l_{0}\ll L_{Y}\ll P_{Y}
. We usually ensure that

\omega_{0}\sim 1
and vary
k_{0},l_{0}
with typically
k_{0}\sim 1
and
0.1\leq l_{0}\leq 0.3
.
The envelopes
ENV(x),ENV(y)
are chosen so that
f,g=0
at the periodic domain
boundaries, see section
4
.

The mass is
\zeta
and mass conservation is expressed in non-dimensional variables by (
8
)
expressed here in the BL asymptotic form,

\zeta_{t}=-\nabla^{2}f-\epsilon[\frac{1}{6}\nabla^{2}g_{t}+g\nabla^{2}f+f_{x}g_{x}+f_{y}g_{y}]\,.

(18)

In the full Euler system the energy equation is

\begin{split}&\it{E}_{t}+\it{F}^{X}_{x}+\it{F}^{Y}_{y}=-\alpha\zeta_{x}\zeta_{t},\\
&\it{E}=\frac{1}{2}\{\int_{-1}^{\epsilon\zeta}\,[\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2}+\frac{\phi_{z}^{2}}{\epsilon}]\,dz+\zeta^{2}\}\,\\
&\it{F}^{(X,Y)}=-\int^{\epsilon\zeta}_{-1}\,\phi_{t}(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})\,dz.\end{split}

(19)

Then using the asymptotic expansion (
10
) this becomes,

\begin{split}&\it{E}=\frac{1}{2}\{(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2})(1+\epsilon\zeta)+\zeta^{2}\}+\epsilon\{\frac{(\nabla f)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\nabla f)(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2})}{6}\},\\
&\it{F}^{(X,Y)}=-(f_{t}f_{\chi})(1+\epsilon\zeta)+\epsilon\frac{1}{6}(f_{\chi}\nabla^{2}f_{t}+f_{t}\,\nabla^{2}f_{\chi}),\hskip 10.22217pt\chi=(x,y).\end{split}

(20)

To leading order in
\epsilon
the energy equation (
19
) reduces to

\begin{split}&\it{E}_{t}+(\zeta f_{x})_{x}+(\zeta f_{y})_{y}+\cdots=-\alpha\zeta_{t}\zeta_{x},\\
&\it{E}=\frac{1}{2}\{(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2})+\zeta^{2}\}+\cdots,\hskip 10.22217pt\zeta=-f_{t}+\cdots.\end{split}

(21)

This reduced form, which is obtained just from the linear long wave equation, suffices for most purposes.

3
Travelling waves

3.1
Cnoidal waves

Benney and Luke, 1964
showed that the BL equation (
12
) has periodic cnoidal and solitary wave solutions,
for which
f,g,\zeta
depend on the phase variable
\vartheta=kx+ly-\omega t
.
The phase velocity normal to the wave front
\vartheta=\hbox{constant}
is

c=\omega/\kappa=1+\epsilon c_{1}+O(\epsilon^{2})
. Here, in order to consider modulated waves,
we extend that, and seek solutions of the form
f,g,\zeta
which depend on
\theta(x,y,t)

with wavenumbers
k,l
and frequency
\omega
given by

\theta_{x}=k,\,\,\theta_{y}=l,\,\,-\theta_{t}=\omega=\kappa c,\,\,\kappa=(k^{2}+l^{2})^{1/2},\quad c=1+\epsilon c_{1}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(22)

k,l,c
are slowly varying relative to the phase, but the scale for the slow variation is not connected to
\epsilon
.
In the sequel omitted terms
[\cdots]
include both higher order terms in
\epsilon
and higher order
slowly varying terms. The equations for conservation of waves come from eliminating

\theta
from (
22
),

k_{t}+\kappa_{x}+\epsilon(\kappa c_{1})_{x}+\cdots=0,\quad l_{t}+\kappa_{y}+\epsilon(\kappa c_{1})_{y}+\cdots=0,\quad k_{y}=l_{x}\,.

(23)

To the leading order in
\epsilon
, (
23
) are equations for
k,l
alone;
the terms in
\epsilon c_{1}
will contain a dependence on other wave parameters such
as the wave amplitude.

The modulated wave
\zeta(\theta,x,y,t)
is periodic in
\theta

with period
2\pi
and the slow dependence on
x,y,t
will be
expressed through the wave parameters. We define a wave average for a single wave

<[\cdots]>=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\,[\cdots]\,d\theta\,.

(24)

The wave is then decomposed into a periodic wave
\hat{\zeta}
with zero mean, and a mean level
D

\zeta=\hat{\zeta}(\theta,x,y,t)+D(x,y,t)\,,\quad<\hat{\zeta}>=0,\quad D(x,y,t)=<\zeta>\,.

(25)

The explicit
x,y,t
dependence in
\hat{\zeta},D
is slowly varying. Wave energy is usefully defined as

E=<\hat{\zeta}^{2}>
obtained from (
21
). Correspondingly,
f,g
are also decomposed into a
periodic wave
\hat{f},\hat{g}
with zero means, and mean values
F,G
,

\hat{g}=\omega\hat{f}_{\theta}-\hat{f}_{t},\quad G=-F_{t}\,.

(26)

The leading order horizontal velocity field
f_{x},f_{y}
is given by

f_{x,y}=(k,l)\hat{f}_{\theta}+\hat{f}_{x,y}+F_{x,y}\,.

(27)

Substitution of (
25
,
26
) into the BL equation (
14
) yields at the leading order
in both
\epsilon
and with respect to slow variation in
x,y,t
,

-2c_{1}\hat{\zeta}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{3}\hat{\zeta}_{\theta\theta}+\frac{3}{2}\kappa\hat{\zeta}^{2}=\hbox{constant}\,.

(28)

Equation (
28
) has the same structure as that for travelling waves of the KdV equation,
see
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
for instance, and hence has a cnoidal wave solution,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\hat{\zeta}=A\{b+cn^{2}(\gamma\theta;m)\},\quad b=\frac{1-m}{m}-\frac{E(m)}{mK(m)}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle A=3\,m\,\Gamma^{2}\,,\,\,c_{1}-D=\frac{2\Gamma^{2}}{3}\left\{2-m-\frac{3E(m)}{K(m)}\right\}\,,\,\,\Gamma=\gamma\kappa\,.

(30)

To leading order the corresponding expressions for
f,g
are given by

\omega\hat{f}_{\theta}=\hat{g}=\hat{\zeta}
.

In (
29
,
30
)
cn(x;m)
is the Jacobi elliptic function of modulus
m
(
0<m<1
)
and
K(m),E(m)
are the elliptic integrals of the first and second kind respectively, all defined by

cn(x;m)=\cos{(\phi)}\,,\quad x=\int_{0}^{\phi}\,\frac{d\phi^{\prime}}{(1-m\sin^{2}{\phi^{\prime}})^{1/2}}\,,\quad 0\leq\phi\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\,,

(31)

K(m)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\,\frac{d\phi}{(1-m\sin^{2}{\phi})^{1/2}}\,,\quad E(m)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\,(1-m\sin^{2}{\phi})^{1/2}\,d\phi\,.

(32)

The cnoidal wave (
29
) is periodic in
\theta
with a period
2\pi=2K(m)/\gamma
,
which defines
\gamma=K(m)/\pi
. The corresponding spatial period is
2\pi/k=2K(m)/\Gamma
.

A
is the wave amplitude and the maximum, minimum values of
\hat{\zeta}
are
A_{M}=A(b+1),A_{m}=Ab
.

When the modulus
m\to 0
,
cn(x;m)\to\cos{(x)}
,
\gamma\to 1/2
,
b\to-1/2

and then the cnoidal wave (
29
) collapses to a linear sinusoidal wave,

\hat{\zeta}=\frac{A}{2}\,\cos{(\theta)}\,,\quad c_{1}-D=-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\,.

(33)

In this limit
A\to 0
, but
A/m=3\Gamma^{2}
stays finite. This linear sinusoidal wave
has a linear dispersion relation
\omega^{2}(1+\epsilon\kappa^{2}/3)=\kappa^{2}
,
relative to the mean level
D
, which reduces to the above expression for
c_{1}

as
\epsilon\to 0
.
Inter alia
this demonstrates that the BL equation (
12
) is linearly stable.

When the modulus
m\to 1
,
cn(x;m)\to\hbox{sech}(x)
,
K(m)\to\infty
,
E(m)\to 1

and
b(m)\to 0
. The cnoidal wave (
29
) becomes a solitary wave train,
defined on a periodic lattice, and riding on a background level
D
.

\hat{\zeta}=A\,\hbox{sech}^{2}(\gamma\theta)\,,\quad A=3\Gamma^{2}\,,\quad c_{1}-D=\frac{2\Gamma^{2}}{3}\,.

(34)

In this limit,
\kappa\to 0
,
\gamma\to\infty
but
\Gamma=\gamma\kappa
stays finite.
Note that
\gamma\theta=\Gamma\Theta
,
\Theta=\theta/\kappa
and we can usefully write that

\Theta=kx/\kappa+ly/\kappa-ct
since
\omega=\kappa c
.

3.2
Modulation theory

The cnoidal wave (
29
) has seven parameters
[A,m,\Gamma,D,c,k,l]
.
Three modulation equations are provided by the conservation of wave equations
(
22
,
23
); there are two relations in (
30
); the remaining two
modulation equations are provided by taking the wave average of the conservation of
mass equation (
18
) and the conservation of energy equation
(
19
,
20
).

The wave average of the conservation of mass equation (
18
) yields, to leading order,

\begin{split}&D=G-\frac{\epsilon}{2}(F_{x}^{2}+F_{y}^{2})-\epsilon E,\quad G=-F_{t}.\\
&D_{t}=-\nabla^{2}F-\epsilon(G\nabla^{2}F+F_{x}G_{x}+F_{y}G_{y})-\epsilon E_{t}.\end{split}

(35)

With only an order
\epsilon
dependence on wave energy
E
effectively this is an
equation for
D
alone, but we note that
D
increases/decreases in the opposite sense to
E
.
Hence to within an order
\epsilon
term,
D(x,y,t)
can be regarded as known.
In the sequel, we shall usually put
D=0,G=0,F=0
.

Then, again to leading order in
\epsilon
, the wave average of the conservation of energy
equation (
19
,
20
), using the reduced form (
21
), yields an equation
for wave energy,

\displaystyle E_{t}+(\frac{k}{\kappa}E)_{x}+(\frac{l}{\kappa}E)_{y}+\cdots=\alpha J\,,

(36)

\displaystyle E=<\frac{1}{2}\{f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2}+f_{t}^{2}\}>=<\hat{\zeta}^{2}>=A^{2}(C_{4}-b^{2})\,,\quad C_{4}=<cn^{4}>\,,

(37)

\displaystyle J=-<\zeta_{t}\zeta_{x}>=\omega k<\hat{\zeta}_{\theta}^{2}>=\frac{k}{\kappa}\frac{16A^{2}\Gamma^{2}}{3}\{-(1-m)b+(2m-1)C_{4}-mC_{6}\}\,.

(38)

Here
C_{n}=<cn^{n}>\,,n=2,4,\ldots
where
C_{2}=-b
and espressions for
C_{4},\ldots

can be found in
Grimshaw and Yuan, 2016
. The wave energy propagates in the
x-y
plane with a velocity

k/\kappa,l/\kappa
which at this leading order in
\epsilon
is the phase velocity.
Since here
\omega=\kappa c\approx\kappa>0
,
kF>0
, (
36
) implies wave growth as

\alpha>0
due a critical level instability in the wind shear profile. But we note that as shown by

Miles, 1957
;
Kharif et al., 2010
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024a
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
sufficient boundary layer dissipation at the water surface
leads to a revised
\alpha<0
and wave decay.

In the linear sinusoidal limit
m\to 0
(
33
) the modulation equation (
36
)
where (
37
,
38
) reduce to

E=<\hat{\zeta}^{2}>=\frac{A^{2}}{8}\,,\quad F=\omega k<\hat{\zeta}_{\theta}^{2}>=\omega k=\frac{A^{2}}{8}\,.

(39)

In the solitary wave limit
m\to 1
(
34
),
\kappa\to 0
,
E\to 0

but
\mathcal{E}=E/\kappa
stays finite. Similarly
F\to 0
but
\mathcal{F}=F/\kappa

stays finite and (
36
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}+\frac{k}{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{x}+\frac{l}{\kappa}\mathcal{E}_{y}=\alpha\mathcal{F}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\frac{2A^{2}}{3\pi\Gamma}=\frac{6\Gamma^{3}}{\pi},\quad\mathcal{F}=\frac{k}{\kappa}\frac{32A^{2}\Gamma}{45\pi}=\frac{k}{\kappa}\frac{32\Gamma^{5}}{5\pi}\,.

(41)

3.3
Solitary wave train

The modulation equations for a solitary wave train are (

,
23

be expressed in terms of
\Gamma

A=3\Gamma^{2}
for a solitary wave (

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}+\frac{k}{\kappa}\Gamma_{x}+\frac{l}{\kappa}\Gamma_{y}=\alpha\frac{k}{\kappa}\frac{16\Gamma^{3}}{45}\,,

(42)

\displaystyle k_{t}+\kappa_{x}+\epsilon(2\kappa\Gamma^{2})_{x}=0,\quad l_{t}+\kappa_{y}+\epsilon(2\kappa\Gamma^{2})_{y}=0,\quad k_{y}=l_{x}\,.

(43)

The modulation variables have been reduced to [
\Gamma,k,l
] where
k,l

satisfy (
43
), which to leading order in
\epsilon
we remind are equations for
k,l
alone.

When the modulation depends only on
t

k,l
are constants, and

\Gamma^{2}=\frac{\Gamma_{0}^{2}}{1-\alpha^{\prime}t},\quad\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha\frac{k}{\kappa}\frac{32\Gamma_{0}^{2}}{45}\,.

(44)

where
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma(t=0)
is the constant initial value. When
\alpha>0

there is wave growth,
\Gamma\to\infty
in finite time,
t=1/\alpha^{\prime}
.
This apparent singularity is essentially the same as that in the KdVB equation
as found by

Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b

the full equations. When

the wave decays,
\Gamma\to 0
as
t\to\infty

on a time scale proportional to
|\alpha^{\prime}|^{-1}
.

Next, we consider modulations only in
t,x
where we recall that the wind forcing is in the
x
-direction.
Then the
y
-wavenumber

is constant, and the modulation equations
(

,
43

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}+S(k)\Gamma_{x}=\alpha S(k)\frac{16\Gamma^{3}}{45}\,,

(45)

\displaystyle k_{t}+S(k)k_{x}+\epsilon(2\kappa\Gamma^{2})_{x}=0,\quad S(k)=\frac{k}{\kappa},\quad\kappa=(k^{2}+l_{0}^{2})^{1/2}\,.

(46)

To leading order in
\epsilon
, equation (

alone, with implicit solution

k=k_{(0)}(x-S(k)t),\quad k(x,t=0)=k_{(0)}(x)\,.

(47)

This solution exists provided that

k_{(0)x}<0
somewhere. With

\Gamma
alone. The solution propagates with speed

S(k)
modulo growth or decay as
\alpha\gtrless 0

When the
\epsilon

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}+S_{0}\Gamma_{x}=\alpha S_{0}\frac{16\Gamma^{3}}{45}\,,\quad S_{0}=S(k_{0})\,,

(48)

\displaystyle k_{1t}+S(k_{0})k_{1x}+(2\kappa_{0}\Gamma^{2})_{x}=0\,.

(49)

The general solution is readily found in the variables
X=x-S_{0}t,t
.
In the travelling frame
\Gamma
is given by (

\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}(X)

Then

(50)

Hence

decreases or increases according as
\Gamma_{0X}\gtrless 0
.
When

,

in finite time and

,
invalidating the approximation of

\alpha<0

\Gamma\to 0
and

as

Analogous solutions can be found for modulations in

with

-wavenumber

is a constant,
and the modulation equations (

,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}+S^{*}(l)\Gamma_{y}=\alpha\frac{k_{0}}{\kappa}\frac{16\Gamma^{3}}{45}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle l_{t}+S^{*}(l)l_{y}+\epsilon^{2}(2\kappa\Gamma^{2})_{y}=,\quad S^{*}(l)=\frac{l}{\kappa},\quad\kappa=(k_{0}^{2}+l^{2})^{1/2}\,.

(52)

The slopes
S(k),S^{*}(l)
are perpendicular. To leading order in
\epsilon
,
equation (

l
alone, with implicit solution

(53)

Similarly to (
47
) this solution exists provided that

, otherwise steepens and breaks;
since
S^{*}_{l}>0
, breaking will occur if
l_{[0]y}<0
somewhere. With

(
51

S^{*}(l)
modulo growth or decay as
\alpha\gtrless 0
,
Retention of the term in

in (

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}+S^{*}_{0}\Gamma_{y}=\alpha S^{*}_{0}\frac{16\Gamma^{3}}{45}\,,\quad S^{*}_{0}=S^{*}(l_{0})\,,

(54)

\displaystyle l_{1t}+S^{*}(l_{0})l_{1y}+(2\kappa_{0}\Gamma^{2})_{y}=0\,.

(55)

As before the general solution is found in the variables
Y=y-S^{*}_{0}t,t

4
Numerical Results

The BL equation written in the system form (
14
) is solved numerically
with a pseudo-spectral method in space and a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method in time.
A description of the code is in the Appendix. Pseuodo-spectral methods are a popular
choice for nonlinear wave equations since the pioneering work of
Fornberg and Whitham, 1978
, see

Milewski and Tabak, 1999
;
Klein and Roidot, 2011
;
Grava et al., 2018
for instance, and was used by us in a study of the
KdV-Burgers (KdVB) and KP-Burgers (KPB) equations,
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
. The code was tested
using the known cnoidal and solitary wave solutions of the BL equation (
14
).
Sponge layers analogous to those used in
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
) were sometimes used at the
outer periodic boundaries to absorb outgoing waves. Details of the numerical scheme
are described in the Appendix.

In the numerical simulations, the initial conditions (
17
) are expressed on the periodic domain,

[-P_{X}<x<P_{X},\,-P_{Y}<y<P_{Y}]
.

\begin{split}&g_{0}(x,y)=ENV(x)ENV(y)A\,COS(x,y),\,\,f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{A}{\omega_{0}}\,ENV(x)ENV(y)SIN(x,y).\\
&A:\quad COS(x,y)=\cos{(k_{0}x+l_{0}y)},\quad SIN(x,y)=\sin{(k_{0}x+l_{0}y)},\\
&B:\quad COS(x,y)=\cos{(k_{0}x)}\cos{(l_{0}y)},\quad SIN(x,y)=\sin{(k_{0}x)}\cos{(l_{0}y)},\\
&ENV(x)=\hbox{sech}(x/L_{X})SMO(x)\,,\quad ENV(y)=\hbox{sech}(y/L_{Y})SMO(y),\\
&SMO(x)=\exp{\{-\frac{x^{2}}{(P_{X}^{2}-x^{2})}\}}\,,\quad SMO(y)=\exp{\{-\frac{y^{2}}{(P_{Y}^{2}-y^{2})}\}}\,.\end{split}

(57)

COS(x,y),SIN(x,y)
are the sinusoidal functions in (
17
) of section
2
, with wavenumbers

k_{0},l_{0}
. In the KPB simulations of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
we used an initial condition in the form of case B.
This can decomposed as
A(\cos{(k_{0}x+l_{0}y)}+\cos{(k_{0}x-l_{0}y)})/2
, each generating a
wave packet. In the KPB equation only the right-going wave is followed. Hence in the present
simulations, in case A we choose an initial condition to generate a right-going wave, where

f_{0}=g_{0}/\omega_{0}
based on the solution of the linearised BL equation. In case B we retain the
initial condition
g_{0}(x,y)
for the KPB simulations of
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
, and adjust
f_{0}(x,y)

to favour a right-going wave as far as possible. We usually set
\omega_{0}\sim 1

where also
\kappa_{0}\sim 1
, and then vary
l_{0}
over the range
0\leq l_{0}\leq 0.3
,
so that
l_{0}
is of order
\epsilon^{1/2}
for comparison with the KPB equation (
16
).
Any left-going wave is absorbed upstream as if necessary we shift the initial wave location upstream,
so that the downstream wave can be followed for as long as possible. As anticipated in section
2
,

ENV(x),ENV(y)
are wave envelopes with length scales
\pi/k_{0}\ll L_{X}\ll P_{X}
,
\pi/l_{0}\ll L_{Y}\ll P_{Y}
.
When used,
SMO(x),SMO(y)
are smoothing functions designed to ensure that
f_{0},g_{0}=0

on and near the boundaries
x=\pm P_{X},y=\pm P_{Y}
. Otherwise, we put
SMO(x),SMO(y)=1
.
noting that in an infinite domain,
P_{X},P_{Y}\to\infty
,
SMO(x),SMO(y)\equiv 1
.

The solutions of the BL system (
14
) depend on the parameter
\epsilon
, the forcing parameter
\alpha
,
and the parameter set
[A,k_{0},l_{0},L_{X},L_{Y}]
in the initial conditions (
57
).
The asymptotic parameter
\epsilon
is explicitly removed from (
14
) by the rescaling

(\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{t})=\epsilon^{-1/2}(x,y,t),\,\,\hat{f}=\epsilon^{1/2}\,f,\,\,\hat{g}=\epsilon g,\,\,\hat{\zeta}=\epsilon\zeta.

(58)

Then (
14
) becomes the same equation but with
\epsilon=1
, that is, after removing
the superscript
[\hat{\cdot}]
,

\begin{split}&f_{t}=-g,\quad g_{t}=-\nabla^{2}f+[\frac{1}{3}\nabla^{2}g_{t}-g\nabla^{2}f-2f_{x}g_{x}-2f_{y}g_{y}]+\beta^{\prime}g_{xt}\,,\\
&\zeta=g-\frac{1}{2}(f_{x}^{2}+f_{y}^{2}+\nabla^{2}g)-\beta^{\prime}g_{x},\quad\beta^{\prime}=\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^{1/2}}\,.\end{split}

(59)

The effect of the parameter
\epsilon
can be recovered since if
f(x,y,t),g(x,y,t),\zeta(x,y,t)

is a solution of (
59
) then
\epsilon^{-1/2}\,f(\epsilon^{-1/2}(x,y,t))
,
\epsilon^{-1}\,g(\epsilon^{-1/2}(x,y,t))
,

\epsilon^{-1}\,\zeta(\epsilon^{-1/2}(x,y,t))
is a solution of (
14
). However, the parameter
\epsilon

has an implicit effect through the initial conditions which we discuss below.
This rescaling is similar to an inversion of the original asymptotic reduction of
Benney and Luke, 1964

described in section
2
, but it is not exactly equivalent to that; it is used here
mainly as a numerical device, with the aim of creating a smaller time for nonlinear and
dispersive effects to appear, leading to solitary wave formation. Especially note that from (
13
)

\alpha/\epsilon^{1/2}=\beta^{\prime}
and does not depend on
\epsilon
.
Likewise, the KPB equation (
16
) expressed in these rescaled variables (
58
),
is formally unchanged after again omitting
[\hat{\cdot}]
,

(2\zeta_{T}+\frac{1}{3}\zeta_{XXX}+3\,\zeta\,\zeta_{X})_{X}+\zeta_{YY}+\beta^{\prime}\zeta_{XXX}=0;\,\,X=x-t,Y=y,T=t\,.

(60)

The initial conditions (
57
) are also rescaled in the same manner;
they take the same form but in the scaled variables
[\hat{\cdot}]
. That is the parameters

[A,k_{0},l_{0},L_{X},L_{Y}]
are replaced by

\hat{A}=\epsilon A,\,\,\hat{k}_{0}=\epsilon^{1/2}k_{0},\hat{l}_{0}=\epsilon^{1/2}l_{0},\,\,\hat{L}_{X}=L_{X}\,\epsilon^{-1/2},\,\,\hat{L}_{Y}=L_{Y}\,\epsilon^{-1/2}\,.

(61)

P_{X},P_{Y}
are not rescaled and are chosen as needed for each numerical simulation.
In these rescaled variables (
61
) the initial amplitude should be small, and the initial length scale
should be long. Thus, although there is formally no
\epsilon
in (
59
) its presence is felt by the choice of
appropriate initial conditions. In the original dimensional variables we follow
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025

to find a suitable estimate of
\epsilon
, and so choose as a prototype a
5
-second wave
with frequency
\omega=2\pi/5=1.257s^{-1}
in a depth of
h=10\,m
. In the shallow water limit,
the phase speed
c=(gh)^{1/2}\,m\,s^{-1}
and so the wavenumber
\kappa=\omega/c=0.127\,m^{-1}
.
For the scaling wave amplitude we set
A_{0}=2.5\,m
, quite large, but well within the dynamical
stability range where the crest-trough amplitude ratio to the depth should be less than about
0.75
.
Using the BL scaling (
5
) these produce values of
\epsilon=A_{0}/h=0.25
,
L=\epsilon^{-1/2}h=20.0\,m
,
with initial wave amplitude, wavenumber
A=1
,
\kappa_{0}=2.54
.
Then, with the further rescaling (
58
) to get (
59
), these imply that in the
initial conditions (
57
) we set
A\sim 0.25
and
\kappa_{0}\sim 1.27
.
We vary
l_{0}
over the range
0.0-0.3
so that
k_{0},l_{0}
satisfy KP requirements, and
we choose
L_{X,Y}\sim 30-60
and note that in the envelopes
ENV(x,y)
the ratios

(x/L_{X},y/L_{Y})
are not changed by (

All the numerical results shown here use the form (

\epsilon=1
.
The results for other smaller values of
\epsilon
are easily inferred using the scaling
(
58
,
61
). As expressed above, the main aims here are to reduce the time
for nonlinear terms to take effect, and to remove the explicit dependence on
\epsilon
.
A crude simple estimate of the nonlinear terms in (
59

f,g,(x,y)
have magnitudes
F,G,L
with a nonlinear time scale
T
.
Then, since at least initially the solution will evolve with speed unity,
f_{t}=-g
implies that
F\sim GL

The nonlinear terms are estimated to have a magnitude
FG/L^{2}\sim G^{2}/L

These balance

\epsilon

\epsilon^{-1}

Using the typical values
A=0.25,\kappa_{0}=1.27
as specified above, the simulations
with envelopes
ENV(x,y)
tended to produce a strong radiation field in the domain
0<x<t

with very little sign of significant solitary wave formation in

Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025

Part of the explanation is because in the BL system (
59

linear two-way long wave equation with rather weak dispersion and nonlinearity, whereas in
the KdV, KP equations, the time evolution is for a rescaled slow time relative to the moving
coordinate
x-t

phase speed for downstream waves in the regularised BL equation (
59

c=(1+\kappa^{2}/3)^{-1/2}

and varies only in the range
0<c<1
, and there is no explicit part of the linearised (
59

which implies evolution of
\kappa

we consider the linearised BL system (
59

with no envelopes, the solution is the sinusoidal wave

(62)

5
Summary and discussion

In this paper we have examined the evolution of wind-generated shallow water waves in the framework of
a modified regularised Benney-Luke (BL) equation. The wind forcing modification is based on Miles critical level instability mechanism,
Miles, 1957
, and is analogous to our previous work on the KdV and KPB equations,
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
. A key essential difference is that the BL equation is isotropic in the two horizontal spatial variables,
x,y
, whereas the KdVB equation is uni-directional and the KPB equation is expressed in a
reference frame moving in the wind direction,
x
, with weak dispersion in the transverse
y
-direction.
The asymptotic derivation from the full Euler equations is described in section
2
and based on the
original work of
Benney and Luke, 1964
. It is based on two small parameters,
\epsilon
the ratio of wave amplitude to the undisturbed water depth, and
\beta
measuring long-wave dispersion, see (
5
).
Since we expect to see the generation of solitary waves we make the usual assumption that

\epsilon=\beta
expressing the balance between weak nonlinearity and long-wave dispersion.
The wind forcing term contains a modified Miles parameter
\beta^{\prime}

where positive/negative values lead to wave growth/decay respectively.

In subsection
3.1
we recover the cnoidal and solitary wave solutions found by
Benney and Luke, 1964
and in
subsection
3.2
we describe the asymptotic theory of wave modulations with a main focus on
the solitary wave train limit in subsection
3.3
. This theory closely follows the analogous theory
for the KPB equation in
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
, as the travelling wave solutions of the BL equation are very close
to those of the KP equation. This modulation analysis is used to give explicit estimates of wave growth/decay according as
\beta^{\prime}
is positive/negative respectively.

The main part of this paper is section
4
where we present some numerical results.
Simulations of the BL equation (
14
) are performed using a pseudo-spectral method in space
and a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method in time. The code is described in an Appendix, and was tested
using the travelling wave solutions of section
3
. The initial conditions are sinusoidal waves enclosed in a slowly varying envelope designed to produce wave packets. Two main cases are considered, (
57
):
Case A with one sinusoidal component, and Case B with two sinusoidal components,
both characterised by
x,y
wavenumbers
k_{0},l_{0}
and an amplitude
A
. The envelopes
ensure smooth decay at the boundaries, periodic in the numerical simulations.
The wind forcing is in the
x
-direction, and we consider two cases when the periodic domain
is relatively narrow or wide in the
y
-direction.

Many simulations were performed, and we present here only a small representative sample.
Our focus is on solitary wave trains and we find that they are eventually formed over large time scales
due to the weak nonlinearity and weak dispersion in the modified BL equation. With no wind forcing,
in the narrow domain case we find that small initial wavenumbers and a large initial amplitude
provide the most favourable conditions for the formation of solitary waves. When wind forcing is included,
in the narrow domain case the wave amplitudes grow/decay in agreement with the predictions from
the modulation theory, similar to our study of the KPB equation in
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
. But in the wide
domain case,
y
-dependent dispersion dominates leading to curved wave fronts and delaying,
even preventing, the formation of solitary waves.

Although the BL equation is isotropic in the horizontal spatial variables
x,y
, a property
inherited from the full Euler equations, it is limited numerically because at leading order it is
the linear two-dimensional long wave equation and, as described in section
4
, it takes a long time,
of order
\epsilon^{-1}
, for nonlinearity and dispersion to take effect and then produce solitary waves.
Unlike the asymptotic reductions to KdV, KP models which evolve on a slow time scale relative to the moving coordinate
x-t
, in a BL model the solution must numerically first follow this moving coordinate
before evolving. Another feature here is that the BL equation we study is regularised, so that
there is no spurious high wavenumber instability. This is absolutely necessary for numerical
simulations but has the effect here that the high wavenumber dispersion needed to prevent
nonlinear steepening is somewhat inhibited. Both these features persist even when
we use the rescaled form (
59
) where formally
\epsilon=1
. Nevertheless, the
regularised BL equation, with or without wind forcing, is a useful tool to study two
space-dimensional shallow water waves. Possible improvements and extensions
include going to the next
\epsilon
term in the BL derivation.

Operational wind wave forecasting is typically based on the Hasselmann equations
for the wave spectrum, and were developed essentially for deep-water waves.
Although operational adjustments can be and are made, they are not totally suitable for
wind waves in shallow water, where the evidence from works such as
Costa et al., 2014
and
our work in this paper and in
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2024b
;
Maleewong and Grimshaw, 2025
is that a model describing a soliton gas is needed.
Whether a model such as this BL system, or a similar Boussinesq-type model,
can be used for operational forecasts remains a challenge. We note that
Onorato et al., 2009

developed a kinetic theory of the classical Boussinesq equations and compared that
with the shallow water limit of the Hasselmann equation, with promising results.
Although some issues about resonances in shallow water remain, this would seem to be a
promising direction for future research. We suggest that an analogous kinetic theory of the
regularised BL equation might be fruitful.

Appendix: Numerical scheme

From the BL equation written in the system form (
14
),

f_{t}=-g,\quad g_{t}=-\nabla^{2}f+\epsilon[\frac{1}{3}\nabla^{2}g_{t}-g\nabla^{2}f-2f_{x}g_{x}-2f_{y}g_{y}]+\alpha g_{xt}\,.

(65)

For
g_{t}
we schematically factorise the operator,

g_{t}=\frac{1}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}\{-\nabla^{2}f-\epsilon\left[g\nabla^{2}f+2f_{x}g_{x}+2f_{y}g_{y}\right]\}

(66)

Then define

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-g:=F_{1}(f,g,t)

(67)

\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}\left\{-\nabla^{2}f-\epsilon\left[g\nabla^{2}f+2f_{x}g_{x}+2f_{y}g_{y}\right]\right\}:=F_{2}(f,g,t)

(68)

These are two first-order ordinary differential equations for
f,g
with initial conditions
f(x,y,0)
and
g(x,y,0)
. These equations will be solved numerically by the fourth order Runge-Kutta method in time
pseudo spectral method in space. We assume periodic boundary conditions based on the Fourier basis.
Let
\mathcal{F}(f)
denotes the two-dimensional Fourier transform of
f
,
\mathcal{F}(f)=\hat{f}(k_{x},k_{y},t)
where
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
are wavenumbers in Fourier space. Similarly,

\mathcal{F}(g)=\hat{g}(k_{x},k_{y},t)
.
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{g})

is the inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{g}
.
Then we approximate the derivative and linear operation for each term in (
68
) using the Fourier transform, then transforming back to the physical space to apply the product of two terms as follows.

First term: define
\mathcal{F}(\nabla^{2})=-(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})=-\kappa^{2}
,

\mathcal{F}\left(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}f\right)=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{f}
, so

-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{f}\right)

Note that the denominator is well behaved, and is not zero for all wavenumbers.

Second term:
\nabla^{2}f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(-\kappa^{2}\hat{f}):=a_{1}
, let
b_{1}=ga_{1}
, then find

\mathcal{F}\left(-\frac{\epsilon}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}b_{1}\right)=-\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{b}_{1}

where
\hat{b}_{1}
is the Fourier transform of the product of
g
and
a_{1}
. Then

-\frac{\epsilon}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}(g\nabla^{2}f)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(-\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{b}_{1}\right)

Third term: Let
f_{x}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(ik_{x}\hat{f}\right):=a_{2}
and
g_{x}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(ik_{x}\hat{g}\right):=b_{2}

-\frac{2\epsilon}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}(f_{x}g_{x})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(-\frac{2\epsilon}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{c}_{2}\right)

where
\hat{c}_{2}
is the Fourier transform of the product of
a_{2}
and
b_{2}
.

Fourth term: Let
f_{y}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(ik_{y}\hat{f}\right):=a_{3}
and
g_{y}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(ik_{y}\hat{g}\right):=b_{3}

-\frac{2\epsilon}{(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\nabla^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x})}(f_{y}g_{y})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(-\frac{2\epsilon}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\kappa^{2}-\alpha ik_{x})}\hat{c}_{3}\right)

where
\hat{c}_{3}
is the Fourier transform of the product of
a_{3}
and
b_{3}
.

Thus all terms on the right- hand side of (
68
) are evaluated. Then we solve this
ordinary differential equation system to obtain
f
and
g
at the next time step using the
fourth-order Runge Kutta method. The surface elevation
\zeta
is found using (
59
).
The boundary conditions in both
x
and
y
domains are periodic in this Fourier basis.
To absorb radiating waves moving to the far field near the boundaries while maintaining periodicity
we use a sponge layer technique. The sponge layer function in the
x
direction is defined by

\text{spg}(x)=\frac{\nu}{2}\left[1+\tanh(\kappa_{s}(x-x_{m}))+(1-\tanh(\kappa(x+x_{m})))\right]

(69)

where
x_{m}=w_{d}P_{X}\pi/2
,
w_{d}
controls the width of sponge layer from the ends of boundaries,

\nu
is the magnitude of the sponge layer, and
\kappa_{s}
controls the smoothness.
The sponge layer in the
y
direction,
\text{spg}(y)
is defined similarly.
The sponge layer in the
x
direction is applied to
f,g
in the physical space by

f(x,y,t+dt)=f(1-\text{spg}(x)),\,g(x,y,t+dt)=g(1-\text{spg}(x))\,.

(70)

Finally,
f(x,y,t+dt),g(x,y,t+dt)
are transformed back into the Fourier space again to update
\hat{f},\hat{g}
for the next time step. In this work, we usually set
\nu=1,\kappa_{s}=0.4
and
w_{d}=1.85
.
