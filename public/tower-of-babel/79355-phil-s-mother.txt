CHAPTER I.

"SOMETHING HAPPENS" TO PHIL.

[Illustration] "I SAY, Phil, you'd best get out of the way. Your
mother's coming."

No need to utter the warning twice. Phil, a ragged, yellow-haired
boy, picked himself up from the doorstep in a moment, signified his
gratitude to Fan the flower-girl by an expressive nod and wink, and
scuttled down the narrow alley, and was out of sight with all the speed
his thin bare legs could accomplish.

Moll Dutton was approaching from the other end of the alley. A coarse,
repulsive-looking woman, wearing torn and draggled garments, and
carrying a huge bundle in her arms, she came staggering along, the
stagger resulting, not from her burden, since the bundle, though
cumbersome, was not very heavy, but from the glass or two to which she
had treated herself on the strength of the excellent bargain in old
clothes which she had been able to effect. Her low, receding forehead,
flat face, narrow, cunning eyes, and cruel mouth told all too plainly
the history of her life and character.

The dwellers in that dim, narrow alley, where scarce six feet of
paving-stone separated the houses on one side from the houses on the
other, had little to commend them. Integrity and uprightness could
not be attributed to any, but there were varying degrees of evil in
the sinful, sorrowful lives lived there. Amongst all, no one was more
hardened in sin, more relentless in cruelty, more lost to all true
womanly feeling, than was Moll Dutton.

"Phil, Phil!" she shouted, as she staggered to the door of the house
in which she occupied a room. "Where's that good-for-nothing young
rascal? Where's he hiding, the lazy imp? He'd better make haste to show
himself, or I'll break every bone in his body when I do find him!"

"Phil's away about his business," said Fan, leaning from an upper
window opposite, whence she could address Moll with impunity. "It's no
use calling to him."

"Business, indeed! The young lazybones!" returned Moll angrily. "It's
little enough business he does. He brought me only a few coppers
yesterday. There's no teaching that boy anything. What's the good
of his blue eyes and his baby face, if he can't make use of them to
wheedle folks into giving?" So saying, she passed into the house.

Phil had indeed gone about his business, with a sudden awakening to a
sense of duty at the news of his mother's approach. He did not like
his "business," which, as the reader will have guessed, was neither
more nor less than begging. Moll had done her best to teach him to
cry and whine and tell untruths calculated to touch the hearts of the
charitable, but she was not pleased with the result of her endeavours.
Phil showed no aptitude for his irregular profession. He succeeded best
when most passive, for a wistful, pleading glance from the childish
blue eyes was more eloquent than the would-be pitiful stories which he
could never utter so glibly as Moll tried to make him.

The alley in which Phil lived lay midway between Lincoln's Inn Fields
and High Holborn. Hurrying from it, he made his way by many a short cut
into Oxford Street. It was time he began to beg, if he would escape a
severe beating from his mother when the day was over.

The short November day was already drawing to its close. A cold drizzle
filled the air, the roads were slushy, the pavements greasy; it was
not weather in which people were disposed to linger. City men, closely
buttoned in the thickest overcoats, were hastening westwards, eager
to gain the shelter and comfort of their homes. Ladies passed by, too
intent upon caring for their skirts and picking their way through the
mud to give a single glance at the white, thin face of the six years
old lad who pleaded for a copper, saying that he had eaten nothing that
day.

Phil began to feel rather hopeless, as one after another turned a deaf
ear to his appeals or pushed aside his uplifted palm. Presently an
old gentleman, short of stature, and very trim in appearance, came in
sight, who took his way through the mud with much caution, and seemed
to have a keen perception of all that passed before his eyes. Phil's
quick instincts warned him that this old gentleman was not one who
could be easily imposed upon, nor one who would too readily part with
his money. Yet he begged of him, though with little hope of success.

At the sound of the boy's voice, the old gentleman stopped short and
surveyed Phil with his shrewd grey eyes. Phil's words faltered, as
beneath their stern gaze, he tried to tell his tale.

"Mother dead, is she? Got no one to take care of you, and have eaten
nothing to-day, eh?" he said, repeating the details of Phil's story in
cold, ironical tones. "My boy, I don't believe a word of it! Now look
at me and tell me the truth; you're too young to have quite forgotten
how to speak it, and I shall know if you tell me a lie. Your mother is
living, isn't she, and she has made up this story for you and sent you
out to beg? It is so, is it not?"

"Yes, sir," faltered Phil, surprised into speaking the very truth,
"but—but—I wish she were dead."

"What!" exclaimed the gentleman, startled and shocked. "You wish that
your mother were dead! Do you know what you are saying?"

"Yes," said Phil stolidly; "I wish that she were dead. She knocks me
about awful sometimes. I do wish that she were dead."

His words but deepened the old gentleman's horror. "You bad boy! You
hard-hearted little wretch!" he exclaimed. "I have always detested
boys, but you are the worst I ever did meet with. Calmly wishing that
his own mother were dead!"

And, raising his hands in horrified protest, the gentleman passed on,
more convinced than before of the enormity of boy-nature. He was a
just and worthy man, the son of a good mother, the husband of a good
wife, but his mind and sympathies were somewhat narrow, and he had not
sufficient imagination to conceive of such a mother as Moll Dutton.

"The boy must be thoroughly bad, to speak of his mother so," he said to
himself.

Phil stood looking after him in bewilderment. He was disappointed at
missing the penny of which he had felt certain when the gentleman
paused to speak to him. He made mental note of the effect produced by
his truth-speaking, and resolved that, if ever again forced to admit
that his mother was living, he would refrain from stating that the fact
was not in accordance with his wishes.

Phil shivered as he looked at his scanty store of coppers. His ragged
garments were wet through, but he did not dare to return home till he
had gained some more pence. Seeing a policeman near, he slunk away,
resolved to try his fortune in a fresh field.

Hurrying along through the narrow dingy streets he knew so well, Phil
soon reached the Strand. The carriages drawn up at the entrance to one
of the theatres made him aware that an afternoon performance was taking
place, and that the audience might soon be expected to disperse. Here
was an opportunity worth waiting for, Phil thought. He stole beneath
the portico, and sat down in the shadow of one of the heavy pillars.

Waiting is a trial of patience at the best of times, and Phil,
shivering in the raw evening air, found it wearisome indeed. He was
hungry too, and a dull weight of misery lay upon his childish heart.
There seemed small chance of his escaping a beating from his mother
that night. He turned up one of his ragged coat-sleeves, and looked
ruefully at certain dark bruises which appeared on his thin little arm.

"I hate her," he said, half aloud; "I do wish that she were dead! Why
shouldn't I? I'd be glad if something 'ud happen to her. If she was to
be found dead in her bed, like old Toiler, I'd be real glad, I would."

Looking at the boy's innocent, childish face, his blue eyes, his yellow
curls, no one would have judged him capable of uttering such unnatural
sentiments. Yet was he to blame if for him the sacred name of mother
had no charm, conveying no sense of protecting love and comforting
tenderness? No motherless child had known a sorer lack of love than
Phil.

Yet he knew that love existed in the sad, dark world into which he had
been born, for the boy's heart cherished a dim memory of a day far back
in the past, when a pale-faced, sweet-voiced woman had come to his
dismal home, a woman who had taken him on to her lap and pressed him
to her bosom, whilst she stroked his curls, and called him "Phil" in
tones which made his name sound something quite new and pleasant. He
remembered the sweet golden butter and the eggs she had brought, and,
what was better, the bunch of roses, which he had kept till they were
brown and dead. Never had Phil tasted such food as he enjoyed that day
seated on the stranger's lap.

A tear had splashed on his cheek as he ate. And looking up, he saw that
she was crying, and wondered why, though the fact had no power to break
his deep content at the moment. He had asked her what was her name, and
she had answered "Lizzie," and told him that he might call her "Aunt
Lizzie," if he liked.

All too soon his mother had come upon the scene, and then the gentle
stranger went away, and Phil had never seen her since, though he had
often dreamed of her.

Something had recalled this one bright spot of memory to Phil's mind
now, as he lingered at the door of the theatre.

"I wish she would come again," he thought, "but that won't never be. If
only something might happen, so that I need not go home any more!"

But he was roused from these unprofitable musings by the bustle which
announced that the play was over. As the people poured out through
the open doors, he made his appeal to one and another with all the
effect he could command. But the opportunity was not so good as he
had imagined. Coming from the warm, bright house into the chill, damp
night, every one was intent upon getting home as speedily as possible,
and Phil found none to listen to him. He was roughly pushed aside
by those who were struggling to secure cab or omnibus. Phil was not
alarmed by the crowding and pushing. He dodged in and out amongst the
people, diving under the heads of horses or creeping behind the wheels
of carriages in his endeavours to follow up those who seemed most
likely to be moved by compassion.

"Now then, boy, don't stand there begging, but be of use if you can,"
an excitable old lady charged him. "I'll give you twopence if you can
get me a cab. See, there is one over the way. Run, quick!"

Phil needed no urging. In another moment he was in the road, fearlessly
running between the passing vehicles. But ere the boy could reach the
other side, the cab was in motion, a signal from some one else having
attracted the driver's attention. Quick as thought, Phil doubled back,
hoping to arrest it midway as it crossed the road. Intent upon his
object, he forgot the dangers of the crowded thoroughfare.

A shout from behind startled him. He looked round, and saw two
prancing, high-bred horses just upon him. Phil darted back, but,
alas! he but escaped one peril to fall into another. The shaft of a
passing cab struck him, and the shock threw him on the ground right in
front of a heavily-laden van drawn by two powerful horses, which were
being driven at a smart pace. There was no time to draw them in—no
possibility of preventing what happened. An agonizing cry rang out on
the air as over Phil's body rolled the heavy wheel.

The usual crowd gathered. A policeman came forward and took charge of
the case. Under his directions, Phil's crushed form was lifted from the
ground and conveyed to the nearest hospital.

"What is it all about?" some people were asking, as they watched the
gathering crowd from the shelter of the theatre portico.

"Only a little boy run over. Such accidents happen every day; and it
is a wonder there are not more of them. Killed, is he? Ah, poor little
fellow! It is a shame that children are allowed to run about so in
London."

And thus dismissing from their minds the commonplace occurrence, the
play-goers went their way, and the stream of traffic flowed on as
before along the Strand. The old lady who had sent Phil on his fatal
errand had secured another cab almost immediately, and had driven off
without caring to remember that she had bade the boy fetch her one.
Perhaps she would have felt some twinges of self-reproach had she known
what befell him.

Poor little Phil! His wish had found a terrible fulfilment. Something
had indeed happened to prevent him from returning to his unhappy home.

CHAPTER II.

IN THE HOSPITAL.

"WILL he live?"

"Impossible to say. His injuries are very complicated. The thighbone
being so crushed, there will be nothing for it but amputation, and he
will hardly stand that, I fear."

"Poor little fellow!" said Sister Kathleen, as she looked upon Phil
lying in his hospital bed. His tiny face, utterly colourless, was sad
to see, as every now and then it quivered, whilst he uttered a sharp,
agonizing cry. "He must suffer terribly."

"Not so much as you suppose. He is unconscious. Those screams result
from the shock to the brain. He will need close attention to-night."

"I will take charge of him myself," said Sister Kathleen; "he shall
have every chance of recovering, poor little fellow."

"I see the case interests you," said the surgeon; "and if you look
after him, he will have every chance. Well, I don't despair of him. We
have brought complications as bad to a successful issue. The worst of
it is, these poor little beggars have as a rule no constitution to go
upon. He looks as if he had been half-starved all his life."

"Ah, and used to hard blows too," said the hospital Sister; "I saw
that there were bruises on his poor little arms when we undressed him.
Perhaps it would be a mercy for him if he did not live, poor child."

The surgeon shrugged his shoulders as he turned away.

Sister Kathleen sighed as she followed him to the next bed.

A tender heart had Sister Kathleen, but not too tender for her calling,
since its tenderness was counterbalanced by sterner qualities, by
strong resolution, self-control, and unflinching courage. She was a
woman of middle stature, strongly built, and quick, healthful energy
characterized her words and actions. She had strongly-marked features
of the Roman type, and steady, dark eyes. She was emphatically a woman
of powerful yet pleasing presence. Her clear, full voice, never raised
high, had power not to charm only, but to overawe and subdue. No nurse
or patient in the wards of which she had the oversight ever dared to
dispute a command from Sister Kathleen. The doctors knew well the worth
of this Sister, and were glad to secure her help in any complicated and
difficult case.

Sister Kathleen kept watch beside Phil's bed that night. He was
restless and feverish, and at times uttered piercing screams, and wild,
delirious words.

"Mother, mother, don't beat me!" he cried. "I couldn't help it; they
wouldn't give me more. Oh, mother! Mother!" Then, in lower tones, "Aunt
Lizzie! If only Aunt Lizzie would come!"

Sister Kathleen laid her cool hand on the child's forehead.

A quieter look came to his face. He murmured, "Aunt Lizzie," and
presently he sank into a doze.

With the morning, Phil woke to full consciousness of his pain. The blue
eyes, looking up bewildered, met the kind gaze of Sister Kathleen. The
bewilderment seemed to deepen for a moment, then his brow cleared, and
with the air of one who has solved a difficulty, he said quietly, "Aunt
Lizzie?"

"No," said the Sister, in her clear, decided tones; "it is not Aunt
Lizzie. I am Sister Kathleen."

A perplexed, troubled look came to the child's face.

"You wonder where you are," said Sister Kathleen, "but don't be afraid.
You are in a hospital. You were run over in the street yesterday, and
they brought you here."

A sharp scream interrupted her. Phil had tried to move, and finding it
impossible, he screamed again and again.

"Yes, yes, you are in great pain, I know," said Sister Kathleen
tenderly, "but you must keep quiet. You must try to be patient."

Phil had not learned to practise patience. It was his habit to kick,
struggle, and scream, and as far as possible defend himself under any
form of suffering. He screamed more loudly than before, and tried to
tear off the bandages that restrained his movements. The Sister had to
call a nurse to her assistance, and together they strapped Phil's arms
to his sides, so as to prevent his doing himself any injury.

Sister Kathleen bent over the little sufferer, and her kind dark eyes
looked into his. Her calm gaze had a soothing influence, and he ceased
to scream.

"What is your name, my child?" she asked.

"Phil," was his reply.

"Phil," she repeated; "that is short for Philip, I suppose. What is
your other name?"

"Ain't got no other," he said; "nobody calls me nothink but Phil."

"But your father has another name? You have a father?"

"No," said Phil, "I ain't. I've got a mother, though," he added, with a
heavy sigh.

"Well, what is your mother's name?"

"Oh, they calls her Moll Dutton," said the boy, his brow contracting
with pain.

"Then your name is Philip Dutton," said the Sister. "Now can you tell
me where you live?"

"In Charles Alley," said the boy; "it's Holborn way."

"Cannot you give me any clearer direction?" she asked. "Well, never
mind; I daresay we can find your mother by that. We will send and tell
her that you are here, for I'm sure she must be very uneasy about you."

"Oh, don't!" cried Phil, looking imploringly at her. "Don't send. I
don't want mother to come here. I'd rather she did not know where I am."

"Whatever next!" said Sister Kathleen, with a smile. "Of course your
mother must be told. Are you afraid that she will be angry with you for
getting into trouble? You need not fear that. I am sure she will be
only too glad to find you living."

"But I don't want to see her. I don't want her to come!" cried Phil,
and he began to scream anew.

"Poor boy, the pain makes him fractious," said Sister Kathleen, as she
left him in the care of a nurse, and went away to take the rest she
needed.


When she saw Phil again, some hours later, he was sleeping under the
influence of an anæsthetic. She looked with tender interest at the
little white face and the golden curls which clustered so thickly over
his brows. He was a pretty boy, she thought, although his face was
marred by want and suffering.

But Sister Kathleen could not linger by his bed then, nor could she
see much of him on the following day, for it was operation day, and a
critical case claimed her attention. She came to look at him in the
evening, however.

It was the hour at which the patients' friends were permitted to visit
them, and as she approached Phil's bed, she saw a woman seated by his
side. She was a young-looking woman, and could hardly have been more
than seven-and-twenty, if so old, but her pale face had a singularly
sad expression, a look of restrained, hopeless grief, which marked her
as one to whom sorrow was no occasional visitant but a daily companion.
She was dressed in neat but homely fashion—a dark straw bonnet,
severely plain, a grey waterproof cloak, strong boots, and woollen
gloves; not at all the kind of attire Phil's mother might be expected
to wear.

But Sister Kathleen did not wait to weigh probabilities. She saw the
sorrowful tenderness of the woman's look as she bent over Phil, the
tears in her eyes, and the dawning comfort on the boy's face as he met
her loving gaze. The Sister laid her hand on the woman's arm, and said,
in a tone of conviction, "You are his mother."

The woman started up, and a low nervous cry escaped her. Her colour
fled utterly. She was white to the very lips as she gazed at Sister
Kathleen with eyes wide-opened, apparently too startled to speak. But
ere there was time to wonder at her silence, a nurse standing by spoke.

"You make a mistake, Sister," she said. "This is not Phil's mother. She
is his aunt."

"Yes," said the woman, finding words at last, but speaking in
tremulous, agitated tones; "I am his aunt—his Aunt Lizzie."

"Ah, then I have heard of you," said Sister Kathleen kindly. "Pray sit
down. The sight of him has distressed you. You are little accustomed to
illness, I daresay?"

The woman murmured an assent as she sat down. But almost immediately
she rose again, in spite of a cry of distress from Phil, and said
hurriedly, as she looked at her watch, "I must be going. I have to
catch a train."

Then, turning to the Sister, she said, with an imploring glance, "May I
speak to you for a minute before I go?"

The pathetic glance of the blue eyes, eyes so like Phil's, went to
Sister Kathleen's heart.

"Certainly," she answered; "come with me."

And she led the way to a small private room opening from the ward.

Phil's aunt stayed only to press some tender, clinging kisses on his
wan lips, then quickly followed the Sister.

"Tell me," she said breathlessly, as soon as they were alone, "is he
much hurt? Will he live?"

"He is very badly hurt," said Sister Kathleen gravely; "several ribs
are displaced, and his thigh sadly crushed. I am grieved to give you
pain, but I fear—I know, indeed, that the surgeons think it necessary
to amputate the limb."

A shiver ran through the aunt's frame. She sank on to a chair, and
turned so white that the Sister moved hastily to her side, fearing she
was about to faint. The woman looked at her with anguish in her glance,
and repeated the question, "Will he live?"

"I hope so," was all Sister Kathleen could say, "but I must not conceal
from you that it is a highly critical case. As far as I can judge, the
chances of life or death are pretty equally balanced. He is terribly
injured, and there are many things against him. Still it is wonderful
what children pull through."

Phil's aunt shivered again, and murmured something that the Sister
could not quite hear, but she fancied she caught the words, "God help
me!"

"You must try to hope for the best," she said gently. "We will do all
in our power for little Phil; you may be sure of that."

The young woman was silent for a few moments. Then she said, in low,
unsteady tones, "But if he lives, he will be a cripple."

"Yes," said Sister Kathleen; "he will be a cripple."

There was the sound of a stifled sob as Phil's aunt rose and hurried to
the door. "I must go," she said brokenly; "I dare not stay longer."

"Have you far to go?" asked the Sister.

"Yes, it is some way. I live in the country, and I came up on purpose
to see Phil. I did not know till this morning that he was here."

Sister Kathleen looked at her in wonder. It was not surprising to hear
that she was from the country. The Sister had felt sure of that, as
she observed the speech and bearing of Phil's aunt. She wondered to
think that little Phil, ragged and wretched as had been his appearance
when brought into the hospital, should have a relative so refined and
respectable as this woman seemed to be. Sister Kathleen accompanied the
stranger to the landing, and stood watching her slight, drooping figure
as she passed down the broad stone staircase.

"So that was not Phil's mother!" the Sister said, as she re-entered the
ward, addressing the nurse in charge.

"No, indeed," she replied; "his mother came last night. You should have
seen her! A ragged, dirty tramp, half-tipsy, and not fit to enter the
ward. I never saw a more dreadful-looking woman."

"And this one was so different," said the Sister.

"Yes; I don't wonder you took her for Phil's mother, for he could not
be more like her if he were her child."

"Poor little boy!" said Sister Kathleen. Then with a change of voice
she added, "I hope he will pull through. I have set my heart on his
recovery."

The nurse wondered why.

CHAPTER III.

PHIL RECEIVES TWO VISITORS.

THERE were circumstances attending Phil's case that made it one of
peculiar interest to the staff of surgeons at the hospital. To save
the little lad's life would be a triumph of surgical skill, and they
were very anxious to win this triumph. Their efforts, aided by Sister
Kathleen's utmost care, were successful. Phil's constitution, enfeebled
as it was by want and ill-usage, bore the shock of the amputation,
though for some hours afterwards only the faintest hope of his recovery
could be entertained. With less careful nursing, he must have slipped
away in his extreme exhaustion. Humanly speaking, it was to Sister
Kathleen he owed his life.

All immediate danger was over. The boy's pulse was good, his skin cool,
and he was sleeping. Without anxiety Sister Kathleen could consign him
to the care of a nurse.

"Keep him as quiet as you can, and let me know when he wakes," she said.

Then she went away to take her rest, and was soon sleeping soundly in
her own room.


Some hours later she was roused by the nurse. "I am sorry to wake you,
Sister," she said, "but what are we to do? Phil's mother is here, and
she wants to see him. She says that she has been refused twice, but she
will see him to-day."

"How is he?" asked the Sister, wide awake in a moment.

"Very well, and still sleeping. He woke once, and I gave him a little
milk, and then he fell asleep again."

"I will come and speak to the woman," said Sister Kathleen, rising at
once.

In the ante-room at the end of the ward she found Moll Dutton, standing
with her arms akimbo and her head thrown back, her whole attitude
expressive of defiance. She was sober, and had made some attempt
at tidying herself for this visit, without, however, electing any
perceptible improvement in her bold, repulsive appearance.

"I've come to see my boy," she was saying, in harsh, defiant tones, as
the Sister entered, "and I'll see him before I go, if I stay here all
night. What right have you to come between a mother and her child? How
could the sight of his own mother harm him? Ain't he a-hankering for
his mother, don't you think?"

Moll's aspect softened somewhat as Sister Kathleen Appeared. And the
nurses, who had been listening with some amusement to the woman's
tirade, suddenly dispersed to their various duties.

"You'll let me see my boy, good lady?" said Moll in a cringing tone,
as she dropped a curtsey. "They told me down-stairs that they thought
you would let me see him to-day. You'll have some pity for a mother's
feelings, and not send me away again without a sight of him?"

"You shall see him," said Sister Kathleen, "but I must ask you to be
content with merely looking at him to-day. He is sleeping, and I do not
wish him to be startled or disturbed."

"Surely the sight of his own mother would not startle him," said Moll,
with a cunning glance out of her narrow eyes.

But the calm, searching gaze she met made her look down uneasily, as
though conscious of the falsity of her words.

Without further parley, Sister Kathleen led the way to Phil's bed. It
was screened off from the rest of the ward, and not till they passed
round the screen could they see anything of the bed or its occupant.
Phil's sleep, as it happened, was over. He had but just opened his
eyes, and was resting them with pleasure on the bright-coloured screen,
when suddenly, at its farther end, beyond the foot of his bed, appeared
the hated form of his mother.

Instantly Phil gave a shrill scream. It seemed less fear than anger
that moved him as he cried passionately, with all the strength he could
muster, "Go away, you nasty old thing! Go away! I don't want to see
you. Sister, Sister, don't let mother come near me. I hate her! I hate
the very sight of her!"

"Highty-tighty!" exclaimed Moll, reddening with annoyance.
"Highty-tighty, young Phil! This is pretty behaviour. Is this how you
speak to your poor mother, who has been waiting about for the last
hour, and has missed her dinner in order to see you? It's well you are
where you are, or you wouldn't dare to speak to me so."

"I don't care," cried Phil wildly; "I never want to see you again. I
wish you were not my mother. Oh, Sister, do send her away!"

"Well, to be sure! You're a nice child!" said Moll, with a coarse
laugh, trying to hide her discomposure.

But now Sister Kathleen interposed. She felt that for the sake of Phil,
whom this agitation could not fail to harm, she must do as he bade her
and send the woman away.

"Go," she said to Moll; "go at once. If you wait outside, I will come
and speak to you presently."

Moll dared not disobey. She went without a word.

Sister Kathleen bent over the little boy and spoke to him with soothing
tenderness.

"Why, Phil, Phil!" she said. "What does this mean? How could you speak
to your mother so?"

"I hate her," cried the boy, between his sobs; "she is a horrid bad
woman. I wish she were dead!"

"Oh, hush, hush!" said the Sister. "This is dreadful! Your life must
have been very unhappy, my poor little Phil, that you should feel thus
towards your mother. But now you must not cry any more."

"You won't let her come again?" he asked, with a nervous shiver.

"No, no, she shall not come near you again. I promise you that."

Phil continued to cry, but less violently. And soon Sister Kathleen was
able to leave him and go in search of his mother.

Too brazen to be long abashed by what had passed, Moll was disposed to
be insolent and self-assertive.

"You might have done me a better turn than to set my child against me,"
she said. "A nice welcome, truly, to give his mother!"

"No one has set him against you," said Sister Kathleen, with righteous
indignation in tone and look. "You know best what it is that causes him
to shrink from you in such fear and dislike. A good mother could never
be so regarded by her child."

For a moment Moll looked guilty and cowed. Then, with a flash of fresh
defiance in her glance, she exclaimed, "Curse him! He was always a
black-hearted little scoundrel, was that young Phil! He has been the
plague of my life. And now they tell me that the doctors have cut off
his leg, and he'll be a cripple for the rest of his days."

"Yes, it is true," said Sister Kathleen, with a sigh; "he will be a
cripple, poor child!"

"Poor child and poor mother, I think," said Moll, in a false, whining
tone, with an evil look in her narrow eyes; "a helpless lad is a sore
burden for a poor woman like me. Well, well, I'll go, since I see you
would rather have my room than my company. I sha'n't be in a hurry to
come again to see my beautiful boy, I can tell you."

"I shall be obliged to forbid your seeing Phil for the present," said
Sister Kathleen; "so it would be of little use your coming."

Muttering angrily, Moll took her departure. The Sister felt as if
she could breathe more freely when relieved of her presence. "Oh, to
think," she said to herself, "that that woman was once a little child,
and to see what she now is—a monster rather than a woman, so hardened
in evil, dead even to the common instincts of womanhood, so utterly a
lost soul!"


Phil had another visitor ere the day was over. Sister Kathleen met her
on the stairs as she came wearily up the long flight to the ward where
Phil lay. The Sister had wondered that she had neither seen nor heard
anything more of Phil's aunt. And here she was, looking if possible
paler and more unhappy than before, with such a look of hopeless sorrow
in her dreamy blue eyes, that Sister Kathleen, eager to give her
comfort, said at once, "Ah, you are troubled about little Phil, but do
not be afraid. He is doing well, and there is good cause to hope that
his life will be spared."

But the woman uttered a low, despairing cry, and such a trembling
seized her that she caught at the bannisters to save herself from
falling.

"Live will he live?" she said in faltering tones. "O God! I have been
praying that he might die!"

"Oh no!" said Sister Kathleen, startled by her words. "You do not know
what you are saying; you are overwrought. Come with me; you must rest
in my room ere you see Phil."

So saying, she took her by the hand and led her from the public
staircase, where nurses and medical students were passing up and down.

Alone with Sister Kathleen in her room, the young woman's composure
completely gave way. Sinking on to a chair, she hid her face in her
hands and sobbed with utter abandonment.

"It is true," she cried, between her sobs; "I have hoped that Phil
would die. I have been praying for it every day since I was here. Would
it not be well if God in His mercy would take him to heaven? Think what
a wretched life Phil has led with that woman. And what will it be in
the future, if he is a cripple? Do you wonder I prayed that he might
die? Would it not be better for him? Oh yes, it would be infinitely
better for poor little Phil!"

"It may seem so to you," said Sister Kathleen, "but how can we know
what will be good for any one? Cannot you trust God to do what is best
for little Phil? Not a sparrow falls to the ground without our Father,
and is not this dear child of more value than many sparrows? The Love
that sacrificed its own life to redeem the world cannot be indifferent
to the suffering of any one child of man. Life is full of sorrow and
darkness, I know; but there is light, there is comfort, there is
healing for its woes in the cross of Christ."

But the sorrowing woman appeared not to heed the words. She was calmer
now, but her contracted brow and quivering lips showed that her mind
still dwelt on painful thoughts.

"He will be a cripple," she said presently, uttering her thought aloud;
"he will be more at her mercy than ever."

"Is it inevitable that he should return to her?" asked Sister Kathleen.
"Could any other arrangement be made for him, I wonder? Perhaps if we
think it over, we can hit upon some plan."

A gleam of comfort came into the young woman's eyes, but quickly her
face clouded again, and she said mournfully, "She would never give him
up; he is her child. She has taught him to beg for her, I believe. She
would never give him up."

"You think not?" said Sister Kathleen. "Well, it is of no use talking
further of it now. Phil will not be able to leave his bed for some time
yet; he is likely to be with us many weeks. We will go to him if you
feel well enough, Miss —. Excuse me, I do not know your name."

The indirect question brought a deep flush into the young woman's
face. She hesitated. There was a perceptible pause ere she replied, in
nervous, tremulous tones, "My name is Austen—Lizzie Austen. I am quite
well now."

"Then we will go to Phil, Miss Austen," said the Sister briskly. "Am
I right in addressing you as 'Miss' Austen? You are not married, I
suppose?"

"No, oh no!" was the hurried reply, given with a gasping, half-choked
utterance.

Sister Kathleen turned to look at her. How white and frail this Miss
Austen looked!

"I can let you see him for a few minutes," said Sister Kathleen, as she
led the way to the ward. "I know he will have a welcome for you, though
he screamed at the sight of his mother, and begged me to send her away."

Miss Austen responded only by a heavy sigh.

A few moments later, she was seated beside Phil's bed.

There could be no doubt that he was glad to see her. Sister Kathleen
was pleased to see the radiant smile with which he welcomed his aunt,
and it was good too to see his delight when she opened her basket and
lifted out a lovely bunch of hothouse grapes.

There were tears in Aunt Lizzie's eyes as she put grape after grape to
his eager lips. How delicious was the cool fruit to his parched little
mouth!

Nor did the great bunch of chrysanthemums which Aunt Lizzie had brought
with her give him less pleasure. She spread out the flowers before
him, and he examined them with delight. There were some of the finest
specimens of chrysanthemum culture—great yellow balls, and balls which
might have been of snow, so spotless was their whiteness, flowers of
deep crimson or of golden brown, and some whose paleness was flecked by
the faintest touches of colour. What pleasure it was to Phil to turn
them over and compare them, and try to decide which of all this great
variety he liked best!

He clung to his aunt, and made a feeble effort to hold her when she
spoke of going.

"If only you would stay with me always, Aunt Lizzie, you and Sister!"
he cried. "I want no one else. I would like you and Sister always to be
with me, and mother never to come near me any more."

CHAPTER IV.

PHIL IN HIS "TANTRUMS."

PHIL continued to make progress in the days that followed, though but
slowly. Since there was no longer cause for anxiety on his account,
Sister Kathleen gave him less of her attention. Other and more critical
cases claimed her care, but she did not forget Phil. She felt for him
the tender interest a motherly woman naturally feels for one whom
she has nursed in pain and peril and helped to bring back to health.
Forlorn little Phil, with his blue eyes and yellow curls, had won a
secure place in Sister Kathleen's loving heart.

"Poor child, he is worse than motherless," she would say to herself at
night when she took her last look at him. "The very name of mother has
a perverted meaning for him, and suggests only harshness and cruelty. I
hardly think he knows what love means."

And with motherly tenderness in her heart, and motherly gentleness of
look and action, Sister Kathleen would tuck the bedclothes round him
and press a kiss on his sleeping brow.

Though she tried not to make a pet of Phil, every one saw that the boy
was very dear to Sister Kathleen, and the fact had its influence with
the nurses. All were disposed to be kind to Phil. He was a favourite
with the doctors, and they had a merry word for him as they came along
the ward. Phil's sharp, quaint replies often made them laugh.

In spite of pain and weakness, Phil was enjoying his hospital
experience, for it was a pleasing contrast to the life he had lived
before. He did not fret over his lost limb, nor trouble himself at all
about how it would be with him when the time came for him to leave
the hospital. He was content to let his life come to him as it would,
asking no questions, but living happily in the present, as children
do, without looking forward or borrowing any shadows from the possible
darkness of coming days.

One would hardly have imagined that the sorrowful experiences of Phil's
young life would have left him capable of being "spoiled" by too much
notice or injudicious petting. But the natural tendency of childhood
to strain to the utmost every indulgence, and tyrannize over rather
than obey its elders, was not lacking even in this poor little waif
of the streets. Phil's fits of fractiousness did not cease as he grew
stronger. His outbreaks of temper became more frequent, till, whenever
his wishes were thwarted or any attempt made to control him, a storm of
fury was certain to ensue.

Sister Kathleen saw nothing of these wilful moods. With her Phil was
always quiet and docile. And for a while, the nurses refrained from
telling her how naughty Phil was, and how much trouble he was causing
in the children's ward, to which he had been removed as soon as he
was able to sit up. But soon they were obliged to make complaint to
her, and the Sister, distressed at what she heard, requested that she
might be summoned to the scene when next Phil gave way to ungovernable
passion.

She was sitting alone by the fire in her private room one afternoon,
enjoying the only quiet time she could secure in the midst of her busy
day, when there came a tap at the door and one of the nurses looked in.

"Please, Sister, could you come to Phil? He's in one of his furies
again. He won't let Nurse Atkins touch him to apply the lotion that was
ordered, and he's going on dreadfully."

"I'll come," said Sister Kathleen, with an involuntary sigh, as she
rose from her easy-chair.

As she entered the children's ward, she heard Phil's voice raised to
its highest pitch. He was struggling in Nurse Atkins' grasp, and his
shrill outcries had very explicit meaning.

"I hate you! I wish you were dead! I should like to throw you on the
ground and trample on you, and cut off your head and kill you, I
should!"

"Phil, Phil!" said Sister Kathleen's calm, clear voice behind him.
"What is this I hear? What is it all about?"

The boy's screams ceased abruptly, and he turned upon her a startled
gaze. He had not expected Sister Kathleen to appear in the ward at
that hour. But it was only a brief pause. The next minute, he began to
scream again with renewed violence.

"He won't let me use the lotion because it makes the skin smart," said
the nurse, looking helplessly at her superior. "He is always like this
now; there is no doing anything with him."

"Leave him to me, nurse," said Sister Kathleen. "Never mind about the
lotion. Phil must be taught that we cannot have such behaviour as this."

The nurse loosed her hold of Phil, and he fell back into his chair,
writhing and screaming in a frenzy of passion, which was heightened by
a sense of its impotence.

"Phil," said Sister Kathleen, bending over him and speaking in her
clearest, most decided tones, "I cannot allow you to disturb the peace
of the ward by making this noise, so I shall take you away till you get
over your passion and feel ashamed of yourself."

So saying, she lifted him in her strong arms and carried him off. Phil
struggled a little, but he was too surprised and too full of wonder as
to what the Sister meant to do with him to make much resistance.

Straight to her own room Sister Kathleen carried him. There she laid
him on her bed, arranging the pillows about him so that he could rest
in the half-sitting posture he liked best. She did this without saying
a word, whilst Phil stole many a half-frightened, questioning glance at
her, and continued to sob. Sister Kathleen left him to himself and sat
down again in her chair by the fire, with her back turned towards Phil.

For a while there was silence, broken only by Phil sobbing beneath
his breath. Gradually his sobs became less frequent. The storm of
passion was over. He was beginning to feel ashamed of himself, and
Sister Kathleen's continued silence made him very uneasy. He moved his
position slightly, so as to catch a sidelong view of her face. How very
grave it was! How still she sat, with her cheek resting on her hand!
Phil wondered of what she was thinking. Did she think him very, very
bad? He wished she would speak to him. Any words, even the most severe
scolding, would be better than this strange, sad silence.

As he waited, each minute seeming as long as half-a-dozen, Sister
Kathleen gave a heavy sigh.

Phil leaned nearer, and stole another anxious glance at her. Was it
a tear he saw sparkling on her eyelashes? He watched her intently,
and tears sprang to his own blue eyes as he saw that Sister Kathleen
was indeed crying. There was no doubt about it, for she had taken her
handkerchief and was wiping the tears away. Phil could contain himself
no longer.

"Oh, Sister, Sister!" he exclaimed, with a sob. "What are you crying
for? Is it because I am so bad?"

She turned quickly at the sound of his voice and smiled on him through
her tears. "Yes and no, my poor little Phil," she said tenderly. "It is
true that thinking of you made me cry."

"Oh, don't cry, don't cry!" he implored her. "I will be good. I won't
never be so naughty again."

"I hope not," she said, rising and coming to him with open arms; "I
believe you will be good, for I have been asking God to make you good."

She lifted him from the bed as she spoke, and, moving back to her
chair, she sat down with Phil in her lap.

And Phil felt strangely happy, in spite of his tears, as he leaned his
head upon her shoulder and felt her arms pressed tenderly about him.

"I will be good," he said earnestly. "Don't cry again, Sister; I can't
bear to see you cry."

"Then I won't cry," she said, dashing away the tears that yet sparkled
in her eyes. "It was thinking of myself, Phil, as well as of you, that
made me cry. When I was a little girl, I used to be just as wilful and
passionate as you have been to-day."

"You?" exclaimed Phil, as if he could hardly believe his ears.

"Yes, I used often to fly into such passions, and I can remember how
patient my dear mother was with me, and how lovingly and wisely she
showed me how wrong it was, and helped me to conquer my temper."

"Your mother?" said Phil in a tone of wonder. "Didn't she beat you? I
can tell you mother makes me smart, if ever I get into my tantrums with
her."

"Oh no!" said Sister Kathleen, with a smile that came accompanied by
tears. "My mother never beat me. She was a good and tender mother."

"I wonder what it's like to have a good mother," said Phil, with an air
of reflection. "Mine's a precious bad one, I know! Didn't your mother
never beat you, not when she was in a rage?"

"She never was in a rage," said Sister Kathleen; "she was always gentle
and full of love. Do you know what love means, my little Phil?"

"Oh yes!" The boy's prompt reply took her by surprise. "You are love,
and Aunt Lizzie is love. I know what love means."

Yes, Phil could recognise love when it smiled on him, though he had
not learned to know it as most children do unconsciously in infancy,
softly sheltered by the brooding love which God has implanted in the
mother-heart, to foreshadow His own infinite love and pity for the
children of men.

"You are right, Phil," said Sister Kathleen; "I love you, and your Aunt
Lizzie loves you, but there is One who loves you far, far more than we
can. The arms of His love, broader, deeper, and more mighty than any
love of earth, enfold your little life. Ah, the thought of that love
comforts me when I think of you, dear little Phil."

The child could not understand. He was looking at her with eyes big
with wonder.

"You know the picture of the Good Shepherd that hangs in the ward?"

Phil nodded his head.

"Of course," he said, "don't you remember telling me about it?—How the
lamb was lost and the Shepherd went out in the cold and stormy night
to look for it, and how He found it, torn and bleeding, amongst the
thorns, and took it in His arms and carried it back to the fold? And
the lamb was so glad and happy to be in the Shepherd's arms again. You
said I was like that little lamb."

"So you are, my Phil, a lonely, forsaken lamb, but the Good Shepherd
loves you and cares for you."

"Will He come and find me?" asked Phil. "Will He take me in His arms?"

"I think He has found you," said Sister Kathleen; "His arms are about
you now, Phil."

"What do you mean?" said the boy, looking puzzled. "I don't see Him or
feel Him. Isn't He a real person?"

"Yes, indeed, though you cannot see Him with those blue eyes of yours,
nor touch Him with your little hands. He is ever near you, nearer than
I am now, ever watching over you and caring for you. Should it not make
you glad, Phil, to know that you have such a loving, tender, mighty
Friend, always with you to help and bless you?"

"Yes," said Phil slowly, "if I could feel sure that He was with me."

"You may be sure of it. I cannot always be with you, nor can your Aunt
Lizzie, but Jesus will never leave you nor forsake you. You can speak
to Him at any time, and He will hear you, and if you listen, you will
hear Him speaking to you in your heart, telling you how He loves you
and asking you to be good because He loves you so. Jesus is grieved
when you are naughty, Phil. He wants you to be happy, and you can never
be happy unless you try to be good and overcome your temper."

Phil was listening earnestly, with grave, upturned face, and Sister
Kathleen went on to tell him how the Good Shepherd had laid down His
life for the sheep, laying it down in sorest sorrow, forsaken, scorned,
and hated by those for whom He died, yet loving them to the end, and
forgiving those who tortured Him with bitter taunt and cruel stroke.

"So you see, Phil," she said, "when we feel like hating people and
wishing they were dead, we should remember Jesus, and how He loved and
forgave those who put Him to such a cruel death, and we should ask Him
to help us to forgive and to love as He did."

"I will try," said little Phil earnestly,—"I will try to be good."

How much of the truth had found a home in the childish mind Sister
Kathleen could not tell, as she met the earnest gaze of Phil's blue
eyes. There was a sweet, grave look on the little face. Apparently he
had forgotten his late trouble in the interest and wonder awakened by
her words. The Sister let him stay and take tea with her, a privilege
which Phil hailed with joy.

The nurses felt a little doubtful as to the Sister's wisdom in thus
"petting" Phil after he had been so naughty. But the boy was so
penitent and subdued when he came back into their care that they had no
just cause to question her judiciousness. Sister Kathleen's talk with
Phil was not without result, as the improvement which from that day was
manifest in his conduct clearly proved.

CHAPTER V.

A PLAN FOR PHIL'S BENEFIT.

MANY weeks had passed since Phil, so terribly injured, had been
carried into the hospital. Christmas-tide, with its holy, gladdening
influences, which are felt even in the wards of our hospitals, since
the love of Christian hearts prompts them to make provision for the joy
of all poor and suffering ones, had come and gone. The children's ward
had been decorated with holly and mistletoe, gay banners and Christmas
mottoes, and a large Christmas tree, glittering with lights and decked
with toys, had delighted the eyes of the little patients. No child had
entered more fully into the joy of Christmas than had Phil.

The Christmas carols which were sung in the ward found a glad echo in
the boy's breast, for, if the words were new to him, he could feel that
they breathed joy and hope and glad, exulting thankfulness. He too
could rejoice in the birth of the Christ-child, for he knew that the
Babe born in Bethlehem was the Friend and Saviour of the world, the
Good Shepherd, who laid down His life for the sheep.

But now the wintry months were past, and that spring had awakened was
evident from the primroses and violets which appeared in the children's
ward. Phil's cure had progressed favourably as the weeks went on. With
the help of his crutch, he could walk or rather hop about the ward
with perfect ease. He would have been dismissed from the hospital a
fortnight earlier, had not Sister Kathleen persuaded the doctors to let
him remain till his health was more fully established. But now, as she
looked at him, she felt with a pang of sorrow that there was no longer
a good excuse for keeping him.

In spite of his maimed condition, Phil's general appearance was better
than when he entered the hospital. Good food and wholesome living had
had their effect. His little face had grown rounder, and there was a
dash of colour in each cheek, whilst a happy light shone in his clear
blue eyes. How soon the improvement would be lost if he went back to
his wretched home and miserable mother!

Moll Dutton had been to the hospital once or twice, to make inquiries
concerning Phil and the probable date of his dismissal from the
hospital. But she had not thought it worthwhile to climb the long
flight of stairs to the children's ward and risk the chance of an
unfilial greeting from her boy. Needless to say, Phil was not sorry she
kept away.

His Aunt Lizzie came to see him at irregular intervals, and he never
failed to hail her appearance with delight. Several weeks had passed
since her last visit, when one bright spring day she entered the ward,
Sister Kathleen had been looking for her coming, and was scarcely less
pleased than Phil to see her enter.

"Phil is still here, I understand," she said, addressing the Sister in
panting, breathless accents; "I was afraid I should find that he had
gone."

"No, he is still here; he leaves next week. But pray sit down, Miss
Austen; you are quite out of breath. I am afraid you are not strong."

"Indeed, I am quite strong," she said hurriedly; "it is only coming up
the stairs that has tired me. Ah, here comes Phil," she added, with a
start and an involuntary shiver. "How quickly he gets along with that
crutch! It makes me tremble to see him."

"Oh, he is very proud of his performance," said Sister Kathleen. "I
must not stay with you now, but let me see you before you leave. I want
to have a talk with you about Phil."

Phil's aunt readily assented, and Sister Kathleen left her with the boy.

Phil was overjoyed to see the flowers Aunt Lizzie had brought him.
Primroses, violets, cowslips, wood-anemones, every treasure of the
spring seemed included in the huge bunch which he proceeded to untie
and to re-make into little nosegays to be distributed amongst his
friends in the ward.

"Do these all grow in the country where you live, aunt?" he asked.

"Yes, Phil," she said; "they grow in the fields and along the hedges in
the lanes."

"And may any one pick them?" he asked.

She nodded her head.

"Oh," he cried, "how lovely it must be to live in the country, and be
able to pick such flowers every day!"

"Phil," said his aunt, "do you know that you are to leave the hospital
next week?"

The boy's face fell. A look of acute distress came to it, and slowly
large tears welled up in the blue eyes. But there was no outbreak of
passionate rebellion against his fate. Apparently Phil had begun to
exercise self-control.

"I knew it must come soon," he said sorrowfully. "Oh dear! Oh dear! I
can't bear the thought of going back to mother. If only I had a good
mother! But she is so bad and wicked. I hate her! No, no, I must not
hate her, but—I can't love her, Aunt Lizzie."

"No," she said tremulously; "you can't love her. Can you love me, Phil?"

"Oh, I do love you," he said emphatically; "I wish you were my mother.
You are so good and kind, Aunt Lizzie."

She started as if his innocent words stabbed her, and an ashy paleness
overspread her face.

"Not good, not good!" she cried, in tones sharp with pain. "You do not
know me, little Phil, or you would not call me good."

"Why not?" he asked, with a puzzled air. "I think you are good. You
bring me flowers and all sorts of nice things, and you never beat me or
call me names. Why are you not good, Aunt Lizzie?"

She did not reply. She put her trembling arms about him and lifted him
on to her lap. Then she pressed his little head to her bosom, kissing
the white forehead and golden curls with passionate fondness.

"I am glad you think me good, little Phil," she murmured; "and yet,
God knows, I do not deserve that you should. Oh, was there ever a more
unhappy woman than I am!"

The child on her lap looked up at her with mild wonder in his blue
eyes. She recollected herself as she met his gaze, and forced back her
emotion.

"Good-bye, my little Phil," she said, kissing him again and again. "May
God watch over you and shield you from harm! If you never see me again,
remember me as one who loves you and would pray for you, could prayer
of hers be of any avail."

"Oh, Aunt Lizzie, don't go yet!" he cried.

But she set him down and broke from his detaining grasp. Going to a
nurse, she begged her to let Sister Kathleen know that she was about to
leave the hospital.

In a few minutes, Sister Kathleen came to her, and they had their talk
in the Sister's private room.

"You guess, I daresay, that I want to speak to you about Phil's
future," the Sister said. "Can nothing be done to save him from his
mother's cruelty and neglect? I have been wondering whether it would be
possible to find another home for him. Excuse my putting the question,
but—you are his aunt—I suppose it would not be convenient for you to
take him to your home?"

The question seemed to surprise Miss Austen. She stared at Sister
Kathleen in amazement for a moment. Then, as comprehension came, a deep
flush suffused her face.

"That is impossible," she said, in low, unsteady tones; "you do not
understand. I live with my father. He is an old man, and getting
feeble; it would worry him to have a child about."

"Even his grandchild," said Sister Kathleen.

"Phil is not—" Miss Austen began to speak in hurried, nervous tones,
then hastily checked herself.

"You do not understand," she said again, after a pause. "I have let
Phil call me 'Aunt Lizzie,' but I am not really his aunt."

"Indeed!" exclaimed the Sister, hardly able to believe what she heard
as she gazed at the face before her, which, worn and sad though it was,
yet seemed to bear a marked resemblance to that of little Phil.

She was puzzled, and longed to ask questions, but Miss Austen's manner
gave her no encouragement to do so.

"Since you can give no help," she said, after a minute, "I will tell
you a thought that has occurred to me. Some friends of mine have
established a home for destitute boys on their estate in Berkshire.
It is known as St. Oswald's Farm Home. In this home from eighty to a
hundred boys are sheltered. They are taught to farm the land, and to
provide entirely for their own necessities, thus learning in addition
to farm-work such occupations as shoemaking, tailoring, baking,
carpentry, etc. There is a schoolmaster to teach the lads, and a matron
to look after their comfort.

"Mr. and Mrs. Jeaffreson's pretty home adjoins the farm, and they take
the deepest interest in the boys, and do their best to settle them
well in life. Many of them on growing up are sent out to the colonies,
where, as a rule, they do exceedingly well. I spent a day with the
Jeaffresons last summer, and was delighted with what I saw of the
home. The situation is charming, and all the arrangements seemed to me
perfect. And now I cannot help thinking what a good thing it would be
for little Phil if he could be received into that happy home."

"It would indeed," murmured Miss Austen, with a deep-drawn sigh.

"I have already written to Mrs. Jeaffreson on the subject, giving her
particulars of Phil's case," continued Sister Kathleen. "She sends
me word that Phil is eligible for admission, and as there is now a
vacancy, he can be received at once if his mother gives her consent.
What do you think of the plan? Would you have any objection to his
going to this home?"

"I!" exclaimed Lizzie Austen in a startled tone. "How could I object?"
Then more quietly she added, "I should be only too thankful to know
that Phil was happily provided for."

"Then it only remains to get his mother's consent," said Sister
Kathleen.

"She will never consent," said Lizzie Austen, with another sigh; "Moll
Dutton will never give up the child."

"Do you think so?" said the Sister. "I quite hope to persuade her to
agree to my proposal. She seems to have no love for the child; why
should she not be glad to have him taken off her hands?"

Miss Austen shook her head. "I hope it may be so," she said, "but I
doubt it."

She had risen, and was fastening her cloak.

"Shall you be here again before Phil leaves us?" asked the Sister.

"It is impossible," said Miss Austen sadly. "My father cannot spare me.
I had hard work to get away to-day. But oh," she added, with a sudden
break in her voice, "I should like to know what becomes of little Phil."

"I will let you know," said Sister Kathleen readily. "If you will give
me your address, I will send you word of the result of my appeal to
Moll Dutton."

"Oh, thank you," said Miss Austen. She hesitated for a moment, then
added rather tremulously, "The address is 'Fairbank Farm, near
Danesbury, Essex.'"

"I shall remember," said Sister Kathleen. Then, seeing the gloom on the
other's face, she went on to say, "Now don't be cast down about Phil. I
have good hope of managing this dreadful Moll Dutton. Why, you are as
fond of Phil as if he were not your nephew merely, but your own child!"

What was there in the carelessly uttered words to move the other so?
Every shred of the composure Lizzie had been struggling to maintain
gave way beneath their influence. She sank on to a chair and broke into
uncontrollable weeping.

Sister Kathleen laid her hand tenderly on her shoulder. "You are in
trouble, I see. This is more than anxiety for little Phil. Will you not
tell me what it is? Perhaps I can help you."

Miss Austen gave an uneasy movement, as if she would shake off the
Sister's hand. "No, no," she cried almost wildly; "you cannot help me.
No one can help me now."

"Except the one Divine Friend who can soothe every sorrow," said Sister
Kathleen gently.

"You mean God," said Lizzie, looking up at her with eyes full of
despair; "but even God cannot help me. He cannot undo the past."

"Indeed He can help you," said the Sister earnestly. "The past cannot,
I know, be other than it is, but God can annul its evil, and take from
memory its sting."

But her words seemed to fall on heedless ears. Lizzie had risen, and
was impatient to be gone. She could not speak as she gave the Sister
her hand. And save by her warm hand-clasp, Sister Kathleen gave no
further expression to her sympathy.

"God help her!" she said to herself, when the frail young form,
prematurely bent beneath the weight of hopeless sorrow, had passed from
her sight. "If ever there was a sorrow-stricken, despairing soul, that
poor woman is such."

CHAPTER VI.

PHIL QUITS THE HOSPITAL.

SISTER KATHLEEN had been over-sanguine in her hope of influencing Moll
Dutton. That loud-voiced, strong-armed virago was not to be persuaded
to relinquish her rights. She came to the hospital bent on regaining
possession of the boy, of whose baby face and yellow curls she had made
capital. At first, she professed to be moved by motherly affection, and
even attempted to fondle Phil. But the boy shrank in terror from her
caresses.

"Has he not the more need of his mother's care since he is a cripple?"
she asked. "Who could look after him and nurse him like his own mother?
It was cruel of any one to wish to take from her her poor lame boy."

But when Sister Kathleen carefully set before her the advantages Phil
would enjoy at St. Oswald's Farm Home, and urged her for her child's
sake to consent to what was so obviously for his good, Moll's stubborn
will asserted itself, and she flatly refused to yield. She abandoned
her assumption of maternal feeling, and without shame revealed her true
motives.

"Give up little Phil!" she cried. "Give him up now! Not for anything
you may say. He'll be worth a mint of money to me, now that he's lost
his leg. His baby face and blue eyes, along of that little crutch, will
draw the money from folk's pockets. It's rare luck to have him as he
is."

"Do you mean to say," exclaimed Sister Kathleen, aghast, "that you can
rejoice to make profit out of your child's misfortune? It is horrible,
monstrous that you should feel so!"

Moll gave a coarse laugh.

"Maybe, lady, maybe. We poor folks can't afford to have fine feelings,
nor to pick and choose our words. I'll make so bold as to remind you
that Phil's my child, and you've no right to keep him from me. If he's
ready, I'd like to be going, for I can't afford to waste my time."

Without attempting further remonstrance, Sister Kathleen left her in
the outer room and went into the ward to summon Phil. Never would
she forget the pitiful, beseeching look Phil gave her when she told
him that he must get ready to accompany his mother. It was as if he
implored her to save him from his mother. But what could she do? She
could not keep him against his mother's will.

"I cannot help you, my poor Phil," she whispered; "you must go with
your mother. But I know where she lives, and as soon as possible I will
come and see you. And you know that the Good Shepherd will be with you.
He will watch over you, my helpless lamb. You must pray to Him every
day, Phil."

"I will," said Phil earnestly. Then a thought struck him which brought
a gleam of hope to his little white face.

"He can do everything, can't He, Sister? Will He make mother die, if I
ask Him?"

"Oh, Phil, Phil!" exclaimed Sister Kathleen, inexpressibly shocked.
"You do not know what you are saying. It is dreadful that you should
think of such a thing."

"Is it naughty?" faltered the child.

"Yes, it is very wrong to wish that any one should die. Pray God to
have mercy on her and make her a better woman, but do not pray that she
may die."

Phil looked puzzled and thoughtful for a few moments, then he said,
"Would it be wrong to ask God to take her quite away, and to give me
another mother—a good, kind mother? I don't want her to die, if only
she might go right away, so that I should never see her again. May I
ask that?"

"Perhaps," said Sister Kathleen, hardly knowing how to reply to the
childish question,—"perhaps you may, Phil. God our Father answers our
prayers, not according to our requests, but according to our needs. He
may not answer as you expect, but He will hear your prayer none the
less. And, Phil, whenever you pray, ask for the gift of the Holy Spirit
to give you right thoughts and feelings, and to teach you how to pray."

"I will," said Phil again. And then, as she kissed him, he threw his
arms about her neck and clung to her, sobbing as if his heart would
break.

"Come, come, Phil; this will not do," she said; "you must be brave."

Presently he raised his head and tried to smile at her, but the smile
was sadder than his tears.


When it was known that he was going, nurses and little patients crowded
round him to bid him goodbye, for Phil had become a great favourite
in the ward. One of the nurses gave him a pair of warm mittens she
had knitted for him. Phil showed little pleasure at the gift. He held
them in his hand for a few moments, looking at them with a very rueful
expression.

"Thank you," he said; "they're very pretty, but it's no good giving
them to me. She wouldn't let me wear them. The people wouldn't give me
any money, don't you see, if I didn't look poor and ragged."

There were tears in many eyes as Phil, moving along wearily by the help
of his crutch, quitted the ward.

"Now then, look sharp, young Phil!" cried his mother roughly, as he
approached her. "No snivelling, mind. I'm your mother, and you've got
to come with me. So make haste, and let me see how fast you can get
over the ground with that fine crutch of yours, which is going to bring
us such luck."

Without a word, Phil followed her as quickly as his lameness would
permit. She caught him in her arms at the head of the stairs and
carried him down the long flight. Thus, he disappeared from Sister
Kathleen's gaze.

CHAPTER VII.

A BURDENED SOUL.

FAIRBANK FARM lay about five miles from Chelmsford, and one mile
beyond the village of Danesbury. The farmhouse in which Lizzie Austen
was born, and where with the exception of two years her life had
been passed, was a low, two-storied house, with ivy growing thickly
about it. It stood in the midst of fields, but was visible from the
Chelmsford Road, from which the length of a meadow separated it. The
shortest way to the house was by the footpath which slanted across the
meadow from the stile at one corner to the garden gate. The road for
vehicles ran beside the hedge of the cornfield beyond.

Neat iron palings enclosed the house and garden. Only a strip of gravel
and a couple of flower-beds lay in front of the house, but the garden
stretched beside the meadow to some distance to the left of the house,
and covered a large area. It was a grand old garden, where from month
to month bloomed almost every flower named in the gardener's almanac.
It had a reputation in the country-side. Lizzie's mother had been an
enthusiastic lover of flowers, and justly proud of the success that
attended her gardening operations. Mrs. Austen's hyacinths, roses,
orchids, asters, and chrysanthemums were alike famous, and had taken
many a prize at Chelmsford flower-shows.

But now for five summers Mrs. Austen's comely form had been missing
from the garden where she had been wont to spend many hours, moving
to and fro in her large straw hat, scissors or rake in hand, as she
watched and tended with utmost care the plants that were so dear to
her. In the old churchyard at Danesbury there was a mound of earth
always kept bright with flowers for her sake.

The high character of the garden was maintained after her death. Farmer
Austen, without sharing his wife's enthusiasm for flowers, had been
proud of her successful horticulture, and in his deep mourning for her
loss, it seemed to him due to her memory to preserve with jealous care
the plants and shrubs which she had planted, and as far as possible to
keep the garden as she would have liked to see it. His daughter Lizzie
tried to carry out his wishes, and, though she had never cared for
gardening, gave much time and attention to "dear mother's flowers," and
to such good purpose that the garden continued to produce some of the
finest specimens of each season's flowers that could be found in the
neighbourhood.

A silent, sad-faced woman was Lizzie Austen after her mother's death.
She had been the brightest, prettiest girl to be seen for many a mile
round, but from that time, she was another being. She had the look of
a woman with a sad history, yet to her acquaintance at Danesbury, her
life-story seemed simple enough.

A high-spirited, somewhat perverse young girl was Lizzie, when,
attracted by her pretty face, Mark Warren, the wealthy owner of
Danesbury Mill, had come courting her. Her parents would have liked
the match well, but Lizzie would not look with favour on her suitor,
for Mark, though worthy in character, was rough in manner and blunt of
speech, and Lizzie's imagination, excited by indiscriminate course of
novel-reading, craved a more romantic lover. His wooing vexed her, and
she was beginning to weary of the monotony of rural life. So she begged
her parents to let her leave home and go to London for a while, to
learn millinery and to see a little of the world.

Farmer Austen had married late in life, and from her infancy, his only
child had been to him a perpetual wonder and delight. He could deny
her nothing. Nor was easy-tempered Mrs. Austen more successful in the
assertion of parental authority. Lizzie always contrived to get her own
way, and in this instance, though at first her parents refused their
consent, Lizzie by coaxing and pleading at last persuaded them to let
her go to London.

Old John Austen won plenty of blame for allowing his daughter to leave
home, but he knew how to reply to every hint of disapproval. It was
well that Lizzie should go away for a time; well both for her and for
Mark Warren. She was going to his cousin, a prosperous milliner at the
West End, whom he knew would take good care of Lizzie. Not that he was
afraid to trust his girl anywhere. She had a head on her shoulders, and
could bold her own with any one. There were not many girls, though he
said it that should not, to be compared with his Lizzie. But whilst he
spoke thus it was evident that the old man missed his daughter sadly,
and was longing for her return.

Lizzie went away early in the year, and in harvest-time she came back,
but only for a brief visit. She told those who questioned her that she
loved London, and had no mind to settle down to country life again. She
looked prettier than ever in her fashionable hat and feathers and her
London-made gown. But there was a change in her demeanour that saddened
Mark Warren, and made what little hope he had still cherished die out
of his heart. She was flippant in speech and restless in manner, and
treated almost with indifference the parents who loved her as the light
of their life.

Lizzie returned to London, and many months passed ere she was again
seen at Danesbury. Her acquaintance did not forget to inquire for her,
and always the answer was the same. Lizzie was well and happy, and was
coming home-soon. But another spring and summer passed without bringing
her, although it was known that her cousin had died, and the millinery
business had passed into other hands.

Farmer Austen was beginning to grow feeble, and it was observed that
the cloud which was now ever on his brow deepened when any one named
his daughter. Mrs. Austen's comely face, too, was losing somewhat
of its colour and roundness, and the corners of her mouth had a
discontented droop. Then it was rumoured that John Austen had told some
one that Lizzie had gone to Paris, to perfect her skill in millinery
and acquire the French language. But that she would certainly come home
to spend Christmas with her parents. Christmas, however, came and went
without bringing Lizzie to her old home.

Early in the year, when the snow lay deep upon the farm, Mrs. Austen
fell ill with an attack of acute bronchitis. Till lately, she had
enjoyed fair health, and the news that the doctor had pronounced her
case hopeless came upon all the neighbours as a shock. A hasty summons
was sent to Lizzie. And at last, she returned home, but only in time to
be with her mother for an hour or two before she died.

Many persons attended the funeral, for the Austens were widely known
and much respected in the neighbourhood.

Curious glances were cast at Lizzie to see what changes time had
wrought in her, and how she appeared to bear the stroke of bereavement.
It was to be expected that she would look pale and sorrowful, but the
change the neighbours saw in her was such as startled them. Could
that wan, despairing-looking woman be the Lizzie Austen whose pretty
girlish form they had known so well? The rosiness had vanished from her
face, her cheeks were thin and hollow. Her eyes looked washed out with
weeping, her hair had lost its brightness, and the indefinable charm of
girlhood was gone. She was a woman now—a weary, sorrow-stricken woman.

Had a few days of grief had power thus to change her? Was it remorse
for neglecting her mother that weighed so heavily upon her heart?

"Depend upon it, she can't forgive herself for staying away so long,"
the gossips said. "It must have been a dreadful shock to come home and
find her mother at the point of death."

Yet, severe though the shock was, it scarcely seemed enough to account
for the utter dejection that marked Lizzie's bearing. She did not rouse
from it as the days went on.

The friends who came to condole with her declared that they could not
make her out. Lizzie had been frank and free of speech in the days
that were gone, but now she had hardly a word to say. Their well-meant
condolences were received by her with an air of passive endurance,
and the questions which they did not hesitate to put obtained but the
briefest replies. It was plain that she shrank from each allusion to
her past life. When her friends assumed that she would remain with her
father for the future, Lizzie said nothing, and her silence was taken
as assent.

Lizzie went back to London, however, but only for a week. At the end
of that time she returned, bringing her boxes with her; and it was
understood that she would not leave her father again.

Her melancholy did not pass away as time went on. No daughter could
have been more tender and devoted than she was, but her old father
often watched her wistfully, longing to see again the Lizzie of former
days. If only she would smile as she used to smile, or utter one of the
merry, saucy sayings which once fell so often from her lips!

"Poor Lizzie does fret sorely after her mother," he would say to a
sympathizing neighbour; "she's another girl from what she was."

There was no need to tell that to any one who had known her in earlier
years.

Lizzie had plenty of work, and no one could accuse her of lack of
industry. In addition to the care of the house and garden, the
superintendence of the dairy devolved on her. The dairy was a large
one, for it was now the most important branch of the farming at
Fairbank. John Austen, like most of the farmers in the neighbourhood,
had suffered from the bad harvests and general agricultural depression,
but he had the good sense to turn as much of his land as possible into
pasturage, and was able to make a fair profit by supplying milk to the
London market.

Lizzie showed herself a good woman of business, and gradually her
father allowed her to undertake more and more of the practical
management of affairs. He continued to look after his men and to attend
the Chelmsford markets. But, when there was business to be done in
London, he was willing that Lizzie, who had no fear of the din and
bustle of the city, and was more used to town ways than he, should
attend to it.

But though Lizzie was ready to go to London whenever required, it was
observed that these visits did not make her more cheerful. If possible,
her face was more sad and her manner more hopeless when she came back.
And she never took advantage of her journey to town to get a new
fashion or a pretty ornament. No one would suppose that she had any
skill in millinery, people said, to see the plain, Quaker-like bonnet
she always wore. Did she wear it as a penance?

In time, the neighbours grew impatient of Lizzie Austen's continued
depression and reserve. Why did she hold herself apart from every one,
refusing each invitation to tea-drinking or harvesting, and going
nowhere, save that on Sunday mornings she accompanied her father to the
little chapel at Danesbury, in which he had held the office of deacon,
until, after his wife's death, he felt himself too old and infirm
longer to fulfil its duties? As they noted her strange ways and dreamy
looks, they began to speak of Lizzie as "peculiar," using the word in
a way that might lead those unacquainted with her to imagine that Miss
Austen's melancholy had reached the extreme of mental derangement.

Lizzie troubled herself not at all about what the neighbours might
think or say of her. There had been a time when she had thought much of
their opinion, but now the heavy weight of trouble which clouded her
life could hardly be lightened or deepened by words of theirs. Alike in
the bright days of summer and the dark days of winter, her heart was
full of bitterness. Life might be more endurable on some days than on
others, that was all.

Happiness, peace, content—such words had no meaning for Lizzie Austen.
To wake at early morn to a sense of hopeless misery, and wish the
day over before it had begun; to set about its round of duty with a
weary, aching heart that gave its weariness to head and limbs; to give
herself no rest, but fill every moment with toil, in the vain hope of
escaping from thoughts which yet mingled their bitterness with every
action,—such was Lizzie's day.

What greater misery is there for a human soul than to know that one's
life is spoiled, utterly spoiled, and that it is one's own wilful folly
that has wrecked it; to look along the coming years, whilst their
number still seems great, and see no ray of hope, no possibility of
escape from the pitiless torture of remorse; and at the same time to
remember with painful vividness the happier days that are for ever gone
and the good that might have been but for one fatal step; to know, too,
that the bitter consequences of one's folly are not suffered by oneself
alone, but that another innocent life is blighted by them?

God pity all spirits bowed beneath this intolerable burden! Such an one
was Lizzie Austen.

CHAPTER VIII.

DISAPPOINTMENT.

A FEW days had passed since Lizzie's last visit to the hospital, when
about six o'clock one evening, she was in the garden attending to her
plants. She was watering some young cuttings that needed special care;
but every now and then she paused in her occupation and looked across
the meadow at the high road, with the expectation of seeing her father
on his return from Chelmsford market. Several vehicles went by, and
Lizzie recognised one and another of the neighbouring farmers, but
her father did not yet come in sight. Lizzie was rather eager for his
coming, because she felt sure that he would call at the post office,
and she hoped that he might bring her a letter from Sister Kathleen.

When next she glanced towards the road, she saw a man walking down the
meadow path to the house. It was Mr. Fennell, the new minister, who had
lately come to officiate at the little village chapel. Lizzie sighed
as she set down the watering-pot. She had not exchanged more than the
merest greeting with Mr. Fennell since he came to Danesbury, and she
had no wish for further intercourse with him. She was sorry he had come
now, when her father was not at home to receive him.

"Good evening, Miss Austen," said the minister in hearty tones. His
round, somewhat florid face had a very kindly expression, and he shook
hands in a way that seemed to show that he would be a good friend.

"I am sorry that my father is not at home, Mr. Fennell," said Lizzie,
"but I expect him every minute."

"I am quite content to await his arrival," said Mr. Fennell, with a
smile, "for in truth I have come to see you, Miss Austen. I have hardly
spoken to you as yet, and I wish to become acquainted with all my
flock, that I may if possible win their confidence and friendship."

Lizzie coloured slightly as she murmured, more politely than sincerely,
that she was pleased to see him. Then she invited him to enter the
house.

"Thank you; if you will allow me, I would rather remain here," said Mr.
Fennell; "I am fond of a garden. Pray don't let me disturb you in your
work."

But Lizzie let her watering-pot stand empty, and, taking him round the
garden, showed him all that it could boast of present beauty or of
promise for the future.

The minister was himself an amateur gardener, and he was interested in
all he saw. But he took a deeper interest in human nature. The sadness
of Miss Austen's face, the contrast between its youthful form and
hopeless expression had struck him when first he saw her. He had heard
her peculiarities discussed, and knew that she was no favourite with
her neighbours, but what he had heard had but excited his sympathy and
made him anxious to be her friend.

Now, as he marked her despondent air and heard the low, sad tones in
which she replied to his remarks, he said to himself, "Here is one
whose heart is crushed beneath a weight of hopeless sorrow. I wish I
knew her history that I might, if possible, help her."

But his efforts to win her confidence were not attended with success.
Lizzie's reserve did not relax as he talked to her. She would not speak
about herself, nor talk freely on any topic save the weather and the
garden.

They were in the greenhouse, and she was showing him a remarkable
species of cactus, when he said, "Ah, yes; I have seen one of the kind
before—in the Jardin des Plantes at Paris. Have you ever been abroad,
Miss Austen?"

He was surprised to see the deep crimson flush which his words brought
to her face.

"Yes, yes," she almost stammered in her confusion,—"once, a long time
ago."

"Ah, indeed! Where did you go?"

"To Paris," she answered, with evident unwillingness.

"A beautiful city, is it not?" he remarked, hardly knowing what he
said, so startled was he at the effect of his words.

"Yes," she said hurriedly, "but I saw very little of it. I went there
on business."

And, with the manifest purpose of changing the subject, she drew his
attention to the state of the vine which was trained across the top of
the greenhouse.

Mr. Fennell made a final and bolder attempt to break through her
reserve.

"I must be going," he said presently, "but will you first let me say
a word to you, Miss Austen? As a pastor it is my duty to inquire into
the spiritual welfare of all my flock. I was sorry to see last Sunday
that your father came alone to the Lord's Table. You have, I know, been
trained in the faith of the Gospel. What is it keeps you from doing
this in remembrance of your Saviour?"

The question took Lizzie by surprise. There was a glow of anger in her
pale cheek as she half turned from Mr. Fennell in her annoyance.

"Pardon me, if I seem over-bold," he said gently. "Believe me, I speak
as your friend."

"I cannot tell you why it is," she said tremulously. "My father is
good, my mother was good, but you must not suppose that I am good."

"None is good save God," he replied. "You do not think that any of us
would dare to come to the Lord's Table trusting in our own goodness?
We come, knowing that we are sinners, to meet with Christ, the Friend
of sinners; and no one, however unworthy, need hold back who can come
in faith, calling Jesus, Lord. Do you not believe in Jesus as your
Saviour?"

Lizzie shrank visibly from his words. She glanced at the house, as if
longing to make her escape into its shelter.

"Do not ask me!" she exclaimed, feeling driven to speak. "Religion is
not for me."

"For whom then is it?" he asked gently. "Forgive me. I cannot help
seeing that you are in trouble, and all sorrowful ones need Jesus. He
calls to Himself the weary and heavy-laden. Will you not obey that
call?"

"I cannot!" she exclaimed, in a tone the desperate misery of which went
to his heart.

"Why not?" he asked. "What is it holds you back? Is it sin? 'This Man
receiveth sinners.'"

Lizzie could bear no more. She felt herself on the verge of tears, and,
resolved not to give way to emotion, she turned from Mr. Fennell with
an abrupt "Good evening."

"Good evening," he said, his voice full of sympathy. "May Christ give
you peace!"

Peace! The word seemed to mock Lizzie as she hastened into the house,
for like a bitter comment came to mind the words: "'There is no peace,
saith my God, to the wicked.'"

She went to her room and sank on her knees beside the bed, weeping as
for long she had not wept. But though she took the attitude of prayer,
she could not pray.

The sound of her father's step below roused her. She rose, and, forcing
herself to be calm, hastily bathed her eyes and went down-stairs.

Her father was calling for her, "Here's a letter for you, Lizzie," he
said.

Lizzie took the letter, and a glance told her that it was the one she
was expecting. But she fetched her father his pipe and tankard of ale,
and stayed to listen to his slow account of what he had heard and done
at Chelmsford, ere she opened her letter, though her fingers trembled
as they closed over it, and her heart was fluttering with impatience to
know its contents.

A fine-looking old man, with snowy hair and keen dark eyes, was John
Austen. Though he was getting bent with age, there was still a certain
stern dignity in his bearing. Indulgent as he had ever been toward
his daughter, he was not generally considered to be a man of mild and
pliable temper.

The men who worked for him knew well that it was a serious thing to
offend the old master, for he was not one who could overlook a breach
of duty or readily pardon a wrong-doer. Above all things, he detested
deceit, and any one whom he convicted of telling a lie, or acting in an
underhand manner, was at once dismissed from his service. The land he
held had been farmed by his father before him, and he was proud of the
respect which in that neighbourhood had ever been accorded to the name
of Austen.

"No one ever doubted the word of an Austen," he was wont to say. "No
one ever knew an Austen have cause to hang his head."

Of late years, there had grown up in Lizzie's heart, intertwined
with her love for her father, a strong element of fear. She shivered
sometimes at the thought of what he would say or do if he knew certain
things which she had hidden from him. She fancied, too, that her
melancholy shrinking from the company of others was an annoyance to his
pride, as being an approach to that hanging of the head to which the
Austens had never been given. Lizzie made an effort to appear cheerful
when with her father. She listened now with an appearance of interest
to all he had to tell her, till at last, the old man ceased to talk and
began to nod as he sat in his arm-chair by the fire.

Lizzie stole noiselessly from the room. She felt sick with hope and
fear as with trembling fingers she opened her letter. It was very
brief. Sister Kathleen had not time to write long letters.

 "DEAR MISS AUSTEN," she said.

 "You will be sorry to hear that my hope has been disappointed. You were
right with regard to Phil's mother. She has refused to give up her
child, and she took him away with her last night. Poor little Phil! We
must pray the Lord to watch over him.—Yours faithfully—

 "SISTER KATHLEEN."

The letter dropped from Lizzie's hand, and she sat gazing before her
with dilated eyes. She had not known how strong had been her hope for
Phil till she felt the force of this disappointment. No sound escaped
her ashen lips, but her spirit was crying out in wild rebellion against
her misery:

 "O God! My punishment is greater than I can bear!"

How long she had sat there in tearless despair she knew not, when her
father's voice roused her. She rose and tried to go to him, but her
trembling limbs refused to support her. She sank fainting to the ground.

CHAPTER IX.

MOLL CHANGES HER ABODE.

LITTLE PHIL, as he trudged painfully behind his mother along the London
streets, looked back with an aching sense of regret to the days he had
passed in the hospital. Moll's voice angrily bidding him move faster
warned him that he need expect no kinder treatment from her than he had
been wont to receive.

His heart sank low indeed when they reached the miserable alley in
which Moll Dutton lived, for at the sight of him, coming along by the
aid of his crutch, the ragged children who were playing in the street
left their play to point at him and shout derisively. The neighbours,
too, showed not the least consideration for the child's feelings, as
they freely commented on his misfortune, and his mother led a laugh at
his crippled appearance.

Phil, shrinking sensitively from the looks and words bestowed on him,
was glad to find shelter in Moll's close, evil-smelling room, hung
with secondhand or perhaps third-hand garments in various stages of
shabbiness and dilapidation, for Moll was professedly a dealer in old
clothes, though she combined with this business other dealings of a
less bona fide character. The dim, dirty room, with its unhealthy
atmosphere, presented a sore contrast to the bright, airy ward in which
Phil had been for so long. He could not have expressed his sense of the
change, but he was keenly alive to it, and felt so unhappy on finding
himself again in this wretched home, that even his dread of his mother
had not power to hold back his tears. Throwing himself on a heap of
rags, he began to cry.

"Now stop that!" cried Moll, giving him a smart blow with her hand.
"I'll have none of that noise. I'm your mother, and you've got to stay
with me and to do as I tell you. And if you don't behave yourself, it
will be the worse for you."

Phil was not indifferent to this threat. He choked down his sobs and
rubbed away his tears with his knuckles.

[Illustration]

His mother offered him some food, but he felt too sick and miserable
to eat. She was not distressed by his want of appetite. Without paying
further heed to him, she took her own meal, and then set to work to
clear the room of the strangely-assorted clothes that hung around it.
Phil watched her in surprise as she took down one thing after another,
packing as many as it would hold into a box which she dragged from
under the bed, and making the rest into a huge bundle.

Phil wondered greatly to see her do this, and at last he ventured to
put a question. "What are you doing, mother? Are you going to take them
all away?"

"Yes, every rag of them, and you into the bargain," was Moll's reply.

"Me!" exclaimed Phil in alarm. "Where are you going to take me?"

"You'll see fast enough. I'm going to make a clean sweep out of this
place. It's not so nice that I should be sorry to leave it," said Moll,
with a grim laugh.

"Do you mean that you are going to leave the house?" asked Phil.

"That's it. We clear out the first thing to-morrow morning," returned
Moll, snapping her fingers with an air of triumph.

"But why?" asked Phil, looking puzzled and somewhat distressed.

"That's no business of yours," said his mother. "Are you sorry? I
didn't know you was so fond of this place."

"I am not fond of it," said Phil. "Where are you going, mother?"

"Never you mind," she replied to him.

And Phil was silent, pondering with a grave, anxious little face this
new aspect of affairs. He could certainly feel no attachment to that
dismal room, but he did not want to leave it now, for he was counting
on Sister Kathleen's promised visit, and the thought that she might
come there only to find him gone filled him with pain. But instinct
warned him to say nothing of this to his mother, though he did not
guess that she was changing her quarters in order that neither Sister
Kathleen nor any one who might be interested in him should be able to
follow him up.

When she had completed her preparations, Moll went out, leaving Phil to
put himself to bed when he felt so inclined.

Alone, Phil could no longer restrain his tears. The return to his
former life was even more hard and bitter than he had fancied it would
be. As he crouched crying on the floor, there was a tap on the door,
and the next moment, the familiar face of Fan the flower-girl was seen
peeping cautiously into the room.

"Are you there, Phil?" she asked, for at first she could not
distinguish him in the dim light.

"Yes, I'm here, Fan," he replied. "I'm glad you've come. You needn't be
afraid, mother's gone out."

Fan was several years older than Phil, but she was his friend, and the
boy loved her. He thought her one to be envied, for Fan, ragged and
untidy as she appeared, had a mother who looked after her in rough but
loving fashion. Phil had seen Mrs. Nankinell take off her shawl to
wrap it round Fan's shivering shoulders in bitter wintry weather. And
once, when times were hard, Fan had told him how her mother had given
her their last piece of bread, refusing to taste a morsel of it. These
things had made their impression on little Phil.

"It would be good to have a mother like that," he had said to himself.

"I thought Moll was out, or I should not have come," remarked Fan as
she approached the boy. "So you've lost your leg, Phil; that's a bad
job, ain't it?"

"Yes," said Phil, with a sigh, "but I can get along very well with
my crutch. I wouldn't have minded about the leg if only I could have
stayed at the hospital. I was so happy there, Fan. You can't think
what a nice place it is. The children's ward is just lovely. There are
pictures and flowers and toys, and oh! the Christmas tree! You 'should'
have seen that!"

"I know!" said Fan, with a sagacious nod. "It's fine times being in a
hospital. I was in one myself once, though only for a few days, but
I know I liked it. I wouldn't mind being taken there again, as long
as they didn't cut off my leg. I shouldn't like that." And Fan gave a
pitying glance at Phil's empty trouser. "But tell me about it, Phil."

Phil was quite ready to relate his experiences, and he talked almost
without a pause for the nest half-hour. He said nothing of the pain
he had suffered, for already he had forgotten it. But he described to
Fan the glories of the Christmas tree and the fun of the Christmas
merrymaking, and told her about Aunt Lizzie's visits, and the flowers
and nice things she had brought him, and spoke in glowing terms of
Sister Kathleen.

"And you say she is coming to see you?" said Fan.

"Yes," said Phil, and then his face clouded as he added, "But oh, Fan,
I'm afraid she won't find me, for mother's going away from here. We're
to be off the first thing to-morrow morning."

"Going away!" exclaimed Fan. "You don't mean it? Well, she's kept it
pretty dark. I don't believe that any one knows that she's about to
flit. Where are you going?"

"I don't know," said Phil sadly; "she won't tell me."

"Ah, then, she doesn't mean any one to know," said Fan shrewdly. "She
wants to get out of the ken of the folks in the alley. That's her
dodge. I say, Phil, I'm real sorry that you're going away."

"So am I," said Phil. "This isn't a nice place, I know, but I'd rather
stay here than go anywhere else. Oh, what shall I do if Sister Kathleen
comes and can't find me?"

"It would be a pity," said Fan, "but I'll watch for her coming, Phil,
and if I can, I'll tell her where you are. Ask your mother again when
she comes home to-night."

"It wouldn't be of no use," said Phil. "She wouldn't tell me. And
besides, I shall most likely be asleep. I never can keep awake when I
want to."

"Can't you?" said Fan. "But I tell you what, if the lady can't find
you, you might go to the hospital to see her. You could find the way to
the hospital again, couldn't you?"

"Why, yes, I believe I could from here," said Phil, looking pleased
with the suggestion. "And if I didn't know how to start from the other
place, I could ask the way. Anyhow I'll try. And I'll pray to God about
it. Do you ever pray, Fan?"

Fan smiled and shook her head.

"I know what you mean," she said; "I've heard a man pray—the man who
used to come last summer and preach at the end of the alley. But mother
says that it's of no good to pray. It's only talking to the air, she
says, and can't make any difference."

"But it does make a difference!" exclaimed little Phil. "Jesus hears us
when we pray, and He'll do what we ask Him. Sister Kathleen says so.
I'm going to pray to Him every day."

"So she's taught you to pray, has she, Phil?" said Fan, looking at him
curiously. "What are you going to pray for?"

"Oh, lots of things," said Phil; "I'm going to ask Him to take mother
away, and give me a new mother—a good one, Fan."

Fan burst into a shrill laugh.

"Well, I never! That's a fine idea!" she exclaimed. "How could you have
a new mother, Phil? Moll's your mother, and you'll never have another."

"I daresay it may be difficult to manage," said Phil calmly, "but Jesus
will know how to do it."

"Really, Phil!" said Fan, and she laughed again.

But Phil was not to be laughed out of his hope. The talk with Fan had
cheered him, and he shed no more tears after she left him, but went at
once to bed, where he was soon sleeping soundly.

It seemed to him the middle of the night when he was roused by a rough
shaking from Moll.

"Get up at once and put on your clothes," she said in a harsh whisper;
"and make no noise about it, mind!"

Moll was already dressed, even to her bonnet and shawl. As he
tremblingly obeyed her, Phil saw that his mother had packed up all
her small possessions. Outside the house, a man was waiting with a
hand-barrow. One by one Moll carried out her few articles of furniture,
and with his help put them on the barrow. This was done as quietly as
possible, and what noise there was failed to rouse any of the dwellers
in the house, who were apparently heavy sleepers. Then Moll bade Phil
take his crutch and come along.

With a sore heart, he followed her from the house. It was hardly yet
light, and no one was astir in the alley.

An old woman, hearing the cart passing, leaned out of an attic window
and shouted to know what was up.

Moll vouchsafed no reply, and, grumbling angrily at having her slumbers
disturbed, the beldame retreated.

Phil's slow progress soon roused his mother's impatience, and she began
to scold him for not making more haste. But the man took pity on the
little cripple, and, lifting him on to the cart, found a seat for him
on the top of the furniture. Thus Phil travelled to his new home ere
yet the day had fully dawned or the tide of busy life had begun to flow
through the London streets.

CHAPTER X.

PHIL HAS A TRYING DAY.

PHIL'S new home was little more agreeable than the old one had been.
Moll had gone eastward, and established herself in a miserable tenement
in Shoreditch.

Phil was constantly in the streets following his "business," for Moll
was anxious to reap as much benefit as possible from his misfortune.
At first, she used to accompany him into the streets, for she had some
fear lest Phil should attempt to run away from her. But she soon found
that the boy would gain more by his begging if left to himself. So she
would repair to a public-house close by, or would watch him from a
distance, thus managing to make Phil feel that she was never far off,
and he could not get away from her if he would.

But after a while, she grew tired of keeping constant watch over
the boy, and would send him out alone, threatening him with severe
punishment if he did not return at the time she named, and still more
cruel treatment if he did not bring her as much money as she expected.

Phil was often to be seen in the City, hanging about the entrances to
Liverpool Street and Broad Street stations. In all weathers he was sent
out, and it was a hard life he led. There came to be a very sad look in
his blue eyes, and a glance at the little maimed form might well make
the most cautious forego prudence and drop a copper into his hand. As a
rule, Phil met with many who were soft-hearted enough to give to him.
But there were days when the police seemed especially on the alert, or
when every one appeared in too great a hurry to pay heed to him, for
the busy crowds who pass along the City streets are not easily arrested
by a call for alms. And when his pockets were light, Phil went back to
his mother in fear and trembling.


Several weeks passed ere Phil found his way to the hospital. He had not
forgotten his purpose.

He thought of it daily, and longed to see Sister Kathleen again, but
fear of his mother, who always questioned him closely as to his doings,
for some time withheld him from making the attempt.

At last, when summer had come, Phil had the opportunity for which he
had been waiting.

One morning, his mother roughly told him that he had better take
himself off till nightfall, as she was going out for the day and would
lock up her room.

"And mind that you make good use of the time," she added, "for I shall
want a nice supper when I come back, and you must bring the money for
it."

Phil needed no second bidding to be off. The freedom thus accorded was
as welcome to him as a holiday to a schoolboy. Hastening out, he lost
no time in getting away from Shoreditch. It was a bright June day. Even
in the City, the heat was not great, and when, leaving the more crowded
thoroughfares, Phil found his way on to the Thames Embankment, a fresh
breeze met him as he went westwards.

Instinctively, he had turned in the right direction for the hospital.
But he wandered on a long, long way without feeling sure that he was
getting any nearer to it. What a big, big place London seemed to the
little crippled-lad, dragging himself wearily along the pavements!
Sometimes he paused to beg of the passers-by or to rest for a while
on a doorstep, but though he grew hot and tired and faint for want of
food, he was still bent on reaching the hospital.

Asking his way and following as well as he could the confusing
directions given to him, Phil at last arrived at his destination, and
found himself before the main entrance to the hospital. Trembling from
fatigue and fear, he ascended the steps with difficulty, and entered
the large hall.

"Now then, what do you want, pray?"

The question, put in deep, severe tones by a tall, broad-shouldered,
soldierly-looking man in official livery, made little Phil fairly shake
from head to foot. He had never seen this porter before, and the august
mien and authoritative air of the dignitary looking down on him from
his superior height, with stern disapproval in his glance, took from
Phil all courage.

"Please, sir," he could only gasp, "can I see Sister Kathleen?"

"No, you can't," was the abrupt reply. "Sister Kathleen's not here. She
has been ill, and has gone away for a change. Now then, be off with
you. We don't want any beggars hanging about."

Phil went without a word. The porter's harsh manner so frightened him
that he did not dare to put another question, nor even thought of
asking to see one of the nurses in whose charge he had been.

He was bitterly disappointed. For weeks, the only ray of light in his
sad young life had come from the hope of seeing Sister Kathleen again,
and now that hope was destroyed; for it seemed to him as he passed out
of the hospital gates that he should never see either Sister Kathleen
or his Aunt Lizzie any more.

He made his way to the Strand, which was familiar ground to him, and
there began to beg. The child's face was so sorrowful, and his voice
had such a plaintive ring, that many a one looked on him with pity and
gave him money.

Presently, Phil ventured to exchange one of his pennies for a roll of
bread. After looking cautiously around, to see that no policeman was
near, he crept into a sheltered doorway and sat down to rest and take
his meal. He had not been there many minutes when a girl passed by,
carrying a basket filled with little nosegays. As he glanced at her,
Phil started up with a cry of delight.

"Fan, Fan!" he cried.

The girl looked round astonished, but she was no less pleased than Phil
when she saw who had called her.

"Why, Phil, it's never you!" she exclaimed.

"Yes, it's me," he said breathlessly. "And oh, Fan, I've been to the
hospital to see Sister Kathleen, and she isn't there; she's gone away."

"Is she? Why, how's that?" asked Fan.

"The man said she had been ill," replied Phil.

"Dear me!" said Fan. "Well, do you know, Phil, I've seen Sister
Kathleen. Many a time I've looked about the streets for you, hoping I
might see you to tell you, but neither you nor your mother were ever to
be seen anywhere."

"We live a long way from here," said Phil. "But where did you see
Sister Kathleen?"

"Why, she came to see you, of course; don't you know she said she
would? I happened to be in the alley that evening, and when I saw her
coming along so neat and nice in her long cloak and black bonnet, I
knew who she was at once. So, when I heard her asking for you, I went
forward and told her that you and your mother had gone away, and nobody
knew where you were living. She looked so sorry. I saw tears come into
her eyes, Phil. And then she said that if I should find out where you
were living, she'd be ever so much obliged to me if I'd let her know."

"Did she?" said Phil, looking pleased.

"Yes, she said just that in so many words. Where are you living, Phil?"

"17 St. Bride's Lane, Shoreditch," said Phil. "But you won't be able to
tell her, Fan. She's gone away from the hospital."

"Won't she never come back no more?" asked Fan.

Phil shook his head. "I don't know," he said.

"P'raps she will," said Fan; "anyhow, I'll ask some day when I'm
passing the hospital. Don't say anything to your mother about it, Phil."

"Oh no!" said Phil, surprised that Fan should think it necessary to say
this, since he had no confidences with his mother. He remembered now
that it was getting late, and his mother would be very angry if he kept
her waiting for supper.

"I must go home," he said, taking hold of his crutch and wearily
raising himself from the doorstep. "Come a little way with me, Fan."

Fan agreed, and walked with him to the bottom of Fleet Street, where
they parted, and Phil went on alone. By this time he was very tired,
and it was all he could do to keep moving on till St. Bride's Lane was
gained. It was almost dark when at length he reached home. He went
up-stairs to Moll's room with dread, expecting that she would punish
him for coming so late. But to his surprise the door was still locked,
and his mother was nowhere to be seen. Somewhat relieved, Phil sat down
on the landing to await her return. So worn out was he that he had not
sat there long ere he fell asleep.

He was roused by some one falling over him in the dark, and then
beginning to scold him violently for being where he was. It was not his
mother, however, but the woman who lived in the next room.

"Waiting for your mother, are you?" she said, as Phil, recovering from
his bewilderment, explained why he was there. "Then you'll have to
wait, it strikes me. Your mother won't be home to-night, nor for many
nights to come, I fancy."

"Why not?" asked Phil sleepily. "Where is she?"

"Where is she? Why, in gaol, to be sure. She's been taken up for
receiving stolen goods from a servant gal."

"Will they keep her there?" asked Phil, too heavy with sleep to
comprehend exactly what had happened, but glad to learn that he need
not expect his mother immediately.

"Little fear; for it's as clear as daylight that she knew the things
were stolen. She'll be tried in a few days, and if they don't give her
six months, I'm much mistaken."

So saying, the woman passed on into her room.

And Phil, curling himself up, was soon asleep again.

He woke the next morning with aching head and limbs, and a general
sense of weariness and sickness. He picked himself up from his hard
couch, and made his way down-stairs and into the street. The hour was
early, and the morning air as fresh as it ever was in that narrow
street, but it failed to revive Phil.

He dragged himself along wearily till he came to a coffee stall, where
some workmen were having breakfast. Phil bought a cup of coffee and
a slice of bread and butter. But whilst he drank the coffee eagerly,
he found to his surprise that he could not get on with the bread and
butter. He was fain to give the greater part of it to a half-starved
cur, which, judging him perhaps as uncared for as itself, made friendly
advances to him.

Phil had not forgotten the news of his mother which he had heard on
the previous night, but it had made such a confused impression on his
mind that he almost fancied it was a dream. But he soon learned that it
was true, for when he returned to the house, he found the neighbours
talking about his mother's arrest. And one and another told him that
he need not expect to see her for some time to come. One of the women
asked him how he would get along without his mother.

Phil made no reply, but he thought he could get along very well, if
only his head would not ache so badly and he did not feel so weak and
queer.

The door of his mother's room was still locked, and Phil tried in vain
to push it open. But the woman who lived in the next room called her
husband to his help, and the man succeeded in forcing back the lock.

When it was known that the door was open, all the inhabitants of the
house flocked into the room, eager to look at Moll's belongings. Later
in the day came a policeman, who searched every hole and corner of
Moll's room, and took possession of certain things he found there. But
no one paid much attention to Phil, who hung about, feeling sick and
wretched.

When at last the neighbours' curiosity was satisfied, and Moll's room
deserted, Phil, conscious of a weary longing for rest, lay down on the
bed. And there, alone and uncared for, he remained all day, dozing most
of the time, but waking at intervals to a sense of pain and thirst and
general discomfort.

CHAPTER XI.

A SAD STORY.

IT was true that Sister Kathleen had been out of health, and had left
the hospital for a while. But she was better, and, although the porter
had not chosen to tell Phil so, she was expected to return to the
hospital on the following day. A hearty welcome awaited her there, for
she had been missed alike by doctors, nurses, and patients.

As she was resting in her own room, word was brought to Sister Kathleen
that Miss Austen had called at the hospital that morning, and, learning
that she was expected in the afternoon, had decided to come again
later, being very anxious to see her. The Sister was pleased to hear
this, for she hoped Miss Austen might bring her news of little Phil,
concerning whom she had many uneasy thoughts.

The porter did not think it necessary to inform Sister Kathleen that a
little crippled boy had come to the hospital on the previous day and
asked to see her. He was only some miserable little beggar, who had no
right to exist, according to the porter's ideas, and certainly should
not be allowed to trouble so important a person as Sister Kathleen.

The Sister was taking her tea when Miss Austen arrived. In spite of
her training to self-control, she started as her visitor entered, so
changed was Lizzie Austen. She had formerly looked pale and melancholy,
but now there was a brilliant flush in her cheeks, which yet were
thin and wasted, the cheek-bones showing their hard outlines beneath
the crimson glow. A fire seemed to burn in her eyes, which looked
unnaturally large, with dark, hollow circles beneath. Sister Kathleen
felt a momentary sense of alarm as she met their wild, troubled gaze.

"How do you do, Miss Austen? I am glad to see you again," she said
almost mechanically, in her usual kind manner, as she took her
visitor's hand. Even through the glove, the burning heat of the hand
made itself felt.

Miss Austen's whole appearance was changed. Her hair was tumbled, her
bonnet awry, as though she had attired herself in careless haste. She
cut short the Sister's words of greeting with the eager question,—

"Where is little Phil? Oh, tell me, where is he?"

"I cannot tell you; I do not know," said Sister Kathleen.

"Not know! Oh, you 'must' know!" was the impatient reply. "You have
seen him since he left the hospital?"

"Unhappily, I have not," said Sister Kathleen. "Within a week of his
leaving us, I sought him at the address his mother gave, but the woman
and child had left the house, and no one could tell me where they had
gone."

A wild cry broke from Lizzie Austen.

"Oh!" she cried, wringing her hands and raising her eyes as if in
despairing protest to One above. "God have mercy on me! My punishment
is greater than I can bear!"

Sister Kathleen looked at her in wonder and fear. Had trouble disturbed
the balance of her mind? Was this strange excitement about little Phil
a form of madness?

"Do not speak so," said the Sister, taking her by the arm and leading
her to a chair; "you do not know what you are saying. You are
overexcited. Sit down and rest. You have been ill, I fear?"

"Yes, I have been ill," said Lizzie, sinking down with an air of
exhaustion, and putting her hand to her head as if to still its
throbbing; "I have been very ill. They thought I should die, but I knew
the rest of death was not yet for me. I cannot die till I have suffered
the full penalty of my sin, or till I have made atonement—oh, that that
were possible! You think I am talking wildly, but if you knew what a
wicked wretch I am, you would spurn me from your presence."

"That I am sure I should not," said Sister Kathleen, anxious to soothe
her. "But now tell me about your illness. What was it?"

"Oh, it was all the mind, the mind!" cried Lizzie. "The doctor said
so at last. I could not rest; I could not forget. I used to hear
little Phil crying and reproaching me every hour of the day and night.
Sometimes I fancied that I had him in my arms, and then Moll Dutton
would come and take him from me, and I cried and struggled, but I could
not get him again. Oh, it was terrible what I endured! Even now I hear
Phil's voice at times. I have been wandering through the streets all
day looking for him, but in vain."

"You should not have done so," said Sister Kathleen, distressed by the
conviction that here indeed was a mind trembling on the borders of
insanity. "How could your friends let you come to London alone?"

"No one knew," said Lizzie; "I came away without saying a word to
father. I could not rest at home. They will think that the fever
returned, and I wandered away without knowing what I was doing. I did
so once when I was ill. I wanted to find Phil, and I got up and went
out. But some one saw me in the field and brought me back. I was ill
for weeks after that."

"But how anxious your father will be when he misses you!" said Sister
Kathleen. "Have you thought of that?"

"Yes," said Lizzie, "but I cannot go back till I have learned something
about Phil. Oh, do help me, do help me to find him!"

"I will certainly give you all the help in my power," said Sister
Kathleen. "But, dear Miss Austen, why are you so troubled about little
Phil? Have you heard anything of him since he left the hospital?"

"No, oh no! That is the misery of it!" exclaimed Lizzie excitedly.

She paused and looked at Sister Kathleen, as if hesitating whether
she should explain her words. The tender pity of the Sister's glance
decided her, for she suddenly rose, and, throwing herself on her knees
beside the other, said, in broken, tremulous accents, "Oh, you are a
good woman! You will be merciful. I will tell you all; I must speak to
some one, or I shall go mad."

"Yes, tell me all your trouble," said Sister Kathleen gently. "Whatever
it may be, I am sure that you have suffered greatly."

"Suffered! Oh, verily! You wonder why I am so troubled about little
Phil. Listen—I am Phil's mother."

"Phil's mother!" exclaimed the Sister incredulously, believing for the
moment that this was a delusion of the poor overwrought brain. "You
Phil's mother! It is impossible. Moll Dutton is his mother."

"She pretends to be, but she is not really his mother. Phil is my
child. He was born in Paris six years ago."

"You astonish me," said Sister Kathleen. "How can it be? You are not
married?"

"No," said Lizzie, in a low, bitter tone, whilst a painful flush
suffused her neck and brow; "but I once thought that I was. I will try
to tell you my sad story.

"It is about eight years, though it seems to me as if a whole lifetime
lay between, since I left home to spend some months in London. My life
had been a happy one till then. I was the only child in my home, and
my parents loved me far, far more than I deserved. Alas, like too many
girls, I did not know when I was happy! I grew weary of country life.
I longed to see London, and persuaded my parents, sorely against their
will, to let me go for a while to a cousin of my father who was a
milliner in the West End. Oh that I had never left my peaceful, happy
home! But it is worse than vain to think of that now.

"I came to London, and the fascinations of town life took hold upon me.
My cousin made much of me, and encouraged my vanity. She used to tell
me that my face was so pretty, it set off any bonnet I might put on for
the customers to see. The people with whom I associated were gay and
unprincipled, and I fell completely under their influence. Yet I knew
better. I knew that there was a life beyond this. I had been taught to
fear the God to whom I must one day give an account of my actions, but
I put from me the thought of God.

"My heart grew cold towards my parents. I no longer cared to go home.
Since those days, I have often wept to think how my indifference must
have wounded my mother's heart. But I must hasten to the bitter end of
my story.

"In my cousin's business I made the acquaintance of a Frenchman, the
traveller for a Paris house. He was a man of thirty, handsome, dark,
with polished manners and a gallant bearing, that fascinated my poor
foolish girl's heart. His manner told me that he admired me, and soon
a warmer feeling than admiration was expressed by his words and looks.
He came to my cousin's shop far more frequently than business required,
and I was proud to think that I was the attraction that drew him there.
I believed that he loved me, and my love went forth to meet his.

"But he spoke no word of marriage for some time. Not till my cousin's
sudden death had thrown me into perplexity with regard to my future did
he ask me to marry him, but then he urged me to take the step without
delay. When I mentioned my parents, and the necessity of gaining their
consent, he persuaded me to seal my fate without consulting them. They
would be prejudiced against a foreigner, he said, and would certainly
oppose our marriage. But if I took the matter into my own hands, and
got married, they would soon forgive me for so doing. How could I
listen to such persuasions you will ask? Ah, how indeed! I am amazed
now to think how weak and foolish I was. Oh, is there greater misery
than to look back on the fatal steps so blindly taken, that have made
your life a wreck, full of remorse and despair? But I loved that man
with a love that knew no shadow of distrust.

"We were married at a registry office one gloomy, foggy day. He did
not wish a religious service, nor did I, for deep in my heart I felt
that it would be a mockery to ask God's blessing on our union. Yet the
prospect before me seemed bright. My husband was well to do. We settled
in expensive lodgings, not far from my former place of business. By
his desire, I allowed my parents to suppose that I was still engaged
in this business, and their letters were sent to me there. I was very
unhappy at thus deceiving them; indeed, from the first there was no
real happiness in my married life.

"A few weeks after my marriage, my father, who rarely left home, gave
himself the trouble of coming to London in order to see me and assure
himself that I was well. The remembrance of that day stings me with
shame even now, for by my husband's orders, I still hid from him the
truth, and had to invent falsehood after falsehood to account for my
being at liberty to accompany him about town. I longed yet dreaded to
make confession, for I feared that he would never forgive me.

"Already my husband was growing indifferent to me, and soon every sign
of the gallant lover had vanished. He was often absent for weeks in
Paris, and when left alone, I was sad enough. At last, a time came
when he stayed away so long that I wrote imploring him to return, for,
weak and depressed, I could not bear the loneliness longer. He wrote
in reply, saying that he did not intend to return to London. He had
obtained a fresh business appointment in Paris, and should reside there
for the future. I was at liberty to make what plans I liked for my
future. He enclosed a sum of money for my immediate necessities, and
advised me to return, after a while, to the business in which I had
been formerly engaged.

"The letter came to me like a cruel blow. It bewildered me utterly. I
could not understand it. Surely, if he were going to live in Paris,
I must live there too. Was I not his wife? What could he mean by
advising me to return to business? As I pondered his strange words,
my bewilderment increased. I could not rest without an explanation,
nor would I be content with a written one. Though I shrank from the
wearisome journey, I resolved to go to my husband without delay.
Excitement gave me strength for what I had to do. Within twenty-four
hours of my receiving his letter, I was in Paris.

"Never can I forget how my husband received me when I arrived at the
house where he was living. My husband, I say, but he met me with the
astounding news that he was not my husband. A marriage contracted
by English law in London meant nothing in Paris, he said. He was
sorry for me, but his parents would never consent to his marrying an
Englishwoman. I had no legal claim upon him, and we must separate.

"I was confounded; I could not believe that his words were true. But
in vain I declared that I was his lawful wife, and pointed to the ring
upon my finger in attestation of the fact. In vain I sobbed and pleaded
with him to have mercy on me. He was not to be moved to acknowledge me
as his wife. With cold, hard cruelty, he made me aware of my deplorable
position, and then required that I would quit the house in which I had
no right to be sheltered. Heart-broken, I left him, and went out alone
into the streets of Paris.

"I cannot dwell upon the misery of the days that followed. Alone and
friendless in Paris, knowing little of the language, I felt utterly
forsaken. A kind-hearted Frenchwoman, to whom I was led to confide my
sad story, advised me to seek advice at the British Embassy. I went
there, but, though kindly received, could find no redress. They told
me that my experience was a common one. The man I had believed to be
my husband had spoken the truth. By the cruel French law, I was not
his wife, and had no right to call myself so. He was at liberty to
repudiate me.

"I went back to my lodgings feeling crushed and hopeless. On the
following day, my child was born. I made no second effort to move the
heart of the man who had so wronged me. As soon as I was able, I left
Paris without seeing him again.

"Dark and hopeless the future looked to me, when with my helpless babe
I arrived in London. I felt little love for my child then. I thought
only of how I could find some one to take charge of him, and thus set
me free to seek employment. I took a lodging in a poor neighbourhood,
for my funds were getting low, and thus I met with Moll Dutton. I had
known her before. She used to come to my cousin's to buy cast-off
clothing. Her appearance was more respectable than it now is, yet I
suspected that she was a bad, unprincipled woman. Somehow she beguiled
me into telling her my story, and when she offered to nurse the child
for me, I scarcely hesitated ere I gave him into her care. He was a
sickly little creature, and—God forgive me!—I hoped he would not live,
for I felt my child to be a burden from which I longed to be free.

"With a view to inquiring whether they had an opening for me, I went to
the house of business formerly conducted by my cousin. There I found
a telegram which had been awaiting me for a couple of days, as no one
knew where I was. My mother was very ill, it said, and I must return
home at once if I would see her alive. I had written to my father
before leaving London, to tell him that I was going to Paris, but since
then I had let him know nothing of my movements. I led him to believe
that I had gone to Paris to make purchases and study the fashions.

"As soon as I recovered from the shock of this sad news, I telegraphed
to say that I would come home without delay. Leaving my babe in Moll
Dutton's care, I started by the next train, and at nightfall reached
my home. Oh, the bitterness of that return! I had thought that I would
throw myself at my father's feet and confess all as soon as I crossed
the threshold. But when he met me with tenderest greeting, and gently
told me that my mother was dying, my words were stayed. I stood by my
mother's bed. She could not speak, but she knew me, and smiled on me
ere she passed away. As I watched her die, it gave me inexpressible
anguish to think how I had deceived her. If only I could have died too!

"Whilst her still form lay within the house, and on the day when we
committed it to the ground, I kept my secret locked within my breast.
My father saw how wretched I was, but he imagined that it was natural
grief for my mother's death which bowed me so low. He mourned her loss
deeply, and when he implored me never to leave him again, I had not the
heart to say that I must do so. Every one took it for granted that I
should now settle down at home; and I wished that I were free to do so,
for I had seen enough of London now.

"I returned to London at the end of a week, but I promised my father
that I would soon come back to him.

"I went at once to Moll Dutton.

"Can you understand the temptation it was to me, when she offered to
take the child off my hands and bring him up as her own, if I would
pay her down a certain sum of money? No! You cannot understand it, but
to me her proposal seemed to offer such relief. I dreaded my father's
anger if he should learn how I had deceived him. I knew how keenly he
would feel the disgrace of my false marriage, and I shrank from the
shame that would be mine if our neighbours at Danesbury knew that I
was a mother, yet had no legal right to the name of wife. So I caught
at Moll's suggestion. I had little difficulty in raising the sum she
mentioned, for my mother had left me money, but I had to tell more lies
to my father to account for my wishing to realize it so quickly.

"With hard, selfish haste the compact was sealed. I made over my child
to Moll Dutton, to be from henceforth her child, with whom I would
never interest myself more.

"But when I thus gave him up, the instincts of a mother asserted
themselves, in spite of my selfish will, and my heart was torn with
pain. Moll took care not to let me see my child again. I paid her the
whole sum agreed upon, and she went off with the child, I knew not
whither.

"But now I was to learn at what a cost I had purchased my liberty.

"I went home to my father, and tried to be a dutiful daughter, but from
that day I knew no peace. The maternal affections Nature had implanted
in me were not to be stifled. Day and night my heart cried out for my
babe. It was torture to me to know that somewhere in London he was
living, bereft of my love and tenderness. This secret sorrow was almost
more than my mind could bear. I saw that the neighbours looked on me
with wonder, and deemed my ways strange; I knew that my father was
distressed by my constant melancholy, but in vain I tried to overcome
it. The misery I had brought upon myself seemed greater than I could
endure.

"As often as I had an excuse for so doing, I came to London for a
day. And as I walked along the streets, I found myself looking about
for my child, forgetting that if I met him he would be changed past
recognition.

"At last, when some years had passed, and time had failed to reconcile
me to my sorrow, I began to make determined efforts to trace Moll
Dutton. By a strange chance, I discovered where she was living. I went
to the house one day when she was out, and found my boy, grown into the
little Phil you know. I saw, alas! that he was sadly neglected. Ah,
what I suffered as I held him in my arms! I could not take him away,
even if Moll would have given him up. It seemed impossible to escape
from the meshes of evil I had woven about my life.

"When Moll came back and found me with him, she was very angry, and
told me I had broken my word, for I had promised never to interfere
with him.

"I pacified her with a gift of money, and she promised to treat
the child more kindly, and to let me know if he were ill or wanted
anything. I had little hope that the promise would be kept, though I
could see that Moll was very anxious to retain the child.

"I did not know till afterwards how wickedly she made gain of him,
keeping him ragged and ill-fed in order that his appearance might
attract pity and bring her money. After this visit to little Phil, I
was more wretched than before, for I knew that to spare myself pain,
I had doomed my child to a life of suffering. Again Moll Dutton moved
away, and I lost all knowledge of her.

"I heard no more of my little Phil until last November, when, to my
surprise, one day I received a letter from Moll Dutton telling me that
Phil was ill, and asking me if I would not send her some money to
meet the expenses of his illness. Instead of sending money, I went to
London that very day, and found my way to the address from which Moll
had written. Her letter was a ruse to get money, and by answering it
in person, I defeated her purpose. She was forced to own that Phil was
not with her, and I drew from her the truth that he had met with an
accident and was lying in this hospital.

"I could not resist the hungry longing I had to see my child again.
I came here, and to my joy he had not forgotten me, but welcomed me
gladly, calling me by the name I had taught him was mine—'Aunt Lizzie.'
But you know the rest—you know now why I was so unhappy at the thought
of Phil's going back to his miserable life with Moll Dutton."

"Yes, I know now," said Sister Kathleen gently; "I understand many
things that puzzled me at the time. Oh, what you must have suffered
under this concealment! With all my heart I pity you."

CHAPTER XII.

NEWS OF PHIL.

LIZZIE AUSTEN had told her story in a quiet, hard tone, resolutely
suppressing emotion, and Sister Kathleen had refrained from
interrupting her with question or remark. But now, beneath Sister
Kathleen's gentle sympathy, the restraint she had put on herself broke
down. She gave one look into the kind eyes, full of tears, which were
bent on her, and then bowed her head on the Sister's lap and burst into
heavy sobs.

"Oh, it is good of you to pity me. You might well despise me, loathe
me, for being what I am—a woman who has cast off her own child, and
doomed it to suffering to screen herself. How can you endure to look
upon such a heartless wretch?"

"Not heartless," said Sister Kathleen, throwing her arm around the
slight, frail form, shaken with sobs, "not heartless, or you would not
have suffered as you have. Oh, I pity you, but the Lord Jesus pities
you far more. He is very sorry that you have brought such misery upon
yourself; for, though you have been cruelly wronged, it was, as you
acknowledge, your own self-will that led you into the snare."

"True, true," said Lizzie mournfully; "it was all my own fault—that is
the misery of it. But how can you say that the Lord is sorry for me? Is
He not angry with the wicked? Surely His anger must be fierce against
me—a woman who has been false to her most sacred trust. Oh, do you know
the words,—

 "'Can a woman's tender care
 Cease towards the child she bare?'

"That hymn is often sung at our chapel, and whenever I have heard it,
I have felt wild with shame and misery to think what a vile, heartless
wretch I was."

"But think of the comfort of the truth it sets forth," said Sister
Kathleen; "a mother's love may fail, but the Lord's love is unchanging.
More tender, more compassionate than the heart of a mother is the heart
of Jesus Christ, our Saviour. No one has such pity for human sorrows,
no one judges so kindly of human sins.

 "'Come unto Me, ye weary,' He says, 'and I will give you rest.'"

"I am weary enough," said Lizzie, in a low, hopeless tone, "but there
is no rest for me.

 "'There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked.'

"You do not know how those words have haunted me."

"There is indeed no peace for the wicked," said Sister Kathleen, "but
there is pardon and peace for those who turn in penitence to the
Saviour.

 "'He that covereth his sins shall not prosper; but whoso confesseth and
forsaketh them shall have mercy.'"

"I have covered my sins, I know," said Lizzie, "but what would you have
me do? My father would never forgive me if he knew."

"Nevertheless, you must tell him all," said Sister Kathleen.

"Impossible!" exclaimed Lizzie, starting up. "Do not ask me to do that.
I could not tell him now, after hiding the truth for so long—it would
be impossible!"

 "'If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our
sins,—'"

said the Sister. "There is no blessing promised to those who hide their
sin."

"I know, I know!" cried Lizzie, wringing her hands. "That is what the
minister said. He came to see me when I was ill, and he kept telling me
that I must confess my sins. I believed he guessed that I had some dark
secret. But how can I confess? You do not know how bitterly my father
feels towards any one who deceives him. It would make him wretched to
know that his own child had deceived him. And he is an old man—the
shock might kill him."

Sister Kathleen was silent. She felt how hard and painful this first
step towards a better life would be, but it seemed to her the only
right step.

After a few moments, Lizzie said, in tremulous accents, "You think me
very wrong?"

"I am very sorry for you," said Sister Kathleen; "tell me, have you
confessed your sins to God?"

"I have tried to," said Lizzie brokenly, "but my heart seems cold and
dead. I have no hope—I cannot pray."

"When you have told your father all, you will be able to confess your
sins to your Father in heaven," said Sister Kathleen. "And how about
your little Phil? Can you do your duty by him if you continue this
course of concealment?"

"God help me!" cried Lizzie wildly. "My little Phil! I must find him, I
must have him in my care. I should have done so differently by him if I
had not been afraid of my father's learning the truth. Moll knew that I
was most anxious to keep it from him, and she used to threaten that if
I broke our agreement and took Phil from her, she would go down to my
home and tell my father all. And I believed she would do it!"

"What a miserable thing for you to be so much in that woman's power!"
said Sister Kathleen. "But you cannot let this state of things
continue. God will help you, if, trusting in Him, you resolve to do the
right, whatever it may cost you."

Again there was silence. Lizzie had turned away, and her face was
hidden in her hands. Presently she raised it, and asked again, "What
would you have me do?"

Sister Kathleen had no hesitation in replying, "Return without delay to
your father, who must be sadly anxious about you. Tell him all without
reserve. Tell him that you mean to seek little Phil, and when you find
him, to fulfil henceforth the duties of a mother to him."

"I dare not," said Lizzie; "and I want to find little Phil. I cannot go
back till I have seen him."

"You must go back," said the Sister firmly; "you cannot seek him
to-night. I will send some one to make inquiries about him to-morrow,
and will let you know the result. I implore you not to add to your
trouble by further concealment. Do what is right now, and God will be
with you. There is forgiveness and peace and a new life before you, I
trust, if you will but abandon your false position."

Lizzie Austen did not immediately reply. Her face was again hidden in
her hands. The Sister hoped that she was praying for strength to do the
right.

Presently Lizzie looked up, and said in quiet, firm tones, in marked
contrast to her previous excitement of manner, "I will go home, and I
will tell my father all. Whatever the result may be, I cannot suffer
more than I have suffered."

"It will cost you much to do so, I have no doubt," said the Sister,
"but in the end, you will be thankful that you have made the effort."

Lizzie had risen, and was preparing to go out.

Sister Kathleen hastily put on her cloak and bonnet, saying that she
would go with her to the station.

Lizzie tried to dissuade her, in vain. And when they went forth into
the streets, she found herself so weak and tremulous that she was glad
to lean on her companion for support.

"Will you pray for me?" she whispered, as the train was about to start.

"Indeed I will," said Sister Kathleen; "and for Phil too. May the Lord
be with you both!"

Sister Kathleen walked back from the station musing on the sad history
she had heard. As she re-entered the hall of the hospital, her glance
fell on an untidy, ill-dressed girl, with a basket of faded flowers on
her arm, who stood there engaged in rather stormy altercation with the
porter.

As she saw the Sister, the girl darted forward, exclaiming, "Ah, you
are the lady who came to see Phil."

"Yes, and you are the girl who told me he had gone away," said Sister
Kathleen. "Have you brought me news of Phil?"

"I met him in the street yesterday," said Fan; "he'd been here to see
you, but they told him—" here the girl darted an indignant glance at
the porter—"that you'd left the hospital. Poor Phil was sore cut up
about it."

"Oh dear! I am very sorry that he missed me," said the Sister. "Did he
tell you where he was living?"

"Of course," returned Fan; "that's what I've come to tell you. He lives
at 17 St. Bride's Lane, Shoreditch."

"Oh, I am very glad to know this!" said Sister Kathleen, her face
lighting up with pleasure. "Thank you so much for bringing me the news.
You have done a kindness both to me and Phil."

She put some money into Fan's hand as she spoke, but the sweet smile
with which she thanked her was the flower-girl's best reward.

CHAPTER XIII.

PHIL'S PRAYER IS ANSWERED.

SISTER KATHLEEN'S hospital duties made it impossible for her to seek
Phil as early as she would have liked on the following day. Without
delay, however, she despatched a telegram to Lizzie Austen to inform
her where Phil was to be found.

But in less time than she could have made the journey from Chelmsford
after receiving the telegram, Miss Austen appeared at the hospital
and requested to see Sister Kathleen. As she glanced at her downcast,
miserable face the Sister guessed what had happened.

"I have told him," Lizzie said, with quivering lips; "and he has cast
me off, as I knew he would. He says I have disgraced him and disgraced
my mother, and that he wishes I were not his daughter, and he never
wants to see me again. Oh, my heart feels like to break when I think of
the hard things he said."

She struggled for a few moments to maintain her composure, but in vain;
the sobs would come.

"I am very sorry for you," said Sister Kathleen gently, "but you must
remember how unprepared your father was to receive such a confession.
It must have given him a severe shock, and it is not strange that he
should utter hasty words in his anger and grief. After a time, he will
repent of what he said."

Lizzie shook her head.

"I cannot think so," she said; "you do not know my father, and how he
scorns all underhand ways. He will never forgive me for deceiving him.
And he loved me so. Oh, if my father is so hard upon me, how can I hope
that God will forgive me?"

"God is infinitely more pitiful than man," said Sister Kathleen; "He
will forgive you for Jesus Christ's sake. And now, as a token that
God is merciful, I have good news for you. I have heard where Phil is
living."

The unhappy mother sprang from her seat, exclaiming eagerly,—

"Oh, my child! My little Phil! Where is he? I must go to him at once."

"He is at some distance from here—in Shoreditch," said Sister Kathleen.
"We will go together in search of him, if you are sure you feel equal
to the undertaking."

"I am strong now," said Lizzie; "the thought of seeing my child gives
me new life. Oh, let us lose no time."

Sister Kathleen hurried her preparations, and in a short time, they
were seated in a cab, being driven towards the City. The driver had
difficulty in finding St. Bride's Lane. But at last, after some
tiresome driving to and fro, they entered the narrow street and
alighted at the door of No. 17.

"Does a woman named Dutton live here?" inquired Sister Kathleen of the
idle, slatternly women who gathered about the door to see the ladies
alight from the cab.

"Moll Dutton? To be sure she did live here, but she's not here now."

"Not here! Oh, where is she?" asked the Sister, in disappointed tones,
whilst Lizzie's heart sickened in new despair.

"Oh, only in gaol. Moll was not too particular how she came by things,
and the police found her out at last. She had stolen things on her
person and in her room. She was a deep one, was Moll, but she's caught
at last."

There was a strange mingling of admiration for Moll's cunning, with
satisfaction at its defeat, in the tones of the woman who gave this
explanation.

"But she had a child—a little boy; what became of him?" asked Sister
Kathleen.

"Oh, Phil, the little cripple. He's here somewhere. I say, where's
Phil? Has any one seen him?"

No one answered. Apparently Phil had not been seen of late.

"Happen he's up in his mother's room," said one. "We've children enough
of our own to look after here without troubling about other folk's
brats, let alone a gaol-bird's."

Sister Kathleen stayed but to ask where Moll's room was, and then
hurried up the narrow, dirty staircase, closely followed by Lizzie
Austen.

"Phil," called the Sister, as she opened the door of Moll Dutton's
close, disordered room. "Phil, are you here?"

There was no reply. Phil's mother pushed past her into the room. A cry
broke from her as she saw on the bed a tiny form stretched motionless.

"My child! My child!" she cried. And then, as he did not stir, "Oh, is
he dead? Have I come too late?"

"He is not dead," said Sister Kathleen, pushing her gently aside and
bending over the child. "He has fainted, I think. Fetch me some water
quickly, if you please. And open the window. Ah, there! He is beginning
to revive."

As she bathed his brow and moistened his lips, the child opened his
eyes and looked with dazed vision at the kind face bent on him.

"Do you know me, Phil?" she said.

"Sister," the boy murmured, and then his heavy eyelids drooped again.

His mother began to sob.

Sister Kathleen turned upon her almost sharply.

"This is no time for crying. Run quickly and get milk and bread. The
poor child has been left to himself all day, and is faint for want of
nourishment, I believe."

In an instant, Lizzie was gone. With all speed she returned, bringing
the food.

Sister Kathleen broke off a morsel of the bread, dipped it in the milk,
and held it to the child's lips. The lips closed over it feebly at
first; then eagerly the food was taken. She held the cup to his lips,
and he drank eagerly, uttering a faint protest when she withdrew it. A
more life-like colour was creeping into the little face. There was a
look of satisfaction in the blue eyes as they met Sister Kathleen's.

"You know me, Phil?" she said again.

"Yes, Sister Kathleen," the child said faintly. "I asked Jesus to make
you come."

"Yes, He has led me to you," said the Sister. "I would have come
before, my poor little Phil, if I had known you were alone here, and so
ill."

"Yes, I'm very ill," said the child quietly, "but I don't mind now.
You'll soon make me better, Sister."

"If it please God, I will," she said brightly, as she felt his thin,
hot hand.

Phil moved his head slightly and tried to look about him.

"Where's mother?" he asked in a whisper, whilst a look of fear came
into his eyes.

Low as the words were uttered, his mother heard them.

"I'm here, Phil, here!" she cried, bending over him and drawing him to
her bosom, whilst she kissed with passionate fondness the little white
face. "My precious child! You are mine now, and I will never give you
up again."

Phil looked startled and amazed for a moment. But when he saw whose
face it was bent so close to his, a smile broke over his own.

"Why, you're not my mother!" he said. "You're Aunt Lizzie. Oh, Aunt
Lizzie, I am so glad you are come!" Then, with a feeble little laugh,
he added, "Mother would never have kissed me like that."

"God bless you, my own poor forsaken child!" she sobbed. "May He
forgive me for my wickedness!"

"Why are you crying, Aunt Lizzie?" he asked, in wonder. "Oh, you are
not going to leave me yet!"

Sister Kathleen had drawn back out of sight, and Phil saw only the face
of his mother gazing tenderly on him through her tears. He thought the
tears meant that she was about to bid him farewell.

"I will never leave you more, my child. Listen, Phil, I am your mother.
Moll Dutton was not really your mother. You are my own dear child, whom
God gave to me, but whom I wickedly forsook."

Phil looked at her in bewilderment.

"You my mother! How can that be?" he said. "Moll Dutton is my mother,
and a bad mother she is too. She's away now, but she'll come back
again, and then she'll beat me for getting ill. You'd better go away
before she comes, Aunt Lizzie; for she's awful when she's in her
tantrums."

"I'll not go from here, Phil, unless I take you with me. I'll never
leave you again. It is true, although you cannot understand it, that I
'am' your mother. God made me your mother."

"Did He?" said the boy, still puzzled. "But she won't let you have me,
I know. She'll want to keep me to beg for her."

"She 'must' let me have you, Phil. You are my child. Moll Dutton has
no right to you but that I gave her. It's true, my child. Ask Sister
Kathleen; she will tell you it is true."

"Yes, Phil, you may believe it," said Sister Kathleen, coming forward
and laying her hand on Lizzie Austen's arm; "this is your mother. Moll
Dutton will have nothing more to do with you."

A gleam of joy lit up the little wasted face.

"Oh," he cried, his weak tones quavering with delight, "I can hardly
believe it, but it has all come round as I prayed that it might. Fan
said it couldn't be, but I knew that God could manage it. He has taken
away my wicked mother and given me a good mother."

"Not a good mother, Phil," cried his mother, breaking again into sobs;
"a wicked mother—the worst mother that ever was—a mother who could cast
off her own child and leave it to suffer! God knows I do not deserve to
have you in my arms this moment."

Little Phil looked distressed at her emotion.

"But you'll be good," he said; "I know that you'll always be good to
me."

"I want to be good," she sobbed. "Thank God, there is hope for the
chief of sinners through Him who died upon the cross to save us. I'll
ask God every day to give me His Holy Spirit for the sake of Jesus
Christ, and to help me to be a good mother to you. Oh, if I could only
atone to you for my past neglect!"

"And you'll never leave me, Aunt Lizzie?" he said.

"Never as long as I live," she replied. "But, Phil, you must call me
mother now."

"Must I?" he said, looking troubled. "I'd rather call you Aunt Lizzie.
I don't want to think of mother any more."

His mother winced as from a blow. Phil's words gave her exquisite pain.
Here was one bitter result of her sin. The very name of mother had for
Phil a cruel sound. The tie between parent and child, which she had
wickedly dared to break, could never now be perfect, as it might have
been. All his life long, Phil would retain the memory of his early
years of hardship and pain. In his poor crippled body, he would bear a
lasting memorial of his mother's sin. Would not the day come, she asked
herself with keenest anguish, when he would reproach her for his being
as he was?

CHAPTER XIV.

PARDON AND PEACE.

SISTER KATHLEEN had soon to return to the hospital, but Phil's mother
remained with him. The Sister sent her such things as Phil most needed,
and she stayed there in Moll Dutton's miserable room till he was well
enough to be removed to a pleasanter dwelling. The feverish attack had
brought the child very low, but under his mother's care, he soon began
to mend. And in less than a week, they moved to some clean, nice rooms
which Sister Kathleen had taken for them at no great distance from the
hospital.

Here Lizzie Austen settled down to a quiet, lonely life of devotion to
her child. The income she derived from the property her mother had left
her could with care be made to suffice for the wants of herself and
Phil.

To the boy, his new life gave unmixed satisfaction. It was delightful
to have Aunt Lizzie always with him, ready to nurse him, pet him, amuse
him to his heart's content. Not that he made great demands on her
attention. It was joy enough for him at first to know that he need not
go forth to beg, and he was quite content to sit and watch his mother
as she sat sewing, sewing always on some garment for him. He wondered
sometimes why she was not so happy as he. For, though she tried hard
for his sake to be cheerful, Lizzie often sighed as she sat sewing, and
at times he saw tears drop upon her work.

Many a painful thought disturbed his mother's heart as she bent over
her sewing. The memory of the past still stung her. She could not look
upon her little crippled Phil without remembering that if she had been
true to her maternal responsibilities, her boy might now be whole and
strong. She could not think without pain of the feeble old man upon
whose declining days the knowledge of her wrongdoing had cast so dark a
shadow. She could fancy him passing long, lonely hours with no one to
render him the little services it was a daughter's right to perform. He
would sit alone, brooding over his wrongs and hardening his heart more
and more against her, even whilst he yearned for her presence.

Oh, how Lizzie longed for some tidings of her father as the weeks went
on! Once she ventured to write to him, but no answer came, though she
looked for it from day to day, and from week to week, till her heart
grew sick with hopeless longing whenever she saw the postman coming
down the street.


Time passed on. Summer and autumn had fled, and Christmas was close at
hand. But still Lizzie was living her sad, quiet life, finding comfort
only in her boy, who clung to her with a warm love and trust which
consoled her, whilst sharpening the sting of self-reproach.

Phil was looking forward to the joys of Christmas. Sister Kathleen had
invited him to the merrymaking at the hospital, and Phil was full of
delight at the thought of the Christmas tree and the good time he would
have. He caught up the tunes of the Christmas carols and sang them
lustily, till his mother's heart ached to hear him, for these old songs
had sad associations for her.

Fan too was to have a happy Christmas. Phil had not forgotten his old
friend. He had spoken of her to his mother, and Lizzie was ready to do
all in her power to befriend the girl who had been kind to her little
Phil when he so sorely needed a friend.

She had taken pleasure in preparing Christmas cheer for Fan and her
mother, and presents of warm clothing that would be serviceable to them
long after Christmas Day had gone by.

But Lizzie's sorrow grew heavier as Christmas approached. When the
memory of the Saviour's birth was kindling in Christian hearts more and
more of good-will towards others, it seemed especially sad that her
father's heart should be alienated from her. She could only pray that
God would soften his feelings towards her.

It was the 24th of December. Lizzie, standing at her window, was
watching the postman as he passed down the street. Though she watched
him, she had no hope that he would bring her a letter. Even when she
saw him cross the road and heard his knock on the door below, she did
not think that the letter could be for her, though her heart began to
beat more quickly at the mere supposition.

A few moments later, however, there was a tap at her door, and her
landlady looked in to say, "Here's a letter for you, Mrs. Austen."

Lizzie took the letter with eager haste. Yes, it bore the Chelmsford
postmark; here was news of her father at last.

The letter was from Mr. Fennell, the minister at Danesbury. He said
that he wrote at her father's request, to beg that she would come home
without delay. Mr. Austen had been very ill, but was now, it was hoped,
on the way to recovery.

 "You will find your father changed in more ways than one," Mr. Fennell
wrote. "He is very anxious to see you. Let me beg that you will come as
soon as possible."

Lizzie was not long in making arrangements for her departure.
Consigning Phil to the care of Sister Kathleen, she set out.


It was early in the afternoon when she reached Chelmsford, but already
daylight was on the wane. The dull grey sky seemed to threaten snow,
and the wind was very keen as Lizzie started on her five miles' walk
along the hard bleak roads. But she was scarcely conscious of the
weather as she hastened along, eager only to reach the bend in the road
from which the meadow path led down to the old ivy-clad house. Nor did
she pay much heed to the sound of wheels on the road behind her, till
a voice called her by name, and she turned, to see Mr. Fennell looking
down on her from the little one-horse gig in which he was wont to drive
about the country.

"How are you, Miss Austen? I am so glad to see you. I did not think you
could have got here so soon. Pray get up beside me, and I'll drive you
to your gate."

Lizzie could not refuse. Mr. Fennell's warm grasp of her hand, as he
helped her up to the seat beside him, told her that, whatever he might
know or suspect, he was as willing to be her friend as ever.

"Your father will be very thankful that you have come," he said.

"How is he?" was all Lizzie could say.

"Better—getting well, I believe; it is the mind rather than the body
that suffers now, but your coming will do him good. Miss Austen, I must
tell you that your father has taken me into his confidence, and told me
the story of your troubles. I hope you will not mind my knowing?"

"Why should I mind?" asked Lizzie tremulously. "Mr. Fennell, I have
been brought too low in shame and penitence before God, to care for
what others may think of my history."

"No one could know it without feeling much sympathy for you," he said;
"you have been cruelly wronged."

Lizzie shivered at his words. She had loved the man who had so wronged
her. She could not think of him without pain. "I did very wrong; I
deserved to suffer," she said.

It seemed to Mr. Fennell that some one else deserved to suffer. But he
said nothing, and the rest of the way was passed in silence. At the
stile, he checked his horse.

Lizzie thanked him and sprang down. The next minute she was hastening
down the well-known path to her home. The house door was on the latch,
as it always was. She opened it and entered. All was quiet in the
sitting-room, but she could hear some one moving in the kitchen. She
did not go there.

She went at once to her father's room above, in the window of which
she had seen the gleam of fire-light as she came down the meadow. She
hesitated when she reached the door, and stood there trembling. In this
room her mother had died; it would be terrible if her father met her
there with bitter, reproachful words. But only for a moment did this
thought stay her. With a prayer for help, she opened the door and went
in.

[Illustration]

Her father was sitting alone in his high-backed cushioned chair by the
fire. The room was in gloom, save for the ruddy gleam from the fire,
which shone on the old man's bowed and wasted form and on his silvery
hair, as he leaned forward looking into the fire with sad and patient
gaze. He did not turn at the sound of Lizzie's step. He imagined it was
the servant who had entered.

"Never mind about the candles, Martha," he said; "I like the fire-light
better."

Lizzie went forward and stood beside him. "Father," she said, "I have
come. Can you receive me? Can you forgive me now?"

"Lizzie!"

No need to ask if he forgave her, when she heard how he uttered her
name. The next moment Lizzie was kneeling by his side and her tears
were falling fast upon his coat-sleeve.

"Thank God you are come, my child!" said the old man brokenly. "Don't
talk of forgiveness."

"But I must," said Lizzie.

"Nay, nay, I was to blame. I was hard and cruel to you. I forgot that
I too was a sinner, and God had shown mercy to me. I was like the
Pharisee, Lizzie; I thought myself so good and respectable. But my God
has humbled me; He has brought me to see that I have treated Him far
worse than ever you treated me. Oh, child, can you forget my harsh
words?"

"Of course I can forget them," replied Lizzie, "but I deserved them
every one. I behaved very, very badly to you, father."

"Ay, but you had suffered enough; I need not have been so hard on you.
You had been deceived and forsaken. I thought of that when I was ill,
Lizzie. And when the minister came to see me, I told him all, and he
said I had been very wrong.

"'What would become of you,' he said, 'if your Father in heaven dealt
with you as you have dealt with your child?'

"That set me thinking, and I saw how sinful my conduct was."

"Never mind, father," she murmured; "I deserved it all, but oh, it is
such comfort to know that you forgive me!"

"Fully and freely, as I hope to be forgiven," he said.

Then a silence fell between them—one of those silences that are more
eloquent than words. Lizzie still knelt by her father's side. She held
his hand in both hers, and every now and then she pressed her lips upon
it.

Presently the stillness was broken by Martha's voice singing below. She
was singing the refrain of a Christmas hymn,—

 "'All glory be to God on high,
 And to the earth be peace.'"

"What is she singing about?" asked the old man suddenly.

"Glory to God, on earth peace," said Lizzie. "Father, do you know that
to-morrow will be Christmas Day?"

"Ay," said her father; "the day on which our Saviour was born. He came
to save us from our sins and give us peace with God. Lizzie, have you
found peace in believing in Jesus?"

"I have, father," she said; "I thought there could be no peace for one
so wicked as I; but Jesus called me, bowed with sin and misery, to
Himself, and He has given me rest."

"Thank God!" said the old man fervently. "You must never leave me more,
my daughter."

"But, father, you forget," she said, with a sudden look of
trouble,—"you forget that I have my little Phil."

"Ah, to be sure—your child; you must take care of him. Where is he?"

"In London. Father, I cannot give him up."

"Why should you? Bring him home here. Poor child, he is worse off than
if he had no father, but I'll do what I can for him whilst my life is
spared. May God bless you both, and give you His peace—the peace of
God, Lizzie, that 'passeth all understanding.'"

"Amen," she murmured, tears of thankfulness springing to her eyes.

And as she sat in the soft fire-light, her head resting against her
father's knee, and his hand clasped in hers, a sense of peace stole
into her heart such as she had not known since as a little child she
sat thus by her father's knee. There was a wonderful calm in the world
outside as the first snowflakes floated softly, softly to the wintry
earth, but the hush of God's own peace had fallen on these hearts that
had sinned and sorrowed, and "when He giveth quietness, who then can
make trouble?"
