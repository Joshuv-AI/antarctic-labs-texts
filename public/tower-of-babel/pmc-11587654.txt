Outcome and toxicities of postmastectomy radiotherapy with integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask
Authors: Hongyan Zhang, Jieying Jin, Li Li, Linwei Wang, Qiuji Wu, Yahua Zhong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAppropriate immobilization setup for postmastectomy radiotherapy may help to improve tumor control and to reduce radiation-related toxicities. This study aims at retrospectively evaluate the outcome and toxicities of postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) with a novel integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask strategy.MethodsBreast cancer patients were treated with modified radical mastectomy and PMRT. Patient immobilization setup was performed with the placement of a 1-cm thickened wax film on the ipsilateral chest wall and an integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask. PMRT was delivered according to the institutional protocol. Dose distribution, disease control, patient survival and radiation-induced toxicities were evaluated.ResultsFour-hundred nineteen eligible patients with complete follow-up information were included in the final analysis. The median follow-up was 40.2 (95%CI: 38.9–41.6) months. Two (0.5%) patients had local recurrence and 48 (11.4%) patients had distant metastasis. There were 22 (5.3%) deaths from all causes, of which 19 were caused by breast cancer. The 3-year overall survival (OS) rate was 94.8%. ER status, PR status, triple negative status, and T stages were significantly related to patient survival (p < 0.05). HER2 expression, N stage were not significantly related to patient survival. Most common radiation-induced toxicities included grade I (87.6%) and grade II (10.2%) dermatitis, and grade I pneumonitis (28.9%) found by chest X-ray or CT scans. No clinical detectable cardiovascular event related to radiotherapy was identified.ConclusionPostmastectomy radiotherapy with integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask leads to favorable outcome and moderate toxicities compared with results reported in literature and might be of clinical significance in breast cancer patient. However, this approach has not been compared directly with postmastectomy radiotherapy without immobilization, and its applicability in other regions with different treatment protocols requires further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13197-3.

Introduction

Breast cancer has become the most prevalent malignant tumor with the highest incidence among women in China. Modified radical mastectomy is still the main surgical method for early-stage breast cancers in China. Adjuvant radiotherapy to the chest wall and regional lymph node area improves local control and prolonged disease-free survival in operated patients with high risk factors [1–4]. With the advancement of radiotherapy techniques and systemic treatment, the extent of surgical resection of early breast cancer is reducing, and radiotherapy is becoming even more important and is more commonly used in these patients [5–7].

Postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) is a critical component of breast cancer treatment, particularly for patients with certain risk factors. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) CSCO guideline recommended PMRT for patients with 4 or more positive nodes, and that PMRT should be considered for patients with 1–3 positive nodes accompanied with other risk factors [8, 9]. Particularly, for patients with positive axillary lymph node, the target volume of PMRT generally includes the chest wall, supraclavicular and infra-clavicular lymph nodal region, and internal mammary and axillary lymph nodal regions under certain circumstances [8]. This target volume is large and irregular and is close to organs at risk such as the spinal cord, humeral head, heart, and lung [10]. Therefore, appropriate immobilization devices and radiotherapy techniques are critical for PMRT. We have improved the PMRT techniques by implementing the following strategies [11]. First, we used the integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask that immobilizes the neck and the chest simultaneously, providing little deviation in patient positioning. Next, considering that chest wall recurrence is a common cause of treatment failure for PMRT [12], to increase dose uniformity to the chest wall, a 1-cm thickened wax film is placed on the surface of the chest wall throughout the radiotherapy course in our institute. Then, an integrated treatment plan covering both the chest wall and regional lymph nodal area is used to avoid dose hot spots or cold spots potentially caused by the junction of different irradiation fields.

In this study, we report the result of a retrospective study of the clinical values of integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask in PMRT. We evaluated the tumor control and side effects, hoping to provide new adjuvant radiotherapy strategy for early-stage breast cancers.

Methods

Patients

This is a single-center retrospective study. We retrospectively analyzed the treatment results of breast cancer patients treated with modified radical mastectomy and PMRT from August 2016 to February 2021. The inclusion criteria were: 1. newly diagnosed invasive breast cancer treated with modified radical mastectomy with negative surgical margins and axillary lymph node dissection; 2. patients with an indication for PMRT and having completed PMRT; 3. an integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask was used in patient positioning and a 1-cm thick wax film was used as bolus; 4. radiotherapy field included chest wall, supra- and infra-clavicular lymph nodal regions with or without axillary or internal mammary lymph nodal regions; 5. an integrated intensity-modulated radiotherapy treatment plan covering both the chest wall and regional lymph nodal was implemented. Exclusion criteria were: 1. not having completed the planned course of radiotherapy for reasons unrelated to treatment toxicities; 2. history of previous chest wall or supraclavicular radiotherapy; 3. stage I breast reconstruction or expander implantation on the affected side of the chest wall; 4. lack of follow-up information. A total of 419 eligible patients were included in the analysis. Informed consent was obtained from each participant. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (IRB number: 2020071 K).

Radiotherapy techniques

Patient positioning

All patients were in supine position. After the placement of a 1-cm thickened wax film (size: 25 cm * 25 cm) on the ipsilateral chest wall, an integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask was used to immobilize the patient on the whole carbon fiber treatment couch (Supplementary Fig. 1). In this cohort of patients, no deep breath hold strategy was implemented. CT scan with or without contrast-enhancement was performed from the mastoid to 5 cm below the diaphragm (covering the whole lung). The slice thickness was set at 3 mm and the obtained images were transferred from the CT simulation workstation to the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS). Target volume, organs at risk (OARs) and normal organs were delineated on the CT images.

Target volume delineation

The supra- and infra-clavicular clinical target volume (CTV) included supraclavicular medial group, lateral group, jugular subclavian vein confluence, the third and the second axillary groups. The upper border was the upper border of the cricoid cartilage. The lower border was lower border of the clavicle head. The medial border was the medial border of internal jugular vein or the lateral border of the internal carotid artery. The lateral border was the medial border of the trapezius muscle or the lateral border of the pectoralis minor muscle. The anterior border was the deep surface of the sternocleidomastoid and the platysma. The posterior border was the ventral surface of the scalene muscle (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1Clinical target volume (CTV) delineation. The extent of the CTV of the chest wall included the free flap. The anterior border was the skin. The posterior border was the posterior border of the pectoralis major muscle. The medial border was the ipsilateral parasternal line. The lateral border was the mid-axillary line. The upper border was the lower border of the clavicle head. And the lower border was set at the level of the contralateral breast fold (Fig. 1B).

The extent of planning target volume (PTV) was obtained by expanding a uniform 3 mm margin from corresponding CTV based on our institution's positioning deviation data (Supplementary Table 1).

OARs delineation

The following OARs were delineated: the heart, left and right lungs, the spinal cord and the contralateral breast.

Radiotherapy planning

Target volume dose coverage

We used 6 MV X-rays for irradiation. Conventional fractionated radiotherapy was delivered as 50 Gy/2 Gy per fraction/25 fractions to the PTVs. A subgroup of patients received hypofractionated radiotherapy with 43.5 Gy/2.9 Gy per fraction/15 fractions [13]. The target volume coverage was in accordance with the requirements of RTOG [14], with 95% of the prescribed dose covering at least 95% of the PTV (Fig. 2). OARs dose constraints

OARs dose constrains were as follows: the average dose to the heart should be < 10 Gy for left side breast cancers, and < 5 Gy for right side breast cancer; the average dose for the ipsilateral lung should be < 13 Gy, and V20Gy < 30%, V5Gy < 60% for the ipsilateral lung; The maximum dose to the spinal cord should be < 25 Gy (Fig. 2).

Patient follow-up

Patients were followed up once every 3 months within the first 2 years after radiotherapy, once every 6 months from 2 to 5 years and once every year thereafter. The last follow-up time was December 1, 2021.

Radiation-induced toxicity evaluation

For acute toxicity, the gravest toxicity was recorded at the end of PMRT and one month post radiotherapy. Acute and chronic radiotherapy-induced toxicities were evaluated and graded based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 3.0) [15].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages) and were compared with Chi-square and Fishers' exact test. Continuous variables were presented as median (range) and were analyzed with Mann–Whitney test. Kaplan–Meier plots were used to compare patient survival and log-rank test and Cox regression were used for survival analysis. R (MathSoft, 4.0.3) (https://www.r-project.org) and SPSS (Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) software package was used to perform statistical analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 450 eligible patients screened, 31 patients were excluded due to lost of follow-up immediately after the completion of radiotherapy without credible information that could be included in the analysis, with a loss-to-follow-up rate of 6.9%. As a result, 419 patients with complete follow-up information were included in the final analysis. The median age was 51 (interquartile range, IQR: 44–56.5) years. There were 3 (0.7%) male breast cancer patients and 197 (47.0%) right breast cancer patients. For histopathological types, 384 (91.7%) were invasive ductal carcinoma, 6 (1.4%) were invasive lobular carcinoma, and 29 (6.9%) were other pathological types. For WHO grades, 6 (1.4%) were WHO grade I, and 297 (70.9%) were grade II and 116 (27.7%) were grade III. For receptor expression, 312 (74.5%) were ER/PR-positive, 130 (31.0%) were HER2-positive, and 47 (11.2%) were triple-negative. In these patients, 170 (40.6%) were diagnosed with TNM stage II and 249 (59.4%) stage III breast cancers. Tumors were in the medial quadrant in 70 (16.7%) patients, in the central region in 22 (5.3%) patients, and in the non-medial/central region in 327 (78.0%) patients. As for cancer treatments, 62 (14.8%) patients received neoadjuvant therapy (chemotherapy and/or targeted therapies), 385 (91.9%) patients received adjuvant chemotherapy, 276 (65.9%) patients received adjuvant endocrine therapy, and 102 (24.3%) patients received adjuvant anti-HER2 therapy (Table 1). All patients received PMRT. The target volume of radiotherapy included the chest wall and supra- and infra-clavicular region. Additionally, 256 (61.1%) patients received internal mammary region irradiation, and 18 (4.3%) patients received axillary region irradiation. Of the 419 patients, 369 (89.6%) were treated with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and 50 (11.9%) were treated with helical tomotherapy. For radiotherapy doses, 346 (82.6%) were prescribed 50 Gy/25 fractions and 73 (17.4%) were prescribed 43.5 Gy/15 fractions. Tumor control and survival

After a median follow-up of 40.2 (95%CI: 38.9–41.6) months, no recurrence on the chestwall was recorded, and 2 (0.5%) patients had regional recurrence, including one axillary recurrence and one supraclavicular recurrence; and 48 (11.4%) patients had distant metastasis. Of the patients who developed metastasis, 16 had bone metastasis, 14 had lung metastasis, 14 had liver metastasis, 8 had brain metastasis, and 8 had distant lymph node metastasis (Table 2). Until the last follow up, there were 23 (5.3%) deaths from all causes, of which 19 were caused by breast cancer, 1 by secondary gastric cancer, 1 by secondary leukemia, 1 by SARS-Cov-2 infection, and 1 by accident. The 3-year overall survival (OS) rate was 94.8% (Fig. 3). Risk factors associated with overall survival

We explored the risk factors that might be related with patients’ overall survival. Through Kaplan–Meier analysis, we found that ER positive status (p = 0.0083), PR positive status (p = 0.00071), early T stage (p = 0.03) were significantly related with better overall survival, while triple negative status (p = 0.0079) was related with reduced overall survival. On the other hand, HER2 expression status, N stage and overall TNM stage did not significantly impact on patients’ survival (Fig. 4). Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that ER status, PR status, TNBC status, T stage, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy were associated with patients’ prognosis. Radiation-induced toxicities

The adverse reactions after radiotherapy included radiation-induced dermatitis: grade I dermatitis in 367 (87.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 2A), grade II in 43 (10.2%) (Supplementary Fig. 2B), and grade III in 9 (2.1%) patients. In addition, 121 (28.9%) patients with radiation-induced pneumonitis were found by chest X-ray or CT scans, all of which were grade I radiation-reduced pneumonitis. No ≥ grade II radiation-reduced pneumonitis occurred (Supplementary Fig. 3A-D). Until the last follow-up, no clinical detectable cardiovascular event related to radiotherapy was identified (Supplementary Table 3). Further, we found that hypofractionated RT resulted less grade II-III dermatitis (Supplementary Table 4). There was no difference in pneumonitis between the two groups (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we reported the results of a retrospective investigation of the clinical values of postmastectomy radiotherapy with integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask. Our results indicated that this modified radiotherapy technique led to favorable clinical outcome and moderate radiation-induced toxicities in breast cancer patients as compared with historical reported results and might provide new radiotherapy strategy for post-operative breast cancer treatment.

PMRT following modified radical mastectomy for breast cancers mainly included earlier two-dimensional treatment and three-dimensional conformal intensity-modulated radiotherapy. The prescribed dose of traditional two-dimensional radiotherapy was defined as the point dose at the center of the irradiation field. For example, the prescribed dose of the supraclavicular field was given at a depth of 3 cm subcutaneously in the center of the irradiation field, whilst the prescribed dose of the chest wall field was given at the maximum dose depth (Dmax). In these cases, the dose covering more than 90% of the target volume was about 90% of the prescribed dose, and there might be a cold or hot spot of the dose at the junction of the chest wall field and the regional lymph nodal field [16]. Therefore, three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy and intensity-modulated radiotherapy technology were more practiced for better dose distribution [17]. IMRT not only gave improved conformity and homogeneity of dose distribution to the target volumes, but also better spared critical OARs from excessive irradiation [18]. The integrated IMRT plan covering the chest wall and regional lymph nodal area also prevents dose hot spots and cold spots in interested area [19]. Since dose distribution in IMRT was more conformal to the target volume and due to the large mobility of the contralateral breast and heart and lungs, multiple positioning before each irradiation requires high repeatability of patient immobilization. Therefore, appropriate immobilization methods are crucial in improving the positioning accuracy of patients undergoing PMRT and reducing radiotherapy positioning errors.

In recent years, the thermoplastic mask immobilization method has been used in the radiotherapy of post-operative breast cancer patients with large pendulous breasts [20, 21]. It has the advantages of limiting the patient's range of breathing movements and good repeatability, which is conducive to improving the stability and repeatability of patient positioning. Obese patients can also achieve good immobilization results. Our center adopted an integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask to immobilize the neck and chest simultaneously [22]. In line with our findings, this method was reported to effectively reduce positioning errors and ensured irradiation accuracy for stereotactic body radiotherapy [23]. In addition, an integrated IMRT plan covering both the chest and regional lymph nodal areas prevented dose hot spots and cold spots, and improved dose distribution. Previously, we demonstrated that chest wall recurrence was a common cause of treatment failure for postmastectomy radiotherapy. Specifically, we found that the proportions of recurrence in the skin layer, pectoralis layer, rib layer, mixed layers were 58.6%, 9.5% 1.7%, 30.2%, respectively. Near 90% of chest wall recurrence involved the skin. Further, the recurrence proportion in the incision periphery was 60.5% [12]. Radiation dose to the chest wall and to the incision periphery was significantly associated with local recurrence [24, 25]. Therefore, we sought to increase the dose and uniformity to the skin and incision periphery by placing a 1-cm thickened wax film (size: 25 cm * 25 cm) on the ipsilateral chest wall throughout the radiotherapy course. This method effectively improved chest wall boosts and reduced chest wall recurrence as compared with historical reports (Supplementary Table 6) [1, 13, 26–28]. Importantly, there was no chest wall recurrence in these 419 patients, although there was one recurrence in the axillary region and one in the supraclavicular region, respectively. The most frequently observed treatment failure was distant metastasis, which reflected the fact that these cancers might be more metastatic and underscored the importance of systematic treatments. After a median follow-up of 40.2 months, 19 (4.5%) patients died of breast cancers. The 3-year OS was as high as 94.8%.

For prognostic factors, well-known histopathological and clinical characteristics were found to impacted on patients’ overall survival. Specifically, ER positivity, PR positivity, non-triple negative status, and early T stage were associated with better survival. HER2 expression, N stage did not significantly affect patient survival. Since the most common failure of treatment was due to distant metastasis, systemic treatment such anti-HER2 therapy and chemotherapy should be addressed to improve the prognosis of risky patients [29]. Novel treatment such as immunotherapy might bring new promise as well [30].

The integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask provided better protection of organs at risk in PMRT of breast cancers. While the use of bolus may improve dose uniformity, it only slightly increases the average or maximum skin dose, thus not leading to a marked increase in skin-related side effects (Supplementary Table 2). The skin reaction of patients was common, but most was mild, with 87.6% grade I, 10.2% grade II, and only 2.1% grade III dermatitis. The incidence and severity of radiation-induced dermatitis observed in this study were in line with those results from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cancer Hospital [13]. Among the 419 patients, 352 patients had chest CT (326 patients) or chest X-ray (26 patients) reviewed within half a year after radiotherapy, and 121 (28.9%) patients had grade I acute radiation-induced pneumonitis. No ≥ grade II radiation-reduced pneumonitis occurred. The incidence of acute radiation-induced pneumonitis was slightly higher in this cohort of patients as compared with that reported in previous studies [13, 31]. However, all pneumonitis were asymptomatic and was resolved or alleviated spontaneously about one year after radiotherapy. These patients were followed up for a median of 40.2 months, and no obvious cardiotoxicity was detected. However, since most patients did not regularly undergo Doppler echocardiography and myocardial enzyme detection during follow-up, the occurrence of long-term cardiotoxicities requires further observation for a longer period of time.

Importantly, the use of an integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask plus a bolus in breast cancer radiotherapy is not universally recommended. Our findings regarding its use are based on our local experience and should not be interpreted as a broad endorsement of this approach outside of China. While our study provides valuable insights into one specific context, further research would be required to validate the effectiveness of these treatments in other countries.

There were several limitations in this study. One limitation is that this was a retrospective study with a short follow-up time. Next, most patients did not regularly review the functional status of organs at risk such as the heart and the thyroid. Therefore, further prospective studies and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the clinical value of integral cervicothoracic thermoplastic mask immobilization in PMRT for breast cancers.

In conclusion, our study described a new immobilization strategy of radiotherapy after modified radical mastectomy for breast cancers, which achieved favorable outcome and moderate toxicities compared with results literature reported in literature. This method might be of clinical importance for PMRT in breast cancer patients.
