Acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefits of integrating mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) into disease outbreak response in Western Uganda: a qualitative process evaluation study
Authors: Biksegn Asrat Yirdaw, Kenneth Kalani, Muhammad Alkasaby, Yang Zhao, Ling Wang, Claire Kwagala, Emmanuel Mfitundinda, Catherine Abbo, Farhana Haque, Julian Eaton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDisease outbreaks cause widespread suffering, loss of lives and significantly increase the risk of mental health and psychosocial problems. Although mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) plays a critical role in addressing the needs of populations affected by outbreaks, limited efforts have been made to integrate MHPSS into outbreak response mechanisms. In this study, we developed, implemented and evaluated priority MHPSS interventions to examine acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefit of MHPSS interventions as part of outbreak response in Uganda.MethodsThe interventions were implemented in Bundibugyo district, Western Uganda, from November 2024 to March 2025 and evaluated through a qualitative process evaluation. The interventions targeted primary healthcare workers, frontline responders, community leaders and district health team leaders. Study participants were selected purposively and data were collected through in-depth interviews. We used a thematic analysis combined with inductive and deductive approaches to capture emerging themes.ResultsWe conducted in-depth interviews with 19 purposively selected individuals representing community members, implementers and public health leaders. Participants perceived the interventions to be acceptable due to active engagement with the community leaders, community health workers, district leadership and collaboration with the local media. The findings strongly suggest that the programme was feasible because it was deliberately designed and systematically implemented with a community-driven approach. The feasibility of the programme was reflected across five key constructs: practicality, cultural appropriateness, perceived benefit, sustainability and implementation challenges. Participants reported that the interventions helped address serious mental health concerns, including reducing the risk of self-harm, suicide and stigma, while equipping frontline workers with stress management skills.ConclusionDespite the high level of acceptability and feasibility reported, several challenges may hinder the uptake and scale-up of MHPSS interventions in outbreak-affected communities, particularly stigma and misconceptions surrounding mental health conditions. These challenges highlight the requirement for sustained community engagement and continuous sensitisation efforts.

Introduction

Infectious disease outbreaks cause widespread suffering, loss of lives, have a serious impact on livelihoods, and contribute to an increase in the global burden of diseases.12 Outbreaks also affect mental health through these adversities and other social determinants.3 In particular, the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic and associated social measures contributed to an increase in the global burden of diseases with an estimated 53.2 million additional cases of depression and 76.2 million new cases of anxiety disorders globally.4 The COVID-19 pandemic led to a 27.6% increase in cases of major depressive disorder and a 25.6% increase in anxiety disorders,4 while people living with mental health conditions were more likely to be hospitalised, experience severe illness and die prematurely. Low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a disproportionately high frequency of infectious disease outbreaks,5 with the African continent reporting the greatest number of outbreaks.6 Mental health and psychosocial needs are therefore a pressing issue following public health emergencies like outbreaks, and a greater understanding of how to address them in LMICs is needed.

Despite the huge mental health and psychosocial impacts of disease outbreaks, mental health interventions are rarely included in outbreak response plans.7 Mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) plays a critical role in addressing the mental health needs of those infected individuals, their families, frontline responders and the wider community.8 In outbreak-prone countries, where national mental health or MHPSS policies or MHPSS strategies exist, outbreak-specific mental health needs are often insufficiently addressed. As a result, effective actions to respond to MHPSS needs during outbreaks remain limited. For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the implementation of MHPSS interventions recommended by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee9 was below 50% across 28 African countries.10 In addition to the lack of political commitment and resource constraints, one possible explanation for this gap is the lack of context-specific evidence to inform strategies and practices for integrating MHPSS into outbreak preparedness and response systems.

Uganda is one of the most vulnerable countries to public health emergencies arising from its geographical location in the meningitis and yellow fever belts, the ‘filovirus triangle’ and proximity to the biodiversity-rich Congo basin ‘hot spot’.11 The country reports the second highest number of public health emergencies annually in Africa and has experienced mpox, cholera, yellow fever and multiple Ebola outbreaks in the last two decades.12 Each outbreak poses different socioeconomic challenges and impacts on mental well-being. In the context of public health emergencies, access to culturally appropriate MHPSS interventions within outbreak preparedness and response programmes is vital. Uganda has made some policy progress in this area, including efforts to integrate MHPSS into Ebola outbreak response since 2018. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Uganda was among the few African countries that developed MHPSS guidelines and incorporated MHPSS components into the pandemic response programmes to foster healing, enhance resilience and contribute to curbing the spread of the infection. MHPSS teams were included in national and district task forces responding to the pandemic13; however, challenges remained regarding consistent coordination and case management,14

Better evidence on MHPSS in public health emergencies could help address some of these shortcomings and improve preparedness and MHPSS delivery during future outbreaks. Unfortunately, much of the evidence currently informing decision-making is derived from high-income countries,15 and its applicability to outbreak contexts in low-income settings remains unclear. LMICs like Uganda need to develop and adapt interventions that are appropriate to their context. With the aim of bridging this evidence gap, we aimed to develop, implement and evaluate MHPSS interventions to provide feasible recommendations for Ugandan leaders, informed by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee9 MHPSS Minimum Service Package (MSP).9 This study examined the acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefit of integrated MHPSS interventions into disease outbreak response to address community mental health needs in Western Uganda.

Methods and settings

We conducted a qualitative process evaluation to examine the acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefit of MHPSS interventions implemented in disease outbreak context in Western Uganda from November 2024 to March 2025. The interventions targeted primary healthcare (PHC) workers, frontline responders, community leaders and district health team leaders. Study participants were purposively selected at various stages of the implementation process. We used the UK Medical Research Council framework for conducting and reporting the evaluation of complex interventions to develop, implement and evaluate the interventions,16 and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines to guide reporting of this study (online supplemental file 1).17 The interventions were implemented in Bundibugyo, while the neighbouring Kasese district served as a control site. The two districts are located in Western Uganda bordering the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), within the Rwenzori Mountains. Bundibugyo has one referral hospital and 29 health centres that serve a population of more than 250 000 people.18 Mental health services in both Bundibugyo and Kasese districts have primarily been provided through clinical case management at the outpatient department of Bundibugyo referral hospital.

MHPSS gap analysis and prioritisation

We conducted a desk-based gap analysis to identify existing MHPSS services, implementation gaps and available resources across all regions of Uganda.19 Following the gap analysis, we organised an MHPSS prioritisation workshop in Kampala in May 2024, bringing together 30 national and international stakeholders to identify priority MHPSS interventions for Uganda. The workshop brought together participants from the Ministry of Health, national Incident Management System (IMS) personnel, the Uganda National Institute of Public Health, the national MHPSS Technical Working Group and international collaborators, such as Africa CDC, WHO AFRO and the global MSP team. The prioritisation exercise started with orientation sessions on core MHPSS components and key activities included in the MHPSS MSP. This was followed by small group discussions and plenary sessions to reach a consensus on relevant criteria to select priority MHPSS activities from the MHPSS MSP framework. Participants subsequently applied these agreed criteria to identify and prioritise MHPSS interventions.

Implementation planning and preparation

Bundibugyo was selected as an implementation site for the priority MHPSS interventions identified in phase I due to its historical vulnerability to various disease outbreaks. This vulnerability is partly related to its location along border with the DRC and partly due to broader public health challenges, including poverty, geographical isolation and limited health services. In addition, the district was identified through the gap analysis as having particularly low MHPSS coverage. A district-level planning workshop was organised in September 2024 involving community leaders, traditional and faith healers, frontline outbreak responders, primary healthcare (PHC) workers and the District Health Team. During the workshop, participants identified several MHPSS service gaps such as shortage of personnel trained in MHPSS, limited availability of locally adapted guidelines and a lack of collaboration among key stakeholders, including faith healers, community leaders and implementers. Following the workshop, a district task force was established to develop an implementation plan and coordinate MHPSS programme activities. Subsequently, another workshop was organised in November 2024 to adapt intervention guidelines and develop key messages that promote mental health and psychosocial well-being during outbreaks.

Implementation

Interventions were delivered for various actors, including training on Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) for PHC workers and a separate MHPSS skills training and psychological first aid for frontline outbreak responders and community leaders. Following these capacity building interventions, we initiated a package of MHPSS interventions for 3 months from December 2024 to March 2025 in Bundibugyo. Following training, we initiated the integration of MHPSS into mpox outbreak response efforts and existing health services. The MHPSS services were implemented by frontline workers, Risk Communication and Community Engagement (RCCE) officers, Village Health Teams (VHT) and community representatives.

Evaluation

Evaluation was conducted at each phase of the project through documentation of intervention delivery, service uptake, engagement of service users and adherence to implementation plans. In addition, we conducted the tabletop simulation exercise in March 2025 to assess the MHPSS preparedness and response capacities in Bundibugyo compared with the control district, Kasese. We brought a comparable number of participants from both districts—who were assigned to two groups according to their respective district. A separate group comprising national emergency response leaders was also formed to explore issues related to national-level coordination issues. During the exercises, workshop facilitators presented various outbreak scenarios accompanied by discussion questions (online supplemental file 2).

Data collection and implementation outcomes

Data were collected by two researchers (EM and CK) from Uganda through in-depth interviews using interview guides. The interviews were conducted in English language between November 2024 and March 2025 during the implementation and evaluation stages. After reviewing common implementation research frameworks, we developed an interview guide designed to address the research questions and objectives. The interview guide was developed in consultation with all the research team and data collectors (EM and CK) to ensure that the language was clear and easily understood by the target participants. It was structured around core thematic areas and included brainstorming, probing and follow-up questions, and concluding wrap-up questions (online supplemental file 3). In terms of reflexivity, the research team recognised that CA and KK are well known for their contributions to mental health in Uganda. To minimise potential bias arising from their professional and social positions, it was agreed that they would not be involved in data collection or direct engagement with participants. We also acknowledge that the engagement of EM (a medical officer) and CK (a psychiatrist) with study participants in conducting in-depth interviews could potentially introduce power imbalances. To mitigate this risk, both EM and CK participated in repeated role-plays and data collection exercises to ensure that interviews were conducted in a neutral and respectful manner. The exercises emphasised adherence to informed consent procedures, the use of clearly formulated questions and careful attention to the tones and flow of conversations to maintain a participant-centred approach. We sought to achieve data saturation by using purposive sampling to collect rich information from a diverse group of participants. After each round of interviews, we conducted preliminary data analysis to identify emerging data patterns, key characteristics and thematic areas before conducting the formal analysis. Data collection was discontinued when no new themes, ideas or outliers emerged from the in-depth interviews. In-depth interviews were conducted in English with purposively selected participants from various groups, all of whom were fluent in English. The primary implementation outcomes examined were acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefit of the interventions. We used a combination of the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework and the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) to draw initial themes relevant to examine acceptability and feasibility of the programme.2021 Acceptability refers to the extent to which service users, implementers and decision-makers consider the interventions to be appropriate based on anticipated or experienced responses to the programme. We assessed acceptability through participants’ reactions, satisfaction and recommendations obtained from in-depth interviews with key informants. Although the definition and scope of feasibility slightly differ, commonly identified feasibility constructs include practicality, demand, cultural appropriateness, sustainability and perceived benefit of the health interventions.22 We explored feasibility of the programme using these constructs through in-depth interviews with community level implementers and district-level decision-makers.

MHPSS preparedness and response capacities of participants were evaluated through the tabletop exercise using a newly developed evaluation framework (online supplemental file 4). The framework included various outbreak scenarios, outline of expected responses and actual responses provided by participants. Expected responses were developed based on the national IMS guidance and MHPSS MSP framework in consultation with local stakeholders in Uganda. Participants’ actual responses were document by independent evaluators prior to being compiled by the research team. Additional data were collected using semi-structured data collection tools and observational notes.

Data analysis

In-depth interviews were digitally recorded and subsequently transferred to a computer for storage and analysis. All the records were transcribed verbatim, cleaned, coded and analysed using Microsoft Excel. We used a thematic analysis integrating both inductive and deductive approaches to capture emerging themes. In the first step, we mapped specific constructs within our dataset using the RE-AIM framework and developed an initial codebook. We then conducted an inductive thematic analysis to capture important domains emerging from the data. Through this initial coding process, we identified several themes that were not fully captured by the RE-AIM framework. These emerging themes aligned well with the TFA framework, which had also informed the interview guide and data collection process. In the second step, we revised the codebook by incorporating components from the TFA and developed an integrated analytical framework. Finally, we conducted a deductive analysis integrated with the earlier inductive findings to generate thematic areas relevant to the study objectives. Two of the research team (BAY and LW) coded patterns, concepts and themes of the data independently. Following initial coding, emerging themes were compared and refined in consultation with YZ aligning with the objectives of the study. Finally, we drew themes and key insights and presented key findings supported with explanatory quotes.

Patient and public involvement

Participants in the intervention design included community representatives and local health service providers at all levels. Community members were also among the study participants, including being given the opportunity to reflect on and assess the acceptability and feasibility of the intervention in their local communities and health services. However, patients were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

A total of 19 in-depth interviews were conducted with key informants to explore the acceptability, feasibility and perceived benefit of the interventions. Of all interviewees, eight (42%) were women representing a range of roles. Nine participants (47.4%) were community representatives including community health workers (named as VHT), traditional healers (referred as community leaders) and community members. The remaining participants were frontline outbreak responders (n=5) and members of the District Health including district-level public health leaders (n=5). MHPSS interventions

During the prioritisation workshop, we identified five priority MHPSS interventions that can be implementable in Uganda during outbreaks (figure 2). Of the five interventions, we selected and implemented three of them in mpox outbreak response in Bundibugyo (table 2). The interventions included three rounds of intensive training for 3–5 days on common mental health conditions, psychological first aid and MHPSS skills relevant to outbreak emergencies. The WHO mhGAP Humanitarian Intervention Guidelines23 and the MHPSS MSP were used as the primary training materials. Stakeholders involved in outbreak response including PHC workers, community health workers and community leaders received the training. Following completion of the training, in collaboration with the District Health Team, we distributed job aids and MHPSS guidelines to PHC facilities and mpox outbreak isolation units in Bundibugyo. This was intended to support the integration of MHPSS services into the mpox outbreak response and across PHC services in the district. Support supervision and reporting were conducted every 2 weeks in selected health facilities. In addition, community mobilisation activities were implemented through the dissemination of key messages that promote mental well-being during the mpox outbreak. Acceptability

Across interviews, participants reported positive reactions from the community, implementers and public health leaders, alluding to high acceptability of the interventions. Interviewees associated the acceptability of interventions with active engagement of community leaders, empowerment of community health workers (VHT members), commitment from district leadership and strong engagement with the local media outlets.

Participants reported that decisions made in consultation with cultural, religious and community leaders enhanced the acceptability of the interventions. Most interviewees argued that collaboration with respected community members and the empowerment of VHT contributed significantly to the acceptability and uptake of interventions.

When certain key people speak, their words are heard fast, because they are very respected. So, the engagement with those kinds of people makes the intervention acceptable. (HW0022)

Some interviewees justified that personal testimonials from individuals who shared their mental health experiences and help-seeking journeys contributed positively to the success of these interventions. Participants also believed that proactive engagement with the local media for dissemination of key messages through talk-shows and radio jingles enhanced the acceptability of the interventions. Similarly, community education programmes delivered through social gatherings were perceived to increase acceptability of the interventions leading to greater service uptake.

Feasibility

The overall findings indicate that the programme was carefully designed and systematically implemented with a community-driven approach. Participants perceived the interventions as relevant and feasible to implement in outbreak emergency contexts where resources are limited and existing mental health services are overwhelmed. They also found the interventions as suitable for intergradation across most outbreak response pillars, including case management, infection prevention and control, surveillance, risk communication and community engagement, as well as PHC system. Given the central findings, we outline feasibility of the programme based on five constructs: practicality, cultural appropriateness, perceived benefit, sustainability and implementation challenges.

Practicality

Following the ongoing capacity-building activities, the interventions were implemented by proactively addressing implementation challenges. Contextual adaptation of the interventions and intensive capacity-building activities contributed to practicality of the programme. While the mental health concepts were easier for PHC workers to understand, many community representatives reported difficulties in understanding certain concepts, particularly the distinction between the symptoms of depression and anxiety. As community representatives do not have formal professional training, many of these concepts were new to them, highlighting the need for further training and practical supervision. Nevertheless, they felt capable of applying basic MHPSS actions, including identifying and referring people experiencing psychological distress.

Mental health support is not that easy to understand. With mental health you don’t see things physically, but you can still analyse it by the situations in the way the person is behaving and can tell you that, that person is psychologically facing problems. (CR003)

Cultural appropriateness

Most interviewees appreciated the amount of attention given to cultural relevance of the MHPSS interventions through appropriate translation and participatory adaptation of materials and guidelines. However, many also emphasised that misconceptions about mental health conditions and reliance on traditional healing practices may hinder the scale up and reach of the programme.

There was a lot of emphasis paid to make the programme appropriate to our culture. The interventions are compatible, but these cultural beliefs need advocacy and education on mental health. (DHT0029)

Perceived benefit

For many of the interviewees, the interventions were perceived to address serious mental health concerns, including reducing the risk of self-harm, suicide and stigma in the community as well as equipping frontline workers with self-stress management skills. Others noted that the inclusion of mental health support improved service delivery by facilitating patient care, enhancing overall patient management, reducing the burden on mental health facilities and benefitting a large number of people. All interviewees agreed that the interventions benefited the wider community by addressing mental health needs, countering misinformation about mpox outbreak and extending the support beyond the discharge of mpox patients from isolation facilities.

This integration reduces the risk and harm including issues of stigma. Stigma is a real matter … people run away when you are isolated … some may be suicidal. These are things you need to control before they happen. (DHT0024)

Mental health integration may also reduce patient and workload in referral hospitals. We already have a decentralised system. (DHT0027)

Interviewees described the programme as the first of its kind in Bundibugyo to bring together diverse stakeholders to address an important capacity gap, despite the district’s experience with recurrent outbreaks. The expertise developed through the programme was highly valued by participants as they felt that it helped them gain the skills and confidence to support colleagues experiencing mental health issues, as well as members of the wider community affected by mpox outbreak. They also reported that the programme played a valuable role in strengthening both local and national capacities.

The programme equipped us with knowledge and skills to identify people suffer from mental breakdowns, which has always been a neglected issue, not seen as a very important issue. (HW0023)

Sustainability

While most interviewees believed that the programme could be sustained if adequate resources were available, some expressed concerns about the need for strong leadership, continued capacity building, ongoing supportive supervision and effective coordination to ensure long-term sustainability. Community representatives and healthcare workers felt that the programme had good potential for sustainability, given the demonstrated commitment of the leadership, coordination between the Ministry of Health and district authorities, and the collaboration with community stakeholders. Despite these positive efforts, District Health Team emphasised that continued capacity-building support, along with the empowerment of village health teams and community leaders, would be essential to maintaining the mental health and psychosocial well-being of the community over time.

The Ministry of Health team together with a partner should work hand-in-hand with the district leadership to coordinate operations and allocate essential resources for the frontline workers. (DHT0026)

Implementation challenges

As with all health programmes, we anticipated potential barriers to the implementation of interventions. With this in mind, we explored challenges that might hinder the uptake and integration of programme. Analysis of the data identified five major thematic areas of barriers: cultural misconceptions and language barriers, competing interests, stigma and discrimination, geographic and weather-related barriers, and limited resources and high financial expectations.

Western Uganda, particularly Bundibugyo, is culturally diverse, with many local languages spoken across communities. Language emerged as a significant challenge, as programme implementers were not able to communicate in all local languages. In addition, translating IEC materials into each local language was perceived as resource intensive and difficult to sustain.

Yeah, there are those beliefs that associate mental illness with witchcraft and so many issues, but all of that is because of lack of information. We should maintain the services, bridge the gaps, and improve in our stock. (DHT0027)

Interviewees perceived competing interests among community stakeholders, including traditional healers, women’s and youth unions, and disability associations, as potential challenges to advancing the programme. Participants reported that some traditional healers may perceive new health programmes and health workers as threats to their business. Similarly, although the primary role of health and disability associations is to advocate for inclusion, interviewees said they may also expect financial support when new programmes are introduced into the community. Participants further noted that resources are limited and that expectations for financial support are high within communities. As a result, like many other health programmes, this MHPSS programme may face challenges to ensure long-term sustainability.

The traditional healers would see it as a threat. They may see it as you are taking their business off. We should bring them on board and provide training and awareness as a critical approach. (HW0023)

Stigma associated with cultural beliefs that link mental health conditions with witchcraft can contribute to discrimination and worsen mental health outcomes. One interviewee highlighted this by noting that epilepsy is still believed to be caused by a curse. Similarly, people coming out of isolation centres whether they are infected by mpox or not experience high levels of stigma and discrimination in their community.

Yeah, we still have our cultural beliefs – maybe like in epilepsy, which is believed as a curse up to now. You can’t separate a person from his or her culture. (DHT0024)

All interviewees representing the community and village health team stated that geographical barriers (mountains, valleys and rivers), as well as difficult weather conditions such as heavy rainfall affecting road access, posed major challenges to reaching rural communities in Bundibugyo. These natural conditions created difficulties not only for service providers but also for older adults, children and women seeking care at the health facilities. However, participants suggested that these challenges could be mitigated through community engagement strategies and capacity building initiatives involving community leaders, caregivers, peer groups to deliver interventions in the community. The use of the two local FM radio stations in Bundibugyo was also considered as an important channel for reaching otherwise inaccessible communities with key messages promoting mental health and psychosocial well-being.

MHPSS preparedness and response

The findings from the tabletop simulation exercise revealed differences in the depth of understanding and applying MHPSS between the implementation district (Bundibugyo) and control district (Kasese). Although participants in both districts actively engaged with the scenarios, their responses reflected notable differences in MHPSS knowledge, and preparedness and response capacity.

In Bundibugyo, participants articulated clearer and more organised MHPSS response strategies, often drawing on prior training or recent implementation of the interventions. For example, when presented with scenarios involving misinformation or school closures, participants from Bundibugyo consistently referenced structured outreach responses including RCCE through radio broadcasts, remote peer support and peer dialogue initiatives. One participant (B4) stated, “We already have trained VHTs and social workers. We can cascade stress management and support through existing community groups.”

In contrast, participants from Kasese approached the same challenges more cautiously and often requiring clarification or additional facilitation. Their discussions reflected a more theoretical understanding of MHPSS, with fewer references to practical experience or existing district-level structures. For example, when asked how mental health support could be provided to frontline outbreak responders, responses tend to focus on general suggestions such as ‘counselling’ or ‘more training’ rather than specific actionable interventions. One participant (K3) remarked, “We are not sure where the mental health services are located. We mostly send people to the hospital.” Although participants from Kasese demonstrated some basic understanding, they appeared to have limitations in translating MHPSS principles into practical strategies. These findings underscore the importance of targeted capacity-building and structured learning opportunities to strengthen MHPSS preparedness, particularly in districts with limited prior exposure to integrated mental health approaches.

In relation to outbreak response activation and coordination, Bundibugyo demonstrated a clear and common understanding of the IMS and the designated role of the MHPSS focal person. In contrast, participants from Kasese expressed more fragmented views regarding leadership responsibilities for MHPSS activities, indicating weaker institutional clarity. Regarding RCCE, participants from Bundibugyo proposed community engagement strategies tailored to social groups, including church groups and sex workers, suggesting a more contextualised and inclusive approach. By comparison, responses from Kasese focused primarily on mass media communication but lacked specific strategies targeting vulnerable populations. With respect to self-care and staff well-being, participants from Bundibugyo proposed multilayered approaches, including peer support mechanisms, structured debriefing sessions and access to psychosocial care for frontline staff. In contrast, participants from Kasese focused mainly on information dissemination, with limited discussion of mechanisms for sustained support or staff well-being monitoring. When discussing monitoring, learning, and evaluation, participants from Bundibugyo reflected on systematic use of data to support iterative planning and identified measurable indicators for MHPSS success, such as the number of trained staff and completed referrals. Participants from Kasese responded more generally to follow-up and supervision activities but experienced difficulties defining indicators or learning loops.

Despite these differences, both district participants described appreciation of the importance of MHPSS within emergency response mechanisms. Notably, participants from Kasese explicitly called for more training opportunities and inclusion in national MHPSS platforms, reflecting growing awareness stimulated by the exercises. Overall, Bundibugyo emerged as a comparatively more prepared and responsive district, as further illustrated in online supplemental file 5, though the evaluation exercise itself was felt to be of benefit among the control group participants.

Discussion

This study examined the acceptability, feasibility, perceived benefit and implementation challenges of a set of MHPSS MSP interventions that were prioritised and implemented in the context of disease outbreaks in Western Uganda. The findings strongly support the theoretical underpinnings of participatory public health models, in which community ownership and contextual appropriateness are foundations for the development of culture-sensitive mental health programmes.24 Collaborative priority setting and programme design led to interventions that were widely acceptable, feasible, and impactful in addressing mental health and psychosocial needs of the community affected by disease outbreaks. Through a qualitative evaluation, this study generated evidence supporting the relevance of integrating culturally adapted MHPSS interventions into disease outbreak preparedness and response frameworks in low-resource settings.25

Cultural, religious and political leaders serve not only as gatekeepers but are also critical in co-design of interventions. Empowered community health workers and frontline outbreak responders can effectively deliver MHPSS interventions and further reinforce the intervention’s potential reach and sustainability, aligning with task-shifting strategies endorsed in global guidelines.9 The participatory translation and adaptation of intervention materials through engagement of trusted voices in the community were seen as critical enablers for the uptake of the interventions. This approach enabled us to redefine and harmonise interventions with social norms around mental health, fostering legitimacy and building trust in the community and pave a path towards scaling up.2627 Cultural adaptation of interventions is advantageous, improving community engagement and ownership of programmes, but it is critical to overcome misconceptions and harmful cultural practices that associate mental health conditions and disease outbreaks with witchcraft or divine punishment.28

While stakeholder involvement and media engagement increased receptivity to the interventions, deeply entrenched stigma related to mental health remained a significant barrier. These findings resonate with common experiences in emergency response and humanitarian contexts, where stigma can negatively affect risk communication, uptake of interventions and potentially the safety of responders. Stigma and discrimination associated with mental health issues require sustained advocacy efforts and culturally tailored health literacy campaigns delivered through community sensitisation, risk communication and community engagement strategies.29 These findings further suggest that it is paramount to consider introducing a targeted intervention that addresses stigma and discrimination against people with mental health problems, and to reduce its overall impact on mental health service utilisation. This may be linked to wider RCCE activities.

This study found that cultural misconceptions continue to influence care-seeking behaviour, often leading individuals away from formal healthcare services, and potentially leading to harmful behaviours (for both infection control and mental well-being). Some degree of resistance from the traditional healers, compounded by competing interests and stigma, highlights the need for inclusive and collaborative approaches.30

In terms of feasibility, this study found that interviewees perceived the MHPSS interventions to be both practical and potentially sustainable. The feasibility of the interventions may be partly attributed to its alignment with district and national-level outbreak preparedness and response system, as well as prior experience of responders in designing interventions. Study participants also expressed optimism regarding the sustainability of the programme, particularly if supported by continued leadership, ongoing capacity building and strong stakeholder collaboration. Nevertheless, differences in understanding mental health concepts and confidence in applying MHPSS skills between formally trained community health workers and frontline healthcare workers highlighted the importance of differentiated training curricula and competency-based supervision. Notably, the practical challenges of implementing MHPSS in geographically and culturally diverse regions—such as the mountain terrain of Bundibugyo—underscore the need for adaptive delivery methods, including the use of local media, radio and other digital platforms.31 Although all participants recognised and valued the role of MHPSS in outbreak response, their perceptions may have been influenced by the study design—specifically, the Hawthorne effect, whereby individuals modify their behaviours or responses when they are aware of being observed.32 For example, participants from the control district appreciated the TTX as a learning exercise, though we used it for evaluation.33

The perceived benefit of the programme was multidimensional. Participants reported improved identification of mental health needs, reduction in stigma and discrimination, and better management of stress and trauma during the mpox outbreak. The integration of MHPSS into the broader outbreak response efforts was also perceived to reduce the burden on referral hospitals and extended the MHPSS services to the wider communities. Successful integration of MHPSS into community and PHC services is a vital strategy to expand access to mental health services and strengthen efficient referral systems to higher levels of care in resource constrained settings, such as Uganda.34 Such integration of MHPSS beyond outbreak response is an important strategy for institutionalising MHPSS services across all levels of the healthcare system of the country. The tabletop exercises indicated that participants from the intervention district demonstrated a better understanding of coordination, more defined outbreak response goals and demonstrated improved MHPSS skill sets compared with participants from the control district. These reported outcomes are consistent with the goals of MHPSS MSP and further validate the strategy of integrating MHPSS into outbreak preparedness and response efforts.9 In support of these findings, there is growing evidence highlighting the effectiveness, efficiency and improved access associated with integrating MHPSS into multisectoral humanitarian programmes.35–37 While a strong body of evidence suggests that multisectoral, integrated MHPSS is both effective and efficient, further research is needed to generate rigorously documented, consistent and measurable evidence on its impact, particularly in low-income settings.

Study limitations

The findings are based primarily on qualitative data and may not be generalisable beyond the implementation district. In addition, this study employed purposive sampling to recruit participants, and this may have introduced a biased group of respondents, further limiting the generalisability of the findings. Although the relatively small number of in-depth interviews provided rich and detailed narratives, the small sample size could have been bigger to fully reflect the scale of the programme. Participants’ responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias, as they may have wished to show support for continued investment and maintain their involvement in the study. Also, the learning and evaluation environment in a tabletop exercise may have reduced knowledge levels on MHPSS readiness between the control and intervention groups. The tabletop exercise simulated outbreak response scenarios rather than observing behaviours and outcomes in real-time outbreak conditions. As such, it was not possible to directly compare key aspects of the intervention, including effectiveness, health system responsiveness and other implementation outcomes. However, the approach offered practical advantages, including feasibility and ethical complexities of carrying out research during emergencies, allowing for useful evaluation even in the context of outbreaks, including predictability.

Conclusion

Although the mental health needs are significant during public health emergencies, collaboratively identifying priority interventions with community leaders, faith and traditional healers, frontline responders and government leaders is a valuable approach to maximise the acceptability, feasibility and benefit of mental health programmes. Despite the high acceptability and feasibility observed of adapted international guidelines, several challenges may hinder the uptake and scale-up of MHPSS interventions in outbreak-affected communities, particularly stigma and misconceptions of mental health conditions. These challenges require sustained community engagement and community sensitisation efforts.
