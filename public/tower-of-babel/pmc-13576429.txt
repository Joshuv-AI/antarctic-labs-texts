Febrile Seizures and School Performance
Authors: Julie Werenberg Dreier, Betina B. Trabjerg, Theresa Wimberley, Bjarke Feenstra, Jakob Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAre febrile seizures associated with school performance?FindingsThis Danish nationwide register-based cohort study comparing 26 173 children with febrile seizures with 645 946 unaffected peers found only minimal differences in national standardized reading and mathematics test scores between the groups. More substantial score differences were observed in children with multiple recurrent hospital contacts with febrile seizures and children who later developed epilepsy.MeaningThese findings suggest that febrile seizures are generally not associated with meaningfully poorer school performance, although children with multiple recurrent episodes or later epilepsy may be at increased risk of impaired academic performance and may require extra support.

Introduction

Febrile seizures affect approximately 2% to 5% of all children, making it the most common neurological condition in childhood.1,2 Witnessing a child experience a febrile seizure can be highly distressing for parents, with many reporting a perception that their child is at risk of dying during the initial or subsequent episodes.3 This naturally raises concerns about sequelae of the seizures and their potential long-term impact on brain health and development. This concern is supported by evidence from animal studies, demonstrating increased seizure susceptibility and cognitive dysfunction involving hippocampal and prefrontal cortex networks following experimental febrile seizures.4,5,6 Nevertheless, in the clinical setting, there is a broad consensus that the prognosis for children with febrile seizures, especially when simple (ie, not prolonged, focal, or recurrent), is highly favorable.7,8 For instance, findings from studies on academic outcomes or intellectual performance, which may capture even subtle impairment in neurocognitive functioning, have generally found that children with febrile seizures have outcomes similar to those of unaffected peers.8,9,10,11,12 In a hallmark US study from 1978 of 431 sibling pairs,9 children with febrile seizures did not have lower IQ or poorer academic performance compared with their unaffected siblings. A later UK study of 398 children with febrile seizures also concluded that children with febrile seizures performed as well as other children in terms of their academic progress and intellect at 10 years of age,8 and a Finnish study from 2011 found no significant difference between children with and without febrile seizures in academic success at ages 12 and 18 years.10 However, a few studies, including a more recent Dutch Generation R study,11 did find that children with recurrent febrile seizures were at risk for delayed language development, and there are other lines of evidence suggesting that some subsets of children with febrile seizures (eg, those with focal or prolonged febrile seizures, with multiple recurrences of febrile seizures, or with underlying neurodevelopmental disorders) are at increased risk of poor neurologic outcomes later in life, including psychiatric disorders,2,13 epilepsy,2,14,15,16 and mortality.17,18 Given the limited sample size of previous studies addressing academic outcomes and the more recent indication that some subsets of children with febrile seizures may be at risk of poor outcomes, we aimed to examine whether febrile seizures were associated with poorer school performance in the largest cohort to date, thereby enabling detailed analyses of the association across febrile seizure subgroups.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This is a nationwide, register-based cohort study. Using the Civil Registration System,19 we identified a source population of all children born in Denmark between 1997 and 2009, who were alive and living in Denmark at age 5 years, and whose parents had been living in Denmark for at least 2 years at the time of their birth, to ensure sufficient data availability to classify parental characteristics for all children (784 085 children). The Civil Registration System contains complete coverage of all individuals living in Denmark since 1968, and includes information on parental links, vital status, place of residence, sex, and a unique personal identification number, which enables exact linkage of individual-level information between national registers and databases. Using this number, we identified all children from the source population who participated in standardized testing in reading or mathematics as part of the Danish National School Test Program20 between 2010 and 2019 (672 119 children [85.7%]) (see flowchart in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).

Register-based research projects based on pseudonymized data are exempted from ethics approval in Denmark. However, the study was conducted and reported in accordance with Danish and European data protection regulations. Under Danish law, informed consent is not required for register-based studies. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cohort studies.

Assessment of Febrile Seizures

From the study cohort, we identified children with febrile seizures from the Danish National Patient Register,21 which includes all inpatient, outpatient, and emergency visits to Danish hospitals during the study period. Diagnostic codes follow the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Children were classified as having febrile seizures if registered with ICD-10 diagnosis code R56.0 between ages 3 months and 5 years without prior records of epilepsy (ICD-10 code G40), severe head trauma (ICD-10 codes S06.1-S06.9), central nervous system infection (ICD-10 codes G00-G09 and A39.0), brain tumor (ICD-10 codes C70-C71 and D32-D33), cerebral palsy (ICD-10 codes G80-G83), or intracranial infarction or bleeding (ICD-10 codes I60-I61 and I63-I64). Age at onset (<1 year, 1-2 years, or ≥3 years) was calculated by subtracting the birth date from the first day of the first hospital contact with febrile seizures. For each child, we also identified all hospital contacts with febrile seizures separated by 7 or more days from age 3 months to 5 years and recorded their total number (1, 2, 3, …, ≥7) and the longest duration (<1 day [outpatient], 1 day, 2 days, or ≥3 days). In addition, hospital-based records of epilepsy (ICD-10 code G40)21 were used to classify children as having neither febrile seizures nor epilepsy, febrile seizures only, epilepsy only, or both conditions. Information on psychiatric diagnoses (ICD-10 codes F00-F99), registered at the time of each test,21,22 were used to categorize children with febrile seizures according to comorbid psychiatric conditions: no psychiatric comorbidity, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and other psychiatric comorbidity. Finally, family history of febrile seizures (yes or no) was based on information from full siblings as in previous work,23 as parental history could not be reliably determined due to incomplete register coverage during the parents’ early childhood.

School Performance

School performance was assessed using norm-based scores from standardized reading and mathematics tests administered through the Danish National School Test Program (2010-2019). During this period, participation was mandatory for all children attending public primary and lower secondary schools (approximately 85% of Danish school-aged children).20 The program included compulsory reading tests in grades 2, 4, 6, and 8 and mathematics tests in grades 3 and 6 (and in grade 8 from 2018).20 Tests were adaptive, computer-based, and initially scored with a Rasch model.24 Scores were then converted by the Danish Ministry of Education to a norm-based scale (range, 1-100 points, with higher scores indicating better performance) reflecting national percentiles from the 2010 pilot tests.20 The number of available tests per child (across both disciplines) varied by birth cohort (range, 1-7 tests), since earlier cohorts had already advanced when the program was introduced and later cohorts were still in lower grades when the study period ended. Standardization allowed joint analysis of scores across disciplines, grades, and years.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from March 2025 to June 2026 using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R statistical software version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Mean differences in standardized test scores between children with and without febrile seizures were estimated using linear regression with 95% CIs. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < .05. Generalized estimating equations provided robust SEs to account for the lack of independence of multiple tests per child. Basic adjusted models included covariates for sex and test characteristics (year of testing, discipline, and grade). The fully adjusted model included additional potential confounders that were identified a priori on the basis of available literature25,26,27: firstborn status, birth weight, gestational age at birth, Apgar score at 5 minutes, maternal age at birth, household income, and maternal and paternal characteristics at birth (country of origin, educational level, diagnosis of epilepsy, and diagnosis of psychiatric disorder). The proportion of children with missing information in any covariate was low (3.6% of children with febrile seizures and 3.5% of children without), and a separate missing category was created for each variable with missing data.28

We estimated the mean score difference between children with and without febrile seizures and explored variation by sex, subject, and grade with interaction terms, and age at first febrile seizure, and number and maximum duration of hospital contacts using children without febrile seizures as reference. The role of epilepsy and psychiatric comorbidity was considered in a time-varying manner by classifying exposure status at the time of each test. Thus, in these analyses, it was possible for children to contribute with tests in multiple categories over time. We also examined the association of school performance with the number of febrile seizure–related hospital contacts restricted to tests not preceded by epilepsy, given the known link between recurrent febrile seizures and epilepsy.2 In further analyses, test scores were dichotomized (<36 vs ≥36 points) using official cutoffs29 to define below-average performance (score <36 points) vs average and above-average (score 36-100 points) and modeled with logistic regression and generalized estimating equations. To account for shared family factors, we compared sixth-grade scores of children with febrile seizures with those of their unaffected siblings using linear regression models with parental fixed effects and adjustment for child-specific and test-specific characteristics. Finally, given the familial aggregation of febrile seizures, we examined whether the association between febrile seizures and school performance differed according to family history of febrile seizures in siblings.

Results

The study population comprised 672 119 children (344 664 boys [51.3%] and 327 455 girls [48.7%]) with 1 748 645 reading tests and 1 044 249 mathematics tests, with a median of 3 (range, 1-4) reading and 2 (range, 1-3) mathematics tests per child. The mean (SD) testing age ranged from 8.9 (0.4) years in grade 2 to 14.9 (0.4) years in grade 8. We identified 26 173 children (3.9%) with a history of febrile seizures (11 521 of 327 455 girls [3.5%] and 14 652 of 344 664 boys [4.3%]). Among these, 5279 (20.2%) experienced their first episode before age 1 year, 18 562 (70.9%) between ages 1 and 2 years, and 2332 (8.9%) at age 3 years or older. A total of 6066 children (23.2%) had more than 1 hospital contact for febrile seizures, and the median (IQR) time between recurrent febrile seizure–related contacts was 5.1 (2.0-11.1) months. Most contacts were brief, with the maximum duration being 1 day or less for 20 659 children (78.9%). Children with febrile seizures, particularly those with recurrent hospital contacts, were more likely than unaffected children to be firstborn, born preterm, have low birth weight, and have a family history of epilepsy or psychiatric disorders (Table 1).

The mean overall test score was 54.7 points (95% CI, 54.6 to 54.9 points) among children with and 55.8 points (95% CI, 55.7 to 55.8 points) among children without a history of febrile seizures (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1), corresponding to a mean difference of −0.9 point (95% CI, −1.2 to −0.6 point) in the basic adjusted model accounting for sex and test characteristics and −0.4 point (95% CI, −0.7 to −0.2 point) in the fully adjusted model additionally accounting for birth and family characteristics. The association was mainly apparent in girls (fully adjusted mean difference, −0.7 point; 95% CI, −1.1 to −0.3 point) and not in boys (fully adjusted mean difference, −0.2 point; 95% CI, −0.5 to 0.2 point; P for interaction = .05), and showed no substantial variation by subject or grade (Figure 1). Compared with children without febrile seizures, the test scores were, on average, lower in those with younger age (<1 year, −0.5 point; 95% CI, −1.1 to 0.0 point) or older age (≥3 years, −1.6 point; 95% CI, −2.4 to −0.7 point) at first febrile seizure, than in children aged 1 to 2 years at first febrile seizure (−0.2 point; 95% CI, −0.5 to 0.1 point) (Figure 2).

We observed increasing score differences with number of hospital contacts with febrile seizures, from −0.1 point (95% CI, −0.4 to 0.2 point) among children with 1 contact, −1.1 points (95% CI, −1.8 to −0.4 points) among those with 2 contacts, and −5.8 points (95% CI, −9.1 to −2.4 points) among those with 7 or more contacts (Figure 2). Similarly, longer hospital admissions were associated with larger score differences (eg, outpatient contact <1 day, 0.2 point [95% CI, −0.2 to 0.6 point] vs ≥3 days, −1.9 points [95% CI, −2.6 to −1.1 points]) (Figure 2). However, this pattern was attenuated when the analysis was restricted to children with only a single hospital contact for febrile seizures (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Epilepsy occurred more commonly in children with (838 of 26 173 children [3.2%]) than in those without (5600 of 645 946 children [0.9%]) febrile seizures. Compared with children without febrile seizures and epilepsy prior to testing, the mean difference in scores was −0.3 point (95% CI, −0.5 to −0.0 point) among children with febrile seizures only, −7.2 points (95% CI, −8.7 to −5.7 points) among those with both febrile seizures and epilepsy, and −6.3 points (95% CI, −6.9 to −5.7 points) among those with epilepsy alone (Table 2). Restricting to tests without preceding epilepsy attenuated the association between febrile seizure recurrence and school performance, but the overall pattern persisted (eg, from −0.0 point [95% CI, −0.3 to 0.3 point] with 1 contact to −2.2 points [95% CI, −6.0 to 1.5 points] with ≥7 contacts) (Table 2). When considering the role of psychiatric comorbidity, we found that febrile seizures were not associated with poorer school performance in the subset of children with febrile seizures with no psychiatric diagnoses before the time of testing (24 931 children; adjusted mean difference, 0.1 point; 95% CI, −0.1 to 0.4 point) (Table 2).

For the sibling comparison, we identified all children from the overall population of 672 119 with at least 1 sixth-grade reading or mathematics test (478 682 children) and grouped them into sets sharing the same mother and father (336 574 sets, including 123 539 full-sibling sets with at least 2 children, of which 8540 were discordant on febrile seizure status). Mean test scores of children with febrile seizures were marginally lower than those of unaffected siblings (mean difference, −0.6 point; 95% CI, −1.0 to −0.3 point) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Furthermore, of the 26 173 children with febrile seizures, 2001 (7.7%) had a sibling with a history of febrile seizures. Analyses incorporating family history showed no association between febrile seizures in the index child and school performance when the child also had an affected sibling (mean difference, −0.1 point; 95% CI, −1.0 to 0.8 point). In contrast, a slight reduction in school performance was observed among children without a sibling with febrile seizures (mean difference, −0.4 point; 95% CI, −0.7 to −0.2 point) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Nonparticipation

From the source population eligible for inclusion (784 085 children), we excluded 111 966 children (14.3%) because of nonparticipation in the Danish National School Test Program. Reasons included attending a private school without a requirement for testing (93 274 children [11.9%]), attending a special-education school (9109 children [1.2%]), and unknown reasons (9583 children [1.2%]). The proportion with febrile seizures was slightly higher among nonparticipants (4612 of 111 966 children [4.1%]) than among included children (26 173 of 672 119 children [3.9%]). Further characteristics of participants and nonparticipants are available in eTable 4 in Supplement 1, whereas characteristics of children with and without febrile seizures among nonparticipants are shown in eTable 5 in Supplement 1.

Discussion

In this cohort study, we observed more than 26 000 children with a history of febrile seizures and compared their performance on standardized reading and mathematics tests from ages 7 to 17 years with that of their nationwide peers. Overall, school performance among children with febrile seizures was largely similar to that of unaffected children, with only minimal differences in test scores. However, our findings suggest that certain subgroups may be at increased risk of poorer academic outcomes, particularly children with multiple recurrent febrile seizures requiring emergency care or hospitalization, and those who later develop epilepsy.

In our population, more than 3 of 4 children with febrile seizures were seen in the hospital only once for a febrile seizure episode. Among these children with only 1 febrile seizure admission, the association with later school performance was minimal, if any; the 95% CI ranged from a 0.4–percentile point decrease to a 0.2–percentile point increase. These findings are consistent with prior key studies,8,9,10,11,30,31,32 examining various cognitive and academic outcomes during childhood and adolescence, that taken together are reassuring. However, our study also found that recurrent hospital contacts for febrile seizures were associated with poorer school performance in a dose-dependent manner, even among children without epilepsy. The evidence on whether recurrent febrile seizures lead to long-term intellectual impairment is mixed. Some studies suggest that outcomes in children with recurrent febrile seizures are similar to those with a single episode8,30 or to their unaffected siblings,9 whereas others have found associations with poorer neuropsychological test performance31,33 and delayed vocabulary development.11 In another study,32 children with early onset of febrile seizures were found to have lower full-scale, verbal, and processing speed IQ, and the authors speculated that this was possibly related to the high recurrence rate of febrile seizures (75%) in this group. Inconsistencies may reflect sample size limitations, as previous studies included only a few hundred children with febrile seizures and were likely underpowered to detect associations with recurrence. Our study included more than 26 000 children with febrile seizures, including over 6000 children with recurrent febrile seizures, enabling us to examine the role of recurrence in greater granularity and detail. However, our ability to identify recurrence depended on hospital contact, meaning our sample may not fully represent all children with recurrent febrile seizures. In Denmark, children are typically hospitalized after their first febrile seizure, but not necessarily for subsequent simple recurrences. Therefore, repeated hospital contacts in our data may reflect more complex or atypical seizure episodes, including febrile status epilepticus, which may have inflated our estimates. In addition, children with recurrent hospitalizations for febrile seizures tended to have more adverse perinatal characteristics, suggesting a potentially higher burden of comorbidity related to early-life adversities; however, the findings remained robust even after adjustment for these differences.

Febrile seizures are classified as simple when they are generalized and resolve within 10 to 15 minutes, and complex if they are either focal, prolonged (ie, >10-15 minutes), or recur within 24 hours. Complex febrile seizures have been linked to worse cognitive and academic outcomes in some,31,34 but not all,8,9,30 studies. In our study, we were unable to distinguish between simple and complex febrile seizures. Instead, we examined how the association with school performance varied by duration of the hospital stay, which may serve as a proxy for seizure complexity. These results did show larger score differences with increasing duration of the hospitalization, although even with hospitalizations lasting 3 days or more, the magnitude of the association remained modest.

Children with febrile seizures are known to be at increased risk of developing epilepsy later in life, with cumulative incidence estimates of around 6% to 7% by young adulthood, and even higher risks among those with recurrent or complex febrile seizures.2,14,15,16 Our results showed that children with febrile seizures who went on to develop epilepsy had substantially poorer school performance than unaffected peers, and possibly also slightly worse performance than children with epilepsy alone. Previous studies of academic outcomes in children with febrile seizures have not reported estimates separately for children who later developed epilepsy,8,9,10,11,12 but febrile seizures and epilepsy can co-occur in certain epilepsy syndromes (eg, genetic epilepsy with febrile seizures plus and Dravet syndrome)35 or in temporal lobe epilepsy following prolonged febrile seizures,36 which are conditions that has been linked to cognitive impairment37 and intellectual disability in severe cases.38 Children with co-occurring febrile seizures and epilepsy have also been shown to be at higher risk of developing severe psychiatric disorders later in life than children with either condition alone,13 suggesting that these children may constitute a particularly vulnerable subgroup at risk of broader neurodevelopmental difficulties. Evidence indicates that there is a substantial genetic correlation between febrile seizures and epilepsy,39 as well as between epilepsy and both psychiatric disorders40 and general cognitive ability,41 highlighting the need for future research to explore the genetic mechanisms underlying the associations observed in the present study.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations. First, ascertainment of febrile seizures relied on register data, and as the case definition (hospital-based record of ICD-10 code R56.0 between 3 months and 5 years of age, without prior records of central nervous system infections, selected neurological conditions, or structural abnormalities) used in the present study has not been formally validated, sensitivity and specificity could not be assessed. A 2006 validation study1 of children born between 1990 and 1992 found a completeness of febrile seizure registration in the Danish National Patient Register of 72% and a positive predictive value of 93% for any febrile seizure code. However, completeness during our study period (children born from 1997 to 2009) is likely higher due to the inclusion of outpatient and emergency contacts in the register from 1995 onward,2 whereas potential reductions in positive predictive value from this broader capture were addressed by applying exclusion criteria for underlying causes of seizures and age restrictions. Children who had experienced status epilepticus before febrile seizures were not excluded by the case definition; however, this applied to a very small number of children and is, therefore, unlikely to have influenced our findings to any appreciable extent. Second, the register-based design limited our ability to assess specific clinical features (eg, whether febrile seizures were simple or complex), requiring the use of proxies. However, even in the clinical situation, it may be difficult to make the distinction between simple and complex features, and complex features may be poorly recognized by distressed parents.35 Third, our analyses did not include children attending independent or special education schools without mandatory participation in the Danish National Test Program, as well as those exempted from testing, which may have biased estimates toward null. The proportion of children with febrile seizures was slightly higher among nonparticipants, suggesting some selection and limiting generalizability of our findings to children with febrile seizures who can attend mainstream schools and participate in testing. This limitation is particularly relevant for populations with a higher baseline risk of seizures, such as children with underlying central nervous system pathology or those in special education settings. Despite these limitations, major strengths of this study include the large number of children with febrile seizures with long-term follow-up, enabling detailed subgroup analyses and the use of standardized assessments of school performance, which minimized the risk of observer bias.

Conclusions

This large nationwide cohort study shows that febrile seizures are generally not associated with any later meaningful impairments in school performance, providing reassurance to most families. Still, our findings also suggest that a smaller subgroup, particularly those who have multiple recurrences of febrile seizures requiring emergency care or hospitalization and those who later develop epilepsy, may be at increased risk of poorer long-term academic outcomes and, thus, may benefit from additional educational support.
