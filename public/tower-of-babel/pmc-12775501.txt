A thermo-electroreduction strategy unlocks propylene glycol synthesis from glycerol at ampere level current density
Authors: Huaiyu Chang, Longhai Zhang, Ziyu Mi, Panyawut Tonanon, Chengyi Liu, Xun Cao, Mingsheng Zhang, Jiajian Gao, Shibo Xi, Richard D Webster, Haobo Li, Wan Ru Leow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of valuable chemicals from glycerol can alleviate its large market surplus and reduce emissions that arise from petrochemical-based feedstocks. Here we show that a thermo-electroreduction strategy can expand the scope of glycerol valorization to a product of higher effective H/C ratio, such as propylene glycol. The thermal dehydration of glycerol unlocks the hydroxyacetone intermediate, which can undergo subsequent electrocatalytic hydrogenation to propylene glycol using Co-cluster-decorated Cu foam electrocatalyst. Theoretical simulations suggest that Co/Cu modulates the adsorption configuration of hydroxyacetone, such that all functional groups are in similar distance from the surrounding water layer and the surface. This can facilitate low-energy-barrier hydrogenation of the O atom on hydroxyacetone by solvated H species through an Eley-Rideal-type mechanism, followed by spontaneous hydrogenation of the adjacent C atom. We thus achieve high Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol of ~81% even at current densities up to 1000 mA/cm2.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

The manufacture of biodiesel generates glycerol as a major byproduct, which can amount to 10 wt% of the biodiesel produced. The increasing demand for renewable biodiesels to replace petroleum-derived fuels has therefore resulted in a large surplus of low-cost glycerol in the market, which can be relieved by developing technologies that use glycerol as a platform chemical to synthesize valuable chemicals. Electrochemical strategies to valorize glycerol are particularly attractive as they can operate under ambient conditions and be powered by renewable electricity. Examples include the electro-oxidation of glycerol to glyceric acid1–3 or dihydroxyacetone4,5, as well as the oxidative cleavage of glycerol to C1 (formic acid)6–11 and C2 (glycoaldehyde and ethylene glycol) chemicals12,13.

We postulate that an electro-reduction strategy may broaden the scope of high demand chemicals achievable14–16, as glycerol has a lower effective H/C ratio compared to petrochemical-derived commodities17. It can also preserve the C3 backbone of glycerol, leading to chemicals of higher market value and profitability. A target chemical can be propylene glycol, which has a significant annual demand to alleviate the glycerol surplus. Propylene glycol has one of the highest carbon intensities among commodity chemicals, partly due to the propylene feedstock that is derived from petrochemicals and has a high carbon footprint of 2.4 tons CO2-equivalent per ton propylene18,19. The remaining CO2 emissions arise from the high demand for fossil-based energy, and as a byproduct of the two-step production process via propylene oxide. These emissions can be mitigated by developing a renewable-energy-powered technology to produce propylene glycol from glycerol.

Here, we propose a thermo-electroreduction strategy to enable the conversion of glycerol to propylene glycol. The dehydration of glycerol at 220 °C unlocks the hydroxyacetone intermediate, which can undergo electrocatalytic hydrogenation to propylene glycol using cobalt-cluster-decorated Cu foam as a cathode (Fig. 1a). While propylene glycol can also be produced through the thermocatalytic hydrogenation of hydroxyacetone, this involves harsh conditions such as high temperatures (>200 °C) and hydrogen pressures (>2 MPa), which meant that high cost H2 must be continuously consumed20. In contrast, our electrochemical process uses water as a cheap and benign hydrogen source.

To demonstrate that an electro-reduction strategy can generate high demand commodities by increasing the effective H/C ratio of a biomass-derived molecule such as glycerol, the electrochemical hydrogenation is performed alongside the electro-oxidation of propylene, a petrochemical-derived molecule, to yield propylene oxide at the anode (Fig. 1a). Specifically, an IrO2/Ti anode converts chloride to hypochlorous acid that reacts with propylene to form propylene chlorohydrin, which is then combined with the stoichiometric amount of alkali formed in the catholyte to form propylene oxide (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2)21,22. Our result shows that pairing the reduction of a biomass-derived molecule with the oxidation of a petrochemical-derived molecule can converge on products of similar markets, such that the amount of valuable C3 products obtained in a single electrochemical reactor can be doubled (Faradaic efficiency ~182% at current density 100 mA/cm2, Fig. 1b, the corresponding chronopotentiometry can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3).

We performed a technoeconomic analysis by combining experimental values of Faradaic efficiency and full-cell potential from Fig. 1b with the simulated reaction and separation processes (Fig. 1c, see detailed calculation method under section “Detailed TEA calculation” in Supplementary Information and Supplementary Fig. 4). The resultant plant-gate levelized cost of propylene glycol ($/ton) produced from thermal glycerol dehydration and electrochemical hydroxyacetone hydrogenation is within the market price, which indicates the profitability of the process (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 8, the corresponding devices and specifications can be found in Supplementary Tables 1 and 10, the simulated values of energy consumption in Supplementary Tables 2 and 11, and the simulated utility consumption values in Supplementary Tables 3 and 12). If this process is coupled to the electro-oxidation of propylene to propylene oxide (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Tables 7–9), the profit margin can be significantly increased despite higher input chemicals costs of consuming both propylene and hydroxyacetone, as the dual production of propylene glycol and propylene oxide can command much higher market values.

Results

Electrode design and characterization

To understand the effect of the electrocatalyst on hydroxyacetone (HA) hydrogenation, we fabricated a Co/Cu electrode through electrodeposition on Cu foam in 0.1 M Co(NO3)2·6H2O aqueous solution at 1.0 mA/cm2 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed the presence of Co, Cu and O, which indicated that Co was successfully deposited on Cu. In particular, the Co 2p spectrum revealed deconvoluted peaks corresponding to Co(OH)2 and metallic Co. High-resolution transmission electron spectroscopy (HRTEM) showed that the Co species exist as small crystalline particles of approximately 8–15 nm (circled in white). The Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) of Co/Cu exhibits characteristic peaks at 7728.29 eV, 7742.12 eV and 7772.82 eV, while the extended X-ray absorption fine-structure (EXAFS) spectrum reveals two main peaks at 1.57 Å and 2.71 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10h), which fit perfectly with Co(OH)2 standard and is consistent with the results of XPS analysis23,24.

However, we do not expect that the Co-based nanoparticles will retain the same oxidation states under the highly reducing conditions during electrochemical HA hydrogenation. To elucidate the nature of the electrocatalyst during reaction, the Co/Cu electrode was characterized post-reaction (Fig. 2). The XPS Co 2p spectrum revealed doublet peaks at 778.45 and 794.28 eV that correspond to Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 of metallic Co25, which suggests that the Co(OH)2 species were reduced to metallic Co during the hydrogenation reaction. HRTEM showed that the Co species with small particle sizes of approximately 5–10 nm. The Co K-edge XANES spectrum of Co/Cu revealed peaks at 7727.99 eV, 7761.72 eV and 7809.94 eV that correspond well to the Co foil standard (Fig. 2h), while the EXAFS spectrum showed a peak located at 2.13 Å that is characteristic of Co-Co interaction (Fig. 2i)26,27. The slight Co-O peak at 1.34 Å may be caused by oxidation of the sample in air. Taken together, this means that the application of negative current during electrochemical HA hydrogenation reduces the Co(OH)2 nanoparticles in Co/Cu to metallic Co. The Cu-K edge XANES and EXAFS of Co/Cu display the same characteristic peaks as that of Cu foil standard (Fig. 2j, k).

Electrochemical hydrogenation of hydroxyacetone to propylene glycol

The Co/Cu electrode was evaluated for the hydrogenation of HA to propylene glycol, in a H-type electrochemical cell where the catholyte (0.2 M HA in 1.0 M aqueous KCl solution) electrolyte and anolyte (1.0 M aqueous KCl solution) are separated by a Nafion® 115 proton exchange membrane. IrO2/Ti was used as the counter electrode, while Ag/AgCl in saturated KCl solution was used as the reference electrode. The configuration of the cell is stated in Supplementary Fig. 11. The Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol is 88(±3)% under 100 mA/cm2 current density (Fig. 3a). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of the post-reaction electrolyte revealed the presence of acetone in addition to propylene glycol (Supplementary Fig. 12a), while on-line gas chromatography revealed that hydrogen gas evolved during reaction (Supplementary Fig. 13). This indicates that the competing reactions are hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) from water and hydrogenolysis of HA to acetone. The pH of the catholyte increases rapidly in the first 300 s and remains stable at 12–13 throughout the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 14), which suggests that the reaction consumes protons from the electrolyte. The stability of the Co/Cu electrocatalyst was evaluated at 100 mA/cm2 current density (Fig. 3a), by using a pump system to circulate 1 M KCl electrolyte into the H-cell from an external reservoir and replenish the HA reactant. To enable the chloride-mediated production of propylene oxide, propylene was sparged into the anolyte at 20 sccm flow rate, the anolyte and catholyte were subsequently combined to produce propylene oxide (Supplementary Fig. 12b). We find that the Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol reduces to about 42% after 70 h of continuous operation (Supplementary Fig. 15), which may be due to the gradual leaching of Co. This is verified by the reduction in Co content in the post-reduction electrode and appearance of Co in the post-reaction electrolyte (XPS, EDX and ICP-MS analysis can be found in Supplementary Fig. 16). This can be circumvented by introducing 0.02 mmol of Co(NO3)2 to the catholyte every 24 h, which replenished the lost Co through electrodeposition onto the Co/Cu. This enabled the system to operate at a stable applied potential of −0.48(±0.03) V vs. RHE for 100 h, with the Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol and propylene oxide averaging 87(±3)% and 89(±3)%, respectively.

We compared the electrochemical performance of Co/Cu against that of Ti/Cu, Fe/Cu, Cu/Cu and Ag/Cu (see Faradaic efficiencies in Fig. 3b and corresponding production rates in Supplementary Fig. 17), which were fabricated by the same electrodeposition method using 0.1 M aqueous metal nitrate solutions (or 0.1 M titanium isopropoxide in ethanol solution for Ti/Cu). EDX spectroscopy indicated that these elements were successfully deposited onto Cu foam (Supplementary Figs. 18–21). Ti/Cu showed a low Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol at 18(±2)% at 100 mA/cm2, and a high Faradaic efficiency towards hydrogen evolution at 83(±4)%. Meanwhile, Cu/Cu and Ag/Cu showed lower Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol (57–67%) compared to Co/Cu, and higher Faradaic efficiencies towards acetone (13–25%). It is worth noting that Ti has a higher hydrogen binding energy than Co, while Cu and Ag have weaker hydrogen binding energies, which may account for the differences in product composition. Fe has the most similar hydrogen binding energy to Co among the metals tested, and Fe/Cu showed a similar production composition with Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol at 83 (±3)%. These observations are well-corroborated with linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), which showed that the reaction and onset potentials of Co/Cu and Fe/Cu are less negative than that of the other electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 22). The Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol show a volcano-type trend when compared against the theoretically calculated hydrogen binding energies on Ti/Cu, Fe/Cu, Co/Cu, Cu/Cu, and Ag/Cu catalysts, such that a moderate binding energy corresponds to optimal selectivity, which further confirms the role of hydrogen binding strength (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). A similar trend was observed when the metals are supported carbon paper (CP), such that Co/CP and Fe/CP showed higher Faradaic efficiencies and electrochemical activity compared to Ti/CP, Cu/CP and Ag/CP (Supplementary Fig. 25a, the corresponding LSVs for each electrode are presented in Supplementary Fig. 25b), which reinforces that hydrogen binding energy of the electrodeposited metal may influence the kinetics of HA hydrogenation.

To understand if the Cu foam plays a catalytic role in the reaction or functions as mere support, we compared the electrochemical performance of Co/Cu with that of bare Cu foam, Co foam and Co electrodeposited on carbon paper (Co/CP). All Cu foam, Co foam and Co/CP electrodes showed catalytic activity towards electrochemical HA hydrogenation, as indicated by Faradaic efficiencies of 53(±5)%, 73(±6)% and 59(±4)% towards propylene glycol, respectively (Fig. 3c). Nevertheless, the Faradaic efficiency of Co/Cu is much higher by at least 15%, while the onset and reaction potentials of Co/Cu are also less negative compared to Cu foam, Co foam and Co/CP (Supplementary Fig. 26). This suggests that the combination of Co and Cu has a synergistic effect that steers selectivity towards propylene glycol. We optimized for the Co loading on Cu by comparing the electrochemical of Co/Cu electrodes fabricated under different electrodeposition current densities (i.e., −0.1, −0.5, −1, −5.0, −7.5 and −10 mA/cm2). EDX and XPS showed that the Co loading generally increases with the electrodeposition current density (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 27–31 for electrodes characterization). The Co/Cu electrode with the highest Faradaic efficiency and electrochemical activity was fabricated under −1.0 mA/cm2 (Supplementary Fig. 32), which, based on XPS (Supplementary Fig. 33), corresponds to a Co loading of 11.48%.

Investigation of the hydrogen transfer mechanism

To understand what makes Co/Cu selective towards HA-to-propylene glycol conversion, the hydrogen transfer mechanism must first be investigated. Electrochemical studies were conducted using the Co/Cu electrode in 1.0 M aqueous KCl electrolyte containing 0.2 M HA, such that the hydrogenation of HA becomes the dominant reaction, and also in the absence of HA, such that HER is the only cathodic reaction. LSV scans showed that the onset potential of HA hydrogenation is less negative than HER, which suggests more favorable kinetics under these reaction conditions (Fig. 4a). The Tafel slope (Fig. 4b) for HA hydrogenation is 133 mV/dec, which indicates that the first proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) step is the rate-determining step. This suggests two possible mechanisms for HA hydrogenation: (1) the dissociation of water in electrolyte and forming surface hydride species (H*) via the Volmer step, followed by Langmuir-Hinshelwood (LH) addition of H* to HA, or (2) the reaction of surface-adsorbed HA with H2O from electrolyte and an electron via the Eley-Rideal (ER)-type PCET step28–31. The first mechanism would depend strongly on the presence of surface H* species to supply the LH step, which can be quenched by introducing tert-butanol (t-BuOH) to the electrochemical HA hydrogenation reaction (Fig. 4d)31,32. The Faradaic efficiency decreased by about 18% with 0.6 M t-BuOH added, which indicates that the proton for HA hydrogenation predominantly originate from H2O in the electrolyte and that surface H* makes only a minor contribution to HA-to-propylene glycol conversion.

Meanwhile, the ER mechanism necessitates interaction between surface-adsorbed HA and H2O solvent, which means that reaction kinetics can be enhanced by introducing a species that donates protons more readily than water. This hypothesis was tested by conducting HA hydrogenation in 2 types of buffers, 1.0 M phosphate and borate, at pH 12 to simulate the catholyte pH in a typical reaction run. The reactions were conducted under a relatively low current density of 50 mA/cm2 to preserve the pH of the buffers throughout the reaction. Under these conditions, the phosphate buffer predominantly comprises HPO42− species that function as explicit proton donors. On the other hand, borate is an innocent buffer that should not substantially promote or inhibit hydrogenation kinetics, which means that the primary proton source is H2O33,34. The Faradaic efficiency of HA hydrogenation (Fig. 4c) in the phosphate buffer is 79(±4)%, which is significantly higher at compared to that in the borate buffer (54(±5)%). We performed isotope labeling experiments (Fig. 4e) and observed that the propylene glycol generation rate decreases significantly in D2O versus H2O solvent (i.e., 0.63 versus 1.81 mmol/cm2/h at 0.5 V). This corresponds to a strong kinetic isotopic effect (KIE) of rH/rD = 2.9/1, which reinforces that the hydrogen transfer to HA via a PCET step is the rate-determining step35.

Next, we studied the influence of electrolyte-based factors on electrochemical HA hydrogenation. The Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol increased from 52(±4)% to 91(±3)% when HA concentration increased from 0.05 to 0.2 M, but remained largely unchanged with further increments in HA concentration (Supplementary Fig. 34a). Meanwhile, varying the KCl concentration of the electrolyte from 0.2 to 1.2 M showed little changes in product composition at the cathode (Supplementary Fig. 34b). However, if paired with the chloride-mediated production of propylene oxide, low KCl concentration can steer anodic selectivity towards oxygen evolution reaction. It can be seen that the Faradaic efficiency towards propylene oxide is lower at 72(±4)% at 0.2 M KCl concentration, as compared to >85% at higher KCl concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 35. The cation of the electrolyte does not appear to play a role in the reaction; Faradaic efficiencies remained roughly ~85% when electrochemical HA hydrogenation was conducted in 0.2 M aqueous LiCl, NaCl and CsCl electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 36).

The influence of electrolyte pH was investigated by conducting electrochemical HA hydrogenation in phosphate buffer; by adjusting the ratios of phosphoric acid to tripotassium phosphate in the phosphate buffer, electrolytes of pHs 1, 4, 7, 12 and 14 can be obtained. A clear relationship can be observed between the product composition and electrolyte pH. At low pHs (i.e., 1 and 4), the H2 Faradaic efficiencies are about 78–91%, which indicates that HER is the dominant reaction. The Faradaic efficiency funnels towards propylene glycol as pH increases, reaching maximum of 79(±4)% at pH 12. This coincides with the equilibrium pH of the catholyte when HA hydrogenation was conducted in aqueous KCl electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 14), which meant that as reaction proceeds and H+ is consumed, the catholyte reaches the optimum pH for HA hydrogenation.

We performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to understand the interfacial structure of the Co/Cu electrocatalyst under reaction conditions. A slab model based on Cu(111) surface was constructed with 4 atomic layers and a unit-cell size of (10.40 × 10.40) Å (Supplementary Fig. 37a, b). A cluster of 3 Co atoms was supported on the Cu(111) surface to simulate the Co/Cu interfacial structure in the Co/Cu electrocatalyst (Supplementary Fig. 37c, d). Considering that the reducing cathodic potential drives the the formation of thermodynamically stable *H-covered surface structures36–38, Pourbaix diagrams were derived for both Cu(111) and Co/Cu(111) by calculating the surface free energies (γ) of surface structures with different hydrogen coverage and selecting the structure with the lowest γ value under selected U and pH conditions (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Figs. 38–40). We elected to take a closer look at the stable Cu(111) and Co/Cu(111) structures at pH = 12 and U = −0.6 V vs RHE; conditions which are favorable for propylene glycol production on the Co/Cu electrode (as exemplified by the high Faradaic efficiency of 90(±4)% and current density −380 mA/cm2 in Supplementary Fig. 41). Under these conditions, no hydrogen coverage was observed on the Cu(111) surface, whereas the Co/Cu(111) surface has a higher hydrogen coverage of 7/16 monolayer, which was primarily localized around the Co sites.

We then utilized these stable Cu(111) and Co/Cu(111) structures as basis for the investigation of HA hydrogenation mechanism. In order for HA to become propylene glycol, both the O atom and the middle C atom must be hydrogenated, it is not known the sequence through which this occurs. The thermodynamic reaction energy profiles for both reaction pathways have been calculated for comparison, as shown in Fig. 5, with an explicit water layer incorporated to simulate the electrocatalytic aqueous solvent environment. The energy profiles of the reaction (Fig. 5c) show that first reaction pathway is more thermodynamic favorable for both pristine Cu(111) and Co/Cu(111). Furthermore, protonation of O atom requires overcoming an endothermic step of 0.42 eV on Cu(111), but proceeds almost spontaneously on Co/Cu(111) with a thermodynamic energy barrier of 0.02 eV. These findings agree with experimental observations that Co/Cu exhibits a significantly higher Faradaic efficiency and current density than Cu foam at −0.6 V (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). Further investigations on the atomic configurations of the reaction intermediates show that the difference in calculated reaction energies arises from variations in adsorption geometries of the reactants and the microenvironment comprising the surrounding solvent water molecules. On the Cu(111) surface (Fig. 5a), the =O and -OH groups of HA point towards the water layer, while the -CH2 and -CH3 point towards to the surface. This stabilized configuration lowers the thermodynamic reaction energy barrier and thereby facilitates the reaction. We performed further comparative calculations on the Co(111) surface (Supplementary Figs. 37e, f, and 43–45), which show full hydrogen coverage under experimental conditions. The HA hydrogenation follows the same reaction pathway in which the O atom is first hydrogenated, with a thermodynamic energy barrier of 0.51 eV, which is similar to that of Cu(111) but significantly higher than that observed on Co/Cu(111). This suggests that the enhanced activity originates from the Co/Cu interface, with a synergistic effect between Co and Cu.

Our calculated mechanism meant that the initial protonation of O atom in HA should generate HO-C* radical as the intermediate, which can be detected by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy39 via spin trapping (Fig. 5d). 5-5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was added as the trapping agent to capture the radicals generated during electrochemical HA hydrogenation under 100 mA/cm2 current density. In the absence of HA such that HER is the only cathodic reaction, the characteristic peaks of DMPO-H* were observed, which is indicative of the water dissociation to OH– and H* when negative current was applied to working electrode. In the presence of HA such that HA-to-propylene glycol conversion becomes dominant, we observed sextet peaks corresponding to DMPO-C* adduct. This corroborates with the findings from our DFT calculations, which indicated that the first hydrogenation occurs preferentially on the O atom rather than the the Cmiddle atom.

Overall glycerol-to-propylene glycol conversion

We envisioned that the rate of HA-to-propylene glycol conversion needs to be high to alleviate the glycerol surplus on the market, and evaluated the electrochemical HA hydrogenation at high current densities of 100, 300, 500 and 1000 mA/cm2 (Fig. 6a, the corresponding production rates towards propylene oxide and propylene glycol can be found in Supplementary Fig. 46). We found that Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol remained high at 79–90% even up to 1000 mA/cm2, which is significantly higher than previously-reported HA-to-propylene glycol electroconversions (Supplementary Fig. 47)33,40–42, as well as prior literature reports in which glycerol was electrochemically valorized to commodity chemicals such as glyceric acid2,3,43–45and dihydroxyacetone (Fig. 6b)1–13,42–52. The reaction is highly compatible with the chloride-mediated production of propylene oxide at the anode, such that the total Faradaic efficiencies towards C3 products are about 153–180%.

To realize an overall glycerol-to-propylene glycol conversion, we integrated the electrochemical system with the dehydration of glycerol to HA (Fig.1a). Specifically, pure glycerol was heated with Cu-SiO2-Al2O3 (characterization of the catalyst in Supplementary Fig. 48) at 220 °C, and the difference in boiling points between glycerol and HA (i.e., 290 vs 145 °C) meant that HA of high purity (83%) can be easily obtained by attaching a Liebig condenser over the thermocatalytic reactor to collect the gaseous product (Fig. 6c, detailed study can found in Supplementary Figs. 49 and 50). The Faradaic efficiency towards propylene glycol (Fig. 6d) remained the same with the synthesized versus commercial HA at 100 mA/cm2, which showed that the electrochemical system is unaffected by presence of glycerol in the reactant. We also looked into the direct use of sea water, an abundant and readily accessible resource, as the supporting electrolyte. The untreated seawater enabled Faradaic efficiencies of 63(±4)% and 47(±3)% towards propylene glycol and propylene oxide (Fig. 6e), respectively, despite the low NaCl concentration (estimated to be 0.34 M based on resistivity) and presence of carbonate and other impurities. The C3 product Faradaic efficiency can be increased by evaporation of seawater to increase the NaCl concentration. This suggests that electrochemical HA hydrogenation can be made even more sustainable through the direct use of crude resources. However, the presence of impurities such as Mg2+ can react with OH- produced by the cathode and leads to white Mg(OH)2 precipitate in the electrolyte and on the electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 51a, insert, XRD of the electrode surface in Supplementary Fig. 51b), thereby causing less-than-optimal Faradaic efficiencies (Supplementary Fig. 51a–c). This issue can be easily resolved by pre-treating the seawater with potassium carbonate (K2CO3) to precipitate and remove these ions (Supplementary Fig. 52a). The treated seawater enabled 100 h of continuous operation, with a stable potential of −0.59(±0.07) V. vs. RHE under 100 mA/cm2 current density. The average Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol and propylene oxide are 84 ± 5% and 90 ± 3% respectively (Supplementary Fig. 52b), which are similar to the values obtained with commercial KCl electrolyte (Fig. 3a).

Discussion

We report a thermo-electroreduction strategy that can expand the scope of glycerol valorization to a product of higher effective H/C ratio, such as propylene glycol. While the glycerol dehydration step requires heat, the energy consumption is about only 246.2 kWh based on our ASPEN simulation, which is about 45.55 kg of CO2 emissions per ton of propylene glycol production53. This is insignificant considering that current industrial production from propylene feedstock has a carbon footprint of 4.17–4.8 tons CO2 per ton propylene glycol, while best available technologies using glycerol feedstock emit 1.9 to 8.8 tons CO2 per ton propylene glycol54. The thermal dehydration step is critical for unlocking the HA intermediate from glycerol, which can undergo subsequent electrocatalytic hydrogenation to propylene glycol using Co-cluster-decorated Cu foam electrocatalyst. Theoretical simulations suggest that Co/Cu modulates the adsorption configuration of HA, inducing a flat-lying orientation on the surface with all functional groups in similar distance from both the surrounding water layer and the surface. This can allow the hydrogenation of O atom on HA via an Eley-Rideal-type pathway to proceed almost spontaneously with a low thermodynamic energy barrier of 0.02 eV. As a result, we achieved high Faradaic efficiencies towards propylene glycol at 79–90% even at current densities up to 1000 mA/cm2. We envision that the high rate of glycerol-to-propylene glycol conversion, coupled with the significant annual demand for propylene glycol, can potentially alleviate the glycerol surplus caused by increasing biodiesel manufacture.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used without further purification. Cobalt(II) nitrate (ACS reagent, ≥98%, Co(NO3)2·6H2O), Hydroxyacetone (contains ≤500 ppm sodium carbonate as stabilizer, technical grade, 90%, HA), Glycerol (ACS reagent, ≥99.5%), Silver nitrate (ACS reagent, ≥99%, AgNO3), Hydrochloric Acid (ACS reagent, 37%, HCl), Potassium chloride (For analysis EMSURE®, KCl), Ferric nitrate nonahydrate (ACS reagent, ≥98%, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), Titanium(IV) isopropoxide (97%), Propylene glycol (≥99%, PG), Dimethyl sulfoxide (ACS reagent, ≥99.9%, DMSO), Propylene oxide (ReagentPlus®, ≥99%, PO), Potassium phosphate (ACS reagent, ≥99%, KH2PO4) and Dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (ACS reagent, ≥98%, K2HPO4) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The seawater used in our experiments was acquired from West Coast Park, Singapore at location 1°18’ N, 103°51’ E, and was filtered for debris and sand particles before use. Cu foam (Cu >99%, thickness 0.5 mm, 100 PPI) was purchased from Latech Scientific Supply Pte. Ltd. The Cu foam was cut into 1 × 1.5 cm2 pieces, cleaned by ultrasonication in ethanol, acetone and 1 M HCl, and washed with deionized water before use. The standard calibration gas mixtures for calibrating gas products in gas chromatography systems and nitrogen gas (99.99%) were purchased from Air Liquide Singapore Pte. Ltd. Nafion® 115 membrane was purchased from Sci Materials Hub.

Synthesis of Co/Cu electrode

To synthesize the Co/Cu electrode, Cu foam (1 × 1 cm2 deposition area) was equipped as the working electrode in a one-pot electrochemical cell containing 0.1 M Co(NO3)2·6H2O aqueous solution, platinum foil counter electrode and Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The Co/Cu electrodes of various Co loading were fabricated through electrodeposition under different current densities (i.e., −0.1 mA/cm2, −0.5 mA/cm2, −1.0 mA/cm2, −5.0 mA/cm2, −7.5 mA/cm2 and −10.0 mA/cm2) for 20 min at room temperature (25 °C), then dried at 70 °C overnight. A Co/Cu electrode fabricated at −1.0 mA/cm2 current density would have a Co loading of 0.35 mg/cm2, as determined by weighing the Cu foam electrode before and after electrodeposition. The Fe/Cu, Cu/Cu and Ag/Cu electrodes were synthesized through similar procedure in 0.1 M aqueous solution of the corresponding nitrate salt, while Ti/Cu was synthesized in 0.1 M Titanium(IV) isopropoxide ethanol solution.

Electrocatalyst characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained on a Hitachi SU8220 FESEM, at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was performed with an on-board AEMTEK® Octane Elite EDS System, with an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were captured on JEOL JEM-2200FS. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was conducted on a PHI Quantum 2000 XPS system. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement was operated on a Bruker® D8 ADVANCE system with an increment of 0.02°. X-Ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was operated on XAFCA beamline at the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS). EPR spectra were performed on aqueous KCl solutions with and without HA at 22(±2) °C, which were transferred to a quartz flat cell before and after −100 mA/cm2 current was applied and recorded using a continuous-wave X-band Bruker ELEXSYS E500 EPR spectrometer. The center field is 3490G, and sweep width 200G, with microwave frequency 9.79 GHz.

Evaluation of electrochemical performance

The cathodic HA-to-propylene glycol reduction coupled with anodic propylene-to-propylene conversion was conducted in a H-type electrochemical cell (purchased from Tianjin Aida) equipped with Co/Cu working electrode (working area 1 × 1 cm2), Ag/AgCl reference electrode and IrO2 electrocatalyst on Ti mesh counter electrode (working area 1 × 1 cm2). The Ag/AgCl reference electrode was calibrated prior to use by comparing its potential against that of a master Ag/AgCl (filled with saturated aqueous KCl) in 3 M KCl solution. The catholyte and anolyte were separated with Nafion® 115 membrane (size 2.5 × 2.5 cm2, thickness 0.125 mm), which had been activated in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution at 80 °C for 2 h and stored in 0.05 M H2SO4 solution prior to use. Unless otherwise stated, the catholyte was 0.2 M HA in 1.0 M KCl aqueous solution and the anolyte is 1 M KCl bubbled continuously with propylene gas at 20 sccm. Slight differences in purity are accounted for when using HA from different sources (90% for commercial HA versus 83% for glycerol-derived HA); the HA amount added to the electrolyte will be adjusted accordingly to ensure HA concentration of 0.2 M. The KCl electrolyte was prepared with MilliQ water (18.2 Ω), stored in a 500 mL amber volumetric flask and consumed within 1 week. Each electrochemical reaction was conducted at −100 mA/cm2 for 30 min (180 °C) unless otherwise stated. All experiments were conducted using a Metrohm Autolab potentiostat PGSTAT204 with iR correction. The potential was corrected by 85% iR compensation based on the electrolyte resistance determined by electrochemical impedance measurement at a high frequency of 100 kHz.

After reaction, the anolyte and catholyte were mixed to generate the propylene glycol and propylene oxide products. These were quantified with quantitative NMR (qNMR) spectroscopy using Bruker AV400SB spectrometer, using DMSO as internal standard. A WATERGATE suppression method was used to remove the signal that arises from water.

The cathodic H2 product was identified with gas chromatography, using an on-line SHIMADZU GC-2030AF system equipped with thermal conductivity detector, flame ionization detector and 20 sccm N2 as carrier gas. The volume of H2 gas evolved was quantified using a U-tube water displacement apparatus.

To evaluate the electrochemical performance under different electrolyte pH, 1.0 M phosphate buffers with specific pH values were prepared using different ratios of phosphoric acid (H3PO4), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4), dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (K2HPO4) and potassium hydroxide (KOH) in water. The pH of the resultant buffers, as determined by a SevenCompact pH/ion meter S220-Std-Kit, were 1.02(±0.06), 3.97(±0.07), 7.07(±0.05), 10.04(±0.09), 12.07(±0.10) and 13.91(±0.03). The 1.0 M borate buffer was prepared by mixing boric acid with aqueous KOH such that a pH value of 11.94(±0.08) was attained.

For the 100-hour stability test, peristaltic pumps were used to cycle the catholyte and anolyte from external reservoirs (200 mL each) into the H-cell. Both the catholyte and anolyte were refreshed every 24 h.

Thermal dehydration of glycerol to HA

The thermochemical dehydration of glycerol to HA was conducted through a thermal dehydration-distillation process. 500 mg of the Cu-SiO2-Al2O3 catalyst was added into a 100 mL round bottom flask containing 30 mL glycerol (Fig. 6c), which was heated with stirring to 220 °C for 18 h. The generated gaseous product was passed through a Liebig condenser, such that HA of 83% purity was collected.

The Cu-SiO2-Al2O3 catalyst was synthesized using the hydrothermal method. Specifically, Cu foam was placed into a Teflon-lined 50 mL autoclave containing 30 mL of 0.9 M aluminium nitrate and 0.1 M trimethoxysilane aqueous solution, which was heated at 120 °C for 5 h. The Cu foam was then removed and calcined in air at 500 °C for 2 h. Finally, the Cu-SiO2-Al2O3 was ground into powder for use in reaction.

DFT computations

Spin-polarized DFT calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)55. The ion–electron interaction was treated using the projector-augmented wave (PAW)56 method, while the exchange–correlation potential was approximated within the generalized gradient approximation using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)57 functional. The plane wave cut-off energy was set to 520 eV. The convergence thresholds for the self-consistent field and ionic steps were set to 1 × 10−5 eV and 0.05 eV Å−1, respectively. DFT-D358 method from Grimme was employed to correct the van der Waals interaction. The dipolar correction was included for all surface configuration optimizations and the symmetrization was disabled. Based on the experimental XRD characterization (Fig. 2g) and the reported lowest surface energy in previous theoretical studies59, a slab model of Cu(111) surface (Supplementary Fig. 37a, b) was constructed with 4 atomic layers and a unit-cell size of (10.40 × 10.40) Å, to simulate the Cu-based catalysts. The bottom two atomic layers were fixed for all the slabs and the vacuum space was 20 Å. The Brillouin zones were sampled by Monkhorst-Pack60 scheme with 3 × 3 × 1 k-grid. A cluster of 3 Co atoms was supported on the Cu(111) surface (Supplementary Fig. 36c, d) to simulate the Co/Cu interface structure formed by Co plating on the Cu catalyst (Fig. 2). The Co(111) surface model (Supplementary Fig. 37e-f) was also constructed with 4 atomic layers and a unit-cell size of (10.04 × 10.04) Å. One explicit water layer with eight H2O molecules was added on the surface to simulate the aqueous environment during the electrocatalytic processes. The atomic coordinates of the optimized models are provided in Supplementary Data 1.
