Evaluation of progesterone receptors with different cutoff values according to menopausal status in hormone-positive early breast cancer
Authors: Ayumi Ogawa, Takeshi Murata, Masayuki Yoshida, Natsuko Ohgi, Hiromi Hashiguchi, Ayako Nakashoji, Hinako Maeda, Chikashi Watase, Eriko Iwamoto, Shin Takayama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe impact of progesterone receptor (PR) expression level on breast cancer prognosis in relation to menopausal status is unclear. This study evaluated the impact of the association between PR expression level and menopausal status on prognosis in patients with estrogen receptor (ER)-positive (> 10%)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer. We retrospectively identified 2,796 patients with ER-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer and classified them into three groups based on PR expression levels: PR-high (> 10%), PR-low (1–10%), and PR-zero (< 1%). We compared the cumulative incidence of any recurrence (AR) and distant recurrence (DR) among the three groups by menopausal status. Over a median follow-up of 84.5 months, there were 324 AR and 264 DR events. In premenopausal patients, the PR-zero group (1,155 patients) had a significantly worse prognosis than did the PR-low (44 patients) and PR-high (60 patients) groups. Meanwhile, postmenopausal patients in the PR-zero (219 patients) and PR-low (167 patients) groups had similar prognosis, both significantly worse than the PR-high group (1,151 patients). Varying cutoff values of PR expression according to menopausal status could help determine more personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23743-z.Keywords: Breast cancer, Hormone receptor-positive, Progesterone receptor, Low-positive, Menopausal statusSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women. In 2020, more than 2 million women were diagnosed with breast cancer, with 0.68 million deaths occurring globally1,2. Modern breast cancer treatments are based on tumor biology, wherein the evaluation of hormone receptors (estrogen receptor [ER] and progesterone receptor [PR]) and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) is important for predicting prognosis and determining treatment strategies3,4. Hormone receptor-positive breast cancer is defined as breast cancer that expresses either ER or PR or expresses both5. The treatment of hormone receptor-positive breast cancer primarily targets estrogen, and the ER predicts the response to anti-estrogen endocrine hormone therapy (EHT)6. PR is an estrogen-regulated gene that is a target of estrogen-induced ER. The presence of PR is considered an indicator of normal estrogen and ER functions. Accordingly, the interaction between ER and PR is considered extremely important for the biological response to EHT7.

The evaluation of hormone receptors that predict the efficacy of endocrine therapy, according to the 2019 ASCO/CAP guidelines, is as follows: 1% to 100% of tumor nuclei positive should be interpreted as ER positive8. However, in recent years, differences in prognosis based on the ER expression levels have become evident9,10. While breast cancer with ER ≥ 10% is highly sensitive to endocrine therapy, breast cancer with ER of 1–9% is less sensitive to endocrine therapy11. This has led to the introduction of the category “ER-low positive” and to increased attention to ER expression levels in treatment decisions12,13. In contrast, the guidelines only state that PR ≥ 1% is positive and PR < 1% or 0% is negative; Therefore, the evaluation of PR is currently not as thorough as that of ER.

As mentioned above, ER and PR are closely related; PR is involved in the growth of ER-positive breast cancer cells14 and directly regulates tumor progression15,16, whereas estrogen stimulation is involved in the growth of PR-positive cells17,18. Hence, when examining PR expression, the state of estrogen exposure, that is, the menopausal status, must be considered. Consequently, PR expression and menopausal status are important factors for predicting prognosis and guiding treatment. However, treatment decisions in clinical practice are based on ER alone, and decisions based on PR have not been fully implemented. The cutoff value of PR expression is still unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the relationship between PR expression and prognosis in patients with ER-positive (> 10%) breast cancer undergoing endocrine therapy compared to menopausal status. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to define PR-low (1% to 10%) and to investigate the cutoff value of PR expression based on menopausal status.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 2,796 patients identified in this study, 1,259 (45.0%) were premenopausal, and 1,537 (55.0%) were postmenopausal women. The pre- and postmenopausal proportions of the PR-high, PR-low, and PR-zero groups were 91.7% vs. 74.9%, 4.8% vs. 10.9%, and 3.5% vs. 14.2%, respectively. Patient ages ranged from 20 to 91 years, with a median age of 54 years. The median follow-up period was 84.5 months (range 0.8–196.1 months).

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of patients classified by PR status before and after menopause (Table 1). The proportion of premenopausal patients was higher than that of postmenopausal patients in the PR-high (91.7% vs. 74.9%, P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed in clinical stage, surgical procedure, histological type, histologic grade, lymph node metastasis status, or radiotherapy between the pre- and postmenopausal groups. The rates of neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy were significantly higher in premenopausal patients than in postmenopausal patients (46.1% vs. 32.5%, P < 0.01).

Local recurrence and distant recurrence events

During the observation period, 324 any recurrence (AR) and 264 distant recurrence (DR) events occurred (premenopausal: AR [n = 152], DR [n = 118]; postmenopausal: AR [n = 172], DR [n = 146]). The most common organs to metastasize were the bone (129 patients), followed by the lung (71 patients) and liver (65 patients).

Comparison of premenopausal patients by PR status

As shown in Table 2, no significant differences were observed in patient background or perioperative treatment between the PR-zero and PR-low groups in premenopausal patients (Table 2); however, the prognosis was significantly worse in patients in the PR-zero group compared with the other group (AR: hazard ratio [HR], 2.34; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.10–5.00; P = 0.03; DR: HR, 2.96; 95% CI: 1.26–6.97; P = 0.01; Fig. 2; Table 3). In contrast, a comparison between the PR-low and PR-high groups (Table 2) showed that the PR-low group had a higher proportion of stage II–III (60.0% vs. 45.3%, P = 0.03) and a higher proportion of histologic grade 3 (40.7% vs. 19.1%, P < 0.01) than those had by the PR-high group. Despite the PR-low group having more advanced and worse grade cancer, no significant difference was observed in the neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy rates compared with the PR-high group (55.0% vs. 45.0%, P = 0.14). Furthermore, regarding survival outcomes, the PR-low group compared with the other groups tended to have a higher rate of early recurrence, within 2–3 years after surgery; however, when considering the entire observation period, the recurrence rate was similar (AR: HR, 0.78; 95% CI: 0.39–1.58; P = 0.49; DR: HR, 0.80; 95% CI: 0.35–1.80; P = 0.58; Fig. 2; Table 3).

Comparison of postmenopausal patients by PR status

As shown in Table 4, in postmenopausal patients, the PR-zero and PR-low groups did not differ significantly in terms of patient background and perioperative treatment (Table 4) and had similar prognoses (AR: HR, 0.76; 95% CI: 0.48–1.20; P = 0.24; DR: HR, 0.81; 95% CI: 0.49–1.35; P = 0.42; Fig. 3; Table 5). When comparing the PR-low and PR-high groups (Table 4), although the percentage of histologic grade 3 and clinical axillary lymph node metastasis cases were higher in the PR-low group, there was no difference in perioperative treatment between the two groups, and consequently, the PR-high group had a significantly better prognosis than that had by the PR-low group (AR: HR, 0.54; 95% CI: 0.36–0.80; P < 0.01; DR: HR, 0.59; 95% CI: 0.38–0.93; P = 0.02; Fig. 3; Table 5).

Prognosis with or without chemotherapy

Among premenopausal patients, the PR-zero group had a worse prognosis than that had by the PR-low group, even when receiving chemotherapy (AR: HR, 2.49; 95% CI: 0.99–6.25; P = 0.05; DR: HR, 2.89; 95% CI: 1.10–7.63; P = 0.03; Fig. 4; Table 3), although the two groups had almost no significant differences in the patient background (Supplementary Table S1). On the other hand, in postmenopausal patients who received chemotherapy, the PR-low group had a similar prognosis to that of the PR-high group (AR: HR, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.39–1.08; P = 0.09; DR: HR, 0.76; 95% CI: 0.43–1.34; P = 0.35; Supplementary Figure S1, Table 5). However, in the population that did not receive chemotherapy but underwent only endocrine therapy, the PR-low group had a significantly worse prognosis than that had by the PR-high group (AR: HR, 0.44; 95% CI: 0.23–0.84; P = 0.01; DR: HR, 0.42; 95% CI: 0.19–0.87; P = 0.02; Fig. 5; Table 5), although the two groups had no significant differences in the patient background (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In the 2019 ASCO/CAP guidelines8, defining PR-low (1–10%) is considered less important than defining ER. However, in this study, patients classified as the PR-zero (< 1%) and PR-low groups before menopause and the PR-low and PR-high (> 10%) groups after menopause had significantly different prognoses, and the importance of defining PR-low was also emphasized. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report that low PR expression is a poor prognostic factor and that the cutoff value of prognosis differs between premenopausal and postmenopausal patients with ER-positive (> 10%) and HER2-negative breast cancer receiving endocrine therapy.

The cutoff values for ER and PR that have been discussed so far were intended to determine eligibility for endocrine therapy. Consequently, many institutions and studies have used 1% and 10% thresholds to define positive and negative results, respectively. The aim of this study was to reevaluate prognosis, based on these established cutoff values, in a population for which endocrine therapy has already been planned. In 2008, Dowsett et al. reported that higher PR expression was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer recurrence19. Since then, there have been several reports on PR expression and prognosis20,21, which are consistent with our findings. The usefulness of PR as a marker for subtype determination was presented at the St. Gallen Consensus Conference in 201322. In the same year, Prat et al. reported that setting a 20% cutoff value for PR expression in ER-positive breast cancer led to significant differences in prognosis23. In 2014, Pudie et al. argued that the lack of PR expression was a strong independent prognostic variable and found a significantly worse disease-free survival in the PR-negative group in ER-positive and node-negative breast cancer24. When the Oncotype DX test became available, the correlation between recurrence score and PR expression status of the tumor was evaluated, and hormone receptor positivity was correlated with recurrence score and higher recurrence scores reported in cases with the PR-low (1%–10%) and PR-negative (0%) groups25.

The relationship between PR and prognosis is gradually becoming clearer, but unfortunately, the evaluation of PR expression adjusted for menopausal status remains controversial. In a study that was the most similar to our method, Hosoda et al. reported that PR expression (PR 0%, 1%–50%, and > 50%) correlated with prognosis in postmenopausal patients but not in premenopausal patients with ER-positive (≥ 10%) and HER2-negative breast cancers26. The TEXT and SOFT trials evaluating adjuvant endocrine therapy in premenopausal women with hormone receptor-positive early breast cancer found that decreased PR and increased Ki-67 expression were associated with a shorter breast cancer-free interval. However, the study concluded that the values of PR and Ki-67 as individual predictors in the selection of adjuvant endocrine therapy are limited (cutoff values were not defined)27. Nishimukai et al. also investigated the clinical significance of PR and Ki-67 in ER-positive (≥ 1%) and HER2-negative early breast cancer and found that high Ki-67 expression (≥ 15%) and low PR expression (≤ 20%) were significant independent factors that worsened distant recurrence in postmenopausal women but not in premenopausal women28.

The reason these studies did not find an association between PR expression and prognosis in premenopausal patients may be the different cutoff values for ER and PR in our study. Although the cutoff value for ER has been set at 1% in the 2019 ASCO/CAP guidelines8, one study reported that hormone receptor-positive breast cancers with ER and PR expression of 1–10% are poor prognostic breast cancers with outcomes similar to triple-negative breast cancer29. In actual clinical practice, the cutoff value of ER for endocrine therapy is likely to be 10% in many cases. When PR was stratified into PR-zero (0%), PR-low (1%–10%), and PR-high (> 10%) according to ER, different prognostic cutoff values were obtained for each menopausal status. The cutoff value for PR in premenopausal patients was set at 1% because the PR-low and PR-high groups had comparable survival outcomes. In postmenopausal patients, the cutoff value for PR was established at 10% because the PR-low and PR-zero groups had similar background and survival outcomes.

Based on the study results, we propose a revision of the treatment strategy for the PR-zero group in premenopausal and the PR-low group in postmenopausal patients, as treatment intensity may be insufficient. First, there were no differences in patient backgrounds between the PR-zero and PR-low groups of premenopausal patients receiving endocrine therapy and chemotherapy. However, the prognosis for the PR-zero group was extremely poor. The chemotherapy included anthracycline- and/or taxane-based regimens, and CDK4/6 inhibitors were not used. Thus, conventional endocrine therapy and chemotherapy may have been insufficient for the PR-zero group, particularly in premenopausal patients. Currently, the addition of CDK4/6 inhibitors, as outlined in the monarchE study30 is being considered for these patients. However, when focusing on 27 cases in the PR-zero group, only one-third (nine cases) met the criteria for high-risk cases in the monarchE study. Since this study was a retrospective analysis conducted prior to the approval of CDK4/6 inhibitors, further research, as well as the consideration of expanded indications, is necessary. Among postmenopausal patients, no significant differences existed in characteristics between the PR-low and PR-high groups treated with endocrine therapy alone. However, the prognosis was poorer in the PR-low group than in the PR-high group. Therefore, some postmenopausal patients with PR-low tumors may have required additional therapy beyond endocrine therapy alone. Multigene assays such as the Oncotype DX test, which evaluates the added benefit of chemotherapy, was not covered by insurance in Japan during the study observation period, and only a few patients could use the test. However, the Oncotype DX test is now covered by insurance in Japan, which makes it easier for us to evaluate the risk of distant metastasis and the benefits of chemotherapy. We believe the PR-low status in postmenopausal patients could be a good indicator of treatment decisions, whether they use the Oncotype DX test or not, to avoid under and over treatment.

Differences in prognosis owing to a lack of PR have been elucidated at the genetic level. PR directly affects the activity of ER target genes in ER- and PR-positive breast cancer cell models. In the absence of PR, ER associates with regulatory elements in the genome and promotes the transcription of several target genes that promote proliferation, including important cell cycle activators, such as cyclin D1 and MYC. Conversely, when PR is activated by progesterone, it can reprogram the ER to different genomic loci, leading to differentiation and cell cycle arrest, such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKN) 2 C and CDKN1B14,31. Thus, PR activation inhibits proliferation15. Consequently, it is now evident that PR is not only a gene target induced by ER but also an ER-related protein that regulates ER behavior.

PR is one of the target proteins induced by estrogen mediated by ER and is highly expressed when blood estrogen levels are high17,18. Consequently, in our study, the percentage of PR-high (> 10%) was significantly higher in the premenopausal group than in the postmenopausal group. Mohammed et al. suggested that low PR expression could be explained by copy number loss of the PGR gene, which encodes PR, and inferred that genomic variation in the PGR gene is a typical mechanism that reduces PR expression14. However, genomic variations in PGR cannot be explained by blood estrogen levels alone, and the mechanism by which resistance is acquired needs to be further elucidated.

Nonetheless, our study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study with a limited number of cases from a single institution. Second, ER-positive breast cancer is generally PR-positive breast cancer, and patients with PR-zero and PR-low levels in this study should be considered a small and special group biologically. The overall proportions of the PR-zero, PR-low, and PR-high groups were 9.8%, 8.2%, and 82.0%, respectively, indicating a significant bias that might affect the statistical analysis. The small number of patients in the PR-zero and PR-low groups limited our ability to draw definitive conclusions, particularly for premenopausal patients. Third, a quantitative evaluation of ER was not included in this study. Because tumors with low ER expression usually have low PR expression32, it is possible that the level of ER expression determines the level of PR expression and is also involved in sensitivity to endocrine therapy. To address this problem, we targeted patients with ER > 10%, who were certain to benefit from endocrine therapy. Fourth, as mentioned above, the prognosis of PR in this study may also differ from other studies due to other factors, such as ER and the histological grade. However, these confounders were not adjusted in this study. Fifth, Ki-67 was not measured in any of the cases and was, therefore, not examined, although it was found to be related in other studies. Finally, this study did not obtain sufficient data on the details of drug therapy, including the content and duration of endocrine therapy; therefore, we could not explore this aspect.

In conclusion, as in previous reports, our study showed that low PR expression was associated with poor prognosis. Our results also suggested that in ER-positive breast cancer (> 10%), where endocrine therapy is required, the cutoff value of PR is 1% in premenopausal patients and 10% in postmenopausal patients. Currently, PR expression is not considered for the treatment of hormone-positive breast cancer. However, in the future, classification based on the rate of PR expression and menopausal status could help refine drug selection, such as endocrine therapy alone or with the addition of chemotherapy or CDK4/6 inhibitors.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study included patients with primary breast cancer who were diagnosed with ER-positive (> 10%) HER2-negative invasive breast cancer based on needle biopsy or surgical specimens and who underwent curative resection at our hospital between January 2006 and December 2016. This study was approved by the Review Board and Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center Hospital, Tokyo, Japan (approval number 2017 − 278). The Review Board and Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center Hospital, Tokyo, Japan waived the need for informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the study. We ensured that participants had the opportunity to opt out by publishing information about the study on our hospital website. Patients who underwent radiofrequency ablation or had bilateral breast cancer, male breast cancer, or stage IV breast cancer were excluded. Additionally, patients who did not receive endocrine therapy were excluded to analyze the group of patients who were scheduled to receive endocrine therapy. Menopausal status was defined as premenopausal if the last menstrual period was within 1 year before the first visit and postmenopausal if the last menstrual period was more than 1 year before the first visit, based on the questionnaire completed by each patient at the first visit. The following clinicopathological information was collected from the medical records: age at initial diagnosis; factors related to stage determination pre- and post-surgery (tumor size, lymph node metastasis status, and number of metastatic lymph nodes); surgical procedure; factors related to treatment strategy (ER status, PR status, HER2 status, and histologic grade); information on neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC); and information on postoperative adjuvant therapy such as endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Additionally, information on recurrence and the site of recurrence was collected as outcomes. All patients underwent partial or total mastectomy, with or without immediate breast reconstruction. Patients with micrometastases were defined as those with positive axillary lymph node metastasis. Disease stages were classified according to the UICC for TMN classification, 8th edition (2017)33. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Evaluation and definition of ER, PR, and HER2

ER, PR, and HER2 expression were evaluated in invasive areas. Needle biopsy specimen results were prioritized in patients who received NAC, whereas surgical specimen results were prioritized in those who underwent surgery first. ER-positive was defined as a J-score of 3a or 3b or an Allred proportion score (PS) of ≥ 3, that is, a positive cell occupancy of ≥ 10%32. Similarly, PR expression evaluated by the J-score or Allred PS was defined as PR-high if the value was > 10%, PR-low if it was 1%–10%, and PR-zero if it was < 1%. Samples were categorized as HER2-negative if their score was 0 or 1 + by immunohistochemistry or 2 + by in situ hybridization.

Statistical analysis

The patients were divided into three groups (PR-high, PR-low, and PR-zero) according to the level of PR expression and compared by menopausal status in terms of clinicopathological factors and prognosis. The chi-squared test, the Fisher exact test, and Bonferroni correction were used to compare the groups with respect to clinicopathological factors. The Fine and Gray competing risk proportional hazards regression model was used to analyze the main event as AR or DR and competing events as death from other causes for prognosis. All recurrence events were defined as ipsilateral chest wall/skin recurrence after total mastectomy, ipsilateral breast recurrence after partial mastectomy, ipsilateral regional lymph node recurrence, or distant organ recurrence. DR events were defined as distant organ recurrence other than local or regional recurrence. Regional lymph nodes were defined as axillary, internal mammary, and supraclavicular lymph nodes on the same side. The HR was adjusted for age, TNM stage, surgical procedure, histologic grade, lymph node metastasis status, and radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and EZR version 1.68 (Jichi Medical University, Japan) was used for analysis.
