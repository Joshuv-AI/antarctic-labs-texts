Concomitant Treatment With Lumasiran and Nedosiran in a Child With Primary Hyperoxaluria Type 1
Authors: Eline Braekman, Ann Raes, Ruth De Bruyne, Patrick Verloo, Agnieszka Prytula
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

Primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) is caused by mutations in the alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase gene, leading to alternative conversion of glyoxylate into oxalate with subsequent oxalate overproduction and deposition in multiple organs.1

We report a case of a 9-year-old boy with infantile PH1. Despite supportive treatment, the patient developed symptomatic systemic oxalosis and became dialysis-dependent at the age of 22 months. Concomitant mitochondrial disorder was suspected based on the presence of neurodevelopmental delay, autism spectrum disorder, epilepsy, liver cirrhosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and elevated plasma lactate. Owing to these comorbidities, he was not eligible for a combined liver and kidney transplantation.

At the age of 6.5 years, oxalate-reducing therapy with lumasiran was initiated. The goal was to reduce plasma oxalate (POx) levels to facilitate an isolated kidney transplantation preceded by splenectomy. The optimal POx level to proceed with an isolated kidney transplantation is unknown; however, 80–90 μmol/l has been suggested in the literature.2 In our patient, however, POx variability remained high, and this target was not attained (Figure 1). Nedosiran was added after 2 years in a dose of 3.5 mg/kg, which resulted in a downward trend in POx (Figure 1). Meanwhile, because of the high therapy burden, dialysis frequency was reduced from 6 to 4 times a week, without a significant rise in POx level (Figure 1). The patient tolerated both therapies well and no adverse effects were established.

Lumasiran and nedosiran are small interfering RNAs, which inhibit the alternative conversion of glyoxylate into oxalate through enzyme inhibition based on RNA interference.1 Randomized controlled trials have shown a rapid and sustained reduction in POx.3, 4, 5

Both small interfering RNAs do not carry the risk of significant adverse effects.1 They are metabolized into shorter oligonucleotides by endonucleases and exonucleases, and dose adaptations for impaired liver or kidney function are not required.1 The potential for drug–drug interaction is low because small interfering RNAs have limited influence on cytochrome P450-mediated drug metabolism.1

This case illustrates that concomitant treatment with lumasiran and nedosiran could be a safe and effective option for patients with PH1 who respond insufficiently to lumasiran alone. Further research is ongoing to determine the long-term safety and efficacy of RNA interference–based therapies for PH1 and to evaluate if they may avoid liver (and even kidney) transplantation.1

More details about the case and innovative treatments for PH1, are presented in the Supplementary Text and References.
