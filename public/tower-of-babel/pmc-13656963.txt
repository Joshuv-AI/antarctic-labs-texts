Lipoprotein(a) and the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index in relation to coronary lesion burden in patients with acute coronary syndrome
Authors: Pinpeng Xie, Zhiyuan Wang, Chaosheng Huang, Wanting Sun, Weichun Zhu, Chungang Zhai, Lei Qiao, Wenqiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] reflects inherited atherothrombotic risk, whereas the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) integrates systemic inflammation, triglyceride-related lipid disturbance, and glucose-related metabolic stress. Their individual and joint association with angiographic coronary lesion burden in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) remain incompletely defined. We examined whether CTI complements Lp(a) in characterizing coronary lesion burden in ACS. This retrospective, single-center study included 2,836 consecutive patients with ACS who underwent coronary angiography. Coronary lesion burden was assessed using continuous Gensini score, a high Gensini score, and multivessel disease (MVD). Multivariable regression, restricted cubic spline analyses, CTI-stratified analyses, incremental receiver operating characteristic analyses, and internally validated machine-learning analyses with SHAP interpretation were performed. Higher Lp(a) and CTI level were both associated with greater coronary lesion burden. Compared with Lp(a) < 75 nmol/L, Lp(a) ≥175 nmol/L was associated with high Gensini score (OR, 1.51 [95% CI, 1.17–1.96]) and MVD (OR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.27–2.26]). Each 1-SD increase in CTI was associated with high Gensini score (OR, 1.47 [95% CI, 1.35–1.60]) and MVD (OR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.08–1.28]). Among inflammatory-lipid indices, CTI showed the most consistent associations and provided the largest numerical incremental discrimination beyond Lp(a). The association between very high Lp(a) and coronary lesion burden was more pronounced at higher CTI levels, particularly for MVD. Machine-learning analyses further supported the relevance of both CTI and Lp(a). In patients with ACS, higher Lp(a) and CTI levels were associated with greater angiographic coronary lesion burden. CTI may complement Lp(a) by capturing inflammatory-metabolic status, supporting their joint assessment for more refined characterization of lesion-burden risk in ACS.Supplementary key words: lipoprotein(a), C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index, acute coronary syndrome, coronary lesion burden, Gensini score, multivessel disease

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide (1). Among patients presenting with acute coronary syndromes (ACS), the extent of coronary involvement has direct implications for risk stratification, revascularization strategy, and prognosis (2). The Gensini score provides an angiographic estimate of the severity of coronary stenosis (3), whereas multivessel disease (MVD) reflects the anatomical spread of coronary atherosclerosis and is particularly relevant when considering complete revascularization in ACS (4). Beyond traditional angiographic assessment, cardiometabolic abnormalities have been linked to more extensive coronary involvement; for example, coronary computed tomography angiography data in patients with type 2 diabetes showed that abnormal low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were associated with greater plaque burden, more severe stenosis, and wider coronary involvement (5).

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is increasingly considered in residual cardiovascular risk assessment because its circulating concentration is largely genetically determined and is not substantially lowered by conventional lipid-lowering therapy (6, 7). Genetic and clinical evidence supports Lp(a) as a causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (6, 7). Its atherogenic effects are mediated through oxidized phospholipid carriage, endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and prothrombotic activity via structural homology to plasminogen, which impairs fibrinolysis (8). Elevated Lp(a) has also been associated with angiographic progression of CAD (9). A coronary computed tomography study further linked elevated Lp(a) to atherosclerotic plaque progression (10). However, whether Lp(a) is related to the angiographic burden of coronary disease in patients with ACS has been less well-characterized, as previous studies have focused mainly on long-term events or disease progression.

Inflammation and metabolic disturbance are central to plaque progression and instability in ACS (2). Several composite indices derived from routine laboratory tests have therefore been examined as practical markers of cardiovascular risk. The neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio has been associated with short-term prognosis in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (11). The monocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (MHR) has been linked to angiographic CAD severity and future cardiovascular events in ACS (12). The C-reactive protein (CRP)-to-HDL-C ratio (CHR) has also been associated with future cardiovascular risk in a large population-based cohort (13). More recently, the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) has emerged as a composite marker that integrates the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index—a validated surrogate for insulin resistance—with CRP, a marker of systemic inflammation (14). Unlike indices that reflect only inflammation (e.g., CHR) or lipid metabolism alone, CTI captures the synergistic interplay between metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation, both established drivers of atherosclerotic plaque progression and instability (15). The TyG index independently predicts cardiovascular events (16), while CRP reflects residual inflammatory risk even after lipid-lowering therapy (17). By combining these pathways, CTI may provide superior risk stratification in ACS patients. However, whether CTI modifies the atherosclerotic risk conferred by Lp(a)—a genetically determined, inflammation-sensitive lipoprotein—remains unexplored. Higher CTI levels have been associated with incident cardiovascular disease in China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)-based studies and with mortality among patients with coronary heart disease and type 2 diabetes in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) analyses (18, 19).

Lp(a) and CTI may represent distinct but interacting dimensions of coronary risk. Lp(a) reflects inherited atherothrombotic susceptibility, whereas CTI reflects the inflammatory-metabolic environment in which atherosclerotic plaques develop and destabilize. Prior evidence from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis suggests that systemic inflammation may amplify the cardiovascular risk associated with elevated Lp(a), as CRP modified the relationship between Lp(a) and cardiovascular events (20). A recent MESA and UK Biobank analysis further suggested that interleukin-6 may identify excess cardiovascular risk among individuals with elevated Lp(a) (21). A systematic review likewise highlighted the relevance of inflammatory status when evaluating Lp(a)-associated cardiovascular risk (22). Whether CTI similarly modifies the association between Lp(a) and angiographic lesion burden in ACS remains unknown. Because the relationships among Lp(a), inflammatory-metabolic indices, and coronary lesion burden may be non-linear, machine learning (ML) methods may help complement conventional regression by capturing complex predictor-outcome patterns in cardiovascular research (23). Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values can further be used to examine the relative contribution of individual predictors within ML models (24).

In this study, we investigated the associations of Lp(a) and inflammatory-lipid composite indices, particularly CTI, with coronary lesion burden in patients with ACS. Coronary lesion burden was assessed using the Gensini score and MVD. We hypothesized that: (1) elevated Lp(a) levels are independently associated with greater coronary lesion burden in ACS patients; (2) CTI, as a composite inflammatory-metabolic marker, provides incremental predictive value beyond Lp(a) alone; and (3) the adverse association between Lp(a) and coronary lesion severity is amplified in patients with higher CTI levels, reflecting synergistic biological interaction between inherited lipid risk and acquired metabolic-inflammatory stress.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective single-center study enrolled consecutive patients admitted to Qilu Hospital of Shandong University between January 2024 and February 2026 with ACS who underwent coronary angiography during the index hospitalization. ACS was diagnosed according to the 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines (25).

Patients were eligible if they had confirmed ACS and available demographic, clinical, laboratory, and angiographic data. Patients were excluded for active malignancy, systemic inflammatory diseases (including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, or other autoimmune conditions requiring immunosuppressive therapy), missing key study variables, severe renal dysfunction, or severe hepatic dysfunction (26). Severe renal dysfunction was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 or maintenance dialysis. Severe hepatic dysfunction was defined as known cirrhosis or chronic liver disease with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > 3 times the upper limit of normal. A complete-case approach was used for the primary analyses. After application of the eligibility criteria, 2,836 patients were included in the final analytic cohort. The selection process is shown in Supplemental Fig. S1.

Data collection and definitions

Demographic characteristics, cardiovascular history, ACS subtype at presentation, laboratory results, and angiographic findings were extracted from the electronic medical record system. Candidate predictors were prespecified according to clinical relevance and data availability before model development. These included Lp(a), conventional cardiovascular risk factors, and inflammatory-lipid composite indices derived from routine blood tests. Fasting venous blood samples were collected before percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Routine laboratory measurements used in this study were obtained from these preprocedural fasting samples. The CRP, triglyceride, and glucose values used to calculate CTI were measured from the same fasting laboratory assessment.

Lp(a) was measured as part of routine clinical care, and all values were reported in nmol/L, consistent with the 2024 focused update to the National Lipid Association scientific statement (27). Lp(a) was analyzed as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable using prespecified groups: normal (<75 nmol/L), intermediate (75 to <125 nmol/L), high (125 to <175 nmol/L), and very high (≥175 nmol/L). These thresholds were selected according to contemporary guidelines and consensus recommendations, with the elevated range further divided to identify patients with very high Lp(a) concentrations (27, 28).

The inflammatory-lipid composite indices included the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR), MHR, CHR, and CTI.

The Gensini score was calculated from diagnostic coronary angiography according to the standard method, which incorporates both the degree of luminal stenosis and the anatomical importance of the affected coronary segment (3). Gensini scores were independently calculated by two experienced interventional cardiologists who were blinded to clinical and laboratory data. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), yielding an ICC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.94), indicating excellent reliability. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Because the Gensini score was right-skewed and no universally accepted clinical cutoff is available, high Gensini score was defined as a value above the cohort median of 53.0. MVD was defined as at least 50% diameter stenosis in two or more major epicardial coronary arteries (29).

CTI was calculated as follows: CTI = 0.412 × ln [CRP, mg/L] + ln [triglycerides, mg/dl × glucose, mg/dl/2]. Triglyceride and glucose concentrations were converted to the required units when necessary. Other indices were calculated as follows: SIRI = neutrophil count × monocyte count/lymphocyte count; SII = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count; PLR = platelet count/lymphocyte count; MHR = monocyte count/HDL-C; NHR = neutrophil count/HDL-C; and CHR = CRP/HDL-C. CTI was evaluated as a continuous variable and, where appropriate, in prespecified data-driven strata for stratified, interaction, and dose-response analyses.

Covariates included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cerebrovascular disease, early family history of cardiovascular disease, and prior PCI. These variables were selected a priori based on established clinical relevance and prior literature. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were constructed to guide covariate selection (Supplemental Fig. S2).

Dyslipidemia was defined operationally as a documented pre-admission diagnosis of dyslipidemia or current lipid-lowering treatment (30). Early family history of cardiovascular disease was defined as coronary heart disease or stroke in a first-degree male relative before 55 years of age or a first-degree female relative before 65 years of age (31). Angiographic calcification was defined as radiopaque density within the coronary arterial wall at the lesion site visible before contrast injection (32). A long lesion was defined as a lesion length ≥ 20 mm, using the prespecified 20-mm angiographic threshold (33). Pre-PCI primary slow coronary flow was defined as delayed distal vessel opacification with TIMI flow grade 2 or a corrected TIMI frame count >27, in the absence of ≥50% fixed epicardial coronary stenosis and after exclusion of coronary spasm, dissection, embolism, or other secondary causes (34). In-stent restenosis was defined as >50% diameter stenosis within a previously implanted stent or within 5 mm of either stent edge (35).

Feature selection and model development

Feature selection and model development were conducted separately for high Gensini score and MVD. For each outcome, the cohort was randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio. All feature selection procedures were restricted to the training set to reduce information leakage.

Candidate variables first underwent univariable logistic regression screening in the training set. Variables associated with the outcome at P < 0.05 were then entered into least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression to reduce dimensionality and identify informative predictors (36). The penalty parameter was selected by 10-fold cross-validation using the minimum cross-validation error and the 1-standard-error rule, as shown in Supplemental Fig. S3.

Pairwise correlations among retained variables were assessed using correlation heatmaps. Highly correlated variables were reviewed for redundancy, and the final feature sets were determined by integrating univariable screening, LASSO selection, correlation assessment, and clinical interpretability.

For each outcome, nine supervised machine-learning algorithms were trained in the training set: logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), random forest, adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), CatBoost, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). The same predictor set was used across algorithms for each outcome to allow direct comparison. Hyperparameter tuning was performed within the training set, and the internal validation set was reserved exclusively for final model evaluation.

Model performance and interpretation

Model performance was evaluated in the training set and internal validation set. Discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score were calculated to compare classification performance across algorithms. Calibration plots were used to examine agreement between predicted and observed risk, and decision curve analysis was performed to assess clinical usefulness across a range of threshold probabilities. Internal validation-set performance is shown in Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table S4, and training-set results are provided in Supplemental Fig. S5.

For MVD, panels D–F show the corresponding analyses. For each outcome, the model with the highest internal validation-set AUC, together with acceptable calibration and clinical usefulness, was selected for interpretation. SHAP analysis was used to estimate the relative contribution and direction of effect of individual predictors in the selected model. SHAP beeswarm plots were generated to visualize predictor importance for high Gensini score and MVD.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.2. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables are presented as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Associations of Lp(a) and inflammatory-lipid composite indices with high Gensini score and MVD were examined using univariable and multivariable logistic regression models. Multivariable linear regression models were additionally used to examine the association between Lp(a) and continuous Gensini score using the same covariate adjustment strategy. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for logistic regression models and β coefficients with 95% CIs for linear regression models. Lp(a) was modeled as both a continuous and a categorical variable, and trend tests were performed across ordered categories. To assess the robustness of the linear-regression findings for the right-skewed Gensini score, two sensitivity analyses were performed using the same fully adjusted covariate set. First, the original Gensini score was analyzed with heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 standard errors. Second, the model was refitted using the log-transformed Gensini score.

Effect modification by CTI was assessed using stratified analyses and multiplicative interaction terms in multivariable logistic regression models. Incremental discrimination after adding CTI and other selected composite indices to Lp(a)-based models was evaluated using ROC analysis and AUC comparisons across nested models. Additional subgroup analyses were performed for continuous and categorical Lp(a). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2,836 patients with ACS were included in the final analytic cohort. According to Lp(a) concentration, 2,061 patients were classified as normal, 330 as intermediate, 170 as high, and 275 as very high (Table 1). Across increasing Lp(a) categories, patients had significantly higher total cholesterol (P < 0.001), LDL-C (P < 0.001), apolipoprotein B (P < 0.001), and lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 levels (P = 0.003), as assessed by the Kruskal–Wallis test (Table 1). Higher Lp(a) categories were also accompanied by higher Gensini scores and a greater number of angiographically diseased vessels. CTI and most inflammatory-lipid composite indices did not differ significantly across Lp(a) categories.

Patients with either angiographic outcome had a higher burden of adverse clinical and angiographic features, including more frequent myocardial infarction presentation, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, in-stent restenosis, calcification, and long lesions. They also had higher Lp(a), CRP, CTI, and most inflammatory composite indices, along with lower HDL-C and eGFR.

Associations of Lp(a) with coronary lesion burden

In multivariable analyses, higher Lp(a) levels were associated with greater coronary lesion burden (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model, each 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) was associated with a 0.38-point increase in Gensini score (β = 0.38, 95% CI 0.17–0.58; P < 0.001). The full model explained 2.7% of the variance in Gensini score (adjusted R2 = 0.027), with Lp(a) contributing an additional 0.4% beyond traditional risk factors (ΔR2 = 0.004, P < 0.001). Each 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) was also associated with higher odds of high Gensini score (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03; P < 0.001) and MVD (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04; P < 0.001). The association was also robust in sensitivity analyses. With HC3 robust standard errors, each 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) remained associated with a 0.375-point higher Gensini score (95% CI 0.164–0.586; P < 0.001). In the log-transformed model, the corresponding coefficient was 0.00608 (95% CI 0.00317–0.00899; P < 0.001), equivalent to an approximately 0.61% higher Gensini score per 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) (Supplemental Table S3).

Performance and interpretation of machine-learning models

Internal validation-set performance is shown in Figure 3 and Supplemental Table S4, and training-set results are provided in Supplemental Table S5. In the internal validation set, machine-learning models showed modest and broadly comparable discrimination across algorithms. For high Gensini score, AUC values ranged from 0.638 for GBM to 0.673 for CatBoost. For MVD, AUC values ranged from 0.641 for GBM to 0.673 for CatBoost. CatBoost achieved the highest AUC for both outcomes, with an accuracy of 0.619 for high Gensini score and 0.632 for MVD. Calibration plots showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed risk, and decision curve analysis suggested net benefit across selected threshold ranges.

CatBoost was therefore selected for SHAP-based interpretation. SHAP analyses showed that both Lp(a) and CTI contributed to model predictions for both outcomes (Fig. 4). CTI was the leading contributor to the high Gensini score model, and Lp(a) remained among the main predictors. For MVD, Lp(a), prior PCI, age, diabetes, sex, and CTI were among the leading contributors.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed for both continuous and categorical Lp(a) across prespecified clinical strata (Supplemental Figures S6–S9). For high Gensini score, the association per 10 nmol/L increase in Lp(a) was generally similar across most subgroups, with smoking status showing the only significant interaction signal in the continuous analysis (P for interaction = 0.037). For MVD, the continuous Lp(a) association was directionally consistent across most subgroups, with significant interaction signals for sex and ACS subtype. Categorical analyses showed broadly similar patterns, and subgroup findings were generally consistent with the main analyses.

Discussion

In this retrospective ACS cohort undergoing coronary angiography, higher Lp(a) levels were associated with greater angiographic coronary lesion burden. CTI showed the most consistent associations across the angiographic outcomes and provided the largest numerical improvement in discrimination beyond Lp(a), although the machine-learning models showed only modest overall discrimination. SHAP analysis identified both Lp(a) and CTI as important contributors to model predictions. The association between very high Lp(a) and MVD showed larger point estimates at higher CTI levels, although formal tests of multiplicative interaction were not statistically significant.

The present findings extend the relevance of Lp(a) from long-term cardiovascular risk to the anatomical expression of coronary disease in ACS. This interpretation is consistent with long-term cohort data suggesting that inflammation, cholesterol, and Lp(a) capture complementary dimensions of future cardiovascular risk (37), as well as participant-level evidence supporting the independent contributions of Lp(a)- and LDL-C-mediated cardiovascular risk (38). In our cohort, the point estimate for the association between very high Lp(a) and MVD (OR = 1.69) appeared numerically larger than that for high Gensini score (OR = 1.51), although these estimates are not directly comparable given the different outcome definitions and event rates. This pattern may suggest that Lp(a) is particularly relevant to the anatomical extent of coronary involvement, consistent with its proposed role in promoting diffuse endothelial dysfunction and widespread plaque formation through oxidized phospholipid carriage and arterial wall inflammation (10, 39, 40).

This relationship is clinically plausible in light of current Lp(a) literature. Recent reviews have emphasized the role of Lp(a) in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and the need to interpret Lp(a)-related risk beyond conventional LDL-C assessment (41). Tsimikas also highlighted the close link between Lp(a), oxidized phospholipid transport, vascular inflammation, and emerging Lp(a)-lowering strategies (42). In the present study, the strongest categorical association was observed among patients with Lp(a) ≥175 nmol/L. Current recommendations emphasize that Lp(a)-associated risk is continuous, but clinically interpretable categories remain useful for identifying patients with marked elevation (27). Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that lower Lp(a) levels are benign; rather, the very high Lp(a) category appeared to identify the subgroup in whom the angiographic signal was most apparent.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous angiographic and coronary imaging studies. In the LipidCardio study, elevated Lp(a) was associated with greater angiographic disease severity, including higher SYNTAX-I and Gensini scores (43). The RELACS study similarly reported that higher Lp(a) levels were associated with greater coronary complexity in patients with ACS (44). A cross-sectional study in chronic CAD patients further reported that Lp(a) ≥30 mg/dl was strongly associated with severe coronary stenosis defined by Gensini score, with an AUC of 0.869 for predicting high angiographic burden (45). Another multicenter study reported that the association between Lp(a) and myocardial infarction was stronger in patients with low-attenuation plaque (46). Although these studies differed from ours in population, imaging modality, and outcome definition, they support the possibility that Lp(a)-related risk may be expressed through more extensive or more vulnerable coronary atherosclerosis. Collectively, these studies support the present finding that Lp(a) elevation is associated with greater coronary lesion burden, and extend this observation to an ACS population with simultaneous assessment of an integrated inflammatory-metabolic index. Notably, our study suggests that the metabolic-inflammatory context—as captured by CTI—modifies the magnitude of Lp(a)-associated coronary risk, a relationship not systematically evaluated in prior studies.

A second major finding was the independent association of CTI with angiographic coronary lesion burden. CTI was associated with both a high Gensini score and MVD, and provided the largest numerical AUC gain beyond Lp(a) among the inflammatory-lipid composite indices. This incremental value should, however, be interpreted according to outcome: the gain was clearer for high Gensini score, whereas the MVD AUC increased only modestly from 0.541 to 0.561. Thus, the MVD finding supports limited incremental information rather than clinically sufficient stand-alone discrimination. CTI combines CRP, triglyceride-related lipid disturbance, and glucose-related metabolic status, thereby capturing a broader current inflammatory-metabolic state rather than a single abnormal pathway. Prior CHARLS-based work linked CTI to future cardiovascular disease risk (19). Another cohort study using CHARLS data also reported an association between CTI and cardiovascular disease (14). A recent study in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome further supported the association between CTI and future cardiovascular risk (47).

The relative performance of CTI may reflect the fact that it captures a broader inflammatory-metabolic state than cell count-derived indices alone. The neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio has been associated with short-term prognosis after STEMI treated with primary PCI (11). The MHR has been linked to coronary disease severity and future cardiovascular events in ACS (12). The CHR has also been associated with future cardiovascular risk in population-based research (13). In our study, several inflammatory indices were associated with a high Gensini score, whereas CTI showed more consistent associations across both a high Gensini score and MVD. This may reflect its ability to simultaneously integrate inflammatory, lipid, and glucose-related metabolic information.

The joint assessment of Lp(a) and CTI may provide additional biological context. Lp(a) is relatively stable and largely inherited, whereas CTI is more dynamic and reflects current inflammatory-metabolic status. Prior work from MESA showed that hsCRP modified the association between Lp(a) and cardiovascular events (20). Our stratified analyses were directionally compatible with these studies, particularly for MVD, but the formal interaction tests were not statistically significant. Several biological mechanisms may explain why an adverse inflammatory-metabolic environment amplifies Lp(a)-associated risk. Insulin resistance, as reflected by the TyG component of CTI, increases endothelial permeability and promotes retention of apoB-containing lipoproteins—including Lp(a)—within the arterial intima (15). Hyperglycemia (48) and hypertriglyceridemia (49) further promote oxidative modification of lipoproteins, rendering Lp(a) particles more susceptible to macrophage uptake via scavenger receptors and accelerating foam cell formation. Concurrently, elevated CRP reflects systemic inflammation that upregulates oxidative stress pathways and increases the generation of oxidized phospholipids (OxPL). Because Lp(a) is a preferential carrier of OxPL (40), inflammatory states may enhance the pro-atherogenic signaling of Lp(a)-bound OxPL through activation of endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and monocytes (39). Additionally, Lp(a)'s structural homology to plasminogen competitively inhibits fibrinolysis, and insulin resistance further impairs fibrinolytic capacity through increased plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) expression (50), creating a compounded pro-thrombotic milieu. Taken together, CTI may capture a biological context in which the genetically determined atherogenic potential of Lp(a) is most fully expressed. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory rather than definitive evidence of effect modification.

Several biological mechanisms may link Lp(a), inflammatory-metabolic disturbance, and angiographic lesion burden. Large-scale genetic studies have identified LPA variants associated with both Lp(a) concentrations and coronary disease (51). Genetically elevated Lp(a) has also been linked to increased myocardial infarction risk (52). Lp(a) carries oxidized phospholipids, which may promote endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and thrombogenicity (40). Experimental human data showed that oxidized phospholipids on Lp(a) can induce arterial wall inflammation and monocyte activation (39). In patients with elevated Lp(a), marked Lp(a) lowering has been associated with reduced pro-inflammatory activation of circulating monocytes (53). These mechanisms are relevant in ACS, where plaque instability, inflammation, thrombosis, and established atherosclerotic burden often coexist. The co-existence of elevated CTI—reflecting heightened insulin resistance and systemic inflammation—may therefore create a permissive environment in which these Lp(a)-mediated pathways are amplified, potentially explaining the larger point estimates observed in the high CTI stratum.

The CTI findings also fit the broader role of inflammation and metabolic disturbance in atherosclerosis. In CANTOS, inhibition of interleukin-1β reduced recurrent cardiovascular events independent of lipid lowering (17). In contrast, low-dose methotrexate did not reduce cardiovascular events in CIRT, highlighting that cardiovascular benefit depends on the inflammatory pathway targeted (54). CTI should therefore not be viewed as a generic inflammatory marker. CTI reflects the convergence of two distinct but interacting pathophysiological axes: the TyG component captures insulin resistance and its downstream effects on lipoprotein metabolism and endothelial function, while the CRP component captures systemic inflammatory activation that destabilizes plaques and promotes thrombosis (16, 17). The combination of these two axes within a single index may help explain why CTI outperformed individual cell count–derived inflammatory markers, which capture only one dimension of this biological context. A nationwide prospective study showed that CTI was associated with cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality across stages 0–3 of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (55). These findings support the relevance of CTI as an integrated inflammatory-metabolic marker, rather than a marker of inflammation alone.

The machine-learning analysis should be viewed as an interpretive complement to conventional regression, not as a replacement for coronary angiography. Machine-learning methods can capture complex predictor-outcome patterns in cardiovascular research, but their performance depends on the available predictors and validation strategy. In this study, the models were evaluated using a single internal validation split and showed only modest discrimination; therefore, they should not be considered externally validated clinical prediction tools. LASSO was used to reduce dimensionality before model development, consistent with its established role in regularized regression. SHAP analysis was used to examine the relative contribution of individual predictors within the selected models. The resulting importance patterns were broadly consistent with the regression analyses: CTI ranked as the leading contributor for high Gensini score, and Lp(a) remained among the main contributors for both angiographic outcomes. However, because SHAP values reflect predictor contributions within the fitted models, they should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical utility or causal importance.

These findings have potential clinical relevance for risk characterization in ACS, but they should not be interpreted as supporting biomarker-based replacement of coronary angiography or as a stand-alone guide for revascularization decisions. Lp(a) and CTI may instead help refine biological risk characterization before or alongside anatomical assessment. Very high Lp(a) may identify patients with greater inherited lipid-related susceptibility, whereas higher CTI may indicate an adverse inflammatory-metabolic state. Because CTI is calculated from routinely available measurements, including CRP, triglycerides, and glucose, it may be a practical marker for recognizing patients in whom elevated Lp(a) occurs together with metabolic and inflammatory activation. In our stratified analyses, patients with both higher CTI and Lp(a) ≥175 nmol/L had the highest observed burden of angiographic disease, particularly for MVD; however, this pattern should be considered exploratory because the multiplicative interaction tests were not statistically significant.

From a clinical perspective, the combined assessment of Lp(a) and CTI may be most useful for refining secondary prevention rather than determining revascularization strategy. Patients with very high Lp(a) and an adverse CTI profile may warrant closer attention to established modifiable risk factors, including LDL-C lowering, glycemic control, triglyceride-rich dyslipidemia, and residual inflammatory risk, according to current clinical standards. Contemporary statements increasingly support Lp(a) testing and risk-based interpretation in clinical practice (27). The emergence of potent Lp(a)-lowering therapies has also increased interest in identifying patients with clinically meaningful Lp(a)-related risk phenotypes, although outcome benefits remain to be established (56). More broadly, the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic framework emphasizes the overlap between metabolic dysfunction and cardiovascular risk, supporting the rationale for evaluating CTI as part of cardiometabolic risk characterization in ACS (57). Whether the combined assessment of Lp(a) and CTI improves treatment selection or long-term outcomes requires external validation and prospective follow-up.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and selection bias cannot be excluded. Second, the analysis was based on angiographic findings during the index hospitalization; therefore, causality cannot be inferred, and long-term clinical outcomes were not assessed. Third, Lp(a) and CTI were measured at baseline only. Lp(a) is generally stable over time, whereas CTI may fluctuate with acute inflammation, glycemic status, and treatment during ACS. Fourth, residual confounding remains possible despite multivariable adjustment. Fifth, the CTI-stratified findings should be interpreted cautiously because multiplicative interaction tests were not statistically significant. Finally, data on apo(a) isoform size, LPA genotype, intravascular imaging, plaque composition, and longitudinal plaque progression were not available. Future studies incorporating genetic information, intravascular imaging, and follow-up outcomes may clarify how Lp(a) and inflammatory-metabolic status jointly relate to coronary lesion burden.

Conclusion

In patients with acute coronary syndrome, higher Lp(a) levels are associated with greater coronary lesion burden, while CTI showed the most informative inflammatory-metabolic composite marker across both high Gensini score and multivessel disease. CTI also appears to identify a clinical context in which the adverse association of elevated Lp(a) becomes more pronounced, particularly for multivessel coronary involvement. These findings suggest that inherited lipid-related risk and inflammatory-metabolic status provide complementary information on coronary disease burden in ACS and may help refine risk stratification in patients with more extensive coronary involvement. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of the retrospective design and the use of a single-center Chinese cohort. Future prospective studies in diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these results and to determine whether serial CTI assessment, metabolic-inflammatory interventions, or integration of CTI and Lp(a) into established ACS risk scores can improve clinical decision-making.
