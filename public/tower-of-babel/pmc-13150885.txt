Effectiveness of interventions involving parents on children’s eating behaviours: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Khorramrouz, Sarah Rae, Michaela Kucab, Elizabeth M Uleryk, Jonathon L Maguire, Petros Pechlivanoglou, Kevin E Thorpe, Elena M Comelli, Catherine S Birken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParents play a pivotal role in shaping their children’s food environment and eating behaviours. Involving parents in interventions designed to promote nutritional outcomes such as dietary intake in children has been shown to improve parental feeding practices. However, it remains unclear how such interventions influence children’s eating behaviour outcomes. This protocol describes the methods of a systematic review evaluating the effectiveness of interventions involving parents in improving the eating behaviours of healthy children aged 0–12 years.Methods and analysisElectronic databases including MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science will be searched from inception to September 2025. A search strategy is developed to identify randomised controlled trials directly involving parents and reporting eating behaviours in children as either primary or secondary outcomes. Two independent reviewers will screen identified records and extract data on study, participant and intervention characteristics. Study results relevant to our primary and secondary outcomes will also be extracted using a prepiloted standardised data extraction form. We will use the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2) and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach to assess risk of bias and certainty of evidence, respectively. Where possible, meta‐analysis using random‐effects models will be performed; otherwise a qualitative summary will be provided.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this study as no primary data will be collected. The findings will provide valuable insights for stakeholders to inform and optimise public health policies and practices aimed at empowering families to promote healthy eating behaviours early in childhood. The results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251076540.

Introduction

Childhood is an important period for the development of eating behaviours.1 Eating behaviours refer to children’s appetitive traits and behavioural responses to food—focusing on how children eat rather than what they eat—and include patterns such as eating pace, responsiveness to food cues and emotional eating.2 These behaviours can be broadly categorised into food-approach behaviours—such as food responsiveness and emotional overeating—which are linked to higher energy intake and risk of obesity, and food-avoidant behaviours—such as satiety responsiveness and food fussiness—which are associated with lower energy intake and risk of underweight and nutritional deficiencies.35 Poor eating behaviours formed during childhood may impair children’s sensitivity to internal signals of satiety and hunger,6 which can negatively impact dietary intake,7 cardiometabolic health8 and overall growth and development.9 Evidence also suggests that eating behaviours established early in life often persist into adulthood, increasing the risk of chronic diseases such as obesity.5

As primary caregivers, parents play a pivotal role in shaping their children’s eating experiences. Throughout childhood, parents are the primary food providers and gatekeepers, regulating what, when and how much their children eat.10 Beyond food provision, parents shape the contexts in which eating occurs—establishing mealtime routines, modelling eating behaviours, determining food availability and accessibility in the home, and creating the emotional atmosphere during meals—factors that collectively create the feeding environment.11 Given this influence, parenting skill development and feeding practices have become central components of interventions aimed at fostering healthy behaviours in children.12 Parent-involved interventions are structured programmes that actively engage parents to improve child health and development by equipping them with skills and strategies to create a supportive environment that promotes children’s behaviour and overall well-being.13 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 intervention programmes with preschool-aged children found that parent-involved interventions can effectively reduce maladaptive feeding practices in the short term, showing significant improvements in reducing pressure to eat, use of food as reward and emotional feeding compared with control groups.14 These findings suggest that interventions can successfully modify parental feeding practices, though questions remain about their long-term effectiveness and broader impact on children’s eating behaviours.

With regard to child-related outcomes, previous reviews have primarily examined the impact of parent-based interventions on children’s dietary intake and weight-related outcomes rather than eating behaviours.12 15 16 A 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis of eight randomised clinical trials (RCTs) involving 1487 parent-child dyads evaluated parent-based interventions targeting obesity prevention and treatment among children aged 5–15 years.17 Although no significant effects on children’s body mass index (BMI) or BMI z-score were observed, some improvements were found in dietary outcomes, such as reduced intake of energy-dense foods. More recently, a 2024 systematic review of 12 studies focused on parenting interventions delivered electronically (eHealth) for children aged 3–6 years.15 The interventions showed significant improvements in dietary behaviours, including increased fruit and vegetable consumption and reduced intake of sugar-sweetened beverages. However, these reviews did not examine eating behaviour outcomes. This remains an important knowledge gap, as early childhood is a sensitive developmental period when parents play a central role in shaping children’s eating behaviours and food environments.

This systematic review will focus on children aged 0–12 years, spanning infancy through middle childhood. Early childhood, including infancy and toddlerhood, is when children develop their first relationships with food and parents are typically the primary recipients of interventions during this period. As children grow older and gain more independence, the parent–child feeding relationship becomes increasingly bidirectional. However, parents and the home environment remain primary influences on children’s eating behaviours throughout middle childhood, before the transition to adolescence when peer and external influences become more prominent. Understanding the effectiveness of interventions involving parents in shaping children’s eating behaviours across this developmental span is essential for designing evidence-based strategies that can be implemented in diverse settings. This review will address a critical gap in the literature and provide actionable evidence for policymakers, health practitioners and researchers working to promote healthy eating behaviours early in life through active parental involvement.

Objectives

The primary objective of this systematic review will be to evaluate the effects of parent-involved interventions on eating behaviours of healthy children aged 0–12 years. Secondary objectives are to assess the effects of these interventions on children’s dietary intake, anthropometric outcomes, quality of life and parental feeding practices. The characteristics and specific features associated with the effectiveness of various intervention programmes will also be examined.

Methods

Design

This systematic review protocol is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols 2015 (PRISMA-P) statement.18 A completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided in online supplemental data 1. If any changes to the protocol are required, we will document the date of the change, details of the amendment and rationale within PROSPERO. We will also mention the amendment(s) within the final manuscript of results.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion of studies for this review will be based on the following eligibility criteria:

Study designs

Only randomised controlled trials will be included. Non-randomised clinical trials, observational studies (cohort, case–control and cross-sectional studies), case series and case reports will be excluded.

Participants

Parents or guardians of healthy children aged 0–12 years who are the direct recipients of the interventions aimed at improving children’s ‘eating behaviours’ will be included. Studies targeting children with overweight or obesity will be eligible; however, studies focusing exclusively on children with medical conditions, such as diabetes and neurodevelopmental disorders, will be excluded.

Interventions

Any intervention that directly targets children’s eating behaviours and actively involves parents or caregivers will be eligible. Interventions may target parents alone or involve both parents and children, provided parents are active participants. Parent involvement is defined as: (1) parents/caregivers are the direct recipients of the intervention content; (2) parents actively engage in the intervention (eg, attend workshops, receive coaching, access online modules, complete structured programmes or receive tailored feedback); or (3) the intervention specifically aims to modify parental practices, attitudes or behaviours that influence children’s eating behaviours. Passive receipt of information (eg, newsletters or pamphlets) will not be considered eligible unless this is part of a structured intervention.

Interventions can be single-component (targeting eating behaviours only) or multicomponent (addressing eating behaviours in combination with other health behaviours such as physical activity, sleep or screen time), provided that eating behaviours are measured as a primary or secondary outcome. Interventions that modify children’s eating behaviours primarily through direct provision of food or nutritional supplementation (eg, school meal programmes, micronutrient supplements) will be excluded, as this review focuses on how parental involvement influences eating behaviours rather than dietary modification through food provision.

Comparators

Eligible comparators include no intervention, delayed intervention (wait-list control), usual care (as defined by the study team, which may include routine health visits or standard educational materials) or an alternative intervention that does not influence eating behaviours as the control (eg, physical activity-only programmes).

Outcomes

The primary outcome will be changes in children’s eating behaviours, defined as appetitive traits and behavioural responses to food. This includes food-approach behaviours (eg, food responsiveness, eating in the absence of hunger, enjoyment of food) and food-avoidant behaviours (eg, satiety responsiveness, slowness in eating, food neophobia), assessed through validated psychometric questionnaires (eg, Child Eating Behaviour Questionnaire) or direct observation in laboratory or naturalistic settings. Studies must report at least one measure of child eating behaviours as a primary or secondary outcome to be eligible for inclusion. Secondary outcomes will include dietary intake, anthropometric measures, quality of life and parental feeding practices, assessed using validated tools and standard protocols. Studies focusing solely on secondary outcomes (eg, dietary intake, nutrient consumption, weight-related outcomes) without assessing eating behaviours will be excluded.

Timing

There will be no restrictions on the duration of the intervention or length of follow-up.

Setting

Interventions will be included regardless of delivery setting, provided that parents or caregivers were the primary recipient of the intervention or were actively engaged in its delivery. Delivery settings may include healthcare environments (such as paediatric or family medicine practices), community settings (including parent support groups or community centres), educational institutions (childcare, preschools or schools), research facilities or home settings. Interventions may be delivered in-person or through digital technologies (including mobile applications, web-based platforms, telecommunication services or text-based messaging). Intervention facilitators may include healthcare practitioners (such as registered dietitians, clinical psychologists or nursing staff), teaching professionals, research personnel or automated digital systems.

Language

We will include articles reported in English, along with non-English studies that can be adequately translated using Google Translate.

Information sources

We will search electronic databases from inception to September 2025: MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Ovid), APA PsycINFO (Ovid), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (EBSCO), Scopus and Web of Science. No date or language restrictions will be applied. The search will be updated at the end of the review to identify newly published or in-press studies prior to the final analysis. To complement our systematic literature search, we will search grey literature sources including clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) to identify registered but unpublished or ongoing trials, and OpenAire to identify open access and institutional repository publications. Searches will be conducted using key terms related to eating behaviours, parent involvement and child age groups. We will also scan the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews identified through the search process. Where applicable, we will search the articles’ supplementary materials to make sure that all relevant data are captured.

Search strategy

In collaboration with a health information specialist (EMU), a comprehensive MEDLINE search strategy was developed using a combination of subject headings and text words related to parent AND children AND eating behaviours, and it then was adapted to other databases. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in online supplemental data 2.

Study records

Selection process

Two authors will independently screen titles and abstracts of all identified records using the Covidence software.19 Prior to the formal screening process, a pilot exercise will be conducted to ensure the consistency and precision of the screening questions. Subsequently, the full-text articles of the selected records will undergo review by the same authors to determine their eligibility for inclusion. If there are any discrepancies between reviewers at any stage of the screening process that cannot be resolved through consensus, a third reviewer will be consulted to facilitate decision making. Cohen’s kappa will be calculated to assess inter-rater agreement. We will contact authors if study information required for inclusion determination is unavailable or ambiguous. Reasons for excluding any full-text manuscripts will be documented at this stage, and we will record the selection process in adequate detail to complete a PRISMA flow diagram.20

Data collection process

Pairs of independent reviewers will extract data using a piloted, standardised data extraction form developed by the review team. For included studies, the following will be extracted: (1) Study characteristics—first author, publication year, country (with World Bank income classification), study design, setting, sample size, funding sources, affiliations of lead and corresponding authors, and declarations of conflicts of interest; (2) Participant characteristics (parent and child)—age, sex, weight status; (3) Intervention characteristics—name of the programme, intervention description, duration (in weeks) and intensity (number of hours or sessions); (4) comparator characteristics—setting, timing, duration and intensity; and (5) Outcomes—study results relevant to our primary outcomes (eating behaviour domains), the tools used to measure them and secondary outcomes.

Any discrepancies between reviewers will be mediated by a third reviewer. If critical information needed for data extraction is unavailable, we will reach out to the corresponding authors a maximum of two times (ie, initial and follow-up) to obtain additional details.

Outcomes and prioritisation

The primary outcome will include various aspects of eating behaviours, whether measured by psychometric questionnaires or direct observations in laboratory settings. Measures of child eating behaviour may include but are not limited to: (1) food approach behaviours (eg, eating in the absence of hunger, enjoyment of food and desire to drink); and (2) food avoidance behaviours (eg, satiety responsiveness, slowness in eating and food neophobia). Definitions for the eating behaviour and its domains are provided in online supplemental data 3.

The secondary outcomes were selected based on current literature identifying factors that influence or are influenced by children’s eating behaviours.1 21 These outcomes include:

Dietary intake measured using validated methods (eg, 24-hour recalls, food frequency questionnaires), including macronutrient and micronutrient intake, food group consumption and specific dietary components (eg, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra processed foods).

Dietary intake measured using validated methods (eg, 24-hour recalls, food frequency questionnaires), including macronutrient and micronutrient intake, food group consumption and specific dietary components (eg, sugar-sweetened beverages, ultra processed foods).

Anthropometric measures (parent-reported or measured by trained staff), including absolute body weight, BMI (zBMI score), waist circumference and body composition measures such as per cent body fat (via skinfold thickness, bioelectrical impedance analysis or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry).

Secondary outcomes will only be extracted when at least one eligible primary outcome is reported, to ensure the review remains focused on eating behaviours as the primary endpoint.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two reviewers will independently assess the risk of bias at the outcome level for each included study, using the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB2).24 The RoB2 for individually randomised trials consists of five domains focusing on randomisation, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. The questions included in each domain will be answered according to five response options (‘yes’, ‘probably yes’, ‘probably no’, ‘no’ and ‘no information’). Each domain is judged as either ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’ based on the answers to the questions, which further results in a proposed overall risk judgement for the specific study being examined. We will use RoB2 tools specific for cluster-randomised trials and crossover trials, which include additional domains relevant to each design. Where required, a third review author will adjudicate discrepancies regarding RoB2 that could not be resolved via consensus. Where meta-analysis is feasible, we will conduct sensitivity analyses by excluding studies with high risk of bias to assess whether this affects the observed effect estimates. Where meta-analysis is not possible, we will incorporate risk of bias considerations into our narrative synthesis.

Data synthesis and analysis

Where studies are sufficiently homogeneous in terms of participants, interventions, comparisons and outcomes, we will conduct meta-analysis. Pooled estimates of effect sizes will be presented as risk ratios with 95% CIs for dichotomous variables and mean difference (MD) with 95% CIs for continuous variables. If different measurement scales are used between studies, a standardised MD with 95% CIs will be presented. Provided there are at least two comparable studies, we plan to pool measures of the same quantitative outcomes using the appropriate random effect model. Forest plots will be used to display results. For studies reporting eating behaviour outcomes at multiple time points (eg, 6-month and 12-month follow-up), we will group outcomes into predefined follow-up periods (eg, short-term: ≤6 months; long-term: >6 months) to ensure comparability across studies. If studies report outcomes at unequal intervals, we will assign the closest time point to the predefined categories. For studies with multiple time points, the primary analysis will focus on the time point closest to the predefined primary follow-up period (eg, 6 months) to maintain consistency, with secondary analyses exploring other time points. Unit of analysis issues will be addressed according to the guidance outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.25 For cluster RCTs, we will adjust for clustering using robust SEs. For cross-over trials, only data from the first phase will be included. For multiarm trials, we will combine or appropriately split arms in line with Cochrane Handbook guidance to avoid double-counting and arms irrelevant to the review question will be dropped. Where necessary, SD will be imputed from SEs, CIs or p values. Statistical analysis will be performed using R software V.4.4.0 or greater.26 Comparisons will be two-tailed using a threshold of p<0.05 for significance.

Where meta-analysis is not feasible due to heterogeneity in interventions, outcomes or insufficient data, we will conduct a structured narrative synthesis following guidance from the Cochrane Handbook. This will involve systematic tabulation of study characteristics, intervention components and outcomes to identify patterns across studies. We will employ appropriate synthesis methods based on the available data and narratively explore intervention characteristics that may be associated with observed effects.

Missing data

If there is important data missing that is required for analysis, we will attempt to contact the corresponding authors of the studies. If we are unable to resolve the issue, we will proceed with an analysis based on the available data and discuss the potential impact of the missing information. Evidence of potential reporting bias will be documented in the ‘Risk of Bias’ table.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be evaluated using forest plots and visual assessment of funnel plots for asymmetry. In addition, we will quantify statistical heterogeneity by conducting an χ² test and calculating the I2 statistic (0–40%: might not be important; 30–60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity; 75–100%: considerable heterogeneity).27 Study heterogeneity will be informed by a narrative description of study characteristics and causes for study heterogeneity will be explored by subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Subgroup analysis

Where sufficient data are available, we will conduct subgroup analyses to explore whether intervention effects differ by: age group (infants and toddlers (0–2 years), preschool children (3–5 years), school-age children (6–12 years)), intervention recipients (parents only vs parent-child dyads), delivery mode (in-person vs remote/digital delivery), study setting income level (low- and middle-income countries vs high-income countries), follow-up duration (short-term (≤6 months) vs long-term (>6 months)) and presence of notable conflicts of interest (trials with notable concern vs no notable concern).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis will be performed to explore the source of heterogeneity by excluding data from studies classified as a high risk of bias.

Meta-biases

Information from trial registers and protocols will be compared with published reports to determine the extent of reporting bias. We will also evaluate publication bias through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression asymmetry test to assess small study effects, provided there are at least 10 studies included.28

Confidence in cumulative evidence

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) will be used to assess the overall certainty of the available evidence for outcome as recommended by the Cochrane handbook.29 Based on our GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence will be assessed across the domains of risk of bias, consistency, directness, precision and publication bias. Our level of certainty will be presented as either high (further research is very unlikely to change our confidence in the estimate of effect), moderate (further research is likely to have an important impact on our confidence in the estimate of effect and may change the estimate), low (further research is very likely to have an important impact on our confidence in the estimate of effect and is likely to change the estimate) or very low (very uncertain about the estimate of effect).

Ethics and dissemination

This is a protocol for a systematic review and does not involve primary data collection; therefore, ethics approval was not required. The results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Findings will also be shared with researchers, clinicians and public health stakeholders through academic conferences and relevant knowledge translation activities.

Patient and public involvement

The research topic for this systematic review was identified as a priority by the Parent and Clinician Advisory Team (PACT), a patient partner group consisting of parents whose children participate in the TARGet Kids! Primary Care Research Network in Ontario, Canada.30 PACT members were not involved in protocol development but will be engaged in disseminating the review findings to relevant audiences, including reviewing plain language summaries and advising on knowledge translation strategies.

Discussion

Poor eating behaviours developed in early childhood can lead to both immediate and long-term health consequences. Parents play a crucial role in shaping children’s eating behaviours by promoting practices and strategies that reinforce the behaviours they consider appropriate for their children. It is essential to identify practical guidance and strategies that help provide parents with the skills needed to establish healthy eating behaviours in their children during these crucial years and promote continued positive eating behaviours into adulthood. This systematic review aims to provide an up-to-date synthesis of interventions that actively involve parents and to assess their impact on children’s eating behaviours, anthropometric measures, dietary intake as well as other related health outcomes, including quality of life and parental feeding practices.

This review has several methodological strengths. By including randomised controlled trials, we will synthesise the highest quality evidence available on intervention effectiveness. The involvement of patient partners throughout the review process ensures the findings will address questions meaningful to families and caregivers. Our comprehensive subgroup analyses will allow us to examine whether intervention effects vary by child age, delivery format and implementation context, providing actionable insights for intervention design and implementation. However, our strict eligibility criteria may exclude informative evidence from pragmatic or community-based studies using non-randomised designs, potentially limiting our understanding of how these interventions perform in real-world settings. Additionally, by focusing specifically on parental involvement, we will not examine system-level or policy interventions that could complement family-based approaches.

Given the growing interest in implementing interventions targeted at parents to improve children’s health behaviours in early childhood, the findings of this study will provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of these approaches. By identifying the key characteristics of effective interventions, this research will be highly relevant to researchers and community health providers who design and deliver behaviour change programmes aimed at families and fostering healthier eating behaviours in children.
