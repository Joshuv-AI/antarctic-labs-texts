Sex hormone-binding globulin and coronary heart disease: a narrative review of associations and biomarker potential
Authors: Xu Liu, Meng Chen, Wen Dai, Haizhu Gao, Xueying Chen, Ronghua Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveCoronary heart disease (CHD) remains the leading cause of global mortality, and traditional cardiovascular risk scores fail to accurately identify high-risk individuals in certain populations. Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) has emerged as a multifunctional metabolic biomarker linking liver function to systemic vascular health. This narrative review aims to synthesize current epidemiological evidence on the association between SHBG and CHD risk, clarify the underlying biological mechanisms, and evaluate its potential clinical utility.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed and Web of Science databases for studies published between January 1981 and August 2024. We included peer-reviewed original research (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional studies), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines investigating the SHBG-CHD relationship in human populations. Evidence synthesis was conducted using the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) framework, with conflicting results critically evaluated based on study design, population characteristics, SHBG measurement methods, and confounding adjustment strategies.Key Content and FindingsA total of 46 relevant studies were included, comprising 32 prospective cohort studies, 8 case-control studies, and 6 meta-analyses. Current evidence consistently shows that lower circulating SHBG levels are independently associated with an increased risk of CHD in both men and women, with this association partially mediated by insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome (MetS), and dyslipidemia. However, significant heterogeneity exists across studies, particularly regarding the strength of association in postmenopausal women and the impact of statin therapy on SHBG metabolism.ConclusionsSHBG holds promise as a complementary biomarker for CHD risk assessment. Lower circulating SHBG levels are consistently associated with elevated CHD risk in both men and women, with this association partially mediated by insulin resistance and MetS. However, significant study heterogeneity (particularly in postmenopausal populations), non-standardized measurement methods, the absence of sex- and ethnicity-specific reference ranges, and unresolved causal relationships currently limit its clinical utility. Future large-scale, multicenter studies with harmonized testing protocols are needed to establish clinical reference thresholds and validate the incremental value of SHBG beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) is primarily caused by coronary atherosclerosis, which leads to coronary artery stenosis or occlusion and further results in myocardial ischemia and hypoxia. Although the incidences of CHD and other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have decreased in several regions of developed Western countries, these diseases have remained among the leading causes of death globally over the past decades. Furthermore, mortality from CVD is on the rise in many developing countries (1). This underscores the critical importance of early screening and intervention for CHD. Traditional cardiovascular risk scores, including the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) and Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE), have been the cornerstone of CHD risk assessment for decades. However, these scores exhibit well-documented limitations in specific populations: they underestimate risk in young adults, women, and individuals with metabolic abnormalities such as insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome (MetS), who represent a growing proportion of CHD cases worldwide. Additionally, traditional scores rely primarily on late-stage metabolic markers, failing to capture early pathophysiological changes that precede overt CVD. This gap underscores the urgent need to identify novel, early biomarkers that can complement existing risk assessment tools and improve the accuracy of CHD risk prediction. It is well known that dyslipidemia, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes are major risk factors for CHD, and obesity and MetS further elevate its risk (2). However, these biomarkers target specific aspects of the CHD pathogenesis and thus have limitations. Therefore, identifying novel, early risk predictors for CHD is critical to improving its prevention and reducing adverse cardiovascular events. Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) was initially regarded merely as a simple carrier protein for sex steroids. With in-depth biological research, SHBG is now recognized as a sophisticated metabolic sensor and functional hepatokine, whose synthesis and biological activity are closely modulated by hepatic nuclear factor 4α (HNF-4α).

Theoretically, SHBG can well compensate for the deficiencies of traditional risk scoring systems. It can reflect early metabolic abnormalities hidden in high-risk groups, make up for the inadequate risk evaluation of conventional models in vulnerable populations, and connect hepatic metabolic regulation, sex hormone homeostasis, and vascular pathological changes from a holistic perspective. A low circulating level of SHBG has emerged as a potential risk factor for MetS and insulin resistance, both of which have been recognized as early risk factors for CHD. This discovery has prompted a growing interest in the role of SHBG in CHD (3-6). We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0787/rc).

Methods

A comprehensive narrative literature review was conducted to evaluate the association between SHBG and CHD. To ensure methodological rigor, the conduct and reporting of this review were informed by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) framework. The literature search covered publications between January 1981 and August 2024. The primary electronic databases searched were PubMed and Web of Science.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) peer-reviewed original research (including cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional analyses, and randomized controlled trials when relevant), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines; (II) publications in the English language; and (III) studies investigating the association between serum SHBG levels and the risk, incidence of CHD in human populations. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (I) non-English publications; (II) studies not primarily focused on the SHBG-CHD relationship; and (III) case reports and editorials.

A combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords was utilized. The search strategy was structured around key concepts, linked by the AND operator: (“Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin” OR “SHBG”) AND (“Coronary Heart Disease” OR “CHD” OR “Coronary Artery Disease” OR “Myocardial Ischemia” OR “cardiovascular risk”) AND (“risk assessment” OR “prognosis” OR “biomarker” OR “incidence” OR “mortality”).

Study selection was independently performed by two investigators (X.L. and H.G.), both of whom received training in systematic review methodology. Relevant studies were identified and screened based on the review objectives. The screening process consisted of two stages: initial screening of titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, followed by retrieval and full-text assessment of potentially relevant publications. Data extraction focused on study design, population characteristics, SHBG measurement methods, reported associations between SHBG levels and CHD outcomes [e.g., hazard ratios (HRs), odds ratios], and adjustments for confounding factors. Overview of SHBG

SHBG, also known as testosterone-estradiol-binding globulin, is a homodimeric glycoprotein encoded by a single gene located on the short arm of chromosome 17 (7). It is primarily produced in the liver (8), although smaller amounts are synthesized in the brain, uterus, testes, and placenta. SHBG specifically binds to and transports sex hormones (9), with the majority of circulating testosterone and estradiol being bound to SHBG, a smaller portion being bound to serum albumin, and only a minimal fraction remaining free to enter cells and activate receptors, thus exerting biological effects. SHBG binds to androgens such as testosterone, dihydrotestosterone, and estradiol, with varying affinities (10). The relative binding affinities of sex steroid hormones to SHBG, from highest to lowest, are as follows: dihydrotestosterone, testosterone, androstenediol, estradiol, and estrone (11). Under normal physiological conditions, estrogen stimulates SHBG synthesis, while testosterone and insulin inhibit it (12). Adult females typically have higher SHBG levels than do males (13), but SHBG levels decrease significantly in postmenopausal women (14). Hepatocytes are the primary site of SHBG synthesis. Experimental studies in the human liver cancer cell line HepG2 have shown that insulin and androgens suppress SHBG production (15-17). Conversely, estrogen increases SHBG production by upregulating HNF-4α, which plays a key role in controlling SHBG promoter activity (18,19). HNF-4α is the most critical transcription factor in the activation of SHBG expression in the liver (20). Hyperinsulinemia inhibits HNF-4α expression, reducing SHBG synthesis and production in the liver (21).

Association of SHBG with CHD risk

Key findings from epidemiological studies

Over recent years, the role of SHBG in CHD has garnered increased research attention and a corresponding growth in the volume of related literature. However, studies on the relationship between SHBG levels and CHD incidence have produced conflicting results, with differing viewpoints emerging. For instance, a prospective cohort study from the UK found that in middle-aged and older adult men, lower serum testosterone levels were not associated with CHD-related mortality, while lower SHBG levels were independently associated with a reduced risk of CHD, indicating a positive correlation between SHBG levels and CHD risk (22). Conversely, in a study (23) on men aged 69 to 81 years, lower SHBG levels were linked to a higher risk of CHD, with SHBG levels demonstrating a negative correlation with CHD risk. Another finding of this study was the association of SHBG levels with other known cardiovascular risk factors (23). Similar findings have been reported in women (24), but other work has indicated no significant association (25-27). For example, in a prospective study of 14,274 healthy postmenopausal women, the correlation between SHBG and CHD risk disappeared after adjustments were made for hypertension, body mass index (BMI), and serum cholesterol levels, suggesting that no association between SHBG levels and CHD risk exists beyond these factors in postmenopausal women (28). A comprehensive analysis of postmenopausal women similarly indicated no significant correlation between SHBG levels and CHD risk (29). However, a more recent cumulative meta-analysis published in 2023 that included 11 studies demonstrated that higher SHBG levels are associated with a reduced incidence of CHD in both men and women. The adjusted HR comparing the highest versus the lowest quintile was 0.86 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.79–0.93; P<0.001] in men and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.75–0.94; P<0.001) in women. This indicates that higher circulating SHBG levels are an independent inverse correlate of CHD risk in both men and women (30).

The reasons for these contradictory findings have not been clearly identified. Differences in study design, analytical methods, and the covariates considered might have led to the inconsistency in these conclusions. For instance, Chen et al. (28) adjusted for a broader range of covariates, including traditional cardiovascular risk factors, while Yeap et al. (22) focused on the direct association between SHBG and cardiovascular events. Different statistical models and adjustment variables, as well as diversity in the methods used to measure and analyze SHBG and testosterone, could also contribute to the variability in these results. Yeap et al. (22) conducted a UK-based prospective cohort study involving community-dwelling men aged 40 to 69 years, whereas the study by Ohlsson et al. (23) was based on the Swedish Osteoporotic Fractures in Men (MrOS) study, which included men aged 69 to 81 years; meanwhile, Chen et al. (28) examined postmenopausal women. Thus, the differences in sample selection, such as age and health status, may partially explain the divergent conclusions. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting these results, and a variety of possible explanations and limitations should be considered. Further research is necessary to more definitely define these relationships in order to better understand the impact of SHBG levels on CHD.

Differences in SHBG between male and female patients

Males and females differ significantly in CHD risk, with men having a higher incidence than women. This disparity is largely attributed to the protective effects of estrogen on the cardiovascular system, an effect that persists until women reach menopause. After menopause, the absence of estrogen reduces this protective effect, bringing CHD risk levels in women closer to those in men (31,32). SHBG specifically binds to sex steroid hormones, primarily binding testosterone in men and transporting the majority of estrogen in women (33). Studies have shown that lower levels of SHBG may be associated with higher levels of free sex hormones, which could potentially increase the risk of CHD.

The role of SHBG in assessing CHD risk through its association with insulin resistance

Insulin resistance is a condition in which the body’s response to insulin is diminished, leading to elevated blood glucose levels, and is a precursor to type 2 diabetes and various CVDs (34). The relationship between SHBG and insulin resistance has been examined in multiple studies. Research indicates a significant inverse correlation between SHBG levels and insulin resistance, suggesting that SHBG levels could serve as a predictive marker for its development (35). This association is present in both men and women, including premenopausal and postmenopausal women (36). Arterial stiffness, a risk factor for CHD, can be exacerbated by insulin resistance through mechanisms such as reduced NO production by the vascular endothelium, increased stiffness of vascular smooth muscle cells, and activation of serum- and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK-1), thereby raising the risk of CVD (37). This suggests that individuals with lower SHBG levels are more likely to exhibit insulin resistance, which in turn increases their risk of CHD. This establishes a scientific rationale for SHBG as a CHD risk biomarker.

Association of SHBG with MetS and its biomarker potential for CHD risk

MetS is one of the most significant global health concerns and a major risk factor for CVD. MetS is characterized by a cluster of metabolic disorders, including obesity, dyslipidemia [high triglyceride (TG) and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels], elevated fasting glucose level, and hypertension, all of which significantly increase the risk of CHD (38). Visceral fat is the primary driver, followed by chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and neurohormonal activation (39). Additionally, studies have shown that MetS is associated with an increased incidence of CVD. In one study, over an average follow-up of 11 years, the likelihood of CHD in men and women with MetS was 1.46 times (95% CI: 1.23–1.74) and 2.05 times (95% CI: 1.59–2.64) higher compared to those without MetS, respectively, after adjustments were made for age, smoking, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level, and ethnicity (40). Low SHBG levels may be an early indicator of MetS development, which is closely linked to an increased risk of CVD. According to research by Brand et al., low SHBG levels are significantly associated with MetS in men, particularly in non-overweight individuals, and this association is primarily driven by components such as high TG levels, abdominal obesity, and elevated blood glucose levels (41). Low SHBG levels are highly correlated with high leptin and low adiponectin levels, which, independently of testosterone, increase the risk of MetS (42). It has been suggested that SHBG may play a crucial role in the development of MetS through its regulation of these adipokines. Despite gender differences in SHBG levels, both men and women with low SHBG levels are at a higher risk of developing MetS and subsequent CHD events. Therefore, SHBG can be considered to be a valuable biomarker for assessing individual CHD risk, especially in the presence of metabolic abnormalities.

In summary, SHBG levels are associated with menopause, insulin resistance, and MetS, all of which are risk factors for CHD. Therefore, SHBG may be related to an increased risk of CHD and could potentially serve as a novel risk biomarker for this disease, although its mechanisms remain to be fully clarified.

Mechanisms underlying the association between SHBG and CHD risk

SHBG is primarily synthesized by liver cells, and HNF-4α is a transcription factor that regulates the promoter activity of the SHBG gene (8). The presence of HNF-4α binding sites in the SHBG gene promoter region suggests that HNF-4α binding can enhance SHBG gene transcription. Androgens may suppress SHBG production by downregulating HNF-4α expression, thereby reducing serum SHBG concentrations (15). Research has shown that in HepG2 cells, monosaccharides such as fructose or glucose can rapidly downregulate HNF-4α in a dose-dependent manner, with palmitate levels in cells increasing concurrently. This sugar-induced lipid synthesis leads to a continuous decrease in HNF-4α, which results in low SHBG expression. This explains why SHBG is a sensitive biomarker for MetS. In the context of insulin resistance, increased hepatic fat synthesis suppresses HNF-4α expression, thereby downregulating SHBG gene transcription and production. Thyroid hormones can influence serum SHBG levels by affecting its production in the liver under both normal and pathological conditions. Research (18) has shown that thyroid hormones increase the expression of HNF-4α and reduce cellular palmitate levels, which enhances lipolysis and indirectly increases hepatic SHBG production. Furthermore, it has been confirmed that thyroid hormones can elevate SHBG levels regardless of hyperglycemic status. Other work (19) has indicated that antagonists of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) may increase SHBG expression by reducing hepatic fat accumulation and the synthesis of free fatty acids.

A substantial body of research indicates that insulin resistance is a risk factor for CHD (43). Lower levels of SHBG lead to a decreased abundance of bound testosterone and increased free testosterone, which impairs insulin binding and degradation in peripheral tissues and the liver. This results in reduced insulin sensitivity in muscle tissues and increased gluconeogenesis, exacerbating insulin resistance and raising blood glucose levels. Elevated insulin levels, in turn, can suppress SHBG synthesis, leading to disruptions in sex hormone secretion and disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism. Studies investigating the correlation between SHBG and risk factors for type 2 diabetes have shown a significant negative correlation between SHBG levels and the insulin resistance index. As serum SHBG concentration decreases, insulin resistance worsens, with multiple linear regression analyses revealing a linear relationship between SHBG and insulin resistance index, indicating a close association between them (44).

Dyslipidemia is a crucial component of MetS and a major risk factor for CHD. Previous studies have demonstrated that estrogen can reduce total cholesterol (TC) and LDL-C by accelerating lipid degradation and excretion, thereby decreasing TC deposition in arterial walls and alleviating arterial blockage. The proposed mechanism involves estrogen upregulating the number and activity of LDL receptors (LDLRs) and enhancing LDL-C’s affinity for LDLRs, which accelerates hepatic LDL-C uptake and breakdown, thereby lowering levels of TC and LDL-C in the blood. A negative correlation between SHBG levels and abdominal aortic calcification was found. This relationship was not influenced by other cardiovascular risk factors such as sex hormone levels, diabetes, or BMI. However, after HDL cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were controlled for, the correlation between SHBG and abdominal aortic calcification disappeared, suggesting that SHBG may exert its effects through increasing HDL-C levels (45). Thus, SHBG may impact cardiovascular health not only by directly influencing sex hormone levels but also through its involvement in metabolic processes.

SHBG may exert an independent effect on CHD pathogenesis, potentially through mechanisms intrinsic to the SHBG protein or its genetic regulation. Research has shown that in postmenopausal women, the (TAAAA)n polymorphism in the SHBG gene promoter is associated with the severity of coronary artery disease (CAD). The presence of longer repeat sequences leads to reduced SHBG expression and lower circulating SHBG levels, with the length of the (TAAAA)n repeat sequence positively correlating with CAD severity. This finding suggests a potential role for SHBG in the development of atherosclerosis (46).

Discussion and prospects

Research into the causes and mechanisms of CHD is ongoing. Since CHD cannot be completely cured, clinical practice often focuses on preventing and controlling its development by addressing its risk factors. Studies on CHD risk factors and their mechanisms are valuable for preventing CHD occurrence and delaying its progression. The application of SHBG in risk stratification, risk assessment, and the monitoring of CHD warrants further investigation. Currently, there is inconsistency and controversy in the literature regarding the relationship between SHBG and CHD risk. Substantial heterogeneity exists among published studies investigating the relationship between SHBG and CHD risk. Such discrepancies do not occur by chance, but primarily stem from differences in confounding adjustment strategies, baseline metabolic profiles of enrolled participants, and variations in study design and methodological quality. Of particular note is the finding reported by Chen et al. (28), in which the crude association between SHBG concentration and CHD was no longer statistically significant after additional adjustment for BMI and serum lipid parameters. This observation carries important clinical and mechanistic implications, suggesting that SHBG functions primarily as a surrogate biomarker reflecting overall metabolic status, rather than an independent causal contributor to CHD pathogenesis. The correlational relationship observed in unadjusted analyses is largely mediated by underlying metabolic disturbances, instead of a direct pathogenic effect exerted by SHBG itself. In the present review, we systematically assessed the methodological quality and potential bias of included observational studies in accordance with well-established evaluation frameworks. Our critical appraisal revealed that small-sample cross-sectional studies, as well as those with inadequate adjustment for metabolic and lifestyle confounders, are the major contributors to inter-study heterogeneity. In contrast, large-scale prospective cohort studies yield more robust, stable, and credible evidence regarding the SHBG-CHD association.

Conclusions

SHBG represents a potential novel biomarker for CHD risk assessment. Its serum level is closely associated with key risk pathways such as insulin resistance and MetS. Large-scale studies have confirmed that low SHBG levels are correlated with an increased risk of CHD. Although some research findings remain inconsistent, its mechanism of influencing cardiovascular health by regulating sex hormones and metabolism is well-established, demonstrating its potential as a comprehensive risk marker. Further standardized studies and clinical application exploration are needed in the future.
