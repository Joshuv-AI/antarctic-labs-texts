Unveiling the role of angiogenesis-related biomarkers in diabetic nephropathy via transcriptomics
Authors: Shiyu Zhao, Cuiping Yang, Linlin Gao, Xuemei Fan, Shiwei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic nephropathy (DN), a common complication of diabetes, is characterized by structural and functional renal damage. Angiogenesis plays a pivotal role in both physiological and pathological renal processes. This study explored angiogenesis-related biomarkers in DN to provide theoretical insights for clinical practice.MethodsIn this study, datasets obtained from public databases were utilized to integrate differential expression analysis with Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. This approach successfully identified biomarkers that exhibited a causal relationship with DN and facilitated the construction of a nomogram based on these genes. Furthermore, the signaling pathways associated with these biomarkers were investigated, along with the differences in the immune microenvironment between DN and normal tissues. Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), one of the most common microvascular complications of diabetes [1, 2], has become the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [3]. Epidemiological data indicated that 30–40% of patients with type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) will progress to DN, which is clinically characterized by persistent proteinuria and/or a progressive decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [4, 5]. This pathological process significantly increases the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death in patients [6]. From a pathogenic perspective, DN results from the combined effects of hemodynamic and non-hemodynamic factors. Early-stage hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia induce intraglomerular hypertension and single-nephron hyperfiltration, which increase glomerular wall tension and shear stress, triggering a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ultimately leading to proteinuria and irreversible kidney damage [7]. Notably, the early stages of DN often lack obvious clinical symptoms. By the time albuminuria is detected, renal injury has typically progressed to an irreversible stage and rapidly deteriorates to ESRD [8]. Although current clinical treatments—such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and MRAs—can partially delay disease progression [9], their therapeutic efficacy remains limited. This underscores the urgent need to further elucidate the pathogenesis of DN and develop novel targeted therapeutic strategies.

Angiogenesis is a highly coordinated physiological process involving intricate interactions among various angiogenic growth factors, endothelial cells (ECs), and the extracellular matrix (ECM) [10]. This process requires ECs to undergo a series of precisely regulated cellular events, including proliferation, survival, migration, morphological changes, anastomosis, and ECM remodeling [11]. Under normal conditions, angiogenesis is tightly controlled by a dynamic balance between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors, and disruption of this balance leads to aberrant angiogenesis with pathological consequences [12]. Angiogenesis, a critical multistep process in the pathophysiology and progression of DN, is significantly modulated by angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) and calcium channel blockers (CCBs) [13]. In the pathogenesis of DN, abnormal glomerular angiogenesis can be observed as early as the initial stages of the disease [14]. Relevant research showed that exosomal miR-30a-5p can inhibit endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and abnormal angiogenesis in glomerular endothelial cells (GECs) by regulating the Notch1/VEGF signaling pathway, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target for DN [15]. Animal model studies have further demonstrated that periglomerular vascular abnormalities in diabetic mice are closely associated with glomerular dysfunction [12], highlighting the critical role of dysregulated angiogenesis in DN progression. Therefore, a deeper understanding of angiogenic regulatory mechanisms and the development of angiogenesis-targeted biomarkers may provide novel therapeutic targets for DN intervention.

Given the pivotal role of angiogenesis in the pathogenesis of DN, this study integrated multi-omics data to identify angiogenesis-related biomarkers for DN (Figure S1). Through comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic sequencing data from DN patients, coupled with Mendelian randomization (MR) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies, we have not only successfully identified key biomarkers but also elucidated their cell type-specific expression patterns. Functional enrichment analysis and regulatory network construction further revealed critical signaling pathways associated with these biomarkers. Notably, MR analysis provided genetic evidence supporting causal relationships between these genes and DN risk. These findings not only offer potential biomarkers for early DN diagnosis but also establish a molecular foundation for developing novel angiogenesis-targeted therapeutic strategies, demonstrating significant translational value for improving clinical outcomes in DN patients.

Methods

Data source

Data on glomerular samples from DN patients were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), with details provided in Table 1. A search for the keyword “angiogenesis” in GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) yielded a total of 2,312 angiogenesis-related genes (ARGs) (score >0.8.). Furthermore, 48 ARGs were retrieved from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). After merging these two sets and removing duplicates, a total of 2,316 unique ARGs were acquired for subsequent analysis. The genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset for DN, identified as ebi-a-GCST90018832, was sourced from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) [16], with an analysis of 24,190,738 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within a European population cohort. Additionally, the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data for genes were obtained from the eQTLGen database (https://eqtlgen.org/phase1.html).

Differential expression and functional enrichment analyses

Differential expression analysis was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DN and normal tissues in the training cohort. For this, the “limma” package (v 1.40.2) was used, with access to the pre-processed expression matrix contained within the Series Matrix File [17]. The criteria for identifying DEGs included a |log2Fold Change (FC)| (|log2FC|) >0.5, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-P.adjust < 0.05. The DEGs were visualized using a volcano plot and a heatmap, which were generated with the “ggplot2” package (v 3.3.6) [18] and the “pheatmap” package (v 1.0.12) [19], respectively. In subsequent analyses, these DEGs were mapped against a set of 2,316 ARGs using the “ggvenn” package (v 0.1.9) [20], enabling the identification of differentially expressed ARGs (DE-ARGs).

Further analyses were conducted on the DE-ARGs using Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) via “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.7.1.001) with a significance threshold established at BH-P.adjust < 0.05 [21]. The “ggpubr” package (v 0.5.0) was employed to visualize the top 5 entries for each GO category and the top 10 significant KEGG pathways [22].

Selection of instrumental variables (IVs) for MR analysis

This study followed the Mendelian reporting specifications for randomised studies (STROBE-MR) [23]. The MR analysis was constructed through “TwoSampleMR” package (v 0.56) [24], DE-ARGs as exposure factors and DN as the outcome variable. MR analysis was based on three fundamental assumptions: (1) a strong correlation was required between genetic variation and risk factors; (2) IVs needed to be independent of confounding factors; and (3) IVs could influence outcomes exclusively through their effects on risk factors.

The extract_instruments function was used to select IVs related to the exposure factors. Exposure factors with fewer than three IVs were excluded, ensuring that the selected IVs demonstrated significant associations (P < 5*10− 5). To refine our IVs selection and mitigate the confounding effects of linkage disequilibrium (LD), the parameters were adjusted to r2 = 0.01 and kb = 100 with clump set to TRUE. Furthermore, the F-statistic was calculated, with an F-statistic greater than 10 considered to indicate appropriate robustness. The formula was F=×, where N represented the sample size of the exposure GWAS study, k was the number of IVs, and R2 indicated the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by the IVs. Subsequently, the harmonise_data function was utilized to align effect alleles with effect sizes, thus ensuring consistency across datasets. This process ultimately determined the appropriate IVs for analysis. Through this rigorous process, the appropriate IVs were ultimately determined, providing a solid foundation for the subsequent analysis.

Exploring the causal association between DE-ARGs and DN

The “mr” function was utilized in conjunction with five distinct algorithms to conduct MR analysis. These algorithms included MR-Egger [25], Weighted Median, Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW), Simple Mode [24], and Weighted Mode. The identification of significant associations (P < 0.05) was facilitated by the IVW method’s robustness in detecting causal relationships.

To ensure the reliability of the MR results, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted as follow: a heterogeneity test [26] was performed to assess the consistency of the effect estimates across different IVs. Horizontal pleiotropy was rigorously evaluated using MR-Egger intercept tests and MR-PRESSO (P >0.05 indicating minimal pleiotropy) [27]. A P-value >0.05 in these tests indicated minimal pleiotropy, supporting the robustness of the causal inferences. A Leave-One-Out (LOO) test [28] was employed to check the stability of the results by excluding one IV at a time. These comprehensive assessments provided a robust evaluation of the MR findings, ensuring their validity and robustness.

Selection of biomarker and construction of nomogram

To further investigate the expression levels of genes causally associated with DN, boxplots were generated to visualize the expression profiles of these genes in DN and normal tissues from the training dataset GSE96804 and the validation dataset GSE104948. To evaluate the diagnostic capability of the genes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for both datasets. The area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated using the “pROC” package (v 1.18.0) [29] in R to quantify their diagnostic performance. Meanwhile, the biomarker chromosome localization analysis was achieved using the “RCircos” package (v 1.2.0) [30] in R language.

Based on the GSE96804 dataset, a nomogram model was constructed using the “rms” (v 6.8-1.8.8) package in R, incorporating the expression levels of selected biomarkers to predict DN diagnosis probabilities. To assess the performance and reliability of this nomogram model, calibration curves and ROC curves were subsequently generated.

Functional and annotation analyses

Based on the training cohort, differential analysis was conducted between the DN group and normal tissues using the R package “limma” (v 3.50.1) [17]. The results were subsequently sorted according to log2FC values. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed utilizing the “clusterProfiler” package, with a significance threshold set at P.adjust < 0.05. The top 10 most significant enrichment pathways were visualized. Additionally, Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) enrichment analysis was carried out between the DN and normal tissues using the “GSVA” and “limma” packages in R, based on the MSigDB database’s KEGG background gene set (c2.cp.kegg.symbols). A threshold of |t| >2 and P.adjust < 0.05 was applied, and the top 5 upregulated and downregulated pathways were ranked according to their |t| values.

Immune infiltration analysis

To evaluate the microenvironment constituted by immune cells, the calculation of the proportion of 22 immune cell types was conducted in the transcriptome data with the help of the “CIBERSORT” package in R language (v 0.1.0) [31]. The bar chart illustrating the infiltration abundance percentage of immune cells in DN and normal tissues was plotted by means of the “ggplot2” R package (v 3.4.1) [32]. Afterwards, the box plot of the immune cell proportion was drawn and the wilcoxon test was implemented between the DN and normal tissues. The “spearman” correlation between biomarkers and differential immune cells was computed using the “psych” package (v 2.3.6) in R language.

Construction of the regulatory network and drug prediction for biomarkers

To gain a deeper understanding of the interactions of biomarkers in the regulatory network, the microRNAs (miRNAs) corresponding to the biomarkers were predicted in the miRDB database (https://mirdb.org/), and simultaneously, the long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and their interaction relationships with miRNAs were predicted in the StarBase database (https://rnasysu.com/encori/), with the aim of constructing the mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA network.

The DGIdb database (http://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/) was utilized to predict therapeutic drugs targeting biomarkers associated with DN. To verify the binding energy between the biomarkers and the drugs, molecular docking was carried out. The 3D molecular conformations of the drug components were downloaded from the PubChem Compound database. Meanwhile, the 3D structure of the protein in PDB format was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Subsequently, the water molecules and small ligand molecules were removed using the PyMOL software and the molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. Finally, the results of AutoDock Vina were visualized using PyMOL.

ScRNA-seq analysis

Following transcriptomic analysis, this study conducted scRNA-seq on the GSE131882 dataset to elucidate cellular mechanisms in DN. All analyses were performed using the Seurat package (version 5.1.0) [33]. The process began with stringent quality control (QC) measures to exclude cells with nFeature_RNA ≥ 3000, nCount_RNA ≥ 7000, or mitochondrial content ≥ 25%.

After log normalization, the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) method was employed to identify the top 2,000 highly variable genes for downstream analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was facilitated by the JackStrawPlot and JackStraw functions, with appropriate principal components (PCs) selected for further analysis. Clustering analysis was conducted using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, and the results were visualized through t-distributed stochastic neighborhood embedding (t-SNE) at a resolution of 0.4, yielding distinct cellular clusters. With the application of CellMarker 2.0, the cell subpopulations were annotated (Table S1), and the cell types associated with DN were identified. Building on this foundation, the developmental trajectories of these cell types were analyzed using pseudo-time analysis in the Monocle package (v 2.26.0). Additionally, intercellular communications were examined using the CellChat package (v 1.6.1) to observe the interactions among the identified cell types.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

In this study, with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Bethune Hospital, Shanxi Academy of Medical Sciences(Ethics Approval Number: [YXLL-2025-024]). Considering clinical practicality and accessibility, a total of 10 DN and 10 control blood samples were collected to validate the expression levels of biomarkers via qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from the samples in accordance with the standard operating procedures provided in the kit manual (ABScript III RT Master Mix). RNA integrity was assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis, and only RNA samples that met the predefined quality criteria were retained for downstream experiments. Subsequently, genomic DNA (gDNA) was removed using the gDNA Remover kit (Abclonal), and the purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) for further analysis.The primer sequences used in the analysis were shown in Table S2. The relative mRNA expression levels were estimated by the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH as an internal reference gene.

Western blotting (WB)

WB analysis was done to detect the expression levels of the target proteins. Initially, RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors was employed to extract total cellular proteins. Subsequently, a double volume of albumin removal reagent was added to eradicate albumin interference. The BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) manufacturer’s instructions were rigorously followed to determine the protein concentration. The protein samples (20–35 µg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes using a moist transfer method. The membranes were incubated with TBST buffer and blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 30 min after transfer to prevent nonspecific binding. The membranes were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies against CD44 (Abways), HSPG2 (Proteintech), LPAR1 (Abways), PTGES (Abclonal), FN (Abclonal), LAMC1 (Abclonal), or ITGB5 (Abclonal), and the internal control Alpha Tubulin (Proteintech). The corresponding secondary antibody, Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Thermofisher), was then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Lastly, the images were acquired in TIFF format, and the protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent detection. Image J software was employed to conduct a quantitative analysis of band intensity. In order to guarantee the reliability of the results, each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

R software (v 4.2.2) was employed to conduct all analyses. The wilcoxon test was employed to analyze the differences between the categories. In this study, a P-value threshold of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for MR and immune infiltration analyses. For all other analyses, a corrected P-value (P. adjust) of less than 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Selection and enrichment analysis of DE-ARGs

In the training cohort of this study, differential expression analysis identified 1,041 DEGs between DN and normal tissues, comprising 478 upregulated and 563 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A and B, Table S2). Subsequently, these DEGs were intersected with 2,316 ARGs, resulting in the identification of 275 DE-ARGs (Fig. 1C). Enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were associated with 2,472 GO terms, including “ECM organization,” “endoplasmic reticulum lumen,” and “epithelial cell proliferation” (Fig. 1D, Table S4). The DE-ARGs were also significantly involved in 67 KEGG pathways, such as the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” “MAPK signaling pathway,” and “Ras signaling pathway” (Fig. 1E, Table S4). Overall, these findings have not only enhanced our understanding of the complex molecular landscape of DN but have also offered potential targets for therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating related signaling pathways to mitigate DN.

Identification of 12 genes with causal relationships to DN

Subsequently, MR analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential causal relationships between 275 DE-ARGs and DN. Unfortunately, of these, 190 biomarkers had available GWAS data. After excluding genes with fewer than 3 IVs and matching with outcome variables, a final set of 154 genes was retained for MR analysis (Table S5). 23 SNPs for genes were identified as having significant associations with DN (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) in the GWAS Catalog and were therefore excluded (Table S6). The IVW method revealed significant causal relationships between 12 genes and DN, consistent with the expression trends of the differentially expressed genes (Fig. 2A). Among these, 8 genes were identified as risk factors, including CD44 [OR = 1.559, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.133–2.145, P = 0.006], HSPG2 [OR = 1.167, 95% CI: 1.023–1.331, P = 0.022], PTGES [OR = 1.225, 95% CI: 1.000–1.501.000.501, P = 0.050] and LPAR1 [OR = 1.082, 95% CI: 1.011–1.157, P = 0.022]. A total of 4 genes were identified as protective factors, including NDUFS8 [OR = 0.792, 95% CI: 0.696–0.900.696.900, P < 0.001] and NAMPT [OR = 0.421, 95% CI: 0.200–0.888.200.888, P = 0.023]. Scatter plots indicated that the expression of 8 genes was positively correlated with an increased risk of DN, while the expression of four genes was negatively correlated (Fig. 2B). Forest plots showed that the effect sizes of the eight genes were consistently above zero, while those of the four genes were below zero (Fig. 2C). Funnel plots confirmed the study’s adherence to the second law of MR (Fig. 2D). Sensitivity analyses further validated the accuracy of the MR analysis; specifically, Cochran’s Q test indicated no heterogeneity in the results (P > 0.05). Pleiotropy tests, including MR-Egger (intercept range: −0.02 to 0.03) and MR-PRESSO methods, showed no confounding biases (all P > 0.05) (Table S7). LOO analysis demonstrated that the effect sizes of the remaining SNPs on DN did not change significantly when each SNP was sequentially excluded (Fig. 2E), emphasizing the robustness of the MR study results.

Ascertainment of CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES as biomarkers and development of a diagnostic nomogram

To further validate the significant expression levels of 12 genes in DN, the expression levels of these genes were analyzed in the training set GSE96804 and the validation set, and box plots were generated. The results demonstrated that CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES exhibited significant expression differences in both the training and validation sets, with consistent expression trends observed (Fig. 3A). Notably, the AUC values for these genes were all greater than 0.70, indicating their strong diagnostic capabilities (Fig. 3B). Consequently, CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES were selected as biomarkers for the diagnosis of DN. Furthermore, the chromosomal localization of the biomarkers was investigated; HSPG2 was located on chromosome 1, CD44 on chromosome 11, and both PTGES and LPAR1 were located on chromosome 9 (Fig. 3C).

Nomogram was constructed based on the expression levels of these biomarkers. In this nomogram, each biomarker’s expression level was assigned a corresponding score, and the scores of all genes were summed to obtain a total score (Total Points). In the nomogram plot, higher total scores correlated with an increased probability of diagnosing samples as disease-positive (Fig. 3D). The AUC value of the nomogram model was calculated to be 0.946, indicating that this model could effectively predict disease occurrence (Fig. 3E). Calibration curves demonstrated that the nomogram exhibited significant predictive accuracy (Fig. 3F). Therefore, the constructed nomogram displayed superior performance in the diagnosis of DN.

Enrichment analysis for unveiling the potential mechanisms of DN

GSEA was conducted to identify pathways significantly enriched in DN and control samples. The results indicated significant enrichment of the “arginine and proline metabolism,” “ECM receptor interaction,” and “focal adhesion” pathway (Fig. 4A). Additionally, GSVA revealed significant differences in pathways such as “medicus pathogen hcmv_ul33 to_gnaq_plcb g_calcineurin_signaling_pathway” and “medicus reference loading of the smc5 smc6 complex” were activated in DN, while “sulfur metabolism” and “medicus reference flcn mtorc1 signaling pathway” were suppressed (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these findings not only enhanced the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying DN but also provided a scientific basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies. Several pathways strongly associated with DN were identified, and their dysregulation may play a critical role in disease progression. Future studies will further explore the specific mechanisms of these pathways.

Revealing immune cell infiltration in DN and control samples

Understanding the role of immune cells in DN has been instrumental in the development of therapeutic strategies targeting specific immune cells. Immune infiltration analysis revealed that 8 out of 22 types of cells exhibited significantly different infiltration levels between DN and control samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A, B). Notably, the infiltration levels of CD8 T cells, M2 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells were significantly elevated in DN samples (P < 0.05). Conversely, the infiltration levels of activated mast cells and neutrophils were found to be lower in DN samples. Additionally, correlation analyses revealed that the strongest negative correlation was observed between neutrophils and PTGES (cor = −0.71, P < 0.001), while the strongest positive correlation was found between M2 macrophages and LPAR1 (cor = 0.81, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). By identifying the differential immune cell types associated with DN and their changes in infiltration levels, a deeper understanding of the immune microenvironment in DN was achieved. This knowledge provided potential targets for future immunotherapy research.

Investigation of potential regulatory mechanisms targeting DN biomarkers, and drug predictions

Through the prediction of miRNAs and lncRNAs associated with biomarkers, 14 miRNAs and 24 lncRNAs were identified, revealing mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA interaction relationships. The constructed network comprised 4 mRNAs, 14 miRNAs, and 24 lncRNAs, totaling 42 nodes and forming 177 interaction relationships (Fig. 6A). Notably, HSPG2 was found to regulate miR-15a-5p, which in turn affected the following lncRNAs: SNHG16, XIST, MIR497HG, FGD5-AS1, AC005261.1, KCNQ1OT1, SNHG1, NEAT1, and AC079781.5. These findings provide a comprehensive view of the regulatory network involving these biomarkers and highlight potential targets for further investigation in DN. In addition, drug prediction analyses revealed associations between DN and specific compounds. Notably, CD44 was linked to cisplatin and acetaminophen, while HSPG2 was associated with cyclosporine (Fig. 6B). Unfortunately, the drugs predicted by HSPG2 were not accompanied by three-dimensional structures, which precluded the acquisition of molecular docking results. In contrast, significant binding affinities were observed between CD44, LPAR1, and PTGES with their respective compounds (Table S8, Fig. 6C).

Single-cell analysis to elucidate the function of biomarkers

ScRNA-seq analysis was employed to gain an in-depth understanding of gene expression patterns at the individual cell level, which subsequently revealed cellular heterogeneity and functional diversity within biological systems. Therefore, functional annotation was conducted on the GSE131882 dataset to clarify the expression profiles of biomarkers across various cell types. After data filtering, a total of 20,438 cells and 27,871 genes were obtained (Figure S2A). Following standardization, 2,000 highly variable genes were identified (Figure S2B). Subsequently, PCA was conducted, and the first 30 PCs were selected for further analysis (Figure S2C). UMAP clustering analysis was then performed, identifying 17 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 7A). Bubble plots revealed high specificity of marker genes, which were used to name the cells (Fig. 7B). A total of 14 cell types were annotated, including Principal (SCNN1G), Distal convoluted tubular (SLC12A3), and Pan leukocyte (PTPRC). Specific markers for each cell type were provided in Fig. 7C.

Among these, CD44 was found to be highly expressed in Pan leukocyte, prompting a pseudotime analysis of this cell type (Fig. 7D). The results showed that the proportion of cells in the disease group was higher in the early stages of differentiation compared to the control group, indicating that Pan leukocyte primarily responds in the early stages of disease onset (Fig. 7E, F). The expression of CD44 along the time trajectory was subsequently examined, revealing that its levels were lower in the later stages of cell differentiation (Fig. 7G). Further analysis elucidated the communication patterns between cell types. Each cell type exhibited specific communication links with others; notably, the frequency and intensity of interactions between Loop of Henle and Artery endothelial were reduced compared to the control group (Fig. 7H).

Expression levels of biomarkers

WB and qRT-PCR analyses were implemented to verify the expression levels of biomarkers. The quantitative results indicated that the mRNA expression levels of all biomarkers were statistically significant (P < 0.05) as compared to the control and DN groups. Furthermore, the DN patient group exhibited a significant trend of upregulating all biomarkers (Fig. 8A). The high consistency of these findings and prior bioinformatics predictions strongly validates their potential as risk factors for DN. At the protein level, the upregulation patterns of these biomarkers were confirmed (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8B). Notably, the concurrent elevation of FN, LAMC1, and ITGB5—key constituents of the “ECM-receptor interaction” pathway—in peripheral blood samples from DN patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8C) provided strong experimental support for the functional activation of this pathway in DN. These findings collectively underscore the role of these matrix-related proteins as core operational elements within the pathway, potentially contributing to renal injury in DN through ECM-receptor mediated processes such as aberrant angiogenesis.

Discussion

DN, one of the most major causes of renal failure worldwide, is characterized by tissue hypoxemia resulting from the reduction in microvascular density during its pathogenesis—this vascular impairment serves as a key driving factor for the morbidity and mortality of patients [34]. Current research demonstrated that angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A) play dual roles in DN: While moderate VEGF-A levels are essential for maintaining glomerular structure, excessive VEGF-A triggers pathological angiogenesis [12]. Compounding this complexity, the balance of angiogenesis inhibitors is disrupted in DN: vasohibin-1 (VASH1) exerts renoprotective effects, whereas vasohibin-2 (VASH2) aggravates diabetic renal injury by promoting mesangial expansion and compromising the glomerular filtration barrier [35]. This intricate interplay between pro- and anti-angiogenic factors underscores the complexity of vascular regulation in DN.

To examine the crucial relationship between angiogenesis and DN pathogenesis, our multi-omics analysis first identified four key angiogenesis-related biomarkers (CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES) with causal associations with DN risk (validated by Mendelian randomization) and robust diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.70 in both training and validation cohorts). The diagnostic nomogram incorporating these biomarkers demonstrated superior predictive capability (AUC = 0.946), underscoring strong clinical applicability for DN risk assessment.

Functional enrichment analyses further uncovered the molecular pathways through which these biomarkers drive DN pathogenesis. GSEA revealed that DN samples were significantly enriched in pathways tightly linked to angiogenesis and renal tissue remodeling, including “arginine and proline metabolism,” “ECM-receptor interaction,” and “focal adhesion”. Among these, the ECM-receptor interaction pathway is of particular significance, as dysregulated ECM remodeling is a central driver of aberrant angiogenesis, glomerular basement membrane thickening, and interstitial fibrosis—key pathological features of DN that contribute to progressive renal dysfunction [36, 37]. To directly establish the relevance of this pathway to DN pathogenesis, we experimentally validated the expression of its core components—(Fibronectin (FN) [38], Laminin subunit γ−1 (LAMC1) [39], Integrin β−5 (ITGB5) [40–42])—in peripheral blood samples from DN patients and controls. WB analysis confirmed that all three pathway proteins were significantly upregulated in the DN group (all P < 0.05). This coordinated overexpression of ECM–receptor interaction elements was consistent with the elevated expression of the previously identified biomarkers (CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES) in DN, as validated by both qRT-PCR and WB. Together, these results demonstrate functional activation of the ECM–receptor interaction pathway in DN and suggest that the dysregulated biomarkers may promote aberrant angiogenesis through modulation of ECM–receptor crosstalk.

Among the four identified biomarkers, CD44 emerges as a critical mediator linking angiogenesis and immune dysregulation—two core pathogenic drivers in DN. As a non-kinase single-pass transmembrane glycoprotein and key ECM receptor [43], CD44 is broadly expressed on hematopoietic/lymphoid cells, governing cell adhesion, migration, and signaling [44]. Our bioinformatics analyses identified CD44 as a DN-related key gene, with roles in renal structural remodeling via the ECM-receptor interaction pathway. This aligns with studies showing CD44 binds ECM components (e.g., FN, LAM) —often via macrophages [45–47]—and directly promotes DN pathology (e.g. glomerular basement membrane thickening, interstitial fibrosis) by regulating ECM remodeling [48]. Notably, CD44 also acts as an endogenous angiogenesis inhibitor on endothelial cells: its expression restricts endothelial proliferation, driving aberrant vascular formation, further connecting CD44-ECM crosstalk to early DN angiogenic dysregulation [49]. Beyond its roles in angiogenesis and ECM remodeling, CD44 exerts critical immunomodulatory effects relevant to DN. Prior work has shown that CD44 is transiently upregulated on activated T cells/innate immune cells, where it orchestrates T cell maturation/apoptosis, shapes neutrophil/macrophage migration/cytokine production, and mediates lymphocyte trafficking [50]. Moreover, CD44 expression levels govern the differentiation of CD34-positive progenitor cells, with elevated CD44 levels facilitating the transformation of these precursor cells into monocytes and dendritic cells [51], while further upregulation during monocyte maturation enables hyaluronan (HA) binding—an essential step for macrophage effector functions (acquired only after maturation into macrophages) [52]. This aligns with our observation of elevated CD44 in pan-leukocytes in DN, where sustained high expression may disrupt normal differentiation. This mechanistic link helps explain the abnormal infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, M1/M2 macrophages and dendritic cells observed in DN. Collectively, CD44 amplifies renal inflammation and fibrosis by boosting immune cell recruitment/activation, consistent with its overexpression in DN renal tissues [53]. However, how CD44 precisely integrates ECM-binding signals to reshape immune cell fates in DN remains unclear and merits deeper investigation.

HSPG2, an agrin-like heparan sulfate proteoglycan, plays a pivotal role in ECM maturation and stabilization [54]. Its interaction with the integrin α2/β1 receptor is critical for exerting anti-angiogenic effects, as this binding might restrict endothelial cell migration and vascular sprouting. Genetic studies further supported HSPG2’s involvement in DN pathogenesis: polymorphisms (e.g., rs3767140) may affect lipoprotein metabolism and glucose regulation, potentially contributing to fasting glucose and lipid abnormalities in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) [55]—key risk factors for DN development. Notably, HSPG2 was also identified as a key candidate gene in Balkan endemic nephropathy (sharing pathological features with DN); in this context, HSPG2 genetic variants may promote renal lesions by disrupting angiogenesis and ECM remodeling [56]. Furthermore, both HSPG2 and CD44 have been implicated in shaping the immune infiltration landscape of DN [57]., highlighting HSPG2’s multifaceted role in DN pathogenesis.

LPAR1, a specific G protein-coupled receptor for lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) [58], contributes to DN pathogenesis through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Experimental evidence demonstrated that LPAR1 signaling not only promotes renal epithelial cell EMT - a critical step in fibrosis - but also induces reactive oxygen species generation, NADPH oxidase expression, and TLR4 upregulation, all of which exacerbate renal inflammation [59]. Preclinical studies further validated LPAR1 as a therapeutic target: inhibitors (e.g., AM095 and ki16425) significantly ameliorate proteinuria in diabetic models by suppressing the MAPK-AKT/KLF5 pathway and reversing EMT [60]. Clinical data supported these findings, with elevated serum LPA levels correlating with renal dysfunction in diabetic patients [61], underscoring LPAR1’s translational relevance.

PTGES (prostaglandin E synthase), is markedly upregulated in inflammatory tissues, shows significantly increased expression in renal tissues of DN patients [61, 62]. The prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) derived from PTGES overexpression acts as a potent inflammatory mediator, promoting the formation of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment in the kidney [63]. Moreover, PTGES expression correlates positively with systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio) and early-stage renal dysfunction (e.g., increased proteinuria) [64], linking its dysregulation to both inflammatory and functional hallmarks of DN. Collectively, these findings established that LPAR1 and PTGES participate in DN development through distinct molecular mechanisms, providing crucial insights into DN pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets.

Building upon the identification of these angiogenesis-related biomarkers, we further explored the immunological landscape of DN, revealing critical immune dysregulation patterns that are functionally tied to these molecular targets. Immune infiltration analysis confirmed significant elevation of CD8⁺ T cells, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells in DN tissues compared to normal controls (all P < 0.05)—populations that not only serve as hallmarks of DN but also interact with the identified biomarkers to drive pathology.

Mechanistically, CD8⁺ T cells dysregulation in DN has been well characterized: preclinical studies in DN rat models showed a marked increase in renal CD8 T cell populations [65], with interleukin-15 (IL-15) promoting their activation and subsequent podocyte injury and glomerulosclerosis [66]. Conversely, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) exert renoprotective effects partially by suppressing CD103 + dendritic cells (DCs)-mediated CD8 + T cell proliferation and activation in the kidneys of DN rats [67], highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this immune axis. These findings align with independent work by Li et al., who reported that PTEN—a DN-protective gene associated with reduced cell senescence—exhibits positive correlations with M2 macrophage and resting dendritic cell infiltration, and negative correlations with monocytes and neutrophils [68]; this pattern mirrors our immune infiltration results, reinforcing the validity of our observations. Macrophage dysfunction also plays a critical role in DN pathogenesis: high-glucose conditions enhance macrophage adhesive and migratory capacities by suppressing autophagy [69], a process that can be targeted to mitigate renal damage in DN. Crucially, autophagy deficiency drives pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization, exacerbating renal inflammation and fibrosis, whereas enhanced autophagy promotes anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, supporting tissue repair [70, 71]. These discoveries suggested that restoring macrophage autophagy homeostasis could serve as a novel therapeutic strategy to rebalance the immune microenvironment in DN.

To further elucidate the cellular mechanisms underlying DN progression, we performed scRNA-seq on renal tissues from the GSE131882 dataset, revealing markedly elevated expression of CD44 in Pan leukocyte populations. This observation is consistent with immunohistochemical studies showing increased infiltration of CD45⁺ leukocytes (a well-established pan-leukocyte marker) in DN renal tissues, with the severity of this inflammatory infiltrate correlating positively with tissue injury [72]. Clinical investigations further identified leukocyte functional abnormalities in T2DM patients, characterized by reduced type I interferon (IFN-β) secretion coupled with elevated α-defensin-1 levels [73], suggesting that both quantitative and qualitative changes in leukocytes contribute to DN development.

Collectively, these data imply that CD44 may regulate leukocyte activation to drive DN pathogenesis. Whereas, flow cytometry or lineage tracing might be required to imply for explaining the CD44’s dynamic expression in regulating pan-leukocyte differentiation in DN (a decrease in CD44 expression during pan-leukocyte differentiation observed may reflect lineage-specific regulation or contextual influences within the DN microenvironment). While direct experimental evidence linking HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES to DN progression via ECM remodeling, angiogenic dysregulation, and immune infiltration remains limited, the functional dysfunctions associated with these biomarkers—including disruption of ECM homeostasis, angiogenic imbalance, and abnormal immune cell recruitment—underscore their potential as therapeutic targets for modulating angiogenic and immune abnormalities in DN. Collectively, these hub genes provided robust evidence for further investigation as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets in DN.

Pharmacogenomic analysis provided clinically actionable insights into therapeutic strategies. Specifically, our drug prediction analysis uncovered significant drug-gene associations: CD44 with cisplatin and acetaminophen, and HSPG2 with cyclosporine. For CD44 targeted interventions, DPP-4 inhibitors were shown to significantly mitigate cisplatin-induced declines in eGFR and reduce acute kidney injury (AKI) incidence in diabetic patients [74]. Notably, acetaminophen combined with N-palmitoylethanolamine demonstrated synergistic analgesic effects for diabetic neuropathic pain without renal impairment [75]. Regarding HSPG2 targeting, cyclosporine combined with mizoribine and losartan effectively attenuated nephrotoxic side effects [76]. These findings not only validated the therapeutic potential of CD44 and HSPG2 as molecular targets but more importantly, provided a rationale for developing precision combination therapies for DN. Current evidence suggested that targeted modulation of these key molecules may achieve dual therapeutic objectives-managing primary disease while preserving renal function-though their precise mechanisms require further elucidation.

This study pioneered a systematic investigation into the association between ARGs and DN, successfully identifying potential diagnostic biomarkers through bioinformatics analysis.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study, which primarily relate to data sources, validation design, and experimental verification: First, the characteristics of the data sources used in our study have impacted both the precision of effect estimates and the generalizability of our findings. While we leveraged GWAS summary data with overall large sample sizes to ensure statistical power for causal inference, these datasets were predominantly derived from European populations. This introduces a critical constraint on population representativeness. Consequently, the causal relationships identified between the biomarkers and DN may not be fully generalizable to non-European populations, this limited our ability to capture the full heterogeneity of the global DN patient population and is reflected in the relatively wide confidence intervals observed for some key findings (e.g., CD44 with an OR of 1.559 and 95% CI: 1.133–2.145), which may indicate limited precision in the effect estimates. Second, our validation design has two notable limitations that restrict the translational value of the identified biomarkers. On the one hand, our blood-based validation is primarily cross-sectional and intended as an initial step to confirm the translational potential of the identified biomarkers, but cannot verify the predictive value or dynamic relevance of the biomarkers over time, this is a critical gap for translating biomarkers into clinical practice. It should also be noted that the initial sample size available for the WB and qRT-PCR validation was relatively limited; hence, the sample cohort has been extended to enhance the reproducibility of these experimental results. On the other hand, we lack renal tissue-specific validation—a key limitation given that the kidney is the primary target organ of DN. Renal tissue studies would provide more direct and tissue-specific evidence for the expression and functional role of the identified biomarkers. However, we currently cannot conduct such validation due to unavoidable practical constraints: Well-characterized DN renal tissue (and matched normal renal tissue) is extremely scarce in clinical practice, as it relies primarily on invasive renal biopsies (limited by clinical indication and patient willingness) or post-mortem collection (restricted by ethical regulations). Third, while MR analysis and external dataset validation identified biomarkers with causal relationships to DN, their precise biological functions in DN pathogenesis await experimental verification. Specifically, our GSEA results highlighted the enrichment of ECM-receptor interaction in DN, yet establishing their direct causal links to DN angiogenesis via functional experiments (e.g., in vitro endothelial dysfunction models) cannot be fully addressed at this stage due to time and resource constraints, thus requires further functional validation.

To address these limitations in future research, we will implement targeted strategies: (1) Collaborate with multi-regional clinical centers to integrate GWAS data from multi-ethnic cohorts and collect larger, homogeneous local samples (aimed at improving the population generalizability of our findings, enhancing the precision of effect estimates and minimizing potential batch effects)—we will further incorporate strict multiple testing correction (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR adjustment) in these confirmatory studies paired with functional experiments. (2) Establish a longitudinal follow-up cohort by collaborating with local hospitals to track biomarker expression and clinical outcomes over 3–5 years, validating the predictive and prognostic value. (3) Partner with clinical nephrology teams to collect qualified renal biopsy samples for tissue-specific validation; (4) Conduct in vitro endothelial dysfunction models (high-glucose HGECs/PBMCs to test biomarker modulation as well as CD8 + T cells/macrophages co-culture assays to explore cell crosstalk) and in vivo work (STZ DN mice with specific biomarkers inhibitors) to investigate how biomarkers regulate ECM-receptor interaction molecules to influence endothelial dysfunction, expanding the interplay between ECM remodeling and angiogenesis in DN.

Conclusion

This study conducted a comprehensive multi-faceted analysis of DN. Through screening DEGs in the training set and integrating MR with external dataset analyses, we identified CD44, HSPG2, LPAR1, and PTGES as robust biomarkers. The constructed nomogram demonstrated excellent predictive accuracy. Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant correlations between these biomarkers and specific immune cell populations. Furthermore, drug prediction provided valuable therapeutic clues. Collectively, these findings provide crucial insights into the pathogenesis of DN and establish a foundation for targeted diagnostic approaches.
