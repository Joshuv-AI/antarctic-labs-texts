Microbiome of Xiphinema elongatum (Nematoda, Longidoridae), isolated from water berry
Authors: Ebrahim Shokoohi, Peter Masoko
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The soil microbiome is crucial for the environment and significantly impacts the ecosystem. Understanding the microbiome and its interaction with soil microorganisms is essential for improving ecological and environmental strategies. In this study, Xiphinema elongatum nematodes were collected from water berry in Sovenga Hills, Limpopo Province, South Africa, and were analyzed their associated bacterial communities using metabarcoding analysis. The findings revealed that X. elongatum forms associations with a wide range of bacterial species. Among the most abundant species identified, we found Sphingomonas sp., a bacterial species commonly found in various habitats and primarily beneficial to plants, and Candidatus Xiphinematobacter, a bacterial species commonly found in nematode species of Xiphinema as an endosymbiont. The analysis using principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the abundance of X. elongatum in the soil is inversely correlated with clay content (r = -0.52) and soil pH levels (r = -0.98), and directly correlated with soil sand content (r = 0.88). This study provides valuable insights into the bacterial species associated with plant-parasitic nematodes in trees in South Africa. It underscores the presence of various potentially detrimental and beneficial nematode-associated bacteria. The results could potentially influence the overall quality of the soil, leading to implications for the productivity and yield of fruit crops. Additionally, the results help us understand the interaction between bacteria and X. elongatum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80877-2.

Introduction

Syzygium cordatum, known as water berry, is an ever green and flowering plant belonging to the Myrtaceae family and holds significant medicinal value in East and Southern Africa1,2. This plant has been recognized and documented in the monographic guide that showcases the most beneficial herbal medicines in South Africa3.

Plant-parasitic nematodes are a diverse group, with over 4100 identified species worldwide. These tiny worms can be ecto/endoparasites, causing substantial economic harm to agricultural production4,5. The results indicated that their annual impact results in losses of 8.8–14.6% of total crop production, amounting to a staggering 100–150 billion dollars globally5.

The nematode species of the genus Xiphinema, which established by Cobb6, are a type of plant-parasitic nematodes that are characterized as migratory ectoparasites, meaning they live on the outside of their host organisms. The genus Xiphinema is considered one of the ten most economically significant genera of plant-parasitic nematodes due to its impact on agriculture and plant health7. They have the ability to infect the root system and transmit certain pathogenic plant nepoviruses8,9. Additionally, Xiphinema contains over 260 valid species, grouping into americanum and non-americanum10,11,1213.

The microbiome encompasses the community of microorganisms residing in and on the bodies of multicellular organisms, including nematodes. It plays a crucial role in producing nutrients, regulating immune responses, modifying host metabolism, and expanding the nematode’s metabolic capabilities. These microorganisms significantly influence the overall well-being, health, and physiology of the nematodes14.

Bacterial association with plant-parasitic nematodes, including Bursaphelenchus xylophilus, Heterodera glycines, and Meloidogyne species, have been studied15–19. Endosymbiont bacteria have been attended by researchers, such as Candidatus Cardinium hertigii, which has been reported with Pratylenchus, Heterodera, Globodera, and Candidatus Wolbachia pipientis with Radophulus, and Pratylenchus20–22.

In addition, the researchers have shown that Xiphinema bears a symbiont bacterium, which is more distinguished in X. americanum group. The surveys showed that Candidatus Xiphinematobacter isolated from Xiphinema species20. The symbiotic bacteria are passed down from the larvae to the female Xiphinema nematodes. These bacteria play a crucial role in inducing thelytokous parthenogenesis, a type of asexual reproduction, in the host nematodes. This unique process enables the nematodes to reproduce without the need for fertilization, producing only female offspring21. Another bacterium, namely Candidatus Xiphinematincola pachtaicus was also introduced, isolated from X. americanum group22.

In South Africa, the members of Xiphinema have already been studied by various scientists2324. However, bacteria associated with Xiphinema have not yet been studied in South Africa. This is the first report of Xiphinema being found in water berry in South Africa. During a soil nematode project in Limpopo Province, South Africa, multiple individuals of the same species of Xiphinema were recovered from water berry soil in March 2023. Microbiome investigations are paramount in elucidating the indigenous bacteria associated with nematodes and their consequential impact on nematode physiology. Notably, empirical evidence has elucidated the role of symbiotic bacteria in the synthesis of crucial vitamins and amino acids, thereby exerting a discernible influence on nematode development and behavioral patterns25,26,2728. Therefore, we encourage the study of the microbiome and molecular identification of the nematode associated with water berry. The aims of the study were: (1) to conduct molecular identification of Xiphinema using 18S and 28S rDNA markers, (2) to study the associated bacteria using metagenomic analysis of 16S rDNA, and (3) to study the relationship between Xiphinema and selected soil parameters.

Materials and methods

Xiphinema sampling and processing

The Xiphinema specimens were acquired from soil samples randomly collected from a water berry field in Sovenga Hills, Limpopo Province, South Africa (with GPS coordinates: 23°53’06.4"S 29°44’17.0"E) as depicted in Fig. 1. The map was made using paint.net version 5.0.13, which is freely available. The base map was built using the freely available 4.01 Biomes in Africa Map. Four soil cores, each weighing approximately 500 g, were collected at intervals of about 2 m from each other at a depth of 30 cm. Nematodes were extracted from the soil samples using the modified tray technique29. For this, 200 g of soil was separately transferred into plastic baskets, and nematodes were recovered after 72 h of incubation. The samples underwent analysis using a Zeiss light microscope at the Aquaculture Research Unit. The Xiphinema specimens were selectively isolated based on their morphological characteristics. One specimen per sample was subjected to molecular analysis, and three specimens were used for microbiome analysis. The remaining specimens were utilized for detailed morphological characterization and microscopic observations. In order to preserve the Xiphinema specimens30, they were first immersed in hot 4% formaldehyde and then transferred to glycerin. Permanent glass slides were used to mount them for observation using a VWR light microscope (Italy) with a camera. Photos of the slides were taken and edited using Adobe® Photoshop® CS.

Xiphinema molecular identification

DNA extraction, PCR, and phylogenetic analysis

In this study, DNA was extracted using the Chelex method. A single Xiphinema specimen was placed in a tube with water, crushed, and mixed. Chelex and proteinase K were added, and the mixture was heated up for 2 h at 56 °C and then centrifuged. The resulting liquid was stored at -20 °C for further analyses31,32.

We amplified the 18S (primers: 988F; 1912R)33 and 28S (primers: D2A; D3B)34 ribosomal RNA genes using PCR32 and subsequently sequenced the PCR products at Inqaba Biotec (Pretoria, South Africa). The resulting sequences were stored in the NCBI database under the accession numbers: PP833542 (18S rDNA) and PP834449 (28S rDNA). Next, the phylogenetic trees were elaborated using the obtained 18S and 28S rRNA gene sequences with the Bayesian inference, implemented in Mr Bayes version 3.2.7 35. We used GTR model using JModelTest 2 36. The analysis was run for 106 generations. The phylogenetic trees were edited using Figtree (version 1.4.4) developed by Rambaut37.

Xiphinema bacterial analysis

In order to characterize the bacteria associated with Xiphinema nematodes, a series of steps were followed. First, the nematodes were disinfected with a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for three minutes, following the method recommended by Esser38. Then for 3 min, transferred to Salvon antiseptic liquid (Johnson & Johnson Pty, South Africa) to remove the germs as possible from the body surface. Then, the specimens of Xiphinema were thoroughly washed with distilled water. First, the total DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA Miniprep Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the DNA were then checked using agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 C spectrophotometer. Next, to amplify the V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene sequences, universal bacteria primers 341f (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 785r (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′)39 were used. The DNA amplification process involved a series of temperature changes. Afterward, the resulting DNA bands were examined using a special dye and a UV light. Following this, the DNA was purified and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform, producing 20 megabytes of data for each sample. This data was deposited in the NCBI database under specific biosample accession numbers SAMN41706266, SAMN41706267, SAMN41706268, SAMN41706269.

Bioinformatics of Xiphinema bacteria

The analysis of the microbiome bioinformatics was performed using the Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME 2 2017.4) software40,41,42. Paired-end reads and a manifest file were imported into QIIME2 to create a compressed paired-end-demux.qza file. The reads underwent pre-processing and filtering using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) plugin43, with raw sequence data being quality filtered through q2-dada2. The input read pairs for Xiphinema isolate N1, isolate N2, isolate N3, and isolate N4 were 219,902, 173,744, 149,516, and 222,651, respectively. Subsequently, the sequences were filtered, resulting in 113,011, 77,471, 76,279, and 111,940 read pairs for Xiphinema isolate N1, isolate N2, isolate N3, and isolate N4 respectively. The amplicon sequences variants (ASVs) were aligned using MAFFT44 through q2-alignment. Taxonomies were assigned to the Denoised Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) using the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin. The phylogeny was constructed using fasttree245 through q2-phylogeny. The samples were then rarefied to 8060 sequences per sample46 (subsampled without replacement), and alpha-diversity metrics (observed features) and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity were calculated using q2-diversity47. Taxonomy assignment to ASVs was carried out using a q2-feature-classifier and the Greengenes sequence database404148.

Soil physicochemical analysis

The Aquaculture Research Unit laboratory analyzed several soil factors, including nitrate and phosphate. The assessment utilized a Hach spectrophotometric device (USA) following the company’s prescribed protocol. Additionally, the pH was measured using the Thermo Scientific Orion-3-Star (USA). The total phosphate (P) and nitrate analysis was carried out following the company protocol49 and utilizing the Cadmium reduction method. Test number 1.14848.0001 was used to measure total phosphate (P), while nitrate analysis was performed using the Cadmium reduction method no. 8171 of DOC316.53.01069 49. Spectrophotometry was the method of choice for analyzing soil properties, and soil texture was studied in accordance with the technique of van Capelle et al.50.

Relationship of Xiphinema and soil physicochemical variables

The study used Pearson correlation in XLSTAT51 to investigate the relationship between the abundance of Xiphinema and soil physicochemical properties. Additionally, a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted as studied by Renčo et al.52 to investigate the linking between soil properties (e.g., pH, nitrate, phosphate, soil texture) and nematode. The abundance of nematodes genera was used to conduct a PCA with XLSTAT51 to arrange the sites. In our analysis, we used additional variables to capture soil properties and understand how they relate to the abundances of Xiphinema. We assigned a score to each variable using principal components analysis. Subsequently, we utilized the scores from the first two components (PC1 and PC2) to create a visual two-dimensional plot based on the eigenvalues calculated by the XLSTAT software.

Result

Xiphinema diagnosis

Xiphinema specimens collected from water berry were identified as X. elongatum based on the morphological and molecular characters. The X. elongtaum was characterized by body length (2093 − 2666 µm; a = 55.1–63.5; b = 5.7–7.4; c = 27.1–33.5; c’ = 2.8–3.3; V = 39–46), odontostyle (100–102 μm), odontophore (59–62 μm), pharynx (341–382 μm), and tail length (75–89 μm) (Supplementary: Fig. S1; Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis of 18S rDNA of South African population of X. elongatum, grouped all X. elongatum with 1.00 posterior probability in non-americanum group clade (Supplementary: Fig. S2). In addition, the 28S rDNA marker also grouped X. elongatum with 1.00 posterior probability within non-americanum group of Xiphinema species (Supplementary: Fig. S3). Besides, 18S rDNA marker of South African X. elongatum showed 99% similarity with Brazilian (acc. nr: AY297824) population of X. elongatum. In addition, 28S rDNA marker showed 99% with South African (OK663118) and 95% with a Chinese (MT193453) population of X. elongatum.

Bacterial diversity associated with X. elongatum

After removing noise and aligning the forward and reverse reads, a total of 967 sequences, representing 280 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs), were compiled from four replicated nematode samples. Among these ASVs, the samples X. elongatum (N1), X. elongatum (N2), X. elongatum (N3), and X. elongatum (N4) contained 184, 97, 56, and 110 ASVs, respectively (Supplementary: Table S2). The rarefaction plot of alpha diversity suggests that the 3965 counts collected during the sampling process are sufficient to capture the diversity of all the samples studied. This conclusion is supported by Figure S4 (Supplementary), which visually represents the data.

The diversity of bacterial communities, as measured by Shannon entropy, was highest in X. elongatum N1 (3.2), moderate in X. elongatum N2 (2.4), and lowest in X. elongatum N4 (0.5), as shown in Fig. S5 (Supplementary). After analyzing the taxonomic classification of the representative sequences, we found that certain types of bacteria are commonly associated with X. elongatum nematodes. Specifically, the most abundant bacteria belong to the phyla Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria, as indicated in Fig. S6 (Supplementary).

In this study, we observed the presence of different bacterial classes and their respective orders and families. The bacterial classes (Supplementary: Fig. S7) identified included Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Spartobacteria, Clostridia, and Actinobacteria. Further analysis revealed the presence of specific orders (Supplementary: Fig. S8) such as Sphingomonadales, Chthoniobacterales, Clostridiales, Aeromonadales, and Actinomycetales, each represented by families such as Sphingomonadaceae, Chthoniobacteraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Propionibacteriaceae, and Succinivibrionacae (Supplementary: Fig. S9).

The prevailing genera found in the study were Sphingomonas, Candidatus Xiphinematobacter, and Succinivibrio. Additionally, Faecalibacterium and Propionibacterium were also prevalent based on the data from Figure S10 (Supplementary). Among the Xiphinema, the most prevalent species identified were Sphingomonas sp., Candidatus Xiphinematobacter sp., Succinivibrio sp., Propionibacterium acnes, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii across all samples (Fig. 2). In addition, we observed several species in association with all four isolates. Notably, Clavibacter (previously known as Corynebacterium sp.) was present in all four isolates of X. elongatum. It is worth mentioning that Clavibacter is recognized as a plant pathogen.

Relationship between soil variables and X. elongatum

The findings indicated a significant negative correlation (p < 0.05) between the number of X. elongatum and soil clay percentage (r = -0.52), as well as soil pH (r = -0.98). Conversely, the number of X. elongatum showed a positive correlation with soil silt content (r = 0.95), soil sand content (r = 0.88), and soil phosphate (r = 0.94). Additionally, there was a positive correlation between the number of X. elongatum and soil nitrate content (r = 0.56) (Fig. 3).

The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) indicated that the distribution of X. elongatum in the soil was largely influenced by two factors, PC1 (74.4%) and PC2 (17.3%), explaining a total of 91.7% of the variation. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that soil physicochemical properties played a significant role in shaping the distribution of X. elongatum in the soil, as depicted in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Principal component analysis between Xiphinema elongatum nematodes collected from water berry and selected soil parameters.

Principal component analysis between Xiphinema elongatum nematodes collected from water berry and selected soil parameters.

We found that X. elongatum specimens were more prevalent in soils with higher sand percentage, indicating that in soils with more sand, silt, and phosphate, there was a higher number of X. elongatum. Conversely, a high clay percentage of the soil is associated with a lower number of X. elongatum. Furthermore, soil pH (r = -0.98) had a significant negative correlation with the number of X. elongatum in the water berry soil of Sovenga Hills.

Discussion

The species of Xiphinema is known as dagger nematodes, which are distributed worldwide7. Dagger nematodes are a significant type of plant-parasitic nematodes, as Archidona-Yuste et al.53 noted. Interestingly, X. brevicolle has been identified as a cause of human infection in France, as Haouchine et al.54 reported.

The nematode species belonging to the Xiphinema are known for their behavior as migratory ectoparasites, targeting the roots of a diverse range of plants. They infest host plants by feeding directly on the root cells, while also transmitting harmful plant nepoviruses. This genus has been conventionally and historically categorized into the americanum-group and the non-americanum-group7. Therefore, Xiphinema species play a critical role in creating environmental, agricultural, and health-related challenges.

The morphometric data of the described South African specimens from Limpopo Province perfectly match the populations of X. elongatum as recorded by Luc and Southey55. Additionally, they are similar to the previously recorded populations from South Africa23, Botswana56, Guiana and Martinique57, China58, Taiwan59, and Ethiopia60.

The long-term co-evolution between the bacteria and the Xiphinema americanum group was discussed by Coomans61 and Vandekerckhove et al.20. The results also showed that Candidatus isolated from X. americanum and X. rivesi were 96% similar in the 16 S rRNA gene, indicating the specific endosymbiotic bacteria of Xiphinema species20. Additionally, high genetic diversity was observed among the Candidatus Xiphinematobacter isolated from the Xiphinema americanum group62–64.

Several studies have reported bacteria associated with the Xiphinema americanum group7,22,65,66. The bacterium Candidatus Xiphinematobacter has been isolated from X. californicum, X. rivesi, X. brevicolle, X. americanum, and unidentified Xiphinema from Italy, Russia, and the USA7. It has also been found in X. pachtaicum from Iran65 and in X. diffusum and X. smile from Syria66. In addition, Candidatus Xiphinematincola pachtaicus was recovered and described from the ovary of X. pachtaicum22.

The bacteria previously isolated were exclusively from the Xiphinema americanum group. However, the association of bacteria with the non-americanum group of Xiphinema has not been investigated. The current study presents findings on the bacterial association with X. elongatum, a species of Xiphinema that does belong to the non-americanum group. This is significant due to the endosymbiotic relationship between bacteria and Xiphinema groups, including the americanum and non-americanum species.

The fact is that endosymbiont bacteria play an essential role in the biology and physiology of Xiphinema species67. The researchers showed that Xiphinematobacter’s fundamental role may be to initiate thelytokous parthenogenesis in the nematode8,68,69. However, this role of association might not apply to all americanum groups of Xiphinema67. In addition, Xiphinematobacter might have a crucial role in the biosynthesis of amino acids and vitamins67. The study findings indicated that the X. americanum group, which relies on phloem, exhibits a mutualistic relationship with Xiphinematobacter. Conversely, Xiphinema species that do not depend on phloem were found to lack a mutualistic relationship with these bacteria. This suggests a correlation between the phloem dependency of Xiphinema species and their interaction with Xiphinematobacter67, providing valuable insights into the ecological dynamics of these organisms. It should be noted that Xiphinematobacter was only recovered from X. elongatum in one sample in the present study. This occurrence might be attributed to the low density of bacteria inside the body of Xiphinema, as indicated by Palomares-Rius et al.22. This low density could be a reason for the bacteria being undetectable by PCR, as also indicated by Palomares-Rius et al.70. Additionally, a study suggested that the absence of symbiont bacteria could be a result of evolutionary processes, particularly for the Candidatus Xiphinematobacter70.

Xiphinema elongatum, a species of nematode, was discovered in significant numbers in the rhizosphere of a water berry tree in the Sovenga Hills of Limpopo Province. This finding raises questions about the potential impact of bacterial symbionts on the nematode’s influence on tree health. It is believed that the bacterial association may play a role in facilitating parthenogenesis and providing essential nutrients for the nematode’s survival in the soil environment. This discovery suggests that alternative methods, such as non-nematicidal chemicals, could be explored for nematode control. Additionally, there is the potential for targeting the bacteria associated with the nematode to impede the development of X. elongatum.

In the present research, our objective was to analyze the bacteria residing within the body of Xiphinema comprehensively. To achieve this, we implemented rigorous methods to eliminate surface bacteria, allowing us to focus exclusively on the bacteria present internally. Furthermore, we suggest a detailed investigation into the specific localization of bacteria within different anatomical regions of Xiphinema, including the intestine and ovary, for future research. This will help to understand the potential role of Xiphinematobacter in the developmental processes of Xiphinema. Additionally, by delving into the intricate relationship between these organisms, a deeper understanding of the symbiotic interactions within their ecosystem is achievable.

Sphingomonas species, which belong to the α-Proteobacteria class, are frequently encountered in the rhizosphere (the soil environment influenced by plant roots) and phyllosphere (the aboveground parts of plants) of various plant species71. During crop rotation, the population of specific bacterial groups, such as Sphingomonas, may fluctuate based on factors such as plowing practices, the introduction of cover crops, and the application of nitrogen fertilization72,73. This species of bacteria was found in association with all Xiphinema samples, implying the presence of Sphingomonas in the rhizosphere of the water berry. The previous studies showed that Sphingomonas was in association with C. elegans74,75, and A. complexus76. This genus of bacteria has been isolated from various habitats77. Sphingomonas is shown to have capabilities, such as excluding heavy metals and preventing their binding by related proteins in the host77, thriving in high salinity77, degradation of organic chemical compounds (e.g., bisphenol)78, and indole acetic acid, gibberellins, sphingan79. However, their role in nematodes, as endosymbiont bacterium, has not been studied yet.

In addition, the majority of Sphingomonas species play a crucial role in supporting plant health and growth. They achieve this by effectively suppressing the proliferation of plant pathogens, encouraging plant development, and bolstering the plant’s ability to withstand environmental stresses80.

Besides the endosymbiont and beneficial bacteria associated with water berry, some plant pathogenic bacteria, such as Clavibacter, were found. Clavibacter is an economically important species of bacteria that affects the yield of various crops. Some species of Clavibacter, such as C. michiganense, cause severe canker disease in tomatoes, resulting in yield losses of up to 84% in commercial tomato fields73,81. Therefore, this bacterium should be seriously attended to avoid severe damage to water berry.

In addition, two human health-related bacteria, including Succinivibrio sp., Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Propionibacterium acnes were found in association with Xiphinema specimens. The bacterium Succinivibrio is found in colon and HIV+ human samples82. Another bacterial species, F. prausnitzii, is the most abundant in the gut of humans and a health promoter of the gut83. Propionibacterium acnes is known as skin bacterium, which is a Gram-positive anaerobic but aerotolerant bacterium exists in the various part of the skin of the human84.

These three bacteria were found in Xiphinema samples because the trees were irrigated with sewage water. Furthermore, Succinivibrio is an indicator of HIV + in the Limpopo Province, as it is in the high range noted by Lebina et al.85. Addressing the bacteria mentioned above with the utmost seriousness and adhering to standard hygiene protocols to prevent potential human health disorders is crucial.

Conclusion

The results strongly indicated a remarkably high diversity of bacteria that are closely associated with X. elongatum. This is the first report on non-americanum species of Xiphinema with an endosymbiont bacterium, Candidatus Xiphinematobacter. The other bacteria associated with X. elongatum have not yet been studied as symbionts. Moreover, despite disinfection and sterilization of the body surface, some bacteria may still adhere to the cuticle surface of X. elongatum. It should be noted that the odontostylet aperture of X. elongatum is not wide enough to capture and transport bacteria from the environment into the body.

Candidatus Xiphinematobacter plays a crucial role in developmental processes and life strategies of Xiphinema. Candidatus Xiphinematobacter is transmitted vertically in Xiphinema through maternal progeny within the ovary. However, the vulva or anus may serve as potential entry points for bacteria. The endosymbiont bacteria have the ability to induce thelytokous parthenogenesis. This method of reproduction is particularly significant in the absence of males, as it allows the population to persist and thrive without the need for fertilization. Furthermore, future studies will enhance our understanding of the interaction between X. elongatum and its symbiotic bacterium, Candidatus Xiphinematobacter.
