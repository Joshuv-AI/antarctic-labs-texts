Lasting Effects of Different Scaled Mass Mortality Events on Soil Microbial Communities
Authors: Abby K. Jones, Heather R. Jordan, Carter L. Wolff, Marcus A. Lashley, Brandon T. Barton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Death is a natural process present in all ecosystems; however, mass mortality events are instances of larger than average numbers of animals dying in a relatively short period of time. These events are increasing in frequency and magnitude, and the effects of mass mortalities — especially their long-term effects — are understudied. To better understand the long-term effects of mass mortalities in terrestrial ecosystems, we conducted experimental mass mortality events to determine if key ecosystem properties remained affected after 4 years. The experiment crossed three types of input treatments (control, carrion, and nutrient additive) with scavenger access (open plots versus fenced plots). To evaluate how increasing carrion biomass affected the ecosystem, sites were randomly assigned biomass (25, 59, 182, 363, 726 kg total (20m2 plots)). Biomasses consisted of feral swine carcasses or the equivalent amount of N, phosphorus, and K nutrients. After 4 years, we found that while soil N did not differ among treatments, soil K and Ca significantly increased with biomass. Microbial communities significantly differed at the 182 kg biomass treatments compared to others and indicated significant effects between carrion and nutrient additive treatments. These results demonstrate that large die-offs, such as mass mortality events, can have long-lasting effects on soil composition through increased soil nutrients and alter soil microbial community (i.e., reduced Bacilliaceae, etc.). These long-lasting impacts can permanently alter the soil community, which can lead to cascading bottom-up effects that can alter the entire ecosystem structure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02625-x.

Introduction

Death is a natural and important part of ecosystems. Single organisms expire and provide nutrients for detritivores and scavengers, allowing the carrion to re-enter the nutrient cycle [1, 2]. The processes involved with the death and decomposition of a single organism are relatively well understood. Though specific details can vary depending on the animal species and local conditions, data have outlined distinct stages of decay, key organisms involved, and the environmental factors influencing the process, including temperature and access to scavengers [1–5]. However, there are also instances of mass death that can introduce extreme amounts of nutrients and carrion into the ecosystem [6–8]. A mass mortality event (MMEs) is defined as a sudden and significant die-off of a larger than average number of organisms (i.e., significantly higher than background mortality levels) within an area in a relatively short period of time, compared to typical background population decline [9, 10]. Although MMEs are increasing in frequency and magnitude [9], limited studies have investigated their ecological effects in terrestrial ecosystems [11].

Emerging literature on MMEs suggests they can have important effects differing from effects caused by the death of a single animal [11–13]. Large amounts of carrion may assemble novel communities of vertebrate and invertebrate scavengers as competition for abundant resources is low (e.g., [13, 14]). When carcasses are more abundant, vertebrate scavengers may become satiated, leaving more carrion to decompose on the landscape and leaving more carrion for insects and microbial decomposition [11]. In the most extreme cases, MMEs may saturate the invertebrate community [11]. Carrion not consumed by animals may then be decomposed entirely by microbes or broken down by exposure to the elements [11].

Naturally occurring MMEs are difficult to study as they are unpredictable. Even if an MME is discovered, research may not begin until a few days after the occurrence, making it difficult to assess what changes have occurred in the environment, let alone differences between the community before and after the event. Therefore, mathematical models have been used in an attempt to assess the impact of species loss on the environment and other species. Mathematical frameworks suggest that a 90% loss of any given species during an MME can recover to its original numbers, though larger MMEs may take longer to recover from [15]. The loss of one species, whether in majority or entirety, could then have cascading effects on other species, such as those that predated on the lost species or those that were predated upon. However, the length of recovery time (i.e., time to reapproach a steady state) and how the community structure and abundance changes is still largely undetermined. While a few MME studies have been published recently, these experiments have been relatively short in duration (e.g., less than 1 year) and therefore only provide insight into the short-term effects of MMEs on different population abundance and dynamic shifts [12,13,16,17 but see [14] for examples of longer studies]. It remains unclear if and when ecosystems will recover from these large disturbance events.

Carcass decomposition is variable over different times and conditions, with it being unknown how the speed of decomposition may vary with single carcasses and MMEs. While carcasses can be consumed quickly, a large amount of nutrients, microorganisms, and carrion fluids will be deposited into the soil, where effects can persist for several years and could increase over time [18]. For example, previous research has shown that nutrient additions of up to 15% more N and 29% more phosphorus from just a single carcass can be detected in the soil for longer than 3 years [20]. Inorganic N and phosphorus can be detected in soil up to 3 years later from larger carrion [20], with bones leaching phosphorus for up to 7 years in the case of MMEs [8]. Leaf N levels from plants feeding on carrion N (within 100 cm of carrion) may be elevated for 2 to 3 years after a single carcass death [21]. Individual carcasses can create increased bacterial biomass even after active scavenging for up to 40 months [21], and new microbes are added to the soil as they leach from carrion [1, 2, 22]. Important microbial decomposer groups are always present at low levels in the soil; however, carrion can introduce new key decomposers and bacteria necessary for N fixation [22]. Further, bacterial phospholipids fatty acids (PFLAs) have been found in higher abundances at carrion sites (30–50%) up to 16 months postmortem [21].

We hypothesized that MMEs can have long-term effects on soil chemistry and microbial communities. Specifically, we predicted that the magnitude of the effect will increase with carrion biomass, where areas impacted by the MME will exhibit significantly higher levels of soil nutrients, particularly N and phosphorus, due to the decomposition of large quantities of organic matter, also persistently altering microbial community composition and potentially long-term changes in ecosystem function compared to unaffected areas. To test this hypothesis, we capitalized on an MME experiment initiated in 2016 [11–13] to investigate the effects of MMEs 4 years after initial placement. Input treatments included nutrient additive plots alongside control (no carrion) and MME biomass plots. Also, at each carrion biomass site, scavenger access and an exclusion treatment to prevent vertebrate scavengers from accessing carrion were included to compare those with the effects of microbial decomposition alone and to measure the specific nutrient dynamics directly from the carrion itself. Nutrient additive plots were included to evaluate the effects of nutrient additions without carrion that included exogenous input of N, phosphorus, and K in percentages previously estimated for Sus scrofa carrion [1, 2]. We predicted that nutrient additive plots would show similar soil nutrient profiles as MMEs, but that scavenger activity would decrease nutrient concentrations from MMEs until a saturation effect was reached. We also predicted that microbial communities would show significantly higher diversity in MMEs compared to other treatments due to the contribution of microbial diversity from carcasses and scavengers.

Methodology

Experimental Design

This experiment began on June 5th, 2016, in the John Starr Forest of Mississippi, owned by Mississippi State University (MSU). Sites were wooded areas of predominantly loblolly pine, with a high percentage of tree cover, and an average daily temperature of 30 °C for the duration of the study (year one). The study consisted of five site locations, each at least 1 km apart from other sites (Fig. 1). The sites were located at the following coordinates: 25 kg: 33° 22′ 44″N 88° 51′ 41″W, 59 kg: 33°20 ′17″ N 88° 55′ 45″W, 182 kg: 33° 21′ 13″N 88° 55′ 13″W, 363 kg: 33° 16′ 38″N 88° 53′ 58″W, and 725 kg: 33° 20′ 35″N 88° 54′ 38″W. Each site contained six treatment plots randomized in a 2 × 3 factorial design that crosses scavenger access (open or fenced to exclude scavengers) with input treatment (feral swine carrion, commercially available nutrient additions, or control with no input) (Fig. 1). Fencing was constructed with rolled wire fencing (2.0 inch × 4.0 inch mesh) surrounding an area of approximately 20 m2, and each plot was further covered with a black plastic mesh on top. Fenced and unfenced replicates of initial decomposition sites of increasing biomass were constructed at least 100 m apart. Feral swine carrion was donated by local landowners conducting feral swine eradication campaigns, and no culling occurred for the purposes of this experiment. Each of the five sites was randomly assigned one of five different biomasses (biomass treatment) of feral swine (Sus scrofa) carrion (25 kg, 59 kg, 182 kg, 363 kg, and 726 kg total per 20 m2 plot). Biomass sizes were chosen to use all the carrion received and in a wide gradient to have equally balanced biomass treatments. Plots assigned to the carrion treatment received swine carcasses that combined to weigh the assigned amount, where the lowest weight represented a single mortality event (individual carcass), with increasing carrion biomass at plots until carrion biomass was comparable to an MME event [23]. Plots assigned to nutrient additive treatments received pelletized Nitrogen (N), potassium (K), and Calcium (Ca) in amounts equivalent to what is expected to be contained within swine carcasses of that biomass, as detailed in [1, 2]. Control treatments received no input. Further details of the study design can be found in [12, 13]. All samples with data described in this manuscript were collected in summer 2020. Soil Nutrients

We collected samples from each plot using a generic soil corer, collecting soil along the northeast, northwest, southeast, and southwest axes of each plot. We obtained enough soil (digging down to ~ 5 to 10 cm) to fill standard soil analysis sample boxes (10 cm × 7.5 cm × 6 cm). Samples were delivered to the MSU Extension Services, who performed C/N analysis with an Elementar Vario Max Cube using official AOAC methods [24]. Dry ashing samples with ICP Spectrophotometer analysis was used to evaluate nutrients including K, phosphorus, magnesium, zinc, and Ca [25]. N (total N), K, and Ca were further assessed as these elements are predominant during short-and long-term carrion decomposition [1, 2, 26].

Soil Microbial Samples

For soil microbial samples, we used a 1.5 mL metal scoop to take top soil (~ 3 to 5 cm deep) from three locations within each plot: near the edge of the plot, the center, and halfway in between. Each was collected at a random subcardinal transect. Soil from these three scoops was homogenized together in a plastic bag. We took 1.5 mL of the homogenized soil and placed it into a pre-labeled whirl pak. We then filled the whirl pak with enough 95% ethanol to cover the sample. Samples were stored in this manner (at room temperature) until DNA extraction.

DNA was extracted using the Zymobiomics DNA Miniprep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified with Qubit 2.0. PCR was conducted using Earth Microbiome Project protocols in a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler. Standard primers (10 mM) of 16sV4 FWD: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA; REV: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT were used. Once amplification was verified, DNA samples were sent to Michigan State University Genomics Core for 16 s targeted metagenomic sequencing. Sequences were analyzed using CLC Genomics Workbench software [27]. All sequences returned with enough reads, with the exception of one sample collected from the 59 kg site (fenced MME plot) and 5 out of 6 collections from the 25 kg biomass site.

Statistical Analyses

Most statistical analyses were performed in [28], and distributions were chosen based on several statistical dispersion tests and [29]. Statistical analyses were performed in CLC Genomics Workbench for microbial results [27]. Statistical analyses for microbial results included NMDS ordination of Bray–Curtis dissimilarities to visualize differences in and among communities. Diversity indices included Shannon, Simpson, and Chao-1 bias-corrected with Mann–Whitney U tests. PermANOVA (Permutational multivariate analysis of variance) evaluates differences within treatment groups and statistically supports NMDS graphs. For nutritional statistics, we tested for normality with Shapiro–Wilk testing. All were non-normal and thus generalized linear modeling (Treatment * Biomass (site) * Fencing; Chi-square testing) was used for testing (gaussian distribution for N, gamma distribution for K and Ca). Emmeans post-hoc testing (Tukey) further evaluated pairwise comparisons across significant factors.

Soil Microbes

Top (20) taxa were discerned from relative abundance tables in individual treatment plots (Fig. 3A and B). Abundances were also viewed amongst biomass and treatment variables (Fig. 3A and B). Lists of Family relative abundance and significantly different taxa across biomass and treatment are included in Supplementary Table 5 and 6, respectively. Bacillaceae, Paenibacillaceae, and Planococcaceae dominated across all biomasses, but were overall highest in the additive or control treatments. Clostridiaceae 1 and Mitochondria, on the other hand, dominated MME treatments and were virtually absent in the additive or control treatments. Lachnospiraceae appeared to have a dose effect, being the highest in the 726 kg treatment, and decreasing with decreasing biomass and from MME to the additive treatment and then the control. Ruminococcaceae was also found highest in the MME experiments, decreasing from the additive treatment to the control. Flavobacteriaceae was found in the additive and control treatments, whereas it was virtually absent in MME treatments, with the highest abundance present at 59 kg treatments. Enterobacteriaceae were also found in the lowest amounts in MME treatments, followed by the control, with the highest abundance at nutrient additive treatments and were found almost entirely at 363 kg treatments. Xanthobacteraceae were found in low amounts in all input treatments, though the vast majority of the abundance was detected at 182 kg treatments. Actinomycetaceae was most dominant among the additive treatments, though it was detectable in the 182 kg and 726 kg treatments. Discussion

Soil Nutrients

Overall, nutrients analyzed were in higher concentrations at sites containing higher biomass of carrion or nutrient addition as nutrients were being added to treatments through either leaching carrion or direct nutrient placement. This is in agreement with other studies finding that carrion leakage increases concentrations of a number of essential elements, including those measured in our study, in soil, plants, or both, producing nutrient hotspots [18, 26]. Calcium was significantly higher in nutrient additive treatments compared to carrion and control plots. Carrion removal by vertebrate scavengers may have reduced or scattered bones present at the carrion plots, reducing the amount of Ca deposited into the soil, whereas Ca from the nutrient additive treatments was immediately absorbed into the soil [8]. Biomass was a significant predictor of soil Ca and a marginally significant predictor for K, but not for N as soil samples were taken years after carcass decomposition (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Studies have shown that carrion from species with high biomass creates large cadaver decomposition islands with substantial nutrient influx into soils when compared to smaller carrion [30]. In addition, smaller animal carcasses may be more quickly removed by scavengers and may reduce the amount of nutrients returned to soil. Scavenger exclusion treatments allowed for comparisons of scavenger and non-scavenger plots to determine the overall potential effects of scavengers as well as providing insight into the effects of scavenger mass mortalities [12, 26]. Overall, there was little to no difference in scavenger access plots with respect to soil nutrient profiles and, subsequently, microbial community structure (though a more comprehensive study should address this).

Some studies have shown that N is generally lost faster into the environment, including through microbes, soil and litter arthropods, and scavenger responses, compared to other nutrients [19, 22], and it also has a generally faster turnover [31]. Carcass biomass can affect N levels and consumption [20], where soil N pulses of larger carcasses take up to 3 years to stabilize, with long-term effects on the plant community [20]. Increased N uptake in plants is also present in carrion events [32]. Plant uptake and the duration of our experiment (4 years) may be the reason as to why N is no longer significantly affected.

However, K can leach from carrion before N, while skeletal components such as Ca are lost at slower rates [19]. Previous work has shown soil K to be largely unaffected by carrion a year after decomposition [20]. Even though K is lost from carrion at a faster rate, the loss does not equate to the rate of K loss in the environment. Leaching of K also differs for different carrion species [20]. Other studies found that there was an effect on K up to a year or two later [33, 34], even up to 3 to 5 years after some instances of carrion, though why remains unclear [35]. MMEs and nutrient additive plots did not differ because both included the addition of K to the soil. K levels are affected by the addition of the nutrient (either directly or through carrion) to the soil and did not return to base level. While partly affected by the nutrient addition/MME, K may also remain in high concentrations due to environmental factors. Excessive K into plant systems through KCl has toxic effects on plant growth [36]. However, plants also uptake K in excess as a surplus (i.e., luxury consumption) [36]. Both of these findings correlate with our results. Increased K was located at larger carrion treatments, showing that whether or not plants may have taken up excess K, K levels would remain higher due to excessive, potentially toxic amounts of K.

Calcium was influenced by both biomass and input treatments. Specifically, Ca increased with carrion biomass, and significant differences were noticed between control and nutrient additive plots. Calcium is lost last in carrion [19] and remains elevated during dry stages [1, 2, 34]. Calcium concentration changes can be found 7 years after death [31]. With the slower loss of Ca and increased amount of skeletal remains, Ca would be in excess across MME sizes. The reasoning for significant Ca differences between control (no carrion) and nutrient additive groups is more complicated. It is logical that these two groups would differ significantly as excessive Ca is directly added to the nutrient additive treatments. Subsequently, it is also feasible that MMEs and nutrient additive treatments would not differ as a similar amount of Ca is added to the soil. However, control plots and MMEs should then also significantly differ from each other. While these input treatments trend towards significance (p = 0.0857), differences here are less noticeable than nutrient additive and control group comparisons. The Ca deposited into the soil from carrion may differ from the direct addition in nutrient additive treatments due to potential removal of carrion (invertebrate and vertebrate scavengers) and scattering of bone (vertebrate scavengers). Biomass may have affected Ca, but it is possible that scavenging from vertebrates and invertebrates may have led to a decreased effect of carrion [19].

Soil Microbes

Microbial Differences Across Biomass

Due to a lack of site replication, reported information on microbial differences across biomass are descriptive to point potential directions for future research to confirm the findings.

The 182 kg biomass MME showed significantly higher relative abundance of Acidobacteria (Subgroup 6, Supplemental Table 5, 6), which are present in pasture and forest soils and are positively associated with nutrient availability [37]. Future research should delve into this group of bacteria to determine if, in the case of MMEs, Acidobacteria decrease due to overwhelming nutrient input or increase to compensate for the increase in soil nutrients from carrion.

In the 363 kg treatments, several taxa were associated with decomposition, plant degradation, N fixing, and animals. Anaerosalibacter was significantly abundant at the larger two MMEs. Anaerosalibacter are associated with carrion soils around 60 days of decomposition [38]. The presence of Anaerosalibacter during long-term decomposition (at large carrion sites) and remains should be further examined. Clostridiaceae 1 (N fixers found in decaying organic matter [39]), Ruminiclostridium 1 (plant decomposition), Micrococcaceae (plant decomposition) [40], and Klebsiella (N fixers for plant hosts) are all present in the highest abundance at 363 kg plots (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5, 6). At the 726 kg treatments, Clostridiaceae 1, Paenibacillus (both associated with animals), Anaerosalibacter (decomposition association), Bacillaceae, and Microbispora amethystogenes were found in high abundance.

Overall, taxa significantly associated with the largest biomass treatments may be due to the large quantity of nutrients and carrion fluids leaching into soil, allowing more bacteria associated with decomposition (such as Anaerosalibacter) to have remained in the soil for this many years. At the same time, there is also the presence of several bacterial groups associated with plant growth and N fixing as new plants emerge, having taken up some of the many nutrients resulting from the large carrion decomposition. However, further research is needed comparing bacterial community structures across replicated carrion biomass trials to determine which of these bacteria are strictly associated with carrion biomass and which are already present in certain soils.

Microbial Comparisons Across Treatments

Across treatments, carrion treatments had a larger relative abundance of species associated with plant decay (Microbispora amethystogenes), plant growth (Clostridiaceae 1), decomposition (Clostridiaceae 1, Clostridium sensu stricto 18, Caldicoprobacter), pathogens (Hathewaya), animals (Clostridium sensu stricto 7, Lachnospiraceae, Anaerostipes), and invertebrates (Clostridium sensu stricto 7) when compared to nutrient additive treatments (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 5) [41–43]. On the other hand, nutrient additive treatments had larger abundances of species normally found in soil (Brevibacterium, Actinomyces, Ochrobactrum, Lysinibacillus) and plants (Actinomyces, Aquibacillus) [44, 45].

Control sites showed a mixture of soil, plant, water, and animal microbes associated with plant growth (Enterobacter sp. (UCD-UG_FMILLET and Corynebacterium 1)), N fixation and mineralization (Acinetobacter), phosphorus and zinc solubilization and enzyme production (Corynebacterium 1), soil carbon (Lactobacillales), and animals [46, 47]. A number of significantly identified taxa known to be animal-associated were also measured. For instance, Providencia has been associated be prevalent in control treatments due to the allowance of general vertebrate and invertebrate organisms. Enterococcus also exists in several environments including animal and human microflora [49] and pig slurries [50]. But, studies have shown Enterococcus increased survival in lower incubation temperatures with more soil moisture [50] and may have subsequently died under carrion due to increased temperatures from decomposition. Sphingobacteriaceae are cosmopolitan organisms that have been isolated from soils while also being a potential agent of infection [51]. Interestingly, Sphingobacteriaceae are also common during summer season carrion decomposition even a year later [52]. Ulvibacter has key importance in manure composting due to its ability to degrade carbon sources [53]; therefore, it was not expected to be in higher relative abundance in control treatments, though it may be driven by soil chemistry or other parameters that need further exploration.

Study Limitations

The primary focus of this study was to analyze changes in soil nutrients as a result of carrion biomass and scavenging. We also measured significant differences in microbial relative abundances according to biomass and treatment, in an effort to measure how microbial communities change in diversity with respect to input from carrion (with or without scavenging), and whether this change in microbial community composition was also due to the enrichment of existing soil microbes from specific nutrients (i.e., K, Ca, N) either through the carrion or nutrient addition. While these data are valuable additions to the field of decomposition ecology, this study is not without limitations. For instance, our nutrient additive treatment only included three nutrients associated with S. scrofa carcasses, omitting a large number of macro and micronutrients as well as other complex ecological processes that may be occurring during carrion decomposition. We also acknowledge that nutritional content can vary even within this species, with different components of the body containing different nutrients in different quantities. Our fencing did not exclude smaller scavengers; therefore, effects from scavengers that could fit through the wire mesh were not measured. Additionally, our data focused on the most abundant microbial taxa, which leaves out analyses of rare species that could be completely unique to one or two treatments. These unique species could provide further interesting perspectives on the lasting effects of different-sized MMEs. Additionally, our research was unable to view the initial microbial samples from these plots before treatments. Therefore, we do not know what microbial taxa may continuously be present in these soils and if certain taxa are only present in certain biomass plots, as these were not replicated based on the previous experimental design. Finally, multiple locations of the different biomass treatments and lack of biomass replication reduced statistical power, leading to largely descriptive and observational data.

Conclusion

Despite some limitations, this research has provided meaningful data about persistent effects of MMEs. After 4 years, carrion treatments contained microbes associated with decomposition of carrion and plant decay as well as potential pathogens that may have arisen from carrion, with potential implications for carrion management practices. However, though we measured the presence of potential pathogens, a comprehensive study design will be needed to validate these findings. Nutrient additive treatments had many species associated with soil and plant growth resulting from the increased addition of nutrients even 4 years ago. Control treatments also showed diverse microbial communities, indicating initial soil diversity in these locations. It is the change in the soil microbial community structure, the specific species, that is altered in the longterm from MMEs. With the changes to both the soil nutrients and microbes, the soil at MMEs is fundamentally altered, which could lead to further cascading effects on plant life, subsequent herbivores, and so on. In the future, it would be of great importance to view changes in the soil communities over multiple years, sampling on a regular basis.
