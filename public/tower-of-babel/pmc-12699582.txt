Study protocol for a multi-site case study evaluation of a Canadian quality improvement collaborative to improve Baby-Friendly practices in community health services
Authors: Sarah E Turner, Jennifer E Enns, Emma Seager, Michelle LeDrew, Britney Benoit, Sonia Semenic, Erna Snelgrove-Clarke, Basirat Shittu, Diane Gordon Pappas, Marsha Campbell-Yeo, Nathan C Nickel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn Canada, many families want to breastfeed, but there are several common challenges they may encounter. Currently, 91% of Canadian families initiate breastfeeding after giving birth, yet only 38% of babies are breastfed exclusively to 6 months. In 1991, the Breastfeeding Committee for Canada (BCC) was established to implement the World Health Organization’s Ten-Step Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative, a series of evidence-based in-hospital practices to support families to breastfeed. Then, in recognition of the need to support breastfeeding beyond the hospital setting, the BCC expanded the Baby-Friendly Initiative (BFI) to apply the Ten Steps to both hospitals and community health settings. However, uptake of the BFI Ten Steps in community settings has been low and methodology on how to optimise implementation of the Ten Steps in community is not well developed. Therefore, the objective of this project is to develop and evaluate a quality improvement collaborative with 25 community health services from across Canada to learn how to best support the implementation of the BFI Ten Steps in community, with the ultimate goal of improving breastfeeding outcomes.Methods and analysisThis protocol describes the activities of the Community Baby-Friendly Initiative Collaborative (CBFI-C) and the methods used to evaluate its effectiveness. We will use the Institute for Healthcare Information Breakthrough Series (IHI-BTS) model, a proven quality improvement model that has been widely used in clinical settings, but is not yet widely used in community settings. The IHI-BTS combines three virtual learning sessions with action cycles that allow the participating sites time to test and track small practice changes. Sites will be asked to track care indicator and breastfeeding outcome data, engage in monthly webinars, receive coaching from trained mentors, participate in focus groups and participate in a final summative workshop. We will use a multi-site case study approach, combining aggregate care indicator data and qualitative data from webinars, focus groups and workshops to evaluate how the CBFI-C model supports community sites in the process of implementing the BFI Ten Steps.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval for this evaluation was obtained from the CHIPER Health Research Ethics Board (Number HS26947-H2025:157)). The results of the CBFI-C evaluation will be shared in a report, peer-reviewed publications and presentations to government and academic audiences. The findings will inform effective quality improvement strategies to enhance uptake of the BFI in community health settings.

Introduction

Breastfeeding and chestfeeding: a population-based strategy to promote health

Breastfeeding is the act of feeding an infant or young child human milk from the breast and chestfeeding is a gender-inclusive way to describe a parent feeding their infant or child human milk from their chest. The promotion of breastfeeding and chestfeeding are highly effective population-based strategies to promote the health of women and gender diverse people who give birth as well as their children.17 Despite the importance of inclusive language, much of the current research and guidelines use the word ‘breastfeeding’. To align with current guidelines,8 we will use the term ‘breastfeeding’ throughout the current paper, acknowledging that the infant feeding experiences of gender diverse people are important and understudied.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that infants are breastfed within an hour of birth, that parents exclusively breastfeed for the first 6 months of life, and that parents breastfeed with appropriate complementary foods for 2 years or beyond.8 The WHO and UNICEF have set a global target of 70% of infants breastfeeding exclusively from birth to 6 months by 2030.9 However, Canada has a long way to go to meet this target: only 38% of infants are breastfed exclusively for the first 6 months, and only 31% are breastfed beyond 1 year of age.10 11 The two most common reasons parents give for stopping breastfeeding before the 6-month mark are perceived insufficient milk supply and breastfeeding difficulties.12 These two reasons could be addressed, in many cases, with proper individual-level and health system support.

The Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative

To make progress towards the 70% exclusive breastfeeding target, the WHO and UNICEF developed the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative, a suite of 10 policies and practices for healthcare settings that support breastfeeding called The Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding.13 Implementation of the Ten Steps in hospitals has been consistently shown to increase breastfeeding initiation, exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months, and sustained breastfeeding.14 15 The Ten Steps have also been shown to reduce breastfeeding inequities among systemically underserved populations1620 and are therefore recognised as best practices in perinatal care.2123 Step 10, providing a transition to community breastfeeding support, is an essential component to sustaining breastfeeding over the longer term.15 The majority of a parent’s breastfeeding journey occurs outside of the hospital; therefore ensuring that evidence-based healthcare practices to support breastfeeding are implemented in community health services, as well as hospitals, has been recognised as a priority in several countries around the world, including in Canada.

The Breastfeeding Committee for Canada

The Breastfeeding Committee for Canada (BCC) is the not-for-profit organisation that oversees the implementation of the BFI Ten Steps in Canada. In recognition of the need to support breastfeeding beyond the hospital setting, the BCC developed an expanded set of Canadian Baby-Friendly Initiative (BFI) guidelines to apply BFI practices to both hospitals and community health services (Box 1).24

However, in Canada, fewer than 10% of hospitals,25 and only five community health services outside of Quebec,26 have fully implemented the BFI Ten Steps, spurring further investigation into best practices for implementation. In 2019, the BCC, Canadian researchers, quality improvement (QI) specialists and patient/clinician partners developed a National Baby-Friendly Quality Improvement Collaborative Project to evaluate the effectiveness of using the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s Breakthrough Series (IHI-BTS) Model27 to support 25 hospital teams to implement the BFI Ten Steps.28 At the end of the project, all hospitals experienced improvements in at least one measurable indicator, three hospitals had achieved BFI designation (ie, implemented all Ten Steps) and six hospitals had officially started the process for designation.28 In addition, hospital QI teams found the individual components of the IHI-BTS model valuable in contributing to their BFI designation progress.28 Now, this same team of Canadian researchers, QI specialists, patient/clinician partners and members of the BCC are extending the IHI-BTS Model to support the BFI Ten Steps in Canadian community health services.

The current project

In the current project, the BCC will develop and run a QI collaborative called the Community Baby-Friendly Initiative Collaborative (CBFI-C), which will include up to 25 community health services from across Canada. The intent behind the CBFI-C is to help sites work towards implementing the BFI Ten Steps using QI strategies to ensure that breastfeeding is supported by high-quality, equitable and evidence-based care. The research team will evaluate participating sites’ experiences with the CBFI-C project, measure care indicators and breastfeeding outcomes, and ultimately determine how well the IHI BTS model supports the implementation of BFI in community health services.

Methods and analysis

IHI-BTS Model for Quality Improvement

The CBFI-C will use the IHI-BTS Model27 to implement small changes that will help up to 25 community health services from across Canada move towards implementing the BFI Ten Steps. The IHI’s BTS Model is designed to bridge the gap between knowledge and practice. Although there is ample evidence in the literature about effective practices to support breastfeeding,161928 we still lack an understanding of how best to implement those practices in a cost-effective and sustainable way in community health services. Research over the last three decades has shown that the BTS Model is an effective QI approach to support healthcare transformation across a wide range of topics within the healthcare system.161928 29

The IHI-BTS model adopted for the current project involves eight key components: (1) topic selection, (2) faculty recruitment, (3) organisation enrolment, (4) learning sessions, (5) action periods, (6) plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles, (7) regular measurement and (8) summative publications.27 29

Population and recruitment

We aim to recruit up to two sites from each province and territory to participate in the CBFI-C. Participation will be limited to a total of 25 sites to ensure sufficient resources for each participating site. Sites will be recruited through the BCC Provincial/Territorial Committee, as well as existing networks and relationships with BFI champions across the country, and asked to complete an application form. To be included in the project, each site must (1) provide prenatal care or work in partnership with another agency/service that provides prenatal care, (2) provide postnatal infant feeding/breastfeeding support and education and (3) offer follow-up transitional care from hospital discharge to home, and up to the child’s 3rd birthday, where applicable. If greater than 25 sites apply, a BCC Screening Committee will review applications and select 25 sites to participate. Priority will be given to sites with self-identified population-level barriers to breastfeeding and lower rates of breastfeeding. The Screening Committee will ensure participating facilities represent a national distribution across Canada.

Each site will be required to identify a QI Site Team that will typically include (at minimum): an administrative leader, a health programme manager, a BFI/nurse lead, and a parent partner (ie, a birthing parent with recent and/or current experience receiving care from the participating site). These teams are responsible for defining, implementing and measuring the small QI changes in their respective sites.

Peer Coach Mentors

Participating centres will be matched with a Peer Coach Mentor from the BFI Peer Coach Mentor program, which was launched by the BCC in 2022.28 Mentors are healthcare staff or client partners who have participated in BFI designation in the past and are equipped to assist with implementing BFI policy and care practices, developing specific aim statements and action plans, using PDSA cycles and other QI strategies, and conducting data collection and analysis to track progress. There are four identified peer coach mentors who will participate in the CBFI-C.

Monthly virtual webinars

Monthly virtual webinars, including CBFI-C sites, Peer Coach Mentors and the BCC Project Director, will ask participating sites to share their progress by reporting on the challenges and benefits of implementing a recent QI change as well as influences of local contextual factors. The monthly webinars will serve as an opportunity to build relationships among the CBFI-C sites and foster an all-teach-all-learn culture.

Data collection at sites

Data collection is an integral component of all QI initiatives. Sites must track their progress to ensure that a change results in an improvement. The BCC will provide data collection training to participating CBFI-C Site Teams to guide QI efforts. Two types of routine data collection will be collected as part of the CBFI-C: chart audits and client surveys. The BCC is working with a consultant healthcare technology company to develop a learning management system where sites can store and track their QI data. This technology will generate aggregate site-level data, displayed as run charts (figure 1) and bar charts, to allow sites and the research team to easily track progress on key indicators over time and compare their progress to the collaborative average (see figure 3 for examples of key indicators).

Chart audits

Members of the CBFI-C Site Teams will conduct monthly chart audits on a sample of up to 30 charts per month and input data into the BCC’s learning management system for QI tracking. A monthly sample of 30 charts has been successfully used in previous BFI QI collaboratives as a valid sample size for the collection of data to guide QI efforts.28 30 The chart audits include reporting on 15 indicators pertaining to the care practices in alignment with the BFI Ten Steps.

Client surveys

Members of the CBFI-C Site Teams will administer a prenatal and postnatal client survey in which families are asked to report on the care they received. Sites will collect information from a sample of up to 30 prenatal and up to 30 postnatal birthing parents at baseline, mid-point and at the close of the project to track progress towards implementing the Ten Steps from the clients’ perspective. Data will be collected and housed on the learning management system and aggregated data will be shared with the research team.

Focus groups

We will hold up to three interim and up to three final virtual 90-minute focus groups with the CBFI-C Site Teams to understand their experiences and perceptions, including barriers and enablers, of using the BTS model to implement the Ten Steps, receiving support from Peer Coach Mentors, and participating in the CBFI-C project (for semi-structured interview guides, see online supplemental Figures S1 and S2). A separate set of 60-minute focus groups will be held at the end of the collaborative with the Peer Coach Mentors to understand their experiences with mentoring sites throughout the CBFI-C (for semi-structured interview guide, see online supplemental Figure S3).

Harvest sessions

Two virtual, summative workshops, named Harvest Sessions, will be held during the final months of the project with CBFI-C Site Team members. At the Harvest Sessions, we will use a series of worksheets and guided activities to examine how contextual factors influenced the project’s success and summarise lessons learnt for future collaboratives.

Patient and public involvement

Our team includes parent partners with lived/living experience of receiving infant feeding support in the Canadian healthcare system who engaged in the development of the grant to fund this research, and some continue to be involved. Each CBFI-C Team will engage at least one patient partner in their QI site team to guide the identification of small changes for improvement and help interpret the care indicator data.

Analysis plan

Objective 1: Determine if and how the IHI-BTS Model supports the implementation of the BFI Ten Steps in community health services

Measuring implementation

We will measure the implementation of BFI care practices using aggregated chart audit and client survey data collected by each QI Site Team. Figure 3 provides example care indicators that each site will track throughout the CBFI-C using chart audit or client survey data. Sites will report baseline data of how widely care indicators are practised as well as updated monthly chart audit data and bi-annual client survey data.

We will conduct collaborative-wide analyses to understand variations in implementation success across sites. Using chart audit data, we will calculate collaborative-wide and site-level, monthly rates for each care practice. We will use run charts and QI time series analyses to identify patterns in rates, monitor trends over time and identify patterns of change.31 We will also test for statistically significant changes over time with generalised estimating equations (GEE), to account for repeated measures, with time as the main explanatory variable and monthly rates for each indicator as the outcome. The distribution (eg, negative binomial distribution) will be selected based on model fit. This approach has been successfully used in a previous BFI QI collaborative.16

Using client survey data, we will calculate the percentage of clients that report experiencing each care indicator at each site for the three data collection time points: baseline, mid-point and at the close of the project. We will conduct descriptive analyses of the client report data to monitor changes in client-reported experiences over the course of the project.

Understanding how the BTS Model supports BFI implementation

The CBFI-C research team will conduct a qualitative descriptive analysis of QI Site Teams’ presentations during monthly webinars to understand whether and how participating in the CBFI-C supported their efforts towards implementing the Ten Steps. QI Site Teams will be trained on using a presentation template to ensure that the webinar includes key information, such as presenting results of the most recent PDSA cycle, recent wins and challenges and influences of local contextual factors. Webinars will be recorded and transcribed, then the transcripts and presentation slides will be thematically coded and analysed in NVivo using inductive qualitative descriptive content analysis.3234 The first three sets of transcripts and slides will be reviewed by at least two independent research team members with expertise in BFI and qualitative descriptive analysis to identify initial codes. A codebook will then be created and modified iteratively as each transcript and slide deck are coded. The remaining data will be coded by two research team members following the codebook. At least two independent research team members will iteratively review the codebook and generate a list of emerging themes and subthemes from the codes. Consensus meetings among the CBFI-C research team and QI Site Teams will be held to review coding, resolve discrepancies and refine emerging themes. To promote trustworthiness,3537 strategies to promote credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability in qualitative research will be utilised. These will include clearly documenting the clinical contexts, maintaining a clear audit trail of analysis processes and decisions, regular team debriefing and member checking to confirm data interpretation.

We will also analyse qualitative data from virtual focus groups with QI Site Team members, held part way through and at the end of the CBFI-C. The interim and final focus groups will gather data on QI Site Team members’ experiences and perceptions, including barriers and enablers, of using the BTS model to implement the BFI Ten Steps and participating in the CBFI-C. Focus groups will be recorded and transcribed, and the transcripts will be coded and analysed using qualitative descriptive content analysis consistent with the approach described above.

Integrating the data

We will integrate the qualitative and quantitative data at two virtual summative workshops, called Harvest Sessions. We will use interactive methods such as data walks, storyboards and worksheets to examine, merge and summarise the findings. We will use multi-site case study methods38 to explore variations in the experiences with how the BTS model may have shaped implementation of the Ten Steps.

Objective 2: Examine how contextual factors influence fidelity to the IHI BTS Model and implementation of the BFI Ten Steps

The data from the monthly webinar presentations and focus groups described for objective 1 will also be analysed to understand how contextual factors such as leadership and staff engagement, resources and competing demands influenced the sites’ ability to participate in all components of the BTS Model. We will apply the same qualitative content analysis to the data as described in objective 1 and focus on context-related themes for this objective. We will integrate the findings from the webinar presentations and focus groups using interactive consensus-building methods during the Harvest Session.

Objective 3: Understand participating sites’ experiences with a Peer Coach Mentor program designed to provide virtual technical support to sites implementing the BFI Ten Steps

The interim and final focus groups described in objective 1 will also include a set of questions about experiences working with Peer Coach Mentors. The questions will explore how working with a Peer Coach Mentor supports progress towards implementing BFI policy and care practices, and areas for improvement. An additional focus group, held at the end of the CBFI-C, will explore the Peer Coach Mentors’ experiences with mentoring sites throughout the CBFI-C. We will explore the barriers and enablers they experienced, strategies that worked in supporting QI Site Teams, and other lessons that can be applied to the Peer Coach Mentor Program moving forward.

All focus groups will be recorded and transcribed. Transcripts will be coded inductively using qualitative descriptive content analysis, consistent with the approach described in objective 1. Consensus building meetings among the CBFI-C research team and Peer Coach Mentor focus group participants will be held to review coding, resolve discrepancies and refine emerging themes.

Objective 4: Document whether participating sites experience improved breastfeeding outcomes.

We will measure the BCC-identified sentinel breastfeeding indicators required for BFI designation: (1) exclusive breastfeeding at entry to the community health service and (2) exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months. The BCC definition of exclusive breastfeeding includes feeding human milk directly at the breast, expressed breast milk or donor human milk and allows the infant to receive oral rehydration solution, syrups (vitamins, minerals, medicines) but does not allow the infant to receive anything else.39 We will calculate aggregated, collaborative-wide and site-level monthly rates for these two sentinel indicators using chart audit data collected by each QI Site Team. Similar to objective 1, we will use run charts and QI time series analyses to identify patterns in breastfeeding rates, monitor trends over time and identify patterns of change.31 We will also test whether the collaborative, and the individual sites, experienced statistically significant improvements in breastfeeding indicators over time using GEEs to account for the longitudinal nature of the repeated chart audit data. We will run a separate model for each indicator with time as the explanatory variable of interest.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval for this evaluation was obtained from the CHIPER Health Research Ethics Board (No. HS26947-H2025:157). Site-level consent for sharing aggregated data for the evaluation was not required because the data will only be shared in aggregate form with no identifying information. Each site is required to sign a contract with the BCC stating the roles and responsibilities of each party for the duration of the collaborative.

The results of this evaluation will be shared among academic audiences in the form of presentations at national and international conferences, seminars and peer-reviewed publications. We will also develop a summary report with key findings and success stories that can be used by the BCC to help gather interest in the implementation of future QI collaboratives using a BTS model. Evaluation results will be shared with the Public Health Agency of Canada and other BCC funders to help secure sustainable financial and resource support for implementing the BFI in Canada. The findings from this project will contribute to the international body of knowledge regarding the processes to support BFI implementation in community health services, with the ultimate goal of improving breastfeeding outcomes.
