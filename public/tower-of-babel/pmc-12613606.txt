The efficacy of adjuvant therapies in improving endometrial function of women undergoing assisted reproductive technology: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xin Li, Jueyun Lu, JuanJuan Zhang, Ting Luan, Chun Zhao, Xiufeng Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAssessment of the endometrium is an essential component in assisted reproduction. While various adjuvant therapies have been used to improve endometrial function, these results are controversial.MethodsThis meta-analysis aimed to compare the effectiveness of various adjuvant treatment strategies in improving the endometrial thickness and clinical outcome of women undergoing assisted reproduction technology (ART). The PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases were systematically searched for articles published from database inception to 30 December 2022, without language restrictions.ResultsSixteen RCTs involving 1325 patients and 4 prospective trials involving 379 patients were ultimately included in our study. Compared with controls, Sildenafil (WMD: 1.66, CI 0.59–2.74), PRP (1.34, 0.54–2.15), and G-CSF (1.27, 0.62–1.93) were significantly improved endometrial thickness. With regard to the Biochemical pregnancy rate, only PRP (4.28, 1.26–14.56) were associated with a significantly increased of BPR, compared with controls. With regard to clinical pregnancy rate, Aspirin, G-CSF, GH and PRP showed a better CPR than control (1.87, 1.06–3.29; 2.03, 1.23–3.34; 1.73, 1.02–2.94; 2.66, 1.27–5.57, respectively). None of the included adjuvant treatments were significantly different from control or from each other on miscarriage rate, implantation rate and live birth rate. The findings from subgroup analysis we planned study population did not differ significantly from those of the primary analyses for most comparisons.ConclusionsFor women undergoing assisted reproductive technology, using adjuvant treatment with Sildenafil, PRP and G-CSF showed better endometrial thickness and clinical outcomes, and in women with thin endometrium, the results were similar to the primary analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08296-3.Keywords: Adjuvant therapies, Endometrium, Assisted reproduction technology, Pregnancy outcome, Endometrial thickness, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

Assisted reproduction technology (ART) include artificial insemination (AI), in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), which are commonly used to treat infertility. Studies have demonstrated that advanced maternal age is associated with lower success rates in ART [1], while good embryo morphology is associated with higher pregnancy rates after IVF [2, 3]. Endometrial characteristics, such as endometrial pattern, endometrial blood flow, and endometrial thickness (EMT) are also prognostic factors [4, 5]. Nevertheless, these parameters should be interpreted with caution, as some studies did not find a significant association between these parameters and ART outcomes [6].

Due to the invasive nature of endometrial biopsy and the unreliability of hormonal milieu assessment, ultrasonography has been recommended as a noninvasive alternative method for evaluating uterine receptivity. Endometrial biopsy is not a feasible and reasonable method for determining gene products. However, gray-scale ultrasound seems to be a reliable method of assessing endometrial thickness from a clinical point of view [7]. EMT can easily be measured by transvaginal ultrasonography (TVU). The process of human implantation is complex. It is widely believed that successful implantation depends on synchronization between healthy and functional embryos and receptive endometrium [8]. There remains a need to explore how to characterize optimal endometrium before embryo replacement. Suitable thickness of endometrium is crucial for embryo implantation and pregnancy maintenance [9]. At present, there is no consensus on the definition of thin endometrium. The mainstream view of thin endometrium is often defined by a cutoff value of 7 mm [4, 5]. Over the last few decades, researchers have proposed a series of adjuvant therapies to improve pregnancy outcomes in patients with thin endometrium during their ART treatment. However, the quality of scientific evidence that supports the efficacy of these therapies in improving EMT and clinical pregnancy rate (CPR), remains controversial. Traditional pairwise meta-analysis only allows the direct comparison of two interventions in patients with thin endometrium [10–12]. However, many of these adjuvant treatment strategies have not been compared in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Without direct evidence, it is difficult to identify the most effective adjuvant therapy for patients with thin endometrium. Network meta-analyses are most appropriate when there are multiple competing interventions for a given condition and limited head-to-head comparative trials [13]. In the field of thin endometrium management, several adjuvant therapies have been proposed but few have been directly compared, making it a suitable topic for network meta-analysis. Network meta-analyses or multiple treatment comparison meta-analyses are useful tools that provide a hierarchy of the effectiveness of multiple treatments and subsequent guidance for clinical decision-making [13, 14].

The aim of this systematic review and network meta-analysis was to compare the effectiveness of various adjuvant therapies in improving endometrial thickness and clinical outcomes for women with thin endometrium undergoing ART. The specific PICO components were:

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study followed guidance and reports for systematic reviews in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) NMA checklist [15] and the Cochrane Handbook [16]. The method and analysis were prespecified in advance and registered on the PROSPERO website (CRD42020219207). All the analyses were based on the previously published research; therefore, they do not require ethical approval and patient consent.

Literature review

A scope search was performed using standard databases, including PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), for studies evaluating the effectiveness of different treatments of infertile women with thin endometrium relevant prospective studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Both free words and index terms were used as search strategies to establish the eligibility of potential trials Supplementary Table 1 in the Supplement. No language restrictions were applied in this study. A systematic database search was conducted from their inception until December 30, 2022. Two of us (X.L. and T.L.) independently reviewed all titles and abstracts, and potentially relevant articles were selected for full-text analysis. During full-text review, the same two reviewers independently conducted the screening, and a third reviewer (C.Z.) helped resolve any disagreements.

Inclusion & exclusion criteria and outcome measurement

We included studies meeting the following criteria: (1) the study was designed as an RCT, quasi-experimental or prospective studies in which medically confirmed pregnancy outcomes (live birth, clinical pregnancy, and miscarriage) were the endpoints; (2) women in the experimental group underwent ART and received various adjuvant therapies; (3) published or unpublished full texts articles, published abstracts presented at scientific meetings, or doctoral dissertations, and (4) articles written in any language. We excluded studies that patients who suffered from uterine myoma, uterine malformations, endometrial polyps, intrauterine adhesion (IUA), and other pathological changes which could affect the endometrial growth; and case reports and reviews were also excluded. We included studies that compared any adjuvant therapy to placebo or standard care in women undergoing ART, with a focus on those with thin endometrium. However, studies that enrolled both women with thin and normal endometrial thickness were also eligible for inclusion.

Outcome measures

Studies were included if they compared any adjuvant therapy to placebo or standard care in women with thin endometrium undergoing ART, regardless of whether they reported endometrial thickness as an outcome. The primary outcomes of interest were clinical pregnancy, live birth, and miscarriage rates. Endometrial thickness was considered a secondary outcome, as it is a surrogate marker of endometrial receptivity and may not directly predict clinical success.

The primary outcome of effectiveness was EMT, EMT was defined as the maximum distance measured by ultrasonography from one endometrial-myometrial interface to the other in the uterine midsagittal plane [17]. Secondary outcomes included the BPR, defined a positive human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) level without a gestational sac, the presence of a gestational sac with or without fetal heart activity, ectopic pregnancy and heterotopic pregnancy was regarded as CPR, MR, defined as the proportion of patients with a spontaneous termination of pregnancy, IR, defined as the number of gestational sacs divided by the number of embryos transferred, PI, LBR, defined as the delivery of a viable infant was considered as the live birth.

Data extraction and quality appraisal

Two of us (X.L. and T.L.) independently extracted the data according to an a priori standardized data extraction sheet. We used a specifically designed form that captured information on study design, trial setting, country, patient characteristics (inclusion criteria, whether the thin endometrium, exclusion criteria, age, BMI), sample sizes, details of the COS protocol, details of the adjuvant treatment, and outcomes. Based on Luo et al.‘s method for statistical analysis [18], continuous variables represented by medians and quartiles (or intervals) were converted to means and SDs. When necessary, we contacted authors to provide unpublished data. To reach consensus, a third reviewer (J.J. ZH) resolved disagreements.

For RCTs (randomized controlled trials), the Risk of Bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [19]. Each study was classified as having low, medium, or high risk of bias. The risks of bias graphs were constructed with Review Manager 5.3 software (Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). The ROBINS-I tool was used to assess each domain of bias for non-randomized trials. Domains of bias included confounding bias, selection bias, bias in classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes and bias in selection of the reported results [20]. Two investigators (X.L. and T.L.) evaluated each study against rubrics provided by the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool and the ROBINS-I tool. If discrepancies occurred, the senior author (C.Z) was consulted to resolve assessments.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We compared different adjuvant treatments through network meta-analyses based on a frequentist framework using a random-effects model. We conducted all network meta-analyses using the mvmeta package in Stata software (version 17.0, Stata Corp LP, 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, TX, USA). Using the results obtained from the network meta-analysis, we presented a summary of treatment effects as odds ratios (OR) or weighted mean difference (WMD) with 95% CI. League tables and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) were produced for each outcome. In addition to estimation of ORs, treatment ranking was ascertained using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), which represents the percentage of the effectiveness for each treatment compared with a hypothetical treatment that would be ranked first without uncertainty [21]. The efficacy of every intervention was expressed as a percentage, which was considered in relation to an imaginary intervention assumed to be the best. The higher the SUCRA value is, the higher the likelihood of effective treatment [22]. We estimated heterogeneity using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, assuming each comparison’s heterogeneity variance is the same. The distributions of study characteristics across studies grouped by comparison were compared to evaluate transitivity. Statistical inconsistencies between direct and indirect evidence were explored based on global and local approaches. As global approach, we used a design-by-treatment interaction model to investigate the inconsistency from all sources in the entire network [23]. Evidence of local inconsistency was checked using the node-splitting method. The studies that reported 100% or 0% events as interventions were excluded since they do not provide sufficient evidence to make conclusions about relative effects. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot was used to assess small study effects in the network. We consider the following subgroups: study population (thin endometrium or normal endometrium) and ART treatment modality (frozen embryo transfer vs. non-frozen embryo transfer).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Our searches of PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) yielded 892 articles (235 from PubMed, 551 from Embase, 106 from CENTRAL). By excluding duplicate and ineligible studies, we selected 16 RCTs [24–39] and 4 prospective studies [40–43] for further analyses. Risk of bias assessment results

Evaluation of a bias risk for all RCTs is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. The tool for assessing risk of bias in randomized trials examined RCTs according to 7 standards, most studies are at high or unclear risk of bias. Within the 16 included trials, 10 trials described in detail the generation of random sequences, 9 trials described their approach of concealment, 7 studies did not describe the blind methods of participants, implementers, or outcome measurers. 9 studies have complete data, and 8 studies may have reporting bias. In addition, the overall risk of bias was moderate for prospective studies based on the ROBINS-I tool (Supplementary Table 2).

Heterogeneity assessment

We assessed heterogeneity among the included studies for each outcome using the I^2 statistic. For the primary outcome of endometrial thickness, no observed heterogeneity was found between the studies comparing sildenafil and G-CSF (I^2 = 0%). For the secondary outcomes, the heterogeneity varied depending on the specific treatment comparisons, with I^2 values ranging from 0% to 81.6%. Low to moderate heterogeneity was observed for the CPR (I^2: 9.9% − 69.3%), while high heterogeneity was found for the LBR when comparing aspirin to control (I^2 = 81.6%). No heterogeneity was observed for the IR and uterine artery PI when comparing relevant treatments. Heterogeneity assessments were not performed for some outcomes due to the limited number of studies. Detailed heterogeneity results for each outcome are presented in the Supplementary Figs. 3.

Pairwise meta-analysis and network meta-analysis results

Primary outcome measure: endometrial thickness (EMT)

In the network meta-analysis, we recruited 14 RCTs that included 1325 women and 4 prospective studies that included 379 women. Of these RCTs, in addition to the control group, one study evaluated a comparison of PRP and G-CSF treatments (40 women), another study evaluated a comparison of Sildenafil and G-CSF treatments (70 women). The remaining RCTs offered one adjuvant treatment in each intervention: GH (one trial; 60 women), Aspirin (four trials; 437 women), G-CSF (four trials; 387 women), PRP (three trials; 213 women), Sildenafil (two trials; 118 women). The prospective studies also offered one adjuvant treatment in each intervention: GH (threes trials; 317 women), G-CSF (one trial; 62 women). The network meta-analysis results of EMT are presented in Table 2. Sildenafil (WMD: 1.66, 95% CI 0.59 to 2.74), PRP (WMD: 1.34, 95% CI 0.54 to 2.15), and G-CSF (WMD: 1.27, 95% CI 0.62 to 1.93) were more efficacious than control/no treatment in women undergoing ART. Considering the predictive intervals in a network meta-analysis, Sildenafil, PRP and G-CSF treatments still led to a significantly higher chance of EMT (Fig. 3). The other treatments did not show a significant difference compared with the control group. When we directly compared these interventions, the results obtained from pairwise meta-analyses and network meta-analyses were consistent (Fig. 4). The SUCRA values evaluated for the five adjuvant treatments and control groups were 85.8% (Sildenafil), 72.7% (PRP), 68.6% (G-CSF), 40.1% (GH), 29.9%(Aspirin) (Fig. 5). Testing for inconsistency resulted in no statistical significance (p-value = 0.714), and there also was no statistically significant inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons tested by node-splitting technique (Aspirin vs. Con p = 0.989, Con vs. G-CSF p = 0.590, Con vs. GH p = 0.991, Con vs. PRP p = 0.938, Con vs. Sildenafil p = 0.399, G-CSF vs. PRP p = 0.938, G-CSF vs. Sildenafil p = 0.399). (Supplementary Table 4 A). The loop-specific approach did not suggest any inconsistency between closed loops (Supplementary Table 5 A).

Secondary outcome measures

Biochemical pregnancy rate (BPR)

For the secondary outcome, 4 studies with a total of 261 women were included in the network meta-analysis. Compared with control, only PRP (OR, 4.28; 95% CI, 1.26–14.56) were associated with a significantly increased of BPR (Supplementary Table 3 A and Supplementary Fig. 4 A). The SUCRA values evaluated for the five adjuvant treatments and control groups were 89.4% (PRP), 57.3% (Sildenafil) (Supplementary Fig. 5 A). The relationship between the interventions did not form a closed loop and therefore no loop inconsistency testing was required. Since the comparisons were head-to-head between interventions and placebo and no loop was formed, a consistency model was used in the analysis. There is no statistically significant inconsistency between direct or indirect comparison detected by node-splitting approach (Supplementary Table 4B).

Clinical pregnancy rate (CPR)

With regard to the CPR as a secondary outcome, 18 studies with 1554 women were included in the network meta-analysis. In terms of CPR, Aspirin, G-CSF, GH and PRP showed a better CPR than control (OR 1.87,95% CI 1.06 to 3.29; OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.23 to 3.34; OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.94; OR 2.66, 95% CI 1.27 to 5.57, respectively) (Supplementary Table 3B and Supplementary Fig. 4B). When we directly compared these interventions, the results obtained from pairwise meta-analyses and network meta-analyses were consistent (Supplementary Fig. 6). The SUCRA values evaluated for the five adjuvant treatments and control groups were 83.2% (PRP), 65.1 (G-CSF), 57.9% (Aspirin), 52.2% (GH), 31.4% (Sildenafil) (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Testing for inconsistency resulted in no statistical significance (p-value = 0.104), and there also was no statistically significant inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons tested by node-splitting technique (Aspirin vs. Con p = 0.990, Con vs. G-CSF p = 0.104, Con vs. GH p = 0.991, Con vs. PRP p = 0.104, Con vs. Sildenafil p = 0.994, G-CSF vs. Sildenafil p = 0.104). The results of local inconsistency tests are presented in Supplementary Table 4 C. The loop-specific approach did not suggest any inconsistency between closed loops (Supplementary Table 5B).

Miscarriage rate (MR)

Another secondary outcome was MR. 8 studies with 256 women were included in the network meta-analysis. None of the included adjuvant treatments were significantly different from control or from each other on MR (Supplementary Table 3 C and Supplementary Fig. 4 C). The SUCRA values evaluated for the five adjuvant treatments and control groups were 61.6% (Aspirin), 50% (G-CSF), 44.4% (GH), 44.4% (Sildenafil), 29.1% (G-CSF) (Supplementary Fig. 5 C). The relationship between the interventions did not form a closed loop and therefore no loop inconsistency testing was required. There is no statistically significant inconsistency between direct or indirect comparison detected by node-splitting approach (Aspirin vs. Con p = 0.989, Con vs. G-CSF p = 0.997, Con vs. GH p = 0.991, Con vs. PRP p = 0.995, Con vs. Sildenafil p = 0.997, Supplementary Table 4D).

Discussion

ART pregnancy outcomes depend on a variety of factors, including a favourable endometrial environment. Adequate thickness of the endometrium is probably essential to accomplish a successful pregnancy in ART cycles [44, 45]. At the same time, thin endometrium in the reproductive cycle could be related to lower rates of implantation as well as increased rates of miscarriages [46]. Thus, many researchers have tried to identify therapeutic approaches to improve endometrial receptivity and growth for women with thin endometrium. There have been a number of clinical trials that have attempted multiple approaches (e.g., granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, sildenafil, or low-dose aspirin), but their results are inconclusive [47]. This study represents the most comprehensive synthesis of data regarding currently available adjuvant treatment strategies for patients with thin endometrium who are undergoing ART.

Our systematic review and network meta-analysis found that among the adjuvant therapies evaluated, sildenafil, PRP and G-CSF were the most effective in improving endometrial thickness. Sildenafil resulted in the greatest increase in endometrial thickness compared to control (WMD: 1.66, 95% CI 0.59 to 2.74), followed by PRP (WMD: 1.34, 95% CI 0.54 to 2.15) and G-CSF (WMD: 1.27, 95% CI 0.62 to 1.93). These therapies also ranked highest on the SUCRA scores for this outcome. In terms of clinical pregnancy rates, PRP, G-CSF and aspirin all showed statistically significant improvements compared to control, with odds ratios ranging from 1.87 to 2.66. PRP had the highest SUCRA ranking, suggesting it may be the most effective option for this key outcome. G-CSF also performed well across multiple outcomes, with trends towards lower miscarriage rates and higher implantation rates compared to control, although not all differences reached statistical significance.

It is known that the thickness of the uterine endometrium affects the success of in vitro fertilization embryo implantation [48]. The most common ultrasonographic parameters investigated in clinical trials are EMT, endometrial pattern and perfusion, which play important roles in endometrial function. During the menstrual cycle, EMT directly correlates with histological thickness, while endometrial pattern changes in lockstep with the menstrual cycle. A meta-analysis indicated that EMT has no predictive capacity for the occurrence of pregnancy, but it does seem to be a factor for the assessment of the probability of conceiving after IVF [5]. Currently, there is limited data about thin endometrium pathological causes. Thin endometrium may result from several insults or may be idiopathic. Myometrial adhesions can be caused by any procedure that destroys the endometrium. Specifically, the endometrial basalis layer is highly susceptible to trauma following postpartum curettage, leading to the formation of adhesions [49]. The anti-estrogenic effects of clomiphene citrate at peripheral sites of the reproductive system are implicated in abnormal endometrial development in women treated with clomiphene citrate [50]. Endometrial thinning can also result from prolonged progesterone therapy depending on the estrogenic state. In a study by Grow et al. [51] combined oral contraceptives maintained a thin endometrium of 4.1 ± 1.6 mm reliably when started early in follicular phase. According to Casper, persisting thin endometrium can persist for years after contraceptive cessation [52]. Angiogenesis and uterine blood flow play a crucial role in supporting endometrial growth [53, 54]. Maekawa found that a high level of blood flow impedance of the uterine radial artery underlies a thin endometrium [55].

Although numerous approaches have been attempted clinically to improve thin endometrium, their results are inconclusive [47]. The present network meta-analysis demonstrates that included PRP adjuvant agents in patients in ART with thin endometrium was the optimal adjuvant treatment in terms of outcome measures, including the EMT, BPR, CPR, IR. PRP, also known as autologous conditioned plasma, is a concentrate of PRP protein derived from fresh whole blood, centrifuged to remove red blood cells and has anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative functions [56]. Platelets in PRP are actively secreted and transformed into their bioactive forms within 10 min of activation, releasing growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor (TGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and epidermal growth factor (EGF) [57]. It was first used in IVF because of its ability to improve endometrial growth and its tissue healing properties in women with thin endometrium [58]. In the study that used the PRP in women with thin endometrium, the endometrial thickness was significantly increased in the PRP group compared with the control. In another experiment, Wang et al. [59]. investigated 20 women suffering from thin endometrium and undergoing failed embryo implantation under conventional estrogen therapy, resulting in the cancellation of the cycle and low pregnancy. Following PRP infusion, the majority of patients showed effective pregnancy and expansion of endometrium. But in another study, there was no correlation between increased endometrial thickness and ET outcomes after PRP treatment [60]. Therefore, more detailed research on the application of PRP is needed to better understand the auxiliary role of PRP in patients with thin endometrium.

In the current meta-analysis, adjuvant treatment using G-CSF also had beneficial effects. G-CSF resulted in the lowest MR, and produced the higher CPR. G-CSF is a type of growth factor that stimulates bone marrow to produce certain types of white blood cells [61]. It is used to treat bone marrow failure and myelosuppression in recombinant form. Research has shown that G-CSF is involved in follicular development, ovulation, and pregnancy, as well as in maternal-embryonic exchange. In a basic experiment, G-CSF is shown to be effective in reducing ischemia/reperfusion injuries of specific tissues [62]. G-CSF was first reported for use in patients with persistent thin endometrium in 2011 [63], they found that after 48 h of intrauterine infusion of G-CSF, the endometrium of four patients with thin endometrium who were not responsive to estrogen or vasodilator drugs increased to more than 7 mm. The direct effect of G- CSF on endometrium is unknown, but immunological factors are believed to be critical to implantation. The G-CSF is known to affect gene expression in the endometrium, including endometrial vascular remodeling, local immunoregulation, and cell adhesion [64].

With regard to adjuvant treatment using Sildenafil, it resulted in the best EMT, and produced the higher BPR. Sildenafil is a specific phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitor, which augments the vasodilatory effects of NO on vascular smooth muscle by preventing degradation of cGMP. Through a cGMP-mediated mechanism, NO release relaxes vascular smooth muscle in animals [65]. In women with thin endometrium associated with failed IVF cycles, sildenafil increased endometrial growth, endometrial blood flow, and pregnancy rates [66, 67]. It is also effective for patients undergoing resection of IUAs (intrauterine adhesions) [68]. Our results indicated that the prospects are good for using Sildenafil in the thin endometrium population. However, these results need to be confirmed in further prospective studies.

Aspirin has been commonly used as an adjuvant in assisted reproduction and empirically for thin endometrium. The main role of aspirin stems from the irreversible inhibition of cyclooxygenase, which reduces the activity of TXA2 and PGs [69]. By preventing vasoconstriction, this function reduces vascular tone and thus improves tissue perfusion, including uterine blood flow velocity [70]. Taking lower doses of aspirin was more effective than taking higher doses because low doses result in a better TXA2/PGI2 ratio and subsequently reduce vascular resistance and improve perfusion [71]. In the current network meta-analysis, aspirin treatment increased IR and LBR, although there was no statistically significant difference.

Recently, mounting evidence suggests that GH might provide an additional potential benefit at the level of the endometrium. Studies on women with poor endometrial development demonstrated that adjuvant GH administration significantly improves EMT and subsequently increases CPR and IR [40, 42]. A meta-analysis evaluated the effectiveness of GH treatment on endometrial quality and function among women undergoing IVF [72], it reported that a significant association was found between GH administration and improved endometrial indicators (EMT, endometrial pattern, and perfusion) among women with thin endometrium. In recent decades, GH has been considered to regulate many physiological functions including growth, metabolism, and reproduction [73]. Evidence suggests GH can improve IVF pregnancy outcomes by modulating endometrial function. In human endometrium during the luteal phase and the whole gestational period, GH, GH receptors, and related growth factors (including insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)) play a vital role in the decidual reaction [74]. Furthermore, GH enhances the metabolism of intimal cells by accelerating cell proliferation and protein metabolism [75]. In the current network meta-analysis, although there were significant differences compared with the control group only in CPR, SUCRA values showed that cotreatment using GH, all could improve pregnancy outcomes including the EMT, BPR, MR, PI and IR. Based on the available evidence, PRP appears to be the most promising option for women with thin endometrium undergoing ART. It had the highest SUCRA ranking for both endometrial thickness and clinical pregnancy and its effects were consistent across studies and patient subgroups. G-CSF is also a reasonable choice given its positive impacts on multiple outcomes. For women who do not respond to or tolerate PRP or G-CSF, sildenafil could be considered as an alternative, especially if the main goal is to increase endometrial thickness. Limitations of the study

While our study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of various adjuvant therapies for thin endometrium in women undergoing ART, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, for some treatments, the available raw data were limited, leading to outcomes that were disproportionately influenced by extreme cases. To draw more definitive conclusions, additional well-designed clinical trials are necessary to thoroughly examine the effectiveness of these treatments. Secondly, to maximize the sample size and ensure an unbiased and efficient estimation, we adopted a relatively loose set of inclusion criteria. However, to reach more robust conclusions, further subgroup analyses based on factors such as the EMT should be conducted in future studies. Thirdly, the included trials reported few adverse events, which is a crucial aspect to consider when selecting clinical treatments. Future analyses must incorporate common and serious side effects to provide a comprehensive assessment of the risk-benefit profile of these adjuvant therapies. Furthermore, no studies have investigated the potential long-term effects of these adjuvant treatments on the development and health of the offspring. Long-term follow-up studies are essential to confirm the safety of these interventions and inform the development of clinical guidelines.

Additionally, while our systematic review focused on adjuvant therapies for thin endometrium in the general population of women undergoing ART, it is important to consider the potential relevance of these interventions for specific subgroups of infertile patients, such as those with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [76]. Given the high prevalence of endometrial dysfunction in PCOS, the adjuvant therapies identified in our analysis may be particularly beneficial for this population [77]. And another limitation of our systematic review is that not all of the included studies exclusively focused on women with thin endometrium. While we aimed to assess the effectiveness of adjuvant therapies in this specific population, the heterogeneity in the study populations may limit the direct applicability of our findings. Future studies should aim to enroll well-defined cohorts of women with thin endometrium to provide more targeted evidence for this subgroup. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge that our systematic review’s search was conducted on December 30, 2022, and additional relevant studies may have been published since that date. While this gap between the search and publication dates can affect the immediacy of our findings, we believe our review still provides a rigorous and comprehensive synthesis of the evidence available up to the search cut-off. Lastly, it is crucial to recognize that endometrial thickness is a surrogate outcome, albeit a widely used marker of endometrial receptivity. The ultimate goal of any intervention in women with thin endometrium undergoing ART is to improve clinical outcomes such as pregnancy and live birth rates. While our analysis suggests that some adjuvant therapies may have beneficial effects on endometrial thickness, further studies are needed to confirm whether these translate into meaningful improvements in reproductive success.

While network meta-analyses offer a valuable tool for synthesizing evidence and comparing multiple interventions, it is important to recognize their inherent limitations. Firstly, the validity of a network meta-analysis depends on the assumption of transitivity, which requires that the distribution of effect modifiers is similar across trials [78]. However, in practice, this assumption may not always hold, particularly when dealing with complex clinical scenarios and heterogeneous study populations. Secondly, network meta-analyses rely on indirect comparisons, which can be less precise than direct head-to-head comparisons and may be more susceptible to bias [79]. Thirdly, the inclusion of studies with varying designs, populations, and outcome measures can introduce heterogeneity and inconsistency, which may limit the reliability of the results [80]. Finally, network meta-analyses are observational in nature and cannot establish causality in the same way as well-designed randomized controlled trials [81]. Therefore, while our study provides valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of different adjuvant therapies for thin endometrium, the findings should be interpreted with caution and used to guide further research rather than directly inform clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, while our systematic review and network meta-analysis suggest that some adjuvant therapies, particularly PRP and G-CSF, may improve endometrial thickness and clinical pregnancy rates in women with thin endometrium undergoing ART, the evidence on live birth rates remains limited and inconclusive. Clinicians should interpret these findings with caution and prioritize shared decision-making with patients, considering individual preferences, values, and potential risks and benefits. Further high-quality research focusing on live birth rates and long-term outcomes is needed to guide evidence-based practice and optimize fertility outcomes for this challenging patient population.
