Assessing shoreline dynamics and beach use in a tropical beach: a citizen science-based approach using CoastSnap
Authors: Leonardo Alonso-Hernández, Gabriela Medellín, Alec Torres-Freyermuth, Karla C González-Celis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal monitoring is a key to understanding the natural and human impacts on the coastal environment. This work investigates the capabilities and limitations of a citizen science-based approach to assess shoreline changes and beach usage on a tropical beach. The CoastSnap platform enables coastline monitoring based on the participation of coastal communities using smartphones. In May 2024, two CoastSnap stations were installed in a small coastal community (Sisal) located on a barrier island in the northern Yucatan Peninsula (Mexico). Image analysis for the 2024–2025 period shows strong participation, with an average of 1.46 snapshots per day. The images acquired by beachgoers allow for the analysis of shoreline changes, public perception, and beach use. Shoreline positions estimated from CoastSnap-derived images were validated with 23 beach surveys conducted fortnightly using a differential global positioning system. The analysis of images collected by beachgoers allowed for the characterization of shoreline changes (subseasonal and Hurricane Milton), with a high spatial and temporal resolution, on beaches located in the vicinity of a pier. Furthermore, image-derived data show that maximum beach occupancy occurs during the weekends of the holiday periods, regardless the season. A sentiment analysis of the user comments suggests an overall positive perception of this coastal site but also reveals concerns about beach litter pollution and increasing coastal development. These results demonstrate the great potential of this citizen science tool for the low-cost high-resolution (local scale) monitoring of coastal environments in tropical regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-026-15886-x.Keywords: CoastSnap, Citizen science, Coastal monitoring, Shoreline dynamics

Introduction

Coastal monitoring programs are essential for assessing both natural and anthropogenic impacts on beaches (Melet et al., 2020). However, understanding beach morphology changes occurring at storm-scale is often difficult due to a lack of high spatial and temporal resolution data (Gomes da Silva et al., 2024; Harley et al., 2019). Therefore, new approaches are needed to overcome current limitations in the data collection, commonly ascribed to infrastructure and financial constraints (Mehmood, 2021).

The advent of remote sensing technology has become a valuable tool for understanding coastal dynamics (Ponte Lira et al., 2016; Brooke & Lewis, 2017; Splinter et al., 2018; Angnuureng et al., 2022; Vitousek et al., 2023; Blais & Akhloufi, 2025; Valverde et al., 2025; Frugier et al., 2026). More specifically, remote sensing techniques, such as shore-based video monitoring systems (Brignone et al., 2012; Holman & Stanley, 2007; Nieto et al., 2010; Taborda & Silva, 2012), drone flights (Casella et al., 2016; Jessin et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2016), and satellite imagery (Graffin et al., 2025; Luijendijk et al., 2018; Martín-Gallego et al., 2025; Turner et al., 2021; Vos et al., 2019, 2023), allow for the acquisition of coastal geomorphologic information at different temporal and spatial scales.

The use of satellite-derived coastline information has increased with the free availability of satellite data through Google Earth Engine (https://earthengine.google.com) and the release of open-source codes for shoreline detection (e.g., CoastSat; Vos et al., 2019). Due to the large temporal and spatial coverage, this approach allows to investigate shoreline changes over the past decades occurring from local (e.g., Pardo-Pascual et al., 2018) to global (Graffin et al., 2025) scales. However, the spatial and temporal resolution might not be sufficient to investigate coastline changes that occur at smaller spatial (i.e., vicinity of small coastal structures) and temporal (i.e., storms) scales.

Over the past few decades, video monitoring systems have proven suitable for the study of coastal processes at local scale (Alegría-Arzaburu et al., 2007; Arriaga et al., 2022; Hage et al., 2018; Medellín et al., 2008; Turner et al., 2004). Shore-based video information allows for continuous data acquisition to quantify different nearshore processes that range from rip-current detection (e.g., Gallop et al., 2009; Wilson et al., 2014), longshore currents (Chickadel et al., 2003), nearshore bathymetry (Andriolo et al., 2025; Holman et al., 2013), wave runup (e.g., Stockdon et al., 2006), shoreline and intertidal topography changes (e.g., Aarninkhof et al., 2003; Gireesh et al., 2026; Ojeda & Guillen, 2008; Santos et al., 2025; Vousdoukas et al., 2011), beach use (Guillen et al., 2008; Jimenez et al., 2007), sea wrack detection (Rutten et al., 2021), and sandbar migration (e.g., Lippmann & Holman, 1989; Price & Ruessink, 2013). Nevertheless, their implementation is often difficult due to infrastructure constraints (Harley et al., 2019). These limitations can be partially overcome with the use of recreational (e.g., Surf Cams) online streaming webcams (Andriolo et al., 2019, 2025; Conlin et al., 2022; Dusek et al., 2019; Splinter et al., 2011) or drone imagery (Turner et al., 2016).

The use of drones provides greater flexibility for data collection, with respect to video-based monitoring stations, with higher spatial resolution and lower economic cost. This approach has exponentially increased during the past decade (Gonçalves et al., 2020), including a range of different coastal applications such as marine litter monitoring (Gonçalves et al., 2020), assessment of eco-geomorphological impacts associated with the presence of large crowds on beaches (Andriolo & Gonçalves, 2023), and estimation of beach-dune volume changes (e.g., Bacino et al., 2026; Young et al., 2021).

Despite technology developments, continuous data collection is difficult using traditional methods, and hence, citizen science has been identified as a key approach to evaluate the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (e.g., Fritz et al., 2019). Citizen science refers to a scientific work or knowledge production with the involvement of citizens (Fritz et al., 2019). This type of initiative facilitates the knowledge exchange between scientists and society (Busch et al., 2016; Conrad & Hilchey, 2011; Danielsen et al., 2014; Harley & Kinsela, 2022). Hence, citizen science projects have become a cost-effective alternative for data acquisition and have facilitated the co-generation of scientific knowledge, increasing community engagement in coastal research and management (Harley & Kinsela, 2022; Pucino et al., 2021). There are several examples of citizen science projects in coastal areas, including drone-based monitoring of beach volume changes (Ierodiaconou et al., 2022), microplastic and mesoplastic distribution (Jones et al., 2025), monitoring nutrient pollution dynamics in coastal waters (Moshi et al., 2022), and monitoring of marine protected areas (Cigliano et al., 2015).

The CoastSnap program (Harley & Kinsela, 2022) is a successful example of a global citizen science project focused on coastal monitoring. This community-based monitoring program uses smartphones to capture images of coastal changes induced by storms, sea level rise, or human activities. The users take photos from fixed frames to generate time series that allow the assessment of spatial and temporal changes on beaches (Harley & Kinsela, 2022). At each station, ground control points are measured to rectify the images and hence obtain quantitative information (Harley et al., 2019). The image analysis over time allows estimating shoreline trends, to assess coastal impacts, and supports coastal decision-making (Harley & Kinsela, 2022).

The CoastSnap project has grown exponentially with currently more than 700 stations across 40 different countries (M. Harley, personal communication, 2026), demonstrating its potential for monitoring coastal dynamics globally (Harley et al., 2023). CoastSnap stations deployed in Delaware (USA) (Hughes et al., 2023) and Langwarder Groden (Germany) (Kempa et al., 2025) are some examples of the social acceptance of this project. Prior studies demonstrated its use to investigate shoreline variability in Chile (Briceño de Urbaneja et al., 2024) and Spain (González-Villanueva et al., 2023), coastal erosion in Australia (Larsson & Junegard, 2024), the impact of Hurricane Fiona along the north shore of Prince Edward Island (Canada) (George et al., 2024), and the community engagement in Spain (Soriano-González et al., 2024). Moreover, CoastSnap has also shown its potential to acquire additional data such as social perception and beach use (Elrick-Barr et al., 2023). Their capacity for the subpixel analysis of beachgoers’ density for mapping coastal occupation has also been demonstrated (Albuquerque et al., 2024). More recently, the CoastSnap stations network has been employed to acquire imagery for rip-current detection (Harley et al., 2026).

This study explores the capability of CoastSnap stations to assess shoreline dynamics on a tropical beach located in the northern Yucatan Peninsula (Mexico). CoastSnap-derived shoreline data, validated with in situ measurements, are further employed to investigate shoreline dynamics, with high spatial and temporal resolution, in the vicinity of a pier. Furthermore, we analyzed social perception (sentiment analysis) and beach occupation at the study site. The results demonstrate the ability of this tool to quantify different indicators relevant to coastal management in tropical regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

Sisal is located on a barrier island off the northern coast of Yucatan, Mexico (21° 09′ 56.20″ N, 90° 02′ 26.44″ O) (Fig. 1). The weather in the study site is tropical with mean annual temperature of 26 °C and mean annual rain totals of 715 mm. The area is characterized by a microtidal regime with a maximum range of 0.8 m during spring tides (Valle-Levinson et al., 2011) and a wide and shallow continental shelf. The beach is composed of carbonated sand with a mean grain diameter D50 = 0.3 mm (Mendoza et al., 2013).

The northern coast of the Yucatan Peninsula is influenced by intense winds associated with sea breezes and synoptic scale events associated with cold fronts (Figueroa-Espinoza et al., 2014). During the winter storm season (September–March), intense NNW winds (Nortes) associated with cold fronts (Appendini et al., 2012) generate energetic swell with Hs > 2 m and Tp ≈ 8 s at 10-m depth. During spring and summer, NE sea breezes prevail, with wind speeds reaching 10–15 m/s (Figueroa-Espinoza et al., 2014; Medellín & Torres-Freyermuth, 2019; Torres-Freyermuth et al., 2017), inducing sea waves with Hs < 1 m at a depth of 10 m and an intense westward alongshore current (up to 0.5 m/s) which drives a persistent littoral transport of 30,000–90,000 m3/yr (Appendini et al., 2012). Therefore, beach accretion and erosion are typical to the east and west of coastal structures, respectively (Franklin et al., 2021; Medellín et al., 2018; Torres-Freyermuth et al., 2019). Furthermore, this area is also prone to the impact of tropical storms and hurricanes (Torres-Freyermuth et al., 2022).

Sisal CoastSnap stations

CoastSnap-based shoreline mapping presents several limitations compared to shore-based video monitoring systems (Harley et al., 2019). Video monitoring systems use a fixed camera mounted on an elevated and stable platform, fixed ground control points with known coordinates, and knowledge of the camera’s intrinsic parameters to rectify the images. On the other hand, the crowd-sourced approach relies on images collected by citizens, using a wide range of smartphone cameras, from stations installed in public spaces.

The location of the stations on the Sisal pier was decided by mutual agreement with the various stakeholders (tourism service providers, local authorities, and the University), considering that this location is easily and safely accessible and, hence, frequently visited by both tourists and local residents. Each station consists of a stainless steel cradle and contains information about this project to encourage community participation in collecting images using their smartphones. On May 20, 2024, two CoastSnap stations were installed on the Sisal pier, overlooking West and East beaches (Figs. 1d–f). The X–Y–Z location of the CoastSnap camera cradle, its elevation above the mean sea level, and the cradle orientation angles (azimuth, tilt, and roll) were measured after the installation. The Sisal Pier East station cradle was installed at an elevation of 3.19 m above mean sea level (msl), with an azimuth of 70° TN and a field of view (FOV) of approximately 250 m alongshore. The Sisal Pier West station cradle was installed at 3.18 m above msl, with an azimuth of 250° TN, and an alongshore coverage of approximately 250 m. Both smartphone cradles were set to a vertical inclination (tilt) of 84.3° and a rotational movement (roll) of 0°. The stations contain instructions and a QR code that links to the CoastSnap application, developed by SPOTERON and compatible with Android and iOS systems, for the direct image upload. The development of this application is supervised by The University of New South Wales (Australia) which currently funds its maintenance (Harley & Kinsela, 2022). The uploaded images can be viewed and downloaded at www.coastsnap.com/map, where the Sisal Pier East and Sisal Pier West stations correspond to station numbers 971288 and 977254, respectively.

Beach profiles

During the study period, 23 beach surveys were carried out along four cross-shore transects located west and east side of the Sisal pier (Fig. 1e). East 1 (E1) and East 2 (E2) transects are located at approximately 20 m and 120 m from the East station, respectively, while transects West 1 (W1) and West 2 (W2) are located 80 m and 180 m from West Sisal station. These transects are part of a long-term monitoring program for Sisal beach, which consists of 20 transects (Medellín et al., 2025), of which only four are within the FOV of the rectified CoastSnap-derived images (Fig. 1e).

Beach profiles were undertaken using a differential global positioning system (DGPS) in real-time kinematics (RTK) mode with a horizontal resolution of approximately 0.5 m. Measurements with a combined vertical and horizontal error greater than 0.05 m were discarded for quality control. Elevation was referenced to the MEX97 geoid, which is a proxy of the mean sea level in this region (Medellín et al., 2025). The shoreline position, defined as the beach profile intersection with the z = 0 m vertical datum, was obtained for each transect and survey. The foreshore slope was calculated from the linear fit of each beach profile for the elevation interval − 0.25 < z < 0.25 m, obtaining mean values of 0.09, 0.10, 0.08, and 0.09 from the 23 beach surveys for transects W2, W1, E1, and E2, respectively.

The remaining four unknowns (focal length of the smartphone lens, azimuth, tilt, and roll), are solved using nonlinear least squares and a minimum of two ground control points (GCPs) located within the camera’s field of view (FOV). However, it is recommended to have more than two GCPs to optimize the nonlinear least squares fit (Harley et al., 2019). Due to the low elevation and limited field of view, it was not possible to install fixed ground control points near the area of interest (the shoreline) which is constantly changing. Therefore, few permanent GCPs (4 west/1 east) were measured owing to a lack of convenient fixed objects within the FOV. In addition, several temporal GCPs were measured (8 west/10 east) by standing at several locations within the image FOV while recording a video from each of the CoastSnap stations. Then, the video frames with the different positions were extracted and combined in the same image (Figure 2b-1 and 2c-1), following the method described in https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xw5LknXdifA. Using the CoastSnap GUI, the image was georectified to real-world coordinates by manually identifying the GCPs located in the image. After this process, additional (virtual) GCPs (1 west/4 east), that were not accessible to measure in the field but can be identified in the image FOV, were readily obtained to process the images (Fig. 2b-2 and c-2).

Different types of shoreline indicators have been used in the scientific literature to investigate shoreline dynamics (Pajak and Leathermean, 2002; Boak & Turner, 2005; Moore et al., 2006). The waterline position (extracted from an image) is defined as the instantaneous boundary between land and water at the instant the image was captured (Almar et al., 2026). Therefore, the waterline depends on water-level fluctuations (waves and tides) present at the time each image was acquired. To accurately compare shoreline positions over time, the mean sea level tidal-datum based shoreline indicator is generally preferred (Cheng and Chang, 2009). In this work, GPS measurements are used to extract the datum-based shoreline position from the intersection of the beach profile with the mean sea level datum (i.e., z = 0), whereas CoastSnap images (acquired at a different stage of the tide cycle) are used to detect the waterline.

The instantaneous beach width (BW), defined in this work as the cross-shore distance between the initial (landward) point of the transect and the waterline, is dependent on the tidal level. The tidal correction is considered a postprocessing step applied to the waterline to account for the horizontal shift of the shoreline, thereby standardizing time series to a vertical datum (Graffin et al., 2025). Therefore, it is necessary to apply a linear tidal correction, using water level data and characteristic beach face slope, to translate each waterline to the shoreline position corresponding to the mean sea level tidal-datum (e.g., Almar et al., 2026; Azorakos et al., 2024; Castelle et al., 2022, 2023; Chen & Chang, 2009; Harley et al., 2019; Vos et al., 2019; among many others). Social perception

To assess the social perception of the study site, an analysis of the registered comments made by CoastSnap users was performed. All comments were exported from the application and analyzed by means of the Text Analytics Toolbox in MATLAB®. For this purpose, each comment was translated (Spanish to English) and tokenized.

Firstly, a sentiment analysis was performed using the vaderSentimentScores function, based on the VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner) algorithm (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014). The VADER algorithm allows to asses the sentiment intensity by providing to each comment a score ranging from −1 (negative) to 1 (positive). The comments were classified into three categories according to their semantic orientation using threshold values of > 0.05 and < –0.05 to differentiate between positive and negative comments, respectively, and values between −0.05 and 0.05 as neutral comments. Sentiment analysis relies on a list of lexical words (lexicon) labeled as positive or negative depending on their semantic orientation (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014). However, some classification limitations were found in the classification algorithm due to the use of a general lexicon. For instance, words as “litter” or “sargassum,” which are often associated with negative environmental conditions in beaches, were classified as neutral. To correct this bias, a list of keywords (“litter,” “dirty,” “pollution,” “seaweed,” “sargassum,” and “dead-fish”) were employed to reclassify those comments which denoted a negative environmental condition but were classified as neutral. The latter allowed to adapt the sentiment analysis to the local context of the study site.

Additionally, an analysis of the individual words (tokens) contained in the comments was performed to build a word cloud. To this effect, words were converted to lowercase, and punctuation characters were removed. Also, low-semantic-value words (stopwords) were discarded. Then, the frequency of occurrence of each word was computed and sorted in descending order to build the cloud. To ensure replicability, every step was performed using available functions within the Text Analytics Toolbox in Matlab® (i.e., lower, erasePunctuation, removeStopWords, bagOfWords, and wordcloud). This analysis is complementary to the sentiment analysis as it provides context to the positive or negative social perception.

Recreational beach use

Images were also employed to investigate the maximum beach occupancy throughout the year on the beach located east from the pier. For the analysis, the method of manual counting, often used to train artificial intelligence algorithms (e.g., Johnston-González et al., 2024), was used. Images taken at the East beach, corresponding to the day and time with the highest number of beach users in each month, were selected to evaluate beach occupation throughout the year. Image processing and analysis were performed using the widely employed open-source software Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012), where images were visually inspected and beachgoers were manually counted using the multipoint tool. To systematize the counting and to reduce potential omissions, the Grid tool was used to divide each image into a mesh of equal-sized squares. The count was conducted within each grid square, assigning a sequential order numbered row by row. For each beach user, a marker was placed directly on their position in the image within their respective grid square, considering both people located on the dry beach and in the water. To improve the accuracy, the counting process was carried out using zoom and navigation tools within the image, allowing for the identification of individuals in densely populated areas. Beach visitors located underneath palm shelters (palapas) were not considered, as their identification was unclear.

When all individuals were marked, the software allowed to export the information associated with each marker corresponding to an identified beachgoer and its image coordinates (U, V). Therefore, the total number of bathers in each image was determined as the total number of markers recorded. Moreover, to evaluate the reproducibility of the method and estimate the uncertainty associated with manual counting, the procedure was repeated by three operators. Hence, the mean and standard deviation of the number of beachgoers (per image) of manual counting were calculated.

Results

Community engagement

Community participation at Sisal CoastSnap stations can be assessed based on the number of images collected over a period of 457 days (06/01/2024 to 08/31/2025). A total of 1332 images were acquired in the two stations, with 57.9% (1.69 images/day) and 42.1% (1.23 images/day) for Sisal Pier East and West Station, respectively. The number of images taken at each station in relation to the month of the year, the day of the week, and the time of day are shown in Fig. 3.

Number of images (June 1, 2024-August 31, 2025) taken at the Sisal Pier CoastSnap stations in relation to: a month of the year, b day of the week, and c time of day. The shaded area and vertical lines in (a) indicate a holiday or vacation periods

High participation was consistently observed since the installation of the CoastSnap stations (Fig. 3), increasing during holidays or vacation periods, especially during the summer breaks (July–August), but also on winter and spring holidays occurring in December and April, respectively. The number of images taken at both stations remained similar during the first five months (Fig. 3a). However, a decrease occurred at the West station from November 2024 to March 2025 (Fig. 3a) attributed to the deterioration of the signage (i.e., QR code) installed next to the camera cradle due to weathering. The signage was replaced in March 2025, returning rapidly to a similar number of snapshots as the East Station in April 2025 (Fig. 3a). The total number of images in August 2025 decreased by 40 images compared to August 2024 (Fig. 3a). This drop in participation can be explained by a harmful algal bloom event that occurred in mid-August 2025, when local authorities banned bathing or swimming due to potential health problems.

Regarding the analysis related to the day of the week, the peaks occurred during the weekends with a maximum number of total snapshots at the two stations of 252 and 250, taken on Saturday and Sunday, respectively (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, the day of the week with the lowest participation was Tuesday with 142 images. The times of the day with the highest number of images are between 16:00 and 19:00 h, when solar radiation decreases toward sunset (Fig. 3c).

Shoreline position mapping from images

Accuracy of CoastSnap-derived shoreline position

CoastSnap-derived beach width, determined by both the (instantaneous) waterline and the (tide-corrected) shoreline, was compared with DGPS in situ measurements at four different alongshore locations (Figs. 5a–d). During the period of study, the beach width presented subseasonal shoreline oscillations of 20 to 30 m at the analyzed cross-shore transects (Figs. 5a–d). Coastline dynamics in transects located in the vicinity of the pier (Fig. 5a and c) were largely influenced by the pier effects on wave transformation (wave diffraction), nearshore current, and littoral transport (partial sediment entrainment). On the other hand, the cross-shore transect located away from the structure and upstream of the dominant longshore transport (E2) presented smaller shoreline/waterline variations (Fig. 5d). CoastSnap-derived beach width evolution, based on the two proxies (waterline and shoreline), captured the seasonal and alongshore variability in agreement with accurate DGPS measurements (Fig. 5a–d).

Time series of beach width derived from CoastSnap data from shoreline (blue crosses) and waterline (black crosses) position at transects: a W1, b W2, c E1, and d E2. e Cross-shore deviations (landward: negative values; seaward: positive values) between image- and in situ DGPS-derived shoreline measurements expressed as box-and-whisker plots (mean: asterisk; median: horizontal line), at different transects between CoastSnap (black: waterline; blue: shoreline) and DGPS data. f Direct comparison (solid black line indicates identity line) between DGPS- and CoastSnap-derived BW (waterline: black crosses; shoreline: blue crosses)

The cross-shore deviations in the shoreline and waterline positions, calculated from the comparison between the image estimates and the ground truth (DGPS data), during the study period are illustrated as box-and-whisker plots (Fig. 5e). Image-derived waterline and shoreline consistently obtained the smallest and largest errors at transect E2 and W1, respectively. The image-derived shoreline presented a better agreement with DGPS data (Table A.1), with mean deviations of 0.80 m (W2), 0.86 m (W1), 0.29 m (E1), and −0.19 m (E2). However, these results represent a small improvement with respect to the CoastSnap-derived waterlines (W2 0.84, W1 0.91, E1 0.84, and E2 −0.15). The CoastSnap-derived information is generally located seaward from the DGPS data in transects W2, W1, and E1. For the two approaches, Transect E2 presented the smallest standard deviation (waterline 2.37 m, shoreline 1.84 m) and RMSD (waterline 2.32 m, shoreline 1.81 m) values, whereas transect W2 showed the largest standard deviation (waterline 4.08 m, shoreline 3.45 m) and RMSD (waterline 4.07 m, shoreline 3.47 m). The beach width comparison between DGPS measurements and CoastSnap-derived shoreline (waterline) data shows that 79% (72%) of the shoreline (waterline) estimations fall within the ± 3 m of the ground truth measurements (Fig. 5f). Therefore, it was found that tide correction (Eqs. 2 and 3) is not critical to describe subseasonal shoreline dynamics and trends in this microtidal environment.

Shoreline dynamics

Shoreline dynamics in the vicinity of the Sisal pier can be investigated using citizen science images collected during the study period. When more than one image per day is available, the image with highest quality (illumination, FOV, and visible GCPs) is selected. Figure 6 shows the relative shoreline position change estimated by CoastSnap (using tide correction derived shoreline) at transects W2 (Fig. 6a) and E2 (Fig. 6b). For comparison, the in situ measurements are also shown. Following Harley et al. (2019), a moving average is applied to the image-derived shoreline position to reduce (swash, rectification) uncertainties. Shoreline changes at the west transect were less sensitive to the impact of Hurricane Milton (October 2024) (Fig. 6a) with respect to E2 that exhibited a shoreline retreat of 15 m (Fig. 6b). The shoreline retreat in E2 was followed by a steady recovery during the following months, with no net beach width change by the end of the annual cycle (Fig. 6b). In contrast, a net beach width increase was observed at W2 by the end of the study period (Fig. 6a). This differentiated behavior can be ascribed to the influence of the pier.

CoastSnap imagery provides high-spatial resolution information on shoreline dynamics. Figures 6 c and 6 d show the spatial and temporal evolution of shoreline position change characterized from images taken at the two stations, where a moving average is applied to the shoreline position derived from crowd-source smartphone images. The shoreline position data covers the entire camera’s field of view, resulting in an alongshore distance of 250 m from the West station (Fig. 6c) and East station (Fig. 6 d) with a 0.5-m resolution. Consistent with the comparison between east and west transects, a distinct behavior (east and west from the pier) is noticed in the spatial–temporal shoreline evolution. From June to October 2024, shoreline advance (eastward distance from the station < 100 m in Fig. 6 d) and retreat (westward distance from the station < 100 m in Fig. 6c) was observed east and west from the pier, respectively. The latter is ascribed to the dominance of westward sediment transport in this area during sea-breeze conditions and the partial entrainment of littoral transport due to the structure presence. The connection between the two beaches, due to the permeability of the pier, is also noticed with sediment accretion occurring in the vicinity of the pier at the West beach (westward distance from the station < 20 m in Fig. 6c) before the impact of Hurricane Milton (October 2024). Hurricane Milton caused a generalized shoreline retreat followed by a slower shoreline recovery in the East beach (Fig. 6 b and d), as compared to the West beach (Fig. 6a and c) which shows smaller changes and a notable faster shoreline recovery during the months following the hurricane impact (from November to March).

Social perception

The CoastSnap platform allows us to investigate social perception by analyzing comments recorded by participants on the SPOTERON application. A total of 343 comments were received in the two CoastSnap stations between June 1, 2024, and August 31, 2025. Sentiment analysis shows that 54% of the comments were classified as positive, 12% as negative, and 34% as neutral.

The sentiment analysis suggested that the social perception about the study site was mostly positive with a significant number of neutral comments (Fig. 7a). Despite negative comments presented a lower percentage of occurrence, CoastSnap users pointed out the presence of beach litter (PET bottles, organic waste, and plastic bags) and the need for beach cleaning. Moreover, participants also complained about the deterioration of the pier infrastructure and the replacement of the traditional light poles with modern headlights. According to the comments, increased coastal development is also a cause for concern. Several comments urged the community to take care of the beach, raise awareness about the importance of its protection, and organize clean-up campaigns. Other comments were focused on environmental conditions of the study site such as the passage of Hurricanes Beryl (July 2024), Helen (September 2024), and Milton (October 2024) (see Fig. 4a) and the red tide event that occurred in August 2025.

a Sentiment analysis results and b word cloud generated from the comments collected at Sisal CoastSnap stations from June 1, 2024, to August 31, 2025

The word cloud, derived from users’ comments, provides additional context for the sentiment analysis, showing that the most frequent word was positive (“beautiful” in Fig. 7b). However, the word “litter” was recurringly mentioned, being among the top ten of more frequent words, whereas “sargassum” and “red-tide” were among the top fifteen (Fig. 7b).

Recreational beach use

The number of beach users, at the beach located East from the pier, shows a strong seasonal variability with maximum beach occupancy ranging from 56 to 414 visitors. Maximum values of beach visitors correspond to Sundays occurring during the spring (20/04/2025: 414 ± 10 visitors) and summer (04/08/2024: 271 ± 9 visitors; 03/08/2025: 240 ± 8 visitors) vacation breaks (Fig. 8b). On the other hand, the month with the lowest daily maximum corresponds to November, June, and October 2024 with 56 ± 4, 72 ± 4, and 88 ± 1 visitors, respectively. The number of beach visitors was poorly correlated (r = 0.031) with the number of uploaded images during the study period. For instance, on the day (20/04/2025) with maximum beach occupancy (> 400 visitors) there were only two image uploads (Fig. 8b). On the other hand, the day (29/12/2024) with maximum uploaded images (i.e., 9) presented a beach attendance of < 150 visitors (Fig. 8b).

Discussion

Community engagement

The smartphone cradles in Sisal are located at a relatively low elevation (3.19 m above the mean sea level) as compared to previous studies with stations located 3.9 to 37 m high (Briceño de Urbaneja et al., 2024; Harley et al., 2019; Robinet et al., 2022). High-elevated CoastSnap stations potentially provide a larger field of view for the shoreline mapping, while low-elevation stations, such as the ones in Sisal, are more accessible and hence allow for greater participation, resulting in a higher temporal resolution data. However, the alongshore extension of 250 m in the FOV coverage in Sisal is satisfactory, considering that a CoastSnap station located 5 m above sea level can cover 400 m (Harley & Kinsela, 2022).

The participation results show an overall average of 1.46 snaps/day for the two Sisal stations, placing them among the top 15 globally during the study period and in the top 10 over the last year (https://www.coastsnap.com/map, last accessed 04/21/2026). The strong participation in Sisal CoastSnap stations was favored by the tropical climate and the increase in tourism in recent years. The peak in participation is highly correlated with holidays regardless the season of the year. This differs with the results obtained at sites located at higher latitudes where the differences in participation are significant between summer and winter months (Harley & Kinsela, 2022). Regarding the participation dependence on the day of the week, the results from the Sisal CoastSnap stations show that more images were taken during the weekend, consistent with Harley and Kinsela (2022) observations in two beaches north of Sydney, Australia. However, for the time of the day a different pattern was found, with the peak participation in Sisal occurring near sunset, whereas 10:00 am is the time of day with the highest participation on Australia beaches. The community expressed a positive perception of the CoastSnap program, highlighting its value. Thus, the high community participation and feedback indicate strong acceptance of this project in the study site. The comments analysis suggests that the participation in Sisal mostly relies on tourists, with less contributions from the local community.

Shoreline position mapping accuracy

The CoastSnap stations used in this study were installed relatively close to the ground level, and therefore, it is relevant to assess the accuracy of the shoreline position mapping with respect to previous works. The errors obtained (RMSD = 1.81–3.47 m) in shoreline position determination are within the range of the errors reported in previous studies using crowd-source imagery (e.g., González-Villanueva et al., 2023; Harley et al., 2019; Splinter et al., 2018). Consistent with prior works (Almar et al., 2012; Harley et al., 2019; Quartel et al., 2006), the largest errors are found at the transect located in the more dynamic area at a longer distance from the station, which corresponds to the transect located downdrift the pier (W2: RMSD = 3.47 m). Arriaga et al. (2022) used a shore-based video-monitoring station, consisting of five cameras placed at a height of 43 m, 1600 m west of the Sisal Pier and 100 m inland, and conducted 100 beach surveys to assess the accuracy of the shoreline derived from the imagery along a 1.1 km stretch of coast. They obtained an average RMSD value of 1.6 m for shoreline detection, which varied according to the distance along the coast relative to the cameras station. The system has not been operational since 2021, and hence a direct comparison with the current results was not possible. Various indicators can be used in shoreline dynamics studies, and, therefore, quantifying their effects is important (Moore et al., 2006). Konstantinou et al. (2023) employed 14 years of topographic survey data from two UK beaches to investigate the effect of tides and waves on satellite derived shoreline estimation. They found that tidal correction significantly improved shoreline estimation at both sites and that beach state could also play a significant role. On the other hand, our accuracy results with CoastSnap indicate that tidal correction (horizontal offset) is not significant in this low-energy microtidal environment, despite the gentle beach slope. This is consistent with the study by Arriaga et al. (2022) in which the time-averaged RMSD value decreased from 1.9 to 1.6 m when considering the tide correction.

Limitations

The Sisal CoastSnap stations were installed on a pier, with the camera cradle facing the shore. Due to the low elevation and limited FOV, it was not possible to install fixed ground control points near the area of interest (the shoreline), which is constantly changing. Therefore, the GCPs used for image rectification were located at fixed elevated positions far from the area of interest. This is not an ideal situation from a photogrammetry perspective. Low image resolution and low-elevation of the camera cradle are a potential source of error and uncertainties for manually identifying the fixed and virtual GCPs employed for the image georectification, located on buildings or structures (e.g., base of harbor light or tip of antenna tower) far away from the area of interest (Fig. 2b–2 and c-2). Therefore, future work will explore the use of the control image (Fig. 2b–1 and c-1) for image registration (e.g., Harely et al., 2019) to avoid relying on the manual selection of the GCPs. However, despite all the (unavoidable) technical limitations, this citizen science approach constitutes a valuable tool for investigating shoreline dynamics on low-energy, microtidal coasts (Fig. 5).

The shoreline position mapping accuracy was not significantly improved in the study site with the tide correction of the detected waterline. This might be related to the low tidal regime and the use of model information (astronomical tide) to conduct the tide correction. The results suggest that large deviations might be ascribed to other limitations. For instance, the use of a constant beach slope (see Table A1) and uncertainties during image rectification are potential sources of error despite the microtidal and low-energy conditions. Future work should be devoted to investigating the role of seasonal sea level changes associated with climate variability (e.g., Almar et al., 2026). There are additional waterline detection sources of error related to either natural or anthropogenic factors, such as the lack of visibility of GCPs due to misplacement of smartphones on the cradle, sunlight reflection, the complexity of beach morphology (i.e., ridge-runnel), the presence of a high density of beachgoers or macrophyte wracks, shadows, and objects near the shore (boats) that can affect the shoreline mapping. The lighting during sunrise and sunset induced a landward/seaward shift of the waterline detection algorithm. A similar problem occurred in the presence of macrophyte wracks, where the waterline position cannot be detected due the color differences near the shore. Most of these limitations have already been pointed out by Harley et al. (2019). In the present work, more than 45% of the images were discarded for waterline detection due to the aforementioned problems, the most common problems being the misplacement of the camera on the cradle (395 images discarded) and the lighting conditions (228 images discarded). On the other hand, the presence of large number of beachgoers induced large noise in the rectified images due to the low elevation of the CoastSnap station which increases their image footprint. The low elevation of the CoastSnap stations also complicates the counting of beachgoers (either manually or with an algorithm) as there is more probability of people overlapping in the oblique images. Therefore, the characteristics (i.e., sunset/sunrise views, easily accessible, and the high number of visitors) that contribute to the strong community participation in this citizen science project can also play a negative role in the image quality. In this work, the beach use analysis relied on manual counting due to large beachgoer overlapping in the camera field of view owing to camera’s low elevation. Automatizing the beachgoers counting process using artificial intelligence (Johnston-González et al., 2024) or deep learning (Proença et al., 2024) could provide near real-time information.

Conclusions

This work demonstrates the usefulness of the CoastSnap citizen science program for monitoring shoreline dynamics, social perception, and beach use on a microtidal tropical beach located on a barrier island. A strong community participation (1.46 images/day) was observed during the study period at the two CoastSnap stations installed on a pier located in Sisal, Mexico. Image-derived shoreline position was validated with shoreline position measured using a DGPS, at four different alongshore locations and over 23 beach surveys, finding mean shoreline cross-shore deviations of 1.8–3.5 m. Accounting for tide correction only represents a small improvement in the accuracy of the shoreline position mapping, with respect to the use of the instantaneous water level interface, in this microtidal environment. The high spatial and temporal resolution allows for assessing shoreline dynamics in the vicinity of the pier, overcoming limitations in the existing DGPS surveys and satellite imagery. The image-derived data captured shoreline changes occurring at both subseasonal (20–30 m) and storm (5 to 15 m) scales. The (image-derived) coastline evolution shows a distinct behavior on the two beaches located east and west of the pier, highlighting the impact of the pier in the alongshore variability of shoreline dynamics. The images collected through citizen science allowed for quantifying the impact of Hurricane Milton (October 2024) and the subsequent beach recovery. Furthermore, the CoastSnap platform allows us to conduct a sentiment analysis from the users’ comments to elucidate the social perception of the study site. The sentiment analysis suggested a positive opinion (54%) about the beach but also points out environmental problems related to beach littering, the deterioration of the pier infrastructure, and coastal development. Beach use analysis shows that maximum beach attendance occurred during the afternoon of weekend days during vacation periods or holidays. A poor correlation (r = 0.031) was found between the daily number of images uploaded by citizens and the maximum number of beachgoers detected on the images of the same date. This result reveals that the number of images uploaded is not a good proxy of beach occupation in the study site.
