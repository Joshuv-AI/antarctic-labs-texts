In-Distribution Steering: Balancing Control and Coherence in Language Model Generation


Abstract
Activation steering methods control large language model (LLM) behavior by modifying internal activations at inference time. However, most existing activation steering methods rely on a fixed steering strength, leading to either insufficient control or unadapted intervention that degrades text plausibility and coherence. We introduce In-Distribution Steering (IDS) , a novel method that adapts steering strength based on the input data distribution in representation space. IDS dynamically adjusts interventions according to how far a given input lies within the distribution, enabling adaptive intervention and generation stability during text generation. Experiments demonstrate that IDS achieves strong accuracy on classification tasks while producing coherent text without collapse, making IDS particularly well suited for real-world applications. Keywords: Alignment, steering, safety, interpretability

1.  Introduction

Despite their remarkable generalization capabilities, autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs) can behave in undesirable ways. LLMs may produce factual inaccuracies
17
, behave unsafely
5
or be misaligned
29
. These problems arise because LLMs learn their objectives from broad web-scale data
35
, which rarely aligns perfectly with the specific requirements of any particular application. As LLMs are increasingly integrated into high-stakes or user-facing applications, it becomes essential to ensure their reliability, safety, and controllability without sacrificing fluency or creativity
34
.

Several approaches have been proposed to control LLM behavior. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is widely used to align models with human preferences
9
;
4
, but it has significant drawbacks: it requires large amounts of data and computing power, and it modifies LLMs internal weights in ways that are difficult to interpret. This opaque modification process can introduce new biases or harm the model’s performance on unrelated tasks
28
. Prompt engineering
20
offers a lighter and more flexible alternative, but its effects are indirect and highly sensitive to context.

Activation steering
27
offers a compelling alternative to RLHF and prompt engineering. Activation steering operates directly on LLMs internal activations during inference, modifying them along learned directions called steering vectors. Grounded in the linear representation hypothesis
23
;
11
;
24
, steering vectors represent directions in the LLMs representation space that corresponds to high-level concepts
8
or behaviors, such as truthfulness
18
, refusal
3
, deceptiveness
13
or faithfulness
7
. By moving activations along such a vector, the LLM can be steered toward a specific behavior without changing its parameters, making activation steering a transparent and low-cost option for LLM control
31
;
27
. Several approaches have been proposed to compute and apply steering vectors in practice
27
;
31
;
14
. However, (1) they rely on a fixed steering strength leading to either over or under steering
27
;
31
, (2) they are not extensively tested on open-ended text generation
14
an (3) they do not aim to generate plausible steered text
27
;
14
.

2.  Background and Related Work

This section first introduces the linear representation hypothesis, which motivates the use of steering vectors. We then describe techniques for computing steering vectors from model activations. Finally, we formalize activation steering that our method builds upon.

2.1.  Background

Linear Representation Hypothesis.

The linear representation hypothesis states that high-level concepts and behavioral properties in neural networks can be encoded as directions in activation space
23
;
11
;
24
. Under this hypothesis, a model’s internal representations can be decomposed into interpretable linear components, where each direction corresponds to a semantic or behavioral feature. This geometric perspective suggests that moving activations along specific directions can induce predictable changes in model behavior without retraining or fine-tuning the model
31
;
27
. Empirical evidence across various architectures supports this view, demonstrating that semantic properties like truthfulness and refusal align with learnable linear directions in representation space
19
;
3
. In the following, we call these behavioral linear representations steering vectors.

Steering Vectors Computation.

Given the linear representation hypothesis, several methods have been proposed to identify which directions in activation space correspond to desired behavioral properties. The most widely used approach is the difference of means (
diff-mean
) method, which relies on constructing contrastive datasets pairs of examples differing in a target property (e.g., toxic vs. non-toxic, refusal vs. non-refusal) and computing the average difference in their internal activations
39
;
27
. This yields a steering vector that captures the direction separating the two behaviors in representation space. Beyond
diff-mean
, several works have proposed supervised probing techniques, where linear classifiers such as logistic regression or support vector machines (SVMs) are trained to separate latent representations associated with different behaviors
1
;
18
. Steering vectors are derived from these learned classifiers as the normal vectors of the decision boundaries, effectively encoding a given behavior.

Activation Steering.

Having established how steering vectors are computed, we now formalize how they are applied to modify model behavior. Activation steering refers to techniques that guide a language model’s behavior by directly modifying its internal activations during inference
27
. As the model processes input text, it computes intermediate representations at each layer encoding semantic and behavioral information that can be manipulated to control the model’s outputs. Formally, activation steering applies the following intervention at specific layers and token positions:

\mathbf{h}_{l,p}\leftarrow\mathbf{h}_{l,p}+\alpha_{l,p}\mathbf{v}_{l}.

(1)

where
\mathbf{h}_{l,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the original activation at layer
l\in L
and token position
p\in T
. Steering is performed by adding a steering vector
\mathbf{v}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which encodes the desired behavioral direction at layer
l
, while the coefficient
\alpha_{l,p}\in\mathbb{R}
controls the intervention strength at that specific layer and token position. This additive intervention shifts the model’s internal representations toward target properties without modifying model parameters.

2.2.  Related Work

27
introduce Contrastive Activation Addition (
CAA
), an activation-steering technique based on steering vectors obtained with
diff-mean
. The authors demonstrate that
CAA
can reliably guide the model toward specific behaviors in both multiple-choice and open-generation settings. To evaluate performance, they report behavioral metrics such as accuracy on contrastive question-answering benchmarks and GPT-4 preference ratings for open-ended generations. A key limitation of
CAA
is its use of a fixed steering factor across all prompts and token positions (
\alpha_{l,p}=\alpha
following Equation
1
). This uniform steering factor can cause understeering or oversteering depending on the input text. Weaker-than-necessary interventions may fail to achieve the desired effect, whereas too strong interventions may push activations outside the natural distribution of the target behavior, increasing the risk of collapse and non plausibility during text generation.

More recently,
14
proposed an approach based on steering vectors obtained with linear probes, called Mechanistic Error Reduction with Abstention (
MERA
). In this method, a calibration threshold is defined to determine when to apply steering, and the steering strength is computed using a closed-form solution. The steering strength scales proportionally with the predicted error of the linear probes. Steering is applied only when the probe’s prediction exceeds the calibrated threshold; otherwise, the model abstains from intervention.
MERA
consistently outperforms several steering baselines such as
CAA
, achieving notable accuracy gains. However,
MERA
is primarily designed for supervised tasks and does not directly address open-ended text generation. Its evaluation does not include text plausibility metrics.

3.  Proposed Approach: In-distribution Steering

This section introduces the core principles of In-distribution Steering (
IDS
), our proposed activation steering approach.
IDS
aims at determining the optimal value of the steering factors at each layer and token position by leveraging the distributions of activations obtained from a dataset of prompts exhibiting the target behavior.
Following Equation
1
, the objective is to determine the optimal value of
\alpha^{l}_{p}
such that the steered activation remains consistent with the distribution of activations at layer
l
corresponding to prompts that exhibit the desired behavior, denoted by
\mathcal{D}^{+}_{l}
. Our method proceeds in three stages:

1.

Distributions Modeling.
We construct a contrastive dataset to characterize the distributions of activations for the positive (aligned behavior) and negative (unaligned behavior) classes, and we define a threshold on a distance measure that determines whether an activation lies within the target distribution.

2.

Optimal Steering Factor Determination.
We formulate the determination of the steering factor as a constrained optimization problem, where the objective is to apply the strongest possible steering while ensuring that activations remain in-distribution. A closed-form solution is derived to allow efficient computation.

3.

Layers Selection.
We restrict interventions to layers that are discriminative for the target behavior, using a threshold on a F1-score as indictor of the discriminative power of a layer.

3.1.  Distributions Modeling

Contrastive Dataset Creation

The first step of our approach consists in modeling two distributions using a contrastive dataset. We model the distribution of the activations at the last token position for prompts in which the target behavior is either present (positive) or absent (negative). Formally, for a given layer
l
, we define the corresponding distributions as

\mathcal{D}^{-}_{l}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{h}^{-}_{l,-1})\quad\quad\mathcal{D}^{+}_{l}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{h}^{+}_{l,-1}),

where
\mathbf{h}^{s}_{l,p}
denotes the activations at token position
p
and layer
l
for prompts in which the behavior is absent (
s=-
) or present (
s=+
).

In the context of multiple-choice questions (MCQs), some existing methods construct contrastive datasets by completing the same prompt with an answer token that either reveals or does not reveal the expected answer. Activations are then collected at the first answer token position as in
27
. While this approach makes more efficient use of the data, since each prompt produces a contrastive pair, the resulting activations are collected at the answer token. Their distribution may differ from that of the prompt tokens. For this reason, we instead construct our datasets by separating prompts where the model naturally exhibits the expected behavior from those where it does not, thereby allowing us to compute the relevant distributions directly on the prompt activations. Steering vectors are then computed for each layer
l
from the difference in means between the two distributions as follows:

\mathbf{v}_{l}=\dfrac{1}{|D^{+}|}\sum\mathbf{h}^{+}_{l,-1}-\dfrac{1}{|D^{-}|}\sum\mathbf{h}^{-}_{l,-1}

(2)

Reducing dimension with PCA

Since the activations lie in a high-dimensional space, directly modeling the distributions is problematic due to the curse of dimensionality
26
. In such settings, distances become less informative and density estimation requires prohibitively many samples. To address this, we first apply a PCA to the union of the two datasets,
\mathbf{h}^{-}_{l,-1}\cup\mathbf{h}^{+}_{l,-1}
, to obtain a lower-dimensional projection of the data. We argue that the separability between the two classes will be preserved in the reduced space if the between-class variance, induced by the presence or absence of the behavior, dominates within-class variance
30
. This way, we ensure that the subsequent modeling steps remain sensitive to interventions in the direction of steering.

Modeling Distances with Mahalanobis

The next step consists in modeling the distance of an activation to the distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\text{pca-}}_{l}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\text{pca+}}_{l}
in the reduced space. For this purpose, we employ the Mahalanobis distance, which explicitly accounts for variability along different directions. This choice is further motivated by the expectation that within-distribution variability will be limited along the concept direction. In contrast, alternative measures such as the Euclidean distance fail to capture this heterogeneity, as they treat all directions as equally informative.

Once the distances are modeled, we define a threshold of the form
\epsilon=d_{0.95}
, where
d_{0.95}
denotes the 95th-percentile of the distance distribution. The choice of the 95th-percentile threshold parallels the use of a significance level of
\alpha=0.05
in hypothesis testing. This threshold provides a general criterion to determine whether a given activation is considered in-distribution (if the distance lies below the threshold) or out-of-distribution (if it lies above).

3.2.  Optimal Steering Factor Determination

Optimization Problem

Following Equation
1
, we define the steering factor
\alpha_{l,p}
at layer
l
and token position
p
as the largest value such that the steered activation remains consistent with the distribution of the target class. Concretely, if we want to steer an example from the negative to the positive class, the problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle{\alpha_{l,p}}

\displaystyle=\max\quad\alpha,

(3)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad d_{M^{+}}^{l}\big(PCA(\mathbf{h}_{l,p}+\alpha\mathbf{v}_{l})\big)^{2}\leq\epsilon_{l}^{2}

where
d_{M^{+}}^{l}
denotes the Mahalanobis distance to the positive distribution in the PCA-reduced space at layer
l
. We then derive a closed-form expression of
\alpha_{l,p}
from Equation
3
to compute the optimal steering factor at each token position.

Closed-Form Solution.

As shown in Figure
2
, two types of solutions can be derived from Equation
3
, depending on wether or not the line of action of the steering vector intercepts the target distribution. These solutions can be expressed as follows:

\alpha=\begin{cases}\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a},&b^{2}-4ac\geq 0,\\[4.0pt]
-\frac{b}{2a},&b^{2}-4ac<0,\end{cases}

(4)

where

\displaystyle a=\|M\mathbf{v}\|^{2},\quad M=\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\,\mathbf{C}^{\top}.

(5)

\displaystyle b=2\,(M\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+^{-1}}PCA(\mathbf{h})-\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+}),

\displaystyle c=\|\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+^{-1}}PCA(\mathbf{h})-\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{pca}}^{+}\|^{2}-\epsilon^{2},

Indeed, let the target distribution
\mathcal{D}
be characterized by mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and positive definite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, which admits a Cholesky decomposition
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
. The Mahalanobis distance of a point
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathcal{D}
can be expressed as:

d_{M}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}=\big\|\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\big\|,

(6)

Similarly, let PCA project a vector
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\text{pca}(\mathbf{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, given by:

\displaystyle PCA(\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{h}-\boldsymbol{\mu})

(7)

\displaystyle\implies PCA(\mathbf{h}+\alpha\mathbf{v})=PCA(\mathbf{h})+\alpha\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{v},

(8)

Combining these results, constraint
3
becomes:

\displaystyle d_{M}^{\text{pca+}}(PCA(\mathbf{h}+\alpha\mathbf{v}))^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\implies\big\|\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}PCA(\mathbf{h})+\alpha M\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}_{\text{pca}}\big\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

where
\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+}
is the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix for the positive class distribution (
\mathcal{D}^{+}
) after PCA projection. This inequality defines a one-dimensional quadratic constraint with closed-form expression highlighted in equation
4
.

3.3.  Layers Selection

In the third step, after determining the optimal value of the steering factors, we introduce mechanisms to insure that the interventions are performed on layers with high discriminative power, while ensuring that the propagation of these interventions across layers stays in-distribution.

Steering Layer Selection Based on F1-Score Performance

To assess whether an intervention should be applied at layer
l
for a given behavior, we evaluate the discriminative power of the corresponding steering vector
\mathbf{v}^{l}
using the F1-score. Specifically, once a steering vector is computed, it is employed as a classifier on the dataset from which it was derived, and the resulting F1-score is recorded. We then impose a threshold of 0.7 on this metric: an intervention at layer
l
will be performed during steering only if the associated F1-score exceeds this threshold. Performance metrics for other values of this F1-score threshold are presented in Section
4.2
.

Metrics.

We measure the impact of steering on aligning previously non-aligned answers. The definition of a non-aligned answer depends on the task: incorrect predictions in classification tasks, or responses failing to exhibit the desired behavior in multi-choice instruction following and open-ended text generation tasks. Following
14
, we evaluate steering methods using the
Steering Performance Impact
(SPI), which quantifies the proportion of unaligned answers corrected by steering when positive, and the proportion of aligned answers wrongfully changed when negative:

\mathrm{SPI}=\begin{cases}\frac{\bar{A}-A}{1-A},&\text{if }\bar{A}>A,\\[6.0pt]
\frac{\bar{A}-A}{A},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(9)

where
\bar{A}
(resp.
A
) denotes the proportion of aligned answers with (resp. without) steering. In the open-generation setting, we evaluate both alignment success and model confidence. We rely on GPT-4.1 to automatically assess the proportion of generations that exhibit the targeted behaviors. We also compute perplexity (PPL) to evaluate the text plausibility.

5.  Discussion

This paper focused on directional steering by moving along a steering vector in the representation space. Recent work has shown that rotating activations in the representation space (angular steering) can outperform unidirectional steering
32
. However, that work does not guarantee that angular steering interventions remain within the data distribution. Extending our approach to angular steering represents a promising direction for future improvement.
We also plan to apply
IDS
to generate more truthful rationales when answering questions that require complex reasoning. This could enhance the performance of recently proposed methods that answer questions in an
explain-then-predict
fashion
6
;
36
;
33
;
38
. Additionally, applying steering during the inference of reasoning models
12
could lead to improved performance on complex tasks.

6.  Conclusion

This work introduces
IDS
, an activation steering methods addressing the fundamental challenge of balancing behavioral control and text coherence in language model text generation. By dynamically adjusting intervention strength based on the input’s position within the target behavior’s activation distribution,
IDS
prevents over and under-steering.

Our experimental evaluation across six language models and seven datasets demonstrates
IDS
’s superior performance compared to established competitors including
CAA
and
MERA
, with consistently optimal Steering Performance Impact (SPI) scores while maintaining text plausibility (perplexity).
IDS
theoretical basis, i.e. modeling activation distributions using PCA and Mahalanobis distance measures, provides a sound approach with a closed-form solution that enables efficient real-time computation.

IDS
enables fine-grained control over LLM behavior while maintaining output quality, making it valuable for both alignment research and practical deployment. It allows stronger safety interventions without losing coherence and adapts in real time to diverse inputs. This positions
IDS
as a practical tool for deploying more controllable and reliable language models in high-stakes applications.

8.3.  Closed-Form Solution

Closed-Form Solution in the Euclidean Case

If we simplify the problem by replacing the Mahalanobis distance with the Euclidean distance to the center of the target distribution in the original space, the optimization becomes considerably easier. Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}
denote the mean of the positive-class distribution. The problem reduces to:

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\max\quad\alpha,

(10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\|\mathbf{h}+\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}\|^{2}<\epsilon^{2}.

(11)

This is a convex quadratic constraint, equivalent to solving for the largest feasible root of the polynomial

f(\alpha)=a\alpha^{2}+b\alpha+c,

(12)

with coefficients

a=\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2},\quad b=2\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\mathbf{h}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}),\quad c=\|\mathbf{h}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}\|^{2}-\epsilon^{2}.

The closed-form solution is obtained from the quadratic formula:

\alpha_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}.

Depending on the discriminant
\Delta=b^{2}-4ac
, three cases arise:

•

If
\Delta>0
, there are two real roots. The feasible region corresponds to the interval between the two roots, and
\alpha^{*}
is the larger root.

•

If
\Delta=0
, there is one real root, which corresponds to the unique feasible solution.

•

If
\Delta<0
, there is no feasible solution that places
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}
strictly inside the distribution. In this case, we take

\alpha^{*}=-\frac{b}{2a},

which minimizes the distance to the target distribution.

Extension to Mahalanobis Distance in PCA Space

When using the Mahalanobis distance in the PCA-reduced space, the problem remains conceptually equivalent to the Euclidean case, provided we account for the linear transformations induced by PCA and covariance normalization.

Let the target distribution
\mathcal{D}^{+}
be characterized by mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}
and positive definite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, which admits a Cholesky decomposition
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
. The Mahalanobis distance of a point
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathcal{D}^{+}
can be expressed as

\begin{split}Maha(\mathbf{x})&=\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+})}\\
&=\big\|\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+})\big\|,\end{split}

(13)

which reduces to a Euclidean norm after applying the linear transformation
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
.

Similarly, let PCA project a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
\text{pca}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, given by:

pca(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}),

(14)

where
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
contains the first
k
principal components. For a steered activation
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}
, we obtain:

pca(\mathbf{h^{\prime}})=pca(\mathbf{h}+\alpha\mathbf{v})=pca(\mathbf{h})+\alpha\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{v}.

(15)

Combining these results, the Mahalanobis constraint becomes

\displaystyle Maha^{\text{pca+}}(pca(\mathbf{h}+\alpha\mathbf{v}))^{2}<\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\implies\big\|\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}pca(\mathbf{h})+\alpha\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}_{\text{pca}}\big\|^{2}<\epsilon^{2}.

Thus, the problem reduces to the same quadratic form as in (
11
), with the substitutions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}\;\rightarrow\;\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}pca(\mathbf{h}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\;\rightarrow\;\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{v},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}\;\rightarrow\;\mathbf{L}_{\text{pca}}^{+^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{+}_{\text{pca}}.

This shows that the same closed-form reasoning applies in both the Euclidean and Mahalanobis settings, with only the appropriate linear transformations required.
