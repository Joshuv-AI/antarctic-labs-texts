Prognostic Impact of Gait Speed Changes During Hospitalization in Patients Undergoing Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation
Authors: Shinya Tajima, Kentaro Hori, Daichi Kobayashi, Kotaro Takizawa, Atsuko Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre- and post-hospitalization gait speed are known prognostic indicators in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI); however, the impact of changes in gait speed during hospitalization on prognosis remains unclear. This study investigated the effect of in-hospital gait speed changes on survival outcomes.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 1,082 patients (mean age 85 years) who underwent TAVI between 2013 and 2022. Gait speed was assessed at admission (pre-TAVI) and at discharge. Patients were classified into 4 groups based on baseline gait speed (normal/slow) and in-hospitalization change (non-decreased/decreased): Normal/Non-DG (n=420); Normal/DG (n=197); Slow/Non-DG (n=385); and Slow/DG (n=80). The primary outcome was post-discharge all-cause mortality. During a mean follow up of 3.1 years, 255 (24%) patients died. In a multivariable Cox model, compared with the Normal/Non-DG group, mortality risk was higher in the Normal/DG group (hazard ratio [HR] 1.905; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.230–2.951; P=0.004) and the Slow/DG group (HR 2.102; 95% CI 1.186–3.728; P=0.011), whereas the Slow/Non-DG group showed no significant difference (HR 1.373; 95% CI 0.927–2.034; P=0.114).ConclusionsA decline in gait speed during hospitalization was associated with a higher risk of post-discharge all-cause mortality. This association remained after multivariable adjustment, even among patients with normal gait speed at admission. These findings suggest that in-hospital gait speed change may serve as a clinically meaningful prognostic marker in patients undergoing TAVI.

Central Figure

Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) has been the standard treatment for aortic stenosis; however, for ineligible patients or those considered high risk, a less invasive procedure known as transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has been developed, and the number of cases using TAVI is increasing. TAVI is minimally invasive and allows for early discharge, and it has been proven to be non-inferior to SAVR in terms of survival prognosis.1,2

Physical function decline has been reported as a factor associated with survival prognosis after TAVI.3,4 One such assessment, gait speed, is simple, reliable, and widely used in clinical practice as it is also associated with the prognosis of older adults.5,6 A decline in preoperative gait speed or a decrease in gait speed post-discharge reportedly worsens survival prognosis after TAVI.7,8 In clinical practice, many patients experience a decline in gait speed during hospitalization for TAVI. However, no studies have investigated the association between changes in gait speed during hospitalization and survival outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the impact of changes in gait speed during hospitalization on survival prognosis.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center retrospective observational study conducted at the Sakakibara Heart Institute in Tokyo, Japan. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sakakibara Heart Institute (no. 24-016). An opt-out form approved by the Ethics Committee was used to obtain informed consent, and was posted on a bulletin board in the hospital.

Participants

We included 1,539 patients who underwent TAVI at the Sakakibara Heart Institute between October 2013 and October 2022. The exclusion criteria were: (1) unable to walk before hospitalization; (2) combined surgical procedure; (3) non-cardiac rehabilitation (CR) during hospitalization; (4) postoperative stroke; (5) transferred to another hospital due to other disease; (6) in-hospital death; (7) missing gait speed data; and (8) unable to follow up.

Clinical and Procedural Characteristics

Clinical information was collected from the medical records as follows. Clinical and functional characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), presence of comorbidity, history of heart failure hospitalization, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, EuroScore, laboratory data at admission (albumin concentration, estimated glomerular filtration rate, hemoglobin level), echocardiographic parameters at admission and discharge (left ventricular ejection fraction, aortic valve area [AVA], mean pressure gradient [PG], maximum velocity [Vmax]), postoperative delirium, postoperative hospital stay, postoperative day of first standing, postoperative day of first ambulation, total CR time during hospitalization, medications at discharge, and gait speed, Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) score, and Barthel Index (BI) assessed at both admission and discharge.

Procedural characteristics included type of approach (transfemoral, trans-subclavian, transaortic, transapical), procedure time, intra-procedural blood loss, ∆hemoglobin, red blood cell (RBC) transfusions, postoperative permanent pacemaker implantation (PMI), all vascular complications, and unplanned access/closure. ∆Hemoglobin was calculated as the pre-TAVI value minus the lowest in-hospital post-TAVI value.

Gait Speed

Gait speed was assessed at baseline (pre-TAVI) and at discharge by trained physical therapists specialized in CR. Baseline gait speed was generally measured on the day before the TAVI procedure (or 2 days before the procedure for patients admitted on a holiday). Discharge gait speed was measured either on the day before discharge or on the day of discharge. The test was conducted in a well-lit ward corridor using a standardized 5-m walkway protocol. The use of walking aids was permitted when necessary, and the same conditions (including walking aid use) were maintained between the baseline and discharge assessments. A 2-m acceleration zone before the 0-m line and a 2-m deceleration zone beyond the 5-m line were provided. Timing began when the first foot crossed the 0-m line and ended when the first foot crossed the 5-m line. Participants were instructed to walk at a pace that was comfortable. Each test was performed twice with sufficient rest between trials, and the fastest of the 2 results was used for analysis. Gait speed measurement in older adults has been reported to demonstrate high inter-rater reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient >0.9.5

The patients were classified into 2 categories according to their baseline gait speed based on previous studies:9,10 Normal walker (Normal) – gait speed ≥0.80 m/s; and Slow walker (Slow) – gait speed <0.80 m/s.

A clinically meaningful change in gait speed has been defined as 0.1 m/s.6 Based on this criterion, in-hospital gait speed change was classified into 2 groups: Non-decreased gait speed (Non-DG) – no decline in gait speed during hospitalization; and Decreased gait speed (DG) – a decline of ≥0.1 m/s during hospitalization.

Based on the baseline gait speed and change in gait speed during hospitalization, patients were classified into the following 4 groups: Normal/Non-DG – baseline gait speed ≥0.8 m/s, no decline in gait speed during hospitalization; Normal/DG – baseline gait speed ≥0.8 m/s, decreased gait speed during hospitalization; Slow/Non-DG – baseline gait speed <0.8 m/s, no decline in gait speed during hospitalization; and Slow/DG – baseline gait speed <0.8 m/s, decreased gait speed during hospitalization.

Clinical Outcome

The primary outcome of this study was all-cause mortality after discharge following TAVI. These events were investigated using medical records and telephone inquiries to patients or family members.

Cardiac Rehabilitation

CR after TAVI was performed in accordance with the guidelines for rehabilitation in patients with cardiovascular disease.11 CR was initiated on postoperative day 1 and continued until discharge under the supervision of physical therapists specialized in CR. The core components of the CR program were implemented as part of an institutional pathway throughout the study period, whereas the progression and exercise dose were individualized for each patient.

Supervised sessions were typically provided 1–2 times per day (approximately 20–40 min per session) and were adjusted according to each patient’s clinical status and exercise tolerance. On Sundays and holidays, patients were encouraged to continue mobilization (e.g., sitting out of bed, standing, and corridor walking) with support from ward staff, and additional supervised sessions were provided when feasible.

The program included early mobilization from bed, bedside standing and stepping exercises, and ambulation training within the hospital. Once independent ambulation was achieved, patients additionally performed progressive mobilization, aerobic exercise (e.g., cycle ergometer), and body weight resistance training in the CR room. Outside supervised sessions, patients were permitted and actively encouraged to ambulate independently within the ward as tolerated following safety confirmation and fall-prevention instructions.

Statistical Analysis

First, the clinical characteristics of the 4 groups classified based on baseline gait speed and gait speed during hospitalization were examined using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as median (quartiles) and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Second, Kaplan–Meier curves were used to create survival curves to compare the primary outcomes of the 4 groups, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the log-rank test. Last, Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed to examine the association between all-cause mortality after hospital discharge and changes in gait speed during hospitalization. In addition to the known prognostic factors, clinical factors that are considered important were included as adjustment factors: age, sex, BMI, presence of chronic kidney disease, albumin at admission, history of heart failure hospitalization, LVEF at admission, BI at admission, delirium, postoperative hospital stay, ∆hemoglobin, RBC transfusions, postoperative permanent PMI, and all vascular complications.12–17 To evaluate whether the association between in-hospital gait speed decline and mortality differed according to baseline gait speed, an interaction term between baseline gait speed and decline status was introduced into the multivariable Cox model. Baseline gait speed was treated as a continuous variable in the interaction analysis. All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 22.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level set at P<0.05.

Results

Clinical Characteristics

Of the 1,539 study participants, 457 were excluded based on the exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample size of 1,082 (Figure 1). Table 1 and Table 2 summarize the clinical and functional characteristics and procedural characteristics of the 4 groups stratified by baseline gait speed and in-hospital changes in gait speed. The median age of the study population was 85 years, and 65% were women. The median gait speed at baseline and discharge was the same at 0.84 m/s. Significant differences were observed in most variables among the 4 groups. In contrast, among the clinical and functional characteristics, no significant differences were observed in BMI, comorbidity excluding musculoskeletal disease, mean PG at admission, Vmax at admission, mean PG at discharge, or medications other than MRA. Furthermore, among the procedural characteristics, no significant differences were observed in the type of approach, postoperative PMI, or unplanned access/closure. Gait Speed and Clinical Outcome

During the mean follow up of 3.1±2.0 years, 255 (24%) patients died. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that changes in gait speed during hospitalization are associated with mortality in patients undergoing TAVI. Previous studies have primarily examined the association between gait speed measured at a single time point, such as at hospital admission or discharge, and clinical outcomes.7,8,18,19 In line with these reports, we also found that patients with slower pre-admission gait speed had significantly higher post-discharge mortality. However, the key finding of our study is that changes in gait speed during hospitalization differentiate outcomes, even among patients with an initially slow gait speed.

The association between in-hospital gait speed decline and mortality observed in this study should be interpreted as prognostic rather than causal. In-hospital gait speed decline is unlikely to represent a direct cause of mortality; rather, it may reflect a patient’s vulnerability to peri-procedural systemic stress, underlying disease severity, peri-procedural complications, or progression of frailty during hospitalization.

Nevertheless, gait speed is a simple and highly reproducible measure that is widely used to assess global physiologic reserve in older patients undergoing TAVI. Early identification of patients who experience in-hospital gait speed decline may facilitate post-discharge risk stratification. Furthermore, because such a decline may signal potentially modifiable vulnerability, it may prompt timely multidisciplinary interventions, including early mobilization, optimization of nutritional management, delirium prevention, and coordinated comprehensive care. In this context, in-hospital gait speed decline may serve as a clinically meaningful prognostic marker.

Because patients were stratified according to baseline gait speed and in-hospital change, regression to the mean may have influenced the observed changes, particularly among patients with higher baseline gait speed. Although such statistical regression could lead to an apparent decline among those with higher baseline values, the observed association between in-hospital gait speed decline and post-discharge mortality is unlikely to be explained solely by regression to the mean. First, we defined ‘decline’ using a clinically meaningful threshold (≥0.1 m/s), which exceeds typical random variability and may reduce misclassification due to minor fluctuations. Second, gait speed was measured twice at each time point using a standardized protocol by trained physical therapists specialized in CR, and the fastest of the 2 measurements was used to reduce the impact of random measurement error. Third, the association between in-hospital decline and mortality persisted after stratification by baseline gait speed (normal vs. slow), indicating that in-hospital decline had prognostic significance in both normal and slow walkers, and remained significant after multivariable adjustment.

Previous studies have shown that post-discharge changes in gait speed are associated with long-term outcomes in patients with cardiovascular disease. For example, in patients with heart failure, a decline in gait speed 6 months after discharge is linked to a higher risk of heart failure readmission and all-cause mortality.20 Similarly, in patients with coronary artery disease, those who improved their gait speed over a 3-year period experienced a reduction in subsequent hospitalization rates.21 Furthermore, in patients undergoing TAVI, a decline in gait speed 1 year after discharge is associated with increased all-cause mortality within 1 to 2 years post-procedure.22 In contrast, few studies have focused on gait speed changes during the brief period of hospitalization. In patients with heart failure, an improvement in gait speed during hospitalization is associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality.23 In the present study, similarly we found that patients whose gait speed declined during hospitalization were at a higher risk of post-discharge mortality. These findings suggest that not only post-discharge changes, but also functional changes occurring during hospitalization, may have prognostic significance.

One of the important findings of this study is that no significant difference in mortality was observed between the Normal/DG and Slow/Non-DG groups. Although baseline physical function differed between these groups, both the Normal/DG and Slow/DG groups remained significantly associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality after multivariable adjustment, whereas the Slow/Non-DG group was not. These results suggest that an in-hospital decline in gait speed may have greater prognostic significance than slow baseline gait speed alone. Although slow baseline gait speed has previously been reported to be associated with a poor prognosis,7 an important finding of the present study is that even among patients with slow baseline gait speed, maintaining gait speed during hospitalization may be associated with a lower risk of mortality. This underscores the importance of evaluating changes between 2 time points rather than relying solely on a single measurement.

An in-hospital decline in gait speed may result from multiple interrelated and multifactorial mechanisms. First, reduced physical activity during hospitalization is a plausible contributor. In patients undergoing TAVI, more than 8 h of bed rest per day is associated with hospital-associated functional decline,24 suggesting that maintaining adequate physical activity during hospitalization is critical to preserving physical performance. The average age of participants was 85 years, and older adults are particularly vulnerable to the effects of inactivity. A prior study in healthy older adults reported that just 10 days of bed rest led to a loss of 1.5 kg in total lean mass and 0.95 kg in lower limb muscle mass, which exceeded the losses observed in younger adults following 28 days of bed rest.25 Although TAVI is typically associated with the advantage of a relatively short hospital stay – with an average of 7 days in this study – our findings suggest that even such a brief period may lead to clinically meaningful decline and subsequent gait speed reduction in older adults. Second, peri-procedural stress and complications may contribute to gait speed decline. In patients undergoing TAVI, peri-procedural hemodynamic instability, bleeding, vascular complications, arrhythmias requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, and delirium may affect the functional recovery trajectories. These events can alter hemodynamics, physical activity levels, and metabolic status, potentially resulting in short-term reductions in walking ability. Third, systemic stress responses and metabolic or inflammatory factors may also play a role. Acute kidney injury, systemic inflammatory responses, reduced nutritional intake, dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, and pre-existing cognitive or psychological conditions may influence physical function and activity levels. However, these factors were not comprehensively assessed in the present study.

In the present study, we adjusted for peri-procedural factors that have been reported to be associated with prognosis in patients undergoing TAVI; nevertheless, in-hospital decline in gait speed remained significantly associated with all-cause mortality after discharge, regardless of baseline gait speed. These findings suggest that gait speed decline may not simply reflect a single peri-procedural complication, but rather might represent the cumulative impact of peri-procedural stress and individual vulnerability.

Taken together, the in-hospital decline in gait speed observed in the present study may represent an integrative marker reflecting systemic frailty, comorbidities, and peri-procedural vulnerability. Considering this multifactorial background, prevention of functional decline during hospitalization likely requires comprehensive peri-procedural management rather than a single intervention. In addition to early mobilization and individualized CR programs during hospitalization, the importance of multidisciplinary peri-procedural care – including nutritional management and delirium prevention – is suggested.

Exercise therapy improves gait ability and activities of daily living in patients undergoing TAVI, and it can be safely implemented even in older patients.26,27 In addition, resistance training, particularly focusing on the lower limbs, is effective in improving gait speed among older adults.28 These strategies may be useful in preventing gait speed decline in patients undergoing TAVI. Future prospective interventional studies are warranted to determine whether preventing or reversing in-hospital gait speed decline can improve long-term outcomes.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective cohort study, and the mean age of the participants was 85 years, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, because only patients who were able to undergo gait-speed assessment during hospitalization were included, and non-ambulatory patients, patients with stroke, in-hospital deaths, and those with missing gait-speed data were excluded, the study population may have been biased toward a relatively healthier cohort. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to the overall real-world TAVI population, particularly to frailer or more severely ill patients, may be limited. Second, we were unable to comprehensively assess several factors that may influence functional decline, including in-hospital physical activity, post-discharge lifestyle, acute kidney injury, inflammatory status, nutritional condition, dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, and baseline cognitive or psychological status. In addition, although baseline functional status was partially accounted for by adjustment for the BI, the SPPB, which more directly reflects physical frailty, was not included in the primary model because of substantial missing data. Therefore, residual confounding related to frailty may not have been fully eliminated. Another limitation is the possibility of reverse causality. Although we adjusted for several perioperative factors, perioperative complications and clinical deterioration may themselves have contributed to reduced mobility and subsequent gait-speed decline. Because not all perioperative events or their severity could be fully captured in this retrospective study, reverse causality cannot be completely excluded. Third, although all patients underwent rehabilitation according to the protocol at Sakakibara Heart Institute, we could not fully account for differences in rehabilitation exposure, including rehabilitation frequency, exercise intensity, day-to-day rehabilitation dose, and unsupervised physical activity. In addition, rehabilitation-related variables such as timing of mobilization and total CR time may themselves have been influenced by postoperative recovery, complications, and length of stay. Therefore, differences in rehabilitation delivery may have contributed, at least in part, to in-hospital gait-speed changes and the observed associations with mortality. Fourth, for the primary outcome of all-cause mortality, the exact cause of death could not be determined in some cases. In addition, rehospitalizations for heart failure at other institutions could not be captured completely; therefore, heart failure readmission was not evaluated as a clinical outcome. Last, although gait speed was assessed using a standardized protocol, repeated measurements during hospitalization may have been subject to day-to-day variability and measurement error. However, such residual measurement error would likely be non-differential with respect to mortality and would bias the results toward the null. Thus, measurement variability alone is unlikely to fully explain the observed associations.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing TAVI, those who experienced a decline in gait speed during hospitalization had a significantly higher risk of post-discharge all-cause mortality. Furthermore, even among patients with normal gait speed at admission, in-hospital gait speed decline remained associated with an increased risk of post-discharge all-cause mortality after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that changes in gait speed during hospitalization may serve as a clinically meaningful prognostic marker in patients undergoing TAVI.
