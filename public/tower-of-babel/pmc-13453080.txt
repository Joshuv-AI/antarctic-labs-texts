Stepwise diagnosis and minimally invasive management of obscure gastrointestinal bleeding secondary to a Meckel’s diverticulum: a case report
Authors: Junjie He, Wei Liu, Ling Tong, Haiyan Yang, Guangjie Li, Lanhua Chen, Yunhai Liao
Published: 2026

Introduction

Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) is defined as persistent or recurrent bleeding from the gastrointestinal (GI) tract with no identifiable source after standard esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy. The term “suspected small bowel bleeding” has been increasingly adopted in contemporary guidelines to describe this entity more precisely, though “OGIB” remains widely used in clinical practice. It accounts for approximately 5% of all GI bleeding cases, with up to 80% of these lesions originating in the small bowel [1]. In young adults, conditions such as Meckel's diverticulum or non-Meckel's small bowel diverticula are significant causes of lower GI hemorrhage, frequently complicated by ulceration from ectopic gastric or pancreatic tissue [2].

Locating these lesions is technically demanding for gastroenterologists due to the length, redundancy, and active peristalsis of the small intestine. Historically, unresolved overt OGIB often required surgical exploratory laparotomy, which is associated with greater tissue trauma, prolonged postoperative ileus, and potential over-resection of the bowel [3]. With advancements in medical technology, the diagnostic paradigm has shifted toward precise endoscopic and radiological localizations [4]. Here, we describe a case of severe OGIB in a young man where a stepwise diagnostic approach—utilizing computed tomography angiography (CTA) as a structural guide and double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) for definitive diagnosis and endoscopic marking—led to a targeted, minimally invasive surgical intervention.

Case report

A 25-year-old previously healthy Chinese man presented to the emergency department with a 20-h history of passing melena, accompanied by generalized fatigue and dizziness. He denied hematemesis, abdominal pain, or a history of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use. His surgical history was notable only for an appendectomy performed 20 years prior. On admission, his vital signs were stable (blood pressure: 122/70 mmHg, heart rate: 77 beats/min). Physical examination revealed a flat, soft abdomen with normal bowel sounds. Digital rectal examination confirmed the presence of melena. Initial laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin level of 12.6 g/dL, which decreased to 11.6 g/dL over the next 24 h. Liver enzymes were mildly elevated, but coagulation profiles were normal.

Given the initial presentation of melena, an upper GI bleed was suspected. An urgent EGD revealed chronic non-atrophic gastritis with bile reflux but no evidence of recent hemorrhage or varices. Shortly after the EGD, the patient developed recurrent hematochezia (approximately 50–100 mL of dark and bright red blood per episode), indicating a rapid transit distal bleeding source. An urgent colonoscopy was attempted on hospital day 3. However, the procedure was aborted as the colonic lumen was entirely filled with solid stool, blood clots, and coffee-ground fluid, precluding safe advancement and mucosal visualization.

To non-invasively identify the bleeding source before further endoscopic attempts, an abdominal CTA was performed. Given the patient's recurrent hematochezia and the need for urgent evaluation, CTA was prioritized over a technetium-99 m pertechnetate scintigraphy (Meckel scan) due to its rapid acquisition and broad diagnostic capability. The imaging revealed a blind-ending diverticular structure with inflammatory wall thickening and segmental enhancement in the distal ileum (Fig. 1a). Notably, no active contrast extravasation was observed at the exact time of the scan, likely due to the intermittent nature of the hemorrhage. Mild stenosis at the origin of the celiac trunk was also noted. Based on the anatomical roadmap provided by the CTA, the patient was prepared for a targeted retrograde DBE. The enteroscope was advanced retrogradely to approximately 110 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve. At this location, it clearly revealed a Meckel's diverticulum associated with multiple irregular, large ulcers with stigmata of recent bleeding (Fig. 1b). To facilitate the subsequent surgical intervention, the lesion was marked with endoclips (Fig. 1c).

The endoclips were deployed strictly for preoperative localization, as no active spurting vessel was identified during the examination. Following the DBE procedure, the bleeding ceased spontaneously. Despite the earlier episodes of hematochezia, the patient's hemoglobin level decreased only mildly from 12.6 g/dL to 11.6 g/dL over 24 h. He maintained hemodynamic stability through intravenous fluid resuscitation alone, and no blood transfusion was required. This spontaneous cessation of hemorrhage and sustained clinical stability allowed the medical team to optimize the patient's condition and schedule an elective, targeted surgery on hospital day 9, which potentially avoided the need for an emergent laparotomy.

On hospital day 9, the patient underwent a laparoscopic segmental small bowel resection. During intraoperative exploration, the surgeon identified a 2 × 2 × 2 cm diverticulum at the anti-mesenteric border of the distal ileum, in the region indicated by the preoperative endoscopic clip markings (Fig. 2a). The clips were localized by gentle intraoperative palpation of the bowel wall with laparoscopic instruments. This localization facilitated a limited, tissue-sparing small bowel segment resection, and a primary side-to-side anastomosis was fashioned. Histopathological examination confirmed a Meckel's diverticulum containing ectopic gastric mucosa. This ectopic tissue was accompanied by adjacent mucosal ulceration and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, confirming the pathophysiological mechanism of the bleeding (Fig. 2b).

Postoperatively, an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol was implemented. Specifically, this included early ambulation on postoperative day 1, early removal of the urinary catheter, an opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia regimen, and the early introduction of oral liquids. This minimally invasive approach and protocol allowed for a rapid return of bowel function, minimal opioid reliance, and a prompt transition to an oral diet. The patient was discharged uneventfully on postoperative day 8. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of symptoms without recurrence of GI bleeding (Table 1).

Discussion

The diagnosis of OGIB requires a systematic, escalating diagnostic algorithm. This case underscores the dynamic relationship between clinical presentation and the origin of GI bleeding. Initially, the patient presented with melena, a symptom traditionally associated with upper GI sources. However, bleeding from the right colon or small bowel can also present as melena if the intestinal transit time is prolonged [5]. The subsequent rapid transition to hematochezia appropriately shifted the clinical suspicion to the mid-to-lower GI tract.

When active lower GI bleeding precludes a clear colonoscopic evaluation, the diagnostic algorithm must be carefully tailored. In young patients, while a Meckel scan is a classic non-invasive tool for detecting ectopic gastric mucosa, its utility is limited during acute overt hemorrhage where time is of the essence. Similarly, capsule endoscopy is highly valuable for stable patients but is often non-diagnostic during active hematochezia due to blood obscuring the visual field. Therefore, non-invasive vascular imaging becomes an essential triage tool. CTA can detect extravasation rates as low as 0.3 mL/min and offers an excellent structural roadmap, even when showing only inflammatory changes or structural anomalies (as in our case) rather than active extravasation [6]. In our case, CTA accurately localized the abnormality to the distal ileum, which prevented a potentially negative or overly broad surgical exploration. Following regional identification by imaging, device-assisted enteroscopy serves as an effective diagnostic tool for evaluating small bowel pathologies [7]. In young patients, Meckel's diverticulum typically presents at the distal ileum and can harbor ectopic gastric mucosa, leading to adjacent mucosal ulceration and painless, severe hemorrhage [8].

This case highlights the practical value of multidisciplinary collaboration between gastroenterologists, radiologists, and surgeons in modern clinical practice. Extensive exploratory laparotomy for OGIB often leads to unnecessary bowel handling and occasionally excessive bowel resection leading to malabsorption. By utilizing DBE for definitive diagnosis and placing endoclips prior to surgery, we facilitated intraoperative lesion identification. This transition from the endoscopy suite to the operating room facilitated a minimally invasive laparoscopic resection and may have contributed to the patient's overall recovery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, for young patients presenting with recurrent overt obscure gastrointestinal bleeding, small bowel diverticular disease should remain high on the differential. A multidisciplinary stepwise algorithm—employing CTA for anatomical triage followed by targeted device-assisted enteroscopy and endoclip marking—may be a useful strategy in selected patients. In the present case, this approach facilitated lesion localization and enabled targeted, tissue-sparing laparoscopic resection.

Patient perspective

"I was extremely anxious when I experienced sudden and significant bleeding. The medical team explained every step of the diagnostic process clearly. I am very grateful for the advanced endoscopic techniques and the minimally invasive laparoscopic surgery, which resulted in a very small incision and allowed me to recover and eat normally much faster than I expected. Since my discharge, I have returned to my normal life without any further bleeding episodes."
