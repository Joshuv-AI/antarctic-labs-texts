Singularity of the axisymmetric stagnation-point-like solution within a cylinder of the 3D Euler incompressible fluid equations
Yinshen Xu 1, Miguel D. Bustamante 1

Abstract
In this paper we investigate analytically the formation of finite-time singularities in the three-dimensional incompressible Euler equations under the specific ansatz of Gibbon, Fokas, and Doering’s vorticity-stretching model ( Gibbon et al. 1999 ) , within a bounded cylindrical domain and under axisymmetric conditions. We derive explicit Lagrangian solutions for the vorticity, its stretching rate, fluid pathlines and velocity components, by utilizing constants of motion associated with the system’s field-dependent infinitesimal continuous symmetries. The central finding is that the existence and nature of a finite-time singularity are determined exclusively by the local geometric structure of the initial vortex stretching rate in the vicinity of its global minimum point. The analysis reveals that whether a singularity forms in a finite time depends on how “flat” the initial vortex stretching rate profile is near the minimum point. Specifically, we demonstrate that the flatter the initial stretching rate is near its minimum, the later the potential singularity occurs, with a sufficiently flat profile completely suppressing a finite-time blowup. For stretching-rate profiles that approach the minimum with a power-law behavior, critical thresholds for the power-law exponent are identified, which separate regular solutions from those that develop a finite-time singularity. These thresholds differ depending on whether the singularity occurs at the cylinder’s centre or on a ring away from the centre, with minima at the centre requiring higher “flatness” to avoid a finite-time singularity of the solution. This work provides a rigorous analytical framework that elucidates how the local geometric structure of the initial conditions governs the potential for singularity formation in 3D fluid flows, offering fundamental insights into the interplay between symmetry, initial data, and the development of extreme events in idealised turbulence.

Introduction

In fluid mechanics, the conservation of momentum for an inviscid, adiabatic fluid is elegantly expressed by Euler’s equation—a cornerstone of the field whose significance cannot be overstated. Since its formulation in 1761 by Leonhard Euler, this equation has had a profound and lasting impact on mathematics, physics, and engineering
(
Euler 1761
)
. We define a
3D incompressible Euler fluid
by its velocity and pressure fields. Let

\bm{u}:\Omega\times[0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

be a vector field and

p:\Omega\times[0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a scalar field, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the spatial domain and
[0,T)\subseteq[0,\infty)
represents the time domain, with
T>0
. Because we will consider classical solutions and explicit formulas where the singularity time is computable in terms of the initial condition, we will assume these fields to be of class
C^{k}
piecewise in terms of spatial derivatives, where
k
is appropriately chosen. If

\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}=-\nabla p\,,\qquad\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0\,,\qquad\text{for \quad all}\qquad\bm{x}\in\Omega\,,\quad t\in[0,T)\,,

(1)

where
\nabla
is the gradient operator, then
\bm{u}
and
p
represent the velocity and pressure fields of a 3D incompressible Euler fluid, and equation (
1
) is referred to as the
3D Euler equation for an incompressible fluid
(the incompressibility condition is
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0
).

Another field playing an important role in this research is the vorticity
\bm{\omega}:=\nabla\times\bm{u}
, which satisfies a dynamical equation that is obtained by taking the curl of the Euler equation (
1
):

\frac{\partial\bm{\omega}}{\partial t}+\left(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{\omega}-\left(\bm{\omega}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{u}=0\,,\qquad\nabla\cdot\bm{\omega}=0\,,\qquad\text{for \quad all}\qquad\bm{x}\in\Omega\,,\quad t\in[0,T)\,,

(2)

which is the so-called Helmholtz vorticity equation. From its definition as a curl, it is obvious that
\nabla\cdot\bm{\omega}=0
.

One of the most intriguing topics in mathematical fluid dynamics is whether a finite-time singularity emerges in the solution of the 3D Euler fluid equations (
1
). Many numerical experiments have revealed the existence of a finite-time singularity, see for example
Gibbon 2008
;
Luo & Hou 2014
;
Choi et al. 2017
;
Larios et al. 2018
;
Hou 2023
. A milestone of the investigation of the regularity of the solution to the 3D Euler equations is the so-called BKM theorem
(
Beale et al. 1984
)
which points out that the convergence of the time integral of the supremum norm (over the spatial domain) of vorticity is a necessary and sufficient condition for 3D Euler fluids to maintain regularity. Subsequently,
Constantin et al. 1996
proved that the finite norm of the velocity field along with vorticity direction vectors of class
C^{1}
constitute a necessary condition to keep 3D Euler flows regular within a finite time. Over the years, considerable research has been conducted on the study of regularity properties and local well-posedness of the 3D Euler fluid equation, which has been greatly summarised by
Drivas & Elgindi 2023
. Generally speaking, using a more universal model will broaden the applicability of the results, but it also implies that the description of the behavior of a specific Euler solution will be more abstract. By sacrificing some universality, such as introducing some symmetry class or other restrictive class, some special solutions may be expressed in a more explicit way, making it easier for us to understand the various behaviors of the solutions, including the properties of potentially singular behaviors. See the studies with axisymmetry
(
Elgindi & Jeong 2020
)
, self-similarity
(
Martínez-Argüello & Rica 2024
)
, and octahedral symmetry
(
Elgindi & Jeong 2021
)
, for instance.

When we further impose a simplifying ansatz, the properties of the Euler flows may even be manifested by explicit equations and solutions. One such ansatz is the stagnation-point-type flow proposed by Gibbon, Fokas and Doering
(
Gibbon et al. 1999
)
, featuring the vortex stretching rate
\gamma(x,y,t)
(see also
Stuart 1988
and
Lundgren 1982
):

\bm{u}(x,y,z,t)=\left(u_{x}(x,y,t),u_{y}(x,y,t),z\gamma(x,y,t)\right),

(3)

where

\displaystyle(x,y,z,t)\in\Omega_{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times[0,T)\,,\quad\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(4)

In the following, we will name this “Gibbon
et al.
’s vorticity-stretching model”, or simply “Gibbon’s model”. Evidently, system (
3
) contains infinite energy, suggesting that if a finite-time singularity occurs then it will do so not at a point but on a one-dimensional manifold of the spatial domain. Physically speaking, this singularity with infinite energy demonstrates a change in some topological structure or the breakdown of the stagnation-type ansatz in (
3
). In what follows we list previous studies that, to the best of our knowledge, are relevant to Gibbon’s stagnation-type model.

As early as 1989, Childress, Ierley, Spiegel and Young exhibited blowup in their numerical analysis of two-dimensional stagnation flow
Childress et al. 1989
. As for the three-dimensional case, equation (
3
), the first numerical experiment was implemented by
Ohkitani & Gibbon 2000
, discovering the finite-time blowup for specific initial conditions and finding the approximate inverse proportional relation
\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in\Omega_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\gamma(x,y,t)|\propto(T_{*}-t)^{-c}
with
c\approx 1
, where
T_{*}
denotes the singularity time and
\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the
2
-torus. Shortly afterwards, an analytic Lagrangian solution for the stretching rate
\gamma(x,y,t)
was given by
Constantin 2000
in the same case
\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{T}^{2}
. In the limiting case
\Omega_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
of an infinite domain, Gibbon, Moore and Stuart obtained, in the case of zero plane vorticity
\omega
, explicit axisymmetric solutions for
\gamma
and pathlines that indeed have finite-time singularity
(
Gibbon et al. 2003
)
. All these solutions find their generalization in the work of
Bustamante 2022
which introduces a rich Lie-algebra structure of infinitesimal contemporaneous symmetries, based on which a general Lagrangian solution of stretching rate and vorticity are attained analytically. Also, to model the corrections that would represent a finite-energy solution, Mulungye, Lucas and Bustamante put forward a pressure field modified on the second derivative in the vertical direction while the Lagrangian solutions are still obtained analytically in terms of a modification parameter
(
Mulungye et al. 2015
)
. Detailed analysis of the existence and asymptotic behaviors of the singular solutions to the 3D Euler fluid equation under this modified pressure field are illustrated, where the asymptotic form of
\gamma
is certainly derived as
\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\gamma(x,y,t)|\propto(T_{*}-t)^{-1}

(
Bustamante 2022
;
Mulungye et al. 2015
;
Mulungye et al. 2016
)
.

Of note is the numerical experiment by
Luo & Hou 2014
of an axisymmetric solution of 3D incompressible Euler equation inside a cylinder (with a slip boundary condition), which disclosed a ring-like singularity located at the cylindrical boundary. Note also that a similar ring-like grow-up (in a domain without boundary
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
) was observed by Ohkitani and Gibbon in their numerical simulation of Gibbon’s model
(
Ohkitani & Gibbon 2000
, Initial condition 2)
. However, as we will explain at the end of section
4
, that configuration does not lead to a finite-time singularity because the local profile of the vorticity stretching rate is ‘flat’ enough at the ring.
Therefore, the axisymmetric solution of Gibbon et al.’s vorticity-stretching model for a domain with boundary is worthy of an investigation. Moreover, the existence of a finite-time singularity for the Gibbon et al.’s model is known to depend on the initial condition of stretching rate as an integral
(
Constantin 2000
;
Bustamante 2022
)
. Nonetheless, it is currently unclear which properties of initial stretching rate, local or global, algebraic or geometric, determine this dependence. On the basis of these views, the singularity of 3D axisymmetric Euler solution under the ansatz of Gibbon et al.’s vorticity-stretching model is discussed in this paper. The existence of the singularity in this case is, as we show in the following, only determined by the local properties of the initial stretching rate in the vicinity of its minimum point.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. In Section
2
, the Lagrangian solutions for the vorticity, its stretching rate and pathlines are derived explicitly, including a formula for the singularity time
T_{*}
. Moreover, new explicit Lagrangian solutions for the velocity field components are obtained, thanks to the axisymmetry assumption. Next, Section
3
discusses the existence of a finite-time singularity and the asymptotic behavior of the solutions: subsection
3.1
gives a couple of examples of “parabolic” initial stretching rate to exhibit how singularity behaves and to provide new explicit Eulerian solutions for the velocity field, the vorticity, its stretching rate and the pathlines; subsection
3.2
investigates the general case of initial stretching rate whose profile approaches the minimum value as a power law, with (possibly) kinks at the positions of the minimum, and how these power-law exponents determine singular behaviors. Finally, conclusions are given in Section
4
.

2
Axisymmetric Solution to the 3D Euler Incompressible Fluid Equations within Gibbon et al.’s Vorticity-Stretching Model Ansatz

In this section, we consider the three-dimensional incompressible Euler fluid under axisymmetric conditions, in the context of the ansatz from
Gibbon et al. 1999
, and derive explicit Lagrangian solutions (i.e., solutions along the pathlines) for the vorticity, vortex stretching rate, and the pathlines themselves, in terms of the initial conditions for the vorticity and the vortex stretching rate. A new feature arising from the axisymmetry condition is that implicit and explicit Lagrangian solutions for the velocity field components can be found. Moreover, Eulerian solutions for all these fields can be found for certain initial conditions. These solutions reveal the potential formation of singularities associated with the initial vortex stretching rate, which motivates the subsequent more detailed analysis of singularity behavior in Section
3
.

To take advantage of the axisymmetric conditions, we further restrict the geometry of the fluid to a cylindrical region. Specifically:

a)

Spatial Domain: The spatial coordinates are defined as
\bm{X}=(r,\theta,z)\in\Omega_{C}=[0,R]\times[0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}
, indicating that the fluid is bounded by a cylindrical region of radius
R
and extends infinitely along the vertical
z
-axis. For convenience, we adopt cylindrical coordinates throughout the discussion, where
\hat{r}
denotes the radial direction centred at the cylinder’s axis (
r=0
),
\hat{\theta}
represents the azimuthal direction oriented counterclockwise around the central axis, and
\hat{z}
is the vertical direction;

b)

Axisymmetry: The fluid is axisymmetric across the entire domain, meaning that both the velocity field
\bm{u}
and the pressure field
p
are independent of the azimuthal coordinate
\theta
. Mathematically, this implies:

\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\theta}=\bm{0},\quad\frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta}=0.

This setup ensures that all physical quantities depend only on the spatial coordinates
r,z,
and time
t
, simplifying the analysis while retaining the essential features of the problem.

2.1
Gibbon et al.’s Stagnation-point-like Solution: Axisymmetric Solution within a Cylinder for an Inviscid Incompressible Fluid

Gibbon, Fokas, and Doering proposed a fluid model—referred to as Gibbon’s vorticity-stretching model
(
Gibbon et al. 1999
)
—to describe the dynamics of vortical structures arising from vortex interactions. This model has been shown to admit singular Lagrangian solutions under
xy
-periodic boundary conditions. In this work, we extend its application to investigate the formation of singularities in solutions of the three-dimensional (3D) incompressible Euler equations under axisymmetric conditions within a cylindrical domain with one lid.

We consider an inviscid, incompressible 3D fluid confined within a cylindrical spatial domain
\Omega_{C}=[0,R]\times[0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}
, where the velocity field is assumed to take the following form:

\bm{u}(r,z,t)=u_{r}(r,t)\hat{r}+u_{\theta}(r,t)\hat{\theta}+z\gamma(r,t)\hat{z},\quad(r,\theta,z,t)\in\Omega_{C}\times[0,T),

(5)

with
T>0
denoting the maximal time interval over which the solution remains regular. The velocity field exhibits a stagnation plane at
z=0
, with radial and azimuthal components symmetric about this plane, while the vertical component is antisymmetric. Consequently, it suffices to analyze the fluid behavior in the subregion where
z\geq 0
.

To account for the cylindrical boundary, we impose the no-flow boundary condition at
r=R
:

u_{r}(r=R,t)=0,

which ensures that the radial velocity vanishes at the cylindrical wall. No explicit boundary conditions are imposed on the azimuthal or vertical components of the velocity field at the
r=R
boundary.

For brevity, we refer to this configuration as the
ASCII flow
, an acronym for
Axisymmetric Stagnation-point-like solution inside a Cylinder of the Inviscid Incompressible 3D fluid equations
. We believe that such an acronym reflects the unique character of the solution.

Given the velocity field defined above, the corresponding vorticity field
\bm{\omega}=\nabla\times\bm{u}
is computed as:

\bm{\omega}(r,z,t)=z\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial r}(r,t)\hat{\theta}+\omega(r,t)\hat{z},

(6)

where the axial component
\omega(r,t):=\bm{\omega}\cdot\hat{z}
represents the vorticity restricted to the plane
z=0
, referred to as the
plane vorticity
. It is explicitly given by:

\omega(r,t)=\frac{\partial u_{\theta}}{\partial r}(r,t)+\frac{u_{\theta}(r,t)}{r}.

(7)

Moreover, the incompressibility condition
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0
leads to the relation:

\gamma(r,t)=-\frac{\partial u_{r}}{\partial r}(r,t)-\frac{u_{r}(r,t)}{r}.

(8)

Remark 2.1.1

The term “vorticity stretching rate” (stretching rate, for short) for
\gamma
in relation to the plane vorticity
\omega
is justified by the following observation. By considering the two-dimensional restriction of the ASCII flow on the plane
z=0
from equation (
5
), we can define the three-dimensional vorticity as
\omega\hat{z}
and substitute it into the Helmholtz vorticity equation (
6
), resulting in

\frac{D\omega}{Dt}=\gamma\omega,

where
\frac{D}{Dt}
denotes the material derivative. In this context,
\omega
represents the vorticity of the reduced two-dimensional fluid. This equation reveals that the rate of change of
\omega
is directly proportional to
\omega
itself, with proportionality coefficient
\gamma
, which functions analogously to a zeroth-order strain tensor. Therefore, the quantity
\gamma
in Gibbon’s velocity model (
5
) is referred to as the stretching rate.

It is evident from equations (
8
) and (
7
) that they represent linear ordinary differential equations in
u_{r}
and
u_{\theta}
, respectively. Solving these yields explicit expressions for the radial and azimuthal velocity components:

u_{r}(r,t)=-\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\gamma(r^{\prime},t)r^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r^{\prime},\quad u_{\theta}(r,t)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\omega(r^{\prime},t)r^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}.

(9)

The velocity field is the centrepiece of the Eulerian description of a fluid. Combining equation (
9
) with the velocity form given in equation (
5
), we deduce that the Eulerian velocity field can be fully expressed in terms of the plane vorticity
\omega
, its stretching rate
\gamma
, and the vertical coordinate
z
. Consequently, the triplet
(\omega,\gamma,z)
captures all information necessary for describing the fluid dynamics within the Eulerian framework.

Taking the limit
r\to 0
in equation (
9
), and assuming
\gamma
and
\omega
are regular fields, we find that

u_{r}(r=0,t)=u_{\theta}(r=0,t)=0,

which ensures that no spatial singularity occurs at the axis of symmetry. Furthermore, due to the no-flow boundary condition
u_{r}(r=R,t)=0
, and the relation between
u_{r}
and
\gamma
in equation (
9
), we deduce that

\langle\gamma(\cdot,t)\rangle=0,

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the volume-averaged integral over the cylindrical domain:

\langle f(\cdot,t)\rangle:=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R}f(r,t)r\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(10)

Substituting the velocity and vorticity fields defined in equations (
5
) and (
6
) into the 3D Euler equation (
1
) and the Helmholtz vorticity equation (
2
), we derive the evolution equations for
\gamma
and
\omega
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial t}+u_{r}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial r}+\gamma^{2}=2\left\langle\gamma^{2}\right\rangle,\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+u_{r}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial r}-\gamma\omega=0.\end{cases}

(11)

We note that the system of partial differential equations (
7
), (
8
), (
11
) is invariant under the following scale transformation:

u_{r}\mapsto ku_{r}(kr,k^{2}t),\quad u_{\theta}\mapsto ku_{\theta}(kr,k^{2}t),\quad\omega\mapsto k^{2}\omega(kr,k^{2}t),\quad\gamma\mapsto k^{2}\gamma(kr,k^{2}t),

(12)

for any nonzero real constant
k
and arbitrary fields
(u_{r},u_{\theta},\omega,\gamma)
. This scale transformation provides a method to generate an infinite family of new solutions from a known solution through simple rescaling. Of course, this transformation may change the boundary conditions at
r=R
, while it respects those at
r=R/k
.

2.2
Solutions for Plane Vorticity, Stretching Rate, Pathlines, and Velocity Field

Analytical solutions for the stretching rate and plane vorticity can be derived by employing infinitesimal contemporaneous symmetries and associated constants of motion. This approach was first introduced by
Bustamante 2022
, where constants of motion are defined as conserved quantities in the Lagrangian framework, which plays a central role in the present study. Explicitly, under the context of axisymmetry, a scalar function
C(r,z,t)
is a constant of motion if it obeys the conservation law

\frac{dC}{dt}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}+u_{r}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}+u_{z}\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}=0,\qquad\text{for \quad all}\quad r\in[0,R],\quad z\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\in[0,T),

(13)

where
u_{r}
and
u_{z}
denote respectively the radial and vertical components of the velocity field.

The identification of symmetries and corresponding constants of motion is not the focus of this work, as it has been thoroughly explored by
Bustamante 2022
. Therefore, we do not elaborate on the derivation of infinitesimal symmetries or their use in constructing conserved quantities. Instead, we present three relevant constants of motion that are essential for our subsequent analysis:

\displaystyle C_{1}(r,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\omega(r,t)\dot{S}(t)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left[\ddot{S}(t)+2\gamma(r,t)\dot{S}(t)\right],

(14)

\displaystyle C_{2}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\dot{S}(t)^{2}}{\ddot{S}(t)+2\gamma(r,t)\dot{S}(t)}-S(t),

\displaystyle C_{3}(r,z,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}z\dot{S}(t)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left[\ddot{S}(t)+2\gamma(r,t)\dot{S}(t)\right].

Here, the time-dependent function
S(t)
is obtained via the solution to a second-order ordinary differential equation:

\ddot{A}(t)+2\left\langle\gamma^{2}\right\rangle A(t)=0,\quad\text{with}\quad A(t):=\dot{S}(t)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(15)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
, defined in equation (
10
), denotes the volume-averaged integral over the cylindrical domain.

It is straightforward to verify that these expressions satisfy the conservation law given in Equation (
13
), confirming their validity as constants of motion. Consequently, along particle trajectories, these quantities remain invariant:

\displaystyle C_{1}(r(r_{0},t),t)=C_{1}(r_{0},0),\quad C_{2}(r(r_{0},t),t)=C_{2}(r_{0},0),\quad C_{3}(r(r_{0},t),z(r_{0},z_{0},t),t)=C_{3}(r_{0},z_{0},0),

where
r(r_{0},t)
denotes the solution to
\mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}t=u_{r}(r,t)
with
r(r_{0},0)=r_{0}
, and
r_{0}
denotes the initial radial position, whereas
z(r_{0},z_{0},t)
denotes the solution to
\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}t=u_{z}(r,z,t)
with
z(r_{0},z_{0},0)=z_{0}
, and
z_{0}
denotes the initial vertical position.

Without loss of generality, we impose the initial conditions:

S(0)=0,\quad\dot{S}(0)=1,\quad\ddot{S}(0)=0.

(16)

Under these conditions, the Lagrangian solutions for the stretching rate
\gamma
and the plane vorticity
\omega
are obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma\left(r(r_{0},t),t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{0}(r_{0})\dot{S}(t)}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)}-\frac{\ddot{S}(t)}{2\dot{S}(t)},

(17)

\displaystyle\omega\left(r(r_{0},t),t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}(r_{0})\left[1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)\right]}{\sqrt{\dot{S}(t)}},

where
\gamma_{0}
and
\omega_{0}
denote the initial values of the stretching rate and plane vorticity, respectively:
\gamma_{0}(r):=\gamma(r,0)
and
\omega_{0}(r):=\omega(r,0)
.

These solutions explicitly describe the evolution of vorticity and its stretching rate in terms of the function
S(t)
, which governs the temporal dynamics of the system.

Remark 2.2.1

In addition to the initial conditions, the quantities
\gamma
,
\omega
, and
z
are all functions of a single time-dependent variable
S(t)
, which is a strictly increasing function of time
t
. The evolution of
S(t)
is governed by the following ordinary differential equation:

\dot{S}(t)=\left\langle\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{0}(\cdot)S(t)}\right\rangle_{0}^{-2},\quad S(0)=0,\quad\dot{S}(0)=1,

(18)

where the angular brackets denote the volume-averaged integral over the initial cylindrical domain:

\langle f(\cdot;t)\rangle_{0}:=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R}f(r_{0};t)\,r_{0}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{0}.

(19)

This average is taken over the Lagrangian coordinates
(r_{0},\theta_{0})
at the initial time, distinguishing it from the Eulerian average over current spatial coordinates. This ODE provides a means to compute
S(t)
explicitly, which in turn allows us to determine other physical quantities such as the stretching rate
\gamma
and vorticity
\omega
.

From equation (
18
), the second derivative of
S(t)
is:

\ddot{S}(t)=2\dot{S}(t)^{\frac{5}{2}}\left\langle\frac{\gamma_{0}(r_{0})}{(1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t))^{2}}\right\rangle_{0}.

(20)

The derivation of equation (
18
) will be presented in the proof of Theorem
1
below.

A fundamental aspect of the Lagrangian formulation of fluid dynamics is the determination of pathlines, which describe the trajectories of fluid particles over time. Based on the assumptions and velocity structure introduced earlier, we derive explicit expressions for the pathline functions:

Theorem 1
(Pathline Solution and Lagrangian Velocity Field)

Consider a three-dimensional incompressible Euler fluid with velocity field given by equation (
5
). Under the Lagrangian description, the radial, vertical, and angular components of the pathline are expressed as follows:

\displaystyle r(r_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\dot{S}(t)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left[\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{2r_{0}^{\prime}}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0}^{\prime})S(t)}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(21)

\displaystyle z(r_{0},z_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{0}\left[1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)\right]}{\sqrt{\dot{S}(t)}},

\displaystyle\theta(r_{0},\theta_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}+\left(\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\omega_{0}(r_{0}^{\prime})r_{0}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{r(r_{0},t^{\prime})^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right),

where
r_{0}=r(t=0)
,
z_{0}=z(t=0)
and
\theta_{0}=\theta(t=0)
denote the initial radial, vertical and angular positions of the fluid particle at
t=0
.

Furthermore, the Lagrangian velocity field can be derived from the pathline solutions and is given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle u_{r}(r(r_{0},t),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{(1+\gamma_{0}S)\ddot{S}-2\gamma_{0}\dot{S}^{2}}{(1+\gamma_{0}S)^{2}}r_{0}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}}{2\dot{S}^{\frac{3}{4}}\left(\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{2r_{0}^{\prime}}{1+\gamma_{0}S}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

(22)

\displaystyle u_{\theta}(r(r_{0},t),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\omega_{0}(r_{0}^{\prime})r_{0}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}}{\left[\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{2r_{0}^{\prime}\sqrt{\dot{S}}}{1+\gamma_{0}S}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}},

\displaystyle u_{z}(r(r_{0},t),z(r_{0},z_{0},t),t)

\displaystyle=z_{0}\gamma_{0}\sqrt{\dot{S}}-\frac{1}{2}z_{0}\ddot{S}\dot{S}^{-\frac{3}{2}}(1+\gamma_{0}S).

Proof:
The expression for
z(r_{0},z_{0},t)
is directly derived from the conservation law associated with the constant of motion
C_{3}
in equation (
14
). Thus, the core of the proof lies in determining the relationship between the radial and angular components of the pathline,
r(r_{0},t)
and
\theta(r_{0},\theta_{0},t)
, and their initial values.

To this end, we consider the Jacobian determinant of the transformation from
(r(r_{0},t),\theta(r_{0},t))
to the initial coordinates
(r_{0},\theta_{0})
, defined as:

J=\frac{\partial(r(r_{0},t),\theta(r_{0},\theta_{0},t))}{\partial(r_{0},\theta_{0})}=\begin{vmatrix}\frac{\partial r}{\partial r_{0}}&\frac{\partial r}{\partial\theta_{0}}\\
\\
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial r_{0}}&\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\theta_{0}}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}\frac{\partial r}{\partial r_{0}}&0\\
\\
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial r_{0}}&1\end{vmatrix}=\frac{\partial r}{\partial r_{0}},

(23)

where the relations
\frac{\partial r}{\partial\theta_{0}}=0
and
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\theta_{0}}=1
follow from axisymmetry.

Using the area-preserving identity:

\iint_{(r,\theta)\in\Omega}r\,dr\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\pi R^{2}=\iint_{(r_{0},\theta_{0})\in\Omega}Jr(r_{0},t)\,dr_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{0},

(24)

we derive the relation:

\left\langle J\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right\rangle_{0}=1,

(25)

where the angular brackets are defined in equation (
19
).

Differentiating the area identity with respect to time yields the evolution equation for the Jacobian:

\frac{\dot{J}}{J}=\frac{\partial u_{r}}{\partial r},\quad J(0)=1,

(26)

which is a first-order linear ordinary differential equation. Combining this with the expression for
J
in equation (
23
), we obtain the following partial differential equation for
r(r_{0},t)
:

\frac{r}{r_{0}}\frac{\partial r}{\partial r_{0}}=\frac{\sqrt{\dot{S}(t)}}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)}.

(27)

Solving this equation yields the radial pathline function
r(r_{0},t)
, consistent with the expression stated in Theorem
1
. Furthermore, by combining equations (
25
) and (
27
), we recover the ordinary differential equation governing
S(t)
, which was introduced in equation (
18
).

Additionally, using the relation
u_{\theta}=r\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}
from equation (
9
) and the Lagrangian solution for the plane vorticity
\omega
given in equation (
17
), we deduce the angular component of the pathline
\theta(r_{0},\theta_{0},t)
, matching the expression provided in Theorem
1
.

Finally, the proof of equations (
22
) follows directly from the definitions
u_{r}=\mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}t,u_{\theta}=r\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}t
and
u_{z}=\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}t
.

This completes the derivation of all components of the pathline under the Lagrangian description.
\square

Remark 2.2.2

The Eulerian solution can be obtained by expressing the initial position
\bm{X}_{0}
as a function of the current position
\bm{X}
and time
t
, i.e.,
\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{X}_{0}(\bm{X},t)
, and substituting this into the Lagrangian solution. However, for the ASCII fluid, the mapping
\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{X}_{0}(\bm{X},t)
is implicit due to the structure of the pathline equations (
21
). As a result, the Eulerian solution cannot generally be expressed in an explicit closed form. Nevertheless, under certain specific Lagrangian solutions, it is possible to derive explicit Eulerian expressions, one of which will be presented in section
3.1
.

Remark 2.2.3

From equation (
18
), we observe that the temporal evolution of the function
S(t)
depends solely on the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
. Consequently, Theorem
1
implies that each pair of Lagrangian vorticity and stretching rate
(\gamma,\omega)
uniquely determines a corresponding horizontal pathline
(r,\theta)
, a property analogous to that of the Eulerian description. For clarity, we define the pair
(\gamma,\omega)
as the
vorticity coordinate
. Based on this observation, once the vorticity coordinate is determined, the full pathline solution for the ASCII fluid becomes known. Therefore, our subsequent analysis will focus exclusively on the dynamics of the vorticity coordinate.

Remark 2.2.4

There are two notable special cases in the pathline solution (
21
) of the ASCII fluid:

a)

Pathlines starting at the centre
: If the initial radial position is
r_{0}=0
, then the radial component of the pathline satisfies:

r(r_{0}=0,t)=0,

(28)

indicating that such pathlines remain on the central axis (
r=0
) for all time;

b)

Pathlines starting at the boundary
: For pathlines originating from the cylindrical boundary at
r_{0}=R
, we obtain:

r(r_{0}=R,t)=R,\quad\theta(r_{0}=R,t)=\theta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\omega_{0}\rangle_{0}t.

(29)

This shows that boundary pathlines do not leave the boundary and rotate with a constant angular velocity equal to
\frac{1}{2}\langle\omega_{0}\rangle_{0}
. These properties are consistent with the no-flow boundary condition and the axisymmetric nature of the flow.

Remark 2.2.5

According to the pathline solutions given in equations (
17
) and (
21
), the vertical component of the pathline satisfies:

z(r_{0},z_{0},t)=-z(r_{0},-z_{0},t),

(30)

which indicates that the motion in the
z
-direction is symmetric about the plane
z=0
. In contrast, the radial and angular components
r(r_{0},t)
and
\theta(r_{0},\theta_{0},t)
are independent of
z_{0}
. This implies that pathlines starting from the same horizontal position (i.e., the same
r_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
) exhibit identical behavior in the
\hat{r}
and
\hat{\theta}
directions, regardless of their initial vertical position.

3
Singularity and Asymptotic Behavior of ASCII Fluid Fields

We observe that the stretching rate
\gamma
, as defined by equation (
17
), may potentially become infinite. This occurs when the condition

1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)=0

(31)

is satisfied for some
r_{0}
and
t
, even if the initial data for
\gamma
and
\omega
are smooth. The specific values of
r_{0}
and
t
at which this condition is met are referred to as the
blowup point
, denoted
r_{0}=r_{-}
, and the
singularity time
, denoted
t=T_{*}
, respectively. That is,

1+\gamma_{0}(r_{-})S(T_{*})=0.

(32)

The singularity time
T_{*}
can be either finite or infinite. An infinite value of
T_{*}
implies the absence of a finite-time singularity. Since
S(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
, the singularity condition
1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)=0
is first satisfied at the minimum point of the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
. Therefore, we conclude that
any blowup must occur at the minimum point(s) of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
.

This leads us to define the
blowup pathline
as the fluid trajectory starting from the minimum point
r_{0}=r_{-}:=\mathrm{argmin}(\gamma_{0})
. In other words, the location of the singularity is determined entirely by the structure of the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
, specifically its minimum value.

To further analyze the existence and magnitude of a finite singularity time
T_{*}
, we examine the relationship between
S(t)
and
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
. From the blowup condition above, we deduce the corresponding critical value of
S
at the singularity:

S_{*}:=S(T_{*})=-\frac{1}{\inf_{r_{0}\in[0,R]}\gamma_{0}(r_{0})},

(33)

where
\inf_{r_{0}\in[0,R]}\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
denotes the global minimum of the initial stretching rate over the domain
[0,R]
.

Using the evolution equation for
S(t)
given in equation (
18
), we derive an expression for the singularity time:

T_{*}=\int_{0}^{S_{*}}\frac{1}{\dot{S}}\,dS=\int_{0}^{S_{*}}\left\langle\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S}\right\rangle_{0}^{2}dS,

(34)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
denotes the volume average over the initial cylindrical domain is defined by equation (
19
).
From the ordinary differential equation governing
S(t)
, it follows that the function
S(t)
is completely determined by the initial profile of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
. A direct inspection of equation (
34
) confirms that the nature of the singularity—whether it forms in finite time or not—is also dictated solely by the properties of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
.

In particular, the singularity time satisfies the following scaling relation:

T_{*}(k\gamma_{0})=\frac{1}{k}T_{*}(\gamma_{0}),\quad k>0,

(35)

which shows that proportionally scaling the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
results in an inverse proportional change in the singularity time
T_{*}
. Thus, a larger (in absolute value) negative minimum of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
leads to an earlier blowup.

It is important to emphasise that our analysis assumes regular initial conditions. We begin by examining the case where
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
takes the form of an exact parabola, and then proceed to more general cases where
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
is continuous but not necessarily differentiable near the blowup point.

3.1
Parabolic Initial Stretching Rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})

In this subsection, we consider a specific example where the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
takes the form of an exact parabola satisfying the area-averaged condition
\langle\gamma_{0}\rangle=0
:

\gamma_{0}(r_{0})={f}\left(1-\frac{2}{R^{2}}r_{0}^{2}\right),

(36)

where
{f}\neq 0
is a constant. In this case, the minimum of
\gamma_{0}
occurs at either
r_{0}=0
or
r_{0}=R
, depending on the sign of
{f}
. This choice offers several advantages:

a)

The profile of
\gamma_{0}
is controlled by a single parameter
{f}
, and its minimum is clearly defined;

b)

The minimum and supremum of
\gamma_{0}
are negatives of each other, leading to the same value for the minimum:
\inf\gamma_{0}=-|{f}|
, regardless of whether
{f}
is positive or negative;

c)

The integrals involved in the Lagrangian solutions can be evaluated explicitly, simplifying the analysis.

Firstly, substituting this form of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
into the ODE governing
S(t)
given in equation (
18
), we obtain:

\dot{S}(t)=4{f}^{2}S(t)^{2}\left[\ln\left|\frac{1-{f}S(t)}{1+{f}S(t)}\right|\right]^{-2}.

(37)

It is important to note that changing the sign of
{f}
does not alter the solution of
S(t)
, as the right-hand side of the above equation remains invariant under such a transformation. Furthermore, from equation (
34
) we derive an explicit expression for the singularity time:

T_{*}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6|{f}|},

(38)

which confirms that the blowup occurs in finite time and depends inversely on the magnitude of
{f}
.

Next, we compute the corresponding Lagrangian solutions for the vorticity coordinate
(\gamma,\omega)
. Substituting the parabolic
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
from equation (
36
) into the general expressions for the Lagrangian vorticity and stretching rate given in equation (
17
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\gamma(r(r_{0},t),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{S}R^{2}}{2{f}S^{2}r_{0}^{2}-R^{2}S(1+{f}S)}+\frac{\dot{S}}{S}-\frac{\ddot{S}}{2\dot{S}},

(39)

\displaystyle\omega(r(r_{0},t),t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}(r_{0})\left(-\frac{2{f}S}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}r_{0}^{2}+\frac{1+{f}S}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}}\right).

The radial pathline function
r(r_{0},t)
and its inverse
r_{0}(r,t)
are derived as:

\displaystyle r(r_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{S}^{1/4}R}{\sqrt{2S}}\left[-\frac{1}{{f}}\ln\left|1-\frac{2{f}Sr_{0}^{2}}{(1+{f}S)R^{2}}\right|\right]^{1/2},\quad r_{0}(r,t)=\left[\frac{R^{2}(1+{f}S)}{2{f}S}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2{f}Sr^{2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}}\right)\right]^{1/2}.

(40)

The angular and vertical components of the pathline are expressed as:

\displaystyle\theta(\theta_{0},r_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}-\frac{2{f}}{R^{2}}\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\omega_{0}(r_{0}^{\prime})r_{0}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r_{0}^{\prime}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}\ln\left|1-\frac{2{f}S}{R^{2}(1+{f}S)}r_{0}^{2}\right|}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

(41)

\displaystyle z(r_{0},z_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2{f}Sz_{0}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}r_{0}^{2}+\frac{z_{0}(1+{f}S)}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}}.

Furthermore, the Eulerian solutions for
\gamma(r,t)
and
\omega(r,t)
can also be written explicitly. Substituting the parabolic
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
into the vorticity coordinate equations, we obtain:

\displaystyle\gamma(r,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{S}}{S(1+{f}S)}\exp\left(\frac{2{f}Sr^{2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}\right)+\frac{\dot{S}}{S}-\frac{\ddot{S}}{2\dot{S}},

(42)

\displaystyle\omega(r,t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}(r_{0}(r,t))\frac{1+{f}S}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}}\exp\left(-\frac{2{f}Sr^{2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}\right).

Using these expressions and substituting into equation (
9
), we derive the velocity components in the Eulerian frame:

\displaystyle u_{r}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{S}^{3/2}R^{2}}{4{f}S^{2}(1+{f}S)}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2{f}Sr^{2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\ddot{S}}{2\dot{S}}-\frac{\dot{S}}{S}\right)r,

(43)

\displaystyle u_{\theta}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+{f}S}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\omega_{0}(r_{0}(r^{\prime},t))r^{\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{2{f}Sr^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}r^{\prime},

\displaystyle u_{z}(r,z,t)

\displaystyle=z\left[-\frac{\dot{S}}{S(1+{f}S)}\exp\left(\frac{2{f}Sr^{2}}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}R^{2}}\right)+\frac{\dot{S}}{S}-\frac{\ddot{S}}{2\dot{S}}\right].

To further investigate the dynamical properties of this configuration, we now proceed to analyze the asymptotic behavior of the blowup pathline as
t\to T_{*}
. This will allow us to examine how the solution evolves near the singularity and determine the nature of the finite-time blowup. We will show that the blowup dynamics are governed by the local behavior of the initial stretching rate near its minimum point, and that the singularity manifests in a self-similar fashion in both the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions.

Theorem 2

If the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}
takes the form given in equation (
36
), with a nonzero constant
{f}
, then the blowup pathline exhibits the following asymptotic behavior in the Lagrangian framework:

\displaystyle\gamma(r_{0}=r_{-},t)\sim-\frac{1}{T_{*}-t},\qquad\omega(r_{0}=r_{-},t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\omega_{0}(r_{-})\sqrt{|f|(T_{*}-t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{W_{-1}\left(-\sqrt{|f|(T_{*}-t)}\right)},

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle S_{*}-\mathrm{e}^{2W_{-1}\left(-\sqrt{|f|(T_{*}-t)}\right)},

where
W_{-1}
denotes the negative branch of the Lambert
W
function, defined by the identity
W_{-1}(z)\mathrm{e}^{W_{-1}(z)}=z
, with
-\mathrm{e}^{-1}\leq z<0
and
W_{-1}(z)\leq-1
.

Proof:
As
t\to T_{*}
, along the blowup pathline we have:

1+\gamma_{0}S=1-|{f}|S=1-\frac{S}{S_{*}},

(44)

namely
S_{*}=1/|{f}|
. Using this relation together with equations (
37
) and (
20
), we derive the leading-order asymptotic expressions for the first and second time derivatives of
S(t)
:

\dot{S}(t)\sim 4\left[\ln\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)\right]^{-2},\quad\ddot{S}(t)\sim\frac{32\left[\ln\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)\right]^{-5}}{S_{*}-S}.

(45)

The explicit asymptotic solution for
S(t)
near the singularity time
T_{*}
can be obtained from the integral expression:

T_{*}-t=\int_{S}^{S_{*}}\frac{1}{\dot{S}}\mathrm{d}S,

(46)

which yields the leading-order approximation:

S(t)\sim S_{*}-S_{*}\mathrm{e}^{2W_{-1}\left(-\sqrt{|{f}|(T_{*}-t)}\right)}.

(47)

Substituting this into the Lagrangian expressions for
\gamma
and
\omega
given in equation (
17
), we compute the asymptotic behavior of the stretching rate along the blowup pathline:

\gamma(r_{0}=r_{-},t)=\frac{\gamma_{0}\dot{S}}{1+\gamma_{0}S}-\frac{\ddot{S}}{2\dot{S}}\sim-\frac{4\left[\ln\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)\right]^{-2}}{S_{*}-S}-\frac{4\left[\ln\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)\right]^{-3}}{S_{*}-S}\sim-\frac{1}{T_{*}-t}.

(48)

Similarly, for the plane vorticity we obtain:

\omega(r_{0}=r_{-},t)\sim\frac{\omega_{0}(r_{-})(1+\gamma_{0}S)}{\sqrt{\dot{S}}}\sim\frac{\omega_{0}(r_{-})\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)}{2\left|\ln\left(1-\frac{S}{S_{*}}\right)\right|^{-1}}\sim\omega_{0}(r_{-})\sqrt{|{f}|(T_{*}-t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{W_{-1}\left(-\sqrt{|{f}|(T_{*}-t)}\right)}.

(49)

The proof is complete.
\square

3.2
Initial Stretching Rate Continuous at the Minimum Point

To gain deeper insight into the relationship between the initial stretching rate and the singular behavior of the ASCII fluid, we consider a more general case in which the initial stretching rate
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
is assumed to be continuous in the neighborhood of its minimum point. For simplicity, we also assume that
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
has only one minimum point—hence, only one potential blowup point.

Suppose there exist positive real numbers
n_{-},n_{+}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
satisfies the following asymptotic condition near the minimum point:

\lambda_{\pm}=\lim_{r_{0}\to r_{-}^{\pm}}\frac{\gamma_{0}(r_{0})-{f}}{{f}|r_{0}-r_{-}|^{n_{\pm}}}\in(-\infty,0),

(55)

where
r_{-}\in[0,R]
denotes the location of the minimum point of
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
, and
{f}:=\gamma_{0}(r_{-})<0
is the minimum value (notice the abuse of notation: in section
3.1
we used
f
for either the minimum or the maximum of
\gamma_{0}
, whereas in this chapter
f
refers to the minimum value of
\gamma_{0}
, so it is necessarily negative). We will exclude the trivial case
{f}=0
as it can only occur if
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})\equiv 0
on
[0,R]
, due to the constraint
\langle\gamma_{0}\rangle=0
.

We then define the exponents
n_{-}
and
n_{+}
as follows:

a)

Let
n_{-}
be the positive number for which the limit
\lambda_{-}
in (
55
) (from the left) is finite and negative. If, in contrast, for all
n_{-}>0
, the limit vanishes (i.e.,
\lambda_{-}=0
), then we define

;

c)

Analogously,

is defined as the positive number for which the limit

in (

) (from the right) is finite and negative. If no such

exists, then we set
n_{+}=+\infty
.

This definition ensures that
n_{\pm}
characterise the leading-order algebraic rate at which
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
approaches its minimum from either side. In particular,
n_{\pm}<\infty
corresponds to power-law behavior near
r_{-}
, while
n_{\pm}=\infty
indicates that
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
approaches
{f}
faster than any power law—such as in the case of Gevrey-class regularity at
r_{-}
.

Accordingly, we can express
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
in a neighborhood of
r_{-}
via a power-law expansion:

\gamma_{0}(r_{0})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{(-)}(r_{0}):={f}\left[1+\lambda_{-}(r_{-}-r_{0})^{n_{-}}-(r_{-}-r_{0})^{n_{-}}\varepsilon_{-}(r_{-}-r_{0})\right],&r_{0}\in[0,r_{-}],\\
\\
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{(+)}(r_{0}):={f}\left[1+\lambda_{+}(r_{0}-r_{-})^{n_{+}}-(r_{0}-r_{-})^{n_{+}}\varepsilon_{+}(r_{0}-r_{-})\right],&r_{0}\in[r_{-},R],\end{cases}

(56)

where the functions
\varepsilon_{\pm}(x)
are bounded in a neighborhood of
x=0
and satisfy

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\varepsilon_{\pm}(x)=0.

(57)

When
r_{-}=0
, the left branch
\gamma_{0}^{(-)}
is not applicable and
n_{-}
is not defined; similarly, when
r_{-}=R
, the right branch
\gamma_{0}^{(+)}
is not applicable and
n_{+}
is not defined.

A useful quantity in what follows is the coefficient
n
that depends on
n_{-}
and
n_{+}
, and dictates whether or not blowup occurs and the way it occurs. We define

n
by:

n:=\begin{cases}n_{-}&\text{if}\quad r_{-}=R\,,\\
\max\{n_{-},n_{+}\}&\text{if}\quad 0<r_{-}<R\,,\\
n_{+}&\text{if}\quad r_{-}=0\,.\end{cases}

(58)

Remark 3.2.1

The cases
0<n<\infty
imply that
\gamma_{0}(r_{0})
approaches
{f}
algebraically from the corresponding side, with leading exponent
n
. On the other hand,
n=\infty
indicates super-polynomial flatness at
r_{-}
. This refined definition correctly identifies the ”flat” case with
n\to\infty
.

Note that the case
n=0
is excluded by construction, as we restrict to
n_{\pm}>0
, and the limit behavior for
n\to 0^{+}
does not correspond to constant behavior in a meaningful asymptotic sense. Instead, a truly flat minimum would manifest as
n=\infty
, consistent with infinite differentiability and absence of a leading power-law term.

By inspecting the structure of the Lagrangian solutions for the vorticity coordinate given in equation (
17
), as well as the expression for the singularity time
T_{*}
in equation (
34
), we observe that the key to analyzing these quantities lies in understanding the asymptotic behavior of the function
S(t)
. To this end, we recall the governing equations for

derived from (

\displaystyle\dot{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)}\right\rangle_{0}^{-2}=\frac{R^{4}}{4}\left[\int_{0}^{R}\frac{r_{0}}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)}\mathrm{d}r_{0}\right]^{-2},

(59)

\displaystyle\ddot{S}(t)

\displaystyle=2\dot{S}^{\frac{5}{2}}\left\langle\frac{\gamma_{0}}{(1+\gamma_{0}S)^{2}}\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{R^{8}}{8}\left[\int_{0}^{R}\frac{r_{0}}{1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)}\mathrm{d}r_{0}\right]^{-5}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\gamma_{0}(r_{0})r_{0}}{\left[1+\gamma_{0}(r_{0})S(t)\right]^{2}}\mathrm{d}r_{0}.

(61)

Notice that, because of the formula (

.
Therefore, a careful analysis of the convergence or divergence properties of

\Lambda_{2}(t)

). In other words,
an unsmooth or asymmetric profile of the initial stretching rate at the minimum point accelerates the formation of the singularity

We emphasise a crucial physical distinction between central and non-central minimum scenarios. While the central minimum occurs on a point, the non-central minimum occurs on a ring, namely a degenerate case when compared to the point case. The ring case, due to the axisymmetry, has a perfectly “flat” profile in the azimuthal direction, which therefore changes the asymptotics in favour of more regular behaviour, hence the required parameter
n
for regular behaviour is smaller compared with the central minimum case.

Finally we briefly address the question: What happens when the location of the minimum of the initial stretching rate is shared by several rings, or by a combination of rings and the central point? Namely, when the set
\{x\in\Omega\quad|\quad\gamma_{0}(x)=\inf\gamma_{0}\}
is the union of two or more elementary sets of the form
\{x\in\Omega\quad|\quad|x|=r_{-}\}
, with
r_{-}\geq 0
. In such a case, the dominant ring (or centre point) is the one for which the integral
\Lambda_{1}
grows faster asymptotically as
t\to T_{*}
. Thus, for example, if the minima of
\gamma_{0}
were at a ring with
n=2
and at the centre with
n=2
, then the asymptotic behaviour of the system would be dominated by the ring with
n=2
because the integral
\Lambda_{1}
would diverge due to the stretching rate’s profile at the ring, and thus the singularity time would still be
T_{*}=\infty
. As another example, a ring with
n=1
combined with a centre point with
n=4
would lead to no finite-time singularity because the integral
\Lambda_{1}
would diverge due to the stretching rate’s profile at the centre.

4
Conclusion

We have applied the vorticity-stretching ansatz by
Gibbon et al. 1999
on cylindrical domains with boundaries, to explore the potential formation of singularities in the three-dimensional incompressible Euler equations. The study incorporates axisymmetry, a cylindrical spatial domain, and no-flow boundary conditions. By employing the Lagrangian framework, explicit solutions for the planar vorticity, its stretching rate, and fluid pathlines are obtained through the use of conserved quantities—constants of motion—that remain invariant along particle trajectories. A key new result stemming from our axisymmetry assumption is that explicit solutions for the Eulerian velocity components can be obtained.

The analysis reveals that the key factor determining whether or not a singularity forms in finite time is the local behavior of the initial vorticity stretching rate near the position of its minimum. Specifically, the support of this minimum (whether it is at the centre point or at a ring) and the way the vorticity stretching rate behaves in its vicinity dictate the development of blowup in the solution. These are considered local properties of the initial data and do not depend on the global configuration of the flow.

Further investigation into the asymptotic behavior of the system shows that different power-law approximations of the initial vorticity stretching rate near the position of its minimum lead to distinct types of singularity. We identified two critical thresholds: one marking the transition from regular to singular solutions (in terms of the formula for the singularity time
T_{*}
), and another distinguishing different rates at which quantities converge or diverge near the singularity time.

The emergence of a ring-shaped blowup region at the “corner” boundary of a cylinder brings to mind the numerical observations in
Luo & Hou 2014
, where a ring-localised singularity was detected in the 3D axisymmetric Euler equations. Our theoretical framework, although in a different class of solutions with respect to the reflection
z\to-z
(in our case both
u_{r}
and
u_{\theta}
are even functions of
z
