Hypomelanotic melanoma of the mandibular gingiva: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shuying Liu, Yumeng Yang, Liyi Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Oral mucosal melanoma (OMM) is a rare and aggressive malignancy that exhibits distinct clinical and pathological characteristics compared to cutaneous melanomas. The hypomelanotic subtype of OMM is even rarer, with clinical signs and symptoms often being nonspecific, complicating the diagnostic process and contributing to a poor prognosis. Due to its rarity and the frequent delays in diagnosis, case reports are an invaluable source of information regarding this condition.Case SummaryA 78-year-old male presented with a painless mass on the left mandibular gingiva, which was initially misidentified as an inflammatory gingival lesion. After ineffective anti-inflammatory treatment, further investigation led to a confirmed diagnosis of hypomelanotic melanoma through histopathological and immunohistochemical examination.ConclusionEarly biopsy plays a critical role in establishing a definitive diagnosis for OMM. Histology combined with immunohistochemistry is the gold standard for diagnosing of hypomelanotic OMM.

Background

Malignant melanoma is an aggressive tumor that significantly threatens human health. It arises from melanocytes and nevus cells, primarily affecting the skin [1]. However, melanomas can also develop from mucosal tissues, which include areas such as the head and neck, anorectal region, vulvovaginal area, and urinary tract. Mucosal melanoma constitutes about 1.3% of all melanoma cases [2]. Among head and neck mucosal melanomas, the most frequent sites of origin are the nose and paranasal sinuses, with the oral cavity following closely behind [3].Oral mucosal melanomas (OMM) account for about 25% of all mucosal melanomas of the head and neck [4].

In comparison to typical cutaneous melanoma, there are significant differences in the site of onset as well as in etiological factors, clinical manifestations, treatment strategies, and prognosis. The etiological factors associated with OMM remain poorly understood. Potential contributing factors may include ill-fitting dentures, smoking habits, mechanical trauma, and family history.Mucosal melanoma is associated with a poor prognosis, with a reported 5-year overall survival rate below 30% in most series [5].

Hypomelanotic or amelanotic melanoma is a subtype of melanoma that includes both hypomelanotic melanoma, which is partially pigmented and light-colored, and amelanotic melanoma (AM), characterized by the absence of melanin pigmentation [6]. This variant is exceptionally rare, with amelanotic melanoma representing less than 2% of all melanoma cases [7]. The diagnosis presents considerable challenges, particularly in ceses involving amelanotic or hypopigmented lesions. Both clinically and microscopically, melanomas can be mistaken for other cancer or may even resemble benign tumors [8, 9].

We present a rare case of primary hypomelanotic malignant melanoma located in the mandibular gingiva and provide a review of relvelant literature on oral mucosal melanoma to enhance clinicians’ awareness of this condition.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old male presented to our clinic with a primary concern regarding a mass on the gingiva in the posterior area of the left mandible, along with swelling in the lower left jaw that had persisted for three days. Six months earlier, he had received removable denture restoration at another hospital which he reported using satisfactorily. He indicated that the mass had significantly increased in size within the last three days. Throughout this period, he denied any pain, fever, or additional discomfort. The patient has a decade-long history of well-controlled diabetes mellitus, managed through medication, and does not report any other systemic diseases, relevant family history, or known drugs or food allergies.

Upon clinical examination, the patient’s oral hygiene appeared satisfactory. The first, second, and third molars in the left mandible were missing. The edentulous area in the left mandible featured a pink, raised gingival tissue measuring approximately 1.5 × 1.5 × 2 cm, which exhibited no tenderness upon palpation. Additionally, two enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the left mandibular region, exhibiting limited mobility and mild tenderness (Fig. 1).

The OPG revealed a low-density lesion measuring approximately 3 × 3 mm in size within the edentulous region of the left mandible, characterized by an irregular alveolar ridge. Additionally, the OPG highlighted a high-density lesion situated at the distal root of the second molar (Fig. 2).

The patient reported experiencing symptoms of short duration, accompanied by lymphadenopathy and tenderness. The gingival mass could potentially be attributed to the removable denture in conjunction with the residual root. While imaging showed concerning findings, the clinical signs were more consistent with benign lesions. Consequently, an inflammatory gingival mass was identified as the preliminary diagnosis.

To promote periodontal mucosal health and ensure proper oral hygiene, the patient received guidance along with a prescription for compound chlorhexidine mouthwash and oral metronidazole tablets for anti-inflammatory treatment. Furthermore, it was recommended that the patient discontinue the use of the removable denture. The removal of the residual root will be scheduled once the inflammation is adequately controlled.

The patient returned for a follow-up appointment three days later, as advised. Unfortunately, the initial anti-inflammatory treatment did not yield the desired results; the mass increased in size rapidly, and a black nodule appeared on the lingual side (Fig. 3). Given these developments, a biopsy was recommended to confirm the diagnosis. The biopsy was conducted obtaining informed consent from the patient.

Under microscopic examination, ulcers were observed on the mucosal surface of the gingiva, which were covered by necrotic tissue and inflammatory exudate. The tumor cells predominantly exhibited epithelial-like characteristics and were organized in nest-like, cord-like, and diffuse growth patterns. This arrangement indicated an invasive growth pattern, with the tumor penetrating deeply into the submucosa. The nuclei of the cells were large and exhibited deep staining, accompanied by distinct nucleoli and an increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio. Notably, obvious pathological mitotic figures were present. The cytoplasm was abundant and lightly stained, with a few cells containing sparse brown pigment granules. The tumor exhibited an invasive boundary, lacking a clear demarcation from the surrounding tissues, and no distinct membranous structures were identified (Fig. 4).

The preliminary pathological diagnosis was consistent with a poorly differentiated malignant tumor.

At the second follow-up examination, the lesion exhibited significant growth, characterized by the appearance black nodules at its edges. The biopsy revealed that the cut surface of the lesion had a light bluish-black hue. Given the findings, the clinician did not dismiss the possibility of melanoma and opted to conduct an immunohistochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining for HMB45, MelanA, S-100, and Ki67 yielded positive results (Fig. 5). Conversely, immunohistochemical staining for BRAF, CK, CD34, and P16 was negative (Fig. 6).

Based on clinical, radiological, histopathological and immunohistochemical assessments, the final diagnosis was established as hypomelanotic melanoma with ulceration of the mandibular gingiva.

The patient underwent a series of interventions, including surgical tumor resection, mandibular rectangular resection, and selective neck dissection, followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Unfortunately, he passed away due to the progression of the tumor within six months after the melanoma diagnosis.

Diagnostic pathway and decision analysis

The diagnostic process of this case underwent a crucial turning point from initial misjudgment to final confirmation.

Initial symptoms and visit (Day 0): The patient visited the hospital due to a painless gingival mass.

Preliminary assessment and diagnosis (Day 0): Based on the clinical manifestations (pink mass, recent denture restoration history, and swollen and tender lymph nodes) and OPG, the preliminary clinical diagnosis was a gingival inflammatory mass. Anti-inflammatory treatment was administered, and the patient was instructed to suspend the use of removable dentures as part of the diagnostic treatment.

Treatment response and disease progression (Day 3): The patient returned for a follow-up visit. Key changes emerged: (1) the anti-inflammatory treatment was ineffective; (2) the mass rapidly increased in size within a short period; (3) black nodules appeared on the lingual side of the tumor. These signs strongly suggested that the lesion was not merely inflammation.

Pathological diagnosis: Conventional HE staining suggested a highly malignant tumor, but the scarce pigment granules within the tumor cells made the diagnosis unclear. Based on clinical suspicion, further immunohistochemical examination was conducted. The results showed HMB45(+), MelanA(+), and S-100(+), leading to a final diagnosis of hypomelanotic melanoma.

Clinical evidence supported the initial inflammatory diagnosis. The mass was pink in color, lacked typical pigmentation, and its appearance was extremely similar to common suppurative granulomas caused by local irritation, such as poor restorations and residual roots. The patient had a recent history of removable denture restoration, providing a reasonable etiological association for the inflammation hypothesis. The high-density shadow suspected to be a residual root, as shown in the panoramic film at the initial diagnosis, further strengthened the idea that the inflammatory mass was caused by foreign body stimulation. It should be noted that hypomelanotic OMM often shows no specific manifestations on conventional X-ray films in the early stage.

The decision to administer antibiotic treatment before biopsy is logical and follows the principle of diagnostic treatment. For cases with clinical manifestations leaning towards benign inflammatory lesions, short-term anti-inflammatory treatment is a reasonable differentiating step. If the treatment is effective, a biopsy can be avoided; otherwise, a biopsy must be performed to rule out a tumor. In this case, this step is precisely part of the identification process.

Discussion

On a clinical basis, OMM is characterized by uniformly brown or black macules, patches, or nodules, often showing variations in colour, including shades of black, brown, gray, purple, and red, as well as areas of depigmentations [10]. OMM is invasive even at smaller sizes. In its advanced stages, the tumor can infiltrate oral structures, including the alveolar bone and palate, such as tooth loosening, loss, and regional lymphadenopathy.

As one of the most aggressive tumor types in the oral and maxillofacial region, OMM is frequently misdiagnosed. This is largely due to early lesions that resemble benign reactive lesions within the oral cavity and the lack of apparent symptoms in their initial stages. Furthermore, some early lesions can be easily overlooked by clinicians leading to a significant delay in diagnosis, which is a critical factor contributing to the poor survival rates associated with OMM.

When clinicians encounter non-pigmented tumors in the oral cavity, accurately diagnosing them as melanoma can be quite challenging. Bansal reported that only 23.6% of these cases were suspected by clinicians to be malignant tumors during the initial consultation, and even fewer—just 9.09%—were distinctly recognized as melanoma [7].

In this case, the patient experienced rapid tumor enlargement and pigmentation within a span of three days. The current literature inadequately addresses this phenomenon and characterizes the swift progression of this case as a significant addition to the clinical spectrum of hypomelanotic melanoma. This underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with the condition and highlights the critical importance of timely biopsy.

This case was initially misdiagnosed as an inflammatory gingival mass, a common diagnostic pitfall associated with hypomelanotic melanoma. This misdiagnosis is consistent with earlier findings that highlight the variability in clinical presentations of OMM, which can include reactive lesions, nonneoplastic proliferative processes, infectious diseases, and various other oral malignancies [11]. The patient had recently received a removable denture restoration, which provided a seemingly plausible etiological link that supported the initial diagnosis of an inflammatory lesion. Consequently, melanoma was excluded due to the lesion’s lack of characteristic pigmentation and its clear association with identifiable local irritants.

Hypomelanotic melanoma exhibits a wide range of clinical manifestations and variability in cellular morphology upon microscopic examination. This variability can manifest in several subtypes, including epithelioid cells, spindle-shaped cells, plasma cell-like cells, small round blue cells, and undifferentiated cells [7, 11]. Even after the pathologist has examined the biopsy tissue, the possibility of misdiagnosis remains. In the case we report, the pathologist at our clinic initially diagnosed the specimen as a poorly differentiated malignant tumor neglecting to consider the melanoma in the primary differential diagnosis.

Conducing a thorough differential diagnosis is crucial when evaluating various tumors. This includes considering poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, high-grade carcinomas, neuroendocrine carcinomas, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and Ewing sarcoma [7].

Histology, combined with immunohistochemistry, remains the gold standard for diagnosis. The presence of melanoma-specific markers, such as HMB45 and MelanA, serves to confirm the origin of melanocytes, which is essential for accurate differentiation. In cases of poorly differentiated carcinoma, the tumor is typically positive for CK while being negative for melanocytic markers. Conversely, lymphoma is characterized by positivity for leukocyte common antigen (LCA) and negativity for S-100 protein. In the case of sarcoma, lineage-specific mesenchymal markers, such as desmin in leiomyosarcoma, are positive.

Immunohistochemical staining is fundamental in the diagnosis of OMM, especially in cases instances where pigmentation is limited or when hypomelanotic melanomas are present. Specific markers frequently employed for melanoma diagnosis include SOX10, S-100, HMB-45, and Melan-A are commonly utilized specific markers for melanoma [12, 13]. However, the sensitivity and specificity of these immunohistochemical markers are not absolute, and reliance on a single marker is inadvisable; therefore, a panel of markers is strongly recommended.

SOX10, a member of the SOX family, serves as a pivotal transcription factor that promotes melanocyte differentiation. It exhibits a robust and specific nuclear staining pattern, which effectively identifies and localizes individual tumor cells with high sensitivity and specificity, making it a valuable indicator for early OMM judgment. While S-100 demonstrates high sensitivity for malignant melanoma, its specificity is notably limited. HMB-45, a specific antibody that targets melanoma cells, is essential for diagnosing and distinguishing hypomelanotic from amelanotic malignant melanoma. The use of immunohistochemistry with HMB-45 and Melan-A provides a highly specific marker for melanocytes.

In the case reported, HMB-45, S-100, Melan-A, and SOX-10 all tested positive. Additionally, the presence of CyclinD1 further corroborates the diagnosis of malignancy.

Prognostic assessment and the associated therapeutic research for mucosal melanoma remain pressing challenges that need to be addressed in clinical practice. The AJCC has established a defined staging system for mucosal melanoma; however, OMM is categorized solely as T3 or T4 for primary designation, which fails to account for early-stage cases [14].There is controversy regarding the classification of early mucosal melanoma. To improve the accuracy of diagnosis and treatment for OMM, it is necessary to establish histologic parameters of prognosis prior to the development of regional or distant metastasis.

Traditional histological staging systems for cutaneous melanoma are not applicable to mucosal melanoma due to the lack of histological landmarks comparable to the dermal papillary and reticular layers. Prasad proposed a prognostically significant three-level microstaging system specifically designed for patients with localized, lymph node-negative, Stage I primary head and neck mucosal melanoma [15].

Level I (in situ and microinvasion) is characterized by tumors that are confined to the epithelial layer, exhibiting either single atypical melanocytes or fewer than ten cells showing microinvasion. Research shows that the median DSS at this stage is 138 months, highlighting an early tumor phase marked by invasion through the basement membrane.

Level II (lamina propria invasion) is recognized when tumors breach the basement membrane but do not penetrate into the submucosa. This stage signifies a crucial shift in prognosis, as the median DSS decreases to 69 months.

Level Ⅲ involves deep tissue infiltration markers, which includes involvement of skeletal muscle, bone, cartilage, and other mesenchymal tissues. Prognosis declines sharply at this level, with median DSS plummeting to just 17 months, indicating that the tumor has gained the capability to invade fascial barriers.

This classification system is organized according to the distinct biological layers of the mucosa, spanning from the basement membrane to the lamina propria and extending to deeper tissues.

In this case presented, the patient was classified as T3 in AJCC staging and Level Ⅲ in Prasad microstaging.

The most common sites for OMM are the hard palate and maxillary gingiva, while occurrences in mandibular gingiva are rare [16]. Mandibular gingival hypopelanotic melanoma is an exceptionally rare condition. A recent review report indicates that, as of 2020, only four cases originating from the mandibular gingiva have been documented in the published literature (Table 1) [17].

Other impacted regions encompass the buccal mucosa, tongue, soft palate, tonsils, and the floor of the mouth. This specific distribution may be associated with mechanical stress and the processes of embryonic development.

In particular, the hard palate and maxillary gingiva experience concentrated mechanical stress during activities such as chewing and swallowing. This type of mechanical stimulation has the potential to trigger the onset of melanoma by activating the MAPK signaling pathway [18].

It has been proposed that the pathogenesis of these melanomas is linked to embryological factors due to the close proximity of frequently affected head and neck mucosal sites—including the lower nasal cavity, maxillary sinuses, hard palate, and upper gingiva. The relationship between these location and the nasal mucosa during embryonic development may account for their shared susceptibility [3, 19].

Furthermore, prognostic factors for OMM remain inadequately defined. Some researches have suggested that the survival rate of melanoma in the mandibular gingiva is significantly lower than that observed in the maxillary and palatal regions [7]. This disparity may be due to the abundant blood supply found in the mandibular bone, couple with its increased susceptibility to early infiltration by cancerous cells.

The prognosis for this patient is unfavorable, likely influenced by several factors, including the advanced stage of the tumor, the patient’s older age, the presence of lymph node metastasis, and a significantly high level of tumor proliferative activity. The Ki67 expression rate of 30% reflects heightened tumor proliferation, while cpositivity further suggests the tumor’s invasive and metastatic potential.

Conclusion ​

Hypomelanotic oral mucosal melanoma located in the mandible is an exceptionally rare tumor that is often linked to a poor prognosis. Its lack of pigmentation, combined with vague clinical characteristics, can create substantial difficulties in diagnosis. This situation highlights the vital need for biopsy and immunohistochemical analysis. Consequently, it is crucial to maintain increased awareness for rapidly expanding non-pigmented lesions in the oral cavity to facilitate accurate diagnosis and effective management.
