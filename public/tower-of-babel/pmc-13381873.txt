Elucidating carbon emission responses to land-use transition using the Kaya–LMDI model: a case study of Hainan, China
Authors: Chunlan Zhao, Yingce Li, Linyuan Wu, Wenxing Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLand-use transition (LUT), a pivotal vector for anthropogenic intervention in the carbon cycle, profoundly influences the formation and evolution of regional carbon emission patterns. This study focuses on Hainan, China’s sole tropical island, and establishes a model accounting for carbon emissions associated with LUT based on related remote-sensing data, socioeconomic statistics, and energy consumption–related data between 2000 and 2025. We combine spatial autocorrelation analysis, an extended logarithmic mean Divisia index decomposition model, and the Tapio decoupling model to systematically elucidate the spatiotemporal features of LUT-associated carbon emissions, their driving factors, and their decoupling relation with economic growth. Notably, Hainan Province features an LUT involving decreasing and increasing proportions of carbon-sink land and carbon-source land, respectively, with construction land expansion being the primary transition mode driving carbon emission growth. The associated carbon emission response features a spatial differentiation pattern of high values concentrated in the north and west and low values localized in the south and east. In addition, carbon sources and sinks demonstrate considerable spatial agglomeration. Economic output is the core driver promoting carbon emission growth, with improvements in land-use efficiency and energy intensity being critical for carbon emission mitigation. During the examined period, the correlation between LUT-associated carbon emissions and economic growth evolves from weak to strong decoupling, demonstrating the remarkable efficacy of peak carbon and carbon neutrality goals in guiding emission reduction–focused LUT. Overall, this research provides a scientific basis for coordinating LUT and low-carbon development in the Hainan Free Trade Port initiative.Keywords: Land-use transition, Carbon emission kaya identity, LMDI decomposition model, Tapio decoupling model, Pressure–state–response modelSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography

Introduction

The increasing emissions of greenhouse gases seriously threaten the global ecosystem1. The contribution of land-use changes to global anthropogenic carbon emissions decreased from over 30% between the Second Industrial Revolution and late 20th century to 12.5% in 2000–20092. Land-use/cover change (LUCC) directly or indirectly alters the composition, processes, and distribution patterns of ecosystems; it is a key factor influencing ecosystem carbon stocks3 and is critical for global sustainable development. Researchers have predominantly examined carbon emission responses induced by changes in land-use patterns across different scales, including global (e.g., investigation of soil organic nitrogen emissions4, national (e.g., research on the spatiotemporal distribution and evolution of carbon emissions in Chinese terrestrial ecosystems5, and provincial (e.g., research on the spatiotemporal features of LUCC-associated carbon emissions in regions such as Henan Province6 scales. Carbon emission estimation methods have evolved from traditional approaches, such as field surveys7, biomass modeling8, and model simulations9, to integrated frameworks combining remote-sensing technology with modeling. Field surveys offer high precision but suffer from notable time lags and spatial limitations; meanwhile, biomass modeling is often restricted to specific vegetation types. With the advancement of remote sensing, GIS, and GPS, combining remote sensing with modeling has become the mainstream approach. For instance, the STIRPAT model analyzes household energy consumption drivers by categorizing emission sources into direct and indirect types but lacks dynamic capability and inadequately addresses heterogeneity10. The geodetector method investigates the drivers of carbon emissions but requires categorical independent variables and may fail in multiscale detection11. The integration of nighttime light data with the Gaussian plume model helps analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of carbon emissions and their correlation with economic activities but is constrained by data quality defects12. Overall, existing models exhibit limitations in systematically characterizing the response processes and mechanisms of LUCC-associated carbon emissions.

The investigation of land-use transition (LUT) represents a novel approach for comprehensively examining LUCC, with LUT referring to temporal changes in land-use morphology associated with shifts in socioeconomic development stages13. Land-use morphology is classified into dominant and recessive forms. The former represents a structure comprising major land-use types in a region during a given period (characterized by quantity and spatial structure); meanwhile, the latter refers to land-use attributes that depend on the dominant morphology but require analysis, testing, detection, and surveying to identify (characterized by property rights, quality, input, output, management mode, and function)14. Current research on the LUT–carbon emission nexus primarily concentrates on the carbon emission processes of single land transformations using traditional methods. Exemplary studies have examined carbon reduction effects in cultivated land utilization and transfer15; utilized nighttime light data and spatial statistical models to correlate urbanization, construction land expansion, and the spatial heterogeneity of carbon emissions16; and quantified the spatial correlation network effects of carbon emissions due to the utilization of cultivated land by improving the gravity model and social network analysis17. However, no systematic theoretical framework for analyzing LUT-associated carbon emissions is available. In particular, the long-term association between LUT and carbon emissions, as well as the corresponding mechanisms, require in-depth analysis.

The pressure–state–response (PSR) model is a structured framework used to analyze dynamic associations among pressure, state, and response in environmental systems18. Herein, this mode is employed to analyze the LUT–carbon emission correlation. “Pressure” refers to factors driving LUTs that induce carbon emissions, such as economic efficiency, land-use structure, population scale, and energy intensity. “State” represents the dynamic status of the regional carbon cycle during the change in land-use morphology (i.e., LUT); it is the direct result of various pressures, including variations in the structure and pattern of land-use types, changes in carbon stocks and emissions across land-use types, and shifts in regional carbon-source and carbon-sink functions. “Response” embodies the targeted measures taken to manage LUT–carbon emission relations and mitigate environmental pressure, encompassing policy optimization, technological innovation, and management mechanism improvement. The PSR response of carbon emissions to LUT reveals the entire process, its drivers, and mechanisms (Fig. 1). We integrate spatial autocorrelation analysis, the extended Kaya–logarithmic mean Divisia index (LMDI) decomposition model, and the Tapio decoupling model to characterize the spatiotemporal features of carbon emission responses to LUT in a layer-by-layer fashion, identify multifactor driving effects, and systematically parse the dynamic decoupling relation with economic growth. Guided by PSR theory and relying on the Kaya–LMDI model, we expand the breadth and depth of studies on the LUT–carbon emission nexus, thereby providing direct decision-making guidance for synergistically promoting low-carbon development and sustainable land use in regions experiencing rapid economic growth.

Data sources and methods

Study area

Hainan is located at the southernmost tip of China and is its only provincial administrative region that lies entirely within the tropical zone, featuring a total land area of ~ 35,400 km2 and maritime area of 2 million km2 (Fig. 2). The terrain exhibits an annular layered structure centered on the Wuzhi Mountains and Yingge Ridge, transitioning from central mountains outward to hills, terraces, and plains19. The area of agricultural land in Hainan Province decreased from 31,988.31 km2 in 2000 to 31,170.84 km2 in 2025 (by ~ 2.56%). Concomitantly, the area of construction land considerably rose from 773.58 to 1,517.52 km2 (by 96.15%), and that of unused land decreased from 136.16 to 87.61 km2 (by 35.65%) (Fig. 3). Given the development of the Hainan Free Trade Port and National Ecological Civilization Pilot Zone, land use in Hainan faces the conflicting imperatives of development and conservation.

Data sources

Land-use data (2000–2025) were provided by the Resource and Environment Science and Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn). These raster datasets had a 30 m×30 m spatial resolution and primarily covered the following land-use types: forest land, cultivated land, construction land, grassland, water bodies, and unused land. Further, energy consumption data were sourced from the China Energy Statistical Yearbook (2025) (http://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/), with socioeconomic development data collected from the Hainan Statistical Yearbook (https://stats.hainan.gov.cn/tjj/tjsu/). Notably, carbon emission coefficients were taken from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories issued by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (https://www.ipcc-nggip.iges.or.jp/public/2006gl/).

For further investigating the correlation of carbon emission pressure with economic development, we used the Tapio decoupling theory. Results

Spatiotemporal features of LUT

Using ArcMap 10.8 software, we processed 30 m × 30 m land-use raster data to generate land-use status maps of terrestrial Hainan for six periods (Fig. 4). Between 2000 and 2025, marked variations in scale proportions and transition rates occurred across the six land-use types (Fig. 5).

Sankey diagram illustrating land-use transitions in Hainan (2000–2025).

Forest land was consistently the dominant land-use type, maintaining a proportion of > 63% but exhibiting overall slow contraction. The proportion of cultivated land remained above 25%, showing a fluctuating downward trend. Construction land exhibited continuous and rapid growth (by 96.1% at the final stage), far greater than that of other land-use types, highlighting notable shifts in land demand driven by Hainan’s rapid urbanization and industrialization.

Fluctuations in grassland and water body areas were relatively moderate. Notably, water bodies considerably expanded in 2010 before stabilizing. Conversely, the area of unused land decreased from 136.16 to 87.61 km2, indicating a gradual improvement in land resource utilization efficiency. Overall, LUT in Hainan was characterized by a structural reconfiguration of construction growth, cultivated land reduction, and ecological stability, with spatial resource allocation increasingly tilting toward nonagricultural uses.

Over the period 2000–2020, net carbon emissions exhibited continuous rapid growth, increasing from 1.7893 million metric tons of carbon (Mt C) to 6.291 Mt C (2.5-fold growth). Growth was fastest between 2010 and 2015. The transition of construction land was the primary contributor to these changes, with the corresponding emissions rising from 4.792 to 6.403 Mt C and comprising > 93% of the total carbon-source contribution. During this period, the elevation of Hainan International Tourism Island development to a national strategy accelerated urbanization and drove high infrastructure investment, leading to a surge in energy consumption associated with construction land. However, carbon emissions markedly declined after 2020. By 2025, net carbon emissions decreased to 5.102 Mt C, in line with the comprehensive implementation of dual carbon goals and dual control policy regarding carbon emission intensity and total volume. Regarding carbon sinks, forest land was the core component, contributing > 95% of total carbon absorption and playing a critical role in carbon sequestration with minimal interannual fluctuation. The carbon absorption capacities of water bodies, unused land, and grassland, exhibited no notable temporal changes throughout the study period, making only minor contributions to the total sink.

The trend in LUT-associated net carbon emissions was highly synchronized with that in carbon-source emissions. Because carbon sinks played a stable regulatory role, variations in net carbon emissions largely reflected changes in carbon sources. Net carbon emissions continuously increased during 2000–2020, with the most rapid growth occurring during 2000–2010 (112.1%), corresponding to the early stages of rapid urbanization. In the subsequent decade (2010–2020), growth notably decelerated, which indicated the preliminary effectiveness of emission control measures. From a long-term perspective (2000–2025), the phasic variations in net carbon emissions were entirely dominated by carbon sources. The stability of carbon sinks (fluctuation amplitude < 2%) meant that they exerted only a weak buffering effect insufficient to alter the overall trend of net emissions. This characteristic provides a clear direction for Hainan to achieve net carbon reduction by optimizing land-use structures and controlling the expansion of carbon sources.

Substantial spatial disparities existed in land use–related carbon emissions across the 18 municipalities and counties of Hainan from 2000 to 2025 (Fig. 6). The temporal evolution of the carbon emission response to LUT exhibited distinct phasic characteristics. In terms of the temporal sequence, 2000–2010 represented the rapid growth of carbon emissions island-wide. For Haikou, net carbon emissions increased more than 66-fold, from 3.03 Mt C in 2000 to 201.04 Mt C in 2010. Dongfang City shifted from − 4.83 to 116.55 Mt C, transforming from a net carbon sink into a major carbon emission source. Similarly, Sanya City witnessed a rapid increase from − 5.81 to 75.73 Mt C. From 2010 to 2020, carbon emissions across the island entered a phase characterized by high-level fluctuations and spatial differentiation. Emissions in Dongfang City continued to climb, reaching 169.51 Mt C in 2020. Changjiang County experienced a surge from 1.15 Mt C in 2010 to 102.10 Mt C in 2020, which indicated a marked acceleration in growth. Haikou City reached 185.82 Mt C in 2015 and remained at a high level of 253.28 Mt C in 2020, although the growth rate decreased.

Temporal patterns of net land-use carbon emissions across municipalities in Hainan.

Regions dominated by ecological functions, such as Qiongzhong and Wuzhishan, remained stable carbon sinks. Qiongzhong remained a carbon sink (consistently negative net emissions) across the whole study period, acting as a stabilizer in the pathway toward carbon neutrality. By 2025, carbon emissions in Danzhou City are projected to reach 283.27 Mt C, making it the largest carbon source on the island. Dongfang City and Changjiang County are expected to remain at high levels. Conversely, emissions in Haikou and Sanya show a substantial decline. Baisha and Baoting continue to be net carbon sinks, which reflects the marked effectiveness of ecological conservation and low-carbon transition policies in these areas.

Spatial characteristics of carbon emission responses to LUT

Spatial autocorrelation analysis and carbon emission accounting revealed that the land-use net carbon emissions in Hainan showed a pronounced spatial differentiation pattern, with high values observed in the west and north and low values in the east and south (Fig. 7).

Spatial patterns of net land-use carbon emissions across municipalities in Hainan.

High-carbon-emission regions were primarily localized in the western and northern municipalities, including Danzhou, Dongfang, Changjiang, and Chengmai. Danzhou City is projected to reach net carbon emissions of 283.27 Mt C (the highest value on the island) in 2025. This spatial distribution closely aligns with the industrial development and urbanization processes of Hainan’s Western Industrial Corridor and the Northern Economic Circle, reflecting the important role of rapid construction land expansion and energy-intensive industry agglomeration in driving regional carbon emissions. In contrast, southern and central municipalities dominated by ecological functions, such as Baisha, Baoting, Qiongzhong, and Wuzhishan, generally maintained low net carbon emission levels. These areas exhibited negative values in most years, demonstrating sustained and stable carbon-sink functions. For instance, Qiongzhong remained a net carbon sink throughout the study period, delivering a net carbon absorption of 11.62 Mt C in 2025. Similarly, Wuzhishan consistently functioned as a carbon sink, absorbing 5.09 Mt C in 2025. These findings indicate that the high forest coverage and intact ecological systems in these regions help maintain the regional carbon balance and provincial ecological security. Although Haikou City, the provincial capital, maintained a high total carbon emission level, it exhibited a notable decrease between 2020 and 2025. This shift was closely associated with policy measures such as urban function optimization, industrial structure adjustment, and the transition to cleaner energy systems, demonstrating the pioneering response and leading role of core cities in the regional low-carbon transition process.

Local Moran’s I was used for identifying spatial agglomeration in regional carbon sources and sinks and characterizing the spatial dependence of land-use carbon emissions in Hainan (Figs. 8 and 9).

High–high agglomeration areas for carbon sources were primarily localized in the western and northern parts of Hainan, areas marked by rapid industrialization and urbanization (e.g., Danzhou, Dongfang, Chengmai, and Changjiang). Time-series data indicated a continuous escalation in net carbon emissions in these regions. For instance, emissions in Danzhou rose from − 1.17 Mt C in 2000 to 283.27 Mt C in 2025; Those in Dongfang increased from − 4.83 to 156.70 Mt C; and those in Changjiang grew from − 2.98 to 32.51 Mt C. Dominated by energy-intensive industries and construction land expansion, these municipalities form a spatially contiguous belt of high carbon emissions, which reflects the profound impact of rapid urbanization and industrialization on the regional carbon emission landscape.

Conversely, high–high clusters of carbon sinks were stably distributed across the ecologically oriented central and southern regions, such as Qiongzhong, Wuzhishan, Baisha, and Baoting. These areas maintained strong carbon sequestration capabilities, with net carbon emissions remaining negative across all years. Qiongzhong remained a notable carbon sink throughout the study period, with net carbon emissions reaching − 11.62 Mt C in 2025. Wuzhishan, Baisha, and Baoting also maintained a long-term negative emission status. Characterized by high forest coverage, excellent ecological environments, and prominent carbon sequestration functions, these regions form a spatially contiguous synergistic zone of efficient carbon sinks, providing critical support for the island-wide carbon balance. The low–low agglomeration areas for carbon sources and sinks exhibited notable spatial overlap, mainly in parts of the northern and western regions with a low proportion of ecological land. These areas generally featured low carbon emission or sink levels but exhibited certain spatial clustering characteristics.

Factors affecting carbon emission response to LUT

During the study period, the cumulative contribution of influencing factors to land use carbon emissions in Hainan Island was − 73.903 Mt C (Table 5), indicating that the overall inhibitory effect outweighed the driving effect. Specifically, the economic efficiency factor, land use structure factor, energy intensity factor, and population scale factor exhibited promoting effects, whereas the land use efficiency factor and land use carbon emission effect factor demonstrated inhibitory effects. Overall, land use carbon emissions presented a distinct trend of “rapid rise followed by deceleration.” The economic efficiency factor remained the core driver propelling carbon emission growth, contributing 406.27 Mt C. Its contribution was particularly pronounced during the accelerated industrialization and urbanization phase from 2005 to 2015, reflecting that economic growth remained highly dependent on a high-carbon development model27. The land use structure factor was consistently positive, suggesting that the expansion of construction land and the increasing proportion of high-carbon land types continue to exacerbate carbon emission pressure. The energy intensity factor was generally positive between 2000 and 2020, reflecting the ongoing challenges in energy structure optimization and efficiency improvement. However, it shifted to a significant negative value between 2020 and 2025, indicating that late-stage energy structure adjustments and energy-saving policies are gradually yielding emission reduction benefits.

Land use carbon emission effect factor

The land use carbon emission effect factor reflects the carbon emission (or absorption) capacity per unit area of different land types. Between 2000 and 2025, the evolution of this factor across the municipalities of Hainan Island exhibited a characteristic transition from facilitation to inhibition. From 2000 to 2010, the majority of cities demonstrated a positive driving effect. For instance, economically active regions such as Chengmai (cumulative 0.05 Mt C) and Danzhou (0.09 Mt C) exhibited relatively distinct carbon emission effects. From 2010 to 2020, a divergence began to emerge, with some cities shifting toward negative values. By the 2020–2025 period, the vast majority of cities had transitioned to significant negative inhibition - such as Danzhou (−0.68 Mt C) and Chengmai (−0.32 Mt C). This indicates that ecological restoration and low-carbon land management measures are gradually yielding results, reflecting a transformation process within this factor from high-carbon dependence to carbon intensity regulation.

Land use structure factor

The land use structure factor characterizes the impact of the proportional composition of different land types on carbon emissions. Overall, the influence of this factor on carbon emissions was minimal yet consistently positive across all periods, with values generally remaining below 0.30 Mt C. This suggests that the expansion of construction land and the proportion of high-carbon land types continue to exert a weak driving force on carbon emissions. For example, economically developed cities such as Danzhou and Haikou recorded cumulative contribution values of 0.38 Mt C and 0.27 Mt C, respectively, reflecting the continued pressure of carbon accretion within their land use structures. Despite the weak overall driving force, this factor remained stable across different time periods, and no significant inhibitory effect resulting from structural optimization has yet been observed.

Land use efficiency factor

The land use efficiency factor reflects the impact of the land area required per unit of economic output on carbon emissions; a negative value indicates the inhibitory effect of intensive land use on emissions. From 2000 to 2025, this factor exhibited significant negative values across all cities, with substantial cumulative inhibitory effects, such as in Danzhou (−26.39 Mt C) and Chengmai (−12.99 Mt C). Temporally, however, the intensity of inhibition gradually weakened. In Danzhou, for instance, the inhibitory value decreased from − 8.53 Mt C in the 2000–2005 period to −0.19 Mt C in the 2020–2025 period. This change indicates a narrowing of emission reduction potential and diminishing marginal benefits of abatement through this specific channel.

Economic efficiency factor

The economic efficiency factor embodies the driving role of economic output corresponding to unit energy consumption on carbon emissions; a positive value indicates that economic growth continues to rely on energy consumption. From 2000 to 2025, this factor acted as a positive driver in all cities, although the values were generally low (consistently below 0.35 Mt C), such as in Danzhou (0.34 Mt C) and Haikou (0.23 Mt C). These findings suggest that while Hainan’s economic growth remains dependent on energy consumption, the driving intensity is relatively limited. It has not generated the strong carbon emission pulling effect often seen in inland industrial provinces, reflecting preliminary success in the transformation of Hainan’s economic structure toward the service sector and green industries.

Energy intensity factor

The energy intensity factor characterizes the impact of energy consumption per unit of economic output on carbon emissions, with its variations reflecting the effectiveness of energy structure transformation and technological progress. The data show that from 2000 to 2010, most cities exhibited a positive driving effect; for example, Haikou and Danzhou recorded high energy intensity factor values, indicating that the high-carbon energy structure had not yet been improved. However, from 2010 to 2025, this factor gradually shifted toward negative inhibition. This trend was particularly pronounced in the 2020–2025 period, where the inhibitory effects in cities such as Haikou and Sanya strengthened, highlighting the critical role of energy structure optimization in carbon abatement.

Population size factor

The population size factor reflects the direct impact of total population changes on carbon emissions. Between 2000 and 2025, the influence of this factor across cities was generally weak and consistently positive, with cumulative contribution values mostly remaining below 0.50 Mt C (e.g., Haikou at 0.30 Mt C, Danzhou at 0.46 Mt C). This indicates that population growth exerts a sustained but moderate driving force on Hainan’s carbon emissions and does not constitute a dominant factor. In the 2020–2025 period, while this factor remained positive for most cities, the values decreased, reflecting that the marginal impact of population scale on carbon emissions is tending toward stability.

Characteristics of driving factor contributions to carbon emission decoupling

The contributions of various driving factors to the decoupling relation exhibited distinct phasic differentiation (Fig. 11). In 2000–2005, the T value (0.543) indicated weak decoupling. During this phase, Tf (0.05) and Tg (0.40) jointly drove carbon emission growth, whereas Tl (− 0.11) and Te (0.15) exerted opposing contributions. This behavior indicates that in the initial stage of economic growth, the economy scale effect remained the primary pressure source for increased carbon emissions. The 2005–2015 period was characterized by rapid industrialization, with the respective decoupling indices equaling 0.47 and 0.48 and indicating an overall weak decoupling. During this time, the contribution of Tg remained above 0.35, becoming the core obstacle to decoupling. This behavior reflects the strong pulling effect of the expansion of high-energy-consumption industries accompanying high-speed economic growth on carbon emissions. From 2015 to 2020, the system maintained a weak-decoupling status (T = 0.39). The contributions of various driving factors converged; Tg exhibited a weakened pulling effect (value = 0.29), whereas Te maintained a positive contribution (0.09). This finding suggests that although energy structure optimization and technological progress had begun, they had not yet fully reversed the trend of carbon emission growth. In 2020–2025, the system remained in a weak-decoupling status with a T value of 0.44. Within its driving mechanism, Tg (0.32) continued to show a positive contribution, while Te (0.10) and Tl (− 0.04) exerted opposing contributions.

Phasic characteristics of decoupling at municipal level

Over the five periods from 2000 to 2025, the decoupling relation of economic growth with land-use carbon emissions in Hainan municipalities experienced initial expansion followed by convergence and subsequent differentiation (Fig. 11). In 2000–2005, most of the province experienced expansive negative decoupling, with carbon emission growth notably outpacing economic growth, a trend most pronounced in Danzhou, Lingao, and Wenchang. However, only Baisha and Baoting achieved strong negative decoupling, while Changjiang and Qiongzhong exhibited weak decoupling. The 2005–2010 phase witnessed general decoupling weakening, with most cities transitioning to weak decoupling and thus signaling a preliminary inhibition of carbon emission acceleration. Expansive negative decoupling persisted in Changjiang, Ledong, Lingshui, Qionghai, Tunchang, and Wanning, whereas Ding’an and Sanya remained in a state of expansive coupling. The 2010–2015 period emerged as a pivotal inflection point, with 12 cities achieving strong negative decoupling marked by considerable emission reductions alongside economic growth, whereas Changjiang exhibited extreme expansive negative decoupling driven by rapid industrial expansion. In 2015–2020, the system largely reverted to a dominant pattern of weak decoupling, which reflected increasing coordination between economic development and emission trajectories. However, Baoting, Ledong, Tunchang, and Wuzhishan retained their expansive negative decoupling status. In 2020–2025, under the constraints of dual carbon goals and policy interventions, the decoupling landscape underwent further differentiation: 11 cities, including Baisha, Baoting, Chengmai, Ding’an, and Dongfang, achieved strong negative decoupling with substantial emission reductions, and seven cities, including Danzhou, Changjiang, and Haikou, continued to exhibit expansive negative decoupling due to rigid industrial structures and energy consumption patterns. This finding indicates that economic growth in these key regions has not yet fully decoupled from carbon emissions.

Characteristics of driving factor contributions to municipal decoupling

The results of decoupling elasticity decomposition (Fig. 12) reveal that the decoupling process in Hainan’s municipalities shifted from being economic growth–led to being efficiency improvement–led. The improvements in Tl and Te were the main drivers of decoupling in Hainan.

In 2000–2020, Tl generally featured a substantial negative contribution. In economically active regions such as Danzhou and Chengmai, Tl values were consistently below − 0.2 across all periods, indicating the effective inhibition of excessive carbon emission rise alongside economic growth. In this period, Te showed a positive contribution in most municipalities, indicating the potential (or need) for energy structure optimization and technological efficiency improvements to play a role in emission reduction. However, during the dual carbon policy intensification period of 2020–2025, the role of Te was fundamentally reversed. All municipalities shifted to a strong negative contribution, with Te values in Haikou, Danzhou, and Dongfang dropping to below − 0.9. This finding highlights the decisive role of cleaner energy structures and technological efficiency improvements in forming strong negative decoupling during this stage. In contrast, Tg consistently presented a positive contribution in 2000–2025. In 2020–2025, Tg values remained above 0.3 in industry-dominated cities such as Danzhou, Changjiang, and Dongfang. This behavior shows that the dependency of economic growth on traditional energy paths had not been fundamentally reversed and was the primary constraint hindering comprehensive deep decoupling. The generally weak and positive contribution of Ts suggests that structural pressures, such as construction land expansion, persisted, although their impact was far below that of efficiency factors. The contributions of Tf and Tp were small across all periods and municipalities, indicating that they are not core variables driving decoupling status evolution.

Robustness test

To evaluate result stability, we compared the estimated carbon emissions due to LUT in Hainan with the CEADs dataset (Fig. 13). Given that the CEADs dataset had not yet been updated to 2025, the comparison was conducted for the overlapping period up to 2020. The results of carbon emission calculations exhibited a temporal trend highly consistent with that of the CEADs dataset for the 2000–2020 period (Pearson r = 0.98 and Spearman ρ = 1)28, which validated the reliability of the estimation method. Discrepancies in absolute values primarily stemmed from differences in research scope: although the CEADs dataset focuses on carbon emissions caused by energy usage, this study deals with direct and indirect land-use emissions, including carbon-sink effects, thus providing a more comprehensive overall valuation. Moreover, sensitivity analysis based on the perturbations of carbon emission coefficients demonstrated that the estimation results for land-use carbon emissions across Hainan’s municipalities maintained high stability. The rates of change remained within a reasonable range, confirming the reliability of the accounting method and result robustness (Fig. 14). For confirming the statistical robustness of the LMDI decomposition results, we performed quantile regression analysis on the six influencing factors. The factors maintained consistent statistical significance and directional influence across the considered quantiles (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8), with no 95% confidence interval containing zero (Fig. 15). This result confirms the stability of the mechanisms underlying each driving factor across various carbon emission scenarios and indicates that the extended Kaya–LMDI decomposition framework possesses strong statistical robustness and explanatory consistency, remaining resilient to the influence of outliers or distributional skewness.

Conclusions and discussion

Conclusions

Focusing on Hainan, we integrated land-use remote-sensing data, socioeconomic statistics, and energy consumption–related data for establishing a model accounting for LUT-based carbon emissions. Spatial autocorrelation analysis, an extended Kaya–LMDI decomposition model, and the Tapio decoupling model were combined for analyzing the spatiotemporal features of LUT-associated carbon emissions, their driving factors, and their decoupling relation with economic growth in 2000–2025. The main conclusions are summarized below.

(1) The land-use structure of Hainan exhibits an LUT characterized by decreasing and increasing proportions of carbon-sink land and carbon-source land, respectively. The continuous and rapid construction land expansion is the primary carbon emission growth driver, consistently accounting for > 93% of total emissions. Forest land, as the core carbon-sink component, maintains a generally stable carbon sequestration function; however, its area is slowly decreasing, which may compromise the stability of the carbon-sink structure.

(2) Land-use carbon emissions display spatial heterogeneity, with high values observed in the west and north and low values in the east and south. Notably, high-value carbon-source areas are clustered in the rapidly industrializing and urbanizing regions of the west and north (e.g., Danzhou, Dongfang, and Changjiang), whereas high-value carbon-sink areas are stably situated in central–southern ecological functional zones (e.g., Qiongzhong, Wuzhishan, and Baisha). Spatial autocorrelation analysis indicates notable spatial agglomeration effects for both carbon sources and sinks.

(3) According to extended Kaya–LMDI decomposition results, the economic output factor is the core force driving carbon emission growth, particularly during the rapid industrialization of 2005–2015. Conversely, Tl and Te play key inhibitory roles, with the inhibitory effect of Tl gradually weakening over time. Post-2020, the Te value shifted from positive to negative, which indicates the gradual efficacy of energy structure optimization and energy-saving policies.

(4) Tapio decoupling analysis indicates that during 2000–2020, economic growth and land-use carbon emissions in Hainan remained in a weak-decoupling state, suggesting a gradually decreasing carbon emission dependence of economic growth. During 2020–2025, this relation shifted to strong decoupling characterized by a notable decrease in carbon emissions against a backdrop of slight economic growth. This behavior reflects the notable emission reduction efficacy due to the implementation of dual carbon goals. However, certain cities (e.g., Danzhou, Changjiang, and Haikou) still exhibit expansive negative decoupling, which indicates an unbalanced regional decoupling process.

Discussion

This study systematically reveals the mechanism of spatiotemporal carbon emission responses to LUT in Hainan, identifying land conversion paths, comparing driver efficiencies, and analyzing dynamic carbon emission structures.

Our work deepens the understanding of the differences between land conversion directions and carbon-source/sink effects. Unlike studies conducted at the national level or in most provinces, which generally emphasize the cultivated land–construction land transformation as the primary contributor to carbon sources29, this research shows that Hainan experiences considerable construction land and water body expansion due to the conversion of cultivated land, a phenomenon rarely discussed systematically in tropical island studies. Furthermore, although Hainan’s forest land, acting as the core carbon sink, is slowly shrinking, its carbon sequestration function remains generally stable. This result differs from those reported for northern temperate forest regions, where logging or degradation leads to notable carbon-sink attenuation30, demonstrating the relative resilience of tropical forest ecosystems in maintaining carbon-sink functions.

This study also strengthens the dual comparison of driver efficiency and intensity. Most studies identify energy intensity and industrial structure as the core inhibitors of carbon emission growth. In contrast, our results show that between 2000 and 2020, Tl exhibited a greater inhibitory effect than Te in Hainan, particularly during the rapid urbanization phase. This driver structure differs from that of industrialized provinces (e.g., Shanxi)31, indicating that in regions with dramatic land-use changes and superior ecological baselines, the contribution of intensive land use to carbon reduction may be more prominent. Additionally, the Te value shifted from positive to negative after 2020, with its inhibitory effect rapidly intensifying. This finding aligns with the national trend of cleaner energy structures following the implementation of dual carbon goals; however, the transformation in Hainan is more pronounced. This behavior reveals the potential role of the region’s smaller energy base and faster clean energy substitution in advancing carbon reduction.

Our work also uncovers the phasic characteristics of carbon emission rates and structures. Consistent with the national trend of gradually slowing carbon emission growth, Hainan also exhibited a slowdown in emission growth from 2000 to 2020. However, this study identifies 2010–2015 as the period with the fastest growth rate, which coincides with the rapid investment and construction cycle following the elevation of the Hainan International Tourism Island construction to a national strategy (late 2009). This behavior reflects the sensitivity and phasic nature of the carbon emission response to policy-driven LUT32. Northern regions feature considerable grassland carbon-sink functions. In contrast, Hainan’s carbon sink is dominated by forest land (> 95%), while the contributions of grasslands and water bodies are minimal. This suggests that in tropical island regions, forest conservation and restoration should be the fundamental path for consolidating carbon-sink functions.

Despite the progress made in scale, methodology, and mechanism analysis, this study has certain limitations. As research relies on multisource data fusion and multimodel integration, the energy consumption data depend on statistical yearbooks, which may be limited by statistical scope and spatial resolution. Future research should attempt to incorporate higher-resolution remote-sensing inversion data (such as those pertaining to the satellite monitoring of greenhouse gases) to enhance the precision of spatial carbon emission simulation. Nevertheless, the models used herein remain effective in revealing the processes and mechanisms of the carbon emission response to LUT.

Policy recommendations

The synergistic advancement of Hainan Free Trade Port construction and the carbon neutrality goal may be facilitated by implementing the following policies.

(1) Strictly enforce ecological zoning and optimize territorial space patterns. The ecological conservation zones in the central and southern regions should be strictly protected by delineating and rigorously enforcing ecological conservation redlines for various carbon-sink lands, including wetlands and forests. In the urban agglomerations of the north and west, the government should promote the intensive utilization and mixed-use layout of construction land to curb disorderly expansion. Ultimately, a low-carbon territorial space pattern characterized by intensification, conservation, control, and optimization in the north, south, west, and east, respectively, should be established.

(2) Implement differentiated management strategies based on regional carbon functions. For high-emission areas (e.g., Danzhou, Dongfang, and Changjiang), the government must strengthen the dual regulation of total energy consumption and energy intensity while strictly regulating project entry and emission supervision. For carbon-sink functional zones (e.g., Qiongzhong, Wuzhishan, and Baisha), ecological compensation mechanisms should be improved to enhance capabilities for carbon-sink consolidation and enhancement. Regarding core cities such as Haikou and Sanya, urban renewal and green transportation systems should be accelerated to demonstrate leadership in the low-carbon transition.

(3) Promote the efficient utilization of land resources and low-carbon agriculture. Mechanisms for controlling incremental quantity and revitalizing the existing stock of construction land should be established to encourage the redevelopment and functional upgrading of inefficient land. In the agricultural sector, ecological farming models, including those associated with intercropping and straw return, should be promoted in the concentrated cultivated areas of the coastal plains, tailored to Hainan’s tropical characteristics. Simultaneously, high-quality paddy fields in central mountainous areas must be strictly protected to enhance the carbon sequestration capacity of soil. This approach explores a unique pathway for Hainan to shift from dependence on land expansion to the synergistic enhancement of efficiency and carbon sinks.

(4) Establish a dynamic monitoring platform for scientific governance. Ground observation, multisource remote sensing, and statistical data should be integrated to build a dynamic monitoring platform for the land-use carbon budget of Hainan. Regular carbon emission accounting and driver assessments should be conducted to provide robust data support for territorial spatial planning, industrial policy formulation, and carbon trading mechanism design and thereby achieve the refined and scientific management of carbon emissions.
