An inexact alternating projection method with application to matrix completion
Stefania Bellavia, Simone Rebegoldi, Mattia Silei

Abstract
We develop and analyze an inexact regularized alternating projection method for nonconvex feasibility problems. Such a method employs inexact projections on one of the two sets, according to a set of well-defined conditions. We prove the global convergence of the algorithm, provided that a certain merit function satisfies the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property on its domain. The method is then specialized to the class of affine rank minimization problems, which includes matrix completion as a special case. We approximate the truncated Singular Value Decomposition of the matrix that has to be projected by means of a Krylov solver, and provide suitable stopping criteria for the Krylov method complying with the theoretical inexactness conditions. The information needed to implement such stopping criteria do not require an extra computational effort as they are a by-product of the Krylov method itself and avoid the so called oversolving phenomena. Results of the numerical validation of the algorithm on matrix completion problems are presented.



keywords
affine rank minimization, alternating projections, inexact projections, krylov subspaces, matrix completion

1
Introduction

This paper is concerned with regularized
alternating
projection methods for computing a point in the intersection of two nonconvex sets
A
and
B
, namely

\text{find }x\text{ such that }x\in A\cap B\quad\quad A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1.1)

The alternating projection method (APM) dates back to the seminal paper
von Neumann 2010
by J. von Neumann in 1950. Starting from a point in the first set, APM generates a sequence of points by alternately projecting onto the two sets. The method converges globally when
A,B
are convex
Bauschke and
Borwein 1996
, and a rich literature has been devoted to the convergence analysis of APM in the convex case
Bauschke and Borwein 1993
;
Bauschke et al. 1997
;
Bauschke and
Borwein 1996
;
Escalante and Raydan 2011
;
Behling et al. 2021
. When one of the sets is nonconvex, APM converges only locally and further hypotheses on the sets and their intersection are required
Drusvyatskiy 2013
;
Drusvyatskiy
et al. 2015
;
Lewis and Malick 2008
;
Lewis et al. 2009
;
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
.

1.1
Contribution of the paper

We focus on a regularized variant of APM (RAPM) proposed in
Attouch et al. 2010
,
for which

global convergence
holds
even in the case of nonconvex sets.
RAPM requires exact projections on both sets; hence, it is not applicable when
the projection on one of the sets
is exact
, while it is not possible or too expensive to compute the exact projection onto the other set. We design a theoretically well-founded inexact variant of RAPM, denoted by iRAPM, that allows to use inexact projections on the latter set. Inspired by
Birgin et al. 2003
;
Ferreira et al. 2022
;
Bonettini
et al. 2023
, we introduce suitable conditions that the inexact projection has to satisfy in order to preserve the theoretical properties of the exact counterpart RAPM.

Our main motivation for this study lies in the application of alternating projection methods to Matrix Completion (MC) problems and,
more generally,
affine rank minimization problems, which consist in finding a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
of minimum rank that satisfies a given system of linear equations:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\textrm{rank}(Z),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t. }{\cal A}(Z)=b,

where
{\cal A}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a linear map and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
Problem (
1.2
) encompasses the MC problem, system identification and control problems, Euclidean embedding, and collaborative filtering
Recht et al. 2010
.

Assuming to know the sought rank of the matrix
Z
, say
r
, we reformulate the affine rank minimization problem as the problem of finding a matrix in the intersection of two sets: the set
\mathcal{C}
of matrices satisfying the linear constraints and the
rank level set
, that is, the nonconvex set
\mathcal{C}_{r}
of matrices of rank less than or equal to
r
. Then, we specialize
APM, RAPM and
iRAPM to this latter class of problems. First, we show that the two sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{r}
satisfy the assumptions that guarantee
the convergence of the three methods
, thus providing a theoretical support to the employment of APMs for solving affine rank minimization problems. Second, we focus on the computation of the projection onto the rank level set, namely the truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the matrix that has to be projected. This is carried out by an iterative method, with an inherent truncation error. In the large scale setting, this is an expensive task and iterative Krylov methods are used
Saad 2011
in order to avoid
full
matrix factorizations. Suitable stopping criteria for this inner iterative process are therefore of crucial importance in order to save Krylov inner iterations, while preserving the same convergence properties as RAPM.
We propose novel stopping criteria for the Krylov method that comply with the theoretical conditions imposed in iRAPM.
The information needed to implement such stopping criteria does not require extra computational effort as it is a by-product of the Krylov method itself.

Some numerical experiments on matrix completion problems to validate the convergence results, the proposed stopping criteria and the performance of the studied methods will be also shown.

1.2
Related works

Early general results on inexact APM schemes in
Kruger and Thao 2016
emerge naturally from the convergence theory of the exact algorithm, but their application to common feasibility problems is not investigated. In the works
Díaz Millán
et al. 2021
;
Drusvyatskiy and
Lewis 2019
, inexact APMs are proposed for problems where, similarly to our setting, the projection onto one of the two sets can be computed exactly while the other projection is inexact. On the one hand, in
Díaz Millán
et al. 2021
, the authors propose an APM where the projection onto one of the sets is approximately computed by means of the conditional gradient method.
On the other hand, in
Drusvyatskiy and
Lewis 2019
, inexact projections are considered onto two possible sets; the set of the solution of mixed equations and inequalities and a certain smooth manifold given by the image under a nonlinear map of an open set containing the iterates. Inexact projections are also considered in
Luke 2013
where the author proposes a theoretical inexactness criterion based on normal cones.
We also highlight that both
Drusvyatskiy and
Lewis 2019
and
Luke 2013
consider easy to project onto the rank level set since the projection of a matrix
Z
is given by its truncated SVD.
Our point of view is different, as the computation of the SVD is expensive and inherently inexact, and differently from
Luke 2013
, we provide inexactness conditions and corresponding stopping criteria for the inner Krylov solver that can be implemented without any additional cost, allowing for the reduction of computational times. Such criteria are inspired by the works
Bonettini
et al. 2020
;
Bonettini
et al. 2021
;
Bonettini
et al. 2023
, where implementable inexactness conditions for computing proximal maps are considered in the context of inexact forward–backward algorithms. Unlike these previous works, we cannot rely on the convexity of the sets involved in problem (
1.1
); for that reason, our definition of inexact projection will be slightly more restrictive.

The APM is widely applied to the Matrix Completion problem under different names. For example, the Expectation-Maximization method is used in statistics for parameters estimation with missing data, and, when data have a matrix structure, some of its variants are equivalent to APM
Guerreiro and
Aguiar 2003
. In the context of denoising and recovering missing data in time series, the Cadzow’s algorithm is well studied
Wang et al. 2021
. It consists in wrapping the time series in a Hankel matrix, applying an SVD truncation to denoise the data, and imputing the missing values. In its basic form, it consists in alternating the same two steps of APM.
Note that the convergence results for RAPM when applied to the rank affine minimization problem provide also a theoretical support to regularized variants of the above approaches.

1.3
Outline

The paper is organised as follows.
After an introduction to APM and RAPM given in Section 2, the iRAPM is proposed and its convergence analyzed in Section 3. Then, the method is specialized to the affine rank minimization problem in Section 4, where the new stopping criteria for the Krylov method used to compute the truncated SVD are presented, see Sections 4.2 and 4.3. In Section 5, numerical results on matrix completion problems are provided showing that the inexact approach is more efficient than the exact counterpart.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we first introduce the notations and basic definitions used in the present work. Then, we recall the APM and RAPM algorithms and state their main convergence results in the case when one of the two sets is nonconvex.

2.1
Notations and basic definitions

The symbol
\|\cdot\|
denotes either the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or the Frobenius norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, depending on the context.
Analogously,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes either the Euclidean or the Frobenius inner product
.
Given two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will use the notation
(x,y)
as a shorthand for
(x^{T},y^{T})^{T}
. Given a non-empty, closed subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the indicator function
\iota_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is given by

\iota_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{if }x\in A\\
+\infty,\quad&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\bar{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty,\-\infty\}
is the extended real numbers set.

Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
and a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\underset{x\in A}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ f(x)
denotes the set of global minimum points of
f
over the set
A
. The projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto the set
A
is defined by

\mathcal{P}_{A}(x)=\underset{y\in A}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}=\underset{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}+\iota_{A}(y).

We say that
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
prox-regular
at a point
\bar{x}\in A
if
\mathcal{P}_{A}
is single-valued around
\bar{x}

(
Luke 2013
, Definition 2.6)
. A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
semialgebraic
if it assumes the form
Bolte et al. 2007

A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\bigcap_{j=1}^{q}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ f_{i,j}(x)=0,\ g_{i,j}(x)<0\},

(2.1)

where
f_{i,j},g_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are polynomial functions for all
1\leq i\leq p
,
1\leq j\leq q
. Likewise, a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is called semialgebraic if its graph is a semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Given a proper, lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, the proximal operator of
f
is denoted by
\operatorname{prox}_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
and defined as

\operatorname{prox}_{f}(x)=\underset{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}+f(y).

Note that, for any non-empty, closed subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{A}(x)=\operatorname{prox}_{\iota_{A}}(x)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
and
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
. The
Fréchet subdifferential
and the
limiting subdifferential
of
f
at
\bar{x}
are defined, respectively, as the sets
(
Rockafellar
et al. 1998
, Definition 8.3)

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}f(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\liminf_{x\to\bar{x},x\neq\bar{x}}\frac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-(x-\bar{x})^{T}w}{\|x-\bar{x}\|}\geq 0\right\},

\displaystyle\partial f(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists\ x^{k}\to\bar{x},\ f(x^{k})\to f(\bar{x}),\ w^{k}\in\hat{\partial}f(x^{k})\to w\ {\text{as}}\ k\to\infty\}.

A point
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
stationary
for
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
if
0\in\partial f(\bar{x})
. We denote with
\|\partial f(x)\|_{-}=\inf_{v\in\partial f(x)}\|v\|
the lazy slope of
f
at
x
.

Given a matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, we denote its Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) as

G=U\Sigma V^{T},

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
are orthonormal matrices and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
is the rectangular diagonal matrix
whose diagonal entries
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(n_{1},n_{2})}
are the singular values of
G
.
We will assume that the singular values are in decreasing order, that is,
\sigma_{i}\geq\sigma_{i+1},\ i=1,\dots,\min(n_{1},n_{2})-1
.
Given
1\leq r\leq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, we also consider the following partitions of matrices
U,V,\Sigma
:
U=[U_{1}\ U_{2}]
,
V=[V_{1}\ V_{2}]
, and
\Sigma=\text{diag}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})
, with
U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},U_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times(n_{1}-r)},V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r},V_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times(n_{2}-r)},\Sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n_{1}-r)\times(n_{2}-r)}
. With these partitions we can write

G=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{T}+U_{2}\Sigma_{2}V_{2}^{T}=[G]_{1}+[G]_{2}.

(2.2)

The matrix
[G]_{1}=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{T}
is the truncated SVD to the first
r
singular values, and in the following we will refer to it as
r
-truncated SVD.

2.2
Alternating projection methods for nonconvex feasibility problems

Given two non empty, closed subsets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider the feasibility problem (
1.1
) and the related minimization problem

\underset{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ L(x,y)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}+\iota_{A}(x)+\iota_{B}(y).

(2.3)

The classical
alternating projection method
(APM) for solving (
1.1
) was first presented by J. von Neumann
von Neumann 2010
. Such a method can be seen as the standard alternating minimization (or Gauss-Seidel) method applied to problem (
2.3
), where the function is cyclically minimized with respect to each block of variables, while keeping the other one fixed. Then, given
y^{0}\in B
, it generates two sequences
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A
and
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
defined as follows:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ L(x,y^{k})=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|x-y^{k}\|^{2}+\iota_{A}(x)=\mathcal{P}_{A}(y^{k})

(2.4)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\underset{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ L(x^{k+1},y)=\underset{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-y\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y)=\mathcal{P}_{B}(x^{k+1})

(2.5)

Although global convergence of method (
2.4
)-(
2.5
) to a solution of problem (
1.1
) holds whenever
A
and
B
are convex, the same property can not be guaranteed in the case where either one of or both sets are nonconvex
Bauschke and Noll 2013
;
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
. On the other hand, the local convergence properties of APM in the nonconvex setting have been investigated under additional assumptions on the sets of interest. In
Lewis and Malick 2008
, the authors prove the local linear convergence rate of APM whenever two smooth manifolds intersect transversally. This result is further generalized in
Lewis et al. 2009
for any two sets intersecting transversally, provided that at least one of them is superregular at an intersecting point. In
Drusvyatskiy 2013
(see also the subsequent work
Drusvyatskiy
et al. 2015
), the authors remove the superregularity assumption and prove the local linear convergence of APM by only requiring transversality (or a slightly weaker version of it). We note that, in practice, checking whether transversality holds requires the knowledge of a point in the intersection, even for semialgebraic sets
(
Drusvyatskiy
et al. 2015
, p. 1648)
. In
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, the authors prove the local convergence of APM at a sublinear rate, by assuming that the two sets intersect separably and at least one of them is
\sigma-
Hölder regular
, with
\sigma\in[0,1)
. Although the convergence rate in
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
is weaker than those obtained in
Drusvyatskiy 2013
;
Drusvyatskiy
et al. 2015
;
Lewis et al. 2009
;
Lewis and Malick 2008
, the separable intersection condition automatically holds when the sets are semialgebraic
(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, Theorem 3)
, which is the case of interest for affine rank minimization problems (see Section
4
for an in-depth analysis).

The definitions of separable intersection and Hölder regularity, as well as the local convergence result derived in
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, are reported below.

Definition 2.1
.

(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016

Two sets
A
and

intersect separably at
\bar{x}\in A\cap B
with exponent
\omega\in[0,2)
and constant

if there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\bar{x}
such that, for any points
a\in A
,
b\in\mathcal{P}_{B}(a)
,
a^{+}\in\mathcal{P}_{A}(b)
,

belonging to
\mathcal{U}
, the following condition holds

\langle b-a^{+},b^{+}-a^{+}\rangle\leq(1-\gamma\|b^{+}-a^{+}\|^{\omega})\|b-a^{+}\|\|b^{+}-a^{+}\|.

Definition 2.2
.

(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, Definition 2)

Let
\sigma\in[0,1)
. The set

is
\sigma-
Hölder regular
with respect to

of

\mathcal{B}(a^{+},(1+c)r)\cap\{b\in\mathcal{P}_{A}(a^{+})^{-1}:\ \langle a^{+}-b^{+},b-b^{+}\rangle>\sqrt{c}r^{\sigma+1}\|b-b^{+}\|\}\cap B=\emptyset,

where
\mathcal{B}(a^{+},(1+c)r)
denotes the ball of center

and radius

.

In
Attouch et al. 2010
;
Attouch et al. 2007
, the authors investigate on the convergence of a regularized modification of APM, obtained by proximal modification of the two alternating steps. Given two strictly positive sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the regularized APM generates two sequences
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A
and
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
such that:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda_{k}L(\cdot,y^{k})}(x^{k})=\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|x-y^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{k}}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}+\iota_{A}(x),

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\operatorname{prox}_{\mu_{k}L(x^{k+1},\cdot)}(y^{k})=\underset{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ \frac{1}{2}\|y-x^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y).

By means of some easy algebraic manipulations, we can interpret the points

and

as projections onto

x^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{A}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+1}x^{k}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}y^{k}\right),\quad y^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{B}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{k}+1}y^{k}+\frac{\mu_{k}}{\mu_{k}+1}x^{k+1}\right).

Definition 2.3
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous function. The function
F
is said to have the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property at
\bar{x}\in\operatorname{dom}(\partial F)
if there exist
\upsilon\in(0,+\infty]
, a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\bar{x}
and a continuous concave function
\phi:[0,\upsilon)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
with
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi\in C^{1}(0,\upsilon)
,
\phi^{\prime}(s)>0
for all
s\in(0,\upsilon)
, such that

\phi^{\prime}(F(z)-F(\bar{x}))\|\partial F(z)\|_{-}\geq 1,

for all
z\in\mathcal{U}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ F(\bar{x})<F(z)<F(\bar{x})+\upsilon\}
. If
F
satisfies the KL property at each point of
\operatorname{dom}(\partial F)
, then
F
is called a KL function.

The convergence results proved in

\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
terminates in a finite number of iterations; otherwise, we have

\|(x^{k},y^{k})-(x^{*},y^{*})\|=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(\tau^{k}),\ \text{for some }\tau\in[0,1),\quad&\text{if }\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}],\\
\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-(1-\theta)/(2\theta-1)}\right),\quad&\text{if }\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1).\end{cases}

3
An inexact regularized alternating projection method

In this section, we propose an inexact version of Algorithm RAPM, which is designed for solving the feasibility problem (
1.1
) whenever the projection onto
B
is costly or does not admit a closed-form implementation. After presenting the method in full detail, we discuss its practical implementation and analyze its convergence in a nonconvex scenario. Regarding the latter aspect, we prove the convergence of the iterates to a stationary limit point of the function
L
and investigate on the convergence rates of the distance to the limit, by assuming that a specific merit function related to
L
satisfies the KL property on its domain.

3.1
The proposed method

We call our proposed method iRAPM - inexact Regularized Alternating Projection Method. iRAPM alternates between an exact (regularized) projection onto
A
and an inexact (regularized) projection onto
B
, generating two sequences
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq A
and
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
. At each iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
, given
x^{k},y^{k}
and two positive parameters
\lambda_{k},\mu_{k}
, the next iterate
x^{k+1}\in A
is computed as an (exact) projected point of the convex combination
(x^{k}+\lambda_{k}y^{k})/(\lambda_{k}+1)
onto
A
, i.e.,

x^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{A}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+1}x^{k}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}y^{k}\right),

whereas the next iterate
y^{k+1}\in B
represents an inexact projection of the point
(y^{k}+\mu_{k}x^{k+1})/(\mu_{k}+1)
onto
B
. In order to introduce the inexactness criteria, we let
Q^{k+1}
be the function defined as

Q^{k+1}(y):=\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+L(x^{k+1},y)-L(x^{k+1},y^{k}).

(3.1)

Let us denote with
{y_{reg}^{k}}
the point
(y^{k}+\mu_{k}x^{k+1})/(\mu_{k}+1)
. Then, we can conveniently write
Q^{k+1}
in (
3.1
) as

\displaystyle Q^{k+1}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-y\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-y\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+\langle x^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k}-y\rangle+\iota_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1+\mu_{k}}{\mu_{k}}\left\langle\frac{\mu_{k}}{1+\mu_{k}}(y^{k}-x^{k+1}),y-y^{k}\right\rangle+\iota_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\left\|y-y^{k}+\frac{\mu_{k}}{1+\mu_{k}}\left(y^{k}-x^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}-\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\left\|\frac{\mu_{k}}{1+\mu_{k}}(y^{k}-x^{k+1})\right\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\left\|y-{y_{reg}^{k}}\right\|^{2}-\frac{(1+\mu_{k})}{2\mu_{k}}\left\|y^{k}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\right\|^{2}+\iota_{B}(y),\quad\forall\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(3.2)

where the first equality follows from the form of
L
given in (
2.3
), the third and fifth are both due to the application of the basic relation
\|a+b\|^{2}=\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2}+2\langle a,b\rangle
, and the sixth follows by using the definition of
y_{reg}^{k}
.
Hence the set of minimum points of
Q^{k+1}
is the projection set of
{y_{reg}^{k}}
onto
B
.
Letting
\hat{y}^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{B}({y_{reg}^{k}})
, we observe that the value
Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})
assumes negative or null sign, since

Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}Q^{k+1}(y)\leq Q^{k+1}(y^{k})=0.

(3.3)

Then, given a prefixed tolerance parameter
\zeta\in(0,1]
, iRAPM computes an approximate projection
y^{k+1}\in B
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle Q^{k+1}(y^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\zeta Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|y^{k+1}-\hat{y}^{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{-\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k+1}(y^{k+1})},

(3.5)

We report iRAPM in its entirety in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

inexact Regularized Alternating Projection Method (iRAPM)

Choose
(x^{0},y^{0})\in A\times B
,
0<\lambda_{-}<\lambda_{+}
,
0<\mu_{-}<\mu_{+}
,
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]
,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]
,
\zeta\in(0,1]
.

For

k=0,1,\ldots

Step 1

Compute
x^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{A}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+1}x^{k}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}y^{k}\right)
.

Step 2

Compute
y^{k+1}\in B
such that (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) hold.

Some comments on the inexactness conditions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) employed in Algorithm

are in order
.

•

Condition (
3.4
) is well defined due to (
3.3
). Furthermore,
Q^{k+1}(y^{k+1})\leq 0
and if
\zeta=1
, the point
y^{k+1}
belongs to the (exact) projection set of
{y_{reg}^{k}}
onto

\zeta\in(0,1)

represents an appropriate approximation that is “sufficiently close” to a point of the projection set. The parameter
\zeta
controls the approximation level of the iterates

•

A bound similar to the one in (

(3.6)

However, if
B
is not convex, then

is not strongly convex and condition (

.

•

3.2
Practical computation of the inexact projections

We now discuss how we can practically implement the regularized inexact projection appearing at Step 2 of Algorithm
2
. At first glance, it looks like enforcing the inexactness conditions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) requires the knowledge of the projection set of
{y_{reg}^{k}}
onto
B
, which we assumed we do not have access to. Nonetheless, we show how to get rid of such an information and still compute a point
y^{k+1}\in B
satisfying (
3.4
)-(
3.5
).

3.2.1
Enforcing condition (
3.4
)

The following discussion is borrowed from
(
Ferreira et al. 2022
, Section 2.1.1)
, which in turn revises a procedure that has been formerly proposed in
Birgin et al. 2003
.
By employing the form of
Q^{k+1}
given in (
3.1
), we can easily rewrite the inexactness condition (
3.4
) as

\|y^{k+1}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}\leq\zeta\|\hat{y}^{k+1}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}+(1-\zeta)\|y^{k}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}.

(3.7)

Condition (
3.7
) is identical to the concept of
feasible inexact projection of
{y_{reg}^{k}}
onto
B
relative to the point
y^{k}\in B
and forcing parameter
\zeta\in(0,1]
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
, which is proposed in
(
Ferreira et al. 2022
, Definition 2)
for convex sets. According to
(
Ferreira et al. 2022
, Section 2.1.1)
, it is possible to define a practical procedure for computing a point
y^{k+1}
satisfying (
3.7
) if there exist two computable, iteration-dependent sequences
\{c^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
\{w^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
complying with the following conditions

\displaystyle c^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{y}^{k+1}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2},\quad\forall\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow+\infty}c^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\|\hat{y}^{k+1}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\rightarrow+\infty}w^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\hat{y}^{k+1}.

(3.8c)

More precisely, the following result holds. The proof, in the context of inexact projection onto convex sets is given in
(
Ferreira et al. 2022
, Section 2.1.1)
; we report the proof in the appendix for sake to completeness and to enlighten that the result is valid also if
B
is not convex.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{c^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
\{w^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
be two sequences complying with (
3.8a
)-(
3.8b
)-(
3.8c
). Then, there exists
\hat{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|w^{{\ell}}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}\leq\zeta c^{{\ell}}+(1-\zeta)\|y^{k}-{y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2},\quad\forall\ \ell\geq\hat{\ell},

(3.9)

which implies that condition
(
3.4
)
holds with
y^{k+1}=w^{{\ell}}
for
\ell\geq\hat{\ell}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
7.1
.
∎

The above procedure has been previously applied only to convex sets with a specific structure
Birgin et al. 2003
;
Ferreira et al. 2022
. In Section
4
, we show how we can find the sequences
\{c^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\{w^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
for computing inexact projections over the rank level set
\mathcal{C}_{r}=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\ \operatorname{rank}(Z)\leq r\}
, which is nonconvex.

3.2.2
Enforcing condition (
3.5
)

We can implement condition (
3.5
) whenever we have at our disposal a vanishing sequence
\{a^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
representing an upper bound for the quantity
\|w^{\ell}-\hat{y}^{k+1}\|
, i.e.

\|w^{\ell}-\hat{y}^{k+1}\|\leq a^{\ell},\quad\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}a^{\ell}=0.

(3.10)

In this case, the following result holds.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\{w^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq B
and
\{a^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be sequences complying with (
3.8c
) and (
3.10
), respectively.
Then there exists
\tilde{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

Q^{k+1}(w^{{\ell}})<0,\quad a^{{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{-\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k+1}(w^{{\ell}})}\quad\forall\ell\geq\tilde{\ell},

(3.11)

which implies that condition (
3.5
) holds with
y^{k+1}=w^{\ell}
for
\ell\geq\tilde{\ell}
.

Proof.

If
y^{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{B}({y_{reg}^{k}})
, one can always set
w^{\ell}=y^{k}
and
a^{\ell}=0
for any
\ell
, so that conditions (
3.8c
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) are trivially satisfied for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\hat{y}^{k+1}=y^{k}
. Then, without loss of generality, we assume that
y^{k}\notin\mathcal{P}_{B}({y_{reg}^{k}})
.
Since
y^{k}\notin\mathcal{P}_{B}({y_{reg}^{k}})
, we have
Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})<0
by definition of
Q^{k+1}
. Then, since condition (
3.8c
) holds and
Q^{k+1}
is a continuous function on
B
, we have

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}Q^{k+1}(w^{\ell})=Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})<0.

Then, for any
0<\epsilon<-Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})
, there exists
\ell_{\epsilon}>0
such that

0<-Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})-\epsilon\leq-Q^{k+1}(w^{\ell}),\quad\forall\ \ell\geq\ell_{\epsilon}.

Likewise, due to the right part of (
3.10
), there exists
\tilde{\ell}\geq\ell_{\epsilon}
sufficiently large such that

a^{{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)(-Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1})-\epsilon)}\leq\sqrt{-\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k+1}(w^{\tilde{\ell}})},\quad\forall\ \ell>\tilde{\ell}

so that (
3.11
) is met and the inexactness condition (
3.5
) holds with
y^{k+1}=w^{{\ell}}
for
\ell\geq\tilde{\ell}
.
∎

Once again, it is possible to compute a sequence
\{a^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
complying with (
3.10
) if
B
is the rank level set; see Section
4
for the details.

3.2.3
Computation of points satisfying (

)-(

)

Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
provide us with an implementable procedure for computing a point

satisfying simultaneously the inexactness conditions (

)-(
3.5

\{c^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\{w^{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
,

complying with (

)-(
3.8b
)-(
3.8c
)-(
3.10
) at any given iteration

, we compute such sequences until the occurrence of the first iteration
\bar{\ell}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\|w^{\bar{\ell}}-{{y_{reg}^{k}}}\|^{2}\leq\zeta c^{\bar{\ell}}+(1-\zeta)\|y^{k}-{{y_{reg}^{k}}}\|^{2}\\
a^{\bar{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{-\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k+1}(w^{\bar{\ell}})}.\end{cases}

(3.12)

Such a procedure is well-defined thanks to Lemma

3.3
Convergence analysis

In the following, we prove the convergence of the iterates sequence generated by Algorithm
2
towards a stationary point of
L
, and derive some convergence rates on the gap between the iterates and their limit point. Our analysis is inspired by
Attouch et al. 2010
and will be carried out by assuming that a certain merit function related to
L
is a KL function. The presence of an inexact projection complying to conditions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) leads to some major changes in the convergence proof of Algorithm
2
in comparison to the one available in
Attouch et al. 2010
.

For our purposes, we introduce the merit function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

F(x,y,\rho)=L(x,y)+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}.

(3.13)

Our analysis relies on the following set of properties, which hold for the sequences generated by iRAPM and involve both the objective function
L
and the related merit function
F
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
and let
\{\hat{y}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be any sequence with
\hat{y}^{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}_{B}({y_{reg}^{k}})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, define the sequences
\{d_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-y^{k})\|^{2}-Q^{k+1}(y^{k+1})},

(3.14)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k+1}(\hat{y}^{k+1}),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mu_{k-1}}\|\hat{y}^{k}-y^{k-1}\|^{2}-2\left(\frac{1-\zeta}{\zeta}\right)Q^{k}(y^{k})}.

(3.16)

The following statements hold true.

(i)

We have

\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-y^{k})\|\leq d_{k},\quad\sqrt{-Q^{k+1}(y^{k+1})}\leq d_{k},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.17)

(ii)

There exists
a>0
such that

L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})+ad_{k}^{2}\leq L(x^{k},y^{k}),\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(iii)

We have

L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})\leq F(x^{k+1},\hat{y}^{k+1},{\rho_{k+1}})\leq L(x^{k},y^{k})+\gamma_{k},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.18)

Furthermore
\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\rho_{k}}=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{k}=0
, and

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\hat{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\|=0.

(3.19)

(iv)

There exists
b>0
such that

\|\partial F(x^{k+1},\hat{y}^{k+1},{\rho_{k+1}})\|_{-}\leq bd_{k},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.20)

Proof.

See Appendix
7.2
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Item (ii) of Lemma
3.3
shows that the function values
\{L(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
monotonically decrease along the iterations, although
y^{k+1}
is computed inexactly. An analogous property is also proved for the RAPM algorithm, see
(
Attouch et al. 2010
, Lemma 3.1(i))
.

The bound in Item (iv) is a modification of the relative error condition used in the analysis of iterative algorithms under the KL property, which for the RAPM algorithm is written as
(
Attouch et al. 2010
, Eq. (24))

\|\partial L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})\|_{-}\leq b\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-y^{k})\|,\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

Differently from
Attouch et al. 2010
, we bound the norm of the subdifferential of the merit function
F
in place of
L
, at the point
(x^{k+1},\hat{y}^{k+1},{\rho_{k+1}})
rather than at the actual iterate
(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})
; furthermore, the norm is bounded with a term proportional to
d_{k}
in place of the norm
\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-y^{k})\|
. These differences are due to the fact that the inexactness conditions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) do not provide any useful information on the subdifferential of
Q^{k+1}
at the point
y^{k+1}
, while combining the optimality of
\hat{y}^{k+1}
and conditions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) we obtain (
3.20
). Similar bounds in the literature have been obtained for inexact proximal–gradient methods adopting analogous inexactness conditions, see e.g.
Bonettini
et al. 2023
;
Bonettini
et al. 2021
.

The bounds in item (iii) are needed to bridge the gap between the actual objective function
L
and the merit function
F
, whereas the limit in (
3.19
) is needed to guarantee that the
KL inequality is applicable to
F
at the point
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})
and that each limit point of
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is stationary for
L
(see the upcoming proofs of Lemma
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
).

Let us define the set of all limit points of the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, namely

\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0})=\{(x^{*},y^{*})\in A\times B:\exists\ \{k_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}\ \text{s.t.}\ (x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})\rightarrow(x^{*},y^{*})\}.

(3.21)

Analogously,
\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
denotes the set of all limit points of the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k},\rho_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. By Lemma
3.3
(iii), we have that
\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Then, we can write
\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
as

\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})=\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0})\times\{0\}.

(3.22)

The following lemma anticipates the main convergence result and contains some basic properties related to the set
\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded. The following statements hold true.

(i)

\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
is non-empty and compact.

(ii)

There exists
L^{*}\in[0,\infty)
such that

F(x^{*},y^{*},0)=L(x^{*},y^{*})=L^{*},\quad\forall\ (x^{*},y^{*})\in\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0}).

(iii)

We have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},y^{k},\rho_{k}),\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0}))=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k}),\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0}))=0.

Proof.

(i) Since
\{(x^{k},y^{k},\rho_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, the set
\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
is nonempty. As it is a countable intersection of compact sets, the set is also compact
(
Bolte et al. 2014
, Lemma 5)
.

(ii) By Lemma
3.3
(ii) and the continuity of the function
L
on the set
A\times B
, it follows that there exists
L^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}L(x^{k},y^{k})=L(x^{*},y^{*})=L^{*},\quad\forall\ (x^{*},y^{*})\in\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0}).

(3.23)

Then, recalling the definition of
F
yields the expected result.

(iii) Since
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and by definition of
\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0})
and
\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
, we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},y^{k}),\Gamma^{*}(x^{0},y^{0}))=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},y^{k},\rho_{k}),\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0}))=0.

Since (
3.19
) holds and
\{(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, we also conclude that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k}),\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0}))=0.

∎

We are now ready to state the main convergence result for the sequence generated by iRAPM, under the assumption that the sequence itself is bounded and
F
is a KL function. The statement is analogous to the one obtained in Theorem
2.2
(i) for the RAPM algorithm. Before stating and proving our result, we require the following uniformized version of the KL property.

Lemma 3.5
.

(
Bolte et al. 2014
, Lemma 6)

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous function and
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a compact set. Suppose that
F
satisfies the KL property at each point belonging to
\Xi
and that
F
is constant over
\Xi
, i.e.
F(z)=\bar{F}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
z\in\Xi
. Then, there exist
\mu,\upsilon>0
and a continuous concave function
\phi:[0,\upsilon)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
with
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi\in C^{1}(0,\upsilon)
,
\phi^{\prime}(s)>0
for all
s\in(0,\upsilon)
such that

\phi^{\prime}(F(z)-\bar{F})\|\partial F(z)\|_{-}\geq 1,\quad\forall\ z\in\bar{\Xi}

(3.24)

where the set
\bar{\Xi}
is defined as

\bar{\Xi}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(z,\Xi)<\mu\ \text{and}\ \bar{F}<F(z)<\bar{F}+\upsilon\}.

(3.25)

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}
be generated by Algorithm
2
. If the function
F
given in (
3.13
) is a KL function,
then either
\|(x^{k},y^{k})\|\rightarrow\infty
, or
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a stationary point of
L
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
, it follows that Algorithm
2
is a special case of the abstract descent algorithm described in
(
Bonettini
et al. 2023
, Condition 3)
. Then, one could apply
(
Bonettini
et al. 2023
, Theorem 5)
to deduce the thesis. For the sake of completeness, we report a simplified convergence proof especially tailored for Algorithm
2
.
Assume that the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
2
is bounded. Let
\{d_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be defined as in Lemma
3.3
, and
L^{*}
as in Lemma
3.4
. By Lemma
3.4
(i)-(ii), the function
F
is constant and equal to
L^{*}
over the compact set
\Xi=\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0})
, therefore we can apply Lemma
3.5
. In what follows
\upsilon,\mu,\phi,\bar{\Xi}
are defined in Lemma
3.5
,
a,b
in Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma
3.3
(iii), Lemma
3.4
(iii) and equation (
3.23
) guarantee the existence of a positive integer
k_{0}
such that

L(x^{k},y^{k})+\gamma_{k}<L^{*}+\upsilon,\quad\operatorname{dist}((x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k}),\Xi^{*}(x^{0},y^{0},\rho_{0}))<\mu,\quad\forall\ k\geq k_{0}.

(3.26)

Without loss of generality, up to a translation of the iteration index, we can assume
k_{0}=0
.

Since
\{L(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a monotone nonincreasing sequence and
L^{*}
is its limit (see Lemma
3.3
(iii) and (
3.23
)),
we have
L(x^{k},y^{k})\geq L^{*}
for all
k\geq 0
, which implies that
\phi
can be applied to both values
L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*}
and
L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-L^{*}
. Then, one can define the following quantity

\phi_{k}=\frac{b}{a}(\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})-\phi(L(x^{k+1},y^{(k+1)})-L^{*})),\quad\forall\ k\geq 0.

(3.27)

Since
\phi
is a monotone increasing function and
\{L(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a monotone nonincreasing sequence, we have
\phi_{k}\geq 0
, for all
k\geq 0
. Let us show that the following inequality holds:

2d_{k}\leq\phi_{k}+d_{k-1},\quad\forall\ k\geq 1.

(3.28)

If
d_{k}=0
, inequality (
3.28
) holds trivially. Otherwise, for any iteration index
k\geq 1
such that
d_{k}>0
and using Lemma
3.3
(ii)-(iii), we can write

L^{*}\leq L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})<L(x^{k},y^{k})\leq F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\leq L(x^{k-1},{y}^{k-1})+\gamma_{k-1},

which, in view of (
3.26
), implies that
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\in\bar{\Xi}
.
Then, combining the KL inequality related to the function
F
at
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})
with Lemma
3.3
(iv) yields:

\phi^{\prime}(F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})-L^{*})\geq\frac{1}{\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}}\geq\frac{1}{bd_{k-1}}.

(3.29)

By employing the concavity of the function
\phi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})-\phi(L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-L^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\phi^{\prime}(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L(x^{k+1},y^{k+1}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{ad_{k}^{2}}{bd_{k-1}},

where the latter inequality follows by applying Lemma
3.3
(ii)-(iii), the monotonicity of
\phi^{\prime}
and (
3.29
).
By recalling the definition of
\phi_{k}
in (
3.27
), the above inequality implies
d_{k}^{2}\leq\phi_{k}d_{k-1}
.
Now taking the square root of both sides and using the inequality
2\sqrt{uv}\leq u+v
on the right-hand side we get inequality (
3.28
). By summing (
3.28
) over
k=0,\ldots,K
and taking the limit for
K\rightarrow\infty
, following the same arguments as in
(
Bonettini
et al. 2023
, Theorem 5)
, we easily get

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d_{k}<\infty.

(3.30)

Finally, by combining (
3.30
) and (
3.17
), we conclude that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|(x^{k+1}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-y^{k})\|<\infty
. Hence the sequence
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in A\times B
.

Since
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
(x^{*},y^{*})
and
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
(due to Lemma
3.3
(iii)), we have
(x^{k},y^{k},\rho_{k})\rightarrow(x^{*},y^{*},0)
; furthermore, thanks to (
3.19
) and the continuity of
F
in
A\times B
, it also follows that
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\rightarrow(x^{*},y^{*},0)
and
F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\rightarrow F(x^{*},y^{*},0)
. Finally, by summing the inequality (
3.20
) for
k=0,\ldots,K
, taking its limit for
K\rightarrow\infty
and recalling that
\{d_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is summable (see (
3.30
)), we get

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}<\infty\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}=0.

To summarize, the sequence
\{(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the following limits:

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})=(x^{*},y^{*},0),\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})=F(x^{*},y^{*},0),

and

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}=0.

Then, it satisfies the hypotheses of
(
Frankel et al. 2015
, Lemma 2.1)
, which ensures that
0\in\partial F(x^{*},y^{*},0)
. Since
\partial F(x^{*},y^{*},0)=\partial L(x^{*},y^{*})\times\{0\}
, it follows
0\in\partial L(x^{*},y^{*})
, i.e.,
(x^{*},y^{*})
is a stationary point of
L
.
∎

Inspired by
Attouch and
Bolte 2009
, we now investigate the convergence rate of
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
when the merit function
F
given in (
3.13
) satisfies the KL property at the limit point
(x^{*},y^{*})
with
\phi(t)=ct^{1-\theta}
, with
c>0,\ \theta\in[0,1[
. Similarly to the result obtained for the RAPM algorithm (see Theorem
2.2
(iii)), the convergence rate of iRAPM depends on the value of
\theta
appearing in the desingularizing function
\phi
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in A\times B
. Additionally, assume that
F
defined in (
3.13
) satisfies the KL property at
(x^{*},y^{*})
with
\phi(t)=ct^{1-\theta}
, with
c>0,\ \theta\in[0,1)
. Then the following convergence rates hold.

(i)

If
\theta=0
, the sequence
(x^{k},y^{k})
terminates in a finite number of iterations.

(ii)

If
\theta\in]0,\frac{1}{2}]
, there exists
C>0
and
\tau\in(0,1)
such that

\|(x^{k},y^{k})-(x^{*},y^{*})\|\leq C\tau^{k}.

(3.31)

(iii)

If
\theta\in]\frac{1}{2},1[
, there exists
C>0
such that

\|(x^{k},y^{k})-(x^{*},y^{*})\|\leq Ck^{-\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta-1}}.

(3.32)

Proof.

(i) If
\theta=0
, then
\phi(t)=ct
and
\phi^{\prime}(t)=c
for
t\in[0,\upsilon)
. By contradiction, assume that there exists an infinite subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})\neq(x^{k},y^{k})
for all
k\in K
. From Lemma
3.3
(i), this means that
d_{k}>0
for all
k\in K
, where
d_{k}
is given in (
3.14
). By using the same reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
3.6
, we conclude that
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\in\bar{\Xi}
for all
k\in K
. Then, for all
k\in K
, the uniformized KL property (i.e. Lemma
3.5
) applies as follows

\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}\geq\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})-L^{*})}=\frac{1}{c},\quad\forall\ k\in K.

(3.33)

By combining (
3.33
) with (
3.20
), we get
d_{k-1}\geq\frac{1}{bc}

\forall\ k\in K
and Lemma
3.3
(ii) yields

\frac{a}{b^{2}c^{2}}\leq L(x^{k-1},y^{k-1})-L(x^{k},y^{k}),\quad\forall\ k\in K,

which is absurd since the right-hand side of the inequality is converging to zero. Then item (i) follows.

(ii)-(iii) If
\theta\in(0,1)
, then let
(x^{*},y^{*})\in A\times B
be the unique limit point of
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. By summing inequality (
3.28
) for all indexes
k,k+1,\ldots,K
with
k<K
, we get

2\sum_{\ell=k}^{K}d_{\ell}\leq\sum_{\ell=k}^{K}\phi_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=k-1}^{K-1}d_{\ell}.

(3.34)

Due to the definition of
\phi_{k}
in (
3.27
) and the nonnegative sign of the desingularizing function
\phi
, one can also write the inequality below:

\sum_{\ell=k}^{K}\phi_{\ell}\leq\frac{b}{a}\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*}).

(3.35)

By applying inequality (
3.35
) to (
3.34
), we get

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\ell=k}^{K}d_{\ell}\leq\frac{b}{a}\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})+\sum_{\ell=k-1}^{K-1}d_{\ell}.

By passing to the limit for
K\rightarrow\infty
and letting

\Delta_{k}=\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty}d_{\ell},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N},

we finally obtain

\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{b}{a}\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})+\Delta_{k-1}-\Delta_{k},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.36)

Let
\bar{\Xi}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined as in the proof of Theorem
3.6
. Since
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\rightarrow(x^{*},y^{*},0)
, we can assume without loss of generality that
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\in\bar{\Xi}
for all
k\geq 0
. Then, the following inequalities hold

\phi^{\prime}(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})\geq\phi^{\prime}(F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})-L^{*})\geq\frac{1}{\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}},

where the first inequality follows from (
3.18
) and the monotonicity of
\phi^{\prime}
, whereas the second one is the application of Lemma
3.5
at the point
(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})
. Recalling that
\phi(t)=ct^{1-\theta}
yields

c(1-\theta)(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})^{-\theta}\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}\geq 1,

and by rearranging terms in the previous inequality and applying Lemma
3.3
(iv), we get

\displaystyle(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})^{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq c(1-\theta)\|\partial F(x^{k},\hat{y}^{k},\rho_{k})\|_{-}\leq bc(1-\theta)d_{k-1}=bc(1-\theta)(\Delta_{k-1}-\Delta_{k}).

Hence, the previous inequality guarantees the existence of a positive constant
q>0
such that

\phi(L(x^{k},y^{k})-L^{*})\leq q(\Delta_{k-1}-\Delta_{k})^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}.

Then, inequality (
3.36
) leads to

\Delta_{k}\leq\frac{bq}{a}(\Delta_{k-1}-\Delta_{k})^{\frac{1-\theta}{\theta}}+\Delta_{k-1}-\Delta_{k},\quad\forall\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.37)

Any nonnegative sequence
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
complying with an inequality of form (
3.37
) converges to zero with rate either
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{k})
,
\tau\in(0,1)
or
\mathcal{O}(k^{-(1-\theta)/(2\theta-1)})
, depending on the value of
\theta
, see the proof of
(
Attouch and
Bolte 2009
, Theorem 2)
. By Lemma
3.3
(i) and the triangular inequality, it is immediate to verify that
\Delta_{k}\geq\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty}\|(x^{\ell+1}-x^{\ell},y^{\ell+1}-y^{\ell})\|\geq\|(x^{k}-x^{*},y^{k}-y^{*})\|
. Hence, the sequence
\{\|(x^{k}-x^{*},y^{k}-y^{*})\|\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
shares the same convergence rates as
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the proof is complete.
∎

4
Application to affine rank minimization

In this section, we focus on problem (
1.2
). Assuming that the rank
r
of the sought matrix
Z
is given, the affine rank minimization problem can be formulated as

\text{find }Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\text{ such that }Z\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{C}_{r}},

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\ {\cal A}(Z)=b\},

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\textrm{rank}(Z)\leq r\}.

(4.3)

Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is an affine subspace, the projection onto it reduces to a least squares problem; however, in applications such as Matrix Completion
Candes and Recht 2009
, it consists in trivial element-wise operations on the matrix. Then, in the following, we consider the projection onto
{\mathcal{C}}
as known in closed form and cheap to compute. Regarding the projection onto the set
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
, we can rely on the truncated SVD of the matrix we would like to project, as the well-known result by Eckart and Young
(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
, Theorem 2.4.8)
states that, given a matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
r<\textrm{rank}(G)\leq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, the nearest matrix of rank
r
to
G
(with respect to
\|\cdot\|
) is given by the
r
-truncated SVD; using the notation given in (
2.2
) we can write

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}(G)=[G]_{1}=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{T}.

(4.4)

We now establish the prox-regularity of
C_{r}
at any matrix of rank
r
and the semialgebraicity of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. These properties allow us to prove the convergence of APM, RAPM and iRAPM when applied to affine rank minimization problems.

Lemma 4.1
.

(
Luke 2013
, Proposition 3.8)

The set
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
is prox-regular at all points
\bar{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
\textrm{rank}(\bar{X})=r
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The sets
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
are closed semialgebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.

Proof.

Note that the set
{\mathcal{C}}
is an affine subspace, as it is defined by a finite number of affine equations. Hence,
{\mathcal{C}}
complies with the definition of semialgebraic set of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
given in equation (
2.1
).
Likewise, given
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\},\ J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n_{2}\}
, we denote with
Z_{IJ}
the submatrix of
Z
obtained by keeping the rows and the columns with indexes in
I
and
J
, respectively. Observe that

\textrm{rank}(Z)\leq r\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\det(Z_{IJ})=0,\ \forall I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\},\ J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n_{2}\},\,\mbox{s.t }|I|=|J|=r+1.

The function
\det:\mathbb{R}^{(r+1)\times(r+1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is polynomial with respect to the elements of
Z
. Therefore we can conclude that
\mathcal{C}_{r}
is a semialgebraic set, as it is defined by a finite number of polynomial equations.
∎

4.1
Convergence Results

We now specialize the convergence properties of APM and RAPM described in Section 2 and iRAPM proposed in Section 3 when applied to the affine rank minimization problem (
4.1
). We first need to adjust the notation. The Euclidean norm naturally becomes the Frobenius norm, the sets
A
and
B
are now
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
, respectively, the alternating projection methods generate two sequences
\{X^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\ \{Y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of matrices and the “regularized” points are defined as follows:

{X_{reg}^{k}}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{k}}X^{k}+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}}Y^{k},\quad{Y_{reg}^{k}}=\frac{\mu_{k}}{1+\mu_{k}}X^{k+1}+\frac{1}{1+\mu_{k}}Y^{k}.

(4.5)

Then, the function
L
in (
2.3
) takes the form

L(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\|X-Y\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}}(X)+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}(Y).

From the analysis carried out in
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
(see Theorem
2.1
) and the analytical properties of the sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, we easily derive the following result for APM applied to the feasibility problem (
4.1
).

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that there exists
\bar{X}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
such that
\textrm{rank}(\bar{X})=r
. Let
\{X^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and
\{Y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
be the sequences generated by APM with
A={\mathcal{C}}
and
B={\mathcal{C}_{r}}
. Then
\{X^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{Y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
locally converge to a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
Z\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{C}_{r}}

with rates
\|X^{k}-Z\|=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\frac{2-\omega}{2\omega}})
and
\|Y^{k}-Z\|=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\frac{2-\omega}{2\omega}})
for some
\omega\in(0,2)
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.2
ensures that
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
are closed semialgebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, and every semialgebraic set is subanalytic, see e.g.
(
Bolte et al. 2007
, p. 1208)
. Since
{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
are closed and subanalytic, it follows that they intersect separably
with
\omega\in(0,2)

(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, Theorem 3)
. Being
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
prox-regular at
\bar{X}
by Lemma
4.1
, it follows that
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
is
\sigma-
Hölder regular with respect to
{\mathcal{C}}
at
\bar{X}
for any
\sigma\in[0,1)
and any arbitrarily small constant
c

(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, Corollary 3)
. Then, the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
hold, and the thesis follows.
∎

Likewise, the following Corollary follows for RAPM when applied to (
4.1
).

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\{X^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and
\{Y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
be the sequences generated by RAPM (Algorithm
1
) with
A={\mathcal{C}}
,
B={\mathcal{C}_{r}}
.

(i)

Either
\|(X^{k},Y^{k})\|\rightarrow\infty
, or the sequence
\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a stationary point of the function
L
.

(ii)

Let
(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
be any point such that
\|\bar{X}-\bar{Y}\|=\min\{\|X-Y\|:\ X\in{\mathcal{C}},\ Y\in{\mathcal{C}_{r}}\}
. If
(X^{0},Y^{0})
is sufficiently close to
(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
, then the whole sequence
\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a point
(X_{\infty},Y_{\infty})
such that
\|X_{\infty}-Y_{\infty}\|=\min\{\|X-Y\|:\ X\in{\mathcal{C}},\ Y\in{\mathcal{C}_{r}}\}
.

(iii)

Suppose that the sequence
\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
(X^{*},Y^{*})\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
.
Then,
\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies one of the following: it terminates in a finite number of iterations; it converges with a linear rate of the form (
3.31
); it converges with a sublinear rate of the form (
3.32
).

Proof.

The function
L
is given by the sum of three semialgebraic functions, which implies that
L
is itself semialgebraic
(
Bolte et al. 2014
, Example 2)
.
Furthermore,
L
is a semialgebraic function that is continuous on its closed domain, which implies that it satisfies the KL property at any point with
\phi(t)=ct^{1-\theta}
, being
c>0
and
\theta\in[0,1)
two parameters depending on the given point
(
Bolte et al. 2007
, Theorem 3.1)
.
Hence, the thesis follows by applying Theorem
2.2
with the choice
A={\mathcal{C}}
,
B={\mathcal{C}_{r}}
.
∎

In analogy with Corollary
4.3
, we can be more precise on the convergence rate of RAPM for affine rank minimization problems if we assume that the iterate sequence converges to the intersection of the two sets. In particular, we state and prove the following Corollary, which is, to the best of our knowledge, new, and whose proof employs a result on inf-projection taken from
Yu et al. 2022
in combination with a property holding for subanalytic sets
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that the sequence
\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by RAPM (Algorithm
1
) converges to
(X^{*},X^{*})
with
X^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}
. Then, there exists
\theta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
such that
\|(X^{k},Y^{k})-(X^{*},X^{*})\|=\mathcal{O}(k^{-\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta-1}})
.

Proof.

We let
m:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be defined as the inf-projection

\displaystyle m(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min_{Y\in{\mathcal{C}_{r}}}\|X-Y\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}}(X)

\displaystyle=\min_{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|X-Y\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}}(X)+\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}(Y)\right)=\min_{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}}L(X,Y).

(4.6)

Let us also denote with
M
the following set-valued function

M(X)=\underset{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\ L(X,Y).

(4.7)

We observe that the hypotheses of
(
Yu et al. 2022
, Theorem 3.1)
hold, indeed:

•

If
\bar{X}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
, then
M(\bar{X})
is single-valued and
M(\bar{X})=\{\bar{X}\}
.

•

\partial L(X,Y)\neq\emptyset,\ \forall\ X\in{\mathcal{C}},\ \forall\ Y\in{\mathcal{C}_{r}}.

•

The function
L
is lower-bounded with respect to
Y
locally uniformly in
X
, which means that, for all
\bar{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the lower-level sets
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,X}=\{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\ L(X,Y)\leq\alpha\}
are uniformly bounded in a neighbourhood of
\bar{X}
. Indeed, note that

\|Y\|\leq\|Y-X\|+\|X\|\leq\sqrt{2\alpha}+\|X\|,\quad\forall\ Y\in\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,X}.

Then, if
X\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}(\bar{X},\epsilon)}
, namely
X
belongs to the ball of center
\bar{X}
and radius
\epsilon
, it follows that the sets
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,X}
are uniformly bounded by
C=\sqrt{2\alpha}+\|\bar{X}\|+\epsilon
for all
X\in B(\bar{X},\epsilon)
.

Therefore, we can apply
(
Yu et al. 2022
, Theorem 3.1)
, which states that, if
\bar{X}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}_{r}
and
L
satisfies the KL property at the point
(\bar{X},\bar{X})
with desingularizing function
\phi(t)=Ct^{1-\theta}
, then the inf-projection
m
defined in (
4.1
) satisfies the KL property at
(\bar{X},\bar{X})
with the same exponent
\theta\in[0,1)
.

Now, we recall that
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}_{r}
are both subanalytic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
. Then, from
(
Noll and Rondepierre 2016
, Lemma 3)
, it follows that the KL exponent
\theta
of
m
at the point
(\bar{X},\bar{X})
must be strictly greater than
\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, we can conclude that the KL exponent of
L
at
(\bar{X},\bar{X})
is greater than
\frac{1}{2}
, otherwise there would be a contradiction due to
(
Yu et al. 2022
, Theorem 3.1)
. At this point, the thesis follows by applying Theorem
2.2
(iii) with the choice
A={\mathcal{C}}
,
B={\mathcal{C}_{r}}
, and
(x^{*},y^{*})=(X^{*},X^{*})
.
∎

For our proposed iRAPM applied to (
4.1
), we can prove the following convergence result.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\{X^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and
\{Y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
be the sequences generated by iRAPM (Algorithm
2
) with

,

\|(X^{k},Y^{k})\|\rightarrow\infty

\{(X^{k},Y^{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a stationary point

of the function

. In the latter case, the sequence satisfies one of the following: it terminates in a finite number of iterations; it converges with a linear rate of the form (
3.31
); it converges with a sublinear rate of the form (
3.32
).

Proof.

Since the merit function

F(X,Y,\rho)=L(X,Y)+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2},

(4.8)

is the sum of two semialgebraic functions, it follows that
F
is itself semialgebraic;
furthermore,

is continuous on its closed domain. Hence, by
(
Bolte et al. 2007
, Theorem 3.1)
,
F
satisfies the KL property at any point with
\phi(t)=ct^{1-\theta}

and
\theta\in[0,1)
.
Then the thesis follows by applying Theorems
3.6
-
3.7
to Algorithm
2
with the choice
A={\mathcal{C}}
,
B={\mathcal{C}_{r}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Suppose that the sequence

generated by iRAPM (Algorithm

) converges to

4.2
Inexact Projection

In the following, we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
Y_{reg}^{k}
be the matrix to be projected and defined in (
4.5
),
\hat{Y}^{k+1}
any matrix belonging to the projection set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}(Y_{reg}^{k})
, and
Y_{k+1}
the inexact projection satisfying (
3.4
)-(
3.5
).

As outlined in Section
3.2
, the accuracy requirements in (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) can be enforced without the knowledge of the exact projection provided that it is possible to define two sequences of scalars
c^{\ell},a^{\ell}
and a sequence of matrices
W^{\ell}
satisfying (
3.8a
)-(
3.8c
) and (
3.10
). Then, the inexact projection is given by
W^{\bar{\ell}}
, where
\bar{\ell}
is the first iteration such that (
3.12
) is met.
In this section, by exploiting the Lanczos process
Saad 2011
;
Larsen 1998
, we are going to show how to compute an approximation of the
r
-truncated SVD of
{Y_{reg}^{k}}
that represents an inexact projection satisfying
(
3.4
)-(
3.5
).
We first briefly describe how the Lanczos method works.

4.2.1
The Lanczos method

We employ the Lanczos procedure for approximate the
r
largest singular values of the matrix
{Y_{reg}^{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
. Given an integer
\ell
with
r\leq\ell\leq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, such a procedure uses Lanczos bidiagonalization, also known as Paige-Saunders bidiagonalization
Paige and Saunders 1982
, to find two orthonormal matrices
P_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times(\ell+1)}
,
Q_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times\ell}
, such that

P_{\ell+1}^{T}{Y_{reg}^{k}}Q_{\ell}=B_{\ell}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c@{\hspace{.5em}}c@{\hspace{.5em}}c@{\hspace{.5em}}c}\alpha_{1}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\\
\beta_{2}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\alpha_{2}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\\
\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\beta_{3}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\smash{\ddots}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\\
\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\smash{\ddots}\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\alpha_{\ell}\\
\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\hfil\hskip 5.0pt&\beta_{\ell+1}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times\ell}.

If we consider the columns partitioning
P_{\ell+1}=[p_{1},\dots,p_{\ell+1}]
and
Q_{\ell}=[q_{1},\dots,q_{\ell}]
, the column vectors
p_{j},q_{j}
are the so-called
Lanczos vectors
and can be built by recurrence, starting from two vectors
q_{0},\ p_{1}
;
indeed the following recurrence holds for
\ell=1,2,\dots
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ell}q_{\ell}

\displaystyle=({Y_{reg}^{k}})^{T}p_{\ell}-\beta_{\ell}q_{\ell-1}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\beta_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle={Y_{reg}^{k}}q_{\ell}-\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell},

(4.10)

where
\alpha_{\ell},\ \beta_{\ell}
are chosen such that the Lanczos vectors have unitary norm. Moreover, we can rewrite the recurrence in matrix form as:

\displaystyle{Y_{reg}^{k}}Q_{\ell}

\displaystyle=P_{\ell+1}B_{\ell}

(4.11)

\displaystyle({Y_{reg}^{k}})^{T}P_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=Q_{\ell}B_{\ell}^{T}+\alpha_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}e_{\ell+1}^{T},

(4.12)

where
e_{\ell+1}
is the
(\ell+1
)-th vector of the canonical basis.
Given the bidiagonal matrix
B_{\ell}
at hand, its SVD
B_{\ell}=U_{B}\Sigma_{B}V_{B}^{T}
can be cheaply computed and the following matrix
G_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
is
defined

G_{\ell}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P_{\ell+1}B_{\ell}Q_{\ell}^{T}}=\underbrace{P_{\ell+1}U_{B}}_{\tilde{U}_{\ell}}\underbrace{\Sigma_{B}}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ell}}\underbrace{V_{B}^{T}Q_{\ell}^{T}}_{\tilde{V}_{\ell}^{T}}=\tilde{U}_{\ell}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ell}\tilde{V}_{\ell}^{T},

(4.13)

where
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\ell}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times\ell}}
is a rectangular diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries
\tilde{\sigma}_{1,\ell},\dots,\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell,\ell}
can be seen as approximations of the largest
\ell
singular values
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\ell}
of
{Y_{reg}^{k}}
.
Note also that the first
\ell
singular values of
B_{\ell}
and
G_{\ell}
are the same and
G_{\ell}
has
\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}-\ell
extra null singular values.

With the aim of approximating the
r
-truncated SVD of
{Y_{reg}^{k}}
, using the notation (
2.2
), we write

B_{\ell}=\underbrace{(U_{B})_{1}(\Sigma_{B})_{1}(V_{B})_{1}^{T}}_{=[B_{\ell}]_{1}}+\underbrace{(U_{B})_{2}(\Sigma_{B})_{2}(V_{B})_{2}^{T}}_{=[B_{\ell}]_{2}}=[B_{\ell}]_{1}+[B_{\ell}]_{2},

with
(U_{B})_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times r},(U_{B})_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1-r)},(V_{B})_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times r},(V_{B})_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times(\ell-r)},\Sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\ell+1-r)\times(\ell-r)}
. Then,
using the notation
G_{\ell}=[G_{\ell}]_{1}+[G_{\ell}]_{2}
, where
[G_{\ell}]_{1}=P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{1}Q_{\ell}^{T}
and
[G_{\ell}]_{2}=P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}
, we can truncate the decomposition in (
4.13
) and define

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[G_{\ell}]_{1}=}\underbrace{P_{\ell+1}(U_{B})_{1}}_{\tilde{U}_{r}}\underbrace{(\Sigma_{B})_{1}}_{\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}}\underbrace{(V_{B})_{1}^{T}Q_{\ell}^{T}}_{\tilde{V}_{r}^{T}}=\tilde{U}_{r}\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}\tilde{V}_{r}.

The preceding discussion indicates how singular values estimates can be obtained via the Lanczos method, but it reveals nothing about the approximation quality of the singular values and corresponding singular vectors.
We refer to
(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
, Section 10.4)
and references therein for the convergence results of the Lanczos procedure. Here, we highlight the following properties that are crucial for our analysis:

•

in exact arithmetic the method has finite termination; indeed, when
\ell=\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, the SVD in (
4.13
) is the exact SVD of
{Y_{reg}^{k}}
. However, the procedure is generally implemented as an iterative procedure due to round-off errors and to avoid to perform a large number of steps;

•

the inexact singular values
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}
approximate the corresponding
\sigma_{i}
from below
(
Saad 2011
, Th. 6.4)
;

•

letting
(U_{B})_{\ell+1,j}
be the last element of the
j
-th column of
U_{B}
, as shown in
Larsen 1998
, we have that

\min_{\sigma\in\sigma({Y_{reg}^{k}})}|\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}_{j,\ell}|\leq|\alpha_{\ell+1}||(U_{B})_{\ell+1,j}|.

(4.14)

In the classic implementations
Larsen 1998
, the index
\ell
is adaptively increased in order to reach the desired accuracy in the approximation of the first
r
singular values, using the error bound given in (
4.14
), that is based only on a byproduct of the Lanczos method.

In the next subsection, we show that also the accuracy requirements
(
3.12
) in iRAPM can be checked with no extra computational cost using information already provided by the Lanczos process.

4.2.2
Inexactness Criteria

Letting
G_{\ell}
be the matrix given in (
4.13
),
note that its first
\ell
singular values are
\tilde{\sigma}_{1,\ell},\dots,\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell,\ell}
, while the remaining are zero.
Although the singular values
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, are well-defined only for
\ell=1,\ldots,\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, we can artificially set
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}=\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
for
\ell>\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. We assume that at step
\ell>r
of the Lanczos procedure it holds
\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}>\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}
, i.e., the
r
and
r+1
singular values of
G_{\ell}
are distinct. This is a reasonable assumption since the method is expected to converge to a
r
-rank matrix. Then, let
\kappa_{\ell}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}>0}
be given by

\kappa_{\ell}=\frac{2}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{(1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}+\gamma\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}-\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}},

(4.15)

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
. For any
\ell>r
, we define the sequences

\displaystyle W^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}[G^{\ell}]_{1},\quad&\text{if }\ell\leq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\},\\
[Y_{reg}^{k}]_{1},\quad&\text{if }\ell>\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}\end{cases}

(4.16)

\displaystyle c^{\ell}

\displaystyle={\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{\ell,n_{1},n_{2}\}}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}},

(4.17)

\displaystyle a^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\kappa_{\ell}\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|&\text{for }\ell\leq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\},\\
0&\text{for }\ell>\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}\end{cases}.

(4.18)

In the following, we show that these sequences satisfy (
3.8a
)-(
3.8c
) and (
3.10
).

Lemma 4.8
.

The sequences (
4.16
) and (
4.17
) satisfy conditions (
3.8a
)-(
3.8c
).

Proof.

From
(
Saad 2011
, Theorem 6.4)
, we have that
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}\leq\sigma_{i}
for all
\ell<\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, and from the finite termination of the method we have
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}=\sigma_{i}
for
\ell\geq\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. Then,

c_{\ell}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{\ell,n_{1},n_{2}\}}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{\ell,n_{1},n_{2}\}}\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\|\hat{Y}^{k+1}-{Y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2},\quad\forall\,\ell\in\mathbb{N},

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}c_{\ell}=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{\ell,n_{1},n_{2}\}}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\|\hat{Y}^{k+1}-{Y_{reg}^{k}}\|^{2}.

Then, (
3.8a
) and (
3.8b
) holds. Condition (
3.8c
) follows by the definition of
W^{\ell}
in (
4.2.1
).
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, denote the singular values of
G_{\ell}
and assume that
\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}>\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}
. Then, the sequences given in (
4.2.1
) and (
4.18
) satisfy condition (
3.10
).

Proof.

The limit in (
3.10
) holds by the finite termination of the Lanczos algorithm and the definitions in (
4.2.1
) and (
4.18
). We now show that the inequality in (
3.10
) holds. We limit ourselves to the case
\ell<\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, as otherwise the thesis holds by (
4.2.1
) and (
4.18
). For simplicity, we omit the
index
k
and let
m=\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. Using the notation given in (
2.2
), we consider the block partitions

Y_{reg}=[Y_{reg}]_{1}+[Y_{reg}]_{2},\quad G_{\ell}={[G_{\ell}]_{1}}+[G_{\ell}]_{2}.

(4.19)

Then, we have that
\hat{Y}^{k+1}=[Y_{reg}]_{1}
and
W^{\ell}=[G_{\ell}]_{1}
.
The SVD of
Y_{reg}
and
G_{\ell}
can be written as

Y_{reg}=U_{Y_{reg}}\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m})V_{Y_{reg}}^{T},\quad G_{\ell}=U_{G_{\ell}}\text{diag}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1,\ell},\dots,\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell,\ell},0,\ldots,0)V_{G_{\ell}}^{T}.

Given
b_{1}\geq 0
and
b_{2}\geq 0
, we observe that the SVD of
[Y_{reg}]_{1}+b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}
and
[G_{\ell}]_{1}+b_{2}[G_{\ell}]_{2}
are

\displaystyle[Y_{reg}]_{1}+b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}

\displaystyle=U_{Y_{reg}}\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r},b_{1}\sigma_{r+1},\dots,b_{1}\sigma_{m})V_{Y_{reg}}^{T}

\displaystyle[G_{\ell}]_{1}+b_{2}[G_{\ell}]_{2}

\displaystyle=U_{G_{\ell}}\text{diag}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1,\ell},\dots,\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell},b_{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell},\dots,b_{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{\ell,\ell},0,\dots,0)V_{G_{\ell}}^{T}.

Then, we have:

\displaystyle[Y_{reg}]_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}([Y_{reg}]_{1}+b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}),\quad\forall\ b_{1}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,\sigma_{r}/\sigma_{r+1})}

(4.20)

\displaystyle[G_{\ell}]_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}([G_{\ell}]_{1}+b_{2}[G_{\ell}]_{2}),\quad\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ b_{2}\in[0,\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}/\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell})}.

(4.21)

Equation (
4.20
) yields

\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-([Y_{reg}]_{1}+b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2})\|^{2}\leq\|[G_{\ell}]_{1}-([Y_{reg}]_{1}+b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2})\|^{2}.

The above inequality implies

\|[G_{\ell}]_{1}-[Y_{reg}]_{1}\|^{2}+\|b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}\|^{2}-2\langle[G_{\ell}]_{1}-[Y_{reg}]_{1},b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}\rangle\geq\|b_{1}[Y_{reg}]_{2}\|^{2},

which yields

\frac{1}{2b_{1}}\|[G_{\ell}]_{1}-[Y_{reg}]_{1}\|^{2}-\langle[G_{\ell}]_{1}-[Y_{reg}]_{1},[Y_{reg}]_{2}\rangle\geq 0.

(4.22)

Using (
4.21
) and following a similar reasoning as before, we get

\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2}-\langle[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1},[G_{\ell}]_{2}\rangle\geq 0.

(4.23)

By summing up (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we obtain:

0\leq\left(\frac{1}{2b_{1}}+\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2}-\langle[G_{\ell}]_{1}-[Y_{reg}]_{1},[Y_{reg}]_{2}-[G_{\ell}]_{2}\rangle.

(4.24)

Recalling that
[Y_{reg}]_{2}=Y_{reg}-[Y_{reg}]_{1}
and
[G_{\ell}]_{2}=G_{\ell}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}
, we have:

-\left(\frac{1}{2b_{1}}+\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2}\leq\langle[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1},Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}\rangle-\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2},

which implies

\left(1-\frac{1}{2b_{1}}-\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2}\leq\langle[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1},Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}\rangle.

By limiting ourselves to the case where
\left(1-\frac{1}{2b_{1}}-\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)>0
, which is verified by imposing
b_{1},b_{2}\geq 1
(where equality cannot hold simultaneously for both), and using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{2b_{1}}-\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}\|.

(4.25)

Let us set
b_{1}=1
and

b_{2}=\gamma+(1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}/\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}=\frac{(1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}+\gamma\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}
, with
\gamma\in(0,1)
. With this choice of
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, the constant in
(
4.25
)
becomes:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{2b_{1}}-\frac{1}{2b_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}{2((1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}+\gamma\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}{(1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}+\gamma\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}}\right)=\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}-\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}{(1-\gamma)\tilde{\sigma}_{r,\ell}+\gamma\tilde{\sigma}_{r+1,\ell}}}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{\ell}},

where
\kappa_{\ell}
is given in (
4.15
).
Then, from (
4.25
), we have:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|[Y_{reg}]_{1}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|\leq\kappa_{\ell}\|Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}\|=a^{\ell},}

and the first part of (
3.10
) is verified.
∎

4.2.3
Computation of the right-hand side of the stopping criteria (
3.12
)

The criteria (
3.12
) require to compute additional quantities, in particular the Frobenius norms
\|W^{\ell}-{Y_{reg}^{k}}\|
and
\|G_{\ell}-{Y_{reg}^{k}}\|
. We show that these two quantities can be computed without additional cost as a byproduct of the Lanczos process.

We start recalling few useful relations. By (
4.11
) it follows
P_{\ell+1}^{T}Y_{reg}^{k}Q_{\ell}=B_{\ell}
and by (
4.13
) we have
G_{\ell}=P_{\ell+1}B_{\ell}Q_{\ell}^{T}
. Moreover, since the columns of
P_{\ell+1}
and
Q_{\ell}
are orthonormal, it holds:

\displaystyle p_{i}^{T}p_{j}

\displaystyle=0,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq\ell+1,\ i\neq j,

(4.26)

\displaystyle q_{i}^{T}q_{j}

\displaystyle=0,\quad 1\leq i,j\leq\ell,\ i\neq j,

(4.27)

\displaystyle(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})p_{i}

\displaystyle=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq\ell+1.

(4.28)

Inspired by
Simon and Zha 2000
, in the next Lemma we show that
\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}
can be computed by recurrence exploiting the entries of
B_{\ell}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let us denote
\omega_{\ell}=\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}
and let
\alpha_{\ell},\ \beta_{\ell+1}
be given in (
4.9
)-(
4.10
). Then,

\omega_{\ell-1}=\omega_{\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}^{2}+\beta_{\ell+1}^{2}.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, we omit the index
k
. From
(
4.12
)
we have:

\displaystyle Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}=Y_{reg}-P_{\ell+1}B_{\ell}Q_{\ell}^{T}

\displaystyle=Y_{reg}-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T}Y_{reg}+\alpha_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}e_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T}

\displaystyle=(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}+\alpha_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T}.

(4.29)

We observe that:

(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}=(I-P_{\ell}P_{\ell}^{T})Y_{reg}-p_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}^{T}Y_{reg}.

(4.30)

Then, (
4.29
), (
4.30
) and (
4.9
), yield:

\displaystyle\omega_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\|Y_{reg}-G_{\ell-1}\|^{2}=\|(I-P_{\ell}P_{\ell}^{T})Y_{reg}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}+{p_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}^{T}Y_{reg}}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}+{\alpha_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T}+\beta_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\omega_{\ell}}+\|\beta_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|^{2}+2\langle(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}+\alpha_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T},\beta_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle.

By using (
4.26
), (
4.27
), and (
4.28
), it follows that the Frobenius inner product is null. In addition, both
p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}
and
p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}
are rank-one matrices and the vectors
p_{\ell+1},p_{\ell}
and
q_{\ell}
are unitary vectors, then
\|p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}\|=1
,
\|p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|=1
and
\|\beta_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell}^{T}+\alpha_{\ell}p_{\ell}q_{\ell}^{T}\|^{2}=\alpha_{\ell}^{2}+\beta_{\ell+1}^{2}
and the thesis follows.
∎

The following Lemma shows the relationship between
\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-W^{\ell}\|^{2}
and
\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, denote the singular values of
G_{\ell}
. Then we have

\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-W^{\ell}\|^{2}=\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=r+1}^{\ell}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}.

Proof.

We omit the iteration index
k
, for the sake of simplicity. We recall that
W^{\ell}=[G_{\ell}]_{1}
, in addition (
4.19
) holds and
[G_{\ell}]_{2}=P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}
, where
[B_{\ell}]_{2}=(U_{B})_{2}(\Sigma_{B})_{2}(V_{B})_{2}
.
Then:

\displaystyle\|Y_{reg}-W^{\ell}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Y_{reg}-[G_{\ell}]_{1}\|^{2}=\|Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}+[G_{\ell}]_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\|Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}+\|[G_{\ell}]_{2}\|^{2}+2\langle Y_{reg}-G_{\ell},P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle}.

We can show that the Frobenius inner product is null.
Indeed, by rewriting
Y_{reg}-G_{\ell}
as in (
4.29
), we have:

\displaystyle\langle Y_{reg}-G_{\ell},P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg}+\alpha_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T},P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})Y_{reg},P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle+\alpha_{\ell+1}\langle p_{\ell+1}q_{\ell+1}^{T},P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\textrm{tr}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(Y_{reg}^{T}(I-P_{\ell+1}P_{\ell+1}^{T})P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\right)+\alpha_{\ell+1}\textrm{tr}\left(q_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}^{T}P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}Q_{\ell}^{T}\right)}.

(4.31)

The first term in (
4.31
) is null by (
4.28
), while the second, for the properties of the trace, becomes
\textrm{tr}\left(Q_{\ell}^{T}q_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}^{T}P_{\ell+1}[B_{\ell}]_{2}\right)
and then it is null for (
4.27
).
On the other hand, by the definition of Frobenius norm, we have
\|[G_{\ell}]_{2}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=r+1}^{\ell}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}
. Then, the thesis follows.
∎

5
Numerical illustration

In this section, we provide numerical tests which illustrate the behaviour of the iRAPM procedure. We focused on the Matrix Completion (MC) problem, which consists in recovering a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
from the knowledge of a subset of its entries; namely, the entries with indexes in the index set
\Omega=\{(i,j):\ M_{i,j}\text{ is known}\}
. In the following, we will write
(M)_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
to denote the matrix obtained by keeping the entries of
M
with indices in
\Omega
and setting to 0 the others, and analogously for
(M)_{\bar{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
\bar{\Omega}
the complement of
\Omega
. Without further constraints, there exists an infinite number of matrices
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
that comply with the condition
Z_{i,j}=M_{i,j}
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
. On the other hand, in most applications, one can assume that the matrix to be recovered has low rank, for more details, see
Candes and Recht 2009
. Problems that can be approached with MC arise in various fields, such as recommendation systems
Ramlatchan
et al. 2018
, image reconstruction
Nguyen et al. 2019
, and data imputation
Agarwal et al. 2018
;
Silei et al. 2023
.

Then, the MC problem is generally formulated as the affine rank minimization problem

\begin{aligned} \min\quad&\textrm{rank}(Z)\\
\textrm{s.t.}\quad&(Z)_{\Omega}=(M)_{\Omega}\end{aligned}.

(5.1)

This is a combinatorial optimization problem that requires a worst-case exponential running time.

In the literature, problem (
5.1
) has been approached in different ways, for example, replacing the rank function with a convex function like the nuclear norm
Candes and Recht 2009
, that is, the sum of the singular values of the matrix, or reformulating it as Semidefinite Programming Problem
Bellavia et al. 2021
.

We are interested in reformulating the MC problem as the feasibility problem (
4.1
). In fact, given an estimate
r
of the rank of the sought matrix, we can reformulate it as the problem of finding a matrix in the intersection of the rank level set and the set of matrices that match the observed entries, i.e.:

\text{find }Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\text{ such that }Z\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{C}_{r}},

(5.2)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\ Z_{i,j}=M_{i,j},\ \forall\ (i,j)\in\Omega\},

(5.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}:\textrm{rank}(Z)\leq r\}.

(5.4)

As the function that associates
M
with
(M)_{\Omega}
is a linear map,
\mathcal{C}
is an affine set and the projection onto it is given by

{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}}(Z)=(M)_{\Omega}+(Z)_{\bar{\Omega}}.

(5.5)

The inexact projection onto
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
in iRAPM is computed via the Lanczos method described in Section
4.2
, equipped with the stopping criteria (
3.12
), while in APM and RAPM we use the standard stopping criterion based on the bound (
4.14
). We note that also in APM and RAPM the projection is not exact; however, we expect it to be more accurate than in iRAPM, more expensive and not justified to preserve the overall convergence behaviour.

For all the three methods under investigation, our implementation of the Lanczos procedure is based on the PROPACK code by Larsen
Larsen 1998
, where we changed the stopping criterion when used in iRAPM. In APM and RAPM we used the standard stopping criterion and default tolerances, that is,
16\varepsilon_{m}
, where
\varepsilon_{m}
denotes the machine precision.

5.1
Projection
operators: computational costs

Before presenting the numerical results, we analyze the computational costs of
the projection operators involved in the MC problem (
5.2
).

As observed above,
projecting
onto
{\mathcal{C}}
consists in an element-wise replacement and can be computed in
n_{1}n_{2}
operations. On the other hand, the projection onto
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
via the Lanczos method described in Section
4.2
has two main parts that concur to the final cost: updating the Lanczos vectors (
4.9
)-(
4.10
), and checking the criterion (
4.14
) for APM and RAPM (the criteria (
3.12
) for iRAPM, respectively).

The Lanczos procedure requires at each iteration two matrix-vector products, that is,
O(n_{1}n_{2})
operations. Then, assuming that the final Krylov subspace dimension at the
k
-th outer iteration is
\bar{\ell}_{k}
, the final cost for building the Krylov subspace is of the order
O(n_{1}n_{2}\bar{\ell}_{k})
.

The standard criterion (
4.14
), employed in APM and RAPM, requires to check
r
inequalities at most, then it costs
O(r)
each time that the stopping criterion is checked. The evaluation of the two Frobenius norms required in the criteria (
3.12
) in the iRAPM procedure can be significantly reduced exploiting Lemma
4.10
and
4.11
. In fact, the two norms can be computed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l}\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}=\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}{-G_{0}}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\sum_{j=2}^{\ell+1}\beta_{j}^{2},\\
\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-W^{\ell}\|^{2}=\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{\ell}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=r+1}^{\ell}\tilde{\sigma}_{i,\ell}^{2}.\end{array}

(5.6)

where
G_{\ell}
is given in (
4.13
). Then, using (
5.6
), the inexact criteria require to compute the Frobenius norm
\|{Y_{reg}^{k}}-G_{0}\|
, whose cost is
O(n_{1}n_{2})
, only once at the beginning of the Lanczos procedure,
3\bar{\ell}_{k}-r
sums
and the check of only 2 inequalities.
Then, in all the alternating projection approaches under investigation, the cost of checking the stopping criteria is dominated by the cost of the Lanczos iterations, and we can conclude that, at a generic iteration
k
, the projection onto
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
costs
O(n_{1}n_{2}\bar{\ell}_{k})
.

We stress that the strategy for increasing the dimension of the Krylov subspace has a crucial impact on the final cost of the projection onto
{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
. The PROPACK code implements an adaptive strategy to reduce the number of criteria checks. This strategy can result in selecting unnecessarily large values of
\bar{\ell}_{k}
. In our implementation of the three alternating projection methods, we chose to check the stopping criteria at each Lanczos iteration in order to select the minimum Krylov dimension for which the criteria are satisfied.

6
Conclusions and perspectives

We have introduced an inexact regularized alternating projection method for computing a point in the intersection of two nonconvex sets. Inexact projections on one of the two sets are allowed. The convergence properties of the methods have been proved in a general setting and then specialized to affine rank minimization problems. For the latter class of problems the inexact projection on the rank level sets amounts to compute an approximate r-truncated SVD. Suitable accuracy requirements and corresponding stopping criteria for the Krylov inner solver have been proposed. Numerical results on matrix completion problems showed that the inexact method is in general less expensive than the exact counterpart.
We observed that the savings in computational cost are concentrated in the first phase of the convergence process. Then, it could be of interest to devise an adaptive strategy for choosing the parameter
\zeta
