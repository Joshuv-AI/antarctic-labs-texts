Algebraic groups generated by semisimple elements Thanks: The article was prepared within the framework of the project ‘‘International Academic Cooperation’’ HSE University
Ivan Arzhantsev

Abstract
. Given a connected linear algebraic group G G , we descrive the subgroup of G G generated by all semisimple elements.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 20G07, 14L17; Secondary 17B45, 14R20

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a connected linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field
{\mathbb{K}}
of characteristic zero. We recall some basic facts on the structure of the group
G
. Let
G^{\red}
and

G^{\ses}
be a maximal connected reductive subgroup and a maximal connected semisimple subgroup of
G
, respectively. Such subgroups are unique up to conjugation, see
[
8
, Theorem 6.5]
. Moreover,
G^{\red}=Z\cdot G^{\ses}
, where
Z
is the central torus on
G^{\red}
, and
G^{\ses}\cap Z
is finite.

Denote by
R(G)
and
R_{\text{u}}(G)
the radical and the unipotent radical of
G
, respectively. By definition,
R(G)
is the maximal connected normal solvable subgroup of
G
, and
R_{\text{u}}(G)
is the maximal normal unipotent subgroup of
G
. We have decompositions

G=G^{\ses}\rightthreetimes R(G)\quad\text{and}\quad G=G^{\red}\rightthreetimes R_{\text{u}}(G);

see
[
8
, Theorem 6.4]
.

Any family
\{X_{i},i\in I\}
of irreducible subvarieties in
G
passing through the unit generates a closed connected subgroup
H
in
G
; see
[
8
, Theorem 3.1.4]
. If the family is stable under conjugation, then the subgroup
H
is normal.

Any unipotent element in
G
is contained in a one-dimensional unipotent subgroup
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.1]
and any semisimple element is contained in a subtorus
[
7
, Theorem 22.2]
. These results and the Jordan decomposition imply that
G
is generated by one-dimensional subgroups.

It is well known that any one-dimensional connected linear algebraic group is isomorphic either to the additive group
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}=({\mathbb{K}},+)
or to the multiplicative group
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}=({\mathbb{K}}\setminus\{0\},\times)
of the ground field
[
7
, Theorem 20.5]
. A
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroup
(resp. a
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroup
) in
G
is a closed subgroup isomorphic to
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
(resp. to
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
).

Let us say that
G
is
additively generated
if
G
is generated by its
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups. It is proved in
[
9
, Lemma 1.1]
that the following conditions on a connected linear algebraic group
G
are equivalent:

(1)

G
is additively generated;

(2)

G
is generated by unipotent elements;

(3)

G
has no nontrivial characters;

(4)

R(G)=R_{\text{u}}(G)
.

If we denote by
G^{\add}
the subgroup generated by all
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups of a linear algebraic group
G
, then
G^{\add}
is a closed normal subgroup in
G
with

G^{\add}=G^{\ses}\rightthreetimes R_{\text{u}}(G)
. Moreover, we have
G=ZG^{\add}
, where
Z
is the central torus in
G^{\red}
.

In
[
9
]
, these observations were applied to compute the Makar-Limanov invariant of an affine variety. Also they are useful in the study of so-called flexible varieties. Namely, let
X
be an affine algebraic variety and
\SAut(X)
be the subgroup of the automorphism group
\Aut(X)
generated by all
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups. Clearly, if an additively generated linear algebraic group
G

acts on
X
, then the image of
G
in
\Aut(X)
is contained in
\SAut(X)
. In particular, if
G
acts on
X
with an open orbit, then
\SAut(X)
acts on
X
with an open orbit, and by
[
3
]
the action of
\SAut(X)
on its open orbit is infinitely transitive.

Let us say that a connected linear algebraic group
G
is
multiplicatively generated
, if
G
is generated by all its
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroups. The aim of this note is to characterize multiplicatively generated subgroups and to describe the maximal multiplicatively generated subgroup
G^{\mult}
in an arbitrary connected linear algebraic group
G
. The corresponding results are formulated in the next section.

2.
Main results

It is easy to see that a connected linear algebraic group
G
consists of semisimple elements if and only if
G
is a torus. The next proposition shows that the class of connected linear
algebraic groups generated by semisimple elements is much wider.

Proposition 1
.

Let
G
be a connected linear algebraic group. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

G
is multiplicatively generated;

(2)

G
is generated by semisimple elements;

(3)

any homomorpism
G\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
is trivial;

(4)

G
has no proper normal subgroup containing
G^{\red}
;

(5)

the derived subgroup
[G,G]
equals
G^{ss}\rightthreetimes R_{\text{u}}(G)
.

Now we come to a description of the subgroup
G^{\mult}
generated by all semisimple elements in a connected linear algebraic group
G
. It is convenient to switch to Lie algebras of algebraic groups in question. Denote by
\mathfrak{g}=\Lie(G)
the tangent algebra of a linear algebraic group
G
. The nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{n}=\Lie(R_{\text{u}}(G))
of
\mathfrak{g}
is a
G^{\red}
-module with respect to the adjoint action. Denote by
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
the sum of all non-trivial simple
G^{\red}
-submodules in
\mathfrak{n}
. Let
\mathfrak{s}
be the subalgebra in
\mathfrak{n}
generated by
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
and
U=\exp(\mathfrak{s})\subseteq R_{\text{u}}(G)
be the corresponding unipotent subgroup.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a connected linear algebraic group and
G^{\mult}
be the subgroup of
G
generated by all semisimple elemets. Then

G^{\mult}=G^{\red}\rightthreetimes U.

Let us recall that a subgroup
G
of a reductive group
F
is
regular
if
G
contains a maximal torus
T
of
F
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
G
be a regular subgroup of a reductive group
F
. Then
G
is generated by semisimple elements. In particular, any parabolic subgroup of a reductive group is generated by semisimple elements.

Example 1
.

Consider a solvable linear algebraic group

G=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&a&d&b\\
0&t&0&c\\
0&0&1&e\\
0&0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right);\ t\in{\mathbb{K}}\setminus\{0\},\ a,b,c,d,e\in{\mathbb{K}}\right\}.

The subgroup
G^{\mult}
is

G=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&a&0&b\\
0&t&0&c\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right);\ t\in{\mathbb{K}}\setminus\{0\},\ a,b,c\in{\mathbb{K}}\right\}.

In this case we can not find a unipotent subgroup of
G
of complementary dimension that has trivial intersection with
G^{\mult}
.
Notice also that the center of
G^{\mult}
is one-dimensional and unipotent.

3.
Proofs

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Any element of a
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroup in
G
is semisimple, and any semisimple element is contained in a subtorus
[
7
, Theorem 22.2]
, so it is a product of elements from
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroups. This proves the equivalence of
(1)
and
(2)
.

Any
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroup is reductive, so it is contained in a maximal reductive sugbroup, i.e., in a subgroup conjugated to
G^{\red}
. This gives the implication
(1)\Rightarrow(4)
. The implication
(4)\Rightarrow(2)
follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F
be a connected reductive group. Then any element of
F
is a product of at most two semisimple elements.

Proof.

The set of all semisimple elements in
F
contains a nonempty subset
V
that is open in
F

[
7
, Theorem 22.2]
. By
[
7
, Lemma 7.4]
, the subset
V\cdot V
coincides with
F
.
∎

Since elements of finite order are dense in
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
and any non-unit element of
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
has infinite order, any homomorphism from
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
to
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
is trivial. So we obtain
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
.

To prove
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
, assume that the normal subgroup
G^{\mult}
is a proper subgroup of
G
. Then the factorgroup
U:=G/G^{\mult}
is a linear algebraic group and any element in
U
is the image of a unipotent element in
G
under the projection
G\to U
. So all elements in
U
are unipotent, and
U
is a unipotent linear algebraic group. Since
U
is solvable, it admits a surjective homomorphism to a commutative unipotent group of positive dimension. A commutative unipotent group is the direct product of
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups.
This proves that there is a surjective homomorphism
U\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
. The composition
G\to U\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
is the desired surjection.

Let us prove that
(3)
is equivalent to
(5)
. Since
[G^{\red},G^{\red}]=G^{\ses}
, we conclude that
[G,G]=G^{\ses}\rightthreetimes L
, where
L\subseteq R_{\text{u}}(G)
is a normal subgroup. Since the group
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
is commutative, any homomorphism
G\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
can be decomposed as
G\to G/[G,G]\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
. Since
G/[G,G]
is a connected commutative
linear algebraic group, it is isomorphic to the direct product of a torus and a commutative unipotent group
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.8]
. So
(3)
is equivalent to the condition that
G/[G,G]
contains no non-unit unipotent element. This is equivalent to
L=R_{\text{u}}(G)
, and so to
(5)
.

This completes the proof of Proposition
1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By Lemma
1
, the subgroup
G^{\red}
is contained in
G^{\mult}
, and the decomposition
G=G^{\red}\rightthreetimes R_{\text{u}}(G)
implies

G^{\mult}=G^{\red}\rightthreetimes U
for some subgroup
U
in
R_{\text{u}}(G)
.

Let
T
be a maximal torus in
G^{\red}
and
\mathfrak{u}=\Lie(U)
.

Lemma 2
.

If
v
is a
T
-semiinvariant vector in
\mathfrak{n}
of nonzero weight, then
v\in\mathfrak{u}
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the one-parameter subgroup in
R_{\text{u}}(G)
with the tangent vector
v
. Fix an isomorphism
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}\to C,\,a\mapsto c(a)
. We have

tc(a)t^{-1}=c(\chi(t)a)

for all
t\in T
,
a\in{\mathbb{K}}
and some nonzero weight
\chi
of the torus
T
.
By Proposition
1
, the subgroup
G^{\mult}
contains all subgroups conjugated to
G^{\red}
. So the element

c(\chi(t)a-a)=(tc(a)t^{-1})c(-a)=t(c(a)t^{-1}c(a)^{-1})

is contained in
G^{\red}
for all
t\in T
and
a\in{\mathbb{K}}
. This proves that the subgroup
C
is contained in
U
and its tangent vector
v
is in
\mathfrak{u}
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

The subspace
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
is contained in
\mathfrak{u}
.

Proof.

Any simple
G^{\red}
-submodule
V
in
\mathfrak{n}
contains a highest weight vector
v
with respect to some Borel subgroup
B
containing the maximal torus
T
. If the module
V
is non-trivial, its highest weight is nonzero. By Lemma
2
, the vector
v
is in
\mathfrak{u}
. Since
\mathfrak{u}
is
G^{\red}
-invariant, we have
V\subseteq\mathfrak{u}
. This proves that
\mathfrak{n}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}
.
∎

Lemma
3
implies
\mathfrak{s}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}
and so
G^{\red}\rightthreetimes\exp(\mathfrak{s})\subseteq G^{\mult}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathfrak{r}=\Lie(G^{\red})
. Then the subalgebra
\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}
is an ideal in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check that for any simple
G^{\red}
-submodule
V
in
\mathfrak{n}
the subspace
[V,\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}]
is contained in
\mathfrak{s}
.
We have

[V,\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}]=[V,\mathfrak{r}]+[V,\mathfrak{s}]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_{1}+[V,\mathfrak{s}],

so it remains to prove that
[V,\mathfrak{s}]\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
.

If
V
is non-trivial, then
V\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_{1}
, and the conclusion follows from the definition of
\mathfrak{s}
. Assume that
V
is one-dimensional trivial.
Since the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{s}
is generated by
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
, it suffices to show that
[V,\mathfrak{n}_{1}]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_{1}
.

Take a non-trivial simple
G^{\red}
-submodule
W
in
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
. It suffices to prove that
[V,W]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_{1}
. The map

V\otimes W\to\mathfrak{n},\quad\sum_{i}v_{i}\otimes w_{i}\mapsto\sum_{i}[v_{i},w_{i}]

is
G^{\red}
-equivariant. Since
V
is one-dimensional trivial, the module
V\otimes W
is isomorphic to
W
. Take a nonzero vector
v\in V
.
The map
W\to[V,W]
,
w\mapsto[v,w]
is surjective and
G^{\red}
-equivariant. So either it is an isomorphism, and then
[V,W]\subseteq\mathfrak{n}_{1}
by the definition of
\mathfrak{n}_{1}
, or
we have
[V,W]=0
. This concludes the proof of Lemma
4
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

The ideal
\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}
is the minimal ideal in
\mathfrak{g}
containing
\mathfrak{r}
.

Proof.

If some ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
contains
\mathfrak{r}
, then by Lemmas
2
and
3
it contains the subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
. On the other hand, Lemma
4
claims that
\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}
is an ideal.
∎

Since any
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroup is contained in a subgroup conjugated to
G^{\red}
, we conclude from Lemma
1
that
G^{\mult}
is the subgroup generated by all subgroups conjugated to
G^{\red}
. In other words,
G^{\mult}
is the minimal normal subgroup of
G
containing
G^{\red}
. By
[
7
, Theorem 13.3]
this is equivalent to the fact that

\Lie(G^{\mult})
is the minimal ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
containing
\mathfrak{r}
. Lemma
5
implies
\Lie(G^{\mult})=\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{s}
and so

G^{\mult}=G^{\red}\rightthreetimes\exp(\mathfrak{s})
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

As
G
is a regular subgroup, the nilpotent ideal
\mathfrak{n}=\Lie(R_{\text{u}}(G))
is a linear span of some root vectors in the reductive Lie algebra
\Lie(F)
. Since all roots are nonzero
T
-weights, Lemma
2
implies
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}_{1}=\mathfrak{s}
, so
G=G^{\mult}
.
∎

4.
Concluding remarks and problems

It is shown in
[
5
,
6
]
that for any nonzero nilpotent element
x
in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
or
\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}({\mathbb{K}})
there is a nilpotent
y
such that
x
and
y

generate
\mathfrak{g}
. This result implies that for any
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroup
H_{1}
in the group
G=\SL_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
or
\Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{K}})
there is a
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroup
H_{2}
such that

H_{1}
and
H_{2}
generate the group
G
. In particular, the groups
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
and
\Sp_{2n}({\mathbb{K}})
are generated by two
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups.

At the same time, any
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups
H_{1},\ldots,H_{d}
in the commutative unipotent group
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}^{n}
define a homomorphism

H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{d}\to{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}^{n},\quad(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\mapsto h_{1}\ldots h_{d},

whose image is of dimension at most
d
. So the group
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}^{n}
is generated by at least
n
one-parameter subgroups.

Problem 1
.

Given an additively generated linear algebraic group
G
, what is the minimal number
k
such that some
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
generate
G
?

Problem 2
.

Given a multiplicatively generated linear algebraic group
G
, what is the minimal number
s
such that some
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroups
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}
generate
G
?

Lemma
1
motivates the following two problems.

Problem 3
.

Given an additively generated linear algebraic group
G
, what is the minimal number
l
such that any element of
G
is a product of at most
l
unipotent elements?

Problem 4
.

Given a multiplicatively generated linear algebraic group
G
, what is the minimal number
m
such that any element of
G
is a product of at most
m
semisimple elements?

The final theme is about surjective morphisms from affine spaces. We say that an algebraic variety
X
is an
A-image
if there is a surjective morphism
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{A}}^{d}\to X

for some positive integer
d
. In this case we have
{\mathbb{K}}[X]^{\times}={\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
and the variety
X
is irreducible and unirational. The last condition means that the field of rational functions
{\mathbb{K}}(X)
can be embedded into the field of fractions
{\mathbb{K}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
of the polynomial algebra
{\mathbb{K}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
.

One may ask whether any unirational variety
X
without non-constant invertible functions is an
A
-image. This holds when
X
is complete
[
4
, Theorem 1.7]
and when
X
is an affine cone
[
1
, Theorem 1]
.

Let
G
be an additively generated linear algebraic group. By
[
3
, Proposition 1.5]
, there is a sequence
H_{1},\ldots,H_{d}
of
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
-subgroups in
G
such that the morphism

H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{d}\to G,\quad(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\mapsto h_{1}\ldots h_{d}

is surjective. This shows that any additively generated linear algebraic group is an A-image. Moreover, this allows to prove that a homogeneous space
G/H
of a connected linear
algebraic group
G
is an A-image if and only if
{\mathbb{K}}[G/H]^{\times}={\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
; see
[
2
, Theorem C]
.

Denote by
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{*}
the punctured affine line
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\setminus\{0\}
. Let us say that an algebraic variety
X
is an
M-image
if there is a surjective morphism
\psi\colon({\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{*})^{d}\to X
for some positive integer
d
. In this case the variety
X
is again irreducible and unirational, but we have no restriction on invertible functions.

Since there is a surjective morphism

{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{*}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{1},\quad x\mapsto x+\frac{1}{x},

any
A
-image is an
M
-image. One may ask whether any unirational algebraic variety is an
M
-image.

Let us return to algebraic groups. Since any connected linear algebraic group
G
is generated by
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{a}}
- and
{\mathbb{G}}_{\text{m}}
-subgroups, again by
[
3
, Proposition 1.5]
there is a sequence

S_{1},\ldots,S_{d}
of connected one-parameter subgroups such that the morphism

S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{d}\to G,\quad(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\mapsto s_{1}\ldots s_{d}

is surjective. This shows that any homogeneous space
G/H
is an
M
-image.
