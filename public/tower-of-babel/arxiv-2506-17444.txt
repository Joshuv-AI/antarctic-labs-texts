Abstract
. We study the contact process on the long-range percolation cluster on ℤ \mathbb{Z} where each edge ⟨ i , j ⟩ \langle i,j\rangle is open with probability | i − j | − s |i-j|^{-s} for s > 2 s>2 . Using a renormalization procedure we apply Peierls-type argument to prove that the contact process dies out if the transmission rate is smaller than a critical threshold. Our methods involve the control of crossing probabilities for percolation on randomly-stretched lattices as in [ 13 ] .



Keywords: contact process; long-range percolation; anisotropic percolation; random environment

MSC numbers: 60K35, 82B43

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Model and main results

The aim of this article is to study the phase transition of the contact process on a random graph with long-range connections and unbounded degree distribution.

Let
s>0
.
Starting from the complete graph on
\mathbb{Z}
, we declare each unoriented edge
\langle x,y\rangle
to be open independently with probability
|x-y|^{-s}
, and closed otherwise.
The subgraph consisting of only the open edges defines a random graph
G=(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E})
, which is almost surely connected since it contains every edge of length one.
See Figure
1

Given
G
, we consider a contact process on
\mathbb{Z}
in which infections can spread exclusively along open edges.
Starting from a given nonempty set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
of infected sites, the system evolves as follows:

•

At rate
\lambda
, each infected site
x
transmits the infection to each site
y
such that
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathcal{E}
.

•

Infected sites recover at rate
1
.

Let
\zeta^{A,G}_{t}
denote the resulting Markov process in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
1
stands for infected and
0
for healthy.
We may omit the dependence on
G
and simply write
\zeta^{A}_{t}
.
We define the critical infection rate
\lambda_{c}
as the threshold value for survival.
That is,

\lambda_{c}=\inf\big\{\lambda>0\colon\zeta^{\{o\}}_{t}\neq\varnothing\text{ for every }t\geqslant 0\big\}.

Standard ergodicity arguments imply that
\lambda_{c}
is almost surely constant.
Since
G
almost surely contains a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
, we conclude that
\lambda_{c}<\infty
.

The interesting question is to determine whether a subcritical phase exists, that is, whether
\lambda_{c}>0
.
When the underlying random graph has unbounded degree distribution, this is often a challenging question due to the presence of vertices with atypically high degrees that are able to sustain infections for extended periods (see Section
1.3
for a discussion on related works on this problem).

For
G=(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E})
, when
s\leqslant 1
, each vertex has infinite degree a.s., therefore the contact processes survives as soon as
\lambda>0
, that is,
\lambda_{c}=0
.
In
[
4
]
, it is shown that if
s
is sufficiently large, then
\lambda_{c}>0
, and it is conjectured that this should hold as soon as
s>2
.
Our main result resolves this conjecture.

Theorem 1
.

If
s>2
then the critical infection rate for the contact process on the graph
G=(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E})
satisfies
\lambda_{c}>0
.

s>2

Remark 1
.

During preparation of the final version of this manuscript, we became aware of the recent work
[
16
]
, in which the authors also prove the same conjecture, among other results.
Both works were developed independently. Although they share some similarities, the proofs rely on different techniques.

While our results establish the existence of a phase transition for
s>2
, the behavior of the model in the intermediate regime
1<s\leqslant 2
remains an open question.
Determining whether a nontrivial critical infection rate exists in this range would provide a more complete understanding of the model, analogous to the well-established phase diagram for other one-dimensional systems with long-range interactions of order
|i-j|^{-s}
such as the Ising model
[
6
,
7
]
and Bernoulli percolation
[
25
,
1
]
.

1.2.
Model and definitions

The contact process introduced by Harris in
[
11
]
is an interacting particle system often regarded as a model for the spread of an infection (or information) in a graph.

We will study the contact process on
G
through its graphical representation.
We now describe the main ideas involved and refer the reader to the classic work by Harris
[
12
]
for a detailed and formal construction.

The construction is based on two independent families of Poisson processes
(\mathcal{I}_{e}\colon e\in\mathcal{E})
with rate
\lambda
and
(\mathcal{R}_{x}\colon x\in\mathbb{Z})
with rate
1
.
The infection evolves on the space-time structure
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where the vertical axis represents time. For each vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and each time
t\in\mathcal{R}_{x}
, we place a lozenge at the point
(x,t)
, marking a recovery event that halts the infection at
x
. Additionally, for each edge
e=\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathcal{E}
and each time
t\in\mathcal{I}_{e}
, we draw a horizontal double arrow between
(x,t)
and
(y,t)
, indicating a potential transmission of the infection from
x
to
y
and from
y
to
x
.

Consider an initial set of infected vertices
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
at time 0.
Infection paths start at points in
A\times\{0\}
and can only move upward along vertical lines (representing the passage of time) until they encounter a lozenge, at which point the path terminates (indicating recovery).
Alternatively, a path can jump horizontally from
(x,t)
to
(y,t)
by crossing a double arrow associated with the edge
\langle x,y\rangle
, enabling the infection to spread to vertex
y
at time
t
.
An illustration of the graphical representation, including a sample infection path starting from the origin
o\in\mathbb{Z}
, is provided in Figure
2
.

1.3.
Related works

The contact process on graphs like the
d
-dimensional Euclidean lattice and the
d
-ary regular tree is a well-understood model (see
[
19
,
20
]
for comprehensive accounts).

Over the past two decades, research has increasingly focused on the contact process on random graphs.
To the best of our knowledge, sufficient conditions for the existence of a non-trivial subcritical phase for the contact process on random graphs with unbounded degree distributions were first established by Ménard and Singh
[
21
]
. Their conditions apply to graphs such as Delaunay triangulations and random geometric graphs.
In
[
4
]
, Can verified these conditions for the long-range percolation graph when
s>102
.
More recently, Jahnel et al.
[
16
]
established the existence of a subcritical phase for all
s>2
, independently of our work.
Their proof relies on a coupling with a random walk on a random environment while ours, as is classic in percolation, is based on a Peierls argument.

In
[
16
]
, the authors also study the phase transition for the contact process on one-dimensional random geometric graphs with i.i.d. random radii.
They show that a phase transition takes place if, and only if, the radius distribution has finite mean.
Our methods work for that kind of graphs as long as the radius has tail distribution decaying as
r^{1+\alpha}
for any
\alpha>0
.
The contact process on the dynamic version of this random graph, where the radii update independently at rate one was studied in
[
9
]
.

Many other authors have considered different kinds of random graphs, mainly Galton-Watson trees and the configuration model.
We now list some of the results in the field and refer the reader to
[
27
]
for a gentle and comprehensive account on the topic.

In
[
26
]
, Pemantle and Stacey studied the contact process on Galton-Watson trees with bounded-degree offspring distributions.
They identified examples where the critical points for local and global survival differ, leading to a double phase transition, as observed in regular
d
-ary trees.
However, the existence of a subcritical phase for Galton-Watson trees with unbounded-degree offspring remained an open question.
Huang and Durrett
[
5
]
showed that if the offspring distribution is subexponential, no subcritical phase exists.
In
[
2
]
, Bhamidi et al. established that a subcritical phase exists if and only if the offspring distribution has exponential tails.

The contact process on random graphs generated by the configuration model on
n
vertices with a power-law degree distribution (with exponent greater than 3) was studied by Chatterjee and Durrett
[
3
]
.
As these graphs are finite, the contact process eventually dies out.
However, they proved that no phase transition occurs, meaning that for any positive infection parameter
\lambda
, the process survives for an exceptionally long time.
Specifically, the extinction time grows at least as a stretched exponential function of the number of vertices.
Mountford et al.
[
23
]
improved this result, showing that the survival time is exponential in the number of vertices.
Additionally, Mountford et al.
[
22
]
established sharp bounds for the density of infected sites.
For the particular case when the degrees equal some constant
d
, also called random
d
-regular graph, Mourrat and Valesin
[
24
]
and Lalley and Su
[
18
]
demonstrated the existence of a phase transition.
The latter work also established a cutoff for the density of infected sites.
Bhamidi et al.
[
2
]
showed that a phase transition occurs if and only if the degree distribution has an exponential moment.

1.4.
Summary of the paper

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on a Peierls-type argument for a percolation model obtained after we perform a one-step renormalization, which we summarize next.

Divide the graph into intervals bounded by cut-points:

We start by partitioning the underlying random graph
G=(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E})
into infinitely many intervals
V_{k}
(
k\in\mathbb{Z}
) whose endpoints are cut-points.
These are vertices
v\in\mathbb{Z}
for which no open edge
\langle x,y\rangle
exists with
x<v<y
.
This implies that there are no edges linking sites from intervals
V_{k}
and
V_{j}
with
k\neq j
.
The existence of a doubly infinite sequence of cut-points is ensured by the assumption that
s>2
.
This is the done in Section
2
.

Controlling the number of vertices and edges within intervals:

We then show that the number of vertices within the intervals
V_{k}
behaves as a sequence of independent random variables with finite
(1+\varepsilon)
-moment, for some sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
(Lemma
3
).
The same holds for the number of edges connecting pairs of vertices in
V_{k}
(Lemma
4
).

Renormalizing to a box lattice:

Next, we partition the time axis into intervals of length
T>0
.
This leads to a tiling of the space-time domain
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
into boxes
V_{k}\times[jT,(j+1)T)
that project horizontally onto
V_{k}
and vertically onto strips of length
T
.

A box is said to be good if:

•

every vertex
x\in V_{k}
has a recovery mark in
[jT,(j+1)T)
;

•

for every pair
x,y\in V_{k}
, there are no transmission attempts along the edge
\langle x,y\rangle
in
[jT,(j+1)T)
.

The construction is carefully made to ensure that the status of each box is independent.
Moreover, the probability that a box
V_{k}\times[jT,(j+1)T)
is good is bounded below by
p^{\phi_{k}}
, where
(\phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d. random variables with finite
(1+\varepsilon)
-moment, and
p=p(\lambda,T)
can be made arbitrarily close to
1
by choosing
T
large enough and
\lambda
small.

This leads to a site percolation model on the renormalized lattice
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where the probability that a site is open depends on the column it belongs to.

This is the content of Section
3
.

Peierls argument:
A Peierls-type argument shows that the contact process dies out if there exists a semicircuit of good boxes surrounding the origin.
In fact, since the left and right boundaries of each box project onto cut-points, if the infection ever enters a good box
V_{k}\times[jT,(j+1)T)
, it cannot propagate to neighboring boxes
V_{k-1}\times[jT,(j+1)T)
,
V_{k+1}\times[jT,(j+1)T)
, or
V_{k}\times[(j+1)T,(j+2)T)
.

Our task now reduces to showing that, for
p
sufficiently large, a semicircuit of open sites surrounding the origin exists almost surely for the above site percolation.
If we were dealing with a percolation model with weak dependencies, standard techniques could be used to establish the existence of such circuits.
However, since the parameters vary across columns, correlations along the vertical axis do not decay with distance (under the annealed law), complicating the implementation of a Peierls-type argument.

Comparison with bond percolation on a randomly stretched lattice:

It turns out that the probability of finding semicircuits in the above site percolation model can be compared with that of a bond percolation model studied in
[
13
]
.

It is defined as follows: Let
\Lambda=\{\xi_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be a sequence of i.i.d. positive random variables.
Conditional on
\Lambda
, each edge
e
of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}
is declared open independently with probability

(1)

p_{e}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p,&\text{if }e=\{(i,j),(i,j+1)\}\text{ for some }i,j,\\
p^{\xi_{i+1}},&\text{if }e=\{(i,j),(i+1,j)\}\text{ for some }i,j.\end{array}\right.

When the sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n}
consists of independent geometric random variables, the model is reminiscent of the one introduced in
[
17
]
and studied further in
[
15
,
14
]
.
In our setting, however, the marginal distribution of
\xi_{n}
is no longer geometric, requiring a different analytical approach.
To handle this, we rely on the results developed in
[
13
]
.
There the authors derive estimates for the probability of crossing rectangles and apply them to show that for
p
large enough the connected component containing the origin is infinite with positive probability.
Here we use crossings of rectangles in order to build a semicircuit around the origin with high probability.
This is done in Sections
4
and
5
.

2.
Control of the environment, cut-points

Our running assumption throughout the article will be that
s>2
.
It implies that the expected number of edges that connect sites on opposite sides of the origin is finite almost surely.
In fact,

(2)

\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{-}}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\mathbb{P}\big(\langle i,j\rangle\text{ is open}\big)=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{-}}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\frac{1}{|i-j|^{s}}<\infty.

We define

(3)

\displaystyle x_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\big\{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\colon\textrm{$\langle i,j\rangle$ is open for some $i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{-}$}\big\},

(4)

\displaystyle x_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\big\{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{-}\colon\text{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is open for some $j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}$}\big\},

with the convention that
\sup(\varnothing)=\inf(\varnothing)=0
.
It follows from (
2
) that both
x_{+}
and
x_{-}
are finite, almost surely.
Notice that, in order to determine
x_{-}
and
x_{+}
one only needs to reveal the states of edges connecting sites on opposite sides of the origin.

A cut-point is a vertex
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that, for every pair of positive integers
i,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, the edge
\langle x-i,x+j\rangle
is closed.
We have

(5)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\text{$o$ is a cut-point})&=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\prod_{j\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}\big(\langle-i,j\rangle\textrm{ is closed}\big)\\
&=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}\prod_{j\geqslant 1}\left(1-\frac{1}{|j+i|^{s}}\right)>0,\end{split}

where we used the assumption
s>2
in the last inequality.
By translation invariance there exist infinitely many cut-points on both sides of the origin, almost surely.
We look for those lying to the right of
x_{+}
exploring the state of edges in the positive half-line as follows.
Define

x_{1}=\min\{x>x_{+}\colon\textrm{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $0\leqslant i<x<j$}\}

and inductively for
n\geqslant 1
,

x_{n+1}=\min\big\{x>x_{n}\colon\textrm{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $x_{n}\leqslant i<x<j$}\big\},

Analogously, we can perform the exploration to the left of
x_{-}
defining

x_{-1}=\max\big\{x<x_{-}\colon\textrm{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $i<x<j\leqslant 0$}\big\}

and inductively for
n\geqslant 1
,

x_{-(n+1)}=\max\big\{x<x_{-n}\colon\text{$\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $i<x<j\leqslant x_{-n}$}\big\},

This yields a bi-infinite sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
of cut-points.

We now define, for each
n\geqslant 1
,

(6)

\theta_{n}=x_{n+1}-x_{n}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\theta_{-n}=x_{-n}-x_{-(n+1)}.

Given the above construction of the sequence
\{x_{k},\penalty\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
, one can readily check that
\{\theta_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
forms an i.i.d. sequence.
We denote by
\theta
a random variable with the same distribution as any of the variables in that sequence.
The following result shows that
\theta
has some moment of order larger than one.

Lemma 1
.

For every
0\leqslant\delta<s-2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta^{1+\delta}\right]<\infty.

For technical reasons, it will be convenient to extract a subsequence from
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ast}}
where each element is also surrounded by adjacent cut-points.
For
k\geqslant 2
, an element
x_{k}
is called a strong cut-point if
\theta_{k-1}=\theta_{k}=1
.
Similarly, for
k\leqslant-2
, the cut-point
x_{k}
is called strong if
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}=1
.
Define

\begin{split}n_{1}&=\min\{n>1\colon\theta_{2n-1}=\theta_{2n}=1\}\quad\textrm{and, for $k\geqslant 1$,}\\
n_{k+1}&=\min\{n>n_{k}\colon\theta_{2n-1}=\theta_{2n}=1\}.\end{split}

Analogously, on the left, define

\begin{split}n_{-1}&=\max\{n<1\colon\theta_{2n+1}=\theta_{2n}=1\}\quad\textrm{and, for $k\geqslant 1$,}\\
n_{-(k+1)}&=\max\{n<n_{-k}\colon\theta_{2n+1}=\theta_{2n}=1\}.\end{split}

(It is not difficult to verify that the
min
and
max
run over nonempty sets and are well-defined.)

We now will pick every other strong cut-point.
Define, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,

(7)

v_{k}=x_{2n_{k}}.

This gives rise to a sequence
\{v_{k},\penalty\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
of strong cut-points spanning
\mathbb{Z}
.

Analogously to (
6
), we define

(8)

\eta_{k}=v_{k+1}-v_{k}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\eta_{-k}=v_{-k}-v_{-(k+1)}.

As before, we have that
\{\eta_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
forms an i.i.d. sequence.
We denote by
\eta
a random variable with the common distribution of
\{\eta_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
.
The following result is an extension of Lemma
1
.

Lemma 2
.

For every
0<\delta<s-2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta^{1+\delta}\right]<\infty.

Given the new sequence of strong cut-points
\{v_{k},\penalty\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
, we will partition
\mathbb{Z}
into intervals
V_{k}
, where

(9)

V_{0}=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}\colon v_{-1}<x<v_{1}\right\};

for each
k>0
,

(10)

V_{2k-1}=\{v_{k}\},\quad V_{2k}=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}\colon v_{k}<x<v_{k+1}\right\};

and, for each
k<0
,

(11)

V_{2k+1}=\{v_{k}\},\quad V_{2k}=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}\colon v_{k-1}<x<v_{k}\right\}.

The random variables
|V_{k}|
are independent, but their laws depend on
k
.
For odd values of
k
, the intervals are degenerate and
|V_{k}|
=1.
When
k\neq 0
is even,
|V_{k}|=\eta_{k/2}-1
, hence, as a consequence of Lemma
2
we have

Lemma 3
.

For every
0<\delta<s-2
and
k\neq 0

(12)

\mathbb{E}\Big[|V_{k}|^{1+\delta}\Big]<\infty.

Remark 2
.

Note that the above discussion does not include
V_{0}
.
In fact, as seen in (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
) the law of
V_{0}
differs from the others.

We will also partition the set of open edges according to the interval
V_{k}
where an edge originates.
Let
\mathcal{E}=\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathcal{E}_{k}
, where

\mathcal{E}_{0}=\big\{\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathcal{E}\colon\{x,y\}\cap V_{0}\neq\varnothing\big\},

and

\mathcal{E}_{k}=\begin{cases}\bigl\{\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathcal{E}\colon\max\{x,y\}\in V_{k}\bigr\},&\penalty\ \textrm{ if }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{-},\\
\big\{\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathcal{E}\colon\min\{x,y\}\in V_{k}\big\},&\penalty\ \textrm{ if }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.\end{cases}

When
k
is odd,
|\mathcal{E}_{k}|=1
.
In fact, since
v_{k}
is a strong cut-point, either
\mathcal{E}_{2k-1}=\{\langle v_{k},v_{k}+1\rangle\}
or
\mathcal{E}_{2k+1}=\{\langle v_{k}-1,v_{k}\rangle\}
according to whether
k
is positive or negative.
The random variables
\{|\mathcal{E}_{k}|,\penalty\ k\in 2\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
are i.i.d. The next result shows that they have finite moments of order greater than one.

Lemma 4
.

If
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then for every
k\neq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left|\mathcal{E}_{k}\right|^{1+\varepsilon}\Big]<\infty.

Remark 3
.

The law of
|\mathcal{E}_{0}|
differs from the others because the same holds for
V_{0}
(see Remark
2
).

The remainder of this section is dedicated to the proof of Lemmas
1
,
2
, and
4
.

For the proof of Lemmas
1
, it will be convenient to first replace the original sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
with a modified version
(x_{k}^{L})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
, which is constructed in a similar manner but disregards edges shorter than
L
.
That is, for any fixed positive integer
L
, we set

x_{1}^{L}=\min\{x>x_{+}\colon\textrm{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $0\leqslant i<x<j$ with $j-i>L$}\},

and, for each
n\geqslant 1
,

x_{n+1}^{L}=\min\{x>x_{n}^{L}\colon\textrm{ $\langle i,j\rangle$ is closed for all $x_{n}^{L}\leqslant i<x<j$ with $j-i>L$}\}.

Similarly, for
x_{n}^{L}
, with
n\leqslant-1
.
We recover the original sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
simply by taking
L=1
.
Writing,

\theta_{n}^{L}=x_{n+1}^{L}-x_{n}^{L}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\theta_{-n}^{L}=x_{-n}^{L}-x_{-(n+1)}^{L}.

gives rise to an i.i.d. sequence
\{\theta_{k}^{L}\colon k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
.
Let
{\theta^{L}}
be a random variable distributed as any of the elements in that sequence.
The content of the next result is similar to that in Lemma
1
except that
\theta
is replaced by
{\theta^{L}}
for
L
large.

Lemma 5
.

Let
0<\delta<s-2
.
There exists
L_{o}=L_{o}(s,\delta)
such that, for every
L\geqslant L_{o}(s,\delta)
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[{(\theta^{L})}^{1+\delta}\Big]<\infty.

Before we proceed to prove Lemma
1
let us introduce the
backward exploration
, reminiscent of a procedure found in
[
8
, Section 2]
.

Y^{L}=\sup\big\{i<0\colon\text{$\langle i,j\rangle$ is open for some $j>0$ with $j-i>L$}\big\}

with the convention
\sup\varnothing=-\infty
.
Let us denote
a(L)=\mathbb{P}(Y^{L}=-\infty)
.
Since
s>2
,
a(L)
is positive and converges to
1
as
L

6.
Proof of Theorem
1

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1
.
As argued in the last paragraph of Section
3
, it suffices to show that for a sufficiently small infection rate
\lambda
, there almost surely exists a semi-circuit of good vertices enclosing the origin in the renormalized lattice
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Lemma
13
enables us to study the existence of such circuits in a different bond percolation process.
We then apply Lemma
8
to compare the probabilities of crossing events, and thus of existence of semi-circuits, in both models.
This lemma establishes that the probability of semi-circuits in the former percolation process is bounded below by the probability in the latter, with a certian parameter
\rho
that can be made as large as we want by decreasing
\lambda
.
As stated in Lemma
13
, this lower bound equals 1, provided that
\rho
is sufficiently large.
The result follows.
