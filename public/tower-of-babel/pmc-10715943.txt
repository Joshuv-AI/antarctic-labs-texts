From medical therapy to mechanical support: strategies for device selection and implantation techniques
Authors: Letizia F Bertoldi, Andrea Montisci, Dirk Westermann, Mario Iannaccone, Vasileios Panoulas, Nikos Werner, Norman Mangner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiogenic shock (CS) is a complex clinical syndrome with a high risk of mortality. The recent, rapid development of temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) has altered CS treatment. While catecholamines remain the cornerstone of CS therapy, tMCS usage has increased. According to shock severity, different treatment strategies including catecholamines alone, catecholamines and tMCS, or multiple tMCS might be used. State-of-the-art implantation techniques are necessary to avoid complications linked to the invasive nature of tMCS. In particular, bleeding and access-site complications might counteract the potential haemodynamic benefit of a percutaneous ventricular assist device. In this review, we describe the role of catecholamines in CS treatment and present the different tMCS devices with an explanation on how to use them according to CS aetiology and severity. Finally, an overview of the best practice for device implantation is provided.Keywords: Inotropes, VIS score, SCAI classes, pVAD, tMCS selection, tMCS implantation

Introduction

Cardiogenic shock (CS) carries a high risk of mortality, with an estimated rate of 50% within 6–12 months. This high mortality risk persists despite widespread availability of early re-vascularization techniques in the setting of CS in acute myocardial infarction (AMI-CS) and, more recently, significant advancements in percutaneous ventricular assist devices (pVADs). The management of CS remains a significant challenge given the limited availability of data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to guide treatment. While pharmacological treatment is often considered the cornerstone of CS therapy, inotropes and vasopressors are typically limited by an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand, as well as metabolic derangements. The current landscape of temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) provides the opportunity to augment cardiac output with subsequent improvement of systemic perfusion. Current tMCS can tackle both left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) failure, and each type of tMCS carries its own advantages and disadvantages including technical issues with insertion, required haemodynamic support, and potential complications. In this review, we discuss the issue of CS treatment from catecholamines escalating to tMCS according to the underlying aetiology and describe the best clinical practice for device insertion and acute optimization of CS patients.

Inotropes in cardiogenic shock

Intravenous inotropic agents are almost universally considered the first-line cardiovascular support treatment for patients presenting CS. As such, in real-world clinical practice, about 90% of CS patients receive inotropes and/or vasopressors.1 However, despite studies investigating the effects of different inotropic agents in CS during the last decades, all failed to generate strong recommendations with regard to improved morbidity and mortality. On the contrary, inotropic agents have well-known negative effects: catecholamines increase heart rate, wall stress, and myocardial oxygen consumption,2 while vasopressors, despite increasing the mean arterial pressure, also increase LV afterload and significantly impair micro-vascular perfusion.3 Those undesirable effects negatively impact myocardial recovery and lead to end-organ dysfunction, negating the benefit of the acutely increased cardiac output. Given the lack of strong recommendations supporting the use of inotropes and their known negative effects, the primary question is not which drug should be used in CS but if there is still a role for inotropes in CS management.

Does a role for inotropes still exist in cardiogenic shock?

The modern approach to CS is based on the shift from hypotension to hypoperfusion. Catecholamines and vasopressors primarily provide an ‘aesthetic’ benefit to haemodynamic parameters by increasing mean arterial pressure but without securing a real benefit on peripheral perfusion.3 Inotropic agents increase myocardial work and oxygen consumption by overdriving physiological protective mechanisms of the damaged heart (stunning and hibernation). When applying a cardioprotective vision of CS management, we should aim for the exact opposite strategy by allowing the myocardium to rest (unloading) to maximize the chance for recovery. In this scenario, early implantation of a pVAD plays a fundamental role. The most recent guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology emphasize this concept by assigning to pVAD the highest class of recommendation (as compared with inotropes) as a first-line strategy in CS management (IIa vs. IIb).4 Thus, the paradigm is shifting towards an inotrope-free strategy for CS management, while awaiting clinical trials to explore this field.5

Practical management of inotropes in cardiogenic shock

Despite the strong scientific rationale of an inotrope-free strategy, inotropes are still widely used as a first-line therapy in clinical practice, primarily due to cost-effectiveness imbalance of an ‘only-tMCS’ management.

Inotropic agents

Data comparing different supportive therapies are conflicting, and all have failed to demonstrate a strong benefit on major outcomes. Specifically, the inotrope levosimendan is the only agent that has shown a potential reduction in mortality when compared with placebo in the early stages of CS6 but failed to demonstrate a benefit when compared with other inotropes. Available evidence on vasopressors suggests that norepinephrine may be associated with reduced mortality compared with dopamine or epinephrine.7,8 However, given the paucity and conflicting nature of current data, in real-world clinical practice, inotrope usage is widely heterogeneous and mainly driven by physician experience.9

Dosage

The maximum acceptable level of inotropes before escalation to tMCS implantation has yet to be clearly defined. Previous studies demonstrated that negative haemodynamic effects and cardiac toxicity correlate with catecholamine dosage.10 However, in several clinical trials, high doses were used with the purpose of maximizing positive haemodynamic effects, shifting the risk–benefit equation in the wrong direction. The ALT SHOCK trial demonstrated satisfactory outcomes with a time-dependent protocol for the management of CS with a clinically targeted quick drug escalation at an upper dose limit (epinephrine 0.12 µg/kg/min) and timely tMCS implantation.11

Some experts also suggest including levosimendan (VIS + 50× levosimendan dose µg/kg/min) and enoximone (VIS + enoximone dose µg/kg/min) in the score.13 Vasoactive-inotropic score is attractive for standardizing the degree of haemodynamic support across different institutions and, if included in management algorithms, could help clinicians to identify patients who might benefit from escalation of support. For this purpose, recent studies are focusing on investigating and defining cut-offs to stratify CS patient’s risk and guide clinical management.

In-hospital mortality rates are significantly higher in patients with higher VIS. Previous studies showed a correlation between mortality and VIS cut-offs (15.6%, 20.8%, 40.2%, and 67.3% for VIS < 10, 10–30, 30–90, and >90),14 and that VIS is an independent predictor for mortality in CS patients, with median values of 19 in survivor vs. 41 in non-survivor patients.15 Furthermore, different studies demonstrated that VIS may be a potential marker for determining the initiation of haemodynamic support with veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO), demonstrating better in-hospital outcomes for initiation of support before reaching high VIS (VIS ranged from 32 to 84).14,16 A retrospective analysis from the Altshock-2 registry revealed that in CS populations, VIS is able to detect a transition towards higher Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) stages, suggesting cut-offs for clinical management (VIS median value for SCAI B = 19, SCAI C = 13, SCAI D = 24, and SCAI E = 40).17 Based on literature evidence and expert consensus, we propose an algorithm for practical management of inotropes in CS (Figure 1).

Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions B and C

Early CS patients (SCAI C and, particularly, SCAI B with ‘pre-shock’ phenotype patients) are at high risk of worsening clinical status and progression towards advanced SCAI classes due to the underestimation of haemodynamic impairment.18 Inotropes still play a role as first-line therapy in these early CS profiles, but their use should be limited to the lowest dose and shortest time interval to avoid end-organ impacts. In these scenarios, up-front pulmonary artery catheter (PAC) placement for accurate risk stratification is strongly recommended. In addition, during the first 24 h, a complete haemodynamic re-evaluation should be assessed every 2 h to ensure early detection of deterioration signs and escalate to tMCS implantation if necessary.

A VIS < 20 is acceptable for risk–benefit balance; therefore, if VIS ≥ 20 is needed for gaining haemodynamic stability, tMCS should be considered. Moreover, if no clinical benefit is reached in terms of haemodynamic recovery (adequate urinary output and serum lactate clearance) and improvement in end-organ function after 24 h of inotropic/vasopressor support, tMCS implantation should be considered. In the setting of AMI complicated by CS, in which early implantation of a pVAD prior to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) showed a positive impact on short- and mid-term mortality as compared with post-PCI implantation,19 special consideration should be given to an early escalation strategy.

Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions D and E

In SCAI D and SCAI E patients, tMCS should be the first line of treatment. In the early phase after SCAI D-E CS diagnosis, inotropic agents have to be considered as a bridge to tMCS, and their use must be limited to the shortest time interval, given an early and timely implantation directly correlates with better outcomes.19 Knowing that VIS score can detect more severe SCAI stages and correlates with mortality, a VIS ≥ 50 should lead to consideration of immediate V-A ECMO implantation. After tMCS implantation, inotropic agents should be completely weaned as soon as possible. In cases of RV dysfunction (while on left uni-ventricular support) or persistent hypotension secondary to a systemic inflammatory response that often accompanies CS a more prolonged infusion may be necessary. The need for inotropic therapy after 48 h on tMCS without haemodynamic improvement should lead to evaluate the patient for tMCS escalation (uni- or bi-ventricular).

Weaning from temporary mechanical circulatory support

Levosimendan infusion during the weaning phase from tMCS should be considered to facilitate successful device explantation. The prolonged vasodilation induced by levosimendan could be a particularly useful tool in this scenario, allowing a gradual weaning and providing a mid-term support in the crucial immediate post-tMCS period. Initial evidence of safety and efficacy is reported for levosimendan for successfully aiding in weaning from V-A ECMO support.20,21

One of the most common clinical scenarios involves patients experiencing CS after AMI, at SCAI Stage C. The primary objectives in these cases are to achieve rapid haemodynamic stabilization, facilitate weaning from inotropes, ensure LV unloading, and create optimal conditions for coronary re-vascularization.22 After re-vascularization, a thorough evaluation with echocardiography and PAC guidance is essential in the subsequent hours.23,24 This evaluation should focus on assessing the adequacy of oxygen delivery to the tissues by assessing haemodynamic improvement. For the LV, improvement in haemodynamics includes an increase in cardiac index and cardiac output (cardiac power output > 0.6) and a reduction in pulmonary wedge pressures. For the right ventricle, the central venous pressure, pulmonary artery pulsatility index, reduction in oxygen extraction (measured by an increase in mixed venous saturations), and increased tissue perfusion characterized by decreased lactate levels, normalized pH, base deficit, and improvement in hourly diuresis should occur. Based on these findings, the future strategy will be determined. Progression to a higher SCAI stage may occur due to RV failure (RVF) or worsening LV function, characterized by persistent signs of shock. In such cases, uni-/bi-ventricular tMCS escalation may be required, involving the implantation of devices such as Impella 5.5, V-A ECMO, Bi-PELLA, or RVAD oxygenator (Protek Duo).22,23

Regardless of aetiology, patients at SCAI Stage D/E or in refractory cardiac arrest (extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation) require immediate stabilization with V-A ECMO. In the hours following the procedure, it is crucial to assess the potential escalation to V-A ECMO + Impella CP or Impella 5.0/5.5 (ECMELLA).25,26 Early escalation to ECMELLA (within 24 h before V-A ECMO implantation or no more than 2 h after) has shown a favourable effect on the survival of CS patients and an increased likelihood of successful weaning from mechanical ventilation.27

In patients who develop RVF during tMCS support and are unresponsive to low-dose inotropes, the initial step is to determine the primary or secondary cause of RVF.22 The monitoring with PAC in this situation is of paramount importance. It allows the determination of several distinctive haemodynamic parameters (i.e. pulmonary artery pulsatility index and pulmonary vascular resistance). Primary RVF is characterized by persistent shock, need for inotropes, and Impella suction alarms due to insufficient preload and requires escalation to bi-ventricular support. This escalation involves the implantation of devices such as Impella RP, V-A ECMO, or Protek Duo. The choice between each device depends on factors such as concomitant hypoxaemia due to lung involvement or the availability and expertise for Impella RP implantation/management. In cases of secondary RVF (resulting from RV pressure overload due to high left filling pressures secondary to inadequate LV unloading), uni-ventricular support with Impella 5.0/5.5 implantation is recommended.22

Cardiogenic shock in patients with acute-on-chronic heart failure typically presents with end-organ dysfunction (e.g. kidney and liver), and there is uncertainty regarding the potential and the timeline for heart recovery. These patients require evaluation for definitive therapy options, such as heart transplant or durable LV assist device (LVAD) implantation. Considering the foreseeable need for prolonged assistance and the importance of preventing physical deconditioning (e.g. ambulation, physical therapy, and oral feeding), the suggested strategy in these cases is the implantation of Impella 5.0 or 5.5.24,28 Preliminary data suggest that, across all the aetiologies of CS, the use of Impella 5.5 might be associated with better outcomes.29

Implantation techniques for temporary mechanical circulatory support

The risk of bleeding in AMI-CS is high and associated with worse outcomes.30 One of the predictors for bleeding events in such patients is the use of a pVAD.30 Bleeding might be differentiated into access-site and non-access-site bleeding, with the latter related to thrombocytopaenia, sepsis, shear-stress-induced acquired von Willebrand syndrome, and the administration of anticoagulation therapies.31,32

In the setting of AMI-CS, tMCS implantation will typically take place in the catheterization lab. When crossing the valve, care should be taken to ensure that the pigtail/wire is not trapped under the mitral valve apparatus. The use of right anterior oblique (RAO) projections and echocardiography can be particularly useful. However, there might be certain situations, where implantation takes place in the intensive care unit (ICU) using ultrasound (US)/echocardiographic imaging. Whenever possible, additional fluoroscopic control, preferably in the catheterization lab or by a C-arm, is recommended.

Femoral arterial access is most commonly used for pVAD implantation. However, percutaneous axillary artery access is typically considered as the first alternative for delivery of a pVAD, especially in patients with severe peripheral artery disease.33 The trans-caval access has also been described for micro-axial flow pump (mAFP) implantation but remains restricted to highly experienced centres and operators.34

While extensive pre-procedural imaging of potential access-site can be performed in elective cases,35 this is not possible in the setting of CS. For a safe femoral access, contemporary access techniques include a combination of fluoroscopy, femoral angiography, and US, with the latter as the most important factor in the setting of CS (Figure 2).36 Successful and safe large-bore femoral access is ensured by a single anterior wall puncture of the common femoral artery, avoiding areas of calcification and punctures that are too high (above the level of the inferior epigastric artery) or too low (superficial or profunda femoral artery; Figure 3). The FAUST RCT assessed US vs. fluoroscopic guidance for femoral access, which failed to show an overall difference in the success of common femoral artery cannulation (the primary endpoint). However, US guidance resulted in significantly reduced rates of vascular complications (1.4% vs. 3.4%; P = 0.04), as well as fewer attempts, more successful first passes, lower risk of inadvertent venous punctures, and shorter time to access.37 In particular, the US-guided femoral puncture in combination with the use of closure devices is associated with fewer bleeding and vascular complications compared with without US guidance.38 Moreover, in trans-femoral trans-catheter aortic valve implantation, US-guided access compared with fluoroscopy guidance resulted in lower rates of access-site complications.39

The use of a micro-puncture set is recommended, as a large retrospective analysis of 17 844 patients undergoing PCI at a single institution found that patients undergoing PCI access with a Micropuncture® needle (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) had significantly lower rates of access-site complications compared with those undergoing access with standard needles (2.5% vs. 3.6%; P = 0.005).40

For large-bore sheath or cannula insertion, a stiff wire should be used to facilitate sufficient pre-dilatation and avoid wire kinking. Whenever possible, skin incisions should be avoided or kept a minimum since oozing can typically occur. Sheath/cannula insertion should be performed under fluoroscopy and never against resistance to avoid vascular injury, perforation, or dissection. In borderline cases, vessel preparation including percutaneous trans-luminal angioplasty or intra-vascular lithotripsy can be applied to facilitate safe pVAD implantation.41 Using a single-access technique for PCI access in mAFP cases appears to reduce access-site complications by reducing the number of access sites.42

Importantly, there is a significant learning curve for large-bore access,36 and recent training recommendations for high-risk PCI highlight the importance of femoral access, closure, and complication management training, with a suggested 100 US-guided femoral access procedures and a minimum of 15 procedures with pVAD support during training.43

Axillary percutaneous implantation using US- and fluoroscopy-guided access requires experience, available suitable material, and a plan for removing the pVAD after successful therapy. A dry closure with the use of balloon inflation can be considered in axillary cases. Surgical axillary or sub-clavian implantation is an option, in particular, if a large mAFP device is to be used or if a prolonged support duration is expected.

Before leaving the catheterization lab

Correct positioning and fixation of tMCS must be controlled and documented before transfer to the ICU. In particular, mAFPs are associated with a high incidence of haemolysis (5–10% in registry data).44 Accurate placement and fixation as well as a reduction of pump speed may decrease haemolysis and associated acute kidney injury.

Limb ischaemia is a common problem in prolonged large-bore access. In particular, in V-A ECMO or ECMELLA, antegrade perfusion techniques are necessary to provide adequate perfusion to the distal limb. In the case of limb ischaemia with mAFP-only use, it might be advisable to exclude vasospasm as a cause of ischaemia by infusing nitrogen via the side port of the 9F tapered sheath ex juvantibus before interventional perfusion techniques (e.g. cross-over bypass) are applied.

The importance of PAC placement and measurement has been well described above.23,24 Therefore, placement should be performed in the catheterization lab to avoid delayed or complicated insertion without fluoroscopic control in the ICU. In instances of deteriorating haemodynamics during PCI, these same measurements in combination with echocardiography and laboratory parameters (e.g. lactate) might help to further escalate tMCS therapy directly in the catheterization lab to avoid unnecessary transport of those critically ill patients.

What does this mean for the clinician?

While catecholamines comprise the cornerstone of CS treatment, invasive haemodynamic measurement might help to identify patients quickly deteriorating and determine which tMCS might be of particular help. Prevention of bleeding and vascular complications during tMCS use is of utmost importance.
