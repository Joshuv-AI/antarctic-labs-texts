Internship Learning Experiences, Challenges, and Resource Preferences Among Junior Doctors in Two Malaysian Tertiary Hospitals: A Pilot Study on the Preliminary Validation of a Multifaceted Questionnaire
Authors: Thai Lun Tan, Siew Ying Ting, Qinglin Lau, Shoen Chuen Chiew, Aaron Ming-Jun Khoo, Kasumaida Binti Mahajir, Mariam Hamid Binti Mustapha, Sy Liang Yong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInternship, the transitional period between undergraduate medical training and full registration as a practicing doctor, plays a key role in shaping career intentions. This study aimed to (a) evaluate the preliminary psychometric properties of an adapted questionnaire assessing junior doctors’ perceptions of internship learning experiences, (b) explore perceived challenges during internship, and (c) examine preferences for learning resources.MethodsA multifaceted questionnaire was developed through the adaptation of a previously validated instrument, with the addition of new items tailored to the local context. The adapted questionnaire subsequently underwent content validation, face validation, and internal consistency assessment prior to finalization. Convenience sampling via voluntary response or network-based convenience sampling was employed to recruit respondents from two tertiary hospitals providing internship training between October 2025 and January 2026.ResultsThe final 40-item questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory content validity and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.801 & 0.826). A total of 95 respondents participated in the pilot study, the majority of whom were senior house officers who had completed at least three clinical postings (n=57, 60.0%). Respondents reported positive perceptions of supervisor support, and professional development. High prevalence of anxiety (n=85, 89.5%) and feelings of being overwhelmed by clinical duties (n=89, 93.7%) was observed, largely attributed to heavy workload, demanding clinical responsibilities, and human resource shortages. Respondents showed strong positive perceptions of on-demand learning tools, including online subscription-based resources (n=81, 85.3%) and social media (n=86, 90.5%), while nearly half reported neutral or negative views on the usefulness of clinical research during internship.ConclusionThis study developed a multifaceted instrument with satisfactory psychometric properties to assess internship learning experiences and resource preferences among junior doctors. The findings highlight the need to strengthen psychological well-being and address human resource challenges during internship training, alongside support for self-directed learning platforms and instilling research curiosity.

Introduction

The transition from medical student to practicing doctor is widely recognized as a pivotal and transformative phase in the professional development of junior doctors. Unlike undergraduate training, internship requires not only the continued acquisition of medical knowledge but also the effective translation of theoretical concepts into clinical practice, alongside the timely fulfilment of mandated logbook requirements throughout their clinical rotations. The demands encountered during internship act as both stimuli for growth as well as sources of difficulty, ultimately forming the cornerstone of life-long learning and professional development.1,2

In Malaysia, medical undergraduates typically complete a five-year training program prior to entering internship. Upon graduation, they are provisionally registered with the Malaysian Medical Council and are required to complete six mandatory clinical rotations, each lasting a minimum of four months. During the internship, they are supervised by a fully registered specialist who serves as their clinical supervisor. Their progress and performance are assessed through logbooks of clinical cases, participation in continuing medical education activities, workplace-based assessments of clinical knowledge and skills, and evaluations of professionalism, attitude, leadership, and clinical practice.3 Under the current training guidelines, interns are expected to work an average of 60–62 hours per week.

Healthcare workforce attrition and staff shortages represent a global challenge, placing increasing pressure on frontline healthcare services and creating an even more demanding learning and working environment for junior doctors during this critical stage of training.1,4 In the United Kingdom, workforce pressures, lack of flexibility, poor work–life balance, and challenging working conditions have been identified as key contributors to junior doctor dissatisfaction, burnout, and decisions to leave the profession or pursue alternative career pathways.5,6 On the other hand, the workforce shortage in Malaysia represents a paradox in which an increasing supply of medical graduates is offset by institutional barriers to effective absorption, retention, and job satisfaction. The introduction of a contract-based employment system in 2016, driven by fiscal constraints and an oversupply of medical officers, has inadvertently restricted opportunities for long-term career progression. This has reduced the appeal of public healthcare sector employment.7 Subsequent attrition has increased workload pressures on remaining staff, while compensation has not kept pace with rising living costs. In contrast, the private sector offers more favourable working conditions and remuneration, further drawing medical professionals away from public service. These factors have contributed to declining morale in the healthcare system, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of workforce depletion and increasing service burden.8–10

Beyond workforce challenges, clinical internship represents a critical transition from traditional, lecture-based learning supported by textbooks and reference materials to a more dynamic, case-based approach. During this period, clinical judgement is progressively refined as each patient encounter becomes central to the development of experience and professional maturity in junior doctors. In parallel, access to information has evolved significantly; on-the-go referencing has become more efficient with the replacement of hard-copy materials by handheld devices and modern digital technology. Consequently, on-demand learning resources are increasingly utilized by medical undergraduates and junior doctors and have been shown to facilitate self-directed learning and knowledge acquisition.11–13 Despite these developments, there remains a paucity of research and locally validated instruments examining learning behaviours, educational resource utilization, and perceptions of internship learning among junior doctors in Malaysia and other low- and middle-income countries (LMIC).

Against this backdrop, understanding how doctors perceive and navigate their learning experiences within these demanding clinical environments is increasingly important, as these formative experiences may shape their career intentions, influence decisions to pursue specialist training, and ultimately affect workforce retention, service delivery, and long-term healthcare workforce planning.14,15 Accordingly, we undertook this study with the following objectives: (a) to evaluate the preliminary psychometric properties of an adapted questionnaire designed to assess junior doctors’ perceptions of internship learning experiences; (b) to explore the perceived internship learning challenges; and (c) to examine their learning resources preference.

Methods

Study Setting

This study employed a cross-sectional multicentre survey design and was conducted in two public tertiary hospitals in Malaysia: Hospital Tengku Ampuan Rahimah (HTAR), Klang, and Hospital Tengku Ampuan Afzan (HTAA), Kuantan. Both institutions are accredited housemanship training centres where clinical training is conducted under the supervision of specialist clinicians. In the context of this study, junior doctors were defined as intern doctors, commonly referred to as house officers (HO), who had completed at least one clinical posting during their housemanship training. Additionally, junior medical officers (JMO) who had completed their housemanship and were awaiting formal placement as medical officers within the public healthcare system were also included in the study population.

Questionnaire Development

The development of this English self-administered online questionnaire began with an extensive literature review and rigorous discussion among the researchers to generate items measuring the constructs of interest. The questionnaire comprised four sections: (a) respondents’ demographic data, (b) perceptions towards internship learning experience, (c) the perceptions towards challenges in learning, and (d) the perceptions towards learning methods or resources usefulness during internship. A total of 29 items were adapted or adopted from a validated questionnaire developed by Zhao et al16 to explore junior doctors’ perceptions towards internship learning experience and related challenges. In addition, 25 newly developed items were included in the final instrument. Perceptions of the internship learning experience were measured using a five-point Likert scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree). Perceptions of challenges during internship were assessed using a five-point frequency scale (very often to never). The usefulness of learning methods or resources during internship was also measured using a five-point Likert scale (very useful to not useful at all), with an additional “no experience” option. Further, three open-ended questions were included to explore supporting and limiting factors in internship learning, as well as to identify other learning resources or methods perceived as particularly useful.

Validation Process

Phase 1: Content Validity and Face Validity

Content validity of the instrument was established through expert review by specialists and medical registrars involved in internship training. A total of seven clinical specialists from various disciplines, including emergency medicine, obstetrics, internal medicine, and paediatrics, as well as one medical registrar, participated in the validation process. Each expert independently reviewed the questionnaire and evaluated the items based on three criteria: content relevance, representativeness, and clarity. Each expert rated each questionnaire item for the three assessment criteria using a four-point ordinal rating scale (1=not relevant/representative/clear, 2=somewhat relevant/representative/clear, 3=quite relevant/representative/clear, and 4=highly relevant/representative/clear). Ratings of 3 or 4 were considered as agreement and used to calculate the Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI) for each item of the questionnaire.

Cognitive debriefing was conducted to assess face validity following content validation. This process involved one house officer, four junior medical officers, and one medical officer. Participants were asked to provide feedback on the appropriateness and clarity of the questionnaire through open-ended discussions, and the time taken to complete the questionnaire was recorded. All feedback from the content and face validation processes was reviewed by the researchers. Based on the feedback, items were either deleted, revised, or retained following in-depth discussion among the research team. Items modified according to expert recommendations were subsequently reviewed by the same or additional reviewers until consensus was achieved. The revised version of the questionnaire was subsequently tested for reliability in the next stage.

Phase 2: Reliability Analysis and Pilot Testing

A total of 30 respondents, comprising junior medical officers, house officers, and medical officers, were recruited using convenience sampling to participate in the internal consistency assessment. The informed consent form and questionnaire were administered via Google Forms, and the survey link was sent to participants to complete the online survey.

The finalized instrument, revised based on the internal consistency analysis, was subsequently distributed to participants via survey link. This link was shared through house officer posting communication channels as well as junior medical officers’ communication groups between 1 October 2025 and 31 January 2026. In the final month of data collection, two house officers were recruited as data collectors to assist in recruiting participants through convenience sampling. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Given the network-based distribution approach, response rates were estimated based on the total number of registered house officers and junior medical officers at each institution during the data collection period.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 was used to conduct statistical analysis. Both item content validity index (I-CVI) and scale content validity index (S-CVI) were calculated to determine the degree of appropriateness of this instrument in measuring the content intended. Items with I-CVI ≥ 78% were considered as appropriate. On the other hand, items with I-CVI between 70 to 78% would be revised and items with I-CVI less than 70% would be removed.17 An S-CVI/Average of 0.90 or higher was considered indicative of excellent content validity.18

In addition, Kappa index was also calculated to measure the inter-rater agreement that adjusts for chance agreement. Items with Kappa index above 0.74 were considered as excellent, whilst 0.60 to 0.74 were good and 0.40 to 0.59 were fair.19

The internal consistency reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with a value of ≥0.70 indicating acceptable reliability.20,21 Item performance was evaluated using corrected item–total correlations (CITC), with a threshold of ≥0.30 considered acceptable.22 Items demonstrating low CITC were further examined for conceptual relevance and redundancy. The effect of item removal on overall reliability was assessed using the “Cronbach’s alpha if item deleted” approach, and decisions regarding item retention were made based on both statistical evidence and theoretical justification.

Ethics Statement

The study was registered with National Medical Research (NMRR) and approved by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) of the Ministry of Health (MOH). MREC Approval Letter: 24–03546-YPQ; NMRR ID: NMRR ID 24–03546-YPQ (IIR). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Content Validity

The instrument underwent three stages of content validation prior to finalisation. All items achieved a satisfactory I-CVI scores for relevance, clarity and representativeness, except A6, A12, B3, B5, B9 and B14. Similarly, all items demonstrated excellent Kappa values, except for the same items (A6, A12, B3, B5, B9 and B14). These items, which did not achieve satisfactory I-CVI scores and excellent Kappa values, were therefore excluded from the final instrument. S-CVI for the perception towards (i) internship learning experience and (ii) perception towards internship learning challenges domains were 0.965 and 0.973 respectively. Overall, the finalised instrument comprised only items with good to excellent content validity. Face Validity

With respect to face validity, a total of one house officer, four junior medical officers, and one medical officer were involved in the assessment. The mean time required to complete the questionnaire was less than 10 minutes, and all participants agreed that the questionnaire was easy to understand.

Reliability

The internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. For Section 2.1 (Perception towards Internship Learning Experience), the final 16-item scale demonstrated acceptable reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.801 following the removal of three items (A15, A16 and A21) due to low CITC and conceptual redundancy. The CITC values for the retained items ranged from 0.093 to 0.689.

For Section 2.2 (Perception towards Internship Learning Challenges), the final 18-item scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.826 after removal of two items (B16 and B19) with low CITC and conceptual redundancy. The CITC values ranged from 0.226 to 0.681. Although several items in both sections had CITC values below the recommended threshold of 0.30, these items were retained as their removal did not result in a meaningful improvement in Cronbach’s alpha. Additionally, these items were considered conceptually important to ensure adequate representation of the constructs (Supplementary Material 1).

The Final Questionnaire

The instrument was rigorously reviewed prior to pilot testing. Item A11 was deleted following a reviewer’s comment that it was irrelevant, and also to reduce the total number of items. Items A17 and B23 were removed as their constructs were considered not to address the intended construct of the survey. Original item numbering was retained for CVI and Cronbach’s alpha analyses to maintain consistency with the source instrument. Items were subsequently renumbered sequentially in the final questionnaire to avoid gaps and enhance clarity, and this numbering was used in the results tables. The final questionnaire which was used in the pilot study contained 40 items as follows: 16 items measuring perceptions towards internship learning experiences; 20 items measuring perceptions towards internship learning challenges; and 4 items measuring perceptions towards the usefulness of learning methods and resources during internship (Supplementary Material 2). Overall, the instrument demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, with good validity and reliability for the constructs measured.

Perceptions Towards Internship Learning Experience

Figure 1 illustrates respondents’ perceptions of their internship learning experience, demonstrating an overall positive trend. Majority of the respondents agreed that clinical supervisors set clear expectations during training (n=58, 61.1%) and demonstrated enthusiasm in both teaching and supervision (n=56, 58.9%). Supervisors were generally perceived as approachable and accessible (n=49, 51.6%), enabling interns to communicate concerns effectively (n=57, 60.0%). Additionally, majority of the respondents acknowledged strong mentoring qualities among supervisors (n=52, 54.7%), with many highlighting encouragements towards independent learning (n=74, 77.9%). Respondents also reported positive perceptions of the learning environment. Most agreed that they were provided with a safe and supportive setting, alongside adequate training resources. Protected time for continuing medical education (CME) sessions was reported by more than half of the respondents (n=52, 54.7%), with access to relevant educational programs during internship (n=51, 53.7%). Furthermore, participants indicated sufficient opportunities for hands-on clinical practice, including procedural skills (n=60, 63.2%). Infection prevention and control measures were also perceived as adequate across both institutions (n=63, 66.3%).

In terms of professional development, many participants expressed a positive outlook on their growth and self-perceived competence. Internship training was widely regarded as valuable in fostering empathy through real-life clinical exposure (n=78, 82.1%). The respondents also reported increased interest in clinical research and development of leadership skills (n=49, 51.6%). Lastly, participants expressed pride in their contributions to their departments (n=74, 77.9%) and demonstrated a clear understanding of the competencies required of a house officer (n=84, 88.4%) (Supplementary Material 3).

Perceived Barriers in Learning During Internship

Figure 2 highlights the perceived barriers experienced by respondents during internship, revealing a mixed but insightful pattern. The decision rule for reporting prevalence rates was based on the conceptual interpretation of the Likert-scale responses when presenting the findings. For negatively worded items, responses of “sometimes,” “often,” and “very often” were categorized as indicating the presence of a barrier, as even intermittent negative experiences may adversely affect learning during internship. In contrast, for positively worded items, only responses of “often” and “very often” were considered indicative of a positive learning experience, as consistent exposure was deemed necessary to reflect a supportive educational environment. A substantial proportion of respondents reported challenges related to psychological well-being, such as difficulty maintaining work–life balance (n=77, 81.1%). Besides, many respondents expressed feelings of stress, nervousness (n=85, 89.5%), and being overwhelmed, likely attributable to high workload demands (n=89, 93.7%) and large patient responsibility (n=81, 85.3%). On the other hand, a substantial number of respondents reported maintaining a sense of hope (n=34, 35.8%) and continued engagement in activities they enjoy (n=38, 40.0%) alongside the ability to concentrate on both professional and personal matters (n=35, 36.8%). Another considerable proportion of respondents maintained a neutral stance on these views.

Work attitudes were generally positive, with more than half of participants expressing satisfaction with their internship experience. Respondents felt confident in their ability to keep up with clinical skills and protocols (n=54, 56.8%) and believed their work had meaningful impact (n=60, 63.2%). Career satisfaction was evident, with many expressing enthusiasm to complete their internship (n=64, 67.4%). These positive attitudes may be supported by strong peer networks (n=70, 73.7%) and the adoption of self-care strategies (n=49, 51.6%).

However, perceptions of future career prospects were more variable. Only a fraction of participants expressed certainty and security, particularly in relation to career progression as contract doctors (n=21, 22.1%). Nevertheless, half of the respondents acknowledged that they were adequately supervised during their training (n=48, 50.5%). Regarding internship preparedness, approximately half of the participants felt sufficiently trained clinically during their undergraduate education (n=33, 34.7%). Lastly, less than half of the respondents believed their clinical knowledge was adequate to meet the demands of housemanship (n=43, 45.3%) (Supplementary Material 4).

Discussion

Existing surveys assessing the learning experiences of Malaysian junior doctors have often been constrained by instruments lacking local validation, with some relying on self-developed questionnaires that were not rigorously tested for content validity or reliability, limiting reproducibility and suitability for longitudinal or comparative research.10,23 Also, the previous studies mainly addressed the negative aspects, such as stress, distress and burnout limiting the information on what is positive and what can be enforced.10,23–27 In this study, we addressed these gaps by developing a novel questionnaire by adapting an instrument originally developed by Zhao et al for internship learning in LMICs.16 By including new items taking into the local clinical practice context, and a novel section assessing perceptions of learning resource usefulness, we established a multi-faceted instrument evaluating internship learning experiences and resources. Importantly, the content validity and reliability results indicate that this instrument is psychometrically promising and could be used to evaluate Malaysian junior doctors, with potential applicability across other LMICs healthcare settings.

In this pilot study, it is encouraging for supervisors to note that majority of junior doctors reported a positive relationship with their supervisors, particularly in areas such as support, accessibility of educational sessions, mentoring skills, and the ability to raise significant concerns. This finding is important, as a strong supervisory relationship is fundamental in healthcare training. It directly influences interns’ learning experiences, reduces attrition rates, contributes to safer clinical practice and ultimately contributes to their careers decision as well as intention.14,15 A systematic review by Snowdon et al demonstrated that effective clinical supervision of health professionals is positively associated with improved quality and effectiveness of care.28 Conversely, Snowdon et al reported an association between clinical supervision deficits and excess patient mortality.28 Admittedly, a perfect satisfaction with clinical supervision among junior doctors is difficult to attain, even in well-equipped workplaces, owing to the demanding nature of clinical practice and persistent workforce constraints affecting both the supervisors and junior doctors.25,29 Yet, efforts and emphasis must be in place to optimize clinical supervision considering the significant impact on junior doctors learning outcomes as well as patient safety. This could be achieved by devising a structured internship curriculum, with clearly defined learning objectives for each clinical posting and scheduled mentorship meetings to ensure consistent guidance. Additionally, regular feedback from both supervisors and junior doctors should be systematically collected to better conceptualize clinical supervision, enhance engagement, identify gaps, and continuously improve the quality of training and supervision.

Burnout, defined as a state of emotional exhaustion, physical fatigue, and cognitive weariness resulting from prolonged and unresolved stressors, is highly prevalent among junior doctors.30 In this regard, Roslan et al reported a 69.1% prevalence of work-related burnout among Malaysian interns in 2021.24 Although burnout was not directly assessed in the present report, its core component, exhaustion, appears to be prevalent within our cohort, as indicated by the high proportion of respondents who reported feeling overwhelmed by patient loads. This is unsurprising given the sharp decline in the healthcare workforce, which has placed unprecedented pressure on the healthcare system, disproportionately affecting junior doctors who are heavily involved in inpatient care and after-hours duties.29 Correspondingly, key barriers to learning identified in this report include high patient load, excessive clerical work, and issues with infrastructure and information technology systems. The observed high prevalence of anxiety is also concerning, as it may represent an extension of burnout syndrome and could adversely impact psychological well-being if left unaddressed. Similar challenges have been reported in the United Kingdom; Kendall et al described the internship year as a “baptism of fire” characterised by work overload and numerous mind-numbing menial tasks.31 Despite these impediments, it is encouraging that most respondents remain motivated to complete their internship and continue to take pride in their work. This is likely driven by a strong sense of professional responsibility, the intrinsic satisfaction derived from contributing to patient care, ongoing opportunities for clinical growth via self-directed experiential learning, and the recognition of their role as essential healthcare members.32

Learning during internships is inherently multisource, encompassing traditional hospital-based pedagogical teaching, experiential learning on the job, and self-directed learning. While debates persist regarding the effectiveness of different teaching styles, structured pedagogical guidance remains highly valued and indispensable for developing clinical competence, as reflected in the strong appreciation for clinician-led hospital-based learning that effectively integrates theory with practice.33 Respondents also highlighted the usefulness of digital and on-demand resources such as ChatGPT, educational YouTube channels, and subscription-based platforms. In particular, ChatGPT was highly cited among the respondents who probably valued for its ability to provide rapid human-like responses and contextually relevant information.34 However, as the integration of AI into medical education becomes increasingly inevitable, concerns regarding overreliance must be addressed. Although AI-generated outputs often exhibit a high degree of verisimilitude, they may at times be inaccurate or insufficiently verified, thereby posing a risk of misleading junior doctors who have not yet fully developed critical appraisal skills when approaching clinical problems.35 Therefore, users must exercise caution when utilizing AI in healthcare contexts, remaining mindful of both its advantages and limitations. Overall, a structured internship curriculum with clearly articulated learning outcomes, coupled with access to reliable resources such as established clinical practice guidelines and reputable reference materials, would significantly optimise their learning experience.

Our study also highlights persistent gaps in research engagement and academic development, with a substantial proportion of interns reporting no prior research experience and hold limited perception towards usefulness of clinical research as learning. This reflects interns’ interest in clinical research was not consistently nurtured or even attenuated upon entering internship, likely reflecting a systemic emphasis on clinical service over academic activity, a pattern commonly observed in service-focused, non-academic institutions.36–38 Given the far-reaching benefits of clinical research and the highly transferable skills it offers across clinical postings, internship programs could benefit from integrating feasible, rotation-appropriate research activities alongside clinical duties to foster academic curiosity. Clinical supervisors also play a vital role in encouraging and guiding such academic endeavours, which can begin with achievable tasks such as case report writing, thereby enabling interns to develop skills in scientific research and autonomous critical thinking. Lastly, clinical research interest should ideally be cultivated progressively from university training rather than being initiated solely during internship.39 Considering the long-term benefits of research capacity, it is imperative for stakeholders and educational authorities to invest in fostering research aptitude among the medical undergraduate and interns.

Strengths and Limitations

An upshot of this study is that, although it is a pilot, it involved 95 interns, which represents a robust sample for exploratory analysis which provides strong preliminary insights. Another key strength is that this novel questionnaire was developed with input from clinical educators from diverse backgrounds, ensuring strong relevance to the local context, while also adapting elements from a well-established international instrument. A limitation, however, is the voluntary response nature of recruitment through communication group may have introduced self-selection bias, whereby house officers with stronger opinions about their internship experience or greater engagement with peer networks were more likely to participate. Additionally, recruitment from only two hospitals may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Future research should include multicentre validation studies involving larger and more diverse populations of interns, as well as cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the questionnaire across different healthcare systems. Furthermore, longitudinal studies and intervention-based research using this instrument could provide deeper insights into evolving internship challenges and evaluate strategies to improve learning environments, training experiences, and junior doctor wellbeing.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this pilot study, utilising a validated instrument with satisfactory psychometric properties, demonstrates that junior doctors generally report positive perceptions of their internship learning experiences, particularly in relation to supervisory support. Nevertheless, heavy workload remains the principal challenge, contributing to a high prevalence of anxiety and feelings of being overwhelmed. While traditional pedagogical approaches continue to be well received, there is growing interest in on-demand learning modalities, including online subscription platforms, social media, and artificial intelligence–assisted learning. Limited exposure to clinical research highlights a critical area for improvement within both internship and undergraduate medical training programmes. Overall, this instrument provides a valuable foundation for broader implementation in public hospital settings across Malaysia and other LMICs. Following further multi-centre and cross-cultural validation, it may serve as a useful tool for evaluating junior doctors’ learning experiences and informing future educational initiatives.
