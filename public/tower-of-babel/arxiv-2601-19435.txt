Ad Insertion in LLM-Generated Responses
Shengwei Xu, Zhaohua Chen, Xiaotie Deng, Zhiyi Huang, Grant Schoenebeck

Abstract
Sustainable monetization of Large Language Models (LLMs) remains a critical open challenge. Traditional search advertising, which relies on static keywords, fails to capture the fleeting, context-dependent user intents–the specific information, goods, or services a user seeks–embedded in conversational flows. Beyond the standard goal of social welfare maximization , effective LLM advertising imposes additional requirements on contextual coherence (ensuring ads align semantically with transient user intents) and computational efficiency (avoiding user interaction latency), as well as adherence to ethical and regulatory standards , including preserving privacy and ensuring explicit ad disclosure. Although various recent solutions have explored bidding on token-level and query-level, both categories of approaches generally fail to holistically satisfy this multifaceted set of constraints. We propose a practical framework that resolves these tensions through two decoupling strategies. First, we decouple ad insertion from response generation to ensure safety and explicit disclosure. Second, we decouple bidding from specific user queries by using “genres” (high-level semantic clusters) as a proxy. This allows advertisers to bid on stable categories rather than sensitive real-time response, reducing computational burden and privacy risks. We demonstrate that applying the VCG auction mechanism to this genre-based framework yields approximately dominant strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC) and individual rationality (IR), as well as approximately optimal social welfare, while maintaining high computational efficiency. Finally, we introduce an "LLM-as-a-Judge" metric to estimate contextual coherence. Our experiments show that this metric correlates strongly with human ratings (Spearman’s ρ ≈ 0.66 \rho\approx 0.66 ), outperforming 80% of individual human evaluators.

Introduction

ChatGPT now has more than 700 million weekly active users
(
1
)
.
However, only around 10 million users are paying subscribers
(
1
)
.
Given the considerable cost of training and deploying LLMs—a recent survey by International Data Corporation (IDC) forecasted $108 billion GenAI spending in the U.S. in 2028
(
21
)
—this poses a challenge of sustainably monetizing LLM services which so many rely on.

A natural approach to monetizing LLM services is to integrate ads into LLM responses, similar to current search engines.
Indeed, a recent survey
(
35
)
revealed that 68% of users have used LLMs for “searching to find facts quickly, like a search engine”, and 57% of them have used LLMs for “getting information about products and services”.
For context, Google Ads had revenues in excess of $260 billion in 2024
(
41
)
.
Thus, introducing ads into LLM responses could largely defray the costs associated with LLM services.

However, the transition from “Search” to “Chat” is not merely a shift in interface; it presents unique structural challenges.
In a search engine, user intent
(
6
)
is naturally encoded by the static keywords, so advertisers can bid on keywords when they want impressions targeting a certain intent. These keywords give a good indication of the users’ state of mind, and, in particular, what goods or services they may be seeking. In an LLM chat, the analogous user intent, we use the same terminology, may shift throughout a complex conversational flow.
For example, an LLM response about a user’s family vacation to New York City might start with a discussion of attractions, then move on to accommodations, transportation, and budget considerations.
Ideally, an ad about transportation would appear surrounded by text about transportation rather than text regarding accommodations.
Placing an ad in the right place not only maximizes its effectiveness but also makes the ad less disruptive for the user experience.

This fundamental shift raises three technical challenges:

1.

Contextual coherence
: How do we identify the evolving user intent in the LLM response to provide coherent ads, so that we can align advertisers’ welfare with specific users’ intents?

2.

Computational efficiency
: How do we insert ads without introducing latency that degrades the real-time user experience?

3.

Social welfare
: How can we design an mechanism that (approximately) maximizes social welfare, defined as the sum of advertiser and platform utilities, in the absence of explicit, static keywords to bid on?

In addition, inserting commercial bias into the organic flow is constrained by
practical considerations
.
For example, on the
regulation
side, LLM platforms must explicitly mark where ads are inserted, to align with the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) policy that “advertising and promotional messages that
are not identifiable as advertising to consumers are deceptive if they mislead consumers into
believing they are independent, impartial, or not from the sponsoring advertiser itself”
(
14
)
.
On the
ethics
side, ads in the LLM response should also be non-deceptive and non-misleading.
Further, if advertisers bid on the level of each query or each token, they may get access to possibly sensitive user prompts, leading to
privacy
issues.

With these challenges in mind, we aim to answer the following research question:

How can we design a practical framework for integrating ads in LLM responses with high advertisers’ economic welfare, contextual coherence, and computational efficiency?

1.1
Existing Solutions and Challenges

Traditional search and display advertising models, which rely on fixed ad slots and static user intent, do not generalize well to this domain. LLM responses are dynamic and lack pre-defined slots, and an ad’s effectiveness depends heavily on its
contextual coherence
with the surrounding context that leads to specific user intents. Conceptually, this resembles
native advertising
, where ads are seamlessly woven into the narrative. However, native ads are typically labor-intensive; for instance, a paid post on the
New York Times
can require 6–12 weeks of planning
(
46
)
. Given that LLM platforms operate in real-time, we require a mechanism that achieves this level of integration with high
computational efficiency
.

In the past two years, there has been a surge of studies on mixing ads into LLM responses. A position paper,
13
, raises several critical factors of LLM advertising, the most important of which is “what to bid for”.
To this end, existing works can be roughly classified into two groups, both of which face challenges regarding computational efficiency and practical concerns that our framework addresses.

The first group
(
12
;
18
)
allows advertisers to bid at the
token or segment level
. Here, advertisers bid repeatedly during generation for their content to appear as the next token. This approach introduces significant latency.
More critically, as reported in
50
, if LLMs are prompted to subtly blend ads into the organic response, the resulting ads can be hard to recognize.
Because the content is generated in real-time by an LLM, it is also susceptible to hallucinations and misinformation.

The second group
(
40
;
11
;
29
;
3
)
focuses on bidding at the
query or response level
. While it avoids token-level generation issues by inserting pre-defined ads, bidding on the entire query forces advertisers to value the whole interaction in aggregate, preventing them from targeting the specific moments within the response (e.g., a travel plan containing both “flight” and “hotel” contexts).
Furthermore, this approach typically requires advertisers to submit a bid for each specific user query. This also imposes a massive computational burden on advertisers, who must perform real-time semantic analysis to value each unique query effectively.

Crucially, both groups of approaches typically expose sensitive user prompts to advertisers, raising significant privacy concerns.

1.3
Our Contributions

Our primary contribution is a practical framework for integrating ads into LLM responses with high economic welfare, high contextual coherence, and high computational efficiency.
En route to this goal, we introduce two layers of decoupling, respectively answering the question of “how ads are displayed” and “what to bid for” raised by
13
.

Figure 2
:
Our framework for ad insertion in LLM-generated responses.

Concerning how ads are displayed, we
decouple ads insertion from response generation
.
In our framework (see
Figure

2
), upon receiving a query, an LLM chatbot first generates an organic response to the query, and independently, advertisers compete for the right to insert their pre-submitted ads in different positions in the organic response without further modification (e.g., at the end of a paragraph).
Such a decoupling comes with two practical advantages:
(1)
Regulation and ethics
concerns related to LLM ads are addressed at their root.
Since ad creatives typically stay unchanged for weeks, the service provider can pre-screen and block deceptive content before integrating them into LLM responses.
Any ad appearance can also be explicitly marked in the response.
(2) The decoupled response-generation and mechanism-design modules can be
independently optimized
with minimal coordination cost.
For example, classic auction mechanisms, such as VCG, can be seamlessly migrated into the new scenario.

The other layer of decoupling operates on the bidding interface, where we
decouple bidding from the response context
.
Recall that, compared to search ads that rely on keywords, in LLM responses, user intent evolves and thus will be slot-dependent. As we discussed in
Section

1.1
, it is infeasible for advertisers to bid on every possible slot in the response context.
Therefore, we introduce the concept of
genres
, high-level semantic clusters (e.g.,
hotels
,
airlines
,
food
), as a tractable proxy for the underlying user
intents
.
Advertisers bid offline on the stable, pre-defined genres.
At inference time, the platform only estimates a
coherence
probability between each genre and each candidate slot in the conversation.
Combining (1) an advertiser’s genre bids and (2) the slot-level genre coherence, we obtain an estimated welfare for assigning that advertiser to that slot by computing the weighted average of an advertiser’s bids using the coherence probabilities as the weights. Conceptually, this is analogous to decomposing a welfare matrix into advertiser-genre and genre-slot factors (see
Figure

3
).

Such a design decouples the advertisers from the user’s response context, yielding three immediate advantages:
(1)
Efficiency:
advertisers no longer need to value every query/token in real time, and the platform runs auctions over a tractable pre-defined genre set rather than over a massive space of query/token-level bids;
(2)
Privacy:
advertisers bid on coarse categories rather than on potentially sensitive user prompts/responses;
(3) Practically, because genres play a role analogous to keywords, the approach can be integrated with existing search-ad infrastructure with limited modification.

Figure 3
:
An illustration of how genres work under the matrix decomposition perspective.

However, in our design, the estimated error on advertisers’ welfare exists because genres may not contain all the information of users’ intents and thus may not perfectly recover true valuations.
This error shrinks as genres become more fine-grained clusters and as coherence estimation becomes more accurate. We now characterize how this approximation affects mechanism design guarantees.

Mechanism design.

We analyze the economic properties of this genre-based auction. A core challenge is that genres are a coarse proxy of true user intent; therefore, the platform cannot know the advertiser’s true valuation for a specific context perfectly.
Despite this information asymmetry, in
Section

3
, we prove that applying the VCG mechanism to the estimated values yields strong theoretical guarantees. Specifically, we show that the mechanism is
approximately
dominant strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) and individually rational (IR). Furthermore, when assuming that advertisers bid truthfully, the mechanism achieves
approximately optimal social welfare
.
We derive error bounds showing that the incentive to deviate from truthful bidding is bounded by the granularity of the genres and the accuracy of the coherence estimation.
In
Section

6
, we empirically demonstrate that computing the VCG allocation and payment is computationally efficient.

Coherence measurement.

For the coherence estimation, in
Section

4
, we propose two methods for measuring the coherence signal based on recent natural language generation (NLG) evaluation techniques, including sentence/word embedding
(
10
;
51
;
27
)
, and LLM-as-a-Judge
(
2
;
23
;
52
)
.

To evaluate their reliability, in
Section

5
, we conduct empirical studies comparing these coherence signals with human-annotated ratings.
Our result shows that Deepseek-r1 and GPT-5-based LLM-as-a-Judge demonstrate the highest Spearman’s correlation coefficient of about 0.66 with the average human ratings over 36 participants.
They outperform more than 80% of humans in terms of correlation with the overall population.
We further observe that the correlation of LLM-as-a-Judge metrics improves consistently with model capability, suggesting continued gains as foundation models advance. We provide all code, experiment materials, and anonymized survey data at
https://github.com/DavidXu999/Ad-Insertion-in-LLM-Generated-Responses
.

2
Framework

In this section, we introduce our framework for ad insertion in LLM-generated responses. As shown in
Figure

2
, when a user sends a query
q
to the LLM service, the LLM first generates an (ad-free) organic response
z
to the query according to its intrinsic rules. Independently, all advertisers submit their ad creative and bidding information for the auction to the LLM service provider. Determined by advertisers’ values, the mechanism finally outputs a final response
\tilde{z}
, which keeps all sentences in
z
and their orders, but inserts several pre-defined ad creatives among them.

Crucially, our framework decouples the insertion of ads from the generation of the response. This separation circumvents the risk of hallucinated ad content and ensures transparency, preventing the subtle blending of ads with organic responses.

Our framework consists of three core modules:

1.

a valuation model where advertisers estimate utility using “genres” as a proxy for latent user intents. This proxy design isolates private user queries from advertisers and enhances computational efficiency by relieving advertisers of the burden of bidding on every individual query.

2.

a contextual coherence estimator module that detects the degree of correlation between a given “genre” and the organic response.

3.

an auction mechanism that combines genre-based bids with the platform’s estimation of contextual coherence to determine ad allocation and payments.

Organic response and ads slots.

Formally, we model the organic response as a sequence of
M
sentences:
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{M}
. For
j\in\{0,1,\cdots,M\}
, let
y_{j}
denote the space between
z_{j}
and
z_{j+1}
.
We denote
y_{0}
as the start and
y_{M}
as the end of the response. Naturally,
y_{j}
’s are the possible ad slots.
Throughout our framework, we consider a single organic response instance
z
to a specific user query. Consequently, we use the index
j
as a shorthand to denote the semantic context at that specific position within the current response
z
. Note that while
j
is merely an index, in our formulation it uniquely identifies the context state for the given response.

2.1
Advertisers’ Valuation and Genres

We now model the advertisers’ valuation structure. Consider
N
advertisers, where each advertiser
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
holds an ad creative
a_{i}
. Let
{w}_{i,j}
be a random variable representing the value derived by the advertiser from an impression at slot
j
.

Figure 4
:
Our model for advertisers’ valuation and platform’s coherence with genres.

A genre acts as a bucket for multiple intents. The platform uses the average value
\tilde{v}_{i,g}
as a static proxy, since the true value
{v}_{i,t}
is impossible to process due to the extremely large intent space
\mathcal{T}
.

Relying on the static proxy averages over various intents, introducing an estimation error (See
Example

2.2
). This error decreases as the granularity of the genre set
\mathcal{G}
increases; in the extreme limit where genres equal intents, the proxy perfectly matches the true value.

Intents and advertiser valuation.

The value
{w}_{i,j}
depends on the specific context and the user’s intent at the slot. We capture this dependency using the concept of an
intent
, denoted by
t\in\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all possible intents. An intent precisely encapsulates the user’s latent thought process while reading the response at a specific slot.

However, the textual context at slot
j
does not fully reveal the user’s private latent factors (e.g., budget constraints, current mood). To capture the randomness arising from this lack of full knowledge, we use
\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]
to represent the probability that intent
t
is active given the context observed at slot
j
.

Example 2.1
.

Consider a user Alice querying an LLM for a travel itinerary to New York City. Upon reading the transportation section of the response (
j
), Alice’s true intent relies on latent factors unknown to the platform. She might desire a “First Class Flight” if she recently received a bonus, or a “Greyhound Coach” if she is budget-constrained.
\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]
can be used to capture this uncertainty.

Conditioning on the intent
t
, the value of an ad creative
a_{i}
is independent of the slot index
j
.
Let
{v}_{i,t}\in[0,\bar{v}]
represent advertiser
i
’s value given user intent
t
. Here,
\bar{v}
is an upper bound of an advertiser’s value. Thus, advertiser
i
’s
ground truth
value at slot
j
is:

\displaystyle{w}_{i,j}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{t}{v}_{i,t}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right].

(1)

However, the challenge lies in the cardinality of
\mathcal{T}
, since intents cover the infinite nuances of natural language conversations. Directly estimating
\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]
or bidding on individual
{v}_{i,t}
is computationally intractable.

Genres as a proxy.

To resolve this intractability, we introduce
genres
as a tractable proxy. We model a genre
g\in\mathcal{G}
as a set of intents, where
\mathcal{G}
is a pre-defined finite set of all possible genres with size
L
that form a partition of the intent space
\mathcal{T}
. That is, genres do not intersect, and their union covers all intents. This formulation aligns with industry standards for contextual targeting, such as the IAB Content Taxonomy
(
20
)
, which groups similar products into verticals (e.g., food, automotive, insurance).
We have the probability of a genre
g
aligns with the context at slot
j
as
\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]=\sum_{t\in g}\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]
.

We suppose the advertiser has knowledge of
\tilde{v}_{i,g}\in[0,\bar{v}]
, the average value brought by the ad creative on each genre
g
.
In practice, this assumption is supported by the fact that advertisers are required to bid on keywords for search engine ads. Thus, the advertiser’s value in
Equation

1
can be approximated as

\displaystyle{w}_{i,j}\approx\sum_{g}\tilde{v}_{i,g}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right].

(2)

Example 2.2
.

Consider “Airlines” as a broad genre acting as a proxy for specific user intents, such as “Business Class” and “Economy”.
While the broad genre remains constant, the underlying intent varies by context: a business travel implies high probability for “Business Class”, while a budget family vacation implies “Economy”.
This aggregation causes a valuation error because advertisers value these intents differently. For instance, a low-cost carrier derives high utility from “Economy” but low utility from “Business Class”.
Since the bid is tied to the coarse genre, it remains static regardless of the slot’s specific content. Consequently, the valuation fails to leverage the business or budget signal present in the context, leading to a mismatch between the proxy value and the true value.

As the granularity of genres increases, the gap between
\sum_{t}{v}_{i,t}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]
and
\sum_{g}\tilde{v}_{i,g}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]
decreases, since each
g
captures more information about the context of
j
.
An extreme case is that when the number of genres is exactly the number of intents, then each genre represents one intent, and the approximation error goes to 0. In
Section

3
, we formally discuss how the approximation error impacts the mechanism’s truthfulness and social welfare maximization.

2.2
Contextual Coherence

A crucial component of the welfare equation (
2
) is the term
\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]
for all genres
g
.
It captures the probability that genre
g
aligns with the context of organic response at position
j
.
Intuitively, if a genre is highly correlated with the text surrounding a slot, this probability increases. Therefore, we term this probability as the
coherence
, denoted as
{c}_{g,j}\triangleq\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]
.
We further denote
\bm{c}_{j}\triangleq({c}_{g,j})_{g\in\{1,2,\cdots,L\}}
.

The concept of contextual coherence has been well-studied in the online advertising industry for decades
(
37
;
16
)
, also known as
semantic matching
or
relevance modeling
. The widespread industrial deployment demonstrates that the estimation
{c}_{g,j}
is computationally feasible and can be implemented on the basis of previous studies.

In our framework, coherence is measured on the platform side since only the platform holds the organic response.
We use
\tilde{c}_{g,j}
to denote the platform’s estimation of
{c}_{g,j}
, and analyze how the estimation error impacts the theoretical bounds in
Section

3
.
In
Section

4
, we will propose two prototype implementations to empirically estimate the coherence with modern LLMs.

2.3
Connection to Low-Rank Approximation of the Welfare Matrix

Combining the genre-based valuation proxy from
Section

2.1
and the coherence defined above, we can interpret our framework as a low-rank approximation of the welfare matrix, as shown in
Figure

3
.

To formalize this, let
\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}\triangleq(\tilde{c}_{g,j})_{g\in\{1,\dots,L\}}
denote the estimated coherence vector for slot
j
, and
\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\triangleq(\tilde{v}_{i,g})_{g\in\{1,\dots,L\}}
denote the genre-based valuation vector for advertiser
i
. The estimated value for advertiser
i
at slot
j
is the inner product:
\tilde{w}_{i,j}=\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}.
Let
\bm{W}_{N\times M}\triangleq({w}_{i,j})
denote the underlying value matrix where rows represent advertisers and columns represent the extremely large space of all possible response contexts (slots). Our framework approximates
\bm{W}
with the production of two lower-dimensional matrices:

1.

A static valuation matrix
\tilde{\bm{V}}_{L\times N}\triangleq(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i})_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}
(Advertiser
\times
Genres), which can be elicited by offline bidding and “frozen” during LLM inference.

2.

A dynamic coherence matrix
\tilde{\bm{C}}_{L\times M}\triangleq(\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j})_{j\in\{1,\dots,M\}}
(Genres
\times
Current Slot), which is updated by the platform in real-time inference.

Here, the rank of both
\tilde{\bm{V}}
and
\tilde{\bm{C}}
is bounded by the number of genres
L
. By limiting
L
to a finite number (e.g., the size of the IAB taxonomy), we reduce the bidding dimensionality from the intractable context space to the tractable genre space.

2.4
Auction Mechanism

Finally, we formally define the auction mechanism. As illustrated in
Figure

4
, the platform conducts the auction using the advertisers’ genre-based bids and its own coherence estimates.

Bids and Allocation.

Each advertiser
i
submits a bid vector
\bm{b}_{i}\triangleq({b}_{i,g})^{\top}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
, where
{b}_{i,g}\in[0,\bar{v}]
represents their estimated value for an impression within genre
g
.
We say an advertiser
truthfully
bids if
\bm{b}_{i}=\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\triangleq(\tilde{v}_{i,g})^{\top}_{g\in\{1,2,\cdots,L\}}
.
The platform determines an allocation
{x}_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
, indicating if ad
a_{i}
is placed in slot
j
, and calculates a payment
{p}_{i}\geq 0
. Let
K
be a pre-determined integer that denotes the number of ads to insert. The allocation must satisfy the following feasibility constraints:

1.

Each slot contains at most one ad:
\sum_{i}{x}_{i,j}\leq 1,\forall j
.

2.

Each advertiser receives at most one slot per response:
\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\leq 1,\forall i
.

3.

Exactly
K
ads are inserted:
\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}=K
.

Ground truth utility.

Given the allocation and payment, each advertiser
i
receives a
ground truth
utility
{u}_{i}
, which can be regarded as a function of all advertisers’ bids
\bm{b}
, since the allocation
{x}_{i,j}
and payment
{p}_{i}
depends on
\bm{b}
.

{u}_{i}(\bm{b})\triangleq\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot{w}_{i,j}-{p}_{i}.

Proxy utility.

However, a core challenge in our framework is that, since the true values are unknown to the platform, there is a misalignment between the advertiser’s ground truth value and the estimated value. With estimated coherence
\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}\triangleq(\tilde{c}_{g,j})^{\top}_{g\in\{1,2,\cdots,L\}}
and values on genres
\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}\triangleq(\tilde{v}_{i,g})^{\top}_{g\in\{1,2,\cdots,L\}}
, recall that the
proxy
value that an advertiser
i
obtains in slot
j
is

\tilde{w}_{i,j}\triangleq\sum_{g}\tilde{v}_{i,g}\cdot\tilde{c}_{g,j}=\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j},

and the
proxy
utility is therefore

\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b})\triangleq\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\tilde{w}_{i,j}-{p}_{i}=\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-{p}_{i}.

2.4.1
Estimation Errors

The gap between the proxy utility
\tilde{u}_{i}
and the ground truth utility
{u}_{i}
stems from two sources: the coarseness of the genre proxy and the platform’s imperfect coherence measurement. We formally define these errors below; they are used in
Section

3
to analyze the approximation bound for truthfulness and welfare maximization.

Valuation error.

Recall that the ground truth value is a sum over intents. We can rewrite this value by grouping intents into their respective genres. Let
{v}_{i,g,j}
denote the advertiser’s
realized value
for genre
g
in the specific context of slot
j
:

{v}_{i,g,j}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{{v}_{i,t}\mid t\in g,j}\right]=\sum_{t\in g}{v}_{i,t}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]}.

This allows us to express the ground truth value in terms of genres:

\displaystyle{w}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}{v}_{i,g,j}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right].

The
valuation error
arises because the advertiser bids a static value
{b}_{i,g}
for the genre (which ideally equals
\tilde{v}_{i,g}
), but the realized value
{v}_{i,g,j}
varies by slot (e.g., a “business trip” slot vs. a “family vacation” slot within the “Airlines” genre).

Definition 2.3

(Valuation Error)
.

We define
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}
as the maximum normalized deviation between the static genre value and the context-specific realized value:

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\triangleq\frac{\max_{i,g,j}\left|\tilde{v}_{i,g}-{v}_{i,g,j}\right|}{\bar{v}}.

where
\bar{v}
is the upper bound of the values
\tilde{v}_{i,g}
.

Coherence error.

The second source of error is the platform’s ability to estimate the coherence of a genre with the slot context.

Definition 2.4

(Coherence Error)
.

We define
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
as the the maximum
\mathcal{L}_{1}
deviation between the estimated coherence vector and the true probability vector of the genres:

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}\triangleq\max_{j}\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j}}\right\|_{1}=\max_{j}\sum_{g}\left|\tilde{c}_{g,j}-{c}_{g,j}\right|.

3
Incentive Compatibility and Social Welfare Maximization

Our primary goal is to find a mechanism to maximize social welfare, defined as the sum of true values of all advertisers:

W(\bm{x})\triangleq\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot{w}_{i,j}.

However, a fundamental challenge is that the platform cannot observe the true value
{w}_{i,j}
derived from latent intents. It can only observe the proxy value
\tilde{w}_{i,j}
derived from genres. This creates a misalignment between the
proxy
objective optimized by the platform and the
ground truth
value and utility experienced by advertisers (as shown in
Figure

4
).

In this section, we analyze how this misalignment affects incentive compatibility and social welfare.

3.1
Incentive Compatibility

We first examine the incentives for advertisers. Since the platform designs the mechanism based on its own estimates, we define properties with respect to the proxy setting versus the ground truth setting.

Definition 3.1

(DSIC and IR in the proxy setting)
.

We say a mechanism is dominant strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) in the proxy setting if truthfully bidding maximizes the proxy utility, or

\tilde{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\geq\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}),\quad\forall i,\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}.

We say a mechanism is individually rational (IR) in the proxy setting if truthfully bidding obtains a non-negative utility, or

\tilde{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\geq 0,\quad\forall i,\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}.

One observes that DSIC and IR mechanisms with respect to the proxy utility satisfy the above properties in the proxy setting.
However, advertisers may still deviate if their true utility
{u}_{i}
differs from
\tilde{u}_{i}
. Since the platform cannot guarantee perfect alignment with latent variables, we aim for
approximate
guarantees in the ground truth setting relative to the upper bound on advertiser values
\bar{v}=\max_{i,t}{{v}_{i,t}}
.

Definition 3.2

(Approximate DSIC and IR in the ground truth setting)
.

We say a mechanism is
\varepsilon
-DSIC in the ground truth setting for
\varepsilon\geq 0
if truthfully bidding maximizes ground truth utility with a compensation of at most
\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
, or

{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})+\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}\geq{u}_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}),\quad\forall i,\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}.

We say a mechanism is
\varepsilon
-IR in the ground truth setting for
\varepsilon\geq 0
if truthfully bidding obtains a utility at least
-\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
, or

{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\geq-\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v},\quad\forall i,\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}.

The following proposition establishes that if the approximation errors (from values on genres and coherence estimation) are bounded, a DSIC and IR mechanism in the proxy setting remains approximately DSIC and IR in the ground truth setting.

Proposition 3.1
.

If a mechanism is DSIC and IR in the proxy setting, it is
(2\varepsilon)
-DSIC and
\varepsilon
-IR in the ground truth setting, with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

For any bids
\bm{b}
, we bound the utility gap between two settings as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b})-{u}_{i}(\bm{b})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\mathchoice{\left(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{c}_{j}\right)}{(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{c}_{j})}{(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{c}_{j})}{(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{c}_{j})}\right|

(where
\bm{v}_{i,j}\triangleq({v}_{i,g,j})^{\top}_{g\in\{1,2,\cdots,L\}}
)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\mathchoice{\left[(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j})^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j})\right]}{[(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j})^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j})]}{[(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j})^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j})]}{[(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j})^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\bm{v}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j})]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\mathchoice{\left[\left\|{\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j}}\right\|_{1}\right]}{[\left\|{\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j}}\right\|_{1}]}{[\left\|{\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j}}\right\|_{1}]}{[\left\|{\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}-\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{\bm{v}_{i,j}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\bm{c}_{j}}\right\|_{1}]}

(Hölder’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\mathchoice{\left[(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\cdot\bar{v})\cdot 1+\bar{v}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}\right]}{[(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\cdot\bar{v})\cdot 1+\bar{v}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}]}{[(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\cdot\bar{v})\cdot 1+\bar{v}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}]}{[(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\cdot\bar{v})\cdot 1+\bar{v}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}]}

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v}.

Therefore, since the mechanism is DSIC in the proxy setting, we have for any
i,\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}
,

\displaystyle{u}_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})+(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})+(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v}

\displaystyle\leq{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})+2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v}.

This implies that the mechanism is
(2\varepsilon)
-DSIC in the ground truth setting for
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\geq\tilde{u}_{i}(\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})-(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v}\geq-(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v},

and the mechanism is
\varepsilon
-IR in the ground truth setting.
∎

3.2
Social Welfare Maximization

Recall that the ground truth value
{w}_{i,j}
is unobservable to the platform, thus, the platform can only optimize the
proxy social welfare
:

\tilde{W}(\bm{x})\triangleq\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{w}_{i,j}.

We now analyze the optimization of this proxy and derive a bound on the approximation error relative to the ground truth social welfare
W(\bm{x})
.

As we have shown, for a DSIC and IR mechanism, deviating from truthful bidding can at most yield a utility gain of
2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\bar{v}
for each advertiser.
However, since advertisers do not have the information of ground truth values of
{v}_{i,j,g}
and
{c}_{g,j}
during bidding,
when
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
are small, it is reasonable to assume that they are also optimizing the proxy value as an alternative, and consequently, they bid truthfully under a DSIC and IR mechanism from the proxy setting.

3.2.1
VCG Auction

The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) auction is known to be a mechanism satisfying DSIC, IR, and maximizing social welfare when advertisers bid truthfully.
Tailored to our setting, after receiving the bids
\bm{b}
of all advertisers and computing the estimated coherence
\tilde{c}_{j}
for each
j
, the platform computes an allocation
\bm{x}^{*}
that maximizes the proxy social welfare
\tilde{W}(\bm{x})
, or

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{*}\triangleq\argmax_{\bm{x}}\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{w}_{i,j}=\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\sum_{j}{x}_{i,j}\leq 1,\quad\forall i;\qquad\sum_{i}{x}_{i,j}\leq 1,\quad\forall j;\qquad\sum_{i,j}{x}_{i,j}=K.

For a better understanding of the above optimization, let

\bm{B}\triangleq[\bm{b}_{1},\cdots,\bm{b}_{i},\cdots,\bm{b}_{N}]_{L\times N},\quad\tilde{\bm{C}}\triangleq[\tilde{\bm{c}}_{0},\cdots,\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j},\cdots,\tilde{\bm{c}}_{M}]_{L\times M}.

Then we are picking
K
indices from the matrix
(\bm{B}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{C}})_{N\times M}
in different rows and columns, to maximize their sum.

As for the payment, each allocated advertiser pays the social cost that its winning incurs on other advertisers, namely,

{p}_{i}=\max_{\bm{x}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j}{x}_{i^{\prime},j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j}x^{*}_{i^{\prime},j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}.

In particular, when advertiser
i
does not get allocated,
{p}_{i}=0
.

The main intuition behind the VCG auction is to align each advertiser’s objective with the platform’s objective – maximizing the proxy social welfare, to guarantee DSIC and IR. To see this, we compute each advertiser’s proxy utility as

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{w}_{i,j}-{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j}x^{*}_{i^{\prime},j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}-\max_{\bm{x}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j}{x}_{i^{\prime},j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}.

Notice that the last term is irrelevant to advertiser
i
, thus its objective is to optimize the sum

\sum_{j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j}x^{*}_{i^{\prime},j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i^{\prime}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}.

Since
\bm{x}^{*}
optimizes
\sum_{i,j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{j}
, advertiser
i
’s optimal strategy is to bid
\bm{b}_{i}=\tilde{\bm{v}}_{i}
, and the corresponding utility is non-negative.

3.2.2
Social Welfare Gap

Since the VCG auction that maximizes the proxy social welfare is DSIC and IR in the proxy setting, we can invoke
Proposition

3.1
and derive that this mechanism is approximately DSIC and IR in the ground truth setting as well.
However, even if we assume advertisers bid truthfully in this case, there remains a gap to the ground-truth social welfare
W(\bm{x})
.
Below, we give a bound on the gap to show that the VCG mechanism behaves well.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose the platform takes the VCG auction that maximizes the proxy social welfare and advertisers bid truthfully, then the gap between the proxy social welfare and the optimal ground truth social welfare
\max_{\bm{x}}W(\bm{x})
is bounded by
2K\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
, with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.2
.

According to
Proposition

3.1
,
\left|\tilde{w}_{i,j}-{w}_{i,j}\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
, thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\tilde{w}_{i,j}-\sum_{i,j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot{w}_{i,j}\leq\sum_{i,j}x^{*}_{i,j}\cdot\varepsilon=K\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}.

This also implies that
\max_{\bm{x}}W(\bm{x})\leq\max_{\bm{x}}\tilde{W}(\bm{x})+K\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
. Putting them together induces the result.
∎

3.3
Interpretation for Approximation Guarantees: the Role of Genres

From
Propositions

3.1
and
3.2
, we establish that when the platform adopts the VCG mechanism with proxy values, the potential utility gain from strategic deviation is bounded by
2\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
for each advertiser. Furthermore, assuming truthful bidding, the realized ground truth social welfare is suboptimal by at most
2K\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\bar{v}
, with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
.
Consequently, our framework provides strong guarantees when both
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
are minimized.

While we discuss the estimation of coherence values
{c}_{j,g}
and the gap
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{C}}
later in
Section

4
, we focus here on drivers for a small valuation gap
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}
. Recall that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}=\max_{i,j,g}\left|\tilde{v}_{i,g}-{v}_{i,g,j}\right|/\bar{v}
. Intuitively, this gap is small if genres provide a good
clustering
of intents, such that the distribution of user intents within a genre remains consistent across different slot contexts.

To formalize this, let
D_{g,j}
denote the conditional distribution of specific intents
t
given a genre
g
and slot
j
:

\mathrm{Pr}_{D_{g,j}}\!\left[{t}\right]\triangleq\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{t\mid j}\right]}{\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]},\quad\forall t\in g.

We quantify the variation between these distributions across different slots using the Total Variation Distance (TVD):
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D,D^{\prime})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\sum\left|D-D^{\prime}\right|
. We now have the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D_{g,j},D_{g,j^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon_{d}
holds for any
j\neq j^{\prime}
, we have
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}=\max_{i,j,g}\frac{\left|\tilde{v}_{i,g}-{v}_{i,g,j}\right|}{\bar{v}}\leq\varepsilon_{d}
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.3
.

Recall that

\displaystyle{v}_{i,g,j}=\sum_{t\in g}{v}_{i,t}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}_{D_{g,j}}\!\left[{t}\right].

Using the property that the difference in expectations is bounded by the TVD scaled by the maximum value, and given
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D_{g,j},D_{g,j^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon_{d}
, we have for any
j\neq j^{\prime}
:

\left|{v}_{i,g,j}-{v}_{i,g,j^{\prime}}\right|\leq\bar{v}\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D_{g,j},D_{g,j^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon_{d}\cdot\bar{v}.

Since
\tilde{v}_{i,g}
is the average value over all slots (i.e.,
\tilde{v}_{i,g}=\mathbb{E}_{j}\!\left[{{v}_{i,g,j}\mid i,g}\right]
), the distance between any single
{v}_{i,g,j}
and the mean
\tilde{v}_{i,g}
cannot exceed the maximum pairwise distance between
{v}_{i,g,j}
and
{v}_{i,g,j^{\prime}}
:

\left|\tilde{v}_{i,g}-{v}_{i,g,j}\right|\leq\varepsilon_{d}\cdot\bar{v},\quad\forall i,g,j.

We therefore have
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{V}}\leq\varepsilon_{d}
.
∎

4
Coherence Measurement Implementation

In this section, we aim to propose implementations to estimate the coherence
{c}_{g,j}=\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{g\mid j}\right]
, representing the probability that a user’s intent belongs to genre
g
at slot
j
.

In a mature advertising ecosystem, this probability is typically estimated using extensive historical data (e.g., click-through rates or conversion logs). However, for a novel framework such as ad insertion in LLM responses, such production data may be unavailable, making it potentially intractable to directly measure this probability.

To bridge this gap, our framework introduces a two-step process:
First, we compute a raw signal measuring the semantic alignment. We term the raw signal the
coherence signal
, denoted as
{s}_{g,j}\in\mathbb{R}
. This score quantifies how well an ad of genre
g
semantically aligns with the organic context at slot
j
. Second, we map this signal to a valid probability distribution. This process is often known as
calibration
in machine learning
(
17
)
. We formally define the calibration step as follows.

Definition 4.1

(calibration function)
.

We define the calibration function
f:\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\Delta^{L}
as a mapping that converts the vector of coherence signals into a probability distribution over genres. For every slot
j
, the estimated coherence vector is given by:

({c}_{g,j})_{g\in\mathcal{G}}=f(({s}_{g,j})_{g\in\mathcal{G}}).

In a production environment,
f
is a learnable module (e.g., via Isotonic Regression
(
49
)
or a scaling function like Softmax with temperature) that fits the raw coherence signals to observed user interactions. For the score of our framework, we focus on deriving high-quality estimates of the raw coherence signals
{s}_{g,j}
, leaving the data-driven calibration to future engineering deployment.

We now introduce two methods to compute the coherence signal, based on sentence embedding and LLM-as-a-judge, respectively.
In
Section

5
, we conduct empirical studies to compare these methods’ alignment with human-annotated ratings.

Sentence embedding.

Sentence embedding models convert a sentence into a numerical vector that represents its semantic meaning.
The cosine similarity between two embedding vectors can then be used to measure the semantic similarity between the sentences.
This method is widely used in retrieval augmented generation (RAG) in LLMs
(
24
)
.

Recall that the organic text is a list of sentences, denoted as
z=z_{1}z_{2}\dots z_{M}
.
We define the embedding-based coherence signal
\tilde{s}_{g,j}^{\operatorname{(embed)}}
as the cosine similarity between the embeddings of the ad genre and its adjacent sentences, minus the cosine similarity between the embeddings of the organic adjacent sentences, which represents the cost of breaking the flow, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{g,j}^{\operatorname{(embed)}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{cos}(\operatorname{embed}(z_{j}),\operatorname{embed}(g))

\displaystyle\ +\operatorname{cos}(\operatorname{embed}(g),\operatorname{embed}(z_{j+1}))

\displaystyle\ -\operatorname{cos}(\operatorname{embed}(z_{j}),\operatorname{embed}(z_{j+1})).

LLM-as-a-Judge.

Recent advancements have demonstrated that LLMs can effectively act as evaluators for natural language quality, a paradigm known as “LLM-as-a-Judge.” Unlike embeddings, which capture local semantic overlap, this method utilizes the LLM’s implicit reasoning capabilities to evaluate rhetorical flow and contextual relevance.

For each insertion
(j,g)
, we present the context, including the user prompt, the organic response, and the slot, as well as the ad genre
g
to the LLM along with a carefully designed prompt (including chain-of-thought reasoning
(
45
)
; see
Section

A.1
for details).
The LLM outputs an estimated coherence signal
\tilde{s}_{g,j}^{\operatorname{(judge)}}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
.

5
Coherence Measurements’ Alignment with Human Evaluation

The two LLM-based coherence signal measurements offer ways to assess how well ad genres integrate with surrounding organic content.
However, a critical question remains:

To what extent do these automated coherence signal measurements align with human perceptions of coherence signals between ads and organic LLM-generated content?

To answer this question, we conduct an experiment comparing LLM-based coherence signal measurements with human perceptions, and compare between
\tilde{s}_{g,j}
and
{s}_{g,j}
.
Our analysis spans a diverse set of organic prompts, ad genres, and insertion positions.
To ensure both diversity and representativeness, we select prompts based on prior surveys of LLM user behavior, and choose ad genres grounded in real-world digital advertising expenditure statistics.

5.1
Experiment Setup

Specifically, we select 7 diverse prompts that reflect typical user interactions with LLMs, informed by survey data from over 500 U.S. adults
(
35

For each prompt, we generate an organic response using OpenAI’s GPT-4o model.

The experiment was conducted before GPT-5 is released.

We select 10 high-spend ad genres based on industry spending patterns in digital advertising
(

The selected genres represent sectors with the highest digital advertising expenditure.

To increase category granularity, we divide the retail sector into three sub-genres: packaged food, apparel, and electronics.
These genres are presented in alphabetical order during the experiment to mitigate ordering effects.

For each generated text, we identify potential ad insertion points primarily at paragraph boundaries, as these represent typical positions for display advertisements in digital content.
For robustness testing, we also selected several insertion points within paragraphs that would potentially disrupt the flow of organic content, allowing us to evaluate how coherence signal metrics perform across varying degrees of contextual disruption.

6.1
Computational Efficiency of VCG

We now empirically evaluate the computational efficiency of the VCG mechanism. While our theoretical results in
Section

3
confirm that VCG is approximately truthful and welfare-optimal, the mechanism is often criticized for its computational cost at scale due to the requirements of maximum weighted matching.

However, the specific constraints of LLM ad insertion mitigate this concern. Although the number of advertisers
N
may be large, the number of candidate slots
M
is naturally limited by the length of the organic response. Furthermore, to maintain user engagement, the number of allocated ads
K
is kept small. This structure suggests that VCG may remain computationally viable even at scale.

7
Further Related Work

Ad insertion in LLMs.

In the past two years, there has been a surge of studies on mixing ads into LLM responses.
A positional paper,
13
, raises several critical factors to consider when conducting such a fusion, including “how to display ads” (modification module), “what to bid for” (bidding module), “how to predict CTR” (prediction module), and “how to conduct an auction” (auction module).

On the problem of “what to bid for”, existing works can be roughly classified into two groups, bidding at the
token or segment level

(
12
;
18
)
and bidding at the
query or response level

(
40
;
11
;
29
;
3
)
.
However, as we have discussed in the Introduction, both solutions could lead to practical concerns, ranging from low computation efficiency, hallucination risks, to privacy leakage. In contrast, our framework introduces a third approach: bidding on
genres
, and effectively resolves these tensions.

While some other recent mechanism design papers, including
4
and
5
, also propose mechanisms that could potentially fit into the scenario of ad auctions in LLM responses, these works focus more on the mechanism design problem rather than providing a full applicable end-to-end framework for mixing ads into LLM responses. In contrast, we explicitly model and quantify the
coherence
between organic responses and ad genres, bridging abstract mechanism design with the linguistic reality of LLM outputs.

Evaluation metrics for natural language generation (NLG).

Natural language generation tasks such as machine translation and summarization have traditionally relied on automatic metrics that compare a model’s output to human-written reference texts. Classic metrics like BLEU
(
34
)
and ROUGE
(
25
)
measure n-gram overlap with reference translations or summaries. In recent years, more advanced metrics have been proposed to capture deeper semantic quality and fluency. For example, BERTScore
(
51
)
uses contextual embedding similarity. BARTScore
(
48
)
, GPTScore
(
15
)
, and GEM
(
47
;
28
)
use pre-trained generative models to score how well a candidate can reproduce the reference or vice versa.

However, inserting advertisements into an LLM-generated response is a new task. We still care about the overall language quality of the augmented response, but the primary evaluation focus is on contextual coherence. Recent work on LLM-as-a-judge evaluation
(
2
;
23
;
52
)
supports using LLMs’ reasoning ability to rate outputs according to the given criteria, which is promising for our needs. In addition, we propose a sentence-embedding-based method that uses cosine similarity between ads and their surrounding content as an additional approach for evaluating contextual coherence.

8
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we present a practical framework for inserting ads into LLM responses with guarantees on advertisers’ economic welfare, contextual coherence, and computational efficiency. Central to our approach are two layers of decoupling: decoupling organic response generation from ad insertion to address legal and moral concerns, and introducing “genres”, decoupling bidding from real-time generation to resolve the privacy and latency bottlenecks inherent in broadcasting full user prompts or responses, The design of genres provides a stable, bid-able interface—functioning effectively as the “keywords” of the LLM era.

Theoretically, we prove that VCG applied to genre-based proxy bidding preserves approximate DSIC, IR (
Proposition

3.1
) and welfare-optimality (
Proposition

3.2
), when the genre granularity sufficiently captures user intent.
Empirically, we demonstrate that this approach is computationally viable at scale and that our proposed LLM-as-a-Judge metric can serve as a human-aligned proxy for estimating contextual coherence signals. Together, these components offer a sustainable path toward the commercialization of large language models.

Our study has certain limitations that suggest directions for future work.

First, our framework relies on LLM methods to provide accurate coherence measurements. While our LLM-as-a-Judge method aligns well with human annotation, further refinements could improve accuracy and efficiency. Future work may explore task-aware embeddings
(
26
)
tailored specifically to ad–context fit, or fine-tuning the evaluator via RLHF
(
7
;
38
)
to better capture human intents for ad insertion.
Additionally, mapping the raw coherence signals to calibrated probabilities using production data remains a crucial step for real-world deployment.
Advances in embedding learning, AI alignment, and generative recommendation systems
(
19
)
will likely offer useful tools in the future.

Second, using genres mediates the trade-off between efficiency and contextual coherence. Our current model assumes a fixed partition of the user intent space. Future work could explore adaptive partitioning or hierarchical taxonomies (e.g.,
Automotive

\rightarrow

Cars

\rightarrow

Sports cars
) under explicit runtime constraints to dynamically determine the optimal genre granularity, minimizing the valuation error
\varepsilon_{V}
while maintaining system scalability, is a promising direction.

Although our framework focuses on contextual relevance, it does not explicitly model user heterogeneity. Integrating personalized coherence measurements that estimate the probability of active genres based on individual user history could significantly enhance ad relevance. This could be achieved by adapting the coherence measurements to be user-specific without altering the fundamental allocation mechanism.

Our framework focuses on the response level, the atomic unit of conversations in real-world LLM interactions. Extending our model to optimal allocations over sequences of interdependent responses, similar to recent work by
3
, is an important next step. Furthermore, moving from a one-shot auction to a dynamic setting where agents manage budgets constraints over time would bridge the gap between our theoretical model and practical ad campaigns.

A.3
Synthetic Data Generation for VCG Runtime

For advertiser bids, we assume a sparse interest model where each advertiser bids on a limited subset of genres. The size of this subset is determined by a Poisson distribution (
\lambda=2
), reflecting that most advertisers target only a few specific verticals. For these selected genres, the bid values are drawn uniformly from the interval
[0,1]
, while valuations for non-selected genres are set to zero.

Simultaneously, we randomly generate a coherence matrix representing the alignment between each potential slot and every ad genre. We initialize these coherence scores uniformly in
[0,1]
but randomly set half of the entries to zero, to simulate the realistic scenario where many genres are contextually irrelevant to a given slot. We ensure that every slot maintains non-zero coherence.
