The role of transcription factor TBX19 in melanoma: a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis and cellular experiments
Authors: Bing Yuan, Kang Huang, Qing Yang, Minghui Li, Hongjun Mei, Junfeng Tan, Lei Wu, Fangzhou Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMelanoma is a highly aggressive skin cancer with a high mortality rate. Although targeted therapies and immunotherapies have advanced, the prognosis for advanced melanoma remains poor. The identification of novel molecular biomarkers is thus crucial for early diagnosis and prognosis prediction. The transcription factor TBX19 has been linked to various biological processes, but its role in melanoma pathogenesis is not well defined.MethodsWe analyzed the expression patterns and the diagnostic and prognostic significance of TBX19 in melanoma using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between TBX19 high- and low-expression groups were identified and subjected to functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. The relationship between TBX19 expression and immune cell infiltration was evaluated. Furthermore, we investigated TBX19-associated signaling pathways and drug sensitivity using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. The functional role of TBX19 was validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments.ResultsTBX19 expression was significantly downregulated in melanoma tissues, and this downregulation was associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Low TBX19 expression was an independent risk factor for poorer overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that TBX19-related DEGs were involved in processes such as keratinization and immune response. TBX19 expression showed a positive correlation with the infiltration of T helper cells and Tcm cells, but a negative correlation with mast cell infiltration among others. Moreover, TBX19 expression was linked to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the RAS-Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (RAS-MAPK) signaling pathway, and it influenced sensitivity to several anticancer drugs. In vitro experiments demonstrated that TBX19 knockdown promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion in A375 cells, upregulated cyclin D1, cyclin E1, N-cadherin, and vimentin, and downregulated E-cadherin. Conversely, TBX19 overexpression yielded opposite effects in SK-MEL-1 cells. An in vivo xenograft model confirmed that TBX19 knockdown accelerated tumor growth.ConclusionsTBX19 functions as a tumor suppressor in melanoma, modulating tumor progression, immune infiltration, and drug responses. Our findings reveal TBX19 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms of melanoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15473-2.

Introduction

Skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) is a highly aggressive form of skin cancer originating from melanocytes. Its global incidence has risen steadily, with an estimated 325,000 new cases and over 57,000 deaths in 2020, according to the World Health Organization, posing a significant public health challenge [1, 2]. While early-stage melanoma is often curable by surgical excision, advanced disease remains difficult to manage due to its high potential for metastasis and frequent development of therapy resistance [3]. The treatment landscape for melanoma has been transformed by targeted therapies (e.g., BRAF and MEK inhibitors) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4), which have improved outcomes for patients with advanced disease [2]. Despite these advances, a substantial number of patients experience relapse or possess inherent resistance to these treatments, highlighting the urgent need to identify new molecular drivers of melanoma progression and biomarkers for predicting response.

The T-box transcription factor 19 (TBX19), also known as TPIT, is a member of the T-box gene family, which is situated on chromosome 1q24 [4]. The TBX19 protein contains several functional domains, including a transcriptional activation domain, a nuclear localization sequence, and a T-domain (a DNA-binding domain characteristic of the TBX family) that enables it to function as a transcriptional activator [5, 6]. TBX19 is primarily expressed in the pituitary gland and adipose tissue, where it regulates hormone production (e.g., ACTH), cell differentiation, and metabolic processes [7–9].

Emerging evidence suggests that TBX19 may also play a role in tumorigenesis across various cancer types, though its functions appear to be context-dependent. For instance, TBX19 has been reported to be upregulated in colorectal cancer (CRC), where its expression correlates with lymph node metastasis, and in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where it contributes to maintaining cancer stemness and promotes metastasis [10–12]. These findings indicate that TBX19 can act as an oncogenic factor in certain malignancies. However, its expression pattern, functional significance, and clinical relevance in melanoma remain entirely unexplored.

This study aims to systematically investigate the role of TBX19 in cutaneous melanoma. We analyzed its expression profile, diagnostic and prognostic value using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Furthermore, we explored the association between TBX19 expression and immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment. Finally, functional experiments were conducted to validate the effects of TBX19 on melanoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Our results uncover TBX19 as a potential tumor suppressor in melanoma, influencing tumor progression, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity, thereby offering new insights into melanoma biology and potential therapeutic avenues.

Materials and methods

Analysis of TBX19 expression in melanoma

TBX19 mRNA expression was analyzed using the XENA TCGA_GTEx-SKCM database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/; https://www.xiantaozi.com/), which comprises 813 normal tissue samples and 469 skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) samples alongside relevant clinicopathological data. Expression was further validated using the “Match TCGA normal and GTEx data” option in the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis platform (GEPIA,http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index. html)), which includes 558 normal and 461 tumor samples. Unavailable or unknown clinical information is considered to be a missing value. RNA-seq data in transcripts per million reads (TPM) format, uniformly processed by the Toil pipeline [13], underwent standardized pre-processing steps, including log2 transformation, background correction, and normalization. The ComBat algorithm from the “sva” R package (version version 3.42.0) was applied to correct for batch effects, with the data source (TCGA or GTEx) specified as the batch variable, thereby adjusting for technical variation while preserving biological signals. Differences in TBX19 expression between tumor and normal tissues were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. As the clinical data for the SKCM cohort was sourced from online databases, it was assumed that written informed consent had already been secured.

Clinical value assessment of TBX19 in melanoma

Patients were stratified into TBX19 high- and low-expression groups based on the median mRNA expression level in the TCGA_GTEx-SKCM cohort. The association between TBX19 expression and key clinicopathological parameters (including gender, race, T/N/M stage, ulceration, Clark level, and Breslow depth) was evaluated using the chi-square test. Samples with missing clinical data (e.g., survival time, tumor stage) were excluded from the respective analyses. The diagnostic value of TBX19 was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Prognostic significance for survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression. A nomogram was constructed with the “rms” R package (version 6.3-0) to predict patient overall survival (OS) [14].

Functional enrichment analysis of TBX19-Related genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between TBX19 high- and low-expression SKCM groups were identified using the DESeq2 R package [15]. Raw read counts were normalized using the median ratio method, and a negative binomial generalized linear model was fitted to test for differential expression. To enhance the robustness of the estimations, the analysis utilized DESeq2’s shrinkage estimators for key parameters, including dispersion and log2 fold changes. Transcripts exhibiting an adjusted P-value (false discovery rate, FDR) below 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1.5 were defined as differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the TBX19-related DEGs [16]. The GO enrichment included biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.3.6) to to identify pathway differences between the expression groups [17]. The reference gene set used for GSEA was c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs. symbols.gmt [All canonical pathways] (3050). A pathway was considered statistically significant if its adjusted P-value was below 0.05 and the FDR was under 0.25 after 1000 permutations. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (confidence score cutoff > 0.4) and visualized with the igraph R package. (version 1.4.1) [18].

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

The infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types in the SKCM tumor microenvironment were quantified using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm implemented in the GSVA R package (version 1.46.0) [19, 20], A bar plot was created with ggplot2 R package (version 3.4.4) to vasualize the immune cell infiltration. The correlation between TBX19 expression and the abundance of each immune cell type was calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient [19].

Analysis of pathway activity and drug sensitivity

The potential involvement of TBX19 in core cancer pathways and its influence on drug sensitivity were assessed using the SKCM dataset within the GSCALite web platform (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/), a resource for gene set cancer analysis [14].

Cell culture

The human melanoma cell lines A375 and SK-MEL-1 were purchased from Pricella (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Gene transfection

The TBX19 gene sequence was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Knockdown (LV-shTBX19) and overexpression (LV-TBX19) lentiviruses for TBX19 were purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China), with the shRNA targeting sequence being “GCCAAGGAAAGAAATCACCTA”. TBX19 knockdown (using lentiviral vector LV-shTBX19) and overexpression (using LV-TBX19) were performed in A375 and SK-MEL-1 cells, respectively. After 48 h, the culture medium was refreshed. Stable transfectants were selected using 4 µg/mL puromycin for two weeks, and resistant colonies were expanded for subsequent experiments.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)​

Total RNA was extracted from the melanoma cells. cDNA synthesis and qPCR amplification were then performed following a previously described protocol [21]. The corresponding primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA), quantified with a BCA assay (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skimmed milk, the membranes were incubated with a primary antibody against TBX19 (A10481, Abclonal, Wuhan, China) at a 1:1000 dilution at 4 ℃ overnight, followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore), with β-actin serving as the loading control.

Cell proliferation and viability assays

The proliferative ability of A375 and SK-MEL-1 cells was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and a colony formation assay. For the CCK-8 assay, 3,000 stably transfected cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The impact of AICAR on cell viability was assessed by MTT assay. SK-MEL-1 cells were plated overnight in 96-well plates (6,000 cells/well) and subsequently treated with 1 mM AICAR (an AMPK agonist) for 48 h. The cells were then incubated with 20 µL of MTT solution (final concentration 50 µg/mL) for 4 h. After solubilizing the formazan crystals with DMSO, the absorbance was quantified at 570 nm using a microplate reader. For the colony formation assay, 500 stably transfected cells were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate and cultured for 10 days in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. The resulting colonies were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 1 h, and imaged using a microscope (Olympus, Japan). The number of colonies was counted using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates.

Cell migration and invasion assays

The migratory and invasive abilities of A375 and SK-MEL-1 cells were evaluated using Transwell assays. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber of the insert (8 μm pore size; Corning, USA) was precoated with Matrigel (R&D Systems, USA), whereas for the migration assay, the chamber was left uncoated. The inserts were then placed into 24-well plates. A total of 5 × 104 cells suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, and 600 µL of complete medium was added to the lower well to serve as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h, the cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated or invaded through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio) for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 1 h. The stained cells were imaged and counted under a microscope. All experiments were conducted in at least three independent replicates.

Xenograft tumor growth assay

The anti-tumor efficacy of TBX19 was evaluated in vivo using a xenograft model. Female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old; n = 5 per group) were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 5 × 106 stable A375 cells (LV-shTBX19 or LV-shcontrol) suspended in 100 µL of Matrigel. Tumor growth was monitored every two days starting from day 6 post-inoculation using digital calipers. Tumor volume (V) was calculated according to the formula V = (L × W2) / 2, where L and W are the longest and shortest diameters in millimeters, respectively. All mice were sacrificed after anesthesia using 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) after 14 days, and the animal study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Fifth Hospital of Wuhan, Hubei, China.

Results

TBX19 expression and clinicopathological correlations in melanoma

Analysis of the XENA TCGA_GTEx-SKCM database revealed that TBX19 expression was significantly downregulated in melanoma tissues compared to normal skin samples (1.387 ± 0.026 vs. 2.238 ± 0.019; P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). This finding was validated using the GEPIA database (Fig. 1B). We further evaluated the association between TBX19 expression and clinicopathological features. As summarized in Table 2, high TBX19 expression was inversely correlated with unfavorable prognostic indicators, including advanced melanoma Clark level (P = 0.028) and greater Breslow depth (P = 0.024). Additionally, high TBX19 expression was associated with a history of radiation therapy (P = 0.003). Notably, male patients and those with greater height or body weight also exhibited elevated TBX19 levels.

Diagnostic and prognostic significance of TBX19 in melanoma

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that TBX19 expression effectively discriminated melanoma tissues from normal samples, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.859 (95% CI: 0.837–0.881; Fig. 1C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with high TBX19 expression had significantly prolonged overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) (Fig. 1D–F). A nomogram integrating TBX19 expression and key clinical parameters was developed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 2A, B). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that high TBX19 expression is an independent favorable prognostic factor in melanoma (Table 3).

Differential expression genes and functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the functional role of TBX19, we identified 722 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between TBX19 high- and low-expression groups from TCGA data, including 326 upregulated and 396 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). A heatmap displayed the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 3B, C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that upregulated DEGs were primarily involved in biological processes such as axon development, cellular components including neuronal cell bodies, and molecular functions such as GABA receptor activity, while downregulated DEGs were enriched in epidermis development and keratin filament organization. KEGG pathway analysis revealed associations with neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions (upregulated) and Staphylococcus aureus infection (downregulated) (Fig. 3D, E). Validation by RT-qPCR in A375 and SK-MEL-1 cells confirmed that TBX19 knockdown reduced the expression of positively correlated genes (e.g., OTOR, SMR3B, SAGE1) and increased negatively correlated genes (e.g., CASP14, SPRR2G, LCE3C), whereas TBX19 overexpression had the opposite effects (Fig. 3F, G). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified hub genes such as RGS7 and NPY, which are implicated in melanoma progression (Fig. 3H), including RGS7 and NPY [22, 23]. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further linked TBX19 to keratinization, formation of the cornified envelope, creation of C4 and C2 activators, initial triggering of complement, binding and uptake of ligands by scavenger receptor, role of LAT2 Ntal Lab on calcium mobilization, Fcgr activation, scavenging of heme from plasma and CD22 mediated BCR regulation (Fig. 4A-I).

TBX19 expression correlates with immune cell infiltration

Given the immunogenic nature of melanoma [24], we assessed the relationship between TBX19 expression and immune infiltration using ssGSEA. TBX19 levels positively correlated with T helper cells, T central memory (Tcm) cells, and gamma-delta T (Tgd) cells, but negatively correlated with mast cells, immature dendritic cells (iDCs), natural killer (NK) cells, DCs, neutrophils, NK CD56bright cells, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (Fig. 5A-C). Specifically, high TBX19 expression was associated with increased T helper cell infiltration and reduced mast cell abundance (Fig. 5D, E), suggesting a potential role in promoting anti-tumor immunity.

Association with signaling pathways and drug sensitivity

Using the GSCALite database, we found that TBX19 expression was inversely associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and RAS-MAPK signaling activity(Fig. 6A). Furthermore, TBX19 levels also influenced sensitivity to anticancer drugs: high expression correlated with resistance to Docetaxel, Elesclomol, GSK269962A, Trametinib, TW-37, Vinblastine, and YK-4-279, but increased sensitivity to AICAR, Navitoclax, and Ruxolitinib (Fig. 6B). To validate the correlation between TBX19 expression and drug sensitivity, we selected the SK-MEL-1 cell line and AICAR for further experimentation. An MTT assay confirmed that TBX19 overexpression enhanced the sensitivity of SK-MEL-1 cells to AICAR, an AMPK agonist (Supplementary Fig. 1).

TBX19 suppresses melanoma progression in vitro and in vivo

Based on CCLE data, we selected A375 (high endogenous TBX19) and SK-MEL-1 (low endogenous TBX19) cells for functional assays (Supplementary Table 1). The relative protein level of TBX19 in A375 and SK-MEL-1 cells was verified by western blot (Supplementary Fig. 2). Western blotting confirmed efficient TBX19 knockdown in A375 and overexpression in SK-MEL-1 cells (Figs. 7A and 8A). TBX19 knockdown promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion in A375 cells (Fig. 7B-H), while overexpression suppressed these processes in SK-MEL-1 cells (Fig. 8B-H). Cell cycle progression and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) are critical for proliferation and invasion, respectively, and exerted crucial role in tumorigenesis and metastasis of melanoma [25, 26]. Mechanistically, TBX19 knockdown upregulated cell cycle promoters (CCND1, CCNE1) and mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, vimentin), while downregulating the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 7I). Conversely, TBX19 overexpression induced opposite effects (Fig. 8I). In a xenograft model, TBX19 knockdown accelerated tumor growth (Fig. 9A, B), confirming its tumor-suppressive role in vivo.

Discussion

Despite remarkable improvements in melanoma treatment achieved through targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, this malignancy remains a major clinical challenge, particularly at advanced stages where options are limited. Consequently, there is a critical need to identify novel therapeutic targets.

Transcription factors play crucial roles in the development and progression of melanoma. Key examples include AP-1, ETS-1, MITF, NF-κB, and SOX10, which regulate melanoma cell proliferation, migration, and survival [27]. Furthermore, several transcription factors, such as MITF, FOXD3, RUNX2, SOX10, the STAT3-PAX3 axis, CREB, and YAP, have been implicated in regulating resistance to BRAF V600E-targeted therapy and tumor invasiveness [28, 29]. These findings suggest that inhibiting specific transcription factors could enhance the sensitivity to BRAF inhibitors. TBX19, a member of the T-box transcription factor family, is known to play vital roles in hormone production, cell differentiation, metabolic processes, and embryonic development [7–9]. Recent studies have also reported that TBX19 regulates tumorigenesis and progression in CRC and HCC [10–12]. To identify a novel therapeutic target for melanoma, this study comprehensively investigated the expression and functional role of TBX19 through integrated bioinformatics analysis and cellular experiments.

By analyzing the TCGA database, we found that TBX19 expression was significantly downregulated in melanoma tissues compared to normal tissues. Notably, although TBX19 expression was downrgulated in melanoma patient tumor tissues, multiple melanoma lines in the CCLE dataset exhibit relatively high TBX19 expression, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. The difference likely stems from the fundamental distinction between patient-derived tumor samples (which contain a complex microenvironment) and established cell lines grown in isolation. Furthermore, it is also possible that expression of TBX19 varies among melanoma subtypes. Analysis of clinicopathological data revealed that low TBX19 expression was associated with several unfavorable prognostic factors, including higher melanoma Clark level, greater Breslow depth, and reduced sensitivity to radiation therapy. Moreover, low TBX19 expression was correlated with poorer patient survival in terms of overall survival, disease-specific survival, and progression-free interval. Collectively, these findings suggest that TBX19 acts as a tumor suppressor in melanoma and represents a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.

To elucidate the functional role of TBX19 in melanoma, we performed GO, KEGG, GSEA, and PPI analyses. GO and KEGG analyses were conducted separately on genes positively and negatively correlated with TBX19 expression. Genes positively correlated with TBX19 were primarily associated with neuronal functions, including axon development (BP), the neuronal cell body (CC), neuropeptide receptor binding (MF), and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (KEGG). In contrast, genes negatively correlated with TBX19 were significantly enriched in skin biology processes, such as epidermis development, keratinocyte differentiation, keratinization, and cornified envelope formation, suggesting a potential role for TBX19 in skin homeostasis. Furthermore, GSEA confirmed the involvement of TBX19-related genes in specific biological processes, including keratinization, complement activation, and B cell receptor regulation. These results indicate that TBX19 is involved in both maintaining epidermal integrity and modulating immune infiltration, highlighting its multifaceted role in melanoma pathogenesis.

The tumor immune microenvironment (TME) consists of diverse immune cells and plays a critical role in the tumorigenesis and progression of melanoma [24]. TBX19 expression was significantly correlated with distinct immune infiltration patterns in the melanoma TME. It showed a positive correlation with anti-tumor immune cells such as T helper and Tcm cells, but a negative correlation with innate immune cells including NK cells, DCs, and mast cells. The positive association with T helper/Tcm cells suggests TBX19 may promote an immune-supportive niche, consistent with the immune-enriched melanoma subtype linked to better immunotherapy response. As a transcription factor, TBX19 could potentially enhance T-cell recruitment/function by modulating cytokine signaling (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ) or antigen presentation. Conversely, the negative correlation with NK cells, DCs, and mast cells may reflect a more complex immunoregulatory role. While generally anti-tumor, NK/DC function can be suppressed in melanoma, and mast cells often promote immunosuppression [30–32]. Thus, their lower infiltration in TBX19-high TMEs might indicate a less immunosuppressive milieu. This pattern resonates with the immune-enriched, non-fibrotic TME subtype associated with improved survival. We hypothesize that TBX19 may skew the immune landscape toward adaptive immunity by regulating key chemokines/cytokines. Future studies should investigate its role in cytokine signaling (e.g., JAK-STAT) or antigen presentation pathways to clarify these mechanisms.

Furthermore, our cellular and in vivo experiments consistently demonstrated that TBX19 significantly suppresses melanoma cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth, unequivocally supporting its role as a tumor suppressor in this context. This finding presents a striking contrast to its documented oncogenic functions in CRC and HCC [10–12].This divergence in TBX19 function underscores a fundamental principle in cancer biology: the role of a molecule is not absolute but is critically determined by the specific tissue or cellular context. The apparent contradictory roles of TBX19 can potentially be explained by several tissue-specific mechanisms. The first reason might be distinct transcriptional networks and interacting partners. The functional outcome of a transcription factor is dictated by the constellation of co-factors and the prevailing transcriptional landscape within a cell. In melanoma, which is derived from the melanocyte lineage, key lineage-specific transcription factors such as MITF, SOX10, and PAX3 define the cellular identity and regulatory environment [33]. It is plausible that in melanoma, TBX19 interacts with or is modulated by this unique set of melanocytic transcription factors, forming protein complexes that drive a transcriptional program favoring differentiation and growth suppression. For instance, a study suggested that TBX19 can form a complex with PRMT1 on the promoter of mitochondrial fission factor (MFF) in liver cancer-initiating cells, promoting their stemness [11]. In melanoma, the interaction partners of TBX19 are likely different, potentially redirecting its activity toward the suppression of oncogenic pathways. Conversely, in CRC or HCC, TBX19 may interact with pro-oncogenic co-factors, leading to the activation of genes that drive proliferation and survival. Secondly, the lineage-dependent chromatin environments might account for these divergence. The epigenetic state and chromatin accessibility vary dramatically across different cell types. The same transcription factor may bind to entirely different sets of genomic enhancers and promoters depending on the lineage-specific chromatin landscape. In melanoma cells, the chromatin regions accessible to TBX19 might be enriched for binding sites of melanocyte-specific factors and could be in a configuration that favors the transactivation of tumor-suppressive genes (e.g., those involved in cell cycle arrest or differentiation). In contrast, in intestinal or hepatic cells, the accessible genomic regions for TBX19 binding might be near oncogenes like CCND1 (cyclin D1), which is frequently amplified in mucosal melanomas and other cancers [34], thereby contributing to its oncogenic function. This lineage-dependent chromatin environment essentially guides TBX19 to different genetic targets, resulting in opposing cellular outcomes. Collectively, the tissue-specific function of TBX19, likely orchestrated by unique interacting partners, lineage-defining transcription factors, and distinct chromatin landscapes, provides a compelling explanation for its dual roles in cancer.

The association between TBX19 and key signaling pathways, particularly RAS-MAPK and EMT, uncovered through GSCALite analysis, provides a crucial mechanistic framework for understanding its tumor-suppressive role in melanoma. Given TBX19’s identity as a transcription factor, its suppressive effects are likely mediated through the transcriptional regulation of critical downstream effectors. Our experimental validation, showing TBX19-mediated modulation of cyclins (CCND1, CCNE1) and core EMT markers (E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin), strongly supports this notion. We hypothesize that TBX19 may function as a transcriptional repressor or co-regulator that dampens the expression of pro-proliferative genes like CCND1 and CCNE1, thereby inducing cell cycle arrest. Concurrently, its potential to inhibit the expression of transcriptional repressors of E-cadherin (e.g., Snail, ZEB1/2) or to directly activate E-cadherin expression could promote a mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET)-like state, reversing the EMT process and suppressing invasion and metastasis [35, 36]. This dual action on both cell cycle progression and cellular plasticity represents a potent mechanism to constrain tumor growth and dissemination. The inhibitory effect on the RAS-MAPK pathway, a central driver of melanoma, suggests another layer of TBX19’s function. TBX19 might achieve this by transcriptionally upregulating negative regulators of the pathway (e.g., DUSP family phosphatases [37] or by repressing positive regulators. This modulation would effectively reduce the downstream oncogenic signaling flux, leading to suppressed proliferation and survival. The convergence of TBX19’s effects on EMT and RAS-MAPK signaling is particularly significant, as these pathways are known to engage in extensive crosstalk, collectively fueling tumor progression [38, 39]. The drug sensitivity analysis further corroborates the functional impact of TBX19; its expression influencing responses to anticancer agents aligns with the premise that TBX19 alters core cellular signaling states. For instance, tumors with high TBX19 might exhibit reduced dependence on oncogenic pathways targeted by specific drugs, thereby modulating therapeutic efficacy. This insight strengthens the potential of TBX19 as a biomarker for predicting response to therapy and for designing personalized combination strategies, such as pairing MAPK pathway inhibitors with agents that target vulnerabilities in tumors exhibiting a TBX19-defined signaling architecture.

Despite the significant findings, our study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, while our bioinformatic analyses leveraged public databases, they lack direct patient-level experimental validation, such as immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of TBX19 protein expression in a large, independent melanoma cohort. This restricts our ability to confirm the protein-level expression and subcellular localization of TBX19 in clinical samples. Second, the cellular and animal experiments were conducted within a single-center framework, which may introduce institutional bias and limit the generalizability of our functional findings. Multi-center validation would strengthen the reliability of our conclusions. A key mechanistic shortcoming is the absence of experiments, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, to directly identify the transcriptional targets of TBX19 in melanoma cells. Consequently, while we propose that TBX19 influences pathways like EMT and RAS-MAPK, the precise direct downstream genes remain unidentified. Finally, the mechanisms underlying TBX19’s role in immune modulation, particularly its correlations with specific immune cell infiltrates, are primarily based on computational inferences and require further direct experimental confirmation (e.g., co-culture assays) to establish causal relationships.

In conclusion, our study has highlighted the tumor-suppressive role of TBX19 in melanoma and its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target. We suggest that future studies focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying TBX19’s function and translating these findings into clinical applications for melanoma treatment.
