Inexact Uzawa-Double Deep Ritz Method for Weak Adversarial Neural Networks
Emin Benny-Chacko, Ignacio Brevis, Luis Espath, Kristoffer G. van der Zee

Abstract
. The emergence of deep learning has stimulated a new class of PDE solvers in which the unknown solution is represented by a neural network. Within this framework, residual minimization in dual norms—central to weak adversarial neural network approaches—naturally leads to saddle-point problems whose stability depends on the underlying iterative scheme. Motivated by this structure, we develop an inexact Uzawa methodology in which both trial and test functions are represented by neural networks and updated only approximately. We introduce the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method, a mesh-free deep PDE solver equipped with a continuous level convergence showing that the overall iteration remains stable and convergent provided the inexact inner updates move in the correct descent direction. Numerical experiments validate the theoretical findings and demonstrate the practical robustness and accuracy of the proposed approach. Keywords: Residual Minimization, Dual Norm, Weak Adversarial Neural networks, Saddle-Point Problems, Inexact Uzawa Method, Deep Ritz method.

1.
Introduction

Solving partial differential equations (PDEs) is a central task in scientific computing, as such equations model a wide range of physical and engineering phenomena. For many systems of interest such as those arising in elasticity, fluid mechanics, electro magnetics, or diffusion, closed-form solutions are not available, and one must resort to numerical methods. The accuracy and stability of these numerical schemes determine the reliability of simulations, while their computational efficiency dictates whether large or complex problems can be handled in practice. Consequently, the design of robust and flexible PDE solvers remains an
active and important area of research.

Classical methods for PDE approximation have been developed over many decades, with the finite element method (FEM) emerging as one of the most established approaches. FEM is grounded in variational formulations of PDEs and provides a systematic framework for constructing stable discretizations based on mesh-dependent trial and test spaces, as discussed in
[
10
,
17
,
19
]
. While FEM enjoys excellent theoretical foundations and has been highly successful in engineering applications, it also exhibits limitations. Mesh generation can be extremely challenging for complicated or evolving geometries, and the computational burden increases sharply in high-dimensional settings. These factors motivate the search for mesh-free and neural network based alternatives.

The emergence of deep learning has stimulated a new class of PDE solvers
[
16
]
in which the unknown function is represented by a neural network function
[
1
,
14
]
. For example,
[
11
]
used shallow networks to minimize residuals of the governing equations, either in strong or weak form, and
[
7
,
8
]
discussed the use of neural networks to improve discrete weak formulations. One of the earliest and most influential examples is in
[
21
]
, the physics-informed neural network (PINN) framework, which incorporates the governing equation and boundary condition directly into neural network loss, i.e., neural network is trained to minimize the strong form residual of PDEs. Although PINNs offer flexibility, they often suffer from an imbalance between different loss terms. Strong form residual minimization can be sensitive to low regularity solutions due to higher-order derivatives
[
9
]
. To address these issues, variational PINNs (VPINNs) were introduced in
[
18
]
, where the residual in the weak form of PDE is minimized; however, VPINNs still employ a neural network for the trial space and rely on classical finite elemental test functions and numerical quadrature for testing. Building on VPINNs, Robust VPINNs (RVPINNs)
[
22
]
introduce a dual-norm residual minimization, adaptively optimizing the test function to align with the largest
residual directions. This adaptive strategy ensures that the residual is minimized where it has the greatest impact, providing stronger error control and improved convergence compared to fixed test space projection. It also advocates min-max formulations or saddle point structure of dual norm minimization as discussed in
[
10
]
.

The Weak Adversarial Network (WAN) method
[
5
]
introduces a neural network test function that plays the role of a test function in a saddle-point residual minimization framework explained in
[
10
]
. Although conceptually appealing, WANs involve numerical instability that was mitigated in
[
23
]
, the Double Deep Ritz Method, which replaces the adversarial structure with two coupled Deep Ritz energy
[
26
]
minimizations for the primal and test networks; however, this method requires nested inner minimization loops, making each iteration computationally demanding and can be sensitive to the accuracy of inner solves.

Parallel to these developments, it has been recognized in numerical analysis that residual minimization in dual norms naturally leads to saddle-point or min-max structures. For such problems, classical iterative solvers such as the Uzawa algorithm,
[
24
,
4
,
6
]
which offer robust convergence guarantees of solving saddle point problems, are considered. In the recent paper
[
2
]
, the authors introduce the Deep Residual Uzawa Algorithm, where a neural network is used for residual update and finite elements for solution update. This work also explains the role of the Uzawa method in tackling minimal residual problems.

In this work, we propose the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz Method, a deep PDE solver that integrates variational neural approximations with the Uzawa iterative scheme. The method is built around an Uzawa iteration scheme explained in
[
2
]
, where here, both the residual and solution variable are represented by neural networks. In contrast with adversarial approaches such as WAN, which rely on unstable min-max dynamics, the proposed framework inherits the classical stability properties of Uzawa iterations and guarantees convergence provided each inner update moves in the correct descent direction. As a result, the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method combines the mesh-free flexibility of neural networks with the robustness of Uzawa iterative schemes, offering a new pathway toward stable and interpretable deep PDE solvers. A closely related line of work is the Ritz-Uzawa Neural Network (RUNN) framework
[
13
]
, which reformulates the classical Uzawa residual and correction steps as neural network optimization problems for solving variational formulations. RUNNs provide neural extensions of Ritz, ultra-weak, and mixed methods by coupling Uzawa iterations with trainable network representations of both primal and dual variables.

The main contributions of this work lie in the formulation of the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz algorithm and in the analysis of its convergence in the continuous setting. We establish a convergence result for an inexact Uzawa scheme in which both update steps are carried out only approximately. In particular, we establish a continuous-level convergence theorem for the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method, showing that the overall iteration remains stable and convergent provided the inner updates proceed in the correct descent direction, even though the Ritz energies are not minimized exactly. Finally, numerical experiments are presented to illustrate and support the theoretical findings, demonstrating the effectiveness and practical robustness of the proposed approach.

In the remainder of this paper, Section
2

Section

This section presents two convergence theorems: one for controlled inexactness and another covering the practically relevant case where each inner update consists of only a single gradient descent step.
Section
4
turns to the neural network approximation of the method, detailing the architecture, algorithmic implementation, and practical considerations.
Section
5
provides numerical experiments that illustrate the behavior of the proposed solver and confirm the theoretical predictions.
Finally, Section
6
summarizes the main findings of the paper.

2.
Methodology

In this section, we first describe the abstract problem setting and recall the dual-residual minimization framework that motivates our approach.
We then express the problem in its equivalent saddle-point form and review the Uzawa iteration at the continuous level, together with its standard convergence properties, following the ideas in
[
20
]
.

2.1.
Abstract Setting

We consider the following abstract variational formulation:

(1)

\text{Find }u\in U\text{ such that: }b(u,v)=\ell(v),\quad\forall v\in V.

where
U
and
V
are real Hilbert trial and test spaces respectively,
b:U\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bilinear form, and
\ell:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous linear functional. Equivalently in operator form:

(2)

\text{Find }u\in U\text{ such that: }Bu=l,

where
B:U\rightarrow V^{*}
is the operator defined by

(Bu,v)_{V^{*},V}=b(u,v)\quad\text{for all }u\in U,\,v\in V.

and
l\in V^{*}
, where
V^{*}
denotes the dual of
V
.
Also,
B
is continuous and bounded, i.e.,

(3)

m\|u\|_{U}\leq\|Bu\|_{V^{*}}\leq M\|u\|_{U},\quad u\in U

for some positive constants
m\leq M
.

2.2.
Residual Minimization and Saddle point problem

The equivalent Minimal Residual formulation for
(
1
)
is,

(4)

u^{*}=arg\min_{u\in U}\|Bu-\ell\|_{V^{*}}=\arg\min_{u\in U}\max_{v\in V}\frac{b(u,v)-\ell(v)}{\|v\|_{V}}

The associated least-squares functional is defined as:

(5)

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|Bu-\ell\|_{V^{*}}^{2}\quad\forall u\in U.

The minimal residual approximation is then obtained by solving:

(6)

u^{*}=\arg\min_{u\in U}J(u)

That is, we need to find the
u\in U
that minimizes the loss function
J(u)
that then ensures that u satisfies the variational problem
(
1
)
as close as possible.

This minimization problem is equivalent to a mixed (saddle-point) formulation, i.e,

(7)

\begin{cases}\text{Find }r\in V,\ u\in U,\text{ such that:}\\
(r,v)+b(u,v)=l(v),\quad v\in V,\\
b(w,r)=0,\quad w\in U.\end{cases}

From
(
4
)
and
(
7
)
, the minimal residual problem is explicitly seen to be a min–max(saddle point)problem. Consequently, iterative methods developed for saddle point systems, such as Uzawa-type algorithms, become natural candidates for solving
. This observation motivates the use of Uzawa approaches in the context of minimal residual formulations

2.3.
Uzawa Method

To solve the saddle-point system (
7
) iteratively, we use the Uzawa algorithm where
r
and
u
are updated iteratively.

We can reformulate (
7
) for
\tau>0
as

(8)

\begin{cases}r\in V,\ u\in U,\\
(r,v)+b(u,v)=l(v),\quad v\in V,\\
(u,w)=\tau^{-1}b(w,r)+(u,w),\quad w\in U.\end{cases}

Thus, the iterative scheme here is:

(9)

\begin{cases}(1)~Solve~for~r^{k}\in V:\\
(r^{k},v)_{V}=l(v)-b(u^{k},v),\quad v\in V,\\
\\
(2)~Update~u^{k+1}~\in U:\\
(u^{k+1},w)_{U}=(u^{k},w)_{U}+\tau~b(w,r^{k}),\quad w\in U\end{cases}

Uzawa Algorithm (
\tau>0
)

1:

Initialize

u^{0}

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

Solve
r^{k}\!\in\!V
such that

4:

(10)

(r^{k},v)_{V}=l(v)-b(u^{k},v)\quad\forall\,v\in V

5:

Update
u^{k+1}\!\in\!U

(11)

(u^{k+1},w)_{U}=(u^{k},w)_{U}+\tau~b(w,r^{k})\quad\forall\,w\in U

If
converged

end

for

We can also reformulate the Uzawa iterations (
9
) in terms of operators as:

(12)

\begin{cases}\langle R_{V}r^{k},v\rangle_{V^{*},V}+\langle Bu^{k},v\rangle_{V^{*},V}=\langle l,v\rangle_{V^{*},V}\\
(u^{k+1},w)_{U}=(u^{k},w)_{U}+\tau~(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k},w)_{U},\end{cases}

which can also be written as

(13)

\begin{cases}R_{V}r^{k}=l-Bu^{k}\\
u^{k+1}=u^{k}+\tau~R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k},\end{cases}

where
R_{V}\colon V\rightarrow V^{*}
is Riesz operator on
V
,
R_{U}^{-1}\colon U^{*}\rightarrow U
is the inverse Riesz operator on
U
and
B^{\dagger}:V\to U^{*}
be the dual operator of
B
, defined by

(B^{\dagger}v,u)_{U^{*},U}=(v,Bu)_{V,V^{*}}=(Bu,v)_{V^{*},V}=b(u,v)\quad\text{for all }u\in U,\,v\in V.

2.3.1.
Convergence analysis

Theorem 2.1
.

Classical Result: Convergence of Exact Uzawa Method

Let
u^{k}\in U\text{ and }r^{k}\in V\text{ for }k=0,1,2,\cdots
be generated through the Uzawa iterative method (
9
). Suppose
u^{*}\in U\text{ and }r^{*}\in V
be the saddle points of the saddle point problem (
7
). Then for
\tau\in(0,\frac{2}{M^{2}})
we have that

(14)

u^{k}\rightarrow u^{*}\in U,r^{k}\rightarrow r^{*}\in V.

Proof.

Upon using the second update rule as in (
13
)

\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}=\|u^{k}+\tau~R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}-u^{*}-\tau~R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{*}\|^{2}_{U}

=\|u^{k}-u^{*}+\tau~R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})\|^{2}_{U}

Now, by definition of norm,

(15)

\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}=\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}+\tau~^{2}\|R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})\|^{2}_{U}+2\tau~(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*}),u^{k}-u^{*})_{U}

Claim(a):
(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*}),u^{k}-u^{*})_{U}=b(u^{k}-u^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})=-\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}

Proof.

(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*}),u^{k}-u^{*})_{U}=B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})(u^{k}-u^{*})=b(u^{k}-u^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})

Also, we have from the first update rule for
r^{*}~and~r^{k}
:

(r^{k},v)_{V}=l(v)-b(u^{k},v)

(r^{*},v)_{V}=l(v)-b(u^{*},v)

On subtracting and substituting
v=r^{k}-r^{*}

(r^{k}-r^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})_{V}=-b(u^{k}-u^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})

\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}=-b(u^{k}-u^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})

\implies b(u^{k}-u^{*},r^{k}-r^{*})=(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*}),u^{k}-u^{*})_{U}=-\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}

.
∎

Using claim(a) in Eq.(
15
), we get

\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}=\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}+\tau~^{2}\|R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})\|^{2}_{U}+2\tau~(R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*}),u^{k}-u^{*})_{U}

=\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}+\tau~^{2}\|R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})\|^{2}_{U}-2\tau~\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}

Using the boundedness property of
R_{U}^{-1}
and
B^{\dagger}
i.e.,
\|R_{U}^{-1}(g)\|_{U}=\|g\|_{U^{\ast}}
and
\|B^{\dagger}v\|_{U^{\ast}}\leq M\|v\|_{V}

\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}\leq\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}+\tau~^{2}M^{2}\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}-2\tau~\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}

=\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}+(\tau~^{2}M^{2}-2\tau~)\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}

Since for
\tau~\in(0,\frac{2}{M^{2}})
we obtain
(\tau~^{2}M^{2}-2\tau~)\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}<0

\implies\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}<\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}

\implies\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}-\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}=0

Now,

\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}\leq\frac{\|u^{k}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}-\|u^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{U}}{\tau~^{2}M^{2}-2\tau~}

\implies\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|^{2}_{V}=0

∎

3.
Uzawa Double Ritz Method

In this section, we present the continuous formulation of the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz Method, which forms the foundation for the neural network algorithm developed in subsequent parts of the paper.
Since Uzawa iterations cannot be solved exactly in practice, we also introduce the inexact Uzawa framework and examine its convergence properties.
We first establish convergence under controlled inexactness and then show that convergence is still ensured when each update is performed approximately using a single gradient descent step.

The Uzawa iterative scheme (
9
) consists of two update rules, one for the residual
r
and one for the solution
u
. Each of these updates can be interpreted as a Ritz minimization problem.

We first consider the update of
r
given in (
10
). For a fixed
u^{k}\in U
, this update is equivalent to solving the Ritz minimization

(16)

r^{k}=\arg\min_{r\in V}\,\mathcal{L}_{u^{k}}(r),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{u^{k}}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\|r\|_{V}^{2}-\ell(r)+b(u^{k},r).

Thus, the variable
r^{k}
is obtained as the unique minimizer of the quadratic functional
\mathcal{L}_{u^{k}}
.

Next, we examine the update of
u
given in (
11
). For fixed
r^{k}\in V
and the previous iterate
u^{k}
, this step is also characterized by a Ritz minimization problem:

(17)

u^{k+1}=\arg\min_{u\in U}\,\mathcal{L}_{r^{k}}(u),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{r^{k}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{U}^{2}-\tau b(u,r^{(k)})-(u^{(k)},u)_{U}.

Algorithm 2

Uzawa Double Ritz Algorithm (
\tau>0
)

1:

Input:
Initial guess
u^{(0)}\in U_{\theta}
, step size
\tau>0

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

Residual Update:

r^{(k)}=\arg\min_{r\in V}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|r\|_{V}^{2}-\ell(r)+b(u^{k},r)\right\}

4:

Solution Update:

u^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{u\in U}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{U}^{2}-\tau b(u,r^{(k)})-(u^{(k)},u)_{U}\right\}

5:

end

for

Hence, each iteration of the Uzawa algorithm can be viewed as performing two successive minimizations:

•

Minimizing the Ritz functional (
16
) to obtain
r^{k}
.

•

Minimizing the functional (
17
) to compute
u^{k+1}

We refer to this interpretation as the
Uzawa Double Ritz Method
. When minimizer
u
and
r
are approximated using neural network parameterizations, this naturally leads to a
Uzawa Deep double-rite method
.

3.1.
Inexact Uzawa Method

In practice, the exact Uzawa updates are typically replaced by approximate ones.
The operator-based update (
13
), if computed exactly, can be expensive, particularly for large-scale or high-dimensional problems. Consequently, it is standard to replace both update steps in the Uzawa iteration with computationally feasible approximations.

At iteration
k
, the exact residual equation

r^{k}=R_{V}^{-1}(\ell-Bu^{k})

would yield the exact dual update
r^{k}
.
Since an exact solution of this problem is typically too costly, we replace it by an approximation

r^{k}_{\delta}
satisfying a prescribed relative accuracy (error bound
\delta
).
This residual update is then used in the subsequent solution update step.

In the exact Uzawa method, the solution u is updated according to

u^{k+1}=u^{k}+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}.

However, because only the approximate residual
r^{k}_{\delta}
is available, one first forms the corresponding
exact update with this approximate input,

u^{k+1}_{\delta}=u^{k}_{\delta}+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}_{\delta}.

As with this update, evaluating this expression exactly may be computationally intensive, and we therefore
introduce an additional approximation
u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, whose deviation from

u^{k+1}_{\delta}
is controlled by a relative error bound(
\epsilon
).

Thus, each iteration subsequently replaced by an inexact computation. The resulting structure forms the basis of the inexact Uzawa framework analyzed in the following section.

Algorithm 3

Inexact Uzawa Iteration

1:

Given:
initial guess
u^{0}_{\delta,\varepsilon}\in U
.

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots
until convergence
do

3:

Compute
r^{k}_{\delta}
as an approximation of

r^{k}=R_{V}^{-1}(l-Bu^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}).

4:

Compute
u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}
as an approximation of

u^{k+1}_{\delta}=u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}+\tau\,R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}_{\delta}.

5:

end

for

In this section, we analyze the convergence behavior of the resulting inexact Uzawa scheme in Algorithm(
3
), in which both the
r
-update and the
u
-update are performed with controlled inexactness. This provides a theoretical foundation for the approximate updates employed in exact Uzawa method.

3.2.
Convergence analysis:

The convergence of the inexact Uzawa method with a single approximate update is analyzed in
[
4
]
.
Building on this perspective, we establish a convergence result for an inexact Uzawa scheme in which both update steps are performed approximately.
The resulting theorem shows that the overall iteration remains convergent, provided the approximation errors at each step are suitably controlled.

Theorem 3.1
.

Convergence of Inexact Uzawa Method

Assume
\|r_{\delta}^{k}-r^{k}\|\leq\delta\|r^{k}\|
and
\|u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta}\|\leq\varepsilon\|u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta}\|
with
\delta>0,\epsilon>0
and

\delta+\varepsilon(1+\delta)<\frac{1-\gamma}{\tau M^{2}}

and
\gamma=\|I-\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}R_{V}^{-1}B\|=\max\{|1-\tau m^{2}|,|1-\tau M^{2}|\}<1
.Then,

u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}\rightarrow u^{*}\in U,r_{\delta}^{k}\rightarrow r^{*}\in V.

Proof.

Let
e^{k}_{r}=r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*}
and
e^{k}_{u}=u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}
. We begin with the residual update rule. From the definition,

R_{V}r^{k}=l-Bu^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}

and subtracting the corresponding relation for the exact solution gives

R_{V}(r^{k}-r^{*})=-B(u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}).

(18)

\implies\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|\leq M\|e^{k}_{u}\|.

Next, decompose the residual error as

R_{V}\big(r^{k}-r^{k}_{\delta}+r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*}\big)=-B(e^{k}_{u}),

which yields

R_{V}(r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*})=R_{V}(r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{k})-B(e^{k}_{u}).

Consequently,

(19)

\|r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*}\|\leq\|r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{k}\|+M\|e^{k}_{u}\|\leq M(1+\delta)\|e^{k}_{u}\|.

We now turn to the contraction property for
\|e^{k}_{u}\|
. The update for
u^{k+1}_{\delta}
satisfies

u^{k+1}_{\delta}-u^{*}=u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}_{\delta}.

By adding and subtracting
u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u^{k+1}_{\delta}-u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}+u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}

\displaystyle=u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*}).

\displaystyle u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*}

\displaystyle=(u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*})+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{k})+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}-r^{*})+(u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=(u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*})-\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}R^{-1}B(u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{*})+\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}(r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{k})

\displaystyle\quad~+(u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta}).

Taking norms and applying the bounds leads to

\displaystyle\|e^{k+1}_{u}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|I-\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}R^{-1}B\|\,\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\tau M\|r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{k}\|+\|u^{k+1}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\tau M\delta\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|+\varepsilon\|u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}-u^{k+1}_{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\tau M\delta\|r^{k}-r^{*}\|+\varepsilon\tau\|R_{U}^{-1}B^{\dagger}r^{k}_{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\tau M^{2}\delta\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\varepsilon\tau M\|r^{k}_{\delta}-r^{*}\|\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:I})}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\tau M^{2}\delta\|e^{k}_{u}\|+\varepsilon\tau M^{2}(1+\delta)\|e^{k}_{u}\|\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:II})}.

\implies\|e^{k+1}_{u}\|\leq\big(\gamma+\tau M^{2}\big(\delta+\varepsilon(1+\delta)\big)\big)\|e^{k}_{u}\|.

By assumption,

\delta+\varepsilon(1+\delta)<\frac{1-\gamma}{\tau M^{2}},

which ensures that,
\|e^{k+1}_{u}\|\leq\|e^{k}_{u}\|
. Hence we conclude that
u^{k}_{\delta,\varepsilon}\to u^{*}\in U
, and by (
19
),
r^{k}_{\delta}\to r^{*}\in V
.
∎

In the Uzawa Double Ritz framework described in Algorithm (
2
), each of the two inner
minimization problems (the
r
update and the
u
update) is not exactly solved. Instead, both are approximated using a few steps of a
gradient descent method applied to the corresponding
Ritz energies.

A key observation here is that even when the inner block gradient iterations are very few, or when
the corresponding Ritz energies are far from being fully minimized,
the overall Uzawa iteration still converges. The essential requirement is that each approximate inner update for
r

and
u
moves in the correct descent direction. Under this directional correctness, the Uzawa outer loop remains convergent.

To formalize this idea, we analyze a simplified setting where each inner
Ritz problem is approximated by a single gradient step of the
block method i.e.,

(20)

\begin{cases}1.~r^{k}=r^{k-1}-\alpha\left(r^{k-1}-R_{V}^{-1}(l-Bu^{k})\right)\\
2.~u^{k+1}=u^{k}-\omega\left(u^{k+1}-u^{k}-\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}r^{k}\right)\end{cases}

The resulting iteration captures precisely the
one-step directional information used in practice and provides a
rigorous basis for the observed robustness of the method.

Recall that
U
and
V
be Hilbert spaces with Riesz maps

R_{U}:U\to U^{\ast}
and
R_{V}:V\to V^{\ast}
.
Let
B:U\to V^{\ast}
be a bounded linear operator satisfying the
continuous inf-sup bounds

m\|u\|_{U}\;\leq\;\|Bu\|_{V^{\ast}}\;\leq\;M\|u\|_{U},\qquad u\in U.

The convergence theorem below is established by demonstrating that the error satisfies a contraction property governed by an associated iteration matrix.
The structure of the proof is inspired by classical arguments from matrix iterative analysis, as developed in
[
15
]
and
[

]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the inexact Uzawa Double Ritz iteration in which each Deep Ritz
problem is approximated by a single block gradient step (
20
) and
(r^{\ast},u^{\ast})
be the saddle point solution of (
7
). Then for

0<\alpha<1,\qquad 0<\omega<1,\qquad 0<\tau<\frac{2}{M^{2}}.

the error,

e_{r}^{k}=r^{k}-r^{\ast}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad e_{u}^{k}=u^{k}-u^{\ast}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty

.

Remark
.

In particular, the method converges even though the Ritz energies are
not minimized exactly. The only requirements are that each block
gradient step moves in the correct descent direction and that the Uzawa
stepsize satisfies
0<\tau<2/M^{2}
.

Proof.

Assume that

0<\alpha<1,\qquad 0<\omega<1,\qquad 0<\tau<\frac{2}{M^{2}},

and recall the one–step gradient iteration (
20
).

Consider first the update for
r^{k}
:

r^{k}=r^{k-1}-\alpha\left(r^{k-1}-R_{V}^{-1}(l-Bu^{k})\right).

then,

(21)

e_{r}^{k}=(1-\alpha)e_{r}^{k-1}-\alpha R_{V}^{-1}B\,e_{u}^{k}.

Next, consider the update for
u^{k+1}
:

u^{k+1}=u^{k}-\omega\left(u^{k+1}-u^{k}-\tau R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}r^{k}\right).

Rearranging,

(1+\omega)u^{k+1}=(1+\omega)u^{k}+\tau\omega R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}r^{k},

and hence,

u^{k+1}=u^{k}+\tilde{\tau}R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}r^{k},\qquad\tilde{\tau}:=\frac{\tau\omega}{1+\omega}.

then we get,

e_{u}^{k+1}=e_{u}^{k}+\tilde{\tau}R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}e_{r}^{k}.

Using (
21
), this becomes

e_{u}^{k+1}=e_{u}^{k}+\tilde{\tau}R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}\left((1-\alpha)e_{r}^{k-1}-\alpha R_{V}^{-1}Be_{u}^{k}\right),

(22)

e_{u}^{k+1}=\left(I-\tilde{\tau}\alpha R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}R_{V}^{-1}B\right)e_{u}^{k}+\tilde{\tau}(1-\alpha)R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}e_{r}^{k-1}.

Combining (
21
) and (
22
), we write the coupled error system as

\begin{bmatrix}e_{r}^{k}\\[4.0pt]
e_{u}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\alpha)I_{V}&-\alpha R_{V}^{-1}B\\[4.0pt]
\tilde{\tau}(1-\alpha)R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}&I_{U}-\tilde{\tau}\alpha R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}R_{V}^{-1}B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}e_{r}^{k-1}\\[4.0pt]
e_{u}^{k}\end{bmatrix}.

where
I_{V}
and
I_{U}
denote identity operators on
V
and
U
.

Let

E^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{r}^{k}\\
e_{u}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{A}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\alpha)I_{V}&-\alpha R_{V}^{-1}B\\[4.0pt]
\tilde{\tau}(1-\alpha)R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}&I_{U}-\tilde{\tau}\alpha R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}R_{V}^{-1}B\end{bmatrix},

so that

(23)

E^{k}=\mathbb{A}E^{k-1}.

The goal is to show that the spectral radius is less than unity, i.e.,

\rho(\mathbb{A})<1,

which guarantees that
\mathbb{A}^{k}\to 0
and therefore

E^{k}\to 0,

establishing convergence of the iteration.

Let

G:=R_{U}^{-1}B^{*}R_{V}^{-1}B:U\to U,

which is symmetric positive definite with spectrum contained in
[m^{2},M^{2}]
.

Consider an eigen pair
(v,w)\in V\times U
,

\mathbb{A}\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}v\\
w\end{pmatrix}.

From the first block row,

(24)

(1-\alpha-\lambda)v=\alpha R_{V}^{-1}Bw,\qquad v=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha-\lambda}R_{V}^{-1}Bw,\qquad 1-\alpha-\lambda\neq 0.

Substituting (
24
) into the second block row yields

(1-\alpha-\lambda)(I_{U}-\alpha\tilde{\tau}G-\lambda I_{U})w=-\tilde{\tau}\alpha(1-\alpha)Gw.

Let
w
be an eigenvector of
G
with eigenvalue
\mu\in[m^{2},M^{2}]
where
w\neq 0
.
On substituting this we get the scalar characteristic equation,

(1-\alpha-\lambda)(1-\lambda)+\lambda\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu=0.

Thus the characteristic polynomial is

(25)

p(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-\left(2-\alpha-\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu\right)\lambda+(1-\alpha).

To show that all eigenvalues satisfy
|\lambda|<1
, we use the Schur-Cohn Test in
[
3
]
, which states that a general quadratic polynomial
p(\lambda)
with real coefficients have modulus of both roots(real or complex conjugates) strictly less than 1 iff
p(1)>0,~p(-1)>0
and
|\text{constant term}|<|\text{leading coefficient}|
.

For the characteristic polynomial (
25
) we have,

p(1)=1-(2-\alpha-\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu)+1-\alpha=\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu>0.

p(-1)=1+(2-\alpha-\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu)+1-\alpha=4-2\alpha-\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu.

Since
\mu\leq M^{2}
and
\tilde{\tau}<\frac{2}{M^{2}}
,

-\tilde{\tau}\alpha\mu>-2\alpha,

so

p(-1)>4-2\alpha-2\alpha=4(1-\alpha)>0.

also,
|1-\eta|<1
is true since
\eta\in(0,1)
.

Therefore, for our assumptions we get
|\lambda_{1}|,|\lambda_{2}|<1\implies\rho(\mathbb{A})<1.

Since
\rho(\mathbb{A})<1
, it follows that
\mathbb{A}^{k}\to 0
, and hence by
(
23
),

E^{k}\to 0,\qquad\text{i.e. }e_{r}^{k}\to 0\ \text{and}\ e_{u}^{k}\to 0.

Therefore,

r^{k}\to r^{*},\qquad u^{k}\to u^{*},

which establishes convergence of the iterative scheme.
∎

In the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method, each outer Uzawa iteration involves two
Ritz minimization problems: one associated with the residual update and the other
associated with the solution update. We employ a block-gradient method to approximate each minimizer. This strategy is
analogous to the gradient-block method described in
[
12
]
, where the
outer and inner neural network parameters are updated in an alternating iteration scheme.

To make the parameter structure explicit, we represent the residual approximation

b

\eta=(c,b)

\mathcal{J}_{u_{\theta}}(d,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{\theta}(d,\beta)\|^{2}-\tau b\!\left(u_{\theta}(d,\beta),r_{\eta}^{(k)}\right)-\big(u_{\theta}^{(k)},u_{\theta}(d,\beta)\big)

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we will see how Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method is implemented for 1D transport problem, and we will also discuss its convergence behavior.

We illustrate the implementation and behavior of the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz method on the one-dimensional transport equation

u^{\prime}(x)=f(x),\qquad x\in(0,1),\qquad u(0)=0,

whose one of the weak formulation is:

\text{Find}~u\in L^{2}(0,1):-\int_{0}^{1}u(x)\,v^{\prime}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,v(x)\,dx\qquad\forall v\in H_{0)}^{1}(0,1).

where
H^{1}_{0)}(0,1)=\left\{\,v\in H^{1}(0,1)\;\middle|\;v(1)=0\,\right\}
.

As discussed in Section
2
, this formulation can be written as a minimum residual problem, equivalently as a min-max saddle formulation suitable for an Uzawa iteration. In this setting, the classical Uzawa updates take the form:

•

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r^{(k)\prime}v^{\prime}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}f\,v\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}u^{(k)}v^{\prime}\,dx

•

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u^{(k+1)}w\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}u^{(k)}w\,dx-\tau\int_{0}^{1}w\,r^{(k)\prime}dx
,

for all
v\in H_{0)}^{1}(0,1)
and
w\in L^{2}(0,1)
.

To construct the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz formulation, we approximate
r
and
u
using one hidden layer neural networks for which exact integration and differentiation are employed. In particular, we take

r_{\eta}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(b_{i}-x),\qquad u_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\,H(x-\beta_{i}),

with trainable parameters

\eta=(c_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

and

\theta=(u_{i},\beta_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
This architectural choice mirrors the regularity structure of the underlying variational problem: the ReLU basis yields piecewise linear approximations of
r
, whereas the Heaviside basis produces piecewise constant approximations of
u
, in accordance with the double Ritz characterization of the Uzawa update.

The numerical results consistently indicate that, even though the inner Deep Ritz routines do not compute fully converged solutions, they nevertheless provide descent directions of sufficient fidelity to maintain the stability of the outer Uzawa iteration. This inexactness does not hinder the overall performance; rather, the resulting iterates for the
u
-variable display a clear contractive pattern, in agreement with the theoretical predictions. The observed decay of the associated energies across iterations further substantiates the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed Uzawa Deep Double Ritz framework.

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz Method, a deep PDE solver that unifies Deep Ritz-type energy minimization with the classical Uzawa framework for saddle-point problems. By employing two neural networks to approximate the trial and test variables, the proposed method provides a mesh-free alternative to classical finite element residual-minimization techniques while retaining the structural stability associated with Uzawa iterations.

A key aspect of this work is the analysis of the convergence behavior of the proposed method under inexact inner updates.
We showed that convergence is ensured as long as each approximate update proceeds in the correct descent direction, thereby reflecting the classical stability properties of Uzawa-type iterations.
This result demonstrates that the method remains robust even when the inner problems are solved only approximately, as is typical in neural network–based optimization.

Numerical experiments further demonstrated the stability of the method, the monotone decay of both deep Ritz energies, and the progressive alignment of the neural approximations with the exact solution. These results confirm that the Uzawa Deep Double Ritz Method forms a reliable and effective framework for neural PDE solvers based on dual-norm residual minimization.
