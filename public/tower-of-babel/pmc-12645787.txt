Pitfalls and paradoxes: blood transfusion in 5 public ICU databases
Authors: Merijn C Reuland, Senta Jorinde Raasveld, Dave A Dongelmans, Paul WG Elbers, Marcella C A Müller, Alexander P J Vlaar
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

There is a large variation in the use of red blood cell (RBC) transfusions in critically ill patients [1]. Artificial Intelligence models might aid clinicians in decision-making, however most studies in transfusion medicine have significant drawbacks [2, 3]. The use of multiple datasets has been suggested as a tool to improve robustness and generalizability [4]. We aim to demonstrate that multi-dataset analysis is feasible, however we also found important challenges. We think that our experience of this multi-dataset analysis could be especially relevant for current federated learning initiatives.

We present here a comparison of red blood cell (RBC), plasma, and platelet transfusions across five publicly available ICU datasets: AmsterdamUMCdb, eICU, HiRID, MIMIC-IV, and SICdb. We used a standardized analysis with the ricu package [5].

We used 187 883 ICU stays from 26 hospitals for our analysis. In eICU, 186 hospitals without RBC transfusions were excluded. For comparisons between datasets we used Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s tests with Bonferroni correction. More details are available in the supplemental materials. The median hemoglobin level four hours before the first RBC transfusion ranged from 7.1 g/dL (1st–3rd quartile 6.4–8.1) in the eICU dataset to 8.5 g/dL (1st–3rd quartile 7.7–9.1) in AmsterdamUMCdb. The median hemoglobin level before transfusion was significantly different for each dataset (p<0.001). The percentage of RBC transfusions with hemoglobin above 9 g/dL ranged from 10% (635 / 6055) in HiRID to 35% (2102 / 5996) in AmsterdamUMCdb (p<0.001). The percentage of missing hemoglobin levels ranged from 4.5% (305 / 6720) in SICdb to 53.1% (927 / 1746) in eICU. Sensitivity analysis regarding presumed bleeding vs non-bleeding status did not alter our findings.

The combination of administered blood components during each ICU stay is shown in Fig. 1. The percentage of ICU stays with only RBC transfusions ranged from 57% (4061 / 7114) in AmsterdamUMCdb to 79% (1592 / 2014) in eICU. ICU stays involving RBC, plasma and platelets ranged from 2% (35 / 2014) in eICU to 13% (943 / 7114) in AmsterdamUMCdb. ICU stays with only platelets transfusion ranged from 2% (146 / 7036) in SICdb to 4% (695 / 18 312) in MIMIC-IV.

To evaluate the effect of severity of illness scores we used two models: First, a linear mixed effect model with the dataset as mixed effect on last hemoglobin prior to RBC transfusion. For the second model, we added SOFA-scores to the first model as fixed effects. The first model had an adjusted intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.102. In the second model the adjusted ICC decreased to 0.072. This means that 3 % of the variance can be attributed to patient level characteristics, while persistent dataset-level differences are still present.

In conclusion, this analysis underscores the difficulty of using large public ICU datasets. Essential information to train models on optimal transfusion strategy is lacking. Information that is available suggests that severity of illness scores only partially explain differences between datasets.

Although the differences we identified are substantial, they likely remain modest compared to the challenges posed by federated learning across multiple hospitals. Beyond model performance, meaningful progress requires a deep understanding of dataset structure, clinical context, and institutional protocols.
