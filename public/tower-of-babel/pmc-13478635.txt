Directed trilithiation enables entropically stabilized heavy-atom-centered triangulenes
Authors: Hajime Gotoh, Soichiro Nakatsuka, Keisuke Kinoshita, Jiping Hao, Takuji Hatakeyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmbedding heavy main-group elements at the core of rigid π-conjugated frameworks is an ideal strategy to achieve stability through geometric confinement, yet its realization has remained synthetically elusive. Here we show that a directed, regioselective trilithiation strategy enables controlled incorporation of heavy elements into triangulene frameworks. Using nitrogen-bridged macrocycles as precursors, antimony-, bismuth-, tin-, and lead-centered triangulenes were synthesized. Single-crystal X-ray analyses reveal exceptionally deep bowl-shaped geometries and one-dimensional columnar assemblies. Despite highly strained bonding environments, these compounds exhibit remarkable thermal and chemical stability, highlighting the effectiveness of entropic stabilization arising from the rigid structural confinement system. Moreover, the antimony-centered triangulene undergoes reversible and controllable redox transformation between trivalent and pentavalent states through chelation and coordination effects, demonstrating how topological embedding translates into distinct functional behavior. This work establishes a general synthetic platform for heavy-atom embedding in curved π-systems and opens opportunities for design of unique main-group architectures.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms, Ligands

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) constitute a central platform in organic materials science because their electronic structures can be systematically tailored through molecular design1–5. Beyond extension of the π-framework, the incorporation of heteroatoms has emerged as a powerful strategy to modulate aromaticity, frontier orbital energies, and solid-state organization6–10. While extensive efforts have focused on second- and third-row main-group elements, the integration of heavier elements into π-conjugated backbones remains highly challenging11–21. This limitation arises from the inherent weakness of heavy-element–carbon bonds combined with the increased structural flexibility associated with diffuse valence orbitals, often resulting in low thermal and chemical stability. Despite these synthetic challenges, organoantimony and bismuth compounds remain highly attractive targets due to their unique Lewis acidity and redox properties22–26. Following the pioneering works on anion sensing by Gabbaï et al.27,28 and redox catalysis by Cornella et al. 29,30, various functional systems have been developed. However, most existing examples feature these heavy elements at peripheral positions with exocyclic substituents (Fig. 1a), not because core embedding was conceptually unfavorable, but because long-standing synthetic constraints have prevented their incorporation into rigid polycyclic frameworks22–52.

Design concept and synthetic strategy for heavy-atom-centered triangulenes.

To overcome this instability, imposing geometric confinement and topological restriction on heavy elements has long been recognized as a fundamental and effective design principle for achieving stability. Fixing a heavy atom at the core of a rigid, symmetric polycyclic scaffold is expected to reduce conformational entropy and suppress lone-pair-driven reactivity, thereby stabilizing otherwise labile heavy element–carbon bonds (Fig. 1b). Triangulenes53–61, characterized by their C3 symmetry and inherently curved frameworks, provide an ideal platform to implement this long-standing design concept. However, despite its conceptual clarity, direct incorporation of heavy atoms at the center of such strained π-systems has remained synthetically elusive, primarily due to the lack of methods that enable regioselective, multivalent functionalization without compromising framework integrity.

Accordingly, the central challenge lies in developing a synthetic strategy that enables controlled, simultaneous formation of multiple carbon–metal bonds at predefined positions within a rigid framework. In the case of triangulenes, installation of a multivalent heavy element at the core requires precise orchestration of several metalation events, a transformation that is difficult to achieve using conventional cross-coupling or stepwise substitution strategies. We reasoned that a directed lithiation approach could overcome this limitation by exploiting an initially generated aryllithium species as an internal directing group to orchestrate successive, regioselective deprotonation events within a rigid macrocyclic framework (Fig. 1c). On this basis, nitrogen-bridged macrocycles bearing a strategically positioned halogen substituent were identified as suitable platforms to enable controlled formation of a well-defined trilithiated intermediate for subsequent heavy-element incorporation.

In this work, we report a general and conceptually simple strategy for the synthesis of heavy-atom-centered triangulenes based on directed, regioselective trilithiation of nitrogen-bridged macrocycles. Using this approach, antimony- and bismuth-centered triangulenes (PnN3) were synthesized, representing the incorporation of heavy pnictogens (Sb and Bi) embedded at the core of a triangulene framework, inspired by the pioneering reports of the lighter phosphorus- and arsenic-centered analogs55,58. Furthermore, to demonstrate the broad utility of our synthetic strategy, the scope was extended beyond group 15 to include tin- and lead-centered analogs (group 14). Single-crystal X-ray analyses reveal that these molecules adopt exceptionally deep bowl-shaped geometries and form one-dimensional columnar assemblies in the solid state. Despite the presence of highly strained C–E–C angles, these compounds exhibit remarkable thermal and chemical stability, which can be rationalized by entropic stabilization arising from rigid structural confinement of the heavy atom. Moreover, the antimony-centered triangulene undergoes a reversible and controllable redox transformation between the trivalent and pentavalent states through chelation and coordination effects, demonstrating how realization of a long-standing design principle can translate into distinct redox behavior in heavy main-group systems. Together, these results establish a viable synthetic route to heavy-atom embedding in curved π-systems and highlight heavy-atom-centered triangulenes as a new class of structurally and functionally unique molecular architectures.

Results

Synthesis of heavy-atom-embedded triangulenes via directed regioselective lithiation

After intensive explorations, we established a one-pot synthetic route to PnN3 consisting of the regioselective trilithiation of a nitrogen-containing macrocycle 1-Cl58 and successive electrophilic substitution using antimony or bismuth trichloride (PnCl3). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that aryllithium produced by the lithium–chlorine (Li/Cl) exchange reaction acts as a directing group to assist the subsequent double deprotonation. The single crystal X-ray structure of the resulting PnN3 presented a deep bowl structure, and the packing structures of the PnN3 were found to be columnar structures along the b-axis. Despite the large curvature, PnN3 exhibited excellent thermal and chemical stabilities due to entropic stabilization. Exceptionally, SbN3 could be oxidized by o-chloranil to form the corresponding pentavalent complex SbVN3(C6Cl4O2). The oxidation was reversible and could be controlled by coordinative additives, such as ethyl acetate and acetonitrile, demonstrating the potential applications of these species in areas such as chemosensing and catalysis.

Figure 2a summarizes the optimization conditions for the regioselective trilithiation of 1-Cl, whereby deuteration was employed to determine the lithiated position based on GC-MS analysis. In the presence of 4.0 equivalents of t-BuLi, 1-Cl underwent lithiation at 30 °C to generate an aryl lithium intermediate, which was subsequently treated with CD3OD to afford 1-D1 (74.4%) as the major product, in addition to 1-D2 (1.7%), 1-D3 (6.3%), and other compounds (1-D4, 1-D5, 1-D6, and 1-H) as the minor products, indicating that a selective Li/Cl exchange reaction took place (entry 1). Notably, trilithiation occurred smoothly at 50 °C to afford 1-D3 (57.2%) as a major product (entry 2). Increasing the heating time to 1 h decreased the ratio of 1-D3 (17.1%) (entry 3), indicating that 1-D3 is unstable at 50 °C. Treatment with 2.0 equivalents of t-BuLi resulted in a selective Li/Cl exchange reaction (entry 4).

Notably, deuterated compounds bearing intact chloride substituents (1-Cl-D1 and 1-Cl-D2) were entirely absent in the GC-MS analysis at any stage of the reaction. This unambiguously indicates that the initial Li/Cl exchange is a strict prerequisite step. Without this initial exchange, attempting direct deprotonation results in a loss of regioselectivity and complex mixtures. This demanding sequence dictates the specific reaction conditions: the 4.0 equivalents of t-BuLi are exactly stoichiometric (two for the irreversible Li/Cl exchange and two for the subsequent double deprotonation), and the elevated temperature is required to overcome the kinetic barrier of the congested internal deprotonations.

Furthermore, to confirm the specific sites of deprotonation, upon comparison of the 1H NMR signals of 1-H and the reaction mixture of entry 2, the integrated value of the hydrogen (HF) atom at the central position decreased from 9.00 to 6.43 (Fig. 2b). Concomitantly, the double doublet signals corresponding to the hydrogen (HA) atom at the ortho position (i.e., at 6.83 ppm) formed a doublet signal at 6.85 ppm. These results suggest that the double deprotonation occurred predominantly at the central position to produce the trilithiated intermediate, 1-Li3. Based on these overall observations, we assumed that the aryllithium intermediate produced from the Li/Cl exchange reaction can act as an essential directing group, associating with the remaining t-BuLi in the nonpolar solvent to facilitate the regioselective deprotonation of the adjacent internal protons.

To support our assumption, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of theory for single reaction pathways from monolithiated azacyclophane 1-Li to dilithiated azacyclophane 1-Li2. These calculations were performed in the gas phase to model the experimental conditions in tert-butylbenzene, which acts as a non-coordinating, bulky thermal medium. The activation energy profiles of TS(1–5) required for the deprotonation of 1-Li are summarized in Fig. 3a. The initial process involves complexation between 1-Li and t-BuLi to form dilithium complex 1-Li·t-BuLi (−32.0 kcal mol−1), in which the incoming t-BuLi is coordinated with the ipso-carbon atom in the aryllithium plane (Supplementary Fig. 20). Subsequently, the deprotonation of hydrogen (H1) takes place via TS1 with an activation energy of +25.8 kcal mol−1. The deprotonation pathways of hydrogen at other positions (H2–5) involving TS(2–5) were also located, and these required significantly higher activation energies (∆G = +43.0, +43.5, +55.2, and +56.1 kcal mol−1, respectively). Notably, the pathways for the deprotonation of H4 and H5 involve isomerization from 1-Li·t-BuLi to INT1 and INT2, respectively. These intermediates (INT1 and INT2) represent isomerized structures of 1-Li·t-BuLi, in which N-phenyl rotation or t-BuLi migration brings H4 and H5, respectively, into close proximity to the t-BuLi unit to facilitate deprotonation (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). The reaction pathway for the deprotonation of the hydrogen located in the para-position of the nitrogen atom (H6) was not determined, likely due to its high activation energy. To account for the large differences in the activation energies, the most feasible pathway via TS1 and the second-most feasible pathway via TS2 are shown in Fig. 3b (Supplementary Figs. 23–25 for TS(3–5)). In the pathway for the conversion of 1-Li·t-BuLi to TS1, C1 and H1 move closer to each other, becoming a formal interaction to promote deprotonation (C1–H1: 1.51 Å, C2–H1: 1.45 Å). The four-membered ring structure of C1–Li–C3–Li1 was retained in TS1, in which the bond distances are comparable to those in 1-Li·t-BuLi (C1–Li: 2.13 to 2.25 Å, C1–Li1: 2.14 to 2.37 Å, C3–Li: 2.18 to 2.12 Å, and C3–Li1: 2.19 to 2.12 Å). The limited structural change occurring upon transformation from 1-Li·t-BuLi to TS1 led to TS1 being relatively stable (+25.8 kcal mol−1), thereby accounting for the regioselective deprotonation of H1. In addition, the pathway involved in the transformation from 1-Li·t-BuLi to TS2 was found to include an analogous interaction to the deprotonation of TS1, as evidenced by the C1–H2 (1.52 Å) and C4–H2 (1.39 Å) bond distances. However, the C1–Li bond (3.77 Å) in TS2 is considerably lengthened compared to that in 1-Li·t-BuLi (2.19 Å), thereby indicating that the pathway toward TS2 involves the cleavage of the C1–Li bond, resulting in a significantly higher energy (+43.0 kcal mol−1). In the pathway involved in the transformation from TS1 to 1-Li2(C2,C3), C2–Li1 bond formation (2.21–2.10 Å) took place while maintaining the C3–Li1 bond, resulting in an exothermic process (−5.4 kcal mol−1). In contrast, in the pathway involved in the transformation from TS2 to 1-Li2(C3,C4), C4–Li2 bond formation (2.60– 2.03 Å) and C3–Li2 bond cleavage (2.22–4.59 Å) occurred in a concerted manner. Although the π-orbital of the benzene ring containing C4 was coordinated to the Li atom (1.98 Å), significant deformation of the macrocyclic structure resulted in an endothermic process (+1.7 kcal mol−1). Although t-BuLi typically exists as a tetramer in apolar solvents, our attempts to locate a transition state involving a tetrameric cluster revealed that the steric bulk of the N-phenyl groups forces the aggregate to dissociate. This suggests that the reactive species facilitating the directed deprotonation is likely a lower-order aggregate, such as the dimer (1-Li·t-BuLi) modeled herein, which strikes a balance between essential lithium coordination and steric accommodation. While the calculated structure of 1-Li2(C2,C3) features a bridging Li atom, organolithium aggregates in solution typically exhibit highly dynamic behavior. We hypothesize that this bridging Li1 coordinates dynamically with the surrounding solvent or the incoming t-BuLi aggregate, facilitating structural reorganization to an accessible transition state for the third deprotonation at the adjacent site. Although the pathway from 1-Li2 to 1-Li3 was not explicitly calculated, these results suggest that aryllithium can act as an efficient directing group in the regioselective lithiation process, which is in good agreement with the experimental results.

We succeeded in the incorporation of antimony and bismuth atoms into 1-Cl via a one-pot route (Fig. 4a). As described above, the initial Li–Cl exchange reaction of 1-Cl and the subsequent regioselective double lithiation of 1-Li proceeded in tert-butylbenzene at 45 °C over 2 h to give intermediate 1-Li3. In the presence of 4.0 equivalents of PnCl3, the electrophilic substitution of 1-Li3 with PnCl3 took place at room temperature to give the desired SbN3 and BiN3 in 47 and 41% yields, respectively. Note that the synthesis of the known arsenic analog55 was intentionally avoided in this study due to the severe toxicity of arsenic precursors.

The remarkably deep bowl-shaped structures of the prepared PnN3 compounds were confirmed by X-ray crystallography (Fig. 4b). Interestingly, the bowl depth (d) of SbN3 (3.36 Å) and BiN3 (3.54 Å) significantly exceeded those of other heteroatom-centered triangulenes (1.44–2.43 Å)53–61 and buckybowls (corannulene; 0.87 Å, sumanene; 1.11 Å)62–64. This dramatic bowl depth can be rationalized by a combination of the fused macrocyclic nature of the triangulene framework and orbital characteristics. The azacyclophane-like framework imposes a strict geometric constraint on the peripheral perimeter. This non-expandable boundary leads to the inherent mismatch with the long Sb–C bond lengths (2.135(4), 2.135(4), and 2.135(4) Å) and Bi–C bond lengths (2.228(6), 2.230(6), and 2.242(6) Å). These bond lengths were similar to those of typical triaryl pnictogen compounds PnAr3 (SbAr3: 2.14–2.23 Å65–69 and BiAr3: 2.25–2.32 Å70–74). To accommodate these long bonds within the fixed peripheral space, the central pnictogen atom must protrude significantly from the plane as a strain-release mechanism. This extreme pyramidalization is further facilitated by the high p-character of the valence orbitals in heavy pnictogens. While typical PnAr3 exhibit bond angles of C–Sb–C (92.5–109.9°) and C–Bi–C (92.0–102.5°), the structural demand of the PnN3 framework forces these angles down to C–Sb–C (83.9, 84.1, and 84.8°) for SbN3 and C–Bi–C (80.6, 80.8, and 81.4°) for BiN3. As a result, the compounds exhibit remarkably sharp bowl cone angles (θ) of 93.3° for SbN3 and 89.5° for BiN3. The energetic penalty for such angle contraction is relatively low for heavy elements like Sb and Bi, whose bonding primarily involves p-orbitals with minimal sp mixing. Consequently, the framework’s geometric demand for a deep bowl shape is uniquely compatible with the electronic preference of the heavy pnictogen center, resulting in triangulene derivatives with an exceptionally deep bowl-shaped geometry.

Moreover, the phenyl group on the nitrogen atom of PnN3 was horizontal to the triangulene skeleton to conjugate with the lone pair of electrons of nitrogen; this result is in good agreement with the structure of PnN3 suggested by DFT calculations performed at the M06/cc-pVTZ-pp (Sb, Bi) and 6-31G(d) (C, H, N) levels of theory (denoted as M06/cc-pVTZ-pp:6-31G(d)) (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17).

Stability arising from geometric confinement and columnar assembly

The prepared PnN3 exhibited unique columnar assembly and exceptional thermal stability (Fig. 5), directly resulting from their rigid, bowl-shaped geometries. From the side view, it is apparent that the PnN3 molecules formed columnar structures along the b axis, in addition to a one-dimensional (1D) structure in which the directional alignments of the neighboring columns were diametrically opposite. In terms of the Sb–π interaction, the benzene ring–Sb distances were 3.53–3.63 Å, while for the Bi–π interaction, the benzene ring–Bi distances were 3.54–3.56 Å, which constitute shorter distances than the sum of the van der Waals radii of Sb, Bi, and carbon (i.e., Sb + C: 2.20 Å + 1.85 Å = 4.05 Å, Bi + C: 2.40 Å + 1.85 Å = 4.25 Å)75. Moreover, from the top view, the 1D structures of the neighboring columns formed CH–π interactions, with CH–benzene ring distances of 2.76 Å. It was therefore assumed that the molecular arrangements of PnN3 are predominantly governed by the Pn–π and CH–π interactions between the adjacent molecules. The 1D self-assembly will pave the way for the widespread application of PnN3 in liquid crystals76–79 and organic semiconductors80–82.

To evaluate whether these supramolecular interactions persist in the solution state, concentration-dependent 1H NMR experiments were performed up to their solubility limits. For SbN3, the concentration was varied from 0.5 to 16.7 mM in CDCl3, while for BiN3, the range was extended from 0.5 to 8.3 mM in C2D2Cl4. Despite these concentration increases, the proton signals remained sharp and well-resolved, with no observable changes in chemical shifts or line broadening (Supplementary Figs. 74 and 75). These results indicate that the intermolecular interactions observed in the solid state are effectively disrupted by solvation, and both compounds exist primarily as discrete monomers in solution within the studied concentration range.

The prepared PnN3 exhibited high thermal and chemical stabilities, despite their sharp C–Pn–C angles. More specifically, during TG–DTA analysis, no weight loss was observed up to 350 °C; in contrast, triphenylbismuth and triphenylantimony (PnPh3) showed weight losses at 200 °C (Fig. 5). This can be accounted for by considering that Pn was fixed at the center of the polycyclic skeleton, thereby entropically stabilizing PnN3. Notably, PnN3 exhibited substantial stability when mixed with 1 N HCl or 1 N NaOH in dichloromethane, as confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. However, under more strongly acidic conditions (12 N HCl), SbN3 underwent decomposition within 0.5 h, indicating a relative susceptibility to highly acidic environments (Supplementary Fig. 73). This difference in acid stability is also reflected in their synthetic conditions; the synthesis of the acid-sensitive SbN3 required the addition of DIPEA as an acid scavenger, whereas BiN3 could be synthesized without a base.

Reversible redox interconversion of the antimony center

We further investigated how the geometrical confinement of the pnictogen center of the PnN3 imparts unique redox properties, distinct from conventional organopnictogen compounds against oxidants, such as m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid (mCPBA), iodine (I2), iodobenzene dichloride (PhICl2), and o-chloranil (Table 1). While SbPh3 is readily oxidized to pentavalent species by a range of oxidants83,84, SbN3 showed no reaction with mCPBA, I2, or PhICl2. However, the oxidation reaction of SbN3 proceeded when o-chloranil was employed, suggesting that the resulting pentavalent antimony is stabilized by the electron-withdrawing nature and chelate coordination of the catecholate ligand. Similar oxidation resistance was observed for BiN3, whereas BiPh3 was oxidized to a pentavalent species by PhICl2, and was decomposed by mCPBA85 or I286. DFT calculations at the M06/cc-pVTZ-pp:6-31G(d) level of theory clarified that the stabilization energies for the oxidation of PnN3 were lower than those of the corresponding PnPh3 (Supplementary Table 7). The calculated difference in oxidation energies indicates that PnN3 is significantly more stable toward oxidation than PnPh3, with stabilization energy differences of 11.0–18.0 kcal mol−1 for Sb and 12.2–29.3 kcal mol−1 for Bi.

Furthermore, this difference in oxidation resistance can be rationalized by considering the nature of the lone-pair (LP) orbital and the molecular orbital energies. The natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis (Table 1) reveals that the rigid triangulene framework increases the s-character of the pnictogen lone pair by sharpening the bond angles around the Pn center. For instance, the s-character increases from 70.6% (SbPh3) to 73.9% (SbN3), and from 80.4% (BiPh3) to 82.7% (BiN3), respectively. This increased s-contribution significantly stabilizes the pnictogen LP-based orbitals by shifting them to deeper energy levels: from SbPh3 [−6.24 (HOMO) and −7.24 (HOMO-4) eV] to SbN3 [−6.87 (HOMO-2) and −8.03 (HOMO-8) eV], and from BiPh3 [−6.38 (HOMO) and −7.20 (HOMO-4) eV] to BiN3 [−7.08 (HOMO-6) and −7.92 (HOMO-8) eV] (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). Importantly, this constrained geometry of the PnN3 framework, enforced by the nitrogen bridges, facilitates effective conjugation of the pnictogen lone pair with the surrounding π-system of the three aromatic rings. This effective delocalization, coupled with the stabilization of the LP-based orbitals to deeper energy levels, synergistically enhances the oxidative stability by shielding the stabilized lone pair from electrophilic attack.

As mentioned above, we found that SbN3 was reversibly oxidized using a bidentate oxidant, such as o-chloranil. According to the variable-temperature 1H NMR analysis (Fig. 6a), the approximate SbN3:SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) integral ratios were 1.9:6.0 (298.15 K), 2.8:6.0 (313.15 K), 3.5:6.0 (323.15 K), 4.8:6.0 (333.15 K), and 6.2:6.0 (343.15 K). In this case, the concentrations of SbN3, o-chloranil, and SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) were calculated to be 2.5 mM in C6D6, where the equilibrium constants of this reaction were 27.1 mM−1 (298.15 K), 13.8 mM−1 (313.15 K), 9.2 mM−1 (323.15 K), 5.7 mM−1 (333.15 K), and 3.8 mM−1 (343.15 K) (Fig. 6b). The enthalpy (ΔH) and entropy (ΔS) changes were determined to be −8.9 kcal mol−1 and −13.9 cal K−1 mol−1, respectively, based on the van’t Hoff equation. The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) was evaluated as −4.1 kcal mol−1 at 343.15 K, where the equilibrium was most clearly monitored for accurate 1H NMR integration (Supplementary Table 2 for complete data at other temperatures).

Interestingly, the equilibrium can be controlled by the addition of a coordinating molecule, such as acetonitrile or ethyl acetate; the ratio of pentavalent SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) was increased from 76 to 96% in both cases (Fig. 7). The coordination energies of acetonitrile and ethyl acetate to SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) were determined by DFT calculations to be −3.2 kcal mol−1 for both. These nearly identical values effectively account for the observed equilibrium shifts. Notably, the coordination energies of acetonitrile and ethyl acetate to SbVPh3(C6Cl4O2) were only +2.5 and +1.3 kcal mol−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 8). This difference highlights the significantly higher Lewis acidity of SbVN3 compared to SbVPh3. These results indicate that the redox system between SbN3 and SbVN3 could be applicable in the fields of chemosensing or catalysis.

We therefore succeeded in determining the hexacoordinated structure in which ethyl acetate is coordinated to SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) by single crystal X-ray structural analysis (Fig. 8g, h). The Sb–C bond lengths (2.067(7), 2.083(6), and 2.092(6) Å) exhibit a significant contraction compared to those of the trivalent SbN3 (2.135(4) Å) and typical SbAr3 compounds (2.14–2.23 Å)65–69, which is consistent with the increased oxidation state of the antimony center. Furthermore, while the C–Sb–C bond angles (89.6, 89.7, and 90.1°) are slightly wider than those of the trivalent SbN3 (83.9, 84.1, and 84.8°), they remain more acute than the values reported for typical SbAr3 (92.5–109.9°)65–69, indicating that the antimony center is subject to strict geometric constraints of the rigid triangulene framework even in the hexacoordinated state. Therefore, the bowl depth (d) was equal to 3.16 Å. The Sb–O3 distance of SbVN3(C6Cl4O2)(AcOEt) (2.323(5) Å) was well within the sum of the van der Waals radii (Sb + O: 2.20 Å + 1.40 Å = 3.60 Å) of the two elements, which is consistent with the presence of a secondary interaction.

General versatility of the directed trilithiation strategy

To demonstrate the versatility of the present methodology, we attempted the incorporation of additional heteroatoms (Sn and Pb) into the macrocycle at the center (Fig. 9a). In the presence of 4.0 equivalents of Bu-SnCl3, the electrophilic substitution of 1-Li3 with Bu-SnCl3 took place at room temperature to result in an 18% yield of the desired SnN3. Moreover, in the presence of 4.0 equivalents of PbBr2, the electrophilic substitution of 1-Li3 with PbBr2 resulted in the formation of the intermediate 1-Pb, which was subsequently treated with methyl iodide to result in a 10% yield of PbN3. These results demonstrate the introduction of Sn or Pb into the fused ring of a polycyclic aromatic compound. We expect that the method using divalent electrophiles will provide a hetero-triangulene having a variety of substituents, such as alkyl and aryl groups on the heteroatom87,88.

The bowl-shaped structures of the prepared SnN3 and PbN3 compounds were confirmed by X-ray crystallography (Fig. 9b). The Sn–C bond lengths (2.112(5), 2.115(5), and 2.129(5) Å) and Pb–C bond lengths (2.179(12), 2.182(12), and 2.196(12) Å) were similar to those of typical triaryl compounds R-SnAr3 and R-PbAr3 (R = aryl, alkyl; R-SnAr3: 2.13–2.15 Å, R-PbAr3: 2.19–2.23 Å)89–92. However, the C–Sn–C bond angles (89.1, 89.2, and 89.2°) and the C–Pb–C bond angles (85.8, 87.0, and 87.8°) were smaller than those in R-SnAr3 (106.6–109.5°) and R-PbAr3 (106.6–110.3°), indicating that SnN3 and PbN3 adopt highly distorted geometries comparable to those observed in PnN3. The bowl depth (d) for SnN3 was equal to 3.30 Å and the bowl cone angle (θ) was 94.2°. Meanwhile, for PbN3, the bowl depth (d) was equal to 3.52 Å and the bowl cone angle (θ) was 88.7°. This indicates that both the compounds had similar bowl depths, with the corresponding elements in the same period (SbN3: d = 3.36 Å, θ = 93.3°; BiN3: d = 3.54 Å, θ = 89.5°). This methodology represents a significant leap in heavy-element chemistry, facilitating the synthesis of triangulene derivatives that were hitherto considered inaccessible. Beyond pnictogens and group 14 elements, this versatile synthetic manifold paves the way for the strategic integration of a broad library of heavy atoms, such as transition metals and lanthanoids, into extended π-conjugated systems.

Discussion

In summary, we have established a general synthetic strategy that enables the realization of heavy-atom-centered triangulenes through directed trilithiation. This approach allows heavy main-group elements to be embedded at the core of rigid polycyclic aromatic frameworks, affording antimony- and bismuth-centered triangulenes with the heavy pnictogens (Sb and Bi) fixed at the center of the triangulene scaffold. Structural analyses revealed exceptionally deep bowl-shaped geometries and one-dimensional columnar assemblies in the solid state. Despite the presence of highly strained bonding environments, these compounds exhibit remarkable thermal and chemical stability, highlighting the effectiveness of entropic stabilization arising from geometric confinement within the polycyclic framework. Furthermore, the antimony-centered triangulene displays a reversible and controllable redox transformation between trivalent and pentavalent states, demonstrating how topological embedding of heavy elements can translate into distinct functional behavior. The applicability of this strategy was further demonstrated by extension to tin- and lead-centered triangulenes, underscoring the generality of the method. We anticipate that this synthetic platform will facilitate the incorporation of a wide range of heavy elements into curved π-systems and stimulate further exploration of their structural and functional properties.

Methods

Materials

Materials were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd. (Wako), Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Aldrich Inc., and other commercial suppliers, and were used after appropriate purification, unless otherwise noted. Florisil (100–200 mesh) was purchased from Kanto Chemical Co., Inc. (Kanto).

Solvent

Anhydrous solvents were purchased from the above-described suppliers and/or dried over molecular sieves 4 A and degassed before use. The water content of the solvent was determined with a Karl Fischer moisture titrator (AQ-2200, Hiranuma Sangyo Co., Ltd.) to be less than 20 ppm.

Computational methods

All calculations were performed with the Gaussian 09 packages unless otherwise noted. The DFT method was employed using the M06 hybrid functional. Structure optimization and the time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculation were conducted with the cc-pVTZ-pp basis set for Sb/Bi/I, and 6-31G(d) basis set for the other atom types (denoted as M06/cc-pVTZ-pp:6-31G(d)). NICS values were evaluated by using the gauge invariant atomic orbital (GIAO) approach at the GIAO-M06/cc-pVTZ-pp:6-31G(d)//M06/cc-pVTZ-pp:6-31G(d) level. Mechanistic studies on the regioselective lithiation were conducted using the B3LYP hybrid functional with the 6-31G(d) basis set (denoted as B3LYP/6-31G(d)). Each stationary point was adequately characterized by normal coordinate analysis (no imaginary frequencies for an equilibrium structure and one imaginary frequency for a transition structure) using the same method as for the geometry optimization. Intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) analyses were carried out throughout the reaction pathways to confirm that all stationary points are smoothly connected to each other using the same method as for the geometry optimization.

The isolated compound was further purified by washing with hexane and acetonitrile to obtain the title compound (10.8 mg, 10% yield, 97% pure on 1H NMR analysis) as a white solid.

Preparation of single crystals suited for X-ray diffraction analysis

SbN3

A MeOH/tetrachloroethane (1.0 mL, v/v = 1/1) solution and MeOH (2.0 mL) were successively layered on top of a tetrachloroethane solution (2.0 mL) of SbN3 (6.20 mg, 10 μmol) in a straight glass tube. The resulting solution was allowed to diffuse slowly at −10 °C. About 1 month later, colorless capillary crystals of the title compound suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained.

BiN3

A MeOH/tetrachloroethane (1.0 mL, v/v = 1/1) solution and MeOH (2.0 mL) were successively layered on top of a tetrachloroethane solution (2.0 mL) of BiN3 (7.08 mg, 10 μmol) in a straight glass tube. The resulting solution was allowed to diffuse slowly at room temperature. About 1 month later, colorless capillary crystals of the title compound suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained.

SbVN3(C6Cl4O2)

After adding SbVN3(C6Cl4O2) (8.65 mg, 10 μmol), dichloromethane (1.0 mL) and AcOEt (0.1 mL) solution in a vial, the solvent was slowly evaporated. One day later, yellow chip-like crystals of the title compound suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained.

SnN3

After adding SnN3 (6.74 mg, 10 μmol), dichloromethane (1.0 mL) solution in a vial, the solvent was slowly evaporated. One day later, colorless chip-like crystals of the title compound suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained.

PbN3

After adding PbN3 (7.21 mg, 10 μmol), dichloromethane (1.0 mL) solution in a vial, the solvent was slowly evaporated. One day later, colorless chip-like crystals of the title compound suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained.

Chemical and thermal stability measurements

Stability of PnN3 under acidic and basic conditions

Acidic and basic conditions: 1 N HCl (2.0 mL) or 1 N NaOH (2.0 mL) was added slowly to a solution of SbN3 or BiN3 (6.20 or 7.07 mg, 10 μmol) in dichloromethane (2.0 mL) at room temperature. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the organic layer was separated. After the solvent was removed in vacuo, 1H NMR spectra were measured in CDCl3 at room temperature. Additionally, SbN3 (6.20 mg, 10 μmol) was treated with 12 N HCl (2.0 mL) in CH2Cl2 (2.0 mL) for 0.5 h at room temperature, which resulted in decomposition of the SbN3 framework as confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 73).

Reactivity of PnN3 with mCPBA, I2, and PhICl2

To a solution of SbN3 or BiN3 (31.0 or 35.4 mg, 50 μmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) was added slowly a solution of an oxidant (mCPBA: 11.2 mg, 65 μmol; I2: 12.7 mg, 50 μmol; or PhICl2: 13.7 mg, 50 μmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) at room temperature. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the solvent was removed in vacuo. The residue was washed with hexane, and 1H NMR spectra were measured in CDCl3 at room temperature, which revealed that both SbN3 and BiN3 remained completely intact, with no evidence of oxidation or decomposition.

Reactivity of BiN3 with o-chloranil

The reactivity of BiN3 with o-chloranil (14.6 mg, 60 μmol) was investigated under the same conditions as described for the preparation of SbVN3(C6Cl4O2). While SbN3 efficiently afforded the pentavalent species as detailed in that section, BiN3 showed no reaction with o-chloranil and was recovered quantitatively.

Reactivity of PnPh3 with mCPBA, PhICl2, and o-chloranil

mCPBA: to a solution of SbPh3 (70.6 mg, 0.20 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) was added slowly a solution of mCPBA (44.9 mg, 0.26 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) at room temperature. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the solvent was removed in vacuo, and 1H NMR analysis of the crude mixture in CDCl3 revealed the formation of a complex reaction mixture along with residual solvent (Supplementary Fig. 63).

PhICl2: to a solution of SbPh3 or BiPh3 (70.6 or 88.1 mg, 0.20 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) was added slowly a solution of PhICl2 (55.0 mg, 0.20 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) at room temperature. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the solvent was removed in vacuo, the crude product was washed with hexane to obtain the SbVPh3(Cl2) or BiVPh3(Cl2) (66.1 mg, 78% yield or 74.6 mg, 73% yield, >99% pure on 1H NMR analysis) as a white solid (Supplementary Figs. 64 and 65).

o-chloranil: to a solution of BiPh3 (88.1 mg, 0.20 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) was added slowly a solution of o-chloranil (59.0 mg, 0.24 mmol) in dichloromethane (1.0 mL) at room temperature. After stirring at room temperature for 1 h, the solvent was removed in vacuo. The residue was washed with hexane, and 1H NMR spectrum was measured in CDCl3 at room temperature, which showed only the starting materials. Other entries (Table 1) for PnPh3 (BiPh3 with mCPBA, PnPh3 with I2, and SbPh3 with o-chloranil) are cited from previous reports83–86 as comparative standards. While SbPh3 was reported to be oxidized by o-chloranil to yield the corresponding adduct (SbVPh3(C6Cl4O2)), the other reactions resulted in C–Pn bond cleavage, leading to a complex mixture.
