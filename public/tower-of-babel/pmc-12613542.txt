Practice of essential newborn care and associated factors among obstetric care providers in rural health centers of Hadiya zone, central Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aleka Mekonnen Tellore, Belayneh Hamdela Jena, Selamawit Getachew Lemma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEssential newborn care (ENBC) practice is an ideal strategy to improve the health status of newborns and avert neonatal deaths. Usually, ENBC is given by health professionals attending labor, delivery, and postnatal care. However, evidence about the level of practice and contributing factors among health professionals working at rural health centers is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the practice of ENBC and associated factors among obstetric care providers in rural health centers of the Hadiya zone, Central Ethiopia.MethodsA health facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 301 health professionals who were working at rural health centers in the Hadiya zone. A self-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The study participants who scored above the median value for the aggregate of 12-item ENBC practices recommended by the World Health Organization were reported as having good practices. A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to identify factors contributing to the good practice, using an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval.ResultsOf the 301 health professionals, 142 [47.2%] of them demonstrated a good practice of ENBC, with a 95% CI [41.1, 53.0%]. Health professionals who have a degree and above level of education [AOR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.11, 5.46], five or more years of work experience [AOR = 5.12, 95% CI: 2.53, 10.35], an interest to work in labor, delivery, and postnatal care units [AOR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.13, 4.88], training on ENBC [AOR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.17, 4.99], and are midwifery professionals [AOR = 5.0, 95% CI: 2.13, 11.11] were significant factors contributing to the good practice of ENBC.ConclusionsNearly half of health professionals working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of rural health centers demonstrated a good practice of ENBC. The finding suggests a still ample number of newborns were not receiving essential care that they need after birth, which may challenge achieving the sustainable development goal set to reduce neonatal mortality. Hence, health managers and stakeholders should strengthen capacity-building trainings and educational opportunities for lower-level obstetric care providers and increase midwifery professionals in the rural health centers.Keywords: Practice, Essential newborn care, Newborn care, Newborn, Hadiya zone, Ethiopia

Background

Essential newborn care (ENBC) practice refers, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), to the care given to all newborns immediately after birth beginning with the delivery of a baby followed by putting the newborn on the mother’s abdomen, drying the infant with a clean towel, assessing breathing while drying, covering with a dry and warm clean towel, clamping and cutting the umbilical cord when pulsation stops, initiating breastfeeding within one hour, eye care (applying Tetracycline eye ointment), immunization (giving recommended vaccines at birth), applying 4% chlorhexidine gel on the cord, administering vitamin K, identifying bands for newborn, weighing and recording newborn’s overall condition at 1 st and 5th minutes after birth using the Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity and Respiration (APGAR) score [1]. ENBC services are usually given by health professionals who attend labor and delivery and give postnatal care. However, the practice of ENBC is largely overlooked even by obstetric care providers working in hospitals where access to ENBC facilities better exists and is not properly implemented [2–4]. Proper implementation of the ENBC is an ideal strategy to improve the health status of newborns and avert neonatal deaths, as most neonatal deaths occur during the first week of life (early neonatal deaths), which contributes to 75% of the overall neonatal mortality [5, 6].

Globally, an estimated 2.3 million children died during the first 28 days of life, or about 18 deaths per 1000 live births, and neonatal death contributes to 47% of the under-5 deaths. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) continues to face the greatest risk of neonatal deaths, as the region accounts for 27 deaths per 1000 live births for the overall global neonatal mortality rate. Central and southern Asia account for the second-largest burden, with 23 deaths per 1000 live births, next to SSA [5, 7]. With 33 per 1000 live births in 2019, Ethiopia’s neonatal mortality rate is still a concern; it did not decline but rather increased from 2016, which was 29 per 1000 live births [8].

Neonatal mortalities are attributed to a lack of quality care at birth, such as a lack of skilled birth attendants and a lack of immediate care for newborns in the first days of life [9]. Demonstrating ENBC as recommended by the WHO may have the potential to avert neonatal mortality, as 70% of neonatal deaths are preventable [1]. However, the good practice of the ENBC ranges from 56.6% in Sidama region public hospitals [3] to 74.8% in south Gondar health facilities [2] and varies from setting to setting by different factors such as type of profession, type and place of health facility, getting training, workloads, level of education, work interest, and availability of facilities necessary to carry out the ENBC. Moreover, the measurement of the level of practice of ENBC (good or poor) varies across studies. In some studies, a mean or median score was used to define good or poor practice, while others used fulfilling all ENBC items as good or, otherwise, poor practice, depending on the nature of the data [2, 3, 10–15]. These variations demand studies be conducted in different settings to understand the level of ENBC practice for evidence-based decision-making.

Ethiopia has been striving to meet the United Nations agenda of sustainable development goals targeted at reducing neonatal mortality to at least 12 per 1000 live births by the end of 2030 [16]. Before that, by 2025, Ethiopia planned to reduce newborn mortality to 21 per 1,000 live births, working with the WHO and partners and emphasizing newborn care [17]. Health professionals who work at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of health centers in Ethiopia were reported to have poor practice of ENBC as compared to those who work in the hospitals [4, 10, 18]. Little is known about the level of ENBC practice and associated factors among obstetric care providers in the health centers of Ethiopia. The findings will have a wider practical implication to improve newborn care and reduce the burden of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Therefore, this study was aimed at assessing the level of ENBC practice and associated factors among health professionals working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units in rural health centers of the Hadiya zone, central Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

A health facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted at rural public health centers in Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia. The capital city of the Hadiya zone and the Central Ethiopia regional state is Hossana town, which is located 232 km from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. The Hadiya zone has thirteen woreda (districts) and seven administrative towns. There are five public hospitals (one teaching and four primary hospitals) and sixty public health centers (seven urban and fifty-three rural) that provide maternal and child health services for the community. There were 471 health professionals (midwives, nurses, and public health officers) who were responsible for attending labor, delivery, and ENBC services at study areas [Hadiya Zone Health Department—Unpublished report]. This study was conducted in seven woredas of the Hadiya zone, from September 20, 2023, up to October 20, 2023.

Participants

For this study, health professionals who were working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of the included rural public health centers in the Hadiya zone during the study period (September 20 to October 20, 2023) were included, irrespective of level of education, service year, or type of profession. Eligible health professionals who were on annual leave at the time of data collection were excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using Epi Info version 7.2.2.6 software. The following assumptions were considered: 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, 80% power, and the proportion of good practice of ENBC of 74.8% from the previous study [2]. This gives a sample size of 290. Adding 3% of the possible non-response rate, the total sample size becomes 299. There are a total of thirteen Woredas in the Hadiya zone. From thirteen Woredas, seven, namely Gibe, Soro, Ana Lemo, Misha, Ameka, Shashogo and Lemo, were selected by a simple random sampling technique (lottery method). Greater than 30% of the Woredas in the zone were included for better representation. In these selected Woredas, there are thirteen rural public health centers (three at Gibe, five at Soro, four at Ana Lemo, four at Ameka, four at Shashogo, three at Misha, and seven at Lemo). A total of 302 health professionals who were working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of the included rural health centers were found. The number of obstetric care providers (302) is nearly equivalent to the calculated sample size (299). Thus, all 302 health professionals (midwives, nurses, and public health officers) who were working in the labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of all selected health centers were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Variables and measurements

The dependent variable is the practice of ENBC, which is a composite outcome of 12-item practice questions adapted from and based on the WHO standardized procedure for ENBC guidelines and related studies [2, 10–14, 19]. ENBC practice, according to the WHO guideline, is sequential care given to all newborn infants starting from the delivery of a baby on the mother’s abdomen, followed by drying and stimulating, evaluation of breathing, cord care, keeping the newborn warm, initiating breastfeeding in the first hour, administering eye ointment, administering vitamin K intramuscularly, weighing, putting on an identification band, and recording. The level of ENBC practice among the health professionals was assessed by using responses for the 12-item questions on a scale of 1–3 points (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, and 3 = always), and the median (fifty percentile) of the sum score was used as a cutoff, and the level of ENBC practice was dichotomized into good (coded as 1) and poor (coded as 0). The health professionals who had scored above the median value were considered to be demonstrating a good practice of ENBC.

The independent variables were individual socio-demographic characteristics such as age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, religion, level of education, work experience, monthly salary, type of profession, knowledge about ENBC, training and interest in work, and institutional characteristics such as workloads, supportive supervision, presence of guidelines, and availability of equipment and drugs. The most technical independent variables were measured as follows: Knowledge about ENBC practice was measured using 17-item questions adapted from different literatures [10, 11, 14]. The study participants’ responses for each of 17 items, which were coded as yes = 1 and no = 0, were aggregated as a total knowledge score by summing up the responses of all 17 items. Then, the mean value of the total knowledge score was calculated, and it was used as a cutoff to categorize the knowledge. Accordingly, those health professionals who scored greater than or equal to the mean value were categorized as having good knowledge (coded as 1), and those who scored below the mean score were categorized as having poor knowledge (coded as 0) during the analysis. Availability of equipment and drugs was measured by asking the health professionals if different equipment and drugs applicable for ENBC and child immunization services as per the WHO guidelines, like antibiotics, weighing scales, thermometers, sterile delivery packs, soap and water, gloves, clean, warm dry towels, covers, and resuscitation equipment near the delivery bed (suction device, bag, and mask), were present or not. Work experience was measured by asking the health professionals how many years they have been working in the health facilities, and the responses were categorized as less than five and five or more years. Training was measured as whether the health professionals received training on ENBC practice or not, and the response was categorized as yes or no. Interest in working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units was measured by asking the health professionals whether they work in the units out of their own interest or under pressure from somebody. Then, the response was categorized as yes if they work in all units according to their interests or no otherwise. Supportive supervision was measured by asking the participants whether they receive supportive supervision (internal or external) regarding ENBC practice or not. If they receive it, it is categorized as yes; otherwise, no.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using a self-administered English questionnaire adapted from the WHO standardized procedure for ENBC guidelines and related previous studies [10–14, 19]. The questionnaire was then modified in the local context by the investigators. The questionnaire was organized into three parts: individual socio-demographic/economic and institution-related characteristics, knowledge of ENBC, and practice of ENBC. Seven BSc nurses collected the data and were supervised by two degree-holder public health professionals.

Data analysis

Data were coded, entered into Epi Data version 3.1 software, and exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Before the analysis, data exploration was done to check for missing values and outliers. A Cronbach’s alpha was used to measure the internal consistency of items used to measure knowledge of ENBC, where a cutoff of 0.70 or greater indicates the items have good internal consistency. For continuous variables, descriptive statistics such as mean, median, and standard deviations were calculated. Frequency and percentages were calculated for the categorical variables. The multicollinearity effect was checked by using the variance inflation factor (VIF), where a VIF greater than 10 indicates the presence of significant multicollinearity. To identify factors contributing to the good practice of ENBC, a binary logistic regression model was considered. In the binary logistic regression, variables that have an association with the good practice of ENBC at P < 0.25 were recruited for the multivariable model adjustment to control potential confounding. In the multivariable logistic regression, variables that have an association with the good practice of ENBC at P < 0.05 with a 95% CI were reported as significant factors. An adjusted odds ratio was used to estimate the effect size. The goodness of fit of the model was checked by the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test, where P > 0.05 shows the model fits the data reasonably well.

Quality control measures

To assure the quality of the data, a pretest was conducted on 5% of participants at the health centers that were not included in the study one week before the actual data collection. Two days of training were given to the data collectors and supervisors regarding the objectives of the study, the data collection procedure, and the significance of the study. To minimize bias related to observation and social desirability, a self-administered questionnaire was used after careful instruction for the study participants. Also, we used data collectors (nurses) outside the included health centers to minimize information bias. Standardized procedures for ENBC and current IMNC guidelines were checked to assure that the response was correct. Close supervision was maintained throughout the data collection process. The collected data was checked and reviewed for completeness and consistency before entry into software. A multivariable model adjustment was done to control bias related to confounding.

Results

Socio‑demographic and economic characteristics

Of the 302 health professionals working at the selected health centers, 301 participated in the study, giving a response rate of 99.6%. Missing data did not occur in this study. The mean age of respondents was 31.82 (± 4.73 SD) years. The majority, 221 (73.4%) of the participants, were female. Most, 185 (61.5%), of the participants were midwives in their profession. Regarding their educational qualifications, 185 (61.5%) participants were at the diploma level, and 116 (38.5%) were at the degree level and above (Table 1).

Knowledge of essential newborn care

Obstetric care providers who scored below the mean value of 13.4 were categorized as having poor knowledge, and those who scored greater than or equal to the mean value of 13.4 were categorized as having good knowledge. Accordingly, the proportion of obstetric care providers who had a good knowledge of ENBC was 144 (47.8%), 95% CI (42.1, 53.6%) (Table 2).

Factors associated with essential newborn care practice

In binary logistic regression, age, sex, level of education, types of profession, work experience, monthly salary, interest in working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units, supportive supervision, training about ENBC, workload, availability of guidelines, and knowledge of ENBC were candidate variables associated with good practice of ENBC at P < 0.25 and recruited for further adjustment to control for potential confounding in the multivariable logistic regression. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, level of education, types of profession, work experience, interest in working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units, and training about ENBC were variables significantly associated with good practice of ENBC at P < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. The multicollinearity was checked for continuous variables (work experience and monthly salary) by using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and the variables had a VIF of 1.24, indicating no multicollinearity. The Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests have shown that the model fits the data reasonably well (chi-square = 6.06, df = 8, and P = 0.64) (Table 4).

Discussions

This study was aimed at assessing the health professionals’ practice of ENBC at rural public health centers in the Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia. Accordingly, nearly half of health professionals working in labor, delivery, and postnatal care units had a good practice of ENBC. The good practice of ENBC was associated with a higher level of education, the midwifery profession, longer work experience, an interest in working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units, and receiving training on ENBC.

In this study, the proportion of health professionals who had good practice of ENBC was 47.2%. The result suggests that still nearly half of the health professionals do not demonstrate ENBC according to the recommended guideline [1]. This situation may pose a poor health status for the newborns, as they do not receive the ENBC that is needed for survival and improved health. The finding of this study was lower than the study done in South Gondar, Ethiopia, 74.8% [2]; Sidama regional state, Ethiopia, 56.6% [3]; Afar, Northeast Ethiopia, 62.7% [10]; Northwestern zone of Tigray, Ethiopia, 59.8% [11]; Northeast Shoa, Ethiopia, 73.8% [12]; Awi zone, Northwest Ethiopia, 62.7% [13]; Bench-Sheko zone, Ethiopia, 61.8% [14]; and Ekiti state, Nigeria, 62.9% [15]. The discrepancy might be due to the study setting; this study was conducted in rural health centers where human resources, follow-up, medication, and materials needed for ENBC practice were limited. Moreover, it might be due to the fact that health professionals who had limited experience or were fresh were usually hired at rural health centers. This might reduce the magnitude of good practice of ENBC, as these professionals are likely to have little exposure to the services and related training. The other possible reason for the difference might be due to the sample size and qualifications and experiences of study participants included in the studies. In this study, all health professionals working in labor, delivery, and postnatal care units who are public health officers, midwives, and nurses in their profession were included. However, others included either nurses only [11], midwives only [15], both midwives and nurses [10], or all health professionals working in the selected health facilities [2, 12], who are likely to practice ENBC differently. These might have resulted in different levels of ENBC practice. Furthermore, the variations might be due to the difference in the operational definition used to categorize good and poor practices of ENBC among health professionals.

In this study, health professionals who have a degree or higher level of education were twice more likely to have a good practice of ENBC as compared to those who have diploma-level education. This finding is consistent with the findings of other studies conducted in Ethiopia [2, 14]. This might be due to the fact that healthcare providers with a higher level of educational status can be familiarized with more common health problems in newborns and have greater decision-making power and skill regarding the implementation of ENBC. However, the finding of our study was not supported by the study conducted in Awi Zone, Northwest Ethiopia [13], which revealed that degree holders were less likely to practice ENBC than diploma holders, and the study conducted in Northeast Ethiopia [12], in which the level of education was not significantly associated with good practice of ENBC. Variation in the level of education alone might not be sufficient to demonstrate a good practice of ENBC; rather, other circumstances, such as training and incentives, could affect the practice in different settings.

The types of professions were found to be related to the good practices of ENBC. In this study, midwives were five times more likely to have good practice of ENBC as compared to nurses. This finding is consistent with the study conducted in the Awi Zone, Northwest Ethiopia [13]. Midwives are frontline health professionals who were trained more specifically for maternal and child health care services than nurses. As a result, midwives might have more chance to work in labor, delivery, and postnatal care units and more chance to receive in-service training on the practice of ENBC than nurses. The result suggests a need to train and assign more midwives than nurses where possible, as the nature of professional trainings might have affected the motivation and practice of ENBC.

Health professionals who have five or more years of work experience were five times more likely to practice ENBC than those with less than five years of work experience. This suggests that work experience gives an opportunity to exercise newborn care practices and improve skills more than lower-level work experience. This finding is consistent with the studies done in the Afar regional state, Ethiopia [10], and northeast Ethiopia [12], in which more work experience of health professionals was associated with good practice in ENBC.

Health professionals who had an interest in working in labor, delivery, and postnatal care units were twice more likely to have good practice of ENBC as compared to those who had no interest in working in those units. The finding is in agreement with the study in the Afar region, Ethiopia [10]. This might be due to the fact that health professionals who have an interest in working in the labor, delivery, and postnatal care units are encouraged to read, get information, and practice the tasks they are interested in doing, which could lead to an increase in the level of good practice at ENBC. Lack of interest might be due to different reasons, such as personal, organizational, or other issues. Further investigation on this issue is needed to improve the practice of ENBC among health professionals in rural areas.

Health professionals who have received training on ENBC were twice more likely to have a good practice of ENBC as compared to those who hadn’t gotten the training. This finding replicates other studies conducted in Ethiopia [2, 3, 13, 14]. This might be because refreshment training helps to update the skills necessary to provide ENBC and improve health professionals’ performance so that they might have a better practice of ENBC than those who did not get the training. Getting the training might have motivated the health professionals to demonstrate good practice of ENBC more responsibly than those who did not get the training.

This study might not be free from limitations that need to be acknowledged. The study participants were told to fill in the correct response without any bias, and their response will not affect their employment negatively. Despite this effort, social desirability bias might have occurred, as the study participants were asked about their professional skills. As a result, they might have feared responding correctly if they didn’t know the practice. This might have overestimated the practice of ENBC. Good practice is a composite outcome of items, and a median score (50th percentile) was considered to define that 50% of the health professionals had either good or poor practice. As a result, there might have been a loss of information to some extent. We were unable to use observations due to fear of observer bias or the Hawthorne effect; instead, we used a self-administered questionnaire, which might not be reliable. As a result, the study participants might have overstated the practice that might not be true in actual practice. Moreover, due to the nature of the cross-sectional study design (egg and chicken dilemma), temporal relationships cannot be ascertained. Despite the limitations, the findings of this study are additional input for evidence-based practice to improve ENBC at rural health centers. The findings may be generalized for similar populations in similar contexts in Ethiopia.

Conclusions

Nearly half of health professionals working at labor, delivery, and postnatal care units of rural health centers demonstrated a good practice of ENBC. This result is by far low, highlighting that half of the health professionals are still practicing the ENBC poorly. As a result, newborns were not receiving the care that they need immediately after birth. This poor practice may be closely linked with neonatal mortality and can challenge achieving the sustainable development goal set to reduce neonatal mortality to below 12 per 1000 live births by 2030. Hence, concerned government bodies such as regional, zonal, and district health managers and other stakeholders or initiatives that work on neonatal health have to strengthen capacity-building trainings, educational opportunities for lower-level obstetric care providers, and the increase of midwifery professionals in the rural health centers, as these are amenable to change.
