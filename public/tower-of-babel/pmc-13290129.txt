Comparative Study of the Flipping Technique and Conventional Vaginal Natural Orifice Transluminal Endoscopic Surgery Myomectomy
Authors: Rihua Xu, Ayijiang⋅Nuerlan, Hua Yao, Dan Tang, Yong Luo, Jing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of the study was to evaluate the clinical application value of the flipping technique in vaginal natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (vNOTES) for myomectomy in cases involving numerous, large, and difficult-to-expose uterine fibroids, particularly those located at the fundus.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 42 patients without fertility requirements but desiring uterine preservation who underwent flipping technique vNOTES myomectomy (case group) and 46 patients who underwent conventional vNOTES myomectomy (control group) at two hospitals between January 2024 and May 2025. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative clinical data were compared between the two groups.ResultsAll surgeries were successfully completed in both groups. Compared with the control group, the flipping technique group had greater uterine volumes, a greater number of fibroids removed intraoperatively, and the ability to remove fibroids at higher positions (e.g., fundus). However, the flipping technique group also experienced longer operative times, greater intraoperative blood loss, and a longer durations before postoperative urinary catheter removal. These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe flipping technique in vNOTES offers advantages similar to conventional vNOTES, such as the absence of abdominal scarring and lower postoperative pain levels. Additionally, it offers the advantages of removing a greater number of fibroids and various types of fibroids, particularly those located at challenging positions such as the uterine fundus. This technique expands the indications for vNOTES myomectomy.

Introduction

Uterine leiomyoma is the most common benign tumor of the female reproductive system in women of childbearing age, with an incidence rate reaching up to 50% [1, 2]. Early stages are often present with no noticeable symptoms. As the condition progresses, symptoms such as menorrhagia, lower abdominal pain and heaviness, and abdominal masses may appear. It is also a leading cause of vaginal bleeding, miscarriage, and infertility [3, 4]. Myomectomy is one of its primary treatment modalities. It can be performed through various approaches, including laparotomy, vaginal surgery, hysteroscopy, multiport laparoscopy, transumbilical single-port laparoscopy, and transvaginal single-port laparoscopy, with each approach associated with its own advantages, disadvantages, and specific indications [5]. Vaginal natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (vNOTES), commonly known as transvaginal single-port laparoscopy, offers several advantages [6]. Since the surgical incision is within the vagina, there are no visible external scars in vNOTES, which results in excellent cosmetic outcomes. Additionally, it causes less disturbance to intra-abdominal organs such as the intestines, leading to faster postoperative recovery of bowel function, relatively less pain, and potentially shorter hospital stays [7, 8]. However, it has a relatively small surgical field of view and a limited operating space. It is generally more suitable for smaller and fewer leiomyomas located in the lower uterine segment or cervix, as these positions are relatively easier to expose and operate on via the natural vaginal orifice. For leiomyomas near the uterine fundus, or those that are numerous, large in size, or heavily adhered, transvaginal surgery is unsuitable because of high surgical difficulty and significant risks [9]. After receiving ethical approval by the hospital, the flipping technique vNOTES myomectomy was innovatively performed. The clinical data of patients who underwent this technique were compared and analyzed with those of patients who underwent conventional vNOTES myomectomy to evaluate its clinical application value.

Materials and Methods

General Data

A retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of 42 patients without fertility requirements but desiring uterine preservation who underwent flipping technique vNOTES myomectomy (case group) and 46 patients who underwent conventional vNOTES myomectomy (control group) at Karamay Central Hospital and Hami Central Hospital (performed by the same surgeon) between January 2024 and May 2025. The surgical technique was selected based on preoperative MRI assessment of fibroid location, number, and the patient’s desire for future fertility. All included patient data were reliable and complete. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the relevant diagnostic criteria from the 2017 “Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Uterine Leiomyomas”; (2) uterine leiomyoma accompanied by symptoms such as menorrhagia, abnormal uterine bleeding, or pressure symptoms, or persistent growth in postmenopausal women not receiving hormone replacement therapy; (3) desire for uterine preservation; patients in the case group had no fertility requirements; (4) preoperative MRI confirmation of the diagnosis of uterine leiomyoma in both groups; and (5) complete clinical data and informed consent for the surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) reproductive tract infection or systemic acute infection; (2) severe underlying medical diseases, such as acute severe heart, liver, or kidney failure; (3) severe coagulation disorders or hematological diseases; (4) assessed by the anesthesiology department as unfit to tolerate anesthesia or surgery; (5) diaphragmatic hernia (contraindication to laparoscopic surgery); (6) severe pelvic adhesions or severe vaginal stenosis preoperatively assessed to prevent transvaginal access to the abdominal cavity; (7) rapid leiomyoma growth and preoperative imaging suggesting possible malignant transformation; and (8) preoperative hemoglobin concentration <80 g/L. There were no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) between the two groups in terms of age, gravidity, parity, BMI, history of pelvic surgery, mode of delivery, presence of a scarred uterus, preoperative hemoglobin level, or type of the largest leiomyoma. These findings indicate that the data from both groups were comparable.

However, a significant difference in uterine volume was observed between the groups (z = −5.564; p < 0.001). The median uterine volume in the control group (175.47 cm3) was significantly lower than that in the case group (291.94 cm3). This difference might be attributed to the case group having a greater number of leiomyomas, whereas the control group primarily consisted solitary leiomyomas selected for the specific conventional procedure, as shown in Table 1.

Methods

Both groups underwent surgery outside the menstrual period, with preoperative vaginal douching performed as part of the preparation. General endotracheal anesthesia was administered, patients were placed in the lithotomy position, and routine disinfection, draping, and urinary catheterization were performed.

In the flipping technique vNOTES group, (1) the cervix was grasped and pulled downward with Allis forceps. Six units of pituitrin were injected into the cervix. Hydrodissection was performed in the vesicocervical space. The vaginal mucosa was incised transversely down to the submucosal layer. The vesicocervical space was dissected superiorly, and the bladder was pushed upward to the level of the vesicouterine peritoneal reflection, which was then incised to enter the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1a). (2) The vaginal mucosa was circumferentially incised around the posterior cervical lip. The rectocervical space was bluntly dissected down to the level of the rectouterine peritoneal reflection, which was incised to enter the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1b). (3) If the anterior or posterior peritoneal reflections could not be opened, the single-port laparoscopic set and instruments could be inserted in a stepwise manner, followed by dissection. If still unsuccessful, conversion to transumbilical single-port laparoscopy or conventional laparoscopy was performed. (4) The vaginal fornix on one side was incised with an electrocautery knife. The uterosacral-cardinal ligament complex was clamped and transected (Fig. 1c), and suture-ligated with 1–0 absorbable sutures, and the suture was left long for marking (Fig. 1d). The same procedure was performed on the contralateral side. (5) The vaginal incision margins were continuously sutured with 1–0 absorbable sutures along the anterior and posterior fornix stumps for hemostasis and to reduce the size of the fornix opening. A single-port platform was placed, with one end in the abdominal cavity and the other outside the vaginal introitus (Fig. 2a). Pneumoperitoneum was established, surgical instruments were inserted, and the pelvic/abdominal cavity was explored to assess the size, location, and number of uterine fibroids. (6) The remaining uterosacral-cardinal ligament and uterine vessels on one side were coagulated and cut. Part of the broad ligament was incised. The same procedure was performed on the contralateral side to allow maximum anterior-posterior flipping of the uterus within the pelvis (Fig. 2b, c). (7) Under endoscopic visualization, the most prominently protruding part of the fibroid was grasped and pulled toward the vaginal incision. The single-port platform was removed. Myomectomy was performed step-by-step vaginally (Fig. 2d). For multiple fibroids, the uterus was flipped anteriorly and posteriorly to maximize fibroid removal. Myometrial cavities were individually sutured with 1–0 absorbable sutures. If the fibroid breached the mucosal layer, the mucosal layer was sutured separately (Fig. 3a). (8) The pneumoperitoneum was reestablished, the laparoscope was reinserted, the uterus was repositioned, and the surgical site was inspected (Fig. 3b). The pelvis was irrigated. Salpingectomy was performed if necessary. (9) The single-port platform was removed again. The stumps of the uterosacral-cardinal ligament complex were sutured to the lateral cervical wall vaginally using 1–0 absorbable sutures to reconstruct the cervical support structures (Fig. 3c). The vaginal stump was sutured to the cervix to reconstruct the fornices. A “T”-shaped pelvic drainage tube was placed (Fig. 3d).

In conventional vNOTES group, (1) depending on the fibroid location, the corresponding anterior or posterior vaginal fornix was selectively and progressively incised to establish the laparoscopic port. The steps for opening the fornices were the same as above. The margins of the incised fornix were continuously sutured with 1–0 absorbable sutures to reduce its size. A single-port laparoscopic platform was used. Pneumoperitoneum was established, instruments were inserted, and the pelvic/abdominal cavity was explored to assess the size, location, and number of fibroids. (2) Under endoscopic visualization, the most prominently protruding part of the fibroid was grasped and pulled toward the vaginal incision. The single-port platform was removed. Myomectomy was performed step-by-step vaginally, and the uterine seromuscular layer was sutured. (3) If traction was difficult, an ultrasonic scalpel was used laparoscopically to incise the most protruding part of the fibroid down to its pseudocapsule. The plane between the uterus and fibroid was dissected bluntly and sharply. The fibroid was rotated and enucleated, and the uterine seromuscular layer was sutured. (4) The pneumoperitoneum was reestablished, the laparoscope was reinserted, the surgical site was inspected, and the pelvis was irrigated. Salpingectomy was performed if necessary. (5) The single-port platform was removed again. The fornix incision was closed with continuous suture. A “T”-shaped pelvic drainage tube was placed.

Postoperative Management

1.Both groups received prophylactic antibiotics for 48 h postoperatively.2.A liquid diet was initiated 6–8 h postoperatively.3.Patients were encouraged to ambulate early within 24 h postoperatively, gradually increasing their activity.4.Urinary catheters were removed as soon as possible within 24–48 h.5.Pelvic drains were removed when drainage was <50 mL/24 h.6.Patients were discharged when the vaginal incision showed no active bleeding, when the temperature had been normal for 48 h, when flatus had passed, and when urination was normal.7.Sexual intercourse and tub baths were prohibited for 3 months. A follow-up outpatient visit was scheduled at 1 month postoperatively.

Observation Indicators

1.Surgical indicators include number of fibroids removed, maximum fibroid diameter, highest fibroid location, presence of pelvic adhesions intraoperatively, number of conversions to other surgical approaches, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss.2.Recovery indicators show pain score at 24 h postoperatively (using a visual analog scale [VAS]: 0–10; higher scores indicate worse pain), time to first ambulation, time to first flatus, time to urinary catheter removal, time to drain removal, highest postoperative temperature, postoperative hospital stay, complications (pelvic infection, intestinal/urinary tract injury, wound hematoma, etc.), and vaginal incision healing. Vaginal incision healing was assessed through gynecological examination at 1 month postoperatively and was graded as follows: grade A (healed well, no hyperemia/exudate); grade B (defective healing, inflammatory reaction such as hyperemia at the stump but no suppuration); and grade C (infected/suppurative incision, presence of hyperemia/bleeding, infection/suppuration, granulation tissue).

Statistical Analysis

All the data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (‾x±s) and were compared using independent sample t tests. Nonnormally distributed continuous data are expressed as medians {interquartile ranges (M[P25, P75])} and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as n (%) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Comparison of Surgical Indicators

All surgeries were completed successfully in both groups without conversion to laparotomy or other laparoscopic approaches. The surgical indicators are shown in Table 2. No significant differences were observed between groups regarding the maximum fibroid diameter removed intraoperatively or the presence of intraoperative adhesions (p > 0.05). Discussion

Comparison of Various Myomectomy Approaches

Compared with abdominal myomectomy, multiport laparoscopy offers better cosmesis, less postoperative pain, earlier ambulation, and faster gastrointestinal recovery. However, it is limited by reduced tactile sensation in the abdomen and two-dimensional vision, making detection of intramural fibroids without significant protrusion difficult. Suturing the fibroid bed and achieving hemostasis, especially for transmural fibroids penetrating the mucosa, are also challenging [10, 11]. Furthermore, the use of a powerful morcellator to extract fragmented fibroids through trocar sites increases the risk of disseminating occult malignant tissue; expert consensus advises against the use of this technique [12].

Transumbilical single-port laparoscopy (LESS) is a further refinement of conventional laparoscopy, offering the advantages of a minimally invasive approach. Specimen extraction via the umbilicus is quick and safe and results in a well-hidden scar that meets cosmetic expectations [13]. However, a weak, recessed umbilical incision is associated with a risk of incisional hernia. Uterine suturing under LESS is technically demanding and requires a prolonged learning curve [14].

Vaginal myomectomy is not associated with these drawbacks but has strict requirements regarding fibroid location, size, number, and vaginal accessibility. It is suitable for smaller and fewer, lower segment fibroids in patients with a sufficiently spacious vagina and no significant uterine adhesions. It does not allow simultaneous adnexal surgery or exploration of the abdominal cavity [15].

vNOTES myomectomy combines single-port laparoscopy with conventional vaginal surgery. It broadens the indications for vaginal myomectomy, overcoming limitations such as a confined operating space and poor visualization. It avoids the use of uterine manipulators, reducing the risks of instrument-related uterine perforation, adjacent organ injury, and endometrial damage. Its advantages include the absence of an abdominal wall incision, minimal trauma, faster recovery, and less pain [7, 8]. Clinically, vNOTES myomectomy can be termed “vaginal myomectomy assisted by transvaginal single-port laparoscopy.” The use of a laparoscope primarily aids in adhesiolysis, abdominal cavity exploration, and adnexal procedures, while fibroid enucleation and myometrial suturing are performed vaginally. Therefore, it still has selective applicability concerning fibroid location, size, and number [16].

Advantages and Limitations of Flipping Technique vNOTES Myomectomy

Flipping technique vNOTES myomectomy involves laparoscopic transection of the paracervical uterosacral-cardinal ligaments and uterine arteries, followed by vaginal enucleation of fibroids and suturing of the myometrial cavities after uterine flipping. This significantly improves the operating space and surgical field of view, enhancing maneuverability and surgical freedom. With the flipping technique, the uterus can be manipulated vaginally in all directions, facilitating palpation and exploration of deep-seated fibroids, leading to more thorough removal, including that of fibroids at the uterine fundus. Surgeons who are proficient in vaginal surgery and vNOTES hysterectomy can master this technique relatively quickly.

Compared with the control group (conventional vNOTES), the case group (flipping technique vNOTES) had a greater number of fibroids removed from higher positions, demonstrating that the flipping technique substantially expands the indications for vNOTES myomectomy. However, owing to the greater number of surgical steps (three vaginal phases and two laparoscopic phases) and the larger number of fibroids removed, the operative time was longer and blood loss was greater.

The catheter removal time was shorter in the control group because patients with posterior wall fibroids did not require anterior colpotomy or bladder dissection, eliminating the need for prolonged catheterization to monitor for bladder injury. However, no significant differences were found in the other postoperative recovery indicators, indicating that the flipping technique did not compromise the minimally invasive advantages of vNOTES.

One case of bladder injury occurred in each of the flipping technique vNOTES group and the conventional vNOTES group. A retrospective review of the 2 patients with bladder injury revealed that both had a prior history of cesarean section. Intraoperatively, dense adhesions were observed between the bladder fundus and the anterior uterine wall. In both cases, a tear in the seromuscular layer of the bladder was identified during dissection of the vesicouterine peritoneal reflection when creating the anterior colpotomy approach. The bladder wall was sutured promptly in both patients, and the urinary catheters were successfully removed after 2 weeks of postoperative indwelling. Both patients recovered well.

Compared with conventional vNOTES (which usually only requires anterior or posterior colpotomy), flipping technique vNOTES requires a circumferential incision of the cervical vaginal wall, creating a larger wound. This theoretically leads to poorer blood supply after suturing and potentially more difficult healing [16]. However, this study revealed no significant difference in incision healing between the groups. This unexpected result might be due to the study’s limited sample size, resulting in insufficient statistical power to detect a true difference. Further validation with larger samples is needed.

Does transecting and reconstructing the paracervical uterosacral-cardinal ligament complex in flipping technique vNOTES affect ligament function, potentially increasing the risk of middle compartment pelvic organ prolapse? On the basis of existing data, directly transecting these ligaments during hysterectomy without reconstruction does not increase the risk of vaginal vault prolapse [17, 18], suggesting that the impact might be minimal. The authors are currently investigating this using dynamic pelvic MRI and comparing preoperative images with those at 6 months and 1 year after the operation. However, the current case numbers are low, and follow-up is inadequate to draw definitive conclusions.

Furthermore, transecting the uterine artery in flipping technique vNOTES significantly affects the uterine blood supply. Therefore, this technique is not considered suitable for patients desiring future fertility [19, 20].

To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior reports on flipping technique vNOTES myomectomy. As an innovative technique, it offers unique minimally invasive advantages, showing promise for wider adoption. This method is also applicable to adenomyomectomy. However, the current number of cases is limited, and follow-up is short. More research data are needed to further evaluate its safety and efficacy.
