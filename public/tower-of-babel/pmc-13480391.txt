Intestinal Ultrasound in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Monitoring Inflammation and Treatment Response
Authors: Alessandro Bruno, Ferdinando D’Amico, Laurent Peyrin-Biroulet, Silvio Danese, Mariangela Allocca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease and Ulcerative Colitis, is a chronic immune-mediated condition that requires long-term monitoring to prevent disease progression and complications. The adoption of treat-to-target strategies has highlighted the limitations of symptom-based assessment and the need for objective tools to evaluate both mucosal and transmural inflammation. In this setting, cross-sectional imaging has gained relevance, and intestinal ultrasound (IUS) has emerged as a noninvasive point-of-care modality for disease monitoring.Aims and MethodsAs the evidence of IUS in the monitoring of IBD continues to expand, we provided a comprehensive overview of its clinical applications and supporting evidence.This narrative review summarizes the current evidence regarding the role of IUS in monitoring therapeutic response in IBD including results of retrospective and prospective studies and meta-analyses.ResultsThe IUS demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy in assessing inflammatory activity in both Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, with strong correlations with endoscopic and biochemical markers. In Crohn’s disease, IUS enables early detection of treatment response to biologic and small-molecule therapies, assessment of transmural healing, and identification of complications such as strictures, fistulas, and abscesses. In ulcerative colitis, IUS has been shown to predict endoscopic remission, clinical response, and long-term outcomes, including the risk of colectomy. Early ultrasound assessment is also valuable in acute severe ulcerative colitis, identifying corticosteroid responses, and the need for rescue therapy or surgery. Several ultrasound-based scoring systems have been proposed; however, only a limited number have undergone robust external validation, and definitions of response and remission remain heterogeneous. Overall, IUS represents a valuable tool for personalized disease monitoring, although wider standardization using validated scores is required for broader implementation.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), including Crohn’s disease1 (CD) and ulcerative colitis2 (UC), are chronic immune-mediated disorders characterized by persistent gastrointestinal inflammation and a substantial impact on patient quality of life. Over time, the therapeutic landscape of IBD has evolved substantially, shifting from conventional therapies, such as mesalamine, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants, to the introduction of biological agents and small-molecule drugs that provide more targeted and effective approaches for achieving clinical remission.3,4

Despite the effectiveness of available therapies, conventional symptom-based treatment approaches are often inadequate in preventing progressive damage and downstream complications.5 Therefore, the approach to IBD care has evolved from a predominantly reactive model to one centered on proactive monitoring to detect relapse early and avert complications.6 This paradigm shift is facilitated by the emergence of effective therapies, validated objective targets for disease control, and data confirming that disease modification with improved long-term outcomes can be achieved. In particular, the treat-to-target (T2T) strategy emphasizes achieving predefined treatment goals, such as endoscopic response, remission, and biomarker normalization, rather than relying solely on symptom control only.7

Since now endoscopic mucosal healing has been considered as the main therapeutic target in IBD drug monitoring.8 However, mucosal healing should be viewed as an early step in the resolution of intestinal inflammation in IBD, rather than as the final therapeutic objective, given the transmural nature of the disease.9 For this reason, in the last few years, the focus has shifted to transmural healing, as reflected in the STRIDE II consensus, initially for CD and more recently for UC. Several studies have demonstrated that transmural healing has been associated with better outcomes in CD,10,11 while UC has historically been regarded as limited to the mucosa and associated with minimal cumulative bowel damage. However, emerging evidence indicates that disease involvement may extend beyond the mucosal layer12 thereby supporting TH as a therapeutic target to be pursued in UC.13

Owing to the variable correlation between clinical symptoms and active intestinal inflammation, assessment of treatment response within a T2T framework requires the use of complementary monitoring tools.13 A central role in evaluation of both mucosal and transmural healing, as well as in monitoring treatment, is played by cross-sectional imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and intestinal ultrasound (IUS), providing assessment and monitoring of IBD.14 IUS and MRI demonstrate comparable diagnostic accuracy for IBD assessment, but each modality offers distinct advantages in specific clinical scenarios. The choice between them depends on clinical context, patient characteristics, and local expertise.

For example, IUS offers significant practical advantages in pediatric IBD patients, particularly for avoiding sedation in young children. The ACR Appropriateness Criteria suggests that IUS may be a suitable alternative for evaluating the ileum in younger children who would otherwise require sedation for MRI examinations.15 Moreover, IUS could be a valid alternative to MRI during pregnancy as it does not require contrast administration and is more readily accessible. IUS demonstrates sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 98% for detecting disease activity in pregnant IBD patients, with moderate-to-strong correlation with clinical activity (r=0.60; p<0.0001) and fecal calprotectin (r=0.73; p<0.0001).16

Guidelines from major societies recognize IUS as an accurate, noninvasive tool for monitoring inflammatory bowel disease.14,17 However, real-world adoption remains heterogeneous, with limited integration into routine clinical practice and relatively low uptake, frequently in favour of endoscopic evaluation.18

Aims and Methods

This narrative review aimed to summarize the available evidence on the use of IUS for monitoring therapeutic responses, focusing on its effectiveness and practical application.

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases to ensure that our review included the latest relevant publications available up to November 15, 2025. We employed specific search terms such as “intestinal ultrasound,” “bowel ultrasound,” “abdominal ultrasound,” “IUS” in conjunction with “Crohn’s disease,” “ulcerative colitis,” “inflammatory bowel disease,” “CD,” “UC,” “IBD.” We limited our search to articles published in the English language. No additional filters for study design, or publication year were applied across the databases. Furthermore, no explicit inclusion or exclusion criteria were predefined, as these are beyond the scope of the present work, a manual screening of the reference list was performed. The final inclusion of articles was based on their relevance to the research objective.

Intestinal Ultrasound

IUS is a noninvasive radiation-free imaging technique that employs high-frequency sound waves to assess the bowel wall and adjacent structures. IUS is performed transabdominally using both low- and high-frequency probes, with the addition of color Doppler evaluation. The spatial resolution of grayscale ultrasound enables detailed visualization of the normal bowel wall layers without the need for contrast enhancement to identify transmural changes or abnormalities in extramural structures.19 These features permit real-time and dynamic assessment of intestinal segments and support rapid bedside evaluation and clinical decision-making20 (Image 1).

Owing to its numerous advantages, the IUS is increasingly being adopted in IBD care.17 Indeed, it is well tolerated by patients who, despite frequently undergoing invasive procedures such as ileocolonoscopy, have expressed a preference for non-invasive monitoring approaches.21 A prospective cohort study showed that patients managed with an IUS-driven care approach reported greater confidence in making informed decisions about their disease management when active disease was identified compared to patients receiving standard care.22

Moreover, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that IUS provides a diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of more invasive and resource-intensive imaging modalities, including CT and MRI, for the detection and characterization of inflammatory activity and disease-related complications in IBD.23,24

For these reasons, IUS has been readily incorporated into close follow-up strategies and T2T approaches for IBD patients. IUS is reproducible and allows serial monitoring without exposing the patients to radiation or requiring bowel preparation.25 The ability to detect early ultrasound changes and perform frequent assessments has the potential to transform disease activity and therapeutic response monitoring, facilitating timely clinical decisions.26

The main ultrasonographic findings used to assess disease activity in IBD are bowel wall thickness (BWT), vascularization of the wall on color Doppler ultrasound (CDS), bowel wall stratification (BWS), and presence of inflammatory mesenteric fat (i-FAT).27,28

The most extensively validated cut-off for BWT is 3 mm, with a value of ≥3 mm generally considered abnormal in both the small bowel and colon.29 Bowel wall vascularization is evaluated using color Doppler imaging, most commonly graded according to the Limberg score (LS)30 or its modified version (mLS), the presence of hypervascularization (mLS ≥2) is highly specific and has a high positive predictive value for histologically active inflammation.31 Additional relevant features include submucosal thickening and alterations in bowel wall stratification, which may be preserved, focally disrupted, or completely lost.32 Furthermore, hyperechogenic spiculations within the submucosa are associated with fibrotic changes in patients with Crohn’s disease.33 Findings extending beyond the bowel wall included enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes, mesenteric hypertrophy or i-FAT, and presence of free fluid.34,35 When these findings are considered together with intramural abnormalities, they offer important insights into both the inflammatory burden and overall extent of the disease.

Recent studies have demonstrated the high sensitivity and specificity of IUS for the assessment of inflammation in both the colon and small bowel. In the colon, pooled analyses indicated that IUS achieved a sensitivity of 86.4% and a specificity of 88.3% for the detection of segmental inflammation using endoscopy as the reference standard. In the rectum, diagnostic performance is lower, with a sensitivity of 74.5% and a specificity of 69.5%, but this limitation is mitigated by transperineal ultrasound (TPUS), which provides an accurate assessment of rectal disease activity and has been shown to perform comparably to endoscopy.36 These estimates were consistent across both CD and UC.29 In the small bowel, IUS demonstrates sensitivity ranging from 83% to 95% and specificity from 70% to 85% for the detection of endoscopic inflammation, with diagnostic performance comparable to that of MRI in the terminal ileum.37,38

This body of evidence supports intestinal ultrasound as a cornerstone for monitoring the therapeutic response in both CD and UC.

Fifteen IUS scores have been developed for CD, however, not all have undergone external validation, and only a minority of them is currently used in routine clinical practice.53 The International Bowel Ultrasound Segmental Activity Score (IBUS-SAS) integrates bowel wall thickness, color Doppler signal, bowel wall stratification (BWS), and inflammatory fat into a composite scoring system (4 × BWT + 15 × i-fat + 7 × CDS + 4 × BWS) and has demonstrated a strong correlation with endoscopic disease activity, as well as excellent inter-rater reliability across multiple validation studies.25 The Bowel Ultrasound Score (BUSS) evaluate BWT and CDS (0.75 × BWT (mm) + 1.65 × CDS), it demonstrated strong correlation with endoscopic activity, and is sensitive to changes in disease activity over time, including response to therapy and achievement of remission.39 BUSS has undergone external validation in multiple independent cohorts and is considered a clinically viable and reproducible tool, both in routine clinical practice and clinical trials.54,55 The simplified Ultrasound Score for Crohn’s disease (SUS-CD) represents an alternative validated tool,56 it is calculated by summing the BWT and CDS for each affected bowel segment.42

Several US-based scoring systems have been proposed for UC. Among these, the Milan Ultrasound Criteria (MUC) is the only index that has undergone robust external validation, demonstrating satisfactory sensitivity and predictive performance, and supporting its applicability in routine clinical practice and trials.39,49 The MUC was derived from a simplified model incorporating the BWT and CDS, developed using endoscopic activity, defined by a Mayo Endoscopic Score (MES) >1, as the reference standard. Distinct threshold values were identified to discriminate between active disease (>6.2) and remission (≤4.3).48 Other scoring systems, including the Ulcerative Colitis–Intestinal Ultrasound (UC-IUS) index57 and IBUS-SAS,58 adopt a more comprehensive approach by integrating additional sonographic features, such as the haustral pattern, i-FAT, and BWS. Although these indices have been externally validated, further evidence is required to clarify their diagnostic performance, particularly with respect to sensitivity and predictive accuracy.38

To date, formal definitions of response and remission have not been established. The cutoff values identified during score development were not uniformly replicated across the validation cohorts, resulting in substantial heterogeneity in the optimal thresholds. Only a limited number of scoring systems have consistently demonstrated robust performance in validation analyses. Among these, BUSS showed reproducible diagnostic accuracy with comparable optimal cutoff values across validation studies conducted.59,60 Similarly, the MUC demonstrated strong performance in the validation cohorts, consistently confirming the cutoff proposed in the original study.60 BUSS for CD and MUC for UC have also been recently acknowledged in an international expert consensus meeting as a standardized scoring system for clinical trials.61

Crohn’s Disease

The integration of IUS into treatment monitoring has enhanced clinical decision making in patients with CD. By visualizing bowel wall changes rapidly, IUS allows timely, evidence-based treatment adjustments, overcoming the limitations of symptom-based and biomarker-driven assessments and predicting disease complications.

Monitoring Therapeutic Response to Advanced Therapies in CD

A growing body of evidence supports the use of IUS to monitor and evaluate treatment responses in patients with CD.

In a 12-month prospective, non-interventional study across 47 German centers, 234 adults with active CD (Harvey-Bradshow Index [HBI] ≥7) underwent treatment intensification and serial clinical and IUS assessments.62 IUS markers improved significantly, with BWT decreasing in all segments (P <0.001) and terminal ileum involvement declined from 75.4% at baseline to 35.8%. Additional improvements were observed in BWS (53.0% to 21.6%), i-FAT (47.0% to 17.9%), and lymphadenopathy (26.1% to 11.9%). Changes were evident at three months and paralleled reductions in vascularization, clinical activity (median HBI 10–3), and CRP levels (50.5–12.3 mg/L), with a significant correlation between BWT and CRP reduction.

In a prospective multicenter study, 188 adultpatients with CD were followed up for 12 months to assess changes in IUS parameters during advanced therapy. Patients received adalimumab (55%), infliximab (16%), vedolizumab (13%), or ustekinumab (16%). TH defined by IUS was achieved at 12 months in 27.5% of patients (number needed to treat 3.6), with rates of 26.8%, 37%, 27.2%, and 20%, respectively. BWT improved significantly at 3 and 12 months (P <0.0001), along with reductions in HBI, CRP, and fecal calprotectin (P <0.0001). Colonic involvement increased the likelihood of early TH, whereas greater baseline BWT predicted lower healing rates at 3 (OR 0.70, P =0.03) and 12 months (OR 0.58, P =0.01). These findings support IUS as an effective tool for monitoring biological responses in Crohn’s disease.

Substantial evidence supports the effectiveness of IUS in monitoring the effectiveness of anti-tumor necrosis factor alpha (anti-TNF-α) agents. A prospective evaluation of 40 patients with endoscopically active CD, starting anti-TNFα therapy, demonstrated that reduction in BWT assessed by IUS, already after 4—8 weeks of follow-up predict endoscopic response and remission (18% (area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC) 0.77; odds ratio [OR): 10.80, P =0.012)).63 Moreover, a multicenter study prospectively evaluated 51 patients with active CD for 2 years after the initiation of anti-TNFα therapy using IUS, evaluating BWT, CDS, and the presence of transmural complications or stenosis to define normalization, improvement, or lack of response.64 This study indicated that patients who experienced IUS improvement at the end of induction reported higher rates of IUS response at 52 weeks (85% vs 28%; P <0.0001), 1 year IUS improvement correlated with remission, and stricture detections was associated with a negative predictive value of response (P =0.0001). A retrospective evaluation of 103 patients in remission with anti-TNF-α who underwent a IUS evaluation after 10 months of therapy highlighted BWT as a single marker of treatment response (3.9 mm in the failure group vs 3 mm in the remission group, (P =0.007, AUROC of BWT >3 mm 0.66, sensitivity, 67.2%; specificity, 69.1%).65

IUS was also used to evaluate the therapeutic response in patients treated with ustekinumab. In a substudy of STARDUST, a randomized controlled trial was designed to compare T2T and standard-of-care treatment strategies in patients with ustekinumab-treated CD. An IUS response was detectable as early as 4 weeks after treatment initiation and continued to improve up to week 48. At week 48, IUS response and transmural remission rates were 46.3% and 24.1%, respectively, with greater effects in colonic disease and biologic-naïve patients. Absence of IUS response at week 4 predicted lack of endoscopic response at week 48 (NPV, 73%), with fair-to-moderate agreement between early IUS findings and long-term endoscopic and biomarker outcomes (κ = 0.21—0.51). Furthermore, BWT assessed using the IUS emerged as a helpful parameter for selecting early responders to ustekinumab in a prospective study.65

A recent prospective evaluation of 93 patients confirmed that early intestinal ultrasound findings have a significant predictive value for long-term endoscopic remission.66 Patients undergoing advanced therapies (43% adalimumab, 43% ustekinumab, 9% infliximab, and 5% vedolizumab) were assessed by IUS and ileocolonoscopy at the end of induction, and after 12 months, it emerged that week-12 ultrasound remission strongly predicted endoscopic remission (59% vs 41%; OR 9.93, 95% CI 3.10—31.80; P <0.001), Persistent ultrasound activity predicted failure to achieve long-term remission (negative predictive value (NPV) 87%, positive predictive value (PPV) 54%).

In addition, CEUS was used in a pilot study to assess microvascularisation in vedolizumab-treated patients. Among the 18 patients treated with vedolizumab, nine achieved a therapeutic response, which was associated with significant reductions in color Doppler vascularization and CEUS-derived amplitude parameters of mural microvascularization, including peak enhancement and wash-in rate. No such changes were observed in non-responders, whereas the time-based parameters remained unchanged.

Detection of CD Complications

Early identification of CD complications, such as strictures, fistulas, or abscesses, has proven to be strongly related to a worse prognosis and a significantly increased risk of surgery,67 therefore, the availability of effective tools for their detection is fundamental to disease monitoring.

IUS demonstrates excellent diagnostic performance for stricture detection, according to a systematic review stricture detection sensitivity ranged from 68% to 100% with corresponding specificity ranging from 86% to 100%.68 An expert panel of gastroenterologists, radiologists, and patient representatives provided a definition of naive or anastomotic small-bowel CD stricture on IUS:69 coexistence of BWT, luminal narrowing, and prestenotic dilatation is needed. Within the same panel, a response to small-bowel strictures was provided. A reduction in stricture length >25% was rated as uncertain, as were all alternative length-based thresholds. Among the proposed definitions, only a >25% reduction in bowel wall thickness and a >50% improvement in luminal narrowing were deemed appropriate. Improvement in prestenotic dilatation was defined as either a >25% reduction in diameter or achievement of a bowel diameter <2.5 cm. In strictures without prestenotic dilatation, response was defined as improvements in structural and vascular parameters, including CDS reduction by at least one LS score point. Notably, a recent meta-analysis reported that even when pre-stenotic dilation was excluded from the stricture definition in IUS assessment, the pooled sensitivity and specificity remained high, at 0.89 (95% CI 0.82—0.93) and 0.85 (95% CI 0.78—0.91), respectively.70 IUS demonstrated effectiveness also in detection of penetrating complications like fistulas or abscesses. A prospective observational study reported an IUS sensitivity of 78% in fistula identification with a specificity of 95% compared with MRI, undetected fistulas had a perirectal localization.71 Moreover, after a prospective evaluation of 625 CD patients, the presence of abscesses was correctly detected in 90.9% of cases, with an accuracy of 86.9%.72

For the detection of Crohn’s disease-related complications, a non-negligible role has also been described for small intestine contrast ultrasound (SICUS) and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), as reported in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis.73

Ulcerative Colitis

The key role of IUS is increasingly recognized in the management of UC, transitioning from an adjunctive assessment to a central tool, demonstrating a strong correlation with clinical and endoscopic disease activity and showing adequate predictive performance for disease progression.74

Monitoring Therapeutic Response to Advanced Therapies in UC

Numerous studies have evaluated the use of IUS for the assessment of therapeutic responses.

A prospective observational study at 42 German centers enrolled 224 UC patients with clinical relapse underwent IUS evaluation.75 At baseline, increased bowel wall thickness was present in 88.5% of patients; within 2 weeks, BWT decreased significantly in the sigmoid (89.3% to 38.6%) and descending colon (83.0% to 42.9%) (P<0.001), remaining low at weeks 6 and 12. BWT normalization at 12 weeks strongly correlated with clinical response (90.5% vs 9.5%; P<0.001).

A prospective, longitudinal study including UC patients with endoscopic disease activity showed that a 6 weeks BWT ≤3.0 mm predicted endoscopic remission (OR 25.13; P =0.012), reduced CDS predicted improvement (OR 0.35; P =0.026), and submucosal thickness at week 6 predicted remission (OR 0.09; P =0.018).76 In this cohort, BWT decreased early with infliximab and tofacitinib, and later with vedolizumab. The role of submucosal hypertrophy was also evaluated in another study that demonstrated a reduction in submucosal thickness in patients achieving endoscopic remission or improvement after starting advanced therapy (biologics or tofacitinib). In the same study, patients with hypoechogenic submucosa more frequently failed (OR 4.44; p=0.03) or multiple therapies (OR 5.63; p=0.009).

A further prospective observational study evaluated the predictive value of IUS described by MUC for biologic treatment response, using colonoscopy as a reference standard, 49 patients were included (59% under infliximab, 29% under vedolizumab, 8% under adalimumab, 4% under ustekinumab). A MUC score ≤6.2 at week 12 was the only independent predictor of endoscopic remission (MES ≤1 and MES = 0; OR 5.80, P=0.010 and OR 10.41, P=0.041) and showed a 96% negative predictive value for MES = 0. A ≥2-point MUC reduction predicted complete remission (AUROC 0.816), with a MUC ≤4.3 providing the highest accuracy (AUROC 0.876).77

BWT measured by IUS correlated with endoscopic and biochemical markers of disease activity in monitoring patients starting infliximab (r=0.43, P=0.0015), and r=0.40, P=0.007 respectively).78 By contrast, in 33 patients treated with upadacitinib, BWT, CDS, BWS, i-FAT, and MUC were correlated with clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic responses by week 8 of therapy (P <0.001).79

The IUS was also adequate for monitoring the response to other Janus Kinase Inhibitors, such as tofacitinib and filgotinib. In a study evaluating effectiveness of tofacitinib in 27 patients, within 8 weeks of therapy, median bowel BWT decreased from 4.30 mm to 3.50 mm (P =0.030), reduction was significantly greater in responders than in non-responders (0.4 vs 0.05 mm; 10.9% vs 0.72%; P =0.026), and was accompanied by improvements in CDS (mLS 2 to 0; P =0.003) and MUC score (7.6 to 6.2; P =0.011).80 All findings correlated with endoscopic activity (ρ = 0.697, 0.488, 0.738) and clinical activity (ρ = 0.570, 0.435, 0.620), whereas no baseline correlations were observed. In this study a BWT cut-off of 3.6 mm at week 8 discriminated endoscopic healing (AUROC 0.952) and clinical response (AUROC 0.873), while MUC cut-offs of 4.9 and 6.55 predicted endoscopic healing (AUROC 0.923) and clinical response (AUROC 0.850), respectively.

Moreover, a reduction in BWT assessed using IUS also predicted endoscopic response in patient starting filgotinib (OR 32.5, P =0.009 and OR 13.8, P =0.035).81 In the same study, all 23 patients underwent also shear-wave elastography (SWE) however, the baseline findings were not predictive of endoscopic response (OR 1.07, P =0.088).

Predicting Colectomy Risk in UC

Despite advances in biologics and small molecules, colectomy continues to play a role in a substantial proportion of patients with UC who fail medical therapy, meta-analyses report 1-, 5-, and 10-year colectomy risks of 2.8%, 7.0%, and 9.6%, respectively, in the modern era.82 Several studies have investigated the use of IUS in predicting the risk of colectomy.

A prospective multicenter study evaluated whether transmural severity constitutes an independent risk factor for colectomy risk in UC, in comparison to endoscopy, 141 patients underwent IUS after endoscopy in a blinded fashion, activity was described respectively by MUC and Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES).83 Both MES (HR 3.15; P=0.02) and MUC (HR 1.48; P<0.001) were associated with colectomy risk, but only MUC remained independently predictive in the multivariable analysis (HR 1.46; P=0.02) and in clinically active patients (OR 1.53; P=0.03). MUC outperformed MES in predicting colectomy (AUROC 0.83 vs 0.71; P=0.02), with an optimal cut-off of 7.7. The prognostic role of IUS was assessed in newly diagnosed UC patients (less than 1 year); 193 patients underwent IUS assessments at diagnosis and at 3 and 12 months.84 IUS-detected inflammation at diagnosis independently predicted colectomy within 3 months, with a BWT >6 mm as the optimal threshold (OR, 38; P <0.0001). At 3 months, 59% achieved TH, which was associated with higher rates of steroid-free clinical remission and reduced steroid use (6% vs 19%; P =0.036), as well as increased rates of clinical and TH at 12 months. A further study investigates IUS predictive value for long-term outcomes in hospitalized patients with severe UC Among 56 patients, 45 (80%) required surgery within 12 months, at 48±24 h evaluation a BWT ≥4 mm predicted colectomy (OR 9.5; P=0.03), shorter time to colectomy, and treatment intensification, while BWT ≥3 mm predicted intervention (OR 3.6; P=0.03).74

IUS in Acute Severe Ulcerative Colitis (ASUC)

Intestinal ultrasound has also been used in hospitalized patients to assess therapeutic response in the setting of ASUC. These patients require treatment with intravenous corticosteroids and rescue therapy with cyclosporine or infliximab.85 A pilot study aimed to determine whether IUS can predict steroid-refractory disease. Ten patients hospitalized for ASUC were enrolled and received IUS 24, 72 hours, and 7 days following steroids therapy initiation.86 Responders to corticosteroid therapy showed a lower median colonic BWT of 4.6 mm (4.2—5.6 mm) than patients requiring salvage therapy, who had a median BWT of 6.2 mm (6–7.9 mm) (P =0.009). Moreover, the presence of any segment with a BWT exceeding 6 mm was significantly associated with the need for salvage therapy (P =0.033). In another blinded observational multicenter study, 56 inpatients with severe UC were assessed using the IUS after 48 ± 24 h and 6 ± 1 days of hospitalisation.87 At 48 ± 24 h, responders showed significantly lower BWT (3.1 vs 4.9 mm), greater absolute (−1.9 vs −0.2 mm), and relative reduction (−35.9% vs −4.1%) (all P <0.001). A ≤20% reduction predicted nonresponse with 84.2% sensitivity and 78.4% specificity (AUROC 0.85), whereas a >20% reduction strongly predicted response (OR 22.6; P =0.001). An additional multi-center prospective cohort study aimed to determine the accuracy of early IUS in predicting IV corticosteroid failure in ASUC, baseline BWT did not differ between corticosteroid responders and non-responders (5.50 vs 5.85 mm; P =0.66), but was significantly lower in responders at 72h (3.85 vs 5.05 mm; P=0.02), with greater muscularis and submucosal thinning. An absolute BWT reduction <1.40 mm or relative reduction <20% predicted non-response (AUROC 0.76—0.78), while combining BWT reduction with absent CDS improved prediction (Significancy 81.3%, specificity 87.5%, AUROC 0.91). The risk of colectomy was also shown to be predictable using early intestinal ultrasound assessment in the acute setting, in a Norwegian study it was reported that a total BWT ≥9 mm at days 2—3 following admission predicted the need for colectomy (according to Lindgren’s Criteria) with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 50%.88

Both AGA and ECCO recommend day 3 for clinical response assessment in ASUC,89,90 and the available evidence suggests that IUS, when available, could take part in this early assessment of treatment response.

Discussion

As emerged from the abovementioned studies, over recent decades, IUS has evolved into a key modality for monitoring treatment response in inflammatory bowel disease. Its noninvasive profile, low cost, and widespread availability have supported its increasing integration into routine clinical practice, enabling rapid and real-time assessment of disease activity.91 Moreover, its patient-friendly nature has made IUS an attractive tool for both IBD standard care and clinical trials, where frequent and repeated evaluations of treatment response are required (Figure 1). In addition to being well-accepted by patients owing to its minimally invasive nature,92 IUS also appears to have a lower environmental impact than endoscopic monitoring in IBD. A recent analysis of the environmental footprint of a colonoscopy attempted to quantify its carbon footprint, which contributed to emissions equal to driving a car for 255 km or 55 days of emissions for an average European household.93 By contrast IUS has a substantially lower environmental impact and carbon footprint, data from systematic reviews indicate that IUS is associated with a substantially lower carbon footprint, estimated at approximately 0.5—1.5 kg CO2-equivalent per examination, compared with endoscopy (7.8—56.4 kg CO2e), CT (7—20 kg CO2e), and MRI (17—22 kg CO2e). In addition, IUS generates minimal waste, with non-reusable materials largely limited to ultrasound gel containers, in contrast to the considerable volume of disposable supplies required for endoscopy.94

Ultrasonography appears to enhance the sustainability of IBD monitoring not only from an environmental perspective but also from an economic standpoint. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that its use contributes to a reduction in the number of invasive procedures, such as colonoscopy, as well as cross-sectional imaging examinations including magnetic resonance imaging. An experience from a tertiary center in Southeast Asia reported that IUS reduced number of colonoscopies and MRI by more than 60%, with a total cost saving amounting to US$ 92,069 and a total reduction in carbon emissions was 2752 kg CO2e.95 Consistent findings also emerged from an additional retrospective study; IUS findings led to MRE avoidance in 79.4% and colonoscopy in 58.8%). There was an estimated cost saving of US$29,619 and 458.19 kg of CO2e compared to MRI and US $25,940 and 557.40 kg of CO2 e compared to endoscopy. Despite the many advantages of IUS, colonoscopy remains the gold standard for colorectal cancer and dysplasia surveillance, and is therefore unlikely to be completely replaced.96

Another area in which transabdominal IUS has shown lower sensitivity and specificity is associated with rectal involvement34 in patients with both ulcerative proctitis and perianal complications. To address this limitation, ultrasound monitoring is increasingly being complemented by TPUS. A Japanese study reported that BWT in TPUS was the only significant independent predictor of rectal endoscopic and histologic healing (P <0.05) with a better outcome than fecal calprotectin.36 Moreover, a study compared cross-sectional imaging modalities sensitivity in detecting perianal complications of CD comparing them to surgical findings, it emerged that TPUS had a 100% sensitivity in fistula detection and was more sensitive for diagnosis of perianal abscesses than MRI and transrectal sonography.97

In addition to the rectum, the proximal segments of the small bowel represent another area in which IUS has demonstrated certain limitation. In the CACTUS-CD study,98 which prospectively compared IUS with CT/MRI in 407 patients with CD, the majority of missed lesions and undetected strictures were located in the proximal and mid small bowel. Cross-sectional imaging changed management in 9.3% of cases, primarily for surgical planning, therapeutic escalation, or stricture intervention.

Despite their recognized advantages, IUS and TPUS have not yet been widely implemented worldwide. Its broader uptake in clinical practice is limited by several logistical and educational challenges, including restricted access to structured training and the absence of fully standardized examination protocols.23 Another persistent concern regarding ultrasonography is operator dependence. However, a reliability study conducted by De Voogd et al demonstrated excellent interobserver agreement among experienced sonographers in the evaluation of IUS parameters.26 Agreement was nearly perfect BWT, with an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.96 (95% CI 0.92—0.98). Comparable data among non-expert operators are limited, with studies reporting only modest agreement between radiologists after fewer than 50 examinations performed.99 Moreover, training requirements remain substantial, and the majority of available evidence is derived from specialized centers with high levels of expertise.100

Additionally, regarding the standardization of ultrasound findings, a recent systematic review examined the range of US-based definitions of treatment response and remission used to assess the therapeutic effectiveness in IBD. However, the marked heterogeneity in definitions across studies underscores the urgent need for a dedicated consensus to standardize ultrasonographic scores and facilitate their consistent application in IBD management.101

Finally, several technological innovations are currently under development for the IUS, which may further enhance its effectiveness and broaden its adoption in clinical practice. A modality that may further enhance the diagnostic capabilities of IUS is elastography, that has been studied in CD, particularly in the assessment of stricturing disease, where it aims to distinguish inflammatory from fibrotic bowel wall changes. Strain elastography and shear wave elastography have shown variable correlations with histological fibrosis and tissue stiffness, with variable diagnostic performance across studies. Quantitative techniques appear more reliable than qualitative color-pattern approaches.102 In UC a recent study demonstrated that elastography outperformed MUC in moderate-severe fibrosis evaluation,103 but evidence remains limited underscoring the need for further investigations.

Handheld devices, remote patient-performed imaging, elastography, CEUS, and artificial intelligence show promise, with AI achieving reported accuracies up to 94% in classifying bowel wall thickening,101 but all require further technical refinement and robust clinical validation before routine implementation.

Based on the available evidence, IUS may serve as a complementary cross-sectional imaging modality alongside endoscopy in the diagnostic work-up of IBD, allowing assessment of both colon and small bowel segments not accessible by colonoscopy and potentially reducing the need for MRI in selected cases. Moreover, its demonstrated ability to predict clinical response to therapy supports its role in longitudinal disease monitoring. In this context, IUS may help extend intervals between endoscopic assessments when appropriate and provide a valuable point-of-care tool, particularly in scenarios of discordance between clinical presentation and biomarkers. Its non-invasive nature also makes it especially attractive in special populations, such as pediatric and pregnant patients, where minimizing invasive procedures is a priority.

Conclusion

IUS has emerged as a highly effective, non-invasive tool for monitoring inflammatory bowel disease, enabling real-time assessment of transmural inflammation. Available evidence supports its role in detecting early treatment response, predicting clinically relevant outcomes, and guiding timely therapeutic decisions. Its favorable non-invasive nature, repeatability, and cost-effectiveness further support its integration into point-of-care IBD evaluation. However, several key limitations remain, including the lack of universally accepted scoring systems, operator dependency, and limited diffusion. Addressing these gaps through standardization, structured training programs, and prospective validation studies will be essential for broader implementation. In this context, and with ongoing technological advancements, IUS holds strong potential to become a cornerstone of personalized disease monitoring and a central component of treat-to-target strategy in IBD.
