The near extinction of the RAPTOR: a cautionary tale
Authors: Andrew W Kirkpatrick, Eric Herget, Shane Smith, Paul B McBeth, Paul Duffy, Mark Nutley, Chad G Ball
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTrauma hybrid operating rooms (THORs) integrate diagnostic imaging with open surgical capabilities to provide both surgical and interventional radiology procedures, such as may be required by exsanguinating patients, in a single physical location. The world’s first trauma program–designed THOR, designated the Resuscitation with Angiography, Percutaneous Treatments, and Operative Repair (RAPTOR) suite, opened in Calgary in 2013. It was considered to be paradigm shifting at the time; however, the physical space has become largely obsolete and is now disfavoured by key multidisciplinary users who are essential to RAPTOR functionality. Many health care systems worldwide are considering building such THORs, and lessons learned from early adopters may be invaluable to other planners. We critically evaluated the physical, operational, and administrative components of our RAPTOR experience in the context of the published literature. While the concept has great merit in saving lives, future-proofing such resources is critical.

Bleeding is the most common cause of preventable death after traumatic injury and remains a substantial global health problem. Most early trauma deaths are due to hemorrhage, with the median time of death occurring 2.6 hours after admission.1 Thus, timeliness is critical in providing hemorrhage control (HC), as delays in HC or resuscitation without HC are associated with death. Severe noncompressible torso hemorrhage with exsanguination presents one of the greatest challenges, as typically an invasive intracavitary source in the thoracoabdominal or retroperitoneal/pelvic cavities must be accessed and controlled. The clinical challenge is compounded by the fact that thoracoabdominal exsanguination is usually addressed in an operating room (OR) with open surgery, while retroperitoneal/pelvic exsanguination usually requires angioembolization.1,2 Traditionally, this has presented an urgent life or death scenario for clinicians who must decide where to transport a dying patient, potentially based on limited clinical and anatomic information, with grave consequences for the patient if the wrong location is selected.

For this reason, we and others were excited to introduce the concept of the trauma hybrid operating room (THOR), which involves colocating standard surgical capabilities with diagnostic imaging and angioembolization capabilities in the same resuscitative environment.2–5 The concept was initially positioned as “comprehensive, expensive, but fundamentally paradigm-shifting.”1 Benefitting from public philanthropy, the world’s first THOR, specifically designed for and by a trauma program and designated the Resuscitation with Angiography, Percutaneous Treatments and Operative Repair (RAPTOR) suite,2 opened in Calgary in 2013. Today, the RAPTOR suite is unfortunately nearing extinction. Although it functions as a trauma OR, interventional radiologists, vascular surgeons, and orthopedic surgeons avoid performing procedures in the suite because of its outdated imaging technology.4 Critically injured but stabilized patients are currently transferred out of the RAPTOR suite to receive post-damage-control computed tomography (CT) for operative planning and then brought back for completion surgery. The message is clear, the RAPTOR suite is near obsolete. What can be learned from this?

The Promise and the Reality

While we believe the promise of saving lives with access to THORs is real, the literature supporting a mortality benefit is sparse and methodologically limited. The literature can be categorized into articles on RAPTOR suites with and without CT imaging, as this represents a major difference in operational paradigms. When a RAPTOR suite has embedded CT capability, it can typically be used as a one-stop, direct-to-OR resuscitative suite, with seemingly improved survival rates among the most critically injured patients.6 Without CT capability, many critically ill patients will be transported to a separate CT suite either before or after the RAPTOR suite.7 Additionally, RAPTOR suites without CT capability have been associated with process improvements, but not efficacy in survival, even assuming the potential for bias in retrospective studies.7–9 Therefore, THORs, including our RAPTOR suite, have not conclusively shown survival benefits despite substantial costs.3,7–9

In Calgary, the RAPTOR suite was originally envisioned as a comprehensive hemorrhage-control suite for exsanguinating patients, but administrative directives prohibited direct-to-OR practices, thus negating the paradigm of RAPTOR resuscitations and creating scenarios of long blood trails from the emergency department (ED) back to the RAPTOR suite following successful resuscitations in the ED.

The RAPTOR team discouraged

We acknowledge that a cooperative and, ideally, dedicated RAPTOR team is crucial for optimal performance in such a challenging multidisciplinary resuscitative environment.4 An unfortunate administrative misunderstanding discouraged interventional radiology specialists from using the RAPTOR suite electively to build familiarity, which prevented it from becoming an interventional radiology home base. Published recommendations note that teamwork is critical and that all involved services should remain active in a RAPTOR space — not only in emergency situations — in order for it to be functional when needed.3,4

Aditionally, program silos substantially hindered multidisciplinary cooperation. From the earliest use of the RAPTOR suite, diagnostic imaging technicians, who typically support general surgery and trauma surgery operative interventions, did not typically use the state-of-the-art ceiling-mounted imaging equipment. Instead, portable fluoroscopic equipment was brought to the RAPTOR suite to replicate the basic capabilities of an ordinary OR. Alternatively, when diagnostic imaging technicians who were familiar with the RAPTOR-dedicated angiography technology responded to a call from the RAPTOR suite, their absence then paralyzed the interventional radiology suites. Unfortunately, this was often the case for vascular surgeons trying to stent a ruptured aorta, or orthopedic surgeons performing complex bone fixations, which quickly disincentivized both of those groups, discouraging participation.

The key takeaways to help other centres

Owing to the early introduction of our RAPTOR suite, we are regularly asked to present on THORs or to peer review related manuscript submissions to journals. Zaman and colleagues recently emphasized the importance of visiting other RAPTOR centres to understand regrets from previous builds before establishing such a resource.4 We consider honesty about experience with such resources to be a responsibility to the greater trauma community. We have assessed our RAPTOR experience in relation to physical and operational factors discussed by Khoo and colleagues3 and Zaman and colleagues,4 to hopefully provide guidance and share lessons learned with the world trauma community (Table 1). Given their expense, RAPTOR suites necessitate adequate funding to staff, and to obtain and maintain the state-of-the-art equipment.4 We suggest that centres seeking to invest in such a resource should prioritize it for use, with the highest-level commitment from administration to support it as well as the services who will use it, and especially the patients who will benefit from it. The concept of “future-proofing” is crucial,4 and means thinking about replacement, upgrades, and integration of potential new technologies from day 1 into the future. Trauma RAPTOR suites should now contain state-of-the-art CT capabilities to be truly functional. And any functional RAPTOR suite will need true multidisciplinary buy-in, welcoming all the players into these complex spaces. For this to happen, RAPTOR suites need to be used regularly, not saved for rare occasions. Thus, we continue to frame the RAPTOR paradigm as having promise to save lives, but we recognize many complexities in retrospect. Hopefully our critique will benefit other planners in their quest to establish the ultimate THOR.
