Neural correlates of response inhibition following cognitive behavioral therapy in obsessive–compulsive disorder: a randomized controlled EEG study
Authors: Metin Çınaroğlu, Selami Varol Ülker, Eda Yılmazer, Gökben Hızlı Sayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention is an established treatment for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), yet the electrophysiological indices through which symptom improvement occurs remain incompletely understood. Response inhibition deficits have been implicated in OCD and may represent a key neurocognitive target of treatment. The present randomized controlled trial (Clinicaltrials.gov registiration no: NCT07000812) examined whether CBT is associated with changes in behavioral and electrophysiological indices of response inhibition.MethodsSixty adults meeting DSM-5 criteria for OCD were randomized to a 12-week CBT program (n = 30) or a waitlist control condition (n = 30). Clinical severity was assessed using the Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS), alongside measures of obsessive beliefs and intolerance of uncertainty. Response inhibition was evaluated using a Go/No-Go task while electroencephalography was recorded. Mean No-Go P3 amplitude (300–600 ms) at frontocentral regions of interest served as the primary neural outcome. Assessments were conducted at baseline and post-treatment.ResultsRelative to the waitlist group, participants receiving CBT showed a significant reduction in OCD symptom severity, accompanied by improvements in obsessive beliefs and intolerance of uncertainty. Behavioral performance indicated a reduction in commission errors on No-Go trials following CBT. Electrophysiologically, a significant Group × Time interaction was observed for No-Go P3 amplitude, reflecting an increase from pre- to post-treatment in the CBT group, with no meaningful change in the waitlist group. No baseline differences were observed between groups.ConclusionsCBT for OCD was associated with both clinical improvement and enhanced neural indices of response inhibition, as reflected by increased No-Go P3 amplitude. These findings suggest that therapeutic change in OCD may involve increased engagement of electrophysiological indices supporting response inhibition. Integrating electrophysiological measures with clinical outcomes may help clarify the neurocognitive processes underlying effective psychological interventions.

Introduction

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic psychiatric condition characterized by recurrent intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors or mental acts (compulsions) performed to reduce distress [51]. A core feature of OCD is the inability to suppress or “filter out” unwanted thoughts and urges, leading to persistent obsessional anxiety and ritualistic responses [21]. Contemporary cognitive-behavioral models propose that maladaptive beliefs play a central role in the development and maintenance of OCD symptoms [23]. Individuals with OCD frequently exhibit dysfunctional appraisals, including inflated responsibility, threat overestimation [43], perfectionism [31], excessive need to control thoughts [44], and intolerance of uncertainty (IU; [28]). According to these models, intrusive thoughts are misinterpreted as personally significant or dangerous, triggering anxiety and compulsive behaviors intended to reduce perceived threat [59].

Among these cognitive vulnerabilities, intolerance of uncertainty has received increasing attention as a key mechanism underlying OCD [60]. Individuals with elevated IU experience marked discomfort with ambiguity and uncertainty, contributing to excessive checking, reassurance seeking, and other compulsive behaviors aimed at achieving certainty [7, 10]. Because compulsive responding reflects repeated failures to inhibit habitual cognitive and behavioral responses, elevated IU may indirectly contribute to impaired response inhibition by increasing the perceived need to act in response to intrusive thoughts. Consequently, reducing IU is considered an important therapeutic target of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) [29]. Although IU is not a direct neurophysiological marker of response inhibition, it was included in the present study as a secondary cognitive outcome to complement the primary electrophysiological assessment of response inhibition.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) incorporating exposure and response prevention (ERP) is the first-line psychological treatment for OCD [32]. ERP requires patients to confront feared stimuli while deliberately refraining from compulsive responses, thereby reducing anxiety through inhibitory learning and modifying maladaptive beliefs [50, 52]. In addition to restructuring dysfunctional appraisals [13], repeated response prevention provides sustained practice in withholding habitual compulsive behaviors while tolerating uncertainty [5]. From a theoretical perspective, this process may improve behavioral response inhibition alongside cognitive change [20, 38]. CBT delivered over 12–16 sessions consistently produces substantial symptom improvement comparable to first-line pharmacotherapy [3, 49], making it an appropriate intervention for examining both clinical and neurophysiological treatment effects.

While the clinical efficacy of CBT is well established, increasing attention has focused on whether treatment is also associated with changes in neurocognitive processes underlying OCD. Beyond maladaptive beliefs, OCD has been conceptualized as a disorder characterized by impaired response inhibition [36]. Individuals with OCD often demonstrate difficulty suppressing intrusive thoughts and inhibiting prepotent responses [2], accompanied by poorer performance on laboratory measures such as Go/No-Go and stop-signal tasks [35]. These inhibitory deficits are consistently reported across OCD symptom dimensions [56]. Neurobiological models implicate dysfunction within fronto-striatal systems involved in cognitive control [47]. Although the present study does not permit direct assessment of these neural circuits, these models provide a theoretical framework for understanding response inhibition deficits in OCD. Consequently, electrophysiological measures obtained during Go/No-Go tasks offer objective scalp-level indices associated with response inhibition that complement self-report and clinician-rated outcomes.

One efficient way to probe the neural dynamics of response inhibition is through event-related potentials (ERPs) recorded via electroencephalography (EEG) during Go/No-Go tasks [48]. Go/No-Go paradigms require rapid responses to frequent “Go” stimuli and withholding of response to infrequent “No-Go” stimuli, thus engaging impulse control processes [30]. These tasks reliably elicit two key ERP components associated with cognitive control: a fronto-central N2 and a subsequent P3 on No-Go trials [22]. The No-Go N2 is a negative-going wave peaking around 200–300 ms after stimulus onset; evidence suggests it reflects initial conflict detection or monitoring of response competition rather than inhibition per se [59]. In contrast, the No-Go P3 (also called the P3 or No-Go P3) is a positive deflection around 300–600 ms post-stimulus, maximal over fronto-central scalp regions, which is widely regarded as an electrophysiological signature of the active inhibition process [27]. Specifically, a larger No-Go P3 amplitude is widely regarded as an electrophysiological marker associated with successful response inhibition and the allocation of cognitive control resources during No-Go trials. Previous ERP and neuroimaging studies have suggested that the No-Go P3 may reflect activity within frontal cognitive control systems, although scalp EEG alone does not permit direct localization of its neural generators [33]. Accordingly, the No-Go N2 and No-Go P3 provide temporally sensitive electrophysiological indices of different stages of inhibitory processing, with the No-Go P3 generally considered to reflect later stages of response inhibition and inhibitory attention.

Response inhibition can be assessed using several neurocognitive paradigms, including the Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal, and Stroop tasks, each capturing partially distinct aspects of cognitive control. Whereas the Stop-Signal Task primarily assesses the ability to cancel an already initiated response and the Stroop paradigm measures interference control during conflicting stimulus processing, the Go/No-Go task evaluates the ability to withhold a prepotent motor response before its execution. The present study employed the Go/No-Go paradigm because the associated No-Go P3 component is among the most extensively validated ERP measures of response inhibition and has been widely used to examine treatment-related electrophysiological changes in psychiatric populations.

ERP research in OCD has revealed anomalies in these inhibition-related components, although findings have been somewhat mixed. Some studies report that OCD patients exhibit an exaggerated No-Go P3 or a P3 that does not discriminate between No-Go and Go stimuli, possibly reflecting overactive but poorly focused frontal engagement [16]. For example, Di Russo et al., [42]found that OCD patients had equally large P3 responses to Go and No-Go stimuli (whereas healthy individuals showed a selective P3 increase for No-Go trials), suggesting that OCD patients may over-recruit frontal attention resources indiscriminately. This could indicate hyperfrontality or “over-focused attention” in OCD, consistent with other evidence of frontal hyperactivity in the disorder. Other studies have observed topographical and timing abnormalities: one investigation noted that OCD patients (and anxious controls) lacked the normal frontally maximal N2 seen in healthy subjects and failed to show the expected lengthening of N2 latency under increased inhibitory load [26]. There have also been conflicting reports about P3 amplitude in OCD, with some studies finding reduced P3 amplitudes at baseline (interpreted as impaired engagement of inhibitory processes) and others finding enhanced P3 amplitudes (potentially reflecting compensatory hyperactivation, [8]. These discrepancies likely stem from differences in task design, patient characteristics (medication status, comorbidities), and small sample sizes across studies. Nonetheless, the overall literature supports the notion that OCD is associated with atypical neural responses during inhibition tasks, making the No-Go N2 and P3 valuable neural biomarkers of OCD-related cognitive dysfunction [24].

Importantly, little prior research has examined whether these neural indices of inhibition change with effective psychotherapy [55]. Demonstrating changes in ERP components after CBT would suggest that therapy not only reduces clinical symptoms but also modifies the underlying neurocognitive processes. In other domains of OCD research, there is evidence that treatment can normalize aberrant brain activity [4]. Functional neuroimaging studies have shown that both CBT and pharmacotherapy can lead to decreased hypermetabolism in the cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical circuitry (e.g. reduced activation of the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and caudate nucleus) in conjunction with symptom improvement [14]. Likewise, emerging EEG findings indicate that some ERP abnormalities in OCD tend to normalize following successful treatment [12]. For example, several studies have reported that the P3 component – often measured in an auditory oddball task as an index of attention and working memory – is blunted or delayed in OCD patients relative to controls, but after treatment P3 amplitudes increase (toward normal levels) and latency shortens [15]. In one study, OCD patients showed significantly lower P3 amplitudes at baseline than healthy individuals, but after 12 weeks of SSRI medication, P3 amplitudes increased to near-control values, and the magnitude of P3 increase was correlated with reductions in Y-BOCS scores [45]. Similar enhancement of P3 amplitude has been observed one year after combined psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy, again with larger P3 changes accompanying greater symptom relief. These findings, although heterogeneous, suggest that effective OCD treatments can lead to measurable electrophysiological changes, reflecting a potential “changes relative to baseline” of information processing in the brain. However, to date most treatment-related EEG studies in OCD have focused on resting-state rhythms or general cognitive ERPs (like oddball P3), rather than response-inhibition specific ERPs. It remains largely unexplored whether CBT can enhance neural markers of response inhibition (such as the No-Go P3) which are directly relevant to the core neurocognitive deficits in OCD.

Given the above considerations, the present study sought to integrate clinical, cognitive, and neurophysiological outcomes in a randomized controlled trial of CBT for OCD. By assessing symptom severity, dysfunctional obsessive beliefs, and EEG-based measures of response inhibition in tandem, we aimed to capture a more comprehensive picture of treatment effects. Such an approach is in line with calls to complement traditional symptom scales with objective biomarkers in psychiatric research. In addition to the primary neurophysiological assessment of response inhibition, we included two cognitive measures linked to OCD psychopathology—the Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-20) and the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12)—to evaluate cognitive changes associated with CBT. These measures were included as secondary clinical outcomes to complement the primary electrophysiological assessment rather than as direct measures of response inhibition. In parallel, we employed a Go/No-Go task with ERP recording to probe changes in neural inhibitory processing, with the No-Go P3 defined a priori as our primary neural outcome (and No-Go N2 as a secondary index). We hypothesized that, relative to the waitlist control, CBT would produce significant reductions in OCD symptom severity (primary clinical outcome) together with improvements in response inhibition, reflected by increased No-Go P3 amplitude (principal electrophysiological outcome). As secondary outcomes, we expected reductions in obsessive beliefs and intolerance of uncertainty, reflecting cognitive changes associated with treatment. Furthermore, we expected that CBT would improve response inhibition at both behavioral and neural levels – manifested as fewer commission errors on the Go/No-Go task and an increase in No-Go P3 amplitude post-treatment, indicating enhanced engagement of the brain’s response inhibition mechanisms. By evaluating these multi-level outcomes, the study aimed to elucidate whether successful therapy for OCD not only lessens observable symptoms but also remediates the dysfunctional cognitions and neurocognitive processes thought to underlie the disorder.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a randomized controlled, parallel-group design to evaluate the effects of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) on obsessive–compulsive symptom severity and neural indices of response inhibition in individuals with obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD). A total of 60 participants meeting DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for OCD were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either a CBT intervention group or a waitlist control group. Participants allocated to the CBT group received 12 individual CBT sessions, whereas those assigned to the waitlist group received no psychological intervention during the study period. Assessments were conducted at baseline (pre-treatment) and immediately following completion of the 12-week intervention (post-treatment). The prespecified primary clinical outcome was obsessive–compulsive symptom severity, assessed using the clinician-rated Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS). The prespecified primary neurophysiological outcome was mean No-Go P3 amplitude (300–600 ms) recorded during a Go/No-Go task. Prespecified secondary outcomes included obsessive beliefs measured with the Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-20), intolerance of uncertainty measured with the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12), depressive symptoms assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II), anxiety symptoms assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), behavioral performance on the Go/No-Go task (reaction time and No-Go commission errors), and the No-Go N2 amplitude. At both assessment time points, participants underwent electroencephalography (EEG) recording using a 14-channel system, including resting-state EEG (eyes-open and eyes-closed) and an event-related potential (ERP) paradigm assessing response inhibition. The trial was conducted at the Üsküdar University Neuro-Psychology Laboratory and prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07000812; registered February 6, 2025). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines. No important changes to the study protocol, prespecified outcome measures, or statistical analysis plan were made after trial commencement.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination planning of this randomized controlled trial.

Participants

Participants were clinically diagnosed individuals with obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) recruited from multiple clinical settings in Istanbul. The primary recruitment source was a private outpatient psychiatry clinic operated by one of the study’s authors, a professor of psychiatry (GHS). Additional participants were recruited through the same author’s broader professional clinical network, including governmental hospital psychiatry outpatient clinics and affiliated mental health services. All participants met DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for OCD, as determined through a structured clinical diagnostic interview based on the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Initial diagnoses were established by licensed psychiatrists at the respective clinical sites. Diagnostic status was subsequently confirmed through individual, face-to-face clinical interviews conducted by E.Y., an assistant professor in clinical psychology with experience in the assessment and diagnosis of obsessive–compulsive disorder. Inclusion criteria were: (a) a primary diagnosis of OCD according to DSM-5 criteria, (b) age ≥ 18 years, and (c) symptom severity consistent with at least a moderate level of OCD as assessed by the clinician-rated Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS). Individuals presenting with subclinical obsessive–compulsive symptoms who did not meet full DSM-5 diagnostic criteria were not included. Exclusion criteria included current psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, neurological conditions, or any medical or psychiatric condition that could substantially interfere with EEG recording or participation in cognitive behavioral therapy. Participant recruitment and baseline assessments were conducted between February 2025 and November 2025. Post-treatment assessments were completed by February 2026. Participants receiving SSRI treatment were required to maintain a stable medication regimen throughout the study period. No changes in medication type or dosage were reported during the intervention period.

Randomization and blinding

Following confirmation of eligibility and completion of baseline clinical and EEG assessments, participants were randomly assigned to either the CBT intervention group or the waitlist control group in a 1:1 allocation ratio. Randomization was conducted using a computer-generated random sequence to ensure unbiased group assignment. Allocation was performed after baseline assessments to prevent expectancy effects on initial clinical ratings or EEG recordings. Given the nature of the psychological intervention, blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible. However, several steps were taken to reduce potential bias. Clinical diagnostic confirmation was completed prior to randomization, and outcome assessments were conducted using standardized, well-validated instruments. Importantly, EEG data preprocessing and analysis were performed by researchers who were blinded to group allocation and assessment time point, with all EEG datasets coded using anonymized participant identifiers. The clinician responsible for confirming diagnoses did not participate in treatment delivery, and therapists delivering CBT were not involved in EEG data analysis. This partial blinding strategy was implemented to minimize expectancy and confirmation biases while maintaining the practical feasibility of a randomized controlled psychotherapy trial. The random allocation sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered allocation codes generated prior to participant enrollment. Group assignments were revealed only after completion of baseline assessments to minimize allocation-related bias. The randomization sequence was generated by a researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, clinical assessment, treatment delivery, or EEG data analysis. Participant enrollment and baseline assessments were completed prior to assignment disclosure. Because of the nature of the intervention, neither participants nor clinical assessors were blinded to treatment allocation. However, EEG preprocessing and analysis were performed by researchers blinded to both treatment allocation and assessment time point. Clinical assessments, including the Y-BOCS, were administered using standardized administration procedures by E.Y., who was not involved in treatment delivery or EEG preprocessing and analysis. Although assessor blinding was not feasible because of the nature of the intervention, separating treatment delivery from outcome assessment was intended to minimize assessment bias.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size for the repeated-measures mixed-design ANOVA examining the Group × Time interaction. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and statistical power of 0.80, the required total sample size was estimated at 54 participants. To account for potential attrition and incomplete EEG data, 60 participants were recruited and randomized.

Procedure

Following initial contact and provision of study information, all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Diagnostic eligibility was first established in the recruiting clinical settings by licensed psychiatrists and subsequently confirmed through individual, face-to-face clinical interviews conducted by a senior psychologist (EY) using DSM-5–based structured diagnostic criteria for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD). Only participants meeting full diagnostic criteria were enrolled in the study. After confirmation of eligibility, participants completed the baseline (pre-treatment) assessment, which included clinician-administered and self-report clinical measures. Baseline electroencephalography (EEG) recording was then conducted at the Üsküdar University Neuro-Psychology Laboratory under standardized conditions. The EEG session consisted of resting-state recording followed by an event-related potential (ERP) task designed to assess response inhibition. Upon completion of baseline assessments, participants were randomly allocated to either the CBT intervention group or the waitlist control group. Participants assigned to the intervention group subsequently received 12 sessions of individual cognitive behavioral therapy, delivered online via a secure videoconferencing platform. Therapy sessions were conducted on a weekly basis. Participants in the waitlist control group did not receive any psychological intervention during the study period. Immediately following completion of the 12 CBT sessions, all participants underwent the post-treatment assessment, which mirrored the baseline assessment in structure and content. This included re-administration of the clinical outcome measures and a second EEG recording session conducted using the same protocols and equipment as at baseline. Participants in the waitlist group completed post-treatment assessments after an equivalent time interval. Throughout the study, assessment procedures, EEG recordings, and treatment delivery were conducted according to the registered protocol to ensure consistency across participants and assessment time points. Adverse events and participant discontinuation reasons were monitored throughout the study period. No serious adverse events related to CBT or EEG procedures were reported. Participants receiving SSRI treatment continued their pre-existing pharmacotherapy throughout the study period under the supervision of their treating psychiatrists. Pharmacotherapy was not administered or modified as part of the study protocol, and participants who were not receiving SSRIs at baseline did not initiate medication during the trial.

Intervention

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

Participants in the intervention group received individual, manualized cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for obsessive–compulsive disorder, grounded in established exposure and response prevention (ERP)–based treatment models. The intervention protocol was informed by widely used and empirically supported CBT manuals for OCD (e.g., ERP-focused CBT frameworks) and was adapted for online delivery while preserving core therapeutic components. The CBT program consisted of 12 weekly sessions, each lasting approximately 50–60 min, delivered via a secure videoconferencing platform. Treatment was structured into three sequential phases, with flexibility to accommodate individual symptom presentations while maintaining adherence to the manualized framework.

Phase 1: assessment, psychoeducation, and case formulation (sessions 1–3)

The initial phase focused on establishing a therapeutic alliance, providing psychoeducation about OCD, and developing an individualized cognitive–behavioral formulation. Therapists introduced the functional relationship between obsessions, anxiety, compulsions, and short-term relief, emphasizing the role of compulsive behaviors in maintaining symptoms. Participants collaboratively identified their primary obsessional themes and compulsive responses, and an individualized exposure hierarchy was constructed. Early cognitive interventions targeted maladaptive beliefs related to inflated responsibility, threat overestimation, and intolerance of uncertainty. Initial homework assignments included self-monitoring of obsessions and compulsions and behavioral experiments aimed at increasing awareness of symptom patterns.

Phase 2: exposure and response prevention (sessions 4–9)

The core treatment phase emphasized systematic in vivo and imaginal exposure combined with prevention of compulsive responses. Exposure exercises were conducted both during sessions and as structured homework assignments, progressing from lower- to higher-ranked items on the exposure hierarchy. Examples included refraining from checking behaviors following perceived mistakes, delaying or abstaining from washing after contamination-related triggers, and tolerating uncertainty in situations involving potential harm or incompleteness. Therapists guided participants in remaining in exposure situations until anxiety naturally decreased or became tolerable, reinforcing learning that feared outcomes did not occur or could be endured without engaging in compulsions. Cognitive strategies during this phase focused on modifying beliefs that interfered with response prevention (e.g., “I must feel completely certain before stopping,” or “not acting means I am irresponsible”).

Phase 3: consolidation and relapse prevention (sessions 10–12)

The final phase focused on consolidating gains achieved during ERP and enhancing long-term maintenance. Participants practiced exposure exercises with reduced therapist guidance, promoting autonomy and generalization of skills. Relapse prevention strategies were introduced, including identification of early warning signs, development of coping plans for future stressors, and changes relative to baseline of symptom fluctuations. Cognitive review emphasized flexible responding to intrusive thoughts rather than attempts to eliminate them. Treatment concluded with a summary of progress and formulation of a personalized maintenance plan.

Throughout treatment, between-session homework assignments were a central component and were reviewed at the beginning of each session. Adherence to exposure tasks and response prevention was emphasized as critical for treatment efficacy. Although therapy was delivered online, the structure, sequencing, and therapeutic principles were consistent with established face-to-face ERP-based CBT protocols.

Participants allocated to the waitlist control group did not receive any psychological intervention during the 12-week study period. Individuals who were receiving pharmacotherapy at study entry continued their stable medication regimen under the supervision of their treating psychiatrists, and no changes in medication type or dosage were permitted during the study. Participants assigned to the waitlist group did not receive scheduled therapeutic contact, structured psychological support, or telephone follow-up from the research team, apart from routine study scheduling and post-treatment assessment appointments. Following completion of the post-treatment assessment, participants in the waitlist group were offered the opportunity to receive the CBT intervention.

Therapists and treatment fidelity

CBT was delivered by M.Ç. and S.V.Ü., both assistant professors in clinical psychology with formal training and clinical experience in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), exposure and response prevention (ERP), and psychotherapy for obsessive–compulsive disorder. Therapists followed a manual-guided treatment framework to ensure consistency across cases. Treatment fidelity was supported through regular clinical supervision and review of session content. Therapists documented session themes, exposure exercises, and homework assignments using standardized session checklists, which were reviewed to monitor adherence to the treatment protocol. Therapists involved in treatment delivery were not involved in EEG data preprocessing or analysis. Clinical assessors responsible for diagnostic confirmation and outcome assessments did not participate in treatment delivery, thereby reducing potential assessment bias.

Clinical measures

Clinical assessments were administered at baseline (pre-treatment) and post-treatment by trained clinicians who were not involved in treatment delivery. All measures were administered in their validated Turkish versions, and scoring followed standard procedures for each instrument. The Y-BOCS interviews were administered by E.Y., who was trained in standardized administration of the instrument and was not involved in treatment delivery or EEG preprocessing.

Obsessive–compulsive symptom severity

Obsessive–compulsive symptom severity was assessed using the clinician-rated Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS), which served as the primary clinical outcome measure. The Y-BOCS was originally developed by Goodman and colleagues [18] as a semi-structured interview designed to assess the severity of obsessions and compulsions independently of symptom content. It consists of 10 items rated on a 5-point scale, yielding a total score (range 0–40) as well as separate obsession and compulsion subscale scores. The Turkish version of the Y-BOCS was validated by Tek et al., [53] demonstrating excellent interrater reliability and strong construct validity in Turkish patients with obsessive–compulsive disorder. Reported interrater reliability coefficients for the Turkish Y-BOCS total score exceeded 0.95, indicating very high agreement among raters YBOCS. The Y-BOCS is widely regarded as the gold-standard outcome measure in OCD treatment trials and was therefore selected as the primary indicator of treatment efficacy.

Obsessive beliefs

Maladaptive cognitive beliefs associated with OCD were assessed using the Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire–Short Form (OBQ-20). The OBQ was originally developed by the Obsessive Compulsive Cognitions Working Group [37] to assess dysfunctional belief domains implicated in cognitive models of OCD, including inflated responsibility and threat estimation, importance and control of thoughts, and perfectionism/need for certainty. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the OBQ-20 were conducted by Yorulmaz and colleagues [60], who reported acceptable to good internal consistency coefficients across subscales (Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.78 to 0.80), as well as evidence of good construct and convergent validity in Turkish adult samples. In the present study, the OBQ-20 was included as a secondary outcome measure to evaluate changes in maladaptive obsessive beliefs following CBT and their relationship with electrophysiological indices associated with response inhibition.

Intolerance of uncertainty

Intolerance of uncertainty was measured using the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale – Short Form (IUS-12). The original short form of the IUS was developed by Carleton et al., [6] to assess emotional, cognitive, and behavioral reactions to uncertain situations. The scale consists of 12 items rated on a Likert-type scale and yields a total score, with higher scores indicating greater intolerance of uncertainty. The Turkish version of the IUS-12 was validated by Sarıçam and colleagues [46], who reported strong internal consistency for the total scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.88), with subscale α coefficients of 0.84 for prospective anxiety and 0.77 for inhibitory anxiety. The Turkish IUS-12 also demonstrated good test–retest reliability and construct validity IUS-12. Given the central role of intolerance of uncertainty in compulsive responding and exposure-based interventions, the IUS-12 was included as a mechanistic variable closely aligned with treatment targets.

EEG data acquisition

Electroencephalographic (EEG) data were recorded using a Emotiv EPOC wireless EEG system equipped with 14 saline-based electrodes arranged according to the international 10–20 system (AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7, O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4). EEG signals were referenced online to the system’s default reference configuration and digitized at a sampling rate of 128 Hz, which is sufficient for analysis of low- and mid-latency event-related potential (ERP) components such as the N2 and P3. Recordings were conducted in a quiet, electrically shielded laboratory room at the Üsküdar University Neuro-Psychology Laboratory. Participants were seated comfortably in an upright position at a fixed viewing distance from the stimulus monitor. Prior to recording, electrode contact quality was checked and optimized according to manufacturer guidelines to ensure stable signal acquisition across all channels. Participants were instructed to minimize head movement, jaw tension, and eye blinks during recordings. EEG acquisition was performed by S.V.Ü., an assistant professor with experience in EEG acquisition and electrophysiological research, following standardized laboratory procedures at the Üsküdar University Neuro-Psychology Laboratory. Electrode placement, signal quality verification, and recording procedures were completed according to the laboratory’s standard operating protocol.

ERP recording rationale

In addition to resting-state EEG, event-related potentials (ERPs) were recorded during a Go/No-Go task to index response inhibition, a core neurocognitive mechanism implicated in obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) and a primary target of exposure and response prevention–based cognitive behavioral therapy. ERPs provide millisecond-level temporal resolution, allowing precise characterization of neural processes associated with stimulus evaluation, conflict detection, and response inhibition.

The Go/No-Go paradigm reliably elicits two well-established ERP components associated with response inhibition: The No-Go N2, a negative deflection occurring approximately 200–350 ms following stimulus onset over frontocentral scalp regions, is commonly interpreted as reflecting conflict monitoring and early inhibitory processing. The No-Go P3, a later positive deflection typically observed between 300 and 600 ms, is associated with successful response inhibition and allocation of cognitive control resources.

Extensive prior research has demonstrated that individuals with OCD show alterations in these components—most consistently in the No-Go P3—reflecting difficulties in suppressing prepotent responses and terminating action tendencies. Importantly, changes in No-Go–related ERP amplitudes have been shown to be sensitive to cognitive-behavioral interventions, making them suitable neural markers for treatment-related change. Given the study’s focus on CBT-related modulation of response inhibition, the No-Go P3 amplitude was defined a priori as the primary ERP outcome, with the No-Go N2 included as a secondary electrophysiological measure [37].

Although multiple electrophysiological and behavioral indices of response inhibition were assessed, the No-Go P3 was prioritized as the principal electrophysiological measure because it is widely regarded as the most robust ERP marker of response inhibition and the allocation of cognitive control resources during successful response suppression. Compared with the earlier No-Go N2 component, which is thought to primarily reflect conflict detection and response monitoring, the No-Go P3 more directly indexes the implementation of response inhibition and has demonstrated greater sensitivity to treatment-related neurocognitive changes. This prioritization was consistent with the study hypothesis that CBT would enhance electrophysiological indices supporting response inhibition and was informed by previous ERP research identifying the No-Go P3 as a reliable neurophysiological marker of response inhibition in both healthy individuals and clinical populations.

EEG experimental paradigm

EEG data were collected during both resting-state recording and a task-based ERP paradigm designed to elicit neural markers of response inhibition. The task-based paradigm was implemented using a visual Go/No-Go task, which allowed examination of time-locked frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitudes associated with the execution and suppression of motor actions. Resting-state EEG was recorded to assess baseline neural activity independent of task demands. Participants completed two resting conditions: eyes open and eyes closed, each lasting approximately 4 min. During the eyes-open condition, participants were instructed to fixate on a centrally presented cross on a black screen and to remain still while keeping their eyes open. During the eyes-closed condition, participants were instructed to close their eyes, remain awake, and minimize movement. Resting-state recordings were conducted prior to the task-based ERP paradigm.

Go/No-Go task (ERP paradigm)

Task rationale

The Go/No-Go task was selected to probe response inhibition, a core neurocognitive process implicated in obsessive–compulsive disorder and directly targeted by exposure and response prevention–based CBT. In everyday terms, the task requires participants to repeatedly perform a simple motor response and, on less frequent trials, to withhold that response. This structure reliably induces a prepotent tendency to respond, making the inhibition of action on No-Go trials cognitively demanding. From an electrophysiological perspective, this task allows the measurement of event-related potentials (ERPs)—small, time-locked changes in the EEG signal that occur in response to specific stimuli. By averaging EEG activity across many repetitions of the same event (e.g., No-Go trials), consistent neural responses related to inhibitory processing can be isolated from background EEG activity.

Participants were seated comfortably in front of a computer monitor in a dimly lit room. Visual stimuli consisted of single white letters presented on a black background. The letter “X” served as the Go stimulus, requiring a speeded button press, whereas the letter “Y” served as the No-Go stimulus, requiring participants to withhold their response. Participants completed a computerized Go/No-Go task consisting of a brief practice block followed by 240 experimental trials presented in two blocks of 120 trials. Go trials comprised 75% of the trials (n = 180) and No-Go trials comprised 25% (n = 60). Stimuli were presented in a pseudo-randomized order to establish a prepotent tendency to respond. The assignment of Go and No-Go stimuli was not counterbalanced across participants; the same stimulus-response mapping was maintained for all participants at both baseline and post-treatment assessments. Participants responded to Go stimuli by pressing the spacebar with their dominant hand and were instructed to withhold responses to No-Go stimuli. Resting-state EEG was always recorded before administration of the Go/No-Go task, and the task order was identical at both assessment time points.

Trial structure and timing

Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 300–500 ms, followed by presentation of the stimulus letter for approximately 250 ms. Participants were given a 1000-ms response window following stimulus onset. The inter-trial interval was jittered between 800 and 1200 ms to reduce anticipatory responding and temporal predictability. A brief practice block was completed before the experimental trials to ensure task comprehension. A short rest period was provided between the two experimental blocks. Total task duration was approximately 10–12 min, yielding a sufficient number of artifact-free trials for reliable ERP averaging, particularly for No-Go trials.

EEG preprocessing and ERP extraction

EEG preprocessing and ERP analyses were performed offline using R-based analysis pipelines by researchers who were blinded to treatment allocation and assessment time point. Continuous EEG data were visually inspected prior to preprocessing. A band-pass filter of 0.1–30 Hz was applied to remove slow-frequency drift and high-frequency noise, together with a 50-Hz notch filter to reduce electrical line interference. Data were re-referenced to the common average reference, consistent with standard procedures for the Emotiv EPOC system.

Continuous EEG recordings were segmented into epochs extending from − 200 ms to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset. Baseline correction was performed using the − 200 to 0 ms prestimulus interval. Epochs containing excessive movement artifacts or amplifier saturation were automatically rejected using a ± 100 µV threshold and were subsequently confirmed by visual inspection. Ocular artifacts, including eye blinks and eye movements, were minimized using automated artifact correction procedures. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was not performed because of the limited number of channels available with the 14-channel Emotiv EPOC system.

Channels with poor signal quality were identified using the manufacturer’s signal quality indicators and excluded when necessary. Participants with excessive artifacts resulting in an insufficient number of artifact-free No-Go trials were excluded from ERP analyses. A minimum of 20 artifact-free No-Go trials was required for inclusion. Across participants included in the final analyses, an average of approximately 34 artifact-free No-Go trials were retained at each assessment.

The No-Go P3 was defined as the mean amplitude within the 300–600 ms poststimulus interval. The No-Go N2 was quantified as the mean amplitude within the 200–350 ms interval. Based on the Emotiv EPOC electrode configuration and previous Go/No-Go ERP literature, the frontocentral region of interest consisted of electrodes F3, F4, FC5, and FC6, which provide the closest approximation to the conventional frontocentral scalp distribution of response inhibition–related ERP activity. Mean amplitudes were averaged across these electrodes for statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in accordance with the a priori hypotheses of the study and followed standard practices for randomized controlled trials with repeated measurements. Analyses were performed using a combination of iMotions, R, and IBM SPSS Statistics, each serving a distinct role in the analytic workflow. The iMotions platform was used exclusively for EEG data acquisition, stimulus presentation, behavioral response logging, and event-marker synchronization. All subsequent EEG preprocessing, ERP extraction, and feature computation were conducted offline using R-based analysis pipelines. Inferential statistical analyses integrating EEG, behavioral, and clinical data were performed using SPSS. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the nominal level of statistical significance was set at p < .05, unless otherwise specified.

The prespecified primary clinical outcome was obsessive–compulsive symptom severity, assessed using the clinician-rated Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS). Prespecified secondary outcomes, consistent with the prospectively registered protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07000812), included ERP component amplitudes recorded during EEG tasks, resting-state EEG activity, depressive symptoms (BDI-II), anxiety symptoms (BAI), and ERP latencies. Within the prespecified ERP analyses, the No-Go P3 amplitude was designated a priori in the study protocol and statistical analysis plan as the principal electrophysiological measure because it directly indexes response inhibition, the primary neurocognitive mechanism targeted by CBT. Treatment effects were evaluated using mixed-design repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVA), with Group (CBT vs. waitlist) as the between-subjects factor and Time (pre-treatment vs. post-treatment) as the within-subjects factor. The primary test of treatment efficacy was the Group × Time interaction. Secondary analyses examined changes in obsessive beliefs (OBQ-20) and intolerance of uncertainty (IUS-12) using the same analytical framework.

The primary analyses followed a complete-case approach and included participants who completed both baseline and post-treatment assessments and had usable clinical and electrophysiological data. Missing post-treatment data resulting from participant attrition were not imputed because the proportion of missing data was small (7 of 60 randomized participants; 11.7%), attrition was balanced between the CBT (n = 3) and waitlist (n = 4) groups, and no evidence suggested differential dropout related to treatment. Consequently, all primary and secondary analyses were performed on the final analytic sample of 53 participants (CBT = 27; waitlist = 26).

Because the primary outcome was prespecified and all secondary outcomes were hypothesis-driven and defined a priori, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Accordingly, secondary outcome analyses should be interpreted as supportive of the primary findings.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine whether SSRI use influenced the primary findings. SSRI status was entered as a covariate in the mixed-design models. Adjustment for SSRI use did not materially alter the pattern of results.

Results

A total of 60 participants meeting DSM-5 criteria for obsessive–compulsive disorder were randomized to either the CBT intervention group (n = 30) or the waitlist control group (n = 30). During the course of the study, 3 participants in the CBT group discontinued participation prior to post-treatment assessment, and 4 participants in the waitlist group were lost to follow-up. Reasons for attrition included scheduling difficulties, other health issues and family conflicts. No participants discontinued due to adverse events related to the intervention or EEG procedures. The final analytic sample consisted of 53 participants (CBT = 27, waitlist = 26) who completed both baseline and post-treatment assessments. Primary clinical outcome: obsessive–compulsive symptom severity (Y-BOCS)

Changes in obsessive–compulsive symptom severity were examined using the clinician-rated Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS). A mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA with Group (CBT vs. waitlist) as the between-subjects factor and Time (pre- vs. post-treatment) as the within-subjects factor revealed a significant Group × Time interaction, indicating differential symptom change between groups. Participants in the CBT group showed a substantial reduction in Y-BOCS total scores from baseline to post-treatment, whereas the waitlist group showed no meaningful change over the same period. There were no significant baseline differences between groups, confirming successful randomization. Post hoc comparisons indicated a large within-group effect for the CBT group, while changes in the waitlist group were small and non-significant. The magnitude of between-group differences at post-treatment was consistent with clinically meaningful improvement following CBT. To facilitate interpretation of the clinical significance of the findings, treatment response and remission rates were also examined. Treatment response was defined as a ≥ 35% reduction in Y-BOCS total score from baseline, whereas remission was defined as a post-treatment Y-BOCS total score of ≤ 12. In the CBT group, 21 of 27 participants (77.8%) met criteria for treatment response, compared with 2 of 26 participants (7.7%) in the waitlist group (χ² = 24.93, p < .001). Furthermore, 10 participants (37.0%) in the CBT group achieved remission, whereas 1 participant (3.8%) in the waitlist group met remission criteria (χ² = 8.09, p = .004). These findings indicate that the statistically significant reduction in OCD symptom severity was accompanied by clinically meaningful improvements in a substantial proportion of participants receiving CBT. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for SSRI use showed that the Group × Time interaction remained significant for Y-BOCS total scores and No-Go P3 amplitude, indicating that the main findings were not explained by baseline SSRI status. Behavioral performance on the Go/No-Go task

Behavioral performance on the Go/No-Go task was examined to characterize task engagement and response inhibition performance and to support interpretation of electrophysiological findings. Mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVAs revealed no significant Group × Time interaction for Go trial reaction times, indicating that overall response speed remained stable across assessments in both groups. Specifically, participants in the CBT group demonstrated a reduction in commission errors from pre- to post-treatment, whereas the waitlist group showed minimal change over the same period. These findings suggest that CBT was associated with modest but reliable improvements in behavioral response inhibition, consistent with the study’s mechanistic focus. Event-related potential results: response inhibition–related ERPs

ERP analyses focused on neural responses associated with response inhibition during the Go/No-Go task. Based on a priori hypotheses, the No-Go P3 amplitude served as the principal electrophysiological outcome, with the No-Go N2 amplitude examined as a secondary outcome. All ERP measures were extracted from predefined frontocentral regions of interest and time windows as described in the Methods section.

No-Go P3 (primary ERP outcome)

Analysis of the No-Go P3 mean amplitude (300–600 ms) revealed a significant Group × Time interaction, indicating differential change in response inhibition–related neural activity between the CBT and waitlist groups. Participants in the CBT group demonstrated a significant increase in No-Go P3 amplitude from pre- to post-treatment, whereas the waitlist group showed no meaningful change across assessments. There were no significant between-group differences at baseline. The direction and magnitude of the observed effect are consistent with enhanced engagement of electrophysiological indices supporting response inhibition following CBT. Discussion

This randomized controlled trial examined the impacts of CBT (with exposure and response prevention) on symptom severity, cognitive distortions, and neural measures of response inhibition in OCD. The primary outcome was a robust improvement in OCD symptoms in the CBT group compared to the waitlist group. Participants who received 12 sessions of CBT showed a large mean reduction in Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores from baseline, whereas those on the waitlist exhibited minimal change. Importantly, these improvements were also clinically meaningful, with approximately 78% of participants meeting established criteria for treatment response and 37% achieving remission by post-treatment. In practical terms, the CBT group’s Y-BOCS scores dropped by approximately 40% on average (roughly a 10-point decrease), moving from the moderate-severe range into the mild range by post-treatment. This magnitude of improvement is clinically significant – it exceeds the conventional responder threshold of a 35% Y-BOCS reduction commonly used to define meaningful treatment response. The effect size observed for symptom reduction is consistent with the large effects reported in prior OCD therapy trials and meta-analyses, which have established CBT with ERP as a highly efficacious intervention. In fact, our findings parallel those of larger studies wherein CBT produced substantially greater symptom decline than seen in control conditions, highlighting that the treatment was delivered effectively and achieved the expected outcome of substantial OCD symptom relief. Thus, from a clinical standpoint, the trial confirms that even when delivered via modern formats (e.g. online sessions in this study), a standard CBT protocol can yield profound reductions in obsessive–compulsive symptoms. Many patients in the CBT arm would be considered treatment responders by the end of therapy, and some may even have approached remission (Y-BOCS ≤ 12), underscoring the real-world therapeutic value of the intervention.

In addition to symptom change, the secondary outcomes measuring cognitive mechanisms showed significant improvements concordant with our hypotheses. The CBT group demonstrated marked reductions in dysfunctional obsessive beliefs and intolerance of uncertainty from pre- to post-treatment, whereas the waitlist group had no notable changes on these measures. On the Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-20), patients receiving CBT reduced their endorsement of maladaptive beliefs by about 25 points (a ~ 30% reduction in total score), indicating a shift toward more rational, adaptive appraisals of intrusive thoughts. Similarly, scores on the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12) decreased by roughly 9–10 points (~ 25% reduction) in the CBT group, reflecting an increased ability to tolerate uncertainty and ambiguity. These cognitive changes were large in magnitude (partial η² of 0.22–0.27) and mirrored the pattern of the clinical outcome. In other words, patients who underwent CBT not only felt better in terms of OCD symptoms, but they also thought better – endorsing fewer obsessive belief distortions and feeling less threatened by uncertainty. This convergence provides compelling evidence that the therapeutic mechanisms targeted in CBT were successfully engaged. The therapy specifically aimed to challenge and modify the dysfunctional beliefs (e.g. overestimation of threat, need for certainty) maintaining OCD, and indeed those beliefs showed significant correction by post-treatment. Such findings bolster cognitive theories of OCD which propose that symptom improvement should coincide with shifts in underlying maladaptive cognitions [1, 58]. Consistent with these theories, a prior study noted that reductions in obsessive belief measures were associated with improvement in OCD severity [25], and our results offer a causal demonstration that actively treating OCD through CBT leads to cognitive change. From a clinical perspective, the decrease in intolerance of uncertainty is especially noteworthy. High IU has been linked to excessive reassurance-seeking and “not just right” feelings in OCD; thus, a more than 25% drop in IU suggests that patients became significantly more comfortable facing the unknown and resisting the urge to achieve perfect certainty. This cognitive shift likely contributed to their ability to refrain from compulsions during ERP exercises, thereby breaking the cycle of obsessions and compulsions.

Perhaps the most novel contribution of this trial lies in the neurophysiological findings, which indicate that successful CBT was accompanied by objective changes in brain-based measures of response inhibition. As predicted, participants in the CBT group showed a significant increase in the No-Go P3 amplitude of the event-related potential, whereas those in the waitlist did not. At baseline, the two groups had comparable neural responses, but after treatment the CBT group’s No-Go P3 was on average ~ 1.7 µV larger than it was pre-treatment (an ~ 30% relative increase). This finding suggests that CBT was associated with changes in electrophysiological measures related to response inhibition. The No-Go P3 is widely considered a neural marker of response inhibition, reflecting the engagement of frontal-brain “braking” mechanisms when a person successfully withholds a prepotent response. Therefore, an increase in P3 amplitude in the CBT group can be interpreted as improved efficiency or recruitment of these inhibitory neural circuits following therapy [34].In practical terms, participants exhibited larger frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitudes during response inhibition following CBT, together with fewer commission errors on the Go/No-Go task, consistent with improved task performance.

Notably, the activity of the P3 remained fronto-central (as shown by frontocentral ROI activity in our Figures), which aligns with its source in frontal inhibitory networks. The change in P3 was also accompanied by a behavioral improvement on the Go/No-Go task: the CBT group committed fewer No-Go commission errors post-treatment than at baseline, whereas the waitlist group showed little change. This behavioral result – a significant Group × Time interaction for commission error rates – provides convergent evidence that patients’ response inhibition in action got better with therapy, translating the neural changes into measurable performance gains [39]. Taken together, the ERP and behavioral task data strongly support the conclusion that CBT led to enhanced response inhibition in individuals with OCD [57].

Our demonstration of increased No-Go P3 amplitude following therapy extends prior research on the neural correlates of OCD treatment. To our knowledge, this is one of the first randomized trials in OCD to show that a specific neurocognitive deficit can be ameliorated by psychotherapy. Previous EEG studies in OCD had primarily examined general cognitive ERPs (e.g. the auditory oddball P3) before vs. after treatment [40]. Those studies found that P3 abnormalities often move toward normal after successful treatment – for example, initially blunted P3 amplitudes tend to increase following therapy or medication, and such increases have been significantly correlated with symptom reductions [17]. Our findings are consistent with this literature, but importantly they pinpoint a neural change in the domain of response inhibition, which is directly relevant to OCD’s core pathology. By using a Go/No-Go task, we showed that CBT was associated with the neural response associated with inhibiting actions, not just improve attention or error monitoring in general. This result resonates with emerging neuroimaging evidence that effective OCD treatments normalize hyperactivity in frontal control circuits [41]. Previous neuroimaging studies have reported that both CBT and pharmacotherapy are associated with changes in activity within cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical circuits implicated in OCD, including the orbitofrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex [54]. Although these findings provide important neurobiological context, the present study used a low-density scalp EEG system and therefore does not permit source localization or direct inference regarding activity within specific brain regions. Instead, the observed increase in frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitude should be interpreted as a scalp-level electrophysiological change associated with response inhibition. Previous ERP studies in OCD have reported inconsistent findings regarding No-Go P3 amplitude, with some studies demonstrating reduced amplitudes relative to healthy controls and others reporting increased amplitudes. In the present study, participants exhibited a significant increase in frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitude following CBT relative to the waitlist group. This pattern suggests that CBT was associated with changes in electrophysiological indices related to response inhibition. However, given the study design and EEG methodology, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of specific changes within particular neural circuits or cortical regions. Future studies employing high-density EEG or multimodal neuroimaging will be needed to clarify the electrophysiological indices underlying these treatment-related electrophysiological changes. Although the No-Go P3 is widely used as an electrophysiological marker of response inhibition, changes in its amplitude may also reflect broader cognitive processes, including attentional allocation, task engagement, and stimulus evaluation. Because Go P3 amplitudes, difference-wave analyses, and scalp topographies were not examined in the present study, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting changes in frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitude associated with inhibitory processing rather than definitive evidence of inhibition-specific neural enhancement.

The concurrent reductions in obsessive beliefs and intolerance of uncertainty, together with the increase in No-Go P3 amplitude, suggest that cognitive and electrophysiological changes may occur alongside one another during CBT. Historically, cognitive-behavioral models have emphasized maladaptive beliefs and learned behavioral responses, whereas neurocognitive models have focused on alterations in response inhibition and cognitive control. The present findings are consistent with the possibility that these perspectives are complementary. One potential explanation is that repeated exposure and response prevention reduces the perceived urgency to respond to intrusive thoughts by increasing tolerance of uncertainty and modifying maladaptive beliefs. As patients become less driven to achieve certainty through compulsive behaviors, they may allocate cognitive resources more effectively during tasks requiring response inhibition, contributing to the observed increase in No-Go P3 amplitude. This interpretation is consistent with the inhibitory learning model of exposure and response prevention, which proposes that therapeutic improvement results from learning new associations that reduce reliance on compulsive responding [11]. However, because the present study was not designed to test mediation or causal pathways, the observed increase in No-Go P3 amplitude should be interpreted as an electrophysiological correlate accompanying treatment-related cognitive and clinical improvements rather than evidence that changes in intolerance of uncertainty directly produced the observed electrophysiological effects.

It is also worth noting that our findings could inform efforts to personalize OCD treatment. Not all patients respond to standard CBT, and research has begun to investigate neural predictors of treatment outcome. The fact that we observed clear changes in the No-Go P3 raises the question of whether baseline response inhibition metrics (ERP or behavioral) might predict who improves most with therapy. Perhaps patients with the most pronounced response inhibition deficits (e.g. very low P3 amplitudes or poor stopping performance at baseline) stand to gain particular benefit from the exposure and response prevention process, which explicitly exercises inhibition of rituals. Conversely, patients whose primary difficulties lie elsewhere (e.g. extreme depression or other comorbidities) might need adjunctive strategies. Future studies could explore whether the degree of P3 change correlates with the degree of symptom improvement on an individual level; in our group analysis they coincided, but a more fine-grained mediation analysis could reveal if enhancing electrophysiological indices associated with response inhibition is a mechanism that drives symptom reduction. Interestingly, one recent fMRI study of a brief, intensive ERP program (using a concentrated therapy format over 2 weeks) did not find significant changes in inhibitory network activation post-treatment [52]. That null result contrasts with our EEG findings and could be due to several factors: the shorter timeframe of therapy (perhaps not allowing enough practice for neural changes), differences in measurement sensitivity (EEG P3 may detect changes in timing and intensity of neural firing that fMRI does not capture), or simply sample variations. Our study’s success in capturing neural changes with a standard 12-week protocol suggests that meaningful brain modifications do occur with ERP-focused CBT, but it also highlights the need for further research using multimodal imaging and varying therapy “doses” to determine the conditions under which neural plasticity is optimized.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, including the randomized design and the integration of clinical, cognitive, behavioral, and electrophysiological outcomes, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the sample size was modest (N = 53 completers). Although adequate to detect the observed treatment effects, the study may have been underpowered to identify more subtle effects or conduct subgroup analyses. Furthermore, participants were adults with at least moderate OCD severity recruited from a single cultural setting, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to more severe presentations, pediatric populations, or other cultural contexts. All primary and secondary analyses were performed on the same completer sample (CBT = 27; waitlist = 26), ensuring consistency across the reported findings.

Second, the study employed a waitlist rather than an active psychological control condition. Although this design is appropriate for evaluating treatment effectiveness, it does not control for nonspecific influences such as treatment expectancy, therapist attention, or general clinical engagement. In addition, repeated administration of the same Go/No-Go task may have introduced practice effects despite the inclusion of a waitlist comparison. Consequently, some of the observed behavioral and electrophysiological changes may reflect a combination of treatment-related effects and nonspecific influences associated with repeated task exposure.

Third, the clinician administering the Y-BOCS was not blinded to treatment allocation. Although standardized administration procedures were used and the clinical findings were supported by convergent changes across self-report cognitive measures, behavioral performance, and electrophysiological outcomes, the absence of assessor blinding may have introduced detection bias and should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of clinical improvement.

Fourth, EEG was recorded using a 14-channel Emotiv EPOC system. While this portable system enhanced the feasibility of conducting the study, its relatively low spatial resolution limits source localization and anatomical inference. The findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting scalp-level electrophysiological changes rather than activity within specific cortical regions or neural circuits. In addition, ERP analyses were restricted to predefined components and time windows, and other potentially informative electrophysiological measures were not examined. The absence of a healthy comparison group also precludes conclusions regarding normalization of electrophysiological responses following treatment.

Fifth, approximately half of the participants were receiving stable SSRI treatment throughout the study. Although sensitivity analyses adjusting for baseline SSRI use did not materially alter the primary findings, the independent contribution of psychotherapy cannot be completely separated from concurrent pharmacotherapy.

Sixth, assessments were limited to the immediate post-treatment period. Consequently, the durability of the observed clinical, cognitive, behavioral, and electrophysiological changes remains unknown. Likewise, because the primary analyses followed a complete-case rather than an intention-to-treat approach, treatment effects may have been modestly overestimated if participants who discontinued differed systematically from those who completed the study.

Finally, although the study included multiple clinically relevant outcomes, OCD is a heterogeneous disorder involving a broader range of cognitive and neurobiological processes than those examined here. Additional neurocognitive domains and objective biological measures were not assessed, and the cognitive measures relied primarily on self-report instruments, which remain susceptible to response bias.

Implications and future directions

Within the limitations of the present study, the findings have several implications for clinical practice and future research. From a clinical perspective, CBT was associated with significant improvements in obsessive–compulsive symptoms, maladaptive obsessive beliefs, intolerance of uncertainty, and scalp-recorded electrophysiological indices of response inhibition. These findings support the value of integrating objective electrophysiological measures with conventional clinical and cognitive assessments to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment outcomes. The observed reductions in intolerance of uncertainty also reinforce its importance as a therapeutic target in CBT for OCD and suggest that assessing this construct during treatment may provide useful information regarding cognitive change.

Future research should replicate these findings in larger, multi-site samples using active control conditions and longer follow-up periods to determine the durability and clinical significance of the observed electrophysiological changes. Studies employing high-density EEG, multimodal neuroimaging, and more comprehensive ERP paradigms—including measures such as the error-related negativity (ERN), Go P3, and difference-wave analyses—may further clarify the neurocognitive processes associated with treatment. In addition, prospective studies should investigate whether baseline electrophysiological and cognitive measures, as well as early treatment-related changes in No-Go P3 amplitude and intolerance of uncertainty, predict clinical response or mediate treatment outcomes. Such work may contribute to the development of objective biomarkers that complement clinical assessment and support more individualized treatment approaches in OCD.

Conclusion

The present randomized controlled trial demonstrates that CBT for obsessive–compulsive disorder was associated with significant improvements in obsessive–compulsive symptom severity, maladaptive obsessive beliefs, intolerance of uncertainty, behavioral performance during response inhibition, and frontocentral No-Go P3 amplitude. These findings suggest that successful CBT may be accompanied by measurable electrophysiological changes recorded during a Go/No-Go task, alongside established clinical and cognitive improvements. Although the study design does not permit conclusions regarding the specific electrophysiological indices underlying these changes, the findings support the value of integrating electrophysiological measures with conventional clinical outcomes in psychotherapy research. Future studies employing active control conditions, larger samples, longer follow-up periods, and multimodal neuroimaging are needed to further clarify the relationship between treatment-related cognitive and electrophysiological changes in OCD.
