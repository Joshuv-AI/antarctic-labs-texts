Lower Bounds for Public-Private Learning under Distribution Shift
Amrith Setlur, Pratiksha Thaker, Jonathan Ullman

Abstract
The most effective differentially private machine learning algorithms in practice rely on an additional source of purportedly public data. This paradigm is most interesting when the two sources combine to be more than the sum of their parts. However, there are settings such as mean estimation where we have strong lower bounds, showing that when the two data sources have the same distribution, there is no complementary value to combining the two data sources. In this work we extend the known lower bounds for public-private learning to setting where the two data sources exhibit significant distribution shift. Our results apply to both Gaussian mean estimation where the two distributions have different means, and to Gaussian linear regression where the two distributions exhibit parameter shift. We find that when the shift is small (relative to the desired accuracy), either public or private data must be sufficiently abundant to estimate the private parameter. Conversely, when the shift is large, public data provides no benefit.



1
Introduction

Differential privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
)
is a formal privacy framework that makes it possible to perform statistical analysis or train large models while giving a strong guarantee of privacy to the individuals who contribute data. Informally, a differentially private algorithm does not reveal too much information about any one individual’s data. While differential privacy has been deployed successfully for statistics and machine learning, many of the most effective deployments work in a
public-private
setting in which there is access to an additional source of
public
data that can be used along with the private data. In this setting, the overall algorithm need not be fully differentially private—its output can reveal arbitrary information about the public data, but must limit the amount of information revealed about the private data. For example, the most effective differentially private predictive and generative models are often initialized with a large pretrained model that is not differentially private
(
Yu et al. 2021a
;
Li et al. 2021
)
, and the best models trained from scratch with only private data have much worse accuracy. This paradigm is most interesting when the public and private data combine to be more than the sum of their parts—by combining public and private data we can train an accurate model even though neither source on its own is large or high-quality enough to train an accurate model.

However, not every application provides us with a useful source of public data, and there are also important questions about whether these purportedly public datasets raise privacy concerns of their own
(
Tramèr et al. 2022
)
. As a result there is a growing body of theoretical work trying to understand whether the use of public data is
necessary
for private training, or can be replaced with better algorithms. These results give inconclusive answers, either showing that combining private and public data
does help
(e.g.
(
Bie et al. 2022
;
Ganesh et al. 2023
)
) or
doesn’t help
(e.g.
(
Bassily et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2024
;
Lowy et al. 2024
)
) depending on the specifics of the learning problem.

The prior work that most directly relates to ours studies
mean estimation
as a test case. Here we are given
n

private samples
and
m

public samples
, all drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
P
, and our goal is to design an algorithm that provides DP for the private samples but can do anything it wants with the public samples and estimates
\mu=\mathbb{E}[P]
. In this setting, it is known that combining public and private data doesn’t help make more effective use of the private data
(
Bassily et al. 2020
)
. Throughout this work, when we say that combining private and public data
doesn’t help
, we mean roughly that whenever we are able to solve the learning problem using the combination of public and private data, then we could also have solved the learning problem (perhaps up to some small contant loss in accuracy) with just the public data or the private data alone.

1.1
Contributions.

The above results raise two questions that we address in this work. First, prior work considers public and private data from the
same distribution
; we consider settings where the public and private data exhibit
distribution shift
, which is a more realistic setting for practical applications
(
Tramèr et al. 2022
)
. Second, we consider whether this phenomenon—that public data doesn’t help make more effective use of private data—extends beyond mean estimation to more realistic supervised learning problems like
linear regression
.

Mean Estimation with Distribution Shift.
In this setup, we assume the private samples are drawn from a distribution
P
and the public samples are drawn from some related distribution
Q\neq P
, and our goal is to estimate the mean of
P
. We know from prior work that when
Q
and
P
are the same, then there is no value to data from
Q
unless we already have enough samples from
Q
to solve the problem, and giving more samples from
Q
can only improve the achievable accuracy. We also know that holding the number of samples from
Q
fixed, shifting
Q
farther from
P
can only make the achievable accuracy worse. However, a priori it might be the case that shifting
Q
away from
P
, but giving many samples from
Q
, would allow us to find a sweet spot where the samples from
Q
are not enough on their own because of the shift, and samples from
P
are not enough because of privacy, but the two can be combined to achieve the desired accuracy.

Our first main result shows that this is not the case—that public data still doesn’t help, even in settings where the public and private data come from different, but related, distributions. That is, we show that if we are able to estimate the mean up to some specified error, then
either
(1) the public data is abundant enough and its distribution
Q
is close enough to
P
that we can estimate the mean using only public data,
or
(2) the private data is abundant enough that we can privately estimate the mean using only the private data.

Specifically, we consider a setting where
P
and
Q
are both Gaussians with
P=N(\mu_{P},\mathbf{I}_{d})
and
Q=N(\mu_{Q},\mathbf{I}_{d})
we promise that
\|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{2}\leq\tau
, and our goal is to find an estimate
\hat{\mu}_{P}
such that
\|\hat{\mu}_{P}-\mu_{P}\|_{2}\leq\alpha
. We assume access to
n
private samples from
P
and
m
public samples from
Q
. Note that if we have access to only private samples from
P
, then we could solve the problem if and only if
n=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}})
. Also note that if
\tau\leq\alpha
then, up to a factor of
2
in the error, we can simply use the public data as if it were from the same distribution as the public data. Thus, if we had access to only public samples from
Q
then we would need that both
\tau<\alpha
and
m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
. Our result shows that, up to constant factors, we need either the private or public data to be sufficient on their own, and there is no complementarity between them.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal; mean estimation when
P\neq Q
)
.

.

1.

When the distribution shift is small, (i.e.,
\tau\lesssim\alpha
) then to solve the mean estimation problem with error
\alpha
in
\ell_{2}
, we must have either enough public data alone to estimate the mean (i.e.,
m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
), or enough total data to solve the mean estimation problem with differential privacy i.e.,
n+m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
.

2.

When the distribution shift is large, (i.e.,
\tau\gtrsim\alpha
), then the entire sample complexity burden falls on private data and we must have enough private data to solve the mean estimation problem with differential privacy (i.e.,
n=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
.

We detail this theorem and sketch its proof in Section
4
.

Linear Regression without Distribution Shift.
Here, we consider the richer setting of private linear regression with Gaussian covariates and Gaussian errors. That is, the data consist of pairs
(\vec{x},y)
where the features
x
are drawn from a standard multivariate Gaussian
N(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
and
y|\vec{x}=\langle\beta,\vec{x}\rangle+\nu
for some ground truth predictor
\beta
and error
\nu
drawn from a standard Gaussian
N(0,1)
. Our goal is to estimate the optimal predictor
\beta
with small excess squared prediction error, which in this setting is equivalent to minimizing
\|\hat{\beta}-\beta\|_{2}^{2}
. For now we assume that the public and private data are both sampled from the same distribution.

Our second main result shows that the same qualitative phenomenon holds in this setting, that either the public data is sufficient to train a good predictor on its own, or the private data is sufficient to privately train a good predictor on its own.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal; linear regression when
P=Q
)
.

To solve the linear regression problem with excess squared prediction error
\alpha^{2}
, we either must have enough public samples alone to solve the linear regression problem (i.e.,
m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
), or we must have enough total data to solve the problem with differential privacy (i.e.,
n+m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
).

Linear Regression with Distribution Shift.
Finally, we consider Gaussian linear regression with
parameter shift
between the public and private distributions. The only change to the setting above is that, for the private data, we have
y|\vec{x}=\langle\beta_{P},\vec{x}\rangle+\nu
, and for the public data, we have
y|\vec{x}=\langle\beta_{Q},\vec{x}\rangle+\nu
for
\beta_{P}\neq\beta_{Q}
. In analogy to the case of mean estimation, we will parameterize the degree of distribution shift by a parameter
\tau
with the promise that
\|\beta_{P}-\beta_{Q}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\tau^{2}
. Note that, analogous to the case of mean estimation, if
\tau\leq\alpha
, then up to a factor of
2
in the error, we can treat the public data as if it came from the same distribution as the private data. The goal is still to estimate
\beta_{P}
with small excess squared prediction error, or equivalently to minimize
\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{P}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Analogous to the case of mean estimation with distribution shift and linear regression without distribution shift, our third main result shows that combining public and private data doesn’t help in this setting.

Theorem 1.3

(Informal; linear regression when
P\neq Q
)
.

.

1.

When the distribution shift is small, (i.e.,
\tau\lesssim\alpha
) then to solve linear regression with excess squared prediction error
\alpha^{2}
, we must have either enough public to solve the linear regression problem (i.e.,
m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
), or enough total data to solve the mean estimation problem with differential privacy (i.e.,
n+m=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
).

2.

When the distribution shift is large, (i.e.,
\tau\gtrsim\alpha
), then the entire sample complexity burden falls on private data and we must have enough private data to solve the mean estimation problem with differential privacy (i.e.,
n=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha\varepsilon}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
.

Proof Techniques.
Our work builds on the fingerprinting method
(
Ullman 2013
;
Bun et al. 2014
;
Dwork et al. 2015
)
, which has been widely applied to proving lower bounds in differential, including in the public-private setting
(
Bassily et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2024
;
Lowy et al. 2024
)
. In particular, we adopt the recently introduced
Bayesian perspective on the fingerprinting method

Narayanan 2023
. As a starting point for our work, we can show that the Bayesian perspective gives an especially clean and simple way to establish lower bounds in the most basic setting of mean estimation with public-private data
without distribution shift,
recovering the result from
(
Bassily et al. 2020
)
. This new proof allows us to extend the results for mean estimation to settings with distribution shift (Theorem
1.1
) by giving an easy way to choose a hard distribution for the public data and easily incorporating the influence of the public samples on the distribution of the private data using conjugate priors. This viewpoint also allows us to extend the lower bounds to settings like linear regression (Theorem
1.2
), where lower bounds were previously only known for the purely private setting
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
and not the public-private setting. Finally, we can combine these two extensions can be combined to the setting of linear regression with parameter shift (Theorem
1.3
). A key technique we employ in this setting is to reinterpret the parameter shift in linear regression as a form of non i.i.d. noise on the labels and then employ methods from generalized least-squares estimation. See Section
2
for a more thorough discussion of these techniques.

1.2
Related Work

The most closely related works to ours are
(
Bassily et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2024
;
Lowy et al. 2024
)
, which give lower bounds for mean estimation and stochastic convex optimization in the public-private setting using fingerprinting techniques, but only consider the setting where the public and private data come from the same distribution. We now review the related work on public-private estimation and machine learning as well as on fingerprinting lower bounds in differential privacy.

Public-private statistical estimation.

A number of prior works have theoretically studied the benefits of public data in other settings, including mean estimation
(
Avent et al. 2020
)
, query release
(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Bassily et al. 2020
;
Fuentes et al. 2024
)
, convex optimization when gradients lie in a low-rank subspace
(
Kairouz et al. 2021
;
Amid et al. 2022
)
, and non-convex optimization
(
Ganesh et al. 2023
)
. In particular
(
Ganesh et al. 2023
)
gives a non-convex optimization setting where public data is necessary to achieve high accuracy. Typically these positive results for combining public and private data arise in settings where the public data can be used for some kind of dimensionality reduction or pretraining, where the dimensionality reduction step is often much more costly under privacy constraints than when using public data. In contrast to our work, many of these positive results formally assume that the public and private data come from the same distribution and doesn’t directly address the question of how close the public and private data need to be in order for these positive results to hold.

The combination of public and private data has also been used effectively for practical machine learning in a number of domains
(
Abadi et al. 2016
;
Papernot et al. 2019
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

;

)
, however these works are primarily empirical, whereas our work is focused on understanding whether combining public and private data is necessary.

Fingerprinting lower bounds in differential privacy.
Fingerprinting methods have become the standard tool for establishing lower bounds on the cost of differentially private statistical estimation, particularly for high-dimensional problems. This method was first introduced in differential privacy in
(
Ullman 2013
;
Bun et al. 2014
)
. This method was subsequently refined and used to prove lower bounds for a wide variety of problems
(

;

;

;

;

2
Technical Overview

In this section, we describe the structure of our proofs at a high level and highlight the new elements we introduce. We present a general proof technique for proving lower bounds for public-private computations under distribution shifts. Our approach starts with the Bayesian approach for proving lower bounds in the private-only setting from
Narayanan 2023
. We show that this approach is particularly well suited to proving lower bounds under distribution shift between public and private data. To start, we will review this Bayesian approach specific to the setting of Gaussian mean estimation, and then we will discuss how to incorporate distribution shift to prove our new results. Later we will discuss the key ideas in adapting this proof to the linear regression setting.

Problem Setup and Prior Work: Private-Only Mean Estimation.

In the private mean estimation problem we have a dataset
X
consisting of
n
samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
drawn i.i.d. from a spherical multivariate normal distribution in
P=N(\mu,\mathbf{I}_{d})
. Our goal is to design an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private algorithm
M(X)
that returns an estimate
\hat{\mu}
such that
\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}\leq\alpha
. Without privacy, the standard algorithm is to compute the empirical mean
\hat{\mu}=\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}X_{i}
and we will satisfy the accuracy goal as long as
n=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
, which is also optimal. We want to know how the sample requirement changes when we require that the estimator is differentially private.

The main technique for proving such lower bounds is the so-called
fingerprinting method
. In this method we design a
test statistic

Z
defined as

\displaystyle Z_{i}=\langle X_{i}-\mu,\hat{\mu}-\mu\rangle\textrm{ and }Z=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}Z_{i}=\langle\bar{X}-\mu,\hat{\mu}-\mu\rangle

(1)

Intuitively each
Z_{i}
measures the covariance between the estimate
\hat{\mu}
and a single data point
X_{i}
. The reason this test statistic is useful is that: (1) an accurate
\hat{\mu}
should depend on many of the data points
X_{i}
, so we would expect the sum of the correlations
Z
to be large on average, and (2) a private
\hat{\mu}
should not depend significantly on the data points
X_{i}
, so we would expect sum of the correlations
Z
to be small on average. Using these two observations, we can try to derive a contribution showing that accurate and private estimation is impossible for certain choices of parameters.

Formalizing and proving that the statistic
Z
is
small
due to privacy is relatively straightforward using the definition of differential privacy. The tricky part is formalizing and proving that the statistic
Z
is large for any mechanism that is sufficiently accurate, and this is the crux of the argument, sometimes called the
fingerprinting lemma
. Although there are many proofs, we outline the main ideas in the proof of
Narayanan 2023
as a starting point, which starts by writing the Markov chain

\mu\longrightarrow X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\longrightarrow\hat{\mu}

The key property of this Markov chain is that
\mu
and
\hat{\mu}
are independent conditioned on the data
X
. Now, a series of calculations gives us a lower bound on
\mathbb{E}_{\mu,X,\hat{\mu}}[Z]
that will depend on specific properties of: (1) the
posterior mean

\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid X]
, (2) the
posterior variance

\mathit{Var}[\mu\mid X]
, and (3) the accuracy of the estimate
\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}
. The third quantity is something we control by assumption and the first two quantities can be controlled by understanding the posterior distribution of
\mu
conditioned on
X
. This framework makes it clear that we should choose a
conjugate prior
so that the posterior
\mu\mid X
has a nice closed form. In this example, the appropriate conjugate prior is itself a Gaussian so that
\mu\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
for an appropriate choice of
\sigma
, so that
\mu\mid X
is also Gaussian with some mean and variance we can write down, although the specific calculations are beyond the scope of this overview.

Incorporating Public Data and Distribution Shift (Thm
1.1
).

Now that we have the basic framework in hand, we can discuss why this approach is ideal for incorporating public data into the lower bounds. Now we have the original distribution
P
with mean
\mu
and samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
that are private. We also have additional public data
X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m}
samples i.i.d. from the same distribution, so the total dataset size is
N=n+m
. We want to design a mechanism
M(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m})
that is differentially private as a function of the first
n
samples.

Although our formal proof diverges slightly from this outline, for this overview, we can think about the same test statistic
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}Z_{i}
computed over both the public and private samples and look at how the proof does and doesn’t change. First, when we try to establish an upper bound on
Z
, we had used the fact that the mechanism is differentially private as a function of each of its samples, which is no longer true. However, we can separately bound
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
using privacy and get a weaker bound on
\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+m}Z_{i}
just using the variance of the distribution. When we try to establish a lower bound on
Z
, we didn’t rely on privacy so not too much changes except that we now have
n+m
samples instead of just
n
samples, which slightly affects the posterior mean and covariance. Although the specific calculations are beyond the scope of this overview, we can use this outline to give a simpler proof of the lower bounds in
(
Bassily et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2024
;
Lowy et al. 2024
)
(captured as the special case of Theorem
1.1
where
\tau=0
).

Now we turn to incorporating distribution shift. Here, we now assume that the private samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are drawn from
P=N(\mu_{P},\mathbf{I}_{d})
and the public samples
X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m}
are drawn from
Q=N(\mu_{Q},\mathbf{I}_{d})
where
\|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{2}\leq\tau
. Note that increasing
\tau
makes the public data less useful and thus should make the problem harder, and thus our goal is ultimately to prove stronger lower bounds on the number of private samples we need to solve the problem.

Once again we consider the same test statistic
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}Z_{i}
and look at how the proof does and doesn’t change compared to the above. In this case the proof of the upper bound on
Z
is conceptually unchanged compared to the case with no distribution shift. The main conceptual difference is in the proof of the lower bound on
Z
and where we require a novel idea. First, we think of
\mu_{Q}=\mu_{P}+v
for some random variable
v
such that
\|v\|_{2}\leq\tau
with high probability, and we will choose the distribution of
v
later. Now, if we elide the variable
v
, we have the Markov chain

\mu_{P}\longrightarrow X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m}\longrightarrow\hat{\mu}

where all these random variables are fully specified and where
X
contains all the public and private data. As before, we want to understand the test statistic
Z
by using properties of the mean and variance of the posterior distribution
\mu_{P}\mid X
. Thus, the key idea is to choose the random variable
v
determining the distribution shift in such a way that
\mu_{P}
has a nice distribution. Specifically, what we do is sample
v
according to a normal
N(0,\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{d}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d})
so that
\|v\|_{2}\leq\tau
with high probability and so that the distribution

(\mu_{P},X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m})

becomes jointly normal in
d(n+m+1)
variables with a particular block diagonal structure that makes it easy to work with. As a result the conditional distribution
\mu_{P}\mid X
is now normal with a particular mean and covariance that we can write down and use to complete the analysis, although the exact calculations are again beyond the scope of this overview.

Extending to Linear Regression (Thms
1.2
and
1.3
).

An advantage of our proof structure is that is makes it easy to extend our lower bounds to richer problems like Gaussian linear regression. Here we follow roughly the same outline as mean estimation: (1) we use a suitable test statistic to prove a lower bound for private-only linear regression, in this case coming from
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
, (2) we derive a simplified Bayesian version of the main fingerprinting lemma for that setting that depends on the distribution of the true parameter conditioned on the data and use a suitable conjugate prior to make this choice easy to work with, (3) we extend this fingerprinting lemma to the setting with additional public data, (4) we extend this fingerprinting lemma to the setting with distribution shift by choosing a random shift from a suitable conjugate prior so that the posterior of the true parameter conditioned on both the public and private data has a closed form that is simple enough to analyze.

The main difference between the two settings is in the choice of test statistic and in carefully defining which parts of the experiment we fix and which we don’t. First, in the private-only setting we have a distribution
P
over labeled samples
(\vec{x},y)
where we assume
\vec{x}
has distribution
N(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
and
y|\vec{x}=\langle\vec{x},\beta\rangle+\nu
where the error
\nu
has distribution
N(0,1)
. Here
\beta
is the true parameter we want to estimate via a differentially private algorithm
\hat{\beta}
.

To prove a tight lower bound for this setting,
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
introduced a suitable test statistic, although the specifics are not important for this discussion. We start by giving a simplified Bayesian version of their analysis. One key step in our analysis is to think of the feature vectors
\vec{x}_{i}
as
fixed
and consider the Markov chain

\beta\longrightarrow y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\longrightarrow\hat{\beta}

Note that we will have to average over the vectors
\vec{x}_{i}
at some point, but since they are normally distributed, the covariance of these vectors is tightly concentrated around
\mathbf{I}_{d}
, so we don’t think of this as an important source of randomness. The advantage of considering this Markov chain is that, for every fixed choice of the feature vectors,
y\mid\beta
is now normally distributed. Therefore, if we choose a normal prior for
\beta
, we will get that the distribution
(\beta,y)
is jointly Gaussian, and
\beta\mid y
is Gaussian with a tractable closed form. These observations are enough to prove the lower bound for public-private linear regression with no distribution shift (Thm
1.2
), albeit with significant technical challenges involved in completing the analysis.

Finally, we consider the setting with
parameter shift
. Here, as before there are
n
private samples and
m
public samples. All samples have features
\vec{x}
chosen from
N(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
, but now we assume that for the private samples we have
y\mid\vec{x}=\langle\vec{x},\beta_{P}\rangle+\nu
and for the public samples we have
y\mid\vec{x}\langle\vec{x},\beta_{Q}\rangle+\nu
, for
\|\beta_{P}-\beta_{Q}\|_{2}\leq\tau
. Here we can apply a similar idea as in the case of mean estimation. We consider the parameter shift
\beta_{Q}=\beta_{P}+v
for a random variable
v
that is chosen from
N(0,\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{d}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d})
so that
\|v\|_{2}\leq\tau
. Now we elide
v
and consider the joint distribution

(\beta_{P},y_{1},\dots,y_{n},y_{n+1},\dots,y_{n+m})

This distribution is jointly normal in
n+m+d
variables and therefore the distribution
\beta_{P}
conditioned on all the private and public data has a normal distribution with tractable mean and covariance, which we can use to complete the proof, albeit with significant technical complexities. We overcome these technical challenges by using techniques from
generalized least squares regression
where we think of the parameter shift as instead being a form of Gaussian label noise, but with a non i.i.d. structure, which helps us suitably modify the test statistic and complete the analysis.

3
Preliminaries

We briefly review the definition of differential privacy and its application in a setting with public and private data. Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
be a dataset consisting of
n
elements. We say two datasets
X,X^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
are
neighboring
if they differ on at most one element.

Definition 3.1

(Differential Privacy
(
Dwork et al. 2006
)
)
.

An algorithm

is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private
if for every pair of neighboring datasets
X,X^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{n}
and every
S\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}

\Pr(M(X)\in S)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr(M(X^{\prime})\in S)+\delta.

We consider learning algorithms that have access to both public and private data. Specifically, we have a dataset

X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m})

where we think of the first
n
samples as
private
and the last
m
samples as
public
. In this setting we say that an algorithm
M:{\mathcal{X}}^{n+m}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private with public and private data
if, for every possible fixed choice of the public samples
x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}
, the algorithm

M(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m})

is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private as a function of the first
n
elements. Note that

may depend in an arbitrary way as a function of the public samples
X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m}
.

In our analysis we will rely heavily on the following technical lemma about random variables that are
indistinguishable
in the sense guaranteed by differential privacy.

Lemma 3.2

(See e.g.
(
Narayanan 2023
)
)
.

Suppose
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1
and
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and that
A,B
are real-valued random variables such that for any set

,

e^{-\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr(A\in S)-\delta\leq\Pr(B\in S)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr(A\in S)+\delta.

Then,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[A-B]}}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}[|A|]+2\sqrt{\delta}\cdot\mathbb{E}[A^{2}+B^{2}].

We defer all further definitions and technical lemmata to later in the paper where they are used.

First we give some notation to state our result and sketch the proof. We have a matrix comprised of
m

n

X_{\mathrm{priv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

X_{\mathrm{priv},i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{priv}},\;\mathbf{I}_{d}),

X_{\mathrm{pub},i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{pub}},\;\mathbf{I}_{d}),

(\varepsilon,\delta)

We split the proof of Theorem

When the sampling error dominates, the shift is largely irrelevant, so our analysis matches the case where
\tau=0
so there is no shift. Our analysis for this case is captured in Theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Mean estimation lower bound for
\tau=0
)

Fix any
\alpha>0
with

Note that the above theorem remains true when

Theorem 4.2

(Mean estimation lower bound for large

Fix any

Proof Sketch.

Our goal is to establish a minimax lower bound on the number of public and private samples needed for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)

\alpha

Following the typical approach of minimax lower bounds, we lower bound the minimax risk with the Bayes risk under a carefully chosen prior over the unknown parameters:
\mu_{P},v

The private mean
\mu_{P}
is sampled from a spherical Gaussian

\mu_{Q}=\mu_{P}+v

i.e.

More interestingly, the statistic that measures the correlation with a public datapoint

Essentially, when we compute the sum

\tau=O(\sqrt{d/m})

to reduce its sensitivity over public data as the shift increases, and a similar effect is also captured by our test statistic. We upper bound the expected sum as

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\varepsilon\alpha+\tfrac{1}{1+m\cdot\nicefrac{{\tau^{2}}}{{d}}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\sqrt{md}+\alpha\tau\sqrt{m}}}\right)}}\right)

using differential privacy arguments we outline in Section

with

when
\tau=O(\sqrt{d/m})
.
For the lower bound, we use the Markov chain argument outlined in Section
2

\mu_{P}\mid X
given all the data, where the posterior mean has lower order dependence on empirical mean of the public data, as the shift grows.
Together, with the upper bound on

and

for small values of
\tau

, for the public data to estimate its own mean
\mu_{Q}
to an accuracy of

\alpha\raisebox{-3.69899pt}{~\shortstack{$>$ \\
[-0.07cm]
$\sim$}}~\tau

∎

The the two results below together imply Theorem

in the Introduction.

{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}-\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}}}\right\|}_{2}=\omega(\sqrt{d/m})

Proof sketch.

While the techniques we use for shifted linear regression are similar to those for shifted mean estimation,
the shift introduces a correlated (heteroskedastic) noise term into the linear regression model. Specifically, if we fix the covariates
X

X\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}

\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{N}+\tfrac{\tau^{2}}{d}PP^{\top},

where
P=[0,X_{\mathrm{pub}}]

adapts the Bayesian fingerprinting framework of Section

\displaystyle\sum_{i}Z_{i}=\langle{M(D)-\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}},\;{X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}-y)}\rangle

(4)

Intuitively the purpose of multiplying by
\Sigma^{-1}
is that it restores the property that each of the terms in the inner product is distributed as a spherical Gaussian, which we relied on in the case of ordinary least squares. The proof then follows the same general structure but with additional technical details arising from the presence of this correction. The main feature of the analysis is that correction nicely interpolates between the cause where
\tau
is large where the public samples are too noisy to be useful and the case where
\tau
is small and the public and private samples are very similar.

6
Discussion and Limitations

In this paper, we discuss lower bounds on the sample complexity requirements for private Gaussian mean estimation and private linear regression in the presence of public data. In practice, it is common to use public data from sources that are different from the ones used to draw private data. Motivated by this, we analyze lower bounds in settings where there is a shift in the distribution of public and private samples. For this, we model the distribution shift, by allowing the public and private means, or linear regression parameters to be shifted by
\tau
in
\ell_{2}
.

Across both mean estimation and linear regression, we establish that whenever the learning objective is achievable using the combination of public and private data, one of the two sources—public or private—must be sufficient on its own whenever the distribution shift is smaller than the accuracy budget.
From a methodological standpoint, our work extends the fingerprinting lower bound technique to new regimes: (1) distribution shift in public-private mean estimation, and (2) private linear regression with and without parameter shift. By adopting a Bayesian lens on the fingerprinting method
(
Narayanan 2023
)
, we simplify existing proofs and open the door to analyzing more realistic learning settings within the learning framework of using public data to improve privacy-utility tradeoffs under differentially private learning.

Appendix B
Useful lemmas for linear regression

We reuse some eigenvalue tail bounds throughout the linear regression lower bounds.

Lemma B.1

(
Wainwright 2019
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be drawn according to the
\Sigma
-Gaussian ensemble.
Then, for
N\geq d
, the minimum singular value
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})
satisfies the lower deviation inequality

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{N}}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\sqrt{\Sigma})(1-\delta)-\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)}{N}}}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

Specializing to the case of
\Sigma=I_{d}
, we have

Corollary B.2
.

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{N}}\leq 1-\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}-\delta}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

and thus,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})^{2}=\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\leq N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}-\delta}}\right)^{2}}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

We also have the following upper tail variant:

Lemma B.3

(
Wainwright 2019
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be drawn according to the
\Sigma
-Gaussian ensemble.
Then, for
N\geq d
, the maximum singular value
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})
satisfies the upper deviation inequality

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{N}}\geq\lambda_{\max}(\sqrt{\Sigma})(1+\delta)+\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)}{N}}}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

Specializing to the case of
\Sigma=I_{d}
, we have

Corollary B.4
.

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{N}}\geq 1+\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}+\delta}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

similarly,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})^{2}=\lambda_{\max}(X^{\top}X)\geq N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}+\delta}}\right)^{2}}}\right]\leq e^{-N\delta^{2}/2}.

The following simple lemma will be useful in converting exponential tail bounds to upper bounds involving
N
.

Fact B.5
.

For
N\geq 400
the following is true:

\displaystyle e^{-N/8}\leq 1/N^{8}.

(24)

The following calculations are also useful in several parts of the proof, so we black-box them here.

.

(thus

Appendix C
Proofs for Linear Regression

C.1
Upper bound

We first upper bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]
.
We can split this into a bound over the sum of public samples
and private samples.
We will bound the private samples using the differential privacy constraint,
and the public samples using concentration.

For the private samples, we can use Proposition
3.2

as in the mean estimation case.

Proposition C.1
.

Assume the conditions in Theorem
5.1
. Then,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{\prime}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{i}^{\prime})^{2}]=O(\alpha^{2})
, where the expectation is taken over the randomness of sampling
\beta
,
\mathcal{D}
, and the randomness in the mechanism
M
.

Proof.

When conditioned on
\beta
,
(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)x_{i}
is independent of
M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta
, and
\mathbb{E}[(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)x_{i}\mid\beta]=0
(because
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{i}x_{i}]=0
). Thus, we have
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{\prime}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{\prime}\mid\beta]]=0
.

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{i}^{\prime})^{2}|\beta]
and similarly use the law of total expectation to get the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{i}^{\prime})^{2}]
.
Note that we have
y_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\beta+\eta_{i}
, thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(Z_{i}^{\prime})^{2}|\beta]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{j,k}(M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta)_{j}(M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta)_{k}(\eta_{i}x_{i})_{j}(\eta_{i}x_{i})_{k}|\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta)_{j}^{2}(\eta_{i}x_{i})_{j}^{2}|\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta)_{j}^{2}|\beta}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\eta_{i}x_{i})_{j}^{2}|\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta)_{j}^{2}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}|\beta}}\right]\leq\alpha^{2}.

The second equality uses the fact
the coordinates of
\eta
and
x_{i}
are sampled independently,
and the third equality uses
that
M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})-\beta

is conditionally independent of
\eta_{i}x_{i}
given
\beta

(because
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are resampled in
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}
).
The final step uses the accuracy of the mechanism
M

and the variance of
\eta_{i}
and
x_{i}
.
∎

Proposition C.2
.

Assume the conditions in Theorem
5.1
. Then,
for
i\in[n]
,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=O(\varepsilon\alpha)
, where the expectation is taken over the randomness of sampling
\beta
,
\mathcal{D}
, and the randomness in the mechanism
M
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}
differ by a single element, and
M
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
differentially private, we can use the fact that the outputs
M(\mathcal{D})
and
M(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})
(and therefore, by the postprocessing property of DP,
Z_{i}
and
Z_{i}^{\prime}
) cannot be too different. In particular, we invoke Proposition
3.2
.

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[Z^{\prime}_{i}]}}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}[|Z^{\prime}_{i}|]+2\sqrt{\delta}\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}+(Z^{\prime}_{i})^{2}]\leq 2(\varepsilon+\sqrt{\delta})\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(Z^{\prime}_{i})^{2}]}+2\sqrt{\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]}.

Next, we apply Proposition
C.1

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\leq O((\varepsilon+\sqrt{\delta})\alpha)+2\sqrt{\delta\cdot\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]}.

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]
with Cauchy-Schwarz followed by the accuracy of

, and the concentration of
\eta_{i}x_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]\;\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|M(\mathcal{D})-\mu_{\mathrm{priv}}\|_{2}^{4}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\eta_{i}x_{i}\|_{2}^{4}]}

\displaystyle=\;\;O(\alpha^{2}d)

Plugging this result into the equation above, we conclude

\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=O(\varepsilon\alpha)
.
∎

Finally, we bound the value of
Z_{i}
for the public samples.

Proposition C.3
.

Assume the conditions in Theorem

. Then, the following holds for the expected sum of the fingerprinting statistics defined for the public samples:

Recall the definition of the fingerprinting statistics:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}Z_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(\mathcal{D})-\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}},\;{\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)x_{i}}\rangle}}\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(\mathcal{D})-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}]}\sqrt{\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}\mathbb{E}[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta_{i}x_{i}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}]}

(37)

The first inequality uses Cauchy-Schwarz, and the second uses the bound on the accuracy of the mechanism
M

Corollary C.4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
, for the expectation taken over the randomness of
\beta,X_{\mathrm{priv}},X_{\mathrm{pub}},\eta
and the mechanism
M

\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=O(n\varepsilon\alpha+\alpha\sqrt{md})

C.2
Fingerprinting lemma (lower bound)

Theorem C.5
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
5.1
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]=\Omega(d)

(38)

where the sum is taken over both the public and private samples.

To prove this theorem,
we follow the same structure as in the mean estimation proof
(conditioning on
X,y
instead of just
X
).

Let
\hat{\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}(X^{T}X+\lambda{I}_{d})^{-1}X^{T}y
for some
\lambda>0
. Then, we again have the breakdown:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\beta},\;{X^{T}y-X^{T}X\beta}\rangle}}\right]

(39)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}+\hat{\beta}-\beta},\;{X^{T}X(\hat{\beta}-\beta)}\rangle}}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X\beta-X\hat{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}},\;{X^{T}X(\beta-\hat{\beta})}\rangle}}\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X(\hat{\beta}-\beta)}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}},\;{X^{T}(X\beta)-X^{\top}y}\rangle}}\right]

(42)

The first term corresponds to the expected error of the nonprivate OLS estimate. We include a short proof for completeness.

Lemma C.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
,

\mathbb{E}_{\beta,X,\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X(\hat{\beta}-\beta)}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]=\sigma^{2}d

Proof.

Rewrite
\hat{\beta}=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}(X\beta+\eta)=\beta+(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\eta

Then
\hat{\beta}-\beta=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\eta

X(\hat{\beta}-\beta)=X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\eta

Finally

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\eta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(\eta^{T}X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\eta)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}d

(44)

∎

\displaystyle={\langle{\mathbb{E}_{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(\mathcal{D})-\hat{\beta}\;|\;X,y}}\right]},\;{\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X^{\top}X\beta-X^{\top}y\;|\;X,y}}\right]}\rangle}

To bound term
1
,
we use the triangle inequality as before:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}+\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(50)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\beta}-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}+\sigma^{2}\cdot\nicefrac{{d}}{{n}}.

(51)

We will next bound the term
2
.

We need the posterior mean
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta|X,y}}\right]
.
We choose the prior
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,b^{-1}I_{d})
where
b
is the precision.
This gives the posterior
\beta|X,y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Lambda^{-1})

where

\displaystyle\Lambda=aX^{\top}X+bI,\quad\mu=a\Lambda^{-1}X^{\top}y.

(52)

The above is a standard derivation for computing the posterior of the linear regression parameter under a Bayesian prior (see Chapter 11.7 in
Murphy 2022
for reference). The parameter
a
here is the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Then we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\beta}[\beta|X,y]=a\Lambda^{-1}X^{\top}y

(53)

Now to remove the conditioning, we can expand

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X\mathbb{E}_{\beta}[\beta|X,y]-X^{\top}y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta|X,y}}\right]-X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(54)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}Xa\Lambda^{-1}X^{\top}y-X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa\Lambda^{-1}-I_{d})X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(56)

Then

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa\Lambda^{-1}-I_{d})X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa\Lambda^{-1}-I_{d})}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(58)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa\Lambda^{-1}-\Lambda\Lambda^{-1})}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(59)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa-\Lambda)}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(60)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}Xa-(X^{\top}Xa+bI))}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(61)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b^{4}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(62)

We now derive upper bounds on
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]
.

Lemma C.7
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(aN+b)^{4}}}}\right)
, when
N=m+n=\Omega(d)
and
b>1/N
.

Proof.

First, note that we can rewrite
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(aX^{\top}X+bI)^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{(a\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)+b)^{4}}}}\right]

(64)

We will split the expectation into two conditional expectations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\;\big|\;\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in(0,t]}}\right]\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in(0,t)]+

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\;\big|\;\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in[t,\infty)]}}\right]\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in[t,\infty)].

(66)

for some
0<t<1
.

For any
\sigma\geq 0
we can bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]<1/b^{4}
,
and when
\sigma\geq t
we additionally have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq 1/(at+b)^{4}
.
Then the above simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\;\big|\;\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in(0,t]}}\right]\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]+

(67)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\;\big|\;\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in[t,\infty)]}}\right]\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in[t,\infty)]

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{4}}\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]+\frac{1}{(at+b)^{4}}\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\geq t]

(69)

We approximate
\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\geq t]\leq 1
, giving

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{4}}\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]+\frac{1}{(at+b)^{4}}

(70)

It then remains to bound
\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]
.

As
X^{\top}X
is symmetric and PSD, we have
\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)=\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)=\sigma_{\min}(X)^{2}
.

Now we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\in(0,t]]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t)

(71)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\sigma_{\min}(X)^{2}<t)

(72)

We can now use Lemma
B.1
.
Setting
t=N(1-\sqrt{d/N}-\psi)^{2}
for some
0<\psi<1-\sqrt{d/N}
,
\Pr(\sigma_{\min}(X)^{2}<t)\leq e^{-N\psi^{2}/2}
.

Using Fact
B.5
, we can upper bound this by, e.g.,
O(1/N^{8})
.

Thus, we have
\frac{1}{b^{4}}\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]=O(\frac{1}{b^{4}N^{8}})
, so in total we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{b^{4}}\Pr[\sigma_{\min}(X^{\top}X)<t]+\frac{1}{(at+b)^{4}}

(73)

\displaystyle\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{b^{4}N^{8}}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(at+b)^{4}}}}\right)

(74)

Assuming
N>100d
, we have the constraint
0<\psi<9/10
.
We set
\psi=1/10
.
Then this gives

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=N(1-\sqrt{d/N}-1/10)^{2}

(75)

\displaystyle=N(9/10)^{2}-2(9/10)\sqrt{dN}+d

(76)

Since we assume
N>100d
(i.e.
d<N/100
),

\displaystyle\geq N(81/100)-(18/10)\sqrt{N^{2}/100}+d

(77)

\displaystyle=N(81-18)/100+d=\Omega(N).

(78)

Thus, for
b>1/N
, we have

\displaystyle O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{b^{4}N^{8}}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(at+b)^{4}}}}\right)\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{b^{4}N^{8}}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(aN+b)^{4}}}}\right)\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(aN+b)^{4}}}}\right).

(79)

∎

Lemma C.8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}y\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N^{2}d^{2}+N^{4}d^{2}/b^{2}}}\right)
, where
N=m+n
.

Proof.

Let
N=m+n
be the total number of datapoints (including both public and private). First, we simplify
X^{T}y=X^{T}(X\beta+\vec{\eta})
.
Then, we use the identity
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
twice and get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}y\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}X\beta\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}\vec{\eta}\|_{2}^{4}}}\right].

(80)

Now, we bound each of the above two terms separately. First, we start with the second term
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}\eta\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}\vec{\eta}\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\sum\nolimits_{i\in[N]}x_{i}\eta_{i}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum\nolimits_{i,j,k,l\in[N]}x_{i}^{T}x_{j}x_{k}^{T}x_{l}\cdot\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\eta_{k}\eta_{l}}}}\right]

Since
\eta_{i}\perp\eta_{j}
whenever
i\neq j
and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta}}\right]=0
, it is easy to see that the only non-zero terms in the expectation above are when: (i)
i=j=k=l
; (ii)
i=j,k=l,i\neq k
; (iii)
(i,j)=(k,l),i\neq j
. Thus, the summation breaks down into:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|X^{T}\vec{\eta}\|_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle=N\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta^{4}}}\right]+N(N-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta^{2}}}\right]}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+N(N-1)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta^{2}}}\right]}}\right)^{2}

(81)

\displaystyle=3N\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+N(N-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]}}\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=O(N^{2}d^{2}).

In the above equations, the first equality uses
x_{i}\perp x_{j}
when
i\neq j
, and
x_{i}\perp\eta_{i},\forall i
. The second uses the fact that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta^{4}}}\right]=3
and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta^{2}}}\right]=1
. Finally, the last equality uses
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]=d
and
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=O(d^{2})
.

Next, we look at the first term in Eq.
80
.
Using the independence of
X
and
\beta
, we bound this as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(82)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(83)

Now we have
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=O(\frac{d^{2}}{b^{2}})
.
Further, we can bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=O(N^{4})
.

This gives an overall bound of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=O(N^{4}d^{2}/b^{2})
. Combining with the previous term gives the result.

∎

Lemma C.9
.

When
b=1/d>1/N
,
m+n=\Omega(d)
, and
\alpha=O(1)
, the expression:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X\mathbb{E}[\beta|X,\eta]-X^{\top}y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}=O(1).

Proof.

From Lemma
C.7
and Lemma
C.8
, we derive the following:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{b^{4}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}}

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot b^{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{(aN+b)^{2}}})\cdot\sqrt{N^{4}d^{2}/b^{2}+N^{2}d^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}d/N+\alpha^{2}}}\right)b^{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{(aN+b)^{2}}})\cdot(\sqrt{N^{4}d^{2}/b^{2}}+\sqrt{N^{2}d^{2}})}

(85)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}d/N+\alpha^{2}}}\right)b^{2}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{(aN)^{2}}})\cdot(N^{2}d/b+Nd)}

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}d/N+\alpha^{2}}}\right)b\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{a^{2}}+\frac{db}{a^{2}N}}}\right)}

(87)

Note that generally we will set
a=\sigma^{2}
.
Let
b=1/d
. Also, note
\alpha=O(1)
. Then:

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}d/N+\alpha^{2}}}\right)(1/d)\cdot(\sigma^{2}d+\sigma^{4}/N)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}d/N+\alpha^{2}}}\right)\cdot(\sigma^{4}+\sigma^{4}/(Nd))}

(89)

\displaystyle=O(1),

(90)

since
\alpha^{2}=O(1)
. ∎

Now, we are ready to prove the final result in Theorem
1.3
. For this we invoke the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]
in Corollary
C.4
and the lower bound of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]=\Omega(d)
induced by Eq.
7
, Lemma
A.6
and Lemma
C.9
. This tells us that there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and values
m_{0},n_{0}
such that for
m\geq m_{0},n\geq n_{0}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle c_{1}\cdot d\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i\in[N]}Z_{i}}}\right]\;\leq\;c_{2}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\varepsilon\alpha+\alpha\sqrt{md}}}\right)

(91)

Thus, either
m=\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}})
or
n\geq\Omega(\frac{d}{\varepsilon\alpha})
. Note that, similar to Lemma
A.8
for Gaussian mean estimation, a similar statistical lower bound also applies for linear regression which implies
n=\Omega(\frac{d}{\alpha^{2}})
. Together this tells us, that when public and private data come from the same distribution, then for privately solving linear regression with an
\varepsilon,\delta
-DP mechanism, where
\delta\leq\varepsilon^{2}/d
, to a parameter estimation accuracy of
\alpha
in
\ell_{2}
, we either need
m=\Omega(d/\alpha^{2})
public samples or the number of public and private samples together must satisfy:
n+m=\Omega(\tfrac{d}{\alpha\varepsilon}+\tfrac{d}{\alpha^{2}})
. This completes the proof of our result in Theorem
5.1
.

Appendix D
Linear Regression with Shifted Parameter

We now consider a variation of linear regression where the public parameter
\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}

is shifted by a random vector
v
sampled from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^{2}/d\cdot I_{d})
.
The goal is to use both the public and private samples to estimate the private parameter
\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}
.

That is, we have
n
samples
(x_{i},y_{i})
such that

\displaystyle y_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}+\eta_{i}

(92)

and
m
samples such that

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{\top}\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}+\eta_{i}

(93)

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{\top}(\beta_{\mathrm{priv}}+v)+\eta_{i}

(94)

Let
X^{\top}=[X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\;\;X_{\mathrm{priv}}^{\top}]
.
Note that
y=X\beta+Pv+\eta
where

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}X_{\mathrm{pub}}\\
0_{n\times d}\end{bmatrix}

(95)

We can define
\Psi=Pv+\eta
to be noise correlated with
X_{\mathrm{pub}}
.

Set
\hat{\beta}
to be the generalized least squares (GLS) estimator
[
Aitken 1936
]

(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
,
where
\Sigma=\mathrm{Cov}(\Psi|X)
.
In particular because
P,v,\eta
are independent and mean zero,

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(Pv+\eta)(Pv+\eta)^{\top}|X}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Pvv^{\top}P^{\top}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta\eta^{T}}}\right]

(96)

\displaystyle=(\tau^{2}/d)PP^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I_{N}

(97)

Then we have
(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X)\hat{\beta}=X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
.

With this in mind, we will use a modified test statistic:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\beta},\;{X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X\beta-y)}\rangle}}\right]

(98)

D.1
Upper bound

As in the non-shifted proof, for the upper bound,
we split the sum over fingerprinting statistics into public and private samples.
For the private samples we will use the differential privacy constraint to bound the sum over samples (as in the non-shifted case),
while for the public samples we can use concentration while taking
into account the parameter shift.

Note that
\Sigma
is block-diagonal,
so that

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\frac{\tau^{2}}{d}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I_{m})^{-1}&0\\
0&1/\sigma^{2}I_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(99)

Hence,
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}=[X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{T}(\frac{\tau^{2}}{d}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I_{m})^{-1}\;\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{priv}}^{\top}]
.
This implies that we can analyze the sum over private samples
using the same analysis as in the non-shifted setting,
using
C.2
,
up to a factor of
1/\sigma^{2}
.

It then remains to bound the sum over public samples using concentration.

Lemma D.1
.

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}Z_{i}}}\right]\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\varepsilon\alpha+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}md-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m^{2}d}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}=(\Sigma^{-1})_{11}=(\frac{\tau^{2}}{d}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}I_{m})^{-1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}Z_{i}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\beta},\;{X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}\beta-y_{\mathrm{pub}})}\rangle}}\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle{M(X,y)-\beta},\;{X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}\beta-(X_{\mathrm{pub}}\beta+X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})}\rangle}}\right]

(101)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]}

(102)

We first bound just the second term.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(103)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right)}}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})^{\top}}}\right)}}\right]

(105)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}|X}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})^{\top}|X}}\right]}}\right])

(106)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right])

(107)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right]

(108)

Applying the Woodbury identity to
\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}
, we get

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{m}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top})X)}}\right]

(109)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X)}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top})X)}}\right]

(110)

The first term is
\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}md
by standard Frobenius norm bounds. Then we need to lower bound the second term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Tr(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X)}}\right]

(111)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}}}\right]

(112)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lambda_{\min}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}\Tr(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right]

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right\|}_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right\|}_{F}^{2}}}\right]

(114)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right\|}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{2}}}\right)}}\right]

(115)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{2}}}\right)}}\right]

(116)

We can break the above conditional expectation into two components, one where
\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})\in(0,t_{1}],\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})\in[t_{2},\infty),t_{1}\leq t_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{2}}}\right)}}\right]

that appear in the above equation can be resolved using Lemma

Plugging this result into the above lower bound, we conclude:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{2}}}\right)}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dm^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)

(117)

Thus, in total, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}v+\eta_{\mathrm{pub}})}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}md-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m^{2}d}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right).

(118)

Finally, from the accuracy assumption of the mechanism

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]}\leq\alpha
. Putting it all together, we arrive at the inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i=n}^{n+m}Z_{i}}}\right]=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}md-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m^{2}d}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)}}}\right).

(119)

The statement of Lemma
D.1
follows from applying the non-shifted upper bound over private samples in Proposition

along with the above result.

∎

Lemma D.2
.

Now, we move to upper bound the second term in Eq.

Lemma D.3

(posterior for generalized least squares)
.

For a fixed covariates matrix
X
, we are given responses
y
drawn from the following Bayesian model for generalized linear regression:

\beta\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\;\Lambda_{0}^{-1}\bigr),\qquad y\mid\beta\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(X\beta,\;\Sigma\bigr),

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},\;y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\Lambda_{0}\succeq 0
is the prior precision, and
\Sigma\succ 0
is noise covariance. Then the mean of the posterior distribution over
\beta\mid X,y
is given by:

\mathbb{E}[\beta\mid X,y]=\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y

Proof.

First, lets apply the Bayes’ rule in its canonical form:

\displaystyle\log p(\beta\mid y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(y-X\beta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(y-X\beta)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta^{\top}\Lambda_{0}\,\beta+\text{const}.

(133)

Then, we can expand the quadratic in
y-X\beta
to get:

\displaystyle(y-X\beta)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(y-X\beta)=y^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y-2\beta^{\top}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y+\beta^{\top}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\,\beta.

(134)

Hence, we can conclude

\displaystyle\log p(\beta\mid y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl[\beta^{\top}\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)\beta-2\beta^{\top}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y\Bigr]+\text{const}

(135)

Next, we apply “completing the squares” trick, where we subtract and add
y^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0})^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
. Note that this term does not contain
\beta
. So, we can treat it as a constant when computing
\log p(\beta\mid X,y)
.

\displaystyle\log p(\beta\mid y)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl[\beta^{\top}\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)\beta

\displaystyle-2\beta^{\top}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y+y^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0})^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y\Bigr]+\text{const}.

(136)

For simplicity of calculations lets first define:
Define the
posterior precision

\Lambda_{\mathrm{post}}:=X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0},\quad\mu_{\mathrm{post}}:=\Lambda_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y.

Substituting the above into Eq.
136
yields:

\displaystyle\log p(\beta\mid y)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(\beta-\mu_{\mathrm{post}})^{\top}\Lambda_{\mathrm{post}}\,(\beta-\mu_{\mathrm{post}})+\text{const},

(137)

since
\Lambda_{\mathrm{post}}\mu_{\mathrm{post}}=X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
.

The above posterior distribution over
\beta\mid X,y
matches the functional form of a Gaussian distribution
\beta\mid y\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu_{\mathrm{post}},\;\Lambda_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}\bigr)
with mean:

\mu_{\mathrm{post}}=\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y,

∎

With the above posterior mean over
\beta
, we are now ready to upper bound the second term in Eq.
124
. Let
\tilde{\Lambda}=\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)
. Now to remove the conditioning, we can expand:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\mathbb{E}_{\beta}[\beta|X,y]-X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta|X,y}}\right]-X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(138)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y-X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}-I_{d})X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(139)

Then,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}-I_{d})X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(140)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}-I_{d})}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(141)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}-\tilde{\Lambda}\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1})}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(142)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X-\tilde{\Lambda})}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(143)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(X^{\top}X\Sigma^{-1}-(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0})))}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(144)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Lambda_{0}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}

(145)

Lemma D.4

(re-stating the posterior over
\beta
)
.

Given the following matrices:

\displaystyle S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}\quad W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}S}}\right)^{-1},

(146)

we can rewrite the posterior
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mid X,y}}\right]
as:

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mid X,y}}\right]=(M+b\mathbf{I}_{d})^{-1}m,

where we can inturn write the matrix
M
and vector
m
as:

M=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWS,\quad m=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}y-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWX_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}y_{\mathrm{pub}}.

Proof.

Matching the form of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta\mid X,y}}\right]
to that in Lemma
D.3
, we need to show:
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWS
, and
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}y-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWX_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}y_{\mathrm{pub}}
.
We first rewrite the noise covariance so that the Woodbury identity applies.
Let

\displaystyle U\;:=\;\begin{bmatrix}X_{\text{pub}}\\[2.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}\!,\qquad C\;:=\;\frac{\tau^{2}}{d}I_{d},

(147)

This means that our
\Sigma
matrix is given by:

\displaystyle\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{m+n}+UC\,U^{\top}.

(148)

Applying Woodbury to the above we get,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{m+n}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\,U\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}U^{\top}U}}\right)^{\!-1}\!U^{\top}.

(149)

Because
U^{\top}U=X_{\text{pub}}^{\top}X_{\text{pub}}=:S
, we define
W
as

\displaystyle W\;:=\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\sigma^{-2}S}}\right)^{\!-1},

(150)

this abbreviates the inverse above and yields

\Sigma^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}UWU^{\top}.

With the above calculations, we start with the matrix term
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X
,

\displaystyle X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X^{\top}UWU^{\top}X

(151)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWS.

(152)

This matches the definition of
M
in the statement of Lemma
D.4
.

M\;=\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWS.

Next, we look at the vector term
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
.

\displaystyle X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}y-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X^{\top}UWU^{\top}y

(153)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}y-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWX_{\text{pub}}^{\top}y_{\text{pub}}.

(154)

This matches the
m
term in the statement of Lemma
D.4
.

m\;=\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}y-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}SWX_{\text{pub}}^{\top}y_{\text{pub}}.

Plugging this into the posterior mean for
\beta
, we get:

\mathbb{E}[\beta\mid X,y]=(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+bI_{d})^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y=(M+bI_{d})^{-1}m.

∎

Lemma D.5
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
5.2
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}m^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}+b)^{4}}}}\right)
,
when
n,m>100d
and
b>1/N
.

Proof.

We have
\tilde{\Lambda}=\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X+\Lambda_{0}\bigr)

where we set
\Lambda_{0}=bI_{d}
for some
b
.
Using the previous transformations,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}+bI_{d}}}\right)

(155)

To bound the spectral norm, we will use a series of transformations to express the spectral norm in terms of eigenvalues of the matrices in the expression.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}+bI_{d}}}\right)}

(156)

(Note that we will eventually take the expectation over the fourth power.)

We want to upper bound this quantity which involves lower bounding the denominator.

Weyl’s inequality gives that
\lambda_{\min}(A-B)\geq\lambda_{\min}(A)-\lambda_{\max}(B)
for real symmetric
A
and
B
.
Moreover, the original expression
\tilde{\Lambda}
is PSD and therefore the eigenvalues are lower bounded by 0,
giving a stronger guarantee than the Weyl lower bound.
Hence,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}+bI_{d}}}\right)

(157)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right)+b

(158)

\displaystyle\geq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\lambda_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X}}\right)-\lambda_{\max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right)}}\right)+b.

(159)

Focusing now on the last term, which we want to upper bound in order to lower bound the previous expression:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right)

(160)

\displaystyle={\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right)}}\right\|}_{2}

(161)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}

(162)

The last line follows from the submultiplicativity of operator norm.

Bounding even further,

\displaystyle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}((\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}))}

(163)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}.

(164)

Putting it all together, we arrive at:

\displaystyle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}}\right)+b}

(165)

Note
{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right\|}_{2}=\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})
.
We now have the expression in terms of min and max eigenvalues of
X^{\top}X
and
X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}
.

Moreover since the expression is positive and monotonic, we also have

\displaystyle{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}\leq\frac{1}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})}}}\right)+b}}\right)^{4}}.

(166)

This expression is upper bounded by
1/b^{4}

when the
\max
term goes to 0.

To bound this, we will do something similar to the non-shifted setting
but instead of bounding a single constant
c
,
we will instead want to bound the three eigenvalue terms with

t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}>0
. (Note that all the involved matrices are PSD.)
Specifically: Let
\lambda_{1}:=\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)
,
\lambda_{2}:=\lambda_{\max}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})
,
\lambda_{3}:=\lambda_{\min}(X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(167)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}|\lambda_{1}\in[t_{1},\infty)\wedge\lambda_{2}\in[0,t_{2}]\wedge\lambda_{3}\in[t_{3},\infty)}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{1}\in[t_{1},\infty)\wedge\lambda_{2}\in[0,t_{2}]\wedge\lambda_{3}\in[t_{3},\infty)}}\right)

(168)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}|\lambda_{1}\in[0,t_{1})\vee\lambda_{2}\in(t_{2},\infty)\vee\lambda_{3}\in[0,t_{3})}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{1}\in[0,t_{1})\vee\lambda_{2}\in(t_{2},\infty)\vee\lambda_{3}\in[0,t_{3})}}\right)

(169)

Again, we will coarsely upper bound the first probability by 1 and
use a union over tail bounds to bound the second probability.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\max(0,\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}t_{1}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}t_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}t_{3}})+b)^{4}}+\frac{1}{b^{4}}\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{1}\in[0,t_{1})\vee\lambda_{2}\in(t_{2},\infty)\vee\lambda_{3}\in[0,t_{3})}}\right)

(170)

We will eventually want
t_{1}=\Omega(N),t_{2}=O(m),t_{3}=\Omega(m)
,
and we would like the second union over tail bounds to be vanishingly small.
Following Lemma
B.6
and
B.7
,
we set
t_{1}=N(1-\sqrt{d/N}-\psi)^{2}
,

t_{2}=m(1+\sqrt{d/m}+\psi)^{2}
,
and
t_{3}=m(1-\sqrt{d/m}-\psi)^{2}
.
We assume
n,m>100d
and set
\psi=1/10

in all cases.
The lemmas then give
t_{1}>63/100\cdot N,t_{2}<64/100\cdot m,t_{3}>63/100\cdot m
.

We can lastly confirm that with these values of
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}

we can drop the
\max
in the denominator of the first term, as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}t_{1}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}t_{2}^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}t_{3}}\geq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{63}{100}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}(\frac{64}{100}m)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(63/100)m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{>}}0

(171)

Since
d/\tau^{2}>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{63}{100}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}(\frac{64}{100}m)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(63/100)m}>\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{63}{100}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}(\frac{64}{100}m)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(63/100)m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{>}}0

(173)

Omitting algebra, and noting that
N=n+m
, this leads to the condition

\displaystyle n>((64/100)^{2}(100/63)-1)m=-0.349m

(174)

which is true. Given that this term is therefore strictly greater than 0 for the values of
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
we have chosen,
we can drop the
\max(0,\cdot)
.

We can then bound the relevant tail probabilities using
Lemma
B.1
,
Lemma
B.3
,
and Fact
B.5
and take a union bound to conclude that

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{1}\in[0,t_{1})\vee\lambda_{2}\in(t_{2},\infty)\vee\lambda_{3}\in[0,t_{3})}}\right)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N^{8}}}}\right).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}m^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}+b)^{4}}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{b^{4}N^{8}}}}\right)

(175)

and the first term dominates, giving the result.

∎

Lemma D.6

(bound on
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]
)
.

We can bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]
with the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}(d^{2}/b^{2}+d^{2})}}\right).

(176)

Proof.

Using the block-diagonal structure of
\Sigma
(and therefore
\Sigma^{-1}
), we can decompose
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y

into
X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{pub}}^{-1}y_{\mathrm{pub}}+X_{\mathrm{priv}}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{priv}}^{-1}y_{\mathrm{priv}}
.
(
\Sigma_{\mathrm{pub}}
is the upper-left block of
\Sigma
corresponding to public data and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{priv}}
the corresponding lower-right block for private data.)
Here,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{priv}}^{-1}
is simply
\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{n}
,
so we can reuse the upper bound from Lemma
C.8

with an additional factor of
\frac{1}{\sigma^{8}}
.

The term incorporating public data is more complicated.

We first note the following useful transformation (applying the Woodbury identity only to
\Sigma_{\mathrm{pub}}^{-1}
):

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{pub}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{m}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}X_{\mathrm{pub}}}}\right)^{-1}X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}.

(177)

Note:
For notational convenience, in the following, we will drop the “
\mathrm{pub}
” term from the subscript since we only need to bound
X_{\mathrm{pub}}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mathrm{pub}}y_{\mathrm{pub}}
.

We will use the following bound in several places:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{m}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X}}\right)^{-1}X^{\top}}}\right)X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(178)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}X^{\top}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X}}\right)^{-1}X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(179)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\lambda_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X^{\top}X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X}}\right)^{-1}X^{\top}X}}\right)}}\right)^{4}}}\right]

(180)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}}}}\right)^{4}}}\right]

(181)

Note that the third inequality follows from the fact that
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\succeq 0
, so we can upper bound the full term by applying an upper bound on the term within
(\cdot)^{4}
since the term inside it is always positive.
Next, we can break the expectation into two conditional expectations.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}}}}\right)^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}}}}\right)^{4}\mid\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\geq t_{1},\lambda_{\max}(X^{\top}X)\leq t_{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)\geq t_{1},\lambda_{\max}(X^{\top}X)\leq t_{2}}}\right)

Using Lemma
B.1
, we can upper bound the above expression by applying a high probability tail bound on
\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)
. When
N\geq 100d
, then with constant probability
\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)=m-\Omega(1)
and
\lambda_{\max}(X^{\top}X)=m-O(1)
. Plugging this result into the above expression, we conclude:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\lambda_{\min}(X^{\top}X)^{2}\frac{1}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}X}}\right\|}_{2}}}}\right)^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}.

(182)

When
\tau\to 0
the term
\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\beta\|^{4}
goes to
m^{4}
(giving the correct contribution of
m^{4}d^{2}
when combined with the contribution of
\beta
) and when
\tau\to\infty
the second part of the term cancels sending the public sample contribution to 0.

Once again, we decompose the term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\beta+Xv+\eta)}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(183)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(Xv+\eta)}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(184)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}Xv}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\eta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(185)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|v}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(186)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+

(187)

\displaystyle\quad\;\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|v}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(188)

Since
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{b}I_{d})
we have
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq d^{2}/b^{2}
. Similarly,
for
v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
we have
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|v}}\right\|}_{2}}}\right]\leq d^{2}
.
Finally,
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
gives
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta}}\right\|}_{2}}}\right]\leq m^{2}
.

We also need the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Sigma^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]

(189)

From Gordon’s theorem
[
Vershynin 2018
]

we have
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{d})^{4}\leq 8(m^{2}+d^{2})
.
With a small variation on the earlier analysis of
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X
, bounding
{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}\leq(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{d})^{2}

using Gordon’s theorem,
we also have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Sigma^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{d})^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{O(m)}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}
.

Overall, this gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\leq O\bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma^{8}}(n^{2}d^{2}+n^{4}d^{2}/b^{2})+

(190)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}(d^{2}/b^{2}+d^{2})+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}(m^{2}+d^{2})(m^{2})\bigr).

(191)

The first two terms follow from using Lemma
C.8
with only private samples (as they are not shifted and the same bound applies).

We can simplify, combining the first two terms and dropping lower-order terms (note that we assume
m\gg d
) to arrive at a form that will later be more convenient:

\displaystyle\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(n+m)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)^{4}(d^{2}/b^{2}+d^{2})}}\right).

(192)

∎

Lastly, in order to bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]
,
we will need a bound on
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\beta}-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]
.

Lemma D.7
.

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\beta}-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{n+m/\kappa}}}\right)
, where
\kappa=m\tau^{2}/d+1
.

Proof.

Recall that
\hat{\beta}
is the GLS estimator, where
\hat{\beta}=(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y
. Throughout the proof, we assume
d,n\gg d
, and that the matrix
X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X
is full rank with constant probability.

Let
\Lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X
. Fixing
\beta
, and taking the expectation over
X,y
, we can simplify the expression as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\hat{\beta}-\beta}}\right\|}_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y-\beta\|^{2}_{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|\Lambda^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y-\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda\beta\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|\Lambda^{-1}(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X\beta+\eta)-\Lambda\beta)\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|\Lambda^{-1}(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(X\beta+\eta)-X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\beta)\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|\Lambda^{-1}(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\eta)\|_{2}^{2}}}\right]

(193)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert\Lambda^{-1}X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\eta\rVert_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Bigl[\,\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\;\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\bigl[\lVert X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\eta\rVert_{2}^{2}\mid X\bigr]\Bigr]

(194)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\!\Bigl[\,\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\;\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}X\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\!\Bigl[\,\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\;\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)\Bigr],

(195)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta\eta^{\top}\mid X}}\right]=\Sigma
.
Since
\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}=1/\lambda_{\min}(\Lambda)^{2}
and

\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)\leq d\,\lambda_{\max}(\Lambda)
,

\displaystyle\lVert\Lambda^{-1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)\;\leq\;\frac{d\,\lambda_{\max}(\Lambda)}{\lambda_{\min}(\Lambda)^{2}}.

(196)

For Gaussian designs whitened by
\Sigma^{-1/2}
one has, with constant probability,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\Lambda)=\Omega\!\bigl(n+m/\kappa\bigr),\qquad\lambda_{\max}(\Lambda)=O\!\bigl(n+m/\kappa\bigr).

(197)

Substituting Eq.
197
into Eq.
196
and taking expectations,

\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\lVert\hat{\beta}-\beta\rVert_{2}^{2}\bigr]\;\leq\;C\,\frac{d\,(n+m/\kappa)}{\bigl(n+m/\kappa\bigr)^{2}}\;=\;O\!\Bigl(\tfrac{d}{\,n+m/\kappa\,}\Bigr),

for an absolute constant
C>0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma D.8
.

When
b=1/d
,
m+n=\Omega(d)
, and
\alpha=O(1)
, the expression:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}X\mathbb{E}[\beta|X,\eta]-X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}=O(1).

Proof.

Let
\gamma(\tau)=d/\tau^{2}+m/\sigma^{2}
. Note that when
\tau\to\infty
,
\gamma(\tau)
approaches
m/\sigma^{2}
(such that
m/\sigma^{2}-\frac{1}{\gamma(\tau)}\to 0
negating the effect of public samples),
and when
\tau\to 0
,
\gamma(\tau)\to\infty
(such that
m/\sigma^{2}-\frac{1}{\gamma(\tau)}\to m/\sigma^{2}
so that public and private samples contribute equally).

From Lemma
D.5
and Lemma
D.6
, we derive the following:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{b^{4}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}}

(198)

We first simplify just the inner square root.
Using the previous lemmas and applying the identity
\sqrt{x+y}\leq C\cdot(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})
:

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)}+b)^{2}}}}\right)\sqrt{O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)}}}\right)^{4}(d^{2}/b^{2}+d^{2})}}\right)}

(199)

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)}+b)^{2}}}}\right)O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)}}}\right)^{2}(d/b+d)}}\right)

(200)

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)})^{2}}}}\right)O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}N-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\frac{m^{2}}{\gamma(\tau)}}}\right)^{2}(d/b+d)}}\right)

(201)

Substituting
b=1/d
and making cancellations:

\displaystyle\leq(d^{2}+d)/d^{2}\leq 1+1/d

(202)

Finally, we substitute into the original expression,
combining with Lemma
D.7
(recall that
\kappa=m\tau^{2}/d+1
and
\alpha=O(1)
):

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\beta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|M(X,y)-\hat{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\sqrt{b^{4}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\Lambda}^{-1}}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|X^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}y}}\right\|}_{2}^{4}}}\right]}}

(203)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{n+m/\kappa}}}\right)+\alpha^{2}}}\right)\cdot O(1+1/d)}=O(1)

(204)

since
\alpha^{2}=O(1)
. ∎

Now, we are ready to prove the final result in Theorem
1.3
. For this we invoke the upper bound on
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]
in Lemma
D.1
and the lower bound of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}Z_{i}}}\right]=\Omega(d)
induced by Lemma
D.2
and Lemma
D.8
. This tells us that there exist positive constants
t_{1},t_{2}
and values
m_{0},n_{0}
such that for
m\geq m_{0},n\geq n_{0}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle t_{1}\cdot d\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i\in[N]}Z_{i}}}\right]\;\leq\;t_{2}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\varepsilon\alpha+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}md-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m^{2}d}{\frac{d}{\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}m}}}\right)}}}\right)

(205)

The upper bound interpolates between the private-sample-only
regime when
\tau\to\infty

and the all-samples regime when
\tau\to 0
(no distribution shift).
In particular, we can analyze the term inside the square root. It can be written as:

md-\frac{m^{2}\tau^{2}}{\kappa},

when
\sigma=1
and
\kappa=m\tau^{2}/d+1
.
Now, consider two regimes for the shift parameter
\tau
:

•

Large shift regime:
Suppose
\kappa=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m\tau^{2}}{d}}}\right)
, which corresponds to the condition
\tau=\omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{d/m}}}\right)
. Then:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|md-\frac{m^{2}\tau^{2}}{\kappa}}}\right|=o(1).

Hence, the only dominant term on the right-hand side becomes
n\varepsilon\alpha
, yielding the bound:

n\geq\frac{d}{\varepsilon\alpha}.

Note, that
n=\Omega(d/\alpha^{2})
is implied by the statistical non-private linear regression lower bound. Thus, in all
n=\Omega(\nicefrac{{d}}{{\varepsilon\alpha}}+\nicefrac{{d}}{{\alpha^{2}}})
.

•

Small shift regime:
Suppose
\tau=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{d/m}}}\right)
. Then:

md-\frac{m^{2}\tau^{2}}{\kappa}=\mathcal{O}(md),

and therefore the right-hand side becomes:

\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\varepsilon\alpha+\alpha\sqrt{md}}}\right),

which matches the bound in the no-shift case. Thus, the sample complexity requirements in the small shift setting of
\tau=O(\sqrt{d/m})
matches the no-shift setting.

•

Finally, we note that in the large shift setting, when
\alpha\raisebox{-3.69899pt}{~\shortstack{$>$ \\
[-0.07cm]
$\sim$}}~\tau
, then the OLS estimator that only uses public samples already satisfies an accuracy of
\alpha
in the
\ell_{2}
norm.

This completes the proof of our result in Theorem
5.2
.
