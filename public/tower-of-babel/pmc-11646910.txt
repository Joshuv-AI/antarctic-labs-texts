Short RP Interval and Long PR Interval

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarked first-degree atrioventricular block with a PR interval ≥500 ms is rare, leading to unusual P-wave placement. In this case, the P waves immediately after the QRS waves complicated rhythm interpretation. Close attention to P-wave morphology and fused premature ventricular complexes can be important for a proper diagnosis.Key Words: atrioventricular block, electrocardiogram, junctional escape rhythm

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man with a history of renal disease and 4-year-old coronary artery bypass grafting presented as asymptomatic because of an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG) during his routine checkup (Figure 1A). He was not taking any antiarrhythmic medication, and his blood examination exhibited no hyperkalemia or azotemia. The ECG from 1 year earlier is depicted in Figure 1B. Discussion and Rationale

The presenting ECG (Figure 1A) revealed no P-wave before the narrow QRS wave. In contrast, there were constant small notches just after the QRS and preceding the T waves, which are considered to be P waves (PP intervals: 750 ms; PR intervals: 590 ms) (red dots in Figure 1A). In instances where QRS waves are followed by a short interval of subsequent P waves (RP intervals: 160 ms), an escape rhythm with retrograde conduction to the atrium is easily imagined. However, closer examination of the previous ECG revealed the P waves to be centrally located between the R waves (PP intervals: 750 ms; PR intervals: 420 ms) (red dots in Figure 1B). The ventricular premature contraction did not perturb the following P-wave interval, suggesting that this P-wave was independent of preceding ventricular excitation (red asterisk in Figure 1B). The ventricular premature contraction occurring just after the P-wave might not conduct in a retrograde fashion because of the atrial functional refractory period. Notably, identical to the previous ECG, the P-wave polarity was positive in the inferior leads, negative in the aVR lead, and positive and/or negative in the V1 lead. The findings argue against retrograde conduction via the atrioventricular node and are consistent with sinus rhythm with marked first-degree atrioventricular block (AVB). Additionally, the P-wave duration ≥120 ms and a notched P-wave morphology in the inferior leads may indicate the presence of an interatrial block.1 Because the patient was asymptomatic and there was no evidence of type 2 AVB, he is being closely monitored for any symptoms or worsening of AVB.

First-degree AVB is defined as a PR interval ≥200 ms. Particularly, a PR interval ≥300 ms can lead to atrioventricular dyssynchrony.2,3 Although our case presented with a rare excessively prolonged PR interval of ≥500 ms, no clear evidence of hemodynamic deterioration such as diastolic mitral regurgitation was observed. One possible mechanism for this prolonged PR interval has been attributed to anterograde slow-pathway conduction. Because the fast pathway typically has a longer refractory period, premature atrial complexes can induce fast-pathway refractoriness and facilitate slow-pathway conduction, causing a significantly prolonged PR interval. This slow-pathway conduction can persist because of continuous retrograde concealment of the fast pathway.

In summary, the P waves appearing immediately after the QRS waves can mimic a junctional rhythm with a retrograde conduction. Comparing with previous ECGs, paying attention to P-wave morphology, and identifying the fused P-wave during a ventricular premature contraction may aid in the correct diagnosis.
