Clinical effectiveness of intensive care unit admission in older adults with COVID-19 during the first pandemic wave in Spain
Authors: Marta Arroyo-Huidobro, Carlos Pérez-López, Natàlia Pallarès, Cristian Tebé Cordomí, Antonella F Simonetti, Gabriela Abelenda-Alonso, Alexander Rombauts, Isabel Oriol Bermudez, Elisenda Izquierdo, Vicente Díaz-Brito, Gemma Molist, Guadalupe Gómez-Melis, Sebastian Videla, Alfons López Soto, Jordi Carratalà, Alejandro Rodríguez-Molinero, MetroSud, Divine study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of ICU admission on mortality and complications of older patients with COVID-19 hospitalized during the first COVID-19 wave in our setting.MethodsAn observational, multicenter, and ambispective study of COVID-19 patients aged > 70 years who were hospitalized during the first wave of the pandemic in the southern metropolitan area of Barcelona, Spain. The primary outcome of the study was the in-hospital mortality rate of patients admitted to the ICU compared with their appropriately matched controls who received care in hospital wards. The secondary outcome was the in-hospital rate of complications in ICU-admitted patients versus their matched controls.ResultsThe mortality rate was 76.6% in ICU-admitted patients and 64.9% among their matched controls, whereas the corresponding rates of complications were 73.5% and 32.4%, respectively. ICU admission shows a statistically significant impact on mortality (OR [95% CI] 2.50 [1.06–6.05]; p = 0.041). Additionally, complications were four times more likely in patients who were admitted to ICUs than in those admitted to general wards (OR [95% CI] 4.03 [1.64–9.91]; p = 0.0024).ConclusionsICU admission was not associated with improved survival in patients older than 70 years during the first COVID-19 wave. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and to assess the adequacy of ICU admission criteria for elderly patients.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07428-4.Keywords: COVID-19, Intensive care unit, Clinical complications, Mortality, Older adults

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a viral respiratory infection caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), declared a global pandemic in March 2020. To date, COVID-19 has caused approximately 777 million cases and 7.1 million deaths worldwide [1, 2]. In Spain, since the start of the pandemic, 13.9 million cases and 121,850 deaths have been reported, of which 3.2 million cases and 115,441 deaths were among patients aged ≥ 60 years [1, 2].

As individuals age, their immune system undergoes several changes, collectively known as immunesenescence. These changes alter the immune response, making it slower and less efficient. As a result, older adults are more vulnerable to infectious diseases, such as COVID-19 [3]. In addition, the age-related physiological changes, the high number of comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, or dementia), and the associated polypharmacy in older adults contribute to poorer health outcomes in this population, such as morbidity and mortality [3].

In fact, age is one of the most relevant factors associated with mortality in patients with COVID-19. A meta-analysis including 611,583 individuals with COVID-19 showed that mortality increased markedly, especially from the sixth decade of life, so that mortality rates in patients aged 80 years or more were six times higher than in younger patients [4]. The increase in mortality with age also applied to patients with COVID-19 admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) [5], where patients aged ≥ 70 years had a 28-day mortality rate of 41% following ICU admission [6].

During the pandemic, the high number of patients with COVID-19—especially the high number of those who were critically ill and required intensive care—often led healthcare systems to the verge of collapse. In Catalonia, a region in the northeast of Spain, the percentage of patients with COVID-19 admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) relative to the number of ICU beds before the pandemic reached 380.06% during the first COVID-19 wave [7]. Due to this limited availability of ICU resources, many older patients with severe disease could not be admitted to ICUs and were instead managed in conventional wards. This exceptional situation allowed us to compare the health outcomes of patients admitted to the ICU with those of other patients with similar severity who did not access to this resource. We hypothesized that ICU admission would benefit the clinical outcomes of these patients, therefore, the lack of access to this resource likely had negative consequences. To test this hypothesis, the aim of the study was to evaluate the impact of ICU admission on the mortality and complications among older patients with COVID-19 hospitalized during the first COVID-19 wave in our setting. This is the first study to analyze the impact of the ICU on elderly patients with COVID-19.

Methods

Study design, population, and data sources

This was an observational, multicenter, and retrospective analysis of a prospectively collected database study of elderly COVID-19 patients hospitalized during the first wave of COVID-19 (i.e., between March 1 and April 29, 2020) in the southern metropolitan area of Barcelona, Spain. The data were obtained from the electronic medical records of the prospective cohort COVID-MetroSud [8, 9], which includes consecutive adult patients diagnosed with COVID-19 and admitted to one of the following healthcare centers in the region: Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge (a tertiary-level hospital), Complex Hospitalari Universitari Moisés Broggi, Consorci Sanitari Alt Penedès i Garraf, Hospital de Viladecans, and Parc Sanitari Sant Joan de Déu (secondary-level hospitals). These centers collectively serve approximately 1,200,000 inhabitants and have around 2,000 beds, including over 150 intensive care beds (expanded to 200 during the peak of the COVID-19 epidemic) and 200 intermediate care beds (i.e., beds intended to offer continuous monitoring and specialized treatments without the complexity of an ICU).

Hospitalized patients aged > 70 years who were diagnosed with COVID-19 using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (rRT-PCR) tests on nasal or pharyngeal swabs were included in the study. Patients with respiratory symptoms compatible with COVID-19 who were treated upon admission but had negative rRT-PCR results for SARS-CoV-2 were excluded, as well as patients who were not admitted, either because they were transferred to other hospitals or because they died in the emergency department. Patients were classified into two subcohorts based on whether they were admitted to an ICU (ICU subcohort) or a general hospital ward (non-ICU subcohort).

Then, patients in the two subcohorts were matched on a 1:1 ratio following a thorough matching procedure to ensure that controls had at least the same health deterioration as ICU-admitted patients. The follow-up period in the ICU subcohort started at ICU admission and ended at the date of death or hospital discharge. In the non-ICU subcohort, patients were followed up from when they fulfilled the matching criteria until death or hospital discharge.

Patient data were prospectively collected using electronic medical records and the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform, version 10.7.0 [10, 11]. All study data were pseudonymized, identified with a case code, and stored without accompanying personal identification data.

Patient data procedures followed the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) and the Organic Law 3/2018 of December 5 on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights in Spain. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge. In addition, the study was conducted following the ethical principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments, and with the requirements of Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and current Spanish regulations. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent from patients, as the study was based on the use of secondary, pseudonymized data. For pseudonymization, all study data were identified by a case code and stored without accompanying personal identification data.

Outcomes and variables

The primary outcome of the study was the in-hospital mortality rate of patients admitted to ICUs compared with their matched controls who received care in hospital wards. The secondary outcome was the rate of in-hospital complications in ICU-admitted patients versus their matched controls.

Demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables were recorded. Demographic variables included age and sex and institutionalized. Baseline clinical data included smoking habit, comorbidities, degree of comorbidity according to the Charlson Index [12], and functional independence prior to hospital admission as measured by the Barthel Index [13]. Clinical data at hospital admission included the presence of cough and dyspnea, oxygen saturation (SatO2), fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), and radiological involvement (none, unilateral, or bilateral). Biochemical variables at hospital admission were serum creatinine, sodium, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels.

Clinical complications during the hospital stay (whether at ICUs or general wards) were also recorded. We classified the complications into four groups: (1) renal complications, including renal failure; (2) cardiological complications, including heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, torsade de pointes, and myocarditis; (3) complications related to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, including sepsis, shock, multiple organ failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation; and (4) acute confusional syndrome during hospitalization.

Matching procedure

Matching criteria included age, sex, SatO2, FiO2, radiological involvement, serum CRP levels, and serum creatinine levels. All these variables had been previously identified as clinically relevant COVID-19 prognostic markers [9].

Patients from the ICU and non-ICU subcohorts were matched on a 1:1 ratio using a two-step procedure, summarized in Fig. 1. In the first step, we used a brute-force algorithm to identify all possible matching controls (i.e., non-ICU-admitted patients) for each patient in the ICU subcohort with the same sex and similar age (difference of up to ± 7 years). Additionally, all controls had to present with a FiO2 > 80%, together with similar SatO2 (difference of up to ± 2%) and CRP levels (difference of up to ± 50 mg/dL) and the same radiological involvement (normal, unilateral, or bilateral) than those of their corresponding ICU-admitted patients at the time of ICU admission. However, since radiological and blood tests were not performed every day, time lags of ± 1 and ± 3 days from ICU admission were allowed for CRP and radiological measurements, respectively. In the second step, we used a propensity score analysis to select one of all possible controls identified in the first step according to its similarity to the corresponding ICU-admitted patient in terms of SatO2, CRP and creatinine levels.

These matching criteria were defined through an iterative process, involving several adjustments, until highly comparable controls were obtained across all parameters in relation to the ICU patients. The number of variables included in the matching was intentionally limited to the minimum required to achieve homogeneous groups, because adding further matching characteristics beyond a certain level of similarity led to an excessive loss of sample size without providing a meaningful improvement in comparability or in the robustness of the results.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD) or the median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th and 75th percentiles), whereas categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages.

To assess the impact of ICU admission on mortality and complications, multivariate logistic regression models were fitted, including variables considered clinically relevant and showing an association with the outcome in bivariate analyses, to account for potential confounding. The estimates were reported with their odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). All model assumptions, including the assessment of residuals, absence of multicollinearity, and overall goodness-of-fit, were properly evaluated. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a two-sided alpha value of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 3.6.1.

Patients who had developed severe complications before being transferred to the ICU—such as acute coronary syndrome, ventricular tachycardia, torsade de pointes, myocarditis, empyema, pulmonary thromboembolism, stroke, or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (i.e., sepsis, shock, multiple organ failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation)—were excluded from the complication analysis, because the occurrence of these events may have triggered ICU admission, introducing potential reverse-causality bias.

In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed for the primary outcome (mortality) by excluding patients with severe complications prior to ICU admission, in order to assess the potential impact of reverse-causality bias. Specifically, we evaluated whether preferential ICU referral of patients who had already developed life-threatening complications could have influenced the estimated association between ICU admission and mortality.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study patients

Cohort COVID-MetroSud included 1539 patients with COVID-19 during the first wave who were > 70 years old. Of them, 88 were admitted to ICUs and 1462 were admitted to general hospital wards. After applying the matching procedure, we could match 154 patients: 77 were admitted to ICUs and 77 were admitted to general hospital wards (Fig. 2).

Patient selection flowchart

The baseline characteristics of the matched study patients are summarized in Table 1. Their median (IQR) age was 74.5 (72.0–77.0) years, and over half of them were male. The comorbidity degree as assessed by the Charlson Index and the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, such as obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and smoking, was similar between the two subcohorts or favored ICU-admitted patients. Regarding respiratory variables, the frequency of cough and dyspnea and the median of SatO2 were comparable between ICU and non-ICU subcohorts, whereas non-ICU patients required more FiO2 than ICU patients. Moreover, serum creatinine, sodium and CRP levels were comparable between ICU and non-ICU subcohorts.

In-hospital mortality and complications

As can be seen in Table 2, deaths occurred in 59 (76.6%) and 50 (64.9%) patients in ICU and non-ICU subcohorts, respectively. Overall, renal complications were the most common, followed by cardiac complications, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and acute confusional syndrome.

Discussion

In this study of the impact of ICU admission on the mortality and complications of older patients with COVID-19 who were hospitalized during the first COVID-19 wave, we found that the rate of mortality and the rate of complications were higher in ICU-admitted patients than in their matched controls. Older age, presence of dyspnea, and ICU admission were associated with higher mortality rates. Additionally, complications were four times more likely to occur in ICU-admitted patients than in patients admitted to general hospital wards.

To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the health outcomes of older patients with COVID-19 admitted to ICUs with those admitted to general hospital wards. This comparison cannot be made through interventional studies due to ethical issues. However, the first COVID-19 wave led to an unprecedented increase in the need for intensive care treatment, with an overload of the public healthcare system in Spain [14]. This exceptional situation allowed us to perform an analysis of the data from patients admitted to ICUs, comparing them with data from patients with very similar characteristics who could not be admitted to an ICU due to a shortage of available beds.

We have found only one previous study analyzing the outcomes of elderly patients admitted to the ICU compared with those not admitted. Valley et al. [15] examined patients aged > 64 years with pneumonia whose need for ICU admission was uncertain and found that, after adjusting for patients’ characteristics, ICU admission was associated with lower mortality. However, the patients in this study did not have COVID-19. The lack of studies evaluating the impact of ICU admission on patients with COVID-19 prevents any comparison with our results.

The mortality rates observed in our study are higher than those reported in previous analyses of older patients hospitalized with COVID-19 during the first wave in Spain. In these studies, the mortality rates reported for patients aged ≥ 70 years, ≥ 75 years, ≥ 80 years, and ≥ 85 years were 35.2%, 37.0%, 44.1%, and 48.8%, respectively [15–18]. However, all these studies included all hospitalized patients, whereas our study focuses on patients admitted to the ICU or those with similar clinical characteristics. Therefore, the differences in mortality rates were expected.

Other studies have reported the complication rates in older patients with COVID-19 during the first wave. For example, in a previous study, we found that the global rate of complications in patients aged ≥ 80 years was 48.6%, ranging from 3.03% to 81.0%, depending on the complication [9]. In patients aged ≥ 75 years, Szklarzewska et al. reported a global rate of complications of 34.3%, ranging from 0.4% to 39.4% [19]. Additionally, other authors found that the rates of different complications in patients aged ≥ 80 years ranged from 0.7% to 35.7% [18], whereas non-cardiac complications in patients aged ≥ 75 years ranged from 5.2% to 22.6% [17]. Nevertheless, these complications corresponded to all hospitalized patients, regardless of their health status and need for intensive care. This fact, together with the variability in the type of complications reported in each study, precludes any direct comparison with our results. Moreover, to our knowledge, no studies have evaluated the impact of ICU admission on the rate of complications in COVID-19.

The main limitation of this study is the potential presence of certain biases and residual confounding, given its observational nature. As we did not anticipate the observed results, we carefully scrutinized the data and procedures to address possible sources of bias. In this regard, every effort was made to minimize bias and confounding, and whenever this was not possible, we examined the direction in which these factors could have affected the results, confirming that in all cases their effect would be opposite to that which could invalidate our findings. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed when deemed pertinent. Below, we summarize some of the critical points considered.

The time from admission until controls became eligible as controls was shorter than the time from admission until ICU admission. However, there is no risk of immortal time bias that could artificially extend ICU patients’ survival, because the analyses disregard the time before patients became comparable and assess mortality only from that point onward. Regarding the direction of this potential bias, if it existed, it would artificially prolong ICU survival, which is not what our results show.

A potential reverse causality bias may have been present in the ICU group if patients were admitted to the ICU precisely because they already had severe complications that conferred a higher a priori risk of death, even before ICU admission. This situation could have led to the erroneous conclusion that ICU admission increases mortality risk. To address this possibility, a sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding patients with life-threatening complications present prior to ICU admission. This analysis yielded a mortality likelihood consistent with that of the primary analysis, although statistical significance was lost due to the reduced sample size. Therefore, the presence of reverse causality bias was considered unlikely.

Given the invasive nature of ICU treatments and the high degree of instrumentation, a greater burden of complications in the ICU group is clinically plausible and does not necessarily imply that baseline severity was inadequately balanced between groups. However, the higher complication rate among ICU patients observed in this study may still be influenced by residual confounding and by possible detection bias, since ICU patients are continuously monitored and undergo more frequent laboratory and clinical assessments, which may increase the likelihood of identifying complications compared with ward patients.

The study included different centers of varying size and level of complexity, but their distribution between the two comparison groups was fairly symmetrical. Therefore, we do not expect inter-center differences to have affected the results.

One might argue that the matching process was not sufficiently strict, for example, allowing for an age difference of up to seven years between groups during the procedure. This decision was made because applying stricter limits of similarity would have caused an excessive loss of sample size for the analysis. Nevertheless, this did not have a real impact on the results, since the final matched groups turned out to be extremely well balanced, as shown in Table 1. Similarly, not all variables from Table 1 were used in the matching procedure for the same reason; however, the overall distribution of variables in the final sample was balanced, which is ultimately what matters. Therefore, what matters is not so much the process by which we achieved the two balanced groups, but rather the fact that the study groups were indeed balanced.

We acknowledge that some information bias may remain unaccounted for, although we could not clearly identify it, and that some degree of residual confounding is inevitable, given the limited information in a study based on secondary data. For these reasons, we recommend that the results be interpreted as a starting point for further research on this topic.

The results might have been different in a situation where the healthcare system was not overloaded (neither ICUs nor wards), and it cannot be confirmed that they are generalizable to non-crisis conditions. Our cohort was treated during the first pandemic wave, when clinical decisions were made under intense triage pressure, therapeutic protocols were still evolving, and neither vaccination nor later viral variants were present. These extraordinary features of early 2020—together with the surge in critical care demand—limit the direct applicability of our findings to subsequent phases of the pandemic or to future respiratory outbreaks occurring under standard conditions. However, under normal circumstances, patients admitted to the ward and those admitted to the ICU are not comparable, and therefore such a study could never have been conducted.

The extreme ICU overload during the first wave is also relevant when interpreting our findings. We do not have detailed data to differentiate standard versus surge or extended ICU beds, nor do we have data on the exact proportion of ICU-trained specialists involved in each case. Although this could have partially affected ICU care quality at peak demand, the matched ICU and non-ICU groups had similar baseline severity, and non-ICU patients were also necessarily managed by non-intensive-care teams. Despite this, survival was higher in the non-ICU group, suggesting that surge-capacity conditions may have contributed to overall outcomes but are unlikely to fully explain the main between-group differences observed in our study.

In our study, we relied on electronic health records, which allowed us to account for premorbid comorbidity, baseline functional status (Barthel index), and cognitive vulnerability (previous cognitive impairment and delirium during hospitalization). However, standardized information on frailty and detailed goals of care, among other relevant variables, was not routinely available. This has two implications: first, it may represent an additional source of residual confounding in our analysis; second, it mirrors real-world acute care practice, where physicians managing older patients—particularly during the first pandemic wave—often lack access to, and therefore do not systematically incorporate, comprehensive geriatric assessment when deciding ICU referral [20]. Consequently, the geriatric information missing from the records for research purposes was also unlikely to have been decisive in ICU triage decisions.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights, as no previous data are available regarding the impact of ICU admission on mortality and complications in older patients hospitalized with COVID-19 during the first pandemic wave. At the same time, we believe that—even if these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to later clinical scenarios—they are highly illustrative of a key message: older patients may not benefit from ICU admission in all circumstances.

Our results indicate that ICU admission does not automatically translate into better outcomes for elderly COVID-19 patients. Frequently, decisions regarding ICU admission for older patients are based on clinicians’ subjective assessments of their functional and cognitive abilities, which may not fully reflect their actual physiological reserve or likelihood of benefiting from intensive treatment [20].

Given that older patients typically have diminished functional reserve and a higher likelihood of adverse outcomes, it is essential to perform a thorough, individualized risk–benefit evaluation before proceeding with ICU admission. Such an assessment should incorporate not only the severity of the current illness but also baseline functional status, comorbidities, and overall prognosis. This careful approach can help avoid subjecting patients to invasive and potentially futile treatments, ultimately ensuring that the level of care provided is both appropriate and aligned with their best interests.

Conclusions

In summary, our results suggest that, in our setting during the first COVID-19 wave, ICU admission was not associated with improved survival in patients older than 70 years, while it was associated with a substantially higher complication burden. Although these findings derive from an exceptional early-pandemic context and should not be extrapolated to routine, non-overloaded conditions, they nonetheless convey an important message: ICU admission should not be assumed to be universally beneficial for older patients. Triage decisions for older adults must be individualized, integrating acute severity with premorbid health status and expected benefit, and considering alternative levels of care when appropriate. Further research should refine ICU admission criteria for older patients in both crisis and non-crisis settings.
