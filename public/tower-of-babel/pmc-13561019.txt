PRKX-mediated stabilization of PD-L1 characterizes an immunosuppressive gastric cancer subtype
Authors: Qiyue Wang, Yinqi Chen, Dongdong Huang, Hui Wu, Tongshuo Wu, Yuejun Han, Chengzhi Cai, Jingjie Li, Yifei Shao, Jian Ruan, Haiping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) derives limited benefit from immunotherapy, with clinical responses observed in only a minority of patients. Increasing evidence suggests that heterogeneity within the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) is a critical determinant of immunotherapeutic efficacy, highlighting the need for precise immune stratification and the identification of molecular biomarkers that shape the TME.MethodsWe integrated single-cell transcriptomic data from our cohort and public datasets to characterize immune microenvironment heterogeneity in GC. Functional experiments were performed using in vitro assays and in vivo mouse models to investigate the molecular mechanisms regulating immune exhaustion. Clinical relevance was evaluated using tumor specimens from patients with GC receiving anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy. Survival analyses and biomarker evaluation were conducted to assess the prognostic and predictive value of candidate markers.ResultsWe identified two distinct GC immune microenvironment subtypes: the immunosuppressive (GC1) and the immune-activated (GC2). PRKX was identified as a key regulator associated with immune heterogeneity and exhaustion. Clinically, a high density of PanCK+ PRKX+ PD-L1+ tumor cells was significantly associated with poor prognosis and served as a robust biomarker predicting 5-year survival in patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, PRKX phosphorylates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) at T285, promoting YWHAE recruitment and preventing UBE2M-mediated ubiquitination and degradation, thereby stabilizing PD-L1 protein. Through this phosphorylation-dependent regulation of PD-L1 stability, PRKX suppresses CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity, promotes immune exhaustion, and limits the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo. Therapeutically, lipid nanoparticle-mediated delivery of PRKX-targeting siRNA effectively suppressed PRKX expression and synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy to enhance antitumor efficacy.ConclusionPRKX drives immune exhaustion in the GC1 subtype by stabilizing PD-L1 through phosphorylation-dependent inhibition of ubiquitination, thereby promoting immune evasion. Targeting PRKX represents a potential strategy to overcome resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy, and PRKX expression may serve as a prognostic biomarker to guide immunotherapy in GC.Keywords: Gastric Cancer, Tumor microenvironment - TME, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is among the top five cancers worldwide in incidence and mortality, causing nearly 1 million new cases and over 650,000 deaths annually.1 2 Despite advances in surgical techniques and systemic therapies, the prognosis for patients with advanced GC remains poor. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment; however, the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, making systemic chemotherapy the primary therapeutic option.3 4 In recent years, with the rapid development of precision medicine, novel treatment strategies for GC have gradually emerged, including immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab, which has been approved for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive recurrent or metastatic GC.5–9 However, the clinical benefit of immunotherapy remains limited, with only a minority of patients achieving durable responses.10

To overcome the limited efficacy of targeted therapies, several clinical trials have employed the combined positive score (CPS) to stratify patients for pembrolizumab treatment.11 Nevertheless, PD-L1 expression is governed by multiple regulatory mechanisms, and the ability of CPS to reliably predict response to immunotherapy and enable precise patient selection remains contentious.12 This biomarker limitation is further compounded by the substantial heterogeneity of tumor-intrinsic programs and the complex immune microenvironment in GC, both of which contribute to the highly variable clinical responses to immunotherapy.13 Therefore, a multidimensional analysis of the molecular landscape of GC is needed to identify and integrate novel biomarkers, thereby enabling more accurate patient stratification and informed immunotherapy decision-making.

In recent years, defining distinct tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) subtypes has emerged as a key strategy for precise patient stratification and improved therapeutic efficacy across various cancers. Several studies have stratified tumor microenvironments (TME) by integrating cellular subpopulations and molecular characteristics.14 Consistently, comprehensive analyses in colorectal cancer, melanoma, and metastatic breast cancer have identified distinct microenvironmental subtypes that correlate with patient prognosis, therapeutic vulnerabilities, and responses to immunotherapy.15–17 These findings highlight the critical role of precise TIME stratification in guiding personalized treatment strategies. However, the molecular characteristics of the immune microenvironment in GC remain poorly understood, highlighting the need for comprehensive profiling to identify novel biomarkers and enable precise patient stratification for improved immunotherapy outcomes.

Among key immune regulators, PD-L1 protein expression is tightly controlled by various post-translational modifications (PTMs), including glycosylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, palmitoylation, and SUMOylation.18–20 These modifications play a key role in determining PD-L1 stability, degradation pathways, and membrane localization. Among these regulatory mechanisms, phosphorylation has emerged as an important modification governing PD-L1 stability and turnover. Previous studies have demonstrated that several kinases, including GSK3β, AMPK, and JAK1, can regulate PD-L1 stability through phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms that influence ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.21–23 Moreover, crosstalk between phosphorylation and other PTMs, such as glycosylation and ubiquitination, further fine-tunes PD-L1 stability and immune checkpoint signaling.24 25 Despite these advances, the kinase networks that regulate PD-L1 stability in specific TIME remain largely unknown, particularly in GC.

To address these challenges, we integrated multi-omics profiling with single-cell transcriptomics to characterize the molecular architecture of the GC TIME. Our analysis identified two distinct immune microenvironment subtypes, an immunosuppressive subtype (GC1) enriched with PD-L1+ epithelial cells and dysfunctional CD8+ T cells, and an immune-activated subtype (GC2) characterized by robust cytotoxic immune responses. Mechanistically, we uncovered PRKX as a key regulator driving immune exhaustion by promoting phosphorylation-dependent stabilization of PD-L1. Furthermore, we demonstrated that lipid nanoparticle-mediated delivery of PRKX-targeting siRNA restores antitumor immunity and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade in vivo. Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism regulating PD-L1 stability and identify PRKX as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for improving immunotherapy outcomes in GC.

Methods

Datasets

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets used in this study were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus database, including GSE163558 (our own cohort),26 GSE134520,27 GSE150290,28 GSE184198,29 GSE231539,30 GSE246662,31 GSE249874,32 and GSE251990.33 Raw SRA files were converted to FASTQ format using the NCBI SRA Toolkit (sra-tools V.3.2.1). The FASTQ files were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and quantified using Cell Ranger count (V.7.0, 10x Genomics) to generate gene expression matrices.

For cohort-level validation and cross-dataset comparison, bulk RNA-seq and corresponding clinical data of The Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) were downloaded and processed from the TCGA database. Details of data sources are summarized in online supplemental table S2.

scRNA-seq analysis

All downstream analyses were conducted in R (V.4.4.2) using the Seurat (V.5) package. Quality control thresholds were set as 200≤nFeature_RNA≤5000 and percent.mt<20%. Doublets or multiplets were identified and removed using scDblFinder (V.1.22.0). Each dataset was normalized using the LogNormalize method (scale.factor=10,000), and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction. Harmony (V.1.2.4) was applied for batch correction and integration across datasets. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed to identify variables that best discriminate between predefined groups based on multivariate data. Cell subtypes were annotated based on canonical marker genes into epithelial, stromal, B cell, myeloid, and T-cell lineages and their subpopulations. Marker genes (differential expression genes, DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with multiple testing correction via the Benjamini-Hochberg method (p<0.05, log2(fold-change) >0.5 and min.pt >0.1). Samples were further stratified into GC1 and GC2 immune microenvironment types based on immune (B, myeloid, T cell) subcluster compositions using PLS-DA and consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) clustering. Functional enrichment of key gene sets was performed using clusterProfiler (V.4.16.0), and cross-validation with TCGA-STAD was conducted via Spearman’s correlation analysis.

Clinical tissue samples

For the training cohort, we retrospectively analyzed tumor samples from 19 patients with primary GC in the prospective clinical trial NCT04065282 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University. All patients received neoadjuvant sintilimab (anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) monoclonal antibody (mAb)) plus CapeOx therapy prior to surgery: sintilimab (3 mg/kg for body weight <60 kg or 200 mg for ≥60 kg, day 1), oxaliplatin (130 mg/m², day 1), and capecitabine (1,000 mg/m², days 1–14, 21-day cycles, total three cycles). All patients had complete 5-year survival follow-up data.

The validation cohort was derived from a retrospective analysis of patients with histologically confirmed GC treated at our institution. Patients received three to five cycles of neoadjuvant SOX chemotherapy (oxaliplatin and S-1) in combination with other anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy, followed by surgical resection. The median follow-up time was 17.9 months. Tumor specimens used for biomarker analysis were obtained from pretreatment biopsy samples collected prior to the initiation of neoadjuvant therapy.

Clinicopathological characteristics, treatment information, follow-up data, and survival outcomes were collected from the medical records. Biomarker data, including the CPS and microsatellite instability status were also collected from the medical records. All tissues analyzed were treatment-naïve pretherapy samples. The patients’ information is presented in online supplemental table S3.

Antibodies

Antibodies used in this study are presented in online supplemental table S4.

Cell lines and culture

Human and murine cell lines used in this study included AGS (human GC cell line), HGC-27 (human GC cell line), HEK-293T (human embryonic kidney), and MFC (murine GC cell line). HEK-293T was cultured in DMEM (Hyclone, Cat# SH30243.01), while AGS, HGC-27 and MFC cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Hyclone, Cat# SH30027.01), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ and routinely authenticated and tested for Mycoplasma contamination. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling prior to cell-based experiments. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored, with the most recent test performed in July 2025 using the Myco-Lumi Luminescent Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Beyotime). Cell lines were stored in liquid nitrogen at low passage numbers (<3 passages). For experiments, cells were used after two to three passages following thawing, corresponding to approximately 7–10 days in culture.

Animal studies

4-week-old female 615 mice were obtained from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology. 6-week-old female nude mice were purchased from Hangzhou Medical College. 6-week-old huPBMC-(MHC-II/B2M-KO M-NSG) mice (Cat. NO. NM-HU-263012) were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee and conducted in compliance with national and institutional guidelines. Mice were monitored regularly for health status and tumor growth. Animals reaching predefined endpoints were humanely euthanized for tissue collection.

Plasmid and siRNA construction, and cell transfection

sgRNAs targeting human PRKX were cloned into the LentiCRISPRv2 vector (BsmBI sites), and YWHAE shRNAs were inserted into pLKO.1-puro (EcoRI/AgeI sites). Coding sequences of PRKX, PD-L1, and YWHAE were subcloned into pcDNA3.1-Flag/HA/Myc for transient expression, and into pLVX-3Flag-MCS-IRES-Puro or pLenti-CMV-Puro vectors for stable overexpression. Transient plasmid transfections were performed in HEK-293T cells using Lipo293 (Beyotime, Cat# C0521) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the generation of stable cell lines, lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting psPAX2, pMD2.G, and the corresponding expression vector into HEK-293T cells. Viral supernatants were collected, filtered through 0.45 µm filters, and used to infect target cells in the presence of polybrene (8 µg/mL). Infected cells were selected with puromycin (1–2 μg/mL) to establish stable overexpression or knockout cell lines. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specifically targeting human UBE2M, human PRKX, and murine Prkx were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), with a non-targeting scrambled siRNA used as a negative control. Cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNAs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 13778030) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The efficiency of gene knockout, knockdown, or overexpression was confirmed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blotting. All sgRNA, shRNA, and siRNA sequences are listed in online supplemental table S5.

In vitro CD8+ T-cell experiments

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors were isolated by density gradient centrifugation. CD8+ T cells were positively selected using CD8 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130–045-201) and activated with anti-CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11161D) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio in the presence of recombinant human interleukin (IL)-2 (30 U/mL; PeproTech, Cat# 200–02) for 72 hours. HGC-27 cells (1×10⁴ per well, 96-well plate) were co-cultured with activated CD8+ T cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 1:5. For the murine CD8+ T-cell co-culture assay, the spleen was collected from MFC tumor-bearing 615 mice and mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Splenocytes were harvested by centrifugation, and red blood cells were lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# C3702). After washing, splenic CD8+ T cells were positively selected using CD8a (Ly-2) MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130–117-044) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated CD8+ T cells were short-term reactivated with anti-mouse CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Dynabeads Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11452D) at a bead-to-cell ratio of 1:1 in the presence of recombinant mouse IL-2 (30 U/mL; Novoprotein Scientific, Cat# CK24) for 72 hours. MFC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1×10⁴ cells per well and allowed to adhere before being co-cultured with the reactivated CD8+ T cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 1:5. After co-culture, the non-adherent CD8+ T cells were removed, and the viability of the remaining adherent MFC cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. For both human and murine co-culture systems, cell viability was measured using CCK-8 (Beyotime, Cat# C0039) by adding 10 µL reagent per well and recording absorbance at 450 nm after 1 hour incubation. Supernatants and cells were collected for RT-qPCR to assess T-cell activation and cytotoxicity.

Subcutaneous tumorigenesis model

A subcutaneous tumor model was established by subcutaneously injecting 1×10⁶ MFC cells into 615 mice and 5×106 HGC-27 cells into nude mice. In addition, a humanized subcutaneous xenograft model was established by subcutaneously inoculating 5×106 HGC-27 cells into huPBMC-(MHCII/B2M-KO M-NSG) mice. Tumor volume (length×width²×0.5) was measured regularly until the experimental endpoint (typically 25 days or on reaching ethical limits). For immunotherapy, 615 mice received anti-PD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell, Cat# BE0101, 200 µg/mouse, intraperitoneal [i.p.]) every 3 days for four doses once tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³. HGC-27 tumor-bearing huPBMC-(MHC-II/B2M-KO M-NSG) mice were treated with an anti-human PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# A2005; 5 mg/kg, intravenous) once weekly. Tumors were harvested at the experimental endpoint for flow cytometric analysis.

For siRNA delivery in the 615 mice model, lipid nanoparticle (LNP) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Cat# HY-K2022) and complexed with siRNAs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the syngeneic 615 mouse model, three mouse Prkx-targeting siRNAs were mixed at equal ratios prior to LNP encapsulation. Similarly, for the humanized huPBMC-(MHC-II/B2M-KO M-NSG) mouse model bearing HGC-27 tumors, three human PRKX-targeting siRNAs were mixed at equal ratios and encapsulated into LNPs. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, LNP-siRNA complexes were administered by intratumoral injection. In the respective combination-treatment groups, LNP-murine Prkx siRNA treatment was administered together with the anti-mouse PD-1 antibody in 615 mice, whereas LNP-human PRKX siRNA treatment was combined with pembrolizumab in huPBMC-(MHC-II/B2M-KO M-NSG) mice, according to the dosing schedules described above.

For survival analysis, mice were observed daily for survival status, general condition, body weight loss, tumor ulceration, impaired mobility, and other signs of distress. Survival time was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to spontaneous death or euthanasia on reaching the predefined humane endpoints approved by the institutional animal ethics committee. Animals euthanized because of humane endpoint criteria were recorded as events in the survival analysis.

Safety evaluation of LNP-siRNA treatment

A separate experiment was conducted to evaluate the in vivo safety of LNP-siPrkx treatment using MFC tumor-bearing 615 mice. The mice were divided into an untreated control group and an LNP-siPrkx treatment group. The untreated control mice received no therapeutic intervention, whereas mice in the treatment group received intratumoral administration of LNP-siPrkx according to the dosing regimen described above. At the experimental endpoint, peripheral blood was collected from anesthetized mice via the retro-orbital venous plexus for hematological and blood biochemistry analysis. Following blood collection, the mice were euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested for histopathological examination.

Flow cytometry

Surface staining was performed in phosphate-buffered saline containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) using manufacturer-recommended antibody dilutions (typically 1:100) at 4°C for 15 min in the dark. For intracellular cytokine staining, activated CD8+ T cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (50 ng/mL), ionomycin (1 µg/mL), and monensin (2 µM) for 6 hours, fixed, permeabilized, and stained at 4°C for 30 min. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo (V.10.x).

Immunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting

Cells were extracted by lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40, 0.5 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH=8.0)) with phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were purified with centrifuging at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The appropriate antibodies were used for immunoprecipitation (IP) or immunoblotting (IB) with supernatants. After 6–8 hours gentle rotation at 4°C, 20 µL of protein A/G magnetic beads (Santa Cruz) were added to each lysate, followed by 1–2 hours of incubation at 4°C with moderate rotation. Beads were washed with NT2 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl, 0.05% NP-40) three times followed by NT2 High Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl, 0.05% NP-40) once for 5 min at 4°C. Then, 50 µL of 1×sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer was used to elute the beads, and the eluted protein or protein complexes were identified by IB. Western blot and Phosbind western blot were performed as previously described.34 35 Primary antibodies specific for the following proteins were used at a dilution ratio of 1:1,000.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA of both CD8+ T cells and HGC-27 cells were extracted by an AFTSpin Bacterial Fast RNA Extraction Kit (Abclonal, Cat# RK30120). Complementary DNA was synthesized by using ABScript Neo RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (Abclonal, Cat# RK20433). RT-qPCR was performed using the 2X Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal, Cat# RK21203). The following primers we used were shown in online supplemental table S6.

Kinase activity

The kinase activity was followed by the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D systems, Cat# EA004). The plasmid of Flag-PRKX, HA-PD-L1, HA-PD-L1ΔICD were transfected in HEK-293T cells. Flag-tagged PRKX protein and HA-tagged PD-L1 or PD-L1ΔICD protein were eluted with 3×Flag Peptide (APExBio), or HA peptide (APExBio) for further analysis.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using the Opal Polaris 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya, Cat# NEL861001KT) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Five consecutive 3 µm paraffin sections were prepared, with one used for H&E staining. Multispectral images were acquired using Akoya Phenocycler Fusion and analyzed in Phenochart, with representative regions of interest (ROIs) determined by two independent pathologists.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Co-immunoprecipitated protein complexes were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), digested in-gel, and analyzed via liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Raw data were processed with MaxQuant for peptide identification and database searching. Candidate binding proteins were screened based on enrichment ratios and statistical significance. The accession number for the mass spectrometry data reported in this paper is Bioproject: PRJCA068957.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software V.10. Intergroup comparisons employed Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test according to data distribution, while multiple-group comparisons used analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by multiple comparisons correction. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Harrell’s Concordance Index (C-index) was calculated separately in the training and validation cohorts to compare the prognostic discriminatory performance of the CPS ≥5 vs <5, microsatellite instability status (microsatellite instability-high [MSI-H] versus microsatellite stable [MSS]), and the abundance of PanCK+PD-L1+PRKX+ tumor cells (high vs low). Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s or Pearson methods. Predictive performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) metrics. All p values were two-sided, with significance defined as p<0.05. Significance thresholds were annotated as follows: ns, not significant; *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001. All analytical methods were validated for appropriateness, with datasets satisfying the underlying assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance between compared groups.

Results

Single-cell landscape profiling identifies distinct immune-activated and immunosuppressive subtypes in GC

To systematically characterize the immune landscape of GC, we integrated a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas by combining our institutional cohort with seven publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets, encompassing 60 primary gastric tumors and 28 normal gastric tissues (Heal) (figure 1A).26–28 30 31 36 After quality control and batch effect correction, a high-quality dataset comprising 672,768 cells was retained for downstream analyses. Cell annotation based on canonical marker genes identified major cellular compartments, including epithelial, stromal, B, myeloid, and T-cell populations, which were further resolved into 80 subclusters (figure 1B, online supplemental figure S1A,B).

PLS-DA of immune subcluster proportions (B cell, myeloid cell, and T-cell subsets) revealed partial overlap between a subset of GC samples and healthy tissues, indicating substantial immune heterogeneity among tumors (figure 1C). We therefore performed cNMF analysis for further stratification, which classified GC samples into two distinct immune microenvironment subtypes, termed GC1 and GC2 (figure 1D). GC1 displayed an overall immune landscape more similar to normal gastric tissue (figure 1E), whereas GC2 exhibited a more pronounced immune-activated phenotype, characterized by enrichment of both innate and adaptive immune populations, including activated myeloid cells and CD8+/CD4+ T cells (figure 1F and online supplemental figure S1C–E). More specifically, GC1 was selectively enriched for exhausted CD8+ T cells, particularly the sc78_T_CD8_Tex_PD-1 subset (figure 1G and online supplemental figure S2A). Consistently, total CD8+ T cells from GC1 displayed a more pronounced exhaustion-associated phenotype, with increased expression of multiple inhibitory receptors compared with those from GC2 and healthy tissues (online supplemental figure S2B). At the subset level, sc78_T_CD8_Tex_PD-1 cells exhibited the strongest exhaustion signature, characterized by elevated expression of PD-1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, LAG3, and TIM-3, further supporting the selective enrichment of exhausted CD8+ T cells in GC1 (online supplemental figure S2C). irGSEA functional scoring within the T-cell compartment showed that GC2 exhibited higher enrichment of the CD8T_myeloid_activation signature, suggesting that T cells in GC2 possess a stronger capacity to promote myeloid cell activation (figure 1H). In contrast, T cells in GC1 showed higher enrichment of the T_exhaustion signature than those in GC2, indicating a more immunosuppressive phenotype accompanied by notably elevated PD-1 expression (figure 1I). Together, single-cell profiling delineated two microenvironmental subtypes, immune-activated (GC2) and immunosuppressive (GC1), providing cellular correlates for differential responses to immunotherapy.

Identification of PRKX as a key regulator of immunosuppressive GC1 subtype

Given that epithelial heterogeneity profoundly influences the TIME, we compared epithelial subcluster composition between the GC1 and GC2 subtypes. Although the overall distribution of epithelial subclusters was broadly similar, two specific subclusters sc11_EpiT_Chief_like and sc20_EpiT_Tumor_CLDN7 were significantly enriched in the GC1 subtype (figure 2A,B). Notably, the sc20 characterized by high PD-L1 expression was more abundant in GC1 (figure 2C). Correlation analysis further revealed that the abundance of sc20 positively correlated with the proportion of PD-1+ exhausted CD8+ T cells (sc78), suggesting that this tumor epithelial subset is closely linked to PD-L1-mediated T-cell dysfunction (figure 2D).

To further elucidate the potential biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of the sc20 subcluster within the GC microenvironment, we performed Gene Ontology biological process enrichment analysis on its marker genes. Multiple enriched pathways were significantly associated with kinase activity (figure 2E). To pinpoint the key kinase involved, we integrated sc20 marker genes, upregulated DEGs in GC from the TCGA-STAD dataset, and PD-L1-interacting proteins identified by co-immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS) in HGC-27 cells. This intersection narrowed down a candidate list of 12 genes, among which PRKX was the only identified protein kinase (figure 2F). Therefore, we focused our subsequent investigations on the biological function of PRKX and its specific role in the immunosuppressive GC subtype. We then verified the expression profile of PRKX across our sequencing cohorts, that PRKX was markedly elevated in the sc20 subset and GC1 tumors (figure 2G, online supplemental figure S3A). Consistently, analysis of the TCGA-STAD cohort showed that PRKX expression was significantly higher in PD-L1high tumors than in PD-L1low tumors and healthy controls (online supplemental figure S3B). Importantly, PRKX expression exhibited a strong positive correlation with the enrichment score of sc78_T_CD8_Tex_PD1 (figure 2H, online supplemental figure S3C). Together, these findings suggest that PRKX is a critical kinase driving PD-L1 stability and immune evasion within the sc20 epithelial cells of the GC1 subtype.

Clinically, we first evaluated the prognostic significance of PRKX in a training cohort of patients with GC treated with sintilimab immunotherapy. Using multiplex IHC on pretreatment tumor sections, we assessed the spatial distribution and expression of PRKX. Consistent with our sequencing results, PRKX was co-localized with PD-L1 within PanCK+ tumor regions (online supplemental figure S3D). PRKX expression was positively correlated with PD-L1 levels, and the density of PanCK+PRKX+PD-L1+ tumor cells was significantly associated with the infiltration of CD8+PD-1+ T cells (figure 2I–K).37 To further assess its prognostic relevance, we performed ROC analysis in the training cohort and found that the density of PanCK+PRKX+PD-L1+ cells showed good discriminative performance for predicting 5-year survival, with an AUC of 0.845 (figure 2L). Based on the optimal cut-off value derived from the ROC curve (292.7), patients were stratified into PRKXhigh and PRKXlow groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that patients in the PRKXhigh group had significantly poorer overall survival than those in the PRKXlow group (figure 2M). To validate the prognostic value of this cut-off, we next applied the same threshold to an independent validation cohort comprising patients with GC treated with other anti-PD-1 antibodies. Consistent with the findings in the training cohort, patients with high densities of PanCK+ PRKX+ PD-L1+ tumor cells exhibited significantly worse survival outcomes than those with low densities (figure 2N-O). We further compared the prognostic discrimination of this cellular biomarker with that of established clinical biomarkers, including the PD-L1 CPS and MSI status. The density of PanCK+PRKX+PD-L1+ tumor cells achieved higher C-index values in both the training and validation cohorts (0.7095 and 0.6723, respectively) than CPS (0.5543 and 0.5085, respectively) and MSI status (0.5139 and 0.5057, respectively) (online supplemental figure S3E). Collectively, these results establish PRKX as a robust prognostic biomarker for anti-PD-1 therapy response and long-term prognosis across different PD-1 inhibitors in GC.

PRKX inhibition restores CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and sensitizes GC to PD-1 blockade

To investigate the molecular basis of PRKX-mediated immune regulation, we first examined its effect on PD-L1 expression. Interestingly, while the knockout of PRKX did not significantly alter the messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of PD-L1, it led to a marked decrease in both total protein and membrane protein levels, suggesting that PRKX regulates PD-L1 through a post-translational mechanism rather than at the transcriptional level (figure 3A–C). Building on this observation, we sought to determine the functional impact of PRKX-mediated PD-L1 stabilization on the TME. In GC cell lines, PRKX knockdown did not significantly affect tumor cell proliferation or viability in monoculture (online supplemental figure S4A). However, in co-culture assays, loss of PRKX in tumor cells markedly enhanced CD8+ T-cell effector function, as indicated by increased expression of IFNG, TNF, and GZMB together with reduced expression of the immunosuppressive marker PDCD1 (figure 3D,E and online supplemental figure S4A,B).

To evaluate the preclinical efficacy of targeting PRKX in vivo, we established subcutaneous tumor models to investigate its role in modulating tumor progression and its synergistic potential with anti-PD-1 therapy. In nude mice, PRKX deficiency alone failed to suppress tumor growth or reduce tumor weight, indicating that loss of PRKX does not exert a substantial tumor-intrinsic effect in the absence of a functional adaptive immune system (online supplemental figure S4C,D). In contrast, in immunocompetent 615 mice, PRKX depletion significantly inhibited tumor growth, and this antitumor effect was further enhanced by anti-PD-1 treatment, resulting in markedly reduced tumor volume and weight at the experimental endpoint (figure 3F,G). Further characterization of the TME revealed that PRKX depletion significantly increased the tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, and the effect was further amplified in the sgPRKX plus anti-PD-1 mAb group (figure 3H). These CD8+ T cells exhibited enhanced functional activity, characterized by reduced proportions of PD-1+T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT)+ exhausted-associated cells and increased frequencies of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α+ interferon (IFN)-γ+ double-positive effector cells (figure 3I,J). Kaplan-Meier analysis further revealed a significant overall difference in survival among the four treatment groups, with the sgPRKX plus anti-PD-1 mAb group showing the longest survival duration (figure 3K). These data collectively demonstrate that targeting PRKX restores T-cell mediated antitumor immunity, thereby serving as a promising approach to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in GC.

PRKX stabilizes PD-L1 by binding its intracellular domain and inhibiting K48-linked ubiquitination

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which PRKX regulates PD-L1, we first examined the interaction between PRKX and PD-L1 proteins. Immunofluorescence staining revealed distinct intracellular co-localization of PRKX and PD-L1, while Co-IP assays confirmed an interaction between endogenous PRKX and PD-L1 proteins (figure 4A,B, online supplemental figure S5A). Based on the predicted PRKX-PD-L1 interaction interface and the structural organization of both proteins, we designed full-length and truncated constructs for subsequent domain-mapping analyses (figure 4C). Since PD-L1 is a single-pass transmembrane protein, we performed domain-mapping experiments using truncation mutants. We found that PRKX domains 1–2 cooperatively bind to the intracellular domain (ICD) of PD-L1 (figure 4D,E). To determine the pathway responsible for PD-L1 degradation on PRKX loss, cells were treated with various inhibitors including Z-VAD-FMK (caspase), MG132 (proteasome), Calpeptin (calpains) and NH₄Cl (lysosomes). The results showed that only MG132 effectively restored PD-L1 protein levels, indicating that PD-L1 undergoes proteasomal degradation (figure 4F). Furthermore, MG132 treatment at multiple time points rescued PD-L1 levels in PRKX-knockout HGC-27 cells, and cycloheximide chase assays demonstrated that the half-life of PD-L1 was significantly shortened in PRKX-deficient cells compared with controls, confirming that PRKX is required to maintain PD-L1 protein stability (figure 4G,H).

To investigate the PTM underlying this stabilization, we next examined the effect of PRKX on PD-L1 ubiquitination. Ubiquitination assays revealed that PRKX knockdown markedly increased the polyubiquitination of PD-L1, while PRKX overexpression produced the opposite effect (figure 4I,J). Moreover, analyses using truncation mutants demonstrated that the domains 1–2 of PRKX interact with the intracellular domain of PD-L1 and modulate its ubiquitination (online supplemental figure S5B). To define the specific ubiquitin linkage involved, we employed a panel of ubiquitin mutants and found that PRKX selectively suppressed K48-linked ubiquitination of PD-L1 (figure 4K). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PRKX binds to the intracellular domain of PD-L1 to shield it from K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby preventing its proteasomal degradation and maintaining high PD-L1 expression in GC cells.

PRKX-mediated phosphorylation of PD-L1 at T285 antagonizes ubiquitination to maintain protein stability and suppress antitumor immunity

PD-L1 protein abundance is regulated by multiple post-translational modifications. Given that PRKX is a cAMP-dependent protein kinase, we hypothesized that it modulates PD-L1 stability through phosphorylation.38 39 Phos-tag western blot analysis revealed that the phosphorylation level of PD-L1 was markedly decreased by PRKX knockout but significantly increased by PRKX overexpression (figure 5A,B). Consistently, in vitro kinase assays confirmed that PRKX directly phosphorylates the ICD domain of PD-L1 (figure 5C).

To define the specific phosphorylation site responsible for this regulation, we generated a series of PD-L1 mutants by substituting candidate residues within the intracellular domain of PD-L1 (T277, T285, T290, S279, and S283) to alanine (A) or glutamic (E) acid. Functional analyses revealed T285 as the critical site, as the T285A mutation abolished the PRKX-induced shift in PD-L1 protein stability (figure 5D and online supplemental figure S6). We further explored the interplay between phosphorylation and ubiquitination. Notably, the T285A mutant exhibited significantly enhanced K48-linked ubiquitination of PD-L1, whereas T285E mutant (phosphor-mimetic) markedly reduced this modification (figure 5E).

We then assessed the functional consequences of PD-L1 T285 phosphorylation in co-culture assays with CD8+ T cells. HGC-27 cells stably expressing PD-L1T285E exerted the strongest suppression of T-cell activity, as evidenced by a marked decrease in the expression of IFN-γ, TNF and GZMB and an upregulation of PDCD1, whereas PD-L1T285A attenuated T-cell suppression, characterized by restored cytokine production (figure 5F,G). Together, these findings demonstrate that PRKX-catalyzed phosphorylation at T285 antagonizes K48-dependent ubiquitination, thereby stabilizing PD-L1 and enhancing its inhibitory effect on T cell-mediated antitumor immunity.

PRKX-mediated T285 phosphorylation recruits YWHAE to shield PD-L1 from UBE2M-dependent ubiquitination and degradation

To identify candidate proteins whose interaction with PD-L1 is regulated by phosphorylation at T285, we performed Co-IP coupled with MS analysis to compare the interactomes of PD-L1WT and the phospho-mimetic PD-L1T285E group (figure 6A). Two phospho-binding proteins, YWHAE and GRB2, were significantly enriched in PD-L1T285E pulldown groups (figure 6B). To determine their biological relevance in GC, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic analysis and found that YWHAE, not GRB2, was selectively upregulated in tumor epithelial cells from patients with GC1 (figure 6C). Moreover, YWHAE expression showed a positive correlation with the abundance of exhausted CD8+ T cells (figure 6D). Consistently, analysis of TCGA-STAD data revealed co-expression of PRKX and YWHAE in gastric tumors (online supplemental figure S7A). Mechanistically, Co-IP assays demonstrated that YWHAE binds PD-L1 and, in cooperation with PRKX, forms a ternary complex that blocks ubiquitination and degradation of PD-L1, leading to its stabilization (figure 6E). Functionally, YWHAE recognizes the phosphorylated T285 residue of PD-L1, preventing K48-linked ubiquitination and maintaining PD-L1 stability (figure 6F). In HGC-27 cells stably overexpressing PD-L1WT, PD-L1T285E, or PD-L1T285A, YWHAE knockdown led to a marked reduction in PD-L1T285E protein levels accompanied by increased ubiquitination, whereas PD-L1WT and PD-L1T285A remained largely unaffected (online supplemental figure S7B). Functional co-culture assays further confirmed that YWHAE depletion attenuated the suppressive effect of PD-L1T285E-expressing cells on CD8+ T-cell activity characterized by restored IFNG, TNF, and GZMB expression (figure 6G,H).

To further elucidate the mechanism by which PRKX regulates PD-L1 ubiquitination, we screened the PD-L1WT interactome by Co-IP/MS and identified UBE2M as the only ubiquitination-related enzyme among the specifically interacting proteins (figure 6I). Interestingly, UBE2M expression was upregulated in the G2 subtype, and it exhibited a significant negative correlation with the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells, suggesting its role as a negative regulator of PD-L1 stability (figure 6J,K, online supplemental figure S7C). Mechanistically, co-expression of PRKX and YWHAE markedly weakened the interaction between UBE2M and PD-L1, coincident with increased PD-L1 protein levels and reduced ubiquitination (figure 6L). In the presence of YWHAE, UBE2M preferentially interacted with PD-L1WT or PD-L1T285A, promoting their K48-linked ubiquitination, whereas its binding to the phosphor-mimetic PD-L1T285E was markedly diminished (figure 6M). Consistent with this, knockdown of UBE2M in HGC-27 cells stably expressing PD-L1WT, PD-L1T285E, or PD-L1T285A led to increased PD-L1 protein abundance and reduced ubiquitination, effectively abolishing the stabilizing effect of YWHAE on PD-L1 (online supplemental figure S7D).

Given the established role of UBE2M in promoting cullin neddylation and activating Cullin-RING ligases,40 41 we re-examined the PD-L1 interactome identified by Co-IP/MS to explore candidate cullin-based E3 ligase machinery potentially involved in UBE2M-dependent PD-L1 ubiquitination. This analysis highlighted several components associated with CUL4-containing ubiquitin ligase complexes. Notably, CUL4B and DDB1 were detected in the PD-L1WT interactome but were absent from the PD-L1T285E interactome, while the interaction scores of the CUL4-associated substrate receptors DCAF7 and DCAF13 were also reduced in the PD-L1T285E group (online supplemental figure S7E,F). These findings suggest that a CUL4B-DDB1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex may participate in UBE2M-dependent ubiquitination of PD-L1. Together, our data revealed that PRKX-mediated T285 phosphorylation recruits YWHAE to competitively block UBE2M-dependent K48-ubiquitination, thereby sustaining PD-L1-mediated immune evasion in GC.

Lipid nanoparticles-delivered PRKX siRNA restores antitumor immunity and sensitizes GC to PD-1 blockade

Recent studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of RNA-based therapies, particularly siRNA-based therapies, in cancer treatment.42 To evaluate the translational potential of our findings, we developed LNP encapsulated with PRKX-targeting siRNA (LNP-siPrkx) for in vivo applications (figure 7A). We first validated the knockdown efficiency of siRNA in MFC cells; siPrkx treatment successfully silenced Prkx at both the mRNA and protein levels, leading to a marked production of cytotoxic effectors, including Ifn, Tnf, and Gzmb, while simultaneously downregulating the exhaustion marker Pdcd1 in CD8+ T cells (figure 7B–D). To assess the therapeutic impact in vivo, we used a subcutaneous MFC tumor model in 615 mice. LNP-siPrkx treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, and this efficacy was significantly enhanced when used in combination with PD-1 blockade, achieving the greatest therapeutic benefit in vivo (figure 7E,F). To ensure the reliability of these observations, we first confirmed the in vivo knockdown efficiency of LNP-siPrkx within the tumor tissues, demonstrating the stability of our experimental delivery system (figure 7G). We further performed characterization of TME and found that LNP-siRNA treatment significantly increased the infiltrated CD8+ T cells, an effect that was further enhanced by combination with anti-PD-1 antibody (figure 7H). Moreover, among tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, the combination treatment reduced the proportion of PD-1+TIGIT+ exhausted-associated cells and a higher proportion of IFN-γ+ TNF-α+ effector cells (figure 7H, online supplemental figure S8A). We also profiled other major immune populations in the TME, including CD4+ regulatory T cell (Treg) cells, tumor-associated macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells, and natural killer cells. No significant differences were observed in the proportions of these immune cells, whereas CD86 expression was significantly increased in the LNP-siPrkx plus anti-PD-1 group, which may contribute to the marked improvement in CD8+ T-cell function (online supplemental figure S8B,C). Moreover, survival analysis showed a significant difference among the four treatment groups, with LNP-siPrkx combined with anti-PD-1 therapy resulting in the longest survival of MFC tumor-bearing 615 mice (figure 7I).

LNP-siPRKX treatment inhibited tumor growth, and its combination with pembrolizumab produced a significant reduction in tumor volume and tumor weight (figure 7J,K). To ensure the reliability of these observations, we also confirmed the in vivo knockdown efficiency of LNP-siPRKX within the tumor tissues, demonstrating the stability of our experimental delivery system (figure 7L). Flow cytometric analysis further showed that LNP-siPRKX increased the infiltration of human CD8+ T cells within the human CD45+ immune-cell population, with an increased proportion observed in the combination-treatment group (online supplemental figure S8D). Moreover, LNP-siPRKX combined with pembrolizumab significantly decreased the proportion of TIGIT+PD-1+ exhausted-associated cells and increased the proportion of TNF-α+IFN-γ+ effector cells among tumor-infiltrating human CD8+ T cells (figure 7M, online supplemental figure S8D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LNP-siPRKX enhances antitumor immunity and improves the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade in a humanized GC model, further supporting its potential for clinical translation.

To evaluate the biosafety of LNP-siPrkx, we performed systemic toxicity assessments. LNP-siPrkx treatment caused no detectable alterations in hematological or serum biochemical parameters, and no histopathological abnormalities were observed in major organs (online supplemental figure S9A–C).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LNP-siPRKX enhances antitumor immunity, improves the efficacy of PD-1 blockade across both syngeneic and humanized GC models, and exhibits a favorable preliminary safety profile, supporting its potential for further translational development.

Discussion

PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown promising efficacy in a subset of patients with GC; however, the overall response rate remains limited, and the mechanisms underlying this variability are not fully understood.43 Although PD-L1 expression has become a standard biomarker for immune checkpoint blockade, its predictive power is limited. Consequently, there is a pressing clinical demand for additional approaches to better identify responders. In this context, comprehensive characterization of the TIME becomes essential for effective patient stratification and for guiding the development of more precise therapeutic strategies.

Current molecular classifications of GC, such as the TCGA subtypes (Epstein-Barr virus [EBV], MSI, genomically stable [GS], chromosomal instability [CIN]), have provided a framework for precision therapy.44 45 Based on these classifications, MSI-H and EBV-positive tumors tend to respond favorably to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, whereas CIN tumors, often characterized by HER2 amplification, are more responsive to targeted therapies. In parallel, other studies have proposed immune-based classification systems that divide tumors into “immune-desert” and “immune-enriched” phenotypes based on immune infiltration and genomic instability.46 However, these existing classifications primarily focus on bulk tumor characteristics and do not fully capture the cellular heterogeneity of the TME. Consequently, refined stratification based on distinct cellular subpopulations remains underexplored, and the molecular mechanisms governing immune microenvironment diversity in GC are not fully elucidated. In our study, we identified two distinct GC immune microenvironment subtypes, an immune-activated subtype and an immunosuppressive subtype. Our single-cell analysis revealed broader differences between these two subtypes. GC2 was characterized by increased representation of activated CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations, together with myeloid activation-associated features, suggesting coordinated engagement of adaptive and innate immune responses in GC2, rather than expansion of a single immune-cell population. In contrast, although the overall immune-cell composition of GC1 was more similar to that of normal gastric tissue, GC1 showed selective enrichment of PD-L1-high epithelial cells and CD8+ T cells with dysfunctional or exhaustion-associated transcriptional features, including high expression of PD-1, TIGIT, and LAG-3. This observation suggests that a relatively “normal-like” immune-cell composition does not necessarily indicate preserved antitumor immune function, and that immune-cell functional states may better reflect local immunosuppression than overall immune-cell abundance alone. Notably, Tregs and M2-like macrophages were not significantly enriched in GC1, while the apparent increase in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in GC2 largely reflected a broader expansion of the myeloid compartment. Therefore, the immunosuppressive phenotype GC1 may not be driven by generalized accumulation of canonical immunosuppressive cell populations, but may instead be more closely associated with tumor epithelial-cell-intrinsic immune-regulatory programs and CD8+ T-cell dysfunction. Collectively, these findings indicate that the overall immune state of GC should be evaluated by integrating immune-cell functional states, cellular composition, and intercellular interactions, rather than being inferred solely from the relative abundance of a single immunosuppressive cell population.

In this study, we identified PRKX as a key regulator of the immunosuppressive GC subtype. PRKX phosphorylates PD-L1 at the T285 site, promoting recruitment of the phospho-binding protein YWHAE, which competitively inhibits the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2M. This phosphorylation event exemplifies the dynamic balance among multiple PTMs, including phosphorylation and ubiquitination, that finely tune PD-L1 stability and function. Previous studies have shown that N-linked glycosylation of PD-L1 at asparagine residues prevents binding of GSK3β, thereby blocking subsequent phosphorylation and ubiquitination and protecting PD-L1 from proteasomal degradation.47 Conversely, phosphorylation mediated by kinases at specific serine/threonine residues can promote ubiquitin-ligase engagement and trigger PD-L1 turnover, especially when glycosylation is lacking.48 Consistent with this complex crosstalk, our findings reveal that phosphorylation not only modulates direct E3 ligase recognition but also recruits adaptor proteins to shield ubiquitination sites, collectively amplifying PD-L1 stability.

Our findings also clarify the specific role of UBE2M in this regulatory axis. Previous studies have established a functional link between UBE2M and Cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase complexes, through which UBE2M facilitates substrate ubiquitination.44 45 Moreover, CRL4 complexes have been reported to promote PD-L1 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, further supporting the involvement of Cullin-based E3 ligases in PD-L1 regulation.49 Consistent with these findings, our data suggest that a CUL4B-based CRL4 complex, including DDB1 and the putative substrate receptors DCAF7 and DCAF13, may represent an E3 ligase machinery involved in UBE2M-dependent PD-L1 ubiquitination. These findings support a model in which PRKX-mediated T285 phosphorylation recruits YWHAE, which sterically or competitively limits the access of the UBE2M-activated CRL4 complex to PD-L1 and consequently reduces K48-linked ubiquitination. This mechanism provides a new perspective on the post-translational regulation of PD-L1 stability.

In recent years, LNP-mediated delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics, particularly siRNA and mRNA, has emerged as a transformative modality in oncology, rapidly advancing from the bench to clinical applications.50 Our study developed an LNP-siPRKX delivery system to therapeutically target the immunosuppressive GC microenvironment. Compared with traditional small-molecule kinase inhibitors, this LNP-siRNA platform offers several distinct translational advantages. First, small-molecule inhibitors designed against kinases frequently target the highly conserved ATP-binding pockets, which often leads to unintended off-target binding across the kinome and subsequent dose-limiting toxicities. In contrast, siPRKX operates at the post-transcriptional level, leveraging base-pairing mechanisms to provide exquisite, sequence-specific silencing of PRKX without cross-reacting with other kinases. Second, while small molecules primarily inhibit the catalytic activity of a kinase, siRNA-mediated knockdown eliminates the entire PRKX protein. This ensures the complete abolition of both its catalytic phosphorylation of PD-L1 and any potential non-catalytic scaffolding interactions within the PD-L1/YWHAE ternary complex. Our in vivo data demonstrated that LNP-siPRKX exhibits an effective remodeling of the TIME. When combined with PD-1 blockade, this strategy circumvents the overlapping toxicities often observed with small-molecule inhibitor combinations, highlighting the LNP-siPRKX platform as a highly specific, safe, and clinically viable strategy for combinatorial immunotherapy in GC.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Although batch-effect correction was performed, the integration of scRNA-seq datasets generated across different cohorts and experimental platforms may still introduce residual technical variation, potentially affecting the estimation of immune-cell proportions and the classification of the GC1 and GC2 subtypes. Moreover, because the single-cell samples were predominantly derived from treatment-naïve primary gastric tumors, the potential association between the GC1 and GC2 immune subtypes and response to immunotherapy remains to be validated in independent cohorts with well-annotated treatment information and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the clinical correlation analyses were conducted in a retrospective cohort with a relatively limited sample size. Although patients in the training cohort had more than 5 years of follow-up, the median follow-up duration in the validation cohort was only 17.9 months because immunotherapy had been initiated more recently in this population. In addition, the validation cohort included different anti-PD-1-based treatment regimens, which may have introduced treatment heterogeneity. These factors may limit the assessment of long-term outcomes and the robustness of the current validation. Future studies will therefore continue to monitor treatment outcomes and long-term survival in these patients and prospectively enroll additional patients from multiple centers to further validate the predictive value and generalizability of PRKX. Finally, although the functional role of PRKX was validated both in vitro and in vivo, and our preliminary safety assessments did not identify obvious treatment-related toxicity, the long-term efficacy and safety, potential off-target effects, biodistribution, tumor-selective delivery, and effects on metastatic progression in additional tumor models require further systematic evaluation before clinical translation.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that PRKX is a key regulator of PD-L1 stability in the immunosuppressive GC subtype (GC1) by modulating the phosphorylation-ubiquitination balance at the T285 site. This regulation promotes immune evasion through enhanced PD-L1 stability and consequent T-cell exhaustion. Clinically, high PanCK+ PRKX+ PD-L1+ cell density is associated with poor prognosis and serves as a robust predictor of survival following anti-PD-1 therapy. Together, these results identify PRKX as a novel biomarker and therapeutic target in GC immunotherapy. Targeting PRKX holds promise to restore PD-L1 PTM balance, reinvigorate CD8+ T-cell function, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes in patients with immunosuppressive tumors.

Conclusions

Our study identifies PRKX as a pivotal regulator of PD-L1 stability through phosphorylation at T285, driving immune evasion in an immunosuppressive GC subtype. By modulating the balance of phosphorylation and ubiquitination, PRKX enhances PD-L1-mediated T-cell suppression and correlates with poor clinical outcomes. These findings establish PRKX as both a prognostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target, offering new avenues to improve immunotherapy efficacy in GC.
