Epidemiological Characteristics of Pediatric Drug Poisoning in Wenzhou, China in 2017–2024
Authors: Chengjie Chen, Huating Xie, Keke Chen, Ruiyao Chen, Yafeng Liang, Guoquan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine the evolving epidemiological characteristics of pediatric drug poisoning in Wenzhou, China from 2017 to 2024, focusing on temporal trends, age/sex-specific risks, and changes in poisoning intent and toxin types, and to generate evidence-based guidance for targeted prevention and treatment strategies at regional pediatric medical centers.MethodsClinical data were retrospectively collected from 1,531 pediatric patients admitted to the emergency observation rooms of Wenzhou Medical University Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital between January 2017 and December 2024. Analyses included demographic characteristics, toxin types, exposure intent, temporal distribution, and clinical outcomes, with a focus on trends by age, sex, and toxin category.ResultsOf the 1,531 patients, 689 (45.0%) were male and 842 (55.0%) were female. Boys under 11 years had a higher poisoning rate, while girls aged ≥ 11 years showed a significant increase in intentional poisoning over time. Toddlers (1–3 years) accounted for the highest number of cases (826, 54.0%), followed by school-age/adolescent children aged 7–16 years (533, 34.8%). Accidental poisoning comprised 68.3% of cases, while intentional poisoning (31.7%) was significantly associated with adolescent females. Single-drug exposures constituted 84.0% of cases. Poisoning incidents peaked in spring (27.8%) and autumn (28.9%), primarily occurring in the afternoon (34.1%) and evening/overnight (40.9%). Most patients (89.9%) were discharged after recovery, while 10.1% required hospitalization. Infants (0–1 years) had an 8.5-fold higher hospitalization risk than school-age/adolescent children. Prescription drug poisoning carried a 5.4-fold higher hospitalization risk compared with pesticide/toxin poisoning, with psychiatric and cardiovascular drugs posing the greatest risks.ConclusionThese findings reveal a dual-peak age distribution pattern for pediatric drug poisoning in Wenzhou: accidental poisoning among young boys during early childhood (commonly involving cardiovascular drugs), and intentional poisoning among adolescent girls (commonly involving psychiatric drugs). These patterns were temporally associated with post-pandemic changes in social structures and rising mental health concerns among adolescents. Our findings support a dual prevention strategy for regional pediatric medical centers: strengthening home safety for young children and enhancing mental health interventions for adolescents. A comprehensive prevention system spanning prescription drug regulation, caregiver education, and mental health services is needed to address emerging public health challenges.Keywords: Pediatric drug poisoning, Medication safety, Mental health, Health disparities, Epidemiological research, Preventive medicine, Post-pandemic era

Introduction

Pediatric drug poisoning is a leading global cause of pediatric emergency visits and injury-related mortality [1–6]. Previous studies have reported a double-peak age distribution: accidental ingestion in early childhood and intentional ingestion in adolescence [7, 8]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic and associated public health interventions (e.g., home quarantine, school closures) have profoundly altered children’s environments and mental health, potentially reshaping poisoning epidemiology [9–13]. Furthermore, both global and regional data indicate rising trends in adolescent psychological distress and deliberate self-harm behaviors. Recent evidence from this region suggests a marked post-pandemic increase in pediatric poisoning cases linked to intentional ingestion of prescription psychotropic drugs. This emerging pattern highlights the need to systematically examine the interplay between mental health conditions and poisoning intent to better understand and address this critical public health challenge. This calls for a re-examination of childhood poisoning [14, 15]—particularly the drivers of intentional ingestion during adolescence—from new perspectives.

As a regional pediatric medical center, the cases treated at our emergency department provide a unique window into this evolving public health issue. In this study, we analyzed 1,531 pediatric drug poisoning cases in Wenzhou from 2017 to 2024, with the following objectives: (1) describe the current epidemiological and clinical characteristics of drug poisoning in detail; (2) identify independent risk factors associated with severe (hospitalization-requiring) outcomes, emphasizing the role of specific drug categories (e.g., psychotropic medications); (3) examine temporal changes in intentional poisoning among children with mental disorders to assess whether post-pandemic social and psychological shifts have amplified this risk; and (4) specifically explore potential emerging trends in poisoning patterns (e.g., intentional vs. unintentional, toxin type) in the post-pandemic era and against the backdrop of socioeconomic transformation. These study findings provide high-level evidence for the development of timely, precise, and age-specific clinical interventions and public health prevention strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a retrospective, cross‑sectional design using historical medical record data. This retrospective study was based on fully anonymized secondary data extracted from electronic medical records.

Study Population and Data Sources

The study hospital is one of three tertiary pediatric medical centers in Wenzhou, serving a catchment area of approximately 9.6 million people, including urban and rural populations across southern Zhejiang Province. The emergency observation room is a short-stay unit with a maximum observation period of 24 h, admitting patients who require extended emergency care beyond a routine emergency department visit but do not meet criteria for inpatient admission. The age cutoff of 16 years follows the standard definition of a pediatric patient in Chinese clinical practice (0–16 years).

The study population comprised all pediatric patients admitted to the emergency observation rooms of Wenzhou Medical University Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital for drug poisoning between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 0–16 years; (2) diagnosis of acute drug or toxicant poisoning based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes T36.0–T65.9, confirmed by the attending physician using clinical presentation, medical history, and toxicological test results; and (3) identified toxicant type. Exclusion criteria were: (1) missing critical clinical data (defined as missing outcome status [discharge vs. admission] or missing toxin type; n = 72 cases were excluded for this reason); (2) iatrogenic poisoning; and (3) non‑drug poisoning (e.g., food poisoning, toxic gas inhalation).

Data Collection and Variable Definitions

Clinical patient data collected via the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system primarily included basic demographic information, including age, sex, household registration type (“urban” or “non-urban” served as a proxy indicator for urbanization and potential migrant status).

Intent of poisoning was categorized as: (1) accidental (unintentional ingestion); (2) intentional (including self‑harm/suicide attempt, as reported by the patient or caregiver, acknowledging potential discordance between reports as noted in prior literature); and (3) intentional administration by others (non‑accidental administration by a caregiver, excluding malicious poisoning by strangers, of which there were zero cases in this cohort).

Mental disorder status was defined based on documentation in the electronic medical record (EMR), including: (a) an existing ICD‑10 diagnosis (F00–F99) from prior outpatient or inpatient psychiatric care; or (b) a new diagnosis made by the attending emergency physician in consultation with psychiatry, based on clinical assessment and caregiver/family interview.

Toxin types were categorized based on ICD-10 coding and National Medical Products Administration classification: (1) prescription drugs (subclassified by the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system into cardiovascular, psychotropic, etc.); (2) over-the-counter (OTC) drugs; (3) chemical agents (cleaning agents, disinfectants, desiccants, etc.); (4) pesticides/insecticides(toxic substances); (5) traditional Chinese medicine (herbal medicines/formulated Chinese medicines); and (6) other.

Quantity of drug ingested was categorized as single agent or multiple agents (≥ 2 types).

Temporal variables were stratified by year of poisoning occurrence, season (Spring: Mar–May; Summer: Jun–Aug; Autumn: Sep–Nov; Winter: Dec–Feb), and time of day. Time of day was defined as the time of drug ingestion, as reported by the patient or caregiver and documented in the electronic medical record. For analysis, the 24‑hour period was divided into morning (6:00–11:59), afternoon (12:00–17:59), and evening/overnight (18:00–5:59).

Treatment outcomes were categorized as “Discharge after emergency care” or “Admitted for inpatient treatment” (including intensive care unit admission). “Admitted for inpatient treatment” served as the key indicator for assessing resource utilization.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.2.0. Categorical data are described as case numbers (n) and percentages (%), with intergroup comparisons conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (applied when > 25% of cells had expected counts < 5). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared using t-tests; non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (M) and interquartile range (IQR), and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Univariate analysis was employed to compare differences in each variable between the hospitalized and non-hospitalized groups. Variables with a P value < 0.1 in univariate analysis, along with clinically relevant factors (age, sex, toxin type, season, time of day, and ingestion intent), were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of hospitalization. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a P-value > 0.05 indicating acceptable model fit. Multicollinearity among independent variables was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF), with a VIF < 5 for all included variables.

Joinpoint regression models (Joinpoint Regression Program, Version 5.0.2) were applied to analyze trends in annual poisoning cases, with a focus on intentional poisonings, from 2017 to 2024. Annual percentage change (APC) was calculated for the overall period and for pre‑specified segments (2017–2019 and 2020–2024), and tested for significance. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was used, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Basic Characteristics and Temporal Trends

This study included 1,531 pediatric patients with drug poisoning, comprising 689 male (45.0%) and 842 female (55.0%) patients, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 0.82:1 (Fig. 1A). The age distribution showed a typical bimodal pattern, with the highest case count in the 1-3-year group (826, 54.0%), followed by the 7-16-year school-age and adolescent group (533, 34.8%). Stratification by sex and age revealed a higher proportion of boys under 11 years, whereas girls significantly outnumbered boys in the ≥ 11-year age group (Fig. 1B).

Demographic and temporal patterns of pediatric drug poisoning cases in Wenzhou (2017–2024). (A) Distribution of perpetrators by sex. (B) Age and sex distribution of cases. (C) Annual trends, highlighting a post-pandemic rise in total cases and an increase in intentional poisonings involving mental disorders

The primary cause of poisoning was accidental ingestion in 1,045 cases (68.3%), while intentional poisoning accounted for the remaining 31.7% (486 cases). The vast majority of cases involved single-drug poisoning (1,286, 84.0%).

A sharp upward trajectory was observed in intentional poisoning cases, particularly among children with mental disorders. This increase was markedly higher for the period 2020–2024 than for 2017–2019 (Fig. 1C). The dual escalation underscores the potential psychological impact of the pandemic on the epidemiology of pediatric poisoning.

Poisoning incidents exhibited distinct temporal patterns (Fig. 2A), with daily peaks occurring in the afternoon (12:00–17:59, 34.1%) and evening/overnight (18:00–5:59, 40.9%). Among children aged 7–16 years, incidents were more concentrated at night (52.0%), while younger children (0–6 years) showed similar incidence rates in the afternoon and night (~ 35%). Seasonal distribution analysis showed peaks in spring (27.8%) and autumn (28.9%) (Fig. 2B).

(A, B) Time-of-day distribution (A) and seasonal distribution (B) of pediatric poisoning cases. (C) Monthly trend in mental disorder diagnoses among pediatric poisoning cases, stratified by intent (intentional vs. accidental)

Joinpoint regression revealed no significant trend in total cases from 2017 to 2019 (APC = − 2.1%, 95% CI: − 5.8% to 1.7%, P = 0.24), followed by a significant increase from 2020 to 2024 (APC = 9.4%, 95% CI: 4.1% to 14.9%, P = 0.003). For intentional poisoning cases, the increase was steeper (2020–2024: APC = 18.7%, 95% CI: 11.2% to 26.5%, P < 0.001). Notably, the proportion of intentional poisoning cases involving mental disorders increased markedly from 28.9% before the pandemic (2017–2019) to 87.1% after 2020, whereas accidental poisonings remained consistently low (0.0% vs. 1.0%). The sharp rise in mental disorder prevalence among intentional cases coincided with the onset of COVID-19 (2020, dashed line) and persisted thereafter, suggesting a strong link between the pandemic period and deliberate poisoning events in children with mental health problems (Fig. 2C).

Distribution of Toxicant Types

Prescription drugs constituted the primary toxicant category, accounting for 930 pediatric poisoning cases (61.0%). The proportion of cases involving these drugs exhibited a significant upward trend from 2017 to 2024 (P < 0.01), peaking at 67.9% in 2024 (Fig. 3A). Among these, psychotropic drugs (antipsychotics and antidepressants) were the most common (404 cases, 43.4%), followed by drugs targeting the cardiovascular (157 cases, 16.9%) and nervous (105 cases, 11.3%) systems.

OTC drugs (227 cases, 15.0%) and toxic substances (e.g., pesticides/insecticides/rodenticides; 242 cases, 16.0%) showed an overall downward trend in poisoning incidents. Among OTC drugs, vitamin supplements (35.7%) and antipyretic analgesics (21.1%) were predominant. Among toxic substances, cockroach bait (40.1%) and insecticides (29.8%) were the primary culprits. Chemical agent poisoning (103 cases, 6.0%) showed a resurgence after 2020, primarily involving household cleaning and disinfection products. Annual case counts were 16, 4, 3, 17, 13, 14, 15, and 21 from 2017 to 2024, respectively, declining from 16 cases in 2017 to 3 in 2019, then rising to 17 in 2020 and remaining elevated thereafter. Traditional Chinese medicine poisoning accounted for a low proportion (2.0%) and has been decreasing annually.

Significant age-related differences in poison type distribution were observed (Fig. 3B, C). Among children aged 0–3 years, accidental ingestion of cardiovascular drugs, toxic substances (cockroach bait), and respiratory system drugs predominated. In the 4-6-year age group, OTC medications (antipyretic analgesics, vitamins) accounted for a higher proportion. Older children (7–16 years) showed a high concentration of prescription drug poisoning (84.6%), with the majority (64.0%) involving intentional ingestion of psychotropic drugs.

When stratified by mental disorder status, differences in poison type distribution were striking (Fig. 3D). In children with mental disorders, intentional poisonings accounted for 94.0% of cases, with prescription drug poisonings comprising 86.2%. Among poisonings involving prescription drugs, psychotropic drugs comprised 75.4% of all cases. In contrast, cases of children without mental disorders showed a more balanced distribution, more commonly involving accidental poisonings by toxic substances (27.2%) or OTC drugs (23.9%). The intergroup differences were highly significant (P < 0.001). Taken together, these findings delineate a clear pattern: school-age and adolescent children (7–16 years) → presence of mental disorder → intentional ingestion of psychotropic prescription drugs → pronounced post-pandemic increase in poisoning cases. These temporal associations are consistent with the hypothesis that pandemic‑related changes may have influenced intentional poisoning risk, but causal inference cannot be established from this observational design.

Univariate Analysis of Poisoning Characteristics

Univariate analysis revealed multifactorial differences in pediatric poisoning characteristics (Table 1). Prescription drug poisoning was significantly more prevalent among female than male individuals (67.3% vs. 52.7%, P < 0.001). Among non-urban children, the distribution of poisoning types mirrored that in the total cohort, whereas urban children exhibited a significantly higher proportion of chemical agent poisoning (19.5% vs. 6.2%, P < 0.001). Seasonal analysis indicated that summer was the peak season for both substance and chemical agent poisoning (30.7% combined). Nighttime was the peak time of day for prescription drug poisoning (65.8%). The intent of poisoning was highly correlated with the type of poison: 89.1% of intentional poisonings involved prescription drugs, while accidental poisonings were also frequently associated with toxic substances (23.0%) and OTC drugs (18.9%).

Clinical Severity and Hospitalization Risk

Prescription drug poisoning was the leading cause of hospitalization, accounting for 76.8% of hospitalized pediatric cases (Table 2). Its hospitalization rate (12.7%) was significantly higher than that of OTC drugs (3.1%) and toxic substances (3.3%). Although chemical agent poisoning was infrequent overall, its hospitalization rate was high (16.5%). Among hospitalized children aged 1–3 years, it accounted for 20.8% of cases, indicating greater clinical severity when it occurs.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis further identified independent risk factors for hospitalization (Table 3). Age emerged as a highly significant predictor: children aged 7–16 years had a markedly reduced hospitalization risk compared with infants aged 0–1 year (aOR = 0.117, 95% CI: 0.025, 0.544, P = 0.006), indicating an 8.5-fold higher risk in infants. Association Between Mental Disorders and Intentional Poisoning

The proportion of intentional poisonings accompanied by a diagnosed mental disorder in the patient increased sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic, rising from 0% to 7.6% before 2020 to 60.1%-89.4% between 2020 and 2024 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3E). This pattern was especially evident among school-age children and adolescents aged 7–16 years, in whom psychotropic prescription drugs constituted the vast majority of toxicants. These findings support a pattern in which mental disorders substantially elevate the risk of intentional poisoning, particularly involving psychotropic medications, and suggest that this association has strengthened in the post-pandemic era.

Discussion

Through systematic analysis of 1,531 pediatric drug poisoning cases in Wenzhou from 2017 to 2024, this study reveals new characteristics and serious challenges in pediatric poisoning epidemiology amid post-pandemic social and psychological shifts. Our findings confirm the classic double-peak age distribution pattern and identify independent predictors of severe illness and hospitalization, providing crucial evidence to support the establishment of a targeted and efficient prevention and control system.

Summary of Key Findings and Interpretation of Contemporary Characteristics

Notably, prescription drugs have become the primary cause of pediatric drug poisoning (61.0%), showing a sustained and significant proportional increase over the past 8 years. Poisoning cases followed a typical bimodal age distribution: the early childhood peak (1–3 years) predominantly involved male individuals and accidental exposure to cardiovascular drugs and household toxins, whereas the school-age/adolescent peak (7–16 years) was overwhelmingly female and intentional, with psychotropic drugs (e.g., antidepressants, antipsychotics) accounting for 64.0%. Further analysis revealed a sharply accelerating trend in the proportion of intentional poisonings involving children with mental disorders since 2020 (P < 0.001), indicating a post-pandemic amplification of psychological risk factors. This subgroup overwhelmingly involved psychotropic prescription drugs, forming a clear pattern: older adolescents → mental disorders → intentional ingestion of psychotropic medications → significantly increased incidence in the post-pandemic period. Although this pattern aligns with multiple global studies [16–18], its underlying drivers may have shifted.

Trend analysis of pandemic milestones indicates a significant rebound in poisoning cases after 2020. This post-pandemic surge may reflect two pathways: increased household medication exposure during lockdowns and the well-documented negative impact of the pandemic on adolescent mental health, which may have contributed to intentional self-harm [19, 20]. Concurrently, intentional poisonings associated with mental disorders displayed the steepest rise among all categories, underscoring the urgency of mental health interventions targeted for adolescents. Additionally, chemical poisoning cases rebounded after 2020 (from 3 cases in 2019 to 17 in 2020, with annual counts remaining between 13 and 21 during 2020–2024), consistent with the widespread use and storage of alcohol-based disinfectants, bleach, and similar products during the pandemic.

In-Depth Analysis of Poisoning Characteristics and Risk Factors

This study reveals distinct temporal patterns in poisoning incidents, providing critical time anchors for targeted prevention and control. Poisonings among young children predominantly occur in the afternoon, potentially linked to caregiver fatigue and intermittent lapses in supervision. Conversely, adolescent poisonings are highly concentrated at night, strongly suggesting an association with nocturnal emotional crises and impulsive self-harm behaviors [21]. Seasonal peaks in prescription drug poisoning in spring and autumn are potentially linked to increased respiratory illnesses and household medication use. Summer peaks in poisoning from toxins and chemical agents correlate with insect vector proliferation and heightened household chemical usage [22].

Importantly, the rise in intentional poisonings among children with mental disorders coincided with increased involvement of psychotropic prescription drugs, reinforcing the role of exposure intent as an independent and synergistic risk factor for severe outcomes.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis further confirmed that younger age (0-1-year) and prescription drug involvement were independent risk factors for hospitalization. Infants faced an 8.5-fold higher hospitalization risk than adolescents (aOR = 0.117, 95% CI: 0.025, 0.544), highlighting their physiological vulnerability and the severity of clinical outcomes following poisoning. The hospitalization risk for prescription drug poisoning was 5.4-fold higher than that for pesticide/toxin poisoning (aOR = 0.186, 95% CI: 0.047, 0.735). This was particularly obvious for psychotropic and cardiovascular medications, which frequently cause severe complications including impaired consciousness and arrhythmias. Despite the low overall incidence of poisoning with chemical agents, their hospitalization rate reached 16.5%, suggesting that such events often result in corrosive injuries or systemic toxicity with poor prognosis.

Building an Evidence-Based Precision Prevention System: From Home to Society

On the basis of these study findings, we call for the establishment of a multitiered, age-graded precision prevention system. For young children (< 7 years), priorities include environmental modifications and caregiver education, enforcement of child-resistant containers, and secure storage of adult medications at heights above 1.5 m. For school-age/adolescent children (≥ 7 years), routine mental health screening should be integrated into outpatient and school settings to identify depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation early. Additionally, hospital–school–community referral networks should be established, alongside strengthened education on the safe use of psychiatric medications, emphasizing overdose risks. The healthcare system and industry stakeholders should implement safety warnings at the point of dispensing, encourage single-dose packaging, and extend child-resistant packaging regulations to household chemicals. Final, public health policy should include targeted campaigns launched strategically ahead of high-risk seasons, as well as promotion of non-toxic pest control options to reduce exposure to toxic substances.

Study Limitations

This single-center retrospective investigation carries inherent selection bias. In some cases, identification of the toxicant was based solely on parental reports, potentially introducing misclassification bias. Additionally, potential confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status and detailed family caregiver structures, were not comprehensively captured. These limitations underscore the need for refinement in future prospective studies.

Conclusions

This study systematically delineates the contemporary epidemiological landscape of pediatric drug poisoning in Wenzhou, highlighting distinct age‑ and intent‑related patterns, as well as temporal changes that may reflect evolving risk factors. The dual-peak pattern of accidental poisoning among young children and intentional poisoning among school-age/adolescent children underscores the need for distinct, age-targeted prevention strategies [7, 8]. The rapid post-pandemic surge in intentional poisonings among children with mental disorders, predominantly involving psychotropic prescription drugs, represents both a medical and mental health crisis. Future efforts should prioritize household medication safety management, mandate child-resistant packaging, and recognize adolescent drug poisoning as a critical indicator of underlying mental health distress requiring timely intervention. Through multisectoral collaboration among healthcare systems, families, industry, and government, the incidence and burden of pediatric drug poisoning can be meaningfully reduced.
