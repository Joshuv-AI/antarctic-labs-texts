Population pharmacokinetic model for meropenem in pediatric patients: a systematic review
Authors: Huaping Gao, Qing Tang, Leying Wu, Yuhua Zhao, Zhenwei Yu, Hongbo Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pharmacokinetics of meropenem in pediatric patients have large interindividual variability. Model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) is a promising way to ensure optimal exposure but relies on a population pharmacokinetic (PPK) model. This review systemically synthesized the available PPK model for meropenem in pediatric patients to facilitate MIPD in these patients.MethodsFour electronic databases were searched for eligible studies that reported the use of the meropenem PPK model for pediatric patients. The study design information, patient characteristic demography, PPK modeling strategy and final PPK parameter estimates were extracted and reviewed.ResultsTwenty studies published from 2006 to 2025 were included in the analysis. Fourteen studies had prospective designs, and four studies were international multicenter studies. Seven studies applied an intensive sampling strategy. Pediatric patients with various characteristics were included, and most had severe infections or were admitted to the ICU. Four studies focused on patients with organ support, mainly renal replacement and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. The sample sizes were usually small (sample sizes no more than 50). Fourteen studies ultimately obtained the 2-CMT model, and the remaining studies used the 1-CMT model, except for one 3-CMT model. Body weight, age and renal function indicators were the most common covariates. The typical CL values ranged from 0.50–13.22 L/h or 0.122–0.526 L/h/kg. The Vcs for the 2-CMT models ranged from 0.969–21.4 L or 0.272–0.57 L/kg. Most models underwent internal validation, but only one study performed external validation.ConclusionThis study successfully integrated the current meropenem PPK models for pediatric patients, which would benefit the clinical application of MIPD. Clinicians should select appropriate models on the basis of patient characteristics carefully, and therapeutic drug monitoring is important at the current stage. External validation and well-designed studies on specific subpopulations, such as patients with organ support, neonates, and specific disease status, are needed.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO, identifier (CRD420261481301).

Introduction

Meropenem is recognized as the first-line treatment for severe infection (Tamma et al., 2022; Mu et al., 2025). It has good in vitro activity against a broad spectrum of pathogens, including ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (Guo et al., 2022). Although resistance to meropenem has increased in recent decades, it remains the cornerstone of anti-infection therapy (Meschiari et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2025). In many trials evaluating the efficacy of novel antibiotics for drug-resistant pathogens, meropenem has been selected for comparison (Carmeli et al., 2025; Martin-Loeches et al., 2022). In terms of the safety profile, meropenem has a greater tolerance dose range and less neurotoxicity than imipenem does (Tan et al., 2021; Salmon-Rousseau et al., 2020). Thus, it is welcomed for pediatric patients with severe infection. It has been reported that the consumption of meropenem in pediatric patients is high (Balkhy et al., 2019).

In the era of drug resistance, antibiotics should be dosed under the guidance of the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) theory (Alikhani et al., 2025; Rao and Landersdorfer, 2021). The PK/PD index that is associated with the efficacy of meropenem is the time fraction of free drug above the minimum inhibitory concentration during a dosing interval (fT% > MIC). The fT% > MIC of meropenem should be greater than 40%–70% for bacteriostatic effects, and an fT% > MIC of 100% is required for maximal effects in critically ill patients (Roberts et al., 2014). The target also depends on the infected microorganism and infection type. Pediatric patients with severe infection always have unique pathophysiological conditions and high interindividual variability in PK (Goulooze et al., 2023). For example, sepsis was reported to be associated with subtherapeutic beta-lactam antibiotic concentrations in both adults and children and was often associated with augmented renal clearance (ARC), which is potentially related to increased cardiac output and renal blood flow (Bradley et al., 2025). Thus, the label dosing regimen of meropenem may often result in suboptimal exposure in children.

Model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) is a promising way to achieve optimal exposure for pediatric patients (Tang Girdwood et al., 2025). However, this approach highly relies on the robustness of the population pharmacokinetic (PPK) model. There are many published meropenem PPK models for pediatric patients, some of which have focused on special subpopulations. However, many models are limited in some aspects, and choosing an appropriate model when model-informed dosing is applied is difficult (Uster et al., 2021). Thus, we performed this systematic review to comprehensively compare the model characteristics, which would be helpful for future MIPD applications and research.

Methods

This systematic review is conducted in accordance with the PRIMSA statement and is registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261481301).

Literature search

Four electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) were searched to identify potentially eligible studies. The detailed search strategies are shown in Supplementary Table S1. The search time range was from the setup of the database to 2025. The language of publication was restricted to English. For the retrieved records, the reviewers performed initial assessments on the basis of the title and abstract, and irrelevant records were excluded. The full text was subsequently retrieved for eligibility. Two reviewers independently performed the literature search and then cross-checked the data. Any discrepancies were resolved with a third reviewer.

Study inclusion

The titles/abstracts of the retrieved studies were screened, and the full texts of the remaining studies were assessed for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the study population was pediatric patients (aged under 18 years); (2) meropenem was administered intravenously; and (3) the meropenem PPK model was developed in the study. If the model focused on pediatric patients but included adult patients, it was also recognized as eligible.

However, the following exclusion criteria were also applied: (1) non-PPK studies, for example, meropenem pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated by noncompartmental analysis (NCA) rather than the PPK method; (2) a review or systematic review of previous meropenem PPK models; (3) a study focused on PPK model application; (4) a Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling study; (5) an external validation study of previous meropenem PPK models; (6) the study drug was not meropenem, such as meropenem-vaborbactam; and (7) key parameters of the PPK model were not reported.

Literature quality assessment

The quality of each included study was assessed according to the combined checklist based on the ClinPK statement and population pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic guidelines (Jamsen et al., 2014; Kanji et al., 2015). The checklist consisted of 35 criteria categorized into five domains: title/abstract, background, methods, results, and discussion/conclusion. The quality of the included studies was expressed as the compliance rate of the criteria. Two reviewers independently assessed the quality, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Data collection

Two reviewers independently performed data collection on the basis of electronic data item forms, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. The following data were extracted: (1) study design information (first author and publication year, study design, country of study performed, sampling strategy, and meropenem assay method); (2) patient characteristic demography of the study enrolled/include patient group, number of included patients, meropenem plasma concentration points, age, sex, and weight distribution; (3) PPK modeling strategy (modeling software, covariate model method, and screened and identified covariates); (4) PPK parameter estimates (typical value of each parameter, covariate model, interindividual variability, residual error, and validation methods); and (5) the PK/PD target used for simulation and dosage regimen recommendations. The missing data were left empty, and the authors were not contacted.

Covariate effect analysis

The effects of various variables on clearance were analyzed. The clearance parameters were mainly the clearance rate (CL) of the central department. For continuous covariates, the median value of the original study was used as the median, and the range of values across studies was used as the endpoint. To facilitate comparisons across different studies, the covariate effects reported in each model were uniformly converted into relative change rates of parameter estimates compared to the reference group. Then, forest plots were generated to visualize the effects of the identified covariates. Changes within the 80%–125% range were considered clinically insignificant.

Results

Literature search, study inclusion and quality assessment

The detailed literature search and study inclusion criteria are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. A total of 493 records were retrieved, and 20 studies reporting the meropenem PPK model for pediatric patients were ultimately eligible for analysis and included (Du et al., 2006; Bradley et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2020; Saito et al., 2021; Wang Y. et al., 2021; Zylbersztajn et al., 2021; Yonwises et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022; Pokorná et al., 2024; Butragueño-Laiseca et al., 2024; Junior et al., 2024; Le et al., 2025; Van Den Anker et al., 2009; Ikawa et al., 2010; Ohata et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2011; Kongthavonsakul et al., 2016; Pettit et al., 2016; Cies et al., 2017; Ohata et al., 2019). The quality of the included studies was assessed by a 35-item checklist, and the detailed results are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Most had a high reporting item compliance rate (>85%), which indicated good quality.

Design characteristics of the included studies

The design characteristics of the 20 included models are shown in Table 1. The studies are published from 2006 to 2025. Fourteen studies had prospective designs, and the remaining 6 studies were retrospective. In terms of the study region, the US was the most common, with 8 studies. Four studies were international multicenter studies. Seven studies applied an intensive sampling strategy. The detailed drug administration and blood collection information are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Fourteen studies used HPLC for the determination of meropenem, and the other four used LC–MS/MS methods.

PPK modeling strategy

The modeling strategies of the included studies are shown in Table 3. NONMEM was the most frequently used modeling software. The covariate screening methods were similar among the studies, with most common forward inclusion criteria of an OFV decrease of 3.84. The exclusion criteria ranged from 3.84 to 10.83. The screened and identified covariates are summarized in Figure 2. Twelve different covariates were incorporated into the final models. In 19 studies, body weight was chosen as a candidate covariate, and in the other study, clearance and V were predominantly scaled by body weight (Carmeli et al., 2025). Finally, two studies failed to include body weight in the final model because of the limited sample size. Renal function was the second most frequently screened, with 18 studies and 11 studies including renal function indicators in the final model. Common renal function indicators included CLCR, Scr, eGFR and other indicators.

PPK parameter estimate

The final PPK model information and parameter estimates are shown in Table 4. Fourteen studies (70%) used the final 2-CMT model, 5 studies (25%) used the 1-CMT model, and one study (5%) used the 3-CMT model. The typical CL values are shown in Figure 3, which indicates that variance and heterogeneity existed among the studies. The typical CL values varied greatly among the studies, ranging from 0.50 to 13.22 L/h. The range of weight-scaled CL was also large (0.122–0.526 L/h/kg). Dosage regimen suggestion

In terms of the results of the PK/PD analysis, %T > MIC was the common index. However, the target value varied across studies. Most studies have performed simulations and suggested optimized dosage regimens for specific patient populations (Table 5). The current meropenem dosage may be sufficient for the normal target (40–50%T > MIC), but a higher dose and prolonged infusion are needed for aggressive targets. Several studies have reported the actual PK/PD target attainment. Du et al. reported %T > MIC values ranging from 72% to 100% for an MIC distribution range of 0.004 to 0.6 μg/mL, and 50%T > 4*MIC attainment rates reported by Smith et al. and Zylbersztajn et al. were also satisfactory (Du et al., 2006; Smith et al., 2011; Zylbersztajn et al., 2021).

Discussion

This systematic review comprehensively analyzed the available meropenem PPK models for pediatric patients. Most studies have yielded 2-CMT models, with body weight and renal function as the most common covariates. The parameters varied greatly among studies, and the interindividual variabilities within studies were also large, which underscores the importance of MIPD and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for these patients. Among the 20 available models, diverse pediatric patients were included, which would be helpful for meropenem MIPD in pediatric patients.

The 20 included studies were distributed on average from 2006 to 2025, which indicated that the precision dosing of meropenem for pediatric patients was always noted. The prospective design of 14 studies would be helpful for controlling for retrospective bias. Four studies were multicenter studies and were more confident in extrapolating the results. However, only 11 countries were included in the included studies. Although no evidence supporting ethic differences exists, more data from other areas are needed, especially Africa and South America (Boutzoukas et al., 2025). Moreover, differences in clinical practice, dosing, and population characteristics may still affect PK.

In terms of patient characteristics, a variety of patients were included. Pharmacokinetics in special populations, including newborns, neonates, young children, and pediatric patients with critical illness or sepsis, were investigated. When dosing pediatric patients with meropenem, physicians and pharmacists can select a PPK model with characteristics similar to those of a specific patient to improve precision dosing. Some models cover a broad age range, but caution should be taken when these models are applied to newborns because of the limited data of this subset. Organ support could alter the pharmacokinetics of drugs and increase dosing complexity (Stitt et al., 2024; Stitt et al., 2022). Pediatric patients who received organ support were included, mainly those who received CRRT and ECMO. However, fewer than 100 patients were treated with CRRT, and the number of patients treated with ECMO was even lower. The PK of meropenem in these patients still needs more data.

Although various modeling software programs have been used, NONMEM is the most frequently used software, as it is the gold standard in PPK modeling (Duong and Marsot, 2024). The modeling strategies among studies were similar, but the significance levels in backward elimination were different. This may be due to the sample size used for modeling. However, overfitting may exist in studies with small sample sizes. 2-CMT was the predominant model structure among all the studies. The good design of the sampling strategy allowed the studies to develop a more complicated model. Although only 7 studies applied intensive sampling, the other studies used a sparse sampling strategy, but the time points covered the dosing interval. The only 3-CMT model study reported a 2-CMT model, as it classified CSF as a single compartment (Ohata et al., 2019). Thus, the 2-CMT model would be more suitable for pediatric patients, but the complexity may cause difficulty in parameter estimation and model application. Regrettably, none of the studies reported condition numbers or shrinkages. The estimated typical CL value of meropenem ranged from 0.50 to 13.22 L/h, and the Vc for the 2-CMT model ranged from 0.969 L to 21.4 L. This is not surprising because of the physiological difference between neonates and young adolescents (Wang K. et al., 2021). Some reports have scaled the CL by body weight, but the CLs ranged from 0.122 to 0.526 L/h/kg, and the variability was large. These findings indicated that TDM is important for obtaining optimal meropenem exposure in pediatric patients (Luque et al., 2024).

Many covariates were tested, and 11 different covariates were incorporated into the final models. These covariates can be divided into two main groups. The first group was the covariate that indicated the growth status of the pediatric patients, mainly body weight and age. Nearly all the studies tested these two variables; 17 studies included body weight (an additional study predominantly included body weight), and 11 studies included age. In pediatric patients with severe disease, body weight is a better indicator of growth status, especially in neonates and infants (Soejima et al., 2022). This is in accordance with the label suggestion of weight-scaled dosing. However, the PK of meropenem in obese and malnourished children was not assessed, and caution should be taken when weight-scaled dosing is used for these patients. The second group was the covariate that indicated renal function. It is quite reasonable that meropenem is eliminated mainly through the renal pathway and that decreased renal function can reduce drug clearance (Soejima et al., 2022). There are many renal function indicators, and the indicators included in these studies include CRCL, eGFR, SCr, CRRT, serum cystatin C, etc. For creatine clearance or glomerular filtration rate estimation, multiple functions were used, and Schwartz and modified Schwartz functions were most frequently used. However, these functions are limited in some subgroups, such as patients aged under 1 year, patients with AKI, and malnourished patients (Kudgus Lokken et al., 2026). It should be noted when these patients are treated with covariates. Other kinds of covariates have seldom been tested. Body weight, age and renal function are insufficient to explain the variability in meropenem PK among individuals.

Among all the included studies, multiple internal model validation methods were applied, but only one model underwent external validation. This may be due to the lack of available datasets and methodological challenges. The common internal validation methods included goodness-of-fit (GOF) plots, visual predictive checks (VPCs), and the bootstrap method. One study performed leave-one-out cross-validation, but it was also internally validated, as the datasets for model development and validation were the same. Each internal validation method has shortcomings. The GOF method is intuitive and fast, but it is highly subjective and lacks quantitative indicators. The VPC method is more suitable for evaluating the overall performance of a model than for estimating the accuracy of the parameters. The bootstrap method cannot be used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model. A combination of multiple internal validation methods would improve the robustness of the model. However, the extrapolation performances of these models are uncertain because of the lack of external validation. When these models are applied, it is necessary to carefully select the model. Clinicians should compare patient characteristics (weight, renal function, and other covariates) with those of patients for model development and select the model with least extrapolation risk. However, common modeling tools, such as NONMEM and Monolix, are difficult to handle, and a pharmacist would be helpful. It is also better to perform local validation or re-estimation of parameters before model application; usually, a dataset of 10–20 patients is needed for external validation (Sherwin et al., 2012; El Hassani et al., 2025). Owing to the large variability in pediatric meropenem PK, TDM is the most reliable method to achieve optimal exposure at the current stage. More accurate dose adjustments could be performed using Bayesian forecasting based on the PPK model and TDM data. When TDM data accumulate, clinicians can practice MIPD, and treatment outcomes may improve along with improved target achievement.

Most studies have performed simulations based on the final PPK model and have provided dosing suggestions. Body weight and renal function are the main patient issues that should be considered when meropenem is given in pediatric patients. The common dose of 20–40 mg/kg every 8-h regimen could achieve 40% T > MIC in most circumstances. However, when the infected microorganism is less susceptible or an aggressive PK/PD target is needed, a higher dose and prolonged infusion should be selected. However, an aggressive dose may be off-label, and the risk and benefit should be evaluated carefully. According to the covariate analysis, body weight has the greatest influence on CL, and a weight-scale dose is preferred. This is in accordance with the simulation data. Renal function influenced CL, but the effect was not significant under some circumstances. Approximately 50% of the dose adjustment may be required for AKI and augmented renal function patients.

Future studies are still needed to address the current limitations of the available models. Well-designed studies on meropenem PK in diverse pediatric patients with specific pathophysiological conditions are needed. Currently, the PK in neonates, patients with CRRT and ECMO support, patients with malnutrition and patients with organ dysfunction is insufficient. Novel covariates should be identified to describe the intraindividual variability. Multicenter external validation of available PPK models or development of a universal PPK model is needed to evaluate the external performance. Advanced models, such as the PBPK model and artificial intelligence technology, are needed to explore the origin of individual variability.

Conclusion

This systematic review successfully integrated the current knowledge about PPK models for pediatric patients. The available PPK models cover diverse pediatric patients, and body weight, age and renal function are the most important covariates. The synthesized evidence provides a valuable reference for future PPK model development and supports individualized meropenem dosing in pediatric clinical practice. Considering the limitations of the current models, multicenter external validation and future well-designed studies in diverse pediatric populations (neonates, septic patients, patients with CRRT and ECMO, etc.) are needed. Clinical decision support tools for precision dosing of meropenem in pediatric patients would be favored.
