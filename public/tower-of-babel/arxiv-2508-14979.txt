Energy-independent tomography of Gaussian states
Lennart Bittel, Francesco A. Mele, Jens Eisert, Antonio A. Mele

Abstract
The exploration of tomography of bosonic Gaussian states is presumably as old as quantum optics, but only recently, their precise and rigorous study have been moving into the focus of attention, motivated by technological developments. In this work, we present an efficient and experimentally feasible Gaussian state tomography algorithm with provable recovery trace-distance guarantees, whose sample complexity depends only on the number of modes, and—remarkably—is independent of the state’s photon number or energy, up to doubly logarithmic factors. Our algorithm yields a doubly-exponential improvement over existing methods, and it employs operations that are readily accessible in experimental settings: the preparation of an auxiliary squeezed vacuum, passive Gaussian unitaries, and homodyne detection. At its core lies an adaptive strategy that systematically reduces the total squeezing of the system, enabling efficient tomography. Quite surprisingly, this proves that estimating a Gaussian state in trace distance is generally more efficient than directly estimating its covariance matrix. Our algorithm is particularly well-suited for applications in quantum metrology and sensing, where highly squeezed—and hence high-energy—states are commonly employed. As a further contribution, we establish improved sample complexity bounds for standard heterodyne tomography, equipping this widely used protocol with rigorous trace-norm guarantees.

Can we design a Gaussian state tomography algorithm whose sample complexity is independent of the state’s energy?

In this work, we answer this question effectively in the affirmative. We present an adaptive and experimentally feasible Gaussian state tomography algorithm that relies exclusively on standard tools available in optical laboratories: auxiliary squeezed state preparation, passive Gaussian operations, and homodyne detection. If the mean total energy of the unknown state is denoted by
E
, we show that the sample complexity of our protocol scales as
\log\log(E)
—a quantity that remains effectively constant (on the order of 10), even when
E
is as large as the energy of the observable universe. In contrast, previous state-of-the-art protocols exhibit sample complexities that scale polynomially with
E
; for instance, standard heterodyne tomography based solely on covariance matrix estimation scales as
\mathcal{O}(E^{2})
, as discussed earlier. Our method thus achieves a double-exponential improvement in energy dependence, while retaining the scaling in the number of modes
n
. This results in a substantial reduction in experimental cost for high-energy states.

The core idea behind our protocol is to first determine—using only a small number of measurements—whether significant squeezing is initially present in the unknown state. If so, we adaptively suppress this squeezing by applying passive Gaussian unitaries in combination with suitably prepared auxiliary squeezed inputs, effectively implementing generalized heterodyne (or generaldyne) measurements
Serafini 2017
. Once the effective squeezing is sufficiently reduced, we perform standard covariance estimation in the rotated basis, and then invert the transformation to reconstruct the original state.
Importantly, this protocol relies exclusively on
offline
squeezing—that is, squeezing applied to auxiliary vacuum states prior to the measurement process—as is routinely available in quantum optics laboratories. At no point is
online
or active squeezing required, making the scheme both experimentally accessible and practically robust.

Moreover, we show that even the mild
\log\log(E)
dependence can be removed entirely—yielding a fully energy-independent tomography scheme—if access to the transposed state is available. In this setting, a simple and experimentally feasible procedure based on double homodyne measurements on the state and its transpose suffices. Recently, considerable attention has been devoted to the fact that access to the transposed state can yield substantial advantages in a variety of quantum learning tasks

.
Gaussian states are Gibbs states of quadratic Hamiltonians in the quadratures. They are fully specified by the first moment
m(\rho)\coloneqq{\rm Tr}[\hat{\mathbf{R}}\rho]
and the covariance matrix
V(\rho)
, which encodes second moments and satisfies the uncertainty relation
V+i\Omega\geq 0

admits a

containing the symplectic eigenvalues.
Gaussian unitaries are unitary operations generated by quadratic Hamiltonians in the quadratures.
Active
and
passive
Gaussian unitaries are fully specified by their associated symplectic matrices
S\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
. A Gaussian unitary is called
passive
if
S\in\mathrm{O}(2n)\cap\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
; otherwise, it is called

. Passive operations—like beam splitters and phase shifters—can be implemented easily with linear optics, whereas active ones, such as squeezing, are more experimentally demanding. Any
S\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)
admits an Euler decomposition
S=O_{1}ZO_{2}
, where
O_{1},O_{2}\in\mathrm{Sp}(2n)\cap\mathrm{O}(2n)
, and
Z=\mathrm{diag}(z_{1},1/z_{1},\dots,z_{n},1/z_{n})
with
z_{j}\geq 1
.

Standard measurement strategies in CV systems routinely used in the lab include
homodyne
,
heterodyne
, and more generally,
generaldyne
detection. Homodyne
measurements on Gaussian states correspond to sampling from a classical Gaussian distribution determined by specific quadrature operators and the corresponding marginal of the state covariance matrix. Heterodyne detection, on the other hand, simultaneously probes both quadratures with added noise—effectively sampling from a Gaussian distribution with the state covariance matrix shifted by the identity. In practice, heterodyne detection is implemented by interfering the input state with a vacuum auxiliary state at a balanced beam splitter, followed by homodyne measurements on both output ports. More generally,
generaldyne
measurements form a broad class of Gaussian POVMs—including homodyne and heterodyne as special cases—which can be realized by combining the input state with an auxiliary Gaussian state via a passive Gaussian unitary, followed by homodyne detection.

.5
Avoidance of active squeezing

Algorithm
2
requires applying an active Gaussian unitary to the input state, followed by heterodyne detection. However, implementing active Gaussian operations is often experimentally demanding. Fortunately, this step can be entirely avoided: the combined effect of an active Gaussian transformation followed by heterodyne measurement can be exactly reproduced using only passive Gaussian operations, a fixed auxiliary squeezed input state, and homodyne measurements (plus classical post-processing)—all of which are routinely available with current photonic technologies
Schnabel 2017b
. In continuous-variable terminology, this alternative scheme corresponds to performing a specific
generaldyne measurement
on the input state
Serafini 2017
.

This construction builds on the well-known equivalence between heterodyne detection and beam-splitter interference followed by homodyne measurements. As reviewed in the Preliminaries, heterodyne detection can be implemented by interfering the input state with vacuum on a balanced beam splitter, followed by homodyne detection of complementary quadratures—typically position on one output mode and momentum on the other. In the variant considered here, the vacuum input is replaced with a suitably chosen squeezed state.
Key aspects of this measurement equivalence are illustrated in Fig. 1.3, with additional technical details provided in the supplementary material (see section
IV.1
).

Full energy-independence via access to the transpose

While the adaptive tomography protocol presented in the previous section achieves sample complexity that is effectively energy-independent—scaling only double-logarithmically with the energy—it is natural to ask whether this residual dependence can be eliminated entirely.

This is indeed possible if one has access to the transposed state
\rho^{T}

.7
Conclusions

In this work, have we introduced three conceptually distinct tomography algorithms for Gaussian quantum states, each with its own significance,
revisiting the old problem of learning Gaussian quantum states from an entirely fresh perspective.
All algorithms are underpinned by a new trace-distance perturbation bound for Gaussian states, which serves as the technical foundation of our results.

First, we have equipped the standard heterodyne tomography protocol—routinely implemented in optical laboratories — with rigorous trace-distance error guarantees. This result bridges the painful gap between experimental practice and the requirements of theoretical applications, where quantitative reconstruction guarantees in trace distance are often crucial. Our analysis offers simple and practical tools for experimentalists to assess the accuracy of their reconstructions. Second, we have presented an adaptive, sample-efficient, and experimentally feasible tomography algorithm whose sample complexity depends essentially only on the number of modes and is independent of the state’s energy up to doubly-logaritmic factors. For all practical purposes, this yields an energy-independent protocol. This makes the algorithm particularly well-suited for quantum sensing applications

All three schemes are compatible with existing experimental capabilities in quantum optics laboratories around the world, relying only on tools already available in photonic platforms: auxiliary squeezed inputs, passive Gaussian unitaries reflecting passive optical elements, and homodyne detection.

The Hilbert space of a continuous-variable system
: Continuous variable systems, such as quantum optical and bosonic systems, are associated with an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, the
n
-mode bosonic Fock space

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\pazocal{H}\coloneqq\mathrm{Span}\left\{\ket{m}:\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\right\}\,,

where

is the

can be defined to be the Hilbert space

For each

th mode
, denoted as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pta_{i}\ket{m}=\sqrt{m_{i}}\ket{m-e_i}\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\,,

(13)

where

is the
i
th canonical basis vector of

. Analogously, the

i
th mode

, is the adjoint of the annihilation operator and satisfies

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pta_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{m}=\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{m+e_i}\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\,,

(14)

The annihilation and creation operator satisfy the

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt[a_{i},a_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{i,j}\hat{\mathbb{1}}\,,

(15)

where
\delta_{i,j}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Moreover, for each

and the

of the

and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{x}_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{a_{j}-a_{j}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}i}\,.

In addition, the

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathbf{\hat{R}}\coloneqq(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n},\hat{p}_{n})^{\intercal}\,.

Using this notation, the canonical commutation relations can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt[\hat{R}_{k},\hat{R}_{l}]=i\,(\Omega_{n})_{k,l}\mathbb{\hat{1}}\qquad\forall\,k,l\in[2n]\,,

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Omega_{n}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt[\hat{\textbf{R}},\hat{\textbf{R}}^{\intercal}]=i\,\Omega_{n}\mathbb{\hat{1}}\,.

The

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{E}_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\hat{x}_{j}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\hat{p}_{j}^{2}}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\textbf{R}}^{\intercal}\hat{\textbf{R}}\,,

and the

Homodyne measurements.

An Homodyne measurement
Serafini 2017
;
Weedbrook et al. 2004
is a standard measurement in quantum optics that allows one to estimate the position and momentum of each bosonic mode. For an
n
-mode system with quadrature operator vector
\hat{\mathbf{R}}=(\hat{x}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n},\hat{p}_{n})^{\intercal},

a homodyne measurement consists of selecting, for each mode
j
, whether to measure
\hat{x}_{j}
or
\hat{p}_{j}
.

If a Gaussian state
\rho
, with first-moment vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and covariance matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
, is measured via homodyne detection, the outcomes follow a Gaussian probability distribution whose mean and covariance are given by restricting
\mathbf{m}
and
V/2
to the measured quadratures.
For example:

•

Measuring all position quadratures
(\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n})
yields outcomes
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
distributed as
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{m}_{x},\,V_{x,x}/2\right),
where
\mathbf{m}_{x}\coloneqq\left(m_{2j-1}(\rho)\right)_{j\in[n]}
and
V_{x,x}\coloneqq(V_{2i-1,2j-1})_{i,j\in[n]}
is the submatrix of
V
corresponding to the position-position elements.

•

Measuring all momentum quadratures
(\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{p}_{n})
gives outcomes
\mathbf{p}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{m}_{p},\,V_{p,p}/2\right),

with
\mathbf{m}_{p}\coloneqq\left(m_{2j}(\rho)\right)_{j\in[n]}
and
V_{p,p}\coloneqq(V_{2i,2j})_{i,j\in[n]}
is the submatrix of
V
corresponding the momentum-momentum elements.

More generally, fixed a symplectic matrix
S
, the homodyne measurement associated with
S
is a projective measurement with respect to the common (generalised) eigenbasis of the
n
commuting observables
\{\sum_{j=1}^{2n}S_{i,j}\hat{\textbf{R}}_{j}\}_{i\in\{1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1\}}
. Measuring all these
n
observables on an
n
-mode Gaussian state
\rho
yields outcomes
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
distributed as
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{m}_{x},\,V_{x,x}/2\right),
where
\mathbf{m}_{x}\coloneqq\left(m_{2j-1}(U_{S}\rho U_{s}^{\dagger})\right)_{j\in[n]}
and
V_{x,x}\coloneqq(V(U_{S}\rho U_{s}^{\dagger})_{2i-1,2j-1})_{i,j\in[n]}
. For example, the homodyne detection associated with the symplectic orthogonal matrix

\displaystyle S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{array}\right)

corresponds to measuring the quadrature observable
X_{\theta}\coloneqq\hat{x}\cos\theta+\hat{p}\sin\theta
.

Experimentally, homodyne measurements are implemented by interfering the signal mode with a strong, phase-controlled
local oscillator
(LO) at a beam splitter, followed by intensity measurements at the output ports
Serafini 2017
. The phase of the LO determines the measured quadrature: a phase of
0
corresponds to
\hat{x}
, a phase of
\pi/2
to
\hat{p}
, and other values to rotated quadratures.

Heterodyne measurement.

The heterodyne measurement
Serafini 2017
;
Weedbrook et al. 2004
allows one to simultaneously extract (noisy) information about both the position and momentum quadratures of each bosonic mode.
When applied to a Gaussian state
\rho
, with first-moment vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and covariance matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
, the heterodyne outcomes follow a classical Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{m},\,\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right),

(37)

where
\mathds{1}
denotes the
2n\times 2n
identity matrix.

Experimentally, heterodyne detection is implemented by evolving the system state
\rho
together with an auxiliary vacuum state
\ket{0}
through a balanced beam splitter
U_{B}
, followed by homodyne detection on both output ports: one measures the position quadratures on one arm and the momentum quadratures on the other.
The beam splitter
U_{B}
is a passive Gaussian operation corresponding to a symplectic orthogonal transformation

\displaystyle B=H\otimes\mathds{1}_{2n},\quad\text{where}\quad H\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

More generally, we have the following.

Lemma 5

((Generalized heterodyne sampling via beam splitter and homodyne detection))
.

Let
\rho(V_{1},m_{1})
and
\rho(V_{2},m_{2})
be two
n
-mode Gaussian states with first-moment vectors
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and covariance matrices
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
. Consider the product state
\rho(V_{1},m_{1})\otimes\rho(V_{2},m_{2})
, and evolve it under the beam splitter transformation
U_{B}
, corresponding to the passive Gaussian unitary associated with the symplectic orthogonal matrix
B=H\otimes\mathds{1}_{2n}
.
Then, perform homodyne measurements of the position quadratures
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n}
on the first output arm and the momentum quadratures
\hat{p}_{n+1},\dots,\hat{p}_{2n}
on the second. The resulting classical measurement outcome
\mathbf{r}=\sqrt{2}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is distributed according to

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m_{1}+\bar{m}_{2},\,\frac{V_{1}+\bar{V}_{2}}{2}\right),

(39)

where

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{2}\coloneqq(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)m_{2},\qquad\bar{V}_{2}\coloneqq(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)V_{2}(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z),

(40)

with
Z\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
.
In particular, if
\rho(V_{2},m_{2})
is the vacuum state (i.e.,
m_{2}=0
and
V_{2}=\mathds{1}_{2n}
), then we recover the heterodyne measurement result

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m_{1},\,\frac{V_{1}+\mathds{1}_{2n}}{2}\right).

(41)

Proof.

The joint state has first moment and covariance matrix

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad V=\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}&0\\
0&V_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

After applying the beam splitter, the state remains Gaussian with transformed first moment and covariance:

\displaystyle m^{\prime}

\displaystyle=Bm=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}+m_{2}\\
m_{1}-m_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(43)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}

\displaystyle=BVB^{\intercal}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}+V_{2}&V_{1}-V_{2}\\
V_{1}-V_{2}&V_{1}+V_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(44)

We then measure
\coloneqq\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n}
on the first output arm and
\coloneqq\hat{p}_{n+1},\dots,\hat{p}_{2n}
on the second through homodyne detection. The outcomes
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})
are Gaussian-distributed with mean and covariance obtained by restricting
m^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}/2
to the measured quadratures. In particular, the restricted output mean vector and covariance matrix are given by

\displaystyle m^{\prime}\big|_{(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}(m_{1})|_{\bm{x}}+(m_{2})|_{\bm{x}}\\
(m_{1})|_{\bm{p}}-(m_{2})|_{\bm{p}}\end{pmatrix},

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime}\big|_{(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}(V_{1})|_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}+(V_{2})|_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}&(V_{1})|_{\bm{x},\bm{p}}-(V_{2})|_{\bm{x},\bm{p}}\\
(V_{1})|_{\bm{p},\bm{x}}-(V_{2})|_{\bm{p},\bm{x}}&(V_{1})|_{\bm{p},\bm{p}}+(V_{2})|_{\bm{p},\bm{p}}\end{pmatrix}.

(46)

Thus,
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{m_{1}+\bar{m}_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},\,\frac{V_{1}+\bar{V}_{2}}{4}\right)
from which we have
\sqrt{2}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{p}_{2})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m_{1}+\bar{m}_{2},\,\frac{V_{1}+\bar{V}_{2}}{2}\right)
.

∎

II
Trace distance bounds for Gaussian states

This section is dedicated to proving a perturbation bound for the trace distance between Gaussian states, presented in Theorem
2
. This bound plays a crucial role in analyzing the performance of our tomography algorithm. Additionally, it is a result of independent interest, with potential applications in continuous variable quantum information theory
Serafini 2017
.
The proof of Theorem
2
is deferred to the end of this section. To establish this result, we introduce two lemmas that provide key intermediate results. These rely on the differentiability properties of Gaussian states, as developed in Ref.
Bittel et al. 2025
.

Lemma 6

((Perturbation bound: first moment))
.

Let
\rho(D,\alpha t^{\prime})
be a Gaussian state with diagonal covariance matrix
D=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes d
, where
d\geq\mathbb{1}
, and first-moment vector
\alpha t^{\prime}
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D,\alpha t^{\prime})\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{2n}t_{k}\left[R_{k},\rho(D,0)\right]\right\|_{1}\leq 2\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}t^{\prime}\right\|_{2},

(47)

where we denoted with
t\coloneqq\Omega t^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The first identity in the claim follows directly from Theorem S13 (Compact formula for the derivative of a Gaussian state) in Ref.
Bittel et al. 2025
.
We now prove the upper bound. Consider first the single-mode case, where the Gaussian state with diagonal covariance matrix, denoted as
\rho_{\tau}
, is a thermal state with parameter
\tau=\frac{d-1}{d+1}
(and equivalently
d=\frac{\tau+1}{1-\tau}
); see, e.g., Ref.
Serafini 2017
. In particular, the thermal state takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau}=(1-\tau)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau^{m}\ket{m}\!\bra{m}.

(48)

We compute the commutator of the position operator
x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a+a^{\dagger})
with
\rho_{\tau}
as

\displaystyle[x,\rho_{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\tau)}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau^{m}\left((a+a^{\dagger})\ket{m}\!\bra{m}-\ket{m}\!\bra{m}(a+a^{\dagger})\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\tau)}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau^{m}\left(-\sqrt{m+1}\ket{m}\!\bra{m+1}+\sqrt{m+1}\ket{m+1}\!\bra{m}\right)\times(1-\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\tau)^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau^{m}\sqrt{m+1}\left(\ket{m+1}\!\bra{m}-\ket{m}\!\bra{m+1}\right).

This generalizes to the multi mode case as

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[t_{i}R_{i},\rho_{D}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}-it_{2i})(1-\tau_{i})\left(\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec m+\vec e_i}\!\bra{\vec m}\right)-h.c.,

(50)

where
p_{\vec{m}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\tau_{i})\tau_{i}^{m_{i}}
is the probability distribution of the Fock state elements.
Thus, taking the one-norm, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i\in[2n]}[t_{i}R_{i},\tau_{\nu}]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\vec{m}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}-it_{2i})(1-\tau_{i})\left(\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec m+\vec e_i}\!\bra{\vec m}\right)-h.c.\right\|_{1}

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\vec{m}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}-it_{2i})(1-\tau_{i})\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec m+\vec e_i}\!\bra{\vec m}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{\vec{m}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}-it_{2i})(1-\tau_{i})\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec m+\vec e_i}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{m}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}-it_{2i})(1-\tau_{i})\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec m+\vec e_i}\right\|^{2}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{m}=0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}^{2}+t_{2i}^{2})(1-\tau_{i})^{2}(m_{i}+1)}

where we have used that
\mathbb{E}(X)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}
.
Next, we can use that

\displaystyle(1-p)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{m}(m+1)=\frac{1}{1-p},

(52)

to obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i\in[2n]}[t_{i}R_{i},\tau_{\nu}]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[n]}(t_{2i-1}^{2}+t_{2i}^{2})(1-\tau_{i})}=2\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}t\|_{2}=2\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}t^{\prime}\|_{2},

(53)

using
1-\tau_{i}=\frac{2}{d_{i}+1}
and that
\Omega
commute with
D
, which conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\rho(D,0)
be a bosonic Gaussian state with diagonal covariance matrix
D=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes d
, where
d\geq\mathbb{1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha X,0)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\left\|\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{2n}(\Omega X\Omega^{\top})_{k,\ell}\left[R_{k},\left[R_{\ell},\rho(D,0)\right]\right]\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}X(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}.

(54)

Proof.

Let
\tilde{X}\coloneqq\Omega X\Omega^{\top}
. By Theorem S13 (Compact formula for the derivative of a Gaussian state) in Ref.
Bittel et al. 2025
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha X,0)\right|_{\alpha=0}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{2n}\tilde{X}_{k,\ell}\left[R_{k},\left[R_{\ell},\rho\right]\right].

(55)

We now expand the quadrature operators
R_{k}
in terms of creation and annihilation operators

\displaystyle R_{2j-1+\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}i^{\alpha}\left((-1)^{\alpha}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger}\right),\qquad\alpha\in\{0,1\},\;j\in[n].

(56)

Substituting this into the double commutator, we find

\displaystyle-4\,\partial_{X,0}\rho(D+\alpha X,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[2n]}\tilde{X}_{i,j}\left[\hat{R}_{i},\left[\hat{R}_{j},\rho\right]\right]

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}i^{\alpha+\beta}\tilde{X}_{2i-1+\alpha,\,2j-1+\beta}\left[(-1)^{\alpha}a_{i}+a_{i}^{\dagger},\;\left[(-1)^{\beta}a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}i^{\alpha+\beta}\tilde{X}_{2i-1+\alpha,\,2j-1+\beta}\Big([a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+(-1)^{\alpha}[a_{i},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(-1)^{\beta}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j},\rho]]+(-1)^{\alpha+\beta}[a_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]\Big)

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(A_{i,j}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+B_{i,j}[a_{i},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+B^{*}_{{i,j}}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j},\rho]]+A_{{i,j}}^{*}[a_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(A_{{i,j}}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+2B_{{i,j}}[a_{i},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+A_{{i,j}}^{*}[a_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]\right),

where in the third line we have defined the matrices

\displaystyle A_{{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}i^{\alpha+\beta}\tilde{X}_{2i-1+\alpha,\,2j-1+\beta},

(59)

\displaystyle B_{{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}}i^{\alpha+\beta}(-1)^{\alpha}\tilde{X}_{2i-1+\alpha,\,2j-1+\beta},

(60)

that satisfy
A=A^{\top}
,
B=B^{\dagger}
.
We now note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B^{*}_{{i,j}}[a^{\dagger}_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{j,i}[a^{\dagger}_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]

(61)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{j,i}\left([\rho,[a^{\dagger}_{i},a_{j}]]+[a_{j},[\rho,a^{\dagger}_{i}]]\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{j,i}[a_{j},[\rho,a^{\dagger}_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{j,i}[a_{j},[a^{\dagger}_{i},\rho]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}[a_{i},[a^{\dagger}_{j},\rho]],

where in the first step we have used Hermiticity of
B
, in the second step we applied the Jacobi identity

[A,[B,C]]=-[B,[C,A]]-[C,[A,B]],

(62)

and in the third step we have used the canonical commutation relation
[a_{i},a^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{{i,j}}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle-4\,\partial_{X,0}\rho(V,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(A_{{i,j}}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+2B_{{i,j}}[a_{i},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+A_{{i,j}}^{*}[a_{i},[a_{j},\rho]]\right).

(63)

Note that the matrices
A
and
B
can be obtained from the matrix
\tilde{X}=\Omega X\Omega^{\top}
via the unitary transformation

\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}&\mathbb{1}\\
i\mathbb{1}&-i\mathbb{1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad M^{\dagger}\tilde{X}M=\begin{pmatrix}B&A\\
A^{\dagger}&B^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

We also introduce the notation

\displaystyle A_{\tau}\coloneqq D_{\tau}AD_{\tau},\qquad B_{\tau}\coloneqq D_{\tau}BD_{\tau},\qquad X_{\tau}\coloneqq(D_{\tau}\oplus D_{\tau})\,X\,(D_{\tau}\oplus D_{\tau}),

(65)

with

\displaystyle D_{\tau}\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}\left(\sqrt{1-\tau_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{1-\tau_{n}}\right).

(66)

We start by estimating the contribution from the
B
-term in Eq. (
63
). As in the proof of the previous lemma, we note that we can write the state
\rho
as a diagonal thermal state in the Fock basis:

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}},\qquad p_{\vec{m}}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\tau_{i})\tau_{i}^{m_{i}},

(67)

with
\tau_{i}=\frac{d_{i}-1}{d_{i}+1}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}\left[a_{i},\left[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\left(a_{i}a_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}+\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}-a_{i}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{j}^{\dagger}-a_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{i}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}B_{i,i}\left((2m_{i}+1)\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}-(m_{i}+1)\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}\!\bra{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}-m_{i}\ket{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_i}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[n]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}B_{{i,j}}\Big(\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\sqrt{m_{i}}\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j - \vec{e}_i}\!\bra{\vec{m}}+\sqrt{m_{j}}\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_j + \vec{e}_i}

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}-\sqrt{m_{j}}\sqrt{m_{i}}\ket{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_i}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_j}\Big).

We now simplify the previous expression by appropriately shifting the summation indices. For this, we use the relation

\displaystyle p_{\vec{m}}\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\tau_{i})\tau_{i}^{m_{i}},\qquad\Rightarrow\quad p_{\vec{m}+\vec{e}_{i}}=\tau_{i}p_{\vec{m}},\qquad{p_{\vec{m}-\vec{e}_{i}}}=\frac{{p_{\vec{m}}}}{\tau_{i}}.

(69)

So we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}\left[a_{i},\left[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}B_{i,i}\left(2m_{i}+1-\tau_{i}(m_{i}+1)-\frac{m_{i}}{\tau_{i}}\right)\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[n]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}B_{{i,j}}\sqrt{(m_{i}+1)(m_{j}+1)}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}B_{i,i}\left(2m_{i}+1-\tau_{i}(m_{i}+1)-\frac{m_{i}}{\tau_{i}}+\frac{m_{i}(1-\tau_{i})^{2}}{\tau_{i}}\right)\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}\sqrt{(m_{i}+1)(m_{j}+1)}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i\in[n]}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}\sqrt{(m_{i}+1)(m_{j}+1)}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}.

We will proceed with the bound

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{i,j}[a_{i}[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]-\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\rho\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{i,j}(\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\sqrt{m_{i}+1}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{j}\!\bra{i}\right\|_{1}.

(70)

We write the matrix
B_{\tau}
in its eigendecomposition
B_{\tau}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\ket{b_k}\bra{b_k}
, yielding

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{i,j\in[n]}B_{{i,j}}(\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\sqrt{m_{i}+1}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{j}\!\bra{i}\right\|_{1}

(71)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}|\lambda_{k}|\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{j\in[n]}b_{kj}^{*}(\sqrt{m_{j}+1}(1-\tau_{j})^{1/2}\ket{j}\times\sum_{i\in[n]}\bra{i}\sqrt{m_{i}+1}(1-\tau_{i})^{1/2}b_{k,i}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}|\lambda_{k}|\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\left\|\sum_{j\in[n]}b_{kj}^{*}(\sqrt{m_{j}+1}(1-\tau_{j})^{1/2}\ket{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}|\lambda_{k}|\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i}(1-\tau_{i})(m_{i}+1)|b_{k,i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}|\lambda_{k}|\sum_{i}|b_{k,i}|^{2}

(72)

\displaystyle=\Tr(|B_\tau|)=2\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}B(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\|_{1}

where we have used that for rank-one operators,

\|\ket{a}\!\bra{b}\|_{1}=\|\ket{a}\|_{2}\|\ket{b}\|_{2}
, and that
p_{\vec{m}}
is a probability distribution. Finally, we have used the identity

\displaystyle(1-\tau_{i})^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau_{i}^{m}(m+1)=1,

(73)

denoted
\tau\coloneqq\mathrm{Diag}(\vec{\tau})
,
D\coloneqq\mathrm{Diag}(\vec{d})
, and used that
1-\tau_{i}=\frac{2}{d_{i}+1}
.
We now consider the term involving the matrix
A
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in[n]}A_{{i,j}}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}A_{{i,j}}\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\Big(a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}+\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{i}^{\dagger}-a_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{j}^{\dagger}-a_{j}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m}}a_{i}^{\dagger}\Big)

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\Big(\sqrt{m_{i}+1+\delta_{{i,j}}}\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{m_{i}-\delta_{{i,j}}}\sqrt{m_{j}}\ket{\vec{m}}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_i - \vec{e}_j}

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{m_{i}+1}\sqrt{m_{j}}\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_j}-\sqrt{m_{i}}\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m} - \vec{e}_i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}A_{{i,j}}\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sqrt{m_{j}+1}\sqrt{m_{i}+1+\delta_{{i,j}}}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})\ket{\vec{m} + \vec{e}_i + \vec{e}_j}\!\bra{\vec{m}}.

This shows that the combined contribution from the
A
-term and the diagonal part of the
B
-term is bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i,j\in[n]}A_{{i,j}}[a_{i}^{\dagger},[a_{j}^{\dagger},\rho]]+\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\rho\right\|_{1}

(75)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(m_{j}+1)(m_{i}+1+\delta_{{i,j}})(1-\tau_{i})^{2}(1-\tau_{j})^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}p_{\vec{m}}\sum_{i,j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(m_{j}+1)(m_{i}+1+\delta_{{i,j}})(1-\tau_{i})^{2}(1-\tau_{j})^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\neq j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau_{i}^{m}(m+1)(1-\tau_{i})^{3}\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau_{j}^{m}(m+1)(1-\tau_{j})^{3}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\sum_{i}|A_{i,i}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})^{5}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tau_{i}^{m}(m+1)(m+2)+\left(\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\neq j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})+2\sum_{i}|A_{i,i}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})+\sum_{i}|A_{i,i}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}B_{i,i}(1-\tau_{i})\right)^{2}}.

As in the earlier part of the proof, we applied the triangle inequality, along with

\|\ket{a}\!\bra{b}\|_{1}=\|\ket{a}\|_{2}\|\ket{b}\|_{2}
.
We also used the bound
\mathbb{E}(X)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}
, which follows from Jensen’s inequality.
In addition, we made use of the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{m}(m+1)(m+2)=\frac{2}{(1-p)^{3}},\qquad\text{for }0<p<1.

(76)

Putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\partial_{\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha X,0)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})+\sum_{i}|A_{i,i}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})^{2}+\left(\sum_{i}(1-\tau_{i})B_{i,i}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(1-\tau_{i})|B_{i,i}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\sum_{i,j}|A_{{i,j}}|^{2}(1-\tau_{i})(1-\tau_{j})+\left(\sum_{i}(1-\tau_{i})B_{i,i}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(1-\tau_{i})|B_{i,i}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\|A_{\tau}\|_{2}^{2}+\Tr(B_\tau)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\|B_{\tau}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\|A_{\tau}\|_{1}^{2}+\|B_{\tau}\|_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\|B_{\tau}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}\|\tilde{X}_{\tau}\|_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\|\tilde{X}_{\tau}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{4}\|\tilde{X}_{\tau}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\tilde{X}(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}X(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\right\|_{1},

where the steps are justified as follows.
we have used that
X
admits the block representation

\displaystyle\tilde{X}=M\begin{pmatrix}B&A\\
A^{\dagger}&B^{*}\end{pmatrix}M^{\dagger},

(78)

under the unitary transformation

\displaystyle M=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}&\mathbb{1}\\
i\mathbb{1}&-i\mathbb{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(79)

Since
D_{\tau}\oplus D_{\tau}
commutes with
M
, the rescaled matrix
\tilde{X}_{\tau}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{\tau}=M\begin{pmatrix}B_{\tau}&A_{\tau}\\
A_{\tau}^{\dagger}&B_{\tau}^{*}\end{pmatrix}M^{\dagger}.

(80)

From this, we observe that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{X}_{\tau}\|_{1}=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}B_{\tau}&A_{\tau}\\
A_{\tau}^{\dagger}&B_{\tau}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{1}\geq\max\left\{\left\|\begin{pmatrix}A_{\tau}&0\\
0&A_{\tau}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{1},\left\|\begin{pmatrix}B_{\tau}&0\\
0&B_{\tau}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{1}\right\}=2\max\left\{\|A_{\tau}\|_{1},\|B_{\tau}\|_{1}\right\},

(81)

as follows from Statement 6 in the list of preliminary inequalities. This justifies the use of
\|\tilde{X}_{\tau}\|_{1}
to upper bound the trace norm contributions from both
A_{\tau}
and
B_{\tau}
.
Finally, we have used the identity
1-\tau_{i}=\frac{2}{d_{i}+1}
, and in the last step, the fact that
D
commutes with
\Omega
.

∎

We are now finally in the position to prove the trace-distance bound, by assembling the results established above.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\rho(V,t)
be a bosonic Gaussian state. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\rho(V+\alpha X,m+\alpha t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\Tr\left(V^{-1}|X|\right)+2\|V^{-1/2}t\|_{2}.

(82)

Moreover, for any two Gaussian states
\rho(V,t)
and
\rho(W,m)
, the bound

\displaystyle\|\rho(V,t)-\rho(W,m)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|V^{-1/2}(m-t)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{4}\Tr\left((V^{-1}+W^{-1})|V-W|\right).

(83)

Proof.

We use the Williamson decomposition
V=SDS^{T}
to obtain

\displaystyle\rho(V+\alpha X,m+\alpha t)

\displaystyle=U_{m}\rho(V+\alpha X,\alpha t)U_{m}^{\dagger}

(84)

\displaystyle=U_{m}\rho(S(D+\alpha S^{-1}XS^{-T})S^{T},\alpha t)U^{\dagger}_{m}

\displaystyle=U_{m}U_{S}\,\rho(D+\alpha S^{-1}XS^{-T},\,\alpha S^{-1}t)\,U_{S}^{\dagger}U_{m}^{\dagger},

where we have used that both displacement of the first moment and squeezing correspond to unitary transformations, which in particular leave the trace norm invariant. Therefore, computing

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\partial_{\alpha}\rho(V+\alpha X,m+\alpha t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}

(85)

is equivalent to computing

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha S^{-1}XS^{-T},\,\alpha S^{-1}t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}.

(86)

We then estimate this trace norm derivative by splitting it as

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha S^{-1}XS^{-T},\,\alpha S^{-1}t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D+\alpha S^{-1}XS^{-T},\,0)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\rho(D,\,\alpha S^{-1}t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}S^{-1}XS^{-T}(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}+2\left\|(D+\mathbb{1})^{-1/2}S^{-1}t\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|D^{-1/2}S^{-1}XS^{-T}D^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}+2\left\|D^{-1/2}S^{-1}t\right\|_{2},

where in the first step we have used the definition of derivative and the triangle inequality, in the second step we have applied the previously established lemmas, and in the last step we have removed the identity component for simplicity. In the special case of pure states (
D=\mathbb{1}
), this last step can be avoided. As such, a more refined analysis could yield sharper sampling bounds in the pure-state regime.
Now observe that

\displaystyle V=SDS^{T}=(SD^{1/2})(D^{1/2}S^{T}),

(88)

so that

\displaystyle V^{-1/2}=UD^{-1/2}S^{-1}

(89)

for some unitary matrix
U
. Since Schatten norms are unitary-invariant, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\rho(V+\alpha X,m+\alpha t)\right|_{\alpha=0}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|V^{-1/2}X(V^{-1/2})^{T}\right\|_{1}+2\left\|V^{-1/2}t\right\|_{2}

(90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\left\|V^{-1/2}XV^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}+2\left\|V^{-1/2}t\right\|_{2},

which concludes the first part of the proof.
Next,

\displaystyle\|\rho(V,t)-\rho(W,m)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho(V,t)-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}+\|\rho(V,m)-\rho(W,m)\|_{1}

(91)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\partial_{\alpha}\rho(V,t+\alpha(m-t))\right\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha+\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\partial_{\alpha}\rho(V+\alpha(W-V),m)\right\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}\|V^{-1/2}\alpha(m-t)\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\Big\|(V+\alpha(W-V))^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\cdot(W-V)\,(V+\alpha(W-V))^{-1/2}\Big\|_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\|V^{-1/2}(m-t)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{4}\left(\|V^{-1/2}(V-W)V^{-1/2}\|_{1}+\|W^{-1/2}(V-W)W^{-1/2}\|_{1}\right),

where we have used

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(V+\alpha(W-V))^{-1/2}(W-V)(V+\alpha(W-V))^{-1/2}\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}\alpha

(92)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\Tr\left[(V+\alpha(W-V))^{-1}|W-V|\right]\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\Tr\left[\big((1-\alpha)V^{-1}+\alpha W^{-1}\big)|W-V|\right]\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\frac{V^{-1}+W^{-1}}{2}|W-V|\right).

∎

III
Gaussian state tomography using heterodyne measurements: A non-adaptive algorithm

We begin by deriving bounds on the number of samples required to estimate the first moment vector and the covariance matrix of an unknown multivariate classical Gaussian distribution.
These bounds will later be applied to the analysis of heterodyne measurement outcomes.

Lemma 9

((Relative error estimation of the covariance matrix))
.

Let
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be
N
i.i.d. samples from an
n
-dimensional Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
(see Eq. (
10
)). Define the empirical mean and covariance estimators as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\hat{x}_{i},\qquad\hat{\Sigma}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(\hat{x}_{i}-\hat{\mu})(\hat{x}_{i}-\hat{\mu})^{T}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{i}^{T}-\hat{\mu}\hat{\mu}^{T}.

(93)

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the bounds

\displaystyle\|\mu-\hat{\mu}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}\|\Sigma\|_{\infty},

(94)

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\|\Sigma\|_{\infty},

(95)

hold, where
\chi\coloneqq\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)}
and
\zeta\coloneqq\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}
. Eq.(
94
) and Eq. (
95
) also imply that

\displaystyle\|\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{N}-\mu)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}},

(96)

\displaystyle(1-\zeta)\Sigma\leq\hat{\Sigma}\leq(1+\zeta)\Sigma.

(97)

Proof.

The bound on the mean estimator follows from Eq. (1.1) in Ref.
Lugosi and Mendelson 2017
, using the inequality
\|\Sigma\|_{1}\leq n\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}
.
For the covariance bound, consider

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(\hat{x}_{i}-\mu)(\hat{x}_{i}-\mu)^{T}-(\hat{\mu}-\mu)(\hat{\mu}-\mu)^{T}-\Sigma\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(\hat{x}_{i}-\mu)(\hat{x}_{i}-\mu)^{T}-\Sigma\right\|_{\infty}+\|(\hat{\mu}-\mu)(\hat{\mu}-\mu)^{T}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\chi^{2}}{N}\right)\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\chi^{2}}{N}\right)\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}+\frac{\chi^{2}}{N}\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}\right)\|\Sigma\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\zeta\|\Sigma\|_{\infty},

(98)

where the inequality in the third line uses Eq. (6.12) from Ref.
Wainwright 2019
.
To prove Eq. (
97
), note that this relation is equivalent to requiring that

\displaystyle-\zeta\mathbb{1}\leq\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbb{1}\leq\zeta\mathbb{1},

(99)

which is true if and only if

\displaystyle\|\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta.

(100)

Now observe that
\Sigma^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi_{i}-\hat{\xi})(\xi_{i}-\hat{\xi})^{\intercal},

(101)

where
\xi_{i}\coloneqq\Sigma^{-1/2}(\hat{x}_{i}-\mu)
is distributed according to
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{1})

Vershynin 2018
, and
\hat{\xi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}
is the empirical mean.
Therefore,
\Sigma^{-1/2}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}
is the empirical covariance matrix for samples drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{1})
, and hence, by Eq.(
98
), the bound
\|\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta
follows. Similarly, we have that by Eq.(
94
),

\|\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{\mu}_{N}-\mu)\|_{2}=\|\hat{\xi}-0\|_{2}\leq\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}.

∎

This allows us to state our first main theorem concerning the recovery guarantees of tomography of an unknown Gaussian state via heterodyne measurements. The key observation behind this theorem is that the probability distribution resulting from a heterodyne measurement on a Gaussian state
\rho
is itself Gaussian
Serafini 2017
, with mean vector
m(\rho)/\sqrt{2}
and covariance matrix
(V(\rho)+\mathds{1})/2
, where
V(\rho)
and
m(\rho)
denote the covariance matrix and mean vector of
\rho
, respectively. Hence, by performing heterodyne measurements on the unknown Gaussian state, we can use the previous lemma to obtain a relative error estimate on the classical covariance matrix
(V(\rho)+\mathds{1})/2
. Accepting this, the remainder of the proof is dedicated to showing how such a relative error propagates to a bound on the trace distance between the true state and the reconstructed Gaussian state.

We show that performing only heterodyne measurements, without any squeezing, results in a tomography algorithm whose sample complexity scales at most quadratically with
\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}
(i.e., at most quadratically with the energy). This improves upon previous bounds for tomography using solely heterodyne measurements
Bittel et al. 2025
.

Theorem 10

((Non-adaptive Gaussian state tomography via heterodyne measurements))
.

Let
\{\hat{r}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be
N
samples obtained from a heterodyne measurement of a Gaussian state
\rho(V,m)
. Define the estimators

\displaystyle\hat{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\hat{\mu}_{h},\quad\hat{V}\coloneqq\frac{2\hat{\Sigma}_{h}}{1-\zeta}-\mathbb{1},

(102)

where
\hat{\mu}_{h}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\hat{r}_{i}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{h}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{h})(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{h})^{T}
are the empirical estimators of the heterodyne samples, and

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}\right),\quad\chi\coloneqq\sqrt{2n}+\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}.

(103)

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 4.3\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)\frac{\sqrt{2n}+\sqrt{2\log\left(2\delta^{-1}\right)}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(104)

This also implies that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, choosing

\displaystyle N>\left(\frac{4.3}{\varepsilon}\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)\left(\sqrt{2n}+\sqrt{2\log\left(2\delta^{-1}\right)}\right)\right)^{2}=O\left(\frac{nE^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\delta^{-1}\right)\right)

(105)

ensures that
\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\right\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon,
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Recall that a sample
\hat{r}
from a heterodyne measurement is distributed according to a classical Gaussian distribution
\hat{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m,\frac{V+\mathbb{1}}{2}\right)
(see the preliminary section). Thus, using Eq. (
94
) from Lemma
9
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\hat{m}-m\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}.

(106)

Moreover, by Eq. (
97
) of the same lemma, the empirical covariance matrix satisfies

\displaystyle(1-\zeta)\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\leq\hat{\Sigma}_{h}\leq(1+\zeta)\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}.

(107)

Hence, the estimator
\hat{V}\coloneqq\frac{2\hat{\Sigma}_{h}}{1-\zeta}-\mathds{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq\hat{V}-V\leq\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}(V+\mathds{1}).

(108)

This implies that
\hat{V}
is a valid covariance matrix: since
\hat{V}\geq V
and
V
is valid, it follows that
\hat{V}+i\Omega\geq V+i\Omega\geq 0
. Thus,
\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})
is a well-defined Gaussian state.
Using the perturbation bound from Theorem
8
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|V^{-1/2}(\hat{m}-m)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{4}\Tr((V^{-1}+\hat V^{-1})|\hat V-V|)

(109)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(i)}}}}{{\leq}}\|V^{-1/2}(\hat{m}-m)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\Tr(V^{-1}(\hat V - V))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(ii)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|V^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{m}-m)\right\|_{2}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\Tr(V^{-1}(V + \id))

\displaystyle\leq\left\|V^{-1/2}\left(\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\left(\frac{V+\mathds{1}}{2}\right)^{-1/2}(\hat{m}-m)\right\|_{2}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left\|\mathds{1}+V^{-1}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iii)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|V^{-1}+\mathds{1}\right\|^{1/2}_{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2}\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iv)}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|V^{-1}+\mathds{1}\right\|_{1}\frac{\sqrt{2}\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(v)}}}}{{\leq}}8.6\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right).

(110)

In step (i), we have used that
\hat{V}-V\geq 0
, which implies
\hat{V}^{-1}\leq V^{-1}
by inequality
7
, and applied the inequality in Point
3
from the list of matrix inequalities in the preliminaries. In step (ii), we invoked Eq. (
108
) and used inequality
4
from the same list. Step (iii) follows from Eq. (
106
), while step (iv) uses inequality
8
from the preliminaries. In the final step (v), we have substituted

\zeta\coloneqq\left(\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}\right)

(111)

and imposed the constraint
\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}\leq 2
; under this condition, solving the inequality for
\chi/\sqrt{N}
yields the claimed bound, which can be verified numerically.
∎

In practical scenarios, the prefactors appearing in the bounds of Theorem
10
can often be improved, leading to tighter and more refined estimates.

Remark 11

(Estimating the number of samples in an experiment)
.

The sample complexity in Theorem
10
depends on
{\mathrm{T}r}(V^{-1})
, which is generally not known a priori in experimental settings. Typically, one assumes an upper bound on the total energy,
\Tr(\rho\hat{E})\leq E
, but this is often a loose proxy for
\Tr(V^{-1})
, leading to overly conservative estimates for the required number of samples.

To address this, one can directly estimate
\Tr(V^{-1})
from heterodyne data. Given an initial batch of samples, the empirical covariance
\hat{V}
can be used to bound
\Tr(V^{-1})
via several inequalities:

\displaystyle\Tr(V^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq 4E,

(112)

\displaystyle\Tr(V^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\Tr(\hat{V}^{-1}),

(113)

\displaystyle\Tr(V^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)\Tr\left(\left(\hat{V}-\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\mathbb{1}\right)^{-1}\right),

(114)

where the first follows from the energy constraint, the second from
V^{-1}\leq\Omega V\Omega^{T}\leq\Omega\hat{V}\Omega^{T}
, and the third from transforming Eq.
108
. Note that the third bound is valid only when
\hat{V}-\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\mathbb{1}\geq 0
.
One can check whether this condition is met in practice. For this, we observe that the trace distance guarantee

\displaystyle\varepsilon\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)\left(2n+\Tr(V^{-1})\right)

(115)

requires

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{\min}(V)}{1+\sqrt{3}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{\min}(\hat{V})}{1+\sqrt{3}}.

(116)

Inserting this into Eq. (
114
), we obtain the refined bound

\displaystyle\Tr(V^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)\Tr\left(\left(\hat{V}-\frac{2\varepsilon\lambda_{\min}(\hat{V})}{1+\sqrt{3}}\mathbb{1}\right)^{-1}\right)

(117)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\right)\left(1-\frac{2\varepsilon}{1+\sqrt{3}}\right)^{-1}\Tr(\hat{V}^{-1}).

While this expression still contains the non-observable parameter
\varepsilon
, it highlights that for high-accuracy regimes (
\varepsilon\ll 1
), Eq.
114
provides an increasingly sharp estimate of
\Tr(V^{-1})
.
This reasoning naturally supports an adaptive tomography strategy: one may collect an initial batch of heterodyne data, estimate
\Tr(V^{-1})
from
\hat{V}
, and use it to refine the required sample size in subsequent rounds, thereby ensuring the target trace distance is met with high confidence.

IV
Gaussian state tomography with squeezed inputs: An adaptive and effectively energy-independent algorithm

Theorem
10
shows that Gaussian state tomography via heterodyne measurements has sample complexity scaling proportionally to
\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{2}
, which in turn grows with the energy of the state. In the following lemma, we show that this energy dependence can be effectively suppressed by adaptively reducing
\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}
.

Lemma 12

((Adaptive step to reduce the squeezing))
.

Let
V
be the covariance matrix of a Gaussian state, and
\hat{V}
its estimate as in Theorem
10
, with Williamson decomposition
\hat{V}=\hat{S}\hat{D}\hat{S}^{\intercal}
. Define

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{new}}\coloneqq\hat{S}^{-1}V\hat{S}^{-\intercal}.

(118)

Then,

\displaystyle\|V_{\mathrm{new}}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}(\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}+1)}.

(119)

In particular, setting
\zeta\leq\frac{1}{4}
yields
\|V_{\mathrm{new}}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{5}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}}
with at least
1-\delta
probability.

IV.1
Measurement scheme using only passive operations and squeezed inputs

Algorithm
2
requires applying an active Gaussian operation to the
input state, followed by heterodyne detection. However, active Gaussian unitaries are often challenging to implement in practice in optical experiments. Fortunately, the combined effect of such an active transformation followed by heterodyne measurement can be exactly reproduced using only passive Gaussian operations together with a fixed auxiliary squeezed input state—both of which are experimentally accessible with current photonic technologies.

This alternative scheme leverages the well-established equivalence between heterodyne detection and beam-splitter interference followed by homodyne measurements. As reviewed in the preliminaries, a heterodyne measurement can be implemented by sending the state to be measured into one port of a balanced beam splitter, with a vacuum state entering the other port, and then performing homodyne measurements of complementary quadratures—typically position on one output
arm and momentum on the other. In the variant we consider, the vacuum input is replaced with a suitably chosen squeezed state. This substitution enables one to simulate the effect of any active Gaussian unitary followed by heterodyne detection using only passive elements and an auxiliary squeezed input state.

The following proposition makes this equivalence precise and provides a detailed description of the resulting measurement scheme (see also the main figure in the text).

((Passive implementation of heterodyne after active Gaussian unitary))

Gaussian state tomography with access to the transposed state: A fully energy-independent protocol

The tomography protocol introduced in the previous section achieves a sample complexity that depends only very mildly on the total energy of the state—specifically, doubly logarithmically on
\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}
. In all physically relevant scenarios, this dependence is negligible, and the protocol can be considered nearly energy-independent.

However, if access to the transpose
\rho^{T}
of the unknown state
\rho

\|V^{-1}\|_{\infty}

Lemma 16

((Scheme with access to the transpose))

\displaystyle V(\rho)\cong\begin{pmatrix}V_{x,x}&V_{x,p}\\

\rho^{T}

\displaystyle V(\rho^{T})\cong\begin{pmatrix}V_{x,x}&-V_{x,p}\\

and all

x_{j}p_{i}

\displaystyle\Tr(x_j p_i \rho)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((x_{j}p_{i}\rho)^{T}\right)=\Tr\left(p_{i}^{T}x_{j}^{T}\rho^{T}\right)=\Tr\left((-p_{i})x_{j}\rho^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Tr(x_j p_i \rho^T),

(152)

demonstrating that the cross-correlations between position and momentum operators acquire a minus sign under transposition, while

and

remain unchanged.

Equivalently, the full covariance matrix of

(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)m(\rho)\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and covariance matrix}\quad\begin{pmatrix}V(\rho)&0\\
0&(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)V(\rho)(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)\end{pmatrix}.

(153)

\rho

\rho^{T}

\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is distributed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(m(\rho)+(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)m(\rho^{T}),\,\frac{V(\rho)+(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)V(\rho)(\mathbb{1}_{n}\otimes Z)}{2}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(2m(\rho),\,V(\rho)\right).

(154)

∎

Theorem 17

((Energy-independent Gaussian state tomography with access to the transposed state))
.

Let
\rho\coloneqq\rho(V,m)
be an unknown
n
-mode Gaussian state with first-moment vector
m\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and covariance matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
.

Let
\{\hat{r}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be
N
i.i.d. samples obtained from a generalized heterodyne measurement on the state
\rho(V,m)\otimes\rho(V,m)^{T}
, following the scheme described in Lemma
16
. Define the estimators

\displaystyle\hat{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\,\frac{\hat{\mu}_{h}}{2},\quad\hat{V}\coloneqq\frac{\hat{\Sigma}_{h}}{1-\zeta},

(155)

where
\hat{\mu}_{h}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\hat{r}_{i}
, and

\hat{\Sigma}_{h}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{h})(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{h})^{\top}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}\leq 8.55n\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}},

(156)

where
\zeta\coloneqq\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}
and
\chi\coloneqq\sqrt{2n}+\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, it suffices to choose
N=O\left(\frac{n^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\delta^{-1}\right)\right)
in order to guarantee that
\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps to Theorem
10
(but is simpler). By Lemma
16
, a sample
\hat{r}
obtained from homodyne measurements on
\rho\otimes\rho^{T}
after applying a beam splitter is distributed as

\hat{r}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(2m,V\right).

(157)

Using Lemma
9
, Eq. (
94
), we can build an estimator
2\hat{m}
for
2m
, such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|V^{-1/2}(2\hat{m}-2m)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}.

(158)

In addition, by Eq. (
97
), the empirical covariance
\hat{V}
satisfies

\displaystyle(1-\zeta)V\leq\hat{\Sigma}_{h}\leq(1+\zeta)V,

(159)

where
\zeta\coloneqq\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\chi^{2}}{N}
.
Hence, the estimator
\hat{V}\coloneqq\frac{\hat{\Sigma}_{h}}{1-\zeta}
satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq\hat{V}-V\leq\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}V.

(160)

Thus,
\hat{V}
is a valid covariance matrix.
Applying the perturbation bound from Theorem
8
, we now obtain

\displaystyle\|\rho(\hat{V},\hat{m})-\rho(V,m)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|V^{-1/2}(\hat{m}-m)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{4}\Tr((V^{-1}+\hat V^{-1})(\hat{V} - V))

(161)

\displaystyle\leq\|V^{-1/2}(\hat{m}-m)\|_{2}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\Tr(V^{-1}(\hat{V} - V))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\times\frac{2\zeta}{1-\zeta}\Tr(V^{-1}V)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}}+2n(1+\sqrt{3})\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}

\displaystyle\leq 17.1n\frac{\chi}{\sqrt{N}},

where in the last step we used a numerical solver (along with the fact that the one-norm difference between two quantum states is always
\leq 2
).

∎
