Educational and clinical training gaps in dental implantology among undergraduate dental students: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdulkarem Ali Mohammed, Baleegh Abdulraoof Alkadasi, Sadeq Altayyar, Zakarya Al-Muaalemi, Omer Abd Al-Aziz Al-Wahbi, Ghadeer Khoshafa, Anas AL-Zaghruri, Ammar Al-Hiagem, Otbah Al-Huthaifi, Hanan Abo-Alrejal, Ahmed Yaseen Alqutaibi, Redhwan Saleh Al-Gabri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental implantology is increasingly essential in contemporary dental practice, yet its integration into undergraduate curricula remains inconsistent—particularly in low-resource settings. This is the first multicenter study in Yemen to evaluate dental implant–related education among undergraduate dental students across five universities. The aim was to assess students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding dental implants and to identify gaps in theoretical instruction and clinical training.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among 721 third-, fourth-, and fifth-year dental students from five Yemeni universities. A validated 33-item close-ended questionnaire collected data on demographics, implant-related education and awareness, knowledge of materials and techniques, attitudes toward treatment choices, cost perception, oral hygiene, complication awareness, and implant practices. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0, applying chi-square and logistic regression analyses to determine associations, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAwareness of dental implants was high (97.4%), yet 63.1% had not studied implantology formally and only 7.4% attended implant courses. Knowledge of key concepts was limited but improved with educational level (p < 0.001), while practical exposure was minimal (9%). Self-perceived (not objectively assessed) competence to place implants was 62.6%, and 87.4% sought further training. Educational level was significantly associated with learning methods, implantation methods, and number of implants placed, but not with gender or awareness of complications.ConclusionsThis multicenter survey revealed high awareness but insufficient theoretical and clinical training in implant dentistry among Yemeni undergraduates. Integrating competency-based modules with early simulation, structured supervision, and faculty development is recommended to align education with international standards in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08354-6.

Background

With increasing life expectancy, there has been a notable shift from complete to partial tooth loss, leading to a growing population of partially dentate adults [1]. Tooth loss affects not only oral function and aesthetics but also compromises mastication, speech, facial support, and psychological well-being. While traditional options such as removable and fixed partial prostheses are still used, dental implants have emerged as a preferred solution, offering superior long-term stability, functionality, and patient satisfaction [2].

As dental implantology continues to expand and gain widespread acceptance, undergraduate education must keep pace. Given the high success rates of implant-supported prostheses and growing patient demand, dental students are expected to graduate with a solid understanding and basic clinical skills in implant therapy [3].

However, despite its significance, limited research has explored students’ perspectives and preparedness in this area [4]. The integration of implantology into dental curricula remains inconsistent, with notable variations in teaching approaches—often limited to theoretical instruction with minimal hands-on experience. These disparities can lead to differences in competence and confidence among graduates. Therefore, evaluating students’ knowledge, attitudes, and clinical exposure is essential to identifying educational gaps and guiding curricular enhancements that promote evidence-based, patient-centered care [3, 5–7].

Several studies suggest that knowledge gaps still persist among dental students regarding the principles of implant placement, osseointegration, and post-operative maintenance. A significant proportion of students may lack practical exposure to implant cases, resulting in limited confidence and preparedness for implant-related clinical decisions [3, 5, 6, 8–11].

In resource-limited countries like Yemen, where oral rehabilitation needs are high, evaluating dental education in implantology is essential. Cultural, financial, and institutional barriers often limit students’ access to comprehensive implant training, affecting their clinical readiness and future engagement with implant therapy. Despite the growing importance of dental implants, data on the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of Yemeni dental students remain scarce. The aim of this study was to assess undergraduate dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding dental implants across five Yemeni universities and to identify perceived gaps in theoretical instruction and clinical training that may inform future curricular improvement.

Materials and methods

Study design, sampling and eligibility

This observational, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted from August 2024 to April 2025 across five dental institutions in Yemen: Ibb University, National University (Ibb and Taiz branches), Al Ataa University, and Al-Saeed University (Fig. 1). The target population was undergraduate dental students in the 3rd–5th years. The sampling frame comprised all students enrolled in these clinical years at the participating universities. Consecutive sampling was used: all students present at the time of the survey and providing informed consent were invited to participate. Exclusions included interns and postgraduate students, students not present during data collection and those who declined consent. Questionnaires with multiple responses or substantial incompleteness were excluded from analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the College of Dentistry, Ethics Committee, Taibah University, Al Madinah, Saudi Arabia (No. TUCDREC/070927), and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (1964 and later amendments). This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines.Fig. 1Map of Yemen showing the geographic distribution of participating and non-participating dental institutions in Ibb and Taiz cities

Participant recruitment

Undergraduate dental students from the five participating universities were invited to participate after obtaining approval from their respective deans or course coordinators. The study’s objectives were explained, and informed consent was obtained. Questionnaires were distributed during lectures and clinical sessions in printed form. Participation was voluntary, with no impact on students’ grades or evaluations, and anonymity and confidentiality were maintained. Students could withdraw at any stage without consequence.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the Single Population Proportion Formula: n = Z²×p(1 − p)/d²; Where n is the required sample size, Z is the standard normal variate corresponding to a 99% confidence interval (Z = 2.56), p is the estimated proportion of the population based on a previous study [10] (p = 0.42), and d is the margin of error (d = 0.05). Thus, the minimum required sample size was 639 participants to achieve statistical significance with 99% confidence and a 5% margin of error. To account for a possible 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was increased to 703 participants.

Questionnaire development and validation

A 33-item, self-administered, close-ended questionnaire was designed based on previously published literature on dental implant education and knowledge, attitude, and practice studies [6–8, 12, 13]. Content validity was confirmed by a panel of three prosthodontists and implantologists from Ibb University and Taibah University. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (0.84), indicating good reliability. A pilot study with 20 students from the same sampling frame was conducted to test clarity, understanding, and applicability of the questionnaire. Feedback from the pilot led to minor wording adjustments; pilot responses were excluded from the final analysis.

Questionnaire structure

The questionnaire was divided into four main sections (Supplementary Table 1):

Data collection

Completed paper-based questionnaires were collected and reviewed for completeness by the research team. All responses were manually entered into a password-protected electronic database, accessible only to the investigators. To ensure confidentiality, no identifying information such as names or student IDs was recorded. Data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes. Anonymity and privacy were maintained throughout the study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests assessed associations between knowledge, attitude, and practice variables and demographic factors (age, gender, academic year), with significance set at p < 0.05. Multivariable analyses were conducted to identify independent predictors: an ordinary least squares (OLS) model examined factors influencing knowledge scores, and a binary logistic regression identified predictors of willingness to offer implant therapy, using knowledge score, sex, age, academic level, and institution as covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study analyzed 721 undergraduate dental students with a response rate of 112.8% (Supplementary Table 2). Females comprised 60.3% of participants (p < 0.05), 80.3% were > 21 years, and 39.5% were in the final (5th) year. There was no significant association between education level and gender (p > 0.05), (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Knowledge and attitude toward dental implants

Educational level, implant education, awareness of dental implants, learning methods and continuing education courses

Most students had not yet studied implants (63.1%), while those who had were predominantly in the 5th year (27%). Awareness was high (97.4%). The primary learning mode was theoretical lectures (46.5%) (P < 0.05), with very limited practical exposure (laboratory 3.2%, surgical assisting 1.8%). Only 7.4% reported attending an implant course. Knowledge patterns varied significantly by age group and education level (p < 0.05), (Table 2; Fig. 3A–B).

Knowledge of implant materials, implantation methods, implant body designs, surface modifications, Brånemark’s theory of osseointegration

Half of students correctly identified titanium as the primary implant material (49.5%), while 31.2% did not know. Endosteal was the most recognized implantation method (56.9%), yet familiarity with implant body designs and surface modifications was limited (e.g., “don’t know” 40.9% and 73.6%, respectively). Awareness of Brånemark’s Theory of Osseointegration was low (16.1%). Knowledge increased with educational level for implantation methods (p < 0.05), but substantial gaps persisted in advanced topics (Table 3; Fig. 3C).

Knowledge and attitude toward factors influencing implant success and recommendations for edentulous patients

Most students (85%) recognized that multiple factors determine implant success, yet only 2.6% identified case selection as the single most critical determinant. A majority (61.6%) perceived implants to have advantages over fixed prostheses (e.g., conservation, longevity, aesthetics). Recommendations to edentulous patients were reported by 56.9% of respondents; among non-recommenders (43.1%), the main barriers were total treatment time (60%) and cost (24.4%), (Table 4).

Practices in dental implants

Only 9% of students had supervised placed implants (most performed 1–3 cases: 81.5%), and the two-stage protocol predominated (75.4% among those who placed implants). The number of implants placed increased with education level (p < 0.05). Despite limited experience, 62.6% felt competent to place implants in future practice and 81.7% intended to offer implant therapy. Among those not intending to offer implants (18.3%), reasons included uncertainty (50%), lack of interest (29.5%), and insufficient confidence (12.1%). A strong training demand was evident: 87.4% wanted courses, and 66% prioritized combined lectures, laboratory, and clinical training (Table 6; Fig. 4).

Results from the multivariable analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Among the 721 respondents, the mean knowledge score was 1.97 ± 0.58 and 81.7% indicated that they would offer implant therapy in their future practice. In the multivariable linear regression, male students had significantly lower knowledge scores than females (β = −0.14, p < 0.05), and students older than 21 years also scored lower (β = −0.17, p < 0.05). Academic level and university affiliation were not significant predictors. In the multivariable logistic regression, the knowledge score was negatively associated with willingness to offer implant therapy (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.37–0.75, p < 0.05), indicating that each additional point in knowledge score corresponded to a 48% reduction in the odds of intending to provide implant therapy. No significant associations were found for sex, age, academic level, or university after adjustment.

Discussion

Awareness of dental implants among undergraduates is generally high across multiple settings—reported by Makkar et al. [14], Saad and Salem et al. [7], Swapna et al. [13], Saxena et al. [15], Chaudhary et al. [16], Sharma et al. [8], AlHemrani et al. [6], Ali et al. [10], Mahdi [9], and Goel et al. [11]—although depth of knowledge and hands-on exposure often remain limited. Despite the highly success rate of implant-supported prostheses over removable prostheses options, it remains underrepresented in undergraduate curricula, leading to limited student knowledge [14]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in Yemen to evaluate the dental education related to implant dentistry across five dental institutions.

A total of 721 students participated in the survey, with 97.4% aware of dental implants as a treatment option. This finding is consistent with previous studies by Makkar et al. [14], Swapna et al. [13], and Siddiqi et al. [17]. which also demonstrated high awareness levels among undergraduate dental students. Female students represented 60.3% of participants, reflecting the higher enrollment of women in dental programs—a trend similarly observed in earlier research by AlHemrani et al. [6], Sharma et al. [8], Chaudhary et al. [16], and Swapna et al. [13].

In spite of high overall awareness, 63.1% of students had not formally studied dental implants, and only 7.4% had attended implant-related courses. Most learning occurred through lectures (46.5%), while hands-on experience—such as surgical assistance (1.8%) and lab training (3.2%)—was minimal. Similar gaps in practical exposure have been reported in comparable settings, suggesting insufficient experiential learning. These findings support targeted curricular integration—earlier preclinical simulation and supervised clinical opportunities—to strengthen competence and confidence.

Significant associations were found between students’ knowledge of implantation methods and their educational level, with awareness increasing from the 3rd to 5th year. This finding aligns with previous studies by Saxena et al. [15] and Swapna et al. [13], which reported that final-year students demonstrated a better understanding of implant concepts, likely due to greater exposure to theoretical knowledge and clinical implant-related subjects. Still, low awareness of advanced topics reveals curriculum gaps, emphasizing the need for enhanced lectures, labs, and clinical training to meet global standards.

In this study, only 49.5% of Yemeni dental students correctly identified titanium as the primary implant material, while 31.2% lacked knowledge regarding implant materials. This percentage is considerably lower than the findings of Swapna et al. [13] and Puri et al. [18], who reported awareness levels of 92–93% among undergraduate dental students in their respective studies. Awareness of implant body designs and surface modifications was also limited, with just 31.1% recognizing screw-type implants and 14% aware of surface treatments. These findings suggest that Yemeni students may not be receiving adequate theoretical and practical exposure to implantology in their dental curriculum compared to peers in other countries, highlighting the need for structured enhancements in implant education.

Furthermore, only 16.1% of students were aware of Brånemark’s Theory of Osseointegration, which aligns with the findings of Swapna et al. [13], who also reported low awareness levels that gradually improved with higher educational advancement. In this study, although 85% of students recognized that multiple factors influence the success of dental implants, only 2.6% identified case selection as the most critical determinant. This finding is considerably lower than the results reported by Swapna et al. [13], where 48.1% of respondents highlighted case selection, and by Sharma et al. [8], who found 25.1% awareness among undergraduate students. This gap suggests that Yemeni undergraduates may lack deeper clinical insight into the complexities of treatment planning, possibly due to limited exposure to clinical implant cases during their training. As literature and global consensus emphasize case selection as a cornerstone for long-term success [13, 19–21], integrating this concept more thoroughly into the curriculum is essential.

Additionally, this study revealed that 17.2% of students viewed dental implants as a more conservative treatment option compared with fixed prostheses, consistent with the findings of AlHemrani et al. [6], Chaudhary et al. [16], and Jivraj and Chee [22]. Moreover, 61.6% of participants acknowledged that multiple factors influence the perceived advantages of implant therapy. Despite this, only 56.9% of students reported recommending dental implants to edentulous patients, with 43.1% citing cost and treatment duration as key barriers. These findings are comparable to those reported in other low-resource settings by Afridi and Ehsan [23], Sohail et al. [24], and Kumar and Chauhan [25], where economic constraints and lengthy treatment times similarly limited patient acceptance of implant therapy. These results underscore the need for enhanced education on the long-term benefits of implants, as well as broader initiatives to improve affordability and public awareness.

In this study, 95% of participants expressed that dental implants are too expensive for most patients, and 93.5% agreed that a patient’s financial status affects treatment choice. These results highlight the need to teach students how to communicate the long-term value of implants effectively. Furthermore, 94% of respondents agreed that implants require more oral hygiene care than natural teeth, consistent with the findings of AlHemrani et al. [6], Saxena et al. [15], Sharma et al. [26], and Tepper et al. [27], which collectively underscore the need for continuous maintenance by both patients and clinicians to ensure long-term implant success. Most participants (91.3%) supported a multi-disciplinary approach, and 85.3% recognized the possibility of technical and biological complications, reflecting good awareness but also a need for more clinical training. However, there was no significant relationship regarding knowledge of these complications across the different education levels.

Aligned with established undergraduate implant-education frameworks—specifically the American Dental Education Association (ADEA) predoctoral report [28], Association for Dental Education in Europe (ADEE)/European program reporting [29], and United States curricular descriptions [30]—our findings support competency-based outcomes, early preclinical simulation, and supervised clinical exposure with quality assurance via Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) and case logs. In our study, only 9% had supervised placed implants—lower than programs with structured training—where supervised undergraduate series report 92.2–98.8% success with adequate preclinical preparation [30–33].

The multivariable analyses revealed an inverse association between implant-related knowledge and willingness to offer implant therapy. This may indicate that students with higher theoretical understanding are more aware of the clinical and financial complexities of implant rehabilitation, leading to greater caution and reduced immediate confidence. Similar findings were reported by Sharma et al. [8], Al-Abdaly et al. [3], and Saad and Salem [7]. Female and younger students showed slightly higher knowledge scores, possibly reflecting stronger engagement with coursework. The absence of significant institutional or academic-level differences suggests similar implant education content across universities. These results underscore the need for balanced theoretical and practical training to enhance both competence and confidence in implant therapy.

Limited experience in Yemen likely reflects resource constraints (equipment, faculty) and restricted clinical opportunities. Despite this, 62.6% reported self-perceived competence and 87.4% expressed interest in further training—consistent with Siddiqi et al. [17] and Kumar and Chauhan [25]; by contrast, educator surveys [28, 29] noted reservations about adding undergraduate implant training due to time and preparedness concerns. The strong demand for further training (87.4%) supports a structured pathway combining lectures, laboratory simulation, and supervised clinics to bridge the knowledge–skills gap.

Compared to previous studies, our findings indicate that the overall knowledge and clinical exposure to implant dentistry among Yemeni undergraduate students remains limited. This gap is largely due to minimal integration of implantology in the current curriculum. To address this, incorporating structured implant education, both theoretical and practical, early in the dental program is essential to better prepare graduates for modern clinical demands.

This study was limited to five institutions in Ibb and Taiz, which may constrain generalizability to all Yemeni dental schools. The clustered design may have introduced intra-group similarities. Data were self-reported, subject to response bias, and collected via a quantitative-only design that may not capture deeper perspectives. Limited hands-on implant training among participants could have influenced responses. The questionnaire was administered in English; despite expert review and acceptable internal consistency, a non-native language may have introduced comprehension bias and deterred some participants. Because the same self-perception instrument was used across 3rd–5th years to capture baseline awareness, educational-level heterogeneity may introduce measurement bias; findings should be interpreted as perceptions rather than objective competence.

Conclusions

This multicenter survey shows high awareness but inadequate theoretical depth and limited clinical exposure in implant dentistry among Yemeni undergraduates. We recommend integrating competency-based implant modules from the 3rd year, introducing simulation/lab training before patient care, and providing structured, staged, supervised training with minimum case-log requirements. Strengthening faculty development and essential infrastructure, fostering inter-university partnerships and continuing professional development, and monitoring outcomes via key performance indicators (e.g., objective structured clinical examination performance, case numbers, confidence scores) can feasibly align dental education with international standards in resource-limited settings.
