Tumor immune microenvironment score predicts efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors-based regimens in advanced non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jiacheng Dai, Huan Yan, Yangqian Chen, Yuda Zhang, Zhe Huang, Zhaohui Ruan, Fang Tian, Haoyue Qin, Qinqin Xu, Jin Wang, Xing Li, Peng Cheng, Changbin Zhu, Nong Yang, Liang Zeng, Yongchang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based regimens have become the standard first-line treatment for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, response rates vary widely, emphasizing the need for a predictive model to optimize patient selection and improve treatment outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 96 treatment-naïve patients with advanced NSCLC who received first-line ICI-based regimens (anti–PD-1 ± platinum chemotherapy) at Hunan Cancer Hospital. Pre-treatment tumor biopsies underwent bulk RNA sequencing to identify differentially expressed genes associated with progression-free survival (PFS). A five-gene Cox proportional hazards model, the Prediction model of Immunotherapy Efficacy (PIE), was developed and validated in two independent cohorts: ORIENT-11 (n = 113; chemo-ICI) and OAK (n = 344; ICI monotherapy). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining (CD8, CD56, CD16, Pan-CK, PD-L1, MAGEA2) was performed to validate immune-cell infiltration patterns and candidate biomarkers across PIE-defined risk groups.ResultsPatients in the long-survival group exhibited enrichment of IL7R+ NK cells, SLC4A10+ CD8+ T cells, and dendritic cells, which were significantly associated with longer PFS. PIE integrated transcriptomic signatures of five biomarkers (CD274, KRT14, FOLR2, SLC31A2, and EFCAB14) to predicting PFS. Compared with patients having low-risk PIE scores, those classified as high-risk demonstrated significantly worse PFS (HR = 2.37, p < 0.001). PIE demonstrated predictive accuracy for 24-month PFS (AUC = 0.768). Additionally, MAGEA2 and MAGEA12 were identified as potential therapeutic targets.ConclusionPIE represents a clinically relevant, transcriptome-based predictive model for evaluating the benefit of ICI treatment in advanced NSCLC. With further prospective validation, PIE may facilitate personalized patient selection and guide the development of novel strategies to overcome primary resistance.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07408-z.Keywords: NSCLC, Immune checkpoint inhibitor, PFS, Prognostic prediction

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide in 2022 [1]. Its pathological classification is complex, encompassing non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which primarily comprises squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, as well as small cell lung cancer—each contributing to a diverse and intricate therapeutic landscape [2]. Beyond targeted therapies directed at oncogenic driver mutations, monoclonal antibodies targeting immune checkpoints, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1, have demonstrated durable clinical responses and substantial overall survival benefits in patients with advanced NSCLC lacking actionable driver mutations [3]. Consequently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been rapidly integrated into the standard treatment paradigm for NSCLC, establishing itself as a cornerstone of modern clinical management [4, 5].

Although ICIs elicit profound and durable responses in many patients with NSCLC, strategies to circumvent or reverse ICI resistance remain elusive. The global multicenter phase III trials KEYNOTE-189 and KEYNOTE-407 revealed that the long-term survival rates of patients with squamous or metastatic NSCLC receiving pembrolizumab (a PD-1 monoclonal antibody) combined with platinum-based chemotherapy exceeded those of patients receiving chemotherapy alone [4, 5]. These findings positioned immunochemotherapy as the standard first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC. However, ICIs do not yield uniform efficacy across patients—over 20% exhibit primary resistance and fail to respond to therapy [6], while more than half of those who initially respond eventually experience disease progression [7]. Thus, while ICIs represent a major breakthrough in the treatment of advanced NSCLC, further investigations into mechanisms of drug resistance and optimization of biomarker-based patient selection are necessary to expand the beneficiary population.

Current clinical research focuses on identifying predictive biomarkers capable of refining patient selection for ICI therapy and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Previous studies have established tumor PD-L1 expression as a critical determinant of response to anti–PD-(L)1 antibodies [8–10]; however, recent clinical trials have demonstrated that patients with PD-L1-negative tumors can also achieve significant clinical benefit [11–14]. The CheckMate-227 trial suggested that a tumor mutational burden (TMB) exceeding 10 mutations per megabase (mut/Mb) is a promising predictor of response to ICI therapy compared with PD-L1 expression [15, 16]. Nevertheless, a subsequent phase III trial revealed that patients with a TMB below 10 mut/Mb also derive benefit from PD-1 inhibition, highlighting the limitations of TMB as a definitive biomarker for patient selection [17].

Beyond PD-L1 expression and TMB, biomarkers reflecting the tumor microenvironment have garnered increased attention [3]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) have been associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy [10, 18], with a dormant TIL signature—characterized by high CD3, high Ki67, and low granzyme B expression—associated with improved overall survival (OS) following immunotherapy [19]. Sun et al. proposed a tumor immune microenvironment (TME) classification model based on RNA-seq data derived from a large-scale randomized controlled trial ORIENT-11 [20], demonstrating patients with advanced NSCLC exhibiting both high PD-L1 expression and high immune infiltration derive superior benefits from first-line immunochemotherapy. In addition to the established role of T cells in direct tumor eradication [21], natural killer (NK) cells [22] and dendritic cells [23] (DCs) have also emerged as key players in anti-tumor immunity. Notably, the anti-tumor functions of these immune cell populations are interconnected, as tumor antigen presentation by DCs is essential for the priming of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes [24]. Consequently, the expression profiles of specific immune checkpoints, lymphocyte populations, or immune-cell subsets hold significant clinical utility for guiding biomarker-driven treatment approaches [25–27].

To address the challenge of identifying reliable biomarkers predictive of ICI-based treatment efficacy in advanced NSCLC, we retrospectively analyzed clinical and pre-treatment RNA-seq data from 96 treatment-naïve patients with advanced NSCLC who received first-line ICI-based regimens at Hunan Cancer Hospital. We compared the gene expression profiles between patients demonstrating long-term benefit from ICI and those with limited response to ICI therapy. Next, we developed a Prediction model of Immunotherapy Efficacy for NSCLC (PIE), incorporating biomarkers derived from a retrospective clinical trial and RNA-seq data. PIE enables more accurate identification of patients with advanced NSCLC who are likely to achieve durable benefit from ICI therapy. By integrating high-dimensional transcriptomic data within a clinically validated framework, this model represents a promising tool for improving patient stratification and advancing personalized immunotherapy strategies in NSCLC management.

Methods

Patient cohorts and sample collection

A clinical cohort of patients diagnosed with NSCLC who received first-line ICI therapy with or without chemotherapy at Hunan Cancer Hospital (HCH) was retrospectively analyzed. The study included 96 patients treated between October 2017 and March 2022. Comprehensive clinical data were collected for each patient, including age, sex, smoking status, disease stage, pathological classification, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, treatment regimen administered (ICI or ICI plus chemotherapy), and treatment outcomes. Tumor PD-L1 expression was assessed using the tumor proportion score (TPS), which quantifies the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting membranous PD-L1 staining at any intensity. Patients received either sintilimab or pembrolizumab, with some receiving chemotherapy according to their pathological subtype. Patients with a PFS less than 6 months were classified as the short-survival group (n = 45), whereas patients with PFS longer than 12 months were categorized as the long-survival group (n = 28). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue samples were collected from lung biopsy specimens prior to treatment and obtained from the Pathology Department of HCH. Sample collection adhered to the protocols of the HCH Institutional Review Board (protocol KY7022287), approved by the HCH Medical Ethics Committee, with all patients providing written informed consent for participation.

To validate our findings, we leveraged publicly available RNA-seq data from two independent clinical cohorts. Cohort 1 consisted of patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD; n = 113) who received sintilimab plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment in the phase III ORIENT-11 trial [28]. Cohort 2 included patients with NSCLC (n = 344) treated with atezolizumab in the OAK cohort (EGAS00001005013) [29]. Transcript-per-million (TPM) normalized RNA-seq data were obtained from the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA) (https://ega-archive.org/).

RNA extraction and RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE tissue blocks using the AmoyDx FFPE RNA Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, Xiamen, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA HS Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA fragmentation was performed at 95 °C for 0–15 minutes, guided by the DV200 value from the Bioanalyzer. Fragmented RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), and strand-specific libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Library sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

RNA-seq data preprocessing

Quality control of RNA-seq data was performed using FastQC software [30]. Next, Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic [31]. Sequence alignment to the reference genome (Homo sapiens assembly GRCh38) was conducted using HISAT2 [32], and read quantification was performed using featureCounts to calculate the TPM for downstream analyses [33].

Differential gene expression analysis

TPM matrices were log2-transformed and quantile-normalized using the preprocessCore package (v1.56.0). Covariates, including sex, age, smoking, and ECOG performance status (PS) score, were controlled using linear regression. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the short-survival and long-survival groups were identified using a linear mixed-effects model implemented in the nlme package. Multiple testing was conducted using FDR. Statistical significance was defined as an nominal p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 0.5.

Gene set variation analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2) [34], with statistical significance defined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA package (v1.42.0) to evaluate functional term enrichment across samples. Gene sets for these analyses were obtained from various knowledge bases in MSigDB, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Hallmark, and Reactome [35, 36].

TME-related signatures were assessed using the ssGSEA method, including functional gene expression signatures and immune-related gene sets [37, 38]. General immune cell classification was referenced from PanglaoDB [39], with immune cell subclassification informed by single-cell annotations of NK cells [40], CD8+ T cells [41], and DCs [23, 42]. Bulk RNA sequencing data enabled further classification of NK cells, CD8+ T cells, and myeloid cells into 14, 7, and 14 subtypes, respectively.

Enrichment analysis in the short-survival group utilized previously reported 28 gene sets [43], following the same methodology as TME signature estimation.

Model construction and validation

A predictive model was developed through a series of analyses: (i) Cox proportional hazards models identified PFS-associated genes; (ii) candidate genes were selected by identifying overlaps between DEGs (short vs long survival groups), PFS-related genes, and immune-related genes (from immune cells in PanglaoDB) [39]; (iii) candidate genes were incorporated into a five-fold cross-validated Cox proportional hazards models, with significant predictors comprising the final predictive model. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) analysis via the pROC package. [44] Discriminative performance was evaluated using AUC and concordance index (C-index) across training and testing datasets.

Multiplex immunofluorescence and quantification

FFPE tumor tissue slides (5 μm) were processed for immunofluorescence. Slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100, and subjected to Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval. Blocked was performed using 5% normal donkey serum. Primary antibodies included CD8 (Cat#70306S, Cell Signaling Technology), CD56 (Cat#MAB-0743, MXB Biotechnologies), CD16 (Cat#16559–1-AP, Proteintech), Pan-CK (Cat#ab7753, Abcam), MAGEA2 (Cat#sc-20034, Santa Cruz), PD-L1 (Cat#ab237726, Abcam), and DAPI (Cat#ab104139, Abcam). Imaging was conducted using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. Quantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence-stained images was performed using the HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA). For each sample, more than three representative regions were selected, and the mean fluorescence intensity was calculated to determine the average expression level.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and proportions (%). Associations between clinical characteristics and survival outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models via the survival package. Kaplan-Meier survival curves illustrated survival differences across subgroups. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software (v4.4.2), with a two-sided p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Independent NSCLC tissue samples from the short survival group (n = 5) and long survival group (n = 5) were selected. Each sample consist of six FFPE slides. Total RNA was extracted from NSCLC FFPE tissues with RNAprep Pure FFPE Kit (DP439, TIANGEN, China). cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (R312-01, Vazyme, China) to remove genomic DNA contamination. RT-qPCR was conducted using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q511-02, Vazyme, China) on the ABI-7500 platform (Applied Biosystems). β-actin was used as the internal control. Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary table 1.

Results

Patient characteristics

To identify potential biomarkers predictive of ICI benefit, we retrospectively analyzed data from 96 patients with NSCLC who received first-line ICI therapy with or without chemotherapy at HCH (Fig. 1A). Detailed baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The average patient age was 63 years, with the majority diagnosed at stage IV (93.8%), smokers (82.3%), and male (85.4%). PD-L1 TPS assessments showed that 68% of patients exhibited TPS < 1%, while 14.6% and 10.4% had TPS values 1–49% and ≥50%, respectively. All patients received ICI therapy, where 25 patients received ICI alone and 71 patients received ICI plus chemotherapy. Tumor biopsies were collected prior to treatment, followed by bulk RNA sequencing and computational transcriptomic analysis. We subsequently developed and validated a predictive model using our cohort and publicly available datasets, with additional validation conducted using multiplex immunofluorescence (Fig. 1A). Baseline clinical characteristics were summarized in Table S2. Patients were stratified based on PFS into three groups: short-survival (PFS < 6 months, n = 45), moderate-survival (PFS 6–12 months, n = 28), and long-survival (PFS > 12 months, n = 23) (Figure S1). The median PFS was 6.5 months. Clinical characteristics remained balanced across the three survival groups (Fig. 1B; Table 1).

Enrichment of immune-related genes in the long-survival group

We first examined differential gene expression between the short- and long-survival groups. Among patients receiving ICIs, we identified 858 DEGs, of which 497 were significantly upregulated in the long-survival group (log2fold change > 0.5 and p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A; Table S3). For patients receiving ICI plus chemotherapy, 372 DEGs were detected, with 128 genes significantly upregulated in the long-survival group (log2fold change > 0.5 and p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B; Table S4). Notably, immune-related genes, including FCER1G, BMP4, TLR4, GSN, and PDE5A, were predominantly upregulated in long-survival group. Contrastingly, genes associated with carcinogenesis, such as KAT2A, EFNB1, CCNB2, and ANXA3, were enriched in the short-survival group (Fig. 2B). Immune cell deconvolution analysis revealed a higher abundance of immune cell subsets, including T cells [41], NK cells [40], and DCs [23, 42], in the long-survival group compared with the short-survival group (Figure S2).

To evaluate the prognostic impact of immune cell subtypes, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, identifying the proportion of IL7R+ NK cell as significantly associated with PFS (Fig. 2C). Additionally, SLC4A10+ T cells and hDC1 (human DC1) showed potential PFS associations (p = 0.058 and p = 0.099, respectively) (Fig. 2D–E). Further stratification of patients into high- and low-expression groups revealed that lower IL7R+ NK cell abundance was significantly associated with poorer PFS (p = 0.032) (Fig. 2F), whereas lower expression of SLC4A10+ T cells and hDC1 exhibited a trend toward decreased PFS, although statistical significance was not reached (p = 0.091 and p = 0.129, respectively) (Fig. 2G–H). Collectively, these findings indicate that baseline tumor tissue from patients in the long-survival group is enriched with immune cell populations, supporting the development of an immune-based predictive model using these biomarkers.

Construction of the predictive model

To establish a robust predictive tool for ICI efficacy, we developed the Prediction model of Immunotherapy Efficacy (PIE) using the following strategy (Fig. 3A). First, we identified 387 PFS-associated genes (p < 0.05). Candidate transcriptional biomarkers were selected by identifying overlap among PFS-related genes, DEGs (short vs long survival groups), and immune-related genes (from the PanglaoDB immune cell gene signatures [39]). Five overlapped genes (CD274, KRT14, FOLR2, SLC31A2, and EFCAB14) were incorporated into a Cox proportional hazards model, which was trained using five-fold cross-validation. Univariate analysis confirmed that all genes included in the PIE model were significantly associated with PFS (p < 0.05) compared with negative-control genes (Fig. 3B). PIE demonstrated superior predictive power compared with models relying on single-gene effects or transcriptional signatures alone (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.583, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.614–4.133, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). Multivariate analysis showed no significant associations between PFS and clinical features including sex, age, disease stage, or PD-L1 protein expression (Figure S3). Thus, PIE represents a refined, biomarker-driven predictive model for ICI response (Fig. 3D).

Discriminative ability and predictive performance of PIE

Patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on their PIE scores, using the median score as the cut-off value. Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group exhibited worse PFS in our cohort (training data; HR = 2.377, p <0.001; Fig. 4A), as well as in ORIENT-11 testing dataset 1; HR = 2.915, p < 0.001; Figure 4B) and OAK (testing dataset 2; HR = 1.257, p = 0.004; Fig. 4C) validation cohorts.

PIE demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, outperforming PD-L1-based selection models (Fig. 3C and Figure S2). In contrast to PIE, PD-L1 gene expression alone was not predictive of PFS in ICI-treated patients across cohorts (HRlong-short = 0.655, p = 0.065; HRORIENT-11 = 0.444, p = 0.003; HROAK = 0.694, p = 0.002) (Fig. 4D–F). Similarly, PD-L1 TPS was not significantly associated with PFS (p = 0.13) (Figure S4A). PD-L1 mRNA and protein levels exhibited a significant correlation (p = 0.004) (Figure S4B).

The PIE model demonstrated strong predictive performance for PFS at 6, 12, and 24 months in the training cohort, with respective AUC values: AUC6-month = 0.669, AUC12-month = 0.659, and AUC24-month = 0.768 (Figure S5A). Five-fold cross-validation further confirmed its predictive ability (AUC = 0.771) (Figure S5B), with robust performance in the independent validation cohort from the ORIENT-11 study (AUC6-month = 0.710, AUC12-month = 0.730) (Figure S5C). Additionally, PIE exhibited strong discriminative ability, achieving a C-index of 0.622 in the training cohort and 0.669 in ORIENT-11 validation cohort, with a pooled C-index of 0.614 (95% CI: 0.546–0.681) (Figure S5D). Notably, PIE outperformed models based solely on PD-L1 mRNA or TPS (Figure S6A–D), increasing AUC by 7.5%, 4.9%, and 14.8% for 6-, 12-, and 24-month PFS predictions, respectively. These findings suggest that PIE effectively identifies patients with NSCLC who are most likely to benefit from ICI therapy, offering a reliable predictive tool for treatment selection.

Immunostaining validation of immune cell enrichment in the long-survival group

To confirm immune cell enrichment in pre-treatment tumor biopsy specimens from long-survival group compared with short-survival group, we performed immunofluorescence staining on tissue samples from eight patients in the long-survival group and six patients in the short-survival group. Consistent with the associated between upregulated expression of genes related to CD8+ T cell and NK cell and extended PFS (Fig. 2F–G), immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated elevated expression of CD8, CD56, and CD16 in the long-survival group (Figure S7A). Quantitative analysis of stained images revealed significantly increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and CD56+ NK cells in the long-survival group compared with representative specimens from the short-survival group (Figure S7B–D). Focusing on the target genes of the PIE model, the reduced expression levels of CD274, EFCAB14, FOLR2, and SLC31A2, as well as the increased expression of KRT14, in the short-survival group were validated by quantitative real-time PCR (Figure S8). These findings further support the role of immune cell composition in predicting ICI treatment outcomes.

Potential therapeutic targets for the short-survival group

Given that the PIE model identified patients who did not benefit from ICI therapy, we sought to explore alternative treatment strategies for this subgroup. Gene set enrichment analysis using 28 curated gene sets [43] revealed significant enrichment of the “anti-CTLA-4 resistance-associated MEGE-A (CRMA)” [45] signature in the short-survival group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A–B). The CRMA genes encode members of the melanoma-associated antigen gene (MAGE) family, including MAGE family member A2 (MAGEA2) and MAGE family member A12 (MAGEA12). Within our cohort, expression level of MAGEA12 and MAGEA2, rather than other MAGE family members, were significantly associated with reduced PFS in patients with NSCLC (Fig. 5C). Patients in the high-expression subgroup for MAGEA2 and MAGEA12 exhibited significantly shorter PFS compared with those in the low-expression subgroup (p = 0.025 and p = 0.026, respectively) (Fig. 5D–E). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed colocalization of MAGEA2 and PD-L1 on tumor cells in the short-survival group, suggesting a potential immunosuppressive role (Figure S9A-B). Collectively, these findings highlight MAGEA2 and MAGEA12 as potential therapeutic targets for patients with primary resistance to ICI therapy, providing a rationale for alternative treatment strategies aimed at overcoming immune evasion mechanisms.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and externally validated a five-gene transcriptomic signature—PIE—to predict treatment benefit from ICI-based regimens in advanced NSCLC. In a training cohort of 96 treatment-naïve patients treated with anti-PD-1 ± chemotherapy, PIE effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, demonstrating significant differences in PFS. These findings were further validated in two independent clinical trial cohorts (ORIENT-11 and OAK), underscoring PIE’s predictive robustness across both monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy settings. A key strength of this study is the development of an effective prognostic model with potential clinical application. Comparative validation indicated that PIE exhibits greater accuracy than previous prediction schemes, supporting its utility in optimizing patient selection and guiding treatment strategies. Furthermore, analysis of the short-survival group identified MAGE gene family members as potential therapeutic targets, offering new avenues for overcoming ICI resistance in NSCLC.

As we proved that the PIE model outperformed PD-L1 expression, we further analyzed the relationship between PD-L1 expression and clinical characteristics to ensure comprehensive evaluation (Table S5). In our cohort, PD-L1 expression showed significant associations with gender, ECOG performance status, disease stage, pathological type, therapeutic regimen, and treatment response. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as several subgroups contained zero counts, which may have inflated the statistical significance and reduced the robustness of the results. No significant differences in PD-L1 expression were observed across age or smoking status groups. These results are consistent with previous studies suggesting that PD-L1 expression alone does not fully capture the heterogeneity of NSCLC or reliably predict ICI responsiveness. Together, our findings highlight the necessity of integrating multi-dimensional biomarkers, such as the PIE model, to achieve more accurate prediction of immunotherapy outcomes.

Previous studies have linked ICI efficacy to immune cell infiltration, though discrepancies exist regarding the predominant immune cell populations. In the OAK and POPLAR cohorts, plasma cells–rather than T cells–were enriched in responders to atezolizumab [46], while the ORIENT-11 cohort reported associations between tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), including B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, with immunochemotherapy benefit [20]. In our study, SLC4A10+ CD8+ T cells, IL7R+ NK cells, and hDC1 were significantly associated with prolonged PFS, highlighting key immune populations that may contribute to ICI responsiveness. Advances in single-cell sequencing have enabled identification of previously indistinguishable immune subsets and gene signatures; however, comprehensive characterization remains necessary to delineate their roles in tumor immunity [47]. While hDC1 involvement in anti-tumor immune response is well established [48], the biological function of IL7R+ NK cells has only recently been reported and warrants further investigation [49, 50]. In advanced NSCLC, SLC4A10+ CD8+ T cells may interact with stromal cells through VEGFB-ADRB2 signaling, promoting a more immunostimulatory microenvironment. Specifically, SLC4A10+ CD8+ T cells may secrete VEGF-B in proximity to ADRB2-expressing macrophages, shifting them toward an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype that enhances antigen presentation and T-cell priming [51].

Despite advancements in ICI prediction models for NSCLC, challenges remain in improving their accuracy and generalizability. A major limitation stems from the lack of multi-omics data in clinical trials evaluating ICI therapy. Prognostic models frequently leverage RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which provides large-scale datasets ( > 1,000 samples) that enhance statistical power and model reliability [52]. However, these models primarily predict overall survival using genomic biomarkers, which may not adequately assess survival outcomes with ICIs. By contrast, Sun et al. successfully utilized the TIL scoring to predict ICI response in the ORIENT-11 study [20], though its complexity limits clinical applicability. PIE integrates clinical data from the ORIENT-11 and OAK cohorts, suggesting potential scalability to broader patient populations. Future studies should expand sample sizes and refine predictive accuracy.

Transcriptomic analysis identified several key genes implicated in anti-tumor immunity. Folate receptor beta (FOLR2), expressed predominantly on macrophages, plays a role in antigen presentation and immune regulation [53]. FOLR2+ macrophages was reported to prime effector CD8+ T cell effectively [54]. Solute carrier family 31 member 2 (SLC31A2) is involved in copper ion transmembrane transport. Recent studies have linked SLC31A2 to copper metabolism and cuproptosis in lung and hepatocellular cancer [55–57]. Although the detailed mechanisms remain incompletely understood, it is plausible that copper metabolism enhances the immune response by increasing production of proinflammatory cytokines by ALPK1 kinase [58]. EF-hand calcium-binding domain 14 (EFCAB14), a membrane-associated calcium-binding protein, is highly expressed in lymphocytes and may mediate intercellular signaling. While its function remains poorly defined in cancer, however, calcium ion may involve in activating immune response through the NFAT pathway [59], NLRP3 inflammasome [60], or cGAS-STING pathway [61]. Moreover, calcium influx is particularly important for cell cycle progression and proliferation of the immune cells [62]. Collectively, our findings suggest potential mechanistic links between these genes and ICI resistance, warranting functional validation.

Additionally, transcriptomic profiling of patients in the short-survival group identified MAGE family members as potential drivers of ICI resistance. The role of MAGE proteins in NSCLC has been reported, but their impact on immunotherapy efficacy remains unexplored [63, 64]. The evolutionary adaptation of MAGE proteins enables binding to diverse HLA molecules [65], possibly influencing immune evasion and ICI resistance. A recent study reported the association between gene expression of MAGE family members and resistance to CTLA-4 inhibitors but not PD-1 blockade in melanoma [45], diverging from our findings in NSCLC. Nevertheless, MAGE proteins have been shown to suppress autophagy, contributing to primary resistance, highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue for overcoming ICI insensitivity through autophagy activation [66].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the training cohort used for PIE was relatively small, which may have affected model’s robustness and predictive accuracy. Although external validation in two clinical trials supports its utility, larger-scale studies are warranted to further evaluate its reproducibility and generalizability in NSCLC immunotherapy. Second, we compared the performance of PIE model only with PD-L1 model, rather than with TMB or TIL-based models, due to the lack of whole-genome sequencing and immunofluorescence data. Third, the observed disparities in median PIE score between our cohort and ORIENT-11 cohort suggest potential technical variability, necessitating further standardization and prospective validation to ensure clinical utility. Fourth, functional experiments are required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which PIE-associated biomarkers influence ICI resistance. Fifth, although immunofluorescence validation and quantitative analyses were performed, the small sample size (n = 14) may have compromised the statistical robustness. Future studies incorporating larger, more diverse patient cohorts with varying responses to ICI therapy are required to confirm these findings. Finally, although multiple testing correction was applied in the differential gene expression analysis, the adjusted values did not reach statistical significance; thus, we chose the nominal p value as an alternative criterion.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that PIE as a promising transcriptome-based prognostic tool for NSCLC immunotherapy, leveraging five key biomarkers to stratify high-risk patients beyond conventional clinical factors. The identification of therapeutic targets for high-risk patients provides avenues for future research into ICI resistance mechanisms. These findings contribute to the foundation for more personalized and precise immunotherapy strategies in advanced NSCLC.
