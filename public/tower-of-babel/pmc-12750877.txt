Deep vision in agriculture: assessing the function of YOLO in the classification of plant leaf diseases (PLDs)
Authors: Chhaya Gupta, Nasib Singh Gill, Preeti Gulia, Sangeeta Duhan, Noha Alduaiji, Piyush Kumar Shukla, Abhishek Dwivedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant leaf diseases (PLDs) can continue to be a significant problem in the agricultural sector, leading to significant losses in production and jeopardizing food security. Early detection is essential, and recent achievements in the domain of deep learning (DL) have made automated high-accuracy solutions possible. The most popular and commonly used of these is the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of object detection models, which have been proposed to detect plant diseases in real time. This review presents a new and in-depth synthesis of YOLO-based methods, including YOLOv1 to YOLOv10 and the domain-specific variants, including CTB-YOLO (coriander), BED-YOLO (YOLOv10n), and RAG-augmented YOLOv8 (coffee). This work compares to previous surveys in that (i) it presents a structured dataset catalog containing information on size, resolution, disease classes, and limitations (such as imbalance and annotation problems); (ii) it provides comparative benchmarking analysis of performance measures (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, mean Average Precision, and frames per second) across versions of YOLO to illustrate trade-offs between speed and accuracy; and (iii) it gives forward-looking discussion on how (ii) open challenges and (iii) future research directions, including lightweight YOLO models to run on mobile. This review presents a summative reference and a new contribution to the progress of the YOLO-based PLD detection approach to sustainable agriculture.

Introduction

Background and motivation

The detection of PLD (PLD) has become one of the most significant topics of research in agricultural technology because it directly affects crop yield, food security, and sustainable farming. Proper and prompt detection of leaf diseases leads to prompt action being taken before it is too late, loss of crops is minimized, and unnecessary pesticides are not used. The classical approaches based on manual examination or classical machine learning to detect diseases have been useful, though, in most cases, they fail to detect the latent manifestations of the disease, or they require scales capable of resolving the requirements of observing on a large scale. Here, convolutional architectures of deep learning (DL) techniques have demonstrated high promise on par with automated PLD detection and classification on digital images.

The basic computer vision activity of object detection is the core of this advancement. Although object detection is popularly used in the medical, transport, and surveillance fields, its application in agriculture, particularly in PLD diagnosis, has become more apparent. Recent deep learning-based detectors are capable of localizing and classifying several disease types in a single image and are therefore very useful in the real-time field. In this category, especially relevant are the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models, which provide a balance between speed and accuracy to support research and real-world implementation in crop health monitoring.

PLD identification using DL has grown in popularity and demonstrated encouraging outcomes in correctly identifying plant diseases from digital images. Plant disease diagnosis has extensively used traditional Machine Learning (ML) methods like feature extraction and classification [1]. These methods teach a classifier to differentiate between healthy plants and those not using features like color, texture, and form taken from images. These techniques were used to detect diseases like rust, leaf spots, and black spots, as well as disease symptoms brought on by abiotic stressors like drought and nutrient deficiencies [2]. However, they are not very good at detecting subtle disease symptoms or detecting diseases in their early stages. These techniques use a network trained to recognize the underlying characteristics of the images, making it possible to identify subtle disease indicators that conventional image processing techniques could miss.

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in PLD has been the subject of a significant amount of research. However, it should be highlighted that several research directions have been taken depending on the various characteristics of AI. A good amount of research has been done on the application of AI in agriculture, particularly in predictive analysis [3]. There are several PLD domains in which machine learning techniques can be used. However, intelligent systems can bring further advancements and benefits to this field. It will be crucial for leaf disease detectors to become broadly applicable and indispensable to boost agricultural productivity in the future. The current work will review, synthesize, and summarize the existing data to better understand how DL has been applied in PLD for classification activities. The actual implementation of AI technology, especially DL techniques, is causing a massive revolution in the agriculture sector. This change is demonstrated by agricultural companies’ initiatives, which utilize DL breakthroughs to create solutions that help farmers and customers with harvesting, grading, plant health monitoring, drink quality, and other tasks [4].

YOLO family overview

Object detection is a core computer vision task that facilitates various subsequent tasks. For instance, it can help with face identification, behavior analysis and recognition, multi-object tracking, instance segmentation, etc. As a result, it has been a prominent research area for the last few decades. A few applications that fall under this category are autonomous driving, intelligent health care, industrial robotics, identity authentication, visual monitoring, and other tasks. YOLO has been highly developed in recent years. YOLO was released in 2016, speeding up the object detection process [5]. In contrast to the conventional two-stage object detection designs, YOLO’s innovative method provided a single architecture that could anticipate anchor boxes and class probabilities while satisfying real-time processing demands [6]. YOLO became popular due to its one-stage design and various iterations from YOLOv1 to YOLOv8 [7]. Each iteration has unique features that distinguish it from the previous one, including some improvements over the previous one. YOLO provides a balance between speed and accuracy. This study also investigates the evolving YOLO architectures, carefully evaluates their effectiveness, and considers prospective future advancements in agribusiness. The paper looks at how YOLO (YOLOv1 to YOLOv8) has changed over time and its efficacy in classifying PLDs.

PLDs represent a significant world agricultural challenge, resulting in high losses in production, financial strain on farmers, and food security risks. Reduced application of pesticides, prompt treatment, and sustainable farming thus require the early and accurate diagnosis of PLDs. Traditional machine learning solutions are not always robust under a wide range of field conditions, and manual inspection methods are often labour-intensive and prone to error. In this regard, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a groundbreaking remedy since it can automatically extract discriminative features of leaf images and can enhance the classification accuracy and scalability. The YOLO (You Only Look Once) family is representative of the DL-based object detection systems due to its capability to detect diseases in real-time with high accuracy; thus, it can be deployed in the field to make timely decisions and is needed in the field. This review indicates the need to develop computer vision algorithms to support precision agriculture by harmonizing the needs of society to have a dependable PLD detecting system with the technological capabilities of YOLO models.

Real-world examples of the use of YOLO-based PLD detection have shown that the models may be useful beyond the controlled experimental environment. As an example, UAV/drones have been designed to visualize extensive areas of farmland, with YOLO models being used to spot and identify crop disease epidemics in rice, tea, and citrus crops. These systems allow monitoring on a large scale with fewer labor needs and enable farmers to obtain timely information to implement specific interventions. Likewise, lightweight YOLO variants (e.g., YOLOv5s, YOLOv8n) have been developed to drive mobile application prototypes on smartphones, where farmers can take pictures of the field leaves and (immediately) obtain a diagnosis of the disease. Experiments on crops such as tomato, potato, and guava have shown that these mobile solutions are feasible and can be used to detect the presence of PLD in real time and on the device in agricultural settings that are resource-constrained. Combined, these case studies highlight the increasing applied utility of YOLO-based structures in assisting precision agriculture.

Comparative analysis of DL and Object detection framework

Recent developments (2024–2025) go beyond YOLOv8 and into families of YOLOv9 and YOLOv10. In the case of coriander, CTB-YOLO focuses on the detectable region of early small-lesion (tip-burn, powdery mildew) that is feature-fusion and edge-device viable, emphasizing real-world, low-latency application in controlled/indoor environments [14]. In the case of coffee, an augmented RAG-based YOLOv8 model integrates detection with retrieval-augmented guidance to provide context-sensitive diagnosis and recommendations to the farmers. Models based on YOLOv9 (e.g., DM-YOLO) achieve higher mAP on difficult leaf lesions in tomatoes and other crops, whereas models based on YOLOv10 (e.g., BED-YOLO, YOLO-vegetable and YOLOv10 on banana leaves) demonstrate better accuracy and throughput and have compact backbones compatible with mobile/edge inference [15]. These studies all show a clear trend of lighter, faster detectors with increased small-object robustness and decision-support capability, which supports real-world readiness.

Methodology used

This work is a complete review descriptive study conducted online using Science Direct, IEEE, Springer, and miscellaneous databases. “YOLO,” “deep learning,” “computer vision,” “leaf diseases,” and “PLDs” were the descriptions utilized to find research publications. The following standards were set for the works included in the current study: publications that were published during recent years, papers that dealt with the subjects of “computer vision” and “deep learning” concerning methods for classifying PLDs, and articles that were fully accessible in the databases mentioned above. The overview of research data did not include theoretical studies or thorough literature reviews. The steps in the analysis process were as follows: (1) look for descriptors such as “PLD” in the databases mentioned above; (2) exclude papers that were published more than five years ago; (3) exclude works that were not relevant; (4) critically read the articles to see if they fit the suggested theme; (5) exclude theoretical articles and literature reviews; and (6) conduct a final analysis. Tables with the key details on the goals and application scenario, the process used for the classification analysis of PLDs, and the results were generated after the articles were chosen based on the criteria for inclusion or exclusion. After the articles were chosen based on the criteria for inclusion and exclusion, tables were created with the key details regarding the application situation and goals, the technique for the PLD categorization analysis, and the findings were produced.

1114 articles containing the terms “deep learning” and “leaf disease(s)” and 79 articles containing the terms “computer vision” and “PLD(s)” were located in the IEEE database. One hundred ten (110) publications were rejected because they had nothing to do with the lead illness area; for instance, the articles solely discussed “deep learning” and had nothing to do with the leaf disease area, or the author’s last name was Leaf. Twenty (20) papers were rejected because they had nothing to do with classifying leaf diseases, such as rust spot prediction, grey spot detection, or fungal infection prediction. Searches for “deep learning” + “leaf disease” and “computer vision” + “PLD” yielded 19 articles at the same time. Lastly, the ten (10) papers were literature reviews, so they were not included in the study. Similarly, three thousand twenty (3020) papers were found in the Science database; two thousand nine hundred forty (2940) were ignored because they had nothing to do with the general theme; forty-five (45) were ignored because they had nothing to do with the identification of PLDs; twelve (12) showed up in searches at the same time, and five (5) papers were ignored in the analysis because they were studies of the literature. Ultimately, 3,748 papers were discovered in the Springer database; however, 3,627 were rejected because they had nothing to do with categorizing PLDs; seven (7) were rejected because they had nothing to do with the theme; two (2) showed up in both searches at the same time; and three (3) papers were rejected in the analysis as they were review papers. Five hundred sixteen (516) papers were discovered in miscellaneous databases from MDPI, IEEE conferences, Elsevier, and others. Five hundred were rejected because they were not related to the classification of PLDs.

To be transparent and rigorous, this review was systematic. Keywords that were used to retrieve relevant literature in the IEEE Xplore, Springer, ScienceDirect (Elsevier), MDPI, and other peer-reviewed repositories included PLD, leaf disease detection, deep learning, computer vision, YOLO, Faster R-CNN, SSD, and object detection in agriculture. The inclusion criteria were that the papers had to be written after the year 2017 and 2025, be peer-reviewed, and be directly related to leaf disease detection or classification in plant leaves using deep learning or object detection models with reported quantitative outcomes like accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, or mAP. The exclusion criteria excluded papers published before 2017, theoretical or review studies that have not been experimentally validated, articles that were not related to PLDs, and articles that did not provide enough methodological or performance details. After the process, 136 primary research articles were added to the list, and it was ensured that not only classical deep learning models (e.g., CNN, ResNet, Inception, DenseNet, MobileNet) but also specific detection models (e.g., YOLOv3–YOLOv8, Faster R-CNN, SSD, RetinaNet) were present in the list to make the review complete and balanced.

In this review, the comparison of YOLO versions and datasets was conducted based on reported results in the literature and a common experimental benchmark. Based on the literature, the synthesis of studies was performed according to the typical measures of performance, such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, mean Average Precision (mAP), and frames per second (FPS). These actions represent the grade of categorization and live recognition effectiveness that are very crucial in the agricultural sector. In addition to literature synthesis, an experimental assessment was performed through the training of YOLO models (YOLOv1-YOLOv8) on the PlantVillage dataset, which is a popular benchmark to classify plant diseases and allows for comparison across different versions directly under identical conditions. This dual approach, literature and dataset-specific benchmarking synthesis, ensures not only the thoroughness but also the comparability of the analysis and highlights the relative merits, trade-offs, and practical suitability of each version of YOLO to identify PLD. Catalog of datasets used for PLD detection

A systematic literature selection process was used to make this review broad and transparent. The identification of the relevant studies was based on the search of the IEEE Xplore, Springer, ScienceDirect (Elsevier), and MDPI (and other peer-reviewed repositories). Some of the keywords were plant leaf disease, leaf disease detection, deep learning, YOLO, object detection in agriculture, Faster R-CNN, and SSD. The following search was conducted between 2017 and 2025 to include both traditional deep learning methods and recent versions of the YOLO algorithm. A preliminary list of more than 220 papers was obtained. Following the elimination of duplicates, reviews, entirely theoretical publications, and articles that were not relevant to the detection of plant diseases, only 136 primary research articles were incorporated into the final synthesis. The inclusion criteria were that papers: (i) were peer-reviewed, (ii) directly detected or classified plant leaf disease with a deep learning or object detector framework, and (iii) provided quantitative results, like accuracy, precision, recall, or F1-score or mean Average Precision (mAP). The exclusion criteria were papers that were published prior to 2017, articles that were based on non-agricultural areas, articles that were not experimentally validated, and articles that did not provide enough information on the annotation. This process was done to make the review systematic, representative, and balanced, that is, it includes both classical deep learning classifiers (CNN, ResNet, Inception, etc.) as well as specialized detection frameworks (YOLOv3-YOLOv8, Faster R-CNN, SSD, RetinaNet).

Although they are widely used, the current PLD datasets have several limitations that limit their real-world use. Imbalanced class distributions occur in many datasets, e.g., in PlantVillage, some diseases (e.g., late blight or rust) are overrepresented, whereas others have very small samples. This same imbalance tends to bias models toward majority classes, which limits the ability to generalize to rare but significant diseases. The other significant constraint is the absence of real-field diversity. Many images are shot in a controlled setting with even lighting and plain backgrounds, which is not representative of the uncontrolled conditions of fields where leaves can overlap, be partially obscured, or be seen under varying weather and lighting situations. Moreover, field-collected datasets are vulnerable to annotation issues as disease symptoms may be insidious, overlapping, or co-occurring and thus challenging to label correctly, regardless of the expertise of the annotator. Deep learning methods for PLD identification

A subfield of AI called deep learning (DL) can correctly classify or predict previously unknown data by generalizing from training data [18]. It is a flexible tool used in any field where data analysis is necessary [19]. The branch of machine learning that uses artificial neural network design is called deep learning [20]. Layers of connected nodes known as neurons are used by artificial neural networks, or ANNs, to process and learn from input data [20]. The input layer or neurons in the layer above provide information to each neuron [21].

The three main types of machine learning are unsupervised, supervised, and reinforcement learning. Training sets are instances of input-output pairings in supervised learning. The method picks up a hypothesis function that converts input to output [22]. When the output values are unlimited, the supervised learning method is termed regression; when the output data is finite and integer, it is called classification. Sorting objects into classes or groups is the concept of classification. Figs. 1 and 2 below illustrate how supervised and uncontrolled learning differs. Unlike ML, deep learning is not divided into any category. However, DL can be used for both supervised and unsupervised learning and reinforcement learning. Many supervised tasks, including sentiment analysis, language translation, image classification and recognition, and more, use deep learning methods like convolutional and recurrent neural networks [24]. Deep learning algorithms like generative models and autoencoders handle unsupervised tasks, such as identifying anomalies, dimensionality reduction, and grouping [25]. Robotics, gaming, and other fields are reinforced by deep reinforcement learning methods such as Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) and Deep Q networks [26, 27].Fig. 1Supervised learning for PLD classification [23]Fig. 2Unsupervised learning for PLD classification [23]

Deep learning models are ideal for applications like picture recognition, voice recognition, and natural language processing because they can extract features from the input. The three most widely used deep learning architectures are convolutional neural networks (CNNs), feedforward neural networks (FNNs), and recurrent neural networks (RNNs). An FNN is the most fundamental type of ANN with a linear information flow. FNNs have been widely used for natural language processing, image classification, and speech recognition [28]. CNNs are designed especially for jobs involving the recognition of images and videos. Because CNNs can automatically learn characteristics from the images, they are perfect for applications like object identification, image segmentation, and image classification [29]. Time series and spoken language are sequential data that RNNs, a neural network, can process. RNNs are ideal for tasks like speech recognition, natural language processing, and translation of languages because they can save an internal state that contains data about the prior inputs [30].

An area of artificial intelligence called computer vision looks to evaluate, decipher, and extract pertinent data from pictures to make choices [22]. It is challenging to recognize objects in their full generality. Identifying and locating particular items inside an image or video is object detection in computer vision. The two primary objectives in this process are object localization, which pinpoints the object’s precise location in the image, and object categorization, which determines the kind or class of object seen in the picture, including the type of leaf illness and how severe the leaf is [31]. Sorting PLDs can be significantly aided by object detection, which improves process efficiency and accuracy. Computer vision tasks can assist in identifying PLD in pictures and movies and distinguishing between various kinds of damaged leaves according to their traits [32]. Using object detection techniques like YOLO, PLD may be automatically categorized into several types [33].

YOLO was developed by Joseph Redmon et al. for real-time object detection in 2015 [34]. YOLO revolutionized object detection by introducing a single, end-to-end neural network that could simultaneously classify and localize objects in an image. Unlike previous object detection methods that used region proposals or sliding windows, YOLO processed the entire image in one pass, making it significantly faster than its predecessors. YOLO is a single-stage detector, meaning it performs classification and localization in one step without relying on a region proposal network like two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN). This design prioritizes speed, making it suitable for real-time applications. When YOLO was released in 2016, it significantly advanced single-shot object identification. The architecture of YOLO is shown in Fig. 4. Motivated by the GoogLeNet architecture, YOLO implemented a novel strategy using (1 × 1) convolution and (3 × 3) convolutional filters instead of GoogLeNet’s inception modules [35]. The Darknet framework was used for training, and the architecture was evaluated using the VOC Pascal Datasets from 2007 and 2012 [36]. YOLO adopted (1 × 1) convolutions and global average pooling as notable characteristics, with 24 convolution layers—only four followed by max-pooling layers. The model was fine-tuned by including two fully connected layers with randomly initialized weights and four more convolutional layers after it had been initially trained on the ImageNet dataset. Except for the final layer, which used a linear activation function, the activation function used the Leaky Rectified Linear Unit (LReLU) [37].Fig. 4The architecture of YOLO [34]

The architecture of YOLO [34]

YOLOv2 is an enhanced version of the original YOLOv1 model, published in 2016. YOLOv2 builds on the speed and simplicity of YOLOv1, addressing some of its weaknesses, such as localization errors and limitations with small objects [38]. It also introduces several innovations that enhance accuracy, especially for multi-scale detection tasks. YOLOv2 was modeled on the once-popular VGG architecture using the darknet-19 framework with 5 max-pooling layers and 19 convolutional layers (Fig. 5) [39]. Each convolutional layer is followed by batch normalization (BN), stabilizing training, and improving network learning. It reduces the need for dropout, allowing the model to converge faster and improve accuracy [40]. One of the most notable aspects was the incorporation of 1 × 1 convolutions for downsampling throughout the input volume’s depth. Instead of pre-training on a low-resolution (224x224) image like YOLOv1, YOLOv2 is pre-trained on a 448 × 448resolution, closer to the final detection resolution [41]. This increases the model’s accuracy on smaller things and enables it to acquire knowledge to detect finer details. YOLOv2 integrated several data augmentation strategies for improved training, including rotations and random crops.Fig. 5The architecture of YOLOv2 [39]

The architecture of YOLOv2 [39]

YOLOv3 was released in 2018, build upon the strengths of YOLOv1 and YOLOv2. This version has improved accuracy for detecting small objects and multi-scale detection while maintaining real-time performance [42]. Many new features and architecture changes were implemented in YOLOv3, making it more robust and accurate. YOLOv3 can make predictions at three scale levels better to handle objects of varying sizes [40]. This model combines the feature maps from previous layers with higher-resolution feature maps from earlier layers. This multi-scale detection approach enables YOLOv3 to detect smaller objects more accurately than YOLOv1 and YOLOv2. YOLOv3 incorporates Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs) that help the model to use high-level and low-level features. YOLOv3 uses DarkNet-53 for feature extraction [43]. This backbone has 53 convolutional layers, significantly more profound than Darknet-19 used in YOLOv2. Darknet-53 combines 1 × 1 and 3 × 3 convolutional layers without fully connected layers, making it highly efficient and robust [43]. Darknet-53 uses residual connections similar to those in ResNet, which helps with gradient flow, allowing for better performance on deeper networks [44]. Like YOLOv2, YOLOv3 uses anchor boxes for bounding box predictions, with three anchor boxes per scale (nine in total). The model predicts bounding boxes as offsets relative to the anchor boxes. Each bounding box has a confidence score and class probabilities [45]. Fig. 6 displays the YOLOv3 architecture.Fig. 6The architecture of YOLOv3 [46]

The architecture of YOLOv3 [46]

YOLOv4 was released in 2020, Alexey Bochkovskiy et al. to further improve the YOLO family by integrating new techniques that enhance accuracy and speed. It is a great option for object detection in real time [47]. YOLOv4 is optimized for GPUs. It provides fast and reliable detection in production environments [48]. YOLOv4 introduces a combination of “Bag of Freebies” (BoF) [49] and “Bag of Specials” (BoS) techniques [50]. These methods increase both the model’s detection quality and the training procedure. “Bag of Freebies” is a technique that will strengthen model accuracy during training without increasing inference costs. Various techniques under “Bag of Freebies” are mosaic data augmentation [51], Self adversarial training (SAT), DropBlock Regularization [52], and Class label smoothing. DropBlock Regularization is a form of dropout that randomly drops entire blocks of feature maps, reducing overfitting and improving generalization. By lowering the model’s confidence in its predictions, class label smoothing helps to improve performance and reduce overconfidence. Fig. 7 shows the architecture of YOLOv4.Fig. 7The architecture of YOLOv4 [53]

The architecture of YOLOv4 [53]

Ultralytics introduced YOLOv5, a milestone in the YOLO family of models. YOLOv5 quickly gained popularity for its user-friendliness, accessibility, and performance on real-time object detection tasks [54]. YOLOv5 is written in PyTorch, which makes it easily accessible and straightforward to train and deploy. The architecture of YOLOv5 continues many of the core ideas from YOLOv4, but it integrates new optimizations and simplifications, allowing it to achieve excellent results with increased efficiency [55]. YOLOv5 offers multiple versions of the model (YOLOv5s, YOLOv5m, YOLOv5l, YOLOv5x), each with a different backbone and size. This modular approach allows for a range of trade-offs between speed and accuracy, making it adaptable for various use cases. YOLOv5 introduces a Focus Layer at the start of the network [56]. The Focus Layer slices the input image into four parts and concatenates them to form a feature map, which reduces spatial dimensions by half and channels by four. This design conserves computational power and increases efficiency. The backbone includes CSPNet-based (Cross-Stage Partial Network) blocks to partition feature maps, which enhances gradient flow and computational efficiency [57]. YOLOv5 uses PANet in the neck, similar to YOLOv4, to aggregate features from different layers and scales, improving multi-scale detection capability. Each detection head output includes bounding box coordinates, an object confidence score, and class probabilities. The architecture is shown in Fig. 8.Fig. 8The architecture of YOLOv5 [58]

The architecture of YOLOv5 [58]

YOLOv6, introduced by Meituan in 2022, enhances object detection performance with a design optimized for production environments and real-time applications [59]. YOLOv6 leverages a series of architectural adjustments, training techniques, and optimizations to deliver high accuracy while maintaining efficient inference speeds, specifically on GPUs. YOLOv6‘s design combines backbone, neck, and head improvements to boost performance and reduce computational costs [60]. Additionally, it introduces enhanced data augmentation, loss functions, and anchor-free detection mechanisms. YOLOv6 introduces the EfficientRep backbone, designed based on the RepVGG (reparameterized VGG) structure [31]. The RepVGG blocks use reparameterization techniques that simplify the architecture during inference by converting complex structures into simple convolutional blocks. Reparametrization allows YOLOv6 to combine multiple convolutions during training but reduce them to a single convolution for inference, optimizing speed and memory efficiency [61]. BiFPN in YOLOv6 is optimized for lightweight performance, allowing for better multi-scale feature fusion without significantly impacting inference time [62]. The architecture of YOLOv6 [31]

A significant development in the YOLO family, YOLOv7 pushes the limits of real-time object identification, emphasizing efficiency and accuracy [63]. YOLOv7 introduces various novel techniques to enhance the model’s detection accuracy without compromising speed, making it one of the most powerful versions in the YOLO series for applications requiring real-time object detection [64]. YOLOv7’s architecture includes enhancements in its backbone, neck, and head. It introduces structural changes and innovative training strategies that improve accuracy and speed. The architecture is depicted in Fig. 110 [65]. YOLOv7 builds on CSPNet (Cross Stage Partial Network), also used in YOLOv4 and YOLOv5. CSPNet is designed to enhance feature learning efficiency by splitting feature maps into two parts, processing one part through a sequence of layers, and then merging them back [66]. This structure helps improve gradient flow and allows for deeper networks without increasing computational complexity. YOLOv7 introduces the ELAN structure in the backbone. By employing many convolutional layers with varying kernel sizes, ELAN enables the system to learn and merge features at different scales [67]. This makes it easier for the network to identify objects of various sizes and orientations. YOLOv7 also introduces a modified partial convolution structure that further boosts efficiency. The architecture of YOLOv7 [65]

YOLOv8 is the latest generation in the YOLO family, strongly focusing on flexibility, ease of use, and high performance. YOLOv8 builds on the successes of its predecessors, bringing together improvements in architecture, training techniques, and post-processing to achieve incredible speed and accuracy [7]. It is highly versatile and is designed to work seamlessly with various applications and platforms. YOLOv8’s architecture includes a modular approach, with refinements across the network’s backbone, neck, and head. Additionally, it offers multiple variants optimized for different performance needs. The architecture for YOLOv8 is depicted in Fig. 11 [68]. The backbone of YOLOv8 is CSP-Darknet, an enhanced CSP (Cross Stage Partial) network. YOLO for gray spot classification

YOLO is a well-known computer vision object detection technique. Using a neural network, the real-time object detection technique YOLO processes a picture in a single forward pass. YOLO accomplishes regression of bounding boxes and object detection in a single step, as opposed to traditional object identification methods that call for multiple processing steps. It can handle up to 60 frames per second, making it quick and effective. To use YOLO, an image is divided into a pixel grid, and the anchor boxes for each cell are determined. Every bounding box’s class probability and confidence score, or the chance that an object will be inside it, are predicted by YOLO. YOLO likewise predicts the bounding box coordinates of the cell.

A comparative analysis of various YOLO variants

The revolutionary idea of real-time object detection utilizing a single-stage architecture with anchor boxes was first presented by YOLOv1, the original version of YOLO. The DarkNet 24 architecture analyzed information at 45 FPS and obtained a fantastic mAP of 63.4% [34]. YOLOv2 carried on using anchor boxes to increase localization precision, building on the achievements of YOLOv1. Utilizing the DarkNet24 architecture, it increased mAP significantly, reaching 69.0% and 52 FPS while maintaining real-time processing capability [118]. By implementing a multi-scale feature extraction architecture and providing novel loss functions, including CIoU, GIoU, and BCE, YOLOv3 demonstrated a balanced approach. At 34 frames per second, it proved its capacity to handle object detection across many scales and obtained a mAP of 57.9% using the Darknet53 framework [119]. YOLOv4 prioritized sophisticated loss functions, such as CIoU, DFL, and BCE, by introducing the CSPDarknet53 architecture to improve bounding box accuracy while maintaining real-time processing. Even though the mAP dropped to 44.3%, the FPS was still intense at 65 [47]. YOLOv5, which used PyTorch to build the Modified CSP v7 architecture, significantly improved accuracy and efficiency. It demonstrated its effectiveness in real-time applications by achieving a mAP of 50.7% and a significant increase in FPS to 200 using a single-stage detection technique and unique loss functions (CIoU, DFL, and BCE) [120]. The YOLOv6, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8 iterations follow show a dedication to iterative enhancements. YOLOv7, built on the RepConvN, obtained a mAP of 56.8%, while YOLOv6, which used the EfficientRep design, increased accuracy to 52.5%. By introducing an anchor-free model, YOLOv8 maintained a remarkable processing speed of 280 FPS while maintaining a high accuracy of 53.9%.

Challenges and discussion

Farming is being revolutionized by DL and related technologies like computer vision, which are lowering operating costs and advancing agriculture to previously unheard-of levels of efficiency, speed, and precision in decision-making. This review demonstrated the potential for ongoing improvements by proving the existence of multiple computational models developed for PLD classification that address a variety of attributes, including different diseases, different PLD architectures, grey spot identification, fungal infection identification, leaf spot identification, and other architectures for other leaf disease identification. Nevertheless, despite these advancements, most AI-based models and technologies are experimental and have not yet been widely implemented in real-world settings. More broadly applicable models are required, particularly in fields where DL is still underutilized, such as alternate spots, which show great unrealized potential for industrial and academic applications.

Limitations, highlights, and challenges

The use of deep learning models based on YOLO has also achieved tremendous success in the detection of plant leaf diseases. Subsequent iterations of YOLOv3 through YOLOv10 have steadily increased detection accuracy and mean Average Precision (mAP), and YOLOv8 and YOLOv10 achieve state-of-the-art results on benchmarking datasets such as PlantVillage. These models also provide a high inference rate, typically more than 100 frames per second, enabling the use of real-time detectors with GPUs, drones, and controlled agricultural systems. In addition, recent specializations, such as CTB-YOLO (coriander), BED-YOLO (YOLOv10n) (tomato), and RAG-augmented YOLOv8 (coffee), show encouraging progress in small-object detection, environmental variability, and customization to decision-support pipelines. Both developments have been paralleled by the growth of datasets of curated and annotated plant disease datasets, which have become a basis to train, benchmark, and speed up research. Irrespective of these successes, there are several issues that have not been addressed. Most YOLO models are still based on edited data like PlantVillage that do not reflect real-field situations in diversity and complexity. This decreases their strength when changing lighting, occlusion, and cluttered backgrounds. Besides that, most datasets have problems of class imbalance and small samples, in particular, rare diseases, which prevent confident detection across all categories. Although some lightweight versions of the YOLO models are available, models that can run on mobile devices, IoT, and UAVs are still limited by power and memory constraints, especially in resource-sparse areas. An additional problem area is interpretability - YOLO models are frequently black box models that provide little information about why a disease is identified, and this limits user trust and adoption by farmers. Finally, one can mention the issue of cross-domain generalization: machine-learned models trained on one crop or region of the world can do worse in new environments, and effective domain adaptation strategies are therefore important.

Current YOLO-based and classical deep learning algorithms have shown good results in the PLD detection, but they still have several shortcomings. Models that are trained on curated sets are usually not effective at generalizing to real-world setups where lighting, occlusion, and background variation are important. Numerous algorithms face the problem of class imbalance, resulting in biased detection towards dominant diseases and poor performance on rare ones. Moreover, the latest versions of YOLO have high accuracy but cannot be run in real time on mobile devices, drones, and IoT systems due to their high processing requirements. The other shortcoming is that most YOLO models are black boxes, making it harder to interpret their outputs and making them less trusted by end users, including farmers. This review overcomes these limitations by summarizing the data on comparative performance across versions of YOLO, classifying strengths and weaknesses of datasets, and detailing solutions to specific shortcomings, including data augmentation and domain adaptation to achieve better generalization, model compression to achieve lightweight models, and hybrid architectures that combine CNN and Transformer modules.

Future scope

Future studies in the field of PLD detection are needed to create lightweight YOLO models that could be deployed to mobile and edge sensors with the help of pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation, so that a farmer could diagnose a diseased plant in real-time right on their device. The other possible trend is the integration of YOLO and UAVs with IoT platforms that can combine aerial imaging with sensor data to survey regions at the macro-level and at a granular level. To facilitate increased usability, explainable AI (XAI) features, such as saliency maps or Grad-CAM, to visualize the most sensitive regions of the leaves that drive predictions and user trust, will be of value. It is also important to consider cross-domain adaptation; models that are trained on curated data do not always perform well in general field settings; transfer learning, domain adaptation, and multi-modal fusion (e.g., RGB with hyperspectral or thermal imaging) have shown potential to enhance this. Finally, the annotation information is also sparse, thus encouraging semi-supervised neural networks and synthetic data generation (e.g., GAN-generated examples) that can expand data and improve generalization without relying extensively on manual labeling.

Despite the impressive advances made in YOLO-based systems to detect plant leaf diseases (PLD), there are several unresolved challenges that need to be solved to make these types of systems applicable in large-scale, real-world applications. A direction that has emerged is the further development of lightweight models that are mobile device and edge-deployment optimized. Already, the variants YOLOv8n and YOLOv10n show that it is possible to achieve high levels of detection accuracy using efficient, compact models, running on both smartphones and UAVs. Additional development of these architectures will allow farmers in resource-limited environments to receive timely diagnosis of their diseases without procuring costly hardware.

One more perspective is the use of YOLO models in connection with edge computing and Internet of Things (IoT) systems. The integration of detection functions into drones, field sensors, and automated farm equipment will allow large-scale monitoring with a limited level of latency. As the edge devices are paired with cloud-based assistance, the future systems will be able to scale further at a lower cost of data transmission and guarantee real-time reactivity in the field. Simultaneously, there is still the problem of dataset limitations. The available PLD datasets are usually unbalanced, cover small areas, and have problems with inconsistencies in their annotations. In future studies, the solution to these gaps must be a priority, so enhanced augmentation methods, synthetic generation through generative adversarial networks (GANs), and semi-supervised learning should be used to provide greater robustness and generalization to different agricultural environments.

It is also essential to enhance the explainability and reliability of YOLO models. As farmers and agronomists are dependent on clear and interpretable choices, explanation-based AI schemes like heatmaps and saliency-based visualizations or attention mechanisms need to be introduced into PLD detection pipelines. By improving interpretability, it will become possible to increase trust in AI-based systems and decrease the mistrust towards the so-called black-box models. Lastly, the improvement of cross-crop and cross-region generalization is required. The existing studies tend to focus on a particular crop in a controlled experimental environment, which does not represent the reality of farms in the real world, where variability in leaf structure, lighting, and disease appearance is present. Increasing the size of datasets and benchmarks to include multiple crops, regions, and growing conditions will be necessary to come up with strong and universally applicable PLD detection systems.

Lastly, the potential for PLD diagnoses is bright because of the revolutionary potential of DL and computer vision, which might considerably increase management confidence and productivity. Therefore, future studies should build on these findings and improve classification methods for broader and more precise use in PLD categorization.

Conclusion

This review has provided a synthesized overview of deep learning methods based on YOLO to detect the disease of plant leaves (PLD), including both the older versions of YOLOv1 and YOLOv10, and newer specialized versions such as CTB-YOLO, BED-YOLO, and RAG-augmented YOLOv8. The comparative analysis shows that the quality of YOLO models has constantly increased in terms of accuracy, mean Average Precision (mAP), and inference time, with the models YOLOv8 and YOLOv10 being the state-of-the-art models on benchmark datasets. But our curated dataset library shows that shortcomings such as class skew, narrow real-field coverage, and annotation noise remain to restrict generalization. Moreover, even as existing YOLO models can record impressive performances in controlled settings, there are still implementation issues with edge devices, cross-domain generalization, and explainability. The primary impact of this synthesis is that PLD detection has evolved to the point where precise, real-time detection systems are technically possible but not yet sufficiently robust to be applicable across the board. To overcome the limitations of current algorithms, one can consider strategies to include lightweight versions of the YOLO algorithm to run on a mobile device, use a hybrid CNN-Transformer structure to represent richer features, domain adaptation to cross-crop and cross-regional generalization, and integrate lightweight explainable AI (XAI) to increase transparency and trust with farmers. YOLO plus UAV and IoT also hold promise as scalable real-time monitors. Overall, this review summarizes the state-of-the-art performance trends of YOLO in PLD detection, but more importantly, presents a roadmap for the future that can guide researchers and practitioners to develop robust, interpretable, and field-ready solutions. It is necessary to bridge the gap between computer vision advances and sustainable agriculture so that the worth of crops, the number of pesticides used, and food insecurity in the entire world can decrease by a long margin, and the use of YOLO-based frameworks is one option to achieve such a goal.
