Rethinking Mitochondrial Parkinson's Disease in the α‐Synuclein Seed Amplification Assays Era
Authors: Marco Percetti, Vidal Yahya, Alessio Di Fonzo, Edoardo Monfrini
Published: 2026

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a heterogeneous disorder regarding etiology, molecular mechanisms, neuropathology, and clinical manifestations. The fil rouge connecting PD patients is a clinical syndrome characterized by progressive levodopa‐responsive parkinsonism, caused by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc). 1

In light of recent efforts toward a biological definition of PD—largely centered on α‐synuclein seed amplification assays (α‐syn‐SAA) 2 , 3 —we aim to emphasize the biological heterogeneity of dopaminergic neurodegeneration. Approximately 12% of “idiopathic” PD (iPD) cases and many cases of PRKN‐PD and LRRK2‐PD show neither α‐synuclein seeding activity in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) nor Lewy pathology in postmortem analyses. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 This evidence calls for complementary approaches to these emerging frameworks.

The direct clinical observation of patients with monogenic PD caused by mitochondrial dysfunction (eg, PRKN, PINK1), as well as individuals with parkinsonism harboring variants associated with primary mitochondrial diseases (eg, POLG, TWNK), has led us to elaborate on the concept of “mitochondrial PD” (MitoPD).

In this viewpoint, we propose MitoPD as an etiological‐biological‐pathological‐clinical subtype of PD. Our goal is to clarify the features shared among MitoPD patients at all these levels and to frame an emerging conceptual question: in a diagnostic era increasingly shaped by fluid‐based α‐syn‐SAA, how should we interpret results in patients whose PD is not primarily driven by α‐synuclein pathology? Could MitoPD be a useful framework to account for part of these cases?

Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Monogenic PD

Autosomal recessive PD due to PRKN and PINK1 mutations is largely attributable to mitochondrial dysfunction, 8 resulting from impaired clearance in dopaminergic neurons, which leads to the accumulation of dysfunctional organelles, increased oxidative stress, and ultimately neurodegeneration. Although encoding proteins with mitochondrial functions, PARK7 and CHCHD2 cause PD also through different biological pathways including protein aggregation and autophagy. 9 , 10

Biallelic PRKN variants are the most common monogenic determinant of early‐onset PD. 11 PRKN‐PD is characterized by a predominant motor involvement, consisting of a slow disease progression with optimal levodopa response and delayed motor fluctuations/dyskinesias. 12 Lower limb dystonia is a typical phenotypic clue. 11 Apart from neuropsychiatric symptoms and REM‐sleep behavior disorder, non‐motor manifestations like dysautonomia and dementia are significantly under‐represented compared to iPD and GBA1‐PD. 11 , 12 , 13 PINK1‐PD is a mimicker of PRKN‐PD. Levodopa response is excellent, yet fluctuations and dyskinesia, as well as cognitive disturbances appear to be slightly more frequent than in PRKN‐PD. 11

The neuropathological substrate of PRKN‐PD is a pure nigropathy with severe and selective neuronal degeneration in SNc standing out for the absence of Lewy pathology in most cases. 4 This anatomical selectivity is clinically mirrored by its predominant motor presentation. Available evidence for PINK1‐PD suggests mild Lewy pathology alongside relatively selective SNc degeneration 4 . However, this pathology is more extensive than that seen in PRKN‐PD, which may explain the higher frequency of motor fluctuations and cognitive impairment. Accordingly, an increased burden of non‐motor symptoms in monogenic forms like LRRK2‐, GBA1‐, SNCA‐, and VPS13C‐PD, positively correlates with the presence of Lewy pathology. 4 , 7 , 14

Parkinsonism in Mitochondrial Diseases

Primary mitochondrial diseases (PMDs) constitute a highly heterogeneous group of disorders that share an origin in defective oxidative phosphorylation caused by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations or pathogenic variants in nuclear DNA (nDNA) genes encoding for proteins involved in mitochondrial function. 15

Movement disorders are common features of PMDs. 16 Parkinsonism is reported in 12% to 43% of patients with PMDs according to different cohorts. 16 It is usually characterized by late onset (sometimes occurring decades after the presentation of typical features like progressive external ophthalmoplegia [PEO] or optic atrophy), good levodopa response, and paucity of the typical non‐motor features of PD.

As for mtDNA mutations, parkinsonism has been reported mostly in patients with multisystemic involvement. 17 As for nDNA variants, POLG‐ and TWNK‐related diseases are the most associated with parkinsonism, 16 , 18 but other genes have been linked as well (eg, OPA1, SPG7). 19 , 20 Most postmortem studies of patients with POLG‐related parkinsonism have shown dopaminergic neuronal loss without Lewy pathology. 21 , 22 Levodopa‐responsive parkinsonism and nigrostriatal dopaminergic deficits have been described in several cases of TWNK‐related PEO. 18 Neuropathological evidence on TWNK‐related disease is sparse and heterogeneous. 23 , 24

Interestingly, nDNA genes involved in mitochondrial parkinsonism cluster in specific biological pathways, in particular those governing mtDNA maintenance 25 (eg, POLG, POLG2, and TWNK) and mitochondrial homeostasis 26 (eg, OPA1, SPG7). This convergence suggests a potential mechanistic role for these pathways in the selective vulnerability of the nigrostriatal system.

Mitochondrial Dysfunction in iPD

Mitochondrial dysfunction remains a key biological mechanism in PD, even when there is no evident genetic cause. Especially for iPD, mitochondrial dysfunction is the result of a complex interaction between environmental and genetic risk factors.

For instance, several neurotoxins cause parkinsonism through mitochondrial dysfunction: 1‐methyl‐4‐phenyl‐1,2,3,6‐tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) and the pesticide rotenone inhibit complex I of the respiratory chain, while the herbicide paraquat generates free radicals harming mtDNA within dopaminergic neurons. 27 In line with these observations, complex I deficiency was identified in the SNc of postmortem brains of iPD patients. 28

Beyond environmental exposures, mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key modulator of innate and adaptive immune responses in PD. Mitochondrial dysfunction promotes neuroinflammation through NLRP3 inflammasome activation and impaired mitophagy, acting as both a primary neurodegenerative driver and an upstream regulator of maladaptive immune responses. 29

The genetic factors linking mitochondrial dysfunction and iPD consist of both mtDNA and nDNA variants. Evidence implicating mtDNA in iPD includes increased heteroplasmic mtDNA mutations in PD brains, reduced CSF cell‐free mtDNA in PD patients, and PD association with specific mtDNA haplogroups. 30 , 31 , 32

Common variants of mitochondrial genes of nDNA have also been associated with PD. A higher cumulative burden expressed by mitochondria‐specific polygenic risk scores (PRS) has been associated with increased PD risk. 33 Importantly, an oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway‐specific PRS (OXPHOS‐PRS) has been validated in patient‐derived cells, demonstrating measurable differences in mitochondrial respiration between high‐ and low‐OXPHOS‐PRS sporadic PD patients. 34 This supports the use of OXPHOS‐PRS as a precision medicine tool to stratify iPD patients into a biologically defined mitochondrial subgroup, without requiring a Mendelian mutation.

In addition, the burden of nonsynonymous rare variants in nuclear genes involved in mtDNA maintenance appears to be significantly enriched in PD, supporting a key role for disrupted mtDNA maintenance in PD pathogenesis and contributing to its missing heritability. 35

Toward a Conceptual Framework for MitoPD

Building on the evidence presented in the preceding paragraphs, we conceptualize MitoPD in a biological‐pathological‐clinical continuum encompassing three distinguishable entities—monogenic forms, iPD, and parkinsonism in PMDs—which are unified by shared mitochondrial dysfunction and overlapping clinical features (Fig. 1).

Within this framework, we define MitoPD along four main layers: etiological, biological, pathological, and clinical.

Biology of MitoPD

Biological mechanisms of MitoPD range from non‐selective low‐grade defects to specific and severe defects driven by strong etiological factors (Fig. S1). In iPD, mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration by affecting neuronal resilience and amplifying age‐related processes through the accumulation of mtDNA damage. As for patients carrying oligogenic contributors, mitochondrial dysfunction mechanisms converge on defective mtDNA maintenance and homeostasis. Eventually, the biological mechanism associated with monogenic drivers is the most specific: dysfunctional mitochondrial clearance.

Neuropathology of MitoPD

The prototypical neuropathology of MitoPD is epitomized by postmortem findings among PRKN‐PD patients presenting selective degeneration of SNc dopaminergic neurons, in the absence of Lewy pathology (“pure nigropathy”). The observation of Lewy bodies in cases with PRKN, PINK1, or PMDs genes can be explained by a higher complexity related to genetic or non‐genetic factors likely modulating the propensity to develop a detectable Lewy pathology, although this remains limited in burden and largely confined to the SNc rather than diffusely involving other brain regions.

Clinical Features of MitoPD

The clinical features of MitoPD are best exemplified by monogenic forms (PRKN‐ and PINK1‐PD), characterized by early onset, foot dystonia at presentation, slow disease progression, and sustained pharmacological response. Dementia and dysautonomia are typically absent or mild, whereas certain neuropsychiatric features such as obsessive‐compulsive behaviors may be more pronounced. When considered collectively and longitudinally, these “red flags” may orient clinicians toward MitoPD, even though none of these features are individually diagnostic. 12

Patients with PMDs may present with variable phenotypes: parkinsonism may coexist with multisystem manifestations (eg, PEO, optic atrophy and myopathy) or may be the predominant feature, closely resembling iPD. In such cases, mitochondrial involvement may be suspected through recognition of subtle features like ptosis, hearing loss, diabetes, polyneuropathy, or short stature.

Finally, in common forms of PD with mitochondrial involvement the clinical presentation aligns with that of other iPD forms, yet slight clinical distinctions are possible, such as a lower prevalence of hyposmia, dementia, and dysautonomia.

In summary, MitoPD is clinically distinguishable from other forms of PD by the under‐representation of certain common features—such as dysautonomia and dementia—and by the possible presence of findings that are atypical for PD, but characteristic of mitochondrial diseases (eg, ptosis). In this way, MitoPD may be differentiated from other PD subtypes, such as “lysosomal” PD, associated with higher prevalence of non‐motor symptoms and dementia (Table 1).

α‐Syn‐SAA and MitoPD

The emergence of α‐syn‐SAA has revolutionized the field of biological stratification in PD, allowing the identification of aggregated α‐synuclein species in vivo. Unsurprisingly, in biallelic PRKN‐ and PINK1‐PD, α‐syn‐SAA is largely negative in contrast to what is observed in other PD forms (Fig. 2). Positivity was reported only in a subset of PRKN‐PD patients when assessed using neuron‐derived extracellular vesicles from blood, 36 whereas conventional CSF and serum assays have consistently yielded negative results 6 , 37 (Fig. 2, Table S1). Within this context, it is likely that patients with MitoPD would be relatively enriched among α‐syn‐SAA‐negative PD cases.

The relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and α‐synuclein seeding competency remains largely unexplored. In PRKN knockout induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived neurons, α‐synuclein seeding kinetics differ only modestly from controls, 38 unlike GBA1‐ or SNCA‐PD models. 39

Notably, a negative α‐syn‐SAA does not exclude α‐synuclein pathology, as detection may be hindered by assay sensitivity limits, conformational strain variability, sample type, or regional restriction of pathology. 40 Conversely, in multiple system atrophy, α‐syn‐SAA can be positive even though the underlying α‐synuclein pathology is predominantly glial rather than neuronal, reminding us that SAA is a marker of seeding activity, not necessarily of Lewy pathology. 40

Conclusions and Future Directions

Progress in developing effective disease‐modifying interventions will depend on a deeper, patient‐specific understanding of PD biology. In this context, emerging biological definitions of PD—such as those centered on α‐synuclein pathology—represent steps forward, but insufficient to capture the diversity of disease mechanisms involved. To advance the field, translational research must shift its focus toward identifying and characterizing distinct multidimensional subtypes of PD, within an inclusive definition of the disorder that fully embraces its heterogeneity.

In this scenario, we propose MitoPD as an etiological‐biological‐pathological‐clinical subtype of PD. Our proposal does not intend to portray disease subtypes as discrete and mutually exclusive entities. Although monogenic forms represent the “prototypical” anchors of this model, most “idiopathic” patients likely lie along a continuum, harboring different subtype hits to varying degrees. Although establishing a diagnosis of MitoPD at the individual level remains challenging, we outline a multi‐layered biomarker roadmap intended to guide empirical validation and stimulate further research in this direction (Table S2). We anticipate that applying the proposed four‐layered framework to define MitoPD will contribute to the ongoing efforts toward biologically grounded stratification in PD and the design of mechanism‐based interventional trials.
