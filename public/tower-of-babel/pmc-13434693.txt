Modelling the structural evolution, electronic structures and optical properties of Sc6Sen0/- (n = 1–12) clusters
Authors: Liyuan Hou, Jucai Yang, Chenliang Hao, Xueyan Dong, Caixia Dong, Zhaofeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe size-dependent structural evolution of scandium–selenium cluster remains poorly understood, particularly regarding the origin of growth transitions and the emergence of stable building blocks. Here, neutral and anionic Sc6Sen0/- (n = 1–12) clusters were systematically investigated by global search techniques combined with B3LYP density functional theory calculations, with additional single-point validation at the B3LYP-D3(BJ) and DLPNO-CCSD(T) levels. The results reveal a clear two-stage growth behavior with n = 8 as the critical turning point. For n = 1–8, Se atoms sequentially occupy the eight electrostatic potential minima sites on the Sc6 octahedral core, ultimately forming the highly symmetric Chevrel-phase Sc6Se8 cluster. This transition point is governed by both geometric and electronic factors. As a result, the clusters undergo structural reorganization for n = 9–12, giving rise to competing link-like and pentagonal-pyramidal motifs. Stability analyses based on average binding energy and second-order energy difference identify Sc6Se8 as the most distinguished cluster in the series. Simulated photoelectron, vibrational, and ultraviolet–visible spectra provide characteristic fingerprints for structural identification. In particular, Sc6Se8 exhibits pronounced aromaticity, strong visible-light absorption, and favorable excitonic features, highlighting its role as a magic super-atomic building block in the Sc–Se system. Subject terms: Density functional theory, Computational chemistry, Chemical physics

Introduction

Metal selenides represent an important class of functional inorganic materials with unique structural, electronic, and optical properties, enabling broad applications in energy conversion, catalysis, microelectronics, and semiconductor technologies1–3. Representative examples include high absorption coefficient and high efficiency CIGS (Cu(In,Ga)Se2) solar cells4, high quantum yield (Zn,Cd)Se quantum dots5,6, two-dimensional photo- and electrocatalytic (Mo,W)Se2 materials7,8, nano-structural NiSe2 for supercapacitor electrode and superionic CoSe2 conductor9, etc. Besides the above-mentioned applications, nanosized metal selenides possess attractive functionalization capability and improved biocompatibility, which are beneficial for constructing multifunctional platforms for bioimaging, biosensing, targeted drug delivery, and photothermal therapies. Representative examples include MoSe2/Au@PEG for cancer photothermal therapies10, Rh3Se8 for bioimaging guidance11, FeSe-based system for drug-delivery applications12, etc. Driven by the growing needs of modern industry and society, the development of metal selenide nanomaterials has become a pressing priority across multiple scientific disciplines.

Pure selenium clusters possess distinctive structural motifs and electronic characteristics, which underpin their broad applicability in catalysis, energy-storage systems, and environmental remediation technologies13,14. However, the presence of unsaturated Se–Se bonds and exposed lone-pair electrons renders selenium clusters intrinsically unstable, leading to structural degradation that compromises device durability. A feasible approach to improving stability and eliminating dangling bonds is to introduce transition metals into selenium clusters. For example, cadmium selenide clusters have attracted extensive attention due to their size-dependent electronic structures and tunable optical properties15,16. In early 2006, Jose et al. integrated experimental characterization with ab initio density functional theory (DFT) calculation, revealing that the initial core was a (CdSe)3 small cluster, and the presence of the ligand (HDA) assisted its growth to a larger (CdSe)n (n ≥ 14) cluster17. Jager et al. employed a genetic algorithm with the DFT method to obtain the energy minimum CdxSey+ (x + y ≤ 26) structures. The results reveal strong composition-dependent trends, where the HOMO-LUMO and optical gaps can vary by more than 2 eV for a fixed size18. Xu et al. theoretically elucidate the structures and spectroscopic properties of small cadmium selenide clusters, where Cd3Se3, (Cd3Se3)2, (Cd3Se3)3, and Cd4Se4 represent the fundamental wurtzite and zinc blende structures, respectively19. Besides the above-mentioned, owing to the widespread use of CdSe in quantum dot technologies, a variety of theoretical investigations have been explored, such as (CdSe)n (n = 1−16)20, Cd33Se3321, and (CdSe)1622, etc. Cui et al. present a systematic first-principles investigation of the size-dependent absorption, emission, and Stokes shifts of (ZnSe)n (n = 1−13). The phenomenon of large Stokes shifts is attributed to the structural differences and electron delocalization on HOMO and LUMO levels23. Nanavati et al. conducted a comparative study of (ZnSe)n (n = 1−13) and surface passivation (ZnSe)n (n = 1−13) by hydrogen atom. The results show surface passivation with partially charged H atoms removes dangling-bonds, restores local tetrahedral coordination, and significantly widens the HOMO–LUMO gaps24. In addition to IIB group transition metal dopants, noble metals doping selenium clusters have also been studied25,26. Li et al. employ the particle swarm optimization algorithm combined with the B3LYP functional to investigate the geometries, stabilities, and electronic structures of CunSe (n = 1−8) clusters. The CunSe clusters exhibit a clear dimensional evolution from one-dimension (n = 1) to two-dimension (n = 2) and three-dimension (n ≥ 3)27. Guo et al. present a comprehensive DFT investigation of the geometries, relative stabilities, electronic structures, magnetic behaviors, and vibrational and electronic spectra of CumSen (m + n = 5) clusters. The results show that stability is strongly correlated with the number of Cu-Se bonds and reaches a maximum for Cu2Se328. Zhang et al. present a comprehensive theoretical investigation of the geometric and spectroscopic properties of (CuSe)n (n = 2−8). By TD-DFT calculation, such clusters have strong absorption in the 200–400 nm region and permissible excited-state transitions up to 10 eV, indicating that these clusters are promising UV-absorbing materials29. Compared with the transition-metal doping strategy, rare-earth-element doping in selenium clusters has been far less explored and has mainly focused on diatomic systems, such as ScSe, YSe, PrSe, NdSe, SmSe, etc30–32. The substantial potential of transition- and rare-earth–metal-doped selenium nanoclusters as advanced functional building blocks highlights the urgent need to develop new multifunctional materials33.

Despite the substantial progress made for CdSe-, ZnSe-, and CuSe-based clusters, the size-dependent structural evolution and growth mechanism of scandium selenium clusters remain largely unexplored. Sc was selected in this study because, as an early transition metal with accessible valence orbitals and strong affinity toward selenium, it is expected to effectively stabilize selenium frameworks and significantly modulate their electronic structures. Existing studies on Sc–Se systems have mainly focused on diatomic species, and a systematic understanding of how Sc incorporation stabilizes selenium clusters, how the preferred structural motifs evolve with cluster size, and what drives the structural transition in the growth process is still lacking. In particular, it remains unclear whether a well-defined magic-size Sc–Se building block can emerge and how such a motif would influence the electronic and spectroscopic properties of the cluster series.

To address the above-mentioned gap, we systematically investigate neutral and anionic Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12) clusters by combining global structural search with density functional theory calculations. The central question of this work is how scandium incorporation governs the size-dependent growth behavior of selenium clusters, especially why a distinct two-stage evolution occurs around n = 8. The results show that the growth pattern can indeed be divided into two regions: a pre-transition stage (n = 1–8), where Se atoms sequentially occupy the eight favorable adsorption sites on the Sc6 octahedral core and ultimately form the Chevrel-phase Sc6Se8 cluster, and a post-transition stage (n = 9–12), where further Se addition induces structural reconstruction into competing link-like and pentagonal-pyramidal motifs. The change in motivation at n = 8 can be attributed to geometric growth and the complementary principle of electrostatic potential. On this basis, the relationships between structural evolution and relative stability, PES fingerprints, vibrational spectra, ultraviolet-visible spectrum, excitation behavior, aromaticity, and super-atomic character are further established. Particular attention is paid to Sc6Se8, whose outstanding performance across energetic, electronic, vibrational, and optical properties identifies it as a magic super-atomic cluster in the Sc–Se system.

Each cluster of electronic states is marked at figure. The growth of neutral Sc6Sen clusters (n = 1−8) is governed by a progressive face-capping process based on the Sc6 octahedral framework. For the selenium-deficient clusters (n = 1−4), Se atoms are successively adsorbed on different faces of the Sc6 octahedron, and the lowest-energy structures generally favor symmetric occupation of equivalent or opposite faces, indicating that dispersed face adsorption is energetically preferred at the early growth stage. As the Se content further increases (n = 5−6), the Se atoms successively capped on the vacant face of the Sc6 octahedron and formed Se2 subunits, reflecting Se–Se interactions in the cluster framework. For Sc6Se7, the low-lying structures can be regarded as defective derivatives of the Sc6Se8 Chevrel-phase cage. Upon reaching n = 8, the highly symmetric Sc6Se8 Chevrel-phase structure with Oh symmetry is formed, corresponding to the completion of face capping on the Sc6 octahedral core and representing a particularly stable structural motif.

That is, the ground state predictions at the B3LYP-D3(BJ) and DLPNO-CCSD(T) levels show that the energetic ordering for n = 1−8 is fully consistent with the B3LYP results, confirming the robustness of the pre-transition growth regime. In contrast, for n = 9−12, the energetic ordering of the lowest-lying isomers becomes method-dependent. This behavior indicates that the post-transition region is characterized by several near-degenerate motifs on a relatively flat potential-energy surface, where small differences in the treatment of dispersion and electron correlation can alter the ordering of competing structures.

To gain a deeper understanding of the growth mechanism of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1−12), a schematic structural evolution diagram is presented in Fig. 3. In general, the growth can be divided into two stages, namely the pre-transition region (n = 1−8) and the post-transition region (n = 9−12), with n = 8 acting as the critical turning point. This two-stage behavior is jointly governed by geometric and electronic-structure effects.

Schematic diagram of structural growth.

From the geometric point of view, Fig. 4a, b show the van der Waals (vdW) surface area and vdW volume of Sc6Sen (n = 1−12). Both quantities increase gradually with increasing Se content, while a pronounced jump is observed from n = 8 to n = 9, indicating a clear structural transition. From the electronic-structure perspective, Fig. 4c, d displays the electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution of the Sc6 octahedral core and its ESP range. Notably, the Sc6 octahedron possesses eight ESP minima, each located above the center of one octahedral face. Se atoms act as electron acceptors during the formation of Sc6Sen clusters. They tend to gain electrons, so they preferentially adsorb at the electron-rich sites (ESP minima) on the Sc6 octahedral core. As a result, the NPA charges in Table S3 show that Se atoms carry negative charges in the final clusters. In other words, the Sc6 octahedral core provides only eight active adsorption sites for Se atoms. When n < 8, the Se atoms are sequentially accommodated at these eight face-centered active sites, leading to the gradual construction of the Sc6Se8 Chevrel-phase structure. However, once the Se number increases to n = 9, all favorable adsorption sites on the Sc6 octahedral core have already been occupied. Further Se incorporation can no longer be accommodated on the intact octahedral framework without introducing unfavorable geometric crowding and electronic destabilization. As a consequence, the system is driven to undergo structural reorganization, giving rise to the link-like and pentagonal-pyramidal growth motifs observed in the post-transition region.

Photoelectron spectroscopy of Sc6Sen− (n = 1−12)

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is a powerful technique with high energy resolution and strong sensitivity to structural variations, making it particularly suitable for the characterization and identification of nanoclusters. Accordingly, the PES of Sc6Sen− (n = 1−12) were simulated using the Outer Valence Green Function (OVGF) method, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. The PES plots set a Gaussian broadening value of 0.25 eV and select a curve of energy less than 5.6 eV. Each peak from energy low to high is denoted as X, A to E in alphabetical order. The vertical detachment energy (VDE) obtained from the ΔSCF and OVGF methods is summarized in Fig. 6a, b, respectively. For Sc6Sen− (n = 1−7), the simulated OVGF PES exhibits a relatively dense distribution of ionization features in the low-energy region, with small separations between adjacent peaks. In contrast, for n > 8, the PES profiles become much more resolved, with well-separated and clearly distinguishable peaks. The VDE values obtained from ΔSCF at the B3LYP and B3LYP-D3(BJ) levels are the same. As shown in Fig. 6a, the VDE values of Sc6Sen− (n = 1−8) obtained from the ΔSCF at B3LYP and OVGF methods exhibit a good linear correlation, with R2 values of 0.95 and 0.92, respectively. This consistency indicates that the predicted VDEs are robust and may serve as useful fingerprints for identifying specific clusters in future experiments. For Sc6Se9−, the two competing motifs, namely the link-like (L) and pentagonal-pyramidal (P) structures, exhibit similar PES profiles; however, they can still be distinguished by their VDE values, with the P structure showing a larger VDE than the L structure. For Sc6Sen− (n = 10−12), the L and P motifs can be readily distinguished by both the number of PES peaks and their VDE values, with the L structures exhibiting larger VDEs than the corresponding P structures. As shown in Fig. 6b, the VDE values predicted by the ΔSCF and OVGF methods display similar size-dependent trends for both the L and P motifs, further supporting their use in the structural identification of specific clusters.

Relative stabilities of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1−12)

To evaluate the stability of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12) clusters, the average binding energy (ABE) and second-order energy difference (Δ2E) were employed as the primary descriptors. The ABE and Δ2E values are defined by Eqs. (1), (2), and (3).

Specifically, ABE reflects the global stability of a cluster by measuring its average binding strength, whereas Δ2E characterizes the relative stability of a given size with respect to its neighboring clusters and is therefore particularly sensitive to size-specific stabilization or magic-size behavior. Since ABE usually varies smoothly with cluster size, it mainly provides information on the overall strengthening of the cluster framework, but does not by itself identify whether a particular size is exceptionally favored. In contrast, Δ2E is well suited to highlight local stability anomalies within the size series. Therefore, the combined use of ABE and Δ2E provides a more balanced assessment of cluster stability from both global and local perspectives.

As shown in Fig. 7a, b, the B3LYP level results reveal that the ABE and Δ2E provide complementary views of cluster stability. In Figs. 7a, for 1 ≤ n ≤ 8, the ABE values of both neutral and anionic clusters generally increase as the number of Se atoms grows, indicating that the progressive incorporation of Se atoms substantially stabilizes the Sc6 framework. For Sc6Sen (n = 9−12), the ABE curve shows a slight decline and then levels off, suggesting that the major energetic stabilization has already been achieved upon formation of the Sc6Se8 framework. Due to the closed shell electronic structure of neutral clusters (except for Sc6Se5), the ABE of neutral clusters is consistently higher than that of their corresponding anionic counterparts, which can be proved by NPA analysis in Table S3 (Supplementary Information). The Δ2E curves in Fig. 7b exhibit three distinct peaks at n = 4, 6, and 8, indicating that such a cluster is more stable than its adjacent ones. Notably, the combined ABE and Δ2E results at the B3LYP level consistently identify Sc6Se8 as the most stable cluster with a magic-size feature in the series from both global and local perspectives. The same conclusion is further supported by the DLPNO-CCSD(T) level results shown in Fig. 7c, d. Although the ground state structures predicted by DLPNO-CCSD(T) level for n = 9–12 are not fully identical to those obtained at the B3LYP level, the overall ABE trend remains unchanged, and Sc6Se8 still exhibits the largest ABE value in the series. More importantly, the Δ2E curves in Fig. 7d again highlight Sc6Se8 as the most pronounced stability maximum in the size series.

Stability trends of neutral and anionic Sc6Sen clusters are evaluated through average binding energies and second-order energy differences at two levels of theory.

a Average binding energies of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12) at the B3LYP level. b Second-order energy differences (Δ2E) at the B3LYP level. c ABEs at the DLPNO-CCSD(T) level. d Δ2E at the DLPNO-CCSD(T) level.

Therefore, the Sc6Se8 Chevrel-phase structure with Oh symmetry exhibits both the highest overall stability and the strongest relative stability with respect to its neighboring sizes, and can be identified as the magic cluster in the series.

Besides the above-mentioned, the energy gap (Egap) as a type of important intrinsic physicochemical property, calculated by Eq. (4), is collected in Fig. S5.

To further determine the effect of temperature, AIMD simulations were performed for each ground-state structure at 298.15 K for 2000 fs, and the results are presented in Figs. S6 and S7. It can be clearly seen that after 2000 fs, the structure is not decomposing; each minimum-energy structure obtained from the AIMD simulation corresponds to the selected structure, further indicating that the optimized structure at the B3LYP level is a local minimum on the potential energy surface.

Infrared and Raman spectra of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1−12)

Infrared and Raman spectra provide key structural characteristics and bond vibrations of clusters. The main difference between an IR-active mode and a Raman-active mode lies in the corresponding selection rule: IR intensity is associated with a change in dipole moment during vibration, whereas Raman intensity is governed by a change in polarizability. Consequently, the dominant peaks in the two spectra of the same cluster are not necessarily identical. In general, vibrational modes dominated by directional Se–Sc bond stretching or bending tend to generate stronger IR intensity, whereas symmetric breathing or collective framework deformations more effectively enhance Raman intensity. The theoretical IR and Raman spectra of neutral Sc6Sen (n = 1–12) are shown in Figs. 8 and 9. The anionic clusters show spectral features and dominant vibrations similar to neutral counterparts seen in Supplementary Information, Fig. S8.

The blue arrows indicate vibrational displacement direction.

IR and Raman spectra of Sc6Sen (n = 9–12).

As shown in Fig. 8, for Sc6Sen (n = 1−8), the strongest Raman peaks are mainly characterized by breathing modes centered on the Sc6 octahedron core, involving concerted inward and outward radial displacements. These motions of the Sc6-centered skeleton produce pronounced Raman responses. In contrast, the strongest IR peaks are mainly associated with directional Se–Sc stretching, stretching-coupled wagging, asymmetric stretching, scissoring, or bending vibrations, which induce larger dipole-moment changes during vibration. Therefore, for n = 1–8, the dominant Raman peaks mainly reflect breathing-type motions of the Sc6 core framework, whereas the dominant IR peaks are governed primarily by localized directional bond vibrations.

As shown in Fig. 9, the IR spectra of Sc6Sen (n = 9−12) exhibit two internally consistent patterns according to structural type. For the link-like structures, the strongest IR peaks at 347, 337, 333, and 323 cm−1 are dominated by direction-selective Se–Sc stretching-dominated framework deformations. For the pentagonal-pyramidal structures, the strongest IR peaks at 356, 351, 346, and 329 cm-1 display the same character.

Overall, the IR and Raman spectra of Sc6Sen (n = 1–12) provide useful vibrational fingerprints for structural identification. For n = 1–8, the dominant spectral features in the 236–272 cm−1 region are characterized by breathing motions of the Sc6 centered framework, which are characteristic of octahedral–core–based structures. In contrast, for n = 9–12, the dominant spectral features shift to the 323–356 cm−1 region, where the strongest peaks of both link-like and pentagonal-pyramidal isomers are dominated by Se–Sc stretching dominated framework deformations, indicating the formation of reconstructed Se–rich motifs. Therefore, the evolution of the characteristic IR and Raman peaks directly reflects the structural transition from Sc6 core-based clusters to reconstructed Se–rich frameworks.

To further elucidate the vibrational characteristics of the Sc doped Se clusters, Sc6Se8 is selected as a representative structure. The potential energy distribution (PED) analysis is performed using the VEDA program developed by Jamróz34,35. The IR and Raman-active vibrational modes of Sc6Se8 are shown in Fig. 10, and the corresponding calculated frequencies νcalc, IR intensities IIR, Raman activities AR, symmetry, PED contributions, and Vibration modes are summarized in Table 1. The most intense IR-active mode at 310.40 cm−1 is a triply degenerate T1u vibration, which corresponds to opposite stretching vibrations between the central Sc4 ring and the Se4 ring on both sides along the X, Y, and Z directions, respectively. The PED analysis shows that the internal coordinates s9, s12, and s10 contribute mainly about 14%, 10%, and 11%, respectively. In contrast, the most intense Raman-active mode at 236.23 cm−1 is an A1g vibration, mainly presenting a breathing vibration of the Sc6 octahedron. Its PED is dominated by the internal coordinate s30, with a contribution of about 13%. These two dominant modes provide a representative example of the different vibrational origins of the strongest IR and Raman responses: the former is dominated by directional stretching motions, whereas the latter is associated with a highly symmetric breathing mode. The mode at 204.49 cm−1 is another triply degenerate IR-active T1u vibration, corresponding to the coupled stretching vibration of the two Sc atoms and opposite torsional vibration of the two Se4 ring along the X, Y, and Z directions. Its major PED contributions come from s26 (13%), s10 (10%), and s2 (10%). The 188.06 cm−1 mode is dominated by a breathing vibration of the Se8 unit. The IR-active mode at 140.70 cm−1 is also a triply degenerate T1u vibration mixing with the bending vibration of Sc6 octahedron and opposite rocking vibration of Se8 along the X, Y, and Z directions, with major PED contributions coming from s25 (12%), s22 (17%), and s16 (10%). The Raman-active modes at 131.05 cm−1 and 111.15 cm−1 both are triply degenerate T2g vibrations. The former involves torsional and stretching vibrations of the Se8 unit, whereas the latter involves bending and rocking vibrations of the Se8 unit together with rocking vibrations of the Sc4 unit. The 122.81 cm−1 mode is a doubly degenerate Eg vibration involving bending, torsional, and breathing vibration along the Z and Y directions, with major PED contributions coming from s19 (15%) and s27 (10%).

Super-atomic character of the Sc6Se8 nanocluster

To elucidate the intrinsic electronic origin of the magic stability of the Sc6Se8 cluster, NPA, Adaptive Natural Density Partitioning (AdNDP)36, and molecular orbital (MO) analysis were carried out, as shown in Table S3 and Fig. 11a, b.

Electronic structure and bonding pattern of the Sc6Se8 cluster revealed by molecular orbital and AdNDP analyses.

a Molecular orbital. b Adaptive Natural Density Partitioning (AdNDP) analysis.

As shown in Table S3, the NPA results indicate that the Se 4s electrons are largely localized. Correspondingly, from Fig. 11b, the AdNDP analysis identifies eight Se-centered one-center two-electron (1c–2e) lone pairs with occupation numbers of about 1.79 |e|, indicating that 16 electrons are localized as nonbonding electron pairs on the Se atoms. The remaining 50 electrons, mainly derived from the Se 4p2 and Sc 3d14s2 valence orbitals, can be assigned to twenty-four 2c–2e bonds and one 6c–2e delocalized bond. So, as shown in Fig. 11b, the closed-shell configuration satisfies the super-atomic rule of 1S21P61D102S21F142P62D10. Therefore, Sc6Se8 can be regarded as a magic cluster with enhanced stability.

Ultraviolet‒visible spectrum of the Sc6Se8 nanoclusters

Because of the outstanding stabilities of Sc6Se8 observed, the further optical properties are explored. The ultraviolet‒visible (UV‒vis) spectrum of Sc6Se8 is calculated by time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT)37 at B3LYP with the BS-3 basis set level. The 120 excited states are considered to cover the full UV‒vis region. The simulated UV–vis spectrum is shown in Fig. 12a with a Gaussian broadening of 0.25 eV, where S denotes the singlet state. As illustrated in Fig. 12a, four distinct absorption peaks can be clearly identified. The strongest peak at 621 nm corresponds to the transition of the ground state S0 to S4 (99.9%). To further understand the transition feature, the natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis38 is performed and shown in Fig. 12b. The transition of S0 → S4 originates primarily from the NTO pair transfer of NTO199 → NTO200 (91.8%), where NTO199 and NTO200 correspond to the highest occupied natural transition orbital (HONTO) and the lowest unoccupied natural transition orbital (LUNTO), respectively. The absorption peak at 503 nm primarily originates from the transitions of S0 → S15, contributing 99.9%. It can be composed of two pairs of NTO transition of NTO199 → NTO200 (48.7%) and NTO198 → NTO201 (48.7%). The second strongest peak appears at 444 nm and is mainly contributed by the S0 → S29 (81.9%) and S0 → S41 (18.0%). The former is dominated by the NTO transitions NTO199 → NTO200 (43.3%) and NTO198 → NTO201 (43.3%), while the latter is primarily associated with the NTO199 → NTO200 (84.4%). The absorption peak at 338 nm primarily originates from the transitions of S0 → S112 with a contribution of 99.9%, which corresponds to four pairs of NTO transitions, including NTO199 → NTO200 (25.1%), NTO198 → NTO201 (25.1%), NTO197 → NTO202 (10.2%), and NTO196 → NTO 203(9.5%).

Excitation behavior analysis of the Sc6Se8 nanocluster

To gain deeper insights into the excitation behavior of Sc6Se8, the hole-electron analysis and inter-fragment charge transfer (IFCT) developed by Lu et al.39 is performed. Figure 14 shows the hole (blue) and electron (gray) iso-surfaces in several key excitations. The fragments 1 and 2 are assigned to the Sc6 octahedron and the Se8 cube. For S0 → S4 (621 nm), the charge transfer is from Se8 to Sc6 with 0.10 a.u. electron. Aromaticity of the Sc6Se8 nanocluster

As optoelectronic building block materials, stability is crucial for maintaining reliable optical responses and long-term device performance. The stability can be effectively evaluated using iso-chemical shielding surfaces (ICSS)40. In three-dimensional space, each point is assigned an ICSS value, which is defined as the negative of the nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS)41. The ICSS is calculated by the gauge-independent atomic orbital (GIAO)42 method at the B3LYP level. As shown in Fig. 15a, the red iso-surface (0.05 ppm) corresponds to magnetic shielding regions, and the blue iso-surface (−0.05 ppm) corresponds to de-shielding regions. Due to the Oh symmetry of the cluster, the shielding and de-shielding regions show a distinct symmetric distribution. Figure 15b shows the ICSS value along the line from the cage center to the outer region. The maximum ICSS value reaches 5.15 ppm at the cage center, indicating a significant magnetic shielding effect in the inner-cage region. As the distance increases, the shielding value gradually decreases. At 0.53 Å out of the center, the ICSS value changes from positive to negative, meaning the critical position from the magnetic shielding region to the de-shielding region. When the distance increases to 1.2 Å, the ICSS value reaches the minimum of -5.96 ppm, corresponding to the strongest magnetic de-shielding effect. Figure 15c shows the color-filled ICSS cross-section. Warm and cool colors correspond to regions with higher and lower magnetic shielding, respectively. The color-filled cross-section agrees with ICSS iso-surfaces, and the cage center exhibits pronounced magnetic shielding. Overall, Sc6Se8 displays aromatic character, which contributes to the enhanced stability of the whole cluster.

Conclusion

In summary, the structural evolution, stability, and spectroscopic properties of neutral and anionic Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12) were systematically investigated by a global search technique combined with density functional theory calculation. The results reveal a distinct two-stage growth pattern with n = 8 as the critical turning point. For n = 1–8, Se atoms sequentially occupy the eight EPS minima sites on the Sc6 octahedral core, leading to the formation of the highly symmetric Chevrel-phase Sc6Se8 cluster. For n = 9–12, further Se incorporation induces structural reorganization into competing link-like and pentagonal-pyramidal motifs. This transition originates from the combined effects of geometric site saturation and electronic-structure reorganization. Method comparison further confirms that the energetic ordering is robust for n = 1–8, whereas the post-transition region is associated with a relatively flat potential-energy surface.

The stability analyses based on average binding energy and second-order energy difference consistently identify Sc6Se8 as the most distinguished cluster in the series. Spectroscopic simulations, including PES, IR, Raman, and UV–vis results, further establish clear structure-dependent signatures that are useful for distinguishing different cluster motifs. The outstanding aromatic, strong visible-light absorption, super-atomic and excitonic properties of Sc6Se8 further indicate that this cluster is not only a stability maximum, but also a suitable building block for next-generation semiconductor optoelectronic materials.

Methods

All computations were carried out using the Gaussian 09 quantum chemistry package43. The initial structures were generated by ABCluster software44–46. Each anionic and neutral Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12) with 400 initial isomers were optimized by B3LYP functional47,48 with BS-1 (Sc for LANL2DZ49 and Se for ECP28MWB50). Second, the low-lying isomers within 0.8 eV were further optimized at the B3LYP level using the BS-II (Sc and Se for def2-TZVP51). Frequency calculations were performed to confirm that the optimized structures correspond to true local minima. Finally, single point energies were calculated at the B3LYP level using the BS-III (Sc and Se for def2-TZVPPD51,52). Visualization of results was performed using Multiwfn53,54 and VMD software55.

o investigate the method effect of Sc6Sen0/− (n = 1–12), each ground state structure was calculated with a single point energy at the BS-III basis set by B3LYP-D3(BJ)47,48,56,57, and DLPNO-CCSD(T)58–60 in ORCA61. The Natural Population Analysis (NPA) was performed by the NBO 762 software.

The solid phase of Sc6Se8 was visualized by VESTA63, and its ultraviolet-visible spectrum was simulated by CP2K64,65 quantum chemistry software using the PBE functional66, with basis set DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH-q1167,68 for Sc and DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH-q667,68 for Se.

The PES spectra were simulated using the Outer Valence Green’s Function (OVGF)69,70 method with the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set for Sc71,72 and Se73, respectively.

The Ab Initio Molecular Dynamics (AIMD) of each cluster was performed by the B97-c functional74 using the ORCA software. Such a method contains the def2-mTZVP basis set and Becke−Johnson damping scheme (D3-BJ).
