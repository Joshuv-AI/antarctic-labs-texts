Treatment Goals for Primary Biliary Cholangitis, an Australian Perspective
Authors: Simone I. Strasser, Alexander J. Thompson, Rohit Gupta, John Lubel, Janine French, Beverly Sacks, Martin Weltman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is an autoimmune liver disease that targets the small intrahepatic bile ducts with diagnosis based on elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels and the presence of anti‐mitochondrial antibodies. Untreated, it may run a progressive course to cirrhosis and hepatic decompensation, at which point liver transplantation is the only effective treatment. This review provides discussion of PBC treatment goals in the Australian context. People with PBC often experience a quality of life burden characterized by fatigue and itch. Extra‐hepatic associations may include osteoporosis, Sjögren's syndrome, and thyroid dysfunction. Established baseline risk factors for progression are male gender, young age at onset (< 45 years), ALP > 1.5 × upper limit of normal (ULN), presence of anti‐gp210 antibodies, and significant liver fibrosis at diagnosis. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is the mainstay of therapy and improves outcomes including transplant‐free survival. Patients with inadequate response to UDCA (about 40%), disease progression, or bothersome symptoms require additional second‐line (2L) therapy. Second‐line treatments include selective peroxisome proliferator‐associated receptor (PPAR) agonists, obeticholic acid (OCA; subject to precautions), and off‐label use of fenofibrate. In Australia, reimbursement for 2L therapies relies on ALP ≥ 1.67 × ULN or total bilirubin between 1 and 2 × ULN after 1 year of UDCA therapy. Normalization of ALP is an aspirational treatment target with increasing evidence for improved outcomes. Proactive and diligent monitoring of patients with PBC allows personalized selection and early initiation of effective and well‐tolerated 2L therapies with potential for improved outcomes.

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is an autoimmune liver disease occurring predominantly in females in which immune‐mediated injury is focused on small intra‐hepatic bile ducts [1]. The complete etiology of PBC remains unknown; however, environmental factors likely trigger an autoimmune process toward bile duct epithelial cells in genetically predisposed individuals [1, 2]. There is significant geographical variability in the prevalence of PBC, although it is increasing in Australia [3] and worldwide [4, 5, 6].

People with PBC may be asymptomatic, but a significant proportion of patients experience a quality of life (QoL) burden due to fatigue, itch, and sicca complex. This chronic cholestatic disease runs a progressive course that can lead to hepatic fibrosis and ultimately cirrhosis and decompensation [1]. Liver transplantation is the only treatment that improves outcome in patients with hepatic decompensation or progressive jaundice [5].

For the past three decades, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) has been the mainstay of therapy and is indicated from time of diagnosis in all patients with PBC as it has a favorable impact on progression and survival [7, 8]. Additional therapy is recommended in patients who do not achieve a satisfactory response to UDCA therapy. Second‐line (2L) treatment options have included fenofibrate and obeticholic acid (OCA), a farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist. In Australia, although fenofibrate is routinely available as a lipid lowering drug, it does not have an approved indication for PBC treatment. In contrast, OCA is indicated for people with PBC with inadequate response or intolerant to UDCA and has been available via the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) since 2021. However, safety concerns or a lack of demonstrable benefit from the Phase 4 COBALT study [9] have led to OCA being withdrawn from use in other markets. The treatment landscape for PBC has been rapidly evolving with two novel peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) agonists, elafibranor and seladelpar, recently being approved by multiple regulators, including the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), for 2L or add‐on treatment of PBC [10, 11]. New therapies represent an opportunity to individualize the treatment journey, with the goal of normalizing liver enzymes, limiting progression of hepatic fibrosis and potentially improving symptoms and long‐term survival [5, 12].

Existing treatment guidelines for PBC from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) [1] and the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) [13] do not yet reflect the evolving landscape and treatment goals. This review aims to provide an up‐to‐date discussion of PBC treatment goals including proactive approaches to monitoring and personalized contemporary treatment in Australia. We position the scientific evidence for an evolving treatment paradigm in the context of current and future drug registration and funded access for Australian patients.

Methods

This is a narrative style expert opinion and does not aim to be a consensus document or provide a systematic review. The authors are experts in the field of PBC from tertiary centers around Australia and attended regular meetings between July and December 2025 to discuss definitions, treatment goals, and practice points and document the updated treatment journey. A patient with lived experience of PBC diagnosis and treatment provided a consumer perspective.

Pathogenesis and Diagnosis

Pathogenesis and Features

PBC is a progressive cholestatic liver disease that when untreated can lead to hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver failure, and death [1, 14]. The pathogenic process involves interplay between genetic and environmental triggers that impact the functioning of small intrahepatic bile ducts [2]. The archetypical liver histology is described as a chronic non‐suppurative cholangitis with granuloma formation alongside progressive destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts. Hallmarks of PBC disease include biochemical markers of cholestasis (particularly elevation of serum alkaline phosphatase [ALP]), in the presence of serum anti‐mitochondrial antibodies (AMA). Mild to moderate increases in transaminases often accompany the elevated ALP and represent hepatocyte injury secondary to bile stasis [11]. Further, there may be an elevation in serum IgM levels and hyperlipidemia [13]. Additional serum markers of PBC, which may be present in AMA‐negative disease, include secondary antibodies, such as GP210, SP100, and PML [15, 16]. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), particularly centromere, homogeneous, speckled, nucleolar, or nuclear dot patterns, may be present in PBC [17].

Diagnosis and Screening

It is not uncommon for females aged 40–70 years to have a diagnosis of PBC made after a finding of elevated serum ALP on routine liver enzyme testing [1]. There may be symptoms such as itch or fatigue that add to suspicion of PBC [2]. Identification of cholestatic liver biochemistry is followed by serum AMA testing [1, 2, 5]. These autoantibodies are strongly associated with PBC, being present in 90% of cases [12]. Notably, patients with normal liver enzymes who have a positive AMA should not be diagnosed with PBC but should be monitored for its development [5]. Testing for PBC‐specific ANA may provide additional diagnostic and prognostic information [1, 12, 13, 18].

A diagnosis of PBC can usually be established without a biopsy although a minority of people will require a liver biopsy to secure the diagnosis [19]. Biopsy can be considered when there is a high suspicion of PBC despite negative autoimmune markers. Liver biopsy may also be indicated throughout the disease course to assess for alternative co‐existent pathology such as metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [19].

Elevated ALP may be of bony origin in osteomalacia, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Paget disease of bone, and bone metastases.

In people who present with cholestatic liver function tests, differential diagnoses to be excluded are biliary obstruction (gallstones, malignant obstruction, benign biliary strictures, chronic pancreatitis), primary sclerosing cholangitis, IgG4‐related disease, drug‐induced cholestasis, sarcoidosis, and atypical presentation of infections [1, 12, 13]. In rare cases where PBC and autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) apparently overlap, determining the primary driving pathology may mean sequential use of PBC‐targeted therapies followed by cautious use of corticosteroids [20]. Here, we focus on the clinical scenario in which PBC is the dominant condition and will not further consider possible PBC‐AIH overlap (or variant) syndrome.

Established diagnostic criteria for PBC comprise two or more of the following: cholestatic liver biochemistry, AMA titer > 1:40, and/or compatible or diagnostic liver histology [19].

Because there is an increased incidence of PBC in first‐degree relatives (FDR), family screening, initially by liver function testing, should be considered for any female FDR aged over 30 [1, 21].

Staging

The emergence of non‐invasive methods for assessment of fibrosis, such as vibration‐controlled transient elastography (VCTE, e.g., Fibroscan) to measure liver stiffness, has reduced the need for staging biopsy. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) may be used as a surrogate marker for PBC progression risk; LSM greater than 9.6 kPa has been associated with a five‐fold increased risk of progression of PBC to cirrhosis and liver‐related death [13]. Increased serum‐based fibrosis scores, such as the aspartate aminotransferase (AST)/platelet ratio index (APRI), fibrosis‐4 (FIB‐4), and enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) score, have also been associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes [22].

Risk Stratification

Baseline Risk Stratification

The strongest risk factors for progression of PBC are male sex, age 45 years or under, and advanced liver fibrosis stage at diagnosis (Table 1) [13]. LSM at baseline independently predicts prognosis in PBC; an LSM cut‐point of 9.6 kPa can be used for risk stratification [13]. Several laboratory markers are useful for identifying patients at risk of worse disease outcomes, including marked elevation of gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT) to 3 × upper limit of normal (ULN) [23], and the presence of PBC‐specific antibodies such as anti‐gp210 [2, 13]. Baseline ALP has been used as a surrogate to predict likelihood of response to UDCA [26]. The presence of decompensated cirrhosis (Child‐Pugh B or C), bilirubin > 2 × ULN, or severe pruritus warrants expedited specialist review [19]. Baseline risk stratification based on broad assessment of patient and disease characteristics at diagnosis may allow early identification of patients who could benefit from early addition of 2L treatment beyond UDCA [19, 24].

Treatment Response Risk Stratification

Biochemical response to UDCA strongly predicts clinical outcomes of patients with PBC [27], and various criteria based on ALP and bilirubin after a year of treatment have been used to guide ongoing management (Table 2). A key threshold is the modified Toronto criteria, defined by persistence of serum ALP > 1.67× ULN and/or serum bilirubin > 2× ULN after 12 months of UDCA therapy, which has been adopted as the main inclusion criteria for clinical trials evaluating novel PBC therapies [33, 34]. The GLOBE score is an internationally relevant and validated risk assessment tool. Age, total bilirubin (TB), albumin, ALP, and platelet count act as independent predictors of liver transplantation or death after 1 year of UDCA therapy [29]. This score allows risk stratification of patients throughout the PBC disease course. It uses age‐specific thresholds beyond which survival significantly deviates from an age‐ and sex‐matched general population [29].

Although all thresholds of ALP are predictive of outcomes, their accuracy for use in identifying high‐risk patients was not found to differ significantly between 1.5 × ULN, 1.67 × ULN, and 3.0 × ULN [13, 26]. Importantly, disease stage affects biochemical response to UDCA, so stage‐specific thresholds such as the Paris‐II criteria have been designed to better fit early‐stage patients [13].

Modern approaches to outcome prediction including the GLOBE score allow easier identification of high‐risk patients.

Recently, the value of serial monitoring of liver stiffness with transient elastography has emerged as a valuable tool to identify disease progression and predict liver‐related outcomes [35, 36]. This should be considered in routine practice.

PBC Treatment

UDCA

UDCA, a hydrophilic bile acid that replaces the hydrophobic or more toxic bile acids from the bile acid pool, is the mainstay of PBC treatment [7, 8, 13, 37, 38]. The recommended dose of UDCA is 13–15 mg/kg/day [39], with people receiving lower doses at risk of sub‐optimal outcomes [13, 40]. Since PBC progresses slowly, individual trials lack the power to address endpoints such as liver transplantation or death. However, long‐term follow‐up of well‐characterized cohorts has shown that the prognosis for people living with PBC has improved significantly as a result of UDCA treatment [29]. Most studies of UDCA have shown beneficial effects using accepted surrogate biochemical parameters. The two most important parameters used to evaluate response to UDCA are ALP and TB [13, 26]. A biochemical response has historically been defined as serum ALP < 1.67 × ULN and bilirubin < 2 × ULN after 12 months of therapy (Table 2). Patients should be monitored for on‐treatment response.

Approximately 60% of patients with PBC achieve ALP < 1.67 × ULN after 12 months of UDCA. However, around 40% are either intolerant or fail to achieve a biochemical response and should be offered 2L therapy to improve prognosis [13, 28, 29, 41]. A study utilizing the UK‐PBC Research cohort observed that one of the parameters associated with inadequate response to UDCA was a delay in starting therapy. This suggests that in PBC, delay to optimal treatment may reduce the likelihood of a response to treatment [32]. There are many clinical implications of diagnosis that can potentially be better managed via early diagnosis and early treatment initiation [42].

Where patients do not achieve ALP < 1.67 × ULN, it is important to differentiate inadequate response to UDCA, which requires 2L therapy, from other explanations. These include the incorrect dose, lack of adherence, and poor absorption or other disorders leading to ALP elevation, including MASLD.

Second‐Line Treatment in Australia

While UDCA therapy has been associated with improvements in liver biochemistry, slower liver histologic progression, and reduced need for liver transplantation, it does not improve fatigue, bone disease, or autoimmune features of PBC and additional strategies are required for these disorders [38]. Advances in the understanding of bile acid physiology have led to development of new agents such as FXR agonists and selective (PPAR) agonists that improve cholestasis in patients with PBC and are predicted to reduce the risk of disease progression [43]. In Australia, the 2L treatment options for patients who do not achieve a biochemical response to UDCA include fenofibrate, OCA, and the recently approved novel PPAR agonists (Table 3). While the literature refers to budesonide as a treatment option [13, 51], a placebo‐controlled randomized trial showed no histologic benefit [51], and it is not recommended in Australian practice. The patient's age and their acceptance of combination therapy are additional considerations for treatment selection (Table 4) [5].

Access to PBS Reimbursed 2L Therapy

To be eligible for subsidized 2L treatment of PBC, Australian patients must either be undergoing concurrent treatment with UDCA or be documented intolerant. Patients must have experienced an inadequate biochemical response to UDCA at a therapeutic dose (Table 2). Specifically, the PBS criteria to access add‐on therapy with OCA or elafibranor are ALP ≥ 1.67 × ULN (having accounted for age, gender and laboratory to laboratory variances in the definition of “normal”) or a TB level of one to two times the ULN, despite 52 weeks of UDCA. Patients with severe liver disease or immunocompromised patients are unable to access subsidized 2L treatment. Concurrent PBS prescribing of PPAR agonists and OCA is not permitted.

Fibrates—Fenofibrate and Bezafibrate

Fibric acid derivatives have been used for many years for the treatment of hyperlipidemia and act mainly by modulation of PPAR activation. Bezafibrate is a weak pan‐PPAR activator, and fenofibrate is more specific for the alpha isoform [52] (Figure 2). Fenofibrate is approved for use in Australia for hyperlipemia and dyslipidemia but not PBC. Evidence for benefit of fenofibrate includes a 12‐month randomized controlled trial in which 117 treatment naïve patients with PBC were assigned to treatment with UDCA plus fenofibrate 200 mg daily or UDCA alone [49]. More of the UDCA plus fenofibrate group met the biochemical response target compared to the UDCA‐only group (81.4% [95% confidence interval (CI) 69.9%–92.9%] versus 64.3% [95% CI 51.9%–76.8%]; p = 0.048) [49]. A further randomized controlled study confirmed addition of fenofibrate can improve biochemical indexes of PBC patients who had an incomplete response to UDCA [48]. A recent review noted that fenofibrate as an adjunct to UDCA in PBC may offer both biochemical and survival benefits [53].

While there is good evidence for bezafibrate use in PBC [50, 54, 55, 56], it is not TGA approved for any indication and availability is limited to individual patient use via Special Access Scheme applications.

There have been safety signals such as elevated creatinine related to fibrates, and prescribers should be cautious when using fibrates in patients with kidney disease [12, 50]. Like other 2L agents, fibrates are discouraged in patients with decompensated liver disease [38].

Fibrates can be considered as a low‐cost add‐on for patients with PBC who have an inadequate response to UDCA (Table 3). In the current Australian context, when a patient is responding to fibrate therapy (serum ALP < 1.67 × ULN), it is reasonable to continue treatment with monitoring, and if any high‐risk factors arise, consider switching to a novel PPAR agonist. Ease of access to fenofibrate also allows 2L treatment of patients with serum ALP 1–1.67 × ULN despite 12 months of UDCA.

OCA

OCA is an FXR agonist that was listed on the PBS in 2021 for the 2L treatment of PBC (Table 3). Approval was based on the Phase 3 POISE study, which enrolled 217 participants with an inadequate response to UDCA (ALP > 1.67 × ULN and/or bilirubin > 2 × ULN after at least 12 months of treatment) or intolerance to UDCA. Participants were randomized to receive OCA 10 mg or OCA 5–10 mg, or placebo for 12 months [47]. The primary end point of ALP < 1.67 × ULN, with a reduction of at least 15% from baseline, and a normal TB level was achieved in more patients in the OCA 5‐ to 10‐mg group (46%) and the OCA 10‐mg group (47%) than in the placebo group (10%; p < 0.001 for both comparisons). However, patients in the active treatment groups experienced a higher rate of serious side effects [47]. There was a higher incidence of pruritus in the OCA 5‐ to 10‐mg group (56%) and 10‐mg group (68%) than in the placebo group (38%). Seven patients (10%) in the 10‐mg group, one (1%) in the 5‐ to 10‐mg group, and zero patients in the placebo group discontinued treatment owing to pruritus [47]. The subsequent COBALT study found that OCA treatment may reduce the risk of negative clinical outcomes in comparison with an external real‐world control cohort, although the primary endpoint was not met [9].

OCA is contraindicated in patients with advanced cirrhosis, defined as cirrhosis with current or prior evidence of liver decompensation or portal hypertension. A “boxed warning” to this effect has been added to the Australian Product Information although the product remains registered by the TGA [57]. As of November 2025, OCA has been voluntarily withdrawn from use by the sponsor in the US due to safety concerns. OCA has also been deregistered by European regulators due to an unfavorable benefit–risk ratio following the COBALT study. We recommend close monitoring of any patient with compensated cirrhosis receiving OCA [38].

In an Australian context, OCA remains a valuable agent in the management of PBC with inadequate response to UDCA. It is recommended that patients receiving OCA therapy who have good tolerability and a target biochemical response continue on it, particularly in view of emerging real‐world outcomes consistently demonstrating a lower risk of hepatic decompensation, liver transplantation, and death in patients treated with OCA [58, 59]. Patients with significant pruritus while receiving OCA should discontinue and switch to a PPAR agonist. In patients without pruritus who meet criteria for addition of 2L therapy, clinicians must decide whether to introduce OCA or a PPAR agonist, accounting for the longer follow‐up and demonstrated benefits of OCA in PBC management. Emergence of side‐effects may lead to a change of therapy, and contraindication to the use of OCA in patients with cirrhosis, portal hypertension or prior decompensation should be noted.

Selective PPAR Agonists—Elafibranor

The dual PPAR‐alpha and PPAR‐delta agonist elafibranor was evaluated in the Phase 3 ELATIVE study (Table 3) [34]. In this randomized controlled trial, 161 patients with PBC with an inadequate response to or unacceptable side effects with UDCA were assigned (in a 2:1 ratio) to receive once‐daily elafibranor 80 mg, or placebo. Treatment with elafibranor resulted in a marked reduction from baseline in ALP as early as 4 weeks which was sustained through week 52. Decreases in immunoglobulin G (IgG), IgM, and anti‐inflammatory markers were also observed. In patients with moderate‐to‐severe pruritus, the mean change in the Worst Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI‐NRS) did not differ significantly between the elafibranor group and the placebo group after 6 or 12 months [34]. Possible reductions in moderate‐to‐severe pruritus were observed using other rating scales after 12 months [34]. Adverse events (AEs) that were more common in the active treatment group included abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting [34]. Elafibranor was approved by the TGA for treatment of PBC in March 2025 [60]. It was listed on the PBS in November 2025 and can be prescribed in combination with UDCA but not OCA.

Selective PPAR Agonists—Seladelpar

The selective PPAR‐delta agonist seladelpar was evaluated in the placebo‐controlled Phase 3 RESPONSE study among patients with PBC with inadequate response or intolerance to UDCA [33]. The primary endpoint was biochemical response, defined as an ALP < 1.67 × ULN, with a decrease of 15% or more from baseline, and normal TB at month 12. A greater percentage of patients treated with seladelpar 10 mg (61.7%) had a biochemical response versus the placebo group (20%). A quarter of the seladelpar group also achieved normal serum ALP. Significant reductions in the pruritus numerical rating scale were observed [33]. AEs leading to discontinuation of treatment were rare [33]. Common AEs included headache (7.8% of seladelpar and 3.1% of placebo) and abdominal pain (7.0% versus 1.5%). Creatinine and creatine kinase levels were similar between groups [33]. Interim results of the long‐term open‐label ASSURE study that included patients from the RESPONSE study showed that the composite biochemical response endpoint (ALP < 1.67 × ULN, ALP decrease > 15%, and normal TB) was met in 73% of patients at month 12, 73% of patients at week 24 and 81% of patients at month 30 [46, 61]. Seladelpar has been approved for use in Europe, the United States, and more recently, Australia.

Rationale for Combination Therapy in PBC

Triple Therapy

Triple therapy under specialist supervision is rarely required for people with PBC. Both double and triple combinations may employ synergistic complementary pathways [62]. Evidence for triple therapy with fenofibrate or bezafibrate plus OCA in combination with UDCA is limited. Such a triple combination was evaluated in a retrospective cohort of 58 patients with difficult‐to‐treat PBC [63]. More recently, 48 Canadian patients using triple combination therapy for PBC were reported to experience significant improvement in ALP, with ALP normalization for 30% of patients after 6 months [62].

Novel Therapy Combinations

As further classes of therapy become available, clinicians should be aware of the underlying pathways of mechanism of action and potential for drug–drug interactions. Selective PPAR agonists should not be used concurrently with fibrates. Specifically, there is no rationale for combining fenofibrate with elafibranor or seladelpar as they have similar mechanisms of action (Figure 2). Potentially, combination treatment with an ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) inhibitor and a drug that reduces bile acid synthesis might both ease pruritus and protect against cholestatic liver injury [43].

Symptom Assessment and Management

Symptom Screening and Patient Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs)

Many patients are asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis. While end‐stage liver disease is associated with progressive symptom burden, the severity of symptoms does not necessarily correlate with the stage of disease in PBC [19]. A goal of PBC treatment is to be free of symptoms, including itch and fatigue, or maintain a low symptom burden [12, 13]. EASL recommends actively screening patients with PBC for the presence of symptoms such as itch, fatigue, and dry eye or sicca complex by asking about them specifically. Once identified, these symptoms can be quantified and monitored [13].

Current and recent studies in PBC incorporate PROMs for QoL and key symptoms. These include the PBC‐40 QoL questionnaire, and its abbreviated form the PBC‐10 [33, 64]. The PBC‐40 comprises 40 questions across six domains relating to fatigue, emotional, social, and cognitive function, general symptoms, and itch [65]. The PBC‐10 is a short, validated questionnaire for assessing health related QoL in patients with PBC in clinical practice [64]. For assessment of itch, the 10 point pruritus numerical rating scale (NRS) has been used to assess the impact of elafibranor, seladelpar, and linerixibat [33, 34, 66, 67]. Another method used in a clinical trial context is the 5D itch scale, a single‐page questionnaire that grades itch according to duration, degree (severity), direction (getting better or worse), disability (impact on QoL), and distribution (skin sites affected), although this has not been validated for PBC [68]. The PROMIS Fatigue Short Form 7a provides a brief questionnaire and is part of the PROMIS‐29 QoL instrument [69, 70]. Directed questioning will uncover symptoms of sicca syndrome. Patient support organizations can also supply helpful tools for tracking and managing fatigue and pruritus.

Itch Management

Itch is a common and troublesome symptom of PBC, traditionally managed with cholestyramine [68]. Antihistamines tend not to be useful. Only a minority of patients taking UDCA obtain benefit from improvement in cholestatic itch. Pruritus is a known dose‐related side effect of treatment with OCA [47, 71], however, there is some evidence for benefit of fenofibrate or bezafibrate on pruritus in PBC [72, 73]. Additional options include off‐label rifampicin, naltrexone or sertraline, each of which has precautions for use [12, 19, 74, 75]. Gabapentin also has a role in the broader context of chronic pruritus [76]. Clinical experience in Australia favors the use of rifampicin and fibrates. Close monitoring is required for rifampicin, due to its potential impact on liver enzymes [19, 20]. Various clinical trials with IBAT inhibitors are underway for difficult‐to‐treat cholestatic pruritus. The Phase 2b trial of linerixibat in patients with PBC and pruritus GLIMMER [77] led to a Phase 3 randomized, placebo‐controlled study (GLISTEN, NCT04950127) [78]. Additional IBAT inhibitors volixibat (NCT05050136) and maralixibat (NCT01904058) are in Phase 2. Other agents being studied for cholestatic pruritus include a kappa‐opioid receptor agonist (NCT03995212) and an inhibitor of the MRGPRX4 receptor on small nerve fibers (NCT05525520). Liver transplantation may be a treatment option of last resort for patients with severe intractable pruritus and dramatically relieves itch.

Fatigue

More than half of PBC patients experience fatigue, which tends to progress over time [38]. Other causes of fatigue such as iron deficiency or thyroid disease should be considered and managed; exercise programs and mindfulness may help [12, 20]. Studies of novel agents such as setanaxib, a dual inhibitor of NADPH oxidase isoforms NOX1 and NOX4, suggested improvement in clinically significant fatigue [79, 80]. It should be noted that, unlike other symptoms of PBC, fatigue often persists after liver transplantation [81].

Extra‐Hepatic Associations of PBC

Symptomatic management of extra‐hepatic associations such as Sjögren's syndrome or osteoporosis, and co‐existent autoimmune disease, may be necessary [13, 20]. Patients can be impacted by multiple comorbid conditions, which should be managed wholistically through tailored therapy.

Person‐Centered Care

To further personalize care, patient considerations and goals beyond clinical measures can be uncovered during early discussion (Table 4). Patient treatment preferences related to cost, ease of use, and tolerance for side effects can be factored in, and QoL prioritized [43]. The impact of pruritus illustrates the impact of symptoms on a person's life well; it is pervasive, causes sleep deprivation, exacerbates fatigue, and may precipitate depression and social isolation [66, 67, 82].

Pregnancy

Historically, pregnancy in PBC was considered rare; however, it is increasingly encountered due to rising maternal age and earlier PBC diagnosis [83]. While women with adequately treated, non‐cirrhotic PBC generally maintain preserved fertility and experience outcomes comparable to the general population, evidence suggests a slightly elevated risk profile [83, 84, 85, 86]. A case control study that included 233 consecutive female patients with PBC and 367 age‐matched controls managed prior to 2015 found no higher risk of miscarriage or preterm delivery in PBC patients [84]. The outcome of pregnancy was favorable in all pregnancies that occurred after PBC diagnosis, UDCA treatment was continued and there was no apparent exacerbation of the liver disease. Further, no fetal malformations occurred and the main problem was placenta previa, observed in 1% of cases [84]. A more recent meta‐analysis pooled 11 studies and suggested patients with PBC may face a higher incidence of miscarriage (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.02–1.58; p = 0.03), have a history of abortion (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.09–2.07; p = 0.01) or be at risk of pre‐term delivery [83]. PBC patients also had an increased likelihood of lifetime smoking (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.17–3.23; p = 0.01).

Although most pregnancies are well‐tolerated, patients with PBC should be monitored for pruritus, which often worsens in the second and third trimesters, and for a biochemical flare in the postpartum period [86]. While patients should be reassured regarding the likelihood of favorable outcomes, multidisciplinary care is recommended to manage potential complications and optimize maternal–fetal health. Patients with established cirrhosis face significantly higher risks, including esophageal variceal bleeding and hepatic decompensation, necessitating preconception counseling and close monitoring by hepatology and maternal–fetal medicine specialists.

For patients presenting with apparent new‐onset cholestasis during pregnancy, clinical vigilance is paramount. In such cases, the diagnostic workup should include serological testing for antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA) to differentiate PBC from intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) [87]. As the distinction between PBC and ICP cannot be made in pregnant women with a diagnosis of PBC, guidelines related to monitoring of non‐fasting bile acid levels in patients with pruritus and clinical interventions in response to elevated bile acid levels should align with Society of Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand (SOMANZ) recommendations for ICP [87].

Emerging Treatment and Assessment Paradigm

Monitoring Schedule

The PBC treatment paradigm is being reviewed so that individual risk is assessed at baseline, and earlier initiation of 2L drugs for high‐risk patients is considered (Figure 3). All PBC patients should be reviewed at least yearly. Closer monitoring of patients who stand to benefit most from provision of add‐on treatment could significantly improve disease outcomes [29, 88]. ALP and bilirubin measurements are key, because they are the two strongest variables used to predict PBC prognosis [13]. However, concerningly, a retrospective cohort study found large proportions (33% to 48%) of US patients treated with UDCA for PBC did not have ALP testing within the recommended 1 year timeframe [89]. A UK audit of the degree to which EASL guidelines were met in the “real‐world” revealed that assessment of bone density and assessment of clinical symptoms (pruritus and fatigue) had the lowest likelihood of being met. This may have been related to lack of tools for symptom assessment [90]. Implementation of a care bundle at 12 hospitals with variable levels of compliance with British Society of Gastroenterology guidelines on the management of PBC significantly improved adherence. The care bundle incorporated a checklist of quality standards and a summary of the guidelines [91].

Early Assessment of Biochemical Response

Measurement of biochemical response after 12 months of UDCA treatment is the most widely validated means to identify patients in need of 2L therapies to prevent progression [13]. Evaluation at 6 months may be equally discriminatory when planning treatment escalation [92, 93]. For high‐risk patients (Table 1) there is a case for evaluation at 3 months. However, in Australia, funded access to 2L therapy with OCA or elafibranor requires a 12‐month period on UDCA monotherapy if tolerated.

Treatment Monitoring for Disease Progression

Non‐invasive fibrosis staging is replacing the historical need for biopsy in assessing progression [5, 22]. Imaging using VCTE is used to measure liver stiffness and to monitor progression [35, 36, 94]. Worsening liver stiffness indicates progression, but it is not expected to improve in response to treatment. The ELF is a blood test that combines measurements of three direct markers of fibrosis: hyaluronic acid (HA), procollagen III amino‐terminal peptide (PIIINP), and tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP‐1) [95, 96]. If the ELF threshold for severe disease (a score of 10 or more) is exceeded, this is a significant finding; however, ELF is not generally used as a continuous measure of progression. The ELF algorithm was evaluated as a non‐invasive measure of PBC disease severity that provides useful long‐term prognostic information [68, 69]. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is an imaging test used as an alternative to VCTE and has been evaluated in PBC [95, 97]. There is limited availability of ELF or MRE in Australia due to lack of government Medicare funding.

Moving the Goal Posts—Complete Biochemical Response

A new concept of “deep response” is emerging, [25, 43, 98] An aspirational treatment goal that has been gathering support is to normalize liver chemistries [5, 12, 98]. A complete biochemical response has been defined as normal levels of TB, ALP, aminotransferases, and albumin, as well as a normal prothrombin index (a derived measure of prothrombin time), at 24 months [50]. Analysis from the Global PBC Study Group found that the 10‐year survival rate of patients with bilirubin in the low normal (< 0.6 × ULN) range was improved compared with those with bilirubin > 0.6 × ULN (91% vs. 79%, p < 0.001). Ten‐year survival was also better in patients with normal ALP compared with those with ALP above ULN but < 1.67 × ULN. (93% vs. 86%) [98].

In a subsequent retrospective international cohort study of 1047 patients with PBC, who attained an adequate response to UDCA according to Paris‐II criteria, improved clinical outcomes (liver‐related complications, liver transplantation, or death) were observed in patients with truly normal ALP levels compared to those with persistent ALP elevation between 1.1 and 1.5 × ULN who remained at risk of poor outcome [25]. Where ALP remains above ULN, further therapeutic efforts have the potential to improve outcomes and reduce the symptomatic burden of living with a chronic condition for people with PBC [25, 43].

Complications and Coexisting Conditions

The aims of long‐term treatment are to minimize the symptom burden, slow disease progression, and manage complications. Liver complications of PBC include esophageal varices and variceal bleeding, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatorenal syndrome, and HCC [20]. To this end, all patients with PBC should have structured lifelong follow‐up including monitoring of bilirubin, ALP, AST, albumin, platelet count, and elastography [13]. All patients with cirrhosis should also be screened for HCC with 6‐monthly ultrasound [1].

Patients with PBC are also at risk of a range of extra‐hepatic complications including osteoporosis and osteopenia, hyperlipidemia, and diseases such as thyroid dysfunction, Sjögren's disease, CREST syndrome, Raynaud's syndrome, celiac disease, and scleroderma [2]. Hence, comprehensive annual monitoring of PBC patients includes measurement of vitamin D, cholesterol, osteoporosis risk and thyroid function tests, some of which may require endocrinology referral [1, 13, 19, 20]. Medicare‐funded bone density testing is available every 2 years for patients with liver disease. Hyperlipidemia in PBC should be considered in the context of concurrent cardiovascular risk factors. In the absence of other metabolic risk factors, hyperlipidemia in the context of PBC is not usually an indication for lipid lowering therapy. Obesity and type 2 diabetes, often with MASLD, are increasingly common globally and may coexist in people with PBC, representing an additional focus for management.

Conclusions

PBC treatment has evolved substantially in the last decade. Current pharmacological therapy not only presents opportunities for optimization of therapy to prevent disease progression but also to improve symptom burden. The focus has shifted away from accepting an improvement in liver biochemistry to well defined targets and is moving toward deep remission (ALP normalization). UDCA remains the mainstay of treatment, but for those patients stratified at high risk of progression or with inadequate response to UDCA, step‐up to combination therapy should be considered. The concept of treatment success should be extended from simply meeting biochemical thresholds to aiming for disease control and survival benefits, symptom resolution, and improved QoL. Refined endpoint definitions can provide clarity for treating clinicians on how to measure success and to determine whether to maintain current line of therapy or consider additional or alternative options. Individualized, evidence‐driven treatment of patients with PBC can increasingly be achieved through diligent monitoring of disease and symptom burden and strategic use of treatments from multiple classes and mechanisms of action.
