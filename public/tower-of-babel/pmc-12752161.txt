Two-stage surgery of atrioesophageal fistula after radiofrequency catheter ablation: case report and literature review
Authors: WeiRan Hou, Ting Fang, Qinchen Liu, Haibo Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrioesophageal Fistula is a rare complication after radiofrequency catheter ablation. In this article, we report a case of atrioesophageal fistula, confirmed by a Chest computed tomography scan and treated by two-stage surgical repair. In addition, we review the case reports about atrioesophageal Fistula in PubMed, with the aim of presenting here our experience and sharing our surgical approach. The methods of treatment and prevention of such complication are also reviewed in this article.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Atrioesophageal fistula, Radiofrequency catheter ablation, Atrium repair, Cervical esophagogastric anastomosis

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia worldwide. The estimated number of individuals with AF globally was 33.5 million [1]. Radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) is often recommended to the patients who fail or decline pharmacological therapy. RFCA is generally safe, but complications may occur. Atrioesophageal Fistula(AOF) is an extremely rare(< 0.3%) but potentially life-threatening complication after RFCA [2, 3].

In this article, we present a case of AOF that was successfully treated by surgery, review the case reports about AOF in PubMed, with the aim of presenting here our experience and sharing our surgical approach.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old man with a history of AF and RFCA was admitted to our hospital. Ten years ago, the patient underwent initial RFCA for atrial fibrillation and recovered uneventfully. Three weeks after the repeat RFCA for recurrent atrial fibrillation, he presented with fever, rigors, chest pain. Emergency blood routine showed white blood cell 8.39 × 10^9/L, neutrophil 6.41 × 10^9/L, neutrophil percentage 76.4%, procalcitonin17.49ng/ml. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrates air bubbles within the mediastinum on coronal (Fig. 1A), transverse (Fig. 1B) and sagittal planes (Fig. 1C). Despite the absence of hematemesis, a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s history and diagnostic findings led to the diagnosis of an AOF. After Multi-Disciplinary Treatment discussion, surgical treatment was recommended, including atrium repair and thoracic esophagectomy in the first stage and esophagogastric anastomosis in the second stage.

Thoracic CT scan. Coronal position (A) and Transverse position (B) and sagittal position (C): Presence of bubbles behind the left atrium (arrowheads). Postoperative sagittal position (D): Presence of tubular stomach (arrowheads)

The surgery was conducted through the 5th right poster lateral thoracotomy. The patient was cannulated through the ascending aorta, with venous cannulation through the right atrium. The ascending aorta was then cross-clamped, and cardiac arrest was induced with cardioplegia. The cardiopulmonary by-pass was established(Fig. 2A), and the left atrium was opened. A 20-mm mucosal defect was observed posterior to the right of the opening of the left inferior pulmonary vein (Fig. 2B). Autologous pericardium was harvested and sutured to cover the ulcer surface to seal the fistula (Fig. 2C). After heart re-beating, thoracic esophagectomy was continued. In the process of releasing the esophagus, it was found that the esophagus and pericardium adhered closely at the level of the inferior pulmonary vein, and difficult to separate. To prevent damage to the repaired atrium, esophagus was dissociated from the muscular layer of the esophagus. After resection of the thoracic esophagus, the distal esophagus was closed and pushed into the abdominal cavity, then the diaphragmatic hiatus was sutured. The proximal esophagus was dragged out from neck for esophagostomy(Fig. 2D). Then the chest is thoroughly flushed and closed.

(A) Venous cannulation through the right atrium (arrowheads). (B) A 20-mm mucosal defect was observed after the left atrium was opened (arrowheads). (C) Atrial surface after suture of mucosal defect (arrowheads). (D) The cervical esophagostomy

The surgery was completed in the supine position with jejunostomy and gastrostomy. In addition to a jejunostomy, a gastrostomy was sited on the lesser gastric curvature. This decision was based on three key considerations: first, by connecting the gastrostomy tube to negative-pressure drainage in the immediate postoperative period, we aimed to ensure the secure healing of gastric cardia closure. Second, the tube provides a long-term secure access for enteral nutrition. Finally, in the second stage surgery, both the gastrostomy site and the lesser curvature can be resected without compromising the construction of a tubular gastric conduit. The postoperative recovery was uneventful, the infection was well controlled, and the nutritional status was stable. Three months after the first stage operation, the second stage operation was successfully completed. With the patient supine, the operation began with a midline upper abdominal incision for gastric mobilization and construction of a tubular gastric conduit. Following this, commencing posterior to the xiphoid process, the retrosternal space was entered under direct vision. Dissection—both blunt and sharp—was carried along the posterior aspect of the sternum to create a tunnel extending to the neck. The gastric conduit was then delivered through this tunnel, and a cervical esophagogastrostomy was completed. Postoperative CT demonstrates optimal positioning of the tubular stomach (Fig. 1D). The patient was discharged after 15 days of the second stage operation.

Discussion and conclusions

AOF was first reported in 2004 as a complication of RFCA for AF [4]. The anatomical relationship between the esophagus and the left atrium makes the esophagus vulnerable to thermal injury during treatment. Thermal injury of RFCA is the basis of AOF, and repeated RFCA increases the risk of AOF. In this case, the fistula developed after the second time RFCA.

At present, it has been reported that esophageal temperature monitoring is used to prevent AOF, but some studies have shown that when esophageal temperature monitoring is used, the esophagus is more vulnerable to injury, a better prevention method is active cooling of the esophagus, which reduces esophageal injury by 83% [5, 6]. In addition, pulsed field ablation, as a new ablation method, can also reduce the damage to the esophagus during ablation [7].

Chest pain and fever are usually the first symptoms of AOF, which was presented in this case. Because of the atypical symptoms, AOF is often not diagnosed at the first time, which will make patients miss the opportunity of treatment. If the condition progresses, the patient may develop hematemesis. The occurrence of hematemesis indicates a poor prognosis and is often a sign of a massive, fatal hemorrhage. Therefore, how to diagnose at an early stage is a great challenge for clinicians. CT and the early clinical manifestations of the patient can provide the basis for diagnosis, but it requires clinicians to keep a high alert for AOF. In this case, chest CT revealed the presence of air behind the left atrium for the first time, which was one of the CT manifestations of AOF.

Once the diagnosis is established, endoscopic or transesophageal ultrasonography should be avoided, as these procedures may cause air emboli into the blood and cause hemodynamic disturbance, resulting in death [8]. In this case, the patient had a history of cerebral infarction, which was considered to be caused by infectious emboli in the blood. In the case report by Reudor Grinberg et al., a 67-year-oldman with AOF experienced left hemiparesis and head CT showed acute ischemia in the left distal M3 territory. A 64-year-old woman with AOF died and a CT scan of the head revealed widespread brain ischemic infarcts [9].

The treatment of AOF has been a hot topic of discussion. The therapeutic strategies for AOF encompass surgical, endoscopic, and conservative approaches. Surgical intervention involves thoracotomy for combined repair of the left atrial and esophageal defects, while non-surgical options include esophageal stenting and conservative management with aggressive pleural drainage and systemic sepsis treatment. The conservative approach to AOF is viewed with skepticism owing to its link to a very high mortality rate. The use of esophageal stent implantation as a treatment method remains controversial. Because the placement of an esophageal stent may further damage the esophagus and heart, leading to more serious complications. In addition, the esophageal stent makes infection difficult to control. In the case report by Berthoud V et al., a 66-year-old patient with AOF died of embolism 48 h after placement of an esophageal stent. The autopsy indicated displacement of the esophageal stent and the presence of bubbles in the left ventricle and left atrial appendage [10]. In the study of Roland Richard Tilz, patients with AOF who received surgical treatment had significantly lower mortality than those who received endoscopic treatment and conservative treatment [11]. A review of the literature identified that non-surgical management of AOF was associated with a very high mortality, with only 3 survivors among 22 reported cases. This contrasts sharply with the surgical outcomes, where 24 of 33 patients survived.

Different surgical methods also have a significant impact on the prognosis of patients. In patients with severe sepsis and critical condition, the less invasive primary repair (involving simple esophageal closure or digestive tract reconstruction) predisposes them to anastomotic leakage. This leak, in turn, can worsen sepsis and impair atrial repair healing, setting off a vicious cycle that often proves fatal. In this case, the surgery is performed in two stages. The advantage of this method is that it can avoid poor healing of the atrial wound that is caused by the fistula in the digestive tract. After improvement in the patient’s condition in the second stage of surgery, the esophageal-gastric anastomosis will heal better. The cardiac wound is prioritized for treatment, which has the advantage of avoiding massive bleeding that may be caused in the process of releasing the esophagus.

Early diagnosis and early surgical treatment are indispensable for the treatment of AOF. Chose the appropriate surgical method will greatly improve the survival rate of patients, and active intervention is of great significance to improve the prognosis.
