Activation functions enabling the addition of neurons and layers without altering outcomes
Sergio López-Ureña

Abstract
In this work, we propose activation functions for neuronal networks that are refinable and sum the identity . This new class of activation functions allows the insertion of new layers between existing ones and/or the increase of neurons in a layer, both without altering the network outputs. Our approach is grounded in subdivision theory. The proposed activation functions are constructed from basic limit functions of convergent subdivision schemes. As a showcase of our results, we introduce a family of spline activation functions and provide comprehensive details for their practical implementation.

Introduction

In recent years, neural networks (NNs) have achieved remarkable success across various domains, including image recognition, natural language processing, and predictive modeling. This widespread success is largely attributed to their ability to learn complex patterns and representations from vast amounts of data. As a result, the architecture of NNs has evolved to include a large number of parameters and layers, enhancing their capacity to solve intricate problems. However, this growth in complexity also poses significant challenges, particularly in optimizing and managing these extensive networks (as discussed by
Deng et al. [2020]
, for instance).

In this line, the optimization of architectures has become a strategic research topic (as explored by
Elsken et al. [2019]
,
Prellberg and Kramer [2018]
). In these works, the progressive modification of the network and the reuse of fitted parameters are key ideas for efficient training (according to
Löf [2019]
). Growing a network—either by increasing the number of neurons in a layer (widening) or by adding new layers (deepening)—creates opportunities for further optimization. A prominent strategy involves transferring the knowledge acquired by a trained NN to a wider or deeper one through
function-preserving
transformations. Crucially, this approach preserves the function of the original network, ensuring that the new NN produces identical results to its predecessor. Preserving the acquired knowledge facilitates the training process of the new larger NN (as demonstrated in
Jin et al. [2019]
).

Existing methods, such as Net2Net by
Chen et al. [2015]
and NetMorph by
Wei et al. [2016]
, have shown promising results compared to training enlarged networks from scratch. However, as
Wei et al. [2019]
observes, these approaches suffer from an initial performance drop during training due to excessive zero-padding in parameter matrices. To this issue,
Wei et al. [2019]
proposes tensor decomposition with randomized initialization, a computationally demanding solution. For layer insertion, prior methods relied on idempotent activation functions (a very restrictive condition, as in
Chen et al. [2015]
) or parameter-dependent activation functions, consisting in a convex combination of some activation function with the identity, as in
Wei et al. [2016]
,
Wei et al. [2019]
.

The present work introduces a novel framework grounded in approximation theory for NN function-preservation. For layer widening, we propose a subdivision-inspired splitting mechanism wherein each neuron is decomposed into scaled and shifted copies. This allows for more localized adjustments within the network, akin to multiresolution analysis, and is achieved through the use of
refinable
activation functions. For layer insertion, we present parameter-free activation functions that sum the identity—a condition that is less restrictive than idempotence. This solution also emerges naturally from subdivision theory, extending its conceptual foundations to neural network design.

The proposed method offers three key advantages. First, it provides an explicit and efficient formula for computing parameters, which circumvents computationally expensive routines and extensive zero padding. Second, it allows for refined modifications via scaling-shifting mechanisms that align with the principles of multiresolution analysis. Finally, it enables layer insertion, regardless of the activation functions employed in other layers.

From now on, we consider
multilayer perceptrons
defined by sequences of layer operators
L:\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
,

L(x)=\sigma(Wx+b)=\left[\sigma\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{0}-1}w_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i}\right)\right]_{i=0}^{n_{1}-1},

where the parameters
W\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}}
and
b\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
are referred to as
weights
and
biases
, respectively. Here,
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
denotes the activation function applied componentwise to the vector
Wx+b
.

Our method hinges on two key properties: The activation function must be
refinable
and must
sum the identity
. The former permits the subdivision of a neuron into multiple neurons (Theorem
3
), while the latter enables the insertion of new layers (Theorems
5
and
7
), both operations maintaining the outcomes of the NN unchanged.

Definition 1
.

A function
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
is
refinable
if there exists
\tau\in\mathds{R}
and some coefficients
a_{l}\in\mathds{R}
,
l=0,\ldots,A-1
,
A\in\mathds{N}
, such that

\sigma(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}a_{l}\sigma(2t+\tau-l),\quad\forall t\in\mathds{R}.

(1)

Definition 2
.

Let
I\subset\mathds{R}
be an interval containing
0
in its interior.
A function
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}

sums the identity
in
I
iff
\exists\mu\in\mathds{R}
and
B\in\mathds{N}
such that

t=\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma(t+\mu-l),\qquad\forall t\in I.

(2)

Activation functions constructed from
basic limit functions
of

are defined in this paper. For an introduction to this topic, readers are referred to
Cavaretta et al. [1991]

Dyn [1992]

Dyn and Levin [2002]

Subdivision theory provides numerous examples of refinable functions, such as the B-Splines, which are examined by
Dyn [1992]
. However, these refinable functions are compactly supported, which inherently makes them non-monotone, whereas activation functions are typically monotone. In Section
3.2
, we demonstrate how non-decreasing refinable functions can be constructed using compactly supported refinable functions.

Previous research has connected the theory of refinable functions and subdivision schemes with NN theory, though in a different way from our approach.
Daubechies et al. [2022]
studied the capability of Multi-Layer Perceptrons to approximate refinable functions. That paper and its references illustrate the wide range of applications for refinable functions, including Computer-Aided Design (through subdivision theory), multiresolution analysis (such as wavelet theory), and Markov chains. NNs have also been employed to design subdivision schemes in a data-driven approach, as shown by
Liu et al. [2020]
.

Before addressing the general case in Section

2
Refining a neural network

2.1
Increasing the number of neurons of a layer by subdivision

This section demonstrates that each neuron can be split into
A
neurons without modifying the outcomes of the NN, where
A
was introduced in Definition
1
. Consequently, the number of neurons in any layer can be multiplied by
A
. To achieve this, the weights and biases must be updated according to the formulas provided in Theorem
3
.

Consider a NN that includes three consecutive layers of neurons, comprising
n_{0},n_{1},n_{2}
neurons, respectively. We denote the operators connecting them by

\displaystyle L^{0}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},\quad L^{0}(x)=\sigma^{0}(W^{0}x+b^{0}),\quad W^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}},\quad b^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},

\displaystyle L^{1}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{2}},\quad L^{1}(x)=\sigma^{1}(W^{1}x+b^{1}),\quad W^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}},\quad b^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{2}},

where
\sigma^{0}
is a refinable activation function, while
\sigma^{1}
may not be refinable. We propose to split a neuron of the middle layer by updating
L_{0},L_{1}
with the two new layer operators

\displaystyle\overline{L}^{0}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}_{1}},\quad\overline{L}^{0}(x)=\sigma^{0}(\overline{W}^{0}x+\overline{b}^{0}),\quad\overline{W}^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}_{1}\times n_{0}},\quad\overline{b}^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}_{1}},

\displaystyle\overline{L}^{1}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}_{1}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{2}},\quad\overline{L}^{1}(x)=\sigma^{1}(\overline{W}^{1}x+b^{1}),\quad\overline{W}^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{2}\times\bar{n}_{1}},

in a way that the outcomes of the layers are not altered, or mathematically speaking:

L^{1}\circ L^{0}=\overline{L}^{1}\circ\overline{L}^{0}.

The layer with
n_{1}
neurons is increased to
\bar{n}_{1}:=n_{1}+A-1
neurons. The parameters
\overline{W}^{0},\overline{b}^{0},\overline{W}^{1}
are defined in Theorem
3
.

Since the neurons in a layer are arbitrarily sorted, we can focus on subdividing the first neuron of the layer to simplify the notation.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\sigma^{0},\sigma^{1}
be activation functions, being
\sigma^{0}
refinable as in (
1
).
Given
W^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}}
,
W^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
and
b^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
, we define the new weights
\overline{W}^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{(n_{1}+A-1)\times n_{0}}
,
\overline{W}^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{2}\times(n_{1}+A-1)}
and biases
\overline{b}^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}+A-1}
as

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{0}_{l,:}:=2W^{0}_{0,:},

\displaystyle\quad\overline{b}^{0}_{l}:=2b^{0}_{0}+\tau-l,

\displaystyle\quad\overline{W}^{1}_{:,l}:=a_{l}W^{1}_{:,0},

\displaystyle\qquad l=0,1,\ldots,A-1,

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{0}_{i+A-1,:}:=W^{0}_{i,:},

\displaystyle\quad\overline{b}^{0}_{i+A-1}:=b^{0}_{i},

\displaystyle\quad\overline{W}^{1}_{:,i+A-1}:=W^{1}_{:,i},

\displaystyle\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n_{1}-1,

where
W_{i,:}
and
W_{:,i}
denote the
i
-th row and column of a matrix
W
, respectively.
Then,

L^{1}\circ L^{0}=\overline{L}^{1}\circ\overline{L}^{0}.

Proof.

First, we show that a part of
L^{0}
is independent of the zero neuron (the one being subdivided), and that the refinability property (
1
) can be used in
[L^{0}(x)]_{0}
:

\displaystyle[L^{0}(x)]_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{0}\left(W^{0}_{i,:}x+b^{0}_{i}\right)=\sigma^{0}\left(\overline{W}^{0}_{i+A-1,:}x+\overline{b}^{0}_{i+A-1}\right)=[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{i+A-1},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n_{1}-1,

\displaystyle[L^{0}(x)]_{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{0}\left(W^{0}_{0,:}x+b^{0}_{0}\right)\overset{\eqref{eq_refinable}}{=}\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}a_{l}\sigma^{0}(2W^{0}_{0,:}x+2b^{0}_{0}-l+\tau)=\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}a_{l}\sigma^{0}(\overline{W}^{0}_{l,:}x+\overline{b}^{0}_{l})=\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}a_{l}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{l},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad W^{1}_{:,0}\cdot[L^{0}(x)]_{0}=\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}a_{l}W^{1}_{:,0}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{l}=\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,l}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{l}.

Now, we conveniently separate the zero neuron (that is being split) from the rest in the definition of
L^{1}
:

\displaystyle L^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}-1}W^{1}_{:,i}x_{i}+W^{1}_{:,0}\cdot x_{0}+b^{1}\right),

\displaystyle\Rightarrow L^{1}(L^{0}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}-1}W^{1}_{:,i}\cdot[L^{0}(x)]_{i}+W^{1}_{:,0}\cdot[L^{0}(x)]_{0}+b^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}-1}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,i+A-1}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{i+A-1}+\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,l}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{l}+b^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1}\left(\sum_{i=A}^{n_{1}+A-2}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,i}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{i}+\sum_{l=0}^{A-1}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,l}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{l}+b^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}+A-2}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,i}\cdot[\overline{L}^{0}(x)]_{i}+b^{1}\right)=\overline{L}^{1}(\overline{L}^{0}(x)).

∎

Remark 4
.

Theorem
3
can be applied to every neuron, resulting in the multiplication of the number of neurons in the layer by
A
. In that case, the weights and biases are updated as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{0}_{Ai+l,:}=2\overline{W}^{0}_{i,:},\quad

\displaystyle\overline{b}^{0}_{Ai+l}=2b^{0}_{i}+\tau-l,\quad

\displaystyle\overline{W}^{1}_{:,Ai+l}=a_{l}\overline{W}^{1}_{:,i},\quad

\displaystyle i=0,\ldots,n_{1}-1,\quad l=0,\ldots,A-1.

Observe that this operation can be repeated as many times as desired, arbitrarily increasing the number of neurons in the layer.

Algorithm
1
in
A
shows how Theorem
3
can be used to widen a layer while preserving the NN function.

2.2
Inserting new layers by summing the identity

In this section, it is demonstrated that new layers can be inserted into a neural network without altering the outcomes, provided that an activation function that sums the identity is used.

Let us consider a NN with two consecutive layers of neurons with
n_{0},n_{1}
neurons. Let
L
be the layer operator connecting them:

L:\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},\quad L(x)=\sigma(Wx+b),\quad W\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}},\quad b\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},

where
\sigma
is an activation function that may not sum the identity.
We are interested in inserting a new layer with
\bar{n}
neurons between them.
This implies splitting the layer operator
L
into two separate operators:

\displaystyle L^{0}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}},\quad L^{0}(x)=\sigma^{0}(W^{0}x+b^{0}),\quad W^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}\times n_{0}},\quad b^{0}\in\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}},

\displaystyle L^{1}

\displaystyle:\mathds{R}^{\bar{n}}\to\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},\quad L^{1}(x)=\sigma(W^{1}x+b^{1}),\quad W^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times\bar{n}},\quad b^{1}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}},

where
\sigma^{0}
must sum the identity. Choosing
W^{0},W^{1},b^{0},b^{1}
according to Theorems
5
or
7
, we can ensure that

L(x)=(L_{1}\circ L_{0})(x),\quad\forall x\in\Omega\subset\mathds{R}^{n_{0}},

(6)

for some set
\Omega
, which depends on the interval
I
where the identity is summed (see Definition
2
).
\Omega
can be made arbitrarily large to ensure that (
6
) holds for all data in the training and test sets, as discussed in Remarks
6
and
8
. Consequently, the outcomes of the NN remain unchanged for the given data and their surroundings.

The effect of this separation is the insertion of a new layer with
\bar{n}
neurons between the layer with
n_{0}
neurons and the layer with
n_{1}
neurons. This can be accomplished in two ways: one with
\bar{n}=Bn_{0}
(Theorem
5
) and the other with
\bar{n}=Bn_{1}
(Theorem
7
), where
B\in\mathbb{N}
is determined by the identity summing property (see Definition
2
). Hence, the first one might be convenient when the number of neurons in the previous layer is smaller than the number of neurons in the next layer (
n_{0}\leq n_{1}
), while the second one might be preferable in the opposite case. The new weights and biases are defined according to these theorems.

Theorem 5
.

For any
W\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}}
,
b\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
,
\beta>0
and any
\sigma^{0}
summing the identity, we have that
L(x)=(L_{1}\circ L_{0})(x)
,
\forall x\in\Omega\subset\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}
holds for
\bar{n}=Bn_{0}
and

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}:\beta x_{i}\in I,\ i=0,\ldots,n_{0}-1\},

\displaystyle W^{0}_{l+iB,:}

\displaystyle:=\beta(e^{i})^{T},\quad b^{0}_{l+iB}:=\mu-l,\quad W^{1}_{:,l+iB}:=\frac{1}{\beta}W_{:,i},\quad b^{1}:=b,

for
i=0,\ldots,n_{0}-1
,
l=0,\ldots,B-1
, and
e^{i}
is the
i
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega
be.
First, we separate the contribution of each neuron in the layer operator
L
:

\displaystyle L(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma(Wx+b)=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}W_{:,i}x_{i}+b\right).

We use that
\sigma
sums the identity as follows:

\displaystyle x_{i}=\frac{1}{\beta}\beta x_{i}\overset{\eqref{defi_sum_identity}}{=}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma^{0}(\beta x_{i}+\mu-l),

which holds because
\beta x_{i}\in I
by hypothesis.
Now, we can write

\displaystyle L(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}W_{:,i}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma^{0}(\beta x_{i}+\mu-l)+b\right)=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\beta^{-1}W_{:,i}\sigma^{0}(\beta x_{i}+\mu-l)+b\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}W^{1}_{:,l+iB}\sigma^{0}(\beta x_{i}+\mu-l)+b\right).

Observe that

\sigma(\beta x_{i}+\mu-l)=\sigma(\beta(e^{i})^{T}x+\mu-l)=\sigma(W^{0}_{l+iB,:}x+b^{0}_{l+iB})=[L^{0}(x)]_{l+iB}.

Then,

\displaystyle L(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}W^{1}_{:,l+iB}[L^{0}(x)]_{l+iB}+b\right)=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=0}^{Bn_{0}-1}W^{1}_{:,i}[L^{0}(x)]_{i}+b\right)=(L^{1}\circ L^{0})(x).

∎

Remark 6
.

If practice, it is desirable that the NN remains invariable under this layer-addition operation for some set of data. If
0
is in the interior of
I
(as demanded in Definition

), we can choose
\beta

since it implies that

|\beta x_{i}|=\frac{\delta|x_{i}|}{2\sup\{|y_{j}|\ :\ j\in\{0,\ldots,n_{0}-1\},\ y\in D\}}<\delta

and then
\beta x_{i}\in I
for all

,

.

Theorem 7
.

For any
W\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{0}}
,
b\in\mathds{R}^{n_{1}}
,
\beta>0
and any
\sigma^{0}
summing the identity, we have that (
6
) holds for
\bar{n}=Bn_{1}
and

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n_{0}}:\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})\in I,\ i=0,\ldots,n_{1}-1\},

\displaystyle W^{0}_{i+ln_{1},:}

\displaystyle:=\beta W_{i,:},\quad b^{0}_{i+ln_{1}}:=\beta b_{i}+\mu-l,\quad W^{1}_{i,:}:=\beta^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}(e^{i+ln_{1}})^{T},\quad b^{1}:=0,

for
i=0,\ldots,n_{1}-1
,
l=0,\ldots,B-1
, and
e^{i}
is the
i
-th vector of the canonical basis of
\mathds{R}^{Bn_{1}}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,n_{1}-1\}
be. Using that
\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})\in I

\displaystyle[L(x)]_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})=\sigma(\beta^{-1}\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i}))\overset{\eqref{eq_identity}}{=}\sigma\left(\beta^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma^{0}(\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})+\mu-l)\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\beta^{-1}\sigma^{0}(W^{0}_{i+ln_{1},:}x+b^{0}_{i+ln_{1}})\right)=\sigma\left(\beta^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}(e^{i+ln_{1}})^{T}\sigma^{0}(W^{0}x+b^{0})\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(W^{1}_{i,:}L^{0}(x)\right)=[L^{1}(L^{0}(x))]_{i}.

∎

Remark 8
.

As in Remark
6
, we can choose
\beta
sufficiently small to guarantee that
D\subset\Omega
, where
D
is the set of data that was being processed by
L
.

Let
\delta>0
be such that
(-\delta,\delta)\subset I
. We select

\beta:=\frac{\delta}{2\sup\{|(W_{i,:}y+b_{i})|\ :\ i\in\{0,\ldots,n_{1}-1\},\ y\in D\}}.

For all
x\in D
, it implies that

|\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})|=\frac{\delta|W_{i,:}x+b_{i}|}{2\sup\{|(W_{j,:}y+b_{j})|\ :\ j\in\{0,\ldots,Bn_{1}-1\},\ y\in D\}}<\delta

and then
\beta(W_{i,:}x+b_{i})\in I
for all
i=0,\ldots,Bn_{1}-1
. That is,
D\subset\Omega
.

The implementations of two methods that insert a new layer into a NN are provided in Algorithms
2
and
3
in
A
, which use Theorems
5
and
7
, respectively.

3
Refinable activation functions that sum the identity

In this section, a method for constructing activation functions
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
that are non-decreasing, continuous, refinable and sum the identity is shown. Before discussing the general results based on subdivision theory, the particular case of B-Splines is introduced.

3.1
Spline activation functions

Let us consider the B-Splines basis functions defined recursively by

\displaystyle\phi_{B^{0}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }0\leq t<1,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\phi_{B^{d}}(t)=(\phi_{B^{d-1}}*\phi_{B^{0}})(t)=\int_{t-1}^{t}\phi_{B^{d-1}}(s)ds,

\displaystyle d\geq 1,

(7)

where
*
denotes the convolution product. In particular, it can be computed (see the Reproducibility section) that

\phi_{B^{1}}(t)=\max\{0,1-|t-1|\},\quad\phi_{B^{2}}(t)=\begin{cases}t^{2}/2,&\text{if }0<t\leq 1,\\
\frac{3}{4}-(t-\frac{3}{2})^{2},&\text{if }1<t\leq 2,\\
\frac{1}{2}(3-t)^{2}&\text{if }2<t\leq 3,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

A more direct definition is

\phi_{B^{d}}(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{d+1}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{d!}\binom{d+1}{l}\max\{t-l,0\}^{d},\quad d\geq 1.

(8)

For a brief overview of B-Splines, refer to
Dyn [1992]
, and for a more comprehensive understanding, consult
De Boor [1978]
. What is significant for our purposes is that
\phi_{B^{d}}
is refinable:

\phi_{B^{d}}(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{d+1}2^{-d}\binom{d+1}{l}\phi_{B^{d}}(2t-l).

(9)

Another properties to take into account is that
\phi_{B^{d}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{d-1}
, its support is
(0,d+1)
and (according to IX-(v) in
De Boor [1978]
)

1=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\phi_{B^{d}}(t-i),\quad t-\frac{d+1}{2}=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}i\phi_{B^{d}}(t-i).

(10)

The equations in (
10
) are related to the
generation of first-degree polynomials
property in subdivision theory and are crucial for the construction of activation functions that sum the identity, as we will see.

For a given refinable function, the associated activation function can be defined as follows.

Definition 9
.

Let
\phi:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
be a refinable function with support contained in
(0,d+1)
. The activation function
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
associated to
\phi
is defined by

\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right).

(11)

For
\phi=\phi_{B^{d}}
, we denote
\sigma=\sigma_{B^{d}}
and we call it the
spline activation function
of degree
d
.

Now, the main properties of the spline activation function are enumerated. Some of these properties are inherited from the properties of B-Splines, while others are proven using the more general result presented in Theorem
13
. The proof can be found in Section
3.2
.

Theorem 10
.

For any
d\in\mathds{N}
, the activation function
\sigma_{B^{d}}
fulfils

(a)

\sigma_{B^{d}}(t)=-1/2
, if
t\leq-d/2
,
\sigma_{B^{d}}(t)=1/2
, if
d/2\leq t
, and
\sigma_{B^{d}}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\phi_{B^{d}}(t+\frac{d}{2}-m)
, if
-d/2\leq t\leq d/2
.

(b)

It is
\mathcal{C}^{d-1}
and odd-symmetric.

(c)

It is refinable with
\tau=d/2
,
A=d+1
and
a_{l}=2^{-d}\binom{d}{l}
.

(d)

For any
B\geq d
, it sums the identity in the interval
I=[-\frac{B-d+1}{2},\frac{B-d+1}{2}]
with
\mu=\frac{B-1}{2}
.

(e)

\sigma_{B^{d}}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{l=0}^{d}(-1)^{l}\binom{d}{l}\max\{t+d/2-l,0\}^{d}.

(f)

It fulfils the recursive formula

\sigma_{B^{d+1}}(t)=\int_{t-1/2}^{t+1/2}\sigma_{B^{d}}(s)ds=(U*\sigma_{B^{d}})(t),
where
U
is the box function:
U(t)=1
if
|t|<1/2
and
U(t)=0
otherwise.

(g)

\sigma_{B^{d}}^{\prime}(t)=\phi_{B^{d-1}}(t+\frac{d}{2})\geq 0
.

According to the last theorem, the derivative of
\sigma_{B^{d}}
is known, which is crucial for applying gradient-based optimization algorithms during the training stage. However, to implement backpropagation, it is necessary to compute the derivative with respect to the function value. The following proposition deals with it for the cases
n=1,2
.

Proposition 11
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{B^{1}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }|\sigma_{B^{1}}(t)|<\frac{1}{2},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\sigma_{B^{2}}^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1+2\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)},&\text{if }-\frac{1}{2}<\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)\leq 0,\\
\sqrt{1-2\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)},&\text{if }0<\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)<\frac{1}{2},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,
\sigma_{B^{1}}^{\prime}(t)=U(\sigma_{B^{1}}(t))
and
\sigma_{B^{2}}^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{\max\{0,1-2|\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)|\}}
.

Proof.

For any
d\in\mathds{N}
, by Theorem
10
-
(a)
, we know that
\sigma_{B^{d}}^{\prime}(t)=0
if
|t|\geq\frac{d}{2}
, that is, if
|\sigma_{B^{d}}(t)|=\frac{1}{2}
. For
d=1
, it is important to note that the function is not differentiable at
t=-1/2
and
t=1/2
, but the derivative can be computed in the rest of the domain.
\sigma_{B^{1}}^{\prime}(t)=1
, if
|\sigma_{B^{1}}(t)|<\frac{1}{2}
, follows from (
3
).

For
d\geq 2
, the function is differentiable everywhere, but the result is not so direct. By Theorem
10
-
(g)
, we know that
\sigma_{B^{d}}^{\prime}(t)>0
for
t\in(-\frac{d}{2},\frac{d}{2})
, thus
\sigma_{B^{d}}:(-\frac{d}{2},\frac{d}{2})\to(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
is bijective. Using the inverse function theorem (for univariate functions), we know that

\sigma_{B^{d}}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{(\sigma_{B^{d}}^{-1})^{\prime}(\sigma_{B^{d}}(t))},\quad|t|<\frac{d}{2}.

However, it is not easy to compute the inverse, in general, since it involve solving a polynomial equation of degree
d
. We can compute it for
d=2
: By (
3
),

\sigma_{B^{2}}(t)=\begin{cases}t(1+t/2),&\text{if }-1<t\leq 0,\\
t(1-t/2),&\text{if }0<t<1.\end{cases}

For
t\in(-1,0]
, we can solve
z=t(1+t/2)\in(-\frac{1}{2},0]
, whose solutions are
t=-1\pm\sqrt{1+2z}
. Since
\sqrt{1+2z}\in(0,1]
, then
-1+\sqrt{1+2z}\in(-1,0]
and
-1-\sqrt{1+2z}\in[-2,-1)
. In conclusion, the sign must be positive and
\sigma_{B^{2}}^{-1}(z)=-1+\sqrt{1+2z}
for
z\in(-\frac{1}{2},0]
. Analogously, we can deduce that
\sigma_{B^{2}}^{-1}(z)=1-\sqrt{1-2z}
for
z\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
. See the Reproducibility section for details. Summarizing, we have that

\sigma_{B^{2}}^{-1}(z)=\begin{cases}-1+\sqrt{1+2z},&\text{if }-\frac{1}{2}<z\leq 0,\\
1-\sqrt{1-2z},&\text{if }0<z<\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Now we compute the derivative of the inverse function

\displaystyle(\sigma_{B^{2}}^{-1})^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2z}},&\text{if }-\frac{1}{2}<z\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2z}},&\text{if }0<z<\frac{1}{2},\end{cases}

and, finally,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sigma_{B^{2}}^{-1})^{\prime}(z)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-2|z|}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{1+2z},&\text{if }-\frac{1}{2}<z\leq 0,\\
\sqrt{1-2z},&\text{if }0<z<\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

∎

3.2
Subdivision-based activation functions

The vast literature on linear subdivision schemes provides a wide range of examples of refinable functions, like the B-Splines, the Daubechies wavelets, etc. We refer to
Dyn [1992]
,
Dyn and Levin [2002]
for a revision of this theory. In the following, some subdivision concepts will be introduced, marked with italics, without going into details.

Given some initial data,
f^{0}
, a
subdivision scheme
iteratively generates a sequence of data,
\{f^{k}\}_{k\in\mathds{N}}
, that converges to a function, in some sense. For instance, in the context of geometric modeling, the initial data could be a set of points in the plane, and the scheme generates denser and denser sets of points. In the limit, a smooth curve is obtained, with a shape determined by the initial data.

More in details, a subdivision scheme may consist in the repeated application of the recursive formula

\displaystyle f^{k+1}_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathds{Z}}a_{i-2j}f^{k}_{j},\qquad\forall i\in\mathds{Z},

(12)

where
f^{k}=[f^{k}_{i}]_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\in\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
is a bi-infinite bounded sequence and
a=[a_{j}]_{j\in\mathds{Z}}
is a finitely supported sequence, called the
mask
of the scheme. For a given initial data
f^{0}
, this iterative process
converges
when the elements of
f^{k}
converges uniformly to the point-evaluations of some continuous function. It is well-known in the subdivision theory that the scheme converges if, and only if, there exists a continuous compactly-supported function
\phi:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
(called
basic limit function
) such that

\phi(t)=\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}a_{l}\phi(2t-l),\quad\forall t\in\mathds{R}.

(13)

Thus,
\phi
is a refinable function.
In addition, for the initial data
f^{0}
, the subdivision scheme converges to the function
\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}f^{0}_{l}\phi(t-l)
. To simplify the notation, we assume that the support of
a
is
[0,d+1]
, for some
d\in\mathds{N}
, and it can be proven that the support of
\phi
is contained in
(0,d+1)
(see Section 2.3 of
Dyn and Levin [2002]
). This assumption is aligned with the B-Spline case of Section
3.1
and is mild.
Then, the sum in (
13
) is finite,

\phi(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{d}a_{l}\phi(2t-l),\quad\forall t\in\mathds{R},

(14)

which coincides with Definition
1
with
A=d+1
and
\tau=0
.

The theory on linear subdivision schemes is well-developed and we can use many of its results. A very convenient theoretical tool for our purposes are the Laurent series. Given a bounded sequence
f=[f_{i}]_{i\in\mathds{Z}}
, the associated Laurent series is

\hat{f}(z):=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}f_{i}z^{i}.

With this concept, (
12
) can be equivalently written as

\hat{f}^{k+1}(z)=\hat{a}(z)\hat{f}^{k}(z^{2}),
and a necessary condition for the convergence of the scheme is that
\hat{a}(1)=2
and
\hat{a}(-1)=0
, or equivalently
\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}a_{2i}=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}a_{2i+1}=1
. This implies that the so-called
reproduction of constant functions
,

\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}\phi(t-l)=1,\quad\forall t\in\mathds{R},

(15)

and that
\hat{a}(z)
can be factorized as

\hat{a}(z)=(1+z)\hat{b}(z),

(16)

for some
Laurent polynomial

\hat{b}(z)
(i.e., the sequence
b=[b_{i}]_{i\in\mathds{Z}}
is finitely supported), with
\hat{b}(1)=1
.

Another property that is essential to obtain a non-decreasing activation function (as asserted in Theorem
13
-
(e)
) is the
monotonicity
of the subdivision scheme. This property ensures that if
f^{0}
is non-decreasing, then the limit function is non-decreasing, which recall that it is
\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}f^{0}_{l}\phi(t-l)
. A sufficient condition for the monotonicity of the scheme is that
\hat{a}(z)
can be factorized as in (
16
) with
b_{l}\geq 0
, for all
l\in\mathds{Z}
.

The last subdivision property that we will use is related with the
generation
of the first degree polynomials. We demand to the basic limit function
\phi
to fulfill

t-\frac{d+1}{2}=\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}l\phi(t-l),\quad\forall t\in\mathds{R}.

(17)

Before addressing the main result, we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
a,b
be finitely supported sequences in
\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
and let
c\in\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
be. Then,

\hat{a}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{i-2m}\right)z^{i},

and, if
\hat{a}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})=\hat{b}(z)\hat{c}(z)
, then

\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{i-2m}=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}c_{i-m},\ \forall i\in\mathds{Z}.

Proof.

For the first part, we compute:

\displaystyle\hat{a}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}a_{m}z^{m}\right)\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}z^{2m}\right)=\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}a_{2m}z^{2m}+z\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}a_{2m+1}z^{2m}\right)\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}z^{2m}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{2(i-m)}\right)z^{2i}+z\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{2(i-m)+1}\right)z^{2i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{2i-2m}z^{2i}+c_{m}a_{2i+1-2m}z^{2i+1}=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(a_{2i-2m}z^{2i}+a_{2i+1-2m}z^{2i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}a_{i-2m}z^{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a_{i-2m}\right)z^{i}.

The second part follows from comparing term by term the last series with the series

\hat{b}(z)\hat{c}(z)=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}c_{i-m}\right)z^{i}.

∎

For a given basic limit function
\phi
, we can define the associated activation function
\sigma
as in Definition
9
. The following theorem states some properties of
\sigma
that are inherited from
\phi
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\phi:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
be the basic limit function, whose support is contained in
(0,d+1)
, associated to a convergent subdivision scheme with mask
a
. Let
b\in\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
be finitely supported, such that
\hat{a}(z)=(1+z)\hat{b}(z)
.
Then, the associated activation function
\sigma:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
fulfills:

(a)

\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}
, if
t\leq-\frac{d}{2}
,
\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{2}
, if
t\geq\frac{d}{2}
, and
\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\phi(t+\frac{d}{2}-m)
, if
-\frac{d}{2}\leq t\leq\frac{d}{2}
.

(b)

\sigma
is as smooth as
\phi
.

(c)

\sigma(t)-\sigma(t-1)=\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}\right)
.

(d)

If
\phi(t)=\phi(d+1-t)
,
\forall t\in\mathds{R}
, then
\sigma(-t)=-\sigma(t)
,
\forall t\in\mathds{R}
.

(e)

\sigma
is refinable with
A^{\sigma}:=d+1
,
a^{\sigma}:=b
and
\tau^{\sigma}:=\frac{d}{2}
. In addition, if
b_{l}\geq 0
,
\forall l\in\mathds{Z}
, then
\sigma
is non-decreasing.

(f)

If
\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}i\phi(t-i)=t-\frac{d+1}{2}
,
\forall t\in\mathds{R}
, and
B\geq d
is chosen, then
\sigma
sums the identity in the interval
I=[-\frac{B-d+1}{2},\frac{B-d+1}{2}]
with
\mu=\frac{B-1}{2}
.

Proof.

(a)
For
t\leq-d/2
, we have that
t+\frac{d}{2}-m\leq-m\leq 0
, for all
m\geq 0
. Thus
\phi(t+\frac{d}{2}-m)=0
and
\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}
since the sum in (
11
) vanishes. For
t\geq d/2
,
t+\frac{d}{2}-m\geq d-m\geq d+1
for all
m\leq-1
. Then,
\phi(t+\frac{d}{2}-m)=0
for all
m\leq-1
and we can extend the sum range:

\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right)\overset{\eqref{eq_reproduce_constant}}{=}-\frac{1}{2}+1=\frac{1}{2}.

For
-d/2\leq t\leq d/2
, we have that
t+\frac{d}{2}-m\leq d-m\leq 0
if
m\geq d
. Thus,
\phi(t+\frac{d}{2}-m)=0
and the sum in (
11
) is finite:

\sigma(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right).

(b)
Since
\phi
is compactly supported, the sum in (
11
) is finite for each
t\in\mathds{R}
. Thus,
\sigma
is as smooth as
\phi
.
For
(c)
, we have that

\displaystyle\sigma(t)-\sigma(t-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right)-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m-1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right)-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}-m\right)=\phi\left(t+\frac{d}{2}\right).

(d)
Suppose that
\phi(t)=\phi(d+1-t)
,
\forall t\in\mathds{R}
. We will prove by induction that
\sigma(t)=-\sigma(-t)
, if
|t|\geq(d-n)/2
, for
n=0,1,\ldots,d
. Observe the case
n=d
prove the symmetry for any
t\in\mathds{R}
. The case
n=0
is a consequence of
(a)
. Now, suppose that
\sigma(t)=-\sigma(-t)
, if
|t|\geq(d-(n-1))/2
. Let us consider
t\geq(d-n)/2
(the case
t\leq-(d-n)/2
is analogous). Then
t+1\geq(d-(n-1))/2
and

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{thm_sigma_properties:difference}}{=}\sigma(t+1)-\phi\left(t+1+\frac{d}{2}\right)\overset{\text{I.H.}}{=}-\sigma(-t-1)-\phi\left(t+1+\frac{d}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\phi\text{ sym.}}{=}-\sigma(-t-1)-\phi\left(d+1-\left(t+1+\frac{d}{2}\right)\right)=-\left(\sigma(-t-1)+\phi\left(-t+\frac{d}{2}\right)\right)\overset{\ref{thm_sigma_properties:difference}}{=}-\sigma(-t).

For
(e)
, we perform a deductive proof to show if other kinds of refinable activation functions
\sigma
are feasible. Denote by
a^{\phi}
the mask associated to
\phi
. We construct a
\bar{\sigma}
that is a linear combination of
\phi
and its shifts. For some
c\in\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
and
c^{\prime},\tau\in\mathds{R}
, we consider

\bar{\sigma}(t)=c^{\prime}+\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\phi(t+\tau-m),

(18)

and suppose that there exists some finitely supported
b\in\ell_{\infty}(\mathds{Z})
such that
\hat{a}^{\phi}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})=\hat{b}(z)\hat{c}(z)
. Then,

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(t-\tau)-c^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\phi(t-m)\overset{\phi\text{ refi.}}{=}\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}a^{\phi}_{i}\phi(2(t-m)-i)=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}a^{\phi}_{i-2m}\phi(2t-i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\phi(2t-i)\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}c_{m}a^{\phi}_{i-2m}\overset{\text{Lemma \ref{lem:tecnico}}}{=}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}\phi(2t-i)\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}c_{i-m}=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i-m}\phi(2t-i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i}\phi(2t-i-m).

If
\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}=\hat{b}(1)=1
, then

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(t-\tau)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}+\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i}\phi(2t-m-i)=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}c^{\prime}+\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i}\phi(2t-m-i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\left(c^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i}\phi(2t-m-i)\right),

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\left(c^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}}c_{i}\phi(2t+2\tau-m-i)\right)\overset{\eqref{eq_reproduce_constant}}{=}\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}b_{m}\bar{\sigma}(2t+\tau-m).

We conclude that
\bar{\sigma}
defined in (
18
) is refinable provided that
\hat{a}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})=\hat{b}(z)\hat{c}(z)
with
\hat{b}(1)=1
.
In particular, the activation function of Definition
9
fulfils this with
c^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2},\tau=\frac{d}{2}
and
c_{m}=1
, if
m\geq 0
, and
c_{m}=0
, if
m<0
.
Observe that
\hat{c}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}=(1-z)^{-1}
and, thus, the requirement
\hat{a}(z)\hat{c}(z^{2})=\hat{b}(z)\hat{c}(z)
is equivalent to
\hat{a}(z)=(1+z)\hat{b}(z)
, which is true since the scheme is convergent by hypothesis, which also implies that
\hat{b}(1)=1
. Since the support of
a
is
[0,d+1]
, then the support of
b
is
[0,d]
. Summarizing, we deduced that

\sigma(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{d}b_{l}\sigma\left(2t+\frac{d}{2}-l\right),

i.e.,
\sigma
is refinable with
A^{\sigma}=d+1
,
a^{\sigma}=b
and
\tau^{\sigma}=\frac{d}{2}
.

In addition,
b
are the coefficients of what is commonly referred to as the
difference scheme
. It is well-established that if
a^{\sigma}_{l}=b_{l}\geq 0
, then
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m}\phi(t-m)
is a non-decreasing function for any non-decreasing sequence
c
. Specifically, since our chosen sequence
c
is non-decreasing, it follows that
\sigma
is also non-decreasing.

For
(f)
, suppose that
t\in[-\frac{B-d+1}{2},\frac{B-d+1}{2}]
.

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma\left(t+\frac{B-1}{2}-l\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{B-1}{2}+\frac{d}{2}-l-m\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{B}{2}+\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-l-m\right).

Since the support of
\phi
is contained in
(0,d+1)
, and
t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-l-m\leq\frac{B-d+1}{2}+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-l-m=B-l-m\leq 0
if, and only if,
B-l\leq m
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{B}{2}+\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma\left(t+\frac{B-1}{2}-l\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sum_{m=0}^{B-l-1}\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-l-m\right)=\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sum_{m=l}^{B-1}\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-m\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{B-1}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-m\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{B-1}\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-m\right)\sum_{l=0}^{m}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{B-1}(m+1)\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-m\right).

Observe that the term
m=-1
can be added, since it is zero. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{B}{2}+\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma\left(t+\frac{B-1}{2}-l\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-1}^{B-1}(m+1)\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d-1}{2}-m\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{B}m\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-m\right).

In addition, the sum can be extended to all the integers:

\displaystyle m\leq-1,

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-m\geq t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}+1\geq-\frac{B-d+1}{2}+\frac{B+d+1}{2}+1=d+1.

\displaystyle m\geq B+1,

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\quad t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-m\leq t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-B-1\leq\frac{B-d+1}{2}+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-B-1=0.

In any of both cases,
\phi(t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-m)=0
, due to the support of
\phi
is contained in
(0,d+1)
. Finally,
using the hypothesis we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{B-1}\sigma\left(t+\frac{B-1}{2}-l\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{B}{2}+\sum_{m\in\mathds{Z}}m\phi\left(t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-m\right)=-\frac{B}{2}+t+\frac{B+d+1}{2}-\frac{d+1}{2}=t.

∎

Remark 14
.

As a consequence of Theorem
13
-
(e)
, we deduce that not all basic limit functions
\phi
are appropriate for constructing refinable activation functions. In particular, ensuring that
b_{l}\geq 0
,
\forall l\in\mathds{Z}
, holds is essential for obtaining a non-decreasing activation function. In subdivision theory, schemes that satisfy
b_{l}\geq 0
,
\forall l\in\mathds{Z}
, are termed monotone. Such schemes inherently fulfil a variation diminishing property, which suppresses oscillatory behavior (see
Yadshalom [1993]
). This is especially important since oscillatory activation functions may lead to performance issues.

In addition to the B-Spline case studied in this paper, other examples of monotone subdivision schemes that generates first-degree polynomials—and thereby allow the definition of proper activation functions as described—include those based on weighted polynomial regression (see
López-Ureña and Yáñez [2024]
).

Remark 15
.

Only a few subdivision schemes yield basic limit functions
\phi
that admit a closed-form expression, such as B-Splines and exponential B-Splines schemes (see
Dyn [1992]
,
Unser and Blu [2005]
).

Nevertheless,
\sigma
can be approximated with arbitrary precision using subdivision. We recall that, when a convergent subdivision scheme is recursively applied to an initial sequence
f^{0}
, the process converges to
\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}f^{0}_{l}\phi(t-l)
. Hence, by considering
f^{0}_{l}:=-\frac{1}{2}
, if
l<0
, and
f^{0}_{l}:=\frac{1}{2}
, if
l\geq 0
, we obtain the limit function

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l<0}\phi(t-l)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\geq 0}\phi(t-l)\overset{\eqref{eq_reproduce_constant}}{=}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l<0}\phi(t-l)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\geq 0}\phi(t-l)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}\phi(t-l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{l\geq 0}\phi(t-l)=\sigma\left(t-\frac{d}{2}\right).

In conclusion,
\sigma\left(t-\frac{d}{2}\right)
is the limit function of the subdivision scheme applied to such initial sequence
f^{0}
.

4
Conclusions

In this work, a new class of activation functions was introduced, characterized by two key properties: they are refinable, and they sum the identity.

It was demonstrated how these properties can be utilized to add new neurons and layers without altering the NN output, at least for any input data belonging to a specific, arbitrarily large, set. Explicit formulas for constructing the new neurons and layers were provided and pseudocode for the algorithms was presented.

The proposed theoretical results were applied to define the spline activation functions within this class. Specific properties were presented for these functions, including formulas for computing
\sigma_{B^{1}}
and
\sigma_{B^{2}}
as well as their derivatives in a manner compatible with backpropagation.

Several open questions remain for future investigation. We hope that these results can lead to improvements when combined with multi-level training algorithms, such as those employed in structural learning, by either enhancing NN performance or accelerating convergence during the training phase, similarly to other function-preserving transformations (e.g.,
Chen et al. [2015]
,
Wei et al. [2016]
,
Wei et al. [2019]
). In particular, we wonder whether this potential improvement depends on the
approximation order
of the subdivision scheme, linked to the refinable activation function. Finally, an efficient and accurate practical implementation requires closed-form expressions for
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
. In this line, extending Proposition
11
to the case
d>2
or to more general refinable functions would be highly beneficial.
