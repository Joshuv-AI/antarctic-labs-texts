Abstract
. Homomorphism is an important structure-preserving mapping between graphs. Given a graph G G and a pattern Q Q , the subgraph homomorphism problem is to find a mapping φ \varphi from Q Q to G G such that adjacent vertices of Q Q are mapped to adjacent vertices in G G . Unlike the subgraph isomorphic mapping that is injective, homomorphism allows multiple vertices in Q Q to map to the same vertex in G G , increasing complexity. We develop 𝖧𝖥𝗋𝖺𝗆𝖾 \mathsf{HFrame} , the first GNN-based framework for subgraph homomorphism, by combining algorithms and machine learning. We show that 𝖧𝖥𝗋𝖺𝗆𝖾 \mathsf{HFrame} is more expressive than the vanilla GNN, i.e., 𝖧𝖥𝗋𝖺𝗆𝖾 \mathsf{HFrame} can distinguish more graph pairs ( Q , G ) (Q,G) such that Q Q is not homomorphic to G G . Moreover, we provide a generalization error bound for 𝖧𝖥𝗋𝖺𝗆𝖾 \mathsf{HFrame} . Using real-life and synthetic graphs, we show that 𝖧𝖥𝗋𝖺𝗆𝖾 \mathsf{HFrame} is up to 101.91 × \times faster than exact matching algorithms, and its average accuracy can reach 0.962.

1.
Introduction

Pattern matching is
to identify instances of a pattern
Q

in a graph
G
, and has received
extensive interest across various fields,

e.g.,
discovering motifs in biology

(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Licheri et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2007
)
,
finding experts in social networks

(
Shi et al. 2017
;
Fan et al. 2013a
;
Brynielsson et al. 2010
)
,
and reasoning on knowledge graph

(
Xu et al. 2019b
;
Teru et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022
)
.

There exist two semantics for pattern matching,
namely subgraph isomorphism and homomorphism.
(1) The isomorphic semantics demands that
the matched instances of
Q
in

G
(
i.e.,
a subgraph of
G
) must be the same as
Q
.
This semantics has been widely studied,

e.g.,
discovering motifs from biological network
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Licheri et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2007
)
.
(2) Homomorphic semantics,
on the other hand, permits that

Q
can be “embedded” into the
identified subgraph of
G
,

i.e.,
the
subgraph can be deviated from
Q
.
It has been widely used in graph query languages
like Cypher
(
Dragovic et al. 2023
)
and SPARQL
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
.
The difference between isomorphism and homomorphism
lies in whether different vertices in
Q

can be mapped to the same vertex in
G
(see Section
2
).

The graph pattern matching problem under
isomorphic semantics
has been extensively studied,
leading to the development
of many algorithms (
e.g.,

(
Han et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Bhattarai et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Arai et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Bi et al. 2016
;
Bonnici et al. 2013
;
Ullmann 1976
;
He and Singh 2008
;
Han et al. 2013
)
; see
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
for a survey and comparison).
All such algorithms work in the following steps:
(1) establish a matching order
{\mathcal{O}}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})

for vertices in pattern
Q
;
(2) compute a set
{\mathcal{C}}(u)
of candidate matches
for each pattern vertex
u
in
Q
,
e.g.,

{\mathcal{C}}(u)
consists of all vertices in
G
that have the same
label with
u
;
(3) iteratively identify matches using
{\mathcal{C}}(u)
as follows:
(a) select a candidate
v_{1}
in
{\mathcal{C}}(u_{1})

for the first vertex
u_{1}
in the order
{\mathcal{O}}
;
(b) from the neighbors
u_{2}
of
u_{1}
,
find a candidate
v_{2}
in
{\mathcal{C}}(u_{2})

that has the same edge labels as
u_{2}

in
Q
;
(c) continue these steps for all other
vertices in
Q
, and check whether the selected candidates
form a match of
Q
;
if not, backtrack to re-examine other vertices in
{\mathcal{C}}(u_{2})
.
These techniques can be adapted
to homomorphic semantics,
except that the identified
subgraphs of
G
are not required to
be the same as
Q
in the last step.
However, such algorithms take exponential
runtime due to backtracking,
and cannot efficiently handle large-scale graphs.
To address this, various optimization strategies
have been developed, including
index structures to filter candidates
(
Kim et al. 2021
;
Bhattarai et al. 2019
;
Han et al. 2019
)
,
and dual simulation
(
Ma et al. 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
and
1-WL algorithms
(
Weisfeiler and Leman 1968
;
Cai et al. 1992
)

to approximate results.
Despite these efforts,
the algorithms still suffer from taking long time due to
the high complexity.

Machine learning (ML), particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs),
has been applied in the subgraph matching
problem
(
Ying et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Roy et al. 2022
;
Ye et al. 2024
;
Lan et al. 2023
)
.
Such solutions work as follows:
(1) exploit a GNN
to compute the embedding for each vertex
in
Q
and
G
, capturing the surrounding
topological structures of the vertex;
and (2) assessing subgraph matching
by comparing these embeddings
within the order-embedding space
(
Ying et al. 2020
)
.
These algorithms are efficient, since
they do not depend on backtracking.
However, their predictions are not accurate,
since their expressive power
is bounded by the 1-WL
algorithm
(
Xu et al. 2019a
)
, an approximate algorithm.
Moreover, these models cannot directly
handle homomorphic semantics,
since they do not
account for properties of homomorphic mappings,

e.g.,
two distinct pattern vertices
can be mapped to the same vertex in
G
,
allowing two graphs
with different sizes be homomorphic to each other.

Generalization error is an important property of machine learning
models, reflecting how well they
work on unseen inputs.
Metrics for generalization error
include Rademacher complexity
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
and
VC dimension
(
Morris et al. 2023
)
.
The Rademacher complexity of GNN models
is established in
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
.
However, non such bound exists
for subgraph isomorphic or
homomorphic mapping models.

In this paper,
we aim to answer the following questions.
(1) Can we integrate algorithmic solution
with an ML-based techniques, to
develop approximate framework for
graph pattern matching under homomorphic semantics?
(2) Does the proposed framework is more expressive than
existing approximate solutions?
And (3) what is the generalization error bound of the proposed framework?

Contribution
. Our contributions
are listed as follows.

(1) We introduce
\mathsf{HFrame}
, a framework designed
for the subgraph
homomorphism problem.
Given
Q
and
G
, it first leverages a

\mathsf{PTIME}
algorithm
\mathsf{DualSim}

(
Ma et al. 2014
)
for graph dual simuation, to identify candidates
set
{\mathcal{C}}(u)

for each vertex
u
in
Q
. Then it invokes
an ML
model
\mathsf{HGIN}
to make predictions
on these candidates.
By combining algorithmic solutions and ML techniques,

\mathsf{HGIN}
can deliver high-quality predictions,
striking a balance between complexity and
accuracy.

(2) We develop an ML
model
\mathsf{HGIN}
for
subgraph homomorphism in
\mathsf{HFrame}
,
building upon
\mathsf{IDGNN}

(
You et al. 2021
)
,
which is tailored for subgraph isomorphism.
Instead of using multisets to gather
messages as in
\mathsf{IDGNN}
,
we adopt sets to gather
embeddings and remove duplicate messages,
since two vertices can be mapped to
the same vertex in homomorphism.
We encode both directions and labels
of edges into embeddings
of vertices, to improve the accuracy
of
\mathsf{HGIN}
.

(3) We first show that
\mathsf{HFrame}
is more expressive than existing models,
and capable of distinguishing more non-homomorphic graphs.
Then we provide the first generalization error bound
for models addressing the subgraph homomorphism problem.

(4) We conducted
extensive experiments on real-life and synthetic graphs,
and found the following:
(a)
\mathsf{HFrame}
is efficient.
It on average outperforms
existing algorithms
for subgraph homomorphism problems
by up to 101.91
\times
, and its accuracy on average is 0.962.
(b)
\mathsf{HFrame}
is effective.
Its accuracy is at least 0.921 on all graphs,
and is on average 21% higher than existing ML solutions.
(c)
\mathsf{HFrame}
performs well on unseen data.
After
\mathsf{HFrame}
is trained on synthetic graphs,
its accuracy can reach 0.892, 0.874 and 0.872
on real-life graphs Citeseerx, IMDB and DBpedia, respectively.

Related work
. We categorized the related work as follows.

Graph pattern matching algorithm
.
Algorithms for the subgraph isomorphism
problem have been
extensively studied.
Ullmann proposed the first backtracking-based algorithm for subgraph isomorphism
(
Ullmann 1976
)
.
Subsequent research in algorithms for exact graph pattern
matching focuses on developing pruning methods
(
Carletti et al. 2017
;
Carletti et al. 2019
)

and improving index structures
(
Bhattarai et al. 2019
;
Sun and Luo 2020
;
Yan et al. 2004
;
Zhao et al. 2007
)
, and so on.

Isomorphic mappings suffer from two major drawbacks:
(1) the decision version of subgraph isomorphism is
NP-complete
(
Cook 2023
)
, and
(2) the requirement of
injective mapping
could be too strict in many cases,
especially when the data source is noisy
(
Yuan et al. 2011
)
.
To address these challenges,
(1) a series of studies focus on designing similarity functions and
deriving matching results through
top-
k
candidates or the establishment of a similarity threshold

(
Fan et al. 2010b
;
Cheng et al. 2013
;
Dutta et al. 2017
;
Wu et al. 2013
;
Khan et al. 2013
;
Zhao et al. 2013
)
.
(2) The other studies relax the
injective map to less restrictive
relations.
Bounded simulation
is developed to bound the lengths of
matching paths
(
Fan et al. 2010a
)
.
Dual simulation and
strong simulation are proposed
in
(
Ma et al. 2011
)
to
bound directions of edges
and
distances of vertices in a match,
respectively.
Subsequently, various simulation-based
matching algorithms have been developed
(
Fan et al. 2013b
;
Fan et al. 2014
;
Fard et al. 2014
;
Gao et al. 2016
;
Du et al. 2018
)
.

ML Models for graph pattern matching
.
Multiple ML models are developed for graph pattern matching
(
Ying et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Roy et al. 2022
;
Ye et al. 2024
;
Lan et al. 2023
)
.
Most of these models are based on Graph Neural Networks (GNNs),
to compute embeddings of vertices in a graph
(
Scarselli et al. 2008
)
,
and make a prediction using the order-embedding space
(
Ying et al. 2020
)

or MLP
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
Most work focus on
enhancing GNNs architecture
(
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2022b
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Bouritsas et al. 2022
;
Feng et al. 2022
)
,
to learn more topological information.
Zhang et al.
(
Zhang et al. 2023a
)

encoded distance information into vertex embeddings.
Geerts et al.
(
Geerts and Reutter 2022
)

proposed a model to aggregate information
from
k
-order neighbors using computational operators.
You et al.
(
You et al. 2021
)
injected a unique
color identity to nodes,
and performed heterogeneous message passing
to obtain the embedding.
Xu et al.
(
Xu et al. 2019a
)

optimized the aggregation function
by using multisets,
to generate distinct embeddings for different nodes.
Maron et al.
(
Maron et al. 2019
)

proposed a model consisting of
both equivariant linear layers and nonlinear activation layers.

There has also been work on
studying properties of GNNs.
(1) The expressive power of these GNN
models can be bounded by
the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) hierarchy
(
Xu et al. 2019a
;
Morris et al. 2019
;
Barceló et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
,
descriptive complexity
(
Grohe 2023
)
,
fragments of FO logic
(
Barceló et al. 2020
)

and global feature map transformer
(
Geerts et al. 2022
)
;
see
(
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
for a recent survey.
(2) The generalization error
bound for GNNs can be measured by
Rademacher complexity
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
, VC dimension
(
Morris et al. 2023
)

and generalization gap
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.

Let

Graphs
.
Consider directed labeled graphs

(2)

Let

.

For each vertex
u\in V

(resp.
incoming
) neighbors

of
u

(resp.

N^{+}_{r}(u){=}\{u_{1}\mid\langle u,r,u_{1}\rangle{\in}E\}

Isomorphism (Iso) and homomorphism (Hom).

We introduce the semantics of
subgraph
isomorphism and homomorphism.
Consider a pattern
Q{=}(V_{Q},E_{Q},L_{Q})
and a graph
G{=}(V_{G},E_{G},L_{G})
.
Here, a pattern is also a directed labeled graph.

Subgraph isomorphism
.
An
isomorphic mapping
from

Q
to
G
,
denoted by

Q\sqsubseteq G
is an injective mapping
\varphi

from
V_{Q}
to
V_{G}

such that
(1) for each
u{\in}V_{Q}
,
u
and
\varphi(u)

carry the same label,
i.e.,

L_{Q}(u)=L_{G}(\varphi(u))
; and
(2) the adjacency relation preserves,

i.e.,

\langle u,r,u^{\prime}\rangle\in E_{Q}
if and only
if
\langle\varphi(u),r,\varphi(u^{\prime})\rangle\in E_{G}
.
When such an injective mapping
\varphi
exists,
we say that
Q
is subgraph isomorphic to
G
.

Subgraph homomorphism
.
A
homomorphic mapping
from
Q
to
G
, denoted by
Q\triangleright G
,
is a mapping

\varphi
from
V_{Q}
to
V_{G}
such that
(1) for each
u\in V_{Q}
,
u
and
\varphi(u)

carry the same label,
i.e.,

L_{Q}(u)=L_{G}(\varphi(u))
;
and (2) when there is
an edge
\langle u,r,u^{\prime}\rangle
in
E_{Q}
,
edge

\langle\varphi(u),r,\varphi(u^{\prime})\rangle

exists in
E_{G}
.
Observe that (a)
\varphi
may not be injective;
and (b) when
\langle\varphi(u),r,\varphi(u^{\prime})\rangle\in E_{G}
,

\langle u,r,u^{\prime}\rangle
may not exist in
E_{Q}
.
When such a mapping
\varphi
exists,
we say that
Q
is subgraph homomorphic to
G
.

.
Since subgraph isomorphism

it has the anti-symmetry property,

i.e.,
for two graphs

if
G{\sqsubseteq}H
and
H{\sqsubseteq}G
,
then
G
and

are the same graph.
However, subgraph homomorphism does not have

That is, there exist two distinct
graphs

and

such that
G\triangleright H
and
H\triangleright G
.

Problem definition
.
The paper studies the following
subgraph
homomorphism problem, denoted by
\mathsf{SHP}
:

given a pattern
Q
and a graph
G
,
whether there exists
a homomorphic mapping
\varphi
from
Q
to
G
.

Remark
. Such decision problem can be applied in the following scenarios:
(1) frequent pattern mining
(
Ying et al. 2024
)
and graph pattern dependency discovery
(
Fan et al. 2024
)
, where
pattern quality is usually determined by the number of vertices matching the pivot
(
i.e.,
a designated vertex) of
Q
,
which can be computed by determining

the existence of matches
on these vertices,
rather than enumerating the matches;
(2) querying for key vertices satisfying some properties defined by patterns,

e.g.,
a SPARQL query from Wikidata
(
Spa 2025
)
to find articles on Punjabi Wikipedia about Pakistani actresses,
which can be modeled as a pattern with Articles as the pivot;
and (3) accelerating enumerations
by filtering candidates
with the decision problem
and inducing a smaller subgraph before enumeration
(see Section
5
).

Existing solutions
.
In this paper, we develop an approximate solution
for
\mathsf{SHP}
.
There exist two categories of approximate solutions,

i.e.,
algorithmic approximations and machine learning based
solutions.
We aim to combine these two solutions to develop
a more accurate solution.
We first
present the two existing
solutions.

Dual Simulation.

Given a pattern
Q=(V_{Q},E_{Q},L_{Q})
and a graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G},L_{G})
,
it is to compute a matching relation
S_{(Q,G)}
of
vertex pairs
(u,v)
where
u\in V_{Q}
and
v\in V_{G}
, such that

u
and
v
have the same neighbors.
Using
S_{(Q,G)}
,
we can approximate the subgraph homomorphic
problem by
checking whether
(u,v)\in S_{(Q,G)}
.

The relation
S_{(Q,G)}\subseteq V_{Q}\times V_{G}

is defined as follows: (1) for each
(u,v)\in S_{(Q,G)}
,
L_{Q}(u)=L_{G}(v)
;
and (2) for each
u\in V_{Q}
,
there exists
v\in V_{G}
such that
(i)
(u,v)\in S_{(Q,G)}
;
(ii) for each outgoing edge
(u,u_{1})
in
E_{Q}
,
there is an outgoing edge
(v,v_{1})
in
E_{G}
with
(u_{1},v_{1})\in S_{(Q,G)}
;
and (iii) for each incoming
edge
(u_{2},u)
in
E_{Q}
, there is
an incoming edge
(v_{2},v)
in
E_{G}
with
(u_{2},v_{2})\in S_{(Q,G)}
.

The relation
S_{(Q,G)}
can be computed in

(

presented in Figure

It repeats these steps for

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
.
Given a graph
G
, a pattern
Q
and
two vertices
u
and
v

from
Q
and
G
, respectively,
GNN-based solution
checks the existence of homomorphic mapping
as follows
(
Vendrov et al. 2016
;
Ying et al. 2020
)
:
(1) compute the embeddings of
u
and
v

using GNNs; let
h_{u}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
and

h_{v}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
be the embeddings
of
u
and
v
, respectively;
and (2) exploit the order embedding space
to check the existence of homomorphic mappings,

i.e.,
a homomorphic mapping
\varphi

exists if and only if

a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
i\in[1,d]
.

To compute the embeddings of vertices,
we can adopt the identity-aware GNN
(
\mathsf{IDGNN}
)
(
You et al. 2021
)
,
since
\mathsf{IDGNN}
is more expressive than vanilla GNN
(
You et al. 2021
)
.
Given a graph
G
and a vertex
u
,
\mathsf{IDGNN}
computes
a
d
-dimensional vector to represent
u
in

G
by iteratively aggregating information
collected from its neighbors.
More specifically,
(1)
\mathsf{IDGNN}
first constructs the
k
-hop ego network
G_{u}

centered at
u
,

i.e.,
the subgraph induced by the
k
-hop neighbors of
u
;
(2) next it computes the intial embedding of
of each vertex
v
in
G_{u}
using
features of
v
,
e.g.,
the label of
v
;
(3) then it iteratively updates embeddings of
all vertices in
G_{u}

as follows:
(a) collect embeddings of neighbors of
a vertex
v

in
G_{u}
; and (b) transform the received embeddings
via message-passing functions;
that is, let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be the
neighbors of vertex
v
; the message-passing function
for the embedding of
v_{i}
is
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}

if
v_{i}=u
; otherwise the message-passing function
is
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}
. This step is repeated for
m
times.
Note that (i) the ego network
G_{u}
is used to
compute the embedding of center
u
only;
and (ii) parameter
m
is also called the number of the layers
in
\mathsf{IDGNN}
.

Remark
.
These two approximate solutions have their pros and cons.

(1)
\mathsf{DualSim}
can process any given patterns and graphs,
no matter whether they have been inspected before or not;
moreover, it ensures that all matches can be returned,

i.e.,
the recall of
\mathsf{DualSim}
is 1;
but it may return extra results,

i.e.,
it will return false positive answer.
Consider graphs
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
in Figure
1
.
There does not exist a homomorpic mapping
from
G_{3}
to
G_{2}
, since
G_{3}
contains
triangles, but
G_{2}
does not.
However,
S_{(G_{2},G_{3})}=V_{G_{2}}\times V_{G_{3}}
,

i.e.,

(v_{i},w_{j})\in S_{(Q,G)}
,
i,j\in[1,6]
.
See the proof of Theorem
4.2

for more details.

(2) GNN models can
achieve good accuracy in
performing tasks like node classification
(
Hang et al. 2021
)
,
recommendation system
(
Sharma et al. 2024
)
and anomaly detection
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
,
and can become more expressive
using more information in the graphs
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Liu and Song 2022
)
.
Moreover, the computation of GNNs
are efficient, since it only exploits
neighbors of a vertex to make a
prediction. The computation can be further accelerated
using sampling techniques when processing large-scale
graphs
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
.
But GNN models may return error prediction,
and miss some matches.

3.
Framework for homomorphic mapping

Figure 3.
The framework of
\mathsf{HFrame}
.
(1) It first computes
S_{(Q,G)}
via
\mathsf{DualSim}
,
to filter candidate sets
{\mathcal{C}}(u)

for each vertex
u
in
Q
,
and construct a subgraph
G^{\prime}
by removing irrelevant vertices and edges
from
G
;
(2) it then generates embeddings of vertices via
\mathsf{HGIN}
; and
(3) it finally makes a prediction via the order embedding space.
Its novelties (marked red) consist
of (a) adopting sets to collect messages,
(b) using cycles to select message-passing functions,
and (c) integrating parameters into the loss function.

We provide
\mathsf{HFrame}
, a framework for the subgraph homomorphic problem,
by integrating algorithms and machine learning models.
The framework is outlined in Figure
3
.
Intuitively, the combination of
\mathsf{DualSim}
and
\mathsf{HGIN}
in
\mathsf{HFrame}
enhances the efficiency
and accuracy of the predictions due to the following mechanisms.
(1)
\mathsf{DualSim}
removes
irrelevant information from
graph
G
, which
boosts the performance of
\mathsf{HGIN}
by focusing
only on essential data.
(2)
\mathsf{HGIN}
optimizes the prediction by
computing only embeddings of vertices,
rather than embeddings of patterns and graphs.
In this way,
\mathsf{HGIN}
can leverage
the locality of subgraph matching,

i.e.,

\mathsf{HGIN}
only inspects
a small neighborhood of each vertex,
which reduces the complexity of
\mathsf{HGIN}
and improves the accuracy of
\mathsf{HFrame}
.
(3)
\mathsf{HGIN}
only verifies candidates
of
the pivot

u_{p}
of
Q
,
since when
Q
is homomorphic to
G
,
the pivot
u_{p}
must be mapped to some vertex in
G
.
The selection of pivot
u_{p}
can be based on the degrees of vertices,

e.g.,
picking the vertex with the maximum degree in
Q

(
Ye et al. 2024
)
.

Homomorphic Neural Node Matching (HGIN)
.
It is to decide, given a patter
Q
, a graph
G
, vertex
u
in
Q
and vertex
v
in
G
,
whether there is a homomorphic mapping
\varphi
from

Q
to
G
such that
\varphi(u)=v
.

Overview
.
We design
\mathsf{HGIN}
by extending
\mathsf{IDGNN}

(
You et al. 2021
)
,
to address the following three challenges from homomorphic semantics.

(
1) We need to ensure that two distinct vertices
in
Q
can be mapped to the same vertex in
G
.
We illustrate two such scenarios in Figure
4
.

Figure 4.
Cases for homomorphism

(a) Consider pattern
Q_{1}
and graph

G_{4}

in Figure
4
.
Assume
that all vertices and edges in
Q_{1}

and
G_{4}
have the same label.
There is a homomorphic mapping
\varphi_{1}
from
Q_{1}
to
G_{4}

as follows:
\varphi_{1}(u_{1}){=}v_{1}
,
\varphi_{1}(u_{2}){=}h_{1}(u_{3}){=}v_{2}
.
Note that
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
are both mapped to
v_{2}
in
h_{1}
.

To handle such case with GNNs, we use
sets
to store received embedddings
of neighbors,
rather than multisets as in
\mathsf{IDGNN}
.
In this way, when embeddings of
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
are the same,
the duplicated one will removed,
and only embedding of
u_{2}
or
u_{3}
is used.
As a result, the embeddings of
u_{1}
in
Q_{1}
and
vertex
v_{1}
in
G_{1}
are the same,
from which
\mathsf{HGIN}
predicts that
Q_{1}
is homomorphic to
G_{4}
.

(b)
Consider
Q_{2}
and
G_{5}
in Figure
4
,
which are two cycles of length 6 and 3, respectively,
and all vertices and edges carry the same label.
There exists a homomorphic mapping
\varphi_{2}

from
Q_{2}
to
G_{5}

as follows:

\varphi_{2}(u_{1})=\varphi_{2}(u_{4})=v_{1}
,

\varphi_{2}(u_{2})=\varphi_{2}(u_{5})=v_{2}
and

\varphi_{2}(u_{3})=\varphi_{2}(u_{6})=v_{3}
.
However,
\mathsf{IDGNN}
can only distinguish the two graphs,
and cannot verify the existence
of such a mapping,
since
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}
and
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}

is not comparable.
To address this, we introduce the following
two extensions.
(a) We enforce that

{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}
is sufficiently larger than
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}

when training
\mathsf{HGIN}
.
(b)
We compute a set
{\mathcal{L}_{Q}}
of
lengths of cycles that contain

u_{1}
in pattern
Q
, and use
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}

only when
vertex
v_{1}

also appears in a cycle of length
l

in
G
satisfying that
l\in{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}
.

To show the effectiveness, consider the following two cases.

(i) When pattern
Q
contains a cycle of
length

l
involving
u
, whereas graph
G

does not have such a cycle involving
v
,
the computation of
u
’s embedding in
Q

invokes
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}
more frequently.
Since
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{1}
is sufficiently larger than
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}
,
the embedding of
u
is larger than that of

v
, indicating that no homomorphic mapping
from
Q
to
G
exists.

(ii) Conversely, if
G
contains a cycle of length
l

involving
v
, whereas
Q

does not,
{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}
is invoked for vertices
within the cycle of length
l
in
both
Q
and
G
, which cannot result in that
the embedding of
u
is larger than
that of
v
, thus retaining the semantics of homomorphic mappings.
Given
Q_{2}
and
G_{5}
in Fig.
4
,
although
G_{5}
contains a cycle of length 3
involving
v_{1}
, whereas
Q_{2}

does not have such a cycle involving
u_{1}
,

{\mathsf{MSG}}_{0}
is invoked to compute embeddings for both
Q_{2}
and
G_{5}
.

(2) Since
\mathsf{IDGNN}
does not support edge labels,
and cannot be directly used on the homomorphism problem,
we extend the R-GCN framework
(
Schlichtkrull et al. 2018
)
.
The extension introduces a relation-specific
transformation, to accommodate edge labels.
More specifically, we categorize edges
by their labels,
and assign different weights to different labels.
To address the directions of edges,
we compute the embeddings
with incoming edges and outgoing edges
separately.
Then we concatenate these embeddings to form the embedding of vertices.

(3)
\mathsf{HGIN}
utilizes the order-embedding space
to check the existence of homomorphic mappings
(
Ying et al. 2020
)
,
involving comparison of values at
each index of the embeddings.
However, the computed embeddings of vertices
might include negative values.
To overcome this, we normalize the embeddings,
to ensure that all values are positive.

Putting these together, we present
\mathsf{HGIN}
in
Figure
3
.

Embeddings of
Q
.
Given a vertex
u
, it first constructs
a subgraph
Q_{u}
of
Q
, induced by
the
m
-hop neighbors of
u
,
where
m
is a predefined parameter;
then it iteratively computes the embedding of
each vertex
u_{c}
in
Q_{u}

as follows: (a) collects the embeddings
of
u_{c}
’s neighbors via a message passing function,
and updates
u_{c}
’s embedding using the collected
embeddings via an aggregation function. More specifically,
it computes the embedding
of
u_{c}
as follows:

(1)

\displaystyle h^{0}_{u_{c}}

\displaystyle=e^{f}_{x}

(2)

\displaystyle h^{k}_{u_{c},i}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}(\sum\nolimits_{r\in R_{G}}{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]}(\{h^{k-1}_{u},u_{n}{\in}N^{-}_{r}(u_{c})\}),h^{k-1}_{u_{c}})

(3)

\displaystyle h^{k}_{u_{c},o}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}(\sum\nolimits_{r\in R_{G}}{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]}(\{h^{k-1}_{u},u_{n}{\in}N^{+}_{r}(u_{c})\}),h^{k-1}_{u_{c}})

(4)

\displaystyle h^{k}_{u_{c}}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{COM}}(h^{k}_{u_{c},i},h^{k}_{u_{c},o}).

Here, (1)
N_{r}^{-}(u_{c})
(resp.
N_{r}^{+}(u_{c})
)
is the set of
neighbors of
u_{c}

which have an
outgoing edge to
u_{c}

(resp. an incoming edge from
u_{c}
) labeled
r
;
and (2) the message passing function

{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]}
collects and transforms
embeddings of neighbors of
u_{c}
;
in this paper, we adopt
matrix multiplication as the message passing functions;
more specifically,

{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]}(N^{-}_{r})
transforms the message to

(5)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{u_{n}\in N^{-}_{r}}W^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]}h^{k-1}_{u_{n}};

here
N^{-}_{r}
is the set of outgoing neighbors;
the message passing functions for incoming neighbors are defined similarly;
(3)
{\bf 1}[u_{n}=u]
is an indicator function that
returns
1
if
u_{n}
and
u
are the same vertex,
and 0 otherwise;
that is, if the neighbor
u_{n}
is the center vertex

u
of
Q_{u}
, then the message
passing function is
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{1}
;
otherwise, the message passing function is
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{0}
;
(4)
{\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}
combines the embeddings
of neighbors and the current embedding of
u_{c}
as follows:

(6)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}({\mathsf{MSG}},e_{2})=W_{1}e_{2}+{\mathsf{MSG}};

and (5)
{\mathsf{COM}}
updates the embedding of

u_{c}
by concatenating the
embeddings of its incoming neighbors
and outgoing neighbors.

Embeddings of
G
.
For a pattern
Q
and a graph
G
, given vertices
u
in
Q
and
v
in
G
,
it first compute the sets
{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}^{u}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{G}^{v}

of all cycles of length
l
in
Q
and
G
containing
u
and
v
, respectively,
where
l
is bounded by
m
,
i.e.,
the number of layers in
\mathsf{HGIN}
.
Let
G_{v}
be the subgraph of
G
, induced by the
m
-hop neighbors of
v
.
Then it iteratively computes the embedding of each vertex
v_{c}
in
G_{v}

in the same way as it does for embedding
Q
, except that
the message-passing functions
for each neighbor
v_{n}
of vertex
v_{c}
in
G_{v}

is
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{{\bf 1}[v_{n}=v][k\in{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}^{u}]}
.
Here
{\bf 1}[v_{n}{=}v][k{\in}{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}^{u}]

is a Boolean function,
which returns
1
when
(1) the neighbor
v_{n}
is the center

v
of
G_{v}
,
and (2)
Q
has a cycle of length
k

containing the center
u
in
Q_{u}
, and
0

otherwise.

Note that when
Q_{u}
does not
have a cycle of length
k

that contains the center
u
,
the massage-passing function is

{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{0}
, regardless of whether

v_{n}
is the center
v
of
G_{v}
or not.

Normalization
.
After
\mathsf{HGIN}
generates embeddings of vertices,
it conducts L2 normalization
to ensure that all embeddings
are positive.
More specifically,
for each vertex
v
,
let
e_{v}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
be its embedding generated via
\mathsf{HGIN}
,
and its L2 norm is
|\!|{e_{v}}|\!|_{2}=\sqrt{x^{2}_{1}+\ldots+x^{2}_{d}}
.
Then the normalized embedding
e^{N}_{v}
of
the original embedding
e_{v}

is defined as

e^{N}_{v}:=\frac{|e_{v}|}{|\!|{e_{v}}|\!|_{2}}
.

Prediction Function
.
Given the embedding
e_{u}{=}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})

of vertex
u
in
Q
and the embedding
e_{v}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})

of
v
in
G
,
it exploits the order embedding space
(
Vendrov et al. 2016
;
Ying et al. 2020
)
,
to check the existence of homomorphic mapping
from
Q
to
G
as follows:

Q
is homomorphic to
G

if and only if

a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
i\in[1,d]
.
To improve the generalization of
\mathsf{HGIN}
,
a threshold
t
is used to
bound the distance between
e_{u}

and
e_{v}
.
More specifically, the prediction function
f
is defined as:

(7)

f\left(e_{u},e_{v}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\mathcal{M}\left(e_{u},e_{v}\right)\leq t;\\
0&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}

Here, function
\mathcal{M}(e_{u},e_{v})

is defined as
|\!|{\max(0,e_{u}-e_{v})}|\!|_{2}^{2}
.

Loss function
.
To train
\mathsf{HGIN}
,
we enhance the max-margin loss function
by integrating
message-passing functions.
This enforces vertex identities
in
\mathsf{IDGNN}
,
by ensuring that

M^{k,r}_{1}
is sufficiently larger than

M^{k,r}_{0}
.
When
Q
has a cycle of length

l
containing
u
, but
G
does not
have such a cycle containing
v
,
computing the embedding of
u

invokes message-passing functions
M^{k,r}_{1}

more frequently than
computing the embedding of
v
.
Thus, the embedding of
u
is larger than
that of
v
,
implying that
Q
is not homomorphic to
G
.
More specifically, the max-margin loss function is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{(Q,u,G,v)\in P}(\mathcal{M}(h_{u},h_{v})-\alpha)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.61108pt+\sum\nolimits_{(Q,u,G,v)\in N}\text{max}\{0,\alpha-\mathcal{M}(h_{u},h_{v})\}

(8)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.61108pt+\sum\nolimits_{k\leq m}\sum\nolimits_{r\in R}\frac{1}{{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{1}-{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{0}},

where (1)
P
is the set of
positive examples

(Q,u,G,v)
such that
there exists a homomorphic mapping
\varphi

from
Q
to
G
such that

\varphi(u)=v
, (2)
N
is the set of negative examples
(Q,u,G,v)

such that there
does not
exist a homomorphic mapping
\varphi

from
Q
to
G
such that

\varphi(u){=}v
,
and (3)
\alpha
is the margin threshold
for the prediction function.

Remark
.
The loss function
\mathcal{L}

differs from the loss function
for subgraph matching in
(
Ying et al. 2020
)

as follows.

(1) We add the term
\frac{1}{{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{1}-{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{0}}

to ensure that

{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{1}

is sufficiently larger than
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{0}
for each edge label
r
.
Intuitively, sufficiently
large
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}_{1}
allocates more weights
to the appearance of center vertices.
Combining with the set
{\mathcal{C}}
of cycle sizes,

\mathsf{HFrame}
can distinguish more negative pairs
than existing solutions,

i.e.,

\mathsf{HFrame}
has more expressive power
(see Section
4
).

(2) We add the term
-\alpha
to penalize
errors incurred by positive training data.
This guarantees that the loss function
is 1-Lipschitz, which is used to prove the generalization bound
for
\mathsf{HFrame}
(Theorem
4.4
).

.
HFrame takes
O(|Q||G|+m\delta_{max}(|Q|+|G|)(f(|Q|)+f(|G|)))
time
and
O(m\delta_{max}d(|G|+|Q|)f_{M})
space for inference,
and
O((|P|{+}|N|)(|Q||G|{+}m\delta_{max}(|Q|{+}|G|)(f(|Q|)+f(|G|))))
time
and
O((|P|{+}|N|)m\delta_{max}d(|G|{+}|Q|)f_{M})
space for training.
Here (1)
m
is number of layers,
(2)
\delta_{max}
is the maximum degree of
Q
and
G
,
(3)
f
is a lower-degree polynomial
denoting embedding time,
(4)
d
is dimension of embeddings,
(4)
f_{M}
is the size of
\mathsf{HGIN}
, and
(5)
|P|
(resp.
|N|
) is the number of positive
(resp. negative) training data.

4.
Theoretical analysis

We first study the expressive power
of
\mathsf{HFrame}
and then establish a generalization error bound for
it.
Since
\mathsf{HFrame}
consists of

\mathsf{DualSim}
and
\mathsf{HGIN}
,
we start with comparing their expressive power,
to justify their combination.
See Appendix for detailed proofs.

(1) Expressive power
.
We consider the following three cases.

(a)
\mathsf{DualSim}
vs. vanilla
\mathsf{HGIN}

.
We first show that
\mathsf{DualSim}
provides
an upper bound for the expressive power
of vanilla
\mathsf{HGIN}
(
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
),
which uses a unified message passing function for all vertices.
More specifically,
the same message passing function
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}

is applied to all neighbors
of vertices. Intuitively, both
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
and
\mathsf{DualSim}
inspect only neighbors of a vertex,
and have similar expressive power.
We prove that there exists
a
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
that is as expressive as
\mathsf{DualSim}
.

Theorem 4.1.

Given a pattern
Q
, a graph
G
,
a vertex
u
in
Q
and a vertex
v
in
G
,
if
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}

returns
\mathsf{false}
,
then
(u,v)\not\in S_{(Q,G)}
,

i.e.,

\mathsf{DualSim}
also determines that
there exists no homomorphism mapping
\varphi

from
Q
to
G
such that
\varphi(u)=v
,
even when all functions
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}

and
{\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}

(k\leq m)
in
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}

are injective and monotonic.

Proof.

We prove it by contradiction.
Assume by contradiction
that there exist a pattern
Q
, a graph
G
,
a vertex
u
in
Q
and a vertex
v
in
G
,
such that
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
returns
\mathsf{false}
,
but
\mathsf{DualSim}
returns
\mathsf{true}
,
i.e.,

(u,v)\in S_{(Q,G)}
.
We deduce a contradiction as follows.
(a) Computing
embeddings of
u
and

can be represented by
trees of height
m
, where
m
is
the number of layers in
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
.
(b) Because
{\mathsf{MSG}}^{k,r}
and
{\mathsf{AGG}}^{k}

(k\leq m)
in
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{v}

are injective and monotonic,
there exists a path

in the
computation tree for

such that
no path from

carries the same labels as

.
However, if these hold,
\mathsf{DualSim}
can also identify
such path and return
\mathsf{false}
, a contradiction.

\Box

(b)
\mathsf{DualSim}
vs.
\mathsf{HGIN}

.
When
\mathsf{HGIN}
uses different message passing functions
for vertices,
it can distinguish the center from other vertices
and
has a higher expressive power than
\mathsf{DualSim}
.

\mathsf{HGIN}

.

,

(2) We next show that
{\mathsf{HGIN}}^{\mathsf{v}}
can distinguish

\mathsf{DualSim}
.
Consider graphs

in Fig.

(a) There is no homomorphic mapping

(b)
\mathsf{HGIN}
returns
\mathsf{false}
given
w_{1}

and
v_{1}
, since

w_{1}
is
in a cycle of length 3
while
v_{1}
does not, which results in a larger embedding
of
w_{1}

than that of
v_{1}
.
(c)
\mathsf{DualSim}
confirms that
(w_{1},v_{1}){\in}S_{(G_{3},G_{2})}
,
since
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
are regular, all vertices have similar neighborhoods,
and
S_{(G_{3},G_{2})}{=}V_{G_{3}}{\times}V_{G_{2}}
.

\Box

(c)
\mathsf{DualSim}
vs.
\mathsf{HGIN}
of finite dimensions
.

\mathsf{HGIN}
represents vertices
with embeddings
of finite dimensions.
For graphs with a large number of labels
(
e.g.
, more edge labels than the embedding dimension),
we can construct a pattern

Q
and a graph
G
with different label sets
such that
(1) the embedding of
Q
is smaller than that of
G
,
but (2)
Q
is not homomorphic to
G
.
However,
\mathsf{DualSim}
can distingush graphs with any number of labels, by directly comparing their labels.

Theorem 4.3.

When the dimension of embeddings is finite,
there exist two graphs that
\mathsf{DualSim}
can distinguish, but
\mathsf{HGIN}
cannot.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathsf{HGIN}
generates
d
-dimensional embeddings.
(a) We select
2d
distinct labels
from the label set
\Gamma

and compute their embeddings using
\mathsf{HGIN}
; then
we choose
d
embeddings
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}

such that each
e_{i}
has the maximum
i
-th element.
(b) We construct a star pattern
Q_{s}
centered at
v
, with
d
edges,
each labeled with one of the
d
picked labels.
We then deduce a contradiction by comparing
the embeddings of
Q_{s}
with that of the
2d
distinct labels.

\Box

Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
imply that

\mathsf{HGIN}
and
\mathsf{DualSim}
have incomparable expressive power with finite embedding dimensions.
We can improve pattern matching by combining
\mathsf{DualSim}
with
\mathsf{HGIN}
.

(2) Generalization bound
.
We give a generalization error bound
for
\mathsf{HFrame}
to measure its prediction accuracy on unseen data.

Theorem 4.4.

The empirical Rademacher complexity of
\mathsf{HFrame}
is
\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{8}{N_{T}}+\frac{12}{\sqrt{N_{T}}}\sqrt{2|R|d^{2}\log T}
, where

\mathcal{T}

maps the training data to its loss,
and
T=16M\sqrt{{N_{T}}d}\max\{B_{x}B_{1},{\mathcal{R}}B_{2}\}
.

Here, (1)
|R|
is the number of edge labels in
Q
and
G
;
(2)
N_{T}
is the
size of training data;
and (3)
M
and
{\mathcal{R}}
are numbers
determined by functions in
\mathsf{HGIN}
.
Assume that the norms of
W_{1}
,
W^{k,r}_{i}
and
h^{k}_{u_{c}}

are bounded by
B_{1}
,
B_{r}
and
B_{x}
, respectively.

Remark
.
We extend
the proof of
the Rademacher complexity for GNNs in
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
.
(1)
\mathsf{HFrame}
takes a pattern
Q
and a graph
G

as input, while GNNs in
(
Garg et al. 2020
)

process only one graph;
(2)
\mathsf{HFrame}
handles patterns and graphs with both labels and directions, which are not explored
in
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
;
(3)
\mathsf{HGIN}
uses distinct message-passing functions
for different neighbors,
a feature absent in GNNs of
(
Garg et al. 2020
)
;
(4)
\mathsf{HGIN}
uses the max-margin loss function
to compare embeddings of vertices, which
complicates the analysis of
\mathsf{HGIN}
.

5.
Experimental study

Using real-life and synthetic graphs,
we evaluated the (1) effectiveness, (2) efficiency,
(3) parameter sensitiveness and (4) generalization
of the proposed framework

\mathsf{HFrame}
.
(5) We also conducted an ablation study on
\mathsf{HFrame}
,
and (6) applied
\mathsf{HFrame}
in
subgraph homomorphism enumeration and frequent pattern mining.

Experimental settings
.
We start with the settings.

Data Graphs.
We used six real-life graphs:
(1) Citation, a citation network with 1,397,240 vertices and 3,016,539 edges,
carrying 16,415 labels
(
Cit 2021a
)
;
(2) Citeseerx, another citation network with 6,540,401 vertices and 15,011,260 edges,
carrying 984 labels
(
Cit 2021b
)
;
(3) DBLP, a collaboration network with 205,783 vertices and 301,561 edges,
carrying 11 labels
(
DBL 2021
)
;
(4) IMDB, a movie database modeled as graph, with 5,101,080 vertices and 5,174,035 edges,
carrying 38 labels
(
IMD 2021
)
;
(5) DBpedia, a knowledge graph with 5,174,170 vertices and 17,494,747 edges
with 9,591 labels
(
DBp 2021
)
; and
(6) YAGO, a knowledge graph with 3,489,226 vertices and 7,351,194 edges
carrying 15 labels
(
YAG 2021
)
.

For synthetic dataset, we
generated a graph

G=(V,E,L)
, controlled by
the number of vertices
|{V}|
(up to 20M),
the number of edges
|{E}|
(up to 40M)
and the number of labels
|{L}|
(up to 500).

Baselines
.
We categorized the baselines as follows.

Algorithms
.
We compared
\mathsf{HFrame}
with the following algorithms
for subgraph matching:
(1) the edge-join approach (
\mathsf{EJ}
)
(
Cheng et al. 2010
)
,
which computes the occurrences for each edge of
the query in the data graph,
finds an optimized plan to join these binary relations,
and finally evaluates the query following the plan;
(2) the path-join approach (
\mathsf{PJ}
)
(
Sun and Luo 2019
)
,
which first generates solutions to each root-to-leaf path of the given query,
and then produces the answer by
joining solutions of these paths;
(3)
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}

(
Wu et al. 2023
)
,
which encodes all possible homomorphisms from a pattern to the graph with the concept of answer graph;
(4) a pattern matching algorithm

\mathsf{DualSim}

(
Ma et al. 2011
)
(see Section
2
);
and (5) popular graph DBMS
\mathsf{Neo4j}

(
Neo 2023
)
.

Among these algorithms,
\mathsf{EJ}
,
\mathsf{PJ}
,
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}

and
\mathsf{Neo4j}
are exact algorithms,
and
\mathsf{DualSim}
is an approximate algorithm.
For a fair comparison, we modified

\mathsf{EJ}
,
\mathsf{PJ}
,
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}

and
\mathsf{Neo4j}
to return only one
match,
as we focus on decision problem
rather than enumeration.

ML models
. We compared with the following
ML models: (6)
\mathsf{NeuroMatch}

(
Ying et al. 2020
)
, which applies
GNN to compute the embedding, and exploits
the order embedding space to make predictions
for subgraph isomorphism;
(7)
\mathsf{D2Match}

(
Liu et al. 2023
)
,
which proposes a degeneracy procedure
that frames subgraph matching as a subtree matching problem;
and (8)
\mathsf{DMPNN}

(
Liu and Song 2022
)
,
which develops dual message passing neural networks
to improve substructure representation.

Training data
.
We constructed training data by sampling from large data graphs
following
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Ying et al. 2020
)
.
It consists of both positive and negative data.
(1) We constructed a set
P
of positive data
(Q_{p},u,G_{p},v)
,

i.e.,
there exists a homomorphic mapping
\varphi

from
Q_{p}
to
G_{p}
such that
\varphi(u)=v
.
For each graph
G
,
we (a) first randomly extract a subgraph
G_{p}

by performing BFS from a random vertex on
G

in 5 to 10 steps,
(b) select a connected graph
Q_{p}
from
G_{p}
as the pattern,
where the number of nodes in
Q_{p}
is between 3 and 20,
(c) randomly copy vertices and edges in
Q_{p}

to enforce the homomorphic semantics,
and (d) pick one vertex
u
from
Q_{p}
as the pivot and
set the same vertex in
G_{p}
as the pivot
v
.
(2) We also construct a set
N
of

negative data

(Q_{n},u,G_{n},v)
,
such that no homomorphic mapping exists from
u

to
v
.
To this end, we first extract positive data

(Q_{p},u,G_{p},v)
as above,
and then calibrate the pattern
Q_{p}

to create pattern
Q_{n}
that has no match
in
G_{p}
, by adding edges randomly to
Q_{p}
or perturbing the pivot in
G_{p}
.

To make ensure that
\mathsf{HFrame}
is robust, we set the ratio of positive to
negative instances as
1:3
.
Moreover, we employed
\mathsf{Neo4j}
to
compute the ground-truth between the patterns
and the graphs.

We sampled 20,000 pairs of query and subgraph from each graph,

i.e.,
5,000 positive and 15,000 negative examples,
and split each dataset into training, validation
and test sets at a ratio of 8:1:1.
As
\mathsf{EJ}
,
\mathsf{PJ}
and
\mathsf{D2Match}
do not
support edge labels,
when comparing with these baselines,
we removed all edge labels
from patterns and graphs for fair comparison.
It should be noted that, since
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}
is only applicable to tree structure,
every query was decomposed into multiple tree queries before conducting the experiments on
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}
.

Evaluation Metric
.
We tested accuracy of

\mathsf{HFrame}
with the metric:

\text{Acc}=(TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)
,
where
TP
,
TN
,
FP
and
FN
denote
the numbers of true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives, respectively.
In terms of efficiency, we measure the end-to-end CPU time
for exact algorithms (
i.e.,

\mathsf{EJ}
,
\mathsf{PJ}
,
\mathsf{SIM}
-
\mathsf{TD}
and
\mathsf{Neo4j)}
;
for
\mathsf{HFrame}
, we measure CPU time for
\mathsf{DualSim}
and GPU time for
\mathsf{HGIN}
.
When there exists no matching for a query, the exact algorithms will exhaustively traverse
the entire search space before returning false.
To ensure the fairness on efficiency comparison,
we imposed a 30-second time limit on the runtime.

Experiment Environments.

We run the experiments on a server running
Ubuntu 11.4.0 with Intel Xeon E5-2680v4 CPU,
NVIDIA Tesla V100 PCIe 32GB GPU and 512GB RAM.

\mathsf{HGIN}
training and inference are performed on GPU,
while the remaining components of
\mathsf{HFrame}
run on CPU.
By default, we set the number of layers
m=5
,
the dimension of embeddings
d=64
, the margin
\alpha=1.5
and the threshold
t=0.1
.
We set the number of iterations for
\mathsf{DualSim}
to 2.
We ran each test 5 times, and report the average here.

. We next report our findings.

Exp-3: Parameter sensitiveness
.
We next evaluated the impacts of the sizes
|Q|
of
patterns (
i.e.,

|V_{Q}|+|E_{Q}|
), the number
m
of layers
in
\mathsf{HGIN}
, the threshold
t
, the dimension
d

of embeddings,
and the average degree
\delta_{\rm avg}
of graphs on the performance of
\mathsf{HFrame}
.
We conducted experiments on dataset Citation.

Varying
|Q|
.
We varied
|Q|
from 10 to 35.
As shown in Table
5
,
(1) when
|Q|
increases,
the accuracy of
|Q|
decreases, as expected,
since making prediction is more challenging
for large patterns;
and (2)
\mathsf{HFrame}
becomes slower when
|Q|
increases,
as expected,
since it takes more time to compute embeddings.

Varying
m
.
We varied
m
from 2 to 7.
As shown in Table
5
,
(1) when

m
increases,

\mathsf{HFrame}
gets slower,
as expected, since it takes more time to compute embeddings;
and (2) the accuracy of
\mathsf{HFrame}
first increases
and then decreases,
i.e.,
its optimal performance achieves
when
m=5
.
This is because when
m
is small,

\mathsf{HFrame}
does not have sufficient information
to make a prediction;
while when
m
is large,

\mathsf{HFrame}
cannot distinguish neighbors
distant from the pivot.

Varying
t
.
We varied the threshold
t
from 0.01 to 0.5.
As shown in Table
5
,
(1) the runtime of
\mathsf{HFrame}
is insensitive
to
t
,
since the threshold is used only when
the embeddings of vertices have been computed;
and (2) the accuracy of
\mathsf{HFrame}
first increases
and then decreases, and its optimal performance achieves
when
t=0.1
.
This is because (a) when
t
is small,

\mathsf{HFrame}
returns
\mathsf{true}
only when
it correctly makes predictions,
and the recall of
\mathsf{HFrame}
is low;
and (b) when
t
is large,

\mathsf{HFrame}
may return
\mathsf{true}
when
Q
is not homomorphic to
G
,
but its embedding is slightly larger than that of
G
,

i.e.,
the precision of
\mathsf{HFrame}
is low.

Varying
d
.
We varied the dimension
d

of embeddings from 16 to 512.
We found the following.
(1)
\mathsf{HFrame}
gets slower as
d
increases,
since the order-embedding space inspects
each dimension of embeddings;
and (2) the accuracy of
\mathsf{HFrame}
first increases
and then decreases, and its optimal performance achieves
when
d=64
.
This is because (a) when
d
is small,
multiple vertices can be mapped
to the same embedding and
the accuracy of
\mathsf{HFrame}
is low;
and (b) if
d
is large,
each embedding has many values,
and it is hard to ensure that the embedding
of
Q
is smaller than that of
G
,
even if
Q
is homomorphic to
G
.

Varying
\delta_{\rm avg}
.
We generated synthetic graphs,
and grouped them based on whether
their average degrees
\delta_{\rm avg}
are in the range
[d_{1},d_{2})
.
Each group contains 5000 graphs.
As shown in Table
5
,
increasing graph density slightly reduces
\mathsf{HFrame}
’s accuracy and increases its running time, as expected.

1

(1)
\mathsf{HFrame}
outperforms

\mathsf{HFrame}

{\mathsf{HFrame}}_{{\mathsf{MS}}}

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a framework
\mathsf{HFrame}
for the subgraph homomorphism problem
by combining algorithmic solutions
and ML models.
We showed that
\mathsf{HFrame}
is more expressive
that the vanilla GNN for subgraph matching.

Using real-life and synthetic graphs,
we experimentally validated the efficiency
and effectiveness of the proposed frameworks.

\mathsf{GNNs}

(

)
.
