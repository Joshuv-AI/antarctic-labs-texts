A Compound Logistic Regression Model for Binary Responses
Anthony Almudevar, PhD, Jacob Almudevar, MSc

Abstract
Logistic regression is the most commonly used method for constructing predictive models for binary responses. One significant drawback to this approach, however, is that the asymptotes of the logistic response function are fixed at 0 and 1, and there are many applications for which this constraint is inappropriate. More flexible models have been proposed for this application, most proceeding by supplementing the logistic response function with additional parameters. In this article we extend these models to allow correlated responses and the inclusion of covariates. This is achieved through the compound logistic regression model , for which the mean response is a function of several logistic regression functions. This permits a greater variety of models, while retaining the advantages of logistic regression. The methods are implemented in an R package CLmodel ( https://github.com/ALMUDEVAR163/CLmodel/ ).

Introduction

We are given paired observations
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
x_{i}
is a
p
-dimensional vector of covariates and
y_{i}
is a binary response, interpreted as a Bernoulli random variable. The problem is to use the observations to estimate a model of the form

P(y=1\mid x)=\pi(x),\,\,y\in\{0,1\},\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}.

(1)

To motivate the methodology proposed in this article we will initially assume that
x=z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
is some biomarker able to predict the presence or absence of a condition, coded as
y=1
or
y=0
. We might reasonably expect response function
\pi(z)
to be monotone in
z
and bounded in
[0,1]
, so there must exist asymptotes:

a_{L}=\lim_{z\rightarrow\,\text{-}\infty}\pi(z)\,\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\,a_{U}=\lim_{z\rightarrow\,\infty}\pi(z).

If
\pi(z)
is increasing, then
a_{U}
(
a_{L}
) is the prevalence of the condition among subjects with high (low) levels of the biomarker. One problem of importance is the identification of a
threshold
level
z_{thr}
, that is, a value near which
\pi(z)
transitions rapidly between the two asymptotes. If
\pi(z)
possesses odd symmetry, then
z_{thr}
can be defined as the point of symmetry, otherwise some canonical definition of
z_{thr}
can be used. We might also introduce a fourth quantity
\alpha
to represent the rate of increase of
\pi(z)
in the neighborhood of
z_{thr}
.

To fix ideas, suppose
z
is some antibody concentration and
y
codes an infection event. A pure
threshold model
is proposed in
3
, given as

\pi_{inf}(z)=a_{L}I\{z<z_{thr}\}+a_{U}I\{z\geq z_{thr}\},

(2)

where
z_{thr}
is a ”correlate of protection” (COP), that is, a concentration of antibody sufficient to confer some level of immunity. Then
a_{U}
and
a_{L}
are the infection rates with and without that protection, respectively. Here,
\pi_{inf}(z)
is simply a discrete step function, with
\alpha
playing no role (equivalently,
\alpha=\infty
). In contrast, in a study reported in
2
protection was observed to vary continuously with antibody levels. A general COP model should anticipate both cases.

Undoubtedly the most commonly used response function for binary response models is the logistic function
\phi(u)=1/(1+\exp(\text{-}u))
. Incorporating a single covariate with an intercept term, the logistic regression model is commonly written

\pi(z)=\phi(\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}z).

(3)

With a simple one-to-one reparametrization Equation (
3
) can be written

\pi(z)=\phi(\alpha(z-z_{thr}))

(4)

to match the elements of the COP model. However, this model is constrained to have asymptotes equal to zero or one, which would be unreasonably restrictive for many applications of this type. Of course, it is possible to construct a generalized logistic function by incorporating additional parameters, for example,

\phi_{g}(t)=a+\frac{b-a}{(c+e^{-d-ft})^{1/g}}

(5)

for suitably bound constants
(a,b,c,d,f,g)

(
13
)
.

We next argue that a reasonable level of generalization can be achieved by setting
c=g=1
in Equation (
5
), which is able to extend model (
4
) to

\pi_{g}(z)=a_{L}+\frac{a_{U}-a_{L}}{1+e^{\text{-}\alpha(z-z_{thr})}},

(6)

using the parameters
(a_{L},a_{U},z_{thr},\alpha)
introduced above. Of course, the advantage of the logistic model of Equation (
3
) is that
z
can be replaced with a vector of covariates
{\tilde{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, with coefficient vector
\tilde{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, the model now becoming
\pi({\tilde{x}})=\phi(\eta)
,
\eta=\tilde{\beta}^{T}{\tilde{x}}
(note that throughout this article, in the context of matrix algebra any vector is assumed to be a column vector). This allows the response distribution to depend on multiple covariates in an intuitive and analytically tractable way.

The purpose of this article is to propose a method of applying this kind of multivariate extension to a model such as Equation (
6
). To this effect we may always rewrite (
6
) as

\pi_{g}({\tilde{x}})=a_{L}+\frac{a_{U}-a_{L}}{1+e^{\text{-}\eta}}=a_{L}+(a_{U}-a_{L})\phi(\eta),

(7)

making use of the linear term
\eta=\tilde{\beta}^{T}{\tilde{x}}
. Then through a simple reparametrization

\eta
can include
\alpha(z-z_{thr})
, as well as additional covariates on which the effective COP threshold would be allowed to depend.

However, we would also like to allow the asymptotes
a_{L}
,
a_{U}
to depend on additional covariates, involving coefficients that admit conventional forms of inference and intuitive interpretations. We next introduce a method by which this may be done.

1.1
Asymptote Model

Following Equation (
7
), since
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are probabilities, we can reasonably represent each by its own logistic response function. Thus, Equation (
7
) can be revised to give

\displaystyle\pi_{c}({\tilde{x}}_{1},{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\eta_{2})+(\phi(\eta_{3})-\phi(\eta_{2}))\phi(\eta_{1})

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\eta_{3})\phi(\eta_{1})+\phi(\eta_{2})(1-\phi(\eta_{1}))

where
a_{L}=\phi(\eta_{2})
and
a_{U}=\phi(\eta_{3})
, and
\eta_{j}=\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{T}{\tilde{x}}_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
. We assume
{\tilde{x}}_{1}
incorporates
z
, and any other covariates predictive of
z_{thr}
, while
{\tilde{x}}_{2}
and
{\tilde{x}}_{3}
incorporate covariates predictive of
a_{L}
and
a_{U}
respectively.

The relationship between the logistic response components in Equation (
8
) can be summarized using a single compounding function
H
, for example,

\displaystyle\pi_{c}({\tilde{x}}_{1},{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(\phi(\eta_{1}),\phi(\eta_{2}),\phi(\eta_{3})),\textdagger\mbox{ where }

\displaystyle H(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{3}u_{1}+u_{2}(1-u_{1}).

(9)

1.2
Vaccine Efficacy Model

We may, however, consider alternative compounding methods. For example, compare model (
6
) to a COP model considered in the literature:

\pi_{inf}(z)=\xi(1-\gamma\kappa(z)),

(10)

(
6
;
5
)
. Here,
\kappa(z)
models COP threshold, and is therefore a function of antibody
z
, with lower and upper asymptotes 0 and 1, representing minimum and maximum protection. This can be reasonably modeled by

\kappa(z)=\phi(\alpha(z-z_{thr})),

(11)

in which case (
10
) and (
11
) are together equivalent to (
6
). In particular, substituting
\kappa(z)=0,1
gives unprotected risk
\pi_{inf}^{max}=\xi=a_{L}
, and fully protected risk
\pi_{inf}^{min}=\xi(1-\gamma)=a_{U}
. Note that the threshold model (
2
) is approached in the limit as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,
\xi
can be interpreted as infection risk attributable to exposure. In addition, recall the definition of vaccine efficacy

VE=1-\frac{r_{v}}{r_{e}}

where
r_{v}
is the rate of disease among vaccinated individuals and
r_{e}
is the rate among unvaccinated individuals. Here, we may set
r_{e}=\xi
,
r_{v}=\xi(1-\gamma)
, therefore

VE=1-\frac{\xi(1-\gamma)}{\xi}=1-(1-\gamma)=\gamma\in(0,1),

so that
\gamma
is directly interpretable as vaccine efficacy.

We note at this point that the threshold, efficacy and exposure components
\kappa(z),\gamma
and
\xi
capture quite distinct contributions to
\pi_{inf}(z)
. This must be anticipated by any methodology attempting to estimate the effect of covariates on a COP model. For example, one covariate may be expected to affect only exposure, another only vaccine efficacy, a third only COP threshold
z_{thr}
. In this case we may substitute separate logistic response functions
\kappa(z)=\phi(\eta_{1})
,
\gamma=\phi(\eta_{2})
and
\xi=\phi(\eta_{3})
, yielding

\pi_{inf}({\tilde{x}}_{1},{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})=\phi(\eta_{3})(1-\phi(\eta_{2})\phi(\eta_{1}))

(12)

(note that while
\gamma
is not a probability we still expect it to be in the interval
(0,1)
). As in Equation (
9
) we can isolate the compounding function
H
,

\displaystyle\pi_{inf}({\tilde{x}}_{1},{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H(\phi(\eta_{1}),\phi(\eta_{2}),\phi(\eta_{3})),\mbox{ where }

\displaystyle H(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{3}(1-u_{2}u_{1}).

(13)

Note that the compounding function
H
used in Equation (
13
) differs from that used in Equation (
9
). We will show below that a considerable advantage of the compound logistic model is that the contributions to model complexity due to the multiple components scale in a linear manner, in the sense that the estimating equations for the model are a weighted sum of estimating equations constructed for each component separately (see Equation (
39
) below).

1.3
Interpretation of Compound Logistic Models

At this point we have proposed constructing new response functions for binary response models by compounding separate logistic
response functions into a single expression defined by some compounding function
H
. Distinct examples are given in
Equations (
9
) and (
13
). We have not imposed any formal restrictions, other than
noting that any logistic component
\phi(\eta_{j})
is, so far, being used to represent a probability, or some other quantity confined to the interval
(0,1)
.

The natural interpretability of the logistic regression model
\pi({\tilde{x}})=\phi(\tilde{\beta}^{T}{\tilde{x}})
is well known. For example,
\tilde{\beta}^{T}{\tilde{x}}
is the predicted log-odds of
P(y=1)
for an observation with covariates
{\tilde{x}}
, alternatively,
\beta_{j}\Delta
is the predicted log-odds ratio between two observations which differ only in covariate
x_{j}
by
\Delta
.

We next show how similar interpretive statements hold for the compound logistic models considered here. To do so,
we assume the existence of some biomarker
z
, and a relationship to a binary outcome
y
governed by parameters

(a_{1},a_{2},z_{thr},\alpha)
as described above.

1.3.1
Asymptote model

Suppose, following Equation (
8
) we write

\pi_{c}({\tilde{x}}_{1},{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})=\phi(\eta_{3})\phi(\eta_{1})+\phi(\eta_{2})(1-\phi(\eta_{1})).

The biomarker
z
is assumed to exist only in
\eta_{1}
, so that
z
represents a natural axis for the model. Suppose interest is in comparing observations with high and low values of
z
. We can, without loss of generality, assume
\phi(\eta_{1})
is increasing in
z
(otherwise, reverse the sign of the appropriate coefficient). Then replace
\phi(\eta_{1})
in (
8
) with a general variable
U
, so that

\pi_{c}(U,{\tilde{x}}_{2},{\tilde{x}}_{3})=\phi(\eta_{3})U+\phi(\eta_{2})(1-U).

Then
U=1
is the limiting case as
z\rightarrow\infty
and
U=0
is the limiting case as
z\rightarrow-\infty
, interpretable simply as high or low biomarker levels, respectively (or maximum or minimum biomarker effect). Then
P(y=1\mid U=1)=\phi(\eta_{3})
and
P(y=1\mid U=0)=\phi(\eta_{2})
, leading to the odds ratio

\frac{Odds(y=1\mid U=1)}{Odds(y=1\mid U=0)}=e^{\eta_{3}-\eta_{2}}.

Thus, if we wish to allow this odds ratio to depend on a specific covariate, we would need to include it in both
{\tilde{x}}_{2}
and
{\tilde{x}}_{3}
. In Section
3.2
below we will consider
the implications of doing so with respect to model identifiability and the stability of numerical fitting algorithms.

Vaccine efficacy model

Consider the sequence of events represented by the decision tree in Figure
1
. For any individual, event
E
is exposure to a pathogen. We assume an individual is either maximally protected (event
U=1
) or has no protection (event
U=0
). Then

if an individual is infected or acquires an infectious disease (whatever the COP is intended to prevent), and is zero
otherwise. We then have the conditional probabilities

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid E^{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid E\cap U=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{v},

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid E\cap U=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{e}.

We also assume the events
E,\{U=1\}
are independent. We can observe
y
and
U
for an individual. Then

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid U=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid E\cap U=1)P(E\mid U=1)

(14)

\displaystyle\,\,\,+P(y=1\mid E^{c}\cap U=1)P(E^{c}\mid U=1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{v}P(E),

\displaystyle P(y=1\mid U=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau_{e}P(E),

(15)

Suppose we set vaccine efficacy

\gamma=1-\frac{P(y=1\mid U=1)}{P(y=1\mid U=0)}.

The infection rate as a function of protection status can be expressed as

\pi_{E,U}(u)=P(y=1\mid E\cap U=0)\left(1-\gamma u\right),\,\,\,u\in\{0,1\}.

(16)

It is easily verified that

\pi_{E,U}(u)=P(y=1\mid E\cap U=u)\,\,\,u\in\{0,1\}.

This formulation as a compound logistic response function allows a separation between covariates predictive of exposure and
covariates predictive of vaccine efficacy. Introducing the expressions of Equation (
14
) and (
15
)
we have vaccine efficacy

\gamma=1-\frac{\tau_{u}P(E)}{\tau_{u^{c}}P(E)}=1-\frac{\tau_{u}}{\tau_{u^{c}}}.

Note that
\tau_{u},\tau_{u^{c}}

\pi_{E,U}(u)=\tau_{u^{c}}P(E)\left(1-\gamma u\right).

We can replace

in (

\phi(\eta_{1})
. For clarity, we may set
\eta_{1}=\alpha(z-z_{thr})
, possibly introducing additional covariates additively. We can introduce a second logistic response function, setting
\gamma=\phi(\eta_{2})
in (

\eta_{2}
can be confined to those predictive specifically of vaccine efficacy. Note that

\phi(-\eta_{2})=1-\phi(\eta_{2})=\frac{\tau_{u}}{\tau_{u^{c}}}

is then interpretable as a relative risk, expected to be strictly less than one. We can then introduce a third logistic response function, setting

P(y=1\mid U=0)=\tau_{u^{c}}P(E)=\phi(\eta_{3})
. Then
\eta_{3}
can include covariates related to exposure, as well as covariates
predictive of an unprotected individual’s risk of infection given exposure.

Inference for General Binary Models

Suppose we are given binary observations
y_{i}
with mean
\pi_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Assume
\pi_{i}=\pi_{i}(\theta)
for parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. The variance of
y_{i}
is then
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(\theta)=\pi_{i}(1-\pi_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. We use the following notation to denote the collection of partial derivatives (assumed to exist):

\pi^{\prime}_{ij}=\pi^{\prime}_{ij}(\theta)=\frac{\partial\pi_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}},\,\,\,i=1,\ldots,n,\,\,\,j=1,\ldots,p.

Let
\tilde{y}
,
\tilde{\pi}
,
\tilde{\tau}
,
\tilde{\pi}^{\prime}_{j}
, be
n
-dimensional column vectors with
i
th element
y_{i}
,
\pi_{i}
,
\tau_{i}
,
\pi^{\prime}_{ij}
, respectively. For any
n
-dimensional (column) vector
\tilde{v}
let
D_{\tilde{v}}
be the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with diagonal
\tilde{v}
. Then
I_{r}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix. For a square matrix
A
, let
A^{1/2}
be any matrix satisfying
A^{1/2}A^{1/2}=A
.

Since the marginal variances of
\tilde{y}
are given by
\tilde{\tau}
, the covariance matrix of
\tilde{y}
can be written

V_{\theta}=D_{\tilde{\tau}(\theta)^{1/2}}R_{\tilde{y}}\,D_{\tilde{\tau}(\theta)^{1/2}},

(18)

where
R_{\tilde{y}}
is the correlation matrix of
\tilde{y}
. That correlation is unrelated to independent variables used in a predictive model, or to unknown parameters more generally, is considered reasonable, so we assume that
R_{\tilde{y}}
does not depend on
\theta
. In this case
V_{\theta}
depends on
\theta
only through
\tilde{\tau}(\theta)
.

2.1
Estimating equations

Under the assumption of independent responses the log-likelihood function for
\theta
is

L(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\log(\pi_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\pi_{i}),

(19)

and standard methods, such as Fisher scoring, may be used to derive maximum likelihood estimate

{\hat{\theta}}_{MLE}=\mbox{argmax}_{\theta}\,L(\theta),

and standard error estimates.

However, if we have subject-level repeated measures the derivation of the true likelihood, which correctly models response correlation, is usually not straightforward. In this case the estimating equation approach may be used, which depends on the distribution of
\tilde{y}
only through the first and second order moments.

Suppose we are given an
n
-dimensional column vector

\tilde{g}(\theta)=[g_{1}(\theta)\ldots g_{n}(\theta)].

Define the
n\times p
matrix differential operator

\Gamma(\tilde{g})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial g_{1}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}}&\ldots&\frac{\partial g_{1}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}&\ldots&\frac{\partial g_{1}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{p}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial g_{n}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{1}}&\ldots&\frac{\partial g_{n}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}&\ldots&\frac{\partial g_{n}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{p}}\end{bmatrix}

Estimating equations generally take the form

G(\theta)=\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})^{T}W_{\theta}^{-1}[\tilde{y}-\tilde{\pi}]=0,

(20)

where
W_{\theta}
is a positive definite matrix for all
\theta
. It will be convenient to define the matrices:

\displaystyle\Omega^{W}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})^{T}W_{\theta}^{-1}\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})

\displaystyle\Omega^{V}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})^{T}V_{\theta}^{-1}\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})

\displaystyle\Omega^{WV}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})^{T}W_{\theta}^{-1}V_{\theta}W_{\theta}^{-1}\Gamma(\tilde{\pi}).

Define solution
G({\hat{\theta}})=0
. This form of estimation is shared by many widely used methods. These include generalized estimating equations (GEE), which extend generalized linear models to allow correlated responses
(
9
)
, or quasi-likelihood estimation, in which the relationship between mean and variance need not be constrained by any distributional assumptions
(
16
)
.

Conditions for asymptotic normality of
{\hat{\theta}}
for these various models are given in, for example,
11
or
16
. It is generally assumed that, with probability approaching 1 as
n\rightarrow\infty
, a unique solution to the system of equations (
20
) exists, or at least that a single solution in a neighborhood of the true parameter
\theta^{*}
can be identified.
More generally, mathematical validation of these conclusions for estimating equations (
20
) for quite general models follows from Theorem 13.1 of
1
. In this case, under conditions permitting application of Taylor’s theorem, we have approximation

{\hat{\theta}}-\theta^{*}\approx\mbox{-}[G^{\prime}(\theta^{*})]^{-1}G(\theta^{*}),

(21)

where
G^{\prime}(\theta)
is the Jacobian matrix of
G(\theta)
with respect to
\theta
. Further approximations rely on the observation that the elements of
G(\theta)
and
G^{\prime}(\theta)
are random sums. Therefore, under general conditions we may use approximations
G^{\prime}(\theta^{*})\approx E_{\theta^{*}}[G^{\prime}(\theta^{*})]
and
G(\theta^{*})\sim N(0,\Omega^{WV}_{\theta^{*}})
, following some matrix algebra. This gives the approximation

{\hat{\theta}}-\theta^{*}\approx\mbox{-}E_{\theta^{*}}[G^{\prime}(\theta^{*})]^{-1}G(\theta^{*}).

(22)

In addition, we have

\displaystyle E_{\theta^{*}}[G(\theta^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle E_{\theta^{*}}[G^{\prime}(\theta^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{-}\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}.

(23)

This leads to approximate distribution

\hat{\theta}\sim N(\theta^{*},\Sigma^{W}_{\theta^{*}})

(24)

where

\Sigma^{W}_{\theta}=\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta}\right]^{-1}\Omega^{WV}_{\theta}\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta}\right]^{-1}.

(25)

If
W_{\theta}=V_{\theta}
, then it is easily verified that

\Sigma_{\theta}^{W}=\left[\Omega^{V}_{\theta}\right]^{-1}.

(26)

Then substitute
{\hat{\theta}}
for
\theta^{*}
, giving
\Sigma^{W}_{\theta^{*}}\approx\Sigma^{W}_{{\hat{\theta}}}
.

Finally, under given regularity conditions the solution
\hat{\theta}
can be obtained as the limit of the iterative
algorithm

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta_{n}}\right]^{-1}G(\theta_{n}),\,\,\ n\geq 0,

(27)

given some suitable initial solution
\theta_{0}
.

We may also wish to estimate fitted curves
\hat{\pi}(\tilde{x})
for arbitrary predictor values
\tilde{x}
. Following
4
the relevant asymptotic variance is

{\mbox{Var}}[\hat{\pi}(x)]=\left[\frac{\partial\pi(x)}{\partial\theta}\right]^{T}\Sigma^{W}_{{\hat{\theta}}}\left[\frac{\partial\pi(x)}{\partial\theta}\right].

(28)

An approximation follows by substituting
{\hat{\theta}}
for
\theta
. If an
\alpha/2
normal critical value is used to construct the confidence bands, then the confidence level
1-\alpha
must be interpreted marginally. In
4
it is shown that a simultaneous
1-\alpha
confidence band results when the critical value
(\chi^{2}_{p,\alpha})^{1/2}
from a
\chi^{2}
distribution with
p
degrees of freedom is used.

2.2
Estimating procedures

So far, the only assumption imposed for weight matrix
W_{\theta}
used in the estimating equations (
20
) is positive definiteness and even this can be relaxed. However, the choice
W_{\theta}=V_{\theta}
is provably optimal, and is therefore the obvious choice, absent other considerations.

2.2.1
Independent responses

If responses are independent, parameters are fit by maximizing the likelihood function (
19
).
This is equivalent to setting
W_{\theta}=V_{\theta}=D_{\tilde{\tau}}
. Inference can be based on approximation with variance matrix (
26
), so that

{\hat{\theta}}\sim N\left(\theta^{*},[\Omega^{V}_{{\hat{\theta}}}]^{-1}\right)

is the relevant approximate distribution.

2.2.2
Dependent responses

If responses are dependent a number of options are available. The maximum likelihood estimates, calculated under the assumption of independence, are still viable estimates, again setting
W_{\theta}=D_{\tilde{\tau}}
, and an approximate density can be based on (
24
). The difference is that
W_{\theta}\neq V_{\theta}
, and so the correct asymptotic distribution is approximated by

{\hat{\theta}}\sim N\left(\theta^{*},\Sigma^{W}_{{\hat{\theta}}}\right),

(29)

which requires an estimation of
V_{\theta}
, and therefore
R_{\tilde{y}}
. This can be obtained by first solving the estimating equations with
W_{\theta}=D_{\tilde{\tau}}
, yielding estimate
{\hat{\theta}}_{I}
. Estimates of the mean and variance of each response
y_{i}
are available as the elements of
\tilde{\pi}=\tilde{\pi}({\hat{\theta}}_{I})
,
\tilde{\tau}=\tilde{\tau}({\hat{\theta}}_{I})
. The Pearson residuals are then

{\hat{r}}_{i}=(y_{i}-\hat{\pi}_{i})/\hat{\tau}_{i}^{1/2},

(30)

from which correlation can be directly estimated, using, for example, the approach proposed in
9
in the context of GEE estimation. The residuals contain information about
R_{\tilde{y}}
, although an unstructured estimation of the entire correlation matrix is generally not feasible. The standard approach is to assume a parametric form for
R_{\tilde{y}}
, estimating the parameters from residuals
{\hat{r}}_{i}
.

Once
R_{\tilde{y}}
is estimated, there are two options. The original estimate
{\hat{\theta}}_{I}
can be accepted, with inference based on (
29
), employing
R_{\tilde{y}}
and Equation (
18
) in the calculation of
\Sigma^{W}_{{\hat{\theta}}}
. However, since an estimate of
V_{\theta}
is now available we can solve estimating equations with
W_{\theta}=V_{\theta}
, yielding solution
{\hat{\theta}}_{R}
with an
asymptotic distribution approximated by

{\hat{\theta}}_{R}\sim N\left(\theta^{*},[\Omega^{V}_{{\hat{\theta}}}]^{-1}\right).

(31)

2.3
Exchangeable correlation

Under exchangeable correlation, random variates are partitioned into groups with common pairwise correlation
\rho
. Intergroup correlation is zero. Groups are typically defined as repeated measures from a common subject within a set of longitudinal data. This defines a parametric form for correlation matrix
R_{\tilde{y}}
which depends on a single parameter
\rho
(Section
2.2.2
).

Methods for estimating
\rho
are described in the literature. Suppose sample indices
i=1,\ldots,n
are partitioned into groups
g=1,\ldots,n_{g}
. Let
g_{i}
identify the group of sample
i
. Then for any
i\neq i^{\prime}
we have
{\mbox{Cor}}\left[y_{i},y_{i^{\prime}}\right]=\rho\neq 0
if
g_{i}=g_{i^{\prime}}
, and
{\mbox{Cor}}\left[y_{i},y_{i^{\prime}}\right]=0
otherwise. It is usually expected that
\rho>0
, but this is not required. Following
9
we have an estimate of
\rho
defined by

{\hat{\rho}}=(N_{pair}-d)^{-1}\sum_{g=1}^{n_{g}}\sum_{\left\{i<{i^{\prime}}:g_{i}=g_{i^{\prime}}=g\right\}}{\hat{r}}_{i}{\hat{r}}_{i^{\prime}},

(32)

where
N_{pair}
is the total number of correlated pairs contributing to the sum, and
d
is the number of estimated parameters.

2.4
Regularization

We will show below that there will be some advantage to stabilizing the model fit by using regularization. The ridge regression solution is to minimize

\Lambda=SSE+\lambda D,

where
SSE
is the model error sum of squares,
\lambda
is a nonnegative penalty constant,
D
is a penalty term given by

D=\theta^{T}B\theta,

\theta
is the
p\times 1
parameter column vector, and
B
is a
p\times p
positive-definite matrix, which does not depend on
\theta
or
\lambda

(
7
)
. This procedure renders the fit more stable while shrinking the parameter estimates towards zero. For logistic regression with independent responses,
SSE
is replaced with deviance. For dependent responses, the log-likelihood, and therefore the deviance is not formally defined. In this case, this form of regularization can be implemented directly into the estimating equations (
20
), giving

G_{\lambda}(\theta)=G(\theta)-\lambda\frac{\partial D}{\partial\theta}=0.

(33)

For independent responses, the solution to these equations is equivalent to

{\hat{\theta}}_{MLE}=\mbox{argmin}_{\theta}\,\text{-}2L(\theta)+\lambda D,

but represents the equivalent penalized fitting criterion when the log-likelihood is not defined. Often, the choice
B=I_{p}
is made, so that

D=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\theta_{j}^{2},\mbox{ and }\frac{\partial D}{\partial\theta}=2\theta.

(34)

The asymptotic distribution of
{\hat{\theta}}
can be deduced using the same method as that based on Equation (
22
), with the first and second order moments of Equation (
23
) replaced by

\displaystyle E_{\theta^{*}}[G_{\lambda}(\theta^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{-}{\lambda}\theta^{*},

\displaystyle E_{\theta^{*}}[G_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\theta^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{-}\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}-{\lambda}I_{p}.

(35)

This gives

\displaystyle{\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\theta^{*}+\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}+{\lambda}I_{p}\right]^{-1}\left[G(\theta^{*})-{\lambda}\theta^{*}\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}+{\lambda}I_{p}\right]^{-1}\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}\theta^{*}+G(\theta^{*})\right],

leading to approximation
{\hat{\theta}}\sim N(\theta^{\lambda}_{0},\Sigma^{W,{\lambda}}_{\theta^{*}})
based on the approximate moments

\displaystyle\theta^{\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\theta^{*}}[{\hat{\theta}}]\approx\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta^{*}}+{\lambda}I_{p}\right]^{-1}\Omega^{W}_{\theta_{0}}\theta_{0}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{W,{\lambda}}_{\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}_{\theta_{0}}[{\hat{\theta}}]\approx\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta_{0}}+{\lambda}I_{p}\right]^{-1}\Omega^{WV}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\Omega^{W}_{\theta_{0}}+{\lambda}I_{p}\right]^{-1},

(37)

(essentially this argument is given in
8
). Inclusion of
D
induces bias, and usually smaller variance, therefore the choice of
{\lambda}
represents a bias-variance trade-off. Selection of
{\lambda}
by cross-validation is discussed in the next section.

2.5
Model selection by cross-validation

Cross validation may be used for variable or model selection, and to select the penalty constant
{\lambda}

defined in (
34
). The strategy is to randomly partition the complete data set (of
n
observations) into
K
groups (as evenly as possible). Each group separately is taken as test data, then
{\hat{\theta}}_{train}
is estimated using the training data (the complete data set after deleting the test data). Then for each response
y_{test}
in the test data, the fitted value
{\hat{y}}_{test}
is calculated based on estimate
{\hat{\theta}}_{train}
and paired predictor
{\tilde{x}}_{test}
.

After applying the procedure to the
K
test groups, each response
y_{i}
is paired with a cross-validated fitted value
{\hat{y}}_{i}^{*}
. The final step is to calculate a goodness-of-fit score

GOF=GOF(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n};{\hat{y}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\hat{y}}_{n}^{*}),

which measures collectively the distances between
y_{i},{\hat{y}}^{*}_{i}
. Possible goodness-of-fit scores include deviance:

dev=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\log({\hat{y}}^{*}_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-{\hat{y}}^{*}_{i}),

area under ROC curve:

AUC=\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in I_{1}}I\{{\hat{y}}^{*}_{i^{\prime}}>{\hat{y}}^{*}_{i}\}+0.5\times I\{{\hat{y}}^{*}_{i^{\prime}}={\hat{y}}^{*}_{i}\}}{|I_{0}|\times|I_{1}|},

where
I_{j}
are the indices for which
y_{i}=j
,
j=0,1
, and the error sum of squares defined by the quadratic:

SSE=\bar{y}^{T}V_{\theta}^{-1}\bar{y}

where
\bar{y}
is an
n\times 1
column vector with
i
th row equal to
y_{i}-y^{*}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
V_{\theta}
is estimated using the complete data set.

In our implementation,
{\lambda}
can be varied over a grid spanning
{\lambda}=0
to some upper bound
\lambda^{max}
. For each proposed
{\lambda}
a model is fit, and evaluated using cross-validation. The choice
\lambda^{GOF}
is the one optimizing whatever
GOF
score is used.

3
Compound Logistic Model

We next show how the elements
\tilde{\pi}(\theta)
,
\tilde{\tau}(\theta)
,
\tilde{\pi}^{\prime}_{j}(\theta)
and the derived components
\Gamma(\tilde{\pi}(\theta))
,
V_{\theta}
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, are specialized to the compound logistic model. Suppose each mean binary response element
\pi_{i}(\theta)
of
\tilde{\pi}(\theta)
is constructed from
m
components
\pi_{ik}(\theta)
,
k=1,\ldots,m
via compounding function
H:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow(0,1)
, giving

\pi_{i}=H(\pi_{i1},\ldots,\pi_{im}),\,\,\,i=1,\ldots,n.

Write the following partial derivatives as:

H^{\prime}_{k}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=\frac{\partial H(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})}{\partial u_{k}},\,\,\,k=1,\ldots,m.

Then for convenience denote

H^{\prime}_{ik}=H^{\prime}_{ik}(\theta)=H^{\prime}_{k}(\pi_{i1}(\theta),\ldots,\pi_{im}(\theta)),

and construct
n
-dimensional column vectors
\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{k}=\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{k}(\theta)=[H^{\prime}_{1k}\ldots H^{\prime}_{nk}]
,
and
\tilde{\pi}_{k}=\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta)=[\pi_{1k}\ldots\pi_{nk}]
,
k=1,\ldots,m
. The derivative of response
\pi_{i}(\theta)
with respect to
\theta_{j}
is then decomposed into

\frac{\partial\pi_{i}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}H^{\prime}_{ik}\frac{\partial\pi_{ik}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}},

(38)

from which it follows that

\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}D_{\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{k}}\times\Gamma(\tilde{\pi}_{k}).

(39)

Interestingly, the gradient matrix of the estimating equation (
20
) for the model
\tilde{\pi}(\theta)
is simply a weighted sum of the gradient matrices for the models
\tilde{\pi}_{k}(\theta)
considered separately.

3.1
Application to Logistic Response Functions

We can develop (
39
) further in the context of logistic regression. Suppose with each of the components we associate
an
n\times p
design matrix
X_{k}
. Then
\pi_{ik}=\phi(\eta_{ik})
,
\eta_{ik}=\theta^{T}{\tilde{x}}_{ik}
, where
{\tilde{x}}_{ik}
is the
i
th row of
X_{k}
.
Then

\frac{\partial\pi_{ik}(\theta)}{\partial\theta_{j}}=\frac{\partial\eta_{ik}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\phi(\eta_{ik})(1-\phi(\eta_{ik}))=x_{ijk}\tau_{ik}

where
\{X_{k}\}_{ij}=x_{ijk}
and
\tau_{ik}=\pi_{ik}(1-\pi_{ik})
. We may then write

\Gamma(\tilde{\pi}_{k})=D_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}X_{k},\,\,\,k=1,\ldots,m,

(40)

so that (
39
) simplifies to

\Gamma(\tilde{\pi})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}D_{\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{k}}D_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}X_{k}.

(41)

Note that we have assumed that each component
\pi_{ik}(\theta)
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, is a function of a common parameter
\theta
. In an application it may be more natural to assign distinct parameters to each component. In this case, it would simply be a matter of partitioning the elements of
\theta
, so that each element
\theta_{j}
appears in only one component. Otherwise, an element may appear in more than one component. The methodology remains the same in either case. This issue is discussed further below.

Example

The data used in this example were collected as part of the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) project. This project originated in 1995 from a national survey by the MacArthur Foundation Research Network on Successful Midlife Development, which was extended into a longitudinal study in 2002 at the University of Wisconsin-Madison through a grant from the National Institute on Aging. The data include interview and questionnaire responses, biomarker measurements, and cognitive assessments of middle-aged adults in the United States, with the goal of identifying the relationships between physical, psychological, and social factors of well-being in adulthood. The collection of survey data for MIDUS 3, the most recent phase of the project and the basis of this paper’s data, was conducted between 2013 and 2014, while the biomarker data were collected between 2017 and 2022. The results of the study have been made available through the Inter-University Consortium of Political and Social Research (ICPSR).

7
Discussion

In this article a collection of statistical methods extending the double-asymptotic binary response model was proposed. These models may have correlated responses, and can include additional covariates which potentially modify the components of the model, including the lower and upper asymptote. This is achieved through what we define as the
compound logistic regression model
, in which the mean response is a differential function of several logistic regression functions. This permits a greater variety of models, while retaining the advantages of standard logistic regression. The collection of methods is implemented in the
R
-package
CLmodel
(
https://github.com/ALMUDEVAR163/CLmodel/
). Additional model features are demonstrated in the vignette.
