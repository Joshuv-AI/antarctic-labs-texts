Subtractive proteomic analysis to identify highly antigenic proteins and reverse vaccinology based novel vaccine development against Haemophillius parainfluenzae
Authors: Abbas Ahmad, Jun Zhao, Sehrish Kakakhel, Salman Ali Khan, Sara Aiman, Abdul Malik, Azmat Ali Khan, Sabiha Fatima, Abdullah K Alshememry, Zaheer Ul-Haq, Kaisong Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaemophillius parainfluenzae (H. parainfluenzae) is increasingly involved in invasive infections disrupting normal skin or mucosal barriers, specifically in immunocompromised individuals. No specific therapeutics or licensed vaccines are available for H. parainfluenzae infections. In the present study, the genome-scan vaccinomics analysis ranked three potential vaccine candidate proteins based on high immunogenic parameters, which were further used to prioritize lead B- and T-cell epitopes. The top-ranked highly immunogenic epitopes were conjugated using suitable linkers (KK, AAY, and GPGPG) and adjuvant (beta-defensin 3) sequences to engineer a potential chimeric vaccine construct. The immunological and physiochemical properties of the designed H. parainfluenzae vaccine exhibited high immunogenicity with an antigenicity score of 0.8688, structural and thermodynamic stability, and significant solubility. The tertiary structural analysis revealed a high-quality and stable 3D structure with > 89% of residues in the favored region of the Ramachandran plot and a Z-score of -4.45. The molecular docking analysis and molecular dynamic simulation studies revealed robust and stable binding interaction between the H. parainfluenzae vaccine and the TLR4 immune receptor. Immune simulation analysis revealed that the H. parainfluenzae vaccine had the potential to trigger both innate and adaptive immune responses in the host. In-silico restriction cloning demonstrated a significant level of gene expression for the H. parainfluenzae vaccine construct in the bacterial expression vector yielding a recombinant plasmid of 6357 bp. Introduction

Haemophillius parainfluenzae (H. parainfluenzae) belongs to the family Pasteurellacae and the genus Haemophilus. The species Haemophilus is characterized as a small, pleomorphic, and fastidious gram-negative coccobacillus that depends on either hemin (factor X) or NAD+ (factor V) for growth. Based on the nutritional requirement this genus is divided into three groups i.e. haem-dependent H. influenzae group (requires Hemin and NAD), the haem-independent H. parainfluenzae group (requires NAD + only), and the H. ducreyi group needs haem for growth but does not require NAD. H. parainfluenza is an opportunistic, multidrug-resistant, bacterium that normally colonizes human oropharyngeal and urogenital tracts1. This pathogen is responsible for causing invasive, chronic, and recurrent diseases including meningitis2, pneumonia3, acute gastroenteritis4, osteomyelitis5, invasive disease2,6,7, biliary tract infection8, bone and joint inflammation9, and endocarditis10,11. Recent reports have shown that this pathogen is associated with genitourinary and sexually transmitted infections as well12–14. One study showed that pregnant females experienced unexpectedly elevated levels of genital carriage, which were predominantly connected to antibiotic-resistant traits12. H. parainfluenza-associated urinary tract infections in children are most commonly identified in boys causing gross reflux, malformation, and bladder dysfunction15. The H. parainfluenza acute urethritis infection is primarily transmitted through direct contact among young people and adults and vertical contact14,16. The increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) H. parainfluenzae strains poses a significant public health challenge. H. parainfluenza shows resistance to B-lactams17–19, fluoroquinolones20, chloramphenicol21, macrolides, cotrimoxazole, and tetracycline22 has severely limited treatment options, and necessitates the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.

The availability of complete genome sequence data in the public domain repositories has enabled the exploration of alternative approaches for combating this pathogen23. While traditional vaccine development relies on whole-cell or subunit vaccine formulations, these approaches have inherent limitations, including potential adverse immune responses, and lengthy development timelines. Recent advancements in immunoinformatics and reverse vaccinology have revolutionized vaccine design by enabling rational selection of antigenic targets based on genomic data24. Similar approaches have been successfully applied against other pathogens, including Monkeypox virus25, Clostridium difficile26, Lumpy skin disease virus27, and Cyprinid herpesvirus28. However, despite these advances, effective vaccine strategies for H. parainfluenzae remain largely unexplored.

Given the rising antibiotic resistance in H. parainfluenzae and the limitations of existing therapeutic strategies, this study was conducted to design a multiepitope vaccine model that can effectively induce both adaptive and cell-mediated immune responses against the pathogen in the host body. To design an effective vaccine model, potential immunogenic epitopes were selected from the complete proteome of the H. parainfluenzae strain. These epitopes were joined using various linkers and adjuvants. The physicochemical and immunogenic properties of the proposed vaccine model were examined to predict its effectiveness. Moreover, to check the vaccine’s interaction with TLR immunological receptors and stability molecular docking and molecular dynamic simulation studies were conducted. This study contributes to the growing field of computational vaccine design by providing a structured and validated approach to developing novel therapeutics against H. parainfluenzae. The findings offer valuable insights into the feasibility of immunoinformatics-driven vaccine development and lay the foundation for future experimental validation and clinical translation.

Methodology

Demonstrates the overall workflow of the current study.

Retrieval of proteome

The initial step in the analysis was the retrieval of the entire proteome of H. parainfluenzae from UniProt29, under the accession number UP00025373. The Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance (CD-HIT) program was utilized to exclude paralogous sequences and to acquire non-redundant proteins with an 80% sequence similarity threshold30. CD-HIT utilized a fast-clustering algorithm based on sequence identity, achieving an accuracy of approximately 90% in redundancy reduction while preserving functionally distinct protein. To filter out human homolog proteins, protein sequences of H. parainfluenzae were scanned against human proteome via the NCBI-BLASTp program31 with E-value (10− 4),query coverage ≤ 70, sequence similarity ≤ 35, and bit score ≤ 10032.

Subcellular localization

Subcellular localization of the resultant human non-homologous proteins was performed using CELLO2GO v.2.5 web server. CELLO2GO employs a support vector machine (SVM)-based prediction model and BLAST homology search, achieving an accuracy of approximately 88% in subcellular localization classification. Moreover, the server provides data categorized by gene ontology (GO) that includes cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes33. The VaxiJen v2.0 web server was utilized to assess the antigenic behavior of the selected proteins at a 0.5 cut-off value, a threshold that provides 93.2% sensitivity and 86.8% specificity in distinguishing antigenic from non-antigenic proteins34, while the allergenicity and toxicity of the pathogen proteins were assessed using Algpred2, which integrates multiple SVM-based classifiers with an accuracy of 85.5% for allergen prediction35 and ToxinPred2 server which combines motif-based detection and SVM models, achieving 88.6% accuracy in toxicity classification36.

B-cell epitopes prediction

B-cell epitopes are pivotal in triggering humeral immunity, allowing the immune system to counter pathogens through the mediation of immunoglobulin response. These epitopes are crucial for the immune system to respond appropriately to specific diseases. The ABCpred online server was executed to predict the B-cell epitope using a threshold value 0.51. ABCpred is a recurrent artificial neural network-based B-cell predictor trained on experimentally validated epitopes, achieving an accuracy of 85.2%. The model evaluates epitopes based on four key parameters, including sensitivity (78.5%), specificity (82.3%), and positive predictive value (PPV) of 80.1%, ensuring reliable selection of immunogenic epitopes37.

CTL and HTL epitope prediction

The NETCTL 1.2 web server was employed to identify cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CTL) epitopes, using a specific threshold score of 0.7538. NetCTL-1.2 is a bioinformatics tool designed for predicting CTL epitopes by integrating MHC-I binding affinity, proteasomal cleavage, and TAP transport efficiency. It provides a comprehensive approach to identifying potential CD8 + T-cell epitopes for vaccine and immunotherapy research. The tool achieves high accuracy, with an AUC (Area Under Curve) of 0.94 for MHC-binding predictions and 0.89–0.92 for overall CTL epitope prediction, making it a reliable choice for T-cell epitope identification. Simultaneously, the IEDB MHC II server39 was used to forecast helper T lymphocytes (HTL) epitopes across five distinct HLA types (DRB1*01:01, DRB1*01:02, DRB1*01:03, DRB1*01:04, and DRB1*01:05), with a percentile score limit of 5. The epitopes carrying IC50 values below 50 nm were chosen for the construction of the vaccine model.

Immunogenic assessment of the selected epitopes

The AllerTop v2.0 web server40 was employed to examine the allergenicity of the B- and T-cell epitopes using the default parameters. Additionally, the antigenicity of the selected B- and T-cell epitopes was evaluated through VaxiJen v2.0 serve41, employing a threshold of 0.4 to predict the most promising antigenic epitopes. To predict the toxicity of the epitopes, the ToxinPred2 server was run using the default parameters42. These tools use 10-fold cross-validation and machine learning techniques to calculate the protective capabilities of the peptide sequences based on a known dataset, achieving high accuracy, with an AUC of 0.86–0.90, making these tools reliable for immunogenicity assessment in vaccine and therapeutic protein design43. The PEP-FOLD3 web tool was used for 3D structural modeling of the epitopes. PEP-FOLD3 is a de novo peptide structure prediction tool that models peptides from 5 to 50 amino acids using a Hidden Markov Model-based conformation sampling and structural alphabet (SA) letters approaches to describe peptide conformations and integrates a coarse-grained force field for structure generation44.

Epitope-based vaccine construct

To design an immunogenic vaccine model, the predicted CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes were combined in different configurations utilizing KK, AAY, and GPGPG linkers45. AAY linkers were employed to connect CTL epitopes, while the GPGPG linkers were incorporated to connect HTL and B-cell epitopes. The human beta-defensin 3 (PDB ID-1KJ6) chain A was connected at the N terminal of the chimeric vaccine construct using the EAAAK linker to boost the immunogenicity46. The linkers used in the vaccine construct will facilitate the generation of binding sites for the TAP transporter, leading to improved stability of immunogens and enhanced presentation of epitopes. Moreover, the GPGPG linkers are involved in the stimulation of HTL responses and maintain the conformational aspects of immunogenicity for both HTL and antibody epitopes47.

Physiochemical properties

ExPASy’s ProtParam server48 was used to make predictions about the vaccine’s physiochemical properties. ProtParam relies on predefined empirical formulas and statistical calculations derived from biochemical studies. It determines physicochemical properties using well-established computational algorithms with an accuracy of approximately 95% in protein property estimation. The tool determines the amino acid content of a peptide vaccine sequence in addition to its molecular weight, stability, aliphatic indices, GARVY (grand average of hydropathicity), and theoretical PI49.

Vaccine 3D structure modeling structure, refinement, and validation

Precise prediction of the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the H. parainfluenzae multiepitope vaccine construct (HP-MEVC) was achieved with the help of the SwissModel server. The server models both homo- and heteromeric compounds using the homology modeling technique, starting with the amino acid sequences of the proteins, and inferring both the stoichiometry and the overall structure of the proteins50. The GalaxyRefine server was executed to refine the vaccine structure. The program is based on the CASP10 assessment, which performs the repacking of the side chains to rebuild them followed by the relaxation of the entire structure using molecular dynamics simulation and enhancing the quality of local and global structures51. The quality of the vaccine 3D structure was validated using the PROCHECK program52 and ProSA-web server53. ProSA-web evaluates the overall quality of the vaccine structure by calculating the Z-score. A Z-score value above the threshold established for natural proteins indicates errors in the protein model54. PROCHECK program evaluates the geometry of the residues in the vaccine structure and generates a Ramachandran plot to evaluate the stereochemical quality of the designed vaccine models.

Molecular docking of the vaccine construct with human TLR receptors

To determine the molecular interaction between HP-MEVC and the immunological receptor, a molecular docking analysis of the vaccine construct with human TLR1 (PDB ID: 6NIH), TLR2 (PDB ID: 3A7C), TLR3 (PDB ID: 2A0Z) and TLR 4 (PDB ID: 3FXI), was carried out55. Similarly, the epitopes used in the vaccine design were separately docked with the human TLR receptors, to study the interactions between the peptides and receptor molecules. The docking studies of the vaccine construct and TLRs were evaluated with the help of the ClusPro 2.0 server which generates multiple models depending on the binding energies. ClusPro is an efficient and comprehensive protein-protein docking program that uses a hybrid docking technique based on small-angle X-ray scattering and experimental data on the protein’s substrate binding site to conduct docking analyses and provide ten models. The binding scores were computed by ClusPro server using the formula: E = 0.40Erep + (−0.40Eatt) + 600Eelec + 1.00 EDARS56. The best-docked MEVC-TLR4 complex was selected based on low docking energy.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulation studies were conducted to evaluate the stability and dynamics of the HP-MEVC-TLR4 docked complex using GROMACS v.202257. The topological parameters were processed by the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. The systems were positioned in a cubic box, using the TIP3P water model, and counter ions were introduced to keep the system neutral58. Subsequently, energy minimization of the systems was achieved by employing the steepest descent technique, with the maximum force threshold less than 1000 kJ mol-1 nm-1. The minimized systems were further equilibrated through NVT and NPT ensembles for 100ps followed by an isobaric-isothermal algorithm to keep the pressure and volume constant. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) algorithm step59 was incorporated to compute long-range electrostatic interaction, with a cut-off distance set to 1.2 nm while bond lengths were restricted through LINCS (linear constraint solver) algorithms60. The production phase consisted of 200 ns production runs completed for the equilibrated systems and coordinated and their energies were recorded for every 10 ps and saved in an output trajectory file. The stability of the simulated systems was evaluated through RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation), RMSF (Root Mean Square Fluctuation), and Rg (Radius of Gyration).

Binding energy calculation

The binding free energy of the vaccine-TLR4 complex was computed using the molecular mechanics generalized borne surface area (MMGBSA) module of the HawDock server61. The structure from the last frame of the simulated trajectory was used as an input structure and subjected to the HawkDock server for analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to identify dominant collective motions in the TLR4-vaccine complex over the 200 ns simulation. A covariance matrix of atomic positional fluctuations was constructed after fitting the trajectory to a reference structure, and eigenvectors representing major motions were extracted. The first principal component (PC1) was analyzed to identify significant conformational changes in TLR4 and the vaccine. Squared fluctuations along PC1 were mapped onto the 3D structure to visualize motion intensity in specific regions using the visual molecular dynamics (VMD) program.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix

Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM) analysis was used to evaluate correlated and anti-correlated motions within the TLR4-vaccine complex during the simulation. The matrix, derived from residue positional fluctuations, highlighted correlated motions (+ 1), and anti-correlated motions (−1). Regions such as the N- and C-terminal domains of TLR4 and the vaccine interface were analyzed to understand motion patterns contributing to stability. Results were visualized with heatmaps and color-coded 3D structures to represent motion intensities.

Codon optimization and in-silico cloning

The codon optimization and reverse translation for the final vaccine construct were conducted through the Java Codon Adaptation Tool (JCAT). JCAT optimizes gene sequences by adjusting codon usage to match the preferences of a specific host organism, improving protein expression. It uses codon usage tables and minimizes secondary structures to enhance translation efficiency. JCAT is most accurate for organisms with well-characterized genomes and is ideal for heterologous gene expression62. Codon optimization is used to identify the most suitable codon that effectively encodes the desired amino in a specific organism. To achieve the maximum expression of the cloned sequence in the E. coli expression system, the peptide vaccine sequence was optimized reversed translated to the sequence of cDNA. JCAT tool determines the maximum possible potential for expression of the cloned vaccine sequence by evaluating its codon adaptation index (CAI) and the proportion of GC content. It has been found that a CAI value of 1 and a GC content of 30–70% produce the best transcriptional and translational results. The Snapgene tool63 was utilized to clone the adapted DNA sequence of the finalized vaccine construct into the E.coli expression system. SnapGene is a molecular biology tool for DNA sequence analysis, restriction enzyme digestion, primer design, and plasmid construction, supporting multiple file formats and virtual cloning workflows63. The E. coli plasmid pET28a(+) was downloaded from the Addgene website64. The E. coli plasmid pET28a(+) is favored for high protein expression due to its T7 promoter, which drives strong transcription and yields higher protein levels compared to other plasmids, making it ideal for recombinant protein production and expression studies.

In-silico immune simulation

In-silico immune simulation studies were conducted for the finalized vaccine construct to evaluate the immunological response of the vaccine via C-ImmSim online server53. The server utilizes a position-specific matrix to predict immune epitopes and employs machine learning methods to predict immune interactions. This server gathers information about the mammalian immune system to antigens, encompassing both humoral and cellular responses. The tool allows users to visualize how immune components interact over time and helps in immunoinformatics by providing insights into antigenic properties and vaccine efficacy. It is particularly useful for vaccine design, immune response modeling, and epitope prediction. The analysis for the vaccine construct was carried out using the protocol previously utilized by27. The analysis was run using all the simulation’s parameters at default for a period of 1 h, 8 h, and 168 h along with selected leukocyte antigens, namely HLA-A*0101 and A*0201, HLA-B*0702 and B*0702 and B*3901, and HLA-DRB1*0101 and DRB1*0401. The simulation volume was set to 10, and immune simulation analysis was performed using 1000 simulation steps.

Results

Retrieval of proteome

The complete proteome of H. parainfluenzae (UniProt accession ID UP000000609) was retrieved from UniProt, yielding a total of 1883 protein sequences. Duplicate protein sequences were removed, and 1858 non-paralogous sequences were acquired after using a CD-Hit suit with a cutoff value of 0.8. A total of 1482 proteins were prioritized after removing human homologous proteins (Supplementary File 1).

Subcellular localization

CELLO2GO classifies proteins based on location into cytoplasmic, periplasmic, extracellular, internal, and external membranes. Only three proteins into the outer membrane A0A369YM23, A0A369YJP5, and A0A369YLF5 were prioritized as a vaccine candidate (Table 1). The shortlisted proteins displayed high antigenicity respectively at a cutoff value of > 0.4. In addition, the finalized proteins were non-allergen and non-toxic implying that targeting these proteins were more suitable for chimeric vaccine construct.

HTL epitopes stimulate the production of T-cell cytokines, which aids in the regulation of the adaptive immune system65. B-cell epitopes prediction

B cells are the primary components of humoral immunity. Antibodies produced by B cells are essential for halting the spread of pathogenic infection66. Therefore, B cell epitopes play a crucial role in articulating the immunological response against a particular disease. ABCpred server predicted eight B-cell epitopes, each made up of 20 amino acids and scoring ≥ 0.90. For the construction of the vaccine, the three B-cell epitopes that performed the best were chosen. The most effective B-cell epitopes were incorporated into the final multiepitope vaccine construct (MEVC) as well (Table 4).

Multiepitope-based Immunogenic construct

The vaccine construct was formulated by integrating the most promising B-cell, CTL, and HTL epitopes using KK, AAY, and GPGPG linkers, accordingly. The adjuvant beta-defensin 3 was linked with peptide vaccine at the N-terminal via EAAAK linker to boost the immunogenic response. The current vaccine construct consists of a total of 15 epitopes, including six CTL, six HTL, and three B-cell epitopes (Fig. 2). HTL epitopes were attached to B-cell epitopes via GPGPG linkers, CTL epitopes were linked through AAY linkers, and adjuvant was fused to epitopes using EAAAK linkers. Different linkers were added to the peptide vaccine to improve the stability and immunogenicity of the designed vaccine construct.

Physiochemical properties of the final vaccine

ProtParam online server was used to check different physiochemical features of the immunogenic vaccine construct. The analysis revealed significant physiochemical features of the immunogenic construct. The molecular weight of the vaccine construct was calculated to be 33.71 kDa. Proteins with molecular weights below 110 kDa are an excellent candidate for large-scale synthesis due to their ease of purification. The instability index was predicted to be 28.16 showing the stability of the vaccine and the estimated aliphatic index of 79.94 depicts the thermostable nature of the vaccine at various temperatures. The predicted GRAVY score of − 0.240 indicates that the vaccine construct is hydrophilic. The immunological parameters demonstrated the vaccine to be highly antigenic with an antigenicity score of 0.8688, with no allergic or toxic effects. All these results strongly support the construct of a good vaccine candidate.

3D structure modeling, refinement, and validation

The 3D structure of the vaccine was modeled via the SwissModel server through a homology modeling approach (Fig. 3A) and it was then refined using the GalaxyRefine tool. This server predicts five refined models of the vaccine candidate and the suitable model was prioritized based on various parameters i.e. GDT-HA, RMSD, weak rotamers, and Rama-favored (Table S1). Similarly, the Z-score predicted by the ProSA-web server was observed to be -4.45 for the final vaccine model (Fig. 3B). The validation of the vaccine candidate via Ramachandran plot displayed that 89.7% of residues were found in the favored region. Additionally, 8.7% of residues were in the allowed region, and 0.4% of residues were in the disallowed region (Fig. 3C).

Molecular docking study

Docking analysis of B- and T-cell epitopes with various Toll-like receptors (TLRs) demonstrated the promiscuous binding affinity of microbial peptides to human immune receptors, suggesting their potential role in immune activation and modulation (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. S1–S9). The docking analysis of the HP-MEVC construct with different TLR receptors revealed varying energy scores: HP-MEVC-TLR1 (-496.4), HP-MEVC-TLR2 (-571.0), HP-MEVC-TLR3 (-569.7), and HP-MEVC-TLR4 (-580.9), with the HP-MEVC-TLR4 complex exhibiting the lowest energy score. Further structural analysis, using PDBsum, provided insights into the molecular interactions within each complex. The HP-MEVC-TLR1 complex displayed 2 salt bridges, 7 hydrogen bonds, and 98 non-bonding interactions between the chains (Fig. 4A). The HP-MEVC-TLR2 complex exhibited 3 salt bridges, 16 hydrogen bonds, and 161 non-bonding interactions (Fig. 4B). Similarly, the HP-MEVC-TLR3 complex demonstrated 5 salt bridges, 6 hydrogen bonds, and 86 non-bonding interactions (Fig. 4C). Notably, the HP-MEVC-TLR4 complex showed 4 salt bridges, 14 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S10), and the highest number of non-bonding interactions (183), indicating strong molecular engagement. These findings indicate the vaccine construct’s capacity for stable binding to immune receptors, suggesting its potential to stimulate an immunological response within the human immune system. Further, we selected the HP-MEVC-TLR4 complex for additional analysis due to its lowest binding energy score and highest number of stabilizing interactions.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics results depicted significant structural stability for the TLR4-vaccine complex following an initial conformational reorientation of both TLR4 and vaccine residues, as shown in Fig. 5. The RMSD profiles revealed that the vaccine maintained a highly stable conformation, with an average RMSD of 2.94 Å± 0.18 relative to the initial and exceptionally low deviation of only 0.19 Å regarding the average structure (top right panel). In a slight contrast, the TLR4 RMSD exhibited moderate fluctuations (top left panel), primarily attributed to the N- and C-terminal regions as corroborated by the RMSF data (middle left panel)67. The TLR4 average RMSD was 3.67 Å ± 0.71 relative to the starting structure but displayed remarkable stability with an RMSD of 0.7 Å when referenced to the average structure (top left panel). The vaccine’s RMSF profiles indicated minimal atomic level fluctuations with all residue fluctuations remaining below the hypothetical stability threshold of 2 Å, showing its consistent stability upon binding to the TLR4 throughout the simulation time (middle right panel). System-wide compactness was assessed using a Rg, which aligned with RMSD trends68. The TLR4 exhibited an average Rg of 31.40 Å ± 0.55 (lower left panel) while the vaccine maintained a compact conformation with an average of 10.47 Å ± 0.11 (lower right panel). These results indicate the stability of the TLR4-vaccine over the entire 200 ns simulation time. Furthermore, the stability was substantiated by MMGBSA binding free energy calculations69. The vaccine demonstrated a strong binding affinity, with an average binding free energy of -41.60 kcal/mol calculated over the final 10 ns of the simulation.

Principle component analysis

The dynamics of the protein and vaccine across the principal components align closely with the patterns observed in the preceding analysis (Fig. 6). The upper left panel of Fig. 6 shows that the protein exhibits minimal moments across the first principal component 1 (PC1). These moments are predominantly localized to the N- and C-terminal regions. Consistent with RMSF analysis, significant moments were confined to these termini, which exhibited slight antiparallel movements. These motions contributed to a more compact conformation of the protein upon the vaccine binding suggesting structural stability. The dynamic cross-correlation map (bottom panel) further highlights these negatively correlated motions. Residues in the N-terminal region exhibit motions that are inversely correlated with those of the C-terminal region supporting the observations of antiparallel adjustments. These moments are also shown in the first two insets which depict the squared fluctuation of the residues across the principal components. In contrast, the vaccine exhibits distinct motions concentrated at the interface with the protein residue (upper right panel and inset 3). These interfacial residues undergo subtle reorientation, moving closer to the protein to enhance the interface stability. This adjustment is visualized in the final inset of the protein-vaccine complex where the indicated the directed movements of these residues. These results not only show the overall stability of the TLR4-vaccine complex but also the localized strain experienced by the vaccine interface residues, which undergo slight shifts to achieve a more energetically favorable conformation.

Immune simulation

The finding of the in-silico immune simulation against H. parainfluenzae displayed a notable rise in the secondary immune response, possibly induced by the proposed vaccine construct. This response pattern closely resembles the immune response observed in real-time immune reactions. The primary response revealed elevated IgM levels. Also, the simulation of secondary and tertiary responses demonstrated a substantial rise in the B-cell population and elevated levels of IgG1, IgG1 + IgG2, IgM, and IgM + IgG antibodies, while a decrease was observed in antigen levels (Fig. 7A and B). These results speculate the establishment of immunological memory, as demonstrated by isotype switching and elevated levels of the memory B-cell population. Consequently, subsequent exposures to chimeric antigens caused a rapid decrease in antigen concentration (Fig. 7C). Similarly, repeated antigen exposure caused increased response for both TC (cytotoxic) and TH (helper) cell population, with the establishment of corresponding memory (Fig. 7D and E). Additionally, throughout the immunization phase, macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer cell populations were stimulated and maintained at elevated levels (Fig. 7F and G, and 7H). Increased concentrations of a cytokine such as IFN-y and interleukins such as IL-2 were also observed (Fig. 7I). These findings suggested that the predicted vaccine construct elicited encouraging immune responses against the pathogen.

Optimization and cloning of vaccine

The sequence of the chimeric vaccine construct was optimized and reverse transcribed into cDNA to enhance the vaccine expression rate in the E. coli expression system. The final length of the reverse translated codon sequence was 987 nucleotides. The optimized sequence exhibited a codon adaptation index (CAI) of 0.979, along with a GC content of 54.44%, suggesting a high potential and reliability of vaccine expression in the E. coli system. Finally, the transcribed sequence was inserted into the pET28a(+) vector by utilizing MscI and BglII as restriction sites with the help of SanpGene software (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Multiepitope-based vaccine designing is an innovative method for creating and developing potential vaccines with increased safety and effectiveness. Multiepitope-based vaccines stimulate precise immune reactions against conserved epitopes, throughout the antigen and prevent harmful reactions to unfavorable epitopes that might result in immune-modulating or immunopathogenic reactions70. In the current investigation, the subtractive proteomic pipeline was utilized using three putative proteins for the designing of an immunogenic construct against H. parainfluenzae. Top-ranked B-cell, MHC-I, and MHC-II epitopes were anticipated from the chosen proteins following strict criteria and parameters. B-cell epitopes are involved in eliciting humoral immunogenic reactions that eliminate pathogens and also generate a memory to defend against any future exposures; however, its effectiveness is often low and tends to decrease with time71. Nevertheless, T cells, including CTL and HTL, are responsible for inducing cell-mediated immunogenic reactions that halt pathogens from spreading by either killing the infected cells or by releasing a variety of antibacterial cytokines that facilitate the generation of enduring immunity72. Hence, the present vaccine construct was formulated by incorporating a diverse combination of B-cell, MHC-I, and MHC-II epitopes along with the incorporation of immune enhancers and flexible adjuvant peptide sequences. Additionally, the inclusion of KK, AAY, and GPGPG linkers serves to increase the stability and expression of the chimeric vaccine construct.

The predicted vaccine construct was also checked for antigenic properties, allergenicity, solubility, and other physiochemical characteristics. The designed vaccine was observed to be stable, basic, and hydrophilic, implying that it has the potential to stimulate an immunogenic response. To explore the interaction between vaccine and host receptor the modeling and refining of vaccine 3D structure is crucial. The H. parainfluenzae vaccine 3D structure significantly improved after refinement and displayed the expected stability which was supported by Ramachandran plot prediction. According to prior studies, TLRs are essential for the recognition of pathogenic proteins that trigger an immune response73. Therefore, a docking study was performed against TLR4 receptors to assess the H. parainfluenzae vaccine’s interaction with host receptors. The docking result predicts robust and stable interaction between the vaccine and TLR receptors, which was further validated by MD simulation analysis.

The 200 ns MD simulation confirmed the stability of the TLR4-vaccine complex, with the vaccine showing minimal fluctuations (average RMSD of 2.94 Å) and compactness (average Rg of 10.47 Å). TLR4 exhibited moderate fluctuations (average RMSD of 3.67 Å), mainly in its N- and C-terminal regions. RMSF analysis showed stable vaccine binding with minimal residue fluctuations (< 2 Å). MMGBSA calculations revealed strong binding affinity (-41.60 kcal/mol) over the final 10 ns. Additionally, PCA and DCCM analyses further validated the complex’s stability. PCA revealed minor motions in TLR4 (N- and C-terminal regions) and subtle vaccine reorientations at the interface, enhancing stability. DCCM highlighted anti-correlated movements between TLR4’s terminal domains, achieving a compact and energetically favorable conformation upon vaccine binding. Such stability criteria are crucial for evaluating protein-protein interactions and be effective in numerous studies74–76.

The prediction of immune simulation analysis closely resembles the pattern that occurs in the natural cellular immunogenic reactions. Multiple encounters with antigens provoked a robust immunogenic reaction and established long-term memory in both B- and T-cells, leading to long-lasting adaptive immunity. Memory in B cells can persist for several months, enabling a more efficient response to future encounters with the same antigen. The increased concentration of T-helper cells, T-cytotoxic, and IGg production, along with increased IFNs and IL-2 levels, indicate an elevated amount of immune response after the first injection. A lower Simpson index value indicates the possibility of diverse immune responses for the present vaccine construct model. The outcomes of the immune simulation confirmed the robust molecular interaction and stability of H. parainfluenzae chimeric vaccine construct in a cellular environment.

While the findings of this study provide valuable insights into the design and stability of the HP-MEVC, several limitations should be acknowledged. This study relies entirely on computational methods, and the predicted vaccine construct has not yet undergone experimental validation. Although molecular docking, MD simulations, and immune response modeling provide strong predictive insights, real biological systems are more complex and may introduce unforeseen variables affecting vaccine performance. The simulated receptor models assume a somewhat rigid conformation, whereas biological receptors exhibit dynamic structural changes that could influence vaccine binding in a live setting. Extended simulations with flexible docking approaches may improve the accuracy of these predictions. Additionally, the absence of in vitro and in vivo validation means that immune responses and stability observed in computational models need experimental confirmation before clinical translation. Another potential limitation is the scalability and manufacturability of the proposed vaccine construct, as successful computational predictions do not always translate into practical vaccine formulations due to issues like expression efficiency and host system compatibility.

To bridge this gap, future studies will focus on experimental validation of the designed vaccine construct. In vitro assays requires to be conducted to assess antigen expression, structural stability, and immunogenicity in mammalian cell lines. Further, in vivo studies performed in suitable animal models will evaluate the vaccine’s efficacy, immune response profile, and safety parameters. These experimental validations will provide crucial insights into the vaccine’s real-world effectiveness, complementing the computational predictions. Moreover, refinement of the vaccine construct through extended simulations and wet-lab optimization will facilitate its potential transition into clinical evaluation.

Conclusion

The present study used Immunoinformatics and subtractive genomics pipelines to identify putative vaccine targets for designing of efficient immunogenic construct. The prioritized proteins were used to examine highly antigenic and non-allergenic B- and T-cell epitopes for the designing of a multiepitope-based vaccine construct. The suggested immunogenic construct was observed to exhibit all favorable characteristics capable of initiating cell mediating and humoral immune response. Additionally, the results of molecular and MD simulation displayed high binding affinity and stability between the designed vaccine model and TLR4. Experimental validation is essential for validating the effectiveness of the proposed immunogenic construct against H. parainfluenzae.
