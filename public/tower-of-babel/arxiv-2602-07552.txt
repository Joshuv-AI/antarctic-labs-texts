Abstract
. We discuss recent progress on topological Helly-type theorems and their variants. We provide an overview of two different proof techniques, one based on the nerve lemma, while the other on non-embeddability.



1.
Introduction

Helly’s theorem, about 100 years old now, is one of the cornerstones of convex geometry and still attracts a lot of attention, generates interesting open problems
[
BK22
]
and has been applied in different areas; see
[
DLGMM19
,
ALS17
]
for recent surveys.

Theorem 1

(Helly’s theorem
[
Hel23
]
)
.

Given a finite family
\mathcal{F}
of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, either
\bigcap\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset
, or one can find a subfamily
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
that has at most
d+1
elements and satisfies
\bigcap\mathcal{G}=\emptyset
.

One readily observes that this property extends beyond convex sets, as it is preserved by any homeomorphism of the underlying space.
In this survey we study to which extent convexity in Helly’s theorem can be replaced by topological conditions.
More precisely, we focus on two different techniques for proving topological Helly-type results. Namely, Section
2
provides an overview of Helly-type theorems that were proved using the nerve lemma or its variants. Section
3
summarizes how similar methods have been used to prove colorful and fractional modifications of Helly’s theorem.
Section
4
is devoted to Helly-type results from the perspective of non-embeddability arguments. Since there has been significant progress in this direction in the past few years, this section goes into more detail and describes how various ideas fit together to enable these new and more general results. In Section
5
we list some related open problems.

In order to present the topic concisely, we start with a few definitions that allow for a somewhat nicer formulation of Helly’s theorem and the related results.

Definition 2

(Helly number
[
DGK63
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a family of sets.
Then we define its
Helly number

h(\mathcal{C})
as the smallest natural number
h
with the following property.
For each finite subfamily
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{C}
with
\bigcap\mathcal{F}=\emptyset
there exists
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
with
\bigcap\mathcal{G}=\emptyset
and
|\mathcal{G}|\leq h
.
If there is no such number, we set
h(\mathcal{C}):=+\infty
.

With this definition, Helly’s theorem states that the Helly number of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is at most
d+1
. Moreover, this bound is tight as can be easily seen by considering hyperplanes in general position.

Given a positive integer
h
,
we want to find properties of
\mathcal{C}
that ensure
h(\mathcal{C})\leq h
.
As mentioned, we study this question in Sections
2
and
4
.
In Section
2
we look at the results that are based on the so-called nerve lemma and its variants. The advantage of this approach is that the obtained bounds are usually tight. The disadvantage comes from the fact that the sets need to satisfy the assumptions of the nerve lemma, typically they need to be all open or all closed and their homology must be trivial in certain dimensions.

In Section
4
we look at topological Helly-type theorems that are derived from non-embeddability results.
The advantage of this approach is that the extra assumptions of the nerve lemma can be lifted. Thus, one can bound the Helly numbers even for sets that are neither open nor closed and have non-trivial homology groups.
The drawback is that if we allow nontrivial homology, the obtained bounds on the Helly number become enormous very quickly and are probably far from being optimal.

Let us now present another definition and the related reformulation of Helly’s theorem.

Definition 3

]
)

Given a collection

Topological properties of the nerve

In this part of the survey, we look at various properties related to the Helly number

4.
Helly type theorems from non-embeddability

Another way to prove Helly type theorems is to relate them to non-embeddability results. This approach dates back to Radon, who used Radon’s lemma in order to prove Helly’s theorem
[
Rad21
]
.

We formulate the lemma in a way that relates it to the other theorems in this section.

Lemma 19

(Radon’s lemma
[

For every linear map

Note that if

Helly’s theorem is an immediate consequence of this lemma.

4.1.
Generalized convexity

Before we present the technique of constrained chain maps, we introduce a useful notion of generalized convexity.

Given a family
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of a topological space
X
and a set
S\subseteq X
, we define

\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}(S):=\bigcap_{S\subseteq F\in\mathcal{C}}F.

If there is no set

for which

, we set
\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}(S):=X
.

It is not hard to see that

is a closure operator (see
[

]
),
i.e., a map from subsets of
X
to subsets of

that is

Extensive:

S\subseteq\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}S
for all
S\subseteq X
,

Monotone:

S\subseteq T\subseteq X
implies
\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}S\subseteq\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}T
and

Idempotent:

\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}(\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}S)=\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}S
for all
S\subseteq X
.

The sets that satisfy
\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{C}}S=S
are called
\mathcal{C}
-closed.
For example, if
\mathcal{C}
is the set of all convex sets, then the

-closed sets are exactly the usual convex sets.

.

[

]

There is a number

where
\alpha
maps each
\ell
-dim simplex
\sigma
to a sum of the
\ell
-dim simplices in
\sd\sigma
and
L
is a
(t-1)
-dimensional skeleton of a simplex on a sufficiently large number of vertices so that we can use the Ramsey theorem (details later). Let
V(L)
denote the set of vertices of
L
. As
L
has dimension
t-1
, we know from induction that for
P
sufficiently large, there is a non-trivial chain map
\gamma^{\prime}
constrained by
(\mathcal{F},P)
. Let
\Psi_{0}\colon L\to 2^{P}
witness the constraints. The map
\Psi_{0}
is then, in a natural way, extended to
\Psi\colon 2^{V(L)}\to 2^{P}
, for details see
[

]
.

We require that

has at least
N_{t+1}(n,m,c)

N_{t+1}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is given by Proposition
37
,

is the number of vertices of the barycentric subdivision of
K
,

is the number of vertices of a barycentric subdivision of a
t

is the maximal number of elements in
\widetilde{H}_{t}(\conv_{\mathcal{F}}S,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
for

finite. By our assumptions,

.

For each
\tau\subseteq V(L)
, we consider the coloring
\rho_{\tau}
, which to each
\sigma\subseteq\tau
on

\gamma^{\prime}(\partial\sigma)
inside
\conv_{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi(\tau))
. That is, the vertices of

play the role of
X
in the Ramsey setting. Proposition
37

gives us an

of

, and a strongly injective
(t+1)
-monochromatic map.
Let
\beta
be induced by the inclusion of

onto

Based on

In other words, the chain map

is constrained by

The

.

Optimality.

As we iteratively use a Ramsey-type argument in the proof of Theorem
32
, we cannot hope that the bound on the constant
r(b,d)
is optimal. However, the result is optimal in the sense that all
\widetilde{\beta}_{i}
,
0\leq i<\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
need to be bounded, see
[
Pat24
, Example 5.1]
.
The only known bounds for the Radon number in Theorem
32
are for
b=0
, in which case the Radon number is at most
d+3
and this is sharp
[
Pat24
, Theorem 5.2]
.

We note that Theorem
32
retains some of the flavor of Theorem
7
. More specifically, to reach the conclusion in Theorem
7
, we only need to control the
i
th homology of
\bigcap\mathcal{G}

for
\mathcal{G}
of a certain size that depends on
i
.
Similarly, from the proof of Theorem
32
it follows that, in the case of trivial homology groups, the theorem can be strengthened to the following form.

Theorem 38
.

Let
b=0
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If for each
i=0,1,\ldots,\lceil d/2\rceil-1
,
\widetilde{\beta}_{i}(\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{F}}S;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\leq b
for all sets
S
of cardinality
i+2
, then
r(\mathcal{F})\leq d+2
.

The statement can be also generalized for
b>0
,
however, the dependence of the cardinality of
|S|
on
i
becomes more complicated.

Further results.

Theorem
32
is formulated for the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, however, the constrained chain map method, as we have seen above, works for any topological space with a forbidden homological minor. We formulate it as a theorem.

Theorem 39
.

There is a number
r_{K}(b)

with the following property. Let
K
be a finite simplicial complex and
\mathbf{R}
a topological space such that
K
is a forbidden homological minor of
\mathbf{R}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of sets in
\mathbf{R}
such that
for every finite set
S\subseteq\mathbf{R}
,

\widetilde{\beta}_{i}(\operatorname{conv}_{\mathcal{F}}S;\mathbb{Z}_{2})\leq b\quad\text{for all }i<\dim K.

Then the Radon number of
\mathcal{F}
is at most
r_{K}(b)
.

For the proof see
[
Pat24
, Prop. 3.6 and 3.7]
and the text above.

Notice that the theorem above holds for manifolds as long as we know that there is a forbidden homological minor for them. It is an open question whether for each real manifold there is a forbidden homological minor.
Similarly, the statement holds for (finite)
k
-dimensional simplicial complexes as each of these complexes embeds in
\mathbb{R}^{2k+1}
.

Another direction of related results is provided by a beautiful result of Holmsen and Lee
[
HL21
]
stating that whenever we have a space
X
with a closure operator
\conv_{\mathcal{C}}
and a Radon number
r
, the fractional Helly number of
\mathcal{C}
is bounded above by some large constant
f(r)
that depends solely on
r
and not on
\mathcal{C}
itself. For that we note that a family
\mathcal{C}
satisfies a
fractional Helly property of order
m
if there exists
an integer
m
and a function
\beta:(0,1]\to(0,1]
for which
the following holds:
Every finite subfamily
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
that contains at least
\alpha\binom{|\mathcal{G}|}{m}
intersecting
m
-tuples
contains an intersecting subfamily of size at least
\beta(\alpha)|\mathcal{G}|
. The smallest integer
m
such that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the fractional Helly property of order
m
is called
the
fractional Helly number
for
\mathcal{C}
. For more details on Helly-type problems in convexity spaces, see a recent survey by Holmsen
[
Hol24
]
.

By a standard method of Alon, Kalai, Matoušek and Meshulam
[
AKMM02
]
, bounded fractional Helly number guarantees a
(p,q)
-theorem, and existence of
\varepsilon
-nets, see also
[
HL21
]
. As Theorem
32
basically says that under the assumptions on constant homology of intersections we have a bounded Radon number; the above-mentioned theorems extend to this setting as well
[
HL21
,
Pat24
]
.
The main drawback is that the fractional Helly number is very large. Patáková
[
Pat24
]
reduced it in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to a linear function in
b
with an optimal value for
b=0
. Further significant improvement was obtained by Goaoc, Holmsen, Patáková
[
GHP24
]
who focused on the fractional Helly and
(p,q)
-theorems. In fact, they showed that the fractional Helly number is
d+1
and that the
(p,q)
-theorem holds for
p\geq q\geq d+1
under the assumptions of bounded homology of intersections.
Their proof is very involved. They introduce a colorful analogue of the ”homology” condition and prove a weak colorful Helly theorem. In order to deal with the
m
-partite structure of the colorful classes, they work with cubical complexes instead of simplicial ones, define chain maps inspired by stair-case convexity, and the Ramsey-type statement is replaced by a subgrid lemma
[
GHP24
, Prop. 4.1]
, which can be of independent interest.

Furthermore, the following result indicates why
\alpha
in fractional Helly-type theorems is assumed to be a fixed constant.
More precisely, if we have a family
\mathcal{F}
of
n
sets in the plane such that for each subfamily
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
its intersection is either empty or path-connected, then by the previous discussion, the fractional Helly number of
\mathcal{F}
is at most
3
, see
[
GHP24
]
.
This means that if cubically many triples of sets from
\mathcal{F}
intersect, a non-trivial fraction of all sets intersect.
The following theorem shows, in a strong form, that assuming only quadratically many intersections of triples is insufficient.

Theorem 40

(
[
KP20
]
)
.

For any
n\geq 6
, there is a family
\mathcal{F}
of
n
sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that the intersection of every subfamily is either empty or path-connected and for which the following holds:

•

For any
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
with
|\mathcal{G}|\geq 4
,
\bigcap\mathcal{G}=\emptyset
.

•

There are at least
\binom{n-1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\left(n^{2}-6n+3\right)
families
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}

with
|\mathcal{G}|=3
and
\bigcap\mathcal{G}\neq\emptyset
.

We conclude this survey by listing some questions and problems that are closely related to the presented theorems. Some of these questions and many more are mentioned in

(1)

In Theorem

(3)

(4)

Suppose that
