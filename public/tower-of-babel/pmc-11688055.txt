Evaluation of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography in Turkish children
Authors: Gül Çirkin, Mesut Akarsu, Yesim Öztürk, Leyla İyilikçi, Yunus Güler, Özlem Gülpinar Aydin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to evaluate the indications, efficacy, and safety of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) in pediatric patients at a single center in Turkey between 2017 and 2021.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted on 50 children (mean age, 12.7 years; range, 1–18 years) who underwent 65 ERCP procedures. Data collected included patient demographics, indications for ERCP, procedural details, success rates, and complications. All procedures were performed using therapeutic duodenoscopes under conscious sedation administered by an anesthesiologist.Results:The primary indications for ERCP were biliary tract issues, including choledocholithiasis (52.3%) and chronic pancreatitis (18.5%). The overall cannulation success rate was 92.3%. Multiple therapeutic interventions, such as sphincterotomies and stent placements, were often required. The most common complication was post-ERCP pancreatitis, observed in 6.1% of cases, all of which were moderate. No fatalities or serious anesthesia-related adverse events were reported.Conclusion:ERCP is an effective and safe therapeutic procedure in pediatric patients when performed in collaboration with adult gastroenterologists, despite technical challenges such as the lack of pediatric-specific duodenoscopes. The findings highlight the need for specialized pediatric ERCP equipment and suggest that centralized ERCP facilities could enhance patient outcomes in Turkey.

1. Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a well-established diagnostic and therapeutic procedure that integrates endoscopy with fluoroscopy, primarily utilized in adults for managing pancreatic and biliary duct diseases.[1,2] Although its use is expanding in pediatric populations, the experience with ERCP in children remains comparatively limited. This limitation is attributed to several factors, including the lower incidence of conditions requiring ERCP in children, the technical challenges and inadequacies of pediatric-specific equipment, and the complexities involved in administering anesthesia to this age group.[2–4]

Globally, pediatric gastroenterology departments often face challenges in performing ERCP due to limited experience with this complex procedure.[4,5] The existing literature on pediatric ERCP mainly comprises case series conducted by experienced adult endoscopists, with a notable lack of studies led and published by pediatric gastroenterologists.[6–9] Currently, there is no standardized protocol for the application of ERCP in pediatric patients. While there is a growing tendency to apply diagnostic and therapeutic approaches similar to those used in adults, the indications for ERCP in children, as well as the specialized equipment and anesthesia considerations, differ significantly.[10]

Research has shown that ERCP is both technically and clinically effective in pediatric patients, with safety and success rates comparable to those observed in adults.[11,12] However, these findings underscore the need for more targeted research focusing on the pediatric population.

The aim of this study is to describe the indications for ERCP in children and assess its application across different pediatric age groups, including infants, children, and adolescents. We also aim to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ERCP procedures performed by adult gastroenterologists in collaboration with pediatric gastroenterologists in a specialized care setting. This retrospective analysis further investigates ERCP-related adverse events in this pediatric cohort.

2. Methods

2.1. Subjects and outcomes

This study is a retrospective review of medical records of patients under 18 years of age who underwent ERCP at Dokuz Eylul University Children’s Hospital between 2017 and 2021. Informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of the patients. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dokuz Eylul University (2021/38-11).

Data collected included patient medical history, body weight, body mass index (BMI) percentiles, and relevant laboratory findings (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, direct bilirubin, blood amylase, and lipase). Additional data on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) results, ERCP indications, technical success rates, ERCP interventions, and related adverse events were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records and analyzed retrospectively.

For conscious sedation during the ERCP procedures, an anesthesiologist administered a combination of midazolam, fentanyl, and propofol intravenously. During the procedure, patient monitoring and follow-up were conducted by both the anesthesiologist and an anesthesia technician.

2.2. ERCP procedures

In our department, ERCP procedures in pediatric patients were conducted in collaboration with adult gastroenterologists. A therapeutic duodenoscope (ED 530XT; Fujinon, Japan) with an 11.5-mm insertion diameter was primarily used, while a diagnostic duodenoscope (ED 450XL5; Fujinon, Japan) with a 10.8-mm insertion diameter was employed in a specific case involving a 12-month-old, 8-kg patient. In addition, a 5.9-mm nasal scope (EG-530NW; Fujinon, Japan) was used for bile duct access in a 5-year-old patient.

For patients undergoing pancreatic cannulation or those who inadvertently received pancreatic cannulation during ERCP, rectal indomethacin was administered prophylactically at a single dose of 2 mg/kg (ENDOL; 50–100 mg) to prevent post-ERCP pancreatitis.

Patients were admitted to the pediatric department 1 day before the procedure. Procedural success was defined by the completion of all planned diagnostic or therapeutic interventions, including deep cannulation of the biliary or pancreatic ducts. Cannulation was performed using a tapered-tip catheter and guide wire, followed by selective sphincterotomy as needed. Stones and sludge were removed with a sterile balloon catheter, and ductal clearance was confirmed via balloon occlusion cholangiography. In cases where ductal clearance was not achieved, a plastic biliary stent was placed, and pancreatic stents or papillary biopsies were performed as indicated.

Post-procedure, all patients were hospitalized for monitoring and received intravenous maintenance fluids before and after the procedure. Adverse events included post-ERCP pancreatitis, perforation, cholangitis, and mortality. Post-ERCP pancreatitis was defined as abdominal pain with serum lipase levels exceeding 3 times the upper limit of normal within 2 weeks post-procedure, with severity classified according to Cotton criteria (mild: 0–3 days hospitalization; moderate: 4–10 days; and severe: >10 days). Hyperamylasemia was defined as a threefold increase in serum amylase levels 24 hours after ERCP, and cholangitis was defined as fever associated with bile duct symptoms following the procedure.[11,12]

2.3. Statistical analysis

The IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics 19 program (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL) was used for the statistical analysis. The results were expressed as median (minimum–maximum) and percentages.

3. Results

The pre-ERCP symptoms in the cohort included jaundice, abdominal pain, vomiting, and asymptomatic presentations. Jaundice was the most prevalent symptom, observed in 54% (n = 27) of the cases. A total of 65 ERCP procedures were performed on 50 children, comprising 29 females and 21 males, with ages ranging from 1 to 18 years. The mean age at the time of ERCP was 12.7 ± 5.7 years. Notably, 20% (n = 12) of the patients were aged ≤5 years. The children’s body weight varied significantly, with a mean weight of 34.9 (range, 8–89) kg and a mean BMI percentile of 51 (range, 2–99; Table 1).

In 50 cases, MRCP was conducted prior to ERCP, with findings from both modalities showing consistent results. The majority of the children (n = 39) underwent a single ERCP intervention, while 8 children required 2 interventions, 2 children had 3 interventions, and 1 child underwent 4 interventions. This latter case, involving biliary obstruction, ultimately led to a diagnosis of botryoid rhabdomyosarcoma.

ERCP indications predominantly involved the biliary tract (81.5%), with choledocholithiasis being the most common finding, followed by chronic pancreatitis as the most frequent pancreatic condition. The specific indications included choledocholithiasis (52.3%), recurrent pancreatitis (18.5%), anastomotic bile duct strictures post-liver transplantation (13.8%), sclerosing cholangitis (4.6%), choledochal cyst (4.6%), hydatid cyst (4%), and malignancy (4%; Table 1).

The overall success rate for papilla cannulation was 92.3%. However, cannulation was unsuccessful in 4 cases involving the common bile duct and in one case involving the pancreatic duct. Therapeutic interventions included biliary sphincterotomies (45.4%), ampullotomies (4.5%), balloon lavage for stone and sludge removal (31.8%), biliary duct stent placement (9.1%), pancreatic duct stent placement (4.5%), and biliary stent removal (4.5%; Table 2). In some cases, multiple stents were placed, with one case requiring balloon dilation of a stenosis in the proximal common bile duct during an ampullotomy for recurrent gallstones. Several cases required multiple therapeutic procedures during a single ERCP session.

The overall adverse event rate was 13.8% (n = 9). The most common adverse event was post-ERCP pancreatitis, observed in 6.1% (n = 4) of the children. These cases were classified as moderate according to the Cotton severity criteria. Post-ERCP hyperamylasemia was identified in 6.6% of the children. Importantly, no bleeding complications were reported following sphincterotomy, and no other significant complications were observed, including any related to conscious anesthesia.

4. Discussion

Although ERCP indications in children are less common than in adults, the procedure’s utilization has increased in recent years, driven by the rising prevalence of obesity and the availability of genetic testing. In pediatric populations, noninvasive diagnostic methods are generally preferred, with MRCP being widely applicable. Indeed, MRCP has largely supplanted diagnostic ERCP, with the latter now primarily employed for therapeutic purposes in children.[13,14] In our study, 55 cases underwent MRCP imaging, with some patients receiving multiple MRCP scans over time for comparison. All ERCP interventions in this cohort were conducted with a therapeutic focus.

A significant challenge in pediatric ERCP is the limited availability of pediatric-specific duodenoscopes. These instruments are recommended for children under 10 kg or younger than 12 months although a therapeutic duodenoscope is often used as an alternative.[15] In our study, 20% of the patients were aged ≤5 years. The therapeutic duodenoscope was used in nearly all cases, except for one 12-month-old, 8-kg liver transplant patient, where a diagnostic duodenoscope was employed due to anatomical stenosis. In another complex case, ERCP began with a therapeutic duodenoscope but required a nasal scope to cannulate the common bile duct, initially suspected to involve parasitic infection or malignancy. This case marked a milestone as the first instance in Turkey where a child’s common bile duct was successfully cannulated using a nasal scope without a cholangioscope, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of botryoid rhabdomyosarcoma. Notably, no adverse events such as tracheal compression, airway obstruction, or the need for endotracheal intubation occurred during these procedures.

Therapeutic ERCP in children is generally performed for both biliary and pancreatic indications, with the distribution of these indications varying by age. Typically, biliary and pancreatic conditions are similarly distributed among children aged 1 to 6 years, pancreatic indications predominate in those aged 7 to 12 years, and biliary indications become more common in children aged 13 to 17 years.[16] Our study’s findings were largely consistent with this distribution although biliary strictures post-liver transplantation were more frequently observed in younger children aged 1 to 6 years. The most common indications in our study were choledocholithiasis, chronic pancreatitis, and post-liver transplant biliary strictures. It is important to recognize that these indications can vary by region; for instance, common bile duct stones are the most frequent indication in Western countries, whereas choledochal cysts are more prevalent in Asian countries.[7] In our cohort, all cases of choledocholithiasis occurred in patients aged >12 years, with BMI values exceeding the 95th percentile. In addition, hereditary spherocytosis was diagnosed in 7 cases, and all patients with gallbladder stones underwent elective cholecystectomy.

Conscious or deep sedation is increasingly recognized as a safe alternative to general anesthesia for pediatric therapeutic interventions.[17] However, general anesthesia may still be required in certain cases, particularly in those with smaller anatomy. Despite involving numerous therapeutic interventions and a patient as young as 1-year old, none of our cases required general anesthesia. This finding contrasts with other pediatric series where deep sedation or general anesthesia is typically preferred.[18,19] Importantly, no serious adverse events, such as cardiorespiratory depression, were associated with deep sedation in our study, underscoring the clinical competence of our anesthesiology team.

One of the most challenging aspects of ERCP is the initial selective cannulation of the bile ducts, which can sometimes fail. The principles of selective bile duct cannulation in children are similar to those in adults although pediatric cases present additional challenges related to patient size and device limitations. Despite these difficulties, our study achieved a successful cannulation rate of 92.3%, which is consistent with the success rates reported in previous pediatric studies.[20] Among the 65 procedures, the common bile duct could not be cannulated in 4 instances, and the pancreatic duct could not be cannulated in 1 of 4 cases involving chronic pancreatitis. Endoscopic therapy for chronic pancreatitis is recommended from the age of 8 years, under conditions similar to those for adults.[4] Studies have shown that endoscopic therapy can effectively reduce pain, decrease the frequency of pancreatitis, and lessen the need for medical care.[21–23] In our study, ERCP was successfully performed in cases with various pancreatitis indications, and therapeutic interventions were effective in alleviating symptoms without provoking pancreatic attacks.

This pediatric series demonstrated a high rate of therapeutic interventions (92.3%), with many cases involving multiple procedures, such as sphincterotomy combined with balloon sweep or subsequent stent placement. After stent removal, biliary drainage was reevaluated through recannulation and balloon sweep. The overall adverse event rate was 13.8%, with post-ERCP pancreatitis being the most common complication, occurring in 6.1% of cases. Three of these cases followed pancreatic cannulation, and 1 followed sphincterotomy and balloon sweep. Although pancreatic cannulation is often considered a risk factor for post-ERCP pancreatitis, our findings suggest that the risk remains low. Our complication rate aligns with previous reports.[24] The median length of hospitalization for post-ERCP pancreatitis was 2 (range, 1–7) days, consistent with moderate post-ERCP pancreatitis according to the Cotton severity classification. Post-ERCP hyperamylasemia was observed in 7.6% of cases, but no fatalities occurred, consistent with the literature. In 10 cases, rectal indomethacin was administered prophylactically, with no subsequent pancreatitis observed. While rectal indomethacin is a standard preventive measure in adults, its efficacy in children is less well-documented.[25] However, recent studies suggest that it is both safe and effective in pediatric populations, without increased risks of bleeding or renal dysfunction.[26]

A significant limitation of our study is the lack of pediatric-specific duodenoscopes in various sizes, which necessitated the use of surgical interventions for smaller children, particularly those under 12 months of age. The literature suggests that ERCP can be beneficial in cases where MRCP imaging is inadequate, particularly in neonatal cholestasis, where it may represent a viable alternative to surgery.[27]

In conclusion, although this is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, it demonstrates that ERCP is an effective and technically successful intervention for pediatric cases with challenging indications. ERCP is a safe procedure with a low rate of serious adverse events in pediatric patients in Turkey. Increased awareness and expertise in ERCP procedures could enhance outcomes and improve pediatric gastroenterology practice, especially with the establishment of a centralized facility for ERCP cases in Turkey.
