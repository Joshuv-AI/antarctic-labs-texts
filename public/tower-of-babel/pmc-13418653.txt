Improving Follow‐Up After Fall Risk Screening: A Quality Improvement Initiative in Primary Care After the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit
Authors: Michelle S. Keller, Hiroshi Gotanda, Allison M. Mays, Ha Hoang, Sonja Rosen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFalls are a leading cause of preventable injury and disability among older adults. Although fall risk screening is routine in the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit (AWV), timely follow‐up after a positive screen is often lacking. We implemented a primary care‐based quality improvement initiative to increase the rate of fall risk prevention interventions provided to older adults at elevated fall risk.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of patients aged ≥ 65 years who completed an AWV between September 2024 and October 2025 and screened positive for fall risk using a three‐item screener and confirmed with an abnormal Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. On January 1, 2025, we implemented a package of two quality improvement strategies to improve the uptake of evidence‐based fall prevention interventions. First, we implemented a SmartSet in the Electronic Health Record which guides clinicians to select a clinically appropriate follow‐up after a positive fall risk screen, and second, the geriatrics clinic proactively reached out to eligible patients to schedule comprehensive fall and bone health assessments. We evaluated follow‐up rates over time. Outcomes measured 120 days post‐screening during the AWV included: (1) the proportion of patients ≥ 65 with any documented follow‐up action post‐screening (physical therapy referral, case management visit, geriatrics referral, or geriatrics clinic visit), (2) any geriatrics referral, (3) any geriatrics visit, (4) any physical therapy referral, and (5) any case management referral.ResultsThree hundred thirty‐five patients aged ≥ 65 years completed an AWV, screened as positive for fall risk, and had an abnormal TUG. Any follow‐up increased from 53.8% to 67.8%. Follow‐up geriatrics clinic visits increased from 7.5% to 40.4%. Primary care referral rates to geriatrics, physical therapy referrals and case management referrals remained stable.ConclusionA proactive package of implementation strategies embedded in primary care significantly improved follow‐up rates after positive fall risk screening.

Introduction

Falls are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults, with nearly one in four adults aged 65 years or older experiencing a fall each year [1]. Fall‐related injuries contribute significantly to healthcare utilization and loss of independence [2, 3, 4], making fall prevention a critical public health priority. The Medicare Annual Wellness Visit (AWV), a yearly visit aimed at identifying risks in older adults and identifying prevention strategies offered at no cost to Medicare beneficiaries [5], offers a structured opportunity to screen for fall risk. However, the identification of fall risk without timely follow‐up and intervention often fails to reduce future fall events.

In 2012, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention released the Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths & Injuries (STEADI) Initiative, which includes three core elements: Screen, Assess, and Intervene [6, 7]. Clinicians are advised to screen for fall risk yearly or if the patient presents with an acute fall; assess modifiable risk factors such as gait, strength and balance; identify fall‐risk increasing medications; ask about home hazards; check visual acuity; measure orthostatic blood pressure; assess feet and footwear; assess vitamin D uptake and identify comorbidities (e.g., osteoporosis) [6]. If there are identified fall risks, clinicians are advised to develop individual care plans, including referrals to physical therapy or evidence‐based exercise or fall‐prevention programs, occupational therapy to assess home safety, ophthalmologist or optometrist referrals for visual impairment concerns, or podiatry for foot concerns. Other interventions include medication reviews to reduce fall risk, education about exercise and proper footwear, and recommendations about vitamin D supplementation.

Despite these guidelines, a multitude of barriers to implementing evidence‐based fall prevention interventions exist in the primary care setting, including time constraints, competing medical priorities, patient engagement and motivation challenges, few educational materials available on fall prevention, a lack of referral resources, a lack of reimbursement, and competing quality improvement projects [8, 9, 10]. Additionally, qualitative interviews with clinicians reveal that they face numerous challenges to fall prevention during clinical encounters, including addressing complex living situations and significant levels of multi‐morbidity [11]. On the patient side, barriers include a lack of knowledge about appropriate falls prevention and their own competing demands (e.g., other chronic conditions, social determinants) [12]. One initiative tracking steps to implementing the CDC STEADI guidelines into routine primary care identified numerous implementation challenges, including the significant amount of time required for the STEADI intervention (15–20 min), which made it challenging to implement in a routine primary care appointment where other issues needed to be addressed [8]. During this initiative, clinicians developed electronic health record (EHR)‐based tools, such as note templates for high‐ and low‐risk patients, flowsheets for tracking measurements such as the vision screen, Timed Get up and Go test [13] assessment, and orthostatic measurements, and SmartSets with all pertinent orders [8]. This initiative led to 64% of high‐risk fall patients receiving STEADI interventions, demonstrating the ability of focused quality improvement programs to improve the uptake of evidence‐based fall prevention strategies [14].

Given the limited time primary care providers have to address a multitude of health concerns in complex older adults, we leveraged a package of quality improvement strategies aimed at leveraging geriatrics expertise within our health system to increase the uptake of evidence‐based fall prevention interventions. In January 2025, the Cedars‐Sinai Medical Group (CSMG) implemented a targeted package of two quality improvement strategies aimed at closing the follow‐up gap for patients who screened positive for fall risk during AWVs in primary care. These quality improvement strategies were initiated after the CSMG population health and quality team identified through chart review that patients with positive fall risk screenings were not reliably receiving follow‐up interventions. The implementation strategies, which are part of the health system's focus on creating an Age‐Friendly Health System [15], focused on patients with a positive fall risk screening and an abnormal TUG [13] result. For the first strategy, we implemented an EHR intervention to guide primary care physicians through fall risk screening and follow‐up options, including a referral to case management, referral to physical therapy, or referral to the geriatrics clinic for a fall risk assessment. As part of the second strategy, patients screening at high risk of falls received a proactive outreach call from the geriatrics clinic to schedule a follow‐up visit focused on fall prevention.

This study evaluates the implementation and early outcomes of this package of strategies by analyzing follow‐up rates over time and patient‐level characteristics. Specifically, we examine the proportion of patients who received follow‐up care, and how this varied across patient demographic subgroups. We assess whether certain groups, such as patients with language preferences other than English (Spanish or other languages), or from minoritized racial and ethnic backgrounds (Black or African American, Latino or Hispanic, Asian), were likely to receive follow‐up care. Findings from this study will inform ongoing efforts to integrate geriatric care more effectively into primary care workflows and address health equity in fall prevention strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study to evaluate a package of two quality improvement strategies aimed at improving follow‐up care for older adults who screened positive for fall risk during the Medicare AWV. The study was conducted across 25 internal medicine and family medicine clinics with 136 unique primary care providers within the CSMG, a large, multisite academic‐affiliated medical network in Southern California affiliated with the Cedars‐Sinai Health System. The health system has one geriatrics clinic in the system with 7 geriatricians who practice both primary and consultative care. Three geriatricians conducted most of the fall prevention consults at the geriatrics clinic, which had capacity for six consults per day. The medical network payor mix is primarily Medicare and private insurance.

Population

The study population included Medicare beneficiaries, including Fee‐For‐Service and Medicare Advantage beneficiaries, aged 65 years and older who completed a face‐to‐face AWV between September 3, 2024, and October 11, 2025. Patients were included if they screened positive for fall risk and had an abnormal result on the TUG test documented in the EHR. Patients with incomplete screening data or who were deceased within the follow‐up period were excluded.

Intervention

The fall risk screening during the AWV includes the following questions: (1) “Are you worried or concerned about falling (yes/no)”; (2) “Do you feel unsteady when standing or walking?” (yes/no); (3) “How many times have you fallen in the past year?” (number), followed by “Were you injured?” Any positive answer to one of the first two questions or a reported fall in the past year flags the patient at higher risk for falls. The TUG test was then performed for any positive fall risk screens. As part of the TUG, the patient is timed by clinic staff rising from a chair, walking 10 ft and returning to sit in the chair. A time of ≥ 12 s is flagged at higher risk for falls.

Beginning January 1, 2025, CSMG implemented a structured workflow whereby patients identified as being at risk for falls during the AWV were referred for appropriate follow‐up care within 120 days. We implemented the package of quality improvement strategies. First, we implemented a SmartSet (Figure 1) for the AWV which guides clinicians to select a clinically appropriate follow‐up after a positive fall risk screen including options of: (1) A referral for a comprehensive fall risk assessment in a geriatrics clinics, (2) a referral to a case manager (i.e., advanced care management, or ACM), or (3) a referral to physical therapy. ACM is a program run by nurse care managers who reach out proactively to patients, provide additional falls prevention education, inquire about additional in‐home risks and needs, and can help arrange for home health services or durable medical equipment.

Example of fall risk screening assessment in the electronic health record during medicare annual wellness visit. The standardized workflow includes screening questions for fall concern and unsteadiness, documentation of fall frequency in the past year (highlighted), injury history, and the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. Patients with ≥ 1 fall in the past year and an abnormal TUG result (≥ 12 s) were eligible for the proactive geriatrics outreach intervention. The Plan section shows available follow‐up options including referrals to geriatrics, physical therapy, and advanced care management (ACM).

The EHR prompts for specific falls interventions were developed by the quality improvement/population health team who received feedback from providers that a list of next best steps to address falls risk would be helpful in the context of a time‐limited visit to support evidence‐based decision‐making. If a provider preferred to address the risk at a future visit, they could document “Addressed Risk” in the EHR and request that the patient schedule a follow‐up visit.

Second, we implemented a proactive outreach call for patients meeting the eligibility criteria, that is, patients with a positive fall risk screening and an abnormal TUG result, from the geriatrics clinic inviting them to come in for a geriatrics fall‐focused visit. The script was as follows: “Your primary care physician referred you for Bone Health & Fall Prevention consult, because you screened positive for fall risk at your last visit (AWV). Can I go ahead and schedule you with a geriatrician for a consult?” The proactive outreach call was implemented to address both barriers at the primary care provider level, where primary care providers may have too many competing demands and limited time to do a comprehensive assessment and provide interventions (e.g., referrals, education), and at the patient level, where patients may be unaware of the availability of geriatrics consultative visits to address fall prevention or may not understand how a fall prevention consult could be beneficial. Prior studies have found that patients often see falls as related to one or two issues, e.g., home safety concerns, and are unaware of the multifactorial nature of falls [16]. Moreover, navigating referrals may introduce an additional barrier, where patients may have to take the additional step of calling and scheduling the consult. Prior studies have found that older adults may not prioritize fall prevention when managing multiple chronic conditions or may perceive falls as an inevitable part of aging rather than a preventable health issue [17, 18].

During the geriatrics visit, which was scheduled for 60 min, fall‐risk prevention strategies included a comprehensive falls assessment [19], focusing on future fall prevention and bone health. In line with the CDC's STEADI initiative, this visit included a gait and balance assessment, a medication review to identify potentially inappropriate medications, and functional and social assessments (e.g., assessment of activities of daily living, ADLs, and independent activities of daily living, IADLs). The geriatrics visit also included an assessment of bone health, reviewing recent DXA scans and/or ordering scans as needed, with discussions and initiation of pharmacotherapy for osteoporosis when appropriate. While the bone health assessment was an explicit component of the geriatrics consultation, similar assessments could be conducted in the primary care setting, though we did not track this systematically. Patients were then provided appropriate referrals for physical therapy, community‐based exercise classes, medication adjustments, referrals for any needed medical equipment, and necessary follow‐up for abnormal screening tests or if the geriatricians assessed that the patients required significant support for fall prevention. For particularly complex patients, geriatricians and primary care physicians co‐managed care, with visits to the geriatrician every 3 to 6 months.

Data Sources and Variables

Patient‐level data were extracted from the EHR, including demographics (age, gender, race, ethnicity, and preferred language); screening data (date of AWV, fall screening result, and TUG result); follow‐up indicators within 120 days of screening (completion of a geriatrics visit, referral to geriatrics from primary care, case management visit, physical therapy referral).

We tracked separate metrics for geriatrics visits: we tracked referrals from primary care providers (PCPs) to geriatrics, and separately, we tracked completed visits to geriatrics. This allowed us to examine whether visits increased due to increased referrals from PCPs due to the implementation of the SmartSet or from the targeted proactive outreach phone call.

Primary outcomes included the proportion of patients with (1) any follow‐up visit within 120 days of the AWV, (2) a primary care referral to geriatrics within 120 days, and (3) a completed geriatric visit within 120 days. Secondary outcomes included receipt of case management or physical therapy within 120 days.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and follow‐up outcomes. To assess gaps in screening practices, we used chi‐square tests to examine differences in TUG test receipt by age, gender, race/ethnicity, and primary language in both the pre‐ and post‐intervention periods. We then compared primary and secondary outcomes between the pre‐intervention period (September–December 2024; n = 80) and post‐intervention period (January–October 2025; n = 255), both overall and within demographic subgroups, using chi‐square tests to assess statistical significance. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age group (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, and 90+ years), gender (female, male), primary language (English, Spanish, other, unknown/unable to obtain), and race/ethnicity (White, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, Asian, and other/unknown). Due to small cell sizes, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, American Indian or Alaska Native, and unknown/not reported categories were condensed into “other/unknown” for racial/ethnic subgroup analyses.

This study was deemed exempt by the Cedars‐Sinai Institutional Review Board as a quality improvement initiative.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 4075 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 years and older completed an in‐person AWV between September 3, 2024 and October 11, 2025 and had a positive fall screening score. Of these, 848 (22.7%) completed the TUG test, with completion rates of 28.9% in the pre‐intervention period (n = 219/759) and 21.1% in the post‐intervention period (n = 629/2981). TUG completion did not differ by gender in either period, but significant differences by age were observed in the post‐period, with older patients less likely to be screened (p = 0.005) (Table S1). Differences by race/ethnicity and primary language emerged in the post‐intervention period only (p < 0.001 and p = 0.010, respectively), with White patients having lower completion rates (18.3%) than Black or African American (27.4%), Hispanic or Latino (28.1%), and Asian (26.6%) patients. English‐speaking patients had lower TUG completion rates (20.9%) compared to Spanish‐speaking patients (29.2%).

Among those who completed the TUG, 335 patients (28.3%) had both an abnormal result and a positive fall screening score and formed the analytic sample for this study (Table 1). This final sample was majority female (68.8%) and racially and ethnically diverse, with 38.2% identifying as White, 24.8% as Black or African American, 16.4% as Hispanic or Latino, and 13.1% as Asian. English was the primary language for 82.4% of patients, followed by Spanish (12.5%), with the remaining patients speaking a variety of other languages or having an unknown language preference. There were no statistically significant differences between the pre‐ and post‐intervention groups across age, gender, race/ethnicity, or primary language.

Post AWV Follow‐Up Outcomes

Follow‐up outcomes after the AWV differed notably between the pre‐ and post‐intervention periods (Table 2). With respect to primary outcomes, the proportion of patients with any follow‐up visit within 120 days increased significantly from 53.8% in the pre‐period to 67.8% in the post‐period (p = 0.024), and completed geriatric visits increased substantially from 7.5% to 40.4% (p < 0.001). Notably, geriatric referrals from primary care, which the quality improvement package was designed to bypass in favor of direct outreach, remained low and did not significantly change (7.5% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.253). Regarding secondary outcomes, PT referrals were modestly higher in the post‐period (47.5% vs. 55.7%) but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.249), and ACM visit rates were similarly unchanged (6.3% vs. 5.9%, p = 1.000).

Rates of geriatric referrals from primary care did not significantly increase in any demographic subgroup following the quality improvement package implementation (Table S2).

In contrast to the referral pathway, geriatric visit rates increased significantly across nearly all subgroups following the implementation strategies (Table 4). Significant increases were observed among patients aged 70–74 (0.0% to 53.3%, p < 0.001), 80–84 (12.5% to 47.1%, p = 0.029), and 85–89 (0.0% to 34.4%, p = 0.04), as well as among both female (5.5% to 39.6%, p < 0.001) and male (12.0% to 41.9%, p = 0.01) patients. Importantly, the intervention appeared to reach patients across racial and ethnic groups, with significant increases observed among White (4.2% to 33.7%, p = 0.01), Black or African American (14.8% to 53.6%, p < 0.01), Hispanic or Latino (9.1% to 45.5%, p = 0.04), and Asian (0.0% to 45.5%, p < 0.01) patients. Increases were similarly observed among both English‐ (9.0% to 39.2%, p < 0.001) and Spanish‐speaking (0.0% to 48.5%, p = 0.008) patients.

Discussion

This quality improvement initiative demonstrated that a structured, proactive outreach model embedded within primary care significantly improved overall and geriatrics‐visit follow‐up rates for older adults who screened positive for fall risk. The intervention achieved significant improvements in both overall follow‐up (53.8% to 67.8%, p = 0.03) and geriatrics visits (7.5% to 40.4%, p < 0.001). The increase in follow‐up geriatric visits was particularly pronounced among female patients and patients aged 70–89 years. All racial and ethnic groups demonstrated significant increases in geriatrics visits, and non‐English speaking patients showed comparable improvements to English‐speaking patients.

Our initiative builds on prior work demonstrating that fall prevention can be successfully implemented by clinicians with varying levels of training. Quinlan and Ryer employed nurses to conduct fall screening during the AWV and deliver two STEADI interventions: home safety assessments and gait, balance, and strength evaluations [12]. They provided comprehensive nurse training on the STEADI initiative, age‐friendly principles, motivational interviewing, and standardized assessment tools, and similarly developed EHR documentation to track implementation. Other approaches have relied on primary care providers to deliver STEADI components, which can be effective [8, 14], but may require ongoing monitoring to prevent fall prevention from being deprioritized among competing demands.

The need for interprofessional approaches reflects the time constraints inherent in primary care. A 2024 American Geriatrics Society workgroup recommended strategies including prioritizing STEADI elements that require minimal time, spreading assessments across multiple visits, and leveraging interprofessional resources such as pharmacists, physical therapists, nurses, and social workers who regularly interact with older adults and can intervene across fall risk domains [20]. Our quality improvement initiative extends this concept by using consultative geriatricians for STEADI assessment and intervention, which reduces the need for extensive training of additional staff, although geriatricians may not be available in all health systems.

Our findings suggest that proactive, system‐level interventions may be effective at promoting health equity and improving outcomes by eliminating patient‐level barriers to accessing follow‐up care [21, 22, 23]. This aligns with growing evidence that structural interventions, rather than solely patient‐directed education or individual primary care provider education, may be more effective at improving outcomes and reducing healthcare disparities [23, 24]. The observation that Black or African American patients had higher geriatric visit rates in the post‐intervention period is encouraging in this regard, particularly given well‐documented disparities in healthcare access and utilization for Black patients in the United States [25, 26]. The proactive outreach model, in which the geriatrics clinic initiated contact rather than requiring patients to navigate referral systems independently, may have reduced one barrier. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. A key limitation of this system‐level approach is that it did not address broader social determinants of health, and it remains unclear whether these gains would generalize to other settings or patient populations. These findings warrant replication in other health system contexts before stronger conclusions can be drawn.

The similarity in follow‐up rates between male and female patients suggests that the intervention did not disproportionately favor one gender, which is important given that women have higher fall rates but men experience more severe fall‐related injuries [27, 28]. The lack of a significant difference by language status is also encouraging. Language‐concordant outreach, availability of interpreter services, use of community health workers, and culturally tailored materials may be important considerations for optimizing engagement among patients with limited English proficiency [29, 30].

The success of this initiative can be attributed to several key features. First, the proactive outreach intervention shifted the responsibility for initiating follow‐up from patients to the healthcare system, reducing the burden on older adults who may face competing health priorities, cognitive challenges, or limited health literacy [17, 18]. Second, by standardizing the workflow and embedding it within routine AWV processes, we created a sustainable system that did not rely on individual primary care provider initiative or memory. Third, as the health system has recently hired a new geriatrician without an established patient panel, there was ample availability for patients to be scheduled for the geriatrics visit, which resulted in very short wait times (2 weeks). This may change as the practice grows busier and waiting times increase.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single‐site initiative within one healthcare system, which may limit generalizability to other settings with different patient populations, resources, or organizational structures. Second, some demographic subgroups (Asians, Hispanic or Latino) had small sample sizes, which limited our statistical power to detect differences. Third, data were not available on other important factors that may influence follow‐up, such as socioeconomic status, transportation access, caregiver availability, health literacy, or prior fall‐related injuries. These unmeasured variables may confound or modify the associations we observed. Fourth, we used EHR data to capture follow‐up visits, which may underestimate true follow‐up if patients sought care outside our system. Fifth, one challenge in our intervention is that geriatricians reported that patients did not always fully understand the purpose of the geriatrics visit, as it had not been mentioned or discussed during their AWV. This may be a challenge that could be addressed by primary care physicians, who could note during the AWV that patients may be referred to a specialized follow‐up service to address fall prevention and bone health. Finally, this analysis focused on whether patients received follow‐up care but did not assess the quality of that care or subsequent clinical outcomes such as fall rates or fall‐related injuries. Future studies will examine interventions within the geriatrics visit post fall screening and longer‐term outcomes such as falls.

Our findings have several important implications. First, healthcare systems seeking to improve fall risk management should consider proactive outreach models that reduce patient‐level barriers to accessing care. Second, quality improvement initiatives should routinely monitor equity metrics across demographic subgroups to ensure that interventions benefit all populations and do not inadvertently widen disparities. Third, the success observed among Black or African American patients suggests that system‐level interventions can be powerful tools for advancing health equity. Fourth, a limitation of our approach is its reliance on consultative geriatricians, which may not be feasible in the many healthcare systems that lack geriatrics clinics or have insufficient geriatric capacity. However, the core strategy, a proactive screening to trigger focused fall prevention visits, could be implemented by internal medicine or family practice providers and could leverage other interprofessional clinicians such as nurse practitioners, clinical pharmacists, physical therapists, or social workers to conduct assessments and interventions.

The sustainability of this proactive approach is an important consideration for broader implementation. Schedulers conducted approximately 9–10 outreach calls per week in our study, a modest workload that was integrated into existing responsibilities. This low call volume suggests the intervention is feasible for ongoing use, though formal incorporation into scheduler or care coordinator role expectations would be necessary for sustained implementation. Scalability to larger populations would require assessment of staffing capacity, and cost‐effectiveness analyses comparing staff time investment to improved outcomes and potential acute care savings would inform health system adoption decisions.

Future research should examine longer‐term outcomes including subsequent fall rates, emergency department visits, and hospitalizations for fall‐related injuries to determine whether improved follow‐up rates translate into meaningful clinical benefits. Moreover, future research is planned on examining patient factors associated with post‐fall prevention visit actions, such as completion of a physical therapy visit or other referrals. Additionally, qualitative research exploring patient and provider perspectives on barriers and facilitators to follow‐up could inform refinements to the intervention and identify population‐specific strategies to further improve engagement.

Conclusion

A proactive, system‐level intervention significantly improved follow‐up rates for older adults identified as high‐risk for falls during routine Medicare AWVs. These findings support the implementation of proactive outreach models in fall prevention programs and highlight the importance of monitoring equity metrics in quality improvement initiatives.
