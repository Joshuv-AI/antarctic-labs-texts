Opportunistic screening for frailty in geriatric patients attending a dental outpatient clinic: a cross-sectional comparison of the FAST and FRAIL scales in a tertiary care hospital in India
Authors: Satya Ranjan Misra, Neeta Mohanty, Satya Sundar Gajendra Mohapatra, Rupsa Das, Mahesh Kumar Raybahadur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a significant issue in older adults and is associated with adverse health outcomes. Screening for frailty in dental settings could identify vulnerable older adults who could benefit from comprehensive geriatric assessment. Our aim was to determine the prevalence of frailty among geriatric patients attending a dental outpatient clinic using two validated tools, the Frailty Assessment and Screening Tool (FAST) and the FRAIL scale, and to evaluate their agreement.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 500 consecutive patients aged ≥ 60 years attending the outpatient department of a tertiary care dental hospital in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India between June 2025 and December 2025. Frailty was assessed using the 14-item FAST (score ≥ 7 = frail, 5–6 = pre-frail, < 5 = robust) and the 5-item FRAIL scale (score 3–5 = frail, 1–2 = pre-frail, 0 = robust). Demographic data were collected. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, Cohen’s kappa for agreement, Spearman’s rank correlation, and binary logistic regression. As no clinical gold standard (like Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment) was employed, analyses of the FRAIL scale against FAST are presented as concordance metrics, not diagnostic accuracy. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement.ResultsThe mean age was 70.8 ± 6.7 years; 410 (82%) were male. According to FAST, 248 (49.6%) were frail, 109 (21.8%) pre-frail, and 143 (28.6%) robust. By FRAIL, 175 (35.0%) were frail, 200 (40.0%) pre-frail, and 125 (25.0%) robust. Agreement between the two scales was moderate (κ = 0.521, 95% CI 0.48–0.56, p < 0.001), with 68.0% concordant classification. The most prevalent deficits on the FAST were limitations in general health (60%), mobility issues (55%), fatigue (50%), memory complaints (50%), and pain limiting daily activities (50%). Age was significantly associated with frailty (OR 1.08 per year, 95% CI 1.05–1.11, p < 0.001). Gender was not a significant predictor in this predominantly male sample, though this analysis is underpowered.ConclusionFrailty is highly prevalent among older adults attending a dental outpatient clinic, with nearly half classified as frail by the multidimensional FAST and over one-third by the simpler FRAIL scale. The two scales show moderate agreement, but FAST captures a broader range of geriatric domains. Routine frailty screening in dental settings may facilitate early identification and referral of at-risk older adults, though further validation against a clinical gold standard is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07706-1.

Background

Life expectancy has been increasing globally resulting in the rise in the population of older adults, accompanied by a rise in age-related health conditions [1]. Frailty has emerged as a significant geriatric syndrome, characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, leading to higher risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines frailty as “a clinically recognizable state in which the ability of older people to cope with every day or acute stressors is compromised by an increased vulnerability brought by age-associated declines in physiological reserve and function across multiple organ systems” [4].

In high-income countries, frailty has been extensively studied but research in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as India is still limited [5, 6]. The socio-cultural and economic differences in LMICs necessitate the use of culturally/ethnically appropriate, easy-to-administer screening tools [7]. Responding to this need, the Frailty Assessment and Screening Tool (FAST) was developed locally and validated in an Indian population, demonstrating excellent psychometric properties (Cronbach’s α = 0.96, test-retest reliability = 0.97) [8]. The FAST instrument comprises 14 items across 10 domains, providing a multidimensional assessment of frailty. Another widely used simple screening tool is the FRAIL scale, which includes five items: Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, and Loss of weight [9]. The FRAIL scale has been validated in various populations and is recommended for rapid frailty screening in clinical practice [10].

Dental hospitals represent an opportunistic setting for frailty screening, as older adults frequently attend for oral health problems that may themselves be associated with frailty (like chewing difficulties affecting nutrition, oral pain impacting quality of life) [11]. Identifying frailty in patients attending dental clinics could enable timely interventions and referrals to geriatric services. However, no previous study has compared the performance of FAST and FRAIL specifically in a dental outpatient population.

This study compares two validated screening instruments against each other to assess their agreement and classification patterns; it is not a diagnostic validation study against a clinical gold standard such as Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment or Fried’s phenotype. Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) determine the prevalence of frailty among geriatric patients attending a tertiary care dental hospital in India using the FAST and FRAIL scales; (2) compare the two scales and assess their agreement; and (3) examine the association of age and gender with frailty status in this population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Oral Medicine clinic, Institute of Dental Sciences, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India, a tertiary teaching hospital, from June 2025 to December 2025. The study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12] (Supplementary file 1 for STROBE checklist).

Participants

Patients aged 60 years or older presenting for any dental/oral complaint during the study period were approached for participation. Exclusion criteria were inability to understand the study questions or provide coherent responses after two explanations (assessed informally by the trained interviewer as a brief cognitive screen), and refusal to provide informed consent. A total of 500 patients were enrolled, and data were collected consecutively. Patients were enrolled regardless of their specific dental diagnosis (like dental caries, mobility, pain, prosthetic needs); the dental clinic served solely as a catchment site for opportunistic screening.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of frailty with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. Based on a previous Indian study reporting frailty prevalence of 45% [8], the minimum required sample size was 381. To account for potential missing data and to enhance precision, we recruited 500 participants. This sample size also provides > 90% power to detect a kappa statistic of 0.5 as significant (α = 0.05) for agreement analysis.

Data collection

After obtaining written informed consent, a trained interviewer (a dental intern) administered the questionnaires in a private room. Demographic information (age and gender) was recorded. The FAST and FRAIL scales were translated into Odia following standard guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of instruments: forward translation by two independent bilingual translators, reconciliation into a single version, back translation by two different bilingual translators blinded to the original versions, and review by an expert committee consisting of dentists and linguists. The pre-final Odia versions were pre-tested on a pilot cohort of 30 Odia-speaking older adults (separate from the main study sample) to assess comprehensibility and cultural appropriateness. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made, and the final Odia versions were used in the study.

While this process ensured linguistic and cultural equivalence, formal psychometric evaluation (like internal consistency, test-retest reliability) of the translated versions within the study sample was not performed. A formal cognitive assessment instrument (like Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment) was not administered for screening purposes due to time constraints in the busy outpatient clinic and also because the primary aim was to evaluate self-report frailty tools in patients who could meaningfully participate.

FAST scale [8]: A 14-item instrument covering 10 domains: nutrition (2 items), memory (1), mobility (2), functional status (2), mood (1), physical performance (1), general health (1), medications/multi-morbidity (2), continence (1), and pain (1). Each item is scored 0 (no deficit) or 1 (deficit present), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 14. Based on the validation study, cut-offs are robust (< 5), pre-frail (5–6), frail (≥ 7). (Supplementary file 2 for FAST scale).

FRAIL scale [9]: A 5-item scale assessing Fatigue (“Have you felt tired most of the time over the past month?“), Resistance (“Do you have difficulty climbing a flight of stairs?“), Ambulation (“Do you have difficulty walking one block?“), Illnesses (presence of five or more illnesses), and Loss of weight (unintentional weight loss > 5% in the past year). Each item is scored 0 or 1, total score 0–5. Cut-offs: robust (0), pre-frail (1–2), frail (3–5). The FRAIL scale was administered immediately after the FAST scale. The average time for complete assessment was approximately fifteen minutes per participant. (Supplementary file 2 for FRAIL scale)

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Institute of Dental Sciences (Proposal No: 2025/ɪ-02). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analysed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables.

The association between frailty status and categorical variables (age, gender) was examined using the chi-square test. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between FAST total score and FRAIL total score. Agreement between the two scales’ classifications was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ) with 95% confidence intervals. Kappa values were interpreted as: <0.20 poor, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, and 0.81–1.00 almost perfect agreement.

Because this study did not employ a clinical gold standard (like Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment or Fried’s phenotype), the FAST scale was used only as a multidimensional comparator. Metrics of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) for the FRAIL scale were calculated to describe concordance with FAST, not diagnostic accuracy. Two sets of concordance analyses were performed: detecting pre-frail/frail versus robust and detecting frail versus pre-frail/robust.

Binary logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of frailty (FAST ≥ 7), with age (continuous) and gender (female vs. male) as independent variables. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke R². Due to the marked imbalance in gender distribution (82% male), the analysis for gender is underpowered and should be interpreted with caution.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the discriminative concordance of the FRAIL scale with FAST-defined frailty, with area under the curve (AUC) reported. An AUC of 0.70–0.80 was considered acceptable, 0.80–0.90 excellent, and > 0.90 outstanding [13]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the 500 participants enrolled in the study, the mean age was 70.8 ± 6.7 years (range 60–91 years). The majority were males (410, 82%). Age distribution was: 60–69 years: 238 (47.6%), 70–79 years: 197 (39.4%), ≥ 80 years: 65 (13.0%).

Frailty prevalence

FAST scale: Based on FAST scores, 143 (28.6%) participants were classified as robust, 109 (21.8%) as pre-frail, and 248 (49.6%) as frail. The mean FAST score was 6.2 ± 2.7 (range 0–14). FRAIL scale: Using the FRAIL scale, 125 (25.0%) were robust, 200 (40.0%) pre-frail, and 175 (35.0%) frail. The mean FRAIL score was 1.9 ± 1.4 (range 0–5).

Domain-specific findings (FAST)

The most frequently reported deficits were general health limitations (60%), mobility issues (55%), fatigue/physical decline (50%), memory complaints (50%), pain limiting daily activities (50%), and appetite/weight changes (45%). Females had a higher prevalence of frailty than males (52.8% vs. 43.9% by FAST), but the difference was not statistically significant (χ² = 0.98, p = 0.61). Given the marked male predominance (82% of the sample), this analysis is underpowered to detect true gender differences.

Binary logistic regression (Table 5) showed that each additional year of age increased the odds of frailty (FAST ≥ 7) by 8% (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.05–1.11, p < 0.001). Gender was not a significant predictor (OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.71–3.19, p = 0.28). However, this analysis is severely underpowered due to the marked gender imbalance (82% male, n = 410; 18% female, n = 90). The wide confidence interval for the female odds ratio (0.71–3.19) reflects this imprecision. Therefore, no definitive conclusion regarding sex differences in frailty can be drawn from these data. The model had a Nagelkerke R² of 0.18, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated good fit (χ² = 6.8, p = 0.56).

FAST total score correlated strongly with FRAIL total score (Spearman’s ρ = 0.79, p < 0.001). ROC analysis (Fig. 1) showed that the FRAIL scale had excellent discriminative concordance identifying FAST-defined frailty (≥ 7), with an area under the curve of 0.92 (95% CI 0.90–0.94).

Discussion

The present cross-sectional study of 500 older adult patients attending a tertiary care dental clinic in India revealed three important findings. Firstly, the prevalence of frailty was remarkably high, with nearly half (49.6%) classified as frail by the multidimensional FAST and over one-third (35.0%) by the simpler FRAIL scale. Pre-frailty was seen in an additional 21.8% (FAST) to 40.0% (FRAIL) of the population, meaning that approximately three-fourths of older dental outpatients demonstrated some degree of frailty. Secondly, the two scales demonstrated moderate agreement (κ = 0.521, 68.0% concordance), with FAST identifying significantly more individuals as frail compared to FRAIL. Lastly, age emerged as a powerful and consistent predictor of frailty, with each additional year increasing the odds by 8%. The analysis of gender was underpowered due to the predominantly male sample, and no definitive conclusion regarding sex differences can be drawn.

Frailty prevalence in context: a comparative perspective

The frailty prevalence observed in our dental outpatient population exceeds most estimates from community-based studies in India and other LMICs. Khandelwal et al. [14], using Fried’s phenotype in hospitalized older adult patients at a tertiary care hospital in New Delhi, reported frailty in 33% of participants. Chatterjee et al. in a community-based study from West Bengal found that 21.4% of older adults were frail as per Fried’s criteria [15]. More recently, Dasgupta et al. [16] applied the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) to a rural elderly population in West Bengal and identified 38.8% as frail. Our FAST-derived prevalence of 49.6% is substantially higher.

Several explanations may account for this discrepancy. First, the FAST is a multidimensional instrument that captures not only physical frailty but also cognitive, psychological, and sensory domains [8]. In contrast, Fried’s phenotype focuses exclusively on physical criteria [3]. The broader net cast by FAST inevitably captures more individuals, especially those with non-physical deficits such as memory impairment, mood disturbance, or pain. Indeed, in our sample, 50% reported memory complaints, 40% had mood symptoms, and 50% had pain, all of which contribute to FAST scores but are not captured by purely physical frailty instruments.

Second, patients attending a dental hospital may represent a frailer subset of the older population. Poor oral health has been increasingly recognized as both a consequence and a contributor to frailty [17, 18]. Edentulism and periodontal disease impair mastication, leading to reduced nutritional intake and weight loss [19]. Oral pain and discomfort can limit physical activity and social engagement, accelerating functional decline [20]. Conversely, frail individuals may have difficulty maintaining oral hygiene and accessing dental care, creating a vicious cycle [21]. However, because detailed oral health indicators were not collected in this study, we cannot directly confirm these associations; our findings only demonstrate that this clinical setting captures a high burden of frailty.

Gender and frailty: an underpowered analysis

Although females in our study showed a higher prevalence of frailty (52.8% vs. 43.9% in males), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.61). The lack of significance is almost certainly attributable to the severe gender imbalance in our sample (82% male, 18% female). Most population-based studies report higher frailty prevalence in women [22, 23], a phenomenon attributed to women’s longer life expectancy, higher rates of disability, and differences in body composition [24]. Our failure to detect a significant gender difference likely represents a Type II error due to insufficient female enrolment. The extreme male-to-female ratio (approximately 4:1) reflects the specific healthcare-seeking patterns of this tertiary care dental hospital and severely limits the generalizability of these findings to female geriatric patients. Future studies should employ stratified sampling to ensure balanced gender representation and provide valid estimates of gender-specific frailty burden.

Comparison between FAST and FRAIL: theoretical and practical implications

The moderate agreement (κ = 0.521) between FAST and FRAIL is consistent with previously published studies comparing different frailty instruments. Theou et al. [25] compared seven frailty scales and found kappa values ranging from 0.35 to 0.75. A study comparing the FRAIL scale with Fried’s phenotype in a European population reported a kappa of 0.48 [26].

The conceptual basis for this moderate agreement lies in the different theoretical foundations of the two instruments. The FRAIL scale is rooted in the physical phenotype model [3], while FAST is grounded in the deficit accumulation model [2] and includes domains such as cognition, mood, continence, and pain. Consequently, FAST identifies individuals as frail based on deficits that may be unrelated to the physical criteria captured by FRAIL.

From a clinical perspective, the choice between FAST and FRAIL depends on the purpose of screening and available resources. The FRAIL scale’s brevity makes it ideal for rapid screening in busy outpatient settings. Its high concordance sensitivity (88.5–89.5%) against the more comprehensive FAST suggests it can effectively identify individuals who may warrant further assessment. However, FAST’s multidimensional nature provides richer clinical information that could inform dental treatment planning, like pain management, mobility accommodations, or nutritional counselling. It is important to emphasize that without a clinical gold standard (like Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment), this study cannot determine which tool is more “accurate” for diagnosing frailty. It only demonstrates that the two tools classify frailty differently and that FAST captures a broader range of deficits.

Clinical implications for dental practice

The high prevalence of frailty in this dental outpatient population has important implications. Routine frailty screening in dental settings could serve as an effective case-finding strategy, identifying vulnerable older adults who might otherwise remain undetected [27]. Given time constraints, a stepped approach may be practical: initial screening with the brief FRAIL scale, followed by comprehensive assessment with FAST or geriatric referral in those screening positive [28–30]. Frailty status should also inform treatment planning, including shorter appointments, ergonomic positioning, simplified oral hygiene regimens, and caregiver involvement [31–33].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the large sample size (largest comparison of FAST and FRAIL to date), use of two culturally adapted instruments, and adherence to STROBE guidelines. However, several limitations must be acknowledged:

Conclusion

Frailty is highly prevalent among older adults attending a tertiary care dental hospital in India, particularly in this predominantly male cohort, with nearly half classified as frail by the multidimensional FAST and over one-third by the simpler FRAIL scale. The two scales demonstrate moderate agreement, but FAST captures a broader range of geriatric domains that may be relevant to comprehensive care planning. Age is a strong predictor of frailty, while gender differences could not be reliably assessed due to sample imbalance. Routine frailty screening in dental settings may facilitate early identification and referral of at-risk older adults, though further validation against a clinical gold standard and prospective evaluation of screening protocols are needed. Future research should validate these findings in more diverse populations, incorporate oral health measures, and evaluate the predictive validity of these screening tools against clinical outcomes.
