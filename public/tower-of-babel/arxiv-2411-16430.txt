Interface Energy and Phase Transformations: A Comparative Analysis of Cahn-Hilliard and CALPHAD-based Models in Ternary Substitutional Alloys
Wolfgang Flachberger, Thomas Antretter, Swaroop Gaddikere-Nagaraja, Silvia Leitner, Manuel Petersmann, Jiri Svoboda

Abstract
Diffusional phase transformations in alloys are modeled either by phase-field methods of Cahn–Hilliard type [ 1 ] , in which a double-well free energy and a gradient term impose an interface energy, or by reactive diffusion models [ 2 ] , in which the driving forces follow from the CALPHAD methodology, the convex hull of the molar free energy, and the interface carries no energy. Their numerical schemes are tied to their free energies: phase-field schemes break down when the interface energy is set to zero, and reactive diffusion codes rely on finite differences. We derive, from the thermodynamic extremal principle, a single finite element scheme that covers both limits: the stationarity of the Rayleighian functional (free energy rate plus one half of the dissipation, with mass conservation imposed by multipliers) with respect to the rates, fluxes and multipliers at a frozen state. Fluxes and chemical affinities are primary unknowns, so no derivative of the affinity is required. The time-discrete scheme is the corresponding incremental minimization; it conserves mass exactly and satisfies a discrete energy–dissipation inequality for every convex molar free energy, including the convex hull without regularization. We make explicit that this last case is a degenerate parabolic problem of Stefan type, whose degeneracy can be removed, without altering the free energy, only by a moving mesh; since our aim is the coupling to continuum mechanics and damage on a fixed mesh, we operate at the boundary of degeneracy and show that mass conservation, the energy balance and the second law remain intact there. Thermodynamic consistency is proved for multi-component systems, a scheme relating Onsager and diffusion coefficients is proposed, and the role of interface energy is studied in binary and ternary examples, the latter with vacancies as a non-conserved component.



Keywords
Interface energy, CALPHAD, Cahn-Hilliard, Thermodynamic extremal principle, Rayleighian, Non-equilibrium thermodynamics, Degenerate parabolic equation, Diffusion, Phase-field method, Mixed finite element method

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

One of the challenges in modern materials science is developing a mathematical description of the evolution of a material’s microstructure and morphology during production, thermal treatment, or usage, which can profoundly impact its mechanical properties and failure behavior. A significant portion of these processes can be attributed to diffusional phase transformations. Although mathematical descriptions are available, modeling and simulation still pose significant challenges in contemporary materials science due to the complex interactions and effects observed in relation to diffusion.
Moreover, many highly specialized models are available, but they are limited by their narrow focus on a specific phenomenon, which can make selecting a single model a compromise. One such example is the Cahn-Hilliard model
[
3
]
, which excels in simulating spinodal decomposition, with existing finite element methods available
[
1
]
. The Cahn-Hilliard model’s most prominent feature is the free energy expressed in terms of a double-well potential, which drives phase separation by imposing interface energy. However, if this interface energy term is set to zero, the available finite element methods break down. On the other hand, there are methodologies, where the interface energy is implicitly assumed to be zero, such as those developed by Svoboda et al.
[
2
,
4
]
, where the CALPHAD method is used to derive the driving forces for diffusion, and the equations of linear thermodynamics are employed
[
5
]
. We will refer to these models as reactive diffusion models. These approaches neglect interface energy and often rely on simple finite difference methods, which are restricted to computations on regular grids.
A novel simulation scheme has been developed in this publication, capable of treating systems both with and without interface energy.
This new methodology enables the use of driving forces derived not only from double-well type potentials but also from the CALPHAD methodology, thereby allowing full control and direct utilization of thermodynamic data. To the authors’ knowledge, an approach this versatile is not available in the literature.
The scheme is derived from the thermodynamic extremal principle (TEP)
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
: the evolution equations, and with them the discrete variational forms, follow from the stationarity of a single functional, the Rayleighian, whose extremal properties have recently been examined in
[
9
]
. We adopt the formulation and the nomenclature of that work throughout.
Additionally, the methodology enables systematic investigations into the influence of interface energy on phase morphologies and is designed to be broadly applicable to a wide range of phase transformation problems, including multi-phase, multi-component systems, with other rate-dependent phenomena such as vacancy generation and annihilation.
To demonstrate this, we consider a vacancy diffusion model
[
10
]
that accounts for vacancy sources and sinks. The crucial role of vacancies in facilitating diffusion in crystals is well-established
[
11
]
, enabling atomic transport and phenomena such as the Kirkendall effect
[
4
]
and diffusion-controlled fatigue and void formation
[
12
]
, particularly at high temperatures. In substitutional alloys, vacancies can be treated as an additional component, making this an ideal test case to showcase the methodology’s capability for ternary systems.

1.2
The Cahn-Hilliard model

In their seminal work, Cahn and Hilliard (1958)
[
3
]

presented a novel approach to define the Helmholtz energy of binary
alloys. They postulated that the total Helmholtz energy
\mathcal{F}[\phi]
of a volume
V
with nonuniform composition can be represented as a functional of the mole fraction
\phi
, comprising the molar free energy
f_{0}(\phi)
of a uniform composition system and a gradient energy term
\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}
. This yields the characteristic free energy functional:

\mathcal{F}[\phi]=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{V}\left(f_{0}(\phi)+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\right)\;dV

(1)

Here,
\Omega
denotes the molar volume of the alloy.
For a stress-free system with equal partial molar volumes of the components,
it can be considered constant.
For
f_{0}(\phi)
, a double-well potential as given in Figure
1
was proposed.
This also implies that the system accounts for interface energy, since the double-well suggests that
the mixed state (
\phi=0.5
) has a higher free energy than a linear
mixture of the individual phases in their equilibrium states
(
\phi_{\alpha}=0.25
,
\phi_{\beta}=0.75
). If no interface energy is
intended, the double-well potential can be replaced by its convex hull
curve
f_{0}^{**}(\phi)
, as indicated by the dashed line in Figure
1
.
This assumption is a widely adopted paradigm in physical chemistry and materials science, known as the common tangent construction or CALPHAD method
[
13
]
.
Using the convex
hull curve instead of the double-well potential introduces an intriguing
change in the behavior of a system’s evolution, which will
no longer focus on minimizing the surface area
between the two phases. Instead, it will solely aim to minimize the
total free energy of the system through phase growth, subject to
boundary conditions and mass conservation.
We will refer to the replacement of
f_{0}(\phi)
with
f_{0}^{**}(\phi)
as case (i) in this publication.
Another noteworthy property of using the convex hull curve is that it can be employed without
regularization, i.e.
\kappa
can be set to zero. The replacement of
f_{0}(\phi)
with
f_{0}^{**}(\phi)
together with
\kappa=0
will be referred to as case (ii). Case (ii) results in a sharp transition between phases and has been extensively employed in various studies, including those by Svoboda et al.
[
14
]
. The Cahn-Hilliard model, along with cases (i) and (ii), will be systematically compared in this work. The three cases are distinguishable solely by their respective molar free energy functions
f_{m}
, i.e., the integrand in equation (
1
). Specifically, their molar free energies are defined as follows:

•

Cahn-Hilliard (interface energy + smooth interface)

f_{m}(\phi):=f_{0}(\phi)+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}

(2)

•

Case (i):
Reactive diffusion with regularization
(no interface energy + smooth interface)

f_{m}(\phi):=f_{0}^{**}(\phi)+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}

(3)

•

Case (ii):
Reactive diffusion
(no interface energy + sharp interface)

f_{m}(\phi):=f_{0}^{**}(\phi)

(4)

Although Cahn and Hilliard referred to the term
\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}

as “gradient energy”, it has become common to call it a regularization
or homogenization term due to its smoothing effect on the interface.
Therefore, by neglecting regularization, the system tends towards discontinuous jumps in the mole fraction between the phases. As is made precise in Section
1.3
, case (ii) is a degenerate parabolic problem: the diffusivity vanishes wherever the convex hull is linear, and the sharp interface is a free boundary. Schemes in which the mole fraction is the only unknown and the gradient of the affinity is required as a continuous field break down at this boundary, which is why numerical treatment by common diffusion schemes has up until now only been possible using the finite difference method (FD)
[
2
,
4
]
and a specific discontinuous Galerkin method which essentially mimics the previous FD schemes
[
15
]
, both of which evaluate flux balances across cell faces instead. More elegant approaches to this problem are available
[
16
]
but these methods are not within the scope of this work since we focus on methods compatible with the standard (continuous) finite element method (FEM), in order to be able to add mechanical coupling in future studies.
Interestingly, Cahn and Hilliard
themselves noted that if
\kappa
is set to zero, the interface
becomes sharp
[
3
]
. They argued that if this case is of interest,
the numerical scheme has to be adapted accordingly.
To this end, we
simply want to motivate the special case of
\kappa=0
by noting
that it can be beneficial to have full control over the interface width
when performing multi-physics simulations, and that case (ii)
corresponds to employing the CALPHAD methodology. Moreover, it can be reasonable
to have a sharp interface when the length scale of the problem permits
it. Thermodynamics relies on the assumption that quantities like
pressure and temperature are assigned to a volume that is large enough
for the assumptions of statistical mechanics to hold. In the numerical
continuum treatment of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, this means that
each volume element in the domain must contain enough particles (atoms
or molecules) to ensure the validity of these assumptions, effectively
constraining the mesh size to be larger than a certain threshold.
However, when there is no interface energy and the length scale allows
it, it becomes legitimate to approximate the interface as sharp. Note
that this statement only holds for the continuum/volumetric treatment,
as there are also sharp interface models, such as those proposed by

[
16
]
, that operate with idealized surfaces representing
the interface. In these models, the interface is treated as a manifold,
allowing for the assignment of interface energy despite its
infinitesimally thin nature.

Figure 1:

f_{0}
represents a general double-well potential as
proposed by Cahn and Hilliard. The molar free energies of the individual
phases are given by
f_{0}^{\alpha}
and
f_{0}^{\beta}

respectively. In an equilibrium system without interface energy the
molar free energy is given by the convex hull curve
f_{0}^{**}
. Therefore, the maximal height difference
\Delta F_{int}
between
f_{0}
and
f_{0}^{**}
represents the interface energy.

1.3
Degeneracy of the sharp-interface limit and the scope of this work

We want to be explicit about the mathematical nature of case (ii), since it decides what can and what cannot be expected from any numerical method on a fixed mesh. As derived in Section
2.1
, the evolution of the mole fraction of a binary system is governed by
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\big(M\,\nabla f_{m}^{\prime}(\phi)\big)
with a positive mobility
M
, so that the effective diffusivity is
Mf_{m}^{\prime\prime}(\phi)
. For the convex hull
f_{m}=f_{0}^{**}
this diffusivity vanishes identically on the two-phase interval
[\phi_{\alpha},\phi_{\beta}]
, on which
f_{0}^{**}
is linear: the equation is
degenerate parabolic
. Its structure is that of the enthalpy formulation of the two-phase Stefan problem
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
,
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=\Delta\beta(\phi)
with a monotone
\beta=Mf_{0}^{**\prime}
that is constant on an interval: the mole fraction plays the part of the enthalpy, the affinity
-f_{0}^{**\prime}(\phi)
that of the temperature (up to sign, an additive constant and a constant factor), and the width
\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\alpha}
of the two-phase interval that of the latent heat. Inside the two-phase region there is no driving force; the mole fraction there changes only through the divergence of the fluxes that the adjacent bulk phases impose at its boundary, and across the interface the mole fraction jumps from
\phi_{\alpha}
to
\phi_{\beta}
while the affinity is continuous. This degeneracy sits in the free energy and is distinct from the degenerate
mobility
of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with
M=M(\phi)
vanishing in the pure phases
[
20
]
, with which it should not be confused.

We are well aware of this degeneracy. It is not a defect of a particular discretization but a property of the continuum model. Regularization (
\kappa>0
) removes it only by changing the free energy, at the price of an interface width that has no physical meaning for a convex free energy; without altering the free energy, it can be avoided only by abandoning the fixed-mesh, volumetric description, that is, by tracking the interface explicitly as a free boundary with a moving-mesh or front-tracking method
[
21
,
22
]
, in which the two-phase region has no volume and the mass balance across the interface is imposed as a jump condition. Such methods are not compatible with the scope of our work. Our aim is to couple diffusional phase transformations and damage to continuum mechanics, and a moving mesh would make the coupled problem too complicated. We therefore restrict ourselves to operating on the boundary of degeneracy: we accept the loss of regularity of the mole fraction at the phase boundary and ask only that the model conserves mass, closes the energy balance exactly, and enforces the second law of thermodynamics. Sections
2.2
and
2.5
show that these three properties survive the degeneracy at the continuous and at the discrete level, for every convex molar free energy, the convex hull included: degeneracy at a phase boundary does not prevent us from enforcing the physics. This is sufficient for what we are doing. A better understanding of the interface itself would require atomistic models; we restrict ourselves to the thermodynamic, that is, the continuum description.

Methodology

2.1
Affinity, chemical potential and thermodynamic consistency in the realm of a variational principle for multi-component systems

Consider a thermodynamic system that is composed of
n

distinct atomic components. The flux
\underline{j}_{i}
and the chemical potential
\mu_{i}
of component
i
are defined for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{j}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{L_{ik}}{T}\nabla\mu_{k}

(5)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial m_{i}}\Big)_{T,V,m_{j\neq i}}

(6)

Here,
m_{i}
is the number of moles of component
i
in a reference
volume element and
F
denotes the total free
energy in the same volume element.
L_{ik}
and
T
represent the
Onsager coefficients and the temperature respectively. This is the usual
form in which the laws of diffusion are presented in the literature, including in
[
23
]
.
Due to the second law of thermodynamics, the following condition must
hold at all times:

\xi:=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\underline{j}_{k}\cdot\nabla\big(\frac{\mu_{k}}{T}\big)\geq 0

(7)

\xi
is the rate of entropy production per unit volume. In a
non-equilibrium system it is greater than zero while it is equal to zero
in an equilibrium state. At constant temperature,
T\xi
is the rate of energy dissipation per unit volume, and we denote its integral over the system by
\mathcal{P}=\int_{V}T\xi\,dV
, the
dissipation
, which has units of power
[
9
]
. A system is said to be thermodynamically
consistent if the inequality
\xi\geq 0
is satisfied and if its
energy and mass are conserved. If a system is to be described by mole fractions

\phi_{i}
instead of moles we can make use of the following relations:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}

(8)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{i}}{m}

(9)

For the mole fractions the following condition must hold:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}=1

(10)

In this work, we focus on isothermal systems with constant molar volume, as in the Cahn-Hilliard model. We therefore impose:

\Omega,T=\text{const.}

(11)

Furthermore, we can make use of the following constraint:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underline{j}_{i}=\underline{0}

(12)

This equation is true in two cases: (I) For diffusion processes in
fluids and solids where all components have equal partial molar volumes

[
23
]
. The equation is then also known as “no
volume flow” constraint. (II) For diffusion of substitutional
components in crystalline solids, e.g. substitutional alloys

[
11
]
(also for different partial molar volumes

\mu_{i}=f_{m}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{ik}-\phi_{k})\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial\phi_{k}},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(13)

Here,

denotes a general molar free energy (convex or double-well or else) and

is the Kronecker Delta. Note that equation

Appendix

\frac{\partial c_{i}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}_{i}=0,\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(14)

where the concentration

of a component

c_{i}=\frac{\phi_{i}}{\Omega},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(15)

Since the system is defined in terms of mole fractions we can express

-th component in terms of the other

components by

\phi_{n}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\phi_{i}

(16)

Therefore, only
n-1
variables are needed to define the system
composition. For compactness, the collective mole fractions of the

as an array:

\{\phi\}:=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
.\\
.\\
\phi_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}

(17)

The same applies to the flux of the
n
-th component if equation (

modified:

\underline{j}_{n}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\underline{j}_{i}

(18)

By rearranging the definition of the volumetric rate of entropy

, we can explicitly express the last element of the

\xi=-\frac{1}{T}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\underline{j}_{k}\cdot\nabla\mu_{k}+\underline{j}_{n}\cdot\nabla\mu_{n}\Big)\geq 0

This gives rise to the opportunity of inserting the expression for the

-th component:

\xi=-\frac{1}{T}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\underline{j}_{k}\cdot\nabla\mu_{k}+\big(-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\underline{j}_{i}\big)\cdot\nabla\mu_{n}\Big)\geq 0

(20)

Since the two sums are equal except for the indices of chemical
potentials the inequality can be simplified:

\xi=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\underline{j}_{k}\cdot\big(\nabla\mu_{k}-\nabla\mu_{n}\big)\geq 0

By reviewing equations (

) and (
6

\underline{j}_{i}:=-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\tilde{L}_{ik}}{T}\big(\nabla\mu_{k}-\nabla\mu_{n}\big),\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

(22)

This ensures the positivity of the rate of entropy production:

\xi=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i,k=1}^{n-1}\tilde{L}_{ik}\big(\nabla\mu_{i}-\nabla\mu_{n}\big)\cdot\big(\nabla\mu_{k}-\nabla\mu_{n}\big)\Big)\geq 0

Note that the Onsager coefficient matrix was also changed from

\mu_{n}:=f_{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\phi_{k}\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial\phi_{k}}

Combining equation (

\bar{\mu}_{i}=-\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi_{i}}=-(\mu_{i}-\mu_{n})\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

\underline{j}_{k}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\tilde{L}_{ik}}{T}\nabla\big(\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi_{i}}\big),\quad\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

It is thus evident, as noted earlier, that reactive diffusion models and the Cahn-Hilliard model are fundamentally equivalent, just distinguishable by their definitions of the molar free energy

(see equations (

) - (

)). Considering a binary system and combining equations (

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\Big(\frac{\tilde{L}}{T}\nabla\Big(\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi}\Big)\Big)

2.2
The thermodynamic extremal principle and the Rayleighian

All equations defining the diffusion problem, (
14
), (
27
) and (
28
), follow from a single variational statement: the thermodynamic extremal principle (TEP). The idea goes back to Onsager’s principle of least dissipation of energy
[
5
]
and Ziegler’s principle of maximum rate of dissipation
[
6
]
, was related to dissipation potentials by Hackl and Fischer
[
27
]
, and has been systematized for materials science by Svoboda et al.
[
7
,
8
]
; see also
[
26
]
for phase-field models and
[
28
,
29
]
for the same principle in soft matter and fluid mechanics, where it is known as the Onsager variational principle. We follow the formulation of
[
9
]
, where the extremal properties of the principle are examined, and use its nomenclature.

Consider a closed, isothermal system with the free energy
\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{V}f_{m}\,dV
of equation (
1
). The TEP requires the rate of the free energy. For a crystalline solid whose lattice is at rest the control volume is fixed in space, and the Reynolds transport theorem reduces to the chain rule:

\dot{\mathcal{F}}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{V}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi_{k}}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\;dV=-\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{V}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\bar{\mu}_{k}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\;dV\,.

(30)

The rate is linear in the rates
\partial\phi_{k}/\partial t
, and the coefficients, the driving forces
\delta f_{m}/\delta\phi_{k}=-\bar{\mu}_{k}
, are state functions: they are evaluated at the given state
\{\phi\}
and are not varied. (For
\kappa>0
the variational derivative in (
30
) arises from the chain rule after an integration by parts with
\nabla\phi_{k}\cdot\underline{n}=0
on
\partial V
; Section
2.5
uses the directional form, which needs no boundary condition.) The dissipation
\mathcal{P}=\int_{V}T\xi\,dV=T\int_{V}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n-1}\tilde{L}^{-1}_{ik}\,\underline{j}_{i}\cdot\underline{j}_{k}\;dV
is a positive-definite quadratic form in the fluxes, with
\tilde{L}^{-1}_{ik}
the kinetic resistance, and the energy balance of the closed system reads
\dot{\mathcal{F}}+\mathcal{P}=0
; the second law enters through
\mathcal{P}\geq 0
. The TEP selects, among all fluxes compatible with this balance, those that render the dissipation stationary. As shown in
[
9
]

(32)

with

. Following
[
9
]
, script letters denote densities and calligraphic letters the functionals of the whole system. The first term of (

[

]
. Four rules make the construction unambiguous

\underline{j}_{k}
and the multipliers
\bar{\mu}_{k}
are varied, at the current instant, and no temporal Euler–Lagrange equations are applied; (c) the state
\{\phi\}
is given, so that the driving forces and the Onsager coefficients are evaluated at it and are not varied; (d) driving forces that contain accelerations are held fixed during the variation. Rule (d) is not needed here: diffusion is inertialess, the driving forces are state functions, and nothing has to be frozen by hand, which is the situation in which the TEP is most elegant.

Stationarity of

with respect to

The stationary point is a minimum with respect to the fluxes (and, through the constraint, the rates) and a maximum with respect to the multipliers, that is, a saddle point of the augmented functional, as for every Lagrange multiplier. The Rayleighian (

A detailed derivation of the principle, as presented in equation (

The concept is also particularly useful for exploring coupled problems of reactive diffusion and continuum mechanics

2.3
Correlation of diffusion coefficients and Onsager’s coefficients

A question that often arises and is of great interest when applying phase-field methods or reactive diffusion methods is to find the dependence of Onsager’s coefficients
\tilde{L}_{ik}
on experimentally measured diffusion coefficients
D_{i}
for all
i,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This is, however, not a trivial task and can hardly be answered in the general case. Fortunately, the investigated cases and the introduced methodology offer the opportunity to formulate
f_{m}
in a specific way that allows straightforward relations between
\tilde{L}_{ik}
and
D_{i}
. This is because both a double well and its convex counterpart can be constructed from quadratic functions that represent the free energies of individual phases. For example, the molar free energy of phase
\alpha
can be represented as (see also Figure
1
):

f_{0}^{\alpha}(\phi):=f_{\alpha}+\frac{k}{2}(\phi-\phi_{eq}^{\alpha})^{2}

(33)

Here,
f_{\alpha}
represents the molar free energy of phase
\alpha
in equilibrium composition (that is,
\phi=\phi_{eq}^{\alpha}
) and
k
controls the curvature of its convex energy. In general
f_{\alpha}
is dependent on temperature and volume (
T,V
) and can be calculated from heat capacity and mechanical properties of a material, but will be considered constant in the present work. This simple quadratic dependency is necessary to give a straightforward dependency on the diffusion coefficients since measuring diffusion coefficients often utilizes Fick’s law of diffusion
[
34
]
. Therefore, it is required that Fick’s law and the thermodynamic definition of flux lead to the same result. For a binary system this means, with
\bar{\mu}=-\partial f_{m}/\partial\phi
according to (
26
):

\underline{j}:=\frac{\tilde{L}}{T}\nabla\bar{\mu}=-\frac{\tilde{L}}{T}\nabla\Big(\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial\phi}\Big)=-\tilde{D}\nabla c

(34)

Here
\tilde{D}
denotes the inter-diffusion coefficient of components 1 and 2. The relation further simplifies to:

\frac{\tilde{L}}{T}\nabla\Big(\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial\phi}\Big)=\tilde{D}\nabla\Big(\frac{\phi}{\Omega}\Big)

(35)

If
f_{m}
is constructed from quadratic functions, the following simplification holds at least in the different bulk phases but not at interfaces:

\frac{\tilde{L}}{T}\nabla\Big(k\cdot(\phi-\phi_{eq})\Big)=\tilde{D}\nabla\Big(\frac{\phi}{\Omega}\Big)

(36)

Since applying derivatives to constants yields zero, a straightforward relation can be found:

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{L}k}{T}\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{D}}{\Omega}\nabla\phi

(37)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{D}T}{k\Omega}

(38)

Obviously, the relation can be criticized for relating the Onsager coefficient
\tilde{L}
correctly only in the bulk phases but not at interfaces, where
f_{m}
is a linear function for a convex potential, and not a quadratic function. However, during simulation, the domain is dominated by bulk phases, and the interfaces only amount to a small fraction, which underscores the potential of this simplification. This is especially true for case (ii), where the representation of the interface is very sharp. Moreover, interface regions in materials science are known for their increased diffusional activity, which could be argued to be captured by this methodology. Additionally, it is worth noting the influence of the parameter
k
. Its role in the construction of the molar free energy
f_{m}
is clear, but since it appears in the Onsager coefficient, it effectively loses its impact in determining the overall kinetics of the model. It can therefore be viewed as a phenomenological parameter that should be chosen to obtain good convergence of the simulation but has no direct consequence for the system’s evolution.

2.4
Scaling the Rayleighian

In the context of PDEs and simulation,
scaling
refers to the process of expressing a physical problem in a canonical form
[
35
]
. This may be achieved by viewing the equations in a non-dimensional setting, changing the unit of field variables and/or formulating the problem with the least possible number of physical parameters. Since a variational description of the system was chosen, the change of dimension is applied at the same fundamental level. Consider the Rayleighian of a binary system:

\mathscr{R}(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t},\underline{j},\bar{\mu}):=\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{T}{2\tilde{L}}|\underline{j}|^{2}+\bar{\mu}\Big(\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}\Big)

(39)

Utilizing the derived dependency of the Onsager coefficient from equation (
37
) yields the following expression:

\mathscr{R}(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t},\underline{j},\bar{\mu}):=\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{k\Omega}{2\tilde{D}}|\underline{j}|^{2}+\bar{\mu}\Big(\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}\Big)

(40)

By reviewing the equations that are obtained by stationarity with respect to
\underline{j}
and
\bar{\mu}
, it is evident that the Rayleighian holds potential for simplification:

\displaystyle\underline{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{D}}{k\Omega}\nabla\bar{\mu}

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\underline{j}

(42)

If equation (
41
) and (
42
) are combined into a second order equation, it can be seen that if the molar volume
\Omega
is constant, it can be dropped from the equations entirely:

\frac{1}{\Omega}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\Big(\frac{\tilde{D}}{k\Omega}\nabla\bar{\mu}\Big)=\nabla\cdot\Big(\frac{\tilde{D}}{k\Omega}\nabla\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi}\Big)

(43)

The same applies for the variation of the Rayleighian with respect to
\partial\phi/\partial t
. To this end the scaled Rayleighian for a binary system is introduced:

\mathscr{R}(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t},\underline{j},\bar{\mu}):=\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac{k}{2\tilde{D}}|\underline{j}|^{2}+\bar{\mu}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}\Big)

(44)

Note that the unit of flux is changed in the scaled Rayleighian from
[\frac{mol}{m^{2}s}]

[\frac{m}{s}]
. Therefore the vector field
\underline{j}
could also be referred to as velocity or drift velocity of the atomic components in the scaled Rayleighian. This ensures that the mole fraction and the chemical affinity can remain in their respective standard unit. In the scaled form, the dissipation is
\mathcal{P}=\int_{V}\frac{k}{\tilde{D}}|\underline{j}|^{2}\,dV
, and the second term of (
44
) is one half of its density, as in (
31
).

2.5

For simulation purposes the Rayleighian (

To illustrate the process in a straightforward manner, we consider a binary system, and we write

Relation to the incremental minimization.

Forms (

2.6
A ternary system with sources and sinks for vacancies

Finally, we want to give an example of how to implement a specific model
within the presented methodology by considering the vacancy diffusion
model derived by Svoboda in
[
10
]
. The concept of
“vacancy” refers to empty or vacant sites in a crystal lattice, which
are crucial for enabling diffusion in alloys. By allowing atoms to
switch positions with vacant sites, vacancies facilitate the movement of
atoms within an alloy. Since vacancies are merely empty sites in a
crystal lattice, it is essential to recognize that they are purely
geometric features and do not constitute matter. This distinction is
important, as it permits us to treat vacancies as a non-conserved
component, allowing for more nuanced modeling of diffusion processes.
Therefore Svoboda suggested treating a binary system with vacancies like
a ternary system (with vacancies constituting the third component). The
composition of the system is then defined by so-called site fractions
instead of mole fractions, which refer to the ratio of the amount of a
component to the number of available lattice sites in the
atomic grid. We begin by defining the free energy of the system
which will just be a superposition of the convex hull from Figure
1
,
namely
f_{0}^{**}
and a simple quadratic contribution of the site
fraction of vacancies
\phi_{0}
:

\mathcal{F}[\phi_{0},\phi_{1}]:=\int_{V}f_{m}\;dV=\int_{V}\Big(f_{0}^{**}(\phi_{1})+\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi_{1}|^{2}+\tfrac{k_{0}}{2}\cdot(\phi_{0}-\phi_{0}^{eq})^{2}\Big)\;dV

(53)

As can be seen, the free energy will consist only of the molar free
energy of the equilibrium system
f_{0}^{**}
if the vacancies
\phi_{0}

assume their equilibrium site fraction
\phi_{0}^{eq}
. A perfect crystal
without vacancies and dislocations may possess less internal energy than
one with imperfections; however, the presence of imperfections increases
the configurational entropy of the crystal, thereby reducing the free
energy. The equilibrium site fraction of vacancies marks the minimum of
the overall free energy of the system with respect to vacancies. The dissipation, one half of which enters the Rayleighian (
31
), is defined according to
[
10
]
by the following functional:

\mathcal{P}[\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\psi]:=\int_{V}\mathscr{P}\;dV=\int_{V}\Big(\frac{k_{0}}{D_{0}}|\underline{j}_{0}|^{2}+\frac{k_{1}}{D_{1}}|\underline{j}_{1}|^{2}+\frac{k_{2}}{D_{2}}|-\underline{j}_{0}-\underline{j}_{1}|^{2}+\frac{1}{A_{\psi}}\psi^{2}\Big)\;dV

(54)

It is worth noting that the diffusion coefficients of individual components,

D_{i}
, are utilized in this formulation. Furthermore, through the definition of the flux of component 2, given by equation (
18
) as
j_{2}:=-j_{0}-j_{1}
, the fluxes become intertwined according to Onsager’s theory. This coupling not only constrains the system and enforces the vacancy mechanism but also gives rise to non-diagonal entries in the resulting Onsager coefficient matrix of the system.
The Onsager coefficients can be calculated as follows (in the scaled form of Section
2.4
; in the unscaled form a factor
T
appears):

\tilde{L}_{ik}=\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{P}}{\partial\underline{j}_{i}\partial\underline{j}_{k}}\Big)_{T,V,\{\phi\}}\Big]^{-1},\quad\forall i,k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

(55)

Consequently, the fluxes are coupled, and a driving force for one component can induce fluxes of other components, as predicted by Onsager’s reciprocal relations.
The function
\psi
is characteristic of this
model and represents the rate of generation and annihilation of
vacancies. Since vacancies are a non-conserved component they can emerge
and vanish in the bulk to facilitate diffusion. Their evolution in
time is therefore also not determined by a pure conservation equation but
rather a transport-type equation. The evolution equations are considered
in the constraint functional
\mathcal{C}=\int_{V}\mathscr{C}\;dV
, whose density in the rate form of Section
2.2
reads, with the coefficients of
\psi
evaluated at the given state (rule (c)),

\mathscr{C}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t},\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1},\psi\Big):=\\
\bar{\mu}_{0}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}_{0}-\psi\cdot(1-\phi_{0})\Big)+\bar{\mu}_{1}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}_{1}+\psi\cdot\phi_{1}\Big).

(56)

As can be seen, the rate of annihilation and generation of vacancies

\psi
not only impacts the evolution equation of vacancies but also
of component
\phi_{1}
. This is because an increase of vacancies also
increases the number of available sites in the lattice which impacts all
site fractions. Therefore, the generation of vacancies also causes a
strain and a change in the molar volume
\Omega
. Strictly, a lattice whose number of sites changes is no longer at rest, and rule (a) of Section
2.2
would call for the material form of the transport theorem together with the mechanical treatment of the problem. This was
nevertheless neglected since it would also require the mechanical
treatment of the problem, which is beyond the scope of this publication.
The final problem is defined by its Rayleighian density, the rate of the free energy (
53
) in the direction of the rates, plus one half of the dissipation, plus the constraint:

\mathscr{R}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t},\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1},\psi\Big):=\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial\phi_{0}}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t}+\frac{\delta f_{m}}{\delta\phi_{1}}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{P}(\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\psi)\\
+\mathscr{C}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t},\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1},\psi\Big)

(57)

The variational form is determined by computing the first variation with respect to the rates
\partial\phi_{0}/\partial t
,
\partial\phi_{1}/\partial t
, the fluxes
\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1}
, the multipliers
\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1}
and the rate
\psi
, at the given state
(\phi_{0},\phi_{1})
:

\displaystyle\delta\int_{V}\mathscr{R}\Big(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial t},\underline{j}_{0},\underline{j}_{1},\bar{\mu}_{0},\bar{\mu}_{1},\psi\Big)\;dV=0.

(58)

After the variation, the time discretization of Section
2.5
is imposed: the rates are replaced by the backward difference quotients
(\phi_{0}-\phi_{0}^{t})/\Delta t
and
(\phi_{1}-\phi_{1}^{t})/\Delta t
, the driving forces are evaluated at the new state, and the coefficients
1-\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{1}
of
\psi
are taken at the previous time level, so that the discrete constraint equations read

\int_{V}\Big(\frac{\phi_{0}-\phi_{0}^{t}}{\Delta t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}_{0}-\psi\,(1-\phi_{0}^{t})\Big)\hat{\bar{\mu}}_{0}\;dV=0,\qquad\int_{V}\Big(\frac{\phi_{1}-\phi_{1}^{t}}{\Delta t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{j}_{1}+\psi\,\phi_{1}^{t}\Big)\hat{\bar{\mu}}_{1}\;dV=0\qquad\forall\hat{\bar{\mu}}_{0},\hat{\bar{\mu}}_{1}.

(59)

2.7
Variable diffusion coefficients

It is commonly observed that the components of an alloy exhibit different diffusion coefficients in different phases. The diffusion coefficients
D_{i}
, or more generally the Onsager coefficients
\tilde{L}_{ik}
, may therefore be chosen phase-dependent, that is, as functions of the mole fraction
\phi
. This requires no special treatment. The Rayleighian is varied with respect to the rates, the fluxes and the multipliers at the given state (rule (c) of Section
2.2
), so that
\phi
is not touched by the variation, and coefficients depending on it enter the stationarity conditions, and the discrete forms (
49
)–(
51
), simply as given functions of the state. In the implicit scheme they may be evaluated at the new state or, for simplicity, at the previous time level.

4
Discussion

4.1
Operating on the boundary of degeneracy

The computations are consistent with the picture of Section
1.3
. For the convex hull without regularization the mole fraction develops a jump, the affinity stays continuous, and everything that happens inside the two-phase region is dictated by the fluxes that the bulk phases impose at its boundary. The scheme does not resolve the interface as a free boundary; it resolves it within a few elements, and the price for this is the indeterminacy of the mole fraction inside the degenerate region, visible as the fluctuations of Figure
3
and as the slightly irregular contour of Figure
9
. This is the behavior known from the enthalpy formulation of the Stefan problem on a fixed mesh
[
18
,
19
]
, in which the position of the interface is recovered only to the accuracy of a mesh cell and a mushy region of positive measure is consumed from its boundaries. The alternative, a sharp-interface or moving-mesh method
[
16
,
21
,
22
]
, removes the degeneracy by construction, at the cost of a mesh that follows the interface, with remeshing, topology changes and field transfer at every step. For the problems we have in mind, diffusional phase transformations coupled to damage and to continuum mechanics in geometries with many inclusions, this cost is prohibitive, and a fixed mesh is the only practical choice.

What the fixed-mesh description retains is the physics. The scheme conserves mass exactly and satisfies the discrete energy–dissipation inequality of Proposition
1
for every convex molar free energy, the convex hull included: the free energy decreases by at least the energy dissipated in each step, and the balance closes exactly once the numerical dissipation of the backward Euler step is accounted for. Neither statement requires the equation to be parabolic, and neither is affected by the degeneracy at the phase boundary. This is the sense in which we operate on the boundary of degeneracy, and it is sufficient for what we are doing: the quantities a thermodynamic model is accountable for, mass, energy and entropy production, are under control, while the fine structure of the interface is not resolved. A more detailed description of the interface would have to come from atomistic models, which lie outside the thermodynamic, continuum description to which we restrict ourselves. We note that the regularized cases (i) and the Cahn–Hilliard model are not degenerate, and that the scheme treats all three cases with the same code; the choice between them is a modeling decision about the interface energy, not a numerical one.

4.2
On the choice of the finite element spaces

The variational forms (
49
)–(
51
) leave the finite element spaces open, and two choices are natural. Both satisfy Proposition
1
, and both have been implemented with the same code, the only differences being the element definitions and the facet-based discretization of the gradient energy. The equal-order continuous spaces used for the figures of this work are desirable for the coupling to continuum mechanics in established finite element frameworks such as ABAQUS, which provide continuous elements only, and they give a smooth representation of the two-dimensional interfaces; their stability for the Darcy-type subproblem rests on the time-derivative term, as discussed in Section
2.5
. The inf-sup stable choice for the dual mixed form is the Raviart–Thomas space for the flux (RTCF on quadrilaterals) together with piecewise constant spaces for the mole fraction and the affinity
[
36
,
38
]
, in which the flux boundary condition
\underline{j}\cdot\underline{n}=0
is imposed on the normal component only, the mass balance holds element-wise, the gradient energy is approximated by a jump penalty on the element facets, and the mole fraction is allowed to be discontinuous, which is the regularity the degenerate case actually has. We regard this choice as the future of the method: it is conservative in the strict, element-wise sense, its flux is a genuine
H(\mathrm{div})
field, and it is the natural starting point for a moving-mesh extension, in which the mass balance across a moving facet is again a flux jump condition; the coupling of a Raviart–Thomas flux to a continuous displacement field is standard in poroelasticity
[
46
]
. Table

The mixed pair gives the sharpest and, at the end, perfectly monotone interface, but the largest fluctuations during the transient: without any gradient control of the piecewise constant mole fraction, the indeterminacy of the degenerate region is fully exposed, whereas the continuity of the equal-order spaces damps it. Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a novel methodology for mathematically
modeling diffusional phase transformations with and without interface energy. By relating our
approach to limiting cases of the Cahn-Hilliard model, we demonstrate
the versatility and potential of our method in handling complex phase
transformations. The method is derived from the thermodynamic extremal principle: the evolution equations are the stationarity conditions of the Rayleighian, the rate of the free energy plus one half of the dissipation, with respect to the rates, fluxes and multipliers at a frozen state, and the time-discrete scheme is the corresponding incremental minimization. The incorporation and explicit solving for
rate-dependent variables like the flux allows us to avoid differentiating the affinity
and to overcome limitations imposed by traditional numerical techniques. We have made explicit that the convex hull without regularization, the CALPHAD limit, is a degenerate parabolic problem of Stefan type, that the degeneracy can be removed only by a moving mesh, which is incompatible with our aim of coupling diffusional phase transformations and damage to continuum mechanics, and that operating on the boundary of degeneracy is nevertheless sound: the scheme conserves mass exactly and satisfies a discrete energy–dissipation inequality, the second law, for every convex free energy and for every choice of conforming finite element spaces.
Our approach to diffusional phase transformations and the phase-field
method provides a novel finite element
approach, offering a promising framework for investigating these
phenomena. We also demonstrated the thermodynamic consistency of the
reactive diffusion models and the phase-field method in general and
