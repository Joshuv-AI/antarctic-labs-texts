Correspondence: circadian melatonin rhythms in critically ill patients with delirium
Authors: Danielle Hunziker, Tatjana Zehnder, Madlaina Forster, Anna Estermann, Núria Zellweger, Jan Huber, Andrea Blum, Salim Abdelhamid, Fenna Stolte, Lua Maria Mengisen, Christian Cajochen, Vitaliy Kolodyazhniy, Martin Siegemund, Alexa Hollinger
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Delirium is a highly prevalent neuropsychological condition in patients treated in Intensive Care Units (ICU), with delirium rates as high as 80%. Occurrence of delirium in critically ill patients is associated with longer hospitalization, increased mortality and long-term cognitive impairment [1]. Despite intense ongoing research evidence for drug prevention or treatment is limited.

Many symptoms of delirium are closely associated with circadian rhythm disruption. Delirious patients often experience nocturnal sleep fragmentation and disruption, daytime somnolence and sleep-wake cycle reversal [2]. The neurohormone melatonin plays a pivotal role in regulating these rhythms. Research concerning delirium and alterations in circadian melatonin plasma levels in ICU patients is sparse and applied methods are heterogeneous [3–5]. Our study therefore focused on the precise analysis of circadian melatonin rhythms in delirious compared to non-delirious ICU patients in order to understand under what circumstances melatonin treatment may work as delirium prevention and treatment.

Due to its rapid metabolization, plasma melatonin levels must be frequently obtained to get a precise proxy for current secretion and the individual circadian timing system [6]. We therefore measured melatonin plasma levels every two hours over a 24-hour period in delirious patients, diagnosed using Intensive care delirium screening checklist (ICDSC), non-delirious patients treated in a mixed ICU of a tertiary care center. Baseline characteristics were comparable among groups with median age of the study population of 74 years and 45% women. For analysis 23 delirious, and 26 non-delirious patients were included (Supplementary Fig. 1). Delirious patients were age-matched (+/- 5 years) and/or Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score-matched (+/- 2 points) with non-delirious ICU patients.

For time series analysis of circadian melatonin secretion profiles, a bimodal skewed baseline cosine function (BSBCF) curve [7] (Supplementary Fig. 2) was fitted to the data using a modified cost function proposed in Gabel et al. [8]. We analysed Dim-light Melatonin Onset, Offset, each determined by applying a threshold of 0.25 of the amplitude, Midpoint Time, representing the average time between Melatonin Onset and Offset, and Amplitude. Furthermore, we tested for differences in variance of Melatonin Onset between delirious and non-delirious patients.

In order to prevent simply reflecting the model fitting rather than physiological timing, we introduced the R2 > = 0.7 threshold for goodness of fit and excluded individuals which did not pass the R2 threshold check. This resulted in the exclusion of 7 individuals (Supplementary Fig. 1; Table 1). A detailed analysis of all data points revealed a typical diurnal profile of melatonin levels in both delirious and non-delirious patients (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, melatonin profiles of delirious patients differed significantly from those of non-delirious patients with a delayed Melatonin Onset (23.8 (SD 2.4) vs. 22.5 (SD 1.6), p = 0.035) and Midpoint Time (mean midpoint time 5.2 (SD 2.1) vs. 3.9 (SD 1.6), p = 0.023; Fig. 1). Regarding Melatonin Offset (p = 0.054) and Amplitude (p = 0.10), there was no significant difference between delirious and non-delirious patients (Fig. 1). Tests of differences in the variance of Dim-light Melatonin Onset revealed greater variance among delirious patients than among non-delirious patients (F(1, 40) = 3.77, p = 0.059, Levene’s test). Of note, higher SOFA score was associated with a later melatonin midpoint time (r = 0.37, p = 0.018) in all patients (Supplementary Fig. 4).

These findings must be interpreted with caution given the relatively small number of patients analysed. Patients were SOFA score-matched, but past and current medical history varied as in all critically ill patient populations, which was not considered in the matching. Thus, the patient population remains heterogeneous. Furthermore, the correlation between higher SOFA score and delayed Midpoint time suggests that changes in circadian rhythm in critically ill patients is also influenced by the severity of illness, and that severe illness influences melatonin secretion independent from development of delirium. In addition, repeated nocturnal sampling may have disrupted patients’ sleep despite central venous or arterial line facilitating blood withdrawal in most cases. However, the ICU environment may in general be stressful for patients.

Nevertheless, by measuring plasma melatonin levels every two hours our findings examined melatonin rhythms with great granularity. Furthermore, using the BSBCF approach, an advanced mathematical approach to the quantification of the full 24 h melatonin profile based on curve fitting and nonlinear optimization, allowed us to extract and compare more parameters in addition to the usual Dim-light Melatonin Onset approach. Dim-Light Melatonin Onset is the most widely used circadian phase marker based on melatonin secretion but it is more prone to variability than Midpoint time as the former takes into account only the rising slope of the melatonin secretion profile. In contrast, our approach with melatonin Midpoint time yields a more robust marker than the onset alone as it is based on both the onset and offset times of the full melatonin secretion profile. Thus, a midpoint-based estimation aims to reduce variability as compared to the conventional Dim-Light Melatonin Onset approach. Our findings show significantly altered circadian melatonin rhythm in delirious compared to non-delirious patients treated in a mixed ICU. These findings suggest that a timed intake of melatonin or its analogues may be used to normalize circadian rhythms and potentially prevent or treat delirium. However, respective results are inconsistent and warrant further research.
