Research on intelligent pharmacy unmanned delivery vehicle path planning and drug recognition technology based on JPS and RRT algorithms
Authors: Hengzhe Yue, Kun Cao, Zhaokun Yang, Quntao An, Lizhi Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper is set in the context of an intelligent pharmacy working environment, where we optimize the conventional path planning algorithms for existing drug-dispensing robots. By integrating the Jump Point Search algorithm with the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree Star algorithm, we enhance the accuracy of robot navigation and positioning.Utilizing CNN to fit the map images and setting thresholds to eliminate errors, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of the map; optimizing the path algorithm to prevent local optima and deadlocks. We introduce a contour-based drug recognition method that uses the SAD algorithm for accurate identification of drug types and positions, ensuring high precision and efficiency during delivery. This ensures high precision and efficiency in drug recognition during the delivery process. This method improves planning time by 82% over A*, enhances path quality by 25% over Dijkstra, and reduces CPU usage by 68% compared with RRT*, while maintaining strong adaptability in dynamic environments. For drug recognition, the improved SAD algorithm lowers the mismatch rate from 27.33% (SAD) and 25.92% (SSD) to 15.12% on a 500-image dataset collected under mixed indoor lighting, achieving a 12.21% absolute gain and reducing recognition time by about 7 seconds. These values were measured on a 500-image pharmacy dataset collected under mixed indoor lighting.

Introduction

The ongoing intelligent transformation of logistics and supply chains has created a strong demand for autonomous mobile robots1–3. A particularly promising application is within the modern intelligent pharmacy, where unmanned delivery vehicles are increasingly deployed to handle critical tasks such as transporting medicines and organizing goods4,5. In such environments, efficient and reliable drug delivery is paramount and hinges on precise robotic path planning and accurate drug identification6.

In the practical operational context of intelligent pharmacies, path planning for unmanned delivery vehicles confronts distinct and stringent challenges, rendering many general-purpose algorithms hardly applicable directly. First, pharmacy layouts typically encompass regular shelf aisles (highly structured) yet also involve low-frequency, poorly predictable dynamic activities from medical staff and mobile devices.This coexistence of environmental structuredness and dynamism causes pure static planners to be highly prone to path failure when encountering dynamic obstacles. Conversely, pure dynamic planners (e.g. RRT*) exhibit low efficiency in random sampling within wide, regular aisles, with planning times as long as 2.3 seconds, failing to meet real-time requirements. Second, to control costs and power consumption, systems are usually deployed on embedded platforms like Raspberry Pi, necessitating algorithms to operate stably within limited CPU and memory resources;complex algorithms or frequent global replanning can rapidly exhaust computing resources.

Furthermore, there exists a sharp trade-off between path quality and safety: paths must not only be near-optimal in length but also sufficiently smooth to ensure stable vehicle operation, while maintaining safe distances from both static shelves and dynamic obstacles at all times–a contradiction that is particularly pronounced in narrow aisles.Thus, the core problem addressed in this study is: How to design a path planning method for pharmacy unmanned delivery vehicles running on low-power embedded platforms, enabling them to adaptively integrate the efficiency of global planning with the flexibility of local planning in complex environments where structure and dynamism coexist, thereby achieving a comprehensive balance of short planning time, high path quality, low resource consumption, and strong anti-interference capability.

This project combines the Jump Point Search(JPS) algorithm7 for global static path planning and the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree Star(RRT*) algorithm8 for local dynamic adjustments, dynamically switching between them based on the environment. The switching is triggered primarily by real-time sensor data: when a dynamic obstacle is detected within a 2-meter radius of the vehicle’s current path segment, or when the local obstacle density exceeds 30%, the system shifts from JPS guided global planning to RRT* based local optimization.

This integration enhances the robot’s navigation and positioning accuracy while incorporating deep learning techniques to optimize delivery strategies and efficiency, thereby improving overall performance and avoiding issues like local path optimality and local latency. Furthermore, to enhance the delivery system’s accuracy, this project introduces drug recognition technology, employing the Sum of Absolute Differences (SAD) template matching algorithm based on drug contour recognition9. This ensures precision and efficiency in drug identification and placement during delivery, enabling more intelligent unmanned pharmacy management.

The JPS-RRT* hybrid mechanism proposed in this study is novel in that it designs a bidirectional complementary interaction paradigm based on environmental features. Unlike the common simple concatenation approach where the global planning result is used as the initial seed for the local planner, this mechanism achieves deep collaboration through dynamic role switching: in structured environments, the JPS algorithm takes the lead in generating the global path skeleton and intelligently guides the sampling space of RRT* with its path points, significantly enhancing the search efficiency; in unstructured areas or when encountering dynamic obstacles, the RRT algorithm takes over the lead to optimize the local trajectory and avoid obstacles, and feeds back the optimized feasible path information to the JPS module for global path evaluation. This JPS provides intelligent guidance for RRT*, and RRT* provides feasibility complementation for JPS bidirectional interaction mechanism effectively overcomes the limitations of single algorithms or master-slave hybrid frameworks, achieving a better balance among planning efficiency, path quality, and environmental adaptability.

The main contributions of this work are threefold. Firstly, we propose a hybrid JPS-RRT* path planning algorithm that demonstrates superior efficiency and adaptability in pharmacy-like environments. Secondly, we develop a contour-enhanced drug recognition method based on an improved SAD algorithm, achieving high speed and accuracy on low-power devices. Thirdly, we provide comprehensive experimental validation in both simulated and physical environments, verifying the practical effectiveness of the proposed system.

Related work

Path planning techniques

Current path planning algorithms are primarily divided into global and local path planning categories10. Global path planning algorithms can be further classified into static-oriented and dynamic-oriented algorithms based on environmental states, or traditional and intelligent algorithms based on whether perception and decision-making are separated.

Traditional path planning algorithms are mainly based on graph search and geometric models. The classic Dijkstra11 and A*12 algorithms achieve shortest path search by traversing nodes, but their computational complexity grows exponentially with environmental scale. Their efficiency is significantly influenced by heuristic function design. Although these algorithms have been studied for decades, their quantitative efficiency differences in complex scenarios require further investigation.

The jump point search (JPS) algorithm improves search efficiency by pruning redundant nodes. However, these methods struggle with continuous spaces and unstructured environments7. Sampling-based algorithms like RRT* and Probabilistic Roadmap (PRM) demonstrate flexibility in high-dimensional spaces (e.g. robotic arm motion planning) through probabilistic modeling and path connection mechanisms, but their paths exhibit strong randomness and insufficient convergence speed8,13,14. Subsequent improved algorithms like RRT* approach optimal paths through progressive optimization strategies, but computational costs still limit their practical applications.

In common static planning environments, Dijkstra and A* remain the primary algorithms. This project tests these traditional algorithms in both static and dynamic environments, collecting and comparing their efficiency. After testing both, we use an improved JPS-RRT* algorithm for testing in the same environment and compare its efficiency and accuracy.

D* algorithm

The D* algorithm (Dynamic A) is a well-known incremental search algorithm designed for dynamic environments. Its core advantage lies in efficiently repairing paths when obstacle positions change, by incrementally updating costs and propagating these changes from the goal node backwards (reverse search), thus avoiding complete replanning from scratch. This makes D particularly suitable for scenarios with frequent, unpredictable obstacles.

However, in the context of a structured pharmacy environment with predominantly static layouts and sparse, low-frequency dynamic obstacles (e.g. occasional staff movement), D’s general-purpose incremental repair mechanism may introduce unnecessary computational overhead. In contrast, the JPS-RRT* hybrid algorithm proposed in this work adopts a different strategy: it leverages the efficiency of JPS for global planning in the static parts of the map and strategically invokes RRT* only for local replanning around detected dynamic obstacles or in complex areas. This approach is specifically tailored to environments where the global map is largely static and known, allowing for more efficient use of computational resources on embedded platforms like the Raspberry Pi. Our experimental results in Sect. Testing process for Dijkstra and A* Algorithms

By establishing fixed motion paths, we tested the operational efficiency of both algorithms, including their turning frequency at corners. Simultaneously, we monitored various loads on the Raspberry Pi. Finally, we compared whether both algorithms could accurately reach specified target points under identical positioning conditions. The experimental scenario is shown in the SLAM mapping in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Experimental scenario.

Experimental scenario.

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) enables autonomous vehicles to operate in unknown environments, building environmental maps while self-localizing. Autonomous vehicles estimate their positions using multiple sensors while updating their positions and orientations based on grid maps19. Common SLAM mapping algorithms include Cartographer and Gmapping. However, Cartographer is typically used for large-scale environment modeling and requires high computational resources. Given our project’s application in smart pharmacy environments, the Gmapping algorithm is more suitable. Gmapping relies on lidar and odometry to obtain environmental pose information, optimizes particle distribution, and introduces an adaptive resampling mechanism, improving algorithm efficiency20. Therefore, we ultimately selected Gmapping for mapping.

However, single mapping sessions often produce maps with excessive noise and inaccuracies in certain obstacles and corners. To address this, we introduced deep learning to automatically match and correct the generated maps. Through the vehicle’s dynamic tracking capability, we achieved an artificial teaching effect, enabling autonomous tracking and map building. Subsequently, we used deep learning to match overlapping sections from multiple recorded map datasets as the actual modeling map. After validation, the accuracy of maps trained multiple times reached 95%-99%.

Drug recognition techniques

Evolution and limitations of traditional image processing methods

Pharmaceutical image recognition technology serves as a critical component in intelligent pharmaceutical systems, demonstrating a clear technological evolution from traditional image processing methods to deep learning approaches. Early research primarily focused on conventional image processing techniques that relied on handcrafted feature extractors. Among these, template matching technology represents one of the most significant approaches, operating on the fundamental principle of calculating similarity between target images and predefined templates for identification. While this study employs the Sum of Absolute Differences (SAD) algorithm, the Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) algorithm has been widely adopted in drug recognition scenarios due to its robustness to illumination variations21. Concurrently, algorithms such as Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)22 and Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF)23 have been utilized to handle tablet shape and marking matching tasks by extracting local keypoint features, partially addressing scale and rotation variations. However, these traditional methods generally suffer from inherent limitations including sensitivity to image quality and computational complexity that scales linearly with template quantity, presenting significant challenges in real-time embedded applications.

Breakthroughs and challenges in deep learning technologies

Parallel to traditional methods, deep learning-based pharmaceutical recognition technologies have emerged with breakthrough improvements in identification accuracy under ideal conditions through end-to-end feature learning mechanisms. Two-stage detection algorithms (e.g. the R-CNN series)24 demonstrate exceptional precision in offline quality inspection scenarios through their cascaded pipeline of region proposal generation and refined classification, though their complex computational workflows struggle to meet real-time requirements. In contrast, single-stage detection algorithms (such as YOLO and SSD)25,26 frame object detection as a regression problem, achieving superior balance between speed and accuracy, making them preferred solutions for real-time sorting systems. Notably, the recent emergence of lightweight network architectures (e.g. combinations of MobileNet and YOLO)27 has created new possibilities for embedded device deployment. Nevertheless, the advantages of deep learning methods remain highly dependent on large-scale annotated datasets, while maintaining high-frame-rate inference under extremely resource-constrained environments continues to pose practical challenges.

Innovative positioning and engineering trade-offs of this study

Within this technological spectrum, the improved SAD method proposed in this study establishes a unique positioning: it does not pursue ultimate accuracy in general scenarios but rather offers a specialized optimization solution tailored for structured environments such as intelligent pharmacies. Compared to traditional template matching, this approach addresses computational efficiency bottlenecks through a contour pre-classification mechanism that constructs a hierarchical template library. Relative to deep learning methods, its core advantages manifest in no training requirements, minimal resource consumption, and strong interpretability. This design philosophy enables the algorithm to efficiently collaborate with path planning modules on unified embedded platforms, ultimately achieving system-level performance optimization that embodies the engineering trade-off wisdom oriented toward practical application scenarios.

Moreover, the proposed improved SAD method fills a practical research gap that is often neglected in deep learning–oriented drug recognition studies. While existing deep learning approaches achieve high accuracy, they implicitly assume the availability of large annotated datasets, sufficient GPU resources, and stable high-resolution imaging. However, intelligent pharmacy systems frequently operate under small-sample conditions, tight computational budgets, and strict real-time constraints, where deploying or updating deep models becomes impractical. Current literature offers limited solutions for these resource-constrained and highly structured environments. By integrating contour-based pre-classification with a hierarchical template library, the improved SAD method provides a lightweight yet reliable alternative specifically optimized for these scenarios. Thus, instead of competing with deep learning approaches in general-purpose recognition tasks, this study complements them by addressing their deployment limitations, thereby filling an important engineering-focused gap in pharmaceutical automation research.

JPS-RRT* algorithm

The Jump Point Search (JPS) algorithm is a path planning algorithm optimized for grid maps, designed to significantly reduce the number of node expansions in A* algorithms. Without environmental preprocessing, it achieves notable acceleration effects. Its core idea involves pruning symmetric paths and directly skipping redundant nodes, retaining only key nodes (jump points) for expansion. The metric Emergency Stops in Table 6 is rigorously defined by the following unambiguous, system-measured criteria. An emergency stop event is recorded if the vehicle’s state simultaneously satisfies the following three conditions for a continuous duration exceeding 2.0 seconds: Zero velocity: The linear velocity of the vehicle, as reported by the wheel encoders, drops below 0.05 m/s.Path planning failure: The path planning module (JPS, RRT, or JPS-RRT) fails to output a feasible collision-free path to the immediate sub-goal.Proximity to obstacle: The minimum distance to the nearest obstacle, measured by the LiDAR point cloud, is less than 0.3 meters.This objective definition captures events where the vehicle is effectively “trapped”, it has stopped because no safe path can be found in its immediate vicinity. The choice of a 2-second duration prevents brief pauses (e.g. for calculation or normal braking) from being misclassified as emergencies. As shown in Table 6, the pure JPS algorithm resulted in the most emergency stops (6 times) because it cannot handle dynamic obstacles, leading to frequent deadlocks. The pure RRT* algorithm performed better (2 stops) due to its inherent ability to react to changes. The hybrid JPS-RRT* algorithm achieved the best result (1 stop) by leveraging the global efficiency of JPS and the local adaptability of RRT*, effectively minimizing unexpected stops.

The main reason is that when an unmanned vehicle gets extremely close to a dynamic obstacle in a short period of time, the planned path will overlap with the obstacle, causing it to be unable to get out of the predicament in time and fall into a dead zone, which leads to an emergency stop. Different algorithms handle this situation differently, so there is a significant difference in the number of emergency stops.

Drug image preprocessing

Image preprocessing plays a crucial role in drug recognition systems, aiming to enhance image quality, remove noise, and highlight key drug features for reliable subsequent recognition and classification. In drug identification, preprocessing mainly improves clarity of shape, color, and texture features while reducing interference from environmental factors and imaging devices.

Image grayscale conversion

First, images undergo grayscale conversion. Grayscale refers to color values in grayscale images ranging from 0 (black) to 255 (white), with intermediate values representing shades. Grayscale histograms statistically analyze pixel distributions for each grayscale value31. In drug recognition, grayscale reduces computational complexity by retaining only brightness information without color data, facilitating efficient edge detection and contour extraction.

Common grayscale methods include maximum value, average value, and weighted average methods. The maximum method uses the highest RGB channel value, potentially causing excessive brightness. The average method calculates the mean RGB value but may lose color details. The weighted average method improves upon this by assigning different weights to RGB channels based on human color sensitivity:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Gray} = 0.299 \times R + 0.587 \times G + 0.114 \times B, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where R, G, and B represent red, green, and blue channel values. These weights align with human eye sensitivity (higher green weight)32. We selected weighted averaging for better color retention and recognition accuracy.

Image denoising

Image denoising is a crucial step in image processing, aiming to eliminate noise introduced during image acquisition, transmission, or environmental interference, thereby restoring the true information of the image33. In pharmaceutical recognition systems, denoising is particularly critical, as noise can distort the shape and contour extraction of pharmaceutical products, ultimately degrading recognition accuracy. Noise introduces undesirable pixel variations, adversely affecting subsequent processing steps.

Among linear filtering methods, Gaussian filtering stands out as an effective approach that overcomes the limitations of simpler neighborhood-based techniques, such as excessive blurring. Conventional methods like mean filtering, which compute a simple average of neighboring pixels, tend to blur fine details and edges. In contrast, Gaussian filtering employs a Gaussian distribution-based weighting scheme, assigning higher importance to nearby pixels during smoothing operations. By computing a weighted average where closer pixels contribute more significantly, Gaussian filtering achieves superior noise suppression while better preserving structural edges compared to median filtering (which is computationally intensive and may lose details under strong noise) and mean filtering (which often blurs structural contours). Consequently, Gaussian smoothing yields higher-quality results with improved edge retention, making it widely applicable in high-precision image processing scenarios.

Given these advantages, this study adopts Gaussian filtering for denoising pharmaceutical images to ensure robust feature extraction and enhanced recognition performance.

Image enhancement

Image enhancement is a pivotal technique in image processing aimed at improving the visual quality of an image by adjusting its brightness, contrast, or detail characteristics, thereby making the salient information more prominent34. This process facilitates the clear presentation of target regions during image analysis, ultimately enhancing the accuracy and reliability of subsequent tasks, such as feature extraction or object recognition. In the field of pharmaceutical identification, image enhancement is widely employed, particularly in scenarios requiring high-quality image input to ensure analytical precision.

Among the most commonly used techniques in image enhancement is grayscale transformation, which improves image contrast by adjusting the grayscale value of each pixel. Grayscale transformation can be categorized into linear and nonlinear methods. Nonlinear transformations, including exponential and logarithmic transformations, are designed to expand high- and low-grayscale regions, respectively. However, these approaches may introduce uneven illumination in cylindrical label imaging. In contrast, linear transformation effectively expands the dynamic range of an image, enhancing its original contrast and rendering it sharper, which significantly improves visual quality.

Drug contour recognition and classification

After preprocessing, drug contours are classified into three categories: circular, capsule-shaped, and elliptical. This preliminary classification reduces computational load and accelerates subsequent template matching.

Drug edge detection

Edge detection plays a pivotal role in pharmaceutical entity recognition, where the boundaries of pharmaceutical entities are typically characterized by significant variations in grayscale intensity. These variations facilitate the precise delineation of drug contours and shapes. To effectively identify distinct feature regions of pharmaceutical products, derivative or second-order derivative-based edge detection methods are commonly employed. This process not only substantially reduces image data volume but also eliminates irrelevant background information while preserving the critical structural features of pharmaceutical entities36. Consequently, it provides enhanced image data support for subsequent drug identification and classification tasks.

Among the prevalent edge detection operators, the Canny and Sobel operators are widely utilized. The Canny operator, a multi-stage edge detection algorithm, is extensively applied in image processing. During edge detection, this operator initially employs a Gaussian filter to smooth the original image, effectively suppressing noise and demonstrating robust anti-noise capabilities. Subsequently, the Canny operator utilizes dual thresholds to detect strong and weak edges separately. This dual-threshold approach excels in ensuring edge detection accuracy and continuity, thereby maintaining the integrity of edge features. Additionally, the Canny operator significantly reduces image data dimensionality while retaining essential edge information, thereby improving computational efficiency. However, the Canny operator is not without limitations; it is prone to misclassifying noise as edges37. Nevertheless, in the context of pharmaceutical recognition, its advantages–including noise resistance, edge feature preservation, and efficient data processing–enable precise extraction of drug image edges. These capabilities provide critical support for subsequent shape- and logo-based identification, substantially enhancing both accuracy and efficiency in pharmaceutical recognition.

In contrast, the Sobel operator is a discrete differential operator for edge detection that identifies edges by computing weighted differences of grayscale intensities in the neighborhood of each pixel. Known for its simplicity and computational efficiency, the Sobel operator performs well in images with gradual grayscale transitions or high noise levels due to its inherent smoothing effect, which mitigates noise interference while emphasizing edge detection. However, its primary drawback lies in its relatively imprecise edge localization38. In pharmaceutical recognition applications, the Sobel operator underperforms compared to the Canny operator in accurately extracting edge features. The Canny operator’s superior noise resilience, edge integrity preservation, and processing efficiency make it more suitable for identifying drug shapes and logos, thereby improving recognition reliability. Given the Sobel operator’s limitations in edge localization, its application in pharmaceutical recognition may compromise accuracy39.

Therefore, in this study, we employ the Canny operator to extract the contour features of pharmaceutical entities, ensuring optimal performance in drug identification tasks.

Drug shape classification

Following edge detection, the subsequent step involves the recognition and classification of pharmaceutical shapes, which are primarily categorized into three types: circular, elliptical, and capsule-shaped. For circular pills, the Hough Circle Detection (HCD) algorithm is employed, implemented via the ‘cv2.HoughCircles’ function in OpenCV. As a computer vision technique, HCD leverages the Hough transform to detect circular objects by searching for parametric representations of circles in an accumulator space. The algorithm converts circular shapes into parametric curves, where intersections in the accumulator array correspond to potential circles. If the accumulated value for a given parameter set exceeds a predefined threshold, the corresponding circle is deemed present in the image40. Experimental results demonstrate that HCD exhibits strong robustness, accurately identifying circular pills in both simple and moderately complex backgrounds while significantly improving recognition speed and precision.

For elliptical pills, contour fitting is applied. First, the grayscale values of all edge pixels corresponding to the elliptical contour are extracted. The k-means clustering algorithm is then utilized to partition these pixels into two classes: one with lower-intensity pixels and their coordinates, and another with higher-intensity pixels and their coordinates. The number of clusters is set to k = 2, which is sufficient because the grayscale features of elliptical tablets usually present two main distributions: one is the bright background or high grayscale area, and the other is the tablet edge or dark area. This bimodal distribution has been observed to remain stable across varying pharmacy imaging conditions, including mixed indoor lighting, fluorescent lighting, and partially shadowed areas, making k = 2 both sufficient and robust for reliable foreground–background separation. Therefore, setting k = 2 can effectively complete the binary classification of “foreground/background”, so that the clustering result can directly reflect the potential edge area.

To avoid the instability of clustering caused by random initialization, this paper adopts an initialization method based on the mean grayscale: the average value of the grayscale of the pixels in the search box is used as the initial classification threshold, and the pixels are divided into two categories: bright area and dark area. The mean grayscale of each category is used as the initial cluster center41. In the recognition environment of the pharmacy, k = 2 is the most direct, reasonable, and robust choice for identifying ellipses. Subsequently, least-squares ellipse fitting is performed, where outliers–points distant from the estimated ellipse boundary–are iteratively removed to refine the inlier set. The final ellipse equation is derived from the optimal inlier set, enabling accurate contour extraction42. Experiments confirm that this method effectively handles irregular or rotated elliptical shapes, maintaining high accuracy across varying backgrounds and illumination conditions.

Drug template matching

Template matching is a method used to identify the presence of a known template image within a larger search image, despite variations caused by factors such as illumination and positional changes during image acquisition. By employing correlation-based search strategies, this approach locates sub-images similar to the template and determines their positions45. Conventional Sum of Absolute Differences (SAD)-based template matching often suffers from limited matching accuracy. To address this issue, this study proposes an enhanced SAD-based template matching algorithm integrated with contour feature classification, thereby improving both the efficiency and precision of pharmaceutical recognition.

SAD-based template matching

The Sum of Absolute Differences (SAD) is a widely used similarity metric in image matching, particularly for template matching and disparity computation in stereo vision. The fundamental principle of this algorithm lies in quantifying the similarity between two image regions by computing the differences in grayscale values of corresponding pixels within image patches. The computational process involves selecting fixed-size windows in both the reference and target images, followed by a pixel-wise comparison of grayscale values within these windows46.

In practical implementation, a center point is first selected in the reference image, around which a fixed-size window is constructed. A corresponding sub-window of identical dimensions is then created at the same location in the target image, and the absolute differences between the grayscale values of corresponding pixels within these windows are computed. These differences are summed to yield a cumulative error value (SAD), which serves as a measure of dissimilarity between the reference and target windows. During the matching process, the algorithm traverses the entire reference image starting from the top-left corner, computing the SAD value for each candidate window relative to the corresponding region in the target image. By evaluating SAD values across different regions, the optimal matching position is identified as the location with the minimal SAD value, indicating the highest degree of similarity between the reference and target image regions.

The SAD algorithm offers notable advantages, including computational simplicity and high efficiency. However, its primary limitations lie in its sensitivity to noise and susceptibility to global illumination variations. To address these challenges, this study integrates contour feature analysis specifically edge feature detection to enhance the overall accuracy of SAD-based template matching in pharmaceutical product recognition.

Contour-enhanced SAD matching

Before SAD matching, drugs are pre-classified by geometric shape (circular, elliptical, capsule-shaped), reducing search space and improving efficiency. Within each class, further distinctions are needed (e.g. cod liver oil vs. oval pills in the elliptical class). Thus, SAD alone is insufficient without contour shape differentiation.

To address the need for further distinctions within each shape class, we constructed dedicated template libraries through a standardized pipeline to ensure representativeness and matching accuracy. The library was built by first capturing images of the five target drug types under controlled lighting conditions using a fixed 5-megapixel industrial camera, yielding 300 raw images from multiple angles. Candidate templates were then manually selected from this pool, with approximately 50 images per category chosen for their clear contours, absence of occlusion, and typical features. To ensure that the template library adequately represents the variations in appearance due to changes in viewing angle and illumination, candidate templates were selected to include images captured under diverse orientations and lighting conditions. This selection strategy ensures that the SAD matching process remains robust against moderate rotations, tilts, and shadows commonly encountered in practical pharmacy environments. These candidate images subsequently underwent the same preprocessing pipeline detailed in Sect. "JPS-RRT* algorithm", including grayscale conversion, Gaussian denoising, and image enhancement, followed by uniform cropping and resizing to a standard 64x64-pixel dimension to normalize scale and brightness. The final template set for each category, comprising about 30-40 templates, was curated by performing an internal consistency check based on SAD values to remove outliers, thereby ensuring high library quality. This process resulted in a finalized template library comprising 130-140 templates per category (specifically: 135 for round tablets, 132 for oval pills, 130 for cod liver oil, 138 for monochrome capsules, and 140 for bicolor capsules). This specific scale was determined to be the optimal trade-off, balancing template diversity against computational load on our target embedded platform (Raspberry Pi). It ensures matching can be completed within a constrained time frame, demonstrating the method’s practicality and scalability for structured environments like intelligent pharmacies, where the drug types are predefined and stable. During the SAD matching process, only the templates from the corresponding contour category are invoked for comparison. This structured approach to library construction not only confines the search space to relevant templates, avoiding unnecessary computational overhead, but also significantly mitigates the risk of mismatches caused by fundamental shape discrepancies.

In the SAD matching procedure, the input target image first undergoes preprocessing, including grayscale conversion, normalization, and size standardization. These steps ensure consistency between the input image and the templates in terms of scale, brightness, and other factors, thereby minimizing interference caused by variations in lighting or dimensions. Subsequently, based on the contour feature classification results obtained in the preliminary stage, the corresponding template library is selected. The SAD values between the target pharmaceutical product and each template in the library are computed, and the template with the smallest SAD value is identified as the matching result. This method eliminates the need for a global search, improving computational efficiency, while simultaneously excluding templates that do not belong to the current shape category during matching, thereby enhancing recognition accuracy48.

To verify the effectiveness of the improved SAD template matching algorithm, this paper conducts a comparative analysis with the traditional SSD (Sum of Squared Differences) algorithm and the deep learning model YOLO.

Both SSD and SAD are template-based matching methods, but SSD requires the calculation of the square of the difference for each pixel, resulting in a significantly higher computational load than SAD, which leads to longer processing time and higher requirements for processor performance25. Through comparative tests in a pharmacy environment, at the same resolution, the operation speed of the improved SAD algorithm in this paper is approximately 10-12% faster than that of the SSD algorithm, and the accuracy rates of both algorithms for drug recognition are close.

YOLO is a typical deep learning-based object detection algorithm that transforms the object detection task into a single regression problem, simultaneously predicting the position and category of the target in a single forward pass. It features end-to-end processing and high-speed detection26. Compared with the YOLO deep learning method, the improved SAD algorithm in this paper has higher recognition accuracy, requires no training, and can run on embedded platforms such as Raspberry Pi with only the deployment environment, achieving good recognition accuracy under limited computing power. It combines lightweight and ease of use, making it suitable for low-power drug recognition tasks in pharmacy robots.

Trade-offs and applicability of the improved SAD algorithm

The results presented in Tables 7 and 8 reveal the inherent trade-offs between the improved SAD algorithm and the YOLO deep learning model across different performance metrics. Although the improved SAD exhibits a higher mismatch rate (17.6%) than YOLO (4.7%) for certain categories, such as monochrome capsules, this elevated error rate remains acceptable within the specific application context of an intelligent pharmacy’s unmanned delivery system. The fundamental reason lies in the system’s design philosophy, which adheres to an engineering trade-off criterion tailored for embedded platforms: prioritizing system-wide real-time performance, low power consumption, and overall reliability, while meeting basic accuracy requirements.

The advantages of the improved SAD method become particularly prominent in resource-constrained scenarios. While the high accuracy of algorithms like YOLO relies on substantial computational load and parameter scale, necessitating GPU acceleration–feasible in high-power, high-cost server-side deployments–their real-time operation on resource-limited embedded edge devices like the Raspberry Pi is challenging. In contrast, the improved SAD algorithm requires no training, has a minimal memory footprint, and can operate efficiently in synergy with the JPS-RRT* path planning module on the same low-power computing platform.Specifically, deployment on a Raspberry Pi 4B (CPU: Quad-core Cortex-A72 1.5 GHz, RAM: 4GB) demonstrated an average processing time of 3–4.5 seconds per image, with CPU utilization around 40–50% and memory usage of 500–700 MB (Table 11). These measurements confirm that the algorithm can function effectively on low-power hardware without compromising system responsiveness.

This integrated design avoids the degradation in real-time performance or system stability that could result from introducing a computationally intensive recognition model, thereby achieving optimal system-level performance. Furthermore, intelligent pharmacy environments typically possess structured characteristics such as relatively stable lighting, regular drug placement, and minimal background clutter, providing favorable conditions for the application of the improved SAD algorithm. Its principle, based on transparent template matching, allows for easy tracing and debugging of mismatch causes, significantly facilitating system maintenance and optimization.

As shown in Table 11, the improved SAD algorithm achieves significantly higher recognition accuracy than traditional SAD and SSD across all five drug categories. Correspondingly, Table 12 demonstrates that the mismatch rate also decreases notably, particularly for round tablets and oval pills.

In terms of processing efficiency, Table 11 shows that the improved SAD algorithm offers the shortest matching time among all compared methods, completing recognition in only 3–4.5 seconds depending on the drug type. Furthermore, the overall comparison in Table 12 highlights that the improved SAD achieves the best balance between accuracy (86.04%) and efficiency (3.99 s), making it highly suitable for resource-limited embedded systems.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel path optimization technique combining JPS and RRT* algorithms for unmanned delivery vehicles in smart pharmacy environments. Experimental results demonstrate that the JPS method’s pruning strategy significantly enhances global path search efficiency, while RRT* ensures path smoothness and dynamic environment adaptability through local optimization. Compared to conventional Dijkstra and A* algorithms, our approach shows superior performance in planning time (reduced by 82%), path quality (25% shorter average length), and computational efficiency (68% lower resource consumption), with enhanced dynamic environment adaptability. Complementing the navigation system, deep learning-optimized SLAM mapping improves positioning accuracy by 40% and environmental perception capabilities.

For drug recognition, our multi-stage framework–incorporating template matching, shape classification, contour identification, and image preprocessing–achieves an average identification accuracy of 86.0% (see Table 12), enabling real-time pharmaceutical classification. Compared with the traditional SAD and SSD template matching algorithms, the improved SAD algorithm demonstrates significant improvements in both recognition accuracy and matching speed. Although it still lags behind the deep learning algorithm YOLO in terms of accuracy, the matching time required by the improved SAD is substantially shorter than that of YOLO. Validated in operational pharmacy settings, this integrated technology significantly improves medication distribution efficiency, reduces manual error rates by approximately 75%, and enhances workplace safety. The proposed solution not only optimizes smart pharmacy logistics but also provides a reference architecture for autonomous mobile robots operating in structured environments. Table 12 further illustrates that while YOLO achieves the highest accuracy, the improved SAD offers the best trade-off between recognition performance and computational efficiency compared with SAD, SSD, and YOLO.Future work will explore integrating reinforcement learning (RL) with the current system, where RL can dynamically optimize template selection, matching thresholds, and preprocessing parameters based on real-time feedback from recognition outcomes. By continuously learning from successes and failures in drug identification, the system can adapt to variations in lighting, occlusion, and placement of pharmaceuticals. This approach aims to improve overall recognition accuracy, reduce mismatch rates, and enable the system to generalize to new or unseen drug types, laying the foundation for more intelligent, autonomous, and robust drug handling in complex pharmacy environments.

Since the JPS algorithm used as the global planning method has high requirements for the accuracy of the map, an accurate map needs to be drawn. If there is a certain error in the corner positions of the map, there is a probability that the vehicle will get stuck in a dead zone during the planning process, thereby causing a system crash. At the same time, when multiple dynamic obstacles appear simultaneously, although the algorithm in this project has reduced the computing power requirement, it is still found that the planning speed has decreased to some extent, resulting in a certain reduction in real-time performance. Therefore, this project is designed for scenarios where there are multiple static obstacles but also a small number of dynamic obstacles, and planning with low computing power can present more superior performance.Furthermore, the proposed drug recognition method has several inherent limitations that warrant consideration. First, its performance is highly sensitive to variations in lighting conditions; significant changes in illumination can affect the quality of contour extraction and reduce matching accuracy. Second, the method requires a relatively clean and uncluttered background to reliably detect and classify drugs. Background noise or overlapping visual elements may interfere with template matching and lead to misidentification. Third, when drugs are partially occluded or stacked on top of each other, the algorithm may struggle to correctly identify individual items, resulting in reduced recognition accuracy. These limitations indicate that while the method is effective in structured and controlled environments, its robustness may be challenged in more complex or dynamic scenarios.

The primary limitation of the current system lies in its performance degradation when facing a high density of simultaneous dynamic obstacles, as the computational load for real-time replanning pushes the limits of the embedded Raspberry Pi platform. To concretely address this challenge, future work will focus on integrating existing, well-established cloud–edge computing paradigms. Specifically, we propose to offload the computationally intensive global path planning and multi-obstacle dynamic scene understanding tasks to the cloud using industrial solutions such as AWS IoT Greengrass or Azure IoT Edge.

In addition to cloud–edge optimization, a significant future direction is the integration of reinforcement learning (RL) into the current JPS–RRT* framework.
