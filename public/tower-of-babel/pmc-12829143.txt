Accessibility, safety, and effectiveness of telerehabilitation for public safety personnel with posttraumatic stress injuries: an interpretive description study
Authors: Katelyn Brehon, Maxi Miciak, Brandon Krebs, Riikka Niemelainen, Bruce D. Dick, Sebastian Straube, Suzette Brémault-Phillips, Douglas P. Gross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPublic safety personnel (PSP) are regularly exposed to stress which can result in the development of posttraumatic stress injuries (PTSIs) and leave them unable to work. The COVID-19 pandemic caused a shift in the delivery of PTSI rehabilitation to telerehabilitation (mainly videoconference, but some telephone-based care).MethodsThe present study employed an Interpretive Description design to explore the lived experiences of PSP, clinicians, and decision-makers regarding the safety, accessibility, and effectiveness of PTSI telerehabilitation. We used purposive and convenience sampling techniques, aiming to maximize variation. Interview data were generated using semi-structured interviews and analyzed using an integrated, team-based approach to Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsOur final sample consisted of 34 participants: 12 PSP, 14 clinicians, and eight decision-makers. Three themes were generated: (1) Responding to the uniqueness of individuals and their contexts; (2) Prioritizing different elements of safety at different times; and (3) Facets of care that lead to perceptions of credibility. While telerehabilitation offers accessibility and flexibility, its application necessitates a nuanced, person-centred approach.ConclusionsClinicians need to tailor the mode of delivery to PSP and be aware of the potential limitations in observing subtle cues and fostering the therapeutic alliance virtually. Decision-makers should consider policies that support a hybrid model of care, balancing PSP preference and the potential need for in-person interventions to address avoidance and facilitate crucial components of care. Ultimately, for PSP, telerehabilitation presents a valuable option for accessing timely, comfortable care, but open communication with clinicians about preferences and any challenges experienced with virtual modalities is paramount.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13905-0.

Background

Public safety personnel (PSP) are workers, such as military members, corrections officers, emergency dispatchers, paramedics, firefighters, and police officers, whose occupational role is to ensure the safety and security of the public [1]. Due to their occupational duties, PSP are regularly exposed to a wide range of stressful and potentially traumatic events, which can result in the development of a wide range of mental health symptoms, classified as posttraumatic stress injuries [1] (PTSIs).

PSP’s daily functioning and work abilities are commonly impacted by PTSI symptoms, leaving them unable to return to their pre-injury occupational positions and roles [2]. Although workers from a wide range of occupational roles are treated in PTSI rehabilitation programs each year [3, 4], PSP are unique due to both the organizational culture they operate within and the frequency and severity of the traumatic events to which they are exposed [5, 6]. Among Canadian PSP populations, the prevalence rate of PTSI is up to 45% [7], while return-to-work rates have been reported to be under 50% following PTSI rehabilitation [2].

Specific interdisciplinary rehabilitation programs are often offered to treat PTSI [2]. These programs focus on integrating treatment components from various disciplines, including psychotherapy, occupational therapy, and physical therapy [8, 9]. PTSI rehabilitation services have traditionally been offered in person. The COVID-19 pandemic, however, saw the provision of PTSI rehabilitation partially shift to telerehabilitation (i.e., rehabilitation services offered through videoconference or telephone) [10]. Despite the easing of COVID-related restrictions, PTSI rehabilitation services have continued to be offered either exclusively or partially through telerehabilitation [10]. However, little is yet known about how clinicians, decision-makers, and PSP perceive and experience PTSI treatment administered through telerehabilitation, leading to questions regarding its safety and effectiveness.

Recent literature suggests that telerehabilitation can be a viable and effective treatment option for both physical and psychological injuries [11–15]. Research also indicates that telerehabilitation can have distinct benefits compared to in-person treatment, such as lower treatment costs [16], increased accessibility [17], reduced time burden for providers [18], enhanced flexibility of care, and reduced wait time for patients [19]. Moreover, telerehabilitation has been suggested to be a viable alternative to in-person treatment for individuals with PTSI, in particular [20–22].

Despite the benefits, some concerns have been raised about the use of telerehabilitation for treating PTSI [23, 24]. One concern is that telerehabilitation may not always be an appropriate mode of delivery for exposure therapy [23], a key component of treating PTSIs [9]. Furthermore, because exposure therapy is such a significant component of PTSI treatment for PSP in particular, there are additional questions regarding the safety, adaptability, and overall effectiveness of PTSI treatment for this population. Although suggestions regarding the use of exposure therapy through telerehabilitation have been made [24], the use of telerehabilitation for exposure therapy in PSP appears to be understudied.

Overall, despite an increase in research about the provision of telerehabilitation services [11–15], little is known about the use of these programs for treating specific subsets of workers, such as PSP with PTSI. To address this gap in the literature, the present study aimed to understand the perceptions and lived experiences of those most closely involved in the PTSI rehabilitation process: PSP, clinicians, and key program decision-makers. In the current study, decision-makers refer to both policy-makers who research, analyze, and consult on developing policies and processes regarding specific programs, and workers’ compensation ‘claim owners’ (i.e., case managers) who work with injured workers and their rehabilitation teams to navigate claim-related issues, plan treatment programs, and develop return-to-work plans. By triangulating the perspectives of these three groups, we aimed to offer a comprehensive perspective on the safety, accessibility, and effectiveness of PTSI telerehabilitation that can be used to inform practice and policy decisions.

Methods

Qualitative methodology, aligning with a constructionist paradigm, was used to explore the shared meaning of using telerehabilitation for PTSI by directly speaking with individuals who experienced the phenomenon—clinicians, decision-makers and importantly, PSP living with PTSI [25]. This study was approved by the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Reference Number: Pro00120406) and was conducted in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Design

Interpretive Description, an approach often employed in practice-focused disciplines [26], was used to provide practical insights into the context of virtual rehabilitation amongst PSP with PTSI. We did not adopt a theoretical frame a priori; however, we acknowledge that various service-related frameworks related to person-centred care, trauma, and quality of care influenced our thinking.

Background of the research team

Our research team consisted of individuals with diverse experience using qualitative research methods (specifically Interpretive Description), along with content expertise in public health, occupational injury and disability, PTSI and mental health, therapeutic relationships, and telerehabilitation. Most members also came from diverse healthcare professions, including psychology, occupational medicine, and physical therapy, and have worked in work disability prevention, and more specifically, with workers who have sustained a PTSI. The main analyses were conducted by three individuals: a trained physiotherapist with PhD training in qualitative research and therapeutic relationship, a PhD student in counselling psychology with previous experience working in the work disability space, and a Master’s-level educated researcher trained in public health with previous research experience in community rehabilitation and work disability. These lenses offered diverse perspectives on the data, which contributed to ongoing reflexivity and subsequent rigour. The perspectives of the full team were also influential throughout the project.

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive and convenience sampling techniques were used with the aim of maximizing variation in our sample among: (1) PSP living with PTSI who received telerehabilitation; (2) rehabilitation clinicians involved in PTSI telerehabilitation; and (3) decision-makers involved in workers’ compensation claims of PSP with PTSI or in developing related policies or processes. We recruited from participating PTSI rehabilitation clinics, the Workers’ Compensation Board – Alberta (WCB-AB), and community rehabilitation clinics.

Clinicians and decision-makers received study information from a member of the research team working in the system. The study’s main inclusion criterion was being involved in the care of PSP with PTSIs who received telerehabilitation during the COVID-19 pandemic or in developing related policies or processes. Those interested in participating in the study contacted a member of the study team and were screened for eligibility. The study team also used these connections to recruit more clinicians and decision-makers as needed.

Clinicians and decision-makers identified and shared study information with PSP. Interested PSP contacted a study team member who then screened them for being: (1) a PSP with a PTSI who received telerehabilitation, (2) able to participate in an interview, and (3) able to provide informed consent. Those eligible were directed to the team member completing the interviews to go through the informed consent process.

Data collection and analysis

Interview data were generated using semi-structured interviews via Zoom or telephone. To ensure confidentiality of the interviews, all participants (PSP, clinicians, and decision-makers) were asked to join the interview in a space that they felt was private and confidential. For PSP in particular, the interviewer regularly checked in to ensure confidentiality had not been compromised. As the interviewers, we were also responsible for ensuring we were conducting the interviews in private, confidential settings and discussed our locations and spaces at the start of each interview prior to beginning to ensure participants were comfortable.

Separate interview guides were developed for each participant group (found in Appendix 1 in supplemental materials). All interview guides addressed the topics of accessibility, safety, and effectiveness of telerehabilitation for PSP with PTSI. Iterative refinements to the interview guides were made to deepen conversations and probing questions were used to clarify or elicit more detailed description. Interviews were audio-recorded, professionally transcribed, and imported into NVivo data management software (version 14) for analysis. Data collection and analysis were concurrent and iterative. All participants completed a demographic questionnaire to facilitate reporting of age, biological sex, gender, ethnicity, and location within the province (i.e., first three digits of postal code).

PSP interviews were conducted by the member of our research team who is currently completing their PhD in counselling psychology and actively works with people under distress to ensure participant discomfort was identified and responded to appropriately. Additionally, all PSP were emailed a resource sheet following the interview that contained the contact information for various psychological resources should they have felt they were needed.

We used reflexive thematic analysis, informed by Braun and Clarke (2006) employing an integrated team-based approach that incorporated individual and collaborative elements. Three study team members (MM, BK, and KB) were involved in the analysis (herein, the analytic team). Each analytic team member (herein, analyst) conducted and analyzed interviews for one of the participant groups to encourage greater understanding within each group. However, we also chose to incorporate a collaborative element into the analysis, which allowed us to integrate and navigate (differing) perspectives between the participant groups, resulting in a cohesive understanding of the phenomenon. The team-based approach also allowed us to navigate and integrate our positions as researchers.

Each analyst created reflexive interview notes after each interview to elucidate our impressions of each participant, of ourselves, and of our interactions during the interviews. Before constructing inductive codes, each analyst (re)read and reflected on each of their group’s transcripts to ensure correctness and to become familiar with the data. The goal was not to land too quickly on ideas but rather to reflect and consider various perspectives when interpreting the data.

Regular analytic team meetings, where the analysts articulated their experiences in collecting and analyzing the data, provoked rich conversations that stimulated theoretical thinking and reflexivity about our positionality, both of which contributed to a rigorous interpretive process and co-construction of conceptual descriptions [26]. Post-meeting journaling acted as an ongoing memo of our thinking and an audit trail of our decisions.

Memoing and reflection occurred during cycles of coding, naming, recoding, and renaming codes, themes, and subthemes [27] until a thematic map evolved that was representative of perspectives of each participant group. This thematic map was further critiqued and refined through memoing and reflection as well as through writing the report and retrieving exemplary quotes [27].

Rigour

Trustworthiness and rigour were enhanced through several strategies to ensure epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic, and interpretive authority [26]. We ensured epistemological integrity by choosing Interpretive Description as our method to address our objective, since it acknowledges the importance of multiple perspectives to understand experiences in clinical contexts. Representative credibility was enhanced by triangulating data sources and investigator triangulation, where we used three analysts to collect and analyze data, thereby recognizing knowledge and perceptions outside a single lens. Analytic logic was enhanced through thick description of methods, participants’ contexts, and interpretations grounded in verbatim participant accounts [28], as well as reflexive journaling and video recordings to document team meetings, which created an audit trail of decisions. Interpretive authority was enhanced through constant reflection and memoing during the data collection and analysis processes about each analyst’s personal positions and experiences and how these factors influenced interpretation.

Themes of the accessibility, safety, and effectiveness of telerehabilitation among PSP with PTSIs

Findings consist of three themes that conceptually describe the accessibility, safety, and effectiveness of telerehabilitation among PSP with PTSIs: (1) Responding to the uniqueness of individuals and their contexts; (2) Prioritizing different elements of safety at different times; and (3) Facets of care that lead to perceptions of credibility in telerehabilitation for providing services to PSP. All participant groups spoke to each theme. Additional quotes for each subtheme can be found in Table 2. Quotes from participant groups are defined by the following acronyms: Public Safety Personnel (PSP), Clinician (CL), and Decision-maker (DM).

“We also developed a procedure for abrupt absences… And if it’s a crisis or emergency, we’ll use our safety and emergency plan that’s in place. So we thought that was really important because we want our clients to be safe.” (DM006)

“I just don’t feel that I can openly talk about some things, especially when [name] is home. I don’t mean it against [them]. I don’t want to hurt [their] feelings in any way; it’s just it’s harder to express some certain feelings.” (PSP008)

“So we have one client where we’ve put her in a hotel so she can have privacy, because after their sessions, quite often they’re upset. They might be crying. They’re emotionally drained and need some like reflection time, some downtime. They can’t always get that at home.” (CL011)

“Yeah, I’m glad the first [time] that I did it was in person. Because I think had I had to do it just virtually with nobody there, it was just a form that was emailed to me, I definitely would have struggled and not known how to answer some of them.” (PSP011)

“I always do [initial assessments] in-person. Because the assessments, I think, are the most critical aspect of it. And we need to get that true – for a CPA [comprehensive psychological assessment], … that is in person, I think, because they need to see [in-person] how that person responds or interacts.” (DM001)

“…sometimes you could bring resources if you wanted specific sounds. I know during the pandemic, often things like Google Maps was a really good tool. Like, if you were doing a virtual session, you could actually view the location at street view, things like that, before physically going there, just to have their response in real time.” (CL005)

“The sessions are structured a certain way. And the first three don’t involve – there’s no exposure work being done. It’s really about the psychoeducation piece and making sure that we’ve identified the event or events that we’re going to be working on…using the assessment and then maybe the first few sessions to really build rapport, I find to be really helpful.” (CL013)

“Also getting folks out of the house on a more regular basis, so having appointments in-person forces them to get out of the house. Which is a huge piece with anyone experiencing psychological injury because avoidance and isolation is so, such a hard piece to recover from. So just having it kind of built in so you have to come here for appointments, that has been a really important piece of the therapy.” (CL006)

“When you’re in person, they can really be in touch with what I’m going through. So if I need a moment, they can kind of really read that and give me the moment. I know my therapists, when I work with them in-person, if I have stress coming out of my body, like from maybe tapping my feet, that’s something you wouldn’t see on a Zoom meeting.” (PSP011)

“And then I think that the in-person is helpful for credibility as we spoke about. But I think, you know, especially honing in on … the return to work, I think it’s helpful in the confidence of the treatment from both the providers, the clinicians and the client.” (DM005)

“I think it just differed to personality. I think it comes down to the person and their own personality and just on how that goes. Because I felt like it didn’t really matter if I was in-person or virtual, once I actually met them, I felt like it was still the same experience. So, I think it’s more person-based and not method-based.” (PSP007)

“Now that I’ve been able to get back to fully in-person, I can see that it’s a lot more preferred. I think it’s more effective because we’re able to, if it’s established, the rapport and the trust a lot quicker…” (CL006)

“But I certainly use [name of virtual platform] as much as I can…with anyone who’s willing to do it with me, because I feel like it’s an extension of getting to know each other and building that relationship and that rapport…You know, I can’t do the work that I need to do with them if they don’t trust me or we don’t build some type of relationship.” (DM004)

“So I would say virtual care has…increased [name of service provider] ability to pair up with, for example, a clinic in Calgary who could then provide virtual care to our client from Edmonton if they needed overflow from our psychologists… it has broadened, broadens our capacity, is what I would call it.” (CL002)

“…we had a lot of complaints from COs [claim owners/case managers] saying they’re not progressing, they’re still working on these community exposures but this person is going to the mailbox and back, and that’s it. The services are going to come to an end right away… [and] I’ve got nothing…” (DM008)

“I find that a lot of them [clinicians] don’t mind the whole telerehab thing because it allows them to have more flexibility with their own schedule…Some of the clinicians are better than others, I find, with respect to recognising within their clients [PSP] of what they need and when they need it…other clinicians are just more apt to follow, ‘well, this is our process and this is all I’m doing.’ And I find that those particular clinicians are the ones that wind up resulting in extended program times, or workers being dissatisfied with the level of care…” (DM002)

“We need to have some parameters because…there’s a big group of community providers that definitely prefer telehealth and do it almost exclusively if they can…We have those that just live for the in vivos and love doing those, which is great. But we have those that are like, I can do in vivos while I’m online with them.” (DM008)

Theme 1 - Responding to the uniqueness of individuals and their contexts. Theme 1 discusses the complex and unique nature of PSP, their job responsibilities and demands, and how these create a need for individualized care. PSP’s personal contexts also impact their ability to access care for PTSI.

Subtheme A - Uniqueness of PSP demands individualized care. All participant groups discussed the unique and complex nature of PSP, which made their rehabilitation more challenging. Social factors (e.g., professional and work culture, conflict with employers, distrust of the workers’ compensation system), extended PTSI claim durations, and the cumulative nature of trauma in this population contributed to the overall complexity of their situations.

Intersecting professional and work cultures can affect recovery, specifically around the idea of the stigma of seeking care and the degree of support PSP may or may not have with PTSI: “…there is sometimes a stigma in reaching out for help. … That can make it difficult to access services and programs” (CL008). PSP spoke about feeling like telerehabilitation was a good form of treatment as it allowed them to avoid the stigma associated with receiving mental health treatment in person: “…when I saw him in person, sometimes, I would go straight from work, and … which isn’t ideal going … into a psychological office wearing a uniform” (PSP004).

The complexity and uniqueness of PSP with PTSI necessitates that they be worked with and given special attention as individuals. Clinicians emphasized the importance of meeting PSP ‘where they are’ and how it was essential to tailor care and services to specific needs and situations to build trust and rapport. Understanding the PSP and their specific needs was essential for utilizing telerehabilitation effectively, which could be applied differently at various stages in their rehabilitation trajectory. This helped to pace the process, including transitioning between telerehabilitation and in-person care.

Clinicians and decision-makers agreed that when optimizing a PSP’s engagement in rehabilitation and determining whether or not to use telerehabilitation, appropriate levels of behavioural activation, exposure, and social support needed to be considered. Where PSP were in the trajectory of their rehabilitation journey and the vigilance associated with their avoidance were also unique individual elements when considering next steps.

All said, even though telerehabilitation may be better than no treatment, participants emphasized that it should not be the default means of delivering and receiving care. It was also acknowledged that telerehabilitation was not for everyone. Clinicians and decision-makers expressed that the best interests of PSP were paramount. The decision to use telerehabilitation should always be with PSP; if PSP do not want telerehabilitation, they should receive care in person.

Subtheme B - Contextual factors dictating access. Telerehabilitation was seen as an opportunity to address PSP’s unique needs because of the ability to adapt to different personal and contextual factors. PSP discussed how telerehabilitation was a more accessible way of connecting with clinicians as it reduced barriers to receiving treatment. For some, seeing clinicians was relatively simple as they lived close to the treatment provider. Telerehabilitation, however, was more convenient for others, such as those living in rural communities. Clinicians noted that telerehabilitation may be the only reason some rural PSP could access services, suggesting that it is a better option than going without care.

Beyond location, clinicians discussed how PSP appreciated the flexibility of telerehabilitation when their life responsibilities impacted services (e.g., child home from school). Access to technology was not seen as a significant limiting factor for telerehabilitation, with PSP reporting that they were able to access services from a range of devices (e.g., computers, tablets, smartphones). Clinicians echoed that access to technology was not a substantial issue in this population. Clinicians were willing to work with whatever technology PSP had available to them.

Telerehabilitation provides a mechanism to address service gaps when accessibility issues arise. It can also be in the ‘back pocket’ for times when contact with a clinician would be helpful for support, but in-person treatment may not be necessary or the PSP does not feel prepared to receive care in person: “I think that if it wasn’t virtual, I don’t know if I would’ve got as far along in my treatment as I would’ve if it was in person because my body would not have been able to accept it” (PSP005).

Theme 2 - Prioritizing different elements of safety at different times. Our participants discussed safety as a multifaceted concept that included physical safety, psychological safety, and privacy and confidentiality.

Subtheme A - Seeking psychological safety and avoiding discomfort. The importance of considering the psychological safety of PSP throughout the treatment process was highlighted across participant groups. All groups indicated that being able to do telerehabilitation sessions in a comfortable environment, such as their homes, was an advantage of telerehabilitation for PSP: “It allowed people to engage in really vulnerable conversations from the comfort of their own home in comfortable clothing, in comfortable spaces, in safe spaces for them” (CL001).

Avoiding the discomfort of attending treatment in person was a major aspect of psychological safety for PSP. Many PSP reported feeling uncomfortable, anxious, and even trapped during in-person treatment: “So maybe just the presence of high security makes you, and certain facilities, makes you feel unsafe just being in that environment compared to at home” (PSP007). Some clinicians viewed their offices or clinic spaces as non-therapeutic, making it challenging to establish trust and comfort during sessions: “When they’re coming into your clinic, depending on what their traumas are, it’s a lot to get to those places. And so they’re coming in sometimes drained and fatigued from just trying to get there” (CL013). Some PSP similarly reported that a heightened emotional state during in-person treatment detracted from their ability to effectively engage in treatment.

Despite the potential advantages of telerehabilitation for promoting psychological safety, all participant groups noted concerns that telerehabilitation could contribute to avoidant tendencies. As such, there was tension present in navigating when care should switch to include some in-person elements, highlighting that an element of timing is important when working through psychological safety versus avoidance. Clinicians and decision-makers emphasized the importance of PSP being in the driver’s seat, while also “…eventually want[ing] to work up towards that tolerance to leaving the house” (DM003).

A tendency for PSP to underreport symptoms, or claim they were not emotionally activated when they were, was discussed by decision-makers. Decision-makers expressed concern from a risk management and patient welfare perspective that if clinicians were not able to identify when PSP were in an elevated emotional state, it could exacerbate their psychological injury. They therefore felt that being in person increased the ability of clinicians to observe and address inconsistencies between reports and behaviours, thereby fostering psychological safety. This was especially the case during exposure sessions, as decision-makers expressed concerns that exposure sessions in community settings and during reintegration phases of treatment could be negatively affected by indiscriminate use of telerehabilitation.

Subtheme B - Impact on physical safety. All participant groups discussed a need to consider physical safety in the rehabilitation of PSP with PTSI. Most discussions around physical safety regarding clinicians related to risk assessment and their ability to gauge body language cues via video during telerehabilitation sessions. Decision-makers expressed concern that clinicians may not be able to adequately observe cues of dysregulation, which was considered a potential major drawback of telerehabilitation: “Well, how does the occupational therapist [OT]… know they’re sweating, their breathing? Like all of those things that we cue in on…” (DM007).

In cases where PSP became dysregulated without a clinician physically present to note cues, decision-makers expressed concern that PSP’s physical safety could be at risk. One decision-maker noted an example where a paramedic decompensated in the back of an ambulance during a reintegration return-to-work session while the OT was delivering their services via telephone from another province. Consistent with this, a small number of PSP expressed concern that physical safety was not being adequately monitored when treatment occurred through telerehabilitation. One factor contributing to the perceived physical safety of PSP during periods of dysregulation over telerehabilitation appeared to be the clinician’s comfort level with telerehabilitation. Some clinicians felt comfortable assessing risk and body language over video software. Clinicians’ experiences and training with virtual care, as well as their perceived ability to establish a therapeutic relationship through telerehabilitation, seemed to impact their comfort assessing body language cues and subsequent risk. Regardless, the majority of PSP reported feeling physically safe when engaging in telerehabilitation: “I got to not be around people. I got to do everything by computer. I felt comfortable. I felt safe” (PSP002).

Comparatively, some PSPs expressed concern regarding physical safety when receiving in-person treatment. For instance, PSP noted that if another worker receiving treatment became dysregulated in the clinic they were attending, they could be unpredictable and potentially dangerous. Additionally, several PSP and clinicians noted that there were physical safety concerns associated with driving to and from in-person sessions in either poor weather conditions or in a distressed emotional state. One PSP noted, “When you’ve gone through a tough session, it’s kind of hard to leave the building and drive home” (PSP006).

Subtheme C - Technological safety. All participant groups discussed technological safety and security in relation to telerehabilitation. Decision-makers felt that telerehabilitation was generally safe, and similarly, clinicians did not share many concerns about the security of virtual platforms. Decision-makers also noted that policies and processes were created to minimize the risk of privacy breaches: “we use a secure platform to make sure we protect the security and confidentiality of our information sent and received with electronic communication” (DM006). To ensure feelings of technological safety, clinicians reviewed the security of the platform being used in the consent process before starting telerehabilitation. Clinicians noted that PSP had few concerns about the safety and security of telerehabilitation: “I’ve never had a client express to me concern about [the security of telerehabilitation] at all” (CL002). Technological safety was also not a major concern expressed by PSP, and most felt that the connections were safe and secure: “…virtual was fine. I didn’t feel like I was being breached of privacy on my computer or anything” (PSP007).

The confidentiality of the PSP’s space when participating in telerehabilitation was one area of consideration regarding safety, as reported across all three participant groups. Decision-makers reported that although PSP were responsible for ensuring their spaces were private, this could sometimes be an issue as clients could not always control who was in their space. Some PSP reported instances where they had concerns over the confidentiality of their space during telerehabilitation. For instance, there were concerns that if PSP had a spouse or child at home while participating in telerehabilitation, they might feel that they did not have proper privacy during the session. PSP had varying opinions about the privacy and confidentiality of in-person spaces, expressing that the in-person spaces at the treatment facility could be detrimental to confidentiality as other clients could see them attending the treatment centre.

Theme 3 - Facets of care that lead to perceptions of credibility in telerehabilitation for providing services to PSP. The credibility of telerehabilitation, or the extent to which virtual delivery could be trusted to be effective for providing services to PSP with PTSI, was discussed by all participant groups. Telerehabilitation may or may not be perceived as legitimate when compared to in-person delivery in terms of providing the best quality care.

Subtheme A - Credibility of rehabilitation practices and techniques through telerehabilitation. Telerehabilitation may be suitable for certain types of psychological assessments. Some decision-makers expressed that telerehabilitation could be trusted for psychological screening, in part because “…the psychologist is able to get the information they need to make a treatment recommendation off of a screen…” (DM006). Completing psychological screens virtually could also have a positive effect on PSP and the accessibility of services since clinicians could assess PSP who were in other geographical locations. Additionally, some PSP preferred completing psychometric measures online because “…you can actually read and think about things a lot more…” (PSP007). However, timing could make a difference since some PSP felt they would not know how to complete the measures without in-person support. Although questionnaires could be administered virtually and various circumstances might demand flexibility in performing initial assessments, initial assessments were perceived to be more trustworthy if completed in person due to the complexity of PTSI combined with PSP’s circumstances and the need to get an accurate sense of responses in real-time.

Treatment of PTSI received via telerehabilitation “really depends on…the type of service that’s being offered…” (DM003). Although psychological counseling (e.g., cognitive behavioural therapy) could be “…super effective…” (DM003) via telerehabilitation, participants tended to agree that interventions directly involving management of psychophysiological signs and symptoms, such as exposure therapy, eye movement desensitization and processing (EMDR), and relaxation techniques were more effective and impactful in person. For example, CL014 voiced that “EMDR is a particularly difficult technique to administer virtually, because the range of eye tracking is difficult to simulate, particularly if someone’s [using video] on their phone or someone’s on a tablet.” All participant groups agreed that the most effective version of EMDR likely occurred in person.

Virtual service delivery could be used for exposure therapy, but once in vivo sessions were necessary, in-person delivery appeared to be more trusted than telerehabilitation. PSP010 expressed that “…the reason the OT was beneficial in person was because we did a lot of situational therapy” and that “…she [the OT] was already there, so it was easier for her to pick up and react to my physical cues as opposed to just watching me on the screen.” However, some clinicians viewed virtual care as an opportunity to provide different forms of exposure (i.e., photos, noises, walk-throughs using virtual mapping services) to start addressing the fear hierarchy sooner than they would in person. All groups felt that prolonged exposure therapy*, which is used quite often with this population, was best delivered in person. However, telerehabilitation could be appropriate at certain stages of that process when the focus of the interaction was psychoeducation and rapport building rather than exposures themselves.

As with prolonged exposure therapy, in-person service delivery could be essential once PSPs are reintegrating into the workplace and resuming their duties because “…that’s completely different than any other TPI [traumatic psychological injury] client, is the reintegration with the first responders” (DM008). In certain circumstances, however, telerehabilitation may be helpful in a limited capacity, such as when additional support was needed or when “… there’s not a lot of providers in that area” (DM008). Interdisciplinary PTSI rehabilitation programs, which require PSP to attend in person for different services, could have aspects effectively delivered virtually at certain points, such as education sessions, building rapport, and initiating behaviour activation at the beginning of the program. Some decision-makers and clinicians, however, felt that in-person delivery could better provide elements important for advancing behaviour activation, socialization, and appropriate monitoring and progression. Both groups commented that PSP going through the process of getting themselves to the program could itself be an integral part of rehabilitation.

1Although telerehabilitation provided flexibility for individualized progression and access to care, decision-makers described tension between ensuring access while enabling progress. Some decision-makers commented that PSP could regress with telerehabilitation and harm could be caused (as described in the safety theme) if social isolation and lack of behavioural activation continued and was left unchallenged.

Subtheme B - Detecting nuances when communicating over telerehabilitation. The recovery trajectories of PSP with PTSI are rarely straightforward, especially given intense physiological and psychological responses to a plethora of physical and social stimuli. These responses manifest in both overt and subtle verbal and non-verbal cues that provide essential information for accurate assessment and appropriate treatment. All participant groups expressed that clinicians’ ability to observe PSP during interventions and in different environments and situations was considered important for communication. Although some clinicians could be “…very good at picking up certain cues while you’re on [video] camera” (PSP007), all participant groups commented that clinicians could have a more difficult time picking up on body language and other non-verbal communication cues over telerehabilitation compared to in-person. This could negatively impact their ability to detect nuances in communication and hence, the PSP’s psychological and physical status. As CL005 expressed, “…it’s just easier to notice when you’re with a person I guess, like I said, those nonverbals.” Participants commented that the ability of clinicians to witness the whole body could be better in-person because they could pick up on “…tell signs…” (DM002) that contributed to more accurate, comprehensive, and responsive physical and functional assessment and treatment.

Case managers rely upon the information that clinicians provide because “…even if I did have eyes and ears on them, it doesn’t matter, because I can’t use that [information]. It has to be from those providers [clinicians]” (DM001). Therefore, clinicians and PSP being in close physical proximity could be very important for gathering information and recommendations that decision-makers could trust. This clarity also offered the opportunity to avoid misunderstandings, including those related to the consistency between PSP’s reports, what clinicians observed, and reports back to case managers. At minimum, video (i.e., not telephone) needed to be used for assessment and treatment: “…it’s harder to adjust in a session or tune into the person when you can’t see them. You can only hear them. So I think that visual element has been so important” (CL012).

Subtheme C - Nurturing the therapeutic relationship. All participant groups shared the belief that the connection between PSP and the clinician, regardless of the mode of delivery, was one of the most important aspects of treatment. The ability to build trust, be in the same physical space, and be flexible when responding to PSP within their particular circumstances were important.

Numerous PSP indicated that they felt like telerehabilitation was slightly less personable than in-person treatment, which made establishing a strong therapeutic bond more difficult or time-consuming: “I felt more connected to the providers I saw in person than anyone I saw virtually. And even if I saw them [visually] virtually it didn’t have that same feel to it” (PSP009). For some PSP, however, communicating through telerehabilitation did not seem to negatively impact their perceived therapeutic bond with their clinician. Similar to PSP, some clinicians found that building a therapeutic relationship was easier in-person, while others were adamant that nothing changed their ability to establish a therapeutic relationship over telerehabilitation if they kept their approach to relationship building the same. That said, a visual connection was viewed as essential for establishing meaningful connections with PSP, however, some felt that in-person interaction was still more effective in this regard, facilitating the “…intensity of the connection…” (DM005).

Subtheme D - Facilitating or blocking service provision. The effect of telerehabilitation on facilitating (or blocking) a service provision pathway and the implications for PSP and others involved in the rehabilitation process was another important dimension of credibility. Telerehabilitation could expedite and facilitate the continuity of services (e.g., by enabling scheduling pivots), which could assist with earlier diagnosis and treatment and access to specific providers and more diverse treatment, as well as fill gaps when providers are not available.

Telerehabilitation could make services more accessible; however, some reported that it was not always efficient, creating barriers and damaging credibility. Telerehabilitation could impact how PSP progress in their return-to-work process, which could affect return-to-work timelines. One decision-maker spoke about how the number of reintegration sessions with the OT increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, which they perceived was related to the exclusive use of telerehabilitation during that time. Decision-makers who were responsible for facilitating and supporting the PSP’s progress advocated for in-person services to address stalls in progress. Furthermore, telerehabilitation could also create more work for others. Case managers, for example, may need to make more wellness calls. Return-to-work facilitators in some organizations had to take on responsibilities usually completed by OTs. Some decision-makers expressed that clinicians needed to critically assess and set boundaries with PSP for appropriate use of telerehabilitation, with the eventual move to in-person, to provide the best quality of care.

Given that most participants felt there was a place for telerehabilitation when working with PSP, there did appear to be support for a hybrid system that combined telerehabilitation and in-person delivery to enable the flexibility to provide necessary services, at the right time, in the right context: “I think virtual is a great hybrid system for first responders with PTSD [posttraumatic stress disorder]. And anyone doing … any treatment that can be delivered online I think, is a fantastic thing to have nowadays” (PSP007).

Discussion

We interviewed PSP, clinicians, and decision-makers to understand the accessibility, safety, and effectiveness of telerehabilitation (i.e., videoconference and/or telephone) when rehabilitating PSP with PTSIs. Our analysis generated three key themes: (1) Responding to the uniqueness of individuals and their contexts; (2) Prioritizing different elements of safety at different times; and (3) Facets of care that lead to perceptions of credibility in telerehabilitation for providing services to PSP.

Our participants discussed how there is a constellation of circumstances that PSP have to navigate in their professional and personal lives, irrespective of their PTSI, that justifies the need for clinicians to provide personalized services. Telerehabilitation was viewed as a tool that can be used to individualize care. The impact of the PSP organizational and workplace culture on care-seeking behaviour is well established in the literature [29–36]. Stigma associated with seeking mental health care is inherent in the culture surrounding PSP with PTSI [29], making care inaccessible. The safety of reaching out for help, as well as whether and how PSP access services, is therefore impacted. Other authors echo this finding and have noted first responders or others with PTSIs worry about how discussing their traumas and mental health difficulties may raise questions about their fitness to work [29–36]. In a profession that is regularly understaffed and overworked, seeking mental health care is not only commonly seen as unfeasible, but it can also be looked down upon. Our participants also described the cumulative nature of the traumas faced by PSP, exemplifying their need for appropriate, accessible, and safe services. Various authors [29, 31, 33, 35] support this finding with discussion of frequent and prolonged [31] back-to-back trauma building over time [29, 33]. More support is necessary to reduce stigma of help seeking, facilitate timely access to clinicians who understand the unique lens needed to work with PSP, and ensure PSP will not be penalized in any way for asking for help.

Telerehabilitation was viewed as a tool that could help ensure PSP could reach appropriate care in a timely manner that was free from stigma and could be adapted to meet contextual factors that are present irrespective of their PSP occupation (i.e., living rurally, having caregiving demands). Other authors have noted how their participants expressed needing mental health support that had flexible scheduling and hours [24, 29, 30] and reduced patient cost and travel time [37], therefore promoting care. Turgoose and colleagues systematically reviewed 41 articles to provide an overview of lessons learned from using tele-therapy (video and phone-based care) to treat military veterans with PTSIs and found those receiving in-person treatments were more likely to dropout compared to those receiving tele-therapy due to logistical reasons such as poor weather, travelling barriers, and family obligations [38]. These findings are supported by other authors [39] further highlighting the need for flexibility when working with trauma-impacted populations such as PSP.

Physical safety was emphasized by all our participants. Some clinicians and decision-makers from our study worried about the physical safety of PSP during telerehabilitation sessions, as some felt that it was challenging to read body language and gauge dysregulation through video software. There is mixed literature on this topic, with several authors supporting the idea that body language and non-verbal cues were more challenging to gauge virtually (both over videoconference and phone-based care). However, some of our clinician participants did not view reading body language as a challenge with telerehabilitation. In a manuscript providing guidelines for implementing prolonged exposure therapy via telerehabilitation during the pandemic, Wells et al.. noted that providers can safely conduct prolonged exposure therapy for patients at low, moderate, and high risk of suicidality if appropriate measures are in place [24]. They propose a need for a backup means of communication for emergencies or technical difficulties [24], which our participants discussed having in place.

Beyond physical safety, PSP and clinicians recognized that receiving treatment in a location of their choosing enabled PSP to avoid professional spaces that were sometimes viewed as not therapeutic, subsequently enhancing psychological safety and increasing acceptability of services. Several authors agree that the home environment provides a feeling of safety [24, 37, 39, 40]. Kettlewell et al. found general trauma patients were more engaged in sessions when they occurred in the comfort of their own home due to improved anonymity and decreased stigma [37]. Gerber and colleagues highlight that providing care where the client feels safest is a facet of trauma-informed care that allows for clients to be more honest when sharing due to the protection provided by the space [39], therefore promoting trust in the encounter. However, clinicians and decision-makers were concerned about perpetuating avoidance. While telerehabilitation provides the opportunity to start treatment in a timelier fashion and in a more comfortable environment, exposure is a necessary component to facilitate recovery from a PTSI by combating avoidance. In other words, while several of our participants discussed opportunities for some elements of exposure therapy to be completed via telerehabilitation, most agreed that all avenues eventually needed to lead to some form of in-person care to ensure safe, efficient, and effective treatment and reintegration into work, and more broadly, society.

Establishing rapport and trust during therapeutic encounters between PSP and those involved in their rehabilitation was noted to be essential for success, regardless of the mode of service delivery. There were mixed views on whether telerehabilitation impacted the ability to develop strong and successful therapeutic relationships, with video capabilities enhancing presence between clinicians and PSP and therefore being viewed as a minimum criterion needed to establish relationships to ensure safe and effective treatment. The literature seems to suggest that telerehabilitation does not impact the ability to establish rapport and trusting, supportive therapeutic relationships [24, 36–39, 41]. Kettlewell and colleagues support the need for video capabilities at a minimum during remote interventions, as seeing someone’s face and gestures was seen as a facilitator of intervention delivery and contributed to the establishment of rapport [37] and a sense of presence. Wells et al.. note high patient and clinician ratings of satisfaction with therapeutic relationship during virtual care, which were comparable to those reported after in-person care [24].

Beyond the clinical interaction, decision-makers brought a unique lens to our study findings regarding credibility and efficiency of care in relation to mode of delivery. Some felt that telerehabilitation may or may not be as legitimate as in-person delivery in terms of providing the best quality care in certain circumstances. Some also noted that the inappropriate use of telerehabilitation could increase the workload of others involved in the rehabilitation process, while compromising care. If telerehabilitation is perceived to negatively impact the ability to provide certain rehabilitation practices and techniques safely and effectively, or if it could block service provision, there could be less desire to utilize it as a modality when providing care to PSP with PTSI. All participant groups, however, commented on its utility and areas where telerehabilitation works to ensure and establish accessible, timely, and trustworthy care. There ultimately needs to be a balance between ensuring care is provided in ways that consider broader consequences of telerehabilitation, while not dismissing its benefits.

A proposed framework for evaluating and implementing telerehabilitation in the rehabilitation of PSP with PTSI

Our comprehensive approach to data collection and analysis enabled us to construct a framework (Fig. 1) that provides a multi-layered view of factors to be considered when evaluating and using telerehabilitation with this population. This framework illustrates important factors to consider when deciding to use and evaluate telerehabilitation at team, organizational, and system levels.

Proposed framework for evaluating and implementing telerehabilitation in the rehabilitation of PSP with PTSI. Note. The framework highlights important considerations for clinicians, decision-makers, and PSP making decisions to use telerehabilitation at clinical, organizational and system levels

Our framework centres around the “complexity of PSP and the PTSI rehabilitation process’’. The three previously described themes form a triangle around the complexity and represent the considerations closest to the clinical, and policy and practice, decisions to use telerehabilitation. Moving outward, the four higher-level themes — Trajectory/Timing, Flexibility to Adapt, Presence, and Trustworthiness — surround the inner triangle themes. These themes, while influencing one another, cross-cut through the triangle themes and are core factors when making decisions about using telerehabilitation at both the clinical and health services levels. The outer hexagon illustrates six dimensions of quality of care — efficiency, effectiveness, acceptability, accessibility, safety, appropriateness [42]. These dimensions offer a perspective of health service delivery at the micro-, meso-, and macro- levels. Appendix 2 (found in supplemental materials) provides a description of the dimensions.

During analysis, we became cognizant and critical of how we were using quality of care language (e.g., acceptability, appropriateness) and one analyst realized that the Alberta Quality Matrix for Health [42] six dimensions of quality appeared to resonate with what participants were describing when considering the clinical interaction, and processes and policies at organizational and broader system level (e.g., WCB-AB). Remaining true to our inductive analytic approach and Interpretive Description,26 we did not intentionally use the matrix to frame our analysis, but continued to be reflexive, individually and collectively, about our interpretations and this language. Near the end of our analysis (Phases 5 and 6 of reflexive thematic analysis), once our thematic structure was stable and we were writing our report, we revisited the Alberta Quality Matrix for Health more explicitly as we considered the audiences that would take up and use the findings, notably, decision-makers working in the context of WCB-AB. We believed the quality dimensions aligned with our findings and also provided a bridge to make the findings more meaningful and useful for decision-makers at meso- and macro- levels. Ultimately, the inclusion of the six dimensions of the matrix helps to make sense and extend the reach of our findings to a system level, which furthers the overarching purpose of our Interpretive Description study—to generate findings that can be applied in the real-world [26].

Expanding on the above point, this framework can be applied at clinical, organizational, and system levels to help clarify a complex situation—how to best use telerehabilitation with PSP with TPI, and then how to evaluate its use. This ranges from making immediate clinical decisions, to developing processes and policies and then monitoring and evaluating of their implementation. For example, transportation and weather are often cited as factors that influence the decision to use telerehabilitation, especially with PSP with TPI as the traumatic situation may have involved driving. At the clinical level, the triangle theme ‘responding to the uniqueness of the individual and their context’ and the core factor, ‘flexibility to adapt’ can enable immediate clinical decisions to use telerehabilitation when transportation or weather is an issue for the PSP, and be combined with ‘trajectory/timing’ when considering the readiness of the PSP to drive. Regarding the same situation, organizations can develop implementation pathways integrating ‘flexibility to adapt’ and ‘trajectory/timing’ to help guide individual clinicians as well as create consistency and manage expectations across the organization with these sorts of decisions. These then roll into quality dimensions to address, for instance, ‘appropriateness’ and ‘safety’ when considering health services more broadly in the organization. Finally, system (e.g., WCB-AB) decision-makers can use the framework working from the health quality dimensions inward to develop policies for the use of telerehabilitation. For example, in the previous example the system-level decision-maker might identify ‘appropriateness’ and ‘safety’ as important dimensions, then use the core factors to further specify the policy. The performance monitoring and evaluation of TPI programs and system-level policies can be informed by the framework, especially if the framework was used to develop the programs and policies. The core factors and the health quality dimensions can be used to identify relevant and important key performance indicators for monitoring and by focusing evaluation objectives and constructing coherent questions for evaluations.

Practice and policy implications

Although telerehabilitation should not be the default mode of delivery, there is a need for critical decision-making in collaboration between PSP, the clinicians, and decision-makers. As we have described, the rehabilitation process for PSP with PTSIs is complex, so blanket rules for using telerehabilitation may not apply in every circumstance for all PSP and clinicians along the rehabilitation trajectory. Factors within the system play a role in deciding the mode of service delivery (e.g., availability of clinicians). Based on our findings, we believe that a hybrid system of service delivery would likely provide the best quality of care for this population moving forward. Since credibility comes from a growing evidence base, we also believe that building practices to measure the credibility (such as key performance indicators or structured monitoring tools) of telerehabilitation services consistently is important for ongoing monitoring and evaluation.

Limitations

Our study was not free from limitations. First, our sample was mainly related to one workers’ compensation context, which may limit the transferability of findings to other contexts and jurisdictions. However, given our fulsome analysis, inclusion of three distinct participant groups, and proposed framework, we believe the results may be transferable to populations without PTSIs and in contexts outside of Alberta. Second, we could have included more private practice, hospital, and community clinical perspectives to further round out the lived experiences we heard. While we recruited two community clinicians, more involvement from this group could have brought a unique private practice lens that may have enhanced the study findings. Third, we had hoped for more perspectives from decision-makers. Given the limited number of individuals fulfilling these roles and their already busy schedules, this was not feasible in our study timelines. Fourth, while our PSP sample included emergency response dispatchers, firefighters, police, and paramedics, the sample consisted of a higher proportion of corrections officers than other PSP groups, which may impact the transferability of the results to different contexts and populations. Fifth, our sample had varying degrees of technological literacy, with some receiving devices from workers’ compensation to support their telerehabilitation appointments. Varying levels of digital exclusion and technological literacy across contexts would limit the applicability and generalizability of our results. Finally, we did not differentiate between videoconference and telephone-based telerehabilitation during interviews. During the preamble to each interview, participants were told how we were defining telerehabilitation (i.e., care delivered via videoconference or phone). This may have impacted how participants viewed telerehabilitation or what they discussed during interviews. However, while distinguishing between videoconference and phone-based care was not a study objective, participants spoke about both when it applied to their particular experiences.

Conclusions

This study illuminates the complex landscape of telerehabilitation for PSP with PTSIs. While telerehabilitation offers accessibility and flexibility, which are vital for reaching diverse PSP, its application necessitates a nuanced, person-centred approach. For clinicians, this means carefully tailoring the mode of delivery to individual PSP needs, the specific therapeutic goals, and being acutely aware of the potential limitations in observing subtle cues and fostering the therapeutic alliance virtually. Decision-makers should consider policies that support a hybrid model of care, ensuring that PSP preferences are prioritized while also recognizing the potential need for in-person interventions to address PTSI-related avoidance. Ultimately, for PSP, telerehabilitation presents a valuable option for accessing timely care in comfortable settings, but open communication between clinicians and PSP about their preferences and any challenges experienced with virtual modalities is paramount. Future research should continue to explore best practices for integrating telerehabilitation into the continuum of care for PSP, including strategies for optimizing therapeutic alliance, accurately assessing risk remotely, and determining appropriate transitions between virtual and in-person services to ensure the delivery of effective and person-centred care.
