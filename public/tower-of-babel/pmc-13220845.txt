Integration of nuclear mechanosensing with integrin-extracellular matrix adhesions
Authors: Sandra Sandria, Megan C. King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDefining the role mechanical forces play in determining cell identity and function is imperative to interpreting cellular and physiological phenomena, as well as inspiring the development of novel therapeutics. As the field of mechanotransduction has grown in size and appreciation, there have been great strides in defining the molecular mechanisms by which cells respond to their mechanical environment. This includes characterization of the interface between the cell’s surface and the extracellular matrix (ECM) via integrin-based adhesions, as well as a deeper understanding of the nucleus as a force sensor capable of driving changes to gene expression. However, disentangling mechanosensitive cellular pathways has proven challenging, particularly defining if and how characterized cellular mechanosensors work in parallel or in unison. In this review, we will highlight the interplay between integrin-ECM adhesions and the nucleus, summarizing what is currently understood about how mechanical information is relayed and integrated across these two mechanoresponsive entities.

Introduction

Cellular mechanotransduction, defined as the conversion of mechanical inputs into downstream biochemical signals, is a well-appreciated phenomenon integral to a wide variety of physiological and pathogenic processes. Indeed, roles for physical forces have been observed in contexts ranging from embryonic development to tumorigenesis, cementing their importance in determining cellular identity, behavior, and function [1,2]. Depending on the specific characteristics of a particular tissue microenvironment, cells are subject to a variety of mechanical stresses and forces; these can be from the environment, such as fluid shear stress and compressive forces, or intrinsically from the cells themselves, including tension at cell adhesions or generated by the intracellular cytoskeleton [3,4]. Different kinds of mechanical stimuli have distinct effects on cells, making the ability to sense and respond appropriately integral for tissue development and subsequent maintenance of proper tissue function and composition [5].

In this review, we focus on cellular mechanosensation arising in response to mechanical properties of the extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM is a complex 3D structure composed of water, proteins, and polysaccharides, which acts as an important structural scaffold providing physical support for both tissue integrity and elasticity [6–8]. However, the ECM is not static; it undergoes constant restructuring and remodeling to maintain tissue-specific biochemical and biophysical properties that, in turn, regulate cell behavior [6,9]. These properties include the viscoelasticity (and its components elasticity and viscosity), porosity, and rigidity/stiffness of the ECM. Briefly, matrix elasticity refers to the ability of the matrix to revert to its original shape after being deformed by an external force, while viscosity describes the time it takes for the matrix to respond to applied force [10,11]. Rather than solely being an elastic solid, or a viscous liquid, the ECM is instead viscoelastic, meaning it is both immediately deformed by applied force (like an elastic solid) with an additional time-dependent component of deformation (like a viscous liquid) [12]. The porosity of the matrix refers to the size of the pores between ECM protein fibers, and the stiffness and rigidity of the matrix is defined as the ECM’s resistance to deformation when subjected to mechanical load or shear, respectively [8,13,14]. All of these properties are subject to constant change as ECM components are produced, enzymatically modified, crosslinked, and degraded [13,15–17]. Adding to the dynamism of the ECM, cells themselves actively respond to these physical and biochemical cues by modulating their intrinsic ECM biogenesis and remodeling activities, exerting their own forces on the ECM and mechanically altering ECM fiber organization, creating bi-directional mechanical feedback [18]. Importantly, disruption of this process and subsequent dysregulated ECM plays critical roles in cancer initiation, fibrosis, and cardiovascular diseases [19,20].

There are several mechanisms by which cells sense and respond to the various mechanical properties of the ECM. The first we will introduce is among the most highly characterized: cellular mechanotransduction via adhesion receptor proteins called integrins (Figure 1). Integrins allow cells to respond to biophysical cues from the ever-changing matrix by binding ECM component ligands with their extracellular domains – this bond is stabilized and strengthened by increased density and crosslinking of ECM components, both of which alter the mechanical environment of the ECM, enhancing properties such as matrix elasticity and stiffness [21,22]. Binding causes a conformational change of the integrin protein into a more open, ‘activated’ state that increases the affinity between the extracellular domain and the ECM ligand, as well as affinity between the integrin’s intracellular domain and proteins important for integrin-adhesion complex (IAC) formation [23,24]. IACs, which include focal adhesions (FAs), are large, multiprotein structures that facilitate interactions with the cytoskeleton and activate important downstream signaling in response to cues from the ECM, including changes in gene expression profiles determining cell and tissue identity [25,26]. Integrin clustering (a pre-requisite of IAC formation), IAC stabilization and maturation, IAC turnover, and IAC association with the actin cytoskeleton are all regulated by mechanical cues from the ECM, allowing cells to alter their behavior and function in a mechanically responsive manner [25,27–29]. The second set of mechanisms we discuss here centers on the nucleus; it is now well appreciated that as the cell’s largest and stiffest organelle the nucleus is an important hub of mechanotransduction [30,31]. Nuclear shape is responsive to the mechanical environment of the cell, for example, becoming more spherical and tall on soft substrates and ellipsoid and flat on stiffer substrates, coincident with observed changes to gene expression, cellular identity, and function [32,33]. The exact mechanisms by which mechanical forces drive nuclear deformations to potentially regulate gene expression and cellular behavior remain elusive, but there are several, likely not mutually exclusive, proposed models and hypotheses. This includes force-induced changes in conformation and phosphorylation states of nuclear envelope proteins, increased localization of mechanosensitive transcription factors to the nucleus, and mechanosensitive chromatin remodeling and genome re-organization [34–36]. Additionally, the nucleus is physically linked to the cytoskeleton via Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton (LINC) complexes, which are composed of inner nuclear membrane (INM) SUN proteins and outer nuclear membrane (ONM) Nesprin proteins (Figure 2). The presence of LINC complexes leads to an intuitive, appealing model for direct mechanotransduction in which forces are transmitted bidirectionally from the ECM to the nuclear interior [37–39]. A perennial challenge in the field of mechanotransduction is the need for experimental approaches that can discriminate between biochemical signaling downstream of tension on IACs and direct nuclear mechanosensing. Both of these mechanotransduction pathways regulate and are regulated by overlapping upstream and downstream biochemical signals; in addition, both impact cytoskeletal regulation and organization, adding another layer of complexity. Thus, untangling direct responses to environmental mechanical cues from possible indirect roles arising from the mechanical state of the cell is difficult [40,41]. Conversely, it has also been challenging to define how mechanosensation and the cellular response is coordinated between integrin-ECM adhesions and the nucleus, though there is growing evidence to support that mechanical information is relayed between the two. Here, we provide an overview of the current state of the field in regard to what is understood about how nuclear mechanosensing is integrated with integrin-ECM adhesions to control cellular mechanoresponses, touching also on instances of nuclear mechanosensation that are likely independent of tension on IACs.

Section 1: mechanosensation of ECM stiffness by integrin-extracellular matrix adhesions

To better understand how nuclear mechanosensation of the ECM is integrated with mechanical information from IACs, we will briefly describe in greater detail the components and characteristics of the ECM and how mechanosensitive IACs are formed in response to, and signal, these changes. For a more complete discussion of extracellular matrix mechanics and IAC composition and assembly, we refer the reader to several excellent recent reviews [8,18,25,42–44]. We also want to note that while there is important work defining how intrinsic properties of the ECM such as viscoelasticity, porosity, and composition are sensed by (and affect) cells, the most progress has been made in understanding cellular responses to changes in ECM stiffness, as a bulk of in vitro studies focus on manipulating and tuning this parameter [11,12,14,45–47]. Therefore, this will be the main biophysical property of the matrix we will focus on and discuss for the remainder of the review.

The physical composition and mechanical characteristics of the ECM are highly tissue dependent and are subject to continual remodeling in response to biochemical and mechanical signals [43]. Key regulators of these changes are resident cells inhabiting the matrix, most notably fibroblasts. Fibroblasts act in concert with other tissue resident cells to produce the structural ECM components, which are synthesized in the lumen of the rough endoplasmic reticulum and trafficked through the Golgi apparatus for secretion [42]. Numerous signaling pathways regulate the new synthesis of ECM components, including glycoproteins such as collagens, proteoglycans, elastin, and fibronectin; together these factors, and their organization and crosslinking, establish ECM stiffness [48]. A number of these synthesis pathways are activated by growth factors that are secreted by the cell and bound to glycosaminoglycan chains attached to the ECM – this includes the cytokine TGF-β1 that is secreted in latent form complexed with Latency Associated peptide (LAP) [49,50]. Binding of latent TGF-β1 by specialized receptors on the cell’s plasma membrane activates the TGF-β pathway; upon activation the effectors of the TGF-β pathway SMADs 2/3 translocate to the nucleus where they bind and activate the promoters of ECM genes such as COL1A1, COL3A1, COL5A2, COL6A1, and COL6A3 [51,52]. Importantly, the binding of TGF-β1 to TGF-β receptors and subsequent pathway activation is driven by ECM stiffness itself, as increased resistance from the ECM facilitates the mechanical release of latent TGF-β1 from its complex with LAP, a prerequisite for strong activation [53,54]. This results in a positive feedback loop as the cells produce more ECM in response to stiffness due to TGF-β pathway activation, resulting in more latent TGF-β1 being disassociated from LAP that in turn further activates TGF-β receptors [53,54]. Intriguingly, there is also evidence that matrix stiffness may directly regulate cellular metabolism in a way that further enhances the ability of cells to produce ECM components, as mouse dermal fibroblasts exhibit increased arginine and proline metabolism important for collagen biosynthesis, as well as increased collagen production, in response to in vitro and in vivo ECM stiffness. Defining the interplay between mechanics and metabolism remains an area of active study [42,55,56].

Cells also play an important role in the assembly of the ECM scaffold from secreted ECM components; while ECM components can spontaneously self-assemble, interactions between glycans and cellular proteins have been observed to be necessary for proper scaffold formation [42]. A well-characterized example of this phenomenon is that of fibronectin, which only forms a fibronectin fibril scaffold for other ECM meshworks after binding integrins and undergoing force-dependent conformational changes in response to cell contractility [57–,42,58,59]. Once secreted and assembled, ECM components then act as substrates for various enzymes secreted by resident cells, adding another layer of regulation to the mechanical characteristics of the ECM, for example, through posttranslational modifications. These enzymes include lysyl oxidases (LOX), transglutaminases, and hydroxylases, all of which crosslink ECM components to create stronger, stabler bonds between ECM molecules, influencing their arrangement, changing their mechanical properties, as well as providing resistance against proteolysis [60–64]. Proteolytic enzymes, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that control ECM turnover via degradation, are also secreted in a control manner [65]. Importantly, both the crosslinking and degradation of ECM components ultimately contribute to determining the stiffness of the ECM. A foundational study from the Weaver lab established associations between collagen crosslinking (including that via LOX activity) and ECM stiffness, while more recent work done in the context of colorectal and breast cancers has further elucidated that inhibiting LOX crosslinking activity decreases ECM stiffness in vitro and in vivo [21,66–68]. Similarly, other work has revealed that increased MMP activity leads to collagen degradation and is associated with subsequent ECM softening [10,11]. Overall, as synthesis, crosslinking and degradation together dictate ECM composition and properties (Figure 3), dysregulation of any of these processes can lead to an excess in ECM components and increased stiffness, as is the case in fibrotic disease. As mentioned in the introduction, integrins allow cells to sense and engage with their highly dynamic ECM by directly binding ECM components. Integrins are well suited for this purpose, functionally existing as heterodimers composed of an α and β subunit. Eighteen different types of α subunits and 8 different types of β subunits exist in at least 24 different combinations allowing binding to a wide variety of glycoproteins [69,70]. When in an inactivated state, integrin heterodimers canonically take on a bent, closed conformation, with the tails of their αβ subunits close together and engaged by inactivating cytoplasmic proteins [25]. Upon binding of the ECM by the integrin’s extracellular domains, critical intracellular adaptor proteins including vinculin and talin are recruited to facilitate the clustering of multiple integrins on the cell surface, leading to the formation of short-lived IACs called nascent adhesions [71]. Notably, integrin clustering itself is a mechanosensitive process, responsive to ECM ligand density and matrix rigidity [28]. Assembly of integrins into adhesions after binding of the ECM then drives an important conformational change in individual integrins into a more open and extended state, significantly increasing their affinity for ECM ligands and the integrin-ECM bond-lifetime, as well enhancing their affinity for intracellular adapter proteins (Figure 4) [71–74]. These specialized adapter proteins (of which hundreds have been identified and have been dubbed ‘the adhesome’ [75]) largely fall into two functional categories: 1) those that provide a stabilizing scaffold for the adhesion either at the plasma membrane or at the site of actin interaction, or 2) those that interact predominantly with actin to regulate its organization [76,77]. Upon recruitment of more adaptor proteins such as focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and paxillin, nascent adhesions mature into more stable, long-lived IACs, that are greater in size and stably linked to the cytoskeleton [70,78]. However, this maturation process is also dependent on (and responsive to) ECM stiffness and subsequent intracellular force generated by the cytoskeleton (Figure 4) [70,71]. Focal adhesions, a more mature class of IAC, are a well-studied example. While much of the spatial and structural information we have about cell-matrix adhesions has been limited by the resolution of confocal microscopy, we do know that focal adhesions are larger and more elongated (around 1–5 μm long, 300–500 nm wide, and on average 50 nm thick), and are strongly anchored to large actin stress fibers [79,80]. As cells probe and sense the changing rigidity of the matrix via their focal adhesions, their actin cytoskeleton is reorganized and they become contractile, deforming the surrounding ECM with their actin stress fibers [79]. Importantly, focal adhesion maturation is dependent on Rho-kinase mediated cytoskeleton contractility, and force generated by cells contracting the matrix further drives integrin activation and conformational changes in adapter proteins aiding adhesion complex formation and stabilization [81–85]. The magnitude of the force generated as the cells contract the ECM depends on the biophysical properties of the matrix; greater matrix stiffness provides more resistance to the contraction, generating greater force important for maturation of the adhesion and downstream processes regulating cellular identity and behavior [70,86]. The ability of cells to exert force on the ECM that scales with its stiffness also provides a means by which cells can ‘indirectly’ sense matrix mechanics. This response is crucial for the cell to properly adapt to its environment; the cell can adjust the tension exerted depending on the ECM properties, ultimately dictating downstream biochemical signaling as well as changes in the actin cytoskeleton important during development, cell differentiation, cell migration, and cell spreading [23,70,87,88]. Section 2: nuclear mechanosensation of tensile forces elicited by ECM stiffness

It has been broadly observed that when the cytoskeleton exerts force on the ECM, the nucleus is put under an ‘equal-but-opposite’ cytoskeletal stress inside of the cell, resulting in nuclear deformations and changes in nuclear shape and morphology [33,35,89–91]. The nucleus consists of three major components, chromatin, the nuclear lamina, and the nuclear membrane; all three are responsive to these mechanical forces [34]. Here, we will give a broad overview of what is currently understood about how the different components of the nucleus are altered in response to ECM stiffness before delving deeper into how they are integrated with integrin-extracellular matrix adhesions.

Chromatin

In addition to its well-established role as our genetic material, chromatin also possesses a non-genetic function as a mechanically important nuclear component providing rigidity crucial to maintaining nuclear shape, which in turn provides genome protection [92,93]. Work from our lab using isolated nuclei from S. pombe was the first to demonstrate that chromatin association with the inner nuclear membrane substantially contributes to nuclear stiffness and nuclear shape [94]. Since then, this observation has been further characterized in mammalian cells, as stretching of isolated nuclei has shown that chromatin governs lamin-independent strain-stiffening responses to short nuclear extensions modeling in vivo strain, and strong correlations between chromatin condensation and nuclear stiffness has been observed in embryonic stem cells [95–97].

It has also been suggested that force on integrins propagated via the LINC complex may be able to alter chromatin directly with potential impacts on gene expression. Using magnetic beads to apply local stresses to focal adhesions can drive chromatin displacements that correlate with the upregulated transcription of a DHFR transgene, as well as rapid induction of certain endogenous mechanosensitive genes consistent with the timescale in which force is propagated from the cell surface to the nucleus [98–101]. However, it remains a major challenge to definitively demonstrate meaningful changes in gene expression as a result of direct cytoskeletal force exerted on chromatin; integrin activation and tension on the nucleus from the cytoskeleton elicit global changes in biochemical signaling and remodeling of nuclear mechanics that could also contribute to observed changes in gene expression [31]. For example, while it has been observed that increased substrate elasticity can alter the ratio of euchromatin versus heterochromatin in cancer cells, it is still difficult to conclude that this response is reflective of mechanosensitive changes in gene expression rather than a nucleus-wide change in mechanical state to prevent nuclear rupture and to insulate genetic material from excess mechanical tension [102,103]. Likewise, despite the identification of a subset of immediate early genes (IEGs) that are strongly activated in response to nuclear deformations, this mechanism of gene regulation remains undefined and has yet to be satisfyingly disentangled from potential cytoplasmic signaling [31,104,105].

Despite these challenges, direct force-mediated regulation of chromatin remains an exciting concept with several interesting mechanistic questions to address in the future. One that is particularly pressing is the need to define how force may selectively activate specific genes over others given that forces (such as those from the ECM) are presumably transduced to large regions of the chromatin [36]. There are a variety of hypotheses to explain this phenomenon. Particular epigenetic modifications could prime specific genes for activation in response to mechanical stimuli, mechanical force could regulate the transcriptional machinery and promoter proximal pausing, or responsiveness to mechanical stimuli could depend on a gene’s specific location in the nucleus [31,99,106]. Addressing these mechanistic questions are shared high priorities for the field.

Nuclear lamina

In addition to chromatin, the nuclear envelope also intimately associates with the nuclear lamina, which is primarily composed of a meshwork of intermediate filaments (composed of A and B type lamins in mammals) residing at the nucleoplasmic face of the inner nuclear membrane (INM) [107]. Lamins, in concert with the INM protein lamin B receptor (LBR) and lamina-associated polypeptide 2 (LAP2), also represent major tethers for peripheral heterochromatin [108,109]. Lamins have a wide range of important functions, providing structural and mechanical support for the nucleus, contributing to genome organization by associating with chromatin in repressive lamina associated domains (LADs), and interacting with membrane proteins integrated into the INM as well as important transcriptional regulators [31,90,107,110–112]. The critical importance of the nuclear lamina is highlighted by the finding that an entire class of diseases (referred to collectively as laminopathies) are caused by mutations in lamins and their binding partners [113]. Intriguingly, lamins have well-established mechanosensitivity and are highly responsive to the state of cell mechanics, including the stiffness of the extracellular environment. Firstly, the Ig-like domain of lamin A/C undergoes conformational changes in response to matrix rigidity in a LINC-complex dependent manner, potentially altering interactions with important binding partners [31,114]. Additionally, there is abundant evidence that A-type lamin levels (through both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms) are increased concomitant with tissue stiffness, with mechanistic roles in regulating changes in chromatin remodeling and cell differentiation in response to matrix stiffness [90,97,115–117]. However, we do want to highlight that though several of these studies provide evidence of slower turnover of lamin A/C in stiff environments, recent work from the Buchwalter lab demonstrates slow turnover of lamin A/C in the context of adipose tissue, demonstrating that this slow turnover is also possible in certain soft tissue environments [118].

Nuclear pore complexes

Another component involved in nuclear mechanotransduction is the nuclear pore complex (NPC), the massive conduit that regulates the passage of molecules between the nucleus and cytoplasm through pores in the nuclear envelope. There is evidence that the regulated passage of transcription factors through NPCs contributes to several mechanosensitive pathways, including the TGF-β pathway described earlier, which relies on NPCs for translocation of the phosphorylated Smad complex into the nucleus [31,119,120]. The nuclear translocation of MKL1/MRTF, which is triggered by depletion of G-actin when the cell increases its F-actin network in a high tension state, allows for its association with SRF in the nucleus and the subsequent activation of cytoskeleton regulatory genes [41,121–123]. Similarly, the well-characterized mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators YAP/TAZ, which act downstream of the Hippo signaling pathway as well as various mechanical cues and cellular stress signals, also translocate to the nucleus thorough NPCs [124].

However, an emerging body of work rejects the notion of the NPC as a passive, static conduit. Instead, it has recently been recognized that the NPC may exist in different mechanical states that could influence the movement of mechanosensitive transcription factors, including YAP and Smad3, into the nucleus [120,125,126]. Although the mechanism by which more dilated NPCs would modulate nuclear import of these cargoes is still an area of ongoing investigation, recent work from our lab provides additional evidence for NPC mechanosensitivity; experiments utilizing pan-expansion microscopy showed clusters of dilated NPCs at the basal nuclear surface suggestive of regions of nuclear envelope tension that depend on LINC complexes [127]. Despite these initial observations, whether NPC mechanosensitivity depends on interactions with the nuclear lamina or other nuclear envelope proteins remains unknown, and addressing this question is an important next step in defining this intriguing mechanism.

Additional nuclear envelope proteins

In addition to the nuclear lamina and chromatin, other mechanosensitive integral membrane proteins embedded in the nuclear envelope are altered in phosphorylation state, turnover, and function in response to tension arising from substrate stiffness. This includes two of the LEM-domain proteins of the inner nuclear membrane, emerin and MAN1/LEMD3, and the LINC complex protein, SUN2. Emerin becomes tyrosine phosphorylated in response to tension forces induced on isolated nuclei by magnetic tweezers [128]. The Src kinase is known to elicit these same modifications, suggesting integration of mechanical and biochemical signals on the emerin phosphorylation state [129]. MAN1 has also demonstrated mechanosensitivity, antagonizing the TGFβ–Smad2/3 signaling pathway in a stiffness-dependent manner [130]. Lastly, extracellular matrix rigidity has been suggested to stabilize SUN2, raising the possibility of a feed forward mechanism in which substrate stiffness enhances force transduction to the nucleus [131].

Section 3: how do integrin-extracellular matrix adhesions communicate mechanical signals from the ECM to the nucleus?

There are multiple mechanisms by which mechanical information sensed by the cell via tension on IACs is integrated with the nucleus, one of which has already been largely described above: force on integrins and subsequent formation of IACs results in remodeling of the actomyosin cytoskeleton and signaling cascades that drive activation and/or nuclear translocation of mechanosensitive transcription factors (e.g. the Rho kinase, Smads, YAP/TAZ, and MKL1/MRTF) that activate specific gene expression profiles, allowing the cell to respond accordingly to its mechanical environment [132,133]. These integrin-dependent, canonical biochemical signaling pathways are well characterized; we refer to these as indirect nuclear mechanotransduction mechanisms [41].

There is also a proposed model of direct nuclear mechanotransduction that is much less understood, albeit attractive; in this model, force is propagated from integrin-ECM adhesions through the actin cytoskeleton and to the nuclear interior via LINC complexes [134,135]. Early studies of nuclear mechanotransduction demonstrated that tugging on integrins through association of RGD peptide-coated beads manipulated by micropipettes led to reorientation of cytoskeletal elements and nuclear deformation [135,136]. Now, with the understanding of the physical and biochemical composition of the LINC complex, there is an intuitive link between integrin-extracellular matrix adhesions and the nuclear interior [137]. Indeed, experiments from Jan Lammerding’s lab applying cytoskeletal tension to cells with disrupted LINC complexes demonstrated that LINC complexes were required for intracellular force transmission and cellular processes dependent on it (eg. nuclear positioning, cell motility, and cytoskeletal organization) [138]. While several studies have documented changes in mechanosensitive gene expression upon the disruption of the LINC complex, the inference that these effects reflect a loss of a direct mechanotransduction is hampered by the coincident effects on the organization and state of all elements of the cytoskeleton, which are well-documented [91,139–142]. Given the established importance of the cytoskeleton in cellular mechanotransduction and the regulation of gene expression, dissecting the direct and indirect effects of the LINC complex has been (and continues to be) a challenge [143].

In one of our recent attempts to unravel this complexity, we showed that tension transduced via the LINC complex is important for repressing the epidermal differentiation program in basal keratinocytes that specifically depends on β1-integrin [144]. This work also provided hints that actomyosin contractility is not sufficient to induce tension on LINC complexes, at least in some contexts. In keratinocytes without β1 integrin expression, β6 integrin is still expressed, and these cells are in fact hypercontractile; nonetheless, LINC complexes are relaxed. Likewise, differentiated keratinocytes with abundant stress fibers integrated into cell-cell junctions also manifest with low LINC complex tension and a relaxed A-type lamin network. Additionally, a recent study reinforces a special relationship between β1-integrin and the LINC complex through the identification of a novel contact site in which a basal actin spot links the nucleus to the plasma membrane in 3D breast acini [145]. Striking co-localization and enrichment of both SUN2 and β1 integrin with the actin spot, as well as loss of this concentrated distribution of SUN2 in acini lacking an actin spot, provides support for a model in which both proteins are closely engaged with the actin network [145].

Additional evidence for direct integration comes from the study of cells like fibroblasts that, when cultured in stiff environments, integrate their nucleus with focal adhesions through an actin filament ‘cap’ above the apical nuclear surface – this perinuclear actin cap tightly regulates nuclear shape and is dependent on actomyosin contractility for its function and the LINC complex for its tethering to the nucleus [146–148]. A small subset of focal adhesions terminate their fibers in this perinuclear actin cap, differing from conventional focal adhesions and therefore potentially specialized for these nuclear interactions [149].

We do want to note that much of the work done to understand the relationship between IACs and the nucleus in mechanosensing involves disruption of LINC complexes either via genetic knockout of particular LINC complex proteins or dominant-negative SUN/KASH over-expression approaches. Both of these experimental approaches have caveats, as genetic ablation of LINC complex proteins can lead to compensatory effects from up-regulation of alternative SUN/nesprin isoforms; while dominant-negative approaches are useful for understanding the impact of disrupting all LINC complexes, they do little to reveal the role of individual LINC complex components and complexes [150,151]. Additionally, disruption of LINC complexes as a means of modeling disruption of tension on the nuclear envelope may prove insufficient, as other axes between the nucleus and the cytoplasm have been observed, including a lamina-nucleolar-ribosomal axis [152].

Additionally, not all force transduction to the nucleus is adhesion-dependent. Again, cells are subject to a variety of forces depending on their tissue contexts. Compressive forces relevant for chondrocytes and cardiomyocytes, for example, do not depend on signaling through integrins [153,154]. Transmembrane actin-associated nuclear (TAN) lines, formed by LINC complex proteins coupled with rearward-moving actin cables, allow for nuclear positioning toward the cell rear and force transduction from the cytoskeleton, and are not directly connected to (or dependent on) focal adhesions [155]. In addition, a growing number of studies illustrate key roles for lamins and LINC complexes in non-adherent cells, raising the possibility that nuclear mechanosensing plays key roles in cells of the hematopoietic system. T cells are among one of the best characterized immune cells in regard to understanding LINC complex function and the role of nuclear lamins; for example, both the LINC complex and A-type lamins were found to mediate T cell immunological synapse formation and activation by driving actin polymerization, and T cells lacking lamins are unable to activate and proliferate properly [156,157]. Recent work in the context of the lung also found that lamin A/C in macrophages is protective against nuclear envelope rupture associated with aging, potentially by increasing the stiffness of the nucleus to counter repetitive deformation as macrophages undergo migration through constricting environments [158]. This finding further emphasizes the potential importance of nuclear mechanics, and likely nuclear mechanosensing, even in cell types that have minimal cell-matrix adhesions.

Lastly, in addition to being adhesion-independent, cellular forces can also be actomyosin contractility independent. For example, when treated with blebbistatin, neutrophils undergo a decrease in cytoskeletal tension but an increase in plasma membrane tension, and while nascent adhesions in fibroblasts have weaker interactions with actin filaments they are capable of strong propulsive forces [159–161]. Indeed, changes in osmotic pressure are capable of changing nuclear size, volume, and chromatin organization independently of actomyosin contractility [162,163].

Section 4: does the nucleus and its mechanics influence adhesion assembly?

While there is extensive characterization of an outside-in model of force transduction from integrin-ECM adhesions to the nucleus, less appreciated is the bidirectionality of this signaling (Figure 5). Indeed, the nucleus is a large, stiff, organelle; its integration with the cytoskeleton could therefore influence the way in which the engaged actin network, microtubules that regulate the turnover of adhesion complexes, and intermediate filaments together impact IACs [164,165]. A first hint comes from our prior work in which we demonstrated that Sun2 null keratinocytes fail to properly organize their microtubule network, resulting in defective cell-cell adhesions [142]. This disruption of proper intercellular adhesion targeting and function resulted in mice with defective hair follicle structure and alopecia, demonstrating the importance of the LINC complex’s integration with adhesions and the cytoskeleton for epidermal tissue integrity [142]. In this work, we tied SUN2 to a role for microtubules; one model is that forces from microtubule polymerization on adhesions at the plasma membrane that drive turnover require microtubule coupling to the nucleus. Other groups have made similar findings; SUN1 in endothelial cells is required to coordinate peripheral microtubule dynamics, regulating Rho activation and stabilizing cell-cell junctions [166]. Work from the Carroll lab provides additional evidence for the importance of Sun proteins in cytoskeletal organization and adhesion formation, showing that SUN2 containing LINC complexes activate RhoA, promoting focal adhesion assembly through cytoskeletal regulation [167]. Intriguingly, SUN1 antagonized SUN2-mediated RhoA activation as well as focal adhesion assembly, demonstrating that different forms of LINC complexes can have antagonistic roles [167].

Several studies have reported that depleting isoforms of nesprins (the cytoplasmic aspect of LINC complexes) also drives dysregulation of integrin-ECM adhesion complexes. siRNA knockdown of nesprin-1 in colon cancer cells negatively affects cell migration, an adhesion dependent process, while depletion of nesprin-2 G in fibroblasts results in impaired TAN line formation, decreased focal adhesion size and number, and reduced generation of traction force [168,169]. A dominant-negative construct of an isolated KASH domain, used to disrupt all LINC complexes, altered cell-matrix adhesion and focal adhesion dynamics in endothelial cells, while nesprin-1 depletion in the same cell line increased the number of focal adhesions and traction force, demonstrating that different nesprin isoforms play distinct roles [89,151]. All of this evidence combined makes a case for the physiological importance of LINC complexes, and perhaps tension transduced from the nucleus to the plasma membrane, in the regulation of cell-ECM adhesions.

One recently appreciated mechanism by which LINC complexes at the nucleus influence the state of the actin cytoskeleton and adhesions is through a role for nesprin-2 in driving ‘inside-out’ actin bundling. There is a direct relationship between the formin FHOD1 and nesprin-2 giant that collaborate to mediate TAN line formation and nuclear movement [170]. Interestingly, silencing of FHOD1 expression did not affect the formation or rearward flow of dorsal actin cables required for nuclear positioning. Instead, FHOD1 disruption prevented the formation of TAN lines and nuclear movement, appearing to mainly function as a physical link to actin [170]. In recent follow-up work, the group has found evidence that nesprin-2 directly promotes actin bundling in concert with FHOD1 [145]. Thus, LINC complexes can influence both the state of the actomyosin cytoskeleton and feedback into adhesion regulation.

Lastly, there is also an important suggestion that the uncoupling of the state of the nucleus and adhesions can contribute to disease states, such as cancer. One example includes work leveraging matched isogenic normal and invasive fibroblasts in the setting of cancer that showed differences in the coordination between nuclear forces and contractile forces [171]. Invasive fibroblasts exerted more contractile forces on their environment while exhibiting decreased force on their nuclei [171]. These cells also display changes in cytoskeletal organization, shape, and directional cell migration, suggesting that force transduction between cell matrix adhesions and the nucleus is altered by the transformation of a normal fibroblast into an invasive fibroblast [171]. The role that altered mechanosensitive dynamics between IACs and the nucleus play in various disease states is a promising avenue for future studies.

Outlook

In this review, we discussed what is currently understood about how nuclear mechanosensation and integrin-containing adhesions at the plasma membrane are integrated to dictate cellular responses to changes in extracellular matrix stiffness. Cellular mechanotransduction is complex as it is rich with integrative mechanisms and many overlapping layers of regulation. It can therefore be quite challenging to parse out what the individual contributions are of a particular mechanosensitive pathway or protein to the overall cellular response. Thus, a key future direction will be to define the independent contributions of force exerted on the nucleus versus force exerted on adhesion complexes and how the two are co-regulated. While great progress has led to the appreciation that the nucleus is a critical component of cell mechanosensation and mechanotransduction, there remains much to learn and understand. However, what will continue to motivate this field is the exciting, interdisciplinary nature of the work. It will take cell biologists working with engineers, biophysicists, material scientists, and computational biologists (and more) to tackle these difficult questions. Equipped with more tools than ever, the secrets of the nucleus under tension are ours to discover!
