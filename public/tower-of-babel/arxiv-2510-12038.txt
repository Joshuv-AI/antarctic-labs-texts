Abstract
. We explicitly connect (discrete-time) quantum walks on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with a four-state Markov additive process via a Feynman-type formula ( 2.13 ). Using this representation, we derive a relation between the spectral decomposition of the Markov additive process and the limiting density of the homogeneous quantum walk. In addition, we consider a space-time rescaling of quantum walks, which leads to a system of quantum transport PDEs in continuous time and space with a phase interaction term. Our probabilistic representation for this type of PDE offers an efficient Monte Carlo computational technique.

1.
Introduction

There are two types of quantum walks, continuous-time and discrete-time quantum walks. In this paper, we only discuss discrete-time quantum walks which were introduced by
Aharonov et al., 1993
and began to draw attention to the quantum computing community since the paper
Ambainis et al., 2001
. We refer readers to the numerous surveys
Kempe, 2003
,
Venegas-Andraca, 2012
,
Konno, 2020
,
Zhou, 2021
,
Kadian et al., 2021
, ordered from the earliest to the most recent, and the references therein for detailed development of the literature of quantum walks.

Despite being defined to quantize classical random walks, quantum walks are deterministic dynamics and were also termed “quantum cellular automata” by
Meyer, 1996
; they are well-known to behave very differently from classical random walks. The characteristic example is its famous ballistic long-time behavior with a bimodal weak limit (see for example
Konno, 2005
,
Grimmett et al., 2004
), which is in big contrast to the diffusive behavior driven by the classical central limit theorem. Thus, it is not surprising that techniques for studying quantum walks are rather non-probabilistic. They include, but are not limited to, path-counting and orthogonal polynomials
Konno, 2005
, Fourier transform
Grimmett et al., 2004

Maeda et al., 2018

Strauch, 2006
, the energy method
Maeda and Suzuki, 2020
and matrix analysis
Cantero et al., 2012
(see also the survey
Konno, 2020
).

In addition, the ballistic behavior described in the previous paragraph is also reflected by the possibility of a hyperbolic space-time rescaling of the quantum walk to a system of transport PDEs with a phase interaction term. This was first stated and termed “quantum transport PDEs” in
Molfetta and Debbasch, 2012
(see also
Maeda et al., 2018
for discussions of this type of Dirac PDEs). We refer the reader to the recent book
Molfetta, 2024
and references therein for developments in Physics between quantum walks and transport PDEs.

Our main contribution is to write a Feynman-type representation for quantum walks, bringing in probabilistic techniques for studying them. With our Feynman formula, we explicitly recognize the amplitude of a quantum walk to be a conditional expectation of a classical four-state Markov additive process. Using the formula, we are able to relate the spectral decomposition of the Markov additive process and the limiting density of quantum walks. In addition, we provide a probabilistic representation for the quantum transport PDE in terms of continuous-time scaling limits of the aforementioned four-state Markov additive process. This offers an efficient Monte Carlo method for such PDE.

Connections between classical processes and quantum walks have been widely studied in the literature. One direction is the problem of mimicking. Since quantum walks are unitary dynamics, taking modulus squares to the amplitudes of states in each step gives a flow of probability marginals if the walk is initialized with a normalized state. It has been studied in
Montero, 2017
and
Yamagami et al., 2022
how to match this flow of marginals with a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain. In addition, it is well-established in the continuous-time literature (see
Carlen, 1984
) that flow of marginals induced by a Schrödinger equation can be mimicked by a homogeneous continuous-time Markov process, called a conservative diffusion. In addition, to incorporate the effect of decoherence to quantum walks,
Attal et al., 2012
introduced open quantum walks; by repeatedly measuring and running an open quantum walk, one naturally obtains quantum trajectories, which are classical stochastic processes. See the survey article
Sinayskiy and Petruccione, 2019
and the introduction section in
Carbone et al., 2022
for details.
Finally, by introducing a walk-type zeta function,
Konno and Sato, 2012
,
Konno et al., 2023
,
Komatsu et al., 2022
recognized universal principles underlying both quantum walks and classical walks with Markov increments so that the walks can be handled in a unifying scheme. We remark that the 4-state Markov additive process induced from our Feynman formulae does not come from any measurement procedure and we intend to use it as a technique to uncover properties of quantum walks instead of comparing with quantum walks under a unifying scheme. Therefore, we believe that the relation between classical processes and quantum walks that we propose is novel and useful.

The paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we recall the definition of a one-dimensional quantum walk, derive our main Feynman formula for the rotation coin
e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}
, and comment on potential Monte Carlo simulations. Next, in Section
3
, we derive a relation between the spectral decomposition of the Markov additive process
and the limiting density of the homogeneous quantum walk , and we demonstrate the derivation of the weak limit for the long-term behavior of quantum walks. In Section
4
, we consider a space-time rescaling of quantum walks, which leads to a system of transport PDEs in continuous time and space, with a phase interaction term. Monte Carlo simulations are very efficient to approximate the solution of such a system. Finally, in Section
5
, we propose extensions to our Feynman formula to more general quantum walks, which include more general homogeneous coins, time-dependent coins (see for example
Bañuls et al., 2006
) and site-dependent coins (see for example
Wójcik et al., 2004
,
Linden and Sharam, 2009
,
Maeda et al., 2018
and
Maeda and Suzuki, 2020
).

Notations

For common notations in the quantum literature, see
Nielsen and Chuang, 2010
and
Hall, 2013
for a more quantum computational and quantum mechanical account respectively. Here we mainly follow notations in

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and denote its unit sphere
S(\mathcal{H}):=\{x\in\mathcal{H}:\norm{x}_{\mathcal{H}}=1\}
. The space of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
L(\mathcal{H})
and the space of unitary operators by
U(\mathcal{H})
.

An alphabet is any set
\Sigma

(2.1)

\displaystyle\ell^{2}(\Sigma):=\{f:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}\mid\norm{f}_{\ell_{2}}^{2}:=\sum_{x\in\Sigma}\absolutevalue{f(x)}^{2}<\infty\},

the complex Hilbert space indexed by

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle:=\sum_{x\in\Sigma}f(x)\overline{g(x)},

and standard orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}:x\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{x=i}\}_{i\in\Sigma}

\{e_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\Sigma}
. If

\{\psi(x)\}_{x\in\Sigma}
via

\displaystyle\psi=\sum_{x\in\Sigma}\psi(x)e_{x}.

Unless otherwise specified, any matrix representation of

is with respect to the standard basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\Sigma}
. For the alphabet

, the ordered basis is always
\{e_{1},e_{-1}\}
. Common unitary matrix representations for

include:

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

One-dimensional quantum walks

In this section we introduce the main object of the paper.

Definition 2.1

(Quantum walks on
\mathbb{Z}
)
.

Quantum walks on
\mathbb{Z}
are discrete-time dynamics of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}=W\psi_{n-1}\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1;\quad\psi_{0}\in L(\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes\ell_{2}(\{\pm 1\})),

W:=SC\in L(\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes\ell_{2}(\{\pm 1\}))

x\in\mathbb{Z}
and the shift operator
S
, which is defined by
Se_{x}e_{z}=e_{x+z}e_{z}
for every
x\in Z,z=\pm 1

S

W
itself is unitary.

2.3.
Deriving the Feynman formula

In this section we derive the Feynman formula we advertised. We begin with the Feynman-path representation:

Lemma 2.3
.

For a homogeneous quantum walk on the 1-D lattice with coin
C_{x}=e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}
, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!}\psi_{0}(x_{n},z_{n}),

where
(x_{n},z_{n})
are being defined by
z_{m}:=(-1)^{k_{m}}z_{m-1}
and
x_{m}:=x_{0}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z_{j}
for
m\geq 1
with
(x_{0},z_{0}):=(x,z)
.

Proof.

First, applying the walk operator on a basis element gives

\displaystyle We_{x}e_{z}=Se^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}e_{x}e_{z}=S\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k}}{k!}e_{x}\sigma_{1}^{k}e_{z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k}}{k!}e_{x+(-1)^{k}z}e_{(-1)^{k}z}.

Then for any state
\psi
, we obtain from

\displaystyle W\psi

\displaystyle=W\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},z\in\{\pm 1\}}\psi(x,z)e_{x}e_{z}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},z\in\{\pm 1\},k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(i\theta)^{k}}{k!}\psi(x,z)e_{x+(-1)^{k}z}e_{(-1)^{k}z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},z\in\{\pm 1\},k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{(i\theta)^{k}}{k!}\psi(x-z,(-1)^{k}z)e_{x}e_{z}

that

(2.7)

\displaystyle(W\psi)(x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k}}{k!}\psi(x-z,(-1)^{k}z).

Repeatedly applying (
2.7
) on
\psi_{n}:=W^{n}\psi_{0}
where
n\geq 1
gives

(2.8)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!}\psi(x-z-(-1)^{k_{1}}z-\cdots-(-1)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n-1}}z,(-1)^{k_{n}}z)

which is exactly the required identity.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The coin
C=e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}
is periodic in
\theta
with period
2\pi
. In the following we restrict
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
where we exclude the trivial case
\theta=0\,\pmod{2\pi}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that (
2.6
)
can be re-written as

(2.9)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x_{0},z_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}i^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}\frac{\theta^{k_{1}}\cdots\theta^{k_{n}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!}\psi_{0}(x_{n},z_{n}).

The Poisson-like coefficients in (
2.9
) and the fact that the factor
i^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}
depends only on
(k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n})\pmod{4}
motivate the following definition.

Definition 2.6
.

Introducing a sequence of i.i.d. Poisson random variables
(N_{1},N_{2},\cdots)
with parameter
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
, we define the Markov additive model
1
1

1

A Markov additive model is a triple that consists of a Markov chain, a real-valued function of it and a sum of that real-valued function of the Markov chain (see for instance
Ney and Nummelin, 1987a
and
Ney and Nummelin, 1987b
).

(S_{n},Z_{n}:=f(S_{n}),X_{n})\in\{0,1,2,3\}\times\{\pm 1\}\times\mathbb{Z}
by

(2.10)

\displaystyle S_{0}=0,\quad S_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}N_{j}\pmod{4}\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1,

(2.11)

\displaystyle Z_{0}=z,\quad Z_{n}=f(S_{n})Z_{0},\quad f(s):=(-1)^{s}\quad\mbox{for}\quad s\in\{0,1,2,3\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad n\geq 1,

(2.12)

\displaystyle X_{0}=x,\quad X_{n}=X_{n-1}-Z_{n-1}=\cdots=X_{0}-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}Z_{j}\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1.

Our main result is as follows:

Theorem 2.7

(Feynman Formula)
.

For the homogeneous quantum walk on the 1-D lattice with coin
C_{x}=e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have:

(2.13)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x,z)

\displaystyle=e^{n\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{S_{n}}\psi_{0}(X_{n},Z_{n})\mid(S_{0},Z_{0},X_{0})=(0,z,x)\right]

for
(n,x,z)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\{\pm 1\}
, where
(S_{\cdot},Z_{\cdot},X_{\cdot})
are
in Definition
2.6
.

Proof.

Starting with the result in Lemma
2.3
, and using Poisson distributions, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x_{0},z_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!}\psi_{0}(x_{n},z_{n})

\displaystyle=e^{n\theta}\sum_{k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}=0}^{\infty}(i)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k_{1}}\cdots e^{-\theta}\theta^{k_{n}}}{k_{1}!\cdots k_{n}!}\psi_{0}(x_{n},z_{n})

\displaystyle=e^{n\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{S_{n}}\psi_{0}(X_{n},Z_{n})\mid(S_{0},Z_{0},X_{0})=(0,z,x)\right],

which is formula (
2.13
) for
n\geq 1
with
x_{0}=x
and
z_{0}=z
.
∎

2.4.
Monte Carlo simulations

It is very tempting to use our formula (
2.13
) to implement a Monte Carlo computation of the wave function
\psi_{n}
or its square modulus to approximate the probability of presence. By brute force, we would generate many realizations of the Poisson random variables
(N_{1}^{(k)},\cdots,N_{n}^{(k)})
for
k=1,\cdots,M
, and then obtain

\psi_{n}(x,z)\approx e^{n\theta}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}i^{S^{(k)}_{n}}\psi_{0}(X^{(k)}_{n},Z^{(k)}_{n}),

where
(S^{(k)}_{n},Z^{(k)}_{n},X^{(k)}_{n})
are computed from
(N_{1}^{(k)},\cdots,N_{n}^{(k)})
according to the formulas in Definition
2.6
. Well, we tried and that doesn’t work well unless using a huge unrealistic number of realizations even for a relatively small time
n
. The detailed study of the sample size to compute it with stochastic simulation methods that include importance sampling, e.g.,
Chatterjee and Diaconis, 2018
is an ongoing project. In fact, we are in a comparable situation with the stochastic model for the telegrapher’s equation described by the great Mark Kac in
Kac, 1974
.
Here is what he wrote about implementing a Monte Carlo computation for a discrete time transport system:

“This is a completely ridiculous scheme. Because in order to have good accuracy
\Delta t
had better be small. I don’t know how small, but certainly you ought to discretize reasonably well. But that means the probability of reversing directions is very close to zero. It is a very unlikely event. This is the type of situation which is very difficult to handle by any kind of Monte Carlo technique. Because with such a small probability you would have to have an enormous number of particles. You would need a really ridiculous number. Otherwise, the fluctuation will be enormous.”

In fact, for our system, due to its Schrödinger nature, the situation is even worse as cancellations between small probabilities are happening and crucial in getting the right answer. This will be made very precise in Section
3
.

However, in this same article, Mark Kac suggests a rescaling of the system to pass to the continuous-time limit. He obtained a probabilistic representation of the solution of a transport system of Boltzmann type and observed that in this limiting situation, Monte Carlo technique makes perfect sense and is amazingly efficient. This has been the basis and widely used technique for solving transport equations with Boltzmann interactions (see
Cessenat et al., 1989
).

We will perform such a rescaling of our model to pass to the continuous-time limit and identify a limiting system of PDEs of Schrödinger transport type for which our probabilistic representation is an efficient Monte Carlo scheme. This will be presented in Section
4
.

3.
Relation between quantum walks and spectral properties of Markov additive models

In this section we concentrate on the case of a quantum walk with homogeneous coin operator
C=I\otimes e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}
.

3.1.
Motivation - ballistic weak limit of quantum walk

If we start the quantum walk described in Theorem
2.7
with the initial state
\psi_{0}\in S(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\{\pm 1\}))
, that is
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}(\absolutevalue{\psi_0(x, 1)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\psi_0(x, -1)}^{2})=1
, then also
\psi_{n}\in S(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\{\pm 1\}))
since the walk operator
W=SC
is unitary. It follows that we can define random variables
\Xi_{n}
supported on
\mathbb{Z}
with probability mass functions defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Xi_{n}=x)=\absolutevalue{\psi_n(x, 1)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\psi_n(x, -1)}^{2},\quad x\in\mathbb{Z},\quad n\geq 0.

Observe that sampling from
\Xi_{n}
is obtained by measuring the underlying quantum system at time
n
, and therefore does not define a process in time. In other words
\Xi_{n}
is only a flow of marginals.

It first appeared in Konno’s work
Konno, 2005
that if we start with the state

(3.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x,z):=R\mathbbm{1}_{x=0,z=1}+L\mathbbm{1}_{x=0,z=-1}\quad\mbox{where}\quad(R,L)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\absolutevalue{R}^{2}+\absolutevalue{L}^{2}=1,

there exists a weak limit for the sequence of random variables
{\Xi_{n}}/{n}
with the limiting probability density function on the real axis given by
f_{K}\cdot\omega_{\psi_{0}}
, where

(3.3)

\begin{split}f_{K}(y;\theta)&:=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{(1-y^{2})\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta-y^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\absolutevalue{y}\leq\cos\theta},\\
\omega_{\psi_{0}}(y)&:=1+(\absolutevalue{R}^{2}-\absolutevalue{L}^{2}+2\tan\theta\imaginary(R\overline{L}))y,\quad y\in\mathbb{R},\end{split}

which could be found in, for instance,
Konno, 2005
;
Sunada and Tate, 2012
with slightly different notations. Indeed one can check that the function
f_{K}\cdot\omega_{\psi_{0}}
is non-negative and integrates to one over
\mathbb{R}
.
In order to derive this convergence, we characterize the distribution of
{\Xi_{n}}/{n}
by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(y_{1}<\frac{\Xi_{n}}{n}\leq y_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,ny_{1}<x\leq ny_{2}}(\absolutevalue{\psi_n(x, 1)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\psi_n(x, -1)}^{2})

\displaystyle=n\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{1}\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{2}\right\rfloor}(\absolutevalue{\psi_n(\ceil{an}, 1)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\psi_n(\ceil{an}, -1)}^{2})\mathrm{d}a,

for every
y_{1}<y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In Figure

2.3
), and on the right we plot the limiting density

in (

) with
\theta=\pi/4
,

in (
3.3
) with
\theta=\pi/4
,
R=L=1/\sqrt{2}
.

On the other hand, given the four-state Markov chain
(S_{n})
defined in Definition
2.6
supported in
\Omega:=\{0,1,2,3\}
, we can define
T_{n}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{S_{j}}
for every
n\geq 1
and
T_{0}:=0
.

Writing the Feynman formula (
2.13
) using the pair
(S_{n},T_{n})
gives:

Proposition 3.1
.

Denoting
\psi_{n}^{\xi}
the nth step states for the quantum walk with initial condition
\psi_{0}^{\xi}(x,z):=\mathbbm{1}_{x=0,z=\xi}
, we have for every fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{\xi}(x,z)=\begin{cases}e^{n\theta}[\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=xz,S_{n}=0)-\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=xz,S_{n}=2)],\quad z=\xi\\
ie^{n\theta}[\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=xz,S_{n}=1)-\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=xz,S_{n}=3)],\quad z=-\xi,\end{cases}

for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and
z=\pm 1
.

Proof.

Substituting the relations
X_{n}=x-zT_{n}
and
Z_{n}=(-1)^{S_{n}}
in the Feynman formula (
2.13
) gives:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x,z)=e^{n\theta}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{S_{n}}\psi_{0}(x-zT_{n},z(-1)^{S_{n}})\right],\quad n\geq 0.

For the initial states
\psi_{0}^{\xi}(x,z):=\mathbbm{1}_{x=0,z=\xi}
with
\xi=1
or
\xi=-1
, we obtain (
3.5
).
∎

By the ergodic theorem for the irreducible aperiodic finite-state Markov chain
(S_{n})
with the doubly stochastic transition probability matrix (see (
3.8
) in the next section for the precise expression),
its stationary distribution is uniform, and so we have:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{T_{n}}{n}=0\quad a.s.

Rescaling the space in (
3.5
) by setting
x=an
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
an\in\mathbb{Z}
, by the theory of large deviations
Dembo and Zeitouni, 2010
, we see that each probability term
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}/n=az,S_{n}=\cdot)
in (
3.5
) is exponentially small for large
n
and
a\neq 0
. Equation (
3.5
) tells us that the quantum walk amplitude
\psi_{n}^{\xi}(an,z)
is a re-scaled (by a factor
e^{n\theta}
) difference of small probabilities led by large deviation.

The remainder of this section is devoted to link the asymptotic behavior of (
3.5
) to the properties of the Markov additive model
(S_{n},T_{n})
.

3.2.
Joint distribution of
(S_{n},T_{n})

Setting up notations, we define
p_{n}(s,t):=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=s,T_{n}=t)
for every
s\in\Omega
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. We denote the transition probability kernel of
(S_{n})
by
P:=\{p(s^{\prime},s)\}_{(s^{\prime},s)\in\Omega^{2}}
. In matrix form, the kernel is given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}\\
p_{3}&p_{0}&p_{1}&p_{2}\\
p_{2}&p_{3}&p_{0}&p_{1}\\
p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}&p_{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}\\
p_{1}\\
p_{2}\\
p_{3}\end{bmatrix}:=\frac{e^{-\theta}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\cosh\theta+\cos\theta\\
\sinh\theta+\sin\theta\\
\cosh\theta-\cos\theta\\
\sinh\theta-\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
p_{s}=\mathbb{P}(N_{1}\equiv s\mod 4)
,
s\in\Omega
can be obtained, for instance, by the probability generating function of the Poisson random variable
N_{1}
. We shall evaluate the characteristic functions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{ikT_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}_{\{S_{n}=s\}}}),\quad k\in(-\pi,\pi),\quad s\in\Omega,\quad n\geq 1,

and invert them to obtain the joint distribution
p_{n}
in (
3.11
).
Indeed, it is straightforward to see that
(p_{n})
satisfies the forward dynamics

\displaystyle p_{n+1}(s,t)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\Omega}p_{n}(s^{\prime},t-f(s^{\prime}))p(s^{\prime},s)\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1,\quad p_{0}(s,t)=\mathbbm{1}_{s=0,t=0},

for all
(s,t)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{Z}
. Taking the Fourier transform on the
t
component and defining the respective Fourier transform
\hat{p}(s,k):=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}p(s,t)e^{ikt}
for every
k\in(-\pi,\pi)
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{n+1}(s,k)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\Omega}\hat{p}_{n}(s^{\prime},k)e^{ikf(s^{\prime})}p(s^{\prime},s)\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1,\quad\hat{p}_{0}(s,k)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{s=0},

for every
(s,k)\in\Omega\times(-\pi,\pi)
. Defining the tilted kernels
P_{k}:=\{p(s^{\prime},s)e^{ikf(s^{\prime})}\}_{(s^{\prime},s)\in\Omega^{2}}
with parameters
k\in(-\pi,\pi)
, we can re-write the Fourier dynamics (
3.9
) to be the matrix dynamics

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{n+1}(k)=P_{k}\hat{p}_{n}(k)\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1,\quad\hat{p}_{0}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{T},

where
\hat{p}_{n}(k):=\{\hat{p}_{n}(s,k)\}_{s\in\Omega}
are vectors and
P_{k}
has the matrix representation

(3.10)

\displaystyle P_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik}p_{0}&e^{ik}p_{1}&e^{ik}p_{2}&e^{ik}p_{3}\\
e^{-ik}p_{3}&e^{-ik}p_{0}&e^{-ik}p_{1}&e^{-ik}p_{2}\\
e^{ik}p_{2}&e^{ik}p_{3}&e^{ik}p_{0}&e^{ik}p_{1}\\
e^{-ik}p_{1}&e^{-ik}p_{2}&e^{-ik}p_{3}&e^{-ik}p_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

By the inverse Fourier transform we can thus obtain the joint marginals of
(S_{n},T_{n})
to be

(3.11)

\displaystyle p_{n}(s,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(P_{k}^{n}\hat{p}_{0}(k))(s)e^{-ikt}\mathrm{d}k.

Subsequent simplifications can be made by studying the spectrum of the matrices
P_{k}
:

Proposition 3.2
.

The subspaces of
\ell_{2}(\Omega)
defined by
A:=span\{e_{0}+e_{2},e_{1}+e_{3}\}
and
B:=span\{e_{0}-e_{2},e_{1}-e_{3}\}
are invariant subspaces of the tilted kernel
P_{k}
. Furthermore the matrix representations of
P_{k}
under
A,B
are given respectively by the diagonalizable matrices

(3.12)

\displaystyle A(k):=e^{-\theta}\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik}\cosh\theta&e^{ik}\sinh\theta\\
e^{-ik}\sinh\theta&e^{-ik}\cosh\theta\end{bmatrix},\quad B(k):=e^{-\theta}\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik}\cos\theta&e^{ik}\sin\theta\\
-e^{-ik}\sin\theta&e^{-ik}\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},

with the eigenvalues of
A(k)
being

\displaystyle\lambda_{A,1}(k)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta}[\cos k\cosh\theta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}k\cosh^{2}\theta-1}],\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\lambda_{A,2}(k)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta}[\cos k\cosh\theta-\sqrt{\cos^{2}k\cosh^{2}\theta-1}],

and the eigenvalues of
B(k)
being

\displaystyle\lambda_{B,1}(k)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta}[\cos k\cos\theta+\sqrt{\cos^{2}k\cos^{2}\theta-1}],\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\lambda_{B,2}(k)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta}[\cos k\cos\theta-\sqrt{\cos^{2}k\cos^{2}\theta-1}],

where we specify the square root function as

\displaystyle\sqrt{c}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{c},&\quad c\geq 0\\
i\sqrt{-c},&\quad c<0\end{cases}.

Proof.

It can be easily checked by direct computation.
∎

Proposition
3.2
tells us that
P_{k}
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues
\lambda_{A,j}(k)
and
\lambda_{B,j}(k)
for
j=1,2
. Regarding the left eigenvectors of
P_{k}
, denoted by
\ell_{A,j}(k):=\{\ell_{A,j}(k,s)\}_{s\in\Omega}
and
\ell_{B,j}(k):=\{\ell_{A,j}(k,s)\}_{s\in\Omega}
; and right eigenvectors of
P_{k}
, denoted by
r_{A_{j}}(k):=\{r_{A,j}(k,s)\}_{s\in\Omega}
and
r_{B_{j}}(k):=\{r_{B,j}(k,s)\}_{s\in\Omega}
, corresponding to the respective eigenvalues
\lambda_{A,j}(k),\lambda_{B,j}(k)
, we can now further decompose (
3.11
)
into the sum of four integrals by spectral decomposition:

(3.13)

\displaystyle p_{n}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}r_{A,j}(k,1)\ell_{A,j}(k,s)\lambda_{A,j}^{n}(k)e^{-ikt}\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}r_{B,j}(k,1)\ell_{B,j}(k,s)\lambda_{B,j}^{n}(k)e^{-ikt}\mathrm{d}k,

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,2}(I_{n}(s,t;A_{j})+I_{n}(s,t;B_{j}))

for every
s\in\Omega
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
where we define for
j=1,2
:

(3.14)

\displaystyle I_{n}(s,t;A_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}r_{A,j}(k,1)\ell_{A,j}(k,s)\lambda_{A,j}^{n}(k)e^{-ikt}\mathrm{d}k,

(3.15)

\displaystyle I_{n}(s,t;B_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}r_{B,j}(k,1)\ell_{B,j}(k,s)\lambda_{B,j}^{n}(k)e^{-ikt}\mathrm{d}k.

3.3.
Derivation of asymptotics via spectral properties

3.3.1.
Quantum walk amplitudes

Since (
3.5
) expresses amplitude of a quantum walk as the difference of joint probability of the pair
(S_{n},T_{n})
, we substitute (
3.13
) into formula (
3.5
) to obtain the key observation:

Theorem 3.3
.

The amplitude
\psi_{n}^{\xi}(x,z)
of the quantum walk starting at the state
\psi_{0}^{\xi}(x,z):=\mathbbm{1}_{x=0,z=\xi}
is given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{\xi}(x,z)=\begin{cases}2e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}I_{n}(0,xz;B_{j}),\quad z=\xi,\\
2ie^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}I_{n}(1,xz;B_{j}),\quad z=-\xi,\end{cases}

where the effects of eigenvalues and eigenvectors from
A(k)
have been cancelled.

Proof.

A direct substitution of (
3.13
) into formula (
3.5
) gives
for
\xi=\pm 1
that:

\displaystyle\psi^{\xi}_{n}(x,z)=\begin{cases}e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left[I_{n}(0,xz;A_{j})+I_{n}(0,xz;B_{j})-I_{n}(2,xz;A_{j})-I_{n}(2,xz;B_{j})\right],\quad z=\xi,\\
ie^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left[I_{n}(1,xz;A_{j})+I_{n}(1,xz;B_{j})-I_{n}(3,xz;A_{j})-I_{n}(3,xz;B_{j})\right],\,\,z=-\xi\end{cases}

for every
x\in\mathbb{Z}
and
z=\pm 1
. By Proposition
3.2
, the left eigenvectors satisfy

\displaystyle\ell_{A,j}(k,0)=\ell_{A,j}(k,2),\quad\ell_{A,j}(k,1)=\ell_{A,j}(k,3),

\displaystyle\ell_{B,j}(k,0)=-\ell_{B,j}(k,2),\quad\ell_{B,j}(k,1)=-\ell_{B,j}(k,3)

for
j=1,2
. Therefore, by the definition of the integrals in (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we have:

\displaystyle I_{n}(0,t;A_{j})=I_{n}(2,t;A_{j}),\quad I_{n}(1,t;A_{j})=I_{n}(3,t;A_{j}),

\displaystyle I_{n}(0,t;B_{j})=-I_{n}(2,t;B_{j}),\quad I_{n}(1,t;B_{j})=-I_{n}(3,t;B_{j})

for every
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j=1,2
. Then (
3.16
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

In fact, the matrices
A(k)
are driving the large deviation regime of the Markov additive model
(S_{n},T_{n})
. Because of the cancellation in the difference of small probabilities as seen in (
3.5
), the only factors that drive the asymptotic behavior of
\psi_{n}^{\xi}
are the matrices
B(k)
.

Remark 3.5
.

In Proposition
3.2

\{e_{0}-e_{2},i(e_{1}-e_{3})\}
for the subspace

, we can rewrite the matrix representation of

over it as

\displaystyle e^{-\theta}\begin{bmatrix}e^{ik}&\\
&e^{-ik}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&i\sin\theta\\
i\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}=e^{-\theta}\diag(e^{ik}.e^{-ik})e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}},

where

is the matrix representation of the quantum walk operator
W:=SC
over the Fourier domain in space, which is first documented in
Grimmett et al., 2004
. One can easily show that (
3.16

3.3
still holds using this matrix representation, which coincides with the integral studied in Equation (1.20) in

We consider the case with the homogeneous coin

,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(Z^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor}\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\left((-1)^{N_{t}}z\right)_{t\geq 0},\quad\mbox{denoted by}\quad(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}

Denoting
X^{\epsilon}_{n}=\left\lfloor y/\epsilon\right\rfloor-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{S_{n}^{\epsilon}}
for

, we have also

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\epsilon X^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor}\right)_{t\geq 0}\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}\left(y-\int_{0}^{t}(-1)^{N_{s}}\mathrm{d}s\right)_{t\geq 0},\quad\mbox{denoted by}\quad(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}.

Here the process

is the well-known telegraph process driven by the direction process
D_{t}:=(-1)^{N_{t}}
; and we can write
Z_{t}=zD_{t}
and
Y_{t}=y-T_{t}
.

Proof.

For (
4.1
), the limiting distribution of an increment
S^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor}-S^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor s/\epsilon\rfloor}
follows by using that the sum of independent Poisson random variables is a Poisson random variable with parameter the sum of the parameters and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor-\lfloor s/\epsilon\rfloor)\epsilon\lambda=\lambda(t-s).

The independence of increments follows from the independence of the random variable
N_{n}
’s.

Convergence (
4.2
) follows from the convergence in distribution of integer-valued random variables.

For convergence (
4.3
) one can introduce the times of increase
n_{j}^{\epsilon}
of
S^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor}
noting that only the increments by
1
matter as the increments by more than
1
are of higher order in
\epsilon
. It follows that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon n_{j}^{\epsilon}=T_{j}
, the
j
’th time of increase of
N_{t}
.

The convergence (
4.3
) follows from
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\lfloor y/{\epsilon}\rfloor=y
and the decomposition of the sum appearing in
\epsilon X^{\epsilon}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor}
over the intervals
[n_{j}^{\epsilon},n_{j+1}^{\epsilon}[
to obtain the convergence to the integral appearing in
Y_{t}
over the intervals
[T_{j},T_{j+1}[
.
∎

Lemma
4.1
motivates us to consider, in (

), the rescaling of space
x\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\left\lfloor y/\epsilon\right\rfloor
, time
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\left\lfloor n/\epsilon\right\rfloor
and the coin parameter
\theta>0
with
\epsilon\lambda
where
\lambda>0
. We also rescale the initial state
\psi_{0}
as follows:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\times\{\pm 1\}\to\mathbb{C}
be a function which is
C_{0}
in the first entry, that is,
\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}\absolutevalue{f(y, \pm 1)}=0
. For every
\epsilon>0
, we define the state vector
\psi_{0}^{\epsilon}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\{\pm 1\})
(not necessarily normalized) by

(4.4)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}^{\epsilon}(x,z)=f(\epsilon x,z)

and let
\psi_{n}^{\epsilon}
be the n-th step state of this initial condition with the homogeneous coin
e^{i\epsilon\lambda\sigma_{1}}
. Then for every
t>0,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
z=\pm 1
, the point-wise limit

(4.5)

\displaystyle\psi(t,y,z):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\psi_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}([y/\epsilon],z)=e^{t\lambda}\mathbb{E}(i^{N_{t}}f(Y_{t},Z_{t})\mid(N_{0},Y_{0},Z_{0})=(0,y,z))

exists.

Proof.

By the Feynman formula (
2.13
), we have

\displaystyle\psi_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}([y/\epsilon],z)

\displaystyle=e^{[t/\epsilon]\cdot\epsilon\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{S_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}}\psi_{0}^{\epsilon}([y/\epsilon]-zT_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon},z(-1)^{S_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{[t/\epsilon]\cdot\epsilon\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{S_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}}f(\epsilon[y/\epsilon]-z\epsilon T_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon},z(-1)^{S_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}})\right].

Together with the weak convergence result in Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(t,y,z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\psi_{[t/\epsilon]}^{\epsilon}([y/\epsilon],z)=e^{t\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{N_{t}}f(y-zT_{t},z(-1)^{N_{t}})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{t\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[i^{N_{t}}f(Y_{t},Z_{t})\mid Y_{0}=y,Z_{0}=z)\right].

∎

It is now very natural to write a system of quantum transport PDEs solved by
\psi
, for which we have the probabilistic representation (
4.5
). In fact, this type of PDEs, known as Dirac PDEs, has been studied in the Physics literature and we refer to the papers
Maeda et al., 2018
and
Maeda and Suzuki, 2020
where nonlinear PDEs are also discussed.

Proposition 4.3
.

The function
\psi(t,y,z)
defined in Theorem
4.2
is a solution to the linear system of PDEs

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\partial_{t}\psi_{+}&=&-\partial_{y}\psi_{+}+i\lambda\psi_{-},\\
\partial_{t}\psi_{-}&=&\partial_{y}\psi_{-}+i\lambda\psi_{+}\end{array}\right.

with
\psi(0,y,z)=f(y,z)
and where
\psi_{\pm}(t,y):=\psi(t,y,\pm 1)
. Additionally, we have the conservation property:

(4.8)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|\psi_{+}(t,y)|^{2}+|\psi_{-}(t,y)|^{2})\mathrm{d}y=1.

for every
t>0
whenever
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{f(y, 1)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{f(y, -1)}^{2}\mathrm{d}y=1
.

Proof.

Let
h>0
and denoting by
\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}
the expectation over the initial condition
(N_{0},Y_{0},Z_{0})=(0,y,z)
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\psi(t+h,y,z)=e^{(t+h)\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}\Big[i^{N_{t+h}}f\Big(y-z\int_{0}^{t+h}(-1)^{N_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,z(-1)^{N_{t+h}}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=e^{(t+h)\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}\Big[i^{N_{h}}\mathbb{E}\Big[i^{N_{t+h}-N_{h}}f\Big(Y_{h}-Z_{h}\int_{h}^{t+h}\!\!\!(-1)^{N_{s}-N_{h}}\mathrm{d}s,Z_{h}(-1)^{N_{t+h}-N_{h}}\Big)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{h}\Big]\Big],

where
\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\sigma(N_{s},\,s\leq h)
. Using the fact that
(M_{t}):=\{N_{t+h}-N_{h}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Poisson process with parameter
\lambda
and initial condition
M_{0}=0
, and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we can compute:

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[i^{N_{t+h}-N_{h}}f\Big(Y_{h}-Z_{h}\int_{h}^{t+h}(-1)^{N_{s}-N_{h}}\mathrm{d}s,Z_{h}(-1)^{N_{t+h}-N_{h}}\Big)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[i^{M_{t}}f\Big(Y_{h}-Z_{h}\int_{h}^{t+h}(-1)^{M_{s-h}}\mathrm{d}s,Z_{h}(-1)^{M_{s}}\Big)\right]=e^{-t\lambda}\psi(t,Y_{h},Z_{h}).

Substituting (
4.10
) into (
4.9
) gives

\begin{split}\psi(t+h,y,z)&=e^{h\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}(i^{N_{h}}\psi(t,Y_{h},Z_{h}))\\
&=e^{h\lambda}\mathbb{P}(N_{h}=0)\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}(i^{N_{h}}\psi(t,Y_{h},Z_{h})\mid N_{h}=0)\\
&\qquad{}+e^{h\lambda}\mathbb{P}(N_{h}=1)\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}(i^{N_{h}}\psi(t,Y_{h},Z_{h})\mid N_{h}=1)+O(h^{2})\\
&=\psi(t,y-hz,z)+ih\lambda\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}\Big[\psi\Big(t,y-\int_{0}^{h}(-1)^{N_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,-z\Big)\Big|N_{h}=1\Big]+O(h^{2}).\end{split}

Using the fact that the first jump time
T
of
(N_{t})
follows the conditional distribution
T\mid N_{h}=1\sim U[0,h]
, which is the uniform distribution over
[0,h]
, we have:

(4.11)

\begin{split}&\qquad\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}\Big[\psi(t,y-z\int_{0}^{h}(-1)^{N_{s}}\mathrm{d}s,-z)\mid N_{h}=1\Big]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{0,y,z}[\psi(t,y-z(T-(h-T)),-z)\mid T\sim U[0,h]]\\
&=\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}\psi(t,y+zh-2zs,-z)\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Substituting (
4.11
) into (
4.10
), we have:

(4.12)

\displaystyle\psi(t+h,y,z)=\psi(t,y-hz,z)+i\lambda\int_{0}^{h}\psi(t,y+zh-2zs,-z)\mathrm{d}s+O(h^{2}).

Subtracting
\psi(t,y,z)
on both sides, dividing by
h
, and sending
h\to 0
gives

(4.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(t,y,z)=-z\partial_{y}\psi(t,y,z)+i\lambda\psi(t,y,-z),

for all
t>0,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
z=\pm 1
, which is (
4.3
).

Regarding the conservation property (
4.8
), one first observes that

(4.14)

\lim_{\absolutevalue{y}\to\infty}\psi_{+}(t,y)=\lim_{\absolutevalue{y}\to\infty}\psi_{-}(t,y)=0,

which follows from the probabilistic representation (

) by the assumption

and the fact that

almost surely using the definition (

. Then by a direct computation using (
4.3
), one gets

(4.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(|\psi_{+}|^{2}+|\psi_{-}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}(|\psi_{-}|^{2}-|\psi_{+}|^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{which implies}\quad\partial_{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(|\psi_{+}|^{2}+|\psi_{-}|^{2})dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\partial_{y}(|\psi_{-}|^{2}-|\psi_{+}|^{2})dy.

Using integration by part and (

is constant in time. Therefore, (

) follows from

.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

If we define

by removing a global phase

, then we can obtain that:

\displaystyle u(t,y,z)=e^{\lambda(1-i)t}\mathbb{E}[i^{N_{t}}f(Y_{t},Z_{t})\mid(N_{0},Y_{0},Z_{0})=(0,y,z)],

which satisfies the system of PDEs

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\partial_{t}u_{+}&=&-\partial_{y}u_{+}-i\lambda(u_{+}-u_{-})\\

Remark 4.5
.

By rescaling time,

5.
Extensions to other quantum walks

In this section, we propose generalization of the Feynman formula (Theorem

) by considering one-dimensional quantum walks with other coin operators, which include more general homogeneous coins, time-dependent coins (see, for example,
Bañuls et al., 2006
) and site-dependent coins (see, for example,

Maeda and Suzuki, 2020
,
Maeda et al., 2018

In the following
(S_{n},Z_{n},X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are always the discrete-time stochastic processes defined in Definition
2.6
which are driven by the sequence of random variables
(N_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
. To write more general Feynman formulae, the sequence

does not necessarily consist of independent Poisson variables and we shall specify our assumptions on
(N_{j})
for each Feynman formula we derive. Meanwhile we define the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(N_{1},\cdots,N_{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
and denote
\mathbb{E}_{s,x,z}
the expectation conditional on the initial value
(S_{0},X_{0},Z_{0})=(s,x,z)
for every
(s,x,z)\in\{0,1,2,3\}\times\{\pm 1\}\times\mathbb{Z}
.
Furthermore, given a sequence of integers
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we define as in Lemma
2.3
the sequences of real numbers
(z_{n},x_{n})
by
z_{m}:=(-1)^{k_{m}}z_{m-1}
and
x_{m}:=x_{0}-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z_{j}
for
m\geq 1
with
(z_{0},x_{0}):=(z,x)
.

5.1.
General unitary coins

General unitary matrices can be written in the form

(5.1)

\displaystyle e^{i\phi}Z_{\beta}e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}}Z_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi}\begin{bmatrix}\beta&0\\
0&\beta^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&i\sin\theta\\

0&\alpha^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(5.2)

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{with}\quad Z_{\omega}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\omega&0\\

where

(Feynman formula for general coins)
.

For every

and initial state

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{\phi;\beta,\alpha;\theta}(x,z)=e^{in\phi}{e^{n\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{0,z,x}\left[i^{S_{n}}(\alpha\beta)^{x-X_{n}}\alpha^{Z_{n}-{z}}\psi_{0}(X_{n},Z_{n})\right],

where the driving random variables are such that

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Lemma
2.3

\begin{split}&\psi_{n}^{\phi;\beta,\alpha;\theta}(x,z)\\

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\beta^{z+(-1)^{k_{1}}z+\cdots+(-1)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n-1}}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\psi_{0}(x-z-(-1)^{k_{1}}z-\cdots-(-1)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n-1}}z,z(-1)^{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}}z),\end{split}

and

5.2.
Site-dependent coins

A quantum walk is space-inhomogeneous when the coin operator is site-dependent, which can be written as a controlled operator:

(5.17)

\displaystyle C=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}e_{x}e_{x}^{*}\otimes C_{x},\quad C_{x}\in U(\{\pm 1\})\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}.

In the following we consider two types of site-dependent coins: a rotation coin with site-dependent global phase and a rotation coin with site-dependent angles of rotation.

5.2.1.
Rotation coin with site-dependent global phase

The coin under consideration in this section is

(5.18)

\displaystyle C=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}e_{x}e_{x}^{*}\otimes e^{iV(x)}e^{i\theta\sigma_{1}},

where
V:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is some real-valued function and
\theta>0
. Denoting by

5.4.
Yet another walk

Let

be the transition matrix of a classical symmetric nearest-neighbor random walk on

. Note that the following discussion generalizes easily to the multi-dimensional case, but for simplicity we stay with the one-dimensional case. Indeed,

is an initial quantum state,

for instance.

Theorem 5.13
.

For a sequence of i.i.d. Poisson random variables

and, given a classical random walk

with transition matrix

and
X_{n}=Y_{S_{n}}
for all
n\geq 1
.
We have the following Feynman formula:

(5.37)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda n}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{S_{n}}e^{i(V(X_{1})+\cdots+V(X_{n}))}\psi_{0}(X_{n})\right].

Proof.

By induction, conditioning on
N_{1}
, and using time-homogeneity of the chain
(X_{n})
, one has:

\displaystyle\psi_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(n+1)}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{S_{n+1}}e^{i(V(X_{1})+\cdots+V(X_{n+1}))}\psi_{0}(X_{n+1})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(n+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}}{k!}i^{k}M^{k}e^{iV(x)}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{S_{n}}e^{i(V(X_{1})+\cdots+V(X_{n}))}\psi_{0}(X_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{i(\lambda M+V(x))}\psi_{n}(x).

∎

The previous result has a continuous-time version which was known for some time as Molchanov formula (see

Consider the continuous-time discrete-space Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-(\lambda M+V(x))\psi.

For a given function

\displaystyle\psi(t,x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda t}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{N_{t}}e^{i\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})\mathrm{d}s}\psi(0,X_{t})\right].

Starting from formula (

\displaystyle\psi(t+h,x)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(t+h)}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{N_{t+h}}e^{i\int_{0}^{t+h}V(X_{s})ds}\psi(0,X_{t+h})\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(t+h)}e^{-\lambda h}e^{ihV(x)}\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{N_{t}}e^{i\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\psi(0,X_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+e^{\lambda(t+h)}e^{-\lambda h}(\lambda h)iM\mathbb{E}_{0,x}\left[i^{N_{t}}e^{i\int_{0}^{t}V(X_{s})ds}\psi(0,X_{t})\right]+O(h^{2}),

and

(5.40)

\displaystyle\psi(t+h,x)-\psi(t,x)=ih\left((V(x)+\lambda M)\psi(t,x)\right)+O(h^{2}),

and dividing by

∎

6.
Conclusion

6.1.
Summary

We introduced a Feynman-type (discrete path-integral) representation that links one-dimensional quantum walks on
\mathbb{Z}
to a four-state Markov
additive process. This representation provides a unified probabilistic handle on the
dynamics: it isolates a tilted-kernel structure whose spectral decomposition explains
the ballistic regime, and it yields a transparent route to continuum limits. Using this
framework, we demonstrated the derivation of the well-known ballistic weak limit for the position distribution, and we
derived a probabilistic representation to the quantum transport PDE, which is a system of transport PDEs with phase interaction term and is the hyperbolic scaling
limit of quantum walks. Meanwhile, the Feynman-type representation is robust across different variants of one-dimensional quantum walks, which include both time-inhomogeneous and site-inhomogeneous coins. Also we noted that our probabilistic representation offers a Monte Carlo way to compute the solution for this type of PDE.

A distinctive feature of our approach is that both asymptotic descriptions — ballistic
limits of quantum walks and large deviation tails of Markov additive processes—arise from the same tilted kernel machinery. The spectral branch that is responsible for the large deviation properties is removed when applying the Feynman formula to quantum walks; the asymptotic analysis then follows from a typical stationary-phase analysis of the remaining spectral branch. On the continuum side, our Feynman formula naturally provides a probabilistic formulation of the solution to the quantum PDE using also hyperbolic scaling limit of the Markov additive process.

6.2.
Outlook

In Section
5
, we demonstrated the robustness of the Feynman formula on inhomogeneous coins; yet these coins can yield non-ballistic behaviors, including localized and hybrid behaviors, as seen in the numerical experiments by
Ahmad et al., 2020
for site-dependent coins and
Panahiyan and Fritzsche, 2018
for time-dependent coins. It is natural to ask how these properties are reflected in the associated Markov additive process. Meanwhile, quantum walks with higher-dimensional state space and chirality space allow one to study entanglement effects involved in a quantum walk (see
Watabe et al., 2008
which discussed quantum walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
Venegas-Andraca et al., 2005
which discussed one-dimensional quantum walks with higher dimensional, entangled coins).
It is natural to extend the robustness of the Feynman formula to these higher-dimensional generalizations. Lastly, we are also currently working on probabilistic representations for the nonlinear quantum walks and their associated Dirac PDEs described in
Maeda et al., 2018
and
Maeda and Suzuki, 2020
.

\displaystyle\{e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{1}\cos\theta)-ik_{1}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}+e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{2}\cos\theta)-ik_{2}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}

(A.14)

\displaystyle+e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{3}\cos\theta)-ik_{3}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}+e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{4}\cos\theta)-ik_{4}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}\}.

Using the fact that
k_{4}=-k_{1}
and
k_{3}=-k_{2}
as in
A.6
, we can simplify the sum of four exponential terms in (
A.12
) into

(A.15)

\displaystyle\qquad 2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) + \sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})+2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_2, a) -\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})

(A.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\cos(\frac{n}{2}[\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) + \Theta_{B_1}(k_2, a)])\cos(\frac{n}{2}[\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) -\Theta_{B_2}(k_2, a)] + \sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\cos(n \Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2} +\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big[\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}-\sgn(a)\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big],

where we used the sum-to-product formula in the first equality, and the following identity for the second equality:

(A.17)

\displaystyle\Theta_{B_{1}}(k_{1},a)+\Theta_{B_{1}}(k_{2},a)=n\pi(1-a),

which follows from the relation
k_{2}=\pi-k_{1}
(see again
A.6
) and the identity
\cos^{-1}(-z)=\pi-\cos^{-1}z
for every
z\in(-1,1)
. Putting (
A.15
) into (
A.12
), we have:

(A.18)

\begin{split}&\frac{1+a}{2\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{f_{K}(a;\theta)}\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\\
\times&\Big[\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})-\sgn(a)\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big].\end{split}

A.3.2.
Formal computation for integrating leading terms

In the previous section, we formally showed that
e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(0,an,B_{j})
is asymptotically equivalent to (
A.18
); in this section we continue from there to formally verify (
3.20
). To begin with, taking modulus square on (
A.18
) gives the asymptotic relation:

(A.19)

\displaystyle e^{2n\theta}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1,2}I_n(0, an, B_j)}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(1+a)^{2}}{4n}f_{K}(a;\theta)\cos^{2}(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})[1-\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k,a) - n\pi(1-a))]

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+a)^{2}}{8n}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+\cos(n\pi(1-a))[1-\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k,a) - n\pi(1-a))].

It follows that we have the asymptotic relation:

(A.20)

\displaystyle\qquad n\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}e^{2n\theta}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1,2}I_n(0, an, B_j)}^{2}\mathrm{d}a

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}\frac{1}{8}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}da+\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}\frac{1}{8}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\cos(n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))\cos(n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a.

Note that this asymptotic relation is valid because the domain of integral is contained in
(-\cos\theta+\epsilon,\cos\theta-\epsilon)
which is away from the problematic boundary points
\pm\cos\theta
. Due to the existence of fast oscillation terms, as
n\to\infty
, the last three integrals in the asymptotic equivalence converge to
0
by both the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma and the method of stationary phase (see
A.3.3
for details). The first term converges and we have

(A.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}e^{2n\theta}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1,2}I_n(0, an, B_j)}^{2}\mathrm{d}a=\frac{1}{8}\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}(1+a^{2})f_{K}(a;\theta)\mathrm{d}a.

A.3.3.
Rigorous justification

Justifying the replacement of
\left\lceil an\right\rceil
with
an
.

Because of the assumption that
a\in(-\cos\theta+\epsilon,\cos\theta-\epsilon)
, we can in fact have the large
n
asymptotic relation (
A.5
) with
a
replaced by
a_{n}:=\left\lceil an\right\rceil/n
, which is uniform across all choices of
a
. The precise argument has been made in Proposition 2.2 of
Sunada and Tate, 2012
. Nonetheless, the formal procedure of using
a
and
a_{n}
yield similar computations.

Justifying the limits in (
A.20
).

The convergence

(A.22)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\cos(n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a=0,

is a direct application of the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma by noting that
f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}
is integrable over
[y_{1},y_{2}]
.
Next, for the convergence:

(A.23)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a=0,

without loss of generality, we consider
0\leq y_{1}<y_{2}
or
y_{1}<y_{2}\leq 0
(otherwise we split into two integrals). Then the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma follows because of the integrability of
f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)
and the fact that

(A.24)

\displaystyle\inf_{a\in(y_{1},y_{2})}\absolutevalue{\frac{d}{da}[2\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \pi(1-a)]}

\displaystyle=\inf_{a\in(y_{1},y_{2})}\absolutevalue{2\Big[\frac{\cos\theta\sin k_1}{\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta\cos^2k_1 }}k_1'(a) - ak_1'(a) -k_1(a)\Big] + \pi}

\displaystyle=2\inf_{a\in(y_{1},y_{2})}\absolutevalue{\frac{\pi}{2}-k_1(a)}>0,

since
y_{1},y_{2}\in(-\cos\theta+\epsilon,\cos\theta-\epsilon)
. For the last convergence in (
A.20
), by the sum-to-product formula, we have

(A.25)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))\cos(n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a

(A.26)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a))\mathrm{d}a

(A.27)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-2n\pi(1-a))\mathrm{d}a.

In fact both limits (
A.26
) and (
A.27
) vanish due to similar reasons. We consider only the limit (
A.26
); assume without loss of generality that
y_{1}\leq 0\leq y_{2}
(the case
y_{1},y_{2}
with the same signs can be obtained by adding two integrals of the previous type). Next, we split the limit (
A.26
) into

(A.28)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{2}n\right\rfloor}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a))\mathrm{d}a

(A.29)

\displaystyle\,\qquad{}+\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor y_{1}n\right\rfloor}^{0}f_{K}(a;\theta)(1+a)^{2}\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a))\mathrm{d}a.

For
(
A.28
)
, we note that

(A.30)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{da}\Theta_{B_{1}}(k_{1},a)=k_{1}(a)=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad a\in[0,y_{2})\quad\mbox{implies that}\quad a=0.

Therefore,
a=0
is the unique critical point to
\Theta_{B_{1}}(k_{1}(a),a)
over the domain of integration. By the method of stationary phase (
A.2
), the integral (
A.28
) is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
so the limit vanishes. For
(
A.29
)
, the limit vanishes similarly to (
A.23
) by noting that

(A.31)

\displaystyle\inf_{a\in{(y_{2},0)}}\absolutevalue{\frac{d}{da}\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)}=\inf_{a\in{(y_{2},0)}}\absolutevalue{k_1(a)}>0,

since
k_{1}(a)
is in the third quadrant as seen in its definition in
A.8
.

A.4.
The remaining two limits

The rigorous justifications of the limits (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) are similar to the case of (
3.20
). In this section we will be only showing the additional formal computations that are needed to verify the two limits.

A.4.1.
Formal computation for (
3.21
)

The only difference between the asymptotic relation for
e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j})
and that of
e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(0,an,B_{j})
as seen in (
A.5
) are the eigenvector terms, which are given by:

(A.32)

\begin{split}r_{B_{1}}(k,0)\ell_{B_{1}}(k,0)&=\frac{1}{4i}\frac{\cos k\sin\theta+i\sin k\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}k\cos^{2}\theta}},\\
r_{B_{2}}(k,0)\ell_{B_{2}}(k,0)&=-\frac{1}{4i}\frac{\cos k\sin\theta+i\sin k\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}k\cos^{2}\theta}}.\end{split}

Their values at the critical points could be simplified by noting that:

(A.33)

\begin{split}\frac{\cos k_{1}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}k_{1}\cos^{2}\theta}}&=\sgn(a)\sec\theta\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2}},\\
\frac{\sin k_{1}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}k_{1}\cos^{2}\theta}}&=a\tan\theta.\end{split}

Using (
A.9
) and (
A.32
) with the corresponding values at the saddle points given by (
A.10
) and (
A.33
) shows that
e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j})
is asymptotically equivalent to the sum:

(A.34)

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\sgn(a)\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2}}\sec\theta}{4i}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^{2}}{n}f_{K}(a;\theta)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\{e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{1}\cos\theta)-ik_{1}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}-e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{2}\cos\theta)-ik_{2}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}

\displaystyle-e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{3}\cos\theta)-ik_{3}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}+e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{4}\cos\theta)-ik_{4}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}\}

\displaystyle+

(A.35)

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{a\tan\theta}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^{2}}{n}f_{K}(a;\theta)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\{e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{1}\cos\theta)-ik_{1}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}+e^{in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{2}\cos\theta)-ik_{2}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}

\displaystyle+e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{3}\cos\theta)-ik_{3}an+i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}+e^{-in\cos^{-1}(\cos k_{4}\cos\theta)-ik_{4}an-i\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4}}\}.

The oscillation term in (
A.34
) is given by

\begin{split}&2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) + \sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})-2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_2, a) -\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})\\
=&-4\sin(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\sin(n \Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2} +\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})\\
=&{}-\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big[\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\\
&{}+\sgn(a)\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big],\end{split}

while the oscillation term of (
A.35
) is given by

\begin{split}&2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) + \sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})+2\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_2, a)-\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})\\
=&4\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\cos(n \Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2} +\sgn(a)\frac{\pi}{4})\\
=&\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big[\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}-\sgn(a)\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big].\end{split}

Therefore,
e^{n\theta}\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j}))
is in turn asymptotically equivalent to:

(A.36)

\begin{split}\quad&i\frac{\sgn(a)\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2}}\sec\theta}{2\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{f_{K}(a;\theta)}\sin(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\\
\quad&\quad\times\Big[\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})+\sgn(a)\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big]\\
\quad&+\frac{a\tan\theta}{2\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{f_{K}(a;\theta)}\cos(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\\
\quad&\quad\times\Big[\cos(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})-\sgn(a)\sin(n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a) - \frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2})\Big].\end{split}

Taking modulus square on (
A.36
) gives the asymptotic equivalence of
e^{2n\theta}\lvert{\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j})}\rvert
:

(A.37)

\displaystyle\frac{(\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2})\sec^{2}\theta}{4n}f_{K}(a;\theta)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos(n\pi(1-a)))[1+\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))]

\displaystyle+\frac{a^{2}\tan^{2}\theta}{4n}f_{K}(a;\theta)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos(n\pi(1-a)))[1-\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a))].

Note that integrals preserve asymptotic equivalence just like the previous case (
A.20
). Using the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma, we have similar to the formal computations in (
A.20
) that:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{1}\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{2}\right\rfloor}e^{2n\theta}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1, 2}I_n(1, an, B_j)}\mathrm{d}a

\displaystyle=\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}\frac{1}{8}f_{K}(a;\theta)((\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2})\sec^{2}\theta+a^{2}\tan^{2}\theta)\mathrm{d}a=\frac{1}{8}\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}(1-a^{2})f_{K}(a;\theta)\mathrm{d}a.

A.4.2.
Formal computation for (
3.22
)

Using the asymptotic equivalence obtained in the two equations (
A.18
) and (
A.36
), we can now deduce the asymptotic equivalence of the product
e^{2n\theta}(\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(0,an,B_{j}))\overline{(\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j}))}
to be:

\displaystyle\frac{(1+a)a\tan\theta}{4n}f_{K}(a;\theta)[1-\sgn(a)\sin(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a))]\cos^{2}\Big(\frac{n\pi(1-a)}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle i\frac{(1+a)\sqrt{\cos^{2}\theta-a^{2}}\sec\theta}{4n}f_{K}(a;\theta)\cos(2n\Theta_{B_1}(k_1, a)-n\pi(1-a)).

Again, by the preservation of asymptotic equivalence with integrals and using the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma, we have similar to (
A.20

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\int_{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{1}\right\rfloor}^{\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor ny_{2}\right\rfloor}e^{2n\theta}\left(\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(0,an,B_{j}))(\overline{\sum_{j=1,2}I_{n}(1,an,B_{j})}\right)\mathrm{d}a

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}}(1+a)a\tan\theta f_{K}(a;\theta)\mathrm{d}a.
