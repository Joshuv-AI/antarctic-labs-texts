Preoperative serum prealbumin as a prognostic marker for survival after gallbladder cancer resection: a multicenter study
Authors: Zhenhao Huang, Jixing Wang, Baoyong Zhou, Rui Liao, Jie Xu, Peng Guo, Zhipeng Liu, Nan You, Rui Tao, Qihui Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePrealbumin, a marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, has shown prognostic value in various malignancies. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of preoperative serum prealbumin levels in gallbladder cancer (GBC) patients undergoing radical resection.MethodsA multicenter retrospective study included 210 patients who underwent radical GBC resection. The optimal prealbumin cutoff (221 mg/L) was identified via ROC analysis. Patients were stratified into high and low prealbumin groups. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were used to assess the association between prealbumin levels and overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS).ResultsPatients with low prealbumin levels (n = 120) had 1-year OS and RFS rates of 65% and 44.1%, respectively. In contrast, high prealbumin patients (n = 90) showed significantly better OS (96.6%) and RFS (90%) (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis confirmed prealbumin as an independent predictor of OS (HR = 0.179, 95% CI: 0.052–0.622, p = 0.007) and RFS (HR = 0.220, 95% CI: 0.102–0.474, p < 0.001). A combined model of prealbumin and hemoglobin improved predictive accuracy for OS (AUC = 0.762) and RFS (AUC = 0.771).ConclusionPreoperative serum prealbumin is an independent prognostic biomarker in GBC patients undergoing radical resection. Combining prealbumin with hemoglobin enhances prediction of postoperative survival.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a relatively rare but highly lethal malignancy of the digestive system [1, 2]. Despite its low incidence, GBC is characterized by aggressive behavior and often presents with nonspecific early symptoms, leading to diagnosis at an advanced stage in most cases [3–5]. Epidemiological data indicate that the 5-year survival rate for GBC is generally below 10%. However, patients diagnosed at an early stage tend to achieve significantly better outcomes [6–8]. Consequently, identifying reliable biomarkers for predicting postoperative prognosis—especially in the early period—has become a major focus of clinical research in GBC. Currently, treatment for gallbladder cancer primarily relies on surgical resection, especially radical gallbladder resection [9, 10]. However, despite being the only potentially curative option, surgical resection is associated with widely variable postoperative outcomes. Postoperative survival is influenced by a range of factors, including patient clinical and pathological characteristics, tumor stage, postoperative complications, and nutritional status [11–13]. Recent evidence increasingly suggests that preoperative nutritional status is closely associated with postoperative outcomes. Serum prealbumin (PA), a sensitive marker of nutritional status, has shown prognostic value in a variety of disease settings.

Serum prealbumin is a low-molecular-weight protein synthesized by the liver and is commonly used to evaluate nutritional status and hepatic function [14, 15]. Compared with albumin, prealbumin has a shorter half-life, making it a more sensitive indicator of acute nutritional changes [16, 17]. In cancer patients, decreased prealbumin levels are frequently associated with tumor presence and progression. Moreover, fluctuations in prealbumin levels are closely associated with immune function, systemic inflammation, and clinical outcomes, particularly during the recovery phase following cancer treatment, where prealbumin serves as a reliable predictor of prognosis [18, 19]. Previous studies have suggested that preoperative serum prealbumin levels may be closely associated with postoperative survival in cancer patients [20, 21]. However, most existing studies are limited by single-center designs, small sample sizes, inadequate control of potential confounding variables, and lack of robust multicenter validation. Therefore, the prognostic value of preoperative serum prealbumin in patients undergoing radical resection for gallbladder cancer remains to be elucidated.

In addition to systemic inflammation–based indices, serum tumor markers play pivotal roles in diagnosis, differential diagnosis, staging, and prognostic assessment across gastrointestinal malignancies. Integrating blood-based markers with imaging features and molecular profiling can improve diagnostic accuracy and individualized decision-making. For example, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, combining CA19-9 with serologic D-dimer and CT features improved discrimination between cancer and benign pancreatic lesions, underscoring the value of multimodal biomarker panels [22]. Moreover, circulating exosomal microRNAs—such as hsa-let-7f-5p—have shown potential for non-invasive detection of metastatic disease [23]. Against this background, we hypothesized that preoperative prealbumin—a low-cost, inflammation- and nutrition-linked biomarker—could complement conventional tumor markers by capturing host-tumor interactions, thereby refining short-term risk stratification after radical resection of GBC.

This multicenter study aims to evaluate the prognostic value of preoperative serum prealbumin levels in predicting postoperative survival in patients with gallbladder cancer undergoing radical resection. It also seeks to explore the clinical utility of prealbumin as a biomarker for postoperative outcome assessment. The findings of this study may offer valuable insights for individualized treatment strategies and enhance postoperative prognostic management in gallbladder cancer.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent radical gallbladder cancer resection (R0 resection) at six centers, including the affiliated Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, the Third Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, the First Affiliated Hospital of the Army Medical University, and the Second Affiliated Hospital of the Army Medical University, between January 2015 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed gallbladder cancer; (2) R0 resection performed; (3) no preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (4) no history of other malignancies; and (5) availability of preoperative serum prealbumin data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of concurrent malignancies; (2) active systemic infection prior to surgery; (3) history of immunotherapy; and (4) severe systemic diseases (e.g., advanced cardiac disease, end-stage renal disease, decompensated cirrhosis) potentially affecting prealbumin levels. A total of 210 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Clinical data collection

All clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. The collected variables included demographic data (age, sex, weight, height), medical history, hematological parameters (white blood cell count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets [PLT], hemoglobin [Hb]), liver function markers (GGT, ALP, TBIL, ALT, AST, albumin, prealbumin), tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9), lymph node status, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss. Preoperative blood samples were obtained within one week prior to surgery. The most recent sample before the operation was used to ensure an accurate assessment of serum prealbumin levels. Tumor staging followed the AJCC/UICC TNM classification, 8th edition (2017) for gallbladder cancer.

Follow-up

Patients were monitored every 3 months during the first postoperative year through outpatient visits and telephone contact. Each visit included history/physical examination, laboratory tests (complete blood count and liver function), and tumor markers (CA19-9, CEA) at the clinician’s discretion. Cross-sectional imaging was obtained at scheduled visits or when clinically indicated, using contrast-enhanced abdominal CT (with chest CT if thoracic disease was suspected) or contrast-enhanced MRI (including MRCP when biliary assessment was required); PET/CT was reserved for equivocal findings. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from surgery to the first occurrence of tumor recurrence or death (whichever occurred first); recurrence required unequivocal radiologic evidence of new or progressive malignant lesions and/or pathologic confirmation, with ambiguous cases adjudicated at multidisciplinary review. All time-to-event analyses were administratively truncated at 12 months; events and times beyond 12 months were censored at 12 months. Median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Final follow-up was completed in December 2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous clinical variables were dichotomized using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with cut-offs chosen to maximize the Youden index. Unless otherwise specified, binary ROC curves were constructed for 12-month overall survival (OS) (event = death within 12 months; non-event = alive at 12 months). Resulting cut-offs (e.g., prealbumin 221 mg/L; hemoglobin 108.5 g/L) were derived within the cohort and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Associations between prealbumin and clinicopathological characteristics were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan–Meier (KM) methods were used to estimate survival functions, and between-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. All time-to-event analyses were administratively truncated at 12 months. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify prognostic factors. For multivariable Cox models, model size was constrained with reference to commonly recommended events-per-variable (EPV) principles, with covariates pre-specified by clinical relevance and supported by univariable screening to mitigate overfitting. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate discrimination for censored time-to-event outcomes, we additionally used time-dependent ROC/AUC at 12 months, and report AUCs with 95% CIs. As a complementary summary of survival, we estimated the 12-month restricted mean survival time (12-month RMST), defined as the area under the KM curve from time 0 to 12 months. We report 12-month RMSTs with 95% CIs and, where appropriate, between-group differences. KM medians were reported only if estimable; otherwise they were denoted as not reached (NR).

Results

Clinical and pathological characteristics of patients

A total of 210 patients who underwent radical resection for gallbladder cancer were included from six participating centers. The mean age of the patients was 60.75 years (range, 30–90 years). Over the entire observation period, 45 deaths (21.43%) occurred for OS and 76 (36.19%) RFS events (recurrence or death). The median follow-up was 12 months (IQR 12–12), estimated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between preoperative serum prealbumin and albumin levels (R = 0.300, p < 0.001; Fig. 1). Based on a cutoff value of 221 mg/L for preoperative serum prealbumin, patients were stratified into low and high prealbumin groups. The prealbumin cut-off of 221 mg/L was obtained by Youden-optimized ROC against 12-month OS. Among the 210 patients, 120 (57.1%) had prealbumin levels < 221 mg/L, while 90 (42.9%) had levels ≥ 221 mg/L. Prealbumin levels were significantly associated with hemoglobin (p < 0.001), albumin (p < 0.001), white blood cell count (p < 0.001), platelet count (p = 0.036), alkaline phosphatase (p < 0.001), gamma-glutamyl transferase (p < 0.001), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA, p = 0.006), cancer antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9, p < 0.001), lymph node metastasis (p = 0.002), total bilirubin (p < 0.001), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, p = 0.003), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, p < 0.001). Baseline characteristics of the 210 patients are summarized in Table 1.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis

Univariate and multivariate analysis of 1-Year overall survival (OS) in postoperative patients

The results of the univariate and multivariate analyses of 1-year overall survival (OS) following radical resection for gallbladder cancer (GBC) are presented in Table 2. Univariate analysis identified significant associations between 1-year OS and the following variables: history of gallbladder disease, hypertension, white blood cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, albumin, prealbumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin (TBIL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), cancer antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), TNM stage, lymph node metastasis, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss. In multivariate Cox regression analysis, platelet count, hemoglobin, prealbumin, TNM stage, and lymph node metastasis remained significantly associated with 1-year OS. Prealbumin was identified as an independent prognostic factor for 1-year OS following radical resection of GBC (HR = 0.179, 95% CI: 0.052–0.622; p = 0.007). Model complexity satisfied commonly recommended events-per-variable (EPV) thresholds, with covariates pre-specified by clinical relevance and univariable screening.

Survival outcomes in gallbladder cancer patients

Prognostic analysis of gallbladder cancer patients based on prealbumin levels

In this study, the 1-year overall survival (OS) was 78.6%, and the 12-month restricted mean survival time (12-month RMST) for OS was 11.0 months overall. By prealbumin group, the low-prealbumin group had a 1-year OS of 65.0% and a 12-month RMST of 10.2 months, whereas the high-prealbumin group had a 1-year OS of 96.7% and a 12-month RMST of 12.0 months. Between-group differences in 1-year OS and 12-month RMST were statistically significant (p < 0.001; Fig. 2A). The 1-year RFS was 63.8% overall, with a 12-month RMST of 9.6 months. In the low-prealbumin group, the 1-year RFS was 44.2% and the 12-month RMST was 8.2 months; in contrast, the high-prealbumin group showed a 1-year RFS of 90.0% and a 12-month RMST of 11.6 months. The between-group differences were significant (p < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

Prognostic analysis of gallbladder cancer patients based on hemoglobin levels

Hemoglobin levels demonstrated a trend similar to that observed for prealbumin. In the low-hemoglobin group, the 1-year overall survival (OS) was 58.1%, and the 12-month restricted mean survival time (12-month RMST) for OS was 9.8 months; in contrast, the high-hemoglobin group had a 1-year OS of 83.8% with a 12-month RMST of 11.2 months. Between-group differences in 1-year OS and 12-month RMST were statistically significant (p < 0.001; Fig. 2C). For RFS, the low-hemoglobin group showed a 1-year RFS of 34.9% and a 12-month RMST of 7.8 months, whereas the high-hemoglobin group had a 1-year RFS of 71.3% and a 12-month RMST of 10.1 months (p < 0.001; Fig. 2D).

Prognostic analysis based on combined prealbumin and hemoglobin levels in gallbladder cancer patients

Given that both hemoglobin and prealbumin significantly influence overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in gallbladder cancer (GBC), we combined these variables to evaluate postoperative outcomes. Patients were stratified by prespecified cutoffs—prealbumin 221 mg/L and hemoglobin 108.5 g/L—into three groups: Group 1 (low prealbumin and low hemoglobin), Group 2 (low prealbumin with high hemoglobin or high prealbumin with low hemoglobin), and Group 3 (high prealbumin and high hemoglobin). The 1-year OS for Groups 1, 2, and 3 was 50.0%, 73.6%, and 96.4%, respectively, with corresponding 12-month RMSTs of 9.4, 10.6, and 12.0 months. Similarly, the 1-year RFS was 27.8%, 52.7%, and 91.6%, with corresponding 12-month RMSTs of 7.2, 8.7, and 11.7 months, respectively. Between-group differences for both OS and RFS were statistically significant (p < 0.001; Fig. 3A–B).

ROC analysis of combined prealbumin and hemoglobin for prognosis in gallbladder cancer patients

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to further evaluate the prognostic performance of prealbumin, hemoglobin (Hb), and the combined prealbumin–hemoglobin model for postoperative survival in patients with gallbladder cancer (GBC). The combined model showed superior predictive accuracy for postoperative overall survival (OS) (AUC = 0.762, 95% CI: 0.684–0.840; Fig. 4A) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (AUC = 0.771, 95% CI: 0.706–0.836; Fig. 4B) compared with either prealbumin or hemoglobin alone.

Discussion

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy that is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor overall prognosis [3–5]. While tumor staging, histopathological subtype, and lymph node metastasis are widely recognized as key prognostic factors in GBC, these tumor-centric indicators do not adequately reflect the patient’s overall physiological status, especially with respect to nutritional and inflammatory conditions. Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers—particularly preoperative indicators—that can comprehensively assess patient condition is crucial for optimizing clinical management and improving prognosis in GBC.

There remains some debate regarding the relative utility of albumin and prealbumin as indicators of nutritional status [23, 24]. Albumin is widely used in clinically evaluating nutritional status and systemic inflammation [25–27]. However, several studies have demonstrated that prealbumin is more sensitive and specific than albumin in detecting protein-energy malnutrition [28, 29]. In the present study, Spearman’s correlation analysis showed a moderate positive correlation between preoperative albumin and prealbumin levels (R = 0.300, p < 0.001; Fig. 1). Furthermore, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that prealbumin, but not albumin, was an independent prognostic factor for postoperative OS and RFS in patients with GBC. As a hepatic protein with a short half-life, prealbumin is a sensitive marker of short-term nutritional fluctuations [16, 17]. Decreased prealbumin levels are commonly associated with malnutrition and heightened inflammatory responses [18, 19, 25–27]. These conditions may adversely affect immune function, tumor progression, and postoperative recovery, thereby compromising long-term survival. Therefore, prealbumin may serve as a more reliable indicator of nutritional status than albumin, particularly in the perioperative setting.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the prognostic significance of preoperative serum prealbumin (PA) levels in patients undergoing surgery for GBC. Prealbumin is a small-molecular-weight hepatic protein that reflects both nutritional status and systemic inflammation [14, 15]. Previous research has linked low prealbumin levels with poor prognosis across various malignancies [30, 31]. Our findings further support the role of prealbumin as a valuable preoperative prognostic marker in GBC. In this multicenter retrospective study involving 210 patients with GBC, preoperative serum prealbumin levels were significantly associated with both OS and RFS. These findings suggest that serum prealbumin levels may serve as an independent prognostic factor and aid in guiding individualized treatment strategies for GBC.

Association between serum prealbumin levels and postoperative survival in gallbladder cancer

Preoperative serum prealbumin levels were significantly associated with postoperative survival outcomes in patients with gallbladder cancer (GBC). In this study, the 1-year overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates were 65.0% and 44.1%, respectively, in the low prealbumin group. In contrast, the high prealbumin group had 1-year OS and RFS rates of 96.6% and 90.0%, respectively. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and multivariate Cox regression confirmed that preoperative prealbumin levels were independent prognostic factors for both OS (HR = 0.179, p = 0.007) and RFS (HR = 0.220, p < 0.001), indicating robust predictive value.

Association between prealbumin and clinicopathological features

Preoperative serum prealbumin levels were significantly associated with several clinicopathological features, including white blood cell count, platelet count, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), lymph node metastasis, and total bilirubin (TBIL). Notably, patients with lymph node metastasis exhibited significantly lower prealbumin levels, suggesting a potential link between reduced prealbumin and immune suppression or metastatic progression. Reduced prealbumin levels may reflect an immunosuppressive state, potentially influencing tumor progression and treatment efficacy. In addition, prealbumin exhibited strong correlations with other nutritional and immune markers, such as hemoglobin and albumin, further supporting its role as a sensitive indicator of nutritional and immune status, and underscoring its potential utility in postoperative prognostic assessment in GBC.

Impact of preoperative nutritional status on prognosis in gallbladder cancer

Preoperative nutritional status plays a critical role in determining treatment outcomes in patients with gallbladder cancer (GBC). Malnutrition prior to surgery can impair surgical tolerance, contribute to postoperative immune dysfunction, and increase the risk of tumor recurrence [32–34]. Prealbumin, a biomarker of short-term nutritional status, effectively reflects the patient’s nutritional condition and provides valuable insights into postoperative recovery. Our findings underscore the importance of preoperative nutritional assessment and support the clinical utility of prealbumin as a prognostic indicator after surgery. Although prealbumin has demonstrated prognostic value in patients with GBC, future research should explore its integration with other clinical and laboratory markers to develop more accurate prognostic models. Moreover, the potential role of prealbumin as a therapeutic target warrants further investigation, particularly regarding its application in improving nutritional status and modulating immune function.

Combined prognostic utility of prealbumin and hemoglobin

We further investigated the prognostic value of combining prealbumin with hemoglobin. Patients were stratified based on prealbumin and hemoglobin levels, and the combined use of these markers significantly improved the accuracy of postoperative survival prediction in GBC patients. Patients with elevated levels of both prealbumin and hemoglobin had markedly better postoperative overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) compared to those assessed by either marker alone. Compared with the use of prealbumin or hemoglobin alone, the combined assessment demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity in predicting postoperative outcomes (AUCs = 0.762 and 0.771, respectively). These findings offer a novel perspective for clinical practice, suggesting that integrating multiple biomarkers may allow for a more comprehensive prognostic assessment and better inform the design of personalized treatment strategies.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design is susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding despite adjustment and the use of data from multiple centers. Second, all time-to-event analyses were administratively truncated at 12 months; accordingly, our inferences concern short-term outcomes summarized by 1-year Kaplan–Meier estimates and 12-month restricted mean survival time (12-month RMST), and may not generalize beyond this horizon. Third, only preoperative prealbumin was analyzed—postoperative dynamics were not captured, precluding assessment of whether longitudinal changes improve prognostication. Finally, heterogeneity in perioperative care and adjuvant treatments across participating institutions was not explicitly modeled, so unmeasured practice variation and other confounders (e.g., nutritional support, intercurrent inflammation) may have influenced the observed associations. Prospective studies with external validation, standardized treatment data, and serial biomarker measurements are warranted to confirm generalizability and clinical utility.

Future directions

To further validate the clinical utility of prealbumin in predicting postoperative prognosis in gallbladder cancer, future research should include prospective clinical trials and integrate additional nutritional and inflammation-related biomarkers to develop more accurate prognostic models. Additionally, given the close interplay between prealbumin, malnutrition, and inflammation, future studies should focus on improving postoperative outcomes by enhancing nutritional support and regulating inflammatory responses.

Conclusion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of patients undergoing radical resection for gallbladder cancer, lower preoperative serum prealbumin was independently associated with worse 12-month outcomes, as evidenced by both 1-year Kaplan–Meier estimates and 12-month restricted mean survival time (12-month RMST) for overall and recurrence-free survival. When combined with hemoglobin, prealbumin improved 12-month risk discrimination compared with either marker alone, suggesting complementary prognostic value for short-term postoperative risk stratification. These findings support the potential utility of integrating prealbumin—an inflammation- and nutrition-linked biomarker—into perioperative assessment; however, they are limited to a 12-month time horizon under administrative truncation and derive cut-offs within the study cohort. Prospective, externally validated studies are warranted to confirm generalizability and to evaluate whether incorporating prealbumin (alone or with hemoglobin) can meaningfully inform postoperative surveillance and adjuvant treatment decisions.
