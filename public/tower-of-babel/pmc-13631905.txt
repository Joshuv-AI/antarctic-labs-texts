Evaluation of venous anatomy after treatment with an intraluminal tissue debulking system in a porcine model
Authors: William Marston, Eric Dooley, Abha Chinubhai
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResidual venous obstruction and in-stent restenosis are common and challenging complications that may occur after interventional procedures performed for acute deep vein thrombosis or chronic iliofemoral venous obstruction. A novel fully integrated thrombectomy system [Recana Thrombectomy Device (RTD)] was designed to treat (in-stent restenosis) and native vessel obstructions. The system includes a debulking catheter, collection baskets, and a family of sheaths. This report describes its use in animal models of native and stented veins to assess safety compared with currently available thrombectomy devices.MethodsThe RTD was studied in comparison to two currently marketed thrombectomy devices, the Argon CLEANER 15 (CLEANER; Argon Medical Devices Inc.) and the Inari ClotTriever (ClotTriever Catheter; Inari Medical, Inc.). Devices were studied in a porcine iliac vein model used in a manner consistent with good laboratory practice for Nonclinical Laboratory Studies. A total of 26 iliac veins in 13 animals were studied to evaluate performance in normal veins (9 animals, 18 iliac veins) and in four animals with eight iliac veins pretreated with iliac stents implanted 21 days before device treatment. Twelve normal iliac veins were treated with the RTD, four normal veins were treated with CLEANER, and two with the ClotTriever. Five prestented iliac veins were treated with the RTD, and three prestented veins were treated with the CLEANER. Procedures were completed under fluoroscopic and intravascular ultrasound guidance. For each model, both iliac veins were treated with 10 passes of the test or control thrombectomy device through the length of the common and external iliac veins. Survival and sacrifice periods were completed at varied time points to evaluate peak histological injury potential, vein wall healing, and vein patency. Necropsy was performed at 5 ± 2 or 30 ± 2 days after catheter intervention for gross evaluation of the iliac veins and other related tissues. Histology was performed on representative sections of treated iliac veins to evaluate wall damage, thrombosis, or other effects of intervention.ResultsAll animals survived to their designated end points with no unexpected mortality. One common femoral vein access complication led to unilateral perivenous hematoma and thrombosis. Another animal showed postintervention venous constriction with eventual vessel occlusion. All other iliac veins (n = 24) remained patent without significant stenosis at necropsy. No clinically relevant lacerations, perforations, or hemorrhagic events were observed with either the Test or Control devices. Histology revealed intimal disruption and inflammation with all devices, more pronounced with the RTD compared with CLEANER but similar to those seen after use of the ClotTriever. Mild perivenous bruising occurred in some Test device cases, without evidence of transmural bleeding.ConclusionsIn an in vivo model of stented and native iliac veins, a novel venous tissue debulking system was able to engage intraluminal material without serious injury. Vein wall trauma was comparable to a commonly used mechanical thrombectomy device, with no transmural lacerations or device-related hemorrhage. These findings support continued development toward initial clinical trials in patients with venous obstruction.Clinical relevanceThis experimental study utilizes a porcine model for examination of a novel venous thrombectomy device in normal and stented iliac veins. The study examines the degree of injury occurring within the vein wall in normal and stented iliac veins after treatment with the test device compared with currently marketed thrombectomy devices. The feasibility of removal of the material causing in-stent restenosis is established using the Test device. The study also provides key information on the response of the iliac vein wall in this model to intraluminal devices that impact the wall during treatment.

Metallic stents are commonly inserted into the inferior vena cava (IVC), iliac, and common femoral veins to treat symptoms related to chronic compression or intrinsic obstruction resulting in restriction of the normal venous outflow from the pelvis and lower extremity. In general, patency rates have been reported to be favorable after these procedures1; however, the cases of early and late thrombosis or obstruction of previously stented veins have been reported. Thrombosis after stenting is reportedly more frequent after stenting is performed for post-thrombotic (PT) disease than after stenting for nonthrombotic obstruction. Patients with poor inflow from the femoral and deep femoral veins to the stented segment have been reported to be at the highest risk of stent thrombosis.2,3 If acute thrombosis occurs obstructing the stented segments, thrombectomy may be performed within a few weeks of thrombosis using standard mechanical venous thrombectomy techniques to remove the acute thrombus. If venous thrombosis is not identified and treated early, intraluminal clot becomes organized and difficult to remove percutaneously.4 Also, in some cases, in-stent stenosis occurs over time which may gradually lead to stent occlusion, reported in 5% of cases in a systematic literature review by Badesha et al.5 The material causing in-stent restenosis is reportedly organized thrombus with less cellularity than the material occurring in arterial poststent cases.6 It is usually firm, organized, and is not amenable to removal with standard thrombectomy devices. It may be compacted against the wall of the stented vein using high-pressure angioplasty balloons, but in some cases this technique does not yield significant lumen gain. Limited options are currently available for treatment of chronic in-stent stenosis that is not amenable to venous thrombectomy or angioplasty.

In this study, we describe preclinical testing of a percutaneous device designed to remove wall adherent thrombus and in-stent vein thickening using a porcine iliac vein model, with and without stents.

Methods

Device descriptions: Test device

The Recana Thrombectomy Device (RTD) is designed to debulk deep venous obstruction in the lower extremities, including thrombus and PT tissue, for improved patency in native vessel or restenosed venous stents. The system is comprised of the debulking catheter, collection basket(s) with delivery sheaths, and a family of sheaths.

The debulking catheter utilizes an adjustable diameter stainless steel coring element to shave, scrape, core, or otherwise liberate obstructive tissue from the vasculature for removal (Fig 1). Handle controls enable expansion and collapse of the coring element to achieve the desired working diameter. The debulking catheter is 0.035″ guidewire and 0.060″ basket shaft compatible and provides a working length of 80 cm for delivery and use via ipsilateral access.Fig 1Recana venous thrombectomy system with helical Coring Element highlighted.

Recana venous thrombectomy system with helical Coring Element highlighted.

Collection basket(s) are self-expanding nitinol wire form and braided structures designed to collect and remove liberated tissue from the vasculature (Fig 2). The basket shaft has a 0.035″ guidewire compatible lumen for over the wire delivery through a 9F Delivery Sheath with a working length of 95 cm for either ipsilateral or contralateral access and placement.Fig 2Collecting baskets for use with Recana coring element. Baskets are 18 and 30 mm in diameter.

Collecting baskets for use with Recana coring element. Baskets are 18 and 30 mm in diameter.

Basket diameters of 18 and 30 mm provide compatibility for use within the iliofemoral segment and inferior vena cava, respectively. During the procedure, baskets may be captured into the sheath and redeployed multiple times using the same delivery sheath to facilitate incremental removal of the desired tissue from the vasculature.

Sheaths for introduction of the debulking catheter and collection baskets into the vasculature and for the recapture and removal of liberated tissue via the collection basket(s) are offered in four-size configurations (13F × 30 cm, 13F × 90 cm, 16F × 30 cm, and 16F × 90 cm) providing optionality based on access vessel size and location.

Device description: Comparator devices

Control device no. 1: Argon Cleaner 15 (CLEANER, Argon Medical Devices Inc.)

The CLEANER Rotational Thrombectomy System is a 7F percutaneous catheter-based system (single-piece construction). A disposable, handheld battery-operated rotator drive unit is attached to a sinuous wire which rotates at approximately 4000 rpm. The wire and atraumatic soft tip are radiopaque for fluoroscopic visualization.

Control device no. 2: ClotTriever Catheter (CT, Inari Medical, Inc.)

The CT Catheter features an atraumatic nitinol coring element that can attain full wall apposition in veins between 6 and 16 mm and a braided collection bag, designed to core and collect clot for extraction.

Porcine iliac vein model

The care and use of animals for this preclinical study were reviewed and approved by local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee before the start of study. Treatment of the animals was in accordance with regulations outlined in the USDA Animal Welfare Act (9 Code of Federal Regulations; parts 1, 2, and 3) and the conditions specified in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.7

Domestic Yorkshire cross swine weighing 72 to 82 kg were anesthetized with Telazol before intubation. They were placed under positive-pressure ventilation and maintained under anesthesia using isoflurane titrated to effect. All procedures were performed using aseptic technique.

Blood samples were collected for clinical pathology and baseline activated clotting time (ACT) before heparinization. Animals were kept warm throughout the preparation and interventional procedures using blankets and/or warming units, as necessary. The initial percent inhalant anesthetic administered, as well as any changes in concentration, were documented. An intravenous (IV) catheter was placed, and IV fluid therapy was initiated and maintained throughout the procedure with 0.9% NaCl or equivalent. The bilateral distal femoral veins were prepped for percutaneous access, and introducer sheaths were inserted in an antegrade direction to access the femoral veins. An arterial sheath was introduced percutaneously for blood collection (as needed), and blood pressure monitoring. Blood was collected for determination of baseline ACT, and a loading dose of heparin was administered. ACT was monitored approximately every 15 to 30 minutes, and additional heparin boluses were administered to maintain a target ACT of ∼250 to 500 seconds. intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance and fluoroscopy were used to assess and map potential target sites. All potential target sites were assessed for vein diameters and lengths.

Study protocol

Four animals underwent preinsertion of IV stents in both iliac veins using nitinol stents in six iliac veins (3 Medtronic Abre stents and 3 Bard Venovo stents) and Elgiloy stents in two iliac veins (Wallstent, Boston Scientific Inc.). All stents were 12 to 16 mm in diameter and 60 to 80 mm in length. Different stent types were used for the study to determine whether the RTD could be operated within veins treated with a variety of stent types without causing disruption of the stent, entanglement on stent edges or struts, or otherwise impairing normal stent function. Twenty one days after stent insertion, the animals were anesthetized for instrumentation with the RTD (n = 5 iliac veins) or CLEANER (n = 3) using the protocol described further.

Nine animals did not undergo prestenting and served to evaluate the changes in normal veins after treatment with the RTD (n = 12 iliac veins), the CLEANER (n = 4), or the ClotTriever (n = 2). In each animal studied, the test device and/or a control device were deployed and used to treat the native or stented bilateral iliac veins as described in Table I. For each limb, the RTD, CLEANER or ClotTriever was inserted through its respective sheath and positioned at the top of the ipsilateral common iliac vein (CIV). The respective device was expanded at this location per manufacturer's instructions and slowly moved down the length of the CIV to the proximal external iliac vein (EIV). When using the RTD, the device was expanded until wall contact with the vein was felt, and the device was rotated in both directions while withdrawing to simulate treatment of a diseased vein. The CLEANER was activated (4000 rpm) during the passage down the CIV to simulate treatment, and the ClotTriever was released from its sheath and left in the open position while pulling it down the CIV to simulate treatment of a diseased vein. Each respective device was recaptured in its sheath and moved to the top of the CIV where it was redeployed, and the procedure was repeated until a total of 10 treatment passages down the CIV had been completed. After completion of the study procedures, guidewires and sheaths were removed and hemostasis obtained with direct pressure at the access site. Animals recovered from anesthesia and were returned to housing in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines. No postoperative anticoagulant medications were administered. Survival and sacrifice periods were completed at varied timepoints optimized to evaluate peak histological injury potential, as well as vein wall healing and vein patency examination. Before euthanasia, bilateral iliac vein venography and IVUS guidance were performed to document vessel integrity and patency.

Necropsy

Euthanasia was performed at either 5 or 30 days after treatment with the thrombectomy devices as listed in Table I. After euthanasia, all animals were subjected to a full necropsy by a Board-Certified Veterinary Pathologist (Study Pathologist). The necropsy consisted of the gross visual assessment of the body surfaces, cavities, and their contents and included the heart, lungs, kidneys, liver, spleen, and brain, which were sampled or immersed whole in 10% neutral buffered formalin for evaluation. Abnormalities were described completely and recorded. The treated segments of the iliac veins were carefully examined bilaterally for evidence of perforation/extravasation and thrombus formation. The treated vessels were then prepared for immersion fixation and shipment to the histology laboratory. The main pulmonary arteries were opened lengthwise to assess for evidence of thromboembolism.

Histopathology

The goal of the histology sectioning was to capture transverse cross-sections through each vessel representing areas located within the treatment site (bound by sutures placed by the study pathologist after explant). Five representative samples (starting approximately 1 cm from the iliac confluence and ending approximately 1 cm from the takeoff of the hypogastric vein) were sectioned from each treated vessel (Fig 3). Histology staining included hematoxylin and eosin and Elastin Trichrome performed on all treated test/control vessel segments. All tissue processing procedures followed the histology laboratory standard operating procedures for tissue processing and with use of their appropriate forms, as per good laboratory practice regulations.Fig 3Sites of transverse iliac vein slices obtained at necropsy for histologic analysis.

Sites of transverse iliac vein slices obtained at necropsy for histologic analysis.

The pathologist used a semiquantitative scoring method for relevant histopathological features on each evaluated vessel section. Since the histopathologic changes were not uniform in all locations of the vessel sections, the pathologist recorded three to five observations of the changes from each vessel section to better characterize the changes seen. The scoring criteria used for guidance are listed in Table II. Relevant histopathological changes were scored in specified categories grouped as endothelial loss, neointimal response, platelets/fibrin/thrombus, venous wall injury, hemorrhage/perforation/extravasation, inflammation, and perivascular fibroplasia. In veins pretreated with iliac stents, morphometric analysis was performed to assess the structural characteristics of the material developing around and covering the stents, and treatment-induced mechanical effects. Statistical analysis

Wall thickness and observed histologic changes after treatment were compared between the test and control devices using the Student t test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute).

Results

A total of 26 CIVs were treated in 13 swine. Eight iliac stents were inserted in four swine before device treatment, using nitinol stents in six veins and Elgiloy stents in two veins. A total of 17 iliac veins (including five stented veins) were treated with the RTD, seven veins with the CLEANER (including three stented veins), and two nonstented veins with the ClotTriever. Operation of the RTD within previously stented iliac veins did not result in any episodes of stent entanglement, significant movement of the stent or other evidence of disruption of the stent on fluoroscopy or IVUS guidance. Euthanasia and necropsy were performed at 5 ± 2 days in 10 animals, and 30 ± 2 days in three animals as listed in Table I.

All animals survived to their designated time of sacrifice. Iliac vein thrombosis was found at the time of euthanasia in two of 26 treated iliac veins (0 of 8 prestented iliac veins). In one animal, dislodgement of the sheath placed in the right common femoral vein occurred during device exchange before deployment or operation of the test device. Perivascular bleeding resulted in a moderate hematoma along the common femoral and iliac veins. The sheath was reinserted using a new access site in the common femoral vein, and the procedure was completed per protocol using the test device. Completion venography and IVUS guidance revealed stenosis of the iliac vein along the site of deployment of the test device. The iliac vein was determined to be thrombosed at 5-day follow-up before euthanasia by venography. In the second animal, the iliac vein was noted to be significantly contracted after treatment with the test device. There was no evidence of thrombus within the vein at study completion on IVUS guidance or venography. This vein was also found to be thrombosed at 30-day follow-up on venography and necropsy. The remaining 24 iliac veins were all found to be patent with no evidence of iliac vein stenosis >30% at the time of pre-euthanasia on venography or IVUS guidance.

Vessel injury on gross examination

No evidence of full thickness injury, vein laceration or catheter penetration with perivascular hemorrhage or pseudoaneurysm was found in any case, other than the case described above due to sheath dislodgement. In all nonstented veins treated with the RTD, mild perivascular bruising was noted in the tissue around the treated segment of iliac vein. This was seen as red blood cells in the perivascular tissue without significant thickening or hematoma in the tissue. Some veins treated with the CLEANER and ClotTriever were also found to have perivascular bruising, but not as frequent as with the RTD. Veins pretreated with stents were not found to have evidence of perivascular bruising after treatment with the RTD or the CLEANER.

In the group of animals receiving stents 21 days before device treatment, significant intraluminal intimal thickening was identified at pretreatment venography (Fig 4). The thickness of intimal thickening varied at different sections on postnecropsy evaluation. The maximal thickness seen on any section averaged 2.6 mm (range, 1.4 -4.4 mm). During treatment with the RTD, small pieces of this intraluminal material were captured by the basket in all cases indicating that the device was able to remove adherent material covering the stented veins. After treatment with the RTD and the CLEANER, the average percent of luminal area compared with neointimal area was determined for each stented iliac vein. Discussion

Intraluminal venous obstruction may occur due to a variety of pathologic mechanisms including acute thrombosis, chronic PT scarring, and vein wall hyperplasia after stenting.8 A variety of catheter-based treatments have been developed to remove acute thrombus from the venous system, but limited options exist for other causes of chronic venous obstruction.9 Since these causes of obstruction are usually firmly attached to the internal vein wall, more aggressive devices are required to separate the abnormal material from the vein wall.10 The test device in this study is a catheter system designed for this purpose. Specifically, the debulking catheter's coring element is configured to engage pathologic material that is adherent to the vein wall and remove it by shaving the material down progressively through multiple catheter rotations. The edges of the coring element are positioned to avoid progressively deeper digging into the adherent wall material to minimize the risk of full thickness wall lacerations or other transmural injury.

It is possible that catheter systems designed for more aggressive removal of attached material would create some response in the vein wall itself. For a device to be clinically useful, it would ideally remove attached material without resulting in significant injury to the vein wall that would result in thrombosis, stenosis or other changes that would negatively affect vein function. In this study, we evaluated the vein wall response to intraluminal treatment with the test device compared to two clinically marketed devices designed for removal of abnormal intraluminal venous material.

Gross examination of the 17 veins treated with the test device identified thrombosis in two veins, one of which was related to technical error. The other 15 veins were patent with no evidence of significant stenosis or transmural wall injury. It is unclear why one vein developed vasospasm after treatment leading to thrombosis, but this is a condition occasionally seen after venous interventions in humans as well. Notably, vasodilators were not administered during or after the procedures and anticoagulants were not administered after procedure completion.

Histologic examination of the nonstented vein wall after treatment with the RTD identified evidence of wall reaction to instrumentation with the RTD and both controls. On average these vein wall changes were rated to be minimal or mild with none reaching a mean score greater than 2.1 where a score of 2 indicated mild changes. The most significant changes were found in the category of endothelial cell loss followed by platelet/fibrin deposition. Changes in all measured histologic categories were more significant in the RTD than those observed with the CLEANER. It would be expected that devices designed to contact the vein wall would be likely to result in some wall changes related to the force with which the device impacts the vein wall. Given the more aggressive mechanism of action of the RTD compared with the CLEANER, it is not surprising that the vein wall changes were greater after instrumentation with the Test device. The changes seen in normal veins after instrumentation in this animal model suggest that the devices studied should only be deployed within abnormal veins containing the pathologic material they are designed to treat.

Vein wall changes after instrumentation with the ClotTriever were comparable to those observed with the RTD with most categories measured to be not statistically different. However, even in this comparison, vein wall changes in all categories other than endothelial cell loss were minimal or mild (Table IV). The overall significance of the endothelial cell loss seen on histology after treatment with the RTD and ClotTriever is unclear. We assume that it occurred due to the components of the devices scraping the vein wall as would be required to remove adherent thrombus resulting in some loss of endothelial cells. There was no evidence of short-term adverse effects on venography or gross examination of the veins 5 days after instrumentation, but the long-term effects of endothelial cell loss cannot be determined from this animal model.

In the animals pretreated with iliac stents, it was interesting to note the significant neointimal material that covered the stents 3 weeks later, resulting in a layer of tissue measuring several millimeters thick in some cases. This neointimal coverage has been reported in other animal models of iliac vein stenting to occur early after stent placement.11,12 The RTD demonstrated the ability to remove neointimal material from the lumen in these cases, with histologic confirmation that the material captured in the collecting basket was fibrous in nature and not acute thrombus. Although encouraging, the limited number of animals studied prevents a comparison of the lumen gain after treatment with the test vs control devices.

There are several limitations to consider in evaluating this data. The animal model chosen has some similarity to the human pelvic venous system but is unable to fully test changes that might occur in clinical use. The veins studied were normal veins with no history of significant compression, thrombosis or PT changes encountered when using these devices clinically. Diseased veins may express different wall changes after treatment with devices that contact the vein wall. The study protocol only evaluated changes occurring in the first 30 days after treatment with the test and control devices. It is possible that longer follow-up would identify additional vein wall changes that might result in functional changes. The number of animals studied, and the length of follow-up are limited by the high cost of large animal studies. Additional study in a larger number of stented and nonstented veins would allow additional histologic analysis that could strengthen the study conclusions.

Conclusions

In an in vivo model of stented and native iliac veins, the novel venous tissue debulking system was able to engage intraluminal material without creating serious injury to the vein wall. Vein wall trauma was comparable to that observed with a commonly used mechanical thrombectomy device, with no transmural lacerations or significant device-related hemorrhage. Stents placed in the iliac veins in this model developed significant neointimal thickness covering the stents within 3 weeks. The RTD demonstrated the ability to remove pieces of fibrous neointima which were captured in the collecting basket. These findings support continued development of the device toward initial clinical use in patients with venous obstruction.
