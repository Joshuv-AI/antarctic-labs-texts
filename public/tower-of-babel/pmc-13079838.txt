Mixed attention mechanism multi-task learning for fetal abdominal standard plane recognition and key anatomical structure detection
Authors: Yapeng Li, Yubing Huang, Zhonghua Liu, Tianen Fu, Shaozheng He, Yaocheng Wan, Yuling Fan, Peizhong Liu, Guorong Lyu, Shunlan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In prenatal ultrasound, accurately identifying fetal abdominal ultrasound standard planes (FAUSP) is challenging due to the complexity of anatomical structures. To address this, we developed FAUSP-NET, a multi-task network that integrates a mixed attention mechanism for real-time FAUSP recognition and anatomical structure detection. The network uses a residual backbone for feature extraction, enhanced by an attention mechanism and Large Selective Kernel Block (LSKblock) for better focus on key regions. A Focal_EIoU loss function addresses class imbalance and improves bounding box regression. Trained on 6767 FAUSP images, FAUSP-NET outperforms 24 popular models, achieving mAP@0.5 of 0.961 and mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.653 in detection, with plane recognition accuracy of 0.972. Its average detection time is 24.1 ms. Doctor evaluations show that FAUSP-NET’s accuracy is comparable to senior physicians, offering significant support for clinical ultrasound diagnostics.

Introduction

Ultrasound is widely used in prenatal diagnosis, screening, and treatment of a wide range of conditions due to its lack of radiation, lower cost, and immediate availability of images1. Over the years, ultrasound imaging has become the screening technique of choice during pregnancy2. It not only assesses fetal growth and development but also observes the dynamic behavior of the fetus, which provides clinicians with extremely important information to help them better understand the spatial anatomical relationships of the fetus and to carefully examine suspected fetal anomalies3.

The main purposes of fetal ultrasound examinations include determining fetal viability, assessing gestational age, performing biometry, and checking for congenital anomalies4. Typically, clinical obstetric ultrasound examinations include manual scanning views, standard plane selection, biometry, and the final diagnosis of diseases. Acquiring standard planes is one of the key steps in conducting biometry and diagnosing fetal diseases5. According to the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) recommendations6, mid-pregnancy is the optimal period for prenatal fetal abdominal ultrasound screening7. Commonly used basic rapid screening views include the abdominal circumference section (ACS), bladder horizontal section (BHS), umbilical cord section (UCS), both kidneys horizontal section (BKHS), right kidney sagittal section (RKS), left kidney sagittal section (LKS), and both kidneys coronal section (BKCS). The details of these views and their critical anatomical structures are thoroughly illustrated in Fig. 1. The ACS is primarily used to measure fetal abdominal circumference, serving as a critical metric for estimating fetal weight and monitoring fetal growth8. The UCS wall entrance section is employed to inspect the position where the umbilical cord enters the fetal abdomen and to detect abnormalities related to the umbilical wall9. The BHS allows for the observation of the fetal bladder, assessing its size and shape, which aids in ruling out urinary and developmental abnormalities10. The BKHS enables physicians to observe the position, size, and morphology of both kidneys, evaluating kidney development11. The LKS and the RKS provide a more detailed examination of each kidney’s length and width12. Lastly, the BKCS concurrently displays the coronal aspect of both kidneys, facilitating a comparison of symmetry and structural integrity between the two sides13. These standard planes are indispensable tools in fetal abdominal diagnosis, which allow doctors to assess the fetal health condition comprehensively.

In clinical applications, ultrasound imaging can be affected by various artifacts such as acoustic shadows, scatter noise, motion blur, and boundary loss14. Acquiring standard planes typically requires manual operation by obstetricians, demanding not only extensive knowledge of fetal anatomy but also good hand-eye coordination and substantial clinical experience15. Due to the uneven distribution of medical resources in some regions, the quality of ultrasound images can be impacted by the level of the physician, where minor operational errors may render the planes unsuitable for abdominal diagnosis16. In clinical practice, it can be challenging to differentiate FAUSP images or anatomical structures due to their high similarity. As illustrated in Fig. 1, LKS and RKS, as well as UCS and BKHS, exhibit significant resemblance. Additionally, the umbilical artery segment (UAS) in BHS and the umbilical cord (CORD) in UCS, as well as the spine (SP) in LKS and RKS, and the rib (RIB) in BKCS, also show structural similarities. These highly similar images or anatomical structures make it more challenging for sonographers to identify standard planes. The quality of the planes obtained by sonographers often relies on personal subjective judgment, inevitably introducing variations among clinicians and making the process time-consuming. For less experienced sonographers, the decision-making process in identifying standard planes is particularly challenging17. In addition to operator experience, differences in ultrasound imaging systems and equipment may also influence image quality. Variations in hardware configuration, transducer frequency, spatial resolution, and built-in image processing algorithms can lead to differences in clarity and diagnostic reliability across devices. Thus, improving the quality of ultrasound planes has become an urgent need in clinical examinations.

Fetal abdominal ultrasound standard planes (FAUSP) plays a pivotal role in fetal growth monitoring, structural anomaly screening, and perinatal risk assessment. However, current clinical practice still relies heavily on manual operation and subjective experience, leading to operator-dependent variability, insufficient consistency of detection results, and reduced efficiency. To address these limitations, we propose FAUSP-NET as a more efficient, objective, and accurate automated detection approach, aiming to enhance the reliability of prenatal care and its clinical applicability. This network can detect 14 different fetal abdominal anatomical structures in real-time and identify 7 types of standard ultrasound planes based on the detection results. This reduces the difficulties and errors encountered by physicians during the procedure and assists them in performing fetal abdominal ultrasound examinations more quickly and accurately. Related work

Deep learning in medical ultrasound analysis

In recent years, deep learning technologies have been extensively applied in the analysis of medical images, particularly in the processing and analysis of ultrasound images18. By mimicking the complex algorithms of human brain information processing, deep learning effectively handles and interprets large volumes of medical data, significantly enhancing the application value in fields such as automated diagnosis and anomaly detection19. These technologies not only greatly improve the accuracy of medical image analysis but also considerably accelerate processing speeds, enabling medical professionals to more quickly obtain crucial diagnostic information, thereby enhancing the efficiency and success rate of patient treatment.

Single-task and multi-task learning approaches in fetal ultrasound analysis

A growing body of research has applied single-task deep learning models to the recognition of fetal ultrasound standard planes. Chen et al. introduced a framework using a Transfer Recurrent Neural Network, integrating deep visual feature extraction and temporal learning models, to automatically detect standard planes in fetal ultrasound. This effectively overcomes the challenge of limited training data20. Chen et al. developed an automated approach that employs deep convolutional neural networks to locate standard planes in fetal abdominal ultrasound images. Tested on 219 videos, this method achieved a classification accuracy exceeding 90%, significantly enhancing the efficacy of prenatal ultrasound diagnostics21. To address the challenges of classifying standard planes in the fetal abdomen, Chen et al. proposed a method using domain-adapted deep CNNs to locate Fetal Abdominal Standard Planes (FASP). This approach effectively captures the complex appearance of FASP, thereby achieving improved classification performance22. Yu et al. designed a deep convolutional neural network architecture to automatically identify standard planes in fetal facial ultrasound. This method also integrates transfer learning and data augmentation techniques to enhance the model’s performance and accuracy in clinical diagnostics23.

With the advancement of deep learning, MTL models have been widely applied in the field of medical image analysis, primarily because they can handle multiple tasks simultaneously, thereby improving efficiency, robustness, and generalization24. In fetal abdominal ultrasound detection involves the tasks of target detection of key abdominal structures and image classification, single-task models often show limited adaptability to complex images25. In contrast, MTL models, by learning multiple related tasks within the same network, are better able to leverage shared feature representations, thereby enhancing generalization across tasks26.

Some scholars have applied multi-task learning to ultrasound detection. To alleviate the workload of sonographers and improve the accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability of standard plane detection in ultrasound, Zhao et al. proposed a model for Ultrasound Standard Plane Detection based on multi-task learning and a hybrid knowledge graph27. Guo et al. introduced a detection framework that combines a coarse-to-fine detection strategy with multi-task learning, enhancing feature representation by integrating traditional and deep learning features. This framework, implemented as an end-to-end network, accurately detects femoral, thalamic, and abdominal ultrasound images, effectively reducing the workload of sonographers28. Baumgartner. et al. developed a multi-task convolutional neural network model for the automatic detection and classification of 13 standard fetal views in 2-D ultrasound, addressing the complex problem of accurately identifying and interpreting standard fetal scanning planes29. To improve the recognition of fetal heart standard planes and the detection of key anatomical structures, Li et al. proposed FHUSP-NET, which can automatically identify five standard planes and ten anatomical structures, thereby enhancing quality control and assisting less experienced physicians30.

Label-efficient and self-supervised learning strategies in fetal ultrasound analysis

Recent advances in label-efficient learning have driven substantial progress in fetal ultrasound analysis, particularly in reducing the reliance on large-scale manual annotations. A variety of contrastive, self-supervised, federated, and domain-specific pretraining strategies have been explored to enhance standard plane recognition under limited labeling condition. To evaluate SimCLR contrastive learning for reducing annotation needs in fetal ultrasound plane identification. Giovanna et al. evaluated SimCLR-based contrastive learning for fetal ultrasound standard plane identification and found it particularly beneficial in scenarios with small datasets and low inter-class variability31. Thunakala et al. proposed VGGSA network integrates a spatial attention module into a VGG-19 backbone to improve multiclass classification of fetal biometry planes in ultrasound imaging32. Cheng et al. introduced a self-supervised local detector and descriptor for probe motion estimation during fetal plane acquisition. By jointly learning local feature representation and motion estimation without requiring manual annotation, this method improves feature matching and generalization, demonstrating the potential of self-supervised strategies for enhancing automation in clinical ultrasound33. Fiorentino et al. introduced a federated denoising framework for fetal standard plane detection that first applies SimCLR on the largest client to learn robust representations, followed by threshold-based KNN relabeling to correct noisy annotations. Noise-free prototypes and the pretrained backbone are then shared with smaller clients, and ensemble majority voting helps mitigate distribution shifts, improving F1-scores under privacy constraints34. In parallel, Ambsdorf et al. investigated domain-specific pretraining using DINOv2 on a 2-million-image fetal ultrasound dataset35.

The aforementioned studies have made significant contributions to automated fetal ultrasound analysis and have advanced label-efficient standard plane recognition. However, these methods primarily focus on enhancing feature representation or correcting noisy labels and therefore do not address the combined challenge of detecting anatomical structures and classifying standard planes in the fetal abdomen. Although effective within their respective domains, existing models such as the cardiac oriented FHUSP-NET do not readily generalize to abdominal ultrasound, where anatomical structures exhibit far greater heterogeneity, more complex spatial relationships, and markedly broader scale variation. These factors make both feature representation and plane identification substantially more difficult. In addition, current approaches may lack the robustness needed to cope with the considerable variability inherent in abdominal imaging. These limitations highlight the need for methods capable of jointly detecting key anatomical structures and classifying standard planes in complex fetal abdominal scenarios. In addition, strategies such as federated denoising or foundation-model pretraining rely on large-scale unlabeled datasets, cross-client prototype sharing, or high computational resources, which are often impractical in routine clinical settings. These limitations further underscore the need for a lightweight, task-specific solution capable of leveraging anatomical cues while remaining label-efficient and clinically deployable.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a multi-task learning model that can perform real-time detection of abdominal anatomical structures and rapid identification of standard planes, which is expected to promote the standardization and automation of fetal abdominal ultrasound examinations and ultimately support improvements in maternal and child health care.

Data acquisition and annotation

From May 2021 to February 2024, we collected 6905 FAUSP images from 3209 pregnant women at Fujian Medical University Second Affiliated Hospital, Quanzhou Children’s Hospital, and Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University. The average age of these women was 33 ± 2 years, and all were in their mid-pregnancy (18 to 24 + 6 weeks). These images originated from a variety of different equipment types, including Resona 8 (Mindray, China), Voluson E6 (GE Healthcare, USA), Voluson E8 (GE Healthcare, USA), Voluson E10 (GE Healthcare, USA), HI Vision Preiru (Hitachi, Japan), Aixplorer (Acoustics, France), Erlangshen (Hitachi, Japan), and Sequoia 512 (Siemens, Germany). After screening, 145 blurry images from 48 pregnant women and 83 images from 30 pregnant women with incomplete data were excluded. The remaining images were allocated into a training set (80%, totaling 5159 images) and a validation set (20%, totaling 1290 images). We meticulously selected 1 to 3 images from each pregnant woman, ensuring data quality and representativeness. Specifically, images were required to (i) meet the diagnostic standards of fetal abdominal ultrasound planes with clearly visible key anatomical structures, (ii) have good overall image quality without motion artifacts or excessive noise, and (iii) be representative of the individual case to ensure dataset diversity while avoiding redundancy. The specific data selection and allocation process is shown in Fig. 3. During the model training process, various data augmentation techniques including mirroring, rotation, scaling, image saturation, and contrast adjustment were employed to enhance the model’s robustness and performance across different settings. Specifically, rotation was applied within ± 10°, translation up to 10% of the image size, scaling factors between 0.5 and 1.5, brightness and contrast adjustments within ± 0.2, horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5, and saturation or hue shifts within a factor of ± 0.1. These augmentation strategies and parameter ranges are derived from the built-in Yolov8 framework, which incorporates widely adopted settings in medical image analysis. This approach aids in optimizing model performance, ensuring efficiency and accuracy in practical applications. The study received approval from the ethics committees of Fujian Medical University Second Affiliated Hospital, Quanzhou Children’s Hospital, and Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University ([2024] Fuzhou Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University Ethics Approval No. (395)). All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study, and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Furthermore, to validate the model’s generalization capability, we selected 228 FAUSP images from 75 pregnant women at Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University. These images were not used during the model training process and were reserved for external data validation.

All data annotations were jointly performed by two doctors with extensive clinical experience. One doctor used the open-source annotation tool LabelImg to precisely mark the key features within the FAUSP images37. After the initial annotations were completed, the second clinician reviewed the annotations made by the first clinician to ensure that all anatomical structures were accurately annotated. In cases of disagreement, the two clinicians engaged in thorough discussions to reach a consensus on the annotation results. This process not only ensured the quality and accuracy of the data but also effectively eliminated potential subjective biases, thereby ensuring the reliability of subsequent research and analysis.

The multi-task model

In this study, FAUSP-NET employs a unified MTL framework to detect 14 key anatomical structures and classify 7 FAUSP simultaneously, achieving high accuracy and real-time performance by eliminating redundant computations compared to single-task models, and the network structure of FAUSP-NET is shown in Fig. 4. In the object detection task, the model provides crucial support for screening fetal abdominal diseases by detecting key anatomical features in FAUSP images in real-time. In the classification task, the model effectively identifies various standard planes based on the results from the anatomical structure detection module, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Fig. 4The structure of FAUSP-NET model.

The structure of FAUSP-NET model.

Inspired by Yolov8, the latest generation of object detection technology known for its advanced network architecture and processing efficiency, our model has been improved and optimized based on the Yolov8 framework38. The backbone network plays a crucial role in extracting shared shallow features, and we have made several enhancements to the original backbone network of Yolov8. In particular, residual structures were incorporated to facilitate deeper feature learning while maintaining effective gradient propagation, thereby reducing the risk of vanishing gradients in deep networks.

In the architecture, the input image sizes of 720 × 370, 768 × 576, and 1260 × 910 are adaptively resized to a uniform dimension of 640 × 640 pixels following the minimum padding rule, preserving the true pixel size of the original images through methods such as maintaining aspect ratio, edge padding, and cropping. This approach effectively reduces data redundancy. Moreover, during the image preprocessing stage, we utilize the Mosaic data augmentation technique, which not only decreases the dependency on batch size but also enhances the generalization capability of the dataset and alleviates the burden on hardware resources to some extent. Additionally, to increase training efficiency and accelerate model convergence, our network is equipped with batch normalization (BN) layers after each convolutional layer, followed by Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions which optimize the overall performance of the network.

The object detection module of our study integrates the design principles of the PANet network by employing a hybrid strategy of the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and Path Aggregation Network (PAN) to establish an efficient multi-scale feature integration architecture. The bottom-up integration strategy of the FPN facilitates the capture of high-level semantic information, significantly improving the model’s ability to recognize smaller or less distinct anatomical markers. Concurrently, the top-down pathway of the PAN enhances the spatial localization capabilities of lower layers, ensuring accurate transmission of spatial information in the higher-level feature maps. We generate deep fusion feature maps at three different scales (large, medium, and small), integrating both shallow spatial features and deep semantic features. By optimizing the sizes of anchor boxes through adaptive methods, we can precisely delineate the bounding boxes of anatomical structures, thereby enhancing the accuracy of fetal abdominal structure detection. Subsequently, 1 × 1 convolutions are employed to expand these feature maps, resulting in three specific detection feature layer outputs. Finally, the Soft Non-Maximum Suppression (Soft-NMS) algorithm is utilized to adjust the confidence scores of overlapping predictions, improving the handling of overlaps in multi-object detection and minimizing the loss of useful information39. This approach accurately locates and identifies key anatomical structures, with the final output including confidence scores and predicted categories for 14 FAUSP anatomical structures.

In our study, we use the detection results of key structures in fetal abdominal ultrasound images to identify standard planes. According to clinical standards, we define the standard fetal abdominal ultrasound planes as follows: ACS should display the umbilical vein (UV), inferior vena cava (IVC), stomach bubble (ST), and abdominal aorta (AO); UCS should clearly show the SP and umbilical CORD; BHS should exhibit the bladder (BL) and UAS; BKHS should display the kidney (K) and SP; LKS should show the left kidney (LK), ST, diaphragm (DI), and SP; RKS should present the right kidney (RK), DI, and SP; BKCS should include the K, umbilical artery (IB), AO, and RIB. The specific identification process is as follows: First, we define the target category dictionary P for each standard plane as: ACS: [UV, IVC, ST, AO], UCS: [SP, CORD], BHS: [BL, UAS], BKHS: [K, SP], LKS: [LK, ST, DI, SP], RKS: [RK, DI, SP], BKCS: [K, IB, AO, RIB]. Next, based on the detection results which include confidence score, class, and bounding box coordinates (x1, y1, x2, y2), we select the class with the highest confidence as the target marker. Finally, by verifying whether these predicted targets completely match the predefined target list for the standard plane, we confirm whether the ultrasound image corresponds to the standard plane and output the number of detected objects and the category of the FAUSP. The design of FAUSP-NET effectively overcomes the challenges of time-consuming and error-prone identification processes that might lead to non-standard selections. It does not rely on extensive pre-training with large datasets but performs inference directly from detection results. This approach not only significantly reduces training time and memory requirements but also accelerates the implementation of FAUSP-NET, aligning the inference process closely with clinical judgment workflows. This makes FAUSP-NET more suitable for clinical applications and provides better interpretability and intuitiveness.

Hybrid attention mechanism in multi-task model networks

Compared with single-type attention, hybrid attention can leverage the strengths of multiple mechanisms, enabling more comprehensive feature representation and improved model robustness40. Ultrasound images typically suffer from low contrast and noise contamination, which significantly affects the visibility of internal structural features of tissues. To more effectively focus on key features within the FAUSP, we have incorporated an Efficient Channel Attention (ECA) module into the foundational part of our model41. The ECA module, through its lightweight and parameter-free design, optimizes the processing of FAUSP images by focusing on channel-level features, thereby enhancing the precision in recognizing critical anatomical structures. This module adaptively extracts key features from FAUSP images, aiding in improving the model’s generalization capabilities and reducing overfitting, thus significantly enhancing performance. The working principle of the ECA module is detailed in Fig. 5, where GAP refers to Global Average Pooling, K denotes the kernel size used in the 1D convolution for channel interaction, and σ represents the sigmoid activation function. Based on the ECA module, we have designed two different convolutional structures: EConv1 and EConv2. EConv1 includes a residual connection, which helps maintain information flow and prevents gradient vanishing issues in deep networks. The residual connection allows primary features to be directly transmitted to subsequent layers, which enhances the efficiency of feature transfer and improves the network’s ability to learn critical features of the fetal abdomen. This enables the model to more effectively recognize and parse complex anatomical structures. EConv2, by simplifying the connection structure, effectively increases computational efficiency, achieving faster and more efficient feature fusion and processing.

We have integrated an Efficient Multi-Scale Attention (EMA) mechanism into the bottleneck section of our model43. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the EMA attention mechanism first divides the input FAUSP images into several groups, with each group containing data of size C//G×H×W, where C represents the number of channels, H is the height, W is the width, and G is the number of groups. Each group undergoes average pooling in both the X and Y directions, resulting in feature maps of size C//G×H×1 and C//G×1×W, respectively. Simultaneously, a 3 × 3 convolution operation is applied to each group, producing feature maps of the same size. This step extracts basic features from the FAUSP images. The pooled feature maps are then concatenated and processed through a 1 × 1 convolution, generating feature maps of size C//G×1 × 1. These feature maps are activated using a Sigmoid function, resulting in new weighted feature maps. Subsequently, these feature maps undergo Group Normalization and are then average pooled in both height and width directions, producing feature maps of size C//G×1 × 1. Next, they are activated using the Softmax function and combined through matrix multiplication to enhance the inter-feature connections and the model’s sensitivity to the FAUSP image content. These processed feature maps are further refined by another round of matrix multiplication and re-weighted using the Sigmoid function, which enhances the nonlinearity and discriminative ability of the features, significantly improving the overall recognition capability of FAUSP-NET. Finally, the model outputs the processed images. Through these steps of grouping, convolutional processing, and subsequent feature recombination, the model effectively extracts and weights key information within the FAUSP images, further enhancing the accuracy of detecting FAUSP anatomical structures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 30.0.0. A paired t-test was employed to evaluate performance differences between models on paired sample datasets. By comparing the detection results from various models, this test helps to determine whether changes in the models have led to significant performance variations under consistent experimental conditions. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted to assess statistical significance. If the p-value falls below this threshold, it indicates a meaningful difference in performance between the models, suggesting substantial effects from the modifications. This methodology offers a rigorous approach to assess the impact of model improvements in scientific research.

Experiments and results

Experimental environment

The experiment was conducted on a computer running Windows 10, equipped with an 11th generation Intel (R) Core (TM) i5-12400 F CPU, 16 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GTX-3060 GPU with 16 GB of GPU memory. The programming environment used was Python 3.7, and the deep learning framework was Pytorch 1.8. Additionally, the model’s learning rate was initialized at 0.01, with the SGD optimizer also starting at a rate of 0.01, an initial batch size of 16, and the total number of epochs was set to 200.

To ensure the fairness of the experiment, uniform parameter configurations were adopted during the training and evaluation of each comparative model, utilizing the same training and validation datasets. Specifically, consistent learning rates, batch sizes, optimizer types, and other key parameters were set. Moreover, to ensure the reliability of the results, the hardware and software environments were kept consistent throughout the experiment.

Evaluation metrics

To ensure the accuracy of the model’s detection capabilities, we employed several commonly used evaluation metrics. In the detection module, evaluations were conducted by calculating the Weighted Foreground Intersection over Union (IoU) between predicted and manually annotated locations. A detection is considered correct only if the IoU value exceeds 0.5. Additionally, we utilized precision, recall, F1-score, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95. Precision indicates the proportion of correct results identified by the model, while recall represents the proportion of correct results detected by the model relative to all correct instances. Average Precision (AP) is used to evaluate the detection results for each anatomical structure; a higher AP value indicates better model performance. The mean Average Precision (mAP) is used to evaluate the overall performance of the detection network, and its value ranges from 0 to 1, with closer to 1 indicating better performance. To validate the effectiveness of the designed components including the Focal_EIoU loss function, LSKblock module, EMA module, and EConv module, we conducted a series of ablation experiments. These results thoroughly validate the effectiveness of the proposed modules and loss function, significantly optimizing the overall performance of the model.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a multi-task deep learning model aimed at assisting ultrasound physicians in automatically detecting and locating key anatomical structures in FAUSP images and identifying FAUSP based on detection results. To enhance the accuracy of detection and classification, we adopted a residual structure in the backbone network of the model, enabling the capture of more complex and detailed features, significantly improving the analysis of complex medical images while maintaining effective gradient flow in deep networks for faster convergence and higher computational efficiency. Furthermore, we introduced an attention mechanism, designed new convolutional layers, and feature fusion modules. By enhancing the extraction, reorganization, and augmented fusion of key anatomical features, we effectively utilized the channel attention mechanism to improve the recognition of crucial features, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness when detecting fetal abdominal structures of varying sizes. We also employed the LSKblock focusing on specific areas within images, significantly increasing sensitivity to local features. Finally, we adopted a new Focal_EIoU loss as the loss function, specifically addressing issues of class imbalance and bounding box regression precision, effectively enhancing model performance and accuracy. The experimental results presented in Tables 2 and 3 demonstrate that FAUSP-NET achieves outstanding performance in detecting key anatomical structures across 14 FAUSP types and in identifying 7 standard abdominal planes. Although FHUSP-NET achieves strong performance in fetal cardiac standard plane recognition and structural detection, its underlying assumptions and feature representations do not generalize well to FAUSP images. The fetal abdomen displays far greater structural heterogeneity, overlapping organ boundaries, and substantial scale variation, making detection and localization considerably more challenging. Consequently, FHUSP-NET does not reach a comparable level of performance when applied to abdominal imaging, reinforcing the need for a model specifically tailored to the characteristics of fetal abdominal ultrasound.

To further validate the accuracy and generalizability of FAUSP-NET, we conducted external testing using untrained data from Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University. By comparing the model’s performance with that of junior, intermediate, and senior physicians, FAUSP-NET demonstrated excellent capability in both anatomical-structure detection and FAUSP identification, with performance comparable to that of senior physicians, as shown in Table 5. In addition, FAUSP-NET completes FAUSP detection and identification in only 1.0% of the time required by senior doctors, substantially improving the efficiency of fetal abdominal ultrasound analysis and helping clinicians assess fetal growth more effectively. Given its lightweight design and high computational efficiency, FAUSP-NET can be feasibly integrated into routine clinical workflows. The model runs in real time on standard GPU-equipped hospital workstations and provides immediate feedback during ultrasound examinations without disrupting standard workflows. Nevertheless, its clinical applicability may be affected by variations in ultrasound devices and operator experience, and deployment may remain challenging in hospitals with limited computational resources.

Despite the significant achievements of our proposed single-stage multi-task model in detecting anatomical structures and classifying standard planes in FAUSP images, there are still some limitations. Firstly, our research primarily focused on FAUSP images, excluding other types of ultrasound images. In the future, we plan to expand our research scope by collecting a broader range of ultrasound images and videos to construct a more comprehensive dataset for standard plane detection, thereby further validating the universality and generalization ability of our model. Secondly, structures such as the IVC, UAS, and DI proved more difficult to detect due to their small size and indistinct boundaries, occasionally leading to misrecognition and incorrect identification of the corresponding standard planes. Future improvements will focus on collecting more high-quality samples, employing fine-grained multi-expert annotations, and incorporating medical prior knowledge to improve detection sensitivity and boundary localization. Finally, although multiple lightweight measures have been adopted, the integrated model’s parameters for detection, classification, and feature extraction networks remain relatively high. Moving forward, we are committed to further optimizing the model parameters to enhance its accuracy and adaptability, better suited for complex and variable application environments. Variability across different ultrasound machines and operator expertise may also affect robustness in practice. While preprocessing and augmentation strategies helped mitigate these effects, further multi-center validation and the adoption of domain adaptation techniques will be necessary to ensure consistent performance across diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose an end-to-end multi-task model, FAUSP-NET, incorporating a Mixed Attention Mechanism for identifying FAUSPs and detecting their key anatomical structures. Through extensive experimental validation, our approach demonstrated strong performance in recognizing seven different FAUSPs and detecting 14 corresponding key anatomical structures. Specifically, when compared to other target detection and classification models, FAUSP-NET achieved a precision of 0.956, recall of 0.931, mAP@0.5 of 0.961, and mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.653 in object detection. In identifying FAUSP images, it attained a precision of 0.967, recall of 0.980, specificity of 0.995, and accuracy of 0.977. The model completed detection and recognition tasks within 24.1 milliseconds per image on our test system, suggesting it could help physicians rapidly locate key anatomical structures. Additionally, external validation indicated promising generalization capabilities, with performance in some cases approaching that of experienced clinicians and greatly surpassing manual screening speeds. Despite these positive findings, the study has certain limitations. First, the data were collected from a limited number of centers, which may affect the model’s generalizability across diverse clinical settings. Second, real-time deployment may require additional optimization or hardware resources in different clinical environments. Moreover, while our external validation demonstrated encouraging results, further prospective or multi-institutional studies will be necessary to rigorously confirm the robustness and broad applicability of FAUSP-NET.

In conclusion, FAUSP-NET has the potential to assist less-experienced doctors in accurately identifying key fetal abdominal structures and to reduce the workload for clinical ultrasound technicians. Looking ahead, our future work will focus on refining the engineering implementation of the proposed model and exploring its application to other medical image detection tasks.
