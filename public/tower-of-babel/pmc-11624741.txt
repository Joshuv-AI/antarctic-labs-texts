Comparative adherence and persistence of single-inhaler and multiple-inhaler triple therapies among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in Japan: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sandra Jokšaitė, Robert Wood, Afisi Ismaila, Lucinda Camidge, Akiko Mizukami, Alexandrosz Czira, Olivia Massey, Masao Yarita, Chris Compton, Rad Siddiqui, Thomas Jennison, Takeo Ishii, Kenichi Hashimoto, Kieran J Rothnie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate and compare medication adherence and persistence for patients newly initiating single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) and multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT) for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in Japan.DesignRetrospective, new-user, active comparator, observational cohort study using inverse probability of treatment weighting.SettingHealth insurance claims data from the Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd, hospital claims database.ParticipantsAdults diagnosed with COPD at age ≥40 years newly initiating MITT or SITT (fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol (FF/UMEC/VI) or formoterol fumarate/budesonide/glycopyrronium) from 1 September 2019 to 31 July 2021.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was medication adherence compared between patients using SITT and MITT, assessed by the proportion of days covered ≥80%. Secondary outcomes included medication persistence (time from index treatment initiation to discontinuation) compared between patients using SITT and MITT and medication adherence compared before and after the switch in a subgroup of patients switching from MITT to SITT.ResultsWe included 2575 MITT and 2962 SITT users with similar baseline characteristics following weighting. The proportion of adherent patients was significantly greater for SITT versus MITT users at 6 months (19.7% vs 10.2%, p<0.0001), 12 months (6.0% vs 3.8%, p=0.0009) and 18 months (3.8% vs 1.4%, p<0.0001) post-index. Median persistence was also significantly higher for SITT versus MITT users (2.0 vs 1.0 months, p<0.001). Comparing specific SITT versus MITT, the proportion of adherent patients at each time point and median persistence was greater for FF/UMEC/VI. In patients switching from MITT to SITT (n=688), the proportion of adherent patients increased postswitch at the class level and for FF/UMEC/VI specifically.ConclusionsPatients with COPD in Japan who were newly initiating SITT had greater medication adherence and persistence compared with those on MITT up to 18 months following initiation.Keywords: Drug Combinations; Medication Adherence; Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Respiratory Therapy; Medication Persistence

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory disorder and a leading cause of death worldwide.1 2 Studies conducted in Japan between 2004 and 2021 have reported an estimated COPD prevalence of 10–11% in individuals aged ≥40 years and up to 30–40% in smokers with cardiovascular disease or patients living in areas with high air pollution.2,6

The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report and Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS) guidelines recommend a stepwise approach to therapy.7,9 GOLD recommend triple therapy with an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), a long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) and a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) for patients with COPD who continue to experience exacerbations or remain symptomatic despite dual therapy.9 Notably, the latest JRS guidelines only recommend the incorporation of ICS for patients with COPD with concomitant asthmatic features and/or with frequent exacerbations as well as blood eosinophilia which may result in the total number of patients with COPD escalated to triple therapy in Japan being lower than in studies in countries whose guidance is in alignment with the GOLD 2023 report.7

Triple therapy for COPD traditionally required the use of multiple inhalers (multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT)), often several times a day.10 Real-world evidence from the USA suggests poor adherence to MITT with a mean proportion of days covered (PDC) of only 37% at 12 months in patients with COPD who are aged ≥40 years.11 Likewise, in a cross-sectional study of Japanese patients with asthma and/or COPD, 22% of patients had poor adherence to inhaled therapy.2 12 Adherence and persistence to maintenance therapy are critical for achieving optimal clinical outcomes;13 14 adherence to COPD therapy has been shown to be associated with improved outcomes for patients including lower rates of exacerbations, fewer hospital admissions, lower healthcare resource utilisation and costs, better health-related quality of life and lower risk of mortality.13,17

Single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) is a more recent development for COPD aiming to reduce treatment complexity and improve persistence and adherence.10 In Japan, two SITT devices are approved by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare for long-term maintenance treatment of patients with COPD. These are fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol (FF/UMEC/VI), approved in March 2019 and launched in May 201918 and formoterol fumarate/budesonide/glycopyrronium bromide (FOR/BUD/GLY), approved in June 2019 and launched in September 2019.19

Real-world studies on adherence to and persistence with treatment regimens that use multiple inhalers among patients with COPD in Japan are limited. Although previous studies conducted in the USA and England have assessed comparative adherence and persistence to SITT versus MITT among patients with COPD,20 21 the profile of Japanese patients with COPD has been shown to differ compared with that of patients in the USA and Europe.22 Given the relatively recent introduction of SITT in Japan, there is a need for real-world evidence to evaluate whether improved adherence to triple therapy among Japanese patients may be achieved by reducing the number of inhalers required. The aim of the current study was to evaluate and compare medication adherence and persistence for patients newly initiating SITT and MITT and for those switching from MITT to SITT for COPD in Japan.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, new-user, active comparator, observational cohort study, employing inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and using health insurance claims data from the Medical Data Vision Co., Ltd (MDV) acute hospital claims database (Tokyo, Japan). The MDV database covers approximately 26% of all acute care hospitals nationally (over 480 facilities) representing approximately 44 million patients making it one of the largest Japanese healthcare data sets.23 The database is composed of information from only contracted Diagnosis Procedures Combination (DPC) hospitals, thus information at other medical sites is unknown. DPC hospitals in Japan provide a range of medical care including acute inpatient care.24 Data are available on administrative claims as well as inpatient and outpatient encounters including all treatment information that was ordered by the doctor in the hospital.

Details of the study design can be found in online supplemental figure S1. The study period was from 1 September 2018 to 31 January 2022. The index date was defined as the first/earliest date (prescription) of triple therapy initiation within the indexing period of 1 September 2019 to 31 July 2021 aligning with the launch date of SITT FOR/BUD/GLY. The baseline period was defined as the 12 months prior to the index date and the minimum follow-up period (including the index date) was 6 months.

Triple therapy was defined as either MITT or SITT use where MITT use was the concomitant use (an overlap in the days’ supply of all components of ≥1 day) of two different inhalers that form a triple therapy (namely ICS/LABA+LAMA or LABA/LAMA+ICS) or three different inhalers in the form of ICS+LAMA+LABA.

Adherence and persistence up to 18 months were compared between the mutually exclusive triple therapy cohorts based on their index triple therapy type: SITT (overall) versus MITT, FF/UMEC/VI versus MITT and FOR/BUD/GLY versus MITT (online supplemental figure S1A). For all patients who initiated SITT within the indexing period, a subpopulation of patients switching from MITT to SITT was identified (online supplemental figure S1B).

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist was used for reporting this study.

Study population

Patients with COPD who were recorded in the MDV database as newly initiating triple therapy were included in the study.

Eligible patients were required to have the following: ≥1 prescription of triple therapy within the indexing period; ≥1 inpatient diagnosis and/or ≥2 outpatient diagnoses of COPD (International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) of J42, J43 or J44) at age ≥40 years any time up to and including the index month; ≥12 months of preindex data; ≥1 diagnosis or medical claim record in the 12 calendar months prior to (but excluding) the index month in addition to ≥1 diagnosis or medical claim record in the 13–18 calendar months prior to the index month; and ≥6 months of data availability following but not including the index calendar month up to the earlier of the last/most recent diagnosis or medical claim record or the end of the study period.

Patients were excluded if they had the following: ≥1 diagnostic code (ICD-10) of any medical conditions incompatible with a COPD diagnosis including conditions related to lung or bronchial developmental anomalies, degenerative processes (cystic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis), pulmonary resection or other significant respiratory disorders (other than COPD) that may have made ascertainment of a COPD diagnosis using electronic medical record data difficult or may substantially change the natural history of the disease and/or ≥1 pharmacy claim for triple therapy within the 12 months prior to the index date.

The specific criteria for each comparison group are shown in online supplemental table S1.

Study outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to compare medication adherence (categorical) at 6, 12 and 18 months following the index date between patients using SITT (overall, FF/UMEC/VI or FOR/BUD/GLY) and patients using MITT.

The secondary objectives included a comparison of medication persistence between patients using SITT (overall, FF/UMEC/VI or FOR/BUD/GLY) and patients using MITT and to compare medication adherence (categorical) in the 6 months prior to and post-treatment switch among patients in the MITT to SITT switch cohort.

The exploratory objectives were to compare medication adherence (continuous) between SITT cohorts and patients using MITT and prior to and post-treatment switch among the MITT to SITT switch cohort.

Data analysis

For the IPTW analysis, weights were derived from propensity scores to minimise potential confounding effects and to estimate treatment effects. Differences in baseline characteristics between MITT and SITT cohorts were assessed using standardised mean differences (SMDs) with an SMD of <10% in the weighted cohort being considered indicative of adequate balance. The propensity score was generated via probability estimates from a logistic regression model in which the type of triple therapy was the binary dependent variable and patient characteristics were used as predictors (online supplemental table S2). The same covariates were included in all propensity score models and IPTW analyses; all covariates considered for inclusion are listed in online supplemental table S3. The selection of covariates for inclusion in the propensity score model was primarily based on background knowledge of the association of pretreatment variables on the outcomes of interest (ie, adherence and persistence). Where the causal influence of a pretreatment variable was not fully known, the strength of the association between the variable and the outcomes was explored via univariable regression (online supplemental table S4); variables with a p value <0.1 were selected for inclusion in the model.

Adherence was measured using PDC which was calculated for each patient by dividing the total number of days ‘covered’ for triple therapy by a fixed time interval (ie, 6, 12 or 18 months) (online supplemental figure S2). Patients were considered ‘covered’ for days in which they had a valid pharmacy claim for all three components of triple therapy (either SITT or MITT) based on the date of claim and projected inhaler duration when taking into account the prescribed dose and the pack size. Patients were categorised as adherent if they had a PDC of ≥80%. The proportion of adherent patients at 6 and 12 months following the index date (and at 18 months for a subgroup analysis) was compared between unweighted and weighted treatment cohorts and OR, 95% CIs and p values were generated from a logistic regression model with p values <0.05 considered significant. To provide additional insights into medication adherence, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using adherence defined as a PDC of ≥50%. Patients switching to the comparator arm in the follow-up period were omitted from the adherence estimates for all non-switch objectives. For the MITT to SITT switch analysis, the proportion of adherent patients was assessed for the 6 months prior to and following a switch from MITT to SITT for all patients and for 12 and 18 months. Patients returning to MITT or switching to the competitor SITT in the follow-up period were not included in the adherence estimates.

For continuous PDC assessment, the mean PDC at 6, 12 and 18 months following the index date was compared between unweighted and weighted cohorts and 95% CIs and p values were generated from a generalised linear model specifying a Gaussian distribution and an identity link. For patients in the MITT to SITT switch cohort, mean adherence was compared in the 6 months prior to the treatment switch to SITT and in the 6 months after the treatment switch to SITT. The most recent inhaled maintenance therapy strategy in the 3 and 12 months prior to the index date was summarised for each of the comparator groups by cross-tabulations with the number and proportion reported for each.

Persistence was defined as the time from treatment initiation at the index date to treatment discontinuation where discontinuation was defined as a gap of ≥14 days between the end of the SITT prescription and the next fill or between periods of MITT use. The date of discontinuation was the last days’ supply of the last SITT claim or the last day of overlap of all three MITT components prior to the 14-day gap. The discontinuation period of ≥14 days was chosen in order to take into consideration the ‘Ryotan’ period during which time FOR/BUD/GLY was only available as a 14-day prescription. This is because during the Ryotan period in Japan, newly approved products were available only on short prescriptions. Persistence was assessed with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier persistence rates were evaluated for all treatment cohorts at monthly intervals up to 3 months following the index date and at 3-monthly intervals thereafter up to 18 months and compared between cohorts using Cox proportional hazards models. Patients were censored if they switched to the comparator arm in the persistence analyses. Due to the real-world nature of this study, sensitivity analyses were conducted using different permissible gaps (≥30, ≥45 and ≥60 days) to define triple therapy non-persistence.

A sensitivity analysis was carried out using the adherence outcomes from the primary objectives and persistence outcomes (using the 14-day permissible gap) from the secondary objectives to assess the impact of the Ryotan period. The index period start date for this study (1 September 2019) coincided with FOR/BUD/GLY approval; thus, for the first year post-approval, only 14-day prescriptions were available for FOR/BUD/GLY compared with 14 and 30 days for FF/UMEC/VI. The sensitivity analysis was carried out for patients with an index date on/after 1 October 2020 (after the Ryotan period for FOR/BUD/GLY) and for patients with an index date before 1 October 2020 (during the Ryotan period for FOR/BUD/GLY).

All analyses were conducted using Stata 17 software, V.17.0 or the latest available version (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Patient attrition

A total of 5537 patients were included in the comparative analysis (online supplemental figure S3A). Of these, 2575 patients (46.5%) initiated MITT and 2962 patients (53.5%) initiated SITT (FF/UMEC/VI (n=2397/2962, 80.9%) and FOR/BUD/GLY (n=565/2962, 19.1%)). Of the patients initiating FOR/BUD/GLY, 144 (25.5%) and 421 (74.5%) started therapy before and after the end of the Ryotan period, respectively.

A total of 748 patients were identified for the MITT to SITT switching analysis of whom 688 (92.0%) were included (online supplemental figure S3B). Of these, 630 patients switched to FF/UMEC/VI (of whom 564 (90.0%) were included in the analysis) and 118 patients switched to FOR/BUD/GLY (of whom 98 (83.1%) were included in the analysis). The mean (SD) time to switch from the last day of MITT supply was 57.1 (46.4) days. The mean time to switch from MITT was similar for the FF/UMEC/VI and the FOR/BUD/GLY cohorts.

Patient characteristics at baseline

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Mean (SD) age and proportion of men, respectively, were 71.8 (11.6) years and 66.4% in the MITT cohort and 74.3 (9.3) years and 77.9% in the SITT cohort (table 1). In total, 44.3% of patients in the MITT cohort and 48.5% of patients in the SITT (overall) cohort had a history of smoking in the 12 months prior to the index date. For most baseline characteristics, values and/or proportions were similar between FF/UMEC/VI and FOR/BUD/GLY cohorts. For the MITT-to-SITT switching analysis, the mean (SD) age was 72.9 (9.7) years and 75.5% were men. A history of smoking in the 12 months prior to the index was recorded in 48.3% of patients (table 1).

Comorbidities

Similar proportions of patients indexed on SITT and MITT had non-respiratory comorbidities (table 1). In contrast, a higher proportion of MITT users had confirmed asthma during baseline compared with SITT users (89.8% vs 69.8%). The proportion of patients with each comorbidity was similar between the FF/UMEC/VI and FOR/BUD/GLY SITT cohorts in the comparative and MITT to SITT switching analyses.

Treatment history

A mean (SD) of 1.9 (1.5) and 1.4 (1.3) medication classes/treatment strategies were received within the 12-month baseline period for the MITT and SITT (overall) cohorts, respectively. The most common medication classes during baseline for the MITT cohort were ICS/LABA (36.8%), oral corticosteroids (OCS; 33.2%) and leukotriene receptor antagonist (31.5%); the most common medication classes during baseline for the SITT (overall) cohort were LABA/LAMA (27.5%), OCS (23.2%) and ICS/LABA (23.1%) (table 1). Similar proportions for each medication class were recorded in the FF/UMEC/VI and FOR/BUD/GLY cohorts.

SITT (overall) versus MITT

For the adherence and persistence analyses, a sufficient balance of weighted data (SMD <10%) was achieved for SITT versus MITT at each time point. The values reported are weighted estimates.

The proportion of patients adherent to therapy (PDC ≥80%) was greater for SITT versus MITT initiators at all time points (6 months: 19.7% vs 10.2%; 12 months: 6.0% vs 3.8%; 18 months: 3.8% vs 1.4%) (figure 1a). The odds of being adherent to therapy were significantly higher among patients indexed on SITT versus MITT at 6, 12 and 18 months postindex (figure 1b). Mean PDC was also significantly higher for patients initiating SITT versus patients initiating MITT (6 months: 52.2% vs 39.7%, p<0.0001; 12 months: 42.1% vs 31.1%, p<0.0001; 18 months: 38.4% vs 27.8%, p<0.0001). When defining adherence as a PDC of ≥50%, patients initiating SITT were significantly more likely to be adherent to therapy at 6, 12 and 18 months postindex versus MITT initiators (online supplemental table S5).

Median persistence (14-day permissible gap) was significantly higher among SITT users compared with MITT users (2.0 months vs 1.0 months) (figure 1c) and remained higher when a permissible treatment gap of 30, 45 or 60 days was used (online supplemental table S6).

FF/UMEC/VI versus MITT

For the adherence and persistence analyses, a sufficient balance of weighted data (SMD <10%) was achieved for FF/UMEC/VI versus MITT at each time point. The values reported are weighted estimates.

The proportion of patients adherent to therapy (PDC ≥80%) was greater for FF/UMEC/VI initiators versus MITT initiators at all time points (6 months: 21.5% vs 10.3%; 12 months: 6.6% vs 3.8%; 18 months: 4.1% vs 1.4%) (figure 2a). The odds of being adherent to therapy were significantly higher among patients indexed on FF/UMEC/VI versus MITT at 6, 12 and 18 months postindex (figure 2b). Mean PDC was also significantly higher for patients initiating FF/UMEC/VI versus patients initiating MITT (6 months: 54.3% vs 39.8%; 12 months: 43.5% vs 31.1%; 18 months: 39.5% vs 27.9%). When defining adherence as a PDC of ≥50%, patients initiating FF/UMEC/VI were significantly more likely to be adherent to therapy at 6, 12 and 18 months postindex versus MITT initiators (online supplemental table S5).

Adherence to and persistence with FF/UMEC/VI at 6 and 12 months were similar during and after the Ryotan period (online supplemental figure S4). The odds of being adherent to FF/UMEC/VI versus MITT at 6 months following the index increased slightly post-Ryotan versus peri-Ryotan (online supplemental figure S4B). Median persistence (14-day permissible gap) remained significantly higher among FF/UMEC/VI users versus MITT users during the peri-Ryotan and post-Ryotan periods with median time on treatment similar during both periods (online supplemental figure S4C).

FOR/BUD/GLY versus MITT

For the adherence analyses, two covariates at 6 months, four covariates at 12 months and six covariates at 18 months had an absolute SMD of >10% following weighting (online supplemental tables S7–S9). For the persistence analyses, three covariates had an SMD of >10% (online supplemental table S10). The values reported are weighted estimates.

At 6 months postindex, the proportion of patients adherent to therapy (PDC ≥80%) was similar between MITT initiators and FOR/BUD/GLY initiators (figure 3a); however, at 12 months postindex the proportion of patients adherent to MITT was greater than the proportion of patients adherent to FOR/BUD/GLY. Adherence at 18 months is not reported due to low sample size. The odds of being adherent to therapy were not significantly different between patients indexed on FOR/BUD/GLY versus MITT at 6 and 12 months postindex (figure 3b). Mean PDC was slightly higher for FOR/BUD/GLY initiators versus MITT initiators at 12 months postindex (33.9% vs 31.1%); however, at 6 months postindex, mean PDC was higher for MITT initiators versus FOR/BUD/GLY initiators (41.7% vs 39.6%). When defining adherence as a PDC of ≥50%, patients initiating FOR/BUD/GLY were significantly more likely to be adherent to therapy at 12 months postindex versus MITT initiators (p=0.0006) and slightly more likely to be adherent to therapy at 6 months postindex versus MITT initiators, although this did not reach statistical significance (online supplemental table S5).

Treatment strategies

In the 12 months prior to the index, 30.0% of MITT initiators received no maintenance therapy and 37.6% received ICS/LABA fixed or open prior to initiating MITT, the most common being FF/VI (37.0%) and BUD/FOR (30.4%) (online supplemental table S11).

In the 12 months prior to the index, 37.5% of SITT initiators received no maintenance therapy and 28.2% received LABA/LAMA prior to initiating SITT, the most common being tiotropium (42.1%) or UMEC/VI (30.7%). Medication classes received in the 12 months prior to the index were similar between the FF/UMEC/VI and FOR/BUD/GLY cohorts (online supplemental table S11).

Discussion

In this Japanese real-world study, patients with COPD who were newly initiating SITT had significantly better adherence to and persistence with therapy compared with patients newly initiating MITT. The proportion of patients who were adherent to therapy (PDC ≥80%) was consistently higher at 6, 12 and 18 months postindex among SITT initiators versus MITT initiators. Patients also had significantly higher odds of being adherent to SITT compared with MITT at each time point. These findings were predominantly driven by the FF/UMEC/VI cohort for which statistical significance was achieved at each time point and results were consistent in the sensitivity analyses (when adherence was defined as PDC ≥50%).

Improved adherence due to simplification of medication regimen has previously been reported for patients with various long-term medical conditions.25 The current findings also align with previous similar studies among patients with COPD. A recent real-world US study reported that patients initiating FF/UMEC/VI were more than two times as likely to be adherent to therapy (PDC ≥80%) compared with those initiating MITT.20 Similarly, a real-world study in England reported that patients newly initiating SITT (FF/UMEC/VI or beclomethasone/formoterol/GLY) were significantly more likely to be adherent to therapy at 6, 12 and 18 months postinitiation versus patients newly initiating MITT.21 However, due to the indexing period of the US-based and UK-based studies occurring before the approval of FOR/BUD/GLY in these markets, data for this SITT were not included in previous studies but were incorporated into the current study.

In this study, limited differences in adherence were observed when comparing patients initiating FOR/BUD/GLY with those initiating MITT; however, interpretation is limited due to the small sample size in the FOR/BUD/GLY cohort. In the sensitivity analysis, the Ryotan period (when only short-length prescriptions were permitted for FOR/BUD/GLY) was shown to have an effect on adherence to and persistence with FOR/BUD/GLY. A higher proportion of patients indexed post-Ryotan were adherent to FOR/BUD/GLY versus MITT at 6 months; however, a higher proportion of patients were adherent to MITT versus FOR/BUD/GLY at 12 and 18 months. These findings support similar studies that indicate that prescription lengths are a common burden in COPD medication adherence with short-length prescriptions impacting the patient’s ability to self-manage their condition.26

In the switching analysis, adherence was increased following a switch from MITT to SITT at all time points. This was largely driven by switching from MITT to FF/UMEC/VI given the low sample size for BUD/GLY/FOR and the lack of apparent difference in adherence when switching from MITT to BUD/GLY/FOR. Adherence to COPD therapy is crucial for achieving optimal clinical outcomes;13 14 however, adherence to MITT has previously been reported to be poor in a real-world setting.11 27 These data suggest that a switch from multiple inhalers to a single-inhaler therapy may be beneficial in terms of increased patient adherence to therapy and therefore improved clinical outcomes.

We also found that patients newly initiating SITT had significantly better persistence with therapy compared with patients newly initiating MITT, mainly driven by the cohort of patients initiating FF/UMEC/VI. When a 14-day gap was used to define non-persistence, the median time to treatment discontinuation was 2 months for SITT users versus 1 month for MITT users. Results were consistent during sensitivity analyses (30, 45 and 60-day gap to define non-persistence).

It should be noted that despite the higher adherence observed for patients receiving SITT versus MITT in the current study, the overall adherence is still relatively low; this represents a need for investigation into other measures which may help further improve adherence among patients with COPD. A current ongoing study in the UK is assessing how technology (add-on inhaler sensors linked to a mobile application) may affect time to treatment failure and clinical outcomes in exacerbating patients with COPD with prior poor adherence.28 Furthermore, persistence estimates for patients receiving SITT were substantially lower among Japanese patients than has been reported in studies conducted in the USA and UK.20 21 Further investigation is needed to better understand this. Our study suggested higher rates of comorbid asthma in the Japanese cohort with 89.8% of MITT initiators and 69.8% of SITT initiators having concomitant asthma compared with 28.5% of SITT users and 35.4% of MITT users in the UK.21 This observation is consistent with the JRS 2018 guidelines followed in Japan during the study period which limited the addition of ICS maintenance therapy to patients with COPD with concomitant asthmatic features and/or with frequent exacerbations as well as blood eosinophilia compared with the GOLD 2020 report.9 29 The relationship between adherence rates and asthma diagnosis requires further investigation.

The overall findings from this study suggest that the introduction of SITT (including FF/UMEC/VI) is an opportunity to improve currently poor medication adherence and persistence among patients receiving MITT who face barriers such as treatment complexity, dosing frequency, number and variety of medications and ease of inhaler use.30 This is particularly important for Japanese patients who present significant barriers to adherence compared with those in the UK and USA due to treatment-related and lifestyle-related factors.2 31

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study were identified that may impact the findings. Although adherence can be indirectly measured via prescription claims, the dispensing of a medication does not guarantee that the medication was taken by the patient as prescribed. Also, the definition of discontinuation has the potential for bias by prematurely removing extremely non-adherent patients from the analysis. However, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a 30, 45 and 60-day allowable gap between prescriptions. Although prescription data from both inpatient and outpatient settings were included in the analysis, the effect of hospital admission on medication adherence was beyond the scope of the study and therefore was not considered. If a patient is transferred to another hospital within the MDV database, data can no longer be tracked continuously due to non-linkable patient records. This may cause a reduction in follow-up time and thus impact the power of the study. Patient records prior to the baseline period and after follow-up were searched to combat loss of power by showing that the patient was not lost to follow-up. Although the IPTW was used to minimise potential confounding between treatment groups prior to the comparison of outcomes, some unmeasured confounding may still be present. The remaining imbalance in some covariates following weighting for the FOR/BUD/GLY versus MITT comparisons means that there may be some residual confounding on the effect estimates for these analyses. Due to the low sample size, comparisons to the FOR/BUD/GLY cohort had less power than the overall SITT versus MITT analyses. Lastly, unlike other countries, COVID-19 did not have a substantial impact on prescribing in Japan. Nevertheless, the potential impact of COVID-19 on the clinical profile of patients and patient beliefs on using inhalers containing ICS must be considered. Based on a feasibility assessment of the MDV database for describing new users of FF/UMEC/VI in Japan, the number of patients with COPD aged ≥40 years with COVID-19 disease codes between January and October 2020 was small. Feasibility counts for an ongoing GlaxoSmithKline study (217636) indicated that COVID-19 had a limited impact on prescribing habits. Despite the pandemic, the number of patients with a claim for SITT increased month on month for 9/15 months throughout 2020 and into the first quarter of 2021 with increases ranging from <1% to 18%. Therefore, it is concluded that the prescribing of triple therapy was largely unaffected by the pandemic.

Conclusion

Patients with COPD in Japan who were newly initiating inhaled triple therapy on a SITT regimen had greater medication adherence and persistence compared with those on an MITT regimen. The increased adherence and persistence for SITT was predominantly driven by patients receiving FF/UMEC/VI and seen for up to 18 months following treatment initiation. The results of this study are relevant to clinical decision-making as they demonstrate that the introduction of SITT (including FF/UMEC/VI) may improve poor medication adherence and persistence by reducing the complexity and burden of treatment.
