On gelation for the Smoluchowski equation
Nicolas Fournier

Abstract
. Motivated by the recent results of Andreis-Iyer-Magnanini [ 2 ] , we provide a short proof, revisiting the one of Escobedo-Mischler-Perthame [ 6 ] , that for a large class of coagulation kernels, any weak solution to the Smoluchowski equation looses mass in finite time. The class of kernels we consider is essentially the same as the one of [ 2 ] : homogeneous kernels of degree γ > 1 \gamma>1 not vanishing on the diagonal, or homogeneous kernels of degree γ = 1 \gamma=1 not vanishing on the diagonal with some additional logarithmic factor. We also show that when γ = 1 \gamma=1 , the power of the logarithmic factor ensuring gelation may depend on the shape of the kernel.

45K05, 45M99

1.
Introduction

The Smoluchowski coagulation equation
[
16
]
describes the time-evolution of the concentration
f_{t}(x)

of particles with mass
x\in(0,\infty)
at time
t\geq 0
, in an infinite particle system.
Assuming that two particles with masses
x
and
y
coalesce
at rate
K(x,y)
to produce a single particle with mass
x+y
, it naturally writes

(1)

\partial_{t}f_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}K(y,x-y)f_{t}(y)f_{t}(x-y){\rm d}y-f_{t}(x)\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x,y)f_{t}(y){\rm d}y.

We refer to the review papers of Aldous
[
1
]
and Laurençot
[
11
]

and to the books of Banasiak-Lamb-Laurençot
[
3
,
4
]
, which contain a lot of information on this
equation and its discrete version.
We of course expect that (
1
) preserves mass,
i.e
that

\int_{0}^{\infty}xf_{t}(x){\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}xf_{0}(x){\rm d}x
for all
t\geq 0
.
However, this is not true if the kernel
K
increases sufficiently quickly for large
masses. Some mass may be lost in finite time, due to the appearance of clusters with infinite mass.
This phenomenon is called
gelation
.

Gelation is easily established for the multiplicative kernel
K(x,y)=xy
, and physicists conjectured
that gelation occurs for
\gamma
-homogeneous kernels
(that is,
K(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^{\gamma}K(x,y)
) with
\gamma>1
.
See Ziff
[
18
]
, Leyvraz-Tschudi
[
13
]
and Hendriks-Ernst-Ziff
[
8
]
.
Leyvraz
[
12
]
and then Buffet-Pulé
[
5
]
rigorously proved gelation when

f_{0}=\delta_{1}
and
K(x,y)=r(x){\bf 1}_{\{x=y\}}
is a
diagonal
kernel, with

r(x)\geq x\log^{2+\varepsilon}(e+x)
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Jeon
[
10
]
was the first to prove rigorously, through probabilistic arguments, the existence of a gelling
solution when
K(x,y)\geq(xy)^{\gamma/2}
with
\gamma>1
.
Escobedo-Mischler-Perthame
[
6
]
found a simpler deterministic argument, stronger
since they showed that
any
solution is gelling. They also
proved many estimates on the rate of decay of the mass and the profile of the solution.
Laurençot
[
11
, Proposition 36]
showed that any solution is gelling
for a class of kernels including
K(x,y)=\sqrt{xy}[\log(e+x)\log(e+y)]^{1+\varepsilon}
with
\varepsilon>0
.

Using a probabilistic approach in the spirit of
[
10
]
,
Andreis-Iyer-Magnanini
[
2
]
recently proved
that gelling solutions exist for a wider class of kernels. They actually deal with the slightly more general
model introduced by Norris
[
15
]
. Concerning (
1
),
they are able to show gelation for any
\gamma
-homogeneous kernel non vanishing near the diagonal with
\gamma>1
.
They can also deal with e.g.
K(x,y)=(x\land y)\log^{3+\varepsilon}(e+x\land y)

with
\varepsilon>0
and others, see Example
5
below and the two lines after. Such kernels were
(really) not covered by
[
10
,
6
,
11
]
.

Our goal is to revisit the proof of
[
6
]
to try include the kernels treated in
[
2
]
.
To this end, we slightly simplify and refine the arguments of
[
6
]
and show,
with a very short proof, that
any
solution looses mass in finite time,
for a class of kernels including
K(x,y)=(x\land y)\log^{2+\varepsilon}(e+x\land y)
.

We also show that the optimality of the power
2
of the logarithmic term is debatable:
we find two
1
-homogeneous kernels
K_{0}(x,y)=(x\land y)(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}-\frac{1}{2})_{+}

and
K_{1}(x,y)=x+y

such that when
K(x,y)=K_{0}(x,y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
, weak solutions are gelling if and only if

\alpha>2
for the mono-disperse initial condition
f_{0}=\delta_{1}
,
while when
K(x,y)=K_{1}(x,y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
,
weak solutions are gelling if and only if
\alpha>1
.

Since
K_{0}(x,y)=0
if
x/y\leq 1/2
, the support of the solution
f_{t}
starting from
f_{0}=\delta_{1}

is naturally included in
2^{\mathbb{N}}
, and we have
K_{0}(2^{n},2^{m})=0
if
n\neq m
. Thus everything
happens as if
K_{0}(x,y)=(x/2){\bf 1}_{\{x=y\}}
. Hence gelation when

K(x,y)=K_{0}(x,y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
with
\alpha>2
and
f_{0}=\delta_{1}
was already known
[
5
]
,
but they showed non-gelation only when
\alpha\leq 1
.

The complexity of
1
-homogeneous kernel is not new:
van Dongen-Ernst
[
17
, Section 6]
and Hermann-Niethammer-Velázquez
[
9
]
showed,
via

rigorous and heuristic results, that the large-time behavior of (non-gelling) solutions depends on the precise
shape of the kernel.

2.
Main result and proof

We always assume at least that
K:(0,\infty)^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is measurable and symmetric,
i.e.

K(x,y)=K(y,x)
.
The following definition is more or less classical, see e.g. Laurençot
[
11
, Theorem 24]
.

Definition 1
.

A measurable family
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of nonnegative measures on
(0,\infty)
is said
to be a weak solution to (
1
) if for all
t\geq 0
,

(2)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s<\infty,

and if for all bounded measurable
\psi:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, all
t\geq 0
,

(3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)f_{0}({\rm d}x)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Delta_{\psi}(x,y)K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s,

where
\Delta_{\psi}(x,y)=\psi(x+y)-\psi(x)-\psi(y)
.

All the terms make sense in (
3
): the first and second one belong to
[0,\infty)\cup\{+\infty\}

and the last one is finite by (
2
) and since
|\Delta_{\psi}|
is bounded.
Moreover, for any bounded measurable
\psi:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

for which
{\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)f_{0}({\rm d}x)<\infty}
, the map
t\to\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)
is continuous
from
[0,\infty)
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Norris
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
found a very general existence result: it suffices that
K

is continuous on
(0,\infty)^{2}
and that
there exists a continuous function
\theta:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, sublinear (that is
\theta(\lambda x)\leq\lambda\theta(x)
if
\lambda\geq 1
)
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta(x)f_{0}({\rm d}x)<\infty
,

K(x,y)\leq\theta(x)\theta(y)
and
\lim_{(x,y)\to\infty}[\theta(x)\theta(y)]^{-1}K(x,y)=0
.
This applies to many physical kernels, possibly diverging for small and large masses.

For
f
a nonnegative measure on
(0,\infty)
and
k\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we set

M_{k}(f)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}f({\rm d}x)
. If applying (
3
) with
\psi(x)=x
,
we would find that
M_{1}(f_{t})=M_{1}(f_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
, because
\Delta_{\psi}=0
.
But this is not licit, since

\psi(x)=x
is not bounded. However, the following observation is very classical.

Remark 2
.

For any weak solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
),
we have
M_{1}(f_{t})\leq M_{1}(f_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 0
. For
a>0
, we set
\psi_{a}(x)=x\land a
and observe that

\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x,y\in(0,\infty)
. By (
3
), this implies
that
\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{0}({\rm d}x)\leq M_{1}(f_{0})
.
The conclusion follows, by monotone convergence.
∎

Here is our main result. See Remark
4
and Example
5
below for applications.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that

(4)

\displaystyle\hbox{there exist $x_{0}>0$ and $r>1$ such that }\;\;\kappa:=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}[H(a)]^{-1/2}{\rm d}a<\infty,

\displaystyle\hbox{where}\;\;H(a)=a\inf\{K(x,y):x,y\in[a,ra]\}.

For any weak solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
) with

M_{1}(f_{0})<\infty
and
f_{0}((x_{0},\infty))>0
, we have

T_{gel}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{1}(f_{t})<M_{1}(f_{0})\}\leq 2\kappa^{2}\Big(\frac{r}{r-1}\Big)^{2}\frac{M_{1}(f_{0})}{[\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}(x-x_{0})f_{0}({\rm d}x)]^{2}}.

Proof.

For
a>0
, we set
\psi_{a}(x)=x\land a
. We have
\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x,y>0
and

\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)=-a
when
x,y\geq a
. Hence

\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)K(x,y)\leq-aK(x,y){\bf 1}_{\{x,y\geq a\}}\leq-aK(x,y){\bf 1}_{\{x,y\in[a,ra]\}}\leq-H(a){\bf 1}_{\{x,y\in[a,ra]\}}.

Applying (
3
) with
\psi_{a}
, we find that for all
t\geq 0
,

(5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)(f_{t}({\rm d}x)-f_{0}({\rm d}x))\leq-\frac{H(a)}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{a}^{ra}\int_{a}^{ra}f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s=-\frac{H(a)}{2}\int_{0}^{t}[F_{s}(a)]^{2}{\rm d}s,

where
F_{s}(a)=f_{s}([a,ra])
. Since
f_{t}
is nonnegative, we conclude that

\int_{0}^{t}H(a)[F_{s}(a)]^{2}{\rm d}s\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{0}({\rm d}x)\leq 2M_{1}(f_{0}).

Multiplying this inequality by
[H(a)]^{-1/2}
and integrating in
a\in[x_{0},\infty)
, using (
4
),
we find

(6)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}[H(a)]^{1/2}[F_{s}(a)]^{2}{\rm d}a{\rm d}s\leq 2\kappa M_{1}(f_{0}).

We next write
M_{1}(f_{s})=A_{s}+B_{s}
, where

A_{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land x_{0})f_{s}({\rm d}x)\quad\hbox{and}\quad B_{s}=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}(x-x_{0})f_{s}({\rm d}x).

We have
A_{s}\leq A_{0}
by (
5
) (with
a=x_{0}
). Morover,
since for all
x\geq x_{0}
,

\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}{\bf 1}_{\{x\in[a,ra]\}}{\rm d}a=x-\max\Big\{x_{0},\frac{x}{r}\Big\}=\min\Big\{x-x_{0},\frac{r-1}{r}x\Big\}\geq\frac{r-1}{r}(x-x_{0}),

we have

\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}F_{s}(a){\rm d}a=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{a}^{ra}f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}a=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}{\bf 1}_{\{x\in[a,ra]\}}{\rm d}af_{s}({\rm d}x)\geq\frac{r-1}{r}B_{s}.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
4
), we get

B_{s}^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{r}{r-1}\Big)^{2}\Big[\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}[H(a)]^{-1/4}[H(a)]^{1/4}F_{s}(a){\rm d}a\Big]^{2}\leq\Big(\frac{r}{r-1}\Big)^{2}\kappa\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}[H(a)]^{1/2}[F_{s}(a)]^{2}{\rm d}a.

Integrating this inequality in
s\in[0,t]
and using (
6
), we conclude that for all
t\geq 0
,

(7)

\int_{0}^{t}B_{s}^{2}{\rm d}s\leq 2\Big(\frac{r}{r-1}\Big)^{2}\kappa^{2}M_{1}(f_{0}).

We have shown that
M_{1}(f_{s})\leq A_{0}+B_{s}
, with
A_{0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land x_{0})f_{0}({\rm d}x)
.
For all
s\in[0,T_{gel})
, we have
M_{1}(f_{s})=M_{1}(f_{0})
and thus

B_{s}\geq M_{1}(f_{0})-A_{0}=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}(x-x_{0})f_{0}({\rm d}x)>0
, since
f_{0}((x_{0},\infty))>0
by assumption.
Inserted in (
7
) (with
t=T_{gel}
), this gives

T_{gel}\Big[\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}(x-x_{0})f_{0}({\rm d}x)\Big]^{2}\leq 2\Big(\frac{r}{r-1}\Big)^{2}\kappa^{2}M_{1}(f_{0}).

∎

Remark 4
.

Assume that
K:(0,\infty)^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is continuous,
\gamma
-homogeneous (i.e.

K(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^{\gamma}K(x,y)
for all
\lambda,x,y>0
)
with
\gamma>1
, and that
K(1,1)>0
. Then (
4
) is met
for any
x_{0}>0
.

Proof.

By continuity, there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

\rho=\inf\{K(x,y):x,y\in[1,1+\varepsilon]\}>0.

Choosing
r=1+\varepsilon
and using the homogeneity of
K
, one can check that

H(a)=a\inf\{K(x,y):x,y\in[a,ra]\}=a^{1+\gamma}\inf\{K(x,y):x,y\in[1,r]\}\geq\rho a^{1+\gamma}.

Since
\gamma>1
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Example 5
.

Fix
\gamma>1
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\theta\geq 0
. If
K(x,y)\geq K_{0}(x,y)
, with (here
u_{+}=\max\{u,0\}
)

\displaystyle K_{0}(x,y)=(x\land y)^{\gamma}\Big(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}\Big)^{\theta}\quad\hbox{or}\quad K_{0}(x,y)=(x\land y)\Big(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}\Big)^{\theta}\log^{2+\varepsilon}(1+x\land y),

\displaystyle K_{0}(x,y)=(x\land y)^{\gamma}\Big(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)_{+}^{\theta}\quad\hbox{or}\quad K_{0}(x,y)=(x\land y)\Big(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)_{+}^{\theta}\log^{2+\varepsilon}(1+x\land y),

then (
4
) is met: any choice of
x_{0}>0
and
r>1
is suitable for the two first kernels,
and any choice of
x_{0}>0
and
r\in(1,2)
is convenient for the two last ones.

None of these four kernels are not covered by
[
10
,
6
,
11
]
. The first and third ones are covered by
[
2
]
,
and they are able to treat the second and fourth ones, with a power
3+\varepsilon
instead of
2+\varepsilon

for the logarithmic term. Hence the improvement of the present work is not huge when compared to
[
2
]
.
However, we can show that
any
weak solution is gelling for those kernels, and the proof is very short.
Let us mention that we are far from being able to deal with kernels vanishing on the diagonal,
such as
K(x,y)=(x^{1/3}+y^{1/3})^{2}|x^{2/3}-y^{2/3}|
mentioned in
[
1
, Table 1]
.

3.
About criticality

By
[
11
, Propositions 33 and 36]
,
the kernel
K(x,y)=(xy)^{1/2}\log^{\alpha/2}(e+x)\log^{\alpha/2}(e+y)
is non-gelling if
\alpha\leq 1
,
but gelling if
\alpha>2
. However, we cannot decide what happens when
\alpha\in(1,2]
.
The same applies to
K(x,y)=(x\land y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
.

All this concerns kernels that are
1
-homogeneous, that is
K(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda K(x,y)
,
up to some logarithmic factor. In Propositions
6
and
7
below, we find two

1
-homogeneous kernels

K_{0}
and
K_{1}
such that
K(x,y)=K_{0}(x,y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
is gelling if and only if
\alpha>2

(
only when
f_{0}=\delta_{1}
),
while
K(x,y)=K_{1}(x,y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
is gelling if and only if
\alpha>1
.
Hence the critical exponent of the logarithmic factor may
depend on the precise shape of the kernel.

Proposition 6
.

Consider

K(x,y)=(x\land y)\Big(\frac{x\land y}{x\lor y}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)_{+}\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y).

(i) If
\alpha>2
, any weak solution to (
1
) such that
M_{1}(f_{0})\in(0,\infty)
is gelling.

(ii) If
\alpha\in(0,2]
, then any weak solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
) starting from
f_{0}=\delta_{1}

is non-gelling.

Proof.

Point (i) follows from Theorem
3
: with the choice
r=3/2
, we have

H(a)=\frac{1}{6}a^{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+a)
. Hence if
\alpha>2
,
\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}[H(a)]^{-1/2}{\rm d}a<\infty

for all
x_{0}>0
. For (ii), we fix
\alpha\in(0,2]
and a weak solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}

issued from
f_{0}=\delta_{1}
and show in several steps the absence of gelation

Step 1.
Here we prove that for any
t\geq 0
,
f_{t}((0,\infty)\setminus 2^{\mathbb{N}})=0
,
where
2^{\mathbb{N}}=\{2^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

To this end, we define
A_{0}=(0,1)
and, for
n\geq 0
,
A_{n+1}=A_{n}\cup(2^{n},2^{n+1})
and we
show by induction that
f_{t}(A_{n})=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

When
n=0
, we apply (
3
) with
\psi={\bf 1}_{(0,1)}
. Since
\Delta_{\psi}(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x>0,y>0
,
we conclude that
f_{t}((0,1))\leq f_{0}((0,1))=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

We next fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and assume that
f_{t}(A_{n})=0
, so that

f_{t}({\rm d}x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{t}(\{2^{k}\})\delta_{2^{k}}({\rm d}x)+{\bf 1}_{\{x\geq 2^{n}\}}f_{t}({\rm d}x)
, for all
t\geq 0
.
We want to
show that
f_{t}((2^{n},2^{n+1}))=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
We apply (
3
) with
\psi={\bf 1}_{(2^{n},2^{n+1})}
.
Using that
f_{0}((2^{n},2^{n+1}))=0
and ignoring the nonpositive terms, this gives

\displaystyle f_{t}((2^{n},2^{n+1}))\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\bf 1}_{\{x+y\in(2^{n},2^{n+1})\}}K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s=I^{n}_{t}+2J^{n}_{t}+K^{n}_{t},

where

\displaystyle I^{n}_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}+2^{\ell}\in(2^{n},2^{n+1})\}}K(2^{k},2^{\ell})f_{s}(\{k\})f_{s}(\{\ell\}){\rm d}s,

\displaystyle J^{n}_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{y\geq 2^{n}}{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}+y\in(2^{n},2^{n+1})\}}K(2^{k},y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}(\{k\}){\rm d}s,

\displaystyle K^{n}_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x\geq 2^{n}}\int_{y\geq 2^{n}}{\bf 1}_{\{x+y\in(2^{n},2^{n+1})\}}K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s.

First,
I^{n}_{t}=0
, since
K(2^{k},2^{\ell})=0
for all
k\neq\ell
and
and since
2^{k}+2^{k}\notin(2^{n},2^{n+1})
for all
k=0,\dots,n
. Next,
J^{n}_{t}=0
,
because
y\geq 2^{n}
and
2^{k}+y<2^{n+1}
imply that
k\leq n-1
, so that
2^{k}\leq y/2
and thus
K(2^{k},y)=0
.
Finally,
K^{n}_{t}=0
since
x\geq 2^{n}
and
y\geq 2^{n}
imply that
x+y\geq 2^{n+1}
.

Step 2.
By Step 1, we may write
f_{t}=\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})\delta_{2^{n}}
.
We show here that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
t\mapsto f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})
is
C^{1}
on
[0,\infty)
and

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})=-K(2^{n},2^{n})[f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}K(2^{n-1},2^{n-1})[f_{t}(\{2^{n-1}\})]^{2}{\bf 1}_{\{n\geq 1\}}.

If first
n\geq 1
, we apply (
3
) with
\psi(x)={\bf 1}_{\{x=2^{n}\}}
and get,
since
f_{0}(\{2^{n}\})=0
,

f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}[{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}+2^{\ell}=2^{n}\}}-{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}=2^{n}\}}-{\bf 1}_{\{2^{\ell}=2^{n}\}}]K(2^{k},2^{\ell})f_{s}(\{2^{k}\})f_{s}(\{2^{\ell}\}){\rm d}s.

Since
K(2^{k},2^{\ell})=0
for
k\neq\ell
, we conclude that

f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}K(2^{n-1},2^{n-1})[f_{s}(\{2^{n-1}\})]^{2}{\rm d}s-\int_{0}^{t}K(2^{n},2^{n})[f_{s}(\{2^{n}\})]^{2}{\rm d}s.

Since
t\mapsto f_{t}(\{2^{k}\})
is continuous for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
by Definition
1
,
the conclusion follows. If next
n=0
, we apply (
3
) with
\psi(x)={\bf 1}_{\{x=1\}}
and get,
since
f_{0}(\{1\})=1
,

\displaystyle f_{t}(\{1\})=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}}[{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}+2^{\ell}=1\}}-{\bf 1}_{\{2^{k}=1\}}-{\bf 1}_{\{2^{\ell}=1\}}]K(2^{k},2^{\ell})f_{s}(\{2^{k}\})f_{s}(\{2^{\ell}\}){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\int_{0}^{t}K(1,1)[f_{s}(\{1\})]^{2}{\rm d}s

as desired. We used that
K(1,2^{k})=0
for all
k\geq 1
.

Step 3.

We now prove that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

b_{n}:=\sup_{t\geq 0}f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})\leq\frac{\log^{\alpha/2}(e+1)}{2^{n}\log^{\alpha/2}(e+2^{n})}.

We proceed by induction. If
n=0
, we know from Step 2 that
t\mapsto f_{t}(\{2^{0}\})
is non-increasing,
whence
b_{0}=f_{0}(\{2^{0}\})=1
.
Fix
n\geq 1
and assume that
b_{n-1}
satisfies the desired estimate. Then

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})\leq-K(2^{n},2^{n})[f_{t}(\{2^{n}\})]^{2}+\frac{1}{2}K(2^{n-1},2^{n-1})b_{n-1}^{2}

by Step 2.
Since
f_{0}(\{2^{n}\})=0
, we classically conclude that

b_{n}\leq\Big(\frac{K(2^{n-1},2^{n-1})}{2K(2^{n},2^{n})}\Big)^{1/2}b_{n-1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log^{\alpha/2}(e+2^{n-1})}{\log^{\alpha/2}(e+2^{n})}b_{n-1}\leq\frac{\log^{\alpha/2}(e+1)}{2^{n}\log^{\alpha/2}(e+2^{n})}.

Step 4.
We show that there are
A,B>0
such that
for any
t>0
,
\int_{0}^{\infty}x\log(e+x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq Ae^{Bt}
.

We fix
a>1
and apply (
3
) with
\psi_{a}(x)=x\log(e+x){\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a\}}
. By Step
1 and since
K(2^{k},2^{n})=0
if
k\neq n
, we find, introducing
A=\log(e+1)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x\log(1+x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)=

\displaystyle A+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{0}^{t}\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(2^{n},2^{n})K(2^{n},2^{n})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\}){\rm d}s.

But

\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,x)=\psi_{a}(2x)-2\psi_{a}(x)\leq{\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a/2\}}2x\log\Big(\frac{e+2x}{e+x}\Big)\leq{\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a\}}2x\log 2,

whence,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x\log(e+x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq

\displaystyle A+\log 2\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{0}^{t}2^{n}{\bf 1}_{\{2^{n}\leq a\}}K(2^{n},2^{n})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\}){\rm d}s.

Using the expression of
K(2^{n},2^{n})
and Step 3, we find

K(2^{n},2^{n})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\})\leq 2^{n}\log^{\alpha}(e+2^{n})b_{n}\leq\log^{\alpha/2}(e+1)\log^{\alpha/2}(e+2^{n})\leq\log^{\alpha-1}(e+1)\log(e+2^{n}),

since
\alpha\in(0,2]
(so that
0<u<v
implies
u^{\alpha/2}v^{\alpha/2}\leq u^{\alpha-1}v
).
Setting
B=(\log 2)(\log^{\alpha-1}(e+1))
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x\log(e+x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq

\displaystyle A+B\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{0}^{t}{\bf 1}_{\{2^{n}\leq a\}}2^{n}\log(e+2^{n})f_{s}(\{2^{n}\}){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A+B\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{a}x\log(e+x)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s

by Step 1 again. Thus
\int_{0}^{a}x\log(e+x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq Ae^{Bt}
by the
Gronwall lemma, whence the result.

Step 5.
We finally conclude that
M_{1}(f_{t})=M_{1}(f_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
.
By (
3
) with
\psi_{a}(x)=x\land a
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{t}({\rm d}x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{0}({\rm d}x)+I_{a}(t),

where

I_{a}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Delta_{\psi_{a}(x,y)}K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s.

By monotone convergence,
\lim_{a\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land a)f_{t}({\rm d}x)=M_{1}(f_{t})
.
It thus suffices to check that
\lim_{a\to\infty}I_{a}(t)=0
for each
t\geq 0
.
For this we use dominated convergence:
since
\lim_{a\to\infty}\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in(0,\infty)
,
and since

\sup_{a>0}|\Delta_{\psi_{a}(x,y)}|=\sup_{a>0}(x\land a+y\land a-(x+y)\land a)\leq\sup_{a>0}([x\land a]\land[y\land a])=x\land y,

we only need that for all
t\geq 0
,

(8)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land y)K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s<\infty.

But

(x\land y)K(x,y)\leq(x\land y)^{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)\leq(x\land y)^{2}\log^{2}(e+x\land y)\leq x\log(e+x)\,y\log(e+y).

Thus (
8
) follows from Step 4.
∎

Proposition 7
.

(i) If
K(x,y)\leq(x+y)\log(e+x\land y)
, then
any weak solution
(f_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
) such that
M_{1}(f_{0})+M_{2}(f_{0})<\infty

is non-gelling.

(ii) If
K(x,y)\geq(x+y)\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)
with some
\alpha>1
,
then any weak solution to (
1
) such that
M_{1}(f_{0})\in(0,\infty)
and

\int_{0}^{1}x|\log x|{\bf 1}_{\{x<1\}}f_{0}({\rm d}x)<\infty
is gelling.

Proof.

We first prove point (i) in three steps.

Step 1.
For any nonnegative measure
f
on
(0,\infty)
,
any
a>0
, any
A\geq M_{1}(f)
,

\int_{0}^{a}y\log(e+y)f({\rm d}y)\leq A\log\Big(e+\frac{1}{A}\int_{0}^{a}y^{2}f({\rm d}y)\Big).

Indeed, the Jensen inequality applied to the probability measure

\mu_{a}({\rm d}y)=Z_{a}^{-1}y{\bf 1}_{\{y\in(0,a]\}}f({\rm d}y)
,
with
Z_{a}=\int_{0}^{a}yf({\rm d}y)
, and the concave function
\log(e+y)
, directly gives

\int_{0}^{a}y\log(e+y)f({\rm d}y)\leq Z_{a}\log\Big(e+\frac{1}{Z_{a}}\int_{0}^{a}y^{2}f({\rm d}y)\Big).

It then suffices to observe that
Z_{a}\leq M_{1}(f)\leq A
and that for
b>0
fixed,
the function
\varphi(z)=z\log(e+z^{-1}b)
is nondecreasing on
[0,\infty)
,
because
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{-b^{2}}{z(b+ez)^{2}}\leq 0
and
\lim_{z\to\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(z)=0
.

Step 2.
We next show that
\sup_{[0,T]}M_{2}(f_{t})<\infty
for all
T>0
.
We apply (
3
) with
\psi_{a}(x)=x^{2}{\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a\}}
. Observing that

\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq{\bf 1}_{\{x+y\leq a\}}2xy\leq 2{\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a,y\leq a\}}xy
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}f_{0}({\rm d}x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{a}xy(x+y)\log(e+x\land y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{2}(f_{0})+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}y\log(e+y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s

by symmetry. Using Step 1 with
A=M_{1}(f_{0})\geq M_{1}(f_{s})
, recall Remark
2
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq

\displaystyle M_{2}(f_{0})+2M_{1}(f_{0})\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}f_{s}({\rm d}x)\Big)\log\Big(e+\frac{1}{M_{1}(f_{0})}\int_{0}^{a}y^{2}f_{s}({\rm d}y)\Big){\rm d}s.

This classically implies that for any
T>0
, there is a constant
C_{T}
depending only on

T
,
M_{2}(f_{0})
and
M_{1}(f_{0})
, such that
\sup_{[0,T]}\int_{0}^{a}x^{2}f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq C_{T}
.
The conclusion follows, letting
a\to\infty
by monotone convergence.

Step 3.
Exactly as in Step 5 of the proof of Proposition
6
, the only difficulty
to show that
M_{1}(f_{t})=M_{1}(f_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
is to show (
8
). But,
since
\log(e+u)\leq 1+u
for all
u\geq 0
,

(x\land y)K(x,y)\leq 2xy\log(e+x\land y)\leq 2xy(1+x\land y)\leq 2(x^{2}+x)y.

Hence for all
t>0
,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(x\land y)K(x,y)f_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s\leq 2\int_{0}^{t}(M_{2}(f_{s})+M_{1}(f_{s}))M_{1}(f_{s}){\rm d}s<\infty

by Step 2 and Remark
2
.

Point (ii) does not follow from Theorem
3
.
The proof below, adapted from
[
7
, Proposition 2.3]
, is completely different
and generally less powerful, since it allows to prove gelation when
K(x,y)=xy^{\gamma-1}+x^{\gamma-1}y

with
\gamma>1
,
but not when
K(x,y)=(xy)^{\gamma/2}
with
\gamma\in(1,2)
neither when
K(x,y)=x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}+x^{\beta}y^{\alpha}

with
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
and
\gamma=\alpha+\beta>1
.
We fix
a>0
and consider

\psi_{a}(x)=x{\bf 1}_{\{x\leq a\}}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}\geq 0.

Using that
\alpha>1
, one can check that
\psi_{a}(x)\leq\kappa x(1+|\log x|{\bf 1}_{\{x<1\}})
,
for some constant
\kappa
not depending on
a
.
We have
\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq 0
if
x>a
or
y>a
, so that for all
x,y>0
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}\Big((x+y)\int_{x+y}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}-x\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}-y\int_{y}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}\Big(x\int_{x}^{x+y}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}+y\int_{y}^{x+y}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}(x\land y)\int_{x\land y}^{x+y}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}(x\land y)\int_{x\land y}^{2(x\land y)}\frac{{\rm d}u}{\frac{u}{2}\log^{\alpha}(e+\frac{u}{2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}\frac{x\land y}{\log^{\alpha}(e+x\land y)}.

Thus
K(x,y)\Delta_{\psi_{a}}(x,y)\leq-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}(x\land y)(x+y)\leq-{\bf 1}_{\{x,y\leq a\}}xy
,
so that (
3

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{a}(x)f_{t}({\rm d}x)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{a}(x)f_{0}({\rm d}x)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{a}xyf_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s.

Since the left hand side is nonnegative and
\psi_{a}(x)\leq\kappa x(1+|\log x|{\bf 1}_{\{x<1\}})

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{a}xyf_{s}({\rm d}y)f_{s}({\rm d}x){\rm d}s\leq 2\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}x(1+|\log x|{\bf 1}_{\{x<1\}})f_{0}({\rm d}x)=:C.

Letting
a\to\infty
, we end with

\int_{0}^{t}[M_{1}(f_{s})]^{2}{\rm d}s\leq C
,
which implies that
T_{gel}\leq C/[M_{1}(f_{0})]^{2}
.
∎
