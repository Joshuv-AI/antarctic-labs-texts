Fecal microbiota transplantation validates the importance of gut microbiota in an ApoE−/− mouse model of chronic apical periodontitis-induced atherosclerosis
Authors: Guowu Gan, Ren Zhang, Yu Zeng, Beibei Lu, Yufang Luo, Shuai Chen, Huaxiang Lei, Zhiyu Cai, Xiaojing Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic apical periodontitis (CAP) has been linked to the development of atherosclerosis, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the role of gut microbiota disruption in CAP-induced atherosclerosis development, focusing on trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)-related metabolites.MethodsThe study utilized fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to transfer gut microbiota from mice with CAP to healthy mice. Atherosclerosis development was assessed by analyzing lesions in the aortic arch and aortic root. Serum lipid and inflammatory factor levels were measured. Composition and diversity of gut microbiota were analyzed using targeted metabolomics, with a focus on the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes. The expression of hepatic flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) and serum TMAO levels were also evaluated.ResultsMice receiving gut microbiota from CAP mice showed increased atherosclerotic lesions compared to controls, without significant differences in serum lipid or inflammatory factor levels. Alterations in gut microbiota composition were observed, characterized by an increase in the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio. Peptostreptococcaceae abundance positively correlated with atherosclerosis severity, while Odoribacteraceae showed a negative correlation. No significant differences were found in hepatic FMO3 expression or serum TMAO levels.ConclusionsThe study confirms the role of gut microbiota disruption in CAP-mediated atherosclerosis development, independent of serum lipid or TMAO levels. Alterations in gut microbiota composition, particularly increased Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio and specific bacterial families, were associated with atherosclerosis severity. These findings highlight the intricate interplay between gut microbiota and cardiovascular health in the context of CAP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05230-5.

Background

The human gastrointestinal tract harbors a densely populated and intricately nuanced consortium of microorganisms that are pivotal in the host’s metabolic and immune functions [1]. The role of the intestinal microbiota in sustaining host well-being has received extensive attention [2]. Investigation has revealed a correlation between intestinal microbiome dysbiosis and afflictions in diverse organs such as the brain, liver, lungs, and kidneys [3]. Despite decades of inquiry into the role of the intestinal microbiome in human health, contemporary advances in sequencing technologies and novel bioinformatics tools have allowed scientists to more precisely delineate the intestinal microbiota. Consequently, the exploration of the intestinal microbiota has emerged as major focus of health science research [4, 5].

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is a method for introducing the intestinal microbiota obtained from the feces of a healthy donor into the gastrointestinal tract of a patient in the course of a disease [6]. This therapeutic modality involves altering the composition of the intestinal microbiota to treat specific maladies. FMT was first recorded in China in the fourth century A.D. For patients with food poisoning or severe diarrhea, approximately one liter of fecal solution was taken orally, leading to the revelation of its life-saving efficacy [7]. In 1958, FMT therapy was used to successfully treat patients with pseudomembranous colitis, marking the first mention of FMT in the medical literature [8]. This inspired scientists to investigate FMT in animal experiments to determine the potential associations between the intestinal microbiota and related diseases, helping to close the gap in causal evidence that is evident in correlation studies. This methodology has become a popular means to evaluate the causal relationships arising from the intricate interplay between the host and microbiota [9]. Presently, studies on FMT indicate potential correlations between the intestinal microbiota and a spectrum of conditions, including gastrointestinal diseases such as metabolic disorders [10], Alzheimer’s disease [11] and atherosclerosis [12]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) constitutes a conglomerate of afflictions involving the heart and blood vessels, encompassing coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular diseases, and rheumatic heart disease, among others. CVD is the predominant cause of mortality worldwide [13]. Atherosclerosis is intricately linked to CVD, resulting from protracted accumulation of plaque resulting from variable risk factors. Endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, high glucose levels, and oxidative stress give rise to the complex process of atherosclerosis in the context of CVD [14]. Experimental findings suggest a direct correlation between alterations in the gut microbiota and cardiovascular health and disease [15–17]. Clinical studies underscore the potential application of FMT in the treatment of cardiac metabolic disorders, including atherosclerosis, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes [18, 19]. Previous animal experiments revealed that mice with chronic apical periodontitis (CAP) had increased atherosclerosis severity and an altered gut microbiota abundance and composition [20]. Moreover, the degree of atherosclerosis was aggravated in mice housed in the same cage as mice in the CAP group, and the serum trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) level was increased in mice in the CAP group [21]. TMAO is a small molecule compound involved in the regulation of energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and immunoinflammatory responses. TMAO production in the human body can be roughly divided into two steps: choline-rich, phosphatidylcholine-rich, and L-carnitine-rich diets result in the generation of trimethylamine (TMA) under the action of the gut microbiota, and then TMA enters the portal circulation and reaches the liver, where it is oxidized by flavin monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) to generate TMAO [22]. A large clinical study provided direct evidence of the relationship between TMAO and CVD [23], which was later confirmed by a series of experiments [24, 25]. The results of a study of the 10-year record of plasma TMAO levels in 760 women showed that long-term elevation of TMAO levels was positively associated with coronary heart disease risk [26].

In the present study, we first cleared the gut microbiota from mice by the administration of four antibiotics; we then collected feces from donor mice to prepare a supernatant to administer to recipient mice via gavage. We then observed the severity of atherosclerosis at the aortic arch and aortic root, changes in serum lipid levels, and alterations in the TMA-FMO3-TMAO signaling pathway to confirm that the CAP-altered gut microbiota promotes atherosclerotic development, as well as to explore the possible underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Animal model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care Guidelines and approved by the Experimental Animal Ethical Committee of Fujian Medical University (protocol number 2020-0041). Fifty male ApoE−/− mice were purchased from Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China) and were raised at the animal laboratory center of Fujian Medical University. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. After 1 week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups by weight: the chronic apical periodontitis group (CAP group, n = 10), control group (Con group, n = 10), chronic apical periodontitis recipient group (FMT-CAP group, n = 15), and control recipient group (FMT-Con group, n = 15). CAP was induced by P. gingivalis infection of the bilateral maxillary first and second molars under anesthesia (0.1 mL/10 g of 1% pentobarbital; Sigma‒Aldrich) according to the protocol described in our previous experiments [20], while mice in the Con group were not treated. Before FMT treatment, the recipient mice were intragastrically administered 200 µL of an antibiotic cocktail that contained 1 g/L ampicillin, 0.5 g/L neomycin, 0.5 g/L vancomycin and 1 g/L metronidazole for three consecutive days [27]. Thereafter, the feces of the mice in the CAP group were collected, prepared into a bacterial suspension, and given to the mice in the FMT-CAP group by gavage beginning on the first day after antibiotic cessation. Similarly, the fecal bacterial suspensions of the mice in the Con group were administered by gavage to those in the FMT-Con group. The frequency of bacterial perfusion was maintained at twice per week for two weeks and then changed to once per week until the 12th week. For the microbiota suspension preparation, 10 fresh feces pellets from donor mice were collected and added to 4 mL of sterilized physiological saline. After mixing, the supernatant was removed, and the mixture was given to recipient mice by oral gavage. The timing of the experimental process is shown in Fig. 1.

Euthanasia

Mice were injected intramuscularly with 100 mg/Kg of 2% pentobarbital to euthanasia. (The experiment needs to pay attention to the gut microbiota, so the intraperitoneal injection method was not used to avoid affecting the experimental results).

Evaluation of atherosclerotic lesions

Samples of the aortic arch were collected, and adherent connective tissue was removed and cleaned. Then, the aortic arch samples were longitudinally opened and stained with oil red O (Sigma‒Aldrich) as previously described [28]. Hearts were embedded in OCT compound and cut into 10 μm sections for histological analysis of atherosclerotic lesions in the aortic root. Measurement of the lesion area and quantification of lipids were based on hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and oil red O staining, respectively. Representative images of the aortic arch and the aortic root were analyzed with Fiji (NIH; https://imagej.net/downloads). The aortic arch sample size was n = 14–15, the aortic root sample size was n = 13–14, and one animal in the control group was judged to be an outlier by statistical calculation.

Measurement of serum lipid, inflammatory factor, TMAO and related metabolite levels

After 12 weeks of intervention, all mice were sacrificed, and blood samples were collected after removal of the right eyeball. After centrifugation, the serum was extracted and immediately stored at -80 °C. TC, TG, HDL-C and LDL-C levels were measured using assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering Co., Ltd.). The serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1 and IFN-γ, were measured using a liquid phase chip kit (Merck Millipore, Germany). Serum levels of choline, betaine, creatinine, L-carnitine and TMAO were quantified by HPLC-MS/MS using a Jasper HPLC instrument coupled to a Triple Quad 4500 mass spectrometer (Sciex, Shanghai, China). An ACQUITY UPLC™ BEH HILIC column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters, USA) was used, the sample volume was 5 µL, the column temperature was 40 °C, the mobile phase A-was composed of 10 mM ammonium formate water containing 0.1% formic acid, and the mobile phase B was acetonitrile.

Sequencing of 16 S rRNA genes

The feces samples were freshly collected one week before the experimental animals were sacrificed. Total genomic DNA was extracted from feces samples using an EZNA® soil kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 variable region was amplified by PCR, and the amplification procedure was as follows: predenaturation at 98 °C for 1 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; and extension at 72 °C for 10 min (PCR instrument: ABI GeneAmp®). PCR products were detected by 2% agarose electrophoresis, purified using a Thermo Scientific GeneJET Gel Recovery Kit, and eluted with Tris-HCl. A library was constructed using an Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit. The constructed library was quantified by a Qubit, and the library was tested and approved for on-line sequencing using a NovaSeq 6000 instrument.

Bioinformatic analysis

All the raw sequences of all the samples were quality controlled, denoised, spliced, and dechimerized to form operational taxonomic units (OTUs), which can also be referred to as signature sequences, using the DADA2 plug-in in QIIME 2 software. Representative sequences from the OTUs were selected and compared to a database (16 S default Greengenes database version 13_8) to obtain species annotation information. LEfSe was used to identify bacteria that differed in abundance between groups and samples. The diversity matrix was then calculated using the QIIME2 core-diversity plugin. Characteristic sequence-level alpha diversity indices, including observed-OTUs, Chao1, Shannon and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (faith-pd) indices, were used to assess the degree of diversity within the samples. The Bray‒Curtis index was used to assess the variability in microbial community structure between samples, which was subsequently demonstrated using PCoA plots. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to understand the relationships between species. Mapping was performed with R (heatmap package).

Immunohistochemical analysis

Fresh liver tissues from mice were collected and fixed with 4% neutral paraformaldehyde for 48 h. After gradient ethanol dehydration, n-butanol transparency induction and paraffin embedding, paraffin Sect. (4 μm) were made for immunohistochemical experiments. After autoclave-based antigen repair and goat serum antigen blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-FMO3 primary antibody (ab126711; Abcam, USA) (1:500). Signal detection was performed using an UltraSensitiveTM SP Immunohistochemistry Kit (Maixin Biotechnology Development Co., Fuzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means ± SEMs. When the data exhibited a normal distribution and variances were homogeneous, an independent t test was performed (version 23.0, IBM SPSS Statistics, NY). When the data did not have a normal distribution, nonparametric analysis was employed. Two-tailed p values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Effect of FMT with faeces from CAP mice on atherosclerosis in mice

The percentage of the atherosclerotic area in the intima-media of the aortic arch in the FMT-CAP group was 1.71 ± 1.25%, and that in the FMT-Con group was 0.84 ± 0.84% (Fig. 2. a-b). The percentage of the atherosclerotic lesion area in the aortic arch increased in the CAP group (Fig. 2. c, P < 0.05). Frozen sections were taken from the aortas of mice near the roots and stained with H&E and oil red O to observe differences in the atherosclerotic plaque area and lipid content. Atherosclerotic plaque formation was observed in the aortic roots of both groups of recipient mice, with plaque areas of 47.84 ± 16.03 × 103 µm2 in the FMT-CAP group and 33.32 ± 12.68 × 103 µm2 in the FMT-Con group (Fig. 2. d-e), and the atherosclerotic areas of mice in the FMT-CAP group were significantly greater than those of mice in the FMT-Con group (Fig. 2. f, P < 0.05). Oil red O staining revealed that the lipid content of atherosclerotic plaques at the aortic root was greater in the FMT-CAP group (Fig. 2. g-i, P < 0.05).

Effect of FMT on serum lipid and inflammatory factor levels in mice

After 12 weeks of FMT, blood was collected retro-orbitally for lipid analysis. Compared with that of mice in the FMT-Con group, there was an increase in the serum TG concentration of mice in the FMT-CAP group (P = 0.05), whereas the differences in TC, LDL-C and HDL-C among the different groups were not statistically significant (Fig. 3. a-d, P > 0.05). The results of the inflammatory factor assay showed that there was no statistically significant difference in the serum concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, or IFN-γ between the two groups of mice (Fig. 3. e-h, P > 0.05).

Discussions

Atherosclerosis is a complex chronic progressive disease with a long course, and the different stages of the disease involve various changes in the arterial wall, which has hindered our research on human atherosclerosis [29]. The emergence and use of animal models of atherosclerosis have addressed many of the limitations inherent in human studies. The ApoE−/− mouse is one of the classic models for atherosclerosis and spontaneously develops atherosclerotic lesions [30]. ApoE−/− mice can form atherosclerotic plaques even when fed a chow diet, which is one of the important reasons why we chose this mouse model, as a high-fat diet strongly affects the gut microbiota [31]. Using FMT, Brandsma et al. [12] demonstrated that a proinflammatory gut microbiota increased inflammation and atherosclerosis in Ldlr−/− mice. This finding is consistent with our results showing that after 12 weeks of FMT. Under a stereomicroscope, the aortas of the mice appeared completely exfoliated. Atherosclerotic plaque formation was not observed in the lower part of the aorta under microscopic observation, so only the aortic arch (including the ascending aorta, the aortic arch, and a portion of the descending aorta) was isolated and stained with oil red O (Fig. 2.a-c). H&E staining (Fig. 2.d-f) and oil red O staining (Fig. 2.g-i) at the aortic arch similarly confirmed increased atherosclerosis in the FMT-CAP group. The above results suggest that gut microbiota disturbance caused by CAP promotes atherosclerosis development. Recent clinical and experimental evidence has highlighted a significant connection between CAP and CVD. Studies have reported that individuals with CAP display elevated markers of endothelial dysfunction, indicating early vascular changes that could contribute to the onset of CVD [32]. Other research has shown impaired endothelial function and early signs of atherosclerosis in patients with apical periodontitis [33]. Additional findings suggest that AP is linked to the presence of carotid plaques and increased carotid intima-media thickness, pointing to its role in the development of atherosclerosis [34]. Experimental models also demonstrate that CAP worsens atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice fed a high-fat diet, correlating with changes in gut microbiota metabolites [35]. These results confirm our hypothesis that the presence of CAP not only contributes directly to the extent of atherosclerosis, but also influences the progression of atherosclerosis by altering the gut microbiota, which provides new insight into the relationship between CAP and CVD.

One of the aims of this study was to analyze the changes in serum lipid levels after FMT. Hyperlipidemia is considered a risk factor for atherosclerosis [36]. Decreased serum levels of HDL-C and increased levels of LDL-C were observed in rats fed a high-fat diet [37]. High levels of HDL-C may prevent the formation of atherosclerotic plaques, thereby reducing CVD incidence. Conversely, high levels of LDL-C are associated with major complications of atherosclerosis, such as cerebrovascular disease, coronary artery disease, and peripheral vascular disease [37]. In the present study, there was a decrease in the levels of TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C in the CAP group, but these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3.a-d). None of the differences in serum inflammatory factors IFNλ, TNF-α, MCP-1 and IL-1β were statistically significant (Fig. 3.e-h). These findings are consistent with those for CAP model mice fed a chow diet [20]. The primary reason for this may be that the mice from the CAP group also had normal lipid levels, and a possible secondary reason is that the mice were fed a chow diet and thus did not have a dietary basis for hyperlipidemia. Finally, CAP may not promote atherosclerosis development through serum inflammatory factors or lipids.

Alpha diversity analysis is an indicator of the species diversity of the gut microbiota and includes two factors: richness and evenness; the Chao1, Faith-pd and observed-OTUs indices reflect the richness of the community, and the Shannon index reflects the diversity and evenness of the community. Alpha results show CAP promotes gut microbiota diversity in mice (Fig. 4.a). The beta diversity indicated that there was a significant difference in the gut microbiota structure between the two groups of recipient mice (Fig. 4.e). This result is similar to the findings of the previous experiment [21], although a different FMT approach was used, it indicates that CAP is able to cause changes in gut microbiota results in mice. Although there are individual differences in the structure of the microbiota, the overall community is generally similar at the phylum level and is mainly composed of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and others [38]. In a balance state, the host and the gut microbiota live in harmony and benefit from each other. In contrast, abnormalities in the types, ratios, and numbers of commensal and pathogenic bacteria can lead to dysbiosis and endanger human health [39]. Species diversity assessment is an important part of gut microbiota analysis, and microbial diversity may be higher, lower, or no different in diseased individuals than in healthy individuals [40].

In a previous study [20], it was shown that CAP mice fed a normal diet had a greater microbiota diversity than controls. This finding is consistent with the results of the present study, in which we observed that the alpha diversity of the gut microbiota was elevated in the recipient mice that were transplanted with material from CAP mice. The significant differences in the gut microbiota community structure between the two groups of recipient mice suggested that FMT with material from CAP mice significantly affected the gut microbiota of the recipient mice (Fig. 4.e). To understand the specific effects of FMT on the gut microbiota community structure in the recipient mice, we analyzed the species composition of the gut microbiota at the phylum level (Fig. 5.a). Under physiological conditions, the gut microbial community is dominated by either Firmicutes or Bacteroidetes, which account for more than 90% of the human gut microbiota [41]. A high Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio is often regarded as an indicator of dysbiosis [42]. Patients with coronary artery disease have an elevated F/B ratio [43]. In addition, type II diabetes [44], obesity [45], and inflammation [46] are also associated with an elevated F/B ratio, which is a risk factor for atherosclerosis. An elevated Actinobacteria abundance has also been suggested as a potential feature of homeostatic dysregulation and increased risk of disease [42]. An evolutionary branching diagram is shown in the figure, with the inner to outer rings representing the level of classification from the phylum to family level (Fig. 5.c). Yellow indicates a characteristic microorganism that did not have a significant difference in abundance between the two groups, red indicates a characteristic microorganism that was more abundant in the FMT-CAP group, and blue indicates a characteristic microorganism that was more abundant in the FMT-Con group. Odoribacteraceae were significantly enriched in the FMT-Con group; Gammaproteobacteria, Enterobacteriales, Enterobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichia, Bacilli, Lactobacillales, and Lactobacillaceae were enriched in the FMT-CAP group (Fig. 5.c). Consistent with our findings, a macrogenomic association analysis revealed a significant increase in the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae in patients with atherosclerotic CVD compared to healthy subjects [47]. There was a positive correlation between the abundance of specific strains of Erysipelotrichia and the serum concentration of TC [48]. In the feces of atherosclerosis-prone patients, the proportion of Erysipelotrichia was significantly correlated with the development of atherosclerotic plaques in the aortic root [19].

It has been shown that Peptostreptococcaceae abundance is positively correlated with atherosclerotic plaque area in ApoE−/− mice [49], which is in line with our findings. Lactobacilli are considered beneficial bacteria in the gut; however, different strains of lactobacilli have been shown to have distinct effects on atherosclerosis, and different results have been obtained with animal models of CVD [41]. In an animal study, Lactobacillus plantarum ZDY04 was shown to attenuate TMAO-induced atherosclerosis in ApoE−/− mice by promoting reverse cholesterol transport [50]. However, Portugal et al. [51] showed that Lactobacillus terreus UFV H2B20 had no effect on cholesterol metabolism or atherosclerotic plaque formation in ApoE−/− mice. In the present study, we observed an increase in the abundance of Lactobacillaceae species in the FMT-CAP group (Supplement Fig. 1), and some members of the Lactobacillus group may be found enriched in animal models that promote atherosclerosis [52]. Unfortunately, due to the limitations of 16 S rRNA sequencing technology, we could not identify specific strains of Lactobacillus with elevated abundance, which is one of the shortcomings of this study, and we will consider using metagenomic sequencing methods for more in-depth analysis in the future. One important limitation of our study is that, while we have identified alterations in bacterial flora associated with CAP, we have not directly tested whether infection with these specific bacterial species alone would produce similar atherosclerotic outcomes as observed in CAP mice. Future studies could focus on isolating and introducing these bacteria into germ-free ApoE−/− mice to determine if similar systemic effects are induced. This approach would help clarify the specific causative role of individual microbial species altered by CAP in the development of atherosclerosis.

The gut microbiome exerts its influence on the host through a variety of mechanisms [53]. Research has shown that the gut microbiota, functioning as an endocrine organ, produces bioactive metabolites such as TMA/TMAO and SCFAs, which play a significant role in influencing various chronic diseases, including CVD [54]. TMAO is an intestinal-derived bacterial metabolite, and its production pathway can be simply summarized in two steps [22]: choline-rich, phosphatidylcholine-rich, and carnitine-rich diets result in the generation of trimethylamine (TMA) under the action of intestinal microorganisms, and then TMA enters the liver and is converted to TMAO under the action of FMO3. TMAO, L-carnitine and phosphatidylcholine have been shown to promote the progression of atherosclerosis in animal experiments and clinical studies [23, 55]. Additionally, elevated levels of TMAO have been linked to a higher incidence and growth of abdominal aortic aneurysms, suggesting that targeting microbiome-derived TMAO could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy [56]. Furthermore, research has highlighted the role of TMAO produced by the aorta and liver in enhancing trained immunity and driving inflammation through ER stress and mitochondrial ROS [57]. These insights into TMAO’s involvement in vascular inflammation provide new potential therapeutic targets for CVD. In our previous experiments, we found that the serum TMAO levels were increased in CAP mice, but there was no statistically significant difference in the TMAO levels in mice in the same cage [21]; therefore, we verified whether TMAO was a key factor in atherosclerosis exacerbation induced by the gut microbiota of CAP mice using the FMT. For TMAO-targeted metabolomics, we used OPLS-DA, a supervised statistical method that incorporates grouping information when analyzing data with the aim of improving discovery of correlations between the data and other variables (Fig. 6.a). There was also no significant difference in the serum levels of choline, creatinine, or L-carnitine, TMAO and TMA precursors, between the two groups of recipient mice, and only the betaine concentration increased in the FMT-CAP group (Fig. 6.b-c). We also measured the FMO3 expression in the liver by immunohistochemistry and found that the expression levels of FMO3 in the two groups were similar (Fig. 6.d-e). Therefore, we hypothesize that in this study, the gut microbiota-TMA-TMAO pathway was not a key pathway involved in promoting atherosclerosis progression in ApoE-/- recipient mice. The low choline content (only 0.2%) of the common diet used in this experiment may also be a reason for the lack of difference in TMAO levels between the two groups, as TMA production requires the presence of TMA precursors in the diet [22]. In the absence of sufficient raw materials (dietary components such as choline), TMA synthesis may be limited, resulting in a small difference in serum TMAO levels despite changes in the gut microbiota composition. We cannot exclude the possibility that a greater difference in the degree of atherosclerosis between the two groups of recipient mice would be observed when the proportion of choline in the diet was increased. Another potential explanation for the unchanged TMAO levels lies in the interaction between diet, gut microbiota and liver function. A diet high in fat, choline, and betaine stimulates hepatic FMO3 expression [58, 59]. In addition, Enterobacteriaceae, which promote the conversion of betaine to TMA [60, 61], were upregulated in this group (Fig. 5.c), and betaine levels were elevated (Fig. 6.c). However, the inherent self-repair mechanisms of the liver may contribute to stabilized levels of FMO3 expression, thus preventing the corresponding elevation of TMAO [62, 63]. In a previous CAP modeling study, we observed elevated Enterobacteriaceae levels in the intestine and elevated serum TMAO [21]. These findings suggest that CAP induces alterations in gut microbiota and changes in liver function, both of which are required to significantly increase TMAO levels.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the intricate interplay among CAP, gut microbiota dysbiosis and atherosclerosis development. According to the FMT results, CAP affects the gut microbiota composition, revealing a new pathway by which CAP promotes atherosclerosis, although the results suggest that TMAO and its associated metabolites are not involved. Future research efforts should aim to further elucidate the specific microbial taxa and metabolic pathways that drive atherosclerosis in the context of CAP, facilitating the development of targeted interventions to mitigate cardiovascular health effects.
