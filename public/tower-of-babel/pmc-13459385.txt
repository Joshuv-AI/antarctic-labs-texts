A qualitative study on the development of socially responsive nurses: a conceptual model based on faculty members and student perspectives
Authors: Fateme Behmaneshpour, Arezoo Dehghani, Marjan Banazadeh, Zahra Eskandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the approaches that has attracted the attention of planners in recent years in order to create a link between the learning of university graduates and the needs of society is the responsive education approach. It is clear that social accountability is not a spontaneous process; there is a need for planned education of students during their academic years and their active participation in social accountability programs. This qualitative study to aimed identify the barriers and facilitators affecting nursing students participation in social accountability programs and to develop a conceptual model for developing socially responsive nurse.MethodsThe present study was a qualitative study with an exploratory inductive content analysis approach. The participants in this study included nursing faculty members and students (N = 15). The sampling method was purposive and continued until data saturation was reached. Data collection and analysis were performed simultaneously. The researcher recorded all interviews using a tape recorder, followed by an immediate verbatim transcription. Subsequently, the text was analyzed using an extractive content analysis method.ResultsAnalysis of interviews with faculty and students led to in the extraction of five core categories of inhibiting factors, which include organizational factors, insufficient positive feedback, barriers related to education and curriculum, and structural and supportive limitations. In addition, seven primary categories of facilitating factors were identified: institutional and legal support, assistance for faculty and students, the development of experiential learning networks, the integration of social accountability into education, individual factors, cultural and social influences, and collaboration with external institutions. The integration of these findings into a holistic conceptual model indicated that bolstering facilitating factors and reducing inhibiting factors can create pathways for the effective engagement of nursing students in social accountability initiatives.ConclusionDeveloping socially responsive nurses requires a systematic and multi-level approach. The proposed model can be used as a roadmap for policymakers and managers to design, plan, and implement socially responsive nursing programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09553-5.Keywords: Social accountability, Social responsibility, Model, Nursing education, Qualitative

Background

Broader context of the issue

One of the missions of universities and higher education institutions is to respond to the expectations and needs of society, and responsiveness is an integral part of the higher education system [1]. In fact, responsiveness is a key criterion for evaluating how well educational institutions address societal needs [2]. Nowadays, educational systems must be responsive to the needs of society [3].

Social accountability is defined as the obligation of individuals, groups, or institutions to explain and justify their actions, and to accept the corresponding consequences, to the society or organization they serve [4]. In the health sciences and associated fields, such as nursing, social accountability can be attained through logical policymaking and efficient interventions in education, research, care, and ongoing evaluation to enhance health [5]. The World Health Organization has highlighted the significance of social accountability in the training of health professionals to tackle health disparities and enhance population health outcomes [6]. Although social accountability is critically important in nursing practice, it has not been sufficiently acknowledged. Additionally, there is no extensive understanding of its various dimensions and the mechanisms for its implementation [7]. More extensive studies, including both quantitative and qualitative research, are need to explore approaches to social accountability within the nursing system and elucidate its various dimensions [8, 9].

Relevance to nursing and health care

Nursing, as a caring science, is committed to improving health standards and responding to the care needs of patients and society [10]. According to the social accountability approach, the nursing profession is obliged to accept responsibility for the quality of care provided to society and to be responsive to the care needs by adopting appropriate policies and strategies [11, 12]. Weak social accountability can weaken the scientific and practical ability of nurses to respond to the care needs, undermine the quality of nursing services and public trust, and ultimately reduce overall satisfaction with healthcare provision [5, 12, 13].

The link between social accountability and the development of social responsive nurse

Although the two concepts of social accountability and social responsiveness are distinct, they are closely related. The results of the study by Chhabra et al. [14] showed that social accountability acts as an institutional requirement, while social responsiveness acts as an individual behavioral driver that turns this requirement into reality, and the two complement each other in practice [14]. The educational system, through policies, curricula, and resources, provides conditions for students to become socially responsible. Therefore, participation in social responsibility programs promotes the development of social responsibility in nurses. This study aims to identify barriers and facilitators of such participation and proposes a conceptual model to link these two concepts. Responsive education is an educational approach that can help nursing students gain a better understanding of the health needs of the community and, as a result, have greater participation in social and health activities [15]. Nowadays, more than ever, we encounter the challenge of equipping graduates to address healthcare needs [16].

Conceptual distinction between social accountability and social responsiveness

In this study, we distinguish between social accountability and social responsiveness as two related but distinct concept. Based on the definition provided above, Social accountability refers to the of institutions and systems to explain and justify their action toward society [3, 4]. In contrast, social responsiveness refers to the willingness and ability of individual nurses and nursing student to activity participate in addressing community health needs. Therefore, “Is the system responsible? Versus “Do individual professional act responsibly” is provided to help readers maintain this distinction through the manuscript.

Well-defined and logically derived study aim

According to the research team, there is currently no comprehensive conceptual model that explains how participation in such programs leads to the development of socially responsive nurses. This knowledge gap is significant because without a clear understanding of these factors, educational interventions cannot be effectively designed or implemented.This qualitative study seeks to identify the barriers and facilitators factors affecting nursing students participation in social accountability programs and to develop a conceptual model for developing socially responsive nurse. The results of this study can be used by policymakers in the higher health education system in universities to effectively guide educational programs towards responding to the needs and expectations of society and achieving their goals and missions.

Methods

Study design

The present study aimed to study, and deeply understand and explore the experiences and perspectives of stakeholders (nursing faculty members and students) on the factors influencing participation in social accountability programs. Since such phenomena are context and concept dependent, a qualitative approach is the most appropriate method to achieve this goal.

Qualitative content analysis is a systematic and flexible method for analyzing interview texts and extracting the main themes and concepts. This method is particularly suitable for research that aims to pinpoint both barriers and facilitators, presenting a conceptual model, hence it was used in this study [17, 18].

Study participants and sampling

The statistical population of this study consisted of two main groups: nursing faculty members, and students. A total of 15 people participated in this qualitative study, 7 of whom were nursing faculty members and 8 were undergraduate nursing students.

In the present study, faculty members were selected based on their experience in implementing educational programs and familiarity with the challenges of the social accountability system, and students were selected as the main target group whose participation in social accountability programs leads to the development of socially responsive nurses. This rational decision aligns with the objective of the study, which is to determine the factors that hinder and promote participation.

Participants in this group were selected based on three inclusion criteria: First they needed theoretical knowledge of social accountability programs. Second, they require practical experience in health care social accountability programs, working as experts or heads of such units. Finally, participants had to express willingness to cooperate and be interviewed. We included nursing students who had participated actively in social accountability programs and were willing to cooperate. The exclusion criterion was unwillingness to cooperate and be interviewed. Purposive sampling was applied to identify individuals with rich experience in this field, with maximum diversity in terms of gender, academic year (for students), and organizational position (for managers). After the initial interviews, participants were asked to introduce others who met the study criteria and could provide valuable insights especially those with unique or contrasting experiences. This measure helped us to identify knowledgeable individuals who might not have been available through the initial targeted participant’s recruitment and selection approach. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached, and no new findings emerged from the interviews.

Prior to each interview, the researcher informed participants about the study’s objectives. Under equal conditions, the interviewer sought to build trust and obtain informed consent. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Face-to-face were conducted. After conducting several open interviews and developing an interview guideline, the semi-structured individual interview method was used. In addition to the interview, participants’ voices were recorded, and pen and paper were also used to take notes. During the interview, an attempt was made to ensure that the interview environment was quiet so that distracting devices and noises would not disrupt the interviewee’s concentration. The interviewers did not press participants’ on questions they could not or wished not to answer and avoided unnecessary interference in their work related details. The interviews had an average duration of 45 min. The initial interviews were carried out using open-ended and unstructured questions. Following the analysis of the preliminary responses and coding, the interview questions were progressively refined and evolved into a more structured format.

Thematic saturation was achieved after the twelfth interview, but for greater certainty, three more interviews were conducted.

The objective criteria for determining if thematic saturation has been reached included: (1) the absence of new codes or concepts arising in two successive interviews (2), the presence of repetitive and recurring response patterns, and (3) validation of saturation by two independent researchers during the coding process.

Data analysis

Inductive content analysis was used to analyze the data obtained from the interviews and identify the effective components in student participation in health care social accountability programs.

The data analysis process followed three sequential coding stages. First, open coding generated 870 initial codes from 15 interview transcripts, and after removing duplicate codes and refining, the number of final codes reached 470. Second, axial coding grouped similar codes into subcategories based on conceptual and semantic similarities. Third, selective coding combined the subcategories into 3 main categories of barriers and 7 main categories of facilitators. Following the categorization of the factors, three distinct types of relationships were identified between the factors that either hinder or promote student participation in social accountability programs: 1) Reinforcing relationships – factors such as “institutional and legal support” strengthen other facilitators.2) Weakening relationships – strengthening facilitators indirectly reduces barriers (e.g., reducing structural constraints and educational barriers). Sequential relationships – effective participation requires three steps: strengthening facilitators, reducing barriers, providing active participation that leads to the integration of social accountability into education and services, and ultimately educating nurses who are accountable to the community.

Beyond the description of categories and subcategories, three layers of conceptual interpretation were extracted from the data: 1 - While participants reported a variety of barriers, “structural and supportive constraints” emerged as the most pervasive barrier across both faculty members and student groups. Similarly, “institutional and legal support” was identified as the strongest facilitator. 2 - The categories were combined into an integrated conceptual model (Fig. 1) that illustrates how barriers and facilitators interact to influence nursing student participation in social accountability programs. 3 - Theoretical implications: Engagement in social accountability programs serves not as a final goal, but as a fundamental method for training socially responsive nurses.

Rigor

To ensure the validity and objectivity of the findings from the qualitative interview phase, we applied the Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria, including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [19].

The credibility of these findings is strengthened by the study’s design: The researcher spent an extended period of time in the research field (over three months) to build trust with participants and gain a deep understanding of the research context. After initial analysis, a summary of the findings was shared with three participants (two students and one faculty member), and they confirmed that the interpretations made accurately reflected their experiences. Regular meetings were held with the research team to discuss emerging themes and challenge potential biases.

To ensure dependability an independent observer (with expertise in qualitative methods) reviewed 30% of the interview transcripts (5 of 15 interviews) and their corresponding coding systems. The observer provided feedback that was discussed and incorporated into the final coding framework. All steps in the data collection process (including interview preparation guides, sampling decisions, and interview conditions) were documented in a research report to provide auditability.

Regarding the confirmability of the research, before conducting the interviews, the researcher explicitly identified and set aside her pre-existing assumptions about barriers and facilitators of student participation based on discussions with the research team. Additionally, the initial transcripts of interviews, coding files, field notes, and minutes were preserved, enabling an external reviewer to track the research process in a detailed manner.

To ensure the study transferability, we provided a complete description of the participant groups (nursing faculty members and nursing students), a description of the sampling method and inclusion criteria, and a step-by-step description of the data analysis process were provided. This detailed description allows readers to assess the transferability of the findings to their own context.

Analysis of interviews with administrators and professors yielded 5 main categories and 16 subcategories related to factors influencing nursing students’ participation in healthcare social accountability programs. The barriers included: (a) educational and curriculum planning barriers with 4 subcategories (lack of educational motivation, unclear processes in social planning, lack of inclusion of social accountability activities in the curriculum, and lack of awareness and insufficient training about social accountability), (b) structural and support constraints with 3 subcategories (limited budget and financial resources, lack of intra- and inter-sectoral coordination, and parallel work of academic institutions), The facilitating components included: (a) institutional and legal support with 3 subcategories (collaboration between universities and the Ministry of Health, cooperation with local institutions, and safety and insurance coverage), (b) support for professors and students with 3 subcategories (reflection of activities in media and social networks, payment of fees or scholarships, and financial and professional encouragement of professors), and (c) development of experiential learning networks with 3 subcategories (holding lessons learned meetings, developing interdisciplinary collaborations, and creating communication networks outside the organization). Discussion

The present qualitative study was conducted with the aim of identifying the barriers and facilitators affecting nursing students’ participation in social accountability programs and developing a conceptual model for socially responsive nurses. The conceptual model (Fig. 1) integrates the perspectives of both faculty members and students regarding barriers and facilitators affecting nursing students participation in social responsive programs.

Model Development Process

Model Development Process

The conceptual model (Fig. 1) was developed based on the findings presented in Tables 2, 3, 4, and 5 through the following four steps: In the first step, barriers and facilitators were identified based on the perspectives of nursing faculty member and students.

In the second step, all identified barriers were grouped as negative inputs and all facilitators as positive inputs that determine the level of participation of nursing students.

In the third step, the outcome was identified. Effective participation leads to the integration of social accountability programs in education, research, and service delivery. This integration ultimately produces socially responsive nurses who are: (a) willing to respond to community health needs, (b) able to act on social determinants of health, and (c) committed to the principles of social accountability.

The fourth step was to arrange the chains of components as a causal chain. As shown in Fig. 1, contextual factors (cultural-sociocultural, and organizational) influence the barriers and facilitators. These barriers and facilitators determine the level of participation. This partnership will lead to the integration of social accountability into education, research, and service. Ultimately, this integration will lead to the development of socially responsive nurses with the characteristics of a willingness to respond to community health needs, the ability to act on the social determinants of health, and a commitment to the principles of social accountability.

The findings of this study led to the development of a conceptual model that considers factors influencing nursing student participation in social accountability programs leading to social responsive nurse (Fig. 1). The present model is conceptually aligned with Boelen’s three-dimensional social accountability model, which emphasizes the three fundamental pillars of education, research, and health services., The present model integrates these three areas into a dynamic cycle [20]. The innovation of the present model lies in drawing multi-level causal relationships and its provision of a practical roadmap for institutionalizing participation through evaluation and resource allocation mechanisms. Another innovation of the present model is its four-stage causal chain: Contextual factors → Barriers/Facilitators → Participation → Integration → Socially responsive nurses. This chain shows that “participation” is not simply a goal, but a mechanism that connects macro-policies to individual outcomes. This model also incorporates unique components such as “doctrinal beliefs” and “collaboration with local religious institutions” in light of Iran’s socio-cultural context.

The present study shows that contextual factors including socio-cultural and organizational factors shape the environment in which social accountability programs are implemented. Social and cultural factors such as peer role models, cultural beliefs about helping others, and societal attitudes toward student participation have a significant impact on students’ willingness to participate (Table 5, Q13–Q15). The findings of a 2023 study, consistent with the results of the present study, showed that community trust in the health care system and the effectiveness of community participation programs can influence the community and system’s perception of participation programs can affect community participation in primary health care in the health sector [21]. A positive attitude of the community towards the presence of students and cooperation with them can have mutual benefits for both parties. Students can use this opportunity to improve their communication and clinical skills, and members of the community can also receive accessible health and medical education.

Cultural beliefs, especially in societies where social participation is valued, can play an important role in encouraging nursing students to participate. In the present study, students reported the following as social and cultural factors influencing student participation. For example, participant number 14 quotes (Table 5, Q13):

Q13 (Community attitudes towards student participation): "When society recognizes the status of a nurse, it will naturally have more trust in her and the knowledge and actions she performs, and it will look at this group with a respectful perspective and with such a perspective, and it will be better able to listen.” (P14).

The organizational factors that were mainly identified by students as influencing participation included: limited access to financial resources, lack of inter-sectoral coordination, and lack of awareness about social accountability programs (Table 4, Q1–Q3). One student said regarding organizational factors influencing participation in social accountability programs (Table 4, Q2):

Q2 (Organizational Factors : Limited access to financial resources): "It's great that internships are held in the form of social responsibility programs in the community, but there is an important issue, which is that the university usually does not provide sufficient financial support and is not one of its priorities, while it has many benefits for students to acquire practical and communication skills." (P 09).

These findings suggest that even if individuals are intrinsically motivated, organizational factors can significantly hinder participation. Our findings on financial constraints reflect a global challenge to education for social accountability, and that transparent reporting to society is one solution to this challenge [22]. Universities are expected to behave in a more accountable, effective, and efficient manner [23]. Social accountability can be not only a cost, but also a source of legitimacy and funding for universities. Many of the strategic challenges that hinder the success of primary health care are rooted in weak strategic inputs, including interdisciplinary collaboration. Studies show that interdisciplinary collaboration is possible and beneficial [24]. The existence of clear mechanisms for collaboration between universities, hospitals, health centers, and other organizations can increase the scope for effective student engagement.

The relationship of social responsiveness programs to the development of socially responsive nurses

The conceptual model presented in this study (Fig. 1) illustrates how participation in social responsiveness programs serves as a primary mechanism for the development of socially responsive nurses. While social accountability operates at the institutional level (through policies, curricula, and structural supports), social responsiveness operates at the individual level (through beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors). Our findings suggest that when students participate in social responsiveness programs with facilitators and by overcoming barriers they acquire the skills, values, and experiences that characterize socially responsive nurses. Thus, it is the development of nurses who are both willing and able to respond to the health needs of the community. This study provides empirical evidence for the link between institutional responsiveness and individual responsiveness.

2.Barriers to Participation

Barriers to Participation

The main factors that barriers student participation in social accountability programs were identified as: (a) Educational and curriculum barriers (Table 2, Q1–Q4); (b) Structural and support constraints (Table 2, Q5–Q7); (c) Organizational Factors; (d) and Insufficient positive feedback (Table 4, Q1–Q6). Consistent with our findings, a 2023 study on implementing social accountability in Iran’s nursing education system identified several requirement: continuous curriculum revision based on community needs, recruitment of qualified students, and creation of a supportive educational environment, purposeful educational design, targeted monitoring and feedback, and early interaction with the community. Participants emphasized the need for continuous revision of the educational curriculum based on the current needs of the community and community-based nursing education [25]. Providing appropriate feedback can play a significant role in student participation. In line with the results of the present study, the study by Mohammadi et al. mentioned that creating motivation and compensation for service is one of the effective factors in developing responsive and community-based education [26]. Motivating students to carry out social accountability programs requires appropriate policies and strategies along with an action plan.

3.Facilitators of Participation

Facilitators of Participation

The main facilitators of student participation were: (a) institutional and legal support (Table 3, Q8–Q11); (b) educational and motivational support (Table 3, Q12–Q13); (c) development of learning networks (Table 3, Q14–Q16); (d) individual factors (human beliefs and values), (Table 5, Q10–Q12); and (e) cooperation with an external institution (Table 5, Q16–Q18). From the participants’ perspectives, institutional and legal supports included cooperation between the Ministry of Health and universities, insurance coverage for students, and reflection of student activities on social networks and media.

Holding lessons learned meetings, developing interdisciplinary collaborations, and establishing external communication networks can provide a dynamic platform for knowledge exchange, continuous improvement, and effective interaction with various stakeholders. This approach not only strengthens collective learning, but also paves the way for greater innovation and flexibility in facing social challenges. Therefore, investing in this area can promote the social responsibility of universities and increase the effectiveness of student programs. Findings consistent with the results of the present study show that implementation patterns in interprofessional education programs, by creating extensive interprofessional interactions based on the health needs of communities, provide the basis for promoting the professional socialization process of health science students in order to acquire an interprofessional identity alongside a professional identity, and thus create the necessary competence and capability to provide comprehensive and integrated client-centered team care in health science graduates [27].

One of the unique findings of this study is the identification of human values and religious beliefs as the strongest drivers of participation among individual factors. Cultural and religious beliefs play a dual role in the model: students with stronger beliefs tend to participate even in the absence of other facilitating factors (e.g., institutional support or material incentives). This distinguishes them from other facilitating.

Furthermore, cultural and religious beliefs strengthen the effect of other facilitating and weaken the impact of barriers. Students with stronger beliefs are less affected by structural constrains (e.g., limited budget or lack of interdepartmental coordination) and are more receptive to institutional support. The findings showed that cultural and religious beliefs are influenced by socio-cultural contextual factors. In other words, the society’s attitude towards student participation and the cultural beliefs existing in the society shape or reinforce these individual beliefs. Unlike factors such as “institutional and legal support” or “educational and motivational support,” which are extrinsic in nature, cultural and religious beliefs are intrinsic in nature and originate within the individual. For this reason, their effect is more stable and less dependent on external circumstances. The data showed that students with stronger religious and cultural beliefs were still willing to participate even in the face of significant structural barriers, such as budget and financial constraints. This “resilient” characteristic is not seen in other facilitating factors.

This findings aligns with international studies such that the results of a 2025 study in Turkey also showed that incorporating religious and cultural dimensions into nursing curricula, along with ethics-based education and reflective practice, can strengthen students’ ability to integrate personal beliefs with professional duties and promote organ donation more effectively [28]. In addition, the findings of a study in Indonesia show that that motivation, religiosity, and spirituality affect student performance. In addition, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation serially mediate the relationship between religiosity and spirituality with student performance [29]. In the study by Mohammadi et al., motivation and compensation for service were mentioned as one of the effective factors in the development of responsive and community-based education [26]. For other regions with similar socio-cultural structures to Iran, we recommend strengthening intrinsic motivations by linking social accountability programs to students’ value systems; conducting cross-cultural research to identify context-specific drivers.

4.Participation mechanism

Participation mechanism

The presented model goes beyond a description by placing the integration of social accountability programs into the cycle of education, research, and service delivery as its central process. The model suggests that overcoming barriers and strengthening facilitators alone is insufficient. These factors must be organized around the design and implementation of concrete social accountability projects.

We suggested the following : a) a mandatory community-based service unit should be included in the undergraduate curriculum in the form of services delivery campaigns, supported by professors and local institutions, b)student participation in social accountability programs should be defined as an indicator for evaluating faculty performance; and c) such participation should be considered a condition for budget allocation.

5.Outcome

Outcome

Based on our findings and the proposed model, a socially responsive nurse is defined by three attributes: (1) Willingness to respond to the health needs of the community. This willingness is primarily driven by individual factors such as beliefs, human values, and intrinsic motivation (Table 5, Q10). Students who internalize these values are more likely to seek out and participate in community-oriented activities. (2) Ability to act on social determinants of health. Through participation in social accountability programs—particularly needs-based service delivery and interdisciplinary collaborations—students acquire the clinical and scientific skills necessary to address complex health needs (Table 5, Q11). (3) Commitment to social accountability principles. Frequent participation and positive reinforcement (e.g., receiving certifications, recognition in scientific societies) foster a long-term commitment to social accountability (Table 5, Q11). This commitment extends beyond the educational period and shapes professional identity.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations that should be noted. First, although maximum diversity was used in the study sampling, the qualitative and exploratory nature of the study and the results are specific to a nursing school in Iran, which limits their applicability to other educational or cultural settings. It may, of course, be relevant to other low- and middle-income countries, particularly those with similar health care and education systems. Further research is needed to determine the generalizability of the study findings. Second, this study relies on self-reported experiences and perceptions, which may have introduced bias. Throughout the coding and theme extraction stages, regular discussion and debate sessions were held among the research team members (who had different experiences and backgrounds). Disagreements in data interpretation were discussed until consensus was reached. This helped to reduce the subjective bias of a single researcher and enrich the analysis. Also, to reduce the effects of these biases, measures such as creating an interview atmosphere based on trust and confidentiality, using open-ended questions, and triangulation techniques, such as comparing the perspectives of participants with different roles, were used.

Conclusion

This study showed that developing socially responsive nurses requires a systematic and multi-level approach. The proposed model can be used as a roadmap for policymakers and managers to design, plan, and implement socially responsive programs. It is suggested that policymakers at the macro level create the necessary incentive to institutionalize this concept by defining a social responsiveness metrics in the accreditation of nursing schools and allocating performance-based budgets.
